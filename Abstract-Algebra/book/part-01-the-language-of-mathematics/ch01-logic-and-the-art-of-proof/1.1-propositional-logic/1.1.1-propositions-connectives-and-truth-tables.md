# 1.1.1 Propositions, Connectives, and Truth Tables

Mathematics begins with statements — claims that are either true or false, not both, not neither. We call such a claim a **proposition**. The statement "17 is prime" is a proposition (it happens to be true); the statement "close the door" is not, since commands lack truth values. This simple requirement — that every proposition have a definite truth value — is the foundation on which all logical reasoning is built. Before we can prove anything, we need a precise vocabulary for assembling and analyzing statements, and that vocabulary begins here.

From simple propositions we build compound ones using five basic **logical connectives**. **Negation** $\neg P$ reverses the truth value of$P$: if$P$ is true,$\neg P$ is false, and vice versa. **Conjunction**$P \land Q$ is true precisely when both$P$ and$Q$ are true. **Disjunction**$P \lor Q$ is true when at least one of$P$,$Q$ is true — this is the inclusive or, not the exclusive kind; it is true even when both hold. **Biconditional**$P \Leftrightarrow Q$ is true when$P$ and$Q$ have the same truth value, whether both true or both false. Each connective captures a concept so basic that once you see it precisely defined, you recognize it everywhere.

The most important connective for mathematical proof is **implication**: $P \Rightarrow Q$, read "if$P$ then$Q$." Its truth table is the one that surprises students most. When$P$ is false, the implication$P \Rightarrow Q$ is declared true regardless of$Q$. This is not a philosophical trick; it reflects ordinary usage. The statement "if it is raining, then the ground is wet" says nothing at all about what happens when it is not raining — so we assign it no blame when it is not raining, and call it true by default. In mathematical terms: an implication with a false hypothesis is **vacuously true**, and vacuous truths appear regularly in algebra. For instance, every element of the empty set has any property you like, because the universal statement ranges over an empty domain and is vacuously true.

A **truth table** systematically records the truth value of a compound proposition under all possible assignments of truth values to its component atomic propositions. With $n$ propositional variables there are$2^n$ rows — one for each possible combination of truth and falsehood. The final column of the table gives the truth value of the compound formula in each case. Truth tables give us a completely mechanical way to check whether a given formula is always true, sometimes true, or never true, and that mechanical quality is precisely their value. Every complex mathematical statement, however elaborate, is ultimately built from these five connectives applied to atomic propositions, and every valid inference ultimately reduces to a truth-table verification. Understanding these tables deeply is what makes the proof strategies of the next sections feel inevitable rather than arbitrary.

A formula that is always true — that has only T's in its final truth-table column — is a **tautology**. A formula that is always false is a **contradiction**. Formulas can also be neither: contingent, sometimes true and sometimes false. The classification of formulas as tautologies, contradictions, or contingencies is the first level of logical analysis, and it directly governs which rules of inference are valid. We return to tautologies in Section 1.1.2, where they become the laws governing logical equivalence.

\begin{definition}
A \textbf{proposition} is a declarative sentence that has a definite truth value, either true (T) or false (F). The five standard \textbf{connectives} are negation $\neg$, conjunction$\land$, disjunction$\lor$, implication$\Rightarrow$, and biconditional$\Leftrightarrow$. Their truth values are defined by the following table, where$P$ and$Q$ range over propositions:
\par
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cc|ccccc}
$P$ &$Q$ &$\neg P$ &$P \land Q$ &$P \lor Q$ &$P \Rightarrow Q$ &$P \Leftrightarrow Q$ \\
\hline
T & T & F & T & T & T & T \\
T & F & F & F & T & F & F \\
F & T & T & F & T & T & F \\
F & F & T & F & F & T & T \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
Construct the truth table for $\neg P \lor Q$ and compare it with$P \Rightarrow Q$. With two variables there are four rows.
\par
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cc|cc}
$P$ &$Q$ &$\neg P$ &$\neg P \lor Q$ \\
\hline
T & T & F & T \\
T & F & F & F \\
F & T & T & T \\
F & F & T & T \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\par
Comparing with the column for $P \Rightarrow Q$ above, we see the final columns are identical in every row. Thus$(\neg P \lor Q)$ and$(P \Rightarrow Q)$ always have the same truth value. This demonstrates a key logical equivalence: implication is expressible as a disjunction. The statement "if it rains, I will bring an umbrella" is logically the same as "either it is not raining, or I have an umbrella."
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Determine which of the following is a tautology: (a) $P \lor \neg P$, (b)$P \land \neg P$, (c)$(P \Rightarrow Q) \Rightarrow (\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P)$.
\par
For (a): when $P$ is T,$\neg P$ is F, so$P \lor \neg P$ is T. When$P$ is F,$\neg P$ is T, so$P \lor \neg P$ is still T. Tautology.
\par
For (b): when $P$ is T,$P \land \neg P$ is$T \land F = F$. When$P$ is F, it is$F \land T = F$. Always false — this is a contradiction.
\par
For (c): this is the **contrapositive law**. Building the full four-row table, one checks that in each row either $P \Rightarrow Q$ is false (making the outer implication vacuously true) or$\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$ has the same truth value as$P \Rightarrow Q$. The result is all T's — a tautology. This confirms that every implication is logically equivalent to its contrapositive, a fact we will use constantly in proof-writing.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
The exclusive-or $P \oplus Q$ is true when exactly one of$P$,$Q$ is true. Express$P \oplus Q$ in terms of the five standard connectives, and build its truth table.
\par
We claim $P \oplus Q \equiv (P \lor Q) \land \neg(P \land Q)$: at least one holds, but not both. Checking:
\par
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cc|ccc}
$P$ &$Q$ &$P \lor Q$ &$P \land Q$ &$(P \lor Q) \land \neg(P \land Q)$ \\
\hline
T & T & T & T & F \\
T & F & T & F & T \\
F & T & T & F & T \\
F & F & F & F & F \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\par
The final column is T exactly when exactly one of $P$,$Q$ is true, confirming the representation. Notice also that$P \oplus Q \equiv (P \land \neg Q) \lor (\neg P \land Q)$, another valid expression for exclusive-or.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Evaluate the compound proposition $(P \Rightarrow Q) \land (Q \Rightarrow R) \Rightarrow (P \Rightarrow R)$ to show it is a tautology. This formalizes the transitivity of implication (the "hypothetical syllogism").
\par
With three variables there are $2^3 = 8$ rows. We check the crucial cases: if$P$ is true, then for the consequent$P \Rightarrow R$ to be false we need$R$ false. But if$R$ is false and$Q \Rightarrow R$ holds, then$Q$ must be false. And if$Q$ is false and$P \Rightarrow Q$ holds, then$P$ must be false — contradiction. So whenever$P$ is true and both implications$P \Rightarrow Q$ and$Q \Rightarrow R$ hold,$R$ must be true, so$P \Rightarrow R$ holds. In the rows where$P$ is false,$P \Rightarrow R$ is vacuously true. The formula is always true: a tautology. This is the logical engine behind chain arguments in direct proofs.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
Truth tables are infallible but slow for formulas with many variables: $n$ variables require$2^n$ rows, which grows quickly. For theoretical purposes — verifying that a given inference form is valid, or that two formulas are logically equivalent — the truth table is definitive. For practical proof-writing, we internalize the key tautologies and equivalences as reusable tools, invoking them by name rather than re-verifying them from scratch each time. The table in this section establishes the ground floor; every rule of inference we use in subsequent chapters is, at bottom, a tautology of the kind established here.
\end{remark}
