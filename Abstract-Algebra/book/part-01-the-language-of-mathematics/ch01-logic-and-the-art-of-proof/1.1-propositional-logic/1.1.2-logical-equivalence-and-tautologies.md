# 1.1.2 Logical Equivalence and Tautologies

Two compound propositions can look very different and yet always agree on their truth value. When this happens — when $P$ and$Q$ have the same truth value under every possible assignment to their component variables — we say$P$ and$Q$ are **logically equivalent**, written$P \equiv Q$. Logical equivalence is not the same as the biconditional$P \Leftrightarrow Q$: the biconditional is a proposition that may be true or false depending on the truth values of$P$ and$Q$, whereas logical equivalence is a meta-level assertion that the biconditional$P \Leftrightarrow Q$ is a tautology — that it comes out true no matter what. We write$P \equiv Q$ to express this stronger claim.

A **tautology** is a proposition that is true under every truth assignment — its truth table has nothing but T's in the final column. Tautologies are not merely curiosities; they are the laws of logic. Every valid rule of inference corresponds to a tautology, and every step we take in a formal proof is licensed by one. The most important tautologies for proof-writing are the logical equivalences we use to transform statements into equivalent but more convenient forms. Learning them is not memorization for its own sake; it is acquiring a toolkit of transformations that will feel indispensable after you have used them a few hundred times.

The most practically important equivalences are the following. **Double negation** states $\neg\neg P \equiv P$: negating twice returns to the original. **De Morgan's laws** —$\neg(P \land Q) \equiv \neg P \lor \neg Q$ and$\neg(P \lor Q) \equiv \neg P \land \neg Q$ — are the key tool for negating compound statements: the negation of "both$P$ and$Q$" is "either not-$P$ or not-$Q$," and the negation of "either$P$ or$Q$" is "neither$P$ nor$Q$." The **contrapositive law**$P \Rightarrow Q \equiv \neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$ tells us that to prove an implication we may prove its contrapositive instead; both carry exactly the same logical content, but one form may be more tractable than the other. The **law of exportation**$(P \land Q) \Rightarrow R \equiv P \Rightarrow (Q \Rightarrow R)$ lets us reorganize hypotheses, and the **distributive laws** say$P \land (Q \lor R) \equiv (P \land Q) \lor (P \land R)$ and$P \lor (Q \land R) \equiv (P \lor Q) \land (P \lor R)$.

Notice that De Morgan's laws for propositions are precisely mirrored by De Morgan's laws for sets (Section 2.1.2). This is not a coincidence. The elements of a set $A$ satisfying a predicate$P(x)$ correspond to the truth assignments making$P$ true, and the Boolean algebra of propositions is structurally identical to the algebra of subsets. Recognizing this parallel makes both theories easier to remember and to transfer between.

One more pair of equivalences deserves emphasis because it governs how we handle conditionals in proofs. The fact that $P \Rightarrow Q \equiv \neg P \lor Q$ says implication is definable from negation and disjunction. This is the source of the "vacuous truth" phenomenon: when$P$ is false,$\neg P$ is true, so$\neg P \lor Q$ is true regardless of$Q$. And the biconditional satisfies$P \Leftrightarrow Q \equiv (P \Rightarrow Q) \land (Q \Rightarrow P)$: to prove an "if and only if" we must prove both directions. This decomposition of the biconditional into two implications is the template for every "if and only if" proof.

\begin{definition}
Two propositions $P$ and$Q$ are \textbf{logically equivalent}, written$P \equiv Q$, if$P \Leftrightarrow Q$ is a tautology — that is,$P$ and$Q$ have the same truth value under every assignment of truth values to their atomic components. A proposition is a \textbf{tautology} if it is true under every truth assignment, a \textbf{contradiction} if false under every assignment, and \textbf{contingent} if neither.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
Verify De Morgan's law $\neg(P \land Q) \equiv \neg P \lor \neg Q$ by truth table.
\par
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cc|cccc}
$P$ &$Q$ &$P \land Q$ &$\neg(P \land Q)$ &$\neg P \lor \neg Q$ & Equal? \\
\hline
T & T & T & F & F & \checkmark \\
T & F & F & T & T & \checkmark \\
F & T & F & T & T & \checkmark \\
F & F & F & T & T & \checkmark \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\par
Columns 4 and 5 are identical, confirming the equivalence. Interpreting: the negation of "both $P$ and$Q$ are true" is "at least one of them is false," which is precisely "not-$P$ or not-$Q$."
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Show that modus ponens is a tautology: $(P \land (P \Rightarrow Q)) \Rightarrow Q$.
\par
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cc|ccc}
$P$ &$Q$ &$P \Rightarrow Q$ &$P \land (P \Rightarrow Q)$ &$(P \land (P \Rightarrow Q)) \Rightarrow Q$ \\
\hline
T & T & T & T & T \\
T & F & F & F & T \\
F & T & T & F & T \\
F & F & T & F & T \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\par
The final column is all T's. This is modus ponens: if we know $P$ and we know$P \Rightarrow Q$, we can conclude$Q$. It is valid precisely because the formula is a tautology.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Show that $P \Rightarrow Q \equiv \neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$ (contrapositive law), and show by counter-example that$P \Rightarrow Q$ is NOT equivalent to its **converse**$Q \Rightarrow P$.
\par
For the contrapositive, compare the truth tables:
\par
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cc|cc}
$P$ &$Q$ &$P \Rightarrow Q$ &$\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$ \\
\hline
T & T & T & T \\
T & F & F & F \\
F & T & T & T \\
F & F & T & T \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\par
The columns are identical: the implication and its contrapositive are equivalent.
\par
For the converse, compare $P \Rightarrow Q$ with$Q \Rightarrow P$ in row 3:$P$ is F and$Q$ is T, so$P \Rightarrow Q$ is T but$Q \Rightarrow P$ is F. They differ, so they are not equivalent. Concretely: "if$n$ is even then$n$ is divisible by 2" is true; its converse "if$n$ is divisible by 2 then$n$ is even" happens also to be true, but this is a separate mathematical fact, not a logical consequence. "If$n > 5$ then$n > 2$" is true; its converse "if$n > 2$ then$n > 5$" is false. The converse of a true implication need not be true.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Use logical equivalences — without a truth table — to simplify $\neg(P \Rightarrow Q)$.
\par
\begin{align*}
\neg(P \Rightarrow Q) &\equiv \neg(\neg P \lor Q) && \text{(implication as disjunction)} \\
&\equiv \neg\neg P \land \neg Q && \text{(De Morgan)} \\
&\equiv P \land \neg Q. && \text{(double negation)}
\end{align*}
\par
So the negation of "if $P$ then$Q$" is "$P$ and not-$Q$": it says the hypothesis holds but the conclusion fails. This is exactly the form a counterexample must take: we need$P$ to be true and$Q$ to be false. Whenever we want to disprove an implication, we look for an instance where the hypothesis is satisfied but the conclusion is not.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
The equivalences established in this section form a complete toolkit for manipulating logical statements in algebra. The contrapositive law, De Morgan's laws, and the equivalence $P \Rightarrow Q \equiv \neg P \lor Q$ appear in nearly every chapter of this book. The habit of simplifying negations by driving$\neg$ inward through compound statements — using De Morgan and double negation — is especially important when working with definitions that involve multiple quantifiers, as we will see in Section 1.2.2. Tautologies are the invariants of propositional logic: they are the truths that hold regardless of the specific content of the propositions involved, and they are precisely what makes logical reasoning transferable from one domain to another.
\end{remark}
