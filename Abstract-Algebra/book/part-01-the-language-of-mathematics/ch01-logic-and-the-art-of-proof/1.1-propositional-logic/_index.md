# 1.1 Propositional Logic

The most basic question one can ask about a mathematical statement is whether it is true or false. Propositional logic is the study of how the truth or falsity of complex statements is determined by the truth or falsity of their components. A *proposition* is any sentence that has a definite truth value — it is either true ($\top$) or false ($\bot$) — and the central objects of study are the *connectives* that combine propositions into new ones: negation $\neg P$ (true exactly when $P$ is false), conjunction $P \wedge Q$ (true when both are true), disjunction $P \vee Q$ (true when at least one is true), and the conditional $P \Rightarrow Q$ (false only when $P$ is true and $Q$ is false). The biconditional $P \Leftrightarrow Q$ asserts that $P$ and $Q$ have identical truth values and is the formal expression of "if and only if."

Truth tables are the decision procedure for propositional logic. Given a compound formula in $n$ propositional variables, there are $2^n$ possible assignments of truth values, and the truth table lists the truth value of the formula on every row. Two formulas are *logically equivalent* if they agree on every row — if no assignment of truth values can distinguish between them. Among the most important equivalences are De Morgan's laws: $\neg(P \wedge Q) \equiv \neg P \vee \neg Q$ and $\neg(P \vee Q) \equiv \neg P \wedge \neg Q$. These laws describe how negation distributes over conjunction and disjunction, and they will appear later as the duality principle in set theory (Section 2.1) and in the theory of ideals. Equally important is the equivalence $P \Rightarrow Q \equiv \neg P \vee Q$, which says that a conditional is a disguised disjunction. From it one derives immediately the equivalence of a conditional with its *contrapositive* $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$, which is the logical foundation of the proof strategy studied in Section 1.3.

A formula that is true under every possible assignment is called a *tautology*. Tautologies are the logical laws — the statements that are true purely in virtue of their logical form, independently of any content. The law of excluded middle ($P \vee \neg P$), the law of non-contradiction ($\neg(P \wedge \neg P)$), and *modus ponens* (the inference from $P$ and $P \Rightarrow Q$ to $Q$) are foundational examples. The system of propositional logic is decidable: given any formula, one can determine in finitely many steps whether it is a tautology, a contradiction, or neither, simply by completing the truth table. This decidability is what makes propositional logic a reliable and fully mechanizable basis for mathematical reasoning, even if the exponential growth of truth tables makes it impractical for large formulas. The section thus establishes both the expressive power and the limits of truth-functional logic, preparing the way for the quantified language introduced in the next section.
