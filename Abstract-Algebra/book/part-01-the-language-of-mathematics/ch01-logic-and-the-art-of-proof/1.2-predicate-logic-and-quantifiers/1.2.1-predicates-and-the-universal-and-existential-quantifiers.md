# 1.2.1 Predicates and the Universal and Existential Quantifiers

Propositional logic handles statements that are simply true or false as given. But most mathematical claims are not about a fixed collection of named objects — they are about all objects of a certain kind, or about the existence of at least one object with a certain property. "Every prime greater than 2 is odd." "There exists a group of order 6 that is not abelian." "For every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists$\delta > 0$ such that$|f(x) - L| < \varepsilon$ whenever$|x - a| < \delta$." To express these, we need a richer language — the language of **predicates** and **quantifiers**.

A **predicate** $P(x)$ is an expression that contains a free variable$x$ and becomes a proposition once we substitute a specific value for$x$. The expression "$x$ is a prime number" is not itself true or false; it becomes a proposition only when$x$ is assigned a value:$P(7)$ is true,$P(9)$ is false. The variable$x$ ranges over some understood **domain of discourse** — here, the positive integers. A predicate is, in essence, a property that elements of the domain may or may not possess. Predicates can also depend on several variables:$P(x, y)$ meaning "$x$ divides$y$" becomes a proposition only once both$x$ and$y$ are specified.

From predicates we form propositions using two quantifiers. The **universal quantifier** $\forall x \in S,\, P(x)$ asserts that$P(x)$ holds for every element$x$ in the set$S$. It is read "for all$x$ in$S$,$P(x)$" or "for every$x$ in$S$,$P(x)$." The **existential quantifier**$\exists x \in S,\, P(x)$ asserts that$P(x)$ holds for at least one element of$S$. It is read "there exists an$x$ in$S$ such that$P(x)$." The domain$S$ is always specified or understood from context; changing the domain can change the truth value entirely.

To prove a universally quantified statement $\forall x \in S,\, P(x)$, we let$x$ be an arbitrary but fixed element of$S$ and prove$P(x)$ for that$x$. Since$x$ was arbitrary, the conclusion holds for all elements. This "let$x \in S$ be arbitrary" opening is the standard first line of a universal proof. To prove an existential statement$\exists x \in S,\, P(x)$, we exhibit a specific element$x_0 \in S$ and verify$P(x_0)$. The element$x_0$ is called a **witness**.

Notice that every mathematical definition and theorem you will encounter in this book is, at its core, a quantified statement. "A group $G$ is abelian" means$\forall a, b \in G,\, ab = ba$. "Every subgroup of an abelian group is normal" is$\forall G\, [G \text{ abelian}] \Rightarrow \forall H \leq G,\, [H \text{ is normal in } G]$. "There exists a non-abelian group of order 8" is an existential statement. Learning to read and write these quantifiers precisely — and to keep careful track of what domain each variable ranges over — is the single most important skill for reading and writing formal mathematics.

\begin{definition}
A \textbf{predicate} on a set $S$ is a function$P: S \to \{\mathrm{T}, \mathrm{F}\}$ assigning to each element$x \in S$ a truth value$P(x)$. Given a predicate$P$ on$S$:
\begin{itemize}
\item The \textbf{universal quantification} is the proposition $\forall x \in S,\, P(x)$, which is true if and only if$P(x) = \mathrm{T}$ for every$x \in S$.
\item The \textbf{existential quantification} is the proposition $\exists x \in S,\, P(x)$, which is true if and only if$P(x) = \mathrm{T}$ for at least one$x \in S$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
Consider the domain $S = \mathbb{Z}$ and the predicate$P(n) = (n^2 \geq 0)$.
\par
The statement $\forall n \in \mathbb{Z},\, n^2 \geq 0$ is true: the square of any integer is non-negative. To prove it, let$n \in \mathbb{Z}$ be arbitrary. We have$n \geq 0$ or$n < 0$. In the first case$n^2 = n \cdot n \geq 0$. In the second case$n = -m$ with$m > 0$, so$n^2 = m^2 > 0$. In either case$n^2 \geq 0$.$\square$
\par
Now consider $Q(n) = (n^2 > 0)$. The statement$\forall n \in \mathbb{Z},\, Q(n)$ is false:$n = 0$ is a counterexample, since$0^2 = 0 \not> 0$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Prove: $\exists x \in \mathbb{R},\, x^2 = 2$.
\par
We exhibit the witness $x_0 = \sqrt{2}$. (This requires knowing that$\sqrt{2}$ exists as a real number — a fact about the completeness of$\mathbb{R}$.) Then$x_0^2 = (\sqrt{2})^2 = 2$.$\square$
\par
The same predicate over the domain $\mathbb{Q}$ gives a false statement:$\exists x \in \mathbb{Q},\, x^2 = 2$ is false, as proved by the irrationality of$\sqrt{2}$ (Section 1.1.3, Example 3). The domain matters.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $G$ be a group. Write the definition of "every element of$G$ has finite order" as a quantified statement, and identify the proof obligation.
\par
The statement is: $\forall g \in G,\, \exists n \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0},\, g^n = e$, where$e$ is the identity element. To prove this for a specific group, we must take an arbitrary$g \in G$ and produce a specific positive integer$n$ (depending on$g$) such that$g^n = e$. The integer$n$ is the witness for the existential quantifier, and it is allowed to depend on$g$ because the$\exists$ appears inside the scope of the$\forall$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Consider the predicate $P(a, b)$ meaning "$a$ divides$b$" on the domain$\mathbb{Z}_{>0} \times \mathbb{Z}_{>0}$.
\par
(a) $\forall a \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0},\, P(a, a)$ — reflexivity of divisibility — is true: every positive integer divides itself.
\par
(b) $\forall a, b \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0},\, P(a,b) \Rightarrow P(b,a)$ — symmetry — is false:$P(2, 6)$ is true ($2 \mid 6$), but$P(6, 2)$ is false ($6 \nmid 2$). The pair$(a,b) = (2,6)$ is a counterexample.
\par
(c) $\forall a, b, c \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0},\, P(a,b) \land P(b,c) \Rightarrow P(a,c)$ — transitivity — is true. Let$a, b, c$ be arbitrary with$a \mid b$ and$b \mid c$. Write$b = ka$ and$c = lb$. Then$c = l(ka) = (lk)a$, so$a \mid c$.
\par
This example previews Section 2.2, where reflexivity, symmetry (and antisymmetry), and transitivity are the defining properties of equivalence relations and partial orders.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
The quantifiers $\forall$ and$\exists$ and the implication$\Rightarrow$ together suffice to express virtually every mathematical definition and theorem. Developing the habit of reading every definition and theorem as a structured sequence of quantifiers — identifying which variables are universally versus existentially quantified, in what order, and over what domains — is the prerequisite for the proof strategies in Section 1.3. It also prepares us for the subtleties of nested quantifiers, where the order of$\forall$ and$\exists$ profoundly affects meaning, which is the subject of the next section.
\end{remark}
