# 1.2.2 Negating Quantified Statements

To disprove a mathematical claim — or to set up a proof by contradiction — we must be able to negate it precisely. The negation of a quantified statement follows a clean rule: quantifiers swap, and the predicate is negated. This rule is the predicate-logic analogue of De Morgan's laws, and it is equally fundamental to proof-writing.

The two negation laws are:
$$\neg(\forall x \in S,\, P(x)) \equiv \exists x \in S,\, \neg P(x)$$
$$\neg(\exists x \in S,\, P(x)) \equiv \forall x \in S,\, \neg P(x).$$
The first says: to show it is not the case that every element has property $P$, it suffices — and is necessary — to exhibit a single element that fails$P$. This is a **counterexample**. The second says: to show there is no element with property$P$, you must demonstrate that every element lacks$P$. These are not merely formal manipulations; they tell us exactly what kind of argument is needed to refute a statement, before we have even started trying.

The logical justification is transparent. The statement $\forall x \in S,\, P(x)$ is true precisely when$P(x)$ holds for every$x$; it is false precisely when$P(x)$ fails for at least one$x$ — that is, when$\exists x \in S,\, \neg P(x)$ holds. Dually,$\exists x \in S,\, P(x)$ is false precisely when no$x$ satisfies$P$ — that is, when every$x$ satisfies$\neg P$, i.e.,$\forall x \in S,\, \neg P(x)$. The two laws are each other's mirror images, and together they say: quantifiers invert under negation.

The reason these laws are so fundamental is that they govern the structure of the argument you must produce. Before you begin to prove or disprove anything, it is worth negating the statement explicitly and reading what you have. The negation tells you: in a proof by contradiction, what you may assume; in a direct refutation, what you must exhibit. In complex cases — where the statement involves nested quantifiers — this process of careful negation is the roadmap to the proof. We will apply this discipline repeatedly when working with definitions involving multiple quantifiers, such as the definition of a normal subgroup or the universal property of a quotient.

Negating a compound statement involving both quantifiers and propositional connectives requires driving the negation all the way in, one layer at a time: swap quantifiers and negate the predicate, then if the predicate is itself compound, apply De Morgan's laws or the rule $\neg(P \Rightarrow Q) \equiv P \land \neg Q$ from Section 1.1.2. The systematic procedure is always the same — peel off one logical symbol at a time, applying the appropriate negation rule at each step.

\begin{example}
Negate the statement: "Every integer is either even or odd."
\par
Formally: $\forall n \in \mathbb{Z},\, (E(n) \lor O(n))$, where$E(n)$ means "$n$ is even" and$O(n)$ means "$n$ is odd."
\par
Negating: $\neg[\forall n \in \mathbb{Z},\, (E(n) \lor O(n))] \equiv \exists n \in \mathbb{Z},\, \neg(E(n) \lor O(n)) \equiv \exists n \in \mathbb{Z},\, (\neg E(n) \land \neg O(n)).$
\par
The negation says: "There exists an integer that is neither even nor odd." To disprove the original, we would need to exhibit such an integer; since no such integer exists, the original statement is true.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Negate the definition of injectivity and interpret the result.
\par
A function $f: A \to B$ is injective if$\forall a_1, a_2 \in A,\, f(a_1) = f(a_2) \Rightarrow a_1 = a_2$.
\par
Negating step by step:
\begin{align*}
&\neg[\forall a_1, a_2 \in A,\, f(a_1) = f(a_2) \Rightarrow a_1 = a_2] \\
&\equiv \exists a_1, a_2 \in A,\, \neg[f(a_1) = f(a_2) \Rightarrow a_1 = a_2] \\
&\equiv \exists a_1, a_2 \in A,\, [f(a_1) = f(a_2) \land a_1 \neq a_2].
\end{align*}
The negation says: $f$ is not injective if and only if there exist two distinct elements of$A$ that map to the same element of$B$. This is exactly what a counterexample to injectivity looks like — a specific pair$a_1 \neq a_2$ with$f(a_1) = f(a_2)$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Negate the statement "Every continuous function on $[0,1]$ achieves its maximum value."
\par
Formally: $\forall f \in C([0,1]),\, \exists x^* \in [0,1],\, \forall x \in [0,1],\, f(x) \leq f(x^*)$.
\par
Negation: $\exists f \in C([0,1]),\, \neg[\exists x^* \in [0,1],\, \forall x \in [0,1],\, f(x) \leq f(x^*)]$
\par
$\equiv \exists f \in C([0,1]),\, \forall x^* \in [0,1],\, \neg[\forall x \in [0,1],\, f(x) \leq f(x^*)]$
\par
$\equiv \exists f \in C([0,1]),\, \forall x^* \in [0,1],\, \exists x \in [0,1],\, f(x) > f(x^*)$.
\par
The negation says: there exists a continuous function on $[0,1]$ such that for every candidate maximum point$x^*$, the function exceeds$f(x^*)$ somewhere. (Of course, by the Extreme Value Theorem, no such function exists — the original statement is a theorem. But the negation clarifies precisely what kind of counterexample would be needed.)
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Use negation to set up a proof by contradiction. Prove that there is no largest natural number.
\par
Negate the conclusion: "There is a largest natural number" means $\exists M \in \mathbb{N},\, \forall n \in \mathbb{N},\, n \leq M$.
\par
Assume this for contradiction. Then there exists $M \in \mathbb{N}$ such that$M \geq n$ for all$n \in \mathbb{N}$. But$M + 1 \in \mathbb{N}$ and$M + 1 > M$, contradicting the assumption that$M$ is an upper bound. The assumption is false; there is no largest natural number.$\square$
\par
Notice that the entire structure of the proof was visible once we wrote down the negation: assuming the negation gave us a concrete $M$ to work with, and the contradiction was immediate.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
The habit of negating a statement before beginning to prove or disprove it is one of the most valuable practices in mathematical writing. It clarifies the target (in a direct proof, we aim for the stated conclusion; in a contradiction, we aim for an absurdity after assuming the negation), and it reveals the structure of a potential counterexample. In algebra, many of the most important theorems are universal claims about all groups, all rings, or all modules, and their negations are existential claims that require explicit counterexamples — or, when the statement is true, are exactly what we must show cannot be constructed. Section 1.2.3 extends these techniques to statements with multiple nested quantifiers, where the negation process requires multiple applications of the rules established here.
\end{remark}
