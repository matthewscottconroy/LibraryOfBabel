# 1.2 Predicate Logic and Quantifiers

Propositional logic is adequate for reasoning about fixed, named statements, but mathematics is overwhelmingly concerned with claims about infinite families of objects — "every integer has a successor," "there exists a prime larger than any given prime," "for any two real numbers $x < y$, there is a rational between them." None of these can be expressed as a truth function of simpler propositions, because they range over entire domains rather than particular named individuals. Predicate logic extends propositional logic by introducing two new mechanisms: predicates and quantifiers.

A *predicate* $P(x)$ is a formula containing a free variable $x$; it is not itself a proposition until $x$ is assigned a value from some domain of discourse $D$. For example, $P(x)$ might mean "$x$ is divisible by 3" (with $D = \mathbb{Z}$) or "$x$ is a group homomorphism" (with $D$ being some class of functions). The *universal quantifier* $\forall x \in D,\, P(x)$ asserts that $P(x)$ holds for every $x$ in $D$, while the *existential quantifier* $\exists x \in D,\, P(x)$ asserts that at least one element of $D$ satisfies $P$. Quantifiers can be nested and alternated, and the order matters enormously: $\forall x\, \exists y\, (y > x)$ says that every element has something larger than it (true in $\mathbb{N}$), while $\exists y\, \forall x\, (y > x)$ says there is a single element larger than everything else (false in $\mathbb{N}$). The ability to write such statements concisely and manipulate them by rule is the primary contribution of predicate logic to mathematical practice.

The negation rules for quantifiers are among the most practically indispensable tools in mathematics. The negation of a universal statement is existential: $\neg(\forall x\, P(x)) \equiv \exists x\, \neg P(x)$. The negation of an existential statement is universal: $\neg(\exists x\, P(x)) \equiv \forall x\, \neg P(x)$. For nested quantifiers, these rules apply one step at a time from the outside in: $\neg(\forall x\, \exists y\, P(x,y)) \equiv \exists x\, \forall y\, \neg P(x,y)$. Understanding these transformations is essential for recognizing what it means to refute a mathematical claim. To disprove $\forall x\, P(x)$, one must exhibit a *counterexample* — a specific $x$ for which $P(x)$ fails. This section thus supplies the formal tools that sit behind every "find a counterexample" exercise and every proof that begins by fixing an arbitrary element of a domain. The predicate-logic framework also sets up the language of definitions in algebra: a group, for instance, is defined by a list of universally quantified axioms, and the content of those axioms becomes readable once one has internalized the notation of this section.
