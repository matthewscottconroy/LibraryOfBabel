# 1.3.1 Direct Proof and Chain Arguments

The most natural proof strategy — and the one we reach for first — is the **direct proof**: assume the hypotheses, apply definitions and previously established facts in a logical chain, and arrive at the conclusion. Every step must be justified by naming the principle that licenses it. This discipline is not bureaucratic formality; it is precisely what separates a convincing mathematical argument from a plausible-sounding sketch.

The structure of a direct proof is a sequence of implications: $P \Rightarrow P_1 \Rightarrow P_2 \Rightarrow \cdots \Rightarrow Q$. Each arrow requires a reason — a definition unpacked, a lemma applied, an arithmetic identity invoked. The reasons in algebraic arguments are typically of three kinds: definitions (unpacking what it means for an integer to be even, for a function to be a homomorphism, for a set to be a subgroup), previously proven lemmas, or basic arithmetic and algebraic identities. Writing out these reasons — even briefly, in parenthetical annotations — is what makes a proof verifiable and what reveals gaps when they exist.

A direct proof should open by assuming the hypotheses and immediately unpacking the definitions that appear in them. If the hypothesis is "$n$ is even," write$n = 2k$ for some$k \in \mathbb{Z}$. If the hypothesis is "$f$ is a group homomorphism," write the defining property$f(ab) = f(a)f(b)$ and note$f(e_G) = e_H$. If the hypothesis is "$H$ is a subgroup of$G$," note that$H$ is closed under the group operation, contains the identity, and is closed under inverses. Unpacking definitions at the start of a proof is not optional — it is how you translate the abstract language of hypotheses into concrete computational content you can work with.

**Chain arguments** arise when we link multiple implications in a sequence to establish something about a longer sequence of objects or to traverse several levels of structure. A divisibility argument is typical: if $a \mid b$ and$b \mid c$ then$a \mid c$ — the proof writes$b = ka$ and$c = lb$, substitutes to get$c = lka$, and reads off divisibility from the explicit expression. The same chain structure appears in transitivity arguments for all the relations we will study in Chapter 2: subset inclusion, congruence modulo$n$, and coset containment all require the same chain-building technique.

A direct proof is the cleanest option when the hypotheses give you something concrete to work with and the path to the conclusion is not blocked by the need to rule out alternatives. Geometric arguments, divisibility calculations, and most algebraic manipulations all fit this pattern. When the direct path is unclear — when the hypothesis is abstract and the conclusion is a specific object — the strategies of the next section become more powerful. But defaulting to direct proof whenever it is viable keeps proofs transparent and verifiable.

\begin{example}
Prove that the sum of two rational numbers is rational.
\par
Let $r, s \in \mathbb{Q}$. By definition of rationality,$r = p/q$ and$s = m/n$ where$p, q, m, n \in \mathbb{Z}$ and$q, n \neq 0$. Compute:
$$r + s = \frac{p}{q} + \frac{m}{n} = \frac{pn + mq}{qn}.$$
Since $p, n, m, q \in \mathbb{Z}$, we have$pn + mq \in \mathbb{Z}$. Since$q \neq 0$ and$n \neq 0$, we have$qn \neq 0$. Therefore$r + s = (pn + mq)/(qn)$ is the ratio of an integer to a nonzero integer, hence$r + s \in \mathbb{Q}$.$\square$
\par
Each step is justified: first by the definition of rationality, then by closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ under multiplication and addition, then by the definition of rationality again.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Prove the transitivity of divisibility: if $a \mid b$ and$b \mid c$ (with$a, b, c \in \mathbb{Z}$), then$a \mid c$.
\par
Assume $a \mid b$ and$b \mid c$. By definition of divisibility,$b = ka$ for some$k \in \mathbb{Z}$ and$c = lb$ for some$l \in \mathbb{Z}$. Substituting the first equation into the second:
$$c = lb = l(ka) = (lk)a.$$
Since $lk \in \mathbb{Z}$, we have$a \mid c$.$\square$
\par
This is the chain argument at its clearest: two separate applications of the definition, followed by substitution, yield the desired divisibility.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $A \subseteq B$ and$B \subseteq C$. Prove$A \subseteq C$.
\par
We use the definition: $A \subseteq B$ means$\forall x,\, x \in A \Rightarrow x \in B$.
\par
Let $x \in A$ be arbitrary. Since$A \subseteq B$, we have$x \in B$. Since$B \subseteq C$, we have$x \in C$. Since$x$ was arbitrary,$A \subseteq C$.$\square$
\par
This is subset inclusion as a chain argument: $x \in A \Rightarrow x \in B \Rightarrow x \in C$. The same proof template works for any transitive relation.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Prove that if $f: G \to H$ and$g: H \to K$ are group homomorphisms, then$g \circ f: G \to K$ is a group homomorphism.
\par
Let $a, b \in G$ be arbitrary. We compute:
\begin{align*}
(g \circ f)(ab) &= g(f(ab)) && \text{(definition of composition)} \\
&= g(f(a)f(b)) && \text{($f$ is a homomorphism)} \\
&= g(f(a))\, g(f(b)) && \text{($g$ is a homomorphism)} \\
&= (g \circ f)(a)\, (g \circ f)(b). && \text{(definition of composition)}
\end{align*}
Since $(g \circ f)(ab) = (g \circ f)(a)(g \circ f)(b)$ for all$a, b \in G$, the composition$g \circ f$ is a homomorphism.$\square$
\par
Each equality is annotated. The proof is a direct chain — no cases, no contradiction — and it will reappear when we study the category of groups.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
The discipline of annotating each step — not just writing a sequence of equalities, but recording why each step is valid — is the most important practical habit in algebraic proof-writing. Annotations need not be lengthy: a parenthetical phrase like "(definition of subgroup)" or "(distributive law)" is sufficient. But writing them, even briefly, forces you to ask whether each step is actually justified, and this is where errors are most often caught. Chain arguments, in particular, can be deceptively easy to extend by one step too many — a step that feels obvious but actually requires a lemma not yet proved. The discipline of annotating every step is the safeguard against this class of error.
\end{remark}
