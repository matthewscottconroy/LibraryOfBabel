# 1.3.2 Proof by Contradiction and Proof by Contrapositive

Some theorems resist direct proof because the hypotheses are hard to use or because the conclusion is a non-existence claim. For these, two indirect strategies are available, each drawing on the logical equivalences established in Section 1.1.2. Both are completely rigorous; they differ in how the proof is structured and, more practically, in which formulation gives you the more workable algebraic environment to operate in.

In a **proof by contradiction** (also called *reductio ad absurdum*) we assume the negation of the conclusion alongside the hypotheses, and derive a statement that is known to be false. Since every step in the deduction is valid, the blame falls on the assumed negation of the conclusion, which must therefore have been false — so the conclusion holds. This strategy is especially powerful when the conclusion is a non-existence statement ("there is no rational solution," "there is no finite generating set," "there is no bijection from $\mathbb{N}$ to$\mathbb{R}$") because assuming the object does exist hands us something concrete to manipulate algebraically until impossibility emerges. It is also natural when the contradiction can be reached quickly by combining the assumed negation with the hypotheses, even if the direct path from hypothesis to conclusion is less clear.

A **proof by contrapositive** takes a different route: instead of proving $P \Rightarrow Q$, we prove the logically equivalent statement$\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$. We assume$\neg Q$ is true and deduce$\neg P$. This is often cleaner than contradiction because it remains a direct argument — we have a specific hypothesis ($\neg Q$) and work toward a specific conclusion ($\neg P$), rather than searching open-endedly for an inconsistency. The contrapositive is the right choice when the negation of the conclusion is more algebraically concrete than the hypothesis. The pattern to recognize: when the hypothesis is a "positive" assertion that is hard to work with directly, and the negation of the conclusion is a "concrete alternative" we can manipulate, the contrapositive is the natural framing.

How do you decide which to use? Consider what the negation of the conclusion looks like. If it gives you a concrete object or equation to work with, contradiction is likely natural. If the negation of the conclusion gives you a clean algebraic hypothesis — "assume $\neg Q$: then$n$ is odd, or$f$ is not injective, or$I$ is not a proper ideal" — and from that you can derive the negation of the hypothesis by direct calculation, the contrapositive is cleaner. In practice, the two strategies often feel interchangeable; the difference is whether you name the assumption as "for contradiction, assume$\neg Q$" (and use both$P$ and$\neg Q$) or as "for the contrapositive, assume$\neg Q$" (and derive$\neg P$ without using$P$). When you use only$\neg Q$ and do not use$P$, you are proving the contrapositive; when you use both$P$ and$\neg Q$ to derive the contradiction, you are strictly doing contradiction.

\begin{example}
Prove by contradiction: there are infinitely many prime numbers.
\par
Assume for contradiction that there are only finitely many primes: $p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_k$. Consider$N = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k + 1$. Since$N > 1$, it has at least one prime divisor$p$. But$p$ cannot be any of$p_1, \ldots, p_k$: for each$i$,$p_i$ divides$p_1 \cdots p_k$ but leaves remainder 1 when dividing$N$, so$p_i \nmid N$. Thus$p$ is a prime not in our list, contradicting the assumption that the list was complete.$\square$
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Prove by contrapositive: if $n^2$ is odd, then$n$ is odd (for$n \in \mathbb{Z}$).
\par
The contrapositive: if $n$ is even, then$n^2$ is even. Assume$n$ is even, so$n = 2k$ for some$k \in \mathbb{Z}$. Then$n^2 = (2k)^2 = 4k^2 = 2(2k^2)$. Since$2k^2 \in \mathbb{Z}$, we have$n^2$ even. The contrapositive is proved, so the original implication holds.$\square$
\par
A direct proof would require taking $n^2 = 2m + 1$ (odd) and extracting the parity of$n$ — which is less clear. The contrapositive gives a clean starting hypothesis.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Prove by contradiction: $\log_2 3$ is irrational.
\par
Assume for contradiction that $\log_2 3 = p/q$ with$p, q \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ and$\gcd(p, q) = 1$. Then$3 = 2^{p/q}$, so$3^q = 2^p$. The left side is odd (a power of the odd number 3) and the right side is even (a positive power of 2). An odd integer cannot equal an even integer — contradiction.$\square$
\par
The contradiction arises by comparing parities, a clean algebraic argument that does not appear from a direct approach.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Prove by contrapositive: if a group $G$ is non-abelian, then$|G| \geq 6$.
\par
Contrapositive: if $|G| < 6$ (i.e.,$|G| \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$), then$G$ is abelian. We verify each case.
\par
Groups of order 1, 2, 3, and 5 are cyclic (orders 1, 2, 3, 5 are either 1 or prime, and groups of prime order are cyclic by Lagrange's theorem). Every cyclic group is abelian.
\par
Groups of order 4 are either cyclic (hence abelian) or isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, which is also abelian (both factors are abelian and the direct product of abelian groups is abelian).
\par
In all cases $|G| < 6$ implies$G$ is abelian. The contrapositive is proved.$\square$
\par
The proof of the contrapositive is a complete case analysis on the possible group orders below 6, each handled directly.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
This might seem surprising, but choosing between contradiction and contrapositive is rarely about logic — it is about which formulation gives you a cleaner starting hypothesis to work with. Both are indirect proofs in that they avoid proving $P \Rightarrow Q$ head-on; both are completely rigorous. In contradiction, you use both$P$ and$\neg Q$, which gives you more material but a more open-ended goal (derive any absurdity). In contrapositive, you use only$\neg Q$ and aim for$\neg P$, which is more structured. Throughout this book we will flag these choices explicitly, so you develop the instinct for which formulation makes the algebraic structure transparent. The key test: if you assume$\neg Q$ and immediately see a path to$\neg P$ without ever invoking$P$, you have a contrapositive proof. If you need to combine$\neg Q$ with$P$ to derive the contradiction, you have a contradiction proof.
\end{remark}
