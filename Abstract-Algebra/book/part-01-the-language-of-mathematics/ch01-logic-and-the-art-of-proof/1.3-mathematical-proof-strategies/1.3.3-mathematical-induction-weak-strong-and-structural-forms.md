# 1.3.3 Mathematical Induction: Weak, Strong, and Structural Forms

Many mathematical facts have the form "property $P(n)$ holds for every non-negative integer$n$." Direct proof is impractical here — we cannot check infinitely many cases one by one. Mathematical induction is the tool that converts this infinite family of claims into a two-step finite argument. It is not a separate axiom appended to logic after the fact; it is equivalent to the well-ordering principle for the natural numbers, and understanding that equivalence makes the method feel inevitable.

In **weak induction** we prove a base case $P(n_0)$ and an inductive step: assuming$P(k)$ (the **inductive hypothesis**), we derive$P(k+1)$. Together, these two facts establish$P(n)$ for all$n \geq n_0$. The base case anchors the chain, and the inductive step says the chain has no weak links. To see why this works, suppose some$n$ failed; let$k$ be the smallest such$n$ with$P(n)$ false. Then$k > n_0$ (by the base case) and$P(k-1)$ holds (by minimality of$k$), so the inductive step would have given$P(k)$ — a contradiction. The logical engine is the **well-ordering principle**: every non-empty subset of$\mathbb{N}$ has a least element. Induction and well-ordering are logically equivalent, and both can be taken as the defining axiom of the natural numbers.

**Strong induction** broadens the inductive hypothesis: instead of assuming only $P(k)$, we assume$P(j)$ holds for all$j$ with$n_0 \leq j \leq k$, and from this full collection derive$P(k+1)$. This is needed when the truth of$P(k+1)$ depends not just on the immediately preceding case but on multiple earlier values. The form of the argument is identical — establish a base case (or set of base cases) and prove the inductive step — but the inductive hypothesis is now richer. Strong induction is not actually stronger in logical power than weak induction; either implies the other. But for certain problems, strong induction is the natural framing.

**Structural induction** is the most general form: induction on any **well-founded relation**, not just the usual ordering on $\mathbb{N}$. A relation$\prec$ on a set$X$ is well-founded if every non-empty subset of$X$ has a$\prec$-minimal element. Given such a relation, if we can show that$P(x)$ holds whenever$P(y)$ holds for all$y \prec x$, then$P(x)$ holds for every$x \in X$. The base cases are the minimal elements (those with no predecessors). To prove a property of all binary trees, one proves it for leaves (base) and then proves: if it holds for each subtree, it holds for the combined tree. To prove a property of all words over an alphabet, one proves it for the empty word and that the property is preserved by appending a letter. The well-foundedness guarantees that every element is eventually reached by working up from the bottom.

\begin{example}
Prove by weak induction: $\sum_{k=1}^{n} k = \dfrac{n(n+1)}{2}$ for all$n \geq 1$.
\par
**Base case** ($n = 1$):$\sum_{k=1}^{1} k = 1 = \frac{1 \cdot 2}{2}$. True.
\par
**Inductive step**: Assume $\sum_{k=1}^{m} k = \frac{m(m+1)}{2}$ for some$m \geq 1$. Then:
\begin{align*}
\sum_{k=1}^{m+1} k &= \left(\sum_{k=1}^{m} k\right) + (m+1) \\
&= \frac{m(m+1)}{2} + (m+1) && \text{(inductive hypothesis)} \\
&= (m+1)\left(\frac{m}{2} + 1\right) \\
&= \frac{(m+1)(m+2)}{2}.
\end{align*}
This is exactly the formula for $n = m+1$. By induction, the formula holds for all$n \geq 1$.$\square$
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Prove by strong induction: every integer $n \geq 2$ has at least one prime divisor.
\par
**Base case** ($n = 2$):$2$ is prime, so$2 \mid 2$.
\par
**Inductive step**: Let $n \geq 3$ and assume every integer$m$ with$2 \leq m < n$ has a prime divisor. Either$n$ is prime — in which case$n$ divides itself, so$n$ has a prime divisor — or$n$ is composite, meaning$n = ab$ with$2 \leq a, b < n$. By the inductive hypothesis applied to$a$, there exists a prime$p$ with$p \mid a$. Since$a \mid n$, by transitivity of divisibility$p \mid n$.$\square$
\par
Strong induction is needed here because $a$ might be much smaller than$n - 1$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Use structural induction to prove that every non-empty finite binary tree with $n$ leaves has exactly$n - 1$ internal nodes.
\par
**Structure**: A finite binary tree is either a single leaf, or a root with a left subtree $T_L$ and a right subtree$T_R$, each themselves binary trees.
\par
**Base case**: A single leaf has 1 leaf and 0 internal nodes; $1 - 1 = 0$. True.
\par
**Inductive step**: Assume the claim holds for $T_L$ and$T_R$. Let$T_L$ have$\ell$ leaves and$\ell - 1$ internal nodes, and let$T_R$ have$r$ leaves and$r - 1$ internal nodes. The combined tree has$\ell + r$ leaves and$(\ell - 1) + (r - 1) + 1 = \ell + r - 1$ internal nodes (the$+1$ is the new root). Since$(\ell + r) - 1 = \ell + r - 1$, the claim holds for the combined tree. By structural induction, it holds for all non-empty finite binary trees.$\square$
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Prove: for every $n \geq 0$, the$n$-th Fibonacci number$F_n$ satisfies$F_n < 2^n$, where$F_0 = 0$,$F_1 = 1$,$F_n = F_{n-1} + F_{n-2}$ for$n \geq 2$.
\par
**Base cases**: $F_0 = 0 < 1 = 2^0$ and$F_1 = 1 < 2 = 2^1$.
\par
**Inductive step**: Let $n \geq 2$ and assume$F_j < 2^j$ for all$j < n$ (strong induction). Then:
$$F_n = F_{n-1} + F_{n-2} < 2^{n-1} + 2^{n-2} \leq 2^{n-1} + 2^{n-1} = 2^n.$$
By strong induction, $F_n < 2^n$ for all$n \geq 0$.$\square$
\par
The recursion for Fibonacci is two steps back, so we need the inductive hypothesis at both $n-1$ and$n-2$, making strong induction necessary.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
Structural induction appears throughout algebra whenever we work with objects defined by recursive or generative processes: words in a free monoid, elements of a free group, polynomials, terms in a formal language, and paths in a Cayley graph are all described by inductive definitions, and proofs about them follow the structural induction scheme. The algebraic content changes across these settings, but the logical structure — base cases for generators, inductive step for the combining operation — is always the same. Recognizing the underlying well-founded structure is what makes the induction argument legible.
\end{remark}
