# 1.3.4 Existence and Uniqueness Arguments

Two distinct questions arise constantly in algebra: does a mathematical object with a given property exist, and if so, is it the only one? These questions are logically independent, and proofs must address them separately. Together they appear in the **"there exists a unique"** quantifier $\exists!$, which packs both demands into a single statement:$\exists! x \in S,\, P(x)$ means$\exists x \in S,\, P(x)$ and$\forall x, y \in S,\, [P(x) \land P(y) \Rightarrow x = y]$. Any proof of uniqueness must establish this equality; any proof of existence must produce (or guarantee the existence of) a witness.

An **existence proof** can take two forms. A **constructive existence proof** explicitly exhibits the object: to show there exists an identity element in a given algebraic structure, you write down a specific element and verify the required properties. Constructive proofs are the strongest kind — they tell you not only that the object exists but how to find it. In algebra, construction often means writing down an explicit formula or algorithm. A **non-constructive existence proof** instead argues that the object must exist without ever producing a specific example, typically by contradiction (assume it does not exist, derive a contradiction) or by an appeal to Zorn's Lemma. Non-constructive arguments often give cleaner proofs and greater generality, but they provide no computational information about the object.

A **uniqueness proof** follows a standard template: assume two objects $x$ and$y$ both satisfy the given property, then prove$x = y$. The assumption of two objects is not an assumption that they are different — it is a neutral starting point that forces us to derive their equality from the defining property alone. The mechanism is always the same: use the defining property of$x$ to evaluate$y$ (or vice versa), or use the defining property of both to establish they must coincide. This template is so uniform that after seeing it a few times, you will recognize it instantly in any setting.

Existence and uniqueness arguments reach their highest importance in the theory of **universal properties**. A universal property is a characterization of an object not by its internal structure but by how it maps to or from other objects — and the characterization is always a uniqueness statement. The quotient group $G/N$, the free group on a set$S$, the tensor product$M \otimes_R N$ — each is defined (up to unique isomorphism) by specifying what maps from or into it look like, and the proof that this definition makes sense is always a combination of an existence argument (the object can be constructed) and a uniqueness argument (any two constructions are isomorphic via a unique isomorphism). Because universal properties are one of the most powerful organizing tools in modern algebra, mastering the existence-uniqueness skeleton now pays compounding dividends.

\begin{example}
Prove that the identity element of a group $G$ is unique.
\par
**Existence**: The identity is given by the group axioms. (In any particular group, you exhibit it explicitly.)
\par
**Uniqueness**: Suppose $e$ and$e'$ are both identity elements of$G$. Then:
$$e = e \cdot e' = e'.$$
The first equality uses the fact that $e'$ is an identity (so$e \cdot e' = e$); the second uses the fact that$e$ is an identity (so$e \cdot e' = e'$). Since$e = e'$, the identity is unique.$\square$
\par
This is the existence-uniqueness template at its simplest: two objects with the same property, and using that property for each in turn forces them to be equal.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Prove that every element $g$ of a group$G$ has a unique inverse.
\par
**Existence**: Given by the group axioms.
\par
**Uniqueness**: Suppose $h$ and$h'$ are both inverses of$g$, meaning$gh = e = hg$ and$gh' = e = h'g$. Then:
$$h = he = h(gh') = (hg)h' = eh' = h'.$$
The second equality uses $gh' = e$; the third uses associativity; the fourth uses$hg = e$; the fifth uses the identity axiom. Hence$h = h'$.$\square$
\par
The proof uses both inverse properties of $g$ together with associativity. The uniqueness argument is a chain of equalities — each step applying one group axiom.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Prove: there exists a unique integer $q$ and a unique integer$r$ with$0 \leq r < d$ such that$n = qd + r$ (the Division Algorithm), for any$n \in \mathbb{Z}$ and$d \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0}$.
\par
**Existence**: Consider the set $S = \{n - kd \mid k \in \mathbb{Z},\, n - kd \geq 0\}$. This set is non-empty (take$k$ sufficiently negative). By the well-ordering principle,$S$ has a least element$r = n - qd$ for some$q$. Since$r \geq 0$ by choice, we have$n = qd + r$. We show$r < d$: if$r \geq d$, then$r - d = n - (q+1)d \geq 0$ and$r - d < r$, contradicting minimality of$r$. So$0 \leq r < d$, and existence is proved.
\par
**Uniqueness**: Suppose $n = qd + r = q'd + r'$ with$0 \leq r, r' < d$. Then$d(q - q') = r' - r$. Since$|r' - r| < d$ and$d \mid d(q - q')$, we have$d \mid (r' - r)$. The only multiple of$d$ with absolute value less than$d$ is 0, so$r = r'$. Then$q = q'$.$\square$
\par
The existence uses the well-ordering principle; the uniqueness uses divisibility. Both steps are necessary — neither implies the other.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Prove: there exists a unique ring homomorphism $\varphi: \mathbb{Z} \to R$ for any unital ring$R$.
\par
**Existence**: Define $\varphi(n) = n \cdot 1_R$ (the$n$-fold sum of$1_R$ for$n > 0$, the$|n|$-fold sum of$-1_R$ for$n < 0$, and$\varphi(0) = 0_R$). One checks directly that$\varphi(m + n) = \varphi(m) + \varphi(n)$ and$\varphi(mn) = \varphi(m)\varphi(n)$ and$\varphi(1) = 1_R$.
\par
**Uniqueness**: Suppose $\psi: \mathbb{Z} \to R$ is any ring homomorphism. Then$\psi(1) = 1_R$ (ring homomorphisms send$1$ to$1$). By induction,$\psi(n) = n \cdot 1_R$ for all$n \geq 0$, and$\psi(-n) = -\psi(n) = -n \cdot 1_R$ for all$n > 0$. Thus$\psi = \varphi$ on all of$\mathbb{Z}$.$\square$
\par
This is the simplest instance of a universal property: $\mathbb{Z}$ is the **initial object** in the category of unital rings. The unique homomorphism to every ring is exactly what "initial" means.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
Recall that in algebra the existence-uniqueness pattern repeats in dozens of contexts: the unique identity, the unique inverse of each element, the unique representative of a coset satisfying a normalization, the unique morphism guaranteed by a universal property. In each case the proof has the same skeleton — construct or assert existence, then assume two copies and equate them. Because universal properties — which are uniqueness statements in disguise — are one of the most powerful organizing tools in modern algebra, mastering the uniqueness argument now pays compounding dividends throughout the rest of the book. Recognizing the pattern "assume two objects with the same property, then derive they are equal" is one of the hallmarks of mathematical maturity.
\end{remark}
