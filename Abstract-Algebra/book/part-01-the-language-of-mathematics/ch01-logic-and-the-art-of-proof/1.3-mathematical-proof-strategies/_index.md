# 1.3 Mathematical Proof Strategies

A proof is a finite sequence of statements, each of which either is an axiom or follows by a rule of inference from earlier statements, terminating in the statement to be proved. That is the formal definition. In practice, mathematicians work with a small repertoire of higher-level strategies — templates for organizing an argument — that translate the formal machinery into humanly-followable reasoning. This section catalogues the four strategies that together cover essentially every proof encountered in a first course in algebra: direct proof, proof by contrapositive, proof by contradiction, and proof by mathematical induction.

A *direct proof* of $P \Rightarrow Q$ begins by assuming $P$ is true and then derives $Q$ through a chain of valid inferences. It is the most transparent strategy when the hypothesis provides something concrete to work with. A *proof by contrapositive* instead proves the logically equivalent statement $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$: one assumes the conclusion is false and derives that the hypothesis must also be false. This strategy is often cleaner when the negation of the conclusion provides stronger algebraic traction than the hypothesis itself. For example, proving "if $n^2$ is even then $n$ is even" is streamlined by contrapositive: assume $n$ is odd, write $n = 2k+1$, and compute $n^2 = 4k^2 + 4k + 1 = 2(2k^2 + 2k) + 1$, which is odd. The argument is tight and entirely constructive. A *proof by contradiction* assumes both $P$ and $\neg Q$, and derives any contradiction whatsoever — the statement $R \wedge \neg R$ for any $R$. The logical warrant is that if $P$ together with $\neg Q$ is inconsistent, then $P$ alone forces $Q$. This strategy is well-suited to existence proofs and to situations where the hypothesis is difficult to use but the negation of the conclusion opens up many cases.

*Mathematical induction* is the proof strategy specific to the natural numbers, and it is justified by the very definition of $\mathbb{N}$ (or equivalently, by the Peano axioms). To prove $\forall n \in \mathbb{N},\, P(n)$, it suffices to establish the *base case* $P(0)$ or $P(1)$ and the *inductive step*: for each $k$, $P(k) \Rightarrow P(k+1)$. The power of induction is that it converts an infinite family of statements into a finite verification; the assumption $P(k)$ in the inductive step is called the *inductive hypothesis*, and exploiting it effectively is an art. *Strong induction* is a variant where the inductive step assumes $P(j)$ for all $j \leq k$, which is often more natural for proofs involving recursive constructions or prime factorizations. A fourth strategy — *proof by cases* — is more an organizational principle than a formal proof rule: one partitions the domain into finitely many exhaustive subcases and handles each separately. It appears throughout, for instance, when one uses modular arithmetic to cover residue classes. Together, these strategies constitute the core toolkit of mathematical argument, and fluency with them is a prerequisite for everything that follows in this book.
