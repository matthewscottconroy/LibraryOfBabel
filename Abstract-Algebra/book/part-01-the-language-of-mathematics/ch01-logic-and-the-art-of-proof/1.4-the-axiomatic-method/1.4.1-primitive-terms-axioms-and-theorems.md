# 1.4.1 Primitive Terms, Axioms, and Theorems

Every mathematical subject has to start somewhere. No matter how careful we are, we cannot define every term — at some point we run out of prior concepts to appeal to, and we must accept certain notions as undefined starting points. These are the **primitive terms** of the theory: in Euclidean geometry, they are "point," "line," and "lies on"; in set theory, they are "set" and the membership relation "$\in$." We can give intuitive descriptions of these notions, but we do not define them formally in terms of simpler concepts. What we can do is specify how they behave by writing down statements about them that we accept as true without proof.

That is the role of **axioms**: statements involving the primitive terms that we accept as true without proof, and from which everything else follows by logical deduction. Axioms are not arbitrary — they are chosen to capture the essential properties of the objects we care about, in as few and as transparent assumptions as possible. In group theory, for instance, we take sets and elements as primitive (borrowing them from set theory), and the group axioms — closure, associativity, existence of an identity, existence of inverses — specify what it means for a set with a binary operation to be a group. Every property of groups is then a **theorem**: a statement that follows by logical deduction from the axioms alone, with no additional assumptions about what groups "really are" or what they look like concretely.

The value of this approach becomes clear when we consider two groups that look nothing alike — the integers under addition and the symmetry group of a regular hexagon — and realize that both satisfy the same axioms and therefore both have an identity element, both have unique inverses, and both satisfy the cancellation law. A theorem proved from the axioms applies to every model of those axioms at once. This is the economy of the axiomatic method: we prove things once, in full generality, and they hold simultaneously for every mathematical structure that satisfies the axioms.

The discipline of working axiomatically also tells us which properties require which assumptions. If we prove a theorem using only associativity and the existence of inverses — without ever using commutativity — then we know the theorem applies to non-abelian groups as well as abelian ones. Tracking which axioms are used in a proof is not pedantry; it is precisely how we map the logical landscape and understand which generalizations are possible. A result proved for all groups automatically applies to all rings (since rings have an underlying additive group), to all modules, and to all other structures containing a group as part of their data. This point becomes acute when we study rings, fields, and modules, where the axiom sets differ in subtle but consequential ways.

A **definition** in the axiomatic framework is not a primitive notion but a shorthand — a name for a combination of existing concepts that we find useful to work with repeatedly. The definition of "abelian group," "ideal," or "exact sequence" introduces a new term by specifying a property expressible in terms of already-defined notions and the primitive terms. Definitions streamline language without adding logical content; everything stated in terms of a definition could in principle be restated without it, but the resulting expressions would be unwieldy.

\begin{example}
The axioms of a group. A group is a set $G$ with a binary operation$\cdot: G \times G \to G$ satisfying:
\begin{enumerate}
\item (Closure) For all $a, b \in G$,$a \cdot b \in G$.
\item (Associativity) For all $a, b, c \in G$,$(a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)$.
\item (Identity) There exists $e \in G$ such that$a \cdot e = e \cdot a = a$ for all$a \in G$.
\item (Inverses) For every $a \in G$, there exists$b \in G$ such that$a \cdot b = b \cdot a = e$.
\end{enumerate}
\par
Note that closure is sometimes subsumed into the definition of "binary operation on $G$," which already requires outputs to lie in$G$. The axiomatic presentation makes explicit what we will use in proofs.
\par
From these four axioms alone — using the proof strategies of Section 1.3 — we can derive that the identity is unique (Section 1.3.4, Example 1), that inverses are unique (Section 1.3.4, Example 2), that the cancellation law $ab = ac \Rightarrow b = c$ holds, and infinitely many more theorems about all groups simultaneously.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Identify which axiom is needed for each step in the following group calculation: if $ab = ac$, then$b = c$.
\par
Assume $ab = ac$. Let$a'$ be the inverse of$a$ (exists by Axiom 4). Then:
\begin{align*}
b &= eb && \text{(Axiom 3: identity)} \\
&= (a'a)b && \text{(Axiom 4: $a'$ is inverse of$a$)} \\
&= a'(ab) && \text{(Axiom 2: associativity)} \\
&= a'(ac) && \text{(hypothesis: $ab = ac$)} \\
&= (a'a)c && \text{(Axiom 2: associativity)} \\
&= ec && \text{(Axiom 4)} \\
&= c. && \text{(Axiom 3)}
\end{align*}
Axioms 2, 3, and 4 are all used; the commutativity axiom (which groups do not necessarily satisfy) is not needed. Therefore cancellation holds in every group, including non-abelian ones.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Two models of the same axioms. Consider the following two structures:
\par
(a) $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$: integers under addition, with identity$0$ and inverses$-n$.
\par
(b) $(S_3, \circ)$: the set of all bijections from$\{1, 2, 3\}$ to itself, under composition.
\par
Both satisfy the group axioms. The theorem "the identity is unique" applies to both. But $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ satisfies$a + b = b + a$ for all$a, b$ (it is abelian), while$(S_3, \circ)$ does not: composing the transpositions$(12)$ and$(13)$ in different orders yields different results. The abelian property is not derivable from the group axioms alone; it requires an additional axiom. A theorem proved purely from the group axioms holds in both structures; one that uses commutativity holds only in the abelian case.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
The axiomatic definition of a ring extends the group axioms. A ring $(R, +, \cdot)$ requires$(R, +)$ to be an abelian group, multiplication to be associative, and the distributive law$a(b + c) = ab + ac$ to hold. The commutativity of multiplication is an additional axiom that distinguishes commutative rings from general ones.
\par
A theorem about rings proved using only associativity of multiplication, distributivity, and the additive group axioms holds in all rings — both commutative and non-commutative. A theorem that uses $ab = ba$ holds only in commutative rings. Tracking which axioms are used is not an optional refinement; it is the mechanism by which a theorem about rings automatically implies a theorem about commutative rings, integral domains, and fields — structures defined by successive additions to the ring axioms.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
The axiomatic method is not a straitjacket; it is a precision instrument. Working within a fixed axiom system, we know exactly what resources are available, and any theorem we prove holds for the entire class of structures satisfying those axioms. This generality is the source of the power of abstract algebra: a single theorem about groups simultaneously applies to permutation groups, matrix groups, symmetry groups of geometric objects, Galois groups of field extensions, and the group of points on an elliptic curve. The work of proving the theorem once — carefully, from the axioms — is repaid every time a new class of examples is recognized to satisfy the same axioms.
\end{remark}
