# 1.4.2 Consistency, Completeness, and Independence

Once we have an axiomatic system, three natural questions arise about its quality: Can it prove a contradiction? Can it prove everything that is true? Are all the axioms genuinely necessary? These questions have precise mathematical formulations, and their answers — some of the deepest results of twentieth-century logic — shape how we think about the foundations of mathematics and circumscribe what can in principle be accomplished by formal proof.

A system is **consistent** if no contradiction is derivable from its axioms — that is, there is no statement $P$ such that both$P$ and$\neg P$ are theorems. Consistency is the minimum requirement for a useful theory: an inconsistent system can prove every statement (since from a contradiction any statement follows by the principle of explosion,$\bot \Rightarrow Q$ for any$Q$), and is therefore useless for distinguishing true from false. We typically establish the consistency of an axiomatic system by exhibiting a **model** — a concrete mathematical object that satisfies all the axioms. If the object exists (within a more trusted framework), then the axioms cannot prove a contradiction, because the object would violate any contradiction.

A system is **complete** if every statement expressible in its language is either provable or disprovable from the axioms — there are no "gaps," no true-but-unprovable statements. And an axiom in a system is **independent** if it cannot be derived from the remaining axioms: removing it would leave some theorems unprovable, and adding its negation would not introduce a contradiction. Independence is the most practically useful of the three notions for everyday mathematics, because it tells us which axioms are genuinely load-bearing and which could be derived from others.

The most celebrated example of independence in geometry is Euclid's parallel postulate. For two millennia mathematicians suspected it could be derived from the other four postulates, and many attempts were made to do so. In the nineteenth century it was shown to be independent: there are consistent geometries — hyperbolic geometry (Bolyai-Lobachevsky) and elliptic geometry (Riemann) — in which the other four postulates hold but the parallel postulate fails. The existence of these models demonstrates independence: if the parallel postulate were a theorem, it would have to hold in every model of the other four axioms, but it does not. The parallel postulate must be an axiom; it cannot be a theorem. This discovery transformed the understanding of geometry and, more broadly, of the relationship between axioms and reality.

A closely related independence result in set theory is the **Axiom of Choice** (AC), which we encounter in Chapter 3. Gödel showed in 1938 that AC cannot be refuted from the other axioms of ZF set theory (i.e., adding AC to ZF creates a consistent system); Cohen showed in 1963 that AC cannot be proved from them either (i.e., negating AC is also consistent with ZF). AC is independent of ZF, just as the parallel postulate is independent of Euclid's other four. We work in ZFC — ZF plus AC — throughout this book, but we will flag results that use AC explicitly.

These considerations reach their deepest expression in **Gödel's incompleteness theorems** (1931): any consistent axiomatic system powerful enough to express basic arithmetic is necessarily incomplete — there exist statements expressible in the language of the system that can neither be proved nor disproved within it. Moreover, the consistency of the system itself is one such unprovable statement. This is not a limitation of particular systems; it is a fundamental feature of formal proof itself. For working mathematicians, Gödel's theorems are less a daily concern than a reminder of the limits of formalization: no single axiom system can capture all mathematical truth.

\begin{example}
Demonstrate consistency of the group axioms by exhibiting a model.
\par
The set $G = \{e\}$ with the operation$e \cdot e = e$ satisfies all four group axioms: closure (the product of any two elements is in$G$), associativity (trivially), identity ($e$ is the identity), and inverses ($e$ is its own inverse). Therefore, the group axioms are consistent — they have at least one model, the trivial group.
\par
This does not rule out that a more elaborate system extending the group axioms might be inconsistent, but the simple group axioms are themselves safe.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Demonstrate independence: the commutativity axiom $\forall a, b \in G,\, ab = ba$ is independent of the group axioms.
\par
In one direction: $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ is a model of all group axioms plus commutativity — so adding commutativity to the group axioms is consistent.
\par
In the other direction: $S_3$, the symmetric group on three elements, is a model of the group axioms but not of commutativity (the transpositions$(12)$ and$(13)$ do not commute). Therefore commutativity cannot be a theorem of the group axioms — there is a model (namely$S_3$) in which it fails.
\par
Since commutativity is consistent with the group axioms (witnessed by $\mathbb{Z}$) and its negation is also consistent (witnessed by$S_3$), the axiom of commutativity is independent of the group axioms.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Show that the associativity axiom is not derivable from only closure, identity, and inverses.
\par
We need a model satisfying closure, identity, and inverses but not associativity. Consider the set $G = \{e, a, b, c\}$ and define a multiplication table with identity$e$ and inverses chosen to make each element its own inverse, but define$a(bc) \neq (ab)c$ explicitly. (Such "quasigroups with inverses" that fail associativity exist and are known as "non-associative loops.") This demonstrates that associativity is a genuinely independent axiom in the definition of a group — it cannot be derived from the others.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
An informal illustration of Gödel's incompleteness. Consider the sentence: "This statement is not provable in system $T$." If this statement were provable in$T$, it would be provable and also claim to be unprovable — a contradiction, assuming$T$ is consistent. So if$T$ is consistent, the statement cannot be provable. But then the statement is actually true (it is not provable), yet$T$ cannot prove it. Gödel's construction formalizes this self-reference within arithmetic, producing a specific arithmetic statement (the "Gödel sentence" of$T$) that is true but unprovable. The incompleteness theorem says every consistent, sufficiently powerful system contains such a statement.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
These considerations reach their deepest expression in Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931): any consistent axiomatic system powerful enough to express basic arithmetic is necessarily incomplete. For working mathematicians, Gödel's theorem is less a daily concern than a reminder of the limits of formalization — and of the fact that mathematics, even when axiomatized, retains depths that no single formal system can fully capture. The independence results we will encounter in the book — the Axiom of Choice, the Continuum Hypothesis — are the more practically relevant instances of the same phenomenon: propositions that are true in some models of our axioms and false in others, and that cannot be settled by the axioms alone.
\end{remark}
