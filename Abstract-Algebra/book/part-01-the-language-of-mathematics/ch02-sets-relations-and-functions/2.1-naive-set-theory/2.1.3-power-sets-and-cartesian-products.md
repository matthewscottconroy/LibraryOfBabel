# 2.1.3 Power Sets and Cartesian Products

Two constructions generate much of the raw material of algebra. The first collects all possible subsets of a given set; the second pairs elements from two sets into ordered couples. Together they underlie structures as diverse as Boolean algebras, groups of symmetries, and vector spaces. Mastering these two operations — and the interplay between them — is prerequisite to understanding virtually every algebraic structure that follows.

\begin{definition}
The **power set** $\mathcal{P}(A)$ of a set$A$ is the set of all subsets of$A$:
$$\mathcal{P}(A) = \{S \mid S \subseteq A\}.$$
\end{definition}

The empty set $\emptyset$ and$A$ itself are always elements of$\mathcal{P}(A)$. If$A$ is finite with$|A| = n$, then$|\mathcal{P}(A)| = 2^{n}$. The proof is a clean induction: the base case$n = 0$ gives$\mathcal{P}(\emptyset) = \{\emptyset\}$, which has$2^{0} = 1$ element. For the inductive step, choose any element$a \in A$ and partition the subsets of$A$ into those that contain$a$ and those that do not. The subsets not containing$a$ biject with$\mathcal{P}(A \setminus \{a\})$, which has$2^{n-1}$ elements by inductive hypothesis; the subsets containing$a$ also biject with$\mathcal{P}(A \setminus \{a\})$ by dropping the element$a$. Together they yield$2 \cdot 2^{n-1} = 2^{n}$ subsets. The notation$2^{A}$ for the power set is a notational reminder of this counting formula and generalises in category theory to the set of all functions from$A$ to a two-element set.

The power set $\mathcal{P}(A)$ is naturally partially ordered by inclusion$\subseteq$, and this poset structure appears in the study of lattices, Boolean algebras, and the lattice of subgroups of a group. The inclusion order on$\mathcal{P}(A)$ is moreover a **Boolean lattice**: any two elements have both a least upper bound (their union) and a greatest lower bound (their intersection), and the complement operation satisfies the laws of a Boolean algebra.

\begin{definition}
The **Cartesian product** of sets $A$ and$B$ is
$$A \times B = \{(a, b) \mid a \in A,\, b \in B\},$$
the set of all ordered pairs with first component from $A$ and second component from$B$.
\end{definition}

The word "ordered" is essential: $(a, b) = (a', b')$ if and only if$a = a'$ and$b = b'$. The pairs$(1, 2)$ and$(2, 1)$ are therefore distinct elements of$\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$. Ordered pairs can be given a purely set-theoretic definition: the **Kuratowski encoding** sets$(a, b) = \{\{a\}, \{a, b\}\}$, and one verifies that$(a, b) = (a', b')$ in this encoding if and only if$a = a'$ and$b = b'$.

If $|A| = m$ and$|B| = n$ then$|A \times B| = mn$, which is the combinatorial multiplication principle. The Cartesian product generalises to any finite (or even infinite) number of factors:$A_{1} \times A_{2} \times \cdots \times A_{n}$ consists of all ordered$n$-tuples$(a_{1}, \ldots, a_{n})$ with$a_{i} \in A_{i}$. The Cartesian plane$\mathbb{R}^{2} = \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}$ and Euclidean space$\mathbb{R}^{n}$ are the most familiar instances.

The reason these two constructions generate most algebraic objects is structural: a **relation** on $A$ is a subset of$A \times A$; a **function** from$A$ to$B$ is a special subset of$A \times B$; a **binary operation** on$A$ is a function from$A \times A$ to$A$. In each case we are specifying a subset of a Cartesian product, and the additional conditions (reflexivity, single-valuedness, associativity) are imposed on top. Understanding Cartesian products and power sets at this foundational level makes all subsequent definitions feel natural rather than arbitrary.

\begin{proposition}
For any sets $A$,$B$,$C$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $A \times (B \cup C) = (A \times B) \cup (A \times C)$.
\item $A \times (B \cap C) = (A \times B) \cap (A \times C)$.
\item $(A \times B) \cap (C \times D) = (A \cap C) \times (B \cap D)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

These distributivity laws follow directly from the definition of ordered pairs and set-builder notation. They are worth internalising because they recur whenever one needs to reason about products of algebraic structures: the direct product of groups, modules, or rings obeys analogous decomposition laws at the level of underlying sets.

**Example 1.** Let $A = \{0, 1\}$. Compute$\mathcal{P}(A)$ and$A \times A$.

Since $|A| = 2$, we expect$|\mathcal{P}(A)| = 4$. Listing all subsets:$\mathcal{P}(A) = \{\emptyset, \{0\}, \{1\}, \{0,1\}\}$. The Cartesian square is$A \times A = \{(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)\}$, which has$2^{2} = 4$ elements. Note that$\mathcal{P}(A)$ and$A \times A$ are different sets with the same cardinality; indeed, the map$\{0\} \mapsto (0,1)$,$\{1\} \mapsto (1,0)$,$\emptyset \mapsto (0,0)$,$\{0,1\} \mapsto (1,1)$ is one (of many) bijections between them.

**Example 2.** Let $A = \{a, b, c\}$. How many elements does$\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(A))$ have?

We have $|A| = 3$, so$|\mathcal{P}(A)| = 2^{3} = 8$. Therefore$|\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(A))| = 2^{8} = 256$. In general, iterating the power-set operation grows the cardinality as a tower of exponentials:$|\mathcal{P}^{(n)}(A)| = 2^{2^{\cdots^{|A|}}}$ ($n$ exponentials). This explosive growth reappears in Cantor's theorem (Section 3.2.3), where it shows there is no largest cardinality.

**Example 3.** Describe the elements of $\{1, 2\} \times \{a, b, c\}$ and verify the cardinality formula.

The product consists of all ordered pairs: $\{(1,a),(1,b),(1,c),(2,a),(2,b),(2,c)\}$. There are$2 \times 3 = 6$ elements, confirming$|A \times B| = |A| \cdot |B|$.

**Example 4.** Show that $A \times B = B \times A$ if and only if$A = B$ or at least one of$A$,$B$ is empty.

If $A = B$, clearly$A \times B = A \times A = B \times A$. If$A = \emptyset$ or$B = \emptyset$, both products are empty and hence equal. Conversely, suppose$A \neq B$,$A \neq \emptyset$,$B \neq \emptyset$. Choose$a \in A \setminus B$ (or$b \in B \setminus A$; at least one such choice is possible). Then$(a, b) \in A \times B$ for any$b \in B$, but$(a, b) \notin B \times A$ since$a \notin B$. Hence the products differ.

\begin{remark}
The non-commutativity of the Cartesian product — as sets, $A \times B$ and$B \times A$ are generally not equal, only canonically bijective via$(a,b) \mapsto (b,a)$ — reflects a general phenomenon in algebra: the "swap" isomorphism between$A \times B$ and$B \times A$ exists but is not the identity. This becomes significant when one studies tensor products of modules, where commutativity of the product is likewise not automatic but only holds up to a canonical isomorphism.
\end{remark}
