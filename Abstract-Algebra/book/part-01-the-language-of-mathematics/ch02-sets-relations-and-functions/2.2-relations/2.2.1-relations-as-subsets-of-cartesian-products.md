# 2.2.1 Relations as Subsets of Cartesian Products

We compare and connect elements of sets constantly in mathematics: one integer divides another, one set is a subset of another, two integers are congruent modulo $n$, one real number is less than another. Each of these comparisons is an instance of a **binary relation**, and the remarkable fact is that the most natural set-theoretic encoding of this intuitive concept is simply a subset of a Cartesian product. Identifying relations with subsets rather than leaving them as informal "comparison rules" anchors all subsequent theory in solid set-theoretic ground.

\begin{definition}
A **relation** from a set $A$ to a set$B$ is a subset$R \subseteq A \times B$. We say that$a$ is related to$b$ — written$a \mathrel{R} b$ — precisely when the ordered pair$(a, b) \in R$. A relation **on**$A$ is a subset$R \subseteq A \times A$.
\end{definition}

The notation $a \mathrel{R} b$ is simply a typographically convenient synonym for$(a, b) \in R$; both mean the same thing. Some authors write$aRb$ without the middle dot, and for specific relations they use symbols:$a \leq b$,$a \mid b$,$a \equiv b \pmod{n}$,$a \sim b$. In each case the underlying object is a set of ordered pairs.

Examples are immediate. The divisibility relation on $\mathbb{N}$ is the subset$\{(a, b) \in \mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N} \mid a \mid b\}$. The less-than relation on$\mathbb{R}$ is$\{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R} \mid x < y\}$. Equality on any set$A$ is the relation$\Delta_{A} = \{(a, a) \mid a \in A\}$, called the **diagonal** of$A \times A$. Congruence modulo$n$ on$\mathbb{Z}$ is the relation$\{(a, b) \in \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z} \mid n \mid (a - b)\}$. The "is a subgroup of" relation on the collection of subgroups of a group$G$ is set-theoretic inclusion, restricted to that collection.

This definition subsumes a broader class of structures than one might initially expect. Even a function $f \colon A \to B$ is, by definition, a special kind of relation from$A$ to$B$: the set of pairs$\{(a, f(a)) \mid a \in A\}$. The additional conditions that distinguish a function from a general relation — that every element of$A$ appears as a first coordinate, and no element appears with two different second coordinates — are conditions on the subset$R$, not on anything beyond set theory.

\begin{definition}
Given a relation $R \subseteq A \times B$, the **domain** of$R$ is$\{a \in A \mid \exists\, b \in B : (a,b) \in R\}$, and the **range** (or **image**) of$R$ is$\{b \in B \mid \exists\, a \in A : (a,b) \in R\}$.
\end{definition}

The **inverse relation** $R^{-1} \subseteq B \times A$ is defined by$R^{-1} = \{(b, a) \mid (a, b) \in R\}$: we simply swap the two coordinates. The inverse of the less-than relation is the greater-than relation; the inverse of divisibility is "is divisible by." This operation makes precise the idea of "reversing the direction" of a relation.

The reason for defining relations as subsets of Cartesian products — rather than leaving the concept informal — is that it anchors everything in sets. We can speak of properties that a relation may or may not possess, prove theorems about relations in full generality, and classify the relations that arise most frequently in mathematics. The next section identifies the four basic properties — reflexivity, symmetry, antisymmetry, transitivity — that single out the two main families of relations appearing throughout algebra.

**Example 1.** Let $A = \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ and$R = \{(a,b) \in A \times A \mid a \mid b\}$. List the elements of$R$.

We include $(a, b)$ precisely when$a$ divides$b$ and both lie in$A$. The pairs are:$(1,1), (1,2), (1,3), (1,4), (2,2), (2,4), (3,3), (4,4)$. The diagonal pairs$(a,a)$ are always included since every integer divides itself.

**Example 2.** Let $R \subseteq \{0,1,2\} \times \{0,1,2\}$ be defined by$R = \{(0,1),(1,2),(0,2)\}$. Write$R$ in infix notation and describe it in words.

In infix notation: $0 \mathrel{R} 1$,$1 \mathrel{R} 2$,$0 \mathrel{R} 2$. In words,$a \mathrel{R} b$ means$a < b$ on$\{0, 1, 2\}$. Indeed, the strict less-than relation on any three-element totally ordered set has exactly three pairs.

**Example 3.** Describe the inverse of the relation $\{(a,b) \in \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z} \mid a \equiv b \pmod{3}\}$.

The inverse consists of pairs $(b, a)$ such that$a \equiv b \pmod{3}$, i.e.,$b \equiv a \pmod{3}$. But this is the same condition by symmetry of congruence. Hence$R^{-1} = R$ — the congruence-mod-3 relation is equal to its own inverse.

**Example 4.** Let $f \colon \{1,2,3\} \to \{a,b,c\}$ be defined by$f(1) = a$,$f(2) = a$,$f(3) = b$. Write$f$ as a subset of$\{1,2,3\} \times \{a,b,c\}$ and identify its range. What extra conditions distinguish this subset from a general relation?

As a set of pairs: $f = \{(1,a),(2,a),(3,b)\}$. The range is$\{a, b\}$ — note that$c$ is not in the range, even though it lies in the codomain. The conditions distinguishing$f$ from a general relation are: (i) every element of the domain$\{1,2,3\}$ appears as a first coordinate (totality), and (ii) no element appears with two different second coordinates (single-valuedness). The relation$\{(1,a),(1,b),(2,a),(3,b)\}$ fails single-valuedness since$1$ is paired with both$a$ and$b$.
