# 2.2.2 Reflexivity, Symmetry, Antisymmetry, Transitivity

Not all relations are equal in structure. We single out four properties that capture the most mathematically important behaviors. Most relations that appear in algebra and analysis satisfy some combination of these four, and identifying which combination is present immediately tells us what kind of object we are dealing with — whether an equivalence relation, a partial order, or something else entirely. It is worth studying each property and its negation in detail, with concrete examples to calibrate intuition.

\begin{definition}
Let $R$ be a relation on a set$A$.
\begin{itemize}
\item $R$ is **reflexive** if$a \mathrel{R} a$ for every$a \in A$.
\item $R$ is **irreflexive** if$a \not R a$ for every$a \in A$.
\item $R$ is **symmetric** if$a \mathrel{R} b$ implies$b \mathrel{R} a$ for all$a, b \in A$.
\item $R$ is **antisymmetric** if$a \mathrel{R} b$ and$b \mathrel{R} a$ together imply$a = b$, for all$a, b \in A$.
\item $R$ is **transitive** if$a \mathrel{R} b$ and$b \mathrel{R} c$ together imply$a \mathrel{R} c$, for all$a, b, c \in A$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

**Reflexivity** requires every element to be related to itself. Equality, divisibility ($a \mid a$ since$a = 1 \cdot a$), and set inclusion ($A \subseteq A$) are all reflexive. The strict order$<$ on$\mathbb{R}$ is not reflexive — no real number is strictly less than itself — and is in fact irreflexive. The empty relation on a non-empty set is irreflexive, and the universal relation$A \times A$ is reflexive.

**Symmetry** captures the idea that the relation holds without regard to the order of its arguments. Equality is symmetric; congruence modulo $n$ is symmetric (if$n \mid (a - b)$ then$n \mid (b - a)$). Divisibility on$\mathbb{N}$ is not symmetric:$2 \mid 6$ but$6 \nmid 2$.

A crucial point: **symmetry and antisymmetry are not opposites**. A relation can be both — the equality relation is both symmetric ($a = b \Rightarrow b = a$) and antisymmetric ($a = b$ and$b = a$ together give$a = b$, trivially). A relation can be neither — the relation$\{(1,2),(2,1),(1,3)\}$ on$\{1,2,3\}$ is neither symmetric (since$(1,3) \in R$ but$(3,1) \notin R$) nor antisymmetric (since$(1,2) \in R$ and$(2,1) \in R$ but$1 \neq 2$).

\begin{proposition}
A relation $R$ on$A$ is antisymmetric if and only if$R \cap R^{-1} \subseteq \Delta_{A}$, where$\Delta_{A} = \{(a,a) \mid a \in A\}$ is the diagonal and$R^{-1} = \{(b,a) \mid (a,b) \in R\}$.
\end{proposition}

This reformulation makes antisymmetry easy to visualize: the "mutual" pairs — those $(a,b)$ for which both$(a,b)$ and$(b,a)$ belong to$R$ — must lie on the diagonal.

**Transitivity** says that relatedness propagates along chains. Divisibility, inclusion, $\leq$, and congruence are all transitive. The relation "is the parent of" on the set of people is not transitive: if Alice is a parent of Bob and Bob is a parent of Carol, Alice is not a parent of Carol. A useful test: draw the relation as a directed graph on the set$A$, with a directed edge from$a$ to$b$ whenever$a \mathrel{R} b$. Transitivity fails exactly when there is a path of length two (two consecutive edges$a \to b \to c$) without the corresponding direct edge$a \to c$.

Non-examples are as instructive as examples. Consider the relation $R = \{(1,2),(2,3)\}$ on$\{1,2,3\}$: it is not transitive because$(1,2)$ and$(2,3)$ are both in$R$ but$(1,3)$ is not. Adding$(1,3)$ produces the minimal transitive extension, called the **transitive closure** of$R$. The transitive closure of a relation is the smallest transitive relation containing it, obtained by closing under the transitivity rule.

These four properties combine in different ways to form the two most important classes of relations: **equivalence relations** (reflexive, symmetric, transitive), treated in Section 2.2.3, and **partial orders** (reflexive, antisymmetric, transitive), treated in Section 2.2.4. A firm grip on each property individually is the prerequisite for understanding why these two combinations are the ones that matter.

**Example 1.** Determine which of the four properties hold for the relation $R = \{(a,b) \in \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z} \mid 3 \mid (a - b)\}$ (congruence modulo 3).

Reflexivity: $a - a = 0 = 3 \cdot 0$, so$3 \mid 0$ and$(a,a) \in R$. Yes. Symmetry: if$3 \mid (a-b)$ then$a - b = 3k$ for some$k \in \mathbb{Z}$, so$b - a = 3(-k)$ and$3 \mid (b-a)$. Yes. Antisymmetry:$3 \mid (a - b)$ and$3 \mid (b - a)$ does not force$a = b$; for example,$(0, 3) \in R$ and$(3, 0) \in R$ but$0 \neq 3$. No. Transitivity: if$a - b = 3j$ and$b - c = 3k$ then$a - c = 3(j+k)$. Yes. Conclusion:$R$ is an equivalence relation (reflexive, symmetric, transitive).

**Example 2.** Let $A = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12\}$ and$R = \{(a, b) \mid a \mid b\}$. Determine all four properties.

Reflexivity: $a \mid a$ for all$a$. Yes. Symmetry:$2 \mid 4$ but$4 \nmid 2$. No. Antisymmetry: if$a \mid b$ and$b \mid a$ then$b = ka$ and$a = lb$ for positive integers$k, l$, giving$a = l(ka) = kla$, so$kl = 1$, hence$k = l = 1$ and$a = b$. Yes. Transitivity: if$a \mid b$ and$b \mid c$ then$b = ja$ and$c = kb$, so$c = kja$, giving$a \mid c$. Yes. Conclusion:$R$ is a partial order.

**Example 3.** Construct a relation on $\{1,2,3\}$ that is symmetric and transitive but not reflexive.

Let $R = \{(1,1),(1,2),(2,1),(2,2)\}$. Symmetric:$(1,2) \in R$ and$(2,1) \in R$; all other pairs are either on the diagonal or absent. Yes. Transitive:$(1,2)$ and$(2,1)$ give$(1,1) \in R$;$(2,1)$ and$(1,2)$ give$(2,2) \in R$; all chains close. Yes. Reflexive:$(3,3) \notin R$. No. This example shows that symmetric + transitive does not imply reflexive.

**Example 4.** Give an example of a relation on $\{a, b, c\}$ that is neither symmetric nor antisymmetric.

Take $R = \{(a,b),(b,a),(a,c)\}$. It is not antisymmetric because$(a,b) \in R$ and$(b,a) \in R$ but$a \neq b$. It is not symmetric because$(a,c) \in R$ but$(c,a) \notin R$. Transitivity also fails:$(b,a)$ and$(a,c)$ are in$R$ but$(b,c)$ is not. This illustrates that the four properties are genuinely independent.
