# 2.2.4 Partial Orders and Total Orders

The second great family of relations — alongside equivalence relations — captures the idea of comparison: that one object may be "smaller than," "contained in," or "below" another in some hierarchy. The formal framework is that of an **order relation**, and it arises throughout algebra in the study of subgroup lattices, chains of ideals, prime spectra, and the statement of Zorn's Lemma. Developing a clear mental picture of partially ordered sets early is an investment that pays dividends at every level of the theory.

\begin{definition}
A **partial order** on a set $A$ is a relation$\leq$ that is reflexive, antisymmetric, and transitive. A set$A$ equipped with a partial order is called a **partially ordered set** or **poset**, written$(A, \leq)$.
\end{definition}

The word "partial" signals that not every pair of elements need be comparable. We say $a$ and$b$ are **comparable** if$a \leq b$ or$b \leq a$; otherwise they are **incomparable**, written$a \parallel b$. The classic illustration is divisibility on$\mathbb{N}$:$2 \leq 6$ (since$2 \mid 6$) and$3 \leq 6$ (since$3 \mid 6$), but$2$ and$3$ are incomparable — neither divides the other. Set inclusion on$\mathcal{P}(S)$ is another partial order, and incomparable elements abound:$\{1,2\}$ and$\{2,3\}$ are each subsets of$\{1,2,3\}$ but neither contains the other.

\begin{definition}
A partial order $(A, \leq)$ is a **total order** (or **linear order**) if every pair of elements is comparable: for all$a, b \in A$, either$a \leq b$ or$b \leq a$.
\end{definition}

The familiar ordering $\leq$ on$\mathbb{R}$ is a total order, as is the lexicographic ordering on words: one compares letter by letter, and any two distinct words can eventually be ranked. A total order gives a definite "ranking" of all elements, while a partial order only specifies a hierarchy among those pairs that are actually related.

Posets are often depicted by **Hasse diagrams**: one draws the elements as vertices and places $a$ below$b$ with a line connecting them whenever$a < b$ (meaning$a \leq b$ and$a \neq b$) and there is no$c$ with$a < c < b$ (i.e., the covering relation). By convention, larger elements are placed higher. The diagram for divisibility on$\{1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12\}$ has$1$ at the bottom,$12$ at the top, with lines connecting$1$ to$2, 3$ and to their multiples in the appropriate order. Reading the Hasse diagram immediately reveals chains, antichains, and covering relations.

\begin{definition}
A **chain** in a poset $(A, \leq)$ is a subset$C \subseteq A$ in which every two elements are comparable. An **antichain** is a subset in which no two elements are comparable. An **upper bound** for a subset$S \subseteq A$ is an element$u \in A$ with$s \leq u$ for all$s \in S$; if an upper bound lies in$S$, it is the **maximum** of$S$. A **least upper bound** (or **supremum**,$\sup S$) is a smallest upper bound. Lower bounds and infima are defined analogously.
\end{definition}

A poset in which every two elements have both a least upper bound and a greatest lower bound is called a **lattice**. The power set $\mathcal{P}(S)$ ordered by inclusion is a lattice:$\sup\{A, B\} = A \cup B$ and$\inf\{A, B\} = A \cap B$. The subgroups of a group, ordered by inclusion, also form a lattice: the join of two subgroups is the subgroup generated by their union, and the meet is their intersection.

Posets reappear throughout the entire algebraic hierarchy. The subgroups of a group $G$, ordered by inclusion, form a poset whose structure encodes much of the structure of$G$. The ideals of a ring, ordered by inclusion, form a poset whose "chains" (totally ordered subsets) are studied via the Noetherian and Artinian conditions. The prime ideals of a commutative ring form a poset called the **spectrum**, which is the starting point of algebraic geometry and provides a bridge between ring theory and topology.

**Example 1.** List all elements, chains of maximum length, and antichains of maximum size in the divisibility poset on $A = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12\}$.

The divisibility relation gives $1 \mid a$ for all$a$,$12$ is divisible by all elements, and the intermediate divisors are$2, 3, 4, 6$. A longest chain is$1 \mid 2 \mid 4 \mid 12$ or$1 \mid 2 \mid 6 \mid 12$ or$1 \mid 3 \mid 6 \mid 12$, each of length 4 (four elements). A largest antichain is$\{4, 3\}$ or, better,$\{4, 6, 3\}$: none of$4, 6, 3$ divides any other ($4 \nmid 6$,$6 \nmid 4$,$3 \nmid 4$,$4 \nmid 3$,$3 \nmid 6$ since$6/3 = 2$ — wait,$3 \mid 6$). Correcting:$3 \mid 6$, so$\{3,6\}$ is not an antichain. A true antichain of size 2:$\{4, 3\}$ (neither divides the other). Of size 3:$\{4, 3, \ldots\}$ — inspecting all pairs, one finds that$\{4, 3\}$ and$\{4, 6\}$ are maximal antichains but no antichain of size 3 avoids all divisibility relations in this poset.

**Example 2.** Show that the lexicographic order on $\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$ is a total order.

Define $(a, b) \leq_{\mathrm{lex}} (c, d)$ iff$a < c$, or$a = c$ and$b \leq d$. Reflexivity:$(a,b) \leq (a,b)$ since$a = a$ and$b \leq b$. Antisymmetry: if$(a,b) \leq (c,d)$ and$(c,d) \leq (a,b)$, then considering cases on whether$a < c$ or$a = c$, one finds in each case that$a = c$ and$b = d$. Transitivity: straightforward case analysis. Totality: given$(a,b)$ and$(c,d)$, either$a < c$,$a = c$, or$a > c$, giving$(a,b) \leq (c,d)$, or requiring comparison of$b$ and$d$, or$(c,d) \leq (a,b)$, respectively.

**Example 3.** In the poset $(\mathcal{P}(\{1,2,3\}), \subseteq)$, find$\sup\{\{1,2\}, \{2,3\}\}$ and$\inf\{\{1,2\}, \{2,3\}\}$.

The supremum is the smallest set containing both $\{1,2\}$ and$\{2,3\}$, which is$\{1,2\} \cup \{2,3\} = \{1,2,3\}$. The infimum is the largest set contained in both, which is$\{1,2\} \cap \{2,3\} = \{2\}$. This illustrates that$\mathcal{P}(S)$ is a lattice with$\cup$ as join and$\cap$ as meet.

**Example 4.** Define a partial order on $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$ by$(a,b) \leq (c,d)$ iff$a \leq c$ and$b \leq d$ (the product order). Show this is not a total order and identify an antichain of size 2.

The pair $(2,3)$ and$(3,2)$ are incomparable:$2 \leq 3$ but$3 \not\leq 2$, so$(2,3) \not\leq (3,2)$; and$3 \not\leq 2$, so$(3,2) \not\leq (2,3)$. Hence$\{(2,3),(3,2)\}$ is an antichain of size 2, and the order is not total. The product order on$\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$ is a key example in combinatorics (Dickson's lemma: every antichain in this order is finite) and appears in the theory of polynomial orderings used by Gröbner basis algorithms.
