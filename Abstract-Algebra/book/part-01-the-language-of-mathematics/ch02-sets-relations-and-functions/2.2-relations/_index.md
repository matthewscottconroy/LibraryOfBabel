# 2.2 Relations

Many of the most important mathematical concepts — divisibility, congruence modulo $n$, being isomorphic to, being a subgroup of — describe a connection between two objects rather than a property of one. A *relation* from a set $A$ to a set $B$ is formally just a subset $R \subseteq A \times B$; we write $a \mathrel{R} b$ as shorthand for $(a, b) \in R$. This definition is maximally general: it makes no assumption about what kind of connection $R$ represents. A relation on $A$ (that is, from $A$ to itself, a subset of $A \times A$) can be reflexive, symmetric, antisymmetric, transitive, total, or various combinations thereof, and these adjectives carve out the two major families of relations studied in this section.

The first family is *equivalence relations*. A relation $\sim$ on $A$ is an equivalence relation if it is reflexive ($a \sim a$ for all $a$), symmetric ($a \sim b \Rightarrow b \sim a$), and transitive ($a \sim b$ and $b \sim c \Rightarrow a \sim c$). Equivalence relations formalize the idea that two elements are "the same for the purposes at hand." The integers modulo $n$ provide the fundamental example: $a \sim b$ iff $n \mid (a - b)$, which is reflexive (every integer is congruent to itself), symmetric (if $n \mid (a-b)$ then $n \mid (b-a)$), and transitive (if $n \mid (a-b)$ and $n \mid (b-c)$, then $n \mid (a-c)$). The *equivalence class* of an element $a$ under $\sim$ is $[a] = \{b \in A : a \sim b\}$, and the key theorem of this section states that equivalence classes are either equal or disjoint — they partition $A$ into non-overlapping blocks. This partition theorem is the cornerstone of the quotient construction studied in Section 2.3.

The second family is *partial orders*. A relation $\leq$ on $A$ is a partial order if it is reflexive ($a \leq a$), antisymmetric ($a \leq b$ and $b \leq a \Rightarrow a = b$), and transitive ($a \leq b$ and $b \leq c \Rightarrow a \leq c$). Note the difference from equivalence relations: antisymmetry replaces symmetry, so if two elements are each "at most" the other, they must be equal. A partial order in which every two elements are comparable ($a \leq b$ or $b \leq a$ for all $a, b$) is called a *total order* or *linear order*. The natural numbers under $\leq$, the real numbers under $\leq$, and the integers under divisibility ($a \mid b$) are all partial orders, but only the first two are total. Partial orders are the setting for Zorn's Lemma (Section 3.3), where the notion of a *chain* — a totally ordered subset — plays the decisive role. The section thus lays not only the groundwork for quotient sets but also the order-theoretic vocabulary that reappears throughout algebra whenever one discusses ideals ordered by inclusion, subgroups ordered by containment, or extensions ordered by degree.
