# 2.4.3 Composition and Inverses

Functions can be combined: we can apply one after another. This operation — **composition** — is the fundamental algebraic operation on functions, and studying its properties already reveals the skeleton of group theory before a single group has been defined. The associativity of composition, the existence of identities, and the characterisation of invertibility in terms of bijectivity are all theorems about sets and functions, and they are precisely the axioms of a group — satisfied by bijections of any set.

\begin{definition}
Given functions $f \colon A \to B$ and$g \colon B \to C$, their **composition** is the function$g \circ f \colon A \to C$ defined by$(g \circ f)(a) = g(f(a))$.
\end{definition}

The notation $g \circ f$ applies$f$ first and$g$ second — the order matters, and the notation is read "g after f" or "g composed with f." For composition to make sense, the codomain of$f$ must equal the domain of$g$; we say$f$ and$g$ are **composable** in this order.

\begin{proposition}
Composition of functions is associative: for $f \colon A \to B$,$g \colon B \to C$,$h \colon C \to D$, we have$h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f$.
\end{proposition}

*Proof.* Both sides send $a \in A$ to$h(g(f(a)))$; the association of the parentheses makes no difference to the result.$\square$

The **identity function** $\mathrm{id}_{A} \colon A \to A$ defined by$\mathrm{id}_{A}(a) = a$ acts as a neutral element:$f \circ \mathrm{id}_{A} = f$ and$\mathrm{id}_{B} \circ f = f$ for any$f \colon A \to B$. The identity and associativity together say that functions between sets form a **category**, and bijections of a single set$A$ form a **group**.

\begin{definition}
A function $f \colon A \to B$ has:
\begin{itemize}
\item a **left inverse** if there exists $g \colon B \to A$ with$g \circ f = \mathrm{id}_{A}$;
\item a **right inverse** if there exists $h \colon B \to A$ with$f \circ h = \mathrm{id}_{B}$;
\item a **two-sided inverse** if there exists $k \colon B \to A$ with$g \circ f = \mathrm{id}_{A}$ and$f \circ k = \mathrm{id}_{B}$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}
Let $f \colon A \to B$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $f$ has a left inverse if and only if$f$ is injective.
\item $f$ has a right inverse if and only if$f$ is surjective (assuming the Axiom of Choice in the surjective case).
\item $f$ has a two-sided inverse if and only if$f$ is bijective. The two-sided inverse is unique and is denoted$f^{-1}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

*Proof sketch.* For (1): if $g \circ f = \mathrm{id}_{A}$ and$f(a) = f(a')$, then$a = g(f(a)) = g(f(a')) = a'$, so$f$ is injective. Conversely, if$f$ is injective, define$g \colon B \to A$ by: for$b \in \mathrm{im}(f)$, let$g(b)$ be the unique$a$ with$f(a) = b$; for$b \notin \mathrm{im}(f)$, set$g(b)$ to any element of$A$. Then$g(f(a)) = a$. For (2): if$f \circ h = \mathrm{id}_{B}$, then for any$b \in B$,$f(h(b)) = b$, so$b$ is in the image of$f$, i.e.,$f$ is surjective. The converse uses AC to choose one element from each non-empty fiber$f^{-1}(\{b\})$.$\square$

When a two-sided inverse exists, it is unique: if $g \circ f = \mathrm{id}_{A}$ and$f \circ h = \mathrm{id}_{B}$, then$g = g \circ \mathrm{id}_{B} = g \circ (f \circ h) = (g \circ f) \circ h = \mathrm{id}_{A} \circ h = h$. This uniqueness argument is purely algebraic and works in any category.

The algebraic significance is immediate. The set of all bijections from $A$ to itself — the **symmetric group**$\mathrm{Sym}(A)$ — is a group under composition: associativity holds for all function composition; the identity$\mathrm{id}_{A}$ is the group identity; and every bijection has a two-sided inverse (which is itself a bijection). When$A = \{1, \ldots, n\}$, this group is$S_{n}$, the symmetric group on$n$ elements.

**Example 1.** Let $f \colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$,$f(x) = 2x + 1$, and$g \colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$,$g(x) = x^{2}$. Compute$g \circ f$ and$f \circ g$ and verify they are different.

$(g \circ f)(x) = g(2x+1) = (2x+1)^{2} = 4x^{2} + 4x + 1$.$(f \circ g)(x) = f(x^{2}) = 2x^{2} + 1$. Since$(4x^{2} + 4x + 1) \neq (2x^{2} + 1)$ in general (e.g., at$x=1$:$9 \neq 3$), composition is not commutative.

**Example 2.** Find the inverse of $f \colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$,$f(x) = \frac{x-3}{2x+1}$ (wherever defined), and verify$f^{-1} \circ f = \mathrm{id}$.

Set $y = \frac{x-3}{2x+1}$. Solving for$x$:$y(2x+1) = x - 3$, so$2xy + y = x - 3$, giving$x(2y-1) = -3-y$, thus$x = \frac{-3-y}{2y-1}$. So$f^{-1}(y) = \frac{-3-y}{2y-1}$. Verify:$f^{-1}(f(x)) = \frac{-3 - \frac{x-3}{2x+1}}{2 \cdot \frac{x-3}{2x+1} - 1} = \frac{\frac{-3(2x+1)-(x-3)}{2x+1}}{\frac{2(x-3)-(2x+1)}{2x+1}} = \frac{-6x-3-x+3}{2x-6-2x-1} = \frac{-7x}{-7} = x$.

**Example 3.** In $S_{3}$ (bijections of$\{1,2,3\}$), let$\sigma = \begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\2&3&1\end{pmatrix}$ (cyclic rotation) and$\tau = \begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\1&3&2\end{pmatrix}$ (swap 2 and 3). Compute$\sigma \circ \tau$,$\tau \circ \sigma$, and$\sigma^{-1}$.

$(\sigma \circ \tau)(1) = \sigma(1) = 2$;$(\sigma \circ \tau)(2) = \sigma(3) = 1$;$(\sigma \circ \tau)(3) = \sigma(2) = 3$. So$\sigma \circ \tau = \begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\2&1&3\end{pmatrix}$.$(\tau \circ \sigma)(1) = \tau(2) = 3$;$(\tau \circ \sigma)(2) = \tau(3) = 2$;$(\tau \circ \sigma)(3) = \tau(1) = 1$. So$\tau \circ \sigma = \begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\3&2&1\end{pmatrix}$. Since$\sigma \circ \tau \neq \tau \circ \sigma$,$S_{3}$ is non-abelian. The inverse of$\sigma$ is$\sigma^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\3&1&2\end{pmatrix}$.

**Example 4.** Prove: if $f \colon A \to B$ is bijective, then$(f^{-1})^{-1} = f$.

Since $f^{-1} \circ f = \mathrm{id}_{A}$ and$f \circ f^{-1} = \mathrm{id}_{B}$, the function$f$ is a left inverse of$f^{-1}$ and a right inverse of$f^{-1}$, hence a two-sided inverse. By uniqueness of the two-sided inverse,$(f^{-1})^{-1} = f$.
