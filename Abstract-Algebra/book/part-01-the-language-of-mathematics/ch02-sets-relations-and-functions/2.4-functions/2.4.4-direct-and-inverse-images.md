# 2.4.4 Direct and Inverse Images

A function $f \colon A \to B$ does not only assign outputs to individual elements; it also acts on subsets of$A$ and on subsets of$B$. These two actions — the direct image and the inverse image — have sharply asymmetric properties that are essential to understand. The asymmetry is not an accident or a technical quirk: it reflects a deep structural difference whose algebraic counterpart appears repeatedly in the study of homomorphisms.

\begin{definition}
Let $f \colon A \to B$ be a function.
\begin{itemize}
\item The **direct image** of $S \subseteq A$ is$f(S) = \{f(s) \mid s \in S\} \subseteq B$.
\item The **inverse image** (or **preimage**) of $T \subseteq B$ is$f^{-1}(T) = \{a \in A \mid f(a) \in T\} \subseteq A$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

A crucial warning: the notation $f^{-1}(T)$ does not presuppose that$f$ has an inverse function. The preimage operation is defined for any function$f$, and we write$f^{-1}(T)$ even when$f$ is not bijective. It simply means "the set of inputs that land in$T$."

\begin{proposition}[Properties of the direct image]
Let $f \colon A \to B$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $f(S_{1} \cup S_{2}) = f(S_{1}) \cup f(S_{2})$ for all$S_{1}, S_{2} \subseteq A$.
\item $f(S_{1} \cap S_{2}) \subseteq f(S_{1}) \cap f(S_{2})$ for all$S_{1}, S_{2} \subseteq A$, with equality if$f$ is injective.
\item $f(S^{c}) \supseteq f(A) \setminus f(S)$, but equality can fail.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Properties of the inverse image]
Let $f \colon A \to B$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $f^{-1}(T_{1} \cup T_{2}) = f^{-1}(T_{1}) \cup f^{-1}(T_{2})$.
\item $f^{-1}(T_{1} \cap T_{2}) = f^{-1}(T_{1}) \cap f^{-1}(T_{2})$.
\item $f^{-1}(T^{c}) = (f^{-1}(T))^{c}$.
\item $f^{-1}(\bigcup_{i} T_{i}) = \bigcup_{i} f^{-1}(T_{i})$ and$f^{-1}(\bigcap_{i} T_{i}) = \bigcap_{i} f^{-1}(T_{i})$ for arbitrary families.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

The second proposition says the inverse image commutes with all Boolean operations: unions, intersections, and complements. The proofs are all direct unfoldings of the definition, using the fact that $f(a) \in T_{1} \cup T_{2}$ iff$f(a) \in T_{1}$ or$f(a) \in T_{2}$, and so on. The first proposition is weaker: the direct image only commutes with unions, not with intersections or complements.

The failure of the direct image to preserve intersections has a clean counterexample: suppose $f \colon \{1,2\} \to \{a\}$ sends both$1$ and$2$ to$a$. Then$f(\{1\} \cap \{2\}) = f(\emptyset) = \emptyset$, but$f(\{1\}) \cap f(\{2\}) = \{a\} \cap \{a\} = \{a\}$. The strict containment$\emptyset \subsetneq \{a\}$ reflects the non-injectivity of$f$.

This asymmetry — inverse images behave well, direct images only partially — is not an accident. In categorical language, the inverse image operation $f^{-1} \colon \mathcal{P}(B) \to \mathcal{P}(A)$ is a lattice homomorphism (preserving all meets and joins), while the direct image$f \colon \mathcal{P}(A) \to \mathcal{P}(B)$ is only a join-preserving map (preserving unions but not meets). This categorical perspective makes clear why the asymmetry is structural and universal.

The algebraic consequences are immediate. For a group homomorphism $\phi \colon G \to H$: the preimage$\phi^{-1}(K)$ of any subgroup$K \leq H$ is a subgroup of$G$; the preimage$\phi^{-1}(N)$ of any normal subgroup$N \trianglelefteq H$ is a normal subgroup of$G$. By contrast, the direct image$\phi(H')$ of a subgroup$H' \leq G$ is always a subgroup of$H$ (because$\phi$ preserves the group operation), but the direct image of a normal subgroup need not be normal. The source of these asymmetries is exactly what we see at the set-theoretic level in the two propositions above.

**Example 1.** Let $f \colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$,$f(x) = x^{2}$. Compute$f(\{-2, -1, 0, 1, 2\})$ and$f^{-1}(\{1, 4\})$.

Direct image: $f(\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}) = \{4,1,0,1,4\} = \{0,1,4\}$. Inverse image:$f^{-1}(\{1,4\}) = \{x \in \mathbb{R} \mid x^{2} \in \{1,4\}\} = \{-2,-1,1,2\}$.

**Example 2.** For $f$ as in Example 1, let$S_{1} = [-2, 0]$ and$S_{2} = [0, 2]$. Verify that$f(S_{1} \cap S_{2}) \subsetneq f(S_{1}) \cap f(S_{2})$.

$S_{1} \cap S_{2} = \{0\}$, so$f(S_{1} \cap S_{2}) = \{0\}$.$f(S_{1}) = f([-2,0]) = [0, 4]$ (since$x^{2}$ on$[-2,0]$ ranges from$0$ to$4$).$f(S_{2}) = f([0,2]) = [0,4]$. Thus$f(S_{1}) \cap f(S_{2}) = [0,4]$, which properly contains$\{0\}$.

**Example 3.** Let $f \colon \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$,$f(n) = [n \bmod 3]$. Compute$f^{-1}(\{[0],[1]\})$ and$f^{-1}([2])$.

$f^{-1}(\{[0],[1]\}) = \{n \in \mathbb{Z} \mid n \equiv 0$ or$n \equiv 1 \pmod 3\} = \{\ldots, -3,-2,0,1,3,4,6,7,\ldots\} = \{n \mid n \not\equiv 2 \pmod 3\}$.$f^{-1}([2]) = \{n \mid n \equiv 2 \pmod 3\} = \{\ldots,-4,-1,2,5,8,\ldots\}$.

Verify that $f^{-1}(\{[0],[1]\}) \cup f^{-1}([2]) = \mathbb{Z}$ and the two sets are disjoint, confirming$f^{-1}$ preserves complementation:$f^{-1}(\{[0],[1]\}) = f^{-1}([2]^{c}) = (f^{-1}([2]))^{c}$.

**Example 4.** Prove that if $f \colon A \to B$ is a bijection and$T \subseteq B$, then$f(f^{-1}(T)) = T$ and$f^{-1}(f(S)) = S$ for all$S \subseteq A$.

For $f(f^{-1}(T)) = T$: since$f$ is surjective, every$t \in T$ has some$a \in A$ with$f(a) = t$, so$a \in f^{-1}(T)$ and$t = f(a) \in f(f^{-1}(T))$, giving$T \subseteq f(f^{-1}(T))$. The other inclusion holds for any$f$: if$b \in f(f^{-1}(T))$, then$b = f(a)$ for some$a \in f^{-1}(T)$, meaning$f(a) \in T$, so$b \in T$. For$f^{-1}(f(S)) = S$: the inclusion$S \subseteq f^{-1}(f(S))$ holds generally. For the reverse, suppose$a \in f^{-1}(f(S))$, so$f(a) \in f(S)$, meaning$f(a) = f(s)$ for some$s \in S$. By injectivity of$f$,$a = s \in S$. Hence$f^{-1}(f(S)) \subseteq S$.
