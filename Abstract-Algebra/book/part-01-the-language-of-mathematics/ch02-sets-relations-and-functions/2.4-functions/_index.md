# 2.4 Functions

The informal notion of a function as a rule that assigns to each input exactly one output is familiar from calculus. Section 2.4 gives this notion a precise set-theoretic foundation and develops its structural properties — injectivity, surjectivity, bijectivity, composition, and inversion — in a form general enough to apply to any sets, with no requirement of continuity, differentiability, or any other analytic structure. This generality is what makes the theory of functions the language of all of algebra.

Formally, a *function* from $A$ to $B$ is a subset $f \subseteq A \times B$ such that for every $a \in A$, there is exactly one $b \in B$ with $(a, b) \in f$; we write $f(a) = b$ for this unique $b$ and $f: A \to B$ for the whole function. The set $A$ is the *domain*, $B$ is the *codomain*, and the *image* or *range* of $f$ is $\mathrm{im}(f) = \{f(a) : a \in A\} \subseteq B$. A function $f: A \to B$ is *injective* (one-to-one) if $f(a_1) = f(a_2) \Rightarrow a_1 = a_2$; geometrically, no two distinct inputs share an output. It is *surjective* (onto) if $\mathrm{im}(f) = B$, meaning every element of the codomain is achieved. It is *bijective* if it is both injective and surjective — a perfect pairing between elements of $A$ and elements of $B$. Bijections are the set-theoretic notion of "same size," and they will be the central object of Chapter 3.

The *composition* of $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ is the function $g \circ f: A \to C$ defined by $(g \circ f)(a) = g(f(a))$. Composition is associative: $h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f$ for any composable triple, which is why the parentheses can be dropped without ambiguity. Injectivity and surjectivity behave well under composition: if both $f$ and $g$ are injective then so is $g \circ f$, and similarly for surjectivity; the converses, which are subtler, are the subject of exercises. A function $f: A \to B$ has a *two-sided inverse* — a function $g: B \to A$ with $g \circ f = \mathrm{id}_A$ and $f \circ g = \mathrm{id}_B$ — if and only if $f$ is bijective. This inverse, when it exists, is unique, and we write it $f^{-1}$. The concept of a two-sided inverse will generalize to homomorphisms with two-sided inverses (isomorphisms), linear maps with two-sided inverses (isomorphisms of vector spaces), and so on: in every algebraic category, the morphisms that possess two-sided inverses are precisely those that establish structural identity between objects. Finally, for any function $f: A \to B$, the *fiber* over a point $b \in B$ is $f^{-1}(b) = \{a \in A : f(a) = b\}$, and the collection of all non-empty fibers is a partition of $A$ — the partition associated (via Section 2.3) to the equivalence relation $a \sim a'$ iff $f(a) = f(a')$. This fiber-quotient relationship encapsulates the first isomorphism theorem in its most elementary form.
