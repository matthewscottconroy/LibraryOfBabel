# Chapter 2 — Sets, Relations, and Functions

If Chapter 1 provides the rules of mathematical reasoning, Chapter 2 provides the objects that mathematics reasons *about*. Almost every structure studied in algebra — groups, rings, fields, modules, vector spaces — is, at its foundation, a set equipped with some additional data. Before any of that additional data can be described, we need a precise and flexible theory of sets, a framework for expressing how elements of sets can be related to one another, and a rigorous definition of the functions that carry information between sets. This chapter builds all three, and in doing so establishes the ambient language that the rest of the book takes for granted.

The chapter opens with naive set theory: the idea, initiated by Cantor in the 1870s, that one can speak of any definite collection of objects as a mathematical entity in its own right. The basic operations — union $A \cup B$, intersection $A \cap B$, set difference $A \setminus B$, and the power set $\mathcal{P}(A)$ — behave according to elegant laws (the distributive, De Morgan, and absorption identities) that will appear again in the algebra of ideals, lattices, and Boolean rings. The Cartesian product $A \times B$ of two sets, the collection of all ordered pairs $(a, b)$ with $a \in A$ and $b \in B$, is the raw material from which both relations and functions are built. We also encounter here the word "naive": Cantor's original approach, which allowed any comprehensible description to define a set, leads directly to Russell's Paradox — the set of all sets that do not contain themselves — and to the recognition that set theory requires a careful axiomatic foundation.

Relations formalize the idea that two objects can stand in some determined connection to each other. A relation on a set $A$ is simply a subset $R \subseteq A \times A$; we write $a \mathrel{R} b$ when $(a,b) \in R$. This single definition encompasses familiar ideas ranging from "$\leq$" on the integers to "has the same birthday as" on a group of people. The most important class of relations for algebra is the equivalence relation: a relation that is reflexive ($a \mathrel{R} a$ for all $a$), symmetric (if $a \mathrel{R} b$ then $b \mathrel{R} a$), and transitive (if $a \mathrel{R} b$ and $b \mathrel{R} c$ then $a \mathrel{R} c$). Equivalence relations are important because they permit a formal notion of "treating two things as identical" — a notion that is central to every quotient construction in algebra. The other major class, partial orders, gives a rigorous account of comparison without requiring any two elements to be comparable, setting up the theory of lattices and ordinals.

Quotient sets distill what equivalence relations actually do: they partition a set into equivalence classes, and the set of all these classes is a new mathematical object — the quotient $A/{\sim}$. The fundamental observation is that equivalence relations and partitions are in perfect correspondence: every equivalence relation produces a partition, and every partition determines a unique equivalence relation whose classes are exactly the parts. This construction is the prototype for quotient groups, quotient rings, and quotient modules, all of which are formed by the same basic move of declaring certain elements to be "the same."

Functions are the mechanism by which sets communicate structure to one another. Defined set-theoretically as subsets $f \subseteq A \times B$ satisfying the condition that each $a \in A$ appears as the first coordinate of exactly one pair, functions come equipped with three fundamental properties: injectivity (distinct inputs produce distinct outputs, $f(a_1) = f(a_2) \Rightarrow a_1 = a_2$), surjectivity (every element of the codomain is hit by at least one input), and bijectivity (both simultaneously). Bijections are especially important because they capture the notion of structural sameness without imposing any algebraic structure: two sets are "the same size" precisely when a bijection exists between them. Composition of functions is associative and respects injectivity and surjectivity, and a function is bijective if and only if it has a two-sided inverse — a fact that the exercises will ask the reader to prove carefully. These ideas, developed cleanly here, will recur as isomorphisms, homomorphisms, and automorphisms throughout every subsequent chapter.
