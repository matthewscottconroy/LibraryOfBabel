# 3.2.1 Countability of $\mathbb{Z}$ and$\mathbb{Q}$: Diagonal Enumeration

At first glance, $\mathbb{Z}$ seems strictly larger than$\mathbb{N}$ — after all, it contains all the negative integers as well. And$\mathbb{Q}$ seems vastly larger still, since between any two integers there lie infinitely many rationals. Yet both sets turn out to be countably infinite. The resolution of this apparent paradox is one of the early lessons in what it means to work with infinity carefully: size is determined by the existence of a bijection, not by naive comparisons of "how many extra elements" a set seems to contain.

The countability of $\mathbb{Z}$ is straightforward once one abandons the idea of listing integers in their natural order. Instead of$0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots$ (which never reaches the negatives) or$\ldots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \ldots$ (which has no starting point), we interleave:$0, 1, -1, 2, -2, 3, -3, \ldots$. The explicit formula is

$$f(n) = \begin{cases} n/2 & \text{if } n \text{ is even} \\ -(n+1)/2 & \text{if } n \text{ is odd,} \end{cases}$$

which defines a bijection $f \colon \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{Z}$ and confirms$|\mathbb{Z}| = |\mathbb{N}| = \aleph_{0}$.

The countability of $\mathbb{Q}$ requires a more ingenious argument: the **diagonal enumeration** (also known as Cantor's first diagonal method). The key idea is to represent$\mathbb{Q}$ as the union of countably many countable sets and then navigate this "two-dimensional" array by sweeping along diagonals.

\begin{theorem}
$|\mathbb{Q}| = |\mathbb{N}|$; the rational numbers are countably infinite.
\end{theorem}

*Proof.* Arrange all fractions $p/q$ with$p \in \mathbb{Z}$ and$q \in \mathbb{N}$,$q > 0$, in an infinite matrix: rows are indexed by$q = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$ and columns by$p = 0, 1, -1, 2, -2, \ldots$. Every rational number appears in this array (in fact infinitely often, for different unsimplified forms). Traverse the array along anti-diagonal sweeps: first$(q,p) = (1,0)$, then$(1,1),(2,0)$, then$(1,-1),(2,1),(3,0)$, and so on, skipping any fraction$p/q$ already listed or with$\gcd(|p|, q) > 1$. This process eventually reaches every fraction in its lowest terms, producing a bijection$\mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{Q}$.$\square$

A cleaner variant: note that $\mathbb{Q} = \bigcup_{q=1}^{\infty} \{p/q \mid p \in \mathbb{Z}\}$, a countable union (indexed by$q \in \mathbb{N}$) of countable sets (each$\{p/q\}_{p \in \mathbb{Z}}$ is in bijection with$\mathbb{Z}$). By the proposition from Section 3.1.3, a countable union of countable sets is countable.

\begin{remark}
The diagonal enumeration technique is not limited to $\mathbb{Q}$. It applies to any "two-dimensional" countable array. In particular,$\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$ is countable: the function$f(m,n) = \frac{(m+n)(m+n+1)}{2} + m$ (the Cantor pairing function) is an explicit bijection$\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$. By induction,$\mathbb{N}^{k}$ is countable for every$k \in \mathbb{N}$.
\end{remark}

The key insight underlying both proofs is that being "two-dimensional" — having both a positive and a negative direction, or having both a numerator and denominator — does not make a set uncountable. The diagonal technique systematically visits every entry in a two-dimensional array, confirming that no entry is missed. Cantor will use a closely related — but opposite — diagonal idea in Section 3.2.2 to prove that $\mathbb{R}$ cannot be enumerated at all.

**Example 1.** Write out the first 10 terms of the diagonal enumeration of $\mathbb{Q}^{+}$ (positive rationals) arranged with$q$ in rows and$p$ in columns.

Row 1 ($q=1$):$1/1, 2/1, 3/1, 4/1, \ldots$. Row 2 ($q=2$):$1/2, 2/2, 3/2, 4/2, \ldots$. Row 3 ($q=3$):$1/3, 2/3, 3/3, 4/3, \ldots$. Diagonal traversal (skipping non-reduced fractions):$1/1 = 1$;$2/1 = 2$, skip$2/2$ (not reduced);$1/2$;$3/1 = 3$; skip$3/3$ and$3/2$ (wait:$3/2$ is reduced); actually$3/2$;$2/3$; skip$1/3$: recount. The first 10 distinct rationals in lowest terms are:$1, 2, 1/2, 3, 1/3, 4, 3/2, 2/3, 1/4, 5, \ldots$

**Example 2.** Use the Cantor pairing function to find $f(3,2)$ and find the pair$(m,n)$ such that$f(m,n) = 10$.

$f(3,2) = \frac{(3+2)(3+2+1)}{2} + 3 = \frac{5 \cdot 6}{2} + 3 = 15 + 3 = 18$. For$f(m,n) = 10$: the value$10$ is in the "diagonal"$m+n = k$ where$\frac{k(k+1)}{2} \leq 10 < \frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{2}$. Testing$k=4$:$\frac{4 \cdot 5}{2} = 10$, so$m + n = 4$ and$m = 10 - 10 = 0$. Hence$(m,n) = (0,4)$.

**Example 3.** Show directly that the set of all polynomials with integer coefficients is countable.

Let $\mathbb{Z}[x]_{n}$ be the set of polynomials of degree$\leq n$ with integer coefficients. Each such polynomial is determined by$(n+1)$ integers, so$\mathbb{Z}[x]_{n}$ is in bijection with$\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}$, which is countable (a finite product of countable sets). Since$\mathbb{Z}[x] = \bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty} \mathbb{Z}[x]_{n}$ is a countable union of countable sets, it is countable.

**Example 4.** Conclude that the set of algebraic numbers is countable.

The algebraic numbers are the roots of all nonzero polynomials in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$. Each polynomial of degree$d$ has at most$d$ roots (by the factor theorem over$\mathbb{C}$). Since$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is countable (Example 3) and each polynomial contributes finitely many roots, the set of all algebraic numbers is a countable union of finite sets, hence countable. Since$\mathbb{R}$ is uncountable (Section 3.2.2) and the algebraic numbers are countable, "most" real numbers are transcendental.
