# 3.2.4 The Schröder–Bernstein Theorem

In practice, producing an explicit bijection between two sets can be genuinely difficult even when one feels certain the sets must have the same cardinality. The **Schröder–Bernstein theorem** provides a crucial shortcut: if we can find an injection from $A$ into$B$ and an injection from$B$ into$A$, then a bijection between them exists — even if constructing one directly is impractical or impossible.

\begin{theorem}[Schröder–Bernstein]
If there exist injections $f \colon A \to B$ and$g \colon B \to A$, then$|A| = |B|$; that is, a bijection$h \colon A \to B$ exists.
\end{theorem}

*Proof.* We construct $h$ by analyzing the "chain" structure induced by$f$ and$g$. For each element$a \in A$, trace back its ancestry:$a$ may have a preimage under$g$ in$B$, which may have a preimage under$f$ in$A$, which may have a preimage under$g$, and so on. Each element of$A$ belongs to exactly one of three types of chains:
\begin{itemize}
\item Chains that eventually terminate at an element of $A$ with no$g$-preimage (an "orphan" in$A$).
\item Chains that eventually terminate at an element of $B$ with no$f$-preimage (an "orphan" in$B$).
\item Chains that are infinite in both directions (infinite chains).
\end{itemize}
Define $h \colon A \to B$ by:$h(a) = f(a)$ if$a$ belongs to a chain of type 1 or type 3 (starts in$A$ or is doubly infinite), and$h(a) = g^{-1}(a)$ if$a$ belongs to a chain of type 2 (starts in$B$). One verifies that$h$ is well-defined (elements of type 2 chains genuinely have$g$-preimages), injective, and surjective.$\square$

This proof is non-constructive in the sense that it identifies pieces and assembles a bijection without writing out an explicit formula, but it is fully rigorous. The theorem's value is enormous: it reduces the problem of showing $|A| = |B|$ to the (usually much easier) problem of finding injections in both directions.

\begin{corollary}
The relation $|A| \leq |B|$ (defined by the existence of an injection$A \to B$) is a partial order on cardinalities: it is reflexive (identity map), transitive (compose injections), and antisymmetric (Schröder–Bernstein).
\end{corollary}

The Well-Ordering Theorem (Section 3.3.3) further implies that any two cardinalities are comparable, making this a total order. Together, these facts confirm that the "sizes" of sets are linearly ordered in a way that behaves like our intuition for finite sizes.

\begin{example}
To show $|(0,1)| = |[0,1]|$: The inclusion$(0,1) \hookrightarrow [0,1]$ is an injection in one direction. In the other, the map$x \mapsto x/2 + 1/4$ sends$[0,1]$ into$(1/4, 3/4) \subset (0,1)$, giving an injection$[0,1] \to (0,1)$. By Schröder–Bernstein,$|(0,1)| = |[0,1]|$.
\end{example}

The theorem also applies in abstract algebraic settings. To show that two algebraic structures have the same underlying set cardinality, it often suffices to embed each into the other. For instance, in the study of algebraic closures, one uses Schröder–Bernstein-type arguments to show that two algebraic closures of the same field have the same cardinality, as a step toward showing they are isomorphic.

\begin{remark}
The theorem was proved in various forms by Cantor, Schröder, and Bernstein in the 1890s. The proof described above (the chain decomposition) is due to König. The result is sometimes called the Cantor–Schröder–Bernstein theorem. Note that the theorem does not hold in all categories: for groups or rings, having mutual injections (injective homomorphisms in both directions) does not imply the structures are isomorphic.
\end{remark}

**Example 1.** Show that $|\mathbb{N}| = |\mathbb{Z}|$ using Schröder–Bernstein.

Inject $\mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{Z}$ via the inclusion$n \mapsto n$. Inject$\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{N}$ via$n \mapsto 2n$ if$n \geq 0$ and$n \mapsto -2n - 1$ if$n < 0$ (the interleaving map). Both are injections. By Schröder–Bernstein,$|\mathbb{N}| = |\mathbb{Z}|$.

**Example 2.** Show that $|[0,1]| = |[0,1)| = |(0,1)|$ without constructing explicit bijections.

$(0,1) \hookrightarrow [0,1)$ is an injection;$[0,1) \hookrightarrow (0,1)$ via$x \mapsto (x+1)/3$ (which lands in$(1/3, 2/3)$). By Schröder–Bernstein,$|(0,1)| = |[0,1)|$. Similarly,$(0,1) \hookrightarrow [0,1]$ by inclusion, and$[0,1] \to (0,1)$ via$x \mapsto (x+1)/3$. By Schröder–Bernstein,$|(0,1)| = |[0,1]|$.

**Example 3.** Show that $|\mathbb{R}^{2}| \leq |\mathbb{R}|$ using an injection, and conclude$|\mathbb{R}^{2}| = |\mathbb{R}|$.

We show $|(0,1)^{2}| \leq |(0,1)|$. Map$(x, y) \in (0,1)^{2}$ to the interleaved decimal$z = 0.x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}y_{2}\ldots \in (0,1)$ (choosing canonical non-terminating representations). This is injective: different pairs produce different interleavings. The inclusion$(0,1) \hookrightarrow (0,1)^{2}$ via$x \mapsto (x, 1/2)$ is also an injection. By Schröder–Bernstein,$|(0,1)^{2}| = |(0,1)|$, so$|\mathbb{R}^{2}| = |\mathbb{R}| = \mathfrak{c}$.

**Example 4.** Show that the Schröder–Bernstein theorem fails for groups: construct groups $G$ and$H$ that each embed injectively into the other, but are not isomorphic.

Let $G = \mathbb{Z}$ and$H = \mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}$. There is an injective homomorphism$G \to H$ (e.g.,$n \mapsto (n, 0)$) and an injective homomorphism$H \to G$ (since$H \cong \mathbb{Z}^{2}$ embeds in$\mathbb{Z}$ — in fact$H$ is a free abelian group of rank 2, and any free abelian group of finite rank embeds in$\mathbb{Z}$ via a suitable map... actually the embedding$\mathbb{Z}^{2} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Z}$ is non-obvious and requires a different example). A cleaner example:$G = \mathbb{Z}$ (free abelian of rank 1) and$H = \mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. Then$G$ embeds in$H$ but$H$ does not embed in$G$ (since$G$ has no elements of order 2). The point stands: set-level bijectivity (Schröder–Bernstein) does not lift to isomorphism.
