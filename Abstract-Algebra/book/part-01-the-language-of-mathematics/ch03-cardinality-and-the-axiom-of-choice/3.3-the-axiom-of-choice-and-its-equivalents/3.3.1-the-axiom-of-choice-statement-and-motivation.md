# 3.3.1 The Axiom of Choice: Statement and Motivation

Suppose you are given a collection of non-empty sets — say, one set for each day of the year — and asked to pick one element from each. There is no difficulty: you simply perform the selection finitely many times. But what if the collection is infinite? What if you are given a non-empty set $A_{i}$ for every natural number$i$, and asked to choose a single element$f(i) \in A_{i}$ from each? Intuitively this still seems reasonable — each set is non-empty, so each selection is individually possible. The difficulty is that "possible infinitely many times simultaneously" is harder to justify than "possible finitely many times," because a formal mathematical proof requires an explicit construction, and no such construction may be available when the sets$A_{i}$ lack distinguishing structure.

\begin{definition}[Axiom of Choice — AC]
For any collection $\{A_{i}\}_{i \in I}$ of non-empty sets indexed by any set$I$, there exists a **choice function**$f \colon I \to \bigcup_{i \in I} A_{i}$ satisfying$f(i) \in A_{i}$ for every$i \in I$.
\end{definition}

When $I$ is finite, the existence of$f$ follows from the axioms of logic by repeated application: first choose$f(i_{1}) \in A_{i_{1}}$, then$f(i_{2}) \in A_{i_{2}}$, and so on. The Axiom of Choice adds something genuinely new only for infinite index sets, and its critical feature is that it guarantees the existence of$f$ even when no explicit rule specifies which element to choose from each$A_{i}$. The axiom is an existence assertion, not a construction.

\begin{remark}
The Axiom of Choice is not provable from the other axioms of Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory (ZF), nor is its negation provable — it is independent of ZF. This was established by Gödel (1938), who showed that if ZF is consistent then so is ZFC (ZF + AC), and Cohen (1963), who showed that ZF + $\neg$AC is also consistent. Despite early controversy, the mathematical community has broadly accepted AC because it is necessary for a sweeping range of fundamental results that one would be loath to abandon: every vector space has a basis, every ring has a maximal ideal, every field has an algebraic closure.
\end{remark}

The Axiom of Choice also underlies results that are not obviously "choice-like." Showing that the union of countably many countable sets is countable requires, in general, a countable number of choices (one enumeration for each set), which is a special case of AC called **Countable Choice** (ACω). Showing that every surjective function has a right inverse requires AC, as observed in Section 2.4.3. Showing that the product of non-empty sets is non-empty — itself sometimes taken as a formulation of AC — is the precise expression of the choice principle.

Several pathological consequences follow from the negation of AC: it becomes consistent that $\mathbb{R}$ cannot be well-ordered, that there exist vector spaces with no basis, that the countable union of countable sets is not necessarily countable, and that there exist Dedekind-finite infinite sets (infinite sets with no countably infinite subset). The last of these is particularly strange: an "infinite" set that cannot be listed even in principle. Mathematicians working in constructive or intuitionistic foundations take such possibilities seriously, but for the classical algebra in this book, we adopt AC as a standard tool.

The Axiom of Choice has two famous equivalent reformulations — Zorn's Lemma and the Well-Ordering Theorem — that are more convenient for most algebraic applications. These equivalences are non-trivial theorems, and recognising that a given proof is "secretly" an application of AC is an important skill in reading modern algebra.

**Example 1.** Show that the Axiom of Choice is needed to prove that every surjective function has a right inverse.

Let $f \colon A \twoheadrightarrow B$ be surjective. A right inverse$h \colon B \to A$ must satisfy$f(h(b)) = b$ for every$b \in B$. For each$b \in B$, the fiber$f^{-1}(\{b\})$ is non-empty (by surjectivity), and we need to choose one element$h(b) \in f^{-1}(\{b\})$. This is exactly a choice function for the collection$\{f^{-1}(\{b\})\}_{b \in B}$, which requires AC when$B$ is infinite and the fibers have no canonical "smallest" or "most natural" element.

**Example 2.** State the Axiom of Choice in terms of Cartesian products.

AC is equivalent to: the Cartesian product $\prod_{i \in I} A_{i} = \{f \colon I \to \bigcup_{i} A_{i} \mid f(i) \in A_{i} \text{ for all } i\}$ is non-empty whenever each$A_{i}$ is non-empty. This is perhaps the most transparent formulation: we are simply asserting that an infinite product of non-empty sets is itself non-empty.

**Example 3.** Give an example of a choice that requires AC (no explicit rule available) and one that does not (an explicit rule exists).

Requires AC: let $A_{i} = \{x \in \mathbb{R} \mid x^{2} > i\}$ for each$i \in \mathbb{N}$. Each$A_{i}$ is non-empty (e.g.,$i+1 \in A_{i}$), but there is no canonical "least" element of$A_{i}$. Choosing one element from each requires AC.

Does not require AC: let $A_{n} = \{n, n+1, n+2, \ldots\}$ for each$n \in \mathbb{N}$. The choice function$f(n) = n$ (choose the smallest element) is explicitly defined without invoking any form of AC, since each$A_{n}$ has a natural well-ordering inherited from$\mathbb{N}$.

**Example 4.** Show that ACω (countable choice) is sufficient to prove that a countable union of countable sets is countable.

Let $A_{1}, A_{2}, A_{3}, \ldots$ be countable sets. For each$n$, the set$E_{n}$ of enumerations of$A_{n}$ (bijections$\mathbb{N} \to A_{n}$, or bijections from some finite set to$A_{n}$) is non-empty. By ACω, choose an enumeration$f_{n}$ from$E_{n}$ for each$n$. Using the diagonal enumeration technique (Section 3.2.1) on the array$(f_{n}(k))_{n,k}$ yields an enumeration of$\bigcup_{n} A_{n}$. Without some form of choice, we cannot select the enumerations$f_{n}$ simultaneously, and the proof breaks down.
