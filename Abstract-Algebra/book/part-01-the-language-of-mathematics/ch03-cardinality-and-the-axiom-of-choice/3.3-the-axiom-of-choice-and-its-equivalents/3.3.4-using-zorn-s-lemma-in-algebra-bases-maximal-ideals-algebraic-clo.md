# 3.3.4 Using Zorn's Lemma in Algebra: Bases, Maximal Ideals, Algebraic Closures

The template from Section 3.3.2 — define a poset of partial solutions, verify chains have upper bounds, invoke Zorn, then exploit maximality — applies across all of abstract algebra. The four applications in this section are foundational existence theorems, each unprovable without some form of AC, and each following the template so closely that once one proof is understood in detail, the others become essentially exercises in recognizing the same pattern.

**Bases of vector spaces.** Every vector space has a basis.

\begin{theorem}
Let $V$ be a vector space over a field$F$. Then$V$ has a basis.
\end{theorem}

*Proof.* Step 1: Let $P$ be the set of all linearly independent subsets of$V$, ordered by inclusion$\subseteq$. Since the empty set is linearly independent (vacuously),$P$ is non-empty. Step 2: Let$\mathcal{C}$ be a chain in$P$. We claim$U = \bigcup_{C \in \mathcal{C}} C$ is an element of$P$, i.e.,$U$ is linearly independent. Take any finite subset$\{v_{1}, \ldots, v_{k}\} \subseteq U$ and scalars$a_{1}, \ldots, a_{k}$ with$\sum a_{i} v_{i} = 0$. Each$v_{i} \in C_{i}$ for some$C_{i} \in \mathcal{C}$. Since$\mathcal{C}$ is a chain, these finitely many sets are totally ordered, so one of them — say$C_{j}$ — contains all others. Then$\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\} \subseteq C_{j}$, which is linearly independent, so$a_{1} = \cdots = a_{k} = 0$. Hence$U$ is linearly independent. Step 3: Zorn's Lemma yields a maximal linearly independent set$B \in P$. Step 4:$B$ spans$V$. If some$v \in V$ were not in$\mathrm{span}(B)$, then$B \cup \{v\}$ would be linearly independent (any vanishing combination$\sum a_{i} b_{i} + cv = 0$ would force$c = 0$ — otherwise$v = -c^{-1}\sum a_{i}b_{i} \in \mathrm{span}(B)$ — and then the$b_{i}$-part vanishes by independence of$B$), contradicting maximality of$B$.$\square$

**Maximal ideals of rings.** Every non-zero ring with identity has a maximal ideal.

\begin{theorem}
Let $R$ be a non-zero commutative ring with identity$1 \neq 0$. Then$R$ has a maximal ideal.
\end{theorem}

*Proof.* Step 1: Let $P$ be the set of all proper ideals of$R$ (ideals$I \subsetneq R$, equivalently$1 \notin I$), ordered by inclusion. The zero ideal$\{0\}$ is proper (since$R \neq 0$), so$P$ is non-empty. Step 2: Let$\mathcal{C}$ be a chain in$P$. The union$U = \bigcup_{I \in \mathcal{C}} I$ is an ideal: if$a, b \in U$ then$a \in I_{1}$ and$b \in I_{2}$ for some$I_{1}, I_{2} \in \mathcal{C}$; one contains the other, so$a - b$ lies in the larger ideal, hence in$U$; and for any$r \in R$,$ra \in I_{1} \subseteq U$. Moreover$U$ is proper: since$1 \notin I$ for any$I \in \mathcal{C}$, we have$1 \notin U$. Step 3: Zorn gives a maximal proper ideal$M$. Step 4:$M$ is a maximal ideal by definition: no proper ideal strictly contains$M$.$\square$

This theorem is used constantly. Every non-unit element $r$ of a commutative ring with identity lies in the ideal$(r) = rR$, which is proper (since$1 \notin (r)$ as$r$ is not a unit), hence lies in some maximal ideal. Maximal ideals correspond to fields via the quotient construction:$R/M$ is a field iff$M$ is a maximal ideal.

**Algebraic closures of fields.** Every field $F$ has an algebraic closure$\overline{F}$.

\begin{theorem}
For any field $F$, there exists an algebraically closed field$\overline{F}$ that is algebraic over$F$.
\end{theorem}

*Proof sketch.* One first constructs a "large enough" overfield $\Omega$ of$F$ containing a root of every polynomial in$F[x]$ (using a clever transfinite construction due to Artin). Step 1: Let$P$ be the set of all subfields of$\Omega$ that are algebraic over$F$, ordered by inclusion. Step 2: The union of a chain of algebraic extensions of$F$ within$\Omega$ is again an algebraic extension (any element in the union satisfies a polynomial over$F$). Step 3: Zorn gives a maximal algebraic extension$\overline{F}$ of$F$ inside$\Omega$. Step 4: Maximality implies$\overline{F}$ is algebraically closed: any polynomial in$\overline{F}[x]$ has a root algebraic over$\overline{F}$, hence algebraic over$F$ (algebraic over algebraic is algebraic), so the root lies in$\overline{F}$.$\square$

**Maximal proper subgroups.** Every finitely generated group has a maximal proper subgroup.

For a group $G$ generated by a finite set$S$, one constructs a Zorn argument on proper subgroups ordered by inclusion. The chain condition holds because the union of a chain of proper subgroups is a proper subgroup (it does not contain some specific element of$G$ that generates$G$ along with$S$). Maximality then gives a subgroup that cannot be extended without equaling$G$.

\begin{remark}
The four applications above illustrate a general principle: in algebra, existence problems involving "maximal" or "complete" objects (basis, maximal ideal, algebraic closure, maximal subgroup) typically reduce to Zorn's Lemma via the poset of "partial witnesses." The common structure — partial solutions ordered by extension — and the common proof technique — unions of chains are upper bounds — make Zorn's Lemma one of the most widely applied results in the entire subject.
\end{remark}

**Example 1.** Show that the ring $\mathbb{Z}$ has infinitely many maximal ideals, and identify them.

By the theorem, every non-zero proper ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ is contained in a maximal ideal. The ideals of$\mathbb{Z}$ are exactly$(n) = n\mathbb{Z}$ for$n \geq 0$. The maximal ideals are the ideals$(p) = p\mathbb{Z}$ for each prime$p$: the quotient$\mathbb{Z}/(p) \cong \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ is a field iff$p$ is prime. Since there are infinitely many primes,$\mathbb{Z}$ has infinitely many maximal ideals.

**Example 2.** Use Zorn's Lemma to show that the polynomial ring $F[x]$ over a field$F$ has a maximal ideal containing$x^{2} + 1$.

The ideal $(x^{2}+1) \subsetneq F[x]$ is proper iff$x^{2}+1$ is not a unit, which holds since$\deg(x^{2}+1) > 0$. By the maximal ideal theorem,$(x^{2}+1)$ is contained in some maximal ideal$M$. Since$F[x]$ is a principal ideal domain, every ideal is principal:$M = (p(x))$ for some irreducible polynomial$p(x)$. Since$M \supseteq (x^{2}+1)$, we have$p(x) \mid x^{2}+1$. If$F = \mathbb{R}$, then$x^{2}+1$ is irreducible, so$M = (x^{2}+1)$ and$F[x]/M \cong \mathbb{C}$.

**Example 3.** Explain why the vector space $\mathbb{R}$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ (the reals as a$\mathbb{Q}$-vector space) has a basis, and why no explicit basis can be written down.

By the theorem, $\mathbb{R}$ has a Hamel basis over$\mathbb{Q}$: a set$B \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ of reals that is linearly independent over$\mathbb{Q}$ and spans$\mathbb{R}$ as a$\mathbb{Q}$-vector space. Such a basis must be uncountable (since$\mathbb{R}$ is uncountable but$\mathbb{Q}$ and any countable basis would generate only countably many linear combinations). No explicit description of$B$ is possible in ZF: the existence of$B$ is purely existential, established via Zorn's Lemma (equivalently AC), and it is consistent with ZF +$\neg$AC that no Hamel basis of$\mathbb{R}$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ exists.

**Example 4.** Show that if $R$ is a commutative ring with identity and$S \subseteq R$ is a multiplicatively closed subset with$0 \notin S$, then there exists a prime ideal$P$ of$R$ with$P \cap S = \emptyset$.

Let $P$ be the set of ideals$I$ of$R$ with$I \cap S = \emptyset$, ordered by inclusion. The zero ideal is in$P$ (since$0 \notin S$). Given a chain$\mathcal{C}$ in$P$, the union$U = \bigcup \mathcal{C}$ is an ideal with$U \cap S = \emptyset$ (since$s \in U \cap S$ would imply$s$ lies in some$I \in \mathcal{C}$, giving$s \in I \cap S = \emptyset$). By Zorn, there is a maximal element$P \in P$. One then shows$P$ is prime: if$ab \in P$ and$a \notin P$, then the ideal$(P, a)$ strictly contains$P$, so by maximality$(P, a) \cap S \neq \emptyset$: some$s = p + ra \in S$ with$p \in P$,$r \in R$. Similarly some$t = q + ub \in S$ with$q \in P$. Then$st = (p+ra)(q+ub) = pq + pub + raq + raub \in P + raub$, and since$st \in S$ and$S$ is multiplicatively closed,$raub \notin P$, giving$b \in P$ after careful tracking.
