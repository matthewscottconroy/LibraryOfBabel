# 3.3 The Axiom of Choice and Its Equivalents

Many important theorems in algebra assert the existence of something — a basis, a maximal ideal, an algebraic closure — without providing an explicit construction. The proofs of such theorems typically invoke the Axiom of Choice, either directly or through one of its equivalent forms. Understanding this axiom, what it says, why it is needed, and what it implies, is indispensable preparation for the later chapters of this book.

The *Axiom of Choice* (AC) states: for any collection $\{A_i\}_{i \in I}$ of non-empty sets indexed by a set $I$, there exists a *choice function* $f: I \to \bigcup_{i \in I} A_i$ such that $f(i) \in A_i$ for every $i$. In other words, one can simultaneously pick one element from each set in the collection, even if the collection is infinite and no explicit rule for the selections is available. For finite collections, AC follows from ordinary logic: make the choices one by one. For infinite collections, no such sequential procedure is available, and the axiom is a genuine assertion of existence. The Axiom of Choice is independent of the remaining axioms of ZF set theory (Zermelo–Fraenkel without Choice), as shown by Gödel (consistency, 1938) and Cohen (independence, 1963).

Zorn's Lemma is the form of the Axiom of Choice most frequently applied in algebra. It states: if $P$ is a non-empty partially ordered set in which every chain (totally ordered subset) has an upper bound in $P$, then $P$ has a maximal element. A maximal element is one that has no strictly larger element above it — not necessarily the largest element, since elements may be incomparable. To prove that every vector space $V$ has a basis, one applies Zorn's Lemma to the partially ordered set of all linearly independent subsets of $V$, ordered by inclusion. Every chain of linearly independent sets has an upper bound (their union), so Zorn yields a maximal linearly independent set — and a maximal linearly independent set is exactly a basis. The same pattern — take the poset of all objects satisfying some property, order by inclusion, show every chain has an upper bound, conclude a maximal element exists — applies verbatim to prove that every non-trivial commutative ring has a maximal ideal, and that every field has an algebraic closure.

The *Well-Ordering Theorem* asserts that every set can be well-ordered: given any set $A$, there exists a total order $\leq$ on $A$ in which every non-empty subset of $A$ has a least element. This is equivalent to the Axiom of Choice (ZF proves AC $\Leftrightarrow$ Zorn $\Leftrightarrow$ Well-Ordering), and its proof from Zorn's Lemma is a satisfying exercise in applying the lemma to the poset of partial well-orderings of $A$. The Well-Ordering Theorem implies that cardinals can be compared: for any two sets $A$ and $B$, either $|A| \leq |B|$ or $|B| \leq |A|$. Without Choice, cardinalities need not be linearly ordered. The section thus closes the chapter by establishing the logical framework — the Axiom of Choice in its three equivalent guises — that will operate silently but powerfully in the background whenever algebra invokes a non-constructive existence argument.
