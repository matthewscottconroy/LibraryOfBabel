# Chapter 3 — Cardinality and the Axiom of Choice

The concept of size seems simple enough for finite collections: the set $\{a, b, c\}$ has three elements, and we verify this by counting. But what does it mean for two infinite sets to have the same size? Cantor's answer, developed in the 1870s and 1880s, is both elegant and deeply surprising: two sets have the same cardinality if and only if there exists a bijection between them. This definition agrees with ordinary counting for finite sets, but when applied to infinite sets it produces a theory that overturns almost every naive intuition. The integers, the rationals, and the even integers all turn out to have the same cardinality — yet the real numbers form a strictly larger infinity. There are not just two infinite sizes but infinitely many, arranged in a strict hierarchy. Chapter 3 develops this theory rigorously and then confronts the powerful and controversial axiom needed to navigate the hierarchy in full generality.

The chapter begins by making the bijection criterion precise. Two sets $A$ and $B$ are said to be equipotent (written $|A| = |B|$) if there exists a bijection $f: A \to B$. We say $|A| \leq |B|$ if there exists an injection from $A$ into $B$. Finite sets behave as expected: $|A| \leq |B|$ and both are finite implies one can be embedded in the other by a simple counting argument. For infinite sets, the situation is more subtle. Dedekind's characterization — that a set is infinite if and only if it admits a bijection with a proper subset of itself — captures something essential: the integers are in bijection with the even integers via $n \mapsto 2n$, yet the even integers are strictly contained in the integers. Far from being paradoxical, this is the defining feature of infinite sets. The Schröder–Bernstein theorem then provides a crucial tool: if $|A| \leq |B|$ and $|B| \leq |A|$, then $|A| = |B|$. This result, which requires no appeal to the Axiom of Choice, allows one to establish equipotence by constructing two injections, one in each direction, rather than a single explicit bijection — a technique that is often far easier in practice.

Cantor's diagonal argument is one of the most celebrated proofs in all of mathematics. It shows that $|\mathbb{N}| < |\mathbb{R}|$: the real numbers cannot be enumerated by the natural numbers. The argument is self-undermining for any proposed enumeration — one constructs, from any list of real numbers in $[0,1]$, a new real number that differs from the $n$-th entry in the $n$-th decimal place, and therefore cannot appear anywhere on the list. The same diagonal technique shows that the power set $\mathcal{P}(A)$ always has strictly greater cardinality than $A$ itself, for any set $A$ whatsoever. This is Cantor's theorem: $|A| < |\mathcal{P}(A)|$. Iterating this construction produces an endless tower $|\mathbb{N}| < |\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})| < |\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N}))| < \cdots$, demonstrating that the infinite hierarchy is inexhaustible. The status of the Continuum Hypothesis — whether there is a set whose cardinality lies strictly between $|\mathbb{N}|$ and $|\mathbb{R}|$ — was shown by Gödel (1938) and Cohen (1963) to be independent of the standard axioms of set theory: it is neither provable nor disprovable from ZF(C).

The Axiom of Choice asserts that for any collection of non-empty sets, there exists a function that selects one element from each set simultaneously — even if the collection is infinite and no explicit selection rule is available. While this sounds like common sense, its consequences are far-reaching and sometimes alarming. The Banach–Tarski paradox shows that, assuming Choice, a solid ball can be decomposed into finitely many pieces and reassembled into two balls of equal size. Within algebra, however, the Axiom of Choice is indispensable. Zorn's Lemma — equivalent to the Axiom of Choice in ZF — is the tool used to prove that every vector space has a basis, every non-trivial ring has a maximal ideal, and every field has an algebraic closure. The Well-Ordering Theorem, also equivalent to Choice, asserts that every set can be given a well-order, a total order in which every non-empty subset has a least element. These three equivalences — Choice, Zorn's Lemma, and the Well-Ordering Theorem — form a tight knot at the heart of modern algebra, and understanding them is essential preparation for the non-constructive existence arguments that appear repeatedly in the chapters ahead.
