# 4.1.2 Consequences of the Axioms: Zero Divisors, Uniqueness

The field axioms are carefully chosen so that many properties we might naively want to assume are actually forced by the axioms rather than stated independently. Working out these consequences is not just a bookkeeping exercise — it trains the habit of asking which properties follow from which assumptions, a skill essential for recognizing when a weaker hypothesis suffices. The theorems proved in this section will feel obvious once you see them, but their content is genuine: they are not part of the definition, yet they hold in every field.

The most fundamental derived facts concern zero and negation. We can prove from the axioms alone that $0 \cdot a = 0$ for every$a \in F$: since$0 = 0 + 0$ (by axiom A3 applied to$0$), we have
$$0 \cdot a = (0 + 0) \cdot a = 0 \cdot a + 0 \cdot a,$$
where the second equality uses distributivity. Now the additive group structure of $(F, +)$ guarantees we can subtract$0 \cdot a$ from both sides, giving$0 = 0 \cdot a$. Similarly,$(-1) \cdot a = -a$: the element$(-1) \cdot a + a = (-1 + 1) \cdot a = 0 \cdot a = 0$, so$(-1) \cdot a$ is the additive inverse of$a$, which by uniqueness of inverses (proved below) is exactly$-a$. These are theorems, not axioms, and a student who can produce these proofs has understood what it means to work axiomatically.

The uniqueness of identities and inverses is another class of consequences. The additive identity is unique: if $0$ and$0'$ both satisfy$a + 0 = a$ and$a + 0' = a$ for all$a$, then setting$a = 0'$ gives$0' + 0 = 0'$ and setting$a = 0$ gives$0 + 0' = 0$; using commutativity,$0 = 0 + 0' = 0' + 0 = 0'$. The same argument gives uniqueness of the multiplicative identity$1$. Additive inverses are unique: if$a + b = 0$ and$a + b' = 0$, then$b = b + 0 = b + (a + b') = (b + a) + b' = 0 + b' = b'$. So there is never any ambiguity in writing$-a$ or$a^{-1}$.

A deeper consequence is the **absence of zero divisors**: if $a \cdot b = 0$ in a field, then$a = 0$ or$b = 0$. The proof is short but significant: if$a \neq 0$, then$a$ has a multiplicative inverse$a^{-1}$, and multiplying both sides of$a \cdot b = 0$ by$a^{-1}$ gives$b = a^{-1}(ab) = a^{-1} \cdot 0 = 0$. This property is what makes the **cancellation law** work: if$a \neq 0$ and$a \cdot b = a \cdot c$, then$a \cdot (b - c) = 0$, so$b - c = 0$ and$b = c$. The absence of zero divisors is precisely the property that distinguishes fields from more general rings, and it is what makes polynomial equations over a field behave as expected: a nonzero polynomial of degree$n$ has at most$n$ roots, a fact that would fail in rings with zero divisors.

To see concretely why zero divisors are problematic, consider $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, which is a ring but not a field. Here$2 \cdot 3 = 6 \equiv 0$, yet neither$2$ nor$3$ is zero. The cancellation law fails:$2 \cdot 1 = 2 \cdot 4 = 2 \pmod{6}$, yet$1 \neq 4$. If we tried to build linear algebra over$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, many standard theorems would collapse. Fields exist precisely to prevent this pathology.

There are also purely algebraic identities that follow from the axioms. For instance, $-(a + b) = -a + (-b)$, since$(a + b) + (-a + (-b)) = (a + (-a)) + (b + (-b)) = 0 + 0 = 0$, confirming that$-a + (-b)$ is the additive inverse of$a + b$. Similarly,$(-a)(-b) = ab$: we compute$(-a)(-b) + (-ab) = (-a)(-b) + (-a)(b) = (-a)(-b + b) = (-a)(0) = 0$, so$(-a)(-b) = ab$ by uniqueness of additive inverses. These identities are used constantly in calculation and should be second nature.

**Example 1.** In any field $F$, prove that if$a^2 = 0$ then$a = 0$. Solution:$a^2 = a \cdot a = 0$. By the absence of zero divisors (with both factors equal to$a$), either$a = 0$ or$a = 0$, so$a = 0$. This shows there are no nonzero nilpotent elements in a field.

**Example 2.** In $\mathbb{F}_{5} = \{0, 1, 2, 3, 4\}$ with arithmetic modulo$5$, find the multiplicative inverse of$3$. We need$3x \equiv 1 \pmod{5}$. Testing:$3 \cdot 2 = 6 \equiv 1 \pmod{5}$. So$3^{-1} = 2$ in$\mathbb{F}_{5}$. One can verify all four nonzero elements are invertible:$1^{-1} = 1$,$2^{-1} = 3$,$3^{-1} = 2$,$4^{-1} = 4$.

**Example 3.** Prove the cancellation law from the zero-divisor property. Suppose $ac = bc$ with$c \neq 0$. Then$(a - b)c = ac - bc = 0$. Since$c \neq 0$ and the field has no zero divisors, we conclude$a - b = 0$, i.e.,$a = b$. This is precisely the argument: no zero divisors implies cancellation.

**Example 4.** Show that in $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$, cancellation fails. Note$2 \cdot 1 = 2$ and$2 \cdot 3 = 6 \equiv 2 \pmod{4}$, so$2 \cdot 1 = 2 \cdot 3$, but$1 \neq 3$ in$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$. The element$2$ is a zero divisor here:$2 \cdot 2 = 4 \equiv 0$, confirming that$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ is not a field ($4 = 2^2$ is not prime).

**Example 5.** Use the axioms to prove that $a(b - c) = ab - ac$ for any$a, b, c$ in a field. We have$a(b - c) = a(b + (-c)) = ab + a(-c)$ by distributivity. Now$a(-c) + ac = a(-c + c) = a \cdot 0 = 0$, so$a(-c) = -ac$. Therefore$a(b-c) = ab + (-ac) = ab - ac$. This subtraction distributivity law follows from the addition distributivity axiom together with the consequence$a(-c) = -(ac)$.
