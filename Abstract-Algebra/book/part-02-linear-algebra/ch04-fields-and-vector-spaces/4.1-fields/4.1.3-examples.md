# 4.1.3 Examples: $\mathbb{Q}$,$\mathbb{R}$,$\mathbb{C}$,$\mathbb{F}_{p}$,$\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}$

No definition lives in isolation; the examples show what the definition is actually capturing and reveal the range of structures it covers. The fields we encounter most often divide naturally into two families: the classical fields built from the real and complex numbers, and the finite fields that arise from modular arithmetic. Together they illustrate both the power and the limits of the field axioms, and each family suggests different directions of development.

The **classical fields** are $\mathbb{Q}$,$\mathbb{R}$, and$\mathbb{C}$, each obtained from the previous by a natural extension process. The rationals$\mathbb{Q}$ are the smallest subfield of$\mathbb{R}$: they consist precisely of those real numbers expressible as a ratio of integers, and every axiom is easily verified. The additive and multiplicative identities are$0$ and$1$; the additive inverse of$p/q$ is$-p/q$; and the multiplicative inverse of$p/q \neq 0$ is$q/p$. The rationals are the "fraction field" of$\mathbb{Z}$, a construction that generalizes: any integral domain embeds into a fraction field, and$\mathbb{Q} = \mathrm{Frac}(\mathbb{Z})$.

The reals $\mathbb{R}$ extend$\mathbb{Q}$ by completing it — adding all limits of Cauchy sequences — and the result is the field underlying classical analysis and Euclidean geometry. The real numbers satisfy the completeness axiom (every bounded nonempty set has a supremum), which goes beyond the field axioms but is essential for analysis. As a field,$\mathbb{R}$ is characterized up to isomorphism among ordered fields by being the unique complete ordered field. The complex numbers$\mathbb{C}$ extend$\mathbb{R}$ by adjoining a square root of$-1$; formally,$\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2 + 1)$. The resulting field is **algebraically closed**: every nonconstant polynomial over$\mathbb{C}$ has a root in$\mathbb{C}$, a deep fact known as the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra. For much of linear algebra the field is$\mathbb{R}$ or$\mathbb{C}$, but many theorems hold over any field.

The **finite fields** $\mathbb{F}_{p} = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ arise for each prime$p$. Since$p$ is prime, every nonzero element of$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ has a multiplicative inverse mod$p$ — this is a consequence of Bézout's theorem, which gives integers$s, t$ with$sp + ta = 1$ whenever$\gcd(p, a) = 1$, so$ta \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$. The element$\overline{a} \in \mathbb{F}_{p}$ then has inverse$\overline{t}$. For example,$\mathbb{F}_{2} = \{0, 1\}$ with$1 + 1 = 0$,$\mathbb{F}_{3} = \{0, 1, 2\}$ with$2^{-1} = 2$ (since$2 \cdot 2 = 4 \equiv 1$), and$\mathbb{F}_{5} = \{0, 1, 2, 3, 4\}$ with the usual arithmetic modulo$5$. Notice that if$n$ is not prime then$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is not a field: in$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, the element$\overline{2}$ has no inverse because$\gcd(2, 6) = 2 \neq 1$, and indeed$\overline{2} \cdot \overline{3} = \overline{0}$ is a zero-divisor relation.

Beyond the prime fields there exist **finite fields** $\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}$ of order$p^{n}$ for every prime$p$ and positive integer$n$. These cannot be constructed as$\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}$ — that quotient ring is not a field for$n \geq 2$ — but rather as degree-$n$ extensions of$\mathbb{F}_{p}$, obtained by adjoining a root of an irreducible polynomial of degree$n$ over$\mathbb{F}_{p}$. Concretely, if$f(x) \in \mathbb{F}_{p}[x]$ is irreducible of degree$n$, then$\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]/(f(x))$ is a field with$p^{n}$ elements. The remarkable theorem, proved in Part VI, is that a field of order$p^{n}$ exists for every prime power$p^{n}$, is unique up to isomorphism, and these are all the finite fields. The finite fields are indispensable in coding theory, cryptography, and algebraic geometry over finite grounds.

**Example 1.** Compute the multiplicative group of $\mathbb{F}_{7}^{\times} = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$. Since$7$ is prime, every nonzero element is invertible modulo$7$. We find:$1^{-1} = 1$,$2^{-1} = 4$ (since$2 \cdot 4 = 8 \equiv 1$),$3^{-1} = 5$ (since$3 \cdot 5 = 15 \equiv 1$),$4^{-1} = 2$,$5^{-1} = 3$,$6^{-1} = 6$ (since$6 \cdot 6 = 36 \equiv 1$). The element$3$ generates$\mathbb{F}_{7}^{\times}$: its powers are$3, 9 \equiv 2, 27 \equiv 6, 81 \equiv 4, 243 \equiv 5, 729 \equiv 1$, cycling through all nonzero elements. In general,$\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}$ is cyclic of order$p-1$.

**Example 2.** Construct $\mathbb{F}_{4}$, the field with four elements. We need a degree-$2$ irreducible polynomial over$\mathbb{F}_{2}$. The polynomial$f(x) = x^2 + x + 1$ has no roots in$\mathbb{F}_{2}$ (check:$f(0) = 1 \neq 0$ and$f(1) = 1 \neq 0$), so it is irreducible over$\mathbb{F}_{2}$. Set$\mathbb{F}_{4} = \mathbb{F}_{2}[x]/(x^2 + x + 1) = \{0, 1, \alpha, \alpha+1\}$ where$\alpha$ denotes the image of$x$ and satisfies$\alpha^2 = \alpha + 1$ (since$\alpha^2 + \alpha + 1 = 0$ in this quotient). The addition and multiplication tables can be computed: for instance,$\alpha \cdot (\alpha + 1) = \alpha^2 + \alpha = (\alpha+1) + \alpha = 1$, so$\alpha^{-1} = \alpha + 1$.

**Example 3.** Why is $\mathbb{Z}/9\mathbb{Z}$ not a field? We have$9 = 3^2$. The element$\overline{3} \in \mathbb{Z}/9\mathbb{Z}$ satisfies$3 \cdot 3 = 9 \equiv 0$, so$\overline{3}$ is a zero divisor and cannot have a multiplicative inverse. Alternatively,$\gcd(3, 9) = 3 \neq 1$, so$3$ is not invertible modulo$9$. The quotient ring$\mathbb{Z}/9\mathbb{Z}$ is a local ring (its unique maximal ideal is generated by$\overline{3}$) but not a field.

**Example 4.** The field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}) = \{a + b\sqrt{2} \mid a, b \in \mathbb{Q}\}$ is a degree-$2$ extension of$\mathbb{Q}$. As a$\mathbb{Q}$-vector space it has dimension$2$, with basis$\{1, \sqrt{2}\}$. The minimal polynomial of$\sqrt{2}$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ is$x^2 - 2$, which is irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$ by the rational root test. Thus$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}) \cong \mathbb{Q}[x]/(x^2 - 2)$ as fields.

**Example 5.** Consider the rational function field $F = \mathbb{R}(t)$, consisting of all quotients$f(t)/g(t)$ of real polynomials with$g(t) \neq 0$, where we identify$f/g = fh/gh$. This is a field under the natural addition and multiplication of rational functions. It is infinite-dimensional over$\mathbb{R}$ (the set$\{1, t, t^2, \ldots\}$ is linearly independent over$\mathbb{R}$) and serves as the function field of the affine line over$\mathbb{R}$. Fields of rational functions over$\mathbb{F}_{p}$ play a central role in number theory and algebraic geometry.
