# 4.2.1 The Vector Space Axioms

The notion of a vector space abstracts the essential features of arrows in the plane and in three-dimensional space: you can add two vectors and you can scale a vector by a number. But stating this abstraction precisely, so that it covers not just geometric arrows but also polynomials, matrices, and function spaces, requires formulating the right axioms over an arbitrary field. The goal is to capture exactly the algebraic behavior that makes "linearity" a coherent concept, no more and no less.

The intuition to hold onto is this: in $\mathbb{R}^n$, we can form linear combinations$\lambda_1 v_1 + \lambda_2 v_2 + \cdots + \lambda_k v_k$ of vectors, and the entire theory of linear algebra — span, independence, bases, linear maps — rests on this operation. The axioms for a vector space say precisely that these linear combinations are well-defined, that they respect the arithmetic of the scalar field, and that the additive structure of the vectors is a group. Everything else in the theory follows.

\begin{definition}
A \textbf{vector space} over a field $F$ is an abelian group$(V, +)$ — whose elements we call \textbf{vectors} — together with a \textbf{scalar multiplication}$F \times V \to V$, written$(\lambda, v) \mapsto \lambda v$, satisfying the following four axioms for all$\lambda, \mu \in F$ and all$u, v \in V$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(VS1)}] $\lambda(u + v) = \lambda u + \lambda v$ (scalar multiplication distributes over vector addition),
\item[\textup{(VS2)}] $(\lambda + \mu)v = \lambda v + \mu v$ (addition in$F$ distributes over scalar multiplication),
\item[\textup{(VS3)}] $(\lambda\mu)v = \lambda(\mu v)$ (scalar multiplication is associative),
\item[\textup{(VS4)}] $1 \cdot v = v$ (the multiplicative identity in$F$ acts trivially).
\end{enumerate}
Together with the four abelian group axioms for $(V, +)$, this gives eight axioms in total.
\end{definition}

The reason for this particular list of axioms is that they capture exactly the algebraic behavior we need to do linear algebra — writing vectors as linear combinations, reasoning about independence, defining linear maps — without assuming anything about the geometric nature of the vectors themselves. Axiom (VS1) ensures that scaling distributes across a sum, which is essential when we expand expressions like $\lambda(u+v)$. Axiom (VS2) ensures that two different scales add up as expected. Axiom (VS3) is the compatibility between scalar multiplication and the multiplication in the field: scaling by$\lambda$ and then by$\mu$ is the same as scaling by$\lambda\mu$ in one step. Axiom (VS4) normalizes the action so that the unit scalar does nothing.

Notice that the field $F$ plays an asymmetric role: its elements are scalars, not vectors, and they multiply vectors from the left. For this reason we speak of a vector space "over$F$," and the field is part of the data. The same underlying set$V = \mathbb{C}$ can be viewed as a vector space over$\mathbb{C}$ (of dimension$1$), over$\mathbb{R}$ (of dimension$2$), or over$\mathbb{Q}$ (of infinite dimension) — the field matters, and different choices yield structurally different objects.

The axioms are satisfied by geometric arrows in $\mathbb{R}^n$, by$m \times n$ matrices, by polynomials of bounded degree, by continuous functions on an interval, and by the solution space of a homogeneous linear differential equation. All of these look very different, but a theorem proved from the axioms alone applies to every one of them simultaneously. This universality is the great advantage of the axiomatic approach.

**Example 1.** Verify that $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ is a vector space over$\mathbb{R}$ under componentwise operations. Addition:$(x_1, y_1) + (x_2, y_2) = (x_1 + x_2, y_1 + y_2)$. Scalar multiplication:$\lambda(x, y) = (\lambda x, \lambda y)$. Check (VS1):$\lambda((x_1, y_1) + (x_2, y_2)) = \lambda(x_1 + x_2, y_1 + y_2) = (\lambda(x_1 + x_2), \lambda(y_1 + y_2)) = (\lambda x_1 + \lambda x_2, \lambda y_1 + \lambda y_2) = \lambda(x_1, y_1) + \lambda(x_2, y_2)$. The other axioms are similarly straightforward.

**Example 2.** The set $\mathbb{C}$ viewed as a vector space over$\mathbb{R}$. Addition is complex addition; scalar multiplication$\lambda \cdot (a + bi) = \lambda a + \lambda bi$ for$\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$. All eight axioms hold. The real and imaginary parts play the roles of two independent directions, so$\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C} = 2$. Note that multiplication of complex numbers is NOT the scalar multiplication here; scalar multiplication uses only real scalars.

**Example 3.** Let $V$ be the set of all$2 \times 2$ matrices with real entries, under entry-wise addition and entry-wise scalar multiplication by real scalars. This is a vector space over$\mathbb{R}$. The zero vector is the zero matrix. The dimension is$4$: a basis is given by the four elementary matrices$E_{11}, E_{12}, E_{21}, E_{22}$, where$E_{ij}$ has a$1$ in position$(i,j)$ and zeros elsewhere.

**Example 4.** Let $V$ be the solution set of the differential equation$y'' + y = 0$, viewed as a subset of the space of smooth functions$\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$. A general solution is$y = A\cos x + B\sin x$. Is$V$ a vector space over$\mathbb{R}$? The sum of two solutions is again a solution (since the equation is linear and homogeneous), and any scalar multiple of a solution is a solution. The zero function ($A = B = 0$) is in$V$. So$V$ is a vector space over$\mathbb{R}$ of dimension$2$, with basis$\{\cos x, \sin x\}$.

**Example 5.** Attempt to form a vector space on $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ by defining scalar multiplication as$\lambda \cdot (x,y) = (\lambda x, 0)$. Check axiom (VS2):$(\lambda + \mu)(x,y) = ((\lambda+\mu)x, 0)$ but$\lambda(x,y) + \mu(x,y) = (\lambda x, 0) + (\mu x, 0) = ((\lambda+\mu)x, 0)$, which agrees. Check (VS3):$(\lambda\mu)(x,y) = (\lambda\mu x, 0)$ and$\lambda(\mu(x,y)) = \lambda(\mu x, 0) = (\lambda\mu x, 0)$, which agrees. But (VS4) fails:$1 \cdot (x,y) = (x, 0) \neq (x,y)$ when$y \neq 0$. So this is not a valid scalar multiplication, and the resulting structure is not a vector space. The unit axiom is more subtle than it looks.
