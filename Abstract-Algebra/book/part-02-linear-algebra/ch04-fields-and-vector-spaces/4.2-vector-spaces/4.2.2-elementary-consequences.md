# 4.2.2 Elementary Consequences: $0 \cdot v = \mathbf{0}$,$(-1)v = -v$

Having stated the eight axioms for a vector space, we immediately face a question: what exactly do we need to prove versus what we are simply assuming? Several properties that might seem like they should be axioms are in fact theorems — they follow inevitably from the axioms we already have. Working out these consequences carefully is not pedantry; it is the first practice in the axiomatic method, where we see how much can be derived from how little. Moreover, these elementary facts are the workhorses of nearly every calculation in linear algebra.

The most fundamental consequence is that the scalar zero multiplies any vector to the zero vector: $0 \cdot v = \mathbf{0}$ for all$v \in V$. Notice carefully the distinction:$0$ on the left is the zero scalar in$F$, while$\mathbf{0}$ on the right is the zero vector in$V$. The proof uses the distributive axiom (VS2) together with the abelian group structure:
$$0 \cdot v = (0 + 0) \cdot v = 0 \cdot v + 0 \cdot v.$$
Now subtract $0 \cdot v$ from both sides — the abelian group structure of$(V, +)$ guarantees we can do this, since every element has an additive inverse — to obtain$\mathbf{0} = 0 \cdot v$.

A symmetric result holds: any scalar times the zero vector is zero, $\lambda \cdot \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$ for all$\lambda \in F$. The proof uses (VS1):
$$\lambda \cdot \mathbf{0} = \lambda \cdot (\mathbf{0} + \mathbf{0}) = \lambda \cdot \mathbf{0} + \lambda \cdot \mathbf{0},$$
and again subtract $\lambda \cdot \mathbf{0}$ from both sides.

Another basic consequence is that $(-1) \cdot v = -v$: the scalar$-1$ sends every vector to its additive inverse. The proof is:
$$v + (-1) \cdot v = 1 \cdot v + (-1) \cdot v = (1 + (-1)) \cdot v = 0 \cdot v = \mathbf{0}.$$
Since $v + (-1)v = \mathbf{0}$, the element$(-1)v$ is the additive inverse of$v$ in$V$, which by uniqueness of inverses in the abelian group$(V, +)$ is exactly$-v$.

These results have an important consequence for negation: $(-\lambda)v = -(\lambda v) = \lambda(-v)$. Indeed,$(-\lambda)v = ((-1)\lambda)v = (-1)(\lambda v) = -(\lambda v)$ using (VS3) and the result just proved. Similarly,$\lambda(-v) = \lambda((-1)v) = (-1)(\lambda v) = -(\lambda v)$. This shows that "putting a negative sign" on either the scalar or the vector gives the same result, which is exactly what we need when simplifying expressions like$-(\lambda v)$.

We can also show that if $\lambda v = \mathbf{0}$, then either$\lambda = 0$ or$v = \mathbf{0}$. If$\lambda \neq 0$, then$\lambda^{-1}$ exists in$F$, and$v = 1 \cdot v = (\lambda^{-1}\lambda) v = \lambda^{-1}(\lambda v) = \lambda^{-1} \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$. This is the vector-space analogue of the no-zero-divisors property of fields: a field scalar kills a vector only if one of them is already zero.

A student who understands why these results must be proved — rather than assumed — has grasped something important: the axioms say only what you give them credit for, and every additional claim requires justification. This discipline pays off throughout linear algebra, where knowing exactly which hypotheses are needed for a given theorem is what allows us to generalize results to new settings. The zero-vector consequences are also practically useful: they are often the quickest way to show that a particular subset is not a subspace (if the zero vector is absent, the set fails immediately).

**Example 1.** Verify that $0 \cdot v = \mathbf{0}$ in$\mathbb{R}^{3}$. Take$v = (1, -2, 5)$. Then$0 \cdot v = 0 \cdot (1,-2,5) = (0 \cdot 1, 0 \cdot (-2), 0 \cdot 5) = (0, 0, 0) = \mathbf{0}$. This is reassuring, but note the abstract proof works for any vector space, not just$\mathbb{R}^{3}$.

**Example 2.** Verify $(-1) \cdot f = -f$ in the vector space$\mathcal{C}([0,1])$ of continuous functions on$[0,1]$. Here$-f$ means the function$x \mapsto -f(x)$. We have$((-1) \cdot f)(x) = (-1) \cdot f(x) = -f(x)$ for every$x \in [0,1]$, confirming$(-1) \cdot f = -f$ as functions.

**Example 3.** Use the consequences to simplify: in any vector space, compute $3v - 3v$. We have$3v - 3v = 3v + (-1)(3v) = 3v + (-3v) = (3 + (-3))v = 0 \cdot v = \mathbf{0}$. This confirms that additive inverses of vectors behave exactly as scalars tell them to.

**Example 4.** Show that if $\lambda v = \lambda w$ and$\lambda \neq 0$, then$v = w$. We have$\lambda v - \lambda w = \mathbf{0}$, so$\lambda(v - w) = \mathbf{0}$. Since$\lambda \neq 0$, multiply both sides by$\lambda^{-1}$:$v - w = \lambda^{-1}\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$, hence$v = w$. This is the scalar cancellation law for vector spaces.

**Example 5.** Show that the set $W = \{v \in \mathbb{R}^{2} \mid v_1^2 + v_2^2 = 1\}$ (the unit circle) is not a subspace. The zero vector$(0,0)$ does not lie in$W$ since$0^2 + 0^2 = 0 \neq 1$. By the consequence$0 \cdot v = \mathbf{0}$, a subspace must contain$\mathbf{0}$, so$W$ is not a subspace. Alternatively:$W$ is not closed under scalar multiplication, since$(1, 0) \in W$ but$2 \cdot (1, 0) = (2, 0) \notin W$.
