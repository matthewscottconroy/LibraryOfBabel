# 4.2 Vector Spaces

With a field $F$ in hand, the next question is: what are the objects that $F$ acts on? This section answers by defining the vector space. A vector space over $F$ is an abelian group $(V, +)$ together with a scalar multiplication $F \times V \to V$ satisfying four axioms that make the two structures compatible: scalar multiplication distributes over both vector addition and field addition, it is associative with field multiplication, and the multiplicative identity of $F$ acts as the identity for scalar multiplication. The axioms are chosen precisely so that every reasonable manipulation of "scaling and combining" works as expected.

Before studying examples, the section establishes a handful of elementary consequences that will be used throughout the text. The most important is that $0_F \cdot v = \mathbf{0}_V$ for every $v \in V$: the field zero annihilates every vector. This is not an axiom but a theorem, proved by writing $0 \cdot v = (0 + 0) \cdot v = 0 \cdot v + 0 \cdot v$ and canceling. Similarly, $(-1) \cdot v = -v$ is derived, not assumed. These derivations matter because they demonstrate that the axiomatic framework is self-consistent and complete: nothing about scalar arithmetic has to be assumed beyond the eight axioms.

The examples are where the definition earns its generality. The column vectors $F^n$ form the prototype, but the section presents several others: the space $F[x]_{\leq n}$ of polynomials of degree at most $n$, the space of all continuous functions $C([a,b])$, and the space $M_{m \times n}(F)$ of $m \times n$ matrices over $F$. In each case, the section checks that the axioms hold, showing that the definition is not artificially narrow. Crucially, the zero space $\{\mathbf{0}\}$ is also a vector space — the trivial one — and plays the role of a degenerate but essential example. Function spaces receive special attention because they show that vector spaces need not be finite collections of tuples: the set of all functions from a set $S$ to $F$ is a vector space under pointwise operations, and this example is vast.

The section concludes with a remark about what the axioms do and do not say. They say nothing about geometry: there is no notion of length, angle, or distance in a bare vector space. Those structures require additional data (an inner product, introduced much later in Chapter 11). What the axioms do capture is the purely algebraic structure of linear combination — and that is precisely what the next sections will exploit.
