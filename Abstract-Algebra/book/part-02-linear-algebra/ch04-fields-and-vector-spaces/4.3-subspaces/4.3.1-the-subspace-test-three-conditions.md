# 4.3.1 The Subspace Test: Three Conditions

Once we have a vector space $V$, we want to identify subsets of$V$ that are themselves vector spaces using the inherited operations. Such a subset is called a **subspace**. Naively, checking whether a subset$W \subseteq V$ is a subspace would require verifying all eight vector space axioms. But most of those axioms are inherited automatically from$V$: associativity of addition in$W$, for example, holds because it holds for all vectors in$V$, regardless of which ones happen to lie in$W$. The three conditions that cannot be inherited for free are the ones that check whether the operations stay inside$W$, and these three are both necessary and sufficient.

Why can't all axioms be inherited automatically? The issue is closure: the operations $+$ and$\cdot$ are defined on all of$V$, but$W$ might not be closed under them. If$u, v \in W$ but$u + v \notin W$, then addition is not a binary operation on$W$ at all, and the axioms about addition become meaningless. The subspace test identifies exactly the three conditions that ensure$W$ is itself an abelian group under$+$ and that scalar multiplication restricts to$W$.

\begin{definition}
A nonempty subset $W \subseteq V$ is a \textbf{subspace} of$V$ if and only if the following three conditions hold:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(S1)}] $\mathbf{0} \in W$ (the zero vector belongs to$W$),
\item[\textup{(S2)}] if $u, v \in W$ then$u + v \in W$ ($W$ is closed under addition),
\item[\textup{(S3)}] if $v \in W$ and$\lambda \in F$ then$\lambda v \in W$ ($W$ is closed under scalar multiplication).
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Given these three conditions, the remaining axioms hold automatically. Commutativity of addition: $u + v = v + u$ holds in$W$ because it holds in$V$. Associativity:$(u+v)+w = u+(v+w)$ for the same reason. Additive inverses: if$v \in W$, then$(-1)v \in W$ by (S3) (taking$\lambda = -1$), and$(-1)v = -v$ by the elementary consequence of Section 4.2.2, so$-v \in W$. The four scalar multiplication axioms (VS1)–(VS4) hold in$W$ because they hold in$V$. Thus a subset satisfying (S1)–(S3) is automatically a vector space.

The test can be condensed even further. An equivalent single condition is that $W$ is closed under arbitrary linear combinations: for all$\lambda, \mu \in F$ and all$u, v \in W$, we have$\lambda u + \mu v \in W$. This single condition implies all three of (S1)–(S3): take$\lambda = \mu = 0$ to get$\mathbf{0} \in W$; take$\lambda = \mu = 1$ to get closure under addition; take$\mu = 0$ to get closure under scalar multiplication. Conversely, (S2) and (S3) together give$\lambda u + \mu v \in W$ by first scaling then adding. The two formulations are interchangeable.

In practice, the order of checking matters strategically. If $W$ does not contain the zero vector, we can immediately declare it is not a subspace and stop — there is no point checking the other conditions. If the zero vector is present, we move to checking closures. Often the fastest route to showing a set is *not* a subspace is to exhibit a counterexample to one of the three conditions.

**Example 1.** Show that $W = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^{2} \mid 3x - 2y = 0\}$ is a subspace of$\mathbb{R}^{2}$. (S1):$(0, 0)$ satisfies$3(0) - 2(0) = 0$. (S2): if$3x_1 - 2y_1 = 0$ and$3x_2 - 2y_2 = 0$, then$3(x_1 + x_2) - 2(y_1 + y_2) = (3x_1 - 2y_1) + (3x_2 - 2y_2) = 0$. (S3): if$3x - 2y = 0$, then$3(\lambda x) - 2(\lambda y) = \lambda(3x - 2y) = 0$. All conditions hold, so$W$ is a subspace.

**Example 2.** Show that $W = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^{2} \mid x^2 + y^2 \leq 1\}$ (the closed unit disk) is not a subspace. Condition (S3) fails:$(1, 0) \in W$ (since$1^2 + 0^2 = 1 \leq 1$), but$2 \cdot (1, 0) = (2, 0) \notin W$ (since$4 > 1$). The set is "curved" — scalar multiples escape the disk.

**Example 3.** Determine whether $W = \{p \in \mathbb{R}[x]_{\leq 3} \mid p(1) = 0\}$ is a subspace of$\mathbb{R}[x]_{\leq 3}$. (S1): the zero polynomial satisfies$0(1) = 0$. (S2): if$p(1) = 0$ and$q(1) = 0$, then$(p+q)(1) = p(1) + q(1) = 0$. (S3): if$p(1) = 0$ and$\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$, then$(\lambda p)(1) = \lambda p(1) = 0$. All hold. So$W$ is a subspace. In fact$\dim W = 3$ (a basis is$\{x-1, x^2-1, x^3-1\}$).

**Example 4.** Show that $W = \{p \in \mathbb{R}[x]_{\leq 2} \mid p(1) = 2\}$ is not a subspace. The zero polynomial satisfies$0(1) = 0 \neq 2$, so (S1) fails. Alternatively, if$p(1) = 2$ and$q(1) = 2$, then$(p+q)(1) = 4 \neq 2$, so (S2) fails. This$W$ is a coset (translate) of the subspace from Example 3, not a subspace.

**Example 5.** Show that the union $W = W_1 \cup W_2$ of two subspaces need not be a subspace. Take$W_1 = \{(x, 0) \mid x \in \mathbb{R}\}$ and$W_2 = \{(0, y) \mid y \in \mathbb{R}\}$ in$\mathbb{R}^{2}$, both subspaces. The union$W_1 \cup W_2$ consists of the two coordinate axes. Take$u = (1, 0) \in W_1 \subseteq W$ and$v = (0, 1) \in W_2 \subseteq W$. Then$u + v = (1, 1)$ is not on either axis, so$(1,1) \notin W$. Condition (S2) fails. The union of subspaces is a subspace only when one contains the other.
