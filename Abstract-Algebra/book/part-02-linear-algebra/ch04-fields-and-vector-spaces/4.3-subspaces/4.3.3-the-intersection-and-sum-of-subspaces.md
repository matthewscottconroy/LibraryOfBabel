# 4.3.3 The Intersection and Sum of Subspaces

Given two subspaces $U$ and$W$ of a vector space$V$, we naturally want to combine them. Two operations are available: intersection and sum. They behave quite differently, and understanding the difference between them is essential for working with the structure of a vector space. The central lesson is that the naive set-theoretic operations — union and intersection — do not both preserve the subspace property, and the correct replacement for union is the sum.

The **intersection** $U \cap W$ is always a subspace. The proof is a direct application of the subspace test: the zero vector belongs to both$U$ and$W$ (since both are subspaces), so it belongs to$U \cap W$; if$u, v \in U \cap W$, then$u, v \in U$ so$u + v \in U$, and$u, v \in W$ so$u + v \in W$, hence$u + v \in U \cap W$; and the same argument handles scalar multiplication. The intersection is the largest subspace contained in both$U$ and$W$ simultaneously. Intuitively,$U \cap W$ is the "common part" — the vectors that belong to both subspaces.

The **union** $U \cup W$, by contrast, is almost never a subspace. To see why, consider$U = \{(x,0) \mid x \in \mathbb{R}\}$ and$W = \{(0,y) \mid y \in \mathbb{R}\}$ in$\mathbb{R}^{2}$. Both are subspaces (the coordinate axes), but the vector$(1,0) \in U$ and the vector$(0,1) \in W$ are both in$U \cup W$, while their sum$(1,1)$ belongs to neither. Closure under addition fails. In general,$U \cup W$ is a subspace if and only if$U \subseteq W$ or$W \subseteq U$ — that is, when one of the subspaces contains the other. The proof: if neither contains the other, pick$u \in U \setminus W$ and$w \in W \setminus U$; then$u + w$ cannot lie in$U$ (otherwise$w = (u+w) - u \in U$) and cannot lie in$W$ (otherwise$u = (u+w) - w \in W$), so$u + w \notin U \cup W$, violating closure.

The right substitute for the union is the **sum** $U + W = \{u + w \mid u \in U, w \in W\}$. This is always a subspace: the zero vector is$\mathbf{0} + \mathbf{0} \in U + W$; if$u_1 + w_1$ and$u_2 + w_2$ are in$U + W$, their sum$(u_1 + u_2) + (w_1 + w_2) \in U + W$; and$\lambda(u + w) = (\lambda u) + (\lambda w) \in U + W$. The sum$U + W$ is the smallest subspace of$V$ that contains both$U$ and$W$. Any subspace containing both must contain all sums$u + w$, so it contains$U + W$; and$U + W$ does contain both$U$ (take$w = \mathbf{0}$) and$W$ (take$u = \mathbf{0}$).

When $U$ and$W$ are finite-dimensional, a beautiful dimension count holds:
$$\dim(U + W) = \dim U + \dim W - \dim(U \cap W).$$
This is the vector-space analogue of the inclusion-exclusion principle for finite sets: $|A \cup B| = |A| + |B| - |A \cap B|$. The proof starts by choosing a basis for$U \cap W$, extending it to a basis for$U$ and separately to a basis for$W$, then showing the union of all these vectors is a basis for$U + W$. The dimension of$U \cap W$ is subtracted because those vectors were counted twice — once in the basis for$U$ and once in the basis for$W$.

**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{R}^{3}$, let$U = \mathrm{span}\{(1,0,0), (0,1,0)\}$ (the$xy$-plane) and$W = \mathrm{span}\{(0,1,0), (0,0,1)\}$ (the$yz$-plane). Then$U \cap W = \mathrm{span}\{(0,1,0)\}$ (the$y$-axis), since a vector in both planes must have zero$x$-coordinate and zero$z$-coordinate, leaving only$y$ free. And$U + W = \mathbb{R}^{3}$, since the three vectors$(1,0,0)$,$(0,1,0)$,$(0,0,1)$ are all in$U + W$ and span$\mathbb{R}^{3}$. Dimension formula:$\dim(U + W) = 2 + 2 - 1 = 3$. Correct.

**Example 2.** Let $U = \{(x,y,z) \in \mathbb{R}^{3} \mid x + y + z = 0\}$ and$W = \{(x,y,z) \mid x - y = 0\}$. Then$\dim U = 2$ and$\dim W = 2$. The intersection$U \cap W$ consists of vectors satisfying both$x + y + z = 0$ and$x = y$: substituting gives$2x + z = 0$, so the solution is$\{t(-1, -1, 2) \mid t \in \mathbb{R}\}$, a$1$-dimensional space. The dimension formula predicts$\dim(U + W) = 2 + 2 - 1 = 3 = \dim \mathbb{R}^{3}$, so$U + W = \mathbb{R}^{3}$.

**Example 3.** Let $U = \mathrm{span}\{x, x^2\}$ and$W = \mathrm{span}\{1, x\}$ in$\mathbb{R}[x]_{\leq 2}$. Then$\dim U = 2$,$\dim W = 2$, and$U \cap W = \mathrm{span}\{x\}$ (the only polynomial in$\mathrm{span}\{x, x^2\}$ that is also in$\mathrm{span}\{1, x\}$ is a multiple of$x$). So$\dim(U \cap W) = 1$ and$\dim(U + W) = 2 + 2 - 1 = 3 = \dim \mathbb{R}[x]_{\leq 2}$, meaning$U + W = \mathbb{R}[x]_{\leq 2}$.

**Example 4.** Verify that the dimension formula can give $\dim(U + W) < \dim V$. Take$V = \mathbb{R}^{4}$,$U = \mathrm{span}\{e_1, e_2\}$,$W = \mathrm{span}\{e_1, e_2, e_3\}$ (where$e_i$ are standard basis vectors). Then$U \cap W = U$ (since$U \subseteq W$), so$\dim(U \cap W) = 2$. Dimension formula:$\dim(U + W) = 2 + 3 - 2 = 3 < 4$. Indeed$U + W = W$.

**Example 5.** Use the formula to prove: if $U$ and$W$ are subspaces of$V$ with$\dim U + \dim W > \dim V$, then$U \cap W \neq \{\mathbf{0}\}$. Proof:$\dim(U \cap W) = \dim U + \dim W - \dim(U+W) \geq \dim U + \dim W - \dim V > 0$. So$U \cap W$ has positive dimension and contains nonzero vectors. For example, if$V = \mathbb{R}^{3}$ and$U$,$W$ are both$2$-dimensional (planes through the origin), then$\dim U + \dim W = 4 > 3$, so their intersection has dimension at least$1$: any two planes through the origin in$\mathbb{R}^{3}$ share at least a line.
