# 4.3.4 Direct Sums: Internal and External

The sum $U + W$ of two subspaces is the smallest subspace containing both, but it may be "tangled" — the same vector in$U + W$ might be writable as$u + w$ in more than one way. When no such ambiguity occurs, the sum takes a special form called a **direct sum**, and this clean decomposition is one of the most useful structural properties a vector space can have. Direct sums are the tool by which we break a complex space into simpler independent pieces.

\begin{definition}
We say $V$ is the \textbf{internal direct sum} of subspaces$U$ and$W$, written$V = U \oplus W$, when two conditions hold:
\begin{itemize}
\item $V = U + W$ (every vector in$V$ can be expressed as$u + w$ with$u \in U$,$w \in W$), and
\item $U \cap W = \{\mathbf{0}\}$ (the only vector in both subspaces is zero).
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

These two conditions together imply that every vector $v \in V$ has a **unique** decomposition$v = u + w$ with$u \in U$ and$w \in W$. To see why uniqueness follows: suppose$v = u_1 + w_1 = u_2 + w_2$. Then$u_1 - u_2 = w_2 - w_1$. The left side lies in$U$ (since$U$ is a subspace and$u_1, u_2 \in U$) and the right side lies in$W$, so both lie in$U \cap W = \{\mathbf{0}\}$. Hence$u_1 = u_2$ and$w_1 = w_2$. Uniqueness of decomposition is what makes direct sums so powerful: we can analyze$U$ and$W$ independently and then combine the results without any interference.

Conversely, if every $v \in V$ has a unique decomposition$v = u + w$, then$U + W = V$ (by existence) and$U \cap W = \{\mathbf{0}\}$ (if$x \in U \cap W$, then$x = x + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0} + x$, giving two decompositions of$x$, hence$x = \mathbf{0}$ by uniqueness). So the three conditions — decomposition, spanning, and trivial intersection — are all equivalent.

The **external direct sum** $U \oplus W$ is a separate construction: given any two vector spaces$U$ and$W$ (which need not be subspaces of a common space), we form the Cartesian product$U \times W$ with componentwise operations$(u_1, w_1) + (u_2, w_2) = (u_1 + u_2, w_1 + w_2)$ and$\lambda(u, w) = (\lambda u, \lambda w)$. This is again a vector space, called the external direct sum. The two constructions — internal and external — are closely related: the external direct sum$U \oplus W$ contains internal copies$\widetilde{U} = \{(u, \mathbf{0}) \mid u \in U\}$ and$\widetilde{W} = \{(\mathbf{0}, w) \mid w \in W\}$, their intersection is$\{(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{0})\}$, and$\widetilde{U} + \widetilde{W} = U \oplus W$. So the external direct sum is an internal direct sum of these two copies. The two notions are isomorphic: if$V = U \oplus W$ internally, then$V \cong U \oplus W$ externally via$u + w \mapsto (u, w)$.

The language of direct sums extends to more than two summands: $V = V_1 \oplus V_2 \oplus \cdots \oplus V_k$ means every vector in$V$ decomposes uniquely as a sum$v_1 + v_2 + \cdots + v_k$ with$v_i \in V_i$. An equivalent condition is that$V = V_1 + \cdots + V_k$ and each$V_i \cap (V_1 + \cdots + \hat{V}_i + \cdots + V_k) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$. The direct sum of$n$ copies of$F$ (as one-dimensional spaces) is$F^{n}$: the standard basis vectors give internal copies$\mathrm{span}(e_i)$, and$F^{n} = \mathrm{span}(e_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathrm{span}(e_n)$.

**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{R}^{3}$, let$U = \{(x, 0, 0)\}$ (the$x$-axis) and$W = \{(0, y, z)\}$ (the$yz$-plane). Then$U \cap W = \{\mathbf{0}\}$ (a vector in both has$x = 0$ and$y = z = 0$, so it is zero). And$U + W = \mathbb{R}^{3}$ (any$(a,b,c) = (a,0,0) + (0,b,c)$). So$\mathbb{R}^{3} = U \oplus W$. Uniqueness: the decomposition of$(a,b,c)$ as$u + w$ forces$u = (a,0,0)$ and$w = (0,b,c)$.

**Example 2.** In $M_{2 \times 2}(\mathbb{R})$, let$\mathrm{Sym}$ be the space of symmetric matrices and$\mathrm{Skew}$ the space of skew-symmetric matrices. Every matrix decomposes as$A = \frac{A + A^T}{2} + \frac{A - A^T}{2}$, the first term symmetric and the second skew-symmetric. If$A \in \mathrm{Sym} \cap \mathrm{Skew}$, then$A = A^T = -A$, so$2A = 0$, hence$A = 0$. Thus$M_{2 \times 2}(\mathbb{R}) = \mathrm{Sym} \oplus \mathrm{Skew}$. Dimensions:$\dim \mathrm{Sym} = 3$,$\dim \mathrm{Skew} = 1$,$3 + 1 = 4 = \dim M_{2 \times 2}(\mathbb{R})$.

**Example 3.** Show that the decomposition $V = U \oplus W$ is *not* a direct sum when$U \cap W \neq \{\mathbf{0}\}$. Take$U = W = \{(x, y) \mid y = 0\}$ in$\mathbb{R}^{2}$. Then$U + W = U = W \neq \mathbb{R}^{2}$, so the spanning condition fails. Even if we tried to write$(1, 0) = (1,0) + (0, 0) = (0, 0) + (1, 0)$, uniqueness fails trivially. The intersection$U \cap W = U \neq \{\mathbf{0}\}$ signals the failure.

**Example 4.** Construct the external direct sum $\mathbb{R}^{2} \oplus \mathbb{R}^{3}$. This is$\mathbb{R}^{2} \times \mathbb{R}^{3} = \{(x_1, x_2, y_1, y_2, y_3) \mid x_i, y_j \in \mathbb{R}\}$ with componentwise operations. It has dimension$2 + 3 = 5$. The copy of$\mathbb{R}^{2}$ sits inside as$\{(x_1, x_2, 0, 0, 0)\}$ and the copy of$\mathbb{R}^{3}$ as$\{(0, 0, y_1, y_2, y_3)\}$.

**Example 5.** Prove that if $V = U \oplus W$ then$\dim V = \dim U + \dim W$. Let$\{u_1, \ldots, u_r\}$ be a basis for$U$ and$\{w_1, \ldots, w_s\}$ a basis for$W$. The combined set$\{u_1, \ldots, u_r, w_1, \ldots, w_s\}$ spans$V$ (since$V = U + W$). Independence: if$\sum a_i u_i + \sum b_j w_j = \mathbf{0}$, then$\sum a_i u_i = -\sum b_j w_j$, where the left side lies in$U$ and the right in$W$, so both lie in$U \cap W = \{\mathbf{0}\}$; then$\sum a_i u_i = \mathbf{0}$ forces$a_i = 0$ (basis), and$\sum b_j w_j = \mathbf{0}$ forces$b_j = 0$. So the combined set is a basis of size$r + s$.
