# 4.3 Subspaces

A subspace is a subset of a vector space that is itself a vector space — not by imposing new operations, but by inheriting the ones already present. This sounds like it could require verifying all eight vector space axioms, but the section shows that most of them are automatic: any nonempty subset $W \subseteq V$ that is closed under addition and scalar multiplication already satisfies them, because associativity, commutativity, and distributivity are inherited from $V$ for free. The upshot is the three-condition subspace test: $W$ is a subspace if and only if it contains the zero vector, is closed under addition, and is closed under scalar multiplication. In practice these can be compressed further: $W$ is a subspace if and only if it is nonempty and $\alpha u + \beta v \in W$ whenever $u, v \in W$ and $\alpha, \beta \in F$.

The section gives a careful gallery of examples and non-examples. Any line or plane through the origin in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is a subspace; a line or plane not passing through the origin is not, because it fails the zero-vector condition. The space of solutions to a homogeneous linear equation $ax + by + cz = 0$ is a subspace, but the affine solution set of an inhomogeneous equation $ax + by + cz = d$ (with $d \neq 0$) is not. This distinction — between homogeneous and inhomogeneous — will return as a recurring theme throughout the text.

The section then studies how subspaces combine. The intersection $W_1 \cap W_2$ of two subspaces is always a subspace, and more generally so is any intersection of an arbitrary family of subspaces. The union $W_1 \cup W_2$, however, is almost never a subspace: it fails unless one of the two subspaces is contained in the other. The correct notion of "joining" two subspaces is the sum $W_1 + W_2 = \{w_1 + w_2 : w_1 \in W_1, w_2 \in W_2\}$, which is the smallest subspace containing both. When the only vector expressible as a sum from both subspaces is zero, i.e., when $W_1 \cap W_2 = \{\mathbf{0}\}$, the sum is called a direct sum and written $W_1 \oplus W_2$; in this case every element of the sum decomposes uniquely.

The section closes with the modular law for subspaces: if $U \subseteq W$, then $U + (W \cap X) = W \cap (U + X)$ for any subspace $X$. This identity governs the lattice of subspaces — the partially ordered set under inclusion — and is the first hint that subspace structure has rich order-theoretic content. The modular law reappears in the theory of modules and in the proof of the Jordan–Hölder theorem, so recognizing it here, in its simplest linear algebraic form, pays dividends later.
