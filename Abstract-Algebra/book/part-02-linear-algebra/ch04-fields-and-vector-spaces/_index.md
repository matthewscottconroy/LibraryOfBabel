# Chapter 4 — Fields and Vector Spaces

Before anything else can be said about linear algebra, two questions demand answers: what are the scalars, and what are the objects they scale? This chapter answers both by introducing the field and the vector space as axiomatic structures. The move to axiomatics is not mere pedantry. It is an insurance policy: every theorem proved in this generality will apply simultaneously to $\mathbb{R}^n$, to complex function spaces, to spaces of polynomials, to matrices, and to finite spaces over $\mathbb{F}_p$, without requiring a separate argument in each case. The abstraction pays dividends immediately.

A field is a set equipped with two operations — addition and multiplication — that together satisfy a small list of interlocking axioms: both operations are commutative and associative, multiplication distributes over addition, every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse, and there are distinct identity elements for each operation. The familiar examples are $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, and $\mathbb{C}$, but the definition also encompasses the finite fields $\mathbb{F}_p = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ for any prime $p$, and the algebraic number fields that arise throughout number theory. One of the first consequences the chapter draws from the axioms is the cancellation law and the uniqueness of identities and inverses — facts that feel obvious in $\mathbb{R}$ but whose proofs make clear exactly which axioms are doing the work. The impossibility of zero divisors in a field is another early consequence: if $ab = 0$ in a field, then $a = 0$ or $b = 0$, a property that distinguishes fields from more general rings.

A vector space over a field $F$ is an abelian group $V$ of "vectors" together with a scalar multiplication $F \times V \to V$ that is compatible with the field structure via four further axioms. The definition is abstract enough to encompass column vectors, polynomials, continuous functions, and infinite sequences, but concrete enough to derive from it the elementary arithmetic that makes linear algebra tractable. The chapter establishes the elementary consequences: the zero vector is unique, $0 \cdot v = \mathbf{0}$ for every $v$, and $(-1) \cdot v = -v$. These derivations from first principles are brief, but they calibrate the reader's understanding of how axiom-driven proofs work before the chapter turns to more structural results.

The most important structural idea introduced here is the subspace: a nonempty subset $W \subseteq V$ that is itself a vector space under the inherited operations. The three-condition subspace test — closure under addition, closure under scalar multiplication, and containment of the zero vector — condenses the verification of all vector space axioms into a manageable check. The chapter then investigates what happens when subspaces interact: their intersection is always a subspace, but their union almost never is. The sum $W_1 + W_2$ is introduced as the correct notion of "join" for subspaces, and the direct sum $V = W_1 \oplus W_2$ as the special case where every element decomposes uniquely. These constructions are not merely organizational — the modular law governing subspace lattices foreshadows the lattice-theoretic language that will appear throughout ring theory and representation theory.

This chapter sets the stage for everything that follows in Part 2. Chapter 5 will impose the further structure of a basis, showing that every finite-dimensional vector space looks, abstractly, like $F^n$. Chapters 6 and 7 will study the maps between vector spaces and their coordinate representations. But all of that depends on the bedrock laid here: the precise definitions, the first consequences, and the recognition that the scalars' arithmetic is not incidental but definitional.
