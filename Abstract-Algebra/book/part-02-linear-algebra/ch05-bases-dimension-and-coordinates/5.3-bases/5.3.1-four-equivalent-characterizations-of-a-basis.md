# 5.3.1 Four Equivalent Characterizations of a Basis

We have been building toward a central definition. A **basis** of a vector space $V$ is an ordered set (or list) of vectors$\mathcal{B}$ that is both linearly independent and spans$V$. This two-part condition is the official definition, but it turns out to be equivalent to three other conditions, each of which captures a different facet of what a basis does. Recognizing the right characterization for a given situation is a practical skill that will be used throughout the remainder of the text.

\begin{theorem}[Four Equivalent Characterizations]
Let $V$ be a vector space over$F$ and let$\mathcal{B} \subseteq V$ be a nonempty subset. The following four conditions are equivalent:
\par
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] $\mathcal{B}$ is linearly independent and$\mathrm{span}(\mathcal{B}) = V$.
\item[(b)] $\mathcal{B}$ is a maximal linearly independent set: it is independent, and for every$v \in V \setminus \mathcal{B}$, the set$\mathcal{B} \cup \{v\}$ is dependent.
\item[(c)] $\mathcal{B}$ is a minimal spanning set: it spans$V$, and for every$b \in \mathcal{B}$, the set$\mathcal{B} \setminus \{b\}$ does not span$V$.
\item[(d)] Every vector in $V$ has a unique representation as a finite linear combination of elements of$\mathcal{B}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

Condition (a) is the definition. Let us trace the logical connections among all four.

**(a) $\Rightarrow$ (d).** Since$\mathcal{B}$ spans$V$, every vector has at least one representation. Since$\mathcal{B}$ is independent, the representation is unique: if$v = \sum_i a_i b_i = \sum_i c_i b_i$, then$\sum_i (a_i - c_i) b_i = 0$, and independence forces$a_i = c_i$ for all$i$.

**(d) $\Rightarrow$ (a).** Existence of representations gives spanning; uniqueness gives independence (the only representation of$0$ is the trivial one, else uniqueness would fail for other vectors).

**(a) $\Rightarrow$ (b).** We have independence. If$v \notin \mathrm{span}(\mathcal{B})$... but wait: since$\mathcal{B}$ spans$V$, there is no such$v$. Every$v \in V$ is in$\mathrm{span}(\mathcal{B})$, so$\mathcal{B} \cup \{v\}$ is dependent for any$v \notin \mathcal{B}$.

**(b) $\Rightarrow$ (a).**$\mathcal{B}$ is independent by assumption. If$\mathrm{span}(\mathcal{B}) \neq V$, pick$v \in V \setminus \mathrm{span}(\mathcal{B})$; then by the proposition of Section 5.2.2,$\mathcal{B} \cup \{v\}$ is independent, contradicting maximality.

**(a) $\Rightarrow$ (c).**$\mathcal{B}$ spans$V$. If some$b \in \mathcal{B}$ could be removed and the rest still spanned$V$, then$b$ would be in the span of the remaining elements, making the original list dependent — contradiction.

**(c) $\Rightarrow$ (a).**$\mathcal{B}$ spans$V$. If$\mathcal{B}$ were dependent, the Dependence Lemma (Section 5.2.2) would give some$b \in \mathcal{B}$ in the span of the others, so$\mathcal{B} \setminus \{b\}$ would still span$V$, contradicting minimality.

\begin{remark}
Characterization (d) is the most powerful computationally, because it says that a basis gives a \emph{coordinate system} with no ambiguity. Once $\mathcal{B}$ is fixed, the abstract vector$v$ is completely encoded by the tuple of scalars$(a_1, \ldots, a_n)$ in its unique expansion, and linear algebra becomes computable arithmetic.
\end{remark}

**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{R}^3$, verify that$\mathcal{B} = \{(1, 0, 0), (0, 1, 0), (0, 0, 1)\}$ is a basis using characterization (d). Any vector$(x, y, z) = x(1,0,0) + y(0,1,0) + z(0,0,1)$ has a unique such representation because the scalars are uniquely determined by the components. Characterization (a) is also direct: independence follows from the trivial solution to$a(1,0,0) + b(0,1,0) + c(0,0,1) = 0$, and spanning is clear.

**Example 2.** In $\mathbb{R}^2$, the set$\mathcal{B} = \{(1, 1), (1, -1)\}$. For characterization (a): is$\mathcal{B}$ independent?$a(1,1) + b(1,-1) = (a+b, a-b) = 0$ forces$a = b = 0$. Does it span? Any$(x, y) = a(1,1) + b(1,-1)$ requires$a = (x+y)/2$ and$b = (x-y)/2$, which is always solvable. So$\mathcal{B}$ is a basis. The unique representation of$(3, 1)$ is$2(1,1) + 1(1,-1)$, confirming characterization (d).

**Example 3.** In $F_{\leq 2}[x]$, consider$\mathcal{B} = \{1+x, x+x^2, 1+x^2\}$. We check characterization (c). The set spans:$1 = \frac{1}{2}[(1+x) - (x+x^2) + (1+x^2)]$, and similarly for$x$ and$x^2$. It is minimal: removing$1+x$ leaves$\{x+x^2, 1+x^2\}$, whose span does not contain$1$ (since all combinations have equal coefficients on$x$ and$x^2$), so$1+x$ is not redundant; similarly for the others. So$\mathcal{B}$ is a basis.

The equivalence of these four conditions also clarifies what can go wrong with sets that are not bases: either they have redundancies (dependent, so failing (b) and (d)), or they are too small (don't span, so failing (c) and (d)), or both. A basis threads the needle exactly: it is large enough to span but has no surplus. This balance — captured formally by condition (d) — is what makes bases the fundamental building blocks of the entire structure theory of vector spaces.
