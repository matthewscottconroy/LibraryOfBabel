# 5.4.2 Dimension of Subspaces; the Dimension Formula for Sums

Once dimension is defined, we can ask how the dimensions of subspaces relate to the dimension of the ambient space. The answers are clean and give us effective tools for computing dimensions without constructing bases explicitly.

\begin{proposition}
If $W$ is a subspace of a finite-dimensional vector space$V$, then$\dim W \leq \dim V$. Moreover,$\dim W = \dim V$ if and only if$W = V$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A basis of $W$ is an independent set in$V$, so by the Replacement Lemma it has size at most$\dim V$. If$\dim W = \dim V = n$ and$\mathcal{B}_W$ is a basis of$W$, then$\mathcal{B}_W$ is an independent subset of$V$ of size$n$. By the$n$-element criterion (the corollary of Section 5.4.1),$\mathcal{B}_W$ is also a basis of$V$. Hence$W = \mathrm{span}(\mathcal{B}_W) = V$.
\end{proof}

This result is useful as a dimension-counting shortcut: to show a subspace $W \subseteq V$ is all of$V$, it suffices to show$\dim W = \dim V$.

The most powerful dimension formula concerns the sum of two subspaces. Recall that for subspaces $U$ and$W$ of$V$, their sum is$U + W = \{u + w : u \in U, w \in W\}$, which is the smallest subspace containing both$U$ and$W$.

\begin{theorem}[Dimension Formula for Sums]
Let $U$ and$W$ be subspaces of a finite-dimensional vector space$V$. Then
\par
$$\dim(U + W) = \dim U + \dim W - \dim(U \cap W).$$
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $d = \dim(U \cap W)$ and choose a basis$\{x_1, \ldots, x_d\}$ for$U \cap W$. By the extension theorem (Section 5.3.3), extend it to a basis$\{x_1, \ldots, x_d, u_1, \ldots, u_p\}$ of$U$ and to a basis$\{x_1, \ldots, x_d, w_1, \ldots, w_q\}$ of$W$, where$\dim U = d + p$ and$\dim W = d + q$. We claim

$$\mathcal{B} = \{x_1, \ldots, x_d, u_1, \ldots, u_p, w_1, \ldots, w_q\}$$

is a basis for $U + W$.

*Spanning:* Any element of $U + W$ has the form$u + w$ with$u \in U$ and$w \in W$. Write$u$ in the basis for$U$ and$w$ in the basis for$W$; the union of both bases spans$U + W$.

*Independence:* Suppose $\sum_i a_i x_i + \sum_j b_j u_j + \sum_k c_k w_k = 0$. Then$\sum_k c_k w_k = -\sum_i a_i x_i - \sum_j b_j u_j \in U$. But also$\sum_k c_k w_k \in W$, so$\sum_k c_k w_k \in U \cap W = \mathrm{span}(x_1, \ldots, x_d)$. Hence$\sum_k c_k w_k = \sum_i \lambda_i x_i$ for some scalars$\lambda_i$. Since$\{x_1, \ldots, x_d, w_1, \ldots, w_q\}$ is a basis for$W$, independence forces all$c_k = 0$ and all$\lambda_i = 0$. Now the original equation becomes$\sum_i a_i x_i + \sum_j b_j u_j = 0$, and independence of the basis of$U$ forces all$a_i = b_j = 0$.

Counting: $|\mathcal{B}| = d + p + q = (d + p) + (d + q) - d = \dim U + \dim W - \dim(U \cap W)$.
\end{proof}

The formula $\dim(U+W) = \dim U + \dim W - \dim(U \cap W)$ is the linear algebra analogue of the inclusion-exclusion principle for finite sets:$|A \cup B| = |A| + |B| - |A \cap B|$. The intersection term corrects for the double-counting of vectors that belong to both subspaces.

\begin{corollary}
$U + W$ is a direct sum (i.e.,$U \cap W = \{0\}$) if and only if$\dim(U \oplus W) = \dim U + \dim W$.
\end{corollary}

**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{R}^4$, let$U = \mathrm{span}(e_1, e_2)$ and$W = \mathrm{span}(e_2, e_3)$. Then$U \cap W = \mathrm{span}(e_2)$, so$\dim(U \cap W) = 1$. The formula gives$\dim(U + W) = 2 + 2 - 1 = 3$. Indeed$U + W = \mathrm{span}(e_1, e_2, e_3)$, which has dimension$3$.

**Example 2.** Let $U = \{(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3 : x = 0\}$ and$W = \{(x, y, z) : z = 0\}$. Then$\dim U = 2$,$\dim W = 2$,$U \cap W = \{(0, y, 0) : y \in \mathbb{R}\}$ has dimension$1$. So$\dim(U + W) = 2 + 2 - 1 = 3 = \dim \mathbb{R}^3$, which confirms$U + W = \mathbb{R}^3$.

**Example 3.** In $M_{2 \times 2}(\mathbb{R})$, let$U$ be the space of symmetric$2 \times 2$ matrices ($\dim U = 3$) and$W$ be the space of skew-symmetric matrices ($\dim W = 1$). Then$U \cap W = \{0\}$ (a symmetric and skew-symmetric matrix must be zero), so$\dim(U + W) = 3 + 1 = 4 = \dim M_{2 \times 2}(\mathbb{R})$. This confirms the decomposition$M_{2 \times 2}(\mathbb{R}) = U \oplus W$ established in Section 7.1.3.

**Example 4.** Let $V = F^4$ and suppose$U$ and$W$ are both$3$-dimensional subspaces. Then$\dim(U + W) \leq 4$ (since$U + W \subseteq V$) and$\dim(U + W) = 3 + 3 - \dim(U \cap W)$, so$\dim(U \cap W) = 6 - \dim(U + W) \geq 6 - 4 = 2$. Any two$3$-dimensional subspaces of$F^4$ must intersect in a subspace of dimension at least$2$. This kind of dimension bound — proving that two large subspaces must have substantial intersection — is a recurring technique in algebra and geometry.

The dimension formula is one of the primary tools for dimension calculations throughout the book. It often allows us to determine dimensions from qualitative information — such as knowing that a certain intersection is trivial — without computing explicit bases.
