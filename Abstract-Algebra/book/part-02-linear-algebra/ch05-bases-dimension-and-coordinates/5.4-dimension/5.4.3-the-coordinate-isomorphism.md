# 5.4.3 The Coordinate Isomorphism $V \cong F^n$

One of the most practically useful consequences of the basis concept is that any $n$-dimensional vector space over$F$ can be identified with$F^n$ — the space of$n$-tuples of scalars — in a way that respects all the linear structure. This identification is not canonical (it depends on a choice of basis), but once a basis is chosen it is completely explicit and allows abstract questions about$V$ to be translated into concrete computations in$F^n$.

\begin{definition}
Let $V$ be an$n$-dimensional vector space over$F$ with ordered basis$\mathcal{B} = (b_1, \ldots, b_n)$. For any$v \in V$, the unique scalars$x_1, \ldots, x_n \in F$ satisfying$v = x_1 b_1 + \cdots + x_n b_n$ are called the **coordinates of$v$ with respect to$\mathcal{B}$**. The **coordinate map** is
\par
$$[\,\cdot\,]_{\mathcal{B}} : V \to F^n, \quad v \mapsto [v]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ \vdots \\ x_n \end{pmatrix}.$$
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Coordinate Isomorphism]
The coordinate map $[\,\cdot\,]_{\mathcal{B}} : V \to F^n$ is a linear isomorphism.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
*Linearity:* If $v = \sum x_i b_i$ and$w = \sum y_i b_i$, then$v + w = \sum (x_i + y_i) b_i$, so$[v+w]_{\mathcal{B}} = [v]_{\mathcal{B}} + [w]_{\mathcal{B}}$. Similarly$[av]_{\mathcal{B}} = a[v]_{\mathcal{B}}$.

*Injectivity:* If $[v]_{\mathcal{B}} = 0$, then all coordinates are zero, so$v = 0$.

*Surjectivity:* Any $(x_1, \ldots, x_n) \in F^n$ is the coordinate vector of$v = x_1 b_1 + \cdots + x_n b_n$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
Every $n$-dimensional vector space over$F$ is isomorphic to$F^n$.
\end{corollary}

Several important points deserve emphasis. First, the isomorphism depends on the ordering of $\mathcal{B}$, not just on the set of basis vectors. Reordering the basis changes the coordinates: if we swap$b_1$ and$b_2$, the first and second coordinates of every vector are swapped. Second, if we choose a different basis$\mathcal{B}'$, we get a different isomorphism and a different coordinate assignment. There is no preferred or natural choice of basis, and the abstract vector$v$ does not come with a canonical coordinate representation.

The relationship between coordinates in two different bases is handled by the change-of-basis matrix (Section 7.4.1). If $\mathcal{B}$ and$\mathcal{B}'$ are two ordered bases of$V$, there is an invertible matrix$P$ — the transition matrix — such that$[v]_{\mathcal{B}} = P [v]_{\mathcal{B}'}$ for all$v \in V$.

**Example 1.** In $V = \mathbb{R}^2$ with basis$\mathcal{B} = ((1,1), (1,-1))$, find$[(3,1)]_{\mathcal{B}}$. We solve$a(1,1) + b(1,-1) = (3,1)$:$a + b = 3$ and$a - b = 1$, giving$a = 2$ and$b = 1$. So$[(3,1)]_{\mathcal{B}} = (2,1)^T$.

**Example 2.** In $V = F_{\leq 2}[x]$ with basis$\mathcal{B} = (1, x, x^2)$, the coordinate map sends$p(x) = a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2$ to$(a_0, a_1, a_2)^T$. With basis$\mathcal{B}' = (1, 1+x, 1+x+x^2)$, the polynomial$p(x) = 3 + 2x + x^2$ has coordinates with respect to$\mathcal{B}'$: we need$c_0 \cdot 1 + c_1(1+x) + c_2(1+x+x^2) = 3 + 2x + x^2$. Matching coefficients:$c_0 + c_1 + c_2 = 3$,$c_1 + c_2 = 2$,$c_2 = 1$. Solving:$c_2 = 1$,$c_1 = 1$,$c_0 = 1$. So$[p]_{\mathcal{B}'} = (1, 1, 1)^T$.

**Example 3.** In $V = M_{2 \times 2}(F)$ with basis$\mathcal{B} = (E_{11}, E_{12}, E_{21}, E_{22})$, the matrix$A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ has coordinate vector$[A]_{\mathcal{B}} = (a, b, c, d)^T \in F^4$.

What this means practically is that any linear algebra question about $V$ can be translated into a question about$F^n$, where vectors are concrete tuples and linear maps become matrices. The power of matrix computations rests entirely on this identification. The coordinate isomorphism is the bridge between the abstract theory and concrete calculation, and it is what makes linear algebra both computationally tractable and conceptually general. At the same time, results phrased in the language of abstract vector spaces — like the rank-nullity theorem — hold for all$n$-dimensional spaces simultaneously, without reference to any particular coordinate system. The abstract and concrete languages are interchangeable; choosing between them is a matter of which is more convenient for the task at hand.

\begin{remark}
A common confusion: the coordinate map $[\,\cdot\,]_{\mathcal{B}}: V \to F^n$ is an isomorphism of vector spaces, not a canonical or basis-independent one. Changing the basis changes the isomorphism, and this change is governed by the transition matrix (Section 7.4.1). The abstract vector$v \in V$ exists independently of any coordinates; the coordinate vector$[v]_{\mathcal{B}}$ is a representation of$v$ in a chosen reference frame. The distinction between "the vector" and "its coordinates in a basis" is philosophically important: it is the distinction between intrinsic and extrinsic descriptions, between coordinate-free mathematics and computational mathematics. Both are necessary and neither is superior.
\end{remark}
