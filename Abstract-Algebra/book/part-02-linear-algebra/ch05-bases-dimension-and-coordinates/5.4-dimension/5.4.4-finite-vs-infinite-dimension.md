# 5.4.4 Finite vs. Infinite Dimension

Most of this chapter has worked in the finite-dimensional setting, where bases are finite and coordinates are finite tuples. But the definitions of span, independence, and basis apply without modification to any vector space, and it is worth taking stock of what happens in the infinite-dimensional case — both because many natural spaces are infinite-dimensional and because the distinction matters for what can and cannot be proved in general.

\begin{definition}
A vector space $V$ over$F$ is **finite-dimensional** if it has a finite spanning set, or equivalently (by the reduction theorem) if it has a finite basis. Otherwise$V$ is **infinite-dimensional**.
\end{definition}

The polynomial ring $F[x]$ is infinite-dimensional over$F$: the monomials$1, x, x^2, \ldots$ are linearly independent (no polynomial of degree$n$ is a linear combination of polynomials of lower degree), and no finite set of polynomials can span all of$F[x]$ (since no finite collection has polynomials of all degrees). The monomials$\{1, x, x^2, \ldots\}$ form an explicit countably infinite algebraic (Hamel) basis. The space$\mathcal{C}([0,1])$ of real continuous functions on$[0,1]$ is infinite-dimensional over$\mathbb{R}$; it contains$F_{\leq n}[x]$ for every$n$, so no finite spanning set exists. The vector space$\mathbb{R}$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ is infinite-dimensional and in fact has uncountable Hamel basis.

For infinite-dimensional spaces, the algebraic (Hamel) basis — a maximal linearly independent set — still exists by Zorn's Lemma (Section 5.3.2), and dimension is still a well-defined cardinal number. The invariance theorem holds with appropriate cardinal arithmetic. However, Hamel bases for spaces like $\mathcal{C}([0,1])$ are uncountably infinite, highly non-constructive, and rarely useful in analysis. This leads to an important conceptual divergence.

\begin{remark}[Algebraic vs. Topological Bases]
In functional analysis, the vector spaces of interest — Banach spaces, Hilbert spaces — carry a topology (coming from a norm or inner product), and one works with a different notion of "basis" suited to that topology. A **Schauder basis** of a Banach space $X$ is a sequence$(e_n)_{n \geq 1}$ such that every$x \in X$ has a unique convergent series expansion$x = \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n e_n$. An **orthonormal basis** of a Hilbert space$H$ is a maximal orthonormal set, whose finite linear combinations are dense in$H$ but do not exhaust$H$ (unless$H$ is finite-dimensional). These topological notions must not be confused with the algebraic Hamel basis defined here.
\end{remark}

The distinction matters because several results that are automatic in finite dimensions can fail or require significant modification in the infinite-dimensional case:

- **Injectivity vs. Surjectivity.** In finite dimensions, a linear map $T: V \to V$ is injective iff it is surjective (Section 6.2.4). In infinite dimensions both implications fail: the right shift$(a_1, a_2, \ldots) \mapsto (0, a_1, a_2, \ldots)$ on$\ell^2$ is injective but not surjective; the left shift is surjective but not injective.

- **Rank-Nullity.** In finite dimensions, $\mathrm{rank}(T) + \mathrm{nullity}(T) = \dim V$ is a precise numerical equation. In infinite dimensions, both rank and nullity may be infinite, and the equation needs to be interpreted as a cardinal equation.

- **Spanning Sets.** In finite dimensions, a spanning set always contains a finite basis. An infinite spanning set of an infinite-dimensional space may contain no countable basis (e.g., the spanning set $\mathcal{C}([0,1])$ of all continuous functions spans$\mathcal{C}([0,1])$ trivially, but extracting a Hamel basis requires Zorn and produces an uncountable set).

**Example 1.** $V = F[x]$,$\dim_F V = \aleph_0$. Hamel basis:$\{1, x, x^2, x^3, \ldots\}$. Every polynomial is a unique finite linear combination of monomials. This basis is explicit and constructive.

**Example 2.** $V = \mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}$ (all sequences of reals),$\dim_{\mathbb{R}} V = 2^{\aleph_0}$. The standard-basis sequences$e_n = (\delta_{ni})_{i \geq 1}$ are independent but do not span (the all-ones sequence$(1,1,1,\ldots)$ is not a finite linear combination of them). A Hamel basis exists by Zorn's Lemma but cannot be described explicitly.

**Example 3.** $\ell^2 = \{(a_n) : \sum |a_n|^2 < \infty\}$ over$\mathbb{R}$. The Hamel basis is uncountable and non-constructive; the useful object is instead the orthonormal basis$\{e_n\}$ in the Hilbert-space sense, where$\sum a_n e_n$ converges in the$\ell^2$-norm for any$(a_n) \in \ell^2$.

Throughout this book we work primarily in finite dimensions and flag explicitly whenever the infinite-dimensional case diverges. The algebraic theory developed here — dimension, coordinates, matrices — is complete and clean in the finite-dimensional setting. For infinite-dimensional spaces, the algebraic theory remains valid but must be supplemented with topological tools to be useful, and that supplementation is the subject of functional analysis.
