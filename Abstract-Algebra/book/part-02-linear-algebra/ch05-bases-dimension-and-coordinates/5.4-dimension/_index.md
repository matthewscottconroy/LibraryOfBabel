# 5.4 Dimension

The dimension of a vector space is defined to be the cardinality of any basis. For this definition to be well-posed, it must be shown that all bases have the same size — and this is a genuine theorem, not an obvious fact. The proof uses the exchange lemma: if $V$ has a basis of size $n$ and another basis of size $m$, then applying the exchange lemma in both directions forces $m \leq n$ and $n \leq m$, so $m = n$. The invariance of dimension is the foundational result of the whole chapter, and it is what gives the word "dimension" its meaning as an intrinsic property of the space, not of any particular basis.

Once dimension is established, the section derives its most immediately useful consequences. A vector space of dimension $n$ over $F$ is isomorphic to $F^n$ via the coordinate map; more precisely, any ordered basis $\mathcal{B}$ of $V$ yields a vector space isomorphism $[\cdot]_{\mathcal{B}} : V \to F^n$ sending $v$ to its coordinate vector. This coordinate isomorphism is canonical given the basis, but changes when the basis changes — the relationship between different coordinate representations is the subject of change-of-basis in Chapter 7. The section also proves the subspace dimension inequality: if $U$ is a subspace of $V$, then $\dim U \leq \dim V$, with equality if and only if $U = V$.

The dimension formula for sums of subspaces, $$\dim(W_1 + W_2) = \dim W_1 + \dim W_2 - \dim(W_1 \cap W_2),$$ is a structural identity analogous to the inclusion-exclusion formula in combinatorics. Its proof proceeds by choosing a basis for $W_1 \cap W_2$ and extending it independently to bases of $W_1$ and $W_2$, then showing the union of all these basis vectors spans $W_1 + W_2$ independently. As a corollary, $W_1 + W_2$ is a direct sum if and only if $\dim(W_1 + W_2) = \dim W_1 + \dim W_2$, which is the numerical signature of the zero-intersection condition.

The section closes with an honest discussion of the finite vs. infinite dimensional divide. Everything proved so far about dimension — the invariance theorem, the coordinate isomorphism, the dimension formula — holds in finite dimensions. In infinite dimensions, the existence of a basis requires the Axiom of Choice, and the "dimension" of an infinite-dimensional space can be any infinite cardinal. The distinction between $\aleph_0$-dimensional and uncountably infinite-dimensional spaces is real and consequential. For most of the remaining chapters, finite-dimensionality is the standing assumption, but the reader should bear in mind that the theory does extend, at a cost in set-theoretic sophistication.
