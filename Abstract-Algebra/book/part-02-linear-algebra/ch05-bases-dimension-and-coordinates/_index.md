# Chapter 5 — Bases, Dimension, and Coordinates

A vector space, as defined in Chapter 4, is a remarkably fluid object: a vector could be a tuple, a polynomial, a function, or a matrix, and all of them satisfy the same axioms. This fluidity is the source of the definition's power, but it also raises an immediate question: how do we measure a vector space? How do we compare two vector spaces and decide whether they are "the same size"? This chapter answers that question by introducing the notions of linear independence, span, and basis, and by proving the central structural theorem: every basis of a finite-dimensional vector space has the same number of elements, and that number — the dimension — is a complete isomorphism invariant for finite-dimensional spaces over the same field.

The path to dimension runs through two complementary ideas. Spanning is about coverage: a set $S \subseteq V$ spans $V$ if every vector in $V$ can be written as a linear combination of elements of $S$. Linear independence is about non-redundancy: a set is linearly independent if the only way to write the zero vector as a linear combination of its elements is to use all zero coefficients. A basis is a set that is simultaneously spanning and linearly independent — minimal among spanning sets and maximal among independent sets. The chapter proves that these three characterizations of a basis are equivalent, and that any linearly independent set can be extended to a basis, and any spanning set can be reduced to one. These "extension and reduction" results are the workhorses of finite-dimensional linear algebra.

The cornerstone result is the Steinitz exchange lemma: if $v_1, \ldots, v_m$ span $V$ and $u_1, \ldots, u_k$ are linearly independent, then $k \leq m$. From this follows immediately the invariance of dimension: any two bases of a finite-dimensional vector space have the same cardinality. The proof of the exchange lemma is a beautiful inductive argument, replacing spanning vectors with independent ones one by one without losing the spanning property. In infinite dimensions, the existence of a basis requires the Axiom of Choice (in the form of Zorn's Lemma), and this subtlety is addressed explicitly: a Hamel basis exists for every vector space, but its elements may be impossible to exhibit concretely.

Once dimension is established, the chapter introduces coordinate vectors. Choosing a (ordered) basis $\mathcal{B} = (b_1, \ldots, b_n)$ for $V$ yields a canonical isomorphism $V \cong F^n$: every vector $v$ corresponds to the unique $n$-tuple of its coefficients in the basis expansion $v = c_1 b_1 + \cdots + c_n b_n$. This coordinate isomorphism is the bridge between abstract vector spaces and the concrete column vectors of matrix algebra. It also makes precise the sense in which "coordinates depend on the choice of basis" — the same vector $v$ has different coordinate vectors with respect to different bases, a theme developed extensively in Chapter 7 when we study change of basis. The dimension formula for sums of subspaces, $\dim(W_1 + W_2) = \dim W_1 + \dim W_2 - \dim(W_1 \cap W_2)$, closes the chapter with an analogue of the inclusion-exclusion principle that will be invoked repeatedly.
