# 6.1.2 Examples: Differentiation, Integration, Projection, Rotation

The definition of linearity is short, but the class of maps it describes is enormous. Before developing the theory further, we ground it with the fundamental examples — the maps that motivated the definition in the first place and that will reappear throughout mathematics.

**Differentiation.** The derivative operator $D: F[x] \to F[x]$, defined by$D(f) = f'$, is linear:$(af + bg)' = af' + bg'$ follows from the linearity of differentiation. More generally,$D$ acts on any vector space of differentiable functions. The fact that differentiation is linear is the reason that solving linear differential equations with constant coefficients reduces to polynomial algebra. Notice that$D$ is not injective — its kernel consists of all constant functions — but it is surjective onto$F[x]$ (every polynomial is the derivative of some polynomial). However,$D: F_{\leq n}[x] \to F_{\leq n-1}[x]$ is surjective but has a nontrivial kernel (the constants), illustrating the rank-nullity theorem:$\dim F_{\leq n}[x] = n+1$,$\mathrm{null}(D) = 1$,$\mathrm{rank}(D) = n$.

**Integration.** The map $I: \mathcal{C}([a,b]) \to \mathcal{C}([a,b])$ defined by$I(f)(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt$ is linear, since the integral of a sum is the sum of integrals and constants pull out. Integration and differentiation are related by the fundamental theorem of calculus:$D \circ I = \mathrm{id}$ (the derivative of$\int_a^x f$ is$f$), but$I \circ D \neq \mathrm{id}$ ($\int_a^x f'(t)\,dt = f(x) - f(a) \neq f(x)$ in general). Their relationship — one is a right inverse of the other but not a left inverse — is a concrete instance of the injectivity-surjectivity asymmetry studied in Section 6.2.

**Projection.** The map $\pi_1: F^n \to F$ defined by$\pi_1(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = x_1$ is linear. More generally, if$V = U \oplus W$ is a direct sum decomposition, the **projection onto$U$ along$W$** is the map$\pi_U: V \to V$ that sends$u + w$ (with$u \in U$,$w \in W$) to$u$. This is linear and satisfies$\pi_U^2 = \pi_U$ (it is idempotent). The two conditions$\pi_U^2 = \pi_U$ and$\pi_W = \mathrm{id} - \pi_U$ with$\pi_W^2 = \pi_W$ capture the direct-sum decomposition algebraically.

**Rotation.** Rotation in $\mathbb{R}^2$ by a fixed angle$\theta$ is linear: it preserves addition and scalar multiplication because rotation is a rigid motion fixing the origin. Its matrix representation in the standard basis is

$$R_\theta = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}.$$

The composition $R_\theta \circ R_\phi = R_{\theta + \phi}$ corresponds to matrix multiplication, and the inverse$(R_\theta)^{-1} = R_{-\theta}$. Rotations in$\mathbb{R}^3$ are also linear, and their matrices form the group$SO(3)$, the subject of later chapters on Lie groups and representation theory.

**Matrix Multiplication.** Multiplication by a fixed $m \times n$ matrix$A$ defines a linear map$T_A: F^n \to F^m$ by$T_A(x) = Ax$. This is the standard source of examples in courses that begin with matrices. The key insight — which we prove in Section 7.3 — is that every linear map between finite-dimensional vector spaces, once bases are chosen, is represented by exactly such a matrix multiplication. So matrices and linear maps are two languages for the same thing, one concrete and computational, the other abstract and coordinate-free.

**Example 1.** Differentiation on $F_{\leq 3}[x]$:$D(a + bx + cx^2 + dx^3) = b + 2cx + 3dx^2$. The matrix of$D$ with respect to the monomial basis$(1, x, x^2, x^3)$ for the domain and$(1, x, x^2)$ for the codomain is

$$[D] = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}.$$

This $3 \times 4$ matrix encodes the rule$D(x^k) = kx^{k-1}$: the$k$-th column is the coordinate vector of$kx^{k-1}$.

**Example 2.** Projection onto the $x$-axis in$\mathbb{R}^2$:$\pi_1(x, y) = (x, 0)$. Matrix:$\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}$. This is idempotent:$\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}^2 = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$. The kernel is the$y$-axis, and the image is the$x$-axis.

**Example 3.** Rotation by $\pi/2$ in$\mathbb{R}^2$:$R_{\pi/2}(x,y) = (-y, x)$. Check linearity:$R_{\pi/2}(a(x_1, y_1) + b(x_2, y_2)) = R_{\pi/2}(ax_1 + bx_2, ay_1 + by_2) = (-(ay_1 + by_2), ax_1 + bx_2) = a(-y_1, x_1) + b(-y_2, x_2) = aR_{\pi/2}(x_1,y_1) + bR_{\pi/2}(x_2,y_2)$.

These examples illustrate a general principle: the most natural operations in analysis, geometry, and algebra tend to be linear. This is why linear algebra has such broad applicability — it is the language in which the structure of these operations can be studied uniformly.
