# 6.1.3 The Space of Linear Maps $\mathcal{L}(V,W)$

Linear maps between two fixed vector spaces do not merely form a collection — they form a vector space in their own right. This is a useful and somewhat surprising fact: the objects of study (the maps) have the same algebraic structure as the objects they act on.

\begin{definition}
Let $V$ and$W$ be vector spaces over$F$. The **space of linear maps from$V$ to$W$**, denoted$\mathcal{L}(V, W)$ (or$\mathrm{Hom}_F(V, W)$), is the set of all linear maps$T: V \to W$, equipped with the pointwise operations:
\par
$$(S + T)(v) = S(v) + T(v), \quad (aT)(v) = aT(v).$$
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
$\mathcal{L}(V, W)$ is a vector space over$F$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
One checks that if $S$ and$T$ are linear, then so are$S + T$ and$aT$: linearity of$S+T$ follows because$(S+T)(u + v) = S(u+v) + T(u+v) = (S(u)+T(u)) + (S(v)+T(v)) = (S+T)(u) + (S+T)(v)$, and similarly for scalar multiplication. The zero map$0: v \mapsto 0_W$ serves as the additive identity. The remaining vector space axioms are inherited from$W$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}
If $\dim V = n$ and$\dim W = m$, then$\dim \mathcal{L}(V, W) = mn$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Choose ordered bases $(b_1, \ldots, b_n)$ of$V$ and$(c_1, \ldots, c_m)$ of$W$. For each pair$(i, j)$ with$1 \leq i \leq n$ and$1 \leq j \leq m$, define the linear map$E_{ij}: V \to W$ by

$$E_{ij}(b_k) = \begin{cases} c_j & \text{if } k = i, \\ 0 & \text{if } k \neq i. \end{cases}$$

The map $E_{ij}$ sends$b_i$ to$c_j$ and kills all other basis vectors. We claim the$mn$ maps$\{E_{ij}\}$ form a basis for$\mathcal{L}(V, W)$.

*Spanning:* Any $T \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$ is determined by the vectors$T(b_i) \in W$. Write$T(b_i) = \sum_j a_{ji} c_j$; then$T = \sum_{i,j} a_{ji} E_{ij}$.

*Independence:* If $\sum_{i,j} a_{ji} E_{ij} = 0$ (the zero map), then applying to$b_k$ gives$\sum_j a_{jk} c_j = 0$. Since$(c_j)$ is a basis for$W$,$a_{jk} = 0$ for all$j$ and$k$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Under the identification of $T$ with its matrix$[T]_{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathcal{C}}$ (Section 7.3.3), the map$E_{ij}$ corresponds to the elementary matrix with a$1$ in position$(j, i)$ and zeros elsewhere. The formula$\dim \mathcal{L}(V, W) = mn$ matches$\dim M_{m \times n}(F) = mn$.
\end{remark}

The case $V = W$ deserves special attention. The space$\mathcal{L}(V, V) = \mathrm{End}(V)$ of **endomorphisms** of$V$ carries an additional operation: composition of maps. Under composition,$\mathrm{End}(V)$ becomes an associative ring (and in fact an$F$-algebra, since$F$ acts by scalar multiplication). Composition is associative —$(S \circ T) \circ U = S \circ (T \circ U)$ for all$S, T, U$ — but generally not commutative. The identity map$\mathrm{id}_V$ serves as the multiplicative identity.

\begin{example}
Let $V = \mathbb{R}^2$. Then$\mathrm{End}(V) \cong M_{2 \times 2}(\mathbb{R})$ as$\mathbb{R}$-algebras. The rotation by$\pi/2$ (matrix$\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$) and the reflection across the$x$-axis (matrix$\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$) are both elements of$\mathrm{End}(V)$. Their compositions in the two orders differ: rotation-then-reflection and reflection-then-rotation give different outcomes, illustrating non-commutativity.
\end{example}

**Example 1.** $\dim \mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^2, \mathbb{R}^3) = 2 \cdot 3 = 6$. A basis consists of the six maps$E_{ij}$ ($i = 1,2$,$j = 1,2,3$) defined above. Explicitly,$E_{11}(e_1) = f_1$,$E_{11}(e_2) = 0$ (where$f_1, f_2, f_3$ is the standard basis of$\mathbb{R}^3$), and so on.

**Example 2.** $\dim \mathcal{L}(F_{\leq 2}[x], F_{\leq 1}[x]) = 3 \cdot 2 = 6$. The differentiation map$D$ is one element of this space.

**Example 3.** $\dim \mathrm{End}(\mathbb{R}^n) = n^2$. Under the isomorphism with$M_{n \times n}(\mathbb{R})$, the$n^2$ elementary matrices form a basis, and composition of endomorphisms corresponds to matrix multiplication.

The study of $\mathrm{End}(V)$ as an algebra — its invertible elements (the automorphisms$\mathrm{Aut}(V) \cong GL_n(F)$), its ideals, and its representation theory — is the subject of the later chapters on eigenvalues, diagonalization, and the structure theorem for linear operators. The space$\mathcal{L}(V, W)$ for$W = F$ is the **dual space**$V^* = \mathcal{L}(V, F)$, which plays a central role in multilinear algebra and differential geometry.

**Example 4.** A subspace of $\mathcal{L}(V,V)$: the set of maps that commute with a fixed$T_0 \in \mathrm{End}(V)$ is the **commutant** (or centralizer)$C(T_0) = \{S \in \mathrm{End}(V) : ST_0 = T_0 S\}$. This is a subspace of$\mathrm{End}(V)$: if$S_1, S_2 \in C(T_0)$, then$(aS_1 + bS_2)T_0 = aS_1 T_0 + bS_2 T_0 = aT_0 S_1 + bT_0 S_2 = T_0(aS_1 + bS_2)$. The commutant is also closed under composition, making it a subalgebra of$\mathrm{End}(V)$.

**Example 5.** The dual space $V^* = \mathcal{L}(V, F)$ has$\dim V^* = n \cdot 1 = n = \dim V$. A basis for$V^*$: given a basis$(b_1, \ldots, b_n)$ for$V$, define the **dual basis**$(\beta_1, \ldots, \beta_n)$ by$\beta_i(b_j) = \delta_{ij}$ (the Kronecker delta). These are$n$ linearly independent elements of$V^*$, hence a basis. The dual basis is fundamental to the theory of bilinear forms, tensor products, and cohomology.

The vector space $\mathcal{L}(V, W)$ serves as the ambient space within which one studies linear maps systematically, applying all the tools of the theory (subspaces, dimension, linear independence) to the maps themselves rather than to the vectors they act on. This "meta" application of linear algebra — treating maps as vectors — is one of the most elegant aspects of the subject, and it underlies the power of representation theory, where one studies the space of all representations of a given algebraic structure.
