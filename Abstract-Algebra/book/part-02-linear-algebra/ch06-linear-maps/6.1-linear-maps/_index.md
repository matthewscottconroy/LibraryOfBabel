# 6.1 Linear Maps

A linear map between vector spaces is a function that commutes with linear combinations: $T(c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2) = c_1 T(v_1) + c_2 T(v_2)$ for all scalars $c_1, c_2$ and all vectors $v_1, v_2$. This single condition — linearity — is one of the most powerful constraints in mathematics. It means that to understand $T$ entirely, it suffices to understand what $T$ does to each element of a basis: once those values are prescribed, the map is completely and uniquely determined. Every other vector in the domain is a linear combination of basis vectors, and linearity forces the output to be the corresponding linear combination of outputs.

The section opens by verifying that linearity implies several immediate consequences: $T(\mathbf{0}) = \mathbf{0}$ (the zero vector is always mapped to zero), $T(-v) = -T(v)$, and $T$ distributes over any finite linear combination. These consequences arise from the two-part definition — $T$ respects addition and $T$ respects scalar multiplication — and they will be used constantly in later arguments. The section then establishes the existence and uniqueness theorem for linear maps: given a basis $(b_1, \ldots, b_n)$ for $V$ and any vectors $w_1, \ldots, w_n \in W$, there exists a unique linear map $T: V \to W$ with $T(b_i) = w_i$ for each $i$. This theorem underpins virtually every construction of linear maps by formula.

The examples are diverse and important. Differentiation $D: \mathbb{R}[x]_{\leq n} \to \mathbb{R}[x]_{\leq n-1}$ is linear by the sum and constant-multiple rules from calculus. Integration $\int_0^x : \mathbb{R}[x]_{\leq n} \to \mathbb{R}[x]_{\leq n+1}$ is also linear. Projection onto the first coordinate $\pi : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ is linear. Rotation in the plane by angle $\theta$ is linear. These examples show that linearity is not an algebraic curiosity but the precise abstract version of the "superposition principle" that underlies differential equations, quantum mechanics, and Fourier analysis.

The section closes by observing that the set $\mathcal{L}(V, W)$ of all linear maps from $V$ to $W$ is itself a vector space under pointwise operations: $(S + T)(v) = S(v) + T(v)$ and $(cT)(v) = c \cdot T(v)$. When $V = W$, composition makes $\mathcal{L}(V, V) = \mathrm{End}(V)$ into a ring — in fact, a non-commutative ring whose invertible elements are the linear isomorphisms. The ring $\mathrm{End}(V)$ is the algebraic home of operators, eigenvalues, and spectral theory, and its study will occupy a significant part of Chapters 9 and 10.
