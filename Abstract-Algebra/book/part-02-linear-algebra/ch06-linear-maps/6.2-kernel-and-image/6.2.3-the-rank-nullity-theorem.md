# 6.2.3 The Rank-Nullity Theorem

The kernel and image of a linear map $T: V \to W$ are both subspaces, and we have defined their dimensions as the nullity$\mathrm{null}(T) = \dim(\ker T)$ and the rank$\mathrm{rank}(T) = \dim(\mathrm{im}\, T)$. These two quantities are not independent: they are constrained by the dimension of the domain$V$ through the Rank-Nullity Theorem, which is the fundamental accounting identity for linear maps.

\begin{theorem}[Rank-Nullity Theorem]
For any linear map $T: V \to W$ with$V$ finite-dimensional,
\par
$$\dim(\ker T) + \dim(\mathrm{im}\, T) = \dim V,$$
\par
or equivalently, $\mathrm{null}(T) + \mathrm{rank}(T) = \dim V$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $k = \mathrm{null}(T)$ and let$(u_1, \ldots, u_k)$ be a basis for$\ker T$. By the extension theorem (Section 5.3.3), extend this to a basis$(u_1, \ldots, u_k, v_1, \ldots, v_r)$ for all of$V$, where$r = \dim V - k$. We claim$(T(v_1), \ldots, T(v_r))$ is a basis for$\mathrm{im}\, T$.

*Spanning:* Any $T(v)$ with$v = \sum a_i u_i + \sum b_j v_j$ satisfies$T(v) = \sum b_j T(v_j)$, since each$T(u_i) = 0$.

*Independence:* Suppose $\sum b_j T(v_j) = 0$. Then$T(\sum b_j v_j) = 0$, so$\sum b_j v_j \in \ker T = \mathrm{span}(u_1, \ldots, u_k)$. Write$\sum b_j v_j = \sum c_i u_i$, i.e.,$\sum c_i u_i - \sum b_j v_j = 0$. Since$(u_1, \ldots, u_k, v_1, \ldots, v_r)$ is a basis of$V$, all coefficients are zero:$c_i = 0$ and$b_j = 0$.

Therefore $\dim(\mathrm{im}\, T) = r = \dim V - k$, which gives the theorem.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The proof constructs two "complementary" pieces of information about $T$: the part of$V$ that$T$ kills (the kernel, of dimension$k$) and the part that survives into$W$ (contributing$r$ independent directions to the image). The dimension of$V$ is exactly the sum of these two pieces. No information can be simultaneously killed and preserved.
\end{remark}

The Rank-Nullity Theorem is the linear algebra analogue of the first isomorphism theorem from group theory, which states that if $\phi: G \to H$ is a group homomorphism, then$G/\ker\phi \cong \mathrm{im}\,\phi$. The linear algebra version is:$V/\ker T \cong \mathrm{im}\, T$ as vector spaces, and taking dimensions gives the Rank-Nullity equation.

**Example 1.** Let $T: \mathbb{R}^4 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ be defined by$T(x_1, x_2, x_3, x_4) = (x_1 + x_2, x_2 + x_3, x_3 + x_4)$. We have$\dim V = 4$. Find$\ker T$: the conditions are$x_1 = -x_2$,$x_2 = -x_3$,$x_3 = -x_4$, so the kernel is$\{(t, -t, t, -t) : t \in \mathbb{R}\}$, which has dimension$1$. By Rank-Nullity,$\mathrm{rank}(T) = 4 - 1 = 3$. Check:$T(e_1) = (1,0,0)$,$T(e_2) = (1,1,0)$,$T(e_3) = (0,1,1)$,$T(e_4) = (0,0,1)$ — these span$\mathbb{R}^3$, confirming rank$3$.

**Example 2.** Let $D: F_{\leq n}[x] \to F_{\leq n}[x]$ be the derivative map (considered as a self-map). Then$\ker D = F_{\leq 0}[x]$ (the constants),$\mathrm{null}(D) = 1$, and$\mathrm{rank}(D) = n+1-1 = n$. The image is$F_{\leq n-1}[x]$ (polynomials of degree$\leq n-1$), which indeed has dimension$n$.

**Example 3.** Let $A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 6\end{pmatrix}$ and$T_A: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^2$. Row-reducing: the second row is$2$ times the first, so the row-reduced form is$\begin{pmatrix}1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}$. There is$1$ pivot, so$\mathrm{rank}(T_A) = 1$. By Rank-Nullity,$\mathrm{null}(T_A) = 3 - 1 = 2$. The null space is$\{x : x_1 + 2x_2 + 3x_3 = 0\}$, a$2$-dimensional plane in$\mathbb{R}^3$.

**Example 4.** Using Rank-Nullity to determine when a system $Ax = b$ has a solution: if$A$ is$m \times n$ and$T_A: F^n \to F^m$, then$b$ is in the image of$T_A$ iff$\mathrm{rank}([A \mid b]) = \mathrm{rank}(A)$ (the augmented matrix has the same rank as$A$). This is the consistency criterion in terms of rank.

In practice, the Rank-Nullity Theorem is most often used to determine one dimension from the other two: if you know the dimension of $V$ and can compute either the rank or the nullity, you get the other for free. This is particularly powerful when one of the subspaces is easy to describe (e.g., the kernel has a clean geometric description) and the other is not.
