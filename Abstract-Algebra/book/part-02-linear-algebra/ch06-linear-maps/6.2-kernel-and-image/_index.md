# 6.2 Kernel and Image

Every linear map $T: V \to W$ comes equipped with two canonical subspaces: its kernel (or null space) $\ker T = \{v \in V : T(v) = \mathbf{0}\}$ and its image (or range) $\mathrm{im}\, T = \{T(v) : v \in V\}$. The kernel lives in the domain and the image lives in the codomain, and together they capture the essential quantitative behavior of $T$. This section proves that both are subspaces, characterizes injectivity and surjectivity in terms of them, and culminates in the rank-nullity theorem — the central dimension-counting identity of linear algebra.

The kernel is always a subspace of $V$: if $T(u) = T(v) = \mathbf{0}$, then $T(u + v) = T(u) + T(v) = \mathbf{0}$ and $T(cv) = cT(v) = \mathbf{0}$. The injectivity criterion is clean and useful: $T$ is injective if and only if $\ker T = \{\mathbf{0}\}$. The proof is a one-liner — $T(u) = T(v)$ iff $T(u-v) = \mathbf{0}$ iff $u - v \in \ker T$ — and it reduces checking injectivity to checking whether the kernel is trivial. Similarly, the image is a subspace of $W$ (it is closed under the operations by linearity), and $T$ is surjective if and only if $\mathrm{im}\, T = W$.

The rank-nullity theorem states: if $V$ is finite-dimensional, then $$\dim V = \dim(\ker T) + \dim(\mathrm{im}\, T).$$ The proof is constructive. Choose a basis $u_1, \ldots, u_k$ for $\ker T$, then extend it to a basis $u_1, \ldots, u_k, v_1, \ldots, v_m$ for all of $V$. Show that $T(v_1), \ldots, T(v_m)$ is a basis for $\mathrm{im}\, T$: they span the image (any $T(v)$ can be expressed using these) and they are independent (a dependence relation would force a nontrivial combination of the $v_i$ into the kernel, contradicting the extension). The equation $k + m = n$ then gives the theorem. The rank of $T$ is $\dim(\mathrm{im}\, T)$ and the nullity is $\dim(\ker T)$; the theorem says rank plus nullity equals the dimension of the domain.

The consequences of rank-nullity are far-reaching. For maps between spaces of equal dimension ($\dim V = \dim W = n$), injectivity and surjectivity become equivalent: a linear map from $V$ to $V$ is injective iff it is surjective iff it is an isomorphism. This is the linear-algebraic analogue of the pigeonhole principle and explains why square matrices are either invertible or not — there is no injective-but-not-surjective square matrix over a field. Rank-nullity also controls the solution structure of linear systems: the homogeneous system $T(v) = \mathbf{0}$ has a unique solution iff $T$ is injective, and the inhomogeneous system $T(v) = w$ has a solution iff $w \in \mathrm{im}\, T$, with the set of all solutions being an affine translate of $\ker T$.
