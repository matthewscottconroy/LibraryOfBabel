# 6.3.2 Classifying Finite-Dimensional Vector Spaces up to Isomorphism

Classification theorems are among the most satisfying results in algebra: they give a complete, non-redundant list of all objects of a given type, up to the appropriate notion of equivalence. For finite-dimensional vector spaces, the classification is essentially immediate once we have the dimension theorem and the coordinate isomorphism — and it turns out to be as clean as possible.

\begin{theorem}[Classification of Finite-Dimensional Vector Spaces]
Two finite-dimensional vector spaces over the same field $F$ are isomorphic if and only if they have the same dimension.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
($\Rightarrow$) If$T: V \to W$ is a linear isomorphism, then$T$ is injective, so$\ker T = \{0\}$, so$\mathrm{null}(T) = 0$. By Rank-Nullity,$\mathrm{rank}(T) = \dim V$. Since$T$ is surjective,$\mathrm{im}\, T = W$, so$\dim W = \mathrm{rank}(T) = \dim V$.

($\Leftarrow$) Suppose$\dim V = \dim W = n$. By the coordinate isomorphism (Section 5.4.3),$V \cong F^n$ and$W \cong F^n$. By transitivity of isomorphism,$V \cong W$.
\end{proof}

The theorem says that dimension is the **only** invariant of a finite-dimensional vector space over $F$. There is nothing else to distinguish spaces beyond how many dimensions they have. For every natural number$n \geq 0$, there is exactly one isomorphism class of$n$-dimensional spaces over$F$, with representative$F^n$.

This is a dramatic contrast with more complicated algebraic structures:
- Finite groups of the same order can be non-isomorphic in many ways (e.g., $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \not\cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$).
- Rings of the same cardinality can differ wildly.
- Even commutative rings with the same additive structure can have different multiplicative structures.

The simplicity of the classification for vector spaces reflects how flexible the axioms are: scalar multiplication allows us to "rescale" and "shear" any basis into any other basis of the same size, removing all geometric rigidity.

\begin{corollary}
Every $n$-dimensional vector space over$F$ is isomorphic to$F^n$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}
A linear map $T: V \to W$ between finite-dimensional spaces of the same dimension$n$ is an isomorphism iff it is injective iff it is surjective.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}
The theorem also tells us that abstract vector spaces add no genuine generality beyond $F^n$ for finite-dimensional spaces. The value of working abstractly is not to escape$F^n$, but to reason about structures without committing to a particular coordinate system, so that results hold for all representations simultaneously.
\end{remark}

**Example 1.** $\mathbb{R}^3 \cong \mathcal{P}_3$ (the space of polynomials of degree at most$2$ — note the standard notation$\mathcal{P}_3$ for the$(3 = 2+1)$-dimensional polynomial space). Both have dimension$3$ over$\mathbb{R}$. An explicit isomorphism: choose any basis of each. With standard basis$(e_1, e_2, e_3)$ and monomial basis$(1, x, x^2)$, the map$T(ae_1 + be_2 + ce_3) = a + bx + cx^2$ is a linear isomorphism.

**Example 2.** $M_{2 \times 2}(\mathbb{R}) \cong \mathbb{R}^4 \cong F_{\leq 3}[x]$. All three spaces have dimension$4$. The three pairwise isomorphisms are:$\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix} \mapsto (a,b,c,d)$, and$(a,b,c,d) \mapsto a + bx + cx^2 + dx^3$.

**Example 3.** Show $\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^2, \mathbb{R}^3) \cong M_{3 \times 2}(\mathbb{R})$. Both have dimension$3 \cdot 2 = 6$ (by Section 6.1.3 and basic matrix counting). The isomorphism is the matrix representation$T \mapsto [T]_{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathcal{C}}$ for any choice of bases$\mathcal{B}$ on$\mathbb{R}^2$ and$\mathcal{C}$ on$\mathbb{R}^3$.

**Example 4.** Two maps from $\mathbb{R}^3$ to$\mathbb{R}^3$:$T_1(x,y,z) = (x, y, 0)$ (projection onto$xy$-plane) and$T_2(x,y,z) = (x+y, y+z, z+x)$. Are they isomorphic as objects in$\mathrm{End}(\mathbb{R}^3)$? This is not about isomorphism of the spaces (both are$\mathbb{R}^3$), but about similarity of the matrices — a finer equivalence relation studied in Section 7.4.2.

The classification theorem closes the question of what finite-dimensional vector spaces over $F$ look like: they are just$F^n$ for various$n$. The interesting mathematics from here on is about the maps between them — and the richer structures (bilinear forms, algebras, modules) built on top of them.

The simplicity of this classification is worth contrasting with the situation for groups, rings, and other algebraic structures. For groups, even among groups of order $p^2$ (prime-squared), there are exactly two isomorphism classes ($\mathbb{Z}/p^2\mathbb{Z}$ and$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$), and for larger orders the classification becomes intractable in general. For rings, the classification of simple Artinian rings by Wedderburn's theorem ($\cong M_n(D)$ for a division ring$D$) is already a nontrivial theorem. Vector spaces are uniquely simple because the scalar action allows any basis to be mapped to any other basis of the same size.

\begin{remark}[What the Theorem Does Not Say]
The theorem classifies vector spaces up to isomorphism, but it does not say that all $n$-dimensional spaces are the "same" in any absolute sense. Different vector spaces of the same dimension arise naturally in different contexts —$\mathbb{R}^3$ as physical space,$\mathbb{R}[x]_{\leq 2}$ as polynomial space,$M_{1 \times 3}(\mathbb{R})$ as row-vector space — and the isomorphisms between them require choices. The abstract statement is that these spaces are structurally identical; the practical statement is that any theorem about abstract$n$-dimensional vector spaces applies to all of them simultaneously.
\end{remark}

The classification also resolves a potential ambiguity in the definition of "dimension": since any two bases of a given vector space have the same size (Section 5.4.1), and since two spaces are isomorphic iff they have the same dimension, the dimension is a complete invariant. The pair (field $F$, dimension$n$) uniquely determines the isomorphism class of a finite-dimensional$F$-vector space.
