# 6.3.3 The Functor Perspective: $\mathcal{L}(V,W)$ Depends Contravariantly on$V$

We close this chapter with a forward-looking observation about how the space of linear maps $\mathcal{L}(V, W)$ responds to changes in$V$ and$W$. This behavior — covariant in one argument, contravariant in the other — is the algebraic origin of a pattern that reappears throughout mathematics, and it is the first hint of category theory in this book.

**Covariance in $W$.** Fix a linear map$g: W \to X$. There is an induced linear map

$$g_*: \mathcal{L}(V, W) \to \mathcal{L}(V, X), \quad g_*(T) = g \circ T.$$

This is **postcomposition with $g$**. One checks$g_*$ is linear:$g_*(aS + bT) = g \circ (aS + bT) = a(g \circ S) + b(g \circ T) = ag_*(S) + bg_*(T)$. Moreover, if$h: X \to Y$ is another linear map, then$(h \circ g)_* = h_* \circ g_*$ (postcomposition is functorial). We say$\mathcal{L}(V, -)$ is **covariant** in$W$: a map$W \to X$ in the same direction induces a map$\mathcal{L}(V,W) \to \mathcal{L}(V,X)$ in the same direction.

**Contravariance in $V$.** Now fix a linear map$f: U \to V$. There is an induced linear map

$$f^*: \mathcal{L}(V, W) \to \mathcal{L}(U, W), \quad f^*(T) = T \circ f.$$

This is **precomposition with $f$**. Again,$f^*$ is linear. But here the direction reverses:$f$ goes from$U$ to$V$, while$f^*$ goes from maps-out-of-$V$ to maps-out-of-$U$. Furthermore, if$e: Z \to U$ is another linear map, then$(f \circ e)^* = e^* \circ f^*$ — composition reverses. We say$\mathcal{L}(-, W)$ is **contravariant** in$V$.

\begin{remark}[Category Theory Language]
In the language of category theory, let $\mathbf{Vect}_F$ denote the category whose objects are$F$-vector spaces and whose morphisms are linear maps. Then:
\par
- $\mathcal{L}(V, -): \mathbf{Vect}_F \to \mathbf{Vect}_F$ is a \emph{covariant functor} (for each fixed$V$).
- $\mathcal{L}(-, W): \mathbf{Vect}_F^{\mathrm{op}} \to \mathbf{Vect}_F$ is a \emph{contravariant functor} (for each fixed$W$), where$\mathbf{Vect}_F^{\mathrm{op}}$ is the opposite category with all arrows reversed.
\par
The functor $\mathcal{L}(-, F) = (-)^* : \mathbf{Vect}_F^{\mathrm{op}} \to \mathbf{Vect}_F$ is the \emph{dual-space functor}, which sends each space$V$ to its dual$V^*$ and each linear map$f: V \to W$ to the transpose$f^*: W^* \to V^*$.
\end{remark}

The reversal of arrows under $f^*$ is not a quirk — it reflects a deep structural fact. In group theory, a homomorphism$\phi: G \to H$ induces a map on function spaces$\phi^*: \mathrm{Hom}(H, A) \to \mathrm{Hom}(G, A)$ for any abelian group$A$, in the opposite direction. In topology, a continuous map$f: X \to Y$ induces a map on cohomology$f^*: H^n(Y) \to H^n(X)$ (this is why cohomology is contravariant). In algebraic geometry, a morphism of varieties$f: X \to Y$ induces a pullback$f^*: \mathcal{O}(Y) \to \mathcal{O}(X)$ on functions. Each of these is a manifestation of the same underlying categorical principle: applying a "hom functor" in the first argument reverses the direction of morphisms.

**Example 1.** Let $f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ be$f(x,y) = (x, y, 0)$ (embedding into the$xy$-plane). Then$f^*: \mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^3, \mathbb{R}) \to \mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^2, \mathbb{R})$ is given by$f^*(\phi) = \phi \circ f$. Any linear functional on$\mathbb{R}^3$ pulls back to a linear functional on$\mathbb{R}^2$: if$\phi(x,y,z) = ax + by + cz$, then$f^*(\phi)(x,y) = ax + by + 0 = ax + by$. The$z$-component of$\phi$ is killed by$f^*$.

**Example 2.** Let $g: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ be$g(x,y) = (x, x+y, y)$. The induced map$g^*: (\mathbb{R}^3)^* \to (\mathbb{R}^2)^*$ sends the functional$\phi_{ijk}(x,y,z) = ix + jy + kz$ to$(g^* \phi_{ijk})(x,y) = ix + j(x+y) + ky = (i+j)x + (j+k)y$. In matrix language,$g^*$ corresponds to the transpose$g^T = \begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix}^T = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ acting on column vectors.

**Example 3.** The dual-space functor $(-)^*$ sends$V$ to$V^* = \mathcal{L}(V, F)$. For finite-dimensional spaces,$\dim V^* = \dim V$ (by the dimension formula of Section 6.1.3 with$W = F$,$\dim W = 1$, so$\dim V^* = n \cdot 1 = n$). Thus$V \cong V^*$ as abstract vector spaces, but not canonically — a linear isomorphism$V \to V^*$ requires a choice of basis or inner product. The functor$(-)^{**}$ (double dual) has a canonical isomorphism$V \cong V^{**}$ for finite-dimensional spaces: the map$v \mapsto (\phi \mapsto \phi(v))$ is natural and requires no choices.

We will encounter the dual space and the contravariant perspective repeatedly in the chapters on multilinear algebra, tensor products, and cohomology. For now, the key takeaway is that the asymmetry between the two arguments of $\mathcal{L}(V,W)$ is not accidental but is a fundamental feature of the theory.
