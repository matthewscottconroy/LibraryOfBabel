# 6.3 Isomorphisms

An isomorphism of vector spaces is a bijective linear map. Being bijective means being both injective and surjective — equivalently, having trivial kernel and full image — but in the linear-algebraic context something extra is true: the inverse of a bijective linear map is automatically linear, too. This is not obvious from the definition of linearity, and the section proves it as a theorem: if $T: V \to W$ is a bijective linear map, then the set-theoretic inverse $T^{-1}: W \to V$ satisfies the linearity conditions. The key is that the inverse inherits linearity from $T$ by "pushing through" the linearity equations.

The payoff is the classification theorem: two finite-dimensional vector spaces over the same field $F$ are isomorphic if and only if they have the same dimension. The "only if" direction is easy — isomorphic spaces must have the same dimension because isomorphisms carry bases to bases. The "if" direction is constructive: given bases $\mathcal{B}$ and $\mathcal{C}$ of $V$ and $W$ respectively, with $|\mathcal{B}| = |\mathcal{C}| = n$, pair up the basis elements and define a bijection $T(b_i) = c_i$; by the existence-uniqueness theorem for linear maps, this extends to an isomorphism. The conclusion is striking: up to isomorphism, there is exactly one $n$-dimensional vector space over $F$ for each non-negative integer $n$, and it is $F^n$. Abstract vector spaces are not structurally richer than coordinate spaces — they are just coordinate spaces in disguise, waiting for a basis to be chosen.

The section also raises an important subtlety: the isomorphism between $V$ and $F^n$ is not canonical. Different choices of basis give different isomorphisms, and no one basis is intrinsically preferred unless additional structure (such as an inner product) is present. This basis-dependence is not a defect but a feature — it reflects the genuine flexibility in how we coordinatize a space, and understanding how isomorphisms change with the choice of basis is exactly the problem of change of basis, treated in Chapter 7.

The section closes with a first look at the dual space $V^* = \mathcal{L}(V, F)$, the vector space of linear functionals on $V$. Since $\dim V^* = \dim V$ (by the formula $\dim \mathcal{L}(V, W) = (\dim V)(\dim W)$ applied with $\dim W = 1$), the dual is isomorphic to $V$ in finite dimensions — but not canonically so. The existence of a natural isomorphism $V \to V^{**}$ (to the double dual) but only an unnatural one $V \to V^*$ is the prototype of the categorical distinction between natural and unnatural transformations, a distinction that becomes rigorous in the language of functors developed in Chapter 12.
