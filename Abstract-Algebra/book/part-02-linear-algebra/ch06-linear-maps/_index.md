# Chapter 6 — Linear Maps

Having defined vector spaces and studied their internal structure, the natural next step is to study the functions between them. Not all functions are interesting from the linear-algebraic point of view — only those that respect the two operations of the vector space, namely addition and scalar multiplication. A function $T: V \to W$ is linear if $T(u + v) = T(u) + T(v)$ and $T(cv) = cT(v)$ for all vectors $u, v$ and all scalars $c$. These two conditions can be combined into the single requirement $T(c_1 u + c_2 v) = c_1 T(u) + c_2 T(v)$, which says that $T$ distributes over linear combinations. Linear maps are the morphisms of the category of vector spaces, and the entire machinery of linear algebra — matrices, determinants, eigenvalues — is ultimately the study of these maps and their properties.

The first section of the chapter establishes the basic language and examples. Differentiation, integration, projection, rotation, and reflection are all linear. So is the zero map and the identity map. The collection $\mathcal{L}(V, W)$ of all linear maps from $V$ to $W$ is itself a vector space, under pointwise addition and scalar multiplication, and when $V = W$ it is even a ring under composition — the ring of endomorphisms $\mathrm{End}(V)$. A linear map is entirely determined by its values on a basis: if $\mathcal{B} = (b_1, \ldots, b_n)$ is a basis for $V$ and we prescribe $T(b_i) = w_i$ arbitrarily in $W$, there is a unique linear map extending this assignment. This is both a tool for constructing linear maps and a sign that bases and linear maps are deeply intertwined.

The chapter's central theorem is the rank-nullity theorem: for any linear map $T: V \to W$ between finite-dimensional spaces, $\dim V = \dim(\ker T) + \dim(\mathrm{im}\, T)$. The kernel $\ker T = \{v \in V : T(v) = 0\}$ measures how much of the domain is "collapsed" to zero, and the image $\mathrm{im}\, T = \{T(v) : v \in V\}$ measures how much of the codomain is actually reached. The rank-nullity theorem says these two quantities always add up to the full dimension of the domain: what is lost in the kernel is exactly compensated by what is gained in rank. The theorem is a special case of the first isomorphism theorem for modules, and the proof — constructing a basis for $V$ by combining a basis for $\ker T$ with preimages of a basis for $\mathrm{im}\, T$ — gives a concrete geometric picture of the decomposition.

Isomorphisms — bijective linear maps — occupy the chapter's final section. An isomorphism is an invertible linear map, and the section proves that the inverse of a linear isomorphism is automatically linear. The classification theorem follows: two finite-dimensional vector spaces over the same field $F$ are isomorphic if and only if they have the same dimension. This is the sense in which the dimension is a complete invariant — up to isomorphism, there is exactly one $n$-dimensional vector space over $F$ for each $n$. The chapter also takes a first look at the dual space $V^* = \mathcal{L}(V, F)$ and the natural transformation perspective on linear maps, previewing the functorial ideas that will become central in Chapters 12 and beyond.
