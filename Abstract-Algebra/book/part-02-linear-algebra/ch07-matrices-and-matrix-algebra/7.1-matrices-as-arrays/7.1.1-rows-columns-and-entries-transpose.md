# 7.1.1 Rows, Columns, and Entries; Transpose

Before we can do anything with matrices, we need a precise language for talking about them. A **matrix** $A \in M_{m \times n}(F)$ is a rectangular array of elements of a field$F$, arranged in$m$ rows and$n$ columns. We write$A_{ij}$ (or$a_{ij}$) for the entry in row$i$ and column$j$, where$1 \leq i \leq m$ and$1 \leq j \leq n$. The matrix$A$ is entirely determined by its$mn$ entries. We often write$A = (A_{ij})$ as shorthand.

For example, a $2 \times 3$ matrix over$\mathbb{R}$ looks like

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} A_{11} & A_{12} & A_{13} \\ A_{21} & A_{22} & A_{23} \end{pmatrix},$$

and a column vector in $F^n$ is an$n \times 1$ matrix, while a row vector is a$1 \times n$ matrix.

\begin{definition}
The **transpose** of $A \in M_{m \times n}(F)$, written$A^T$, is the$n \times m$ matrix defined by
\par
$$(A^T)_{ij} = A_{ji}.$$
\par
Equivalently, the rows of $A$ become the columns of$A^T$, and the columns of$A$ become the rows of$A^T$.
\end{definition}

The transpose satisfies $(A^T)^T = A$ and is an involution on square matrices. Several matrix families are defined by their behavior under the transpose.

\begin{definition}
A square matrix $A \in M_{n \times n}(F)$ is:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{symmetric} if $A^T = A$ (equivalently,$A_{ij} = A_{ji}$ for all$i,j$);
\item \textbf{skew-symmetric} (or anti-symmetric) if $A^T = -A$ (equivalently,$A_{ij} = -A_{ji}$, which forces$A_{ii} = 0$).
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

**Example 1.** Compute the transpose of $A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6\end{pmatrix}$. The transpose is$A^T = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 4 \\ 2 & 5 \\ 3 & 6\end{pmatrix}$, a$3 \times 2$ matrix. The first row of$A$ becomes the first column of$A^T$, and so on.

**Example 2.** A symmetric matrix: $A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 5 & 6 \\ 3 & 6 & 9\end{pmatrix}$. Check:$A_{12} = 2 = A_{21}$,$A_{13} = 3 = A_{31}$,$A_{23} = 6 = A_{32}$. A skew-symmetric matrix:$B = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 & -2 \\ -1 & 0 & 3 \\ 2 & -3 & 0\end{pmatrix}$. Check: diagonal entries are all zero, and$B_{12} = 1 = -B_{21}$, etc.

**Example 3.** Transpose of a column vector: if $v = \begin{pmatrix}x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3\end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^3$, then$v^T = \begin{pmatrix}x_1 & x_2 & x_3\end{pmatrix}$ is a row vector. The dot product of$v$ and$w$ can be written as$v^T w$ (a$1 \times 3$ matrix times a$3 \times 1$ matrix, yielding a$1 \times 1$ "matrix," i.e., a scalar).

The transpose is a linear operation on $M_{m \times n}(F)$:$(aA + bB)^T = aA^T + bB^T$. It also satisfies the fundamental identity$(AB)^T = B^T A^T$ (proved in Section 7.2.3), which shows that the transpose reverses the order of matrix multiplication — a pattern ubiquitous in algebra (just as$(ab)^{-1} = b^{-1}a^{-1}$ in groups).

\begin{proposition}
Every square matrix $A$ decomposes uniquely as$A = S + K$ where$S$ is symmetric and$K$ is skew-symmetric.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Set $S = \frac{A + A^T}{2}$ and$K = \frac{A - A^T}{2}$. Then$S^T = \frac{A^T + A}{2} = S$ and$K^T = \frac{A^T - A}{2} = -K$, and$S + K = A$. Uniqueness follows because the space of symmetric and skew-symmetric matrices intersect only in$\{0\}$.
\end{proof}

This decomposition is the simplest instance of a recurring theme: decomposing a space according to the eigenvalues of an involution. Here, the involution $A \mapsto A^T$ has eigenvalues$\pm 1$, and the symmetric matrices are the$+1$-eigenspace while the skew-symmetric ones are the$-1$-eigenspace. The dimensions are$\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$ and$\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ respectively, summing to$n^2 = \dim M_{n \times n}(F)$, consistent with the direct sum decomposition.

**Example 4.** Decompose $A = \begin{pmatrix}4 & 1 \\ 7 & 2\end{pmatrix}$ into symmetric and skew-symmetric parts:$S = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}8 & 8 \\ 8 & 4\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}4 & 4 \\ 4 & 2\end{pmatrix}$ and$K = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0 & -6 \\ 6 & 0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -3 \\ 3 & 0\end{pmatrix}$. Check:$S + K = \begin{pmatrix}4&1\\7&2\end{pmatrix}$.

**Example 5.** Subspaces defined by transpose conditions: in $M_{2 \times 2}(\mathbb{R})$, the set of symmetric matrices has basis$\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\right\}$ (dimension$3$), and the skew-symmetric matrices have basis$\left\{\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}\right\}$ (dimension$1$). Their dimensions sum to$4 = \dim M_{2 \times 2}(\mathbb{R})$.

The notation for matrix entries and the transpose convention are not merely bookkeeping. The entry $A_{ij}$ (row index first, column index second) is the standard convention in linear algebra; some texts in physics or applied mathematics use the transposed convention$A_{ji}$, which can cause confusion. Throughout this book we use the standard convention, consistent with the rule that the matrix of a linear map$T: F^n \to F^m$ is an$m \times n$ matrix with$(i,j)$ entry equal to the$i$-th coordinate of$T(e_j)$.

The transpose also interacts with the inner product structure on $\mathbb{R}^n$:$\langle Ax, y \rangle = \langle x, A^T y \rangle$ for all$x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and$y \in \mathbb{R}^m$. This identity — that the transpose is the "adjoint" of$A$ with respect to the standard inner product — motivates the generalization to the conjugate transpose$A^* = \overline{A^T}$ for complex matrices, which is the adjoint with respect to the Hermitian inner product. Symmetric matrices generalize to self-adjoint (Hermitian) matrices$A^* = A$ in the complex setting, which play a central role in quantum mechanics and spectral theory.
