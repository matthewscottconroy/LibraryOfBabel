# 7.1.3 Symmetric, Skew-Symmetric, Diagonal, and Triangular Matrices

Certain patterns of zeros and symmetry recur so often in matrix theory that it is worth naming them carefully. These structural classes — symmetric, skew-symmetric, diagonal, triangular — are not merely a catalog; they are the building blocks around which much of the theory is organized.

**Symmetric and skew-symmetric matrices** have already appeared in Section 7.1.1. The space of $n \times n$ symmetric matrices has dimension$\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$: we may freely choose the$n$ diagonal entries and the$\binom{n}{2}$ entries strictly above the diagonal, with the entries below the diagonal then determined by$A_{ji} = A_{ij}$. The space of skew-symmetric matrices has dimension$\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$: the diagonal must be zero and we freely choose the strictly upper-triangular entries, with the lower half determined by$A_{ji} = -A_{ij}$.

\begin{proposition}
Every square matrix $A \in M_{n \times n}(F)$ with$\mathrm{char}(F) \neq 2$ decomposes uniquely as$A = S + K$ where$S = \frac{A+A^T}{2}$ is symmetric and$K = \frac{A-A^T}{2}$ is skew-symmetric.
\end{proposition}

The splitting $M_{n \times n}(F) = \mathrm{Sym}_n(F) \oplus \mathrm{Skew}_n(F)$ is the eigenspace decomposition of the involution$A \mapsto A^T$ with eigenvalues$+1$ and$-1$.

\begin{definition}
A square matrix $A \in M_{n \times n}(F)$ is:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{diagonal} if $A_{ij} = 0$ for all$i \neq j$. We write$A = \mathrm{diag}(a_1, \ldots, a_n)$ where$a_i = A_{ii}$.
\item \textbf{upper triangular} if $A_{ij} = 0$ for all$i > j$ (all entries below the main diagonal are zero).
\item \textbf{lower triangular} if $A_{ij} = 0$ for all$i < j$ (all entries above the main diagonal are zero).
\item \textbf{upper (resp. lower) unitriangular} if it is upper (resp. lower) triangular with $1$'s on the diagonal.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

The simplest fact about triangular matrices: the determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal entries (a fact we will prove carefully in Chapter 8). This makes triangular matrices computationally convenient — the eigenvalues are visible on the diagonal.

\begin{proposition}
The product of two upper triangular matrices is upper triangular. Similarly for lower triangular.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If $A$ and$B$ are upper triangular, then$(AB)_{ij} = \sum_k A_{ik} B_{kj}$. For$i > j$: if$k \leq i-1$ then$k < i$ so$A_{ik} = 0$ (since$k < i$ and$A$ is upper triangular means$A_{ik} = 0$ only when$i > k$); if$k \geq i$ then$k \geq i > j$ so$B_{kj} = 0$ (since$k > j$). Hence$(AB)_{ij} = 0$ for$i > j$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
The set of $n \times n$ upper triangular matrices with entries in$F$ forms a subalgebra of$M_{n \times n}(F)$.
\end{corollary}

**Example 1.** A diagonal matrix: $A = \mathrm{diag}(2, -1, 3) = \begin{pmatrix}2&0&0\\0&-1&0\\0&0&3\end{pmatrix}$. Multiplication by a diagonal matrix scales rows:$A \cdot B$ multiplies the$i$-th row of$B$ by$a_i$. Multiplication on the right scales columns.

**Example 2.** An upper triangular matrix:
$$U = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 4 & 5 \\ 0 & 0 & 6\end{pmatrix}.$$

The determinant is $1 \cdot 4 \cdot 6 = 24$. The$LU$ decomposition expresses any invertible matrix as a product of a lower triangular and an upper triangular matrix, which is what Gaussian elimination produces.

**Example 3.** Decompose $A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 3 \\ 5 & 2\end{pmatrix}$ into symmetric and skew-symmetric parts:
$$S = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1+1 & 3+5 \\ 5+3 & 2+2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 4 \\ 4 & 2\end{pmatrix}, \quad K = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0 & 3-5 \\ 5-3 & 0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}.$$
Check: $S + K = \begin{pmatrix}1&3\\5&2\end{pmatrix} = A$. Indeed$S^T = S$ and$K^T = -K$.

**Example 4.** Products of triangular matrices: $\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}4&5\\0&6\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}4&17\\0&18\end{pmatrix}$, which is again upper triangular with diagonal entries$4 \cdot 1 = 4$ (no, actually$1 \cdot 4 = 4$) and$3 \cdot 6 = 18$ — i.e., the products of the corresponding diagonal entries.

These matrix classes are not just convenient notation; they arise naturally throughout the theory. Row reduction produces upper triangular matrices; the LU decomposition produces a lower and an upper triangular factor; the Schur triangularization theorem says every square matrix over $\mathbb{C}$ is similar to an upper triangular matrix; and the diagonal matrices are exactly the simplest case, corresponding to operators that are simultaneously diagonalizable.
