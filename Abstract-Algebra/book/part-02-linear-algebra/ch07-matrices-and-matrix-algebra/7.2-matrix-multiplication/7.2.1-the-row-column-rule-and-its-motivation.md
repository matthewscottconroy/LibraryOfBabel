# 7.2.1 The Row–Column Rule and Its Motivation

Matrix multiplication is not defined componentwise — and the reason why is instructive. We want matrices to encode linear maps, and we want the product of two matrices to correspond to the composition of the corresponding linear maps. That requirement turns out to force a very specific definition, which is exactly the row–column rule.

\begin{definition}
For $A \in M_{m \times n}(F)$ and$B \in M_{n \times p}(F)$ — note that the number of columns of$A$ must equal the number of rows of$B$ — the **matrix product**$AB$ is the$m \times p$ matrix defined by
\par
$$(AB)_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} A_{ik} B_{kj}.$$
\par
This is the **dot product of the $i$-th row of$A$ with the$j$-th column of$B$**.
\end{definition}

The dimension requirement ($A$ is$m \times n$,$B$ is$n \times p$) is essential: the shared inner dimension$n$ is the one that is "contracted" in the sum and disappears from the output. A helpful mnemonic:$(m \times n)(n \times p) = m \times p$.

To see why this is the right definition, think of the columns of $B$ as vectors$b_1, \ldots, b_p \in F^n$. Then$AB$ is the matrix whose$j$-th column is$Ab_j$ — the result of applying the linear transformation encoded by$A$ to the vector$b_j$. In other words,$AB = [Ab_1 \mid Ab_2 \mid \cdots \mid Ab_p]$. This makes the connection to linear maps transparent:$A$ represents a linear map$F^n \to F^m$, and$B$ represents another linear map$F^p \to F^n$ (its columns tell us where the standard basis vectors of$F^p$ go), so$AB$ represents their composition$F^p \to F^n \to F^m$.

\begin{proposition}[Distributivity and Associativity]
Matrix multiplication distributes over addition and is associative wherever defined:
\begin{align*}
A(B + C) &= AB + AC, \\
(A + B)C &= AC + BC, \\
(AB)C &= A(BC).
\end{align*}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
All three follow from direct computation using the row–column rule, or more elegantly from the fact that composition of functions is associative and distributes over pointwise addition (since both sides represent the same composed linear maps).
\end{proof}

**Example 1.** Compute $AB$ for$A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4\end{pmatrix}$ and$B = \begin{pmatrix}5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8\end{pmatrix}$:

$$(AB)_{11} = 1\cdot5 + 2\cdot7 = 19, \quad (AB)_{12} = 1\cdot6 + 2\cdot8 = 22,$$
$$(AB)_{21} = 3\cdot5 + 4\cdot7 = 43, \quad (AB)_{22} = 3\cdot6 + 4\cdot8 = 50.$$

So $AB = \begin{pmatrix}19 & 22 \\ 43 & 50\end{pmatrix}$.

**Example 2.** Compute $AB$ for$A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 & -1 \\ 2 & 1 & 0\end{pmatrix} \in M_{2 \times 3}(\mathbb{R})$ and$B = \begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 2 \\ 3\end{pmatrix} \in M_{3 \times 1}(\mathbb{R})$:

$$AB = \begin{pmatrix}1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 2 + (-1) \cdot 3 \\ 2 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 2 + 0 \cdot 3\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1 - 3 \\ 2 + 2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-2 \\ 4\end{pmatrix}.$$

This is the matrix-vector product $Ab$ for$b = (1,2,3)^T$, which computes where the linear map sends the vector$b$.

**Example 3.** Composition of rotations: the rotation matrices $R_\theta = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$ satisfy$R_\theta R_\phi = R_{\theta+\phi}$. Verify for$\theta = \phi = \pi/2$ (both$= \begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$):

$$R_{\pi/2}^2 = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1\end{pmatrix} = R_\pi.$$

This confirms that two $90°$ rotations compose to a$180°$ rotation.

**Example 4.** The row–column rule from the linear-map perspective: if $T: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ has matrix$A = \begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix}$ and$S: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ has matrix$B = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&3\end{pmatrix}$, the composition$S \circ T$ has matrix$BA = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2&1&3\\0&3&3\end{pmatrix}$. The product$BA$ encodes: first apply$T$ (sending$\mathbb{R}^3$ to$\mathbb{R}^2$), then apply$S$ (sending$\mathbb{R}^2$ to$\mathbb{R}^2$).

There is also a useful **column interpretation** of the product $AB$: the$j$-th column of$AB$ is$A$ times the$j$-th column of$B$. This makes it easy to see the effect of multiplication when$B$ is given as a list of column vectors. Symmetrically, the$i$-th row of$AB$ is the$i$-th row of$A$ times$B$ — so multiplication also distributes over rows in the other argument.

A third useful perspective: if we write $A$ as a list of row vectors$A = \begin{pmatrix}a_1^T \\ \vdots \\ a_m^T\end{pmatrix}$ and$B$ as a list of column vectors$B = [b_1 \mid \cdots \mid b_p]$, then$(AB)_{ij} = a_i^T b_j$, the dot product of the$i$-th row of$A$ with the$j$-th column of$B$. There is also a useful **outer product** expansion:$AB = \sum_{k=1}^{n} (\text{$k$-th column of$A$})(\text{$k$-th row of$B$})$, where each term is a rank-1 matrix. This sum-of-rank-1-matrices decomposition is the foundation of many algorithms in numerical linear algebra and data science.

\begin{remark}
The dimension requirement for matrix multiplication — the inner dimensions must match — is not a convention but a necessity. In order for the product $AB$ to encode the composition of linear maps,$A$ must transform the output space of$B$. If$A$ is$m \times n$ (mapping$F^n \to F^m$) and$B$ is$n \times p$ (mapping$F^p \to F^n$), then$AB$ maps$F^p \to F^m$ via$F^p \xrightarrow{B} F^n \xrightarrow{A} F^m$. The shared$n$ is consumed in the composition and does not appear in the output dimensions$m \times p$.
\end{remark}
