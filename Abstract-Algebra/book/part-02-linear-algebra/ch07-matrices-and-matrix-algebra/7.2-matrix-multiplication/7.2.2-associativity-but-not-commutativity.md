# 7.2.2 Associativity but Not Commutativity

Matrix multiplication is associative but not commutative. These two properties together place matrix algebras among the most important examples of non-commutative rings — structures where the order of multiplication matters and cannot be freely rearranged.

\begin{theorem}[Associativity of Matrix Multiplication]
Whenever the dimensions are compatible, $(AB)C = A(BC)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Both sides have $(i,j)$ entry equal to

$$\sum_{k,\ell} A_{ik} B_{k\ell} C_{\ell j}.$$

For $(AB)C$:$\bigl((AB)C\bigr)_{ij} = \sum_\ell (AB)_{i\ell} C_{\ell j} = \sum_\ell \bigl(\sum_k A_{ik}B_{k\ell}\bigr) C_{\ell j} = \sum_{k,\ell} A_{ik}B_{k\ell}C_{\ell j}$.

For $A(BC)$:$\bigl(A(BC)\bigr)_{ij} = \sum_k A_{ik}(BC)_{kj} = \sum_k A_{ik}\bigl(\sum_\ell B_{k\ell}C_{\ell j}\bigr) = \sum_{k,\ell} A_{ik}B_{k\ell}C_{\ell j}$.

Both sums are equal.
\end{proof}

Associativity also follows abstractly from the fact that composition of functions is always associative: $(f \circ g) \circ h = f \circ (g \circ h)$ for any functions with compatible domains and codomains.

The situation with commutativity is quite different. For non-square matrices $A \in M_{m \times n}(F)$ and$B \in M_{n \times p}(F)$ with$m \neq p$, the product$AB$ is defined but$BA$ is not (dimensions are incompatible). Even for square matrices of the same size,$AB$ and$BA$ are generally unequal.

\begin{example}
Let $A = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ and$B = \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$. Then
\par
$$AB = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}, \quad BA = \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.$$
\par
These are different: $AB \neq BA$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
A rotation $R_\theta$ and a reflection$M = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$ (reflection across the$x$-axis) in$\mathbb{R}^2$ do not commute for most$\theta$:$R_\theta M \neq M R_\theta$ unless$\theta$ is a multiple of$\pi$.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
In the theory of groups and rings, commutativity is an extra assumption, not a default. The matrix algebra $M_{n \times n}(F)$ is a ring (under addition and multiplication), but not a commutative ring for$n \geq 2$. The study of non-commutative rings — including matrix algebras, group algebras, and universal enveloping algebras — is one of the major themes of modern algebra.
\par
The failure of commutativity is not a defect but a feature. In the physical world, the order in which you apply operations can matter: rotating an object and then translating it yields a different result from translating first and then rotating. The algebra of linear maps correctly captures this order-dependence.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
For square matrices $A, B \in M_{n \times n}(F)$, the **commutator** is$[A, B] = AB - BA$.
\end{definition}

The commutator measures the failure of commutativity: $AB = BA$ iff$[A,B] = 0$. The commutator is bilinear and antisymmetric:$[A,B] = -[B,A]$. It also satisfies the **Jacobi identity**:$[A,[B,C]] + [B,[C,A]] + [C,[A,B]] = 0$. These properties make$M_{n \times n}(F)$ into a **Lie algebra** under the bracket$[A,B]$, denoted$\mathfrak{gl}_n(F)$. Lie algebras are the infinitesimal version of Lie groups and play a central role in physics and differential geometry.

**Example 1.** Verify associativity: $(AB)C$ where$A = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$,$B = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$,$C = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$. Compute$AB = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$, then$(AB)C = \begin{pmatrix}4&4\\2&3\end{pmatrix}$. Also$BC = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\2&3\end{pmatrix}$, then$A(BC) = \begin{pmatrix}4&4\\2&3\end{pmatrix}$. They agree.

**Example 2.** Non-commutativity with diagonal matrices: $\mathrm{diag}(1,2) \cdot \begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}a&b\\2c&2d\end{pmatrix}$ (scales rows), while$\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix} \cdot \mathrm{diag}(1,2) = \begin{pmatrix}a&2b\\c&2d\end{pmatrix}$ (scales columns). These are equal only if$b = 2b$ and$2c = c$, i.e.,$b = c = 0$.

**Example 3.** The center of $M_{n \times n}(F)$: the matrices that commute with all others are precisely the scalar multiples of$I_n$. To see this, suppose$A$ commutes with$E_{ij}$ for all$i,j$:$AE_{ij} = E_{ij}A$. Computing:$(AE_{ij})_{k\ell} = A_{ki}\delta_{j\ell}$, so the$(k,j)$ entry of$AE_{ij}$ is$A_{ki}$. On the other hand,$(E_{ij}A)_{k\ell} = \delta_{ki}A_{j\ell}$, so the$(i,j)$ entry of$E_{ij}A$ is$A_{jj}$ and the$(k,j)$ entry is$0$ for$k \neq i$. Setting$k \neq i$:$A_{ki} = 0$, so$A$ is diagonal. Setting$k = i$ and comparing the$(i,j)$-entries:$A_{ii} = A_{jj}$ for all$i,j$. So all diagonal entries are equal, meaning$A = \lambda I_n$ for some scalar$\lambda$.

**Example 4.** The commutator $[A,B] = AB - BA$ for$A = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ and$B = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$:$AB = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix} = B$ and$BA = \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix} = 0$. So$[A,B] = B - 0 = B$. This computation is one of the defining relations of the Lie algebra$\mathfrak{gl}_2$:$[E_{11}, E_{12}] = E_{12}$,$[E_{11}, E_{21}] = -E_{21}$,$[E_{12}, E_{21}] = E_{11} - E_{22}$.

These examples make clear that the non-commutativity of matrix multiplication is not an obstacle but rather the source of structure. The study of how much two elements fail to commute — captured by the commutator — is the foundation of Lie theory, quantum mechanics (where observables are matrices and $[A,B] \neq 0$ reflects the Heisenberg uncertainty principle), and much of modern algebra.
