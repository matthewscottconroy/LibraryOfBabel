# 7.2 Matrix Multiplication

Matrix multiplication is the operation that makes matrices more than a bookkeeping device. Given an $m \times n$ matrix $A$ and an $n \times p$ matrix $B$, their product $AB$ is the $m \times p$ matrix whose $(i,j)$ entry is the dot product of the $i$-th row of $A$ with the $j$-th column of $B$: $(AB)_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^n a_{ik} b_{kj}$. The inner dimension $n$ must match, and the outer dimensions $m$ and $p$ determine the size of the result. The definition looks arbitrary until Section 7.3 reveals that it is the unique rule making composition of linear maps correspond to multiplication of their matrices.

The section carefully verifies the algebraic properties. Associativity holds: $(AB)C = A(BC)$ whenever the dimensions are compatible. This follows from the associativity of composition of linear maps, and can also be verified directly by computation, with the inner sum rearranging. Distributivity over addition holds in both arguments. What fails, conspicuously, is commutativity: $AB$ and $BA$ are defined only when $A$ and $B$ are both square (same size), and even then $AB \neq BA$ in general. Non-commutativity is not a defect of the definition but a faithful reflection of the fact that the composition of most transformations — e.g., rotation followed by projection is not the same as projection followed by rotation — depends on the order. The section gives explicit $2 \times 2$ examples and encourages the reader to seek geometric interpretations.

The identity matrix $I_n$ is the $n \times n$ matrix with ones on the main diagonal and zeros elsewhere. It satisfies $AI_n = A$ and $I_m A = A$ for any $m \times n$ matrix $A$, playing the role of the multiplicative identity in the ring of square matrices. The relationship between the transpose and multiplication is the reversal rule $(AB)^T = B^T A^T$: transposing a product reverses the order. This is the matrix-level expression of the fact that the adjoint of a composition is the reversed composition of adjoints, a theme revisited in Chapter 11.

The section also introduces block matrix multiplication, showing that when matrices are partitioned into blocks of compatible sizes, the blocks multiply by the same row-column rule as scalar entries — as long as the sub-block multiplications are defined. Block multiplication is more than a notational convenience: it is a computational strategy that reduces large problems to smaller ones, and it is the correct framework for analyzing matrices whose structure decomposes into direct sums.
