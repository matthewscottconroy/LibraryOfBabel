# 7.3.3 The Isomorphism $\mathcal{L}(V,W) \cong M_{m \times n}(F)$

The constructions of the previous two subsections fit together to give a clean structural statement: the space of linear maps and the space of matrices are not merely in bijection — they are isomorphic as vector spaces, and the isomorphism intertwines composition with matrix multiplication.

\begin{theorem}
Fix ordered bases $\mathcal{B} = (b_1, \ldots, b_n)$ on$V$ and$\mathcal{C} = (c_1, \ldots, c_m)$ on$W$. The map
\par
$$\Phi: \mathcal{L}(V, W) \to M_{m \times n}(F), \quad \Phi(T) = [T]_{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathcal{C}},$$
\par
is a vector space isomorphism.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
*Linearity:* The $j$-th column of$[S + T]_{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathcal{C}}$ is$[(S+T)(b_j)]_{\mathcal{C}} = [S(b_j) + T(b_j)]_{\mathcal{C}} = [S(b_j)]_{\mathcal{C}} + [T(b_j)]_{\mathcal{C}}$, which is the sum of the$j$-th columns of$[S]$ and$[T]$. Hence$[S + T]_{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathcal{C}} = [S]_{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathcal{C}} + [T]_{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathcal{C}}$. Similarly$[cT]_{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathcal{C}} = c[T]_{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathcal{C}}$.

*Injectivity:* If $[T]_{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathcal{C}} = 0$, then$[T(b_j)]_{\mathcal{C}} = 0$ for every$j$, so$T(b_j) = 0$ for every basis vector$b_j$. Since$T$ is determined by its values on a basis,$T = 0$.

*Surjectivity:* Given any $A \in M_{m \times n}(F)$, define$T: V \to W$ by$T(b_j) = \sum_i A_{ij} c_i$ for each$j$, and extend linearly. Then$[T]_{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathcal{C}} = A$ by construction.
\end{proof}

Notice that this isomorphism is between vector spaces only: it respects addition and scalar multiplication. The additional compatibility with multiplication — that composition of maps corresponds to matrix multiplication — is the content of Section 7.3.2. Together they give:

\begin{corollary}
The map $\Phi: \mathrm{End}(V) \to M_{n \times n}(F)$,$\Phi(T) = [T]_{\mathcal{B}}$, is an isomorphism of$F$-algebras: it respects addition, scalar multiplication, and multiplication (composition$\leftrightarrow$ matrix product), and sends the identity map to the identity matrix.
\end{corollary}

This means $\mathrm{End}(V) \cong M_{n \times n}(F)$ as rings (and as$F$-algebras). Every structural property of$\mathrm{End}(V)$ — its invertible elements, its center, its ideals — corresponds to the same property of$M_{n \times n}(F)$.

\begin{remark}
The isomorphism $\Phi$ depends on the choice of bases$\mathcal{B}$ and$\mathcal{C}$. Different bases give different isomorphisms, related by the change-of-basis transformation (Section 7.4). The abstract algebra$\mathrm{End}(V)$ is intrinsic to$V$; the specific matrix algebra$M_{n \times n}(F)$ is a coordinate presentation of it.
\end{remark}

**Example 1.** $\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^2, \mathbb{R}^2) \cong M_{2 \times 2}(\mathbb{R})$ via the standard basis. The map$T(x,y) = (ax+by, cx+dy)$ corresponds to$\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$. Addition of maps corresponds to addition of matrices, scaling of a map corresponds to scaling of a matrix. Composition of maps:$(T_1 \circ T_2)(x,y)$ corresponds to$[T_1][T_2]$.

**Example 2.** $\mathcal{L}(F_{\leq 2}[x], F_{\leq 1}[x]) \cong M_{2 \times 3}(F)$ via the monomial bases. Differentiation$D$ corresponds to$\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&2\end{pmatrix}$ (from Section 7.3.1). The "double differentiation" map$D^2: F_{\leq 3}[x] \to F_{\leq 1}[x]$ has matrix$\begin{pmatrix}0&0&2&0\\0&0&0&6\end{pmatrix}\cdot$... more carefully,$D^2(1)=0$,$D^2(x) = 0$,$D^2(x^2) = 2$,$D^2(x^3) = 6x$, giving$[D^2] = \begin{pmatrix}0&0&2&0\\0&0&0&6\end{pmatrix}$.

**Example 3.** To compute with linear maps, pick convenient bases, convert to matrices, compute, and convert back. For example, if $T: V \to V$ is an endomorphism and we want to compute$T^3$, we compute$[T]_{\mathcal{B}}^3$ by matrix multiplication. The abstract calculation$(T \circ T \circ T)$ becomes concrete matrix multiplication$[T]^3$.

The practical implication is: to compute with linear maps, choose any convenient bases, convert to matrices, compute using matrix arithmetic, and convert back. The abstract theory guarantees nothing is lost in translation — the isomorphism is exact, and every structural property (injectivity, rank, composition) is perfectly reflected in the matrix language.

**Example 4.** The kernel and image of a map via its matrix: for $T: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ with$[T] = \begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\4&5&6\end{pmatrix}$ (in standard bases),$\ker T$ corresponds to the null space of this matrix and$\mathrm{im}\, T$ corresponds to its column space. Row reduction gives RREF$\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-1\\0&1&2\end{pmatrix}$: rank$2$, null space spanned by$(1,-2,1)^T$. The isomorphism$\Phi$ converts this matrix-level computation directly into information about$T$.

**Example 5.** The isomorphism is not canonical: if we choose different bases $\mathcal{B}'$ on$V$ and$\mathcal{C}'$ on$W$, we get a different isomorphism$\Phi': \mathcal{L}(V,W) \to M_{m \times n}(F)$. The two isomorphisms$\Phi$ and$\Phi'$ differ by conjugation by change-of-basis matrices:$\Phi'(T) = Q^{-1} \Phi(T) P$ where$P$ is the transition matrix on$V$ and$Q$ is the transition matrix on$W$. This is the matrix version of the change-of-basis formula studied in Section 7.4.

\begin{remark}
The isomorphism $\Phi: \mathcal{L}(V,W) \to M_{m \times n}(F)$ can be used to count:$\dim \mathcal{L}(V,W) = mn$ (Section 6.1.3) corresponds to the fact that there are$mn$ degrees of freedom in an$m \times n$ matrix. The dimensions of various subspaces of$\mathcal{L}(V,W)$ — for instance, the subspace of maps of rank at most$r$ — have geometric interpretations as determinantal varieties in$M_{m \times n}(F)$, connecting linear algebra to algebraic geometry.
\end{remark}
