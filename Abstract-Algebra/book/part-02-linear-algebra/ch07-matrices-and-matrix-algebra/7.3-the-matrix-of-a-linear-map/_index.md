# 7.3 The Matrix of a Linear Map

This section is the conceptual center of Chapter 7. It explains what matrices are really for: they are the coordinate representations of linear maps, and matrix multiplication is composition in disguise. The construction is explicit. Let $T: V \to W$ be a linear map, let $\mathcal{B} = (b_1, \ldots, b_n)$ be an ordered basis for $V$, and let $\mathcal{C} = (c_1, \ldots, c_m)$ be an ordered basis for $W$. For each basis vector $b_j$, the image $T(b_j)$ is a vector in $W$, and it has a unique representation $T(b_j) = \sum_{i=1}^m a_{ij} c_i$ in the $\mathcal{C}$-basis. The $j$-th column of the matrix $[T]_{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathcal{C}}$ is the coordinate vector of $T(b_j)$ with respect to $\mathcal{C}$. The notation records which bases are used, and the choice of bases is part of the data.

The key theorem is: matrix multiplication corresponds exactly to composition of linear maps. If $T: V \to W$ has matrix $[T]_{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathcal{C}}$ and $S: W \to X$ has matrix $[S]_{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathcal{D}}$, then $[S \circ T]_{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathcal{D}} = [S]_{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathcal{D}} \cdot [T]_{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathcal{C}}$. This is why matrix multiplication is defined the way it is — the definition was reverse-engineered from composition. The proof writes out both sides in terms of coordinate expansions and observes that the sums match. As a corollary: the matrix of the identity map in any basis is the identity matrix, and the matrix of the inverse of an isomorphism is the matrix inverse.

The section also establishes the isomorphism $\mathcal{L}(V, W) \cong M_{m \times n}(F)$ for finite-dimensional spaces: the correspondence $T \mapsto [T]_{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathcal{C}}$ is a vector space isomorphism between the space of linear maps and the space of $m \times n$ matrices. This means that every abstract linear algebra problem about maps between finite-dimensional spaces can in principle be converted to a problem about matrices, solved computationally, and then interpreted back. The conversion always requires a choice of bases, but the theorem guarantees that the choice does not change the abstract answer — only the coordinates.

A practical consequence emphasized in this section is how to compute with the matrix of a map. To find where $T$ sends an arbitrary vector $v = \sum_j x_j b_j$, one simply computes $[T]_{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathcal{C}} \cdot [v]_{\mathcal{B}}$: the matrix times the coordinate vector gives the coordinate vector of the image. This is the fundamental computational template: represent, multiply, interpret. Every algorithm in numerical linear algebra is an elaboration of this template.
