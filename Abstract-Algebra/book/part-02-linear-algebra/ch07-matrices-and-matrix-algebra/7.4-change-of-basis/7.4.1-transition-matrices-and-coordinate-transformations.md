# 7.4.1 Transition Matrices and Coordinate Transformations

We have seen that every linear map $T: V \to W$ can be represented as a matrix, but that matrix depends on the choice of bases. A natural question arises: if we switch from one basis to another, how does the matrix change? The answer involves a special matrix called the **transition matrix** (or change-of-basis matrix), which encodes the relationship between two coordinate systems.

\begin{definition}
Let $V$ be$n$-dimensional with two ordered bases$\mathcal{B} = (b_1, \ldots, b_n)$ and$\mathcal{B}' = (b'_1, \ldots, b'_n)$. The **transition matrix from$\mathcal{B}'$ to$\mathcal{B}$**, written$P = P_{\mathcal{B}' \to \mathcal{B}}$, is the$n \times n$ matrix whose$j$-th column is$[b'_j]_{\mathcal{B}}$ — the coordinate vector of the$j$-th new basis vector expressed in the old basis.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}
For any $v \in V$,$[v]_{\mathcal{B}} = P \cdot [v]_{\mathcal{B}'}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $v = \sum_j \alpha_j b'_j$ in the new basis. Then$v = \sum_j \alpha_j b'_j = \sum_j \alpha_j \sum_i P_{ij} b_i = \sum_i \left(\sum_j P_{ij} \alpha_j\right) b_i$. The$i$-th coordinate in$\mathcal{B}$ is$\sum_j P_{ij} \alpha_j = (P [v]_{\mathcal{B}'})_i$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}
The transition matrix $P_{\mathcal{B}' \to \mathcal{B}}$ is always invertible, and
\par
$$P_{\mathcal{B}' \to \mathcal{B}}^{-1} = P_{\mathcal{B} \to \mathcal{B}'}.$$
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The composition of the coordinate maps $[\,\cdot\,]_{\mathcal{B}'} \circ ([\,\cdot\,]_{\mathcal{B}})^{-1} : F^n \to F^n$ corresponds to the matrix$P_{\mathcal{B} \to \mathcal{B}'}$ by the same argument. Since$P_{\mathcal{B} \to \mathcal{B}'} P_{\mathcal{B}' \to \mathcal{B}} [v]_{\mathcal{B}'} = P_{\mathcal{B} \to \mathcal{B}'} [v]_{\mathcal{B}} = [v]_{\mathcal{B}'}$ for all$v$, we get$P_{\mathcal{B} \to \mathcal{B}'} P_{\mathcal{B}' \to \mathcal{B}} = I_n$.
\end{proof}

**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{R}^2$, let$\mathcal{B} = (e_1, e_2)$ (standard) and$\mathcal{B}' = ((1,1), (1,-1))$. The transition matrix from$\mathcal{B}'$ to$\mathcal{B}$ is$P = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$ (columns are the new basis vectors in$\mathcal{B}$-coordinates, which are just the vectors themselves since$\mathcal{B}$ is the standard basis). Verify:$[v]_{\mathcal{B}} = P [v]_{\mathcal{B}'}$. For$v = (2,0)$:$[(2,0)]_{\mathcal{B}'} = (1,1)^T$ (since$(2,0) = 1 \cdot (1,1) + 1 \cdot (1,-1)$). Then$P \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2\\0\end{pmatrix} = [v]_{\mathcal{B}}$. Correct.

**Example 2.** In $F_{\leq 2}[x]$, let$\mathcal{B} = (1, x, x^2)$ and$\mathcal{B}' = (1, 1+x, 1+x+x^2)$. Express the new basis in old coordinates:$[1]_{\mathcal{B}} = (1,0,0)^T$,$[1+x]_{\mathcal{B}} = (1,1,0)^T$,$[1+x+x^2]_{\mathcal{B}} = (1,1,1)^T$. So

$$P = \begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\0&1&1\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.$$

This is upper unitriangular. Its inverse is $P^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix}1&-1&0\\0&1&-1\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$ (computed by row reduction), which is the transition matrix from$\mathcal{B}$ to$\mathcal{B}'$.

**Example 3.** To compute $[v]_{\mathcal{B}'}$ from$[v]_{\mathcal{B}}$: use$[v]_{\mathcal{B}'} = P^{-1} [v]_{\mathcal{B}}$. For$v = 3 + 2x + x^2$,$[v]_{\mathcal{B}} = (3,2,1)^T$. Then$[v]_{\mathcal{B}'} = P^{-1}(3,2,1)^T = \begin{pmatrix}1&-1&0\\0&1&-1\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}3\\2\\1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}$. Check:$1 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot (1+x) + 1 \cdot (1+x+x^2) = 3 + 2x + x^2$. Correct.

In practice, to compute $P = P_{\mathcal{B}' \to \mathcal{B}}$ when both bases are given as lists of vectors in$F^n$, form the matrix$[b_1 \mid \cdots \mid b_n]$ (the old basis as columns) and the matrix$[b'_1 \mid \cdots \mid b'_n]$ (the new basis as columns), then$P = [b_1 \mid \cdots \mid b_n]^{-1} [b'_1 \mid \cdots \mid b'_n]$. When$\mathcal{B}$ is the standard basis,$P = [b'_1 \mid \cdots \mid b'_n]$ directly. The next subsection shows how transition matrices govern the transformation of the matrix of a linear operator under a change of basis.

**Example 4.** In $\mathbb{R}^3$, let$\mathcal{B} = (e_1, e_2, e_3)$ (standard) and$\mathcal{B}' = ((1,0,1), (0,1,1), (1,1,0))$. The transition matrix$P = P_{\mathcal{B}' \to \mathcal{B}}$ has the new basis vectors as columns:$P = \begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\0&1&1\\1&1&0\end{pmatrix}$. This matrix is invertible (check:$\det P = 1(0-1) - 0 + 1(0-1) = -2 \neq 0$). For$v = (2,1,3)$,$[v]_{\mathcal{B}} = (2,1,3)^T$, and$[v]_{\mathcal{B}'} = P^{-1}(2,1,3)^T$.

\begin{remark}
The transition matrix $P_{\mathcal{B}' \to \mathcal{B}}$ is the matrix of the identity map$\mathrm{id}: V \to V$ when the domain is given the basis$\mathcal{B}'$ and the codomain is given the basis$\mathcal{B}$. That is,$P_{\mathcal{B}' \to \mathcal{B}} = [\mathrm{id}]_{\mathcal{B}'}^{\mathcal{B}}$. This is consistent with the fundamental equation:$[v]_{\mathcal{B}} = [\mathrm{id}(v)]_{\mathcal{B}} = [\mathrm{id}]_{\mathcal{B}'}^{\mathcal{B}} \cdot [v]_{\mathcal{B}'}$. The chain of transition matrices satisfies$P_{\mathcal{B}'' \to \mathcal{B}} = P_{\mathcal{B}' \to \mathcal{B}} \cdot P_{\mathcal{B}'' \to \mathcal{B}'}$ — just as composition of coordinate changes corresponds to matrix multiplication.
\end{remark}

The theory of transition matrices is the technical foundation for the change-of-basis formula $[T]_{\mathcal{B}'} = P^{-1}[T]_{\mathcal{B}}P$ for operators (Section 7.4.2). It also underlies the definition of similarity: two matrices are similar iff they represent the same linear operator in different bases, which is exactly the condition$A' = P^{-1}AP$.
