# 7.4.3 Invariants of Similarity: Trace, Determinant, Characteristic Polynomial

If two matrices are similar, they represent the same linear operator in different coordinate systems. Any quantity that claims to measure a property of the underlying operator must be the same for all matrices in a given similarity class; we call such a quantity a **similarity invariant**. Invariants are powerful: they let us prove that two matrices are not similar (by computing different invariant values) and they carry intrinsic geometric meaning independent of any basis choice.

\begin{definition}
The **trace** of $A \in M_{n \times n}(F)$ is$\mathrm{tr}(A) = \sum_{i=1}^n A_{ii}$, the sum of the diagonal entries.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Cyclic Property of Trace]
For any $A \in M_{m \times n}(F)$ and$B \in M_{n \times m}(F)$,$\mathrm{tr}(AB) = \mathrm{tr}(BA)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\mathrm{tr}(AB) = \sum_i (AB)_{ii} = \sum_i \sum_k A_{ik}B_{ki} = \sum_k \sum_i B_{ki}A_{ik} = \sum_k (BA)_{kk} = \mathrm{tr}(BA)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
The trace is a similarity invariant: $\mathrm{tr}(P^{-1}AP) = \mathrm{tr}(A)$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By the cyclic property: $\mathrm{tr}(P^{-1}(AP)) = \mathrm{tr}((AP)P^{-1}) = \mathrm{tr}(A(PP^{-1})) = \mathrm{tr}(A)$.
\end{proof}

The determinant is also a similarity invariant: $\det(P^{-1}AP) = \det(P^{-1})\det(A)\det(P) = \det(A)$, using the multiplicativity$\det(XY) = \det(X)\det(Y)$ and$\det(P^{-1}) = \det(P)^{-1}$. We will prove the properties of determinants carefully in Chapter 8; for now we state the invariance as a consequence.

The richest invariant is the **characteristic polynomial**.

\begin{definition}
The **characteristic polynomial** of $A \in M_{n \times n}(F)$ is
\par
$$p_A(\lambda) = \det(\lambda I_n - A) \in F[\lambda].$$
\par
This is a monic polynomial of degree $n$ in the variable$\lambda$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
The characteristic polynomial is a similarity invariant: if $A' = P^{-1}AP$, then$p_{A'}(\lambda) = p_A(\lambda)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\det(\lambda I - A') = \det(\lambda I - P^{-1}AP) = \det(P^{-1}(\lambda I - A)P) = \det(P^{-1})\det(\lambda I - A)\det(P) = \det(\lambda I - A)$.
\end{proof}

The characteristic polynomial encodes the full eigenvalue data of $A$: its roots are precisely the eigenvalues. Expanding$p_A(\lambda) = \lambda^n - \mathrm{tr}(A)\lambda^{n-1} + \cdots + (-1)^n \det(A)$ shows that the trace is the coefficient of$\lambda^{n-1}$ (up to sign) and the determinant is the constant term (up to sign), so both are expressible in terms of the characteristic polynomial.

**Example 1.** For $A = \begin{pmatrix}3&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$:$\mathrm{tr}(A) = 5$,$\det(A) = 6$. The characteristic polynomial is$p_A(\lambda) = \det\begin{pmatrix}\lambda-3&-1\\0&\lambda-2\end{pmatrix} = (\lambda-3)(\lambda-2) = \lambda^2 - 5\lambda + 6$. Eigenvalues are$\lambda = 2$ and$\lambda = 3$, consistent with$\mathrm{tr}(A) = 2+3 = 5$ and$\det(A) = 2 \cdot 3 = 6$.

**Example 2.** Non-similar matrices with the same trace and determinant but different characteristic polynomials: over $\mathbb{F}_2$,$A = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ has$p_A(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - \lambda - 1 = \lambda^2 + \lambda + 1$ (over$\mathbb{F}_2$) and$B = I_2 + A$ would need more care. Actually for$2 \times 2$ matrices over any field, having the same trace and determinant is equivalent to having the same characteristic polynomial, so they suffice to distinguish similarity classes with distinct eigenvalues.

**Example 3.** Two matrices with the same characteristic polynomial that are NOT similar: $A = \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ and$B = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ both have$p(\lambda) = \lambda^2$. But$A = 0$ is similar only to itself, while$B$ (the Jordan block) is not similar to$A$ since$A = 0$ but$B \neq 0$. This shows that the characteristic polynomial does not alone determine the similarity class; finer invariants (minimal polynomial, rational canonical form) are needed.

Similarity invariants are the foundation of the theory of canonical forms. The Jordan canonical form theorem says that over an algebraically closed field, every matrix is similar to a Jordan canonical form, which is determined completely by the elementary divisors — a refinement of the characteristic polynomial data. These are the subjects of the later chapters, for which the present discussion lays the groundwork.

**Example 4.** Trace as the sum of eigenvalues: for $A = \begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{pmatrix}$, the characteristic polynomial is$p_A(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - 5\lambda - 2$ (compute:$\det(\lambda I - A) = (\lambda-1)(\lambda-4) - 6 = \lambda^2 - 5\lambda - 2$). The sum of eigenvalues is$\frac{5 + \sqrt{33}}{2} + \frac{5 - \sqrt{33}}{2} = 5 = \mathrm{tr}(A)$. The product of eigenvalues is$\frac{5+\sqrt{33}}{2} \cdot \frac{5-\sqrt{33}}{2} = \frac{25-33}{4} = -2 = \det(A)$.

The **Cayley–Hamilton theorem** states that every matrix satisfies its own characteristic polynomial: $p_A(A) = 0$. For the matrix above,$p_A(A) = A^2 - 5A - 2I = \begin{pmatrix}7&10\\15&22\end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix}5&10\\15&20\end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$. This remarkable theorem — proved in Chapter 8 via the theory of the minimal polynomial and the structure theorem for modules over PIDs — shows that the characteristic polynomial is not just a similarity invariant but actually a "vanishing polynomial" for the matrix itself. It provides a way to express powers of$A$ above$n-1$ in terms of lower powers, making$A$ "algebraic over$F$."

\begin{remark}
The **minimal polynomial** $m_A(\lambda)$ of$A$ is the monic polynomial of least degree satisfying$m_A(A) = 0$. It divides$p_A(\lambda)$ and they have the same roots (over the algebraic closure). The minimal polynomial is a finer invariant than the characteristic polynomial: matrices with the same characteristic polynomial can have different minimal polynomials, distinguishing them as similarity classes. The pair$(p_A, m_A)$ together determine the rational canonical form, which classifies similarity classes over any field.
\end{remark}
