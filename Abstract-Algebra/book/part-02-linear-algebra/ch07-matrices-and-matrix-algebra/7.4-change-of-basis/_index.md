# 7.4 Change of Basis

A single linear operator $T: V \to V$ can be represented by many different matrices, one for each choice of ordered basis for $V$. The question addressed in this section is: how do two such matrix representations relate to each other? The answer is the change-of-basis formula, and it introduces one of the most important equivalence relations in linear algebra — similarity.

Suppose $\mathcal{B}$ and $\mathcal{B}'$ are two ordered bases for the same $n$-dimensional space $V$. The transition matrix (or change-of-basis matrix) $P = P_{\mathcal{B} \to \mathcal{B}'}$ from $\mathcal{B}$ to $\mathcal{B}'$ is the matrix whose $j$-th column is the coordinate vector of the $j$-th new basis vector $b'_j$ expressed in the old basis $\mathcal{B}$. The transition matrix is always invertible: its inverse is the transition matrix in the reverse direction. The key identity relating the two matrix representations of $T$ is $[T]_{\mathcal{B}'} = P^{-1} [T]_{\mathcal{B}} P$. This formula says that changing basis conjugates the matrix by the transition matrix.

Two square matrices $A$ and $B$ are called similar if there exists an invertible matrix $P$ such that $B = P^{-1} A P$. Similarity is an equivalence relation: it is reflexive (take $P = I$), symmetric, and transitive. Similarity classes are exactly the sets of all matrix representations of a single linear operator — changing the representative within the class corresponds to changing the basis. The geometry of the operator is independent of the chosen representative; what changes is only the coordinate expression.

Not all properties of a matrix are invariant under similarity — the individual entries certainly change. But some scalar quantities are similarity invariants and therefore intrinsic properties of the underlying linear operator: the trace $\mathrm{tr}(A) = \sum_i a_{ii}$, the determinant $\det(A)$, the rank, and more generally all coefficients of the characteristic polynomial. The section proves the trace invariance using the cyclic property $\mathrm{tr}(AB) = \mathrm{tr}(BA)$ and the determinant invariance using the multiplicative property $\det(P^{-1}AP) = \det(P)^{-1}\det(A)\det(P) = \det(A)$. These invariants are the first bridge between matrix algebra and the spectral theory of Chapters 9 and 10, where the question of finding the "simplest" matrix in a similarity class is answered by the theory of canonical forms.
