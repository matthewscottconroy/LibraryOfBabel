# 7.5.2 The Unique Reduced Row Echelon Form

Row echelon form simplifies a matrix but leaves ambiguity — different reduction sequences can yield different echelon forms. By continuing the reduction process, we eliminate that ambiguity entirely and arrive at a form that is both simpler and unique.

\begin{definition}
A matrix is in **reduced row echelon form** (RREF) if it is in row echelon form and additionally:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(RREF1)] Each pivot equals $1$.
\item[(RREF2)] Each pivot is the only nonzero entry in its column.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

In other words, RREF requires that each pivot column is a standard basis vector $e_i$. Once we have a matrix in REF, we achieve RREF by: (1) scaling each pivot row so the pivot becomes$1$, and (2) eliminating all entries above each pivot using row replacement (the "back-substitution" step).

\begin{theorem}[Uniqueness of RREF]
Every matrix has a unique reduced row echelon form.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The pivot positions are determined by the linear dependency structure of the columns of $A$, which is an intrinsic property not affected by row operations: a column is a pivot column iff the corresponding column of$A$ is not a linear combination of the columns to its left. The pivot columns are uniquely determined. Given the pivot positions, the entries of the RREF in each row are uniquely determined by the requirement that the pivot columns are standard basis vectors and the non-pivot columns express their columns of$A$ in terms of the pivot columns.
\end{proof}

The number of pivots in the RREF is the **rank** of $A$, which equals$\dim(\mathrm{col}\, A) = \dim(\mathrm{row}\, A)$. The positions of the non-pivot columns identify the free variables in any associated linear system. Everything we need to understand the solution structure is encoded in the RREF.

\begin{remark}
RREF is produced by Gauss–Jordan elimination: Gaussian elimination to reach REF, followed by back-elimination and pivot-scaling. The result is unique regardless of the sequence of row operations used, as the theorem guarantees.
\end{remark}

**Example 1.** Continue from Example 1 of Section 7.5.1: REF is $\begin{pmatrix}1&3&5\\0&2&4\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}$. Scale row 2 by$1/2$:$\begin{pmatrix}1&3&5\\0&1&2\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}$. Eliminate above pivot in column 2:$R_1 \to R_1 - 3R_2$:$\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-1\\0&1&2\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}$. This is RREF. Pivots in columns 1 and 2; column 3 is free.

**Example 2.** From Example 2 of Section 7.5.1: REF $\begin{pmatrix}2&4&-2\\0&1&1\\0&0&4\end{pmatrix}$. Scale:$R_1/2$,$R_3/4$:$\begin{pmatrix}1&2&-1\\0&1&1\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$. Back-eliminate:$R_2 \to R_2 - R_3$:$(0,1,0)$;$R_1 \to R_1 + R_3$:$(1,2,0)$;$R_1 \to R_1 - 2R_2$:$(1,0,0)$. RREF:$\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix} = I_3$. All columns are pivot columns; matrix has full rank.

**Example 3.** A $3 \times 5$ matrix:

$$A = \begin{pmatrix}1&2&0&1&3\\2&4&1&3&7\\0&0&2&1&1\end{pmatrix}.$$

$R_2 \to R_2 - 2R_1$:$\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0&1&3\\0&0&1&1&1\\0&0&2&1&1\end{pmatrix}$.$R_3 \to R_3 - 2R_2$:$\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0&1&3\\0&0&1&1&1\\0&0&0&-1&-1\end{pmatrix}$. Scale$R_3$ by$-1$:$\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0&1&3\\0&0&1&1&1\\0&0&0&1&1\end{pmatrix}$. Back-eliminate:$R_2 \to R_2 - R_3$:$(0,0,1,0,0)$;$R_1 \to R_1 - R_3$:$(1,2,0,0,2)$. RREF:

$$\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0&0&2\\0&0&1&0&0\\0&0&0&1&1\end{pmatrix}.$$

Pivot columns: 1, 3, 4; rank $= 3$. Free variables correspond to columns 2 and 5.

The uniqueness of RREF has a powerful conceptual consequence: it means that the fundamental structural data of a matrix — its rank, its pivot positions, the linear dependencies among its columns — are intrinsic, independent of the particular sequence of operations used to reveal them. RREF is the canonical form under the equivalence relation of "same row space," just as similarity is the canonical form under the equivalence relation of "same linear operator."

**Example 4.** RREF over $\mathbb{F}_2$ (the field with two elements):$A = \begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\0&1&1\\1&0&1\end{pmatrix}$ over$\mathbb{F}_2$.$R_3 \to R_3 + R_1$ (since$-1 = 1$ in$\mathbb{F}_2$):$\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\0&1&1\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix}$.$R_3 \to R_3 + R_2$:$\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\0&1&1\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}$.$R_1 \to R_1 + R_2$:$\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\0&1&1\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}$. RREF. Pivot columns: 1, 2. Rank$= 2$. Free variable: column 3. Null space basis:$x_3 = 1$,$x_2 = 1$,$x_1 = 1$ (from$x_1 + x_3 = 0$ and$x_2 + x_3 = 0$ in$\mathbb{F}_2$). So$\ker A = \mathrm{span}\{(1,1,1)^T\}$ over$\mathbb{F}_2$.

\begin{remark}[RREF and Linear Independence]
Given a set of vectors $\{v_1, \ldots, v_k\} \subseteq F^n$, form the matrix$A$ with these vectors as columns and row-reduce to RREF. The set is linearly independent iff every column is a pivot column (rank$= k$). If some columns are non-pivot, the RREF explicitly shows which vectors are linear combinations of the others: the non-pivot columns of$A$ equal the corresponding columns of the RREF, expressed in terms of the pivot columns.
\end{remark}

The RREF algorithm (Gauss–Jordan elimination) has time complexity $O(mn \min(m,n))$ for an$m \times n$ matrix, making it practical for matrices of moderate size. Over finite fields, the algorithm is exact and terminates in finitely many steps with a perfect result. Over$\mathbb{R}$ or$\mathbb{C}$, numerical issues (round-off errors) require the use of partial pivoting and careful implementation; the numerically stable version is the basis of all practical linear algebra software.
