# 8.1.1 Multilinearity and the Alternating Property

Rather than defining the determinant by a formula and then deriving its properties, we take the opposite approach: we specify the properties we want, and then show that they uniquely determine the function. This axiomatic method reveals *why* the determinant behaves as it does, rather than presenting its properties as lucky coincidences of some ad hoc formula. It also generalizes more readily: once we know which axioms characterize the determinant, we can recognize determinant-like constructions wherever they appear, including in the theory of differential forms and the exterior algebra.

The central question motivating the determinant is: what is the "signed volume" of the parallelepiped spanned by $n$ vectors in$F^n$? We want a function that scales when we scale a vector, flips sign when we reflect, and vanishes when the vectors are coplanar. These intuitions lead directly to the two structural axioms below.

We define the determinant as a function $\det: M_{n \times n}(F) \to F$ satisfying two structural axioms, where we view a matrix as an ordered$n$-tuple of its row vectors$(r_1, \ldots, r_n)$ with each$r_i \in F^n$. The first axiom is **multilinearity**:$\det$ is linear in each row separately, with all other rows held fixed. Explicitly, for any fixed index$i$ and any scalars$\alpha, \beta \in F$ and row vectors$r_i, r_i' \in F^n$:

$$\det(r_1, \ldots, \alpha r_i + \beta r_i', \ldots, r_n) = \alpha \det(r_1, \ldots, r_i, \ldots, r_n) + \beta \det(r_1, \ldots, r_i', \ldots, r_n).$$

This says that as we vary one row while keeping all others fixed, the determinant responds linearly. Geometrically, doubling one edge of a parallelepiped doubles its volume; decomposing one edge as a sum of two vectors decomposes the parallelepiped into two sub-parallelepipeds whose volumes add.

The second axiom is the **alternating property**: swapping any two rows negates the determinant. That is, if $A'$ is obtained from$A$ by interchanging rows$i$ and$j$ (with$i \neq j$), then$\det(A') = -\det(A)$. The sign change reflects the orientation reversal: swapping two edges of a parallelepiped is a reflection, which flips the orientation of the spanning vectors from right-handed to left-handed (or vice versa) in every even dimension.

From these two axioms alone, several important consequences follow immediately. If two rows of $A$ are identical, then swapping them leaves$A$ unchanged but (by the alternating property) negates$\det(A)$; since$\det(A) = -\det(A)$ forces$\det(A) = 0$, any matrix with two equal rows has determinant zero. Geometrically this is correct: if two edges of a parallelepiped point in the same direction, the figure is degenerate and has zero volume. Multilinearity also shows that scaling row$i$ by$c$ scales the determinant by$c$:$\det(\ldots, c r_i, \ldots) = c \det(\ldots, r_i, \ldots)$.

The third consequence — that adding a scalar multiple of one row to another leaves the determinant unchanged — requires both axioms together. We compute:
$$\det(\ldots, r_i + c r_j, \ldots, r_j, \ldots) = \det(\ldots, r_i, \ldots, r_j, \ldots) + c \det(\ldots, r_j, \ldots, r_j, \ldots).$$
The second term has $r_j$ appearing in two different rows, so by the equal-rows consequence it is zero. Hence the determinant is unchanged. This is derived in detail in Section 8.1.3.

These properties are what make row reduction a viable computational tool for determinants. Each elementary row operation has a known, simple effect on $\det$: scaling multiplies by the scalar, swapping negates, and adding a multiple of one row to another leaves$\det$ unchanged. By performing Gaussian elimination while tracking these changes, we can reduce any matrix to upper triangular form and read off the determinant from the diagonal entries.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Verify multilinearity in the first row for $2 \times 2$ matrices. Let$r_1 = \alpha u + \beta v$ where$u = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$,$v = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$, and$r_2 = \begin{pmatrix} c \\ d \end{pmatrix}$. Then:
\begin{align*}
\det\!\begin{pmatrix} \alpha u + \beta v \mid r_2 \end{pmatrix}^T &= \det\!\begin{pmatrix} \alpha & \beta \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} = \alpha d - \beta c.
\end{align*}
On the other hand:
\begin{align*}
\alpha \det\!\begin{pmatrix} u \mid r_2 \end{pmatrix}^T + \beta \det\!\begin{pmatrix} v \mid r_2 \end{pmatrix}^T &= \alpha \det\!\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} + \beta \det\!\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} = \alpha d + \beta(-c) = \alpha d - \beta c.
\end{align*}
The two expressions agree, confirming linearity in row 1.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Verify the alternating property for $3 \times 3$ matrices by swapping rows 1 and 2 of$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 9 \end{pmatrix}$. The determinant of$A$ via Sarrus's rule (or cofactor expansion) is$1(45-48) - 2(36-42) + 3(32-35) = -3 + 12 - 9 = 0$. After swapping rows 1 and 2 we get$A' = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 7 & 8 & 9 \end{pmatrix}$, with$\det(A') = 4(18-24) - 5(9-21) + 6(8-14) = -24 + 60 - 36 = 0 = -\det(A)$. Here$\det(A) = 0$ because row 3 is the sum of rows 1 and 2, confirming linear dependence kills the determinant.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Use multilinearity to factor out scalars. If $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 3 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$, we can factor$2$ from row 1 and$3$ from row 2:
$$\det(A) = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot \det\!\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = 6 \cdot 0 = 0.$$
The last determinant is zero because both rows are equal. This computation illustrates why $\det(cA) = c^n \det(A)$ for an$n \times n$ matrix: each of the$n$ rows acquires a factor of$c$, and multilinearity allows us to factor each out independently.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** Demonstrate that adding a multiple of row 2 to row 1 preserves the determinant. Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$, so$\det(A) = 5 - 6 = -1$. Add$4$ times row 2 to row 1 to get$A' = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + 8 & 3 + 20 \\ 2 & 5 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 9 & 23 \\ 2 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$. Then$\det(A') = 45 - 46 = -1 = \det(A)$. The determinant is unchanged, as the axioms predict.
\end{example}
