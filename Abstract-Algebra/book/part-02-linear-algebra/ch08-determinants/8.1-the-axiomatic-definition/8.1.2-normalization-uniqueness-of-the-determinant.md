# 8.1.2 Normalization; Uniqueness of the Determinant

Multilinearity and the alternating property together constrain what $\det$ can be, but they do not pin it down completely: the zero function satisfies both. To eliminate this trivial solution and to anchor the scale of the function, we add a third axiom, the **normalization condition**:$\det(I_n) = 1$, where$I_n$ is the$n \times n$ identity matrix. Geometrically, this says the unit hypercube — the parallelepiped spanned by the standard basis vectors — has volume$1$. Without this condition, any constant multiple of$\det$ would also satisfy the first two axioms.

With the normalization condition in place, the three axioms together uniquely determine the determinant. This is a theorem, not merely a hope, and its proof is constructive: it produces the Leibniz formula (Section 8.2.1) as the only possible value for $\det(A)$ given the three axioms.

The uniqueness argument proceeds by expanding $\det(A)$ using multilinearity, row by row. Each row$r_i = \sum_{j=1}^n A_{ij} e_j$ (where$e_1, \ldots, e_n$ are the standard basis vectors) can be expanded by linearity in the$i$-th row, yielding a sum over all choices of one standard basis vector from each row:

$$\det(A) = \sum_{j_1=1}^n \cdots \sum_{j_n=1}^n A_{1,j_1} \cdots A_{n,j_n} \cdot \det(e_{j_1}, \ldots, e_{j_n}).$$

By the alternating property, any term in which two indices $j_k$ are equal is zero (since the corresponding determinant has two identical rows). The surviving terms are precisely those where$(j_1, \ldots, j_n)$ is a permutation of$(1, \ldots, n)$. For a permutation$\sigma$, the matrix with rows$e_{\sigma(1)}, \ldots, e_{\sigma(n)}$ is a permutation matrix$P_\sigma$, and by the alternating and normalization axioms its determinant is$\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)$ (since$P_\sigma$ is obtained from$I_n$ by$N(\sigma)$ row swaps, each costing a sign change). Therefore:

$$\det(A) = \sum_{\sigma \in S_n} \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) \prod_{i=1}^n A_{i, \sigma(i)}.$$

Since this Leibniz formula is derived solely from the three axioms, any function satisfying those axioms must agree with it on every matrix. The zero function fails normalization, and any other multiple fails normalization differently. Uniqueness is established.

The significance of this uniqueness result is conceptual as much as computational. It shows that there is no choice involved in defining the determinant: the three natural requirements we would impose on any "signed volume function" — linearity in each direction, sign-change under reflection, and the unit cube having volume $1$ — completely and uniquely determine the function. Any formula for the determinant — cofactor expansion, the Leibniz sum, row reduction — is just a different way to compute the same uniquely defined object. This perspective, common throughout modern algebra, will recur when we study other universal constructions such as tensor products and free algebras.

A corollary is that verifying just the three axioms for any proposed determinant formula is sufficient to prove the formula correct. This is often easier than a direct calculation: rather than verify the Leibniz formula by expanding a determinant from first principles, one checks that the Leibniz formula satisfies multilinearity, the alternating property, and normalization — and the uniqueness theorem does the rest.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Uniqueness forces $\det$ of a diagonal matrix. Let$D = \mathrm{diag}(d_1, \ldots, d_n)$. By multilinearity, factor$d_i$ from row$i$ for each$i$:
$$\det(D) = d_1 \cdots d_n \cdot \det(I_n) = d_1 \cdots d_n \cdot 1 = d_1 \cdots d_n.$$
No choice is involved: the axioms force this value. For $D = \mathrm{diag}(2, 3, 5)$, we get$\det(D) = 30$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Verify that the zero function fails uniqueness. The function $f \equiv 0$ satisfies multilinearity (trivially) and the alternating property (trivially), but$f(I_n) = 0 \neq 1$, so it fails normalization. The function$f(A) = 2\det(A)$ satisfies multilinearity and the alternating property, but$f(I_n) = 2 \neq 1$. Only$\det$ itself passes all three tests.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Use uniqueness to evaluate $\det$ of a permutation matrix. Let$P$ be the$3 \times 3$ matrix with columns$e_2, e_1, e_3$ (swapping the first two columns of$I_3$, i.e., rows$e_2, e_1, e_3$). We obtain$P$ from$I_3$ by one row swap, so by the alternating property$\det(P) = -\det(I_3) = -1$. This agrees with the Leibniz formula: the only nonzero term comes from the permutation$\sigma = (1\ 2)$, which has$\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) = -1$, giving$\det(P) = (-1) \cdot P_{1,\sigma(1)} P_{2,\sigma(2)} P_{3,\sigma(3)} = (-1)(1)(1)(1) = -1$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** The uniqueness theorem in action: an alternative formula. Suppose someone proposes that $\det(A) = \sum_{\sigma \in S_n} \prod_i A_{i,\sigma(i)}$ (the Leibniz formula without the sign). Check: for$n=2$, this gives$\det\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix} = 1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 = 1$ (normalization passes). But the alternating property fails: swapping rows gives$\det\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix} = 0 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 1 = 1$, which is not$-1$. The proposed formula does not satisfy the alternating axiom, so by uniqueness it cannot be the determinant.
\end{example}
