# 8.1.3 Consequences: Zero Rows, Equal Rows, Row Operations

With the three axioms for the determinant established — multilinearity, alternating property, and normalization — we can derive a complete set of consequences that govern the behavior of $\det$ under all elementary row operations. These are theorems, not additional assumptions; they follow purely from the axioms and require no formula. Understanding them is essential for computational efficiency and for the geometric interpretation of the determinant.

The most basic consequence concerns **zero rows**. If row $i$ of$A$ is the zero vector, then by multilinearity in row$i$ we can factor out$0$: for any vector$r$,
$$\det(\ldots, 0 \cdot r, \ldots) = 0 \cdot \det(\ldots, r, \ldots) = 0.$$
Alternatively, we can write $0 = r - r$ for any row vector$r$ and use multilinearity; either way,$\det(A) = 0$ whenever any row is zero. Geometrically, if one edge of a parallelepiped has zero length, the figure is degenerate and has zero volume.

Next, **equal rows**: if rows $i$ and$j$ of$A$ are identical (with$i \neq j$), then swapping rows$i$ and$j$ leaves$A$ unchanged. By the alternating property, this swap negates$\det(A)$. So$\det(A) = -\det(A)$, forcing$\det(A) = 0$. This has the same geometric flavor: if two edges of the parallelepiped point in the same direction, the figure is flat and has zero volume. Crucially, this result follows from the alternating property alone — no formula needed.

From these, we derive the effect of each **elementary row operation**:

**Type I (row swap).** Swapping rows $i$ and$j$ negates$\det$. This is just the alternating property itself.

**Type II (row scaling).** Multiplying row $i$ by$c \neq 0$ multiplies$\det$ by$c$. By multilinearity in row$i$:$\det(\ldots, c r_i, \ldots) = c \det(\ldots, r_i, \ldots)$.

**Type III (row addition).** Adding $c$ times row$j$ to row$i$ (with$i \neq j$) leaves$\det$ unchanged. The key calculation is:
$$\det(\ldots, r_i + c r_j, \ldots, r_j, \ldots) = \det(\ldots, r_i, \ldots, r_j, \ldots) + c \det(\ldots, r_j, \ldots, r_j, \ldots).$$
The second determinant has $r_j$ in both position$i$ and position$j$, so by the equal-rows result it is zero. Thus only the first term survives, and the determinant is unchanged.

Together, these consequences tell us exactly how $\det$ transforms under each step of Gaussian elimination. This is the key to the row-reduction method for computing determinants (Section 8.2.4): we track the cumulative effect of each row operation on the determinant, reduce to upper triangular form, and read off the determinant as the product of the diagonal entries times the accumulated sign and scaling factors.

Beyond computation, these consequences give a clean characterization of when $\det(A) = 0$: the rows of$A$ are linearly dependent. Indeed, if the rows are linearly dependent, one row can be written as a linear combination of the others; a sequence of Type III operations then reduces that row to zero, giving$\det(A) = 0$. Conversely, if the rows are linearly independent, then$A$ is invertible, and row reduction reaches an upper triangular matrix with nonzero diagonal entries, so$\det(A) \neq 0$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Show that $\det\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{pmatrix} = 0$. Row 2 is zero, so by the zero-row consequence, the determinant is zero immediately — no computation needed.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Compute $\det\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ by tracking row operations. The direct formula gives$6 - 4 = 2$. Let us reproduce this by elimination. Subtract$\frac{1}{2}$ times row 1 from row 2 (Type III, no change to$\det$):
$$\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
The determinant of this upper triangular matrix is $2 \cdot 1 = 2$. Since we performed only a Type III operation,$\det(A) = 2$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Use the equal-rows consequence to evaluate
$$\det\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Rows 1 and 3 are identical, so $\det = 0$ by the equal-rows result. No calculation is needed. This matrix is singular: the vectors$(1,2,3)$,$(4,5,6)$,$(1,2,3)$ are linearly dependent since rows 1 and 3 are equal.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** Show how a Type III operation helps compute a $3 \times 3$ determinant. Let$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 2 & 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. Subtract row 1 from row 3 (Type III): new row 3 becomes$(0, 2, -2)$. Subtract 2 times row 1 from row 2 (Type III): new row 2 becomes$(0, 3, 0)$. We get$\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$. This is upper triangular below the first column, and we can now use cofactor expansion or further reduction. Neither operation changed$\det$, so$\det(A) = \det\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$. Expanding along column 1:$\det = 2 \det\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 2 & -2 \end{pmatrix} = 2(-6 - 0) = -12$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 5.** Scaling one row scales the determinant. Let $B$ be the matrix obtained from$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ by scaling row 1 by$5$ and row 2 by$3$:$B = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$. Then$\det(B) = 5 \cdot 3 \cdot \det(I_2) = 15$. More generally,$\det(cI_n) = c^n$ because each of the$n$ rows is scaled by$c$.
\end{example}
