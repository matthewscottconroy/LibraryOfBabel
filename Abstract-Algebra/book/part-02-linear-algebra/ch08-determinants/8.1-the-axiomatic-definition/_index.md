# 8.1 The Axiomatic Definition

The determinant can be introduced in many ways — as a formula, as a volume function, as the product of eigenvalues — but the most intellectually satisfying starting point is axiomatic. This section defines the determinant via three properties and shows that these properties, taken together, force a unique function. The three axioms are: (1) multilinearity in the columns, meaning that $\det$ is linear in each column when all others are fixed; (2) the alternating property, meaning that swapping any two columns reverses the sign; and (3) normalization, meaning that $\det(I) = 1$.

The power of the axiomatic approach is that consequences follow from the axioms alone, before any formula is written down. For instance, if two columns are equal, swapping them does not change the matrix but — by the alternating property — should negate the determinant. These two facts together force $\det(A) = -\det(A)$, hence $\det(A) = 0$. More generally, if the columns are linearly dependent, one column is a linear combination of the others; expanding by multilinearity reduces the determinant to a sum of determinants of matrices with repeated columns, each of which is zero. Therefore the determinant vanishes whenever the columns are dependent — which is precisely when the matrix is not invertible. The axiomatic approach makes this cascade of consequences immediate and transparent.

The effects of elementary row and column operations are derived directly from the axioms. Swapping two columns multiplies the determinant by $-1$. Multiplying a column by a scalar $c$ multiplies the determinant by $c$. Adding a multiple of one column to another leaves the determinant unchanged. From these, one reads off the effect of each type of elementary matrix: type-I (swap) multiplies $\det$ by $-1$, type-II (scale) multiplies by the scalar, type-III (shear) leaves $\det$ unchanged. This provides a complete recipe for computing the determinant via row reduction, developed in Section 8.2.

An important subtlety: the axioms as stated define a determinant function on columns. The same axioms apply to rows — multilinearity and alternating in the rows, with $\det(I) = 1$ — and yield the same function. That the row-determinant and column-determinant coincide is equivalent to the identity $\det(A^T) = \det(A)$, which the section proves as a corollary of the uniqueness theorem. The uniqueness argument — that any function satisfying the three axioms must equal the Leibniz formula — is deferred to Section 8.2 but foreshadowed here: once the axioms force the behavior on elementary matrices and the elementary matrices generate all invertible matrices, the function is uniquely pinned down on invertible matrices, and then on all matrices by continuity of the reasoning.
