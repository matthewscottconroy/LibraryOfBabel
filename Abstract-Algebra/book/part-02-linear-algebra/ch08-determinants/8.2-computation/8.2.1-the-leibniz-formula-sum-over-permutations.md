# 8.2.1 The Leibniz Formula: Sum over Permutations

The uniqueness argument of Section 8.1.2 tells us what the determinant must be, and working through that argument yields an explicit formula. The result is the **Leibniz formula** (sometimes also called the Leibniz expansion or the permutation expansion): for an $n \times n$ matrix$A$ over a field$F$,

$$\det(A) = \sum_{\sigma \in S_n} \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) \prod_{i=1}^{n} A_{i,\sigma(i)}.$$

Here the sum ranges over all $n!$ permutations$\sigma$ of$\{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$, and$\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) \in \{+1, -1\}$ is the sign of the permutation (defined precisely in Section 8.2.2). Each term in the sum selects one entry from each row and one entry from each column — column$\sigma(i)$ from row$i$ — and contributes that product with the sign of the permutation. Crucially, every row and every column appears exactly once in each term, so the sum truly involves a "system of distinct representatives" for rows and columns.

To see why this formula arises, recall how we derived it in Section 8.1.2: expand $\det$ using multilinearity in each row in turn. Writing$r_i = \sum_j A_{ij} e_j$ and expanding row by row gives a sum over all$n$-tuples$(j_1, \ldots, j_n)$:
$$\det(A) = \sum_{j_1, \ldots, j_n} A_{1,j_1} \cdots A_{n,j_n} \cdot \det(e_{j_1}, \ldots, e_{j_n}).$$
The determinant of a matrix whose rows are standard basis vectors $e_{j_1}, \ldots, e_{j_n}$ is zero unless the$j_k$ are all distinct (by the equal-rows consequence), and equals$\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)$ when$(j_1, \ldots, j_n) = (\sigma(1), \ldots, \sigma(n))$ for a permutation$\sigma$ (since we can sort this to the identity via$N(\sigma)$ transpositions). The Leibniz formula follows.

For $n = 2$, the formula gives$\det \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} = ad - bc$, corresponding to the two permutations$\mathrm{id}$ (sign$+1$, contributing$A_{1,1}A_{2,2} = ad$) and the transposition$(1\ 2)$ (sign$-1$, contributing$-A_{1,2}A_{2,1} = -bc$). For$n = 3$, there are$6 = 3!$ permutations producing six terms; the three even permutations contribute positive terms and the three odd permutations contribute negative terms, reproducing Sarrus's rule. For$n = 4$, there are$24$ terms; beyond that the formula is theoretically important but computationally intractable.

For large $n$, the Leibniz formula is computationally hopeless: it has$n!$ terms, which grows faster than any exponential. The row-reduction method of Section 8.2.4 reduces this to$O(n^3)$ operations, at the cost of more bookkeeping. The Leibniz formula remains the indispensable theoretical foundation, however: it is what allows us to prove that the determinant is a polynomial in the entries of$A$ (hence continuous), that it satisfies$\det(A^T) = \det(A)$, and that it transforms correctly under changes of basis.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Compute $\det\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ via the Leibniz formula. The two permutations in$S_2$ are$\mathrm{id} = \begin{pmatrix}1&2\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ with$\mathrm{sgn} = +1$ and$\tau = (1\ 2)$ with$\mathrm{sgn} = -1$. The contributions are:
$$\mathrm{id}: (+1) \cdot A_{1,1} A_{2,2} = 3 \cdot 4 = 12, \qquad \tau: (-1) \cdot A_{1,2} A_{2,1} = (-1)(1)(2) = -2.$$
Thus $\det = 12 - 2 = 10$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Apply the Leibniz formula to a $3 \times 3$ matrix. Let$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$. List all six permutations in$S_3$ with their signs and products:
\begin{align*}
\sigma = (1)(2)(3),\ \mathrm{sgn}=+1 &: A_{11}A_{22}A_{33} = 1\cdot1\cdot2 = 2\\
\sigma = (1\ 2\ 3),\ \mathrm{sgn}=+1 &: A_{12}A_{23}A_{31} = 2\cdot1\cdot0 = 0\\
\sigma = (1\ 3\ 2),\ \mathrm{sgn}=+1 &: A_{13}A_{21}A_{32} = 0\cdot3\cdot1 = 0\\
\sigma = (1\ 2),\ \mathrm{sgn}=-1 &: A_{12}A_{21}A_{33} = 2\cdot3\cdot2 = -12\\
\sigma = (1\ 3),\ \mathrm{sgn}=-1 &: A_{13}A_{22}A_{31} = 0\cdot1\cdot0 = 0\\
\sigma = (2\ 3),\ \mathrm{sgn}=-1 &: A_{11}A_{23}A_{32} = 1\cdot1\cdot1 = -1
\end{align*}
Summing: $\det(A) = 2 + 0 + 0 - 12 + 0 - 1 = -11$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Use the Leibniz formula to verify $\det(A^T) = \det(A)$. In the formula for$\det(A^T)$, the$(i,j)$ entry of$A^T$ is$A_{j,i}$, so:
$$\det(A^T) = \sum_\sigma \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) \prod_i (A^T)_{i,\sigma(i)} = \sum_\sigma \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) \prod_i A_{\sigma(i),i}.$$
Substituting $\tau = \sigma^{-1}$ (so that$\sigma(i) = j \Leftrightarrow \tau(j) = i$) and using$\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma^{-1}) = \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)$:
$$= \sum_\tau \mathrm{sgn}(\tau) \prod_j A_{j, \tau(j)} = \det(A).$$
This shows $\det(A^T) = \det(A)$ purely from the Leibniz formula, confirming that all row-based properties also hold for columns.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** Exploit sparsity: many terms in the Leibniz sum may be zero. For an upper triangular matrix $U$ with diagonal entries$u_{11}, \ldots, u_{nn}$, any permutation$\sigma \neq \mathrm{id}$ has some$\sigma(i) < i$, meaning$U_{i,\sigma(i)} = 0$ (since$U$ is upper triangular). Thus the only nonzero term is$\sigma = \mathrm{id}$, giving$\det(U) = \prod_{i=1}^n u_{ii}$. For$U = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 7 & 2 \\ 0 & 5 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, we get$\det(U) = 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 4 = 60$ with no further computation.
\end{example}
