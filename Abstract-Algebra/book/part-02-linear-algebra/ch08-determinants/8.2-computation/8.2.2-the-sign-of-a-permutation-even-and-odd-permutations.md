# 8.2.2 The Sign of a Permutation; Even and Odd Permutations

The Leibniz formula depends critically on assigning a sign $\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) \in \{+1, -1\}$ to each permutation. The definition of this sign is a beautiful piece of combinatorics, and understanding it illuminates both the determinant and the structure of the symmetric group.

An **inversion** of $\sigma \in S_n$ is a pair$(i,j)$ with$i < j$ but$\sigma(i) > \sigma(j)$ — informally, a pair of elements that$\sigma$ places "out of natural order." For example, the permutation$\sigma = \begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\3&1&2\end{pmatrix}$ (which sends$1 \mapsto 3$,$2 \mapsto 1$,$3 \mapsto 2$) has inversions$(1,2)$ (since$\sigma(1) = 3 > 1 = \sigma(2)$) and$(1,3)$ (since$\sigma(1) = 3 > 2 = \sigma(3)$), but not$(2,3)$ (since$\sigma(2) = 1 < 2 = \sigma(3)$). The inversion count$N(\sigma) = 2$ for this permutation.

In general $N(\sigma)$ ranges from$0$ for the identity (which has no inversions) to$\binom{n}{2}$ for the reversal permutation$\sigma(i) = n+1-i$ (which reverses all pairs). The **sign** (or **signature**) of$\sigma$ is then defined as

$$\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) = (-1)^{N(\sigma)}.$$

A permutation is **even** if $\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) = +1$ and **odd** if$\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) = -1$. The key algebraic fact is that$\mathrm{sgn}$ is a group homomorphism$S_n \to \{+1, -1\}$:$\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma\tau) = \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) \cdot \mathrm{sgn}(\tau)$. This multiplicativity is not obvious from the inversion-count definition; one proof observes that composing with a transposition (a permutation swapping exactly two elements) changes$N(\sigma)$ by an odd number, hence changes the parity. Every transposition has sign$-1$, and since every permutation can be written as a product of transpositions, the sign is determined by the parity of the number of transpositions used (which is well-defined even though the decomposition is not unique).

The kernel of the homomorphism $\mathrm{sgn}: S_n \to \{+1,-1\}$ is the subgroup of even permutations, denoted$A_n$ and called the **alternating group**. The map$\mathrm{sgn}$ is surjective for$n \geq 2$ (the transposition$(1\ 2)$ has sign$-1$), so by the first isomorphism theorem$A_n$ is a normal subgroup of index$2$ in$S_n$, with$|A_n| = n!/2$. The alternating group is a fundamental object in group theory: for$n \geq 5$,$A_n$ is simple (has no nontrivial normal subgroups), and this simplicity is the key obstruction to solving degree-$5$ polynomials by radicals.

The connection between the sign function and the determinant is conceptually essential. The alternating property of the determinant — that swapping two rows negates it — corresponds algebraically to the fact that transpositions have sign $-1$. More precisely: the Leibniz formula can be rewritten as saying that$\det$ is the unique map that, when applied to an$n$-tuple of basis vectors$(e_{\sigma(1)}, \ldots, e_{\sigma(n)})$, returns$\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)$. The sign of a permutation is the "canonical determinant" of a permutation matrix.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Count inversions for $\sigma = (2, 4, 1, 3) \in S_4$ (writing$\sigma$ as a sequence of values). The pairs$(i,j)$ with$i < j$ but$\sigma(i) > \sigma(j)$: check$(1,2)$:$2 < 4$, no inversion;$(1,3)$:$2 > 1$, inversion;$(1,4)$:$2 < 3$, no;$(2,3)$:$4 > 1$, inversion;$(2,4)$:$4 > 3$, inversion;$(3,4)$:$1 < 3$, no. Total:$N(\sigma) = 3$, so$\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) = (-1)^3 = -1$ (odd permutation).
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Verify that every transposition is odd. Let $\tau = (i\ j)$ swap$i$ and$j$ with$i < j$ and fix all other elements. The elements$i, i+1, \ldots, j$ are "rearranged" by$\tau$:$i$ moves to position$j$ and$j$ moves to position$i$. The number of inversions created is$(j-i)$ (from$i$ "crossing over"$j-i-1$ elements between$i$ and$j$, plus 1 for the swap of$i$ and$j$ themselves) plus$(j-i-1)$ more (from$j$ crossing back), but a careful count gives$N(\tau) = 2(j-i) - 1$, which is odd. Hence$\mathrm{sgn}(\tau) = -1$ for every transposition.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Write $\sigma = (1\ 2\ 3) \in S_3$ as a product of transpositions and compute the sign. We have$(1\ 2\ 3) = (1\ 3)(1\ 2)$ (first apply$(1\ 2)$, then$(1\ 3)$). This is a product of$2$ transpositions, so$\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) = (-1)^2 = +1$ (even). Alternatively, the only inversions of$(1\ 2\ 3)$ viewed as a cycle sending$1\to 2, 2\to 3, 3\to 1$ (i.e., the sequence$(2,3,1)$) are$(1,3)$ since$2 > 1$ and$(2,3)$ since$3 > 1$, giving$N = 2$ and$\mathrm{sgn} = +1$. Both methods agree.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** Use the multiplicativity $\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma\tau) = \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)\mathrm{sgn}(\tau)$ to determine the sign of$\sigma = (1\ 2)(3\ 4)(1\ 3) \in S_4$. Each factor is a transposition with sign$-1$. Since there are three transpositions,$\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) = (-1)^3 = -1$. To verify: the composition sends$1 \mapsto 3 \mapsto 4$,$2 \mapsto 1 \mapsto 3 \mapsto 4$... let us compute directly.$(1\ 3)$ first:$1\to3,3\to1$. Then$(3\ 4)$:$3\to4,4\to3$. Then$(1\ 2)$:$1\to2,2\to1$. Tracking$1$:$1 \xrightarrow{(1\ 3)} 3 \xrightarrow{(3\ 4)} 4 \xrightarrow{(1\ 2)} 4$. Tracking$2$:$2 \xrightarrow{(1\ 3)} 2 \xrightarrow{(3\ 4)} 2 \xrightarrow{(1\ 2)} 1$. Tracking$3$:$3 \xrightarrow{(1\ 3)} 1 \xrightarrow{(3\ 4)} 1 \xrightarrow{(1\ 2)} 2$. Tracking$4$:$4 \xrightarrow{(1\ 3)} 4 \xrightarrow{(3\ 4)} 3 \xrightarrow{(1\ 2)} 3$. So$\sigma = (1\ 4\ 3\ 2)$, a 4-cycle. Its inversion count: the sequence is$(4,1,2,3)$. Inversions:$(1,2),(1,3),(1,4)$, giving$N=3$ and$\mathrm{sgn}=-1$. Confirmed.
\end{example}
