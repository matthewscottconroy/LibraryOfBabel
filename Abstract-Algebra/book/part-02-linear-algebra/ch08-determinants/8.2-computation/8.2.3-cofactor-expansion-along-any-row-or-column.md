# 8.2.3 Cofactor Expansion along Any Row or Column

The Leibniz formula gives an explicit expression for the determinant but is impractical for $n \geq 4$. A more structured approach is **cofactor expansion** (also called Laplace expansion), which reduces the determinant of an$n \times n$ matrix to a linear combination of$(n-1) \times (n-1)$ determinants. This enables recursive computation and, more importantly, provides an algebraic identity that is the foundation of the adjugate formula and Cramer's rule.

For each pair of indices $(i,j)$ with$1 \leq i,j \leq n$, define the **$(i,j)$-minor**$M_{ij}$ to be the determinant of the$(n-1) \times (n-1)$ submatrix obtained from$A$ by deleting row$i$ and column$j$. The **$(i,j)$-cofactor** is the signed minor:
$$C_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j} M_{ij}.$$
The sign $(-1)^{i+j}$ follows a checkerboard pattern: it is$+1$ when$i+j$ is even and$-1$ when$i+j$ is odd. In a$3 \times 3$ matrix, the sign pattern is$\begin{pmatrix}+&-&+\\-&+&-\\+&-&+\end{pmatrix}$.

The cofactor expansion along row $i$ is:
$$\det(A) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} A_{ij} C_{ij} = \sum_{j=1}^{n} A_{ij} (-1)^{i+j} M_{ij}.$$

This formula holds for **any** choice of row $i$. An analogous formula holds for cofactor expansion along any column$j$:
$$\det(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} A_{ij} C_{ij} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} A_{ij} (-1)^{i+j} M_{ij}.$$

To see why both formulas hold, one can verify directly from the Leibniz sum: when expanding along row $i$, group the$n!$ permutations by the value$\sigma(i) = j$. For each fixed$j$, the sub-sum over permutations with$\sigma(i) = j$ gives$A_{ij}$ times the$(n-1)!$-term sum for the minor$M_{ij}$, with sign$(-1)^{i+j}$ arising from the number of row/column transpositions needed to bring row$i$ and column$j$ to the top-left position.

The practical value of cofactor expansion is that we can choose whichever row or column has the most zeros, minimizing the number of nonzero terms. For a row with $k$ nonzero entries, we compute only$k$ minors of size$(n-1) \times (n-1)$. For$3 \times 3$ matrices, cofactor expansion is a standard and efficient method. For larger matrices, row reduction (Section 8.2.4) is asymptotically faster, but cofactor expansion remains indispensable for theoretical purposes: it underlies the adjugate formula (Section 8.3.2) and Cramer's rule (Section 8.3.3), and it arises naturally in the theory of characteristic polynomials.

An important identity related to cofactor expansion is the **alien cofactor identity**: if we expand "along the wrong row," the sum is zero. That is, for $i \neq k$:
$$\sum_{j=1}^n A_{ij} C_{kj} = 0.$$
This is because the sum computes the determinant of a matrix with row $i$ replaced twice — at positions$i$ and$k$ — which is a matrix with two equal rows and hence determinant zero. This identity is precisely what makes the adjugate formula work, as we see in Section 8.3.2.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Compute $\det\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 3 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$ by expanding along row 1. The cofactors along row 1 are:
\begin{align*}
C_{11} &= (+1)\det\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\2&-2\end{pmatrix} = -8-2 = -10,\\
C_{12} &= (-1)\det\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&-2\end{pmatrix} = (-1)(0-1) = 1,\\
C_{13} &= (+1)\det\begin{pmatrix}0&4\\1&2\end{pmatrix} = 0-4 = -4.
\end{align*}
Thus $\det = 2(-10) + (-1)(1) + 3(-4) = -20 - 1 - 12 = -33$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Use the column with the most zeros. Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 5 & 4 & 7 \end{pmatrix}$. Expanding along column 3: only$A_{33} = 7$ is nonzero (the other entries in column 3 are 0). Thus$\det(A) = 7 \cdot C_{33} = 7 \cdot (+1) \det\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\2&1\end{pmatrix} = 7 \cdot (3 - 0) = 21$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Illustrate the alien cofactor identity. Using the same matrix from Example 1, compute $\sum_j A_{1j} C_{2j}$ (expanding row 1 entries against row 2 cofactors). The cofactors of row 2 are:
\begin{align*}
C_{21} &= (-1)\det\begin{pmatrix}-1&3\\2&-2\end{pmatrix} = (-1)(2-6) = 4,\\
C_{22} &= (+1)\det\begin{pmatrix}2&3\\1&-2\end{pmatrix} = -4-3=-7,\\
C_{23} &= (-1)\det\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\1&2\end{pmatrix} = (-1)(4+1)=-5.
\end{align*}
Then $A_{11}C_{21} + A_{12}C_{22} + A_{13}C_{23} = 2(4) + (-1)(-7) + 3(-5) = 8 + 7 - 15 = 0$. The alien cofactor sum is indeed zero.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** Expand a $4 \times 4$ determinant recursively. Let$A = \begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&2\\3&1&0&0\\0&0&2&1\\0&1&0&3\end{pmatrix}$. Expand along row 1 (two nonzero entries:$A_{11}=1$ and$A_{14}=2$):
$$\det(A) = 1 \cdot C_{11} + 2 \cdot C_{14}.$$
$C_{11} = \det\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&2&1\\1&0&3\end{pmatrix}$. Expand this along row 1:$1\cdot\det\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&3\end{pmatrix} = 1(6-0)=6$. So$C_{11}=6$.
\par
$C_{14} = (-1)^{1+4}\det\begin{pmatrix}3&1&0\\0&0&2\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix} = -\det\begin{pmatrix}3&1&0\\0&0&2\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}$. Expand along row 1:$3\cdot C'_{11} = 3\cdot\det\begin{pmatrix}0&2\\1&0\end{pmatrix} = 3(0-2) = -6$, and$1\cdot C'_{12} = (-1)\det\begin{pmatrix}0&2\\0&0\end{pmatrix} = (-1)(0)=0$. So the$3\times 3$ determinant is$-6$, giving$C_{14} = -(-6) = 6$.
\par
Finally $\det(A) = 1(6) + 2(6) = 18$.
\end{example}
