# 8.2.5 Block Triangular Matrices

Many matrices that arise in applications and in structural theory have a natural block form, where the matrix decomposes into submatrices of specified sizes. The determinant behaves elegantly with respect to such structure: for block triangular matrices, $\det$ factors as a product of the block determinants. This is a theorem that vastly simplifies many computations, reducing a large matrix to several smaller ones.

Suppose $A$ has the block upper triangular form
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} B & C \\ 0 & D \end{pmatrix},$$
where $B$ is a$p \times p$ matrix,$D$ is a$q \times q$ matrix,$C$ is a$p \times q$ matrix, and$0$ denotes the$q \times p$ zero matrix in the lower-left block. Then
$$\det(A) = \det(B) \cdot \det(D).$$

One way to prove this is via the Leibniz formula: any permutation $\sigma \in S_{p+q}$ contributing a nonzero term must satisfy$\sigma(i) \leq p$ for$i \leq p$ (since$A_{ij} = 0$ for$i > p$ and$j \leq p$). This forces$\sigma$ to decompose as a permutation of$\{1,\ldots,p\}$ composed with a permutation of$\{p+1,\ldots,p+q\}$, and the product of entries factors accordingly as a product over$B$ and over$D$. The signs also factor:$\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) = \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma|_{\{1,\ldots,p\}}) \cdot \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma|_{\{p+1,\ldots,p+q\}})$. The entire sum therefore factors as$\det(B) \cdot \det(D)$.

A more structural proof uses row reduction: apply elementary row operations within the first $p$ rows to reduce$B$ to upper triangular form; this does not affect the lower-right block$D$ (since the lower-left block is zero). Then apply operations within the last$q$ rows to reduce$D$ to upper triangular form. The full matrix becomes upper triangular with the diagonal entries of$B$ followed by those of$D$, so$\det(A)$ equals the product of all diagonal entries, which factors as$\det(B) \cdot \det(D)$.

The upper-right block $C$ plays no role in the determinant — the result holds regardless of what$C$ is. Geometrically, one can think of the block triangular matrix as a transformation that first acts on a$p$-dimensional subspace (via$B$) and then on a complementary$q$-dimensional subspace (via$D$), with a "shear" in between. Shear operations do not change volume, so the total volume scaling is the product of the volume scalings of$B$ and$D$.

The result generalizes to block diagonal matrices with any number of blocks: $\det(B_1 \oplus B_2 \oplus \cdots \oplus B_k) = \det(B_1) \cdots \det(B_k)$. It also applies to block **lower** triangular matrices$\begin{pmatrix}B & 0 \\ C & D\end{pmatrix}$ by exactly the same argument (or by taking the transpose and applying$\det(A^T) = \det(A)$).

This factoring property is enormously useful when analyzing the characteristic polynomial of a block triangular matrix, since $\det(\lambda I - A) = \det(\lambda I_p - B) \cdot \det(\lambda I_q - D)$: the characteristic polynomial factors as the product of the characteristic polynomials of the diagonal blocks. This provides a key tool for studying invariant subspaces and for the Jordan canonical form.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Compute the determinant of the $4 \times 4$ block upper triangular matrix
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 4 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 5 & 6 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$$
with $B = \begin{pmatrix}2&3\\1&4\end{pmatrix}$ and$D = \begin{pmatrix}5&6\\3&2\end{pmatrix}$. Then$\det(B) = 8-3=5$ and$\det(D) = 10-18=-8$. Thus$\det(A) = 5 \cdot (-8) = -40$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Block diagonal matrix. Let $A = \mathrm{diag}(B_1, B_2, B_3)$ where$B_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&3\end{pmatrix}$,$B_2 = (5)$ (a$1\times 1$ block), and$B_3 = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$. Then$\det(B_1) = 3$,$\det(B_2) = 5$,$\det(B_3) = 4-1=3$. The full$5 \times 5$ determinant is$3 \cdot 5 \cdot 3 = 45$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Demonstrate that the upper-right block $C$ does not matter. Consider
$$A = \begin{pmatrix}2 & C_{12} \\ 0 & 3\end{pmatrix}$$
for any $1\times 1$ blocks, i.e.,$A = \begin{pmatrix}2&c\\0&3\end{pmatrix}$ for any scalar$c$. The determinant is always$\det\begin{pmatrix}2&c\\0&3\end{pmatrix} = 6$, regardless of$c$. This confirms that the off-diagonal block contributes nothing to the determinant.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** Application to characteristic polynomials. If $T$ has an invariant subspace giving block upper triangular form$A = \begin{pmatrix}B&C\\0&D\end{pmatrix}$, then
$$p_T(\lambda) = \det(\lambda I - A) = \det\!\begin{pmatrix}\lambda I_p - B & -C \\ 0 & \lambda I_q - D\end{pmatrix} = \det(\lambda I_p - B)\cdot\det(\lambda I_q - D) = p_B(\lambda)\cdot p_D(\lambda).$$
For example, if $B = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ (a Jordan block with eigenvalue 1) and$D = (3)$, then$p_T(\lambda) = (\lambda-1)^2(\lambda-3)$.
\end{example}
