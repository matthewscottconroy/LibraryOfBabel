# 8.2 Computation

Section 8.1 defined the determinant axiomatically and established its properties. This section constructs an explicit function satisfying those axioms — the Leibniz formula — and then develops the practical computational methods that follow from it. The section also proves uniqueness: the Leibniz formula is the only function satisfying the three axioms, confirming that the axiomatic and explicit approaches define the same object.

The Leibniz formula is $$\det(A) = \sum_{\sigma \in S_n} \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) \prod_{i=1}^n a_{i,\sigma(i)},$$ where the sum ranges over all $n!$ permutations of $\{1, \ldots, n\}$ and $\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) = \pm 1$ is the sign of the permutation. The sign is defined by writing $\sigma$ as a product of transpositions (pairwise swaps); the sign is $+1$ if the number of transpositions is even and $-1$ if it is odd. That this is well-defined — i.e., that the parity of the number of transpositions is an invariant of $\sigma$ — requires proof, and the section provides it by showing that every permutation has a well-defined parity via its cycle decomposition.

For practical computation the Leibniz formula is unusable for large $n$ (it has $n!$ terms), so the section develops two efficient alternatives. Cofactor expansion expresses the determinant of an $n \times n$ matrix as a linear combination of $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ determinants: expanding along row $i$ gives $\det(A) = \sum_j (-1)^{i+j} a_{ij} M_{ij}$, where $M_{ij}$ is the minor obtained by deleting row $i$ and column $j$. Cofactor expansion is valid along any row or column; choosing a row or column with many zeros minimizes the work. Row reduction to triangular form is more efficient for large matrices: the determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal entries, and row operations have known effects on the determinant, so one can track the overall scaling factor as the matrix is reduced.

Block triangular matrices receive special treatment: $\det\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix} A & B \\ 0 & D \end{smallmatrix}\bigr] = \det(A) \det(D)$. This identity, proved by expanding the Leibniz sum according to which columns the permutation takes from the upper-left block, is a powerful computational shortcut whenever a matrix has block-triangular structure. It also foreshadows the factorization of the characteristic polynomial when an operator decomposes into invariant subspaces.
