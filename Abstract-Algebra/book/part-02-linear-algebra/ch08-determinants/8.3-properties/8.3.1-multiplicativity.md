# 8.3.1 Multiplicativity: $\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$

The most important property of the determinant beyond its defining axioms is multiplicativity: for any two $n \times n$ matrices$A$ and$B$ over a field$F$,

$$\det(AB) = \det(A) \cdot \det(B).$$

This single identity encapsulates the interaction between the determinant and matrix multiplication, and it has far-reaching consequences that permeate all of linear algebra and much of modern mathematics. It is, in retrospect, not a coincidence: the determinant measures "volume scaling," and applying two successive linear maps scales volume by the product of their individual scaling factors.

The most elegant proof uses the uniqueness theorem from Section 8.1.2. Fix $B$ and define a function$\varphi: M_{n \times n}(F) \to F$ by$\varphi(A) = \det(AB)$. We claim$\varphi$ satisfies the three determinant axioms:
- **Multilinearity**: The rows of $AB$ are linear functions of the rows of$A$ (specifically, row$i$ of$AB$ is the same linear combination of rows of$B$ as row$i$ of$A$ is of the standard basis). Scaling or adding a multiple of one row of$A$ to another has the same effect on the rows of$AB$, so$\varphi$ is linear in each row of$A$.
- **Alternating property**: Swapping rows $i$ and$j$ of$A$ swaps the same rows of$AB$, which negates$\det(AB)$.
- **Normalization (modified)**: $\varphi(I) = \det(IB) = \det(B)$.

By the uniqueness theorem, the unique multilinear alternating function normalized to take value $\det(B)$ at$I$ is$\det(B) \cdot \det$. Hence$\varphi(A) = \det(B) \cdot \det(A)$, which is the desired identity.

The consequences cascade. Since $AA^{-1} = I$ implies$\det(A)\det(A^{-1}) = \det(I) = 1$, we get$\det(A^{-1}) = \det(A)^{-1}$ for invertible matrices. A matrix is invertible if and only if$\det(A) \neq 0$ (since$\det(A) = 0$ means$A$ is singular, and conversely). Taking$B = A^T$ and using the fact (from Section 8.2.1) that$\det(A^T) = \det(A)$, we see that all column-based properties mirror their row counterparts.

Similar matrices $A$ and$A' = P^{-1}AP$ satisfy$\det(A') = \det(P^{-1})\det(A)\det(P) = \det(P)^{-1}\det(A)\det(P) = \det(A)$. This confirms that the determinant is a similarity invariant — it depends only on the linear operator$T$, not on the basis used to represent it. This is essential for the definition of the characteristic polynomial in Section 8.4.

More structurally, $\det$ is a group homomorphism from the group of invertible$n \times n$ matrices$GL_n(F)$ (under multiplication) to the multiplicative group$F^\times$. The kernel of this homomorphism is the **special linear group**$SL_n(F) = \{A \in GL_n(F) : \det(A) = 1\}$, a normal subgroup of$GL_n(F)$ with quotient$GL_n(F)/SL_n(F) \cong F^\times$. The special linear group is a central object in Lie theory, algebraic geometry, and number theory.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Verify multiplicativity directly for $2 \times 2$ matrices. Let$A = \begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{pmatrix}$ and$B = \begin{pmatrix}5&6\\7&8\end{pmatrix}$. Then$\det(A) = 4-6=-2$ and$\det(B) = 40-42=-2$. Compute$AB = \begin{pmatrix}1\cdot5+2\cdot7 & 1\cdot6+2\cdot8\\3\cdot5+4\cdot7&3\cdot6+4\cdot8\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}19&22\\43&50\end{pmatrix}$. Then$\det(AB) = 19\cdot50-22\cdot43 = 950-946=4 = (-2)(-2) = \det(A)\det(B)$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Use multiplicativity to compute a determinant. Let $A = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}^{10}$, i.e.,$A$ is the tenth power of a lower triangular matrix. Rather than computing$A$ explicitly, use$\det(A) = \det\!\left(\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\right)^{10} = 1^{10} = 1$. No matrix multiplication needed.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Show that the map $\det: GL_n(F) \to F^\times$ is a group homomorphism. We need: (i)$\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$ (the homomorphism property — just proved), (ii)$\det(I) = 1$ (the identity maps to the identity), (iii)$\det(A^{-1}) = \det(A)^{-1}$ (inverses map to inverses). All three hold by the results above. The kernel is$SL_n(F)$; for example,$SL_2(\mathbb{R}) = \{A \in M_2(\mathbb{R}): \det(A) = 1\}$ consists of area-preserving linear maps.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** Similarity invariance. Let $A = \begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ (rotation by$90°$, with$\det(A) = 1$) and$P = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ (with$\det(P)=1$). Then$P^{-1}AP = \begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$. Regardless of the outcome,$\det(P^{-1}AP) = \det(P^{-1})\det(A)\det(P) = 1 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 = 1 = \det(A)$, confirming similarity invariance without computing$P^{-1}AP$ explicitly.
\end{example}
