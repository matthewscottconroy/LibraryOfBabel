# 8.3.2 The Adjugate Matrix and the Formula for the Inverse

Cofactor expansion gives not just a method for computing determinants, but also an explicit formula for the inverse of a matrix. The key construction is the **adjugate** (also called the *classical adjoint*) of $A$, which packages all the cofactors of$A$ into a single matrix. The adjugate is significant both as a theoretical tool — appearing in Cramer's rule, in formulas for implicit differentiation, and in algebraic geometry — and as a demonstration that the inverse, when it exists, is an explicit rational function of the entries of$A$.

Recall from Section 8.2.3 that the $(i,j)$-cofactor of$A$ is$C_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j} M_{ij}$, where$M_{ij}$ is the$(i,j)$-minor (the determinant of$A$ with row$i$ and column$j$ deleted). The **adjugate**$\mathrm{adj}(A)$ is the transpose of the matrix of cofactors:
$$\mathrm{adj}(A)_{ij} = C_{ji} = (-1)^{i+j} M_{ji}.$$
Note carefully: the $(i,j)$ entry of$\mathrm{adj}(A)$ is the$(j,i)$ cofactor of$A$, not the$(i,j)$ cofactor. The indices are transposed. This is not a typo — it is essential for the fundamental identity to hold.

The fundamental identity relating the adjugate to the determinant is:
$$A \cdot \mathrm{adj}(A) = \det(A) \cdot I_n.$$

To verify, compute the $(i,k)$ entry of$A \cdot \mathrm{adj}(A)$:
$$[A \cdot \mathrm{adj}(A)]_{ik} = \sum_{j=1}^n A_{ij} \cdot \mathrm{adj}(A)_{jk} = \sum_{j=1}^n A_{ij} C_{kj}.$$

When $i = k$, this sum is the cofactor expansion of$\det(A)$ along row$i$, giving$\det(A)$. When$i \neq k$, the sum$\sum_j A_{ij} C_{kj}$ is the "alien cofactor" sum from Section 8.2.3 — it computes the determinant of the matrix obtained from$A$ by replacing row$k$ with row$i$. Since this matrix has two identical rows (rows$i$ and the original row$k$ are now both$r_i$), its determinant is zero. Combining:$[A \cdot \mathrm{adj}(A)]_{ik} = \det(A) \cdot \delta_{ik}$, so$A \cdot \mathrm{adj}(A) = \det(A) \cdot I$.

By a symmetric argument (or by taking transposes), $\mathrm{adj}(A) \cdot A = \det(A) \cdot I$ as well.

When $\det(A) \neq 0$, we can divide both sides by$\det(A)$ to obtain the **inverse formula**:
$$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(A)} \mathrm{adj}(A).$$

This formula provides an explicit expression for $A^{-1}$ with each entry being a rational function of the entries of$A$. It is theoretically powerful: it implies that matrix inversion is a smooth (indeed, real-analytic) operation on the open set$\{A : \det(A) \neq 0\}$, and it makes the entries of$A^{-1}$ explicit. For practical computation, however, row reduction is far more efficient: the adjugate formula requires computing$n^2$ cofactors, each a determinant of size$(n-1) \times (n-1)$, leading to$O(n \cdot (n-1)!)$ work by direct computation. Row reduction is$O(n^3)$. For$n \geq 4$, there is no contest.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Compute the inverse of $A = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\5&3\end{pmatrix}$ via the adjugate formula.$\det(A) = 6-5=1$. The cofactors:$C_{11}=3$,$C_{12}=-5$,$C_{21}=-1$,$C_{22}=2$. The cofactor matrix is$\begin{pmatrix}3&-5\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}$, and the adjugate (its transpose) is$\mathrm{adj}(A) = \begin{pmatrix}3&-1\\-5&2\end{pmatrix}$. Thus$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{1}\begin{pmatrix}3&-1\\-5&2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}3&-1\\-5&2\end{pmatrix}$. Verify:$\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\5&3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}3&-1\\-5&2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}6-5&-2+2\\15-15&-5+6\end{pmatrix} = I$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Compute $\mathrm{adj}(A)$ for$A = \begin{pmatrix}1&2&0\\0&1&1\\1&0&2\end{pmatrix}$. The nine cofactors:
\begin{align*}
C_{11}&=\det\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}=2,\quad C_{12}=-\det\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}=1,\quad C_{13}=\det\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}=-1,\\
C_{21}&=-\det\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}=-4,\quad C_{22}=\det\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&2\end{pmatrix}=2,\quad C_{23}=-\det\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\1&0\end{pmatrix}=2,\\
C_{31}&=\det\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}=2,\quad C_{32}=-\det\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}=-1,\quad C_{33}=\det\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{pmatrix}=1.
\end{align*}
Thus $\mathrm{adj}(A) = \begin{pmatrix}C_{11}&C_{21}&C_{31}\\C_{12}&C_{22}&C_{32}\\C_{13}&C_{23}&C_{33}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2&-4&2\\1&2&-1\\-1&2&1\end{pmatrix}$.
$\det(A) = 1\cdot2+2\cdot(-1)+0 = 0$? Let us recheck:$\det(A)=1(2-0)-2(0-1)+0=2+2=4$. So$A^{-1}=\frac{1}{4}\begin{pmatrix}2&-4&2\\1&2&-1\\-1&2&1\end{pmatrix}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** The adjugate of a singular matrix. If $\det(A)=0$, then$A\cdot\mathrm{adj}(A)=0$, so$\mathrm{adj}(A)$ either is the zero matrix or has image contained in the null space of$A$. For$A = \begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix}$ (rank 1,$\det=0$), the cofactors are$C_{11}=4$,$C_{12}=-2$,$C_{21}=-2$,$C_{22}=1$, and$\mathrm{adj}(A)=\begin{pmatrix}4&-2\\-2&1\end{pmatrix}$. Indeed$A\cdot\mathrm{adj}(A)=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}4&-2\\-2&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}=0\cdot I$.
\end{example}
