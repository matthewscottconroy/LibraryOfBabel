# 8.3 Properties

With the determinant defined and computable, this section develops its deepest properties: multiplicativity, the adjugate formula for the matrix inverse, Cramer's rule, and the geometric interpretation as signed volume. These properties reveal why the determinant is not just a numerical trick but a fundamental invariant that connects algebra, geometry, and analysis.

Multiplicativity — $\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$ — is the central theorem of this section. The proof proceeds in two steps. First, if $A$ is singular (not invertible), then $AB$ is also singular (since $\mathrm{rank}(AB) \leq \mathrm{rank}(A) < n$), so both $\det(A) = 0$ and $\det(AB) = 0$. Second, if $A$ is invertible, then $A$ can be written as a product of elementary matrices $A = E_1 \cdots E_k$, and one checks the formula for each elementary matrix type, then chains them together. The conclusion is that $\det: GL_n(F) \to F^\times$ is a group homomorphism from the general linear group to the multiplicative group of the field. Its kernel — the matrices with determinant 1 — forms the special linear group $SL_n(F)$, a fundamental object in algebra and geometry.

The adjugate matrix provides an explicit formula for the matrix inverse. Define $\mathrm{adj}(A)$ as the transpose of the cofactor matrix: $(\mathrm{adj}(A))_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j} M_{ji}$. Then $A \cdot \mathrm{adj}(A) = \det(A) \cdot I$. This identity, verified by cofactor expansion, shows that when $\det(A) \neq 0$, the inverse is $A^{-1} = \det(A)^{-1} \mathrm{adj}(A)$. Cramer's rule for solving $Ax = b$ follows immediately: the $i$-th component of the solution is $x_i = \det(A_i)/\det(A)$, where $A_i$ is the matrix obtained by replacing the $i$-th column of $A$ with $b$. Cramer's rule is not computationally efficient (it requires $n+1$ determinant evaluations), but it is theoretically illuminating: it makes the dependence of the solution on the data explicit and linear.

The geometric interpretation of the determinant unifies the algebraic properties with spatial intuition. The absolute value $|\det(A)|$ is the $n$-dimensional volume of the parallelepiped (parallelepiped) spanned by the column vectors of $A$. The sign of $\det(A)$ records orientation: positive if the columns form a right-handed frame (one consistently oriented with the standard basis) and negative otherwise. Linear maps with $|\det| > 1$ expand volumes, those with $|\det| < 1$ contract them, and $|\det| = 0$ means the image is lower-dimensional — a collapse to a hyperplane or lower. This interpretation makes $\det$ the natural tool for the change-of-variables formula in multivariable calculus, where the Jacobian determinant measures the local volume distortion of a differentiable map.
