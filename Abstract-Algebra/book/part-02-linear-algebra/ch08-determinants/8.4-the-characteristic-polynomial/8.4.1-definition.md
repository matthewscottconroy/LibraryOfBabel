# 8.4.1 Definition: $p_T(\lambda) = \det(\lambda I - A)$

Among all the information one might extract from a matrix or a linear operator, the eigenvalues are often the most important: they tell us the directions that the operator simply scales, and the scaling factors by which it acts on those directions. But eigenvalues are roots of a polynomial, and polynomials are objects we can work with algebraically. The right tool for systematically packaging and computing eigenvalues is the **characteristic polynomial**, which encodes all eigenvalue data of an operator in a single polynomial.

The motivation for the definition is straightforward. A scalar $\lambda_0 \in F$ is an eigenvalue of$T$ if and only if$T - \lambda_0 \mathrm{id}$ is not invertible — that is, if and only if$\det(\lambda_0 I - A) = 0$ for any matrix$A$ representing$T$. So the eigenvalues are exactly the zeros of the function$\lambda \mapsto \det(\lambda I - A)$. We give this function a name.

\begin{definition}
Let $T: V \to V$ be a linear operator on an$n$-dimensional vector space$V$ over a field$F$, and let$A = [T]_{\mathcal{B}}$ be its matrix in some basis$\mathcal{B}$. The **characteristic polynomial** of$T$ is
$$p_T(\lambda) = \det(\lambda I - A) \in F[\lambda].$$
\end{definition}

Here $\lambda I - A$ denotes the$n \times n$ matrix obtained by subtracting$A$ from the scalar matrix$\lambda I_n$; its entries are linear polynomials in$\lambda$. The determinant of a matrix with polynomial entries is itself a polynomial (by the Leibniz formula, it is a sum of products of entries), so$p_T(\lambda)$ is indeed a polynomial in$\lambda$.

The convention $\det(\lambda I - A)$ rather than$\det(A - \lambda I)$ is standard: it gives a **monic** polynomial (leading coefficient$+1$), since the diagonal entries of$\lambda I - A$ are$\lambda - A_{11}, \ldots, \lambda - A_{nn}$, and the leading term$\lambda^n$ comes from the product of these. The alternative$\det(A - \lambda I) = (-1)^n p_T(\lambda)$ is also used in some sources, so be aware of sign conventions.

The eigenvalues of $T$ are precisely the roots of$p_T$. To see why:$\lambda_0$ is an eigenvalue if and only if$Av = \lambda_0 v$ for some nonzero$v$, i.e.,$(\lambda_0 I - A)v = 0$, i.e.,$\lambda_0 I - A$ is singular, i.e.,$\det(\lambda_0 I - A) = 0$, i.e.,$p_T(\lambda_0) = 0$. The characteristic polynomial is thus the key to finding all eigenvalues: to find them, solve$p_T(\lambda) = 0$.

Over an algebraically closed field (like $\mathbb{C}$),$p_T$ factors completely into linear factors, so every$n \times n$ matrix has exactly$n$ eigenvalues (counted with multiplicity). Over$\mathbb{R}$, there may be no real roots if the polynomial has only complex roots; for example, a rotation by$90°$ has characteristic polynomial$\lambda^2 + 1$, which has no real roots, confirming that no real vector is simply scaled by the rotation.

The characteristic polynomial will be shown in Section 8.4.3 to be independent of the choice of basis — it is a true invariant of $T$, not of its matrix$A$. This independence is crucial: it means$p_T$ is a property of the linear operator itself, not an artifact of how we write it down.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Compute the characteristic polynomial of $A = \begin{pmatrix}3 & 1 \\ 2 & 4\end{pmatrix}$. We have$\lambda I - A = \begin{pmatrix}\lambda-3 & -1 \\ -2 & \lambda-4\end{pmatrix}$, so$p_A(\lambda) = (\lambda-3)(\lambda-4) - (-1)(-2) = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 12 - 2 = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10$. The eigenvalues are roots:$\lambda = \frac{7 \pm \sqrt{49-40}}{2} = \frac{7 \pm 3}{2}$, giving$\lambda = 5$ and$\lambda = 2$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** A matrix with complex eigenvalues. Let $A = \begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ (rotation by$90°$). Then$p_A(\lambda) = \det\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&1\\-1&\lambda\end{pmatrix} = \lambda^2+1$. The roots are$\lambda = \pm i \in \mathbb{C}$, so$A$ has no real eigenvalues. Over$\mathbb{C}$, we could find eigenvectors in$\mathbb{C}^2$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Characteristic polynomial of an upper triangular matrix. Let $U = \begin{pmatrix}2&5&3\\0&1&4\\0&0&3\end{pmatrix}$. Then$\lambda I - U = \begin{pmatrix}\lambda-2&-5&-3\\0&\lambda-1&-4\\0&0&\lambda-3\end{pmatrix}$, which is upper triangular with diagonal entries$\lambda-2$,$\lambda-1$,$\lambda-3$. The determinant of an upper triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal entries, so$p_U(\lambda) = (\lambda-2)(\lambda-1)(\lambda-3)$. The eigenvalues are$1, 2, 3$ — exactly the diagonal entries. This generalizes: for any triangular matrix, the eigenvalues are the diagonal entries.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** The characteristic polynomial encodes more than just the eigenvalues. For $A = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$,$p_A(\lambda) = (\lambda-2)^2$. The only eigenvalue is$\lambda = 2$ with algebraic multiplicity$2$. But the matrix$A = 2I + \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ is not diagonalizable — there is only a one-dimensional eigenspace. The characteristic polynomial alone does not tell us this; we need additional invariants (the minimal polynomial, or the Jordan form) to distinguish this matrix from$2I$.
\end{example}
