# 8.4.2 Degree, Leading Term, and Coefficients

The characteristic polynomial $p_T(\lambda) = \det(\lambda I - A)$ is a degree-$n$ polynomial in$\lambda$, but beyond knowing it is monic of degree$n$, we can say a great deal about its coefficients. These coefficients are not arbitrary: they are specific polynomial invariants of$A$, and the two extreme ones — the coefficient of$\lambda^{n-1}$ and the constant term — are especially important because they equal (up to sign) the trace and determinant of$A$.

To extract the coefficients, expand $\det(\lambda I - A)$ via the Leibniz formula. The diagonal entries of$\lambda I - A$ are$\lambda - A_{11}, \ldots, \lambda - A_{nn}$; the off-diagonal entry in position$(i,j)$ with$i \neq j$ is$-A_{ij}$, which has no$\lambda$ factor. Each term in the Leibniz sum$\sum_\sigma \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_i (\lambda I - A)_{i,\sigma(i)}$ selects one entry from each row. For the product to contribute to the degree-$n$ or degree-$(n-1)$ terms in$\lambda$, it must use as many diagonal entries as possible (since only diagonal entries contain$\lambda$).

The leading term $\lambda^n$ comes from the identity permutation alone, taking all diagonal entries:$\prod_i (\lambda - A_{ii})$, which contributes$\lambda^n$ (and lower-order terms). The coefficient of$\lambda^{n-1}$: from the same product$\prod_i (\lambda - A_{ii})$, picking$\lambda$ from all factors except one and$-A_{ii}$ from that one gives$-A_{11}\lambda^{n-1} - \cdots - A_{nn}\lambda^{n-1} = -\mathrm{tr}(A)\,\lambda^{n-1}$. Any other permutation$\sigma \neq \mathrm{id}$ uses at least two off-diagonal entries (since$\sigma$ moves at least two elements), contributing terms of degree at most$n-2$. So the coefficient of$\lambda^{n-1}$ is exactly$-\mathrm{tr}(A)$.

For the constant term: set $\lambda = 0$. Then$p_T(0) = \det(-A) = (-1)^n \det(A)$ (since$-A$ is$A$ with each row scaled by$-1$, contributing a factor$(-1)^n$). So the constant term is$(-1)^n \det(A)$.

The full polynomial takes the form:
$$p_T(\lambda) = \lambda^n - \mathrm{tr}(A)\,\lambda^{n-1} + \cdots + (-1)^n \det(A).$$

The intermediate coefficients can also be computed: the coefficient of $\lambda^{n-k}$ is$(-1)^k e_k(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n)$, where$e_k$ is the$k$-th elementary symmetric polynomial in the eigenvalues$\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n$ (over the algebraic closure). In terms of the matrix, the coefficient of$\lambda^{n-k}$ is$(-1)^k$ times the sum of all$k \times k$ principal minors of$A$.

The two extreme coefficients carry deep information. The trace equals $\sum_i \lambda_i$ (sum of eigenvalues counted with algebraic multiplicity), while the determinant equals$\prod_i \lambda_i$ (their product). These identities follow from writing$p_T(\lambda) = \prod_{i=1}^n (\lambda - \lambda_i)$ over the algebraic closure and comparing coefficients: the degree-$(n-1)$ coefficient of$\prod(\lambda - \lambda_i)$ is$-\sum_i \lambda_i$, and the constant term is$(-1)^n \prod_i \lambda_i$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Verify the trace-determinant formulas for $A = \begin{pmatrix}3&1\\2&4\end{pmatrix}$. We have$\mathrm{tr}(A) = 7$ and$\det(A) = 10$. From Example 1 of Section 8.4.1,$p_A(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10$. Indeed the coefficient of$\lambda^{n-1} = \lambda^1$ is$-7 = -\mathrm{tr}(A)$, and the constant term is$10 = (-1)^2\det(A) = \det(A)$. The eigenvalues are$2$ and$5$, and$2+5=7=\mathrm{tr}(A)$,$2\cdot5=10=\det(A)$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Compute the characteristic polynomial of $A = \begin{pmatrix}1&2&0\\0&3&1\\0&0&2\end{pmatrix}$ by reading off the diagonal. Since$A$ is upper triangular,$\lambda I - A$ is also upper triangular with diagonal entries$\lambda-1$,$\lambda-3$,$\lambda-2$. Thus$p_A(\lambda) = (\lambda-1)(\lambda-3)(\lambda-2)$. Expanding:$= \lambda^3 - 6\lambda^2 + 11\lambda - 6$. The coefficient of$\lambda^2$ is$-6 = -(1+3+2) = -\mathrm{tr}(A)$, and the constant term is$-6 = (-1)^3(1)(3)(2) = (-1)^3\det(A)$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Intermediate coefficient from principal minors. For $A = \begin{pmatrix}2&1&0\\1&3&1\\0&1&4\end{pmatrix}$, the coefficient of$\lambda^1 = \lambda^{3-2}$ is$(-1)^2$ times the sum of all$2 \times 2$ principal minors. The three$2 \times 2$ principal minors (obtained by deleting row/column$k$ for$k=1,2,3$) are:$\det\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&4\end{pmatrix}=11$,$\det\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&4\end{pmatrix}=8$,$\det\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}=5$. Sum$= 24$. Coefficient of$\lambda$ is$+24$. The full polynomial:$\mathrm{tr}(A)=9$,$\det(A)=2(11)-1(4)+0=18$, so$p_A(\lambda)=\lambda^3-9\lambda^2+24\lambda-18$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** Relationship to Newton's identities. If $p_k = \sum_i \lambda_i^k$ denotes the$k$-th power sum of eigenvalues, then Newton's identities relate the$p_k$ to the elementary symmetric polynomials$e_k$ (i.e., the coefficients of$p_T$). For$n=2$:$p_1 = e_1 = \mathrm{tr}(A)$ and$p_2 = e_1^2 - 2e_2 = \mathrm{tr}(A)^2 - 2\det(A) = \mathrm{tr}(A^2)$. For$A = \begin{pmatrix}3&1\\2&4\end{pmatrix}$:$p_2 = 7^2 - 2(10) = 49-20=29$. Direct check:$A^2 = \begin{pmatrix}11&7\\14&18\end{pmatrix}$,$\mathrm{tr}(A^2)=29$. These identities allow one to compute the coefficients of$p_T$ from the traces of powers of$A$.
\end{example}
