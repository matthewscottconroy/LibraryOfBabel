# 8.4.3 Similarity Invariance; the Characteristic Polynomial as an Invariant of $T$

The characteristic polynomial $p_T(\lambda) = \det(\lambda I - A)$ is computed from a matrix$A$ representing the operator$T$ in some basis, but the definition insists that it is an invariant of$T$ itself. For this to be meaningful,$p_T$ must be the same no matter which basis we choose to represent$T$. The verification is direct and short, but the conceptual consequence is important:$p_T$ is a basis-independent invariant of the linear operator.

\begin{proposition}
If $A' = P^{-1}AP$ is similar to$A$ (with$P$ invertible), then$\det(\lambda I - A') = \det(\lambda I - A)$.
\end{proposition}

The proof uses only the multiplicativity of $\det$ and the fact that$P^{-1}(\lambda I)P = \lambda I$:
\begin{align*}
\det(\lambda I - A') &= \det(\lambda I - P^{-1}AP)\\
&= \det(P^{-1}(\lambda I)P - P^{-1}AP)\\
&= \det(P^{-1}(\lambda I - A)P)\\
&= \det(P^{-1})\det(\lambda I - A)\det(P)\\
&= \det(\lambda I - A),
\end{align*}
where the last step uses $\det(P^{-1})\det(P) = 1$.

This result means that $p_T$ is a genuine invariant of the linear operator$T: V \to V$, not of any particular matrix representing it. It is the first of many "basis-independent" invariants that we will encounter. Since any two matrices representing the same linear operator are similar, they have the same characteristic polynomial. Conversely — and this is an important point — the converse is false in general: matrices with the same characteristic polynomial need not be similar.

The standard counterexample is the pair $A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$ and$B = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix} = I_2$. Both have characteristic polynomial$(\lambda-1)^2$, but they are not similar:$B$ is in the center of$M_2(F)$ (it commutes with everything), while$A$ does not commute with$\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$. More intrinsically,$A \neq I$ but$A$ has only one-dimensional eigenspace for$\lambda = 1$, while$I$ has two-dimensional eigenspace. The characteristic polynomial cannot distinguish them.

The characteristic polynomial does provide a useful necessary condition for similarity: if $p_A(\lambda) \neq p_B(\lambda)$, then$A$ and$B$ cannot be similar. This already makes$p_T$ a powerful tool for ruling out similarity. The complete invariant for similarity over an algebraically closed field is the Jordan normal form (Chapter 10), which records both the characteristic polynomial and the sizes of the Jordan blocks. The characteristic polynomial is recovered from the Jordan form as the product of the characteristic polynomials of the individual blocks.

The invariants encoded in $p_T$ include the trace ($-$ coefficient of$\lambda^{n-1}$), the determinant (constant term up to sign), and in principle all$k \times k$ principal minors (as coefficients of intermediate powers). These are all similarity invariants, computable from any matrix representation of$T$.

The characteristic polynomial thus serves as the bridge between the determinant (Chapter 8) and the eigenvalue theory and canonical forms of Parts II and III. Everything developed here — the axiomatic definition of $\det$, multiplicativity, cofactor expansion — feeds into the definition and invariance of$p_T$, and from there into the classification of linear operators up to similarity.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Verify directly that similar matrices have the same characteristic polynomial. Let $A = \begin{pmatrix}2&3\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and$P = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$. Then$P^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and
$$A' = P^{-1}AP = \begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}2&3\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2&4\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2&6\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.$$
Then $p_A(\lambda)=(\lambda-2)(\lambda-1)=\lambda^2-3\lambda+2$ and$p_{A'}(\lambda)=(\lambda-2)(\lambda-1)=\lambda^2-3\lambda+2$. Same polynomial, as expected.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Two matrices with the same characteristic polynomial but different Jordan forms. Let $A = \begin{pmatrix}3&1\\0&3\end{pmatrix}$ and$B = \begin{pmatrix}3&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix} = 3I_2$. Both have$p(\lambda) = (\lambda-3)^2$. But$A \neq B$:$A$ has a one-dimensional eigenspace$\mathrm{span}\{e_1\}$ for$\lambda = 3$, while$B = 3I$ has all of$F^2$ as eigenspace. The Jordan form of$A$ is$\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\0&3\end{pmatrix}$ itself (already a Jordan block), while$B$ is already diagonal. They are not similar because$A - 3I = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ has rank 1 while$B - 3I = 0$ has rank 0.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Use the characteristic polynomial to rule out similarity. Let $A = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$ and$B = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&3\end{pmatrix}$. Then$p_A(\lambda) = (\lambda-1)(\lambda-2) = \lambda^2-3\lambda+2$ and$p_B(\lambda) = (\lambda-1)(\lambda-3)=\lambda^2-4\lambda+3$. Since$p_A \neq p_B$,$A$ and$B$ are not similar. No further computation is needed.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** Characteristic polynomial of a projection. Let $P$ be a projection onto a$k$-dimensional subspace of$F^n$ (so$P^2 = P$). The eigenvalues of$P$ can only be$0$ (for vectors in$\ker(P)$) and$1$ (for vectors in$\mathrm{im}(P)$), so$p_P(\lambda) = \lambda^{n-k}(\lambda-1)^k$. For any basis in which$P$ is block diagonal$\begin{pmatrix}I_k&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$, the characteristic polynomial is immediate. The invariance result tells us this holds in any basis.
\end{example}
