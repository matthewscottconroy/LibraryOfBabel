# 8.4 The Characteristic Polynomial

The characteristic polynomial is the bridge between the determinant and eigentheory. For an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ over a field $F$, the characteristic polynomial is defined as $$\chi_A(\lambda) = \det(\lambda I - A),$$ a polynomial in the variable $\lambda$. The roots of $\chi_A$ in $F$ are precisely the eigenvalues of $A$: the values $\lambda_0$ for which $A - \lambda_0 I$ is singular, i.e., for which there exists a nonzero vector $v$ with $Av = \lambda_0 v$. The characteristic polynomial thus encodes all eigenvalue information in a single algebraic object, and its structure — degree, coefficients, factorization pattern — determines the spectral properties of $A$.

Computing $\chi_A(\lambda) = \det(\lambda I - A)$ using cofactor expansion reveals the structure of the polynomial. It has degree $n$ (the leading term is $\lambda^n$ from the diagonal entries), constant term $(-1)^n \det(A)$ (obtained by setting $\lambda = 0$), and coefficient of $\lambda^{n-1}$ equal to $-\mathrm{tr}(A)$ (from the $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ minor structure). The trace and determinant appear as the first and last non-leading coefficients, confirming their status as the most basic spectral invariants.

The central result of this section is that the characteristic polynomial is a similarity invariant. If $B = P^{-1}AP$, then $$\chi_B(\lambda) = \det(\lambda I - P^{-1}AP) = \det(P^{-1}(\lambda I - A)P) = \det(P^{-1})\det(\lambda I - A)\det(P) = \chi_A(\lambda).$$ This calculation — which uses the multiplicativity of the determinant and the fact that $P^{-1}(\lambda I)P = \lambda I$ — shows that similar matrices have the same characteristic polynomial. Therefore $\chi_A$ is not just a property of the matrix $A$ but of the underlying linear operator $T$: it is well-defined independently of the basis used to represent $T$. The characteristic polynomial is thus an intrinsic invariant of $T$, just as the trace and determinant are.

The section anticipates the spectral theory of Chapter 9 by asking: when does the characteristic polynomial factor completely into linear factors over $F$? Over $\mathbb{C}$, the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra guarantees that every polynomial splits, so every complex matrix has eigenvalues. Over $\mathbb{R}$, real matrices may have only complex eigenvalues (e.g., rotation matrices), and over $\mathbb{F}_p$, the polynomial may be irreducible for other reasons. The extent to which the characteristic polynomial splits — and the relationship between the algebraic multiplicities (the exponents in the factored characteristic polynomial) and the geometric multiplicities (the dimensions of eigenspaces) — is the central question of Chapter 9. The gap between these two multiplicities is precisely what prevents a matrix from being diagonalizable and drives the theory toward Jordan canonical form.
