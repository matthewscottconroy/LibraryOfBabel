# Chapter 8 — Determinants

The determinant is a function that assigns to each square matrix a single scalar, and it has the remarkable property of detecting exactly one thing: whether the matrix is invertible. That a single number could encode invertibility — a structural question about an $n \times n$ array of $n^2$ entries — is already surprising. What is more surprising is how much additional geometry, algebra, and combinatorics the determinant simultaneously encodes: the signed volume of the parallelepiped spanned by the columns, the sign of the permutation underlying the Leibniz formula, and the algebraic key to Cramer's rule, the adjugate matrix, and the characteristic polynomial. This chapter develops all of these threads from a single axiomatic foundation.

The axiomatic approach is the cleanest. A determinant is characterized as the unique function $\det: M_{n \times n}(F) \to F$ that is multilinear and alternating in the columns and satisfies $\det(I) = 1$. Multilinearity means that $\det$ is linear in each column when all other columns are held fixed. Alternating means that $\det$ changes sign when any two columns are swapped. Normalization pins down the scale. From these three axioms alone, the chapter derives every property of the determinant: that it vanishes when any two columns are equal, that it is zero precisely when the columns are linearly dependent, and that elementary row operations have predictable effects. The existence of a function satisfying these axioms — the Leibniz formula — is proved in the computation section, establishing that the axioms are satisfiable.

The Leibniz formula expresses $\det(A)$ as a signed sum over all permutations $\sigma \in S_n$: $$\det(A) = \sum_{\sigma \in S_n} \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) \prod_{i=1}^n a_{i,\sigma(i)}.$$ Each term is a product of $n$ entries, one from each row and each column, weighted by the sign of the permutation. For a $2 \times 2$ matrix this gives the familiar $ad - bc$; for $3 \times 3$ it gives the six-term formula. The sign of a permutation — $+1$ for even permutations and $-1$ for odd ones — is introduced carefully, with the fact that every permutation decomposes into transpositions and that the parity of the number of transpositions is well-defined. Cofactor expansion along any row or column is derived as a computational corollary of the Leibniz formula, and row reduction provides the most efficient method in practice.

The chapter's most important property is multiplicativity: $\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$. This single equation has far-reaching consequences. It implies that $A$ is invertible iff $\det(A) \neq 0$ (since $\det(A)\det(A^{-1}) = \det(I) = 1$), and it makes the map $\det: GL_n(F) \to F^\times$ a group homomorphism from the invertible matrices to the multiplicative group of the field. The geometric interpretation is equally beautiful: $|\det(A)|$ is the factor by which $A$ scales $n$-dimensional volumes, and the sign of $\det(A)$ records whether $A$ preserves or reverses orientation. The adjugate matrix formula $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(A)}\,\mathrm{adj}(A)$ and Cramer's rule for solving $Ax = b$ both fall out of the cofactor expansion. The chapter closes by defining the characteristic polynomial $\chi_T(\lambda) = \det(\lambda I - T)$, a degree-$n$ polynomial whose roots are the eigenvalues of $T$, which opens the door to Chapter 9.
