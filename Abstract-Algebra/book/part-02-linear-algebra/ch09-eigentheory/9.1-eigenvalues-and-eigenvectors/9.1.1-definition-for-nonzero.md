# 9.1.1 Definition: $Tv = \lambda v$ for Nonzero$v$

A linear operator $T: V \to V$ acts on the whole space, transforming every vector in a potentially complex way. But some of the most important operators in mathematics and physics have special "preferred directions" — vectors that$T$ does not rotate or deflect, but merely stretches or compresses. The study of these preferred directions is eigentheory, and it is one of the central themes of linear algebra. The question we begin with is: are there any directions that$T$ leaves invariant, merely scaling along them?

The intuition is geometric. If we think of $T$ as a transformation of space, an eigenvector is a vector$v$ such that$T(v)$ points in the same direction as$v$ (or its opposite). The transformation acts on the line through$v$ as a pure stretching, contracting, or reflection. Such vectors encode the "axes" of$T$, the directions along which$T$ behaves as simply as possible.

\begin{definition}
Let $T: V \to V$ be a linear operator on a vector space$V$ over a field$F$. A scalar$\lambda \in F$ is an **eigenvalue** of$T$ if there exists a nonzero vector$v \in V$ satisfying
$$Tv = \lambda v.$$
Such a vector $v$ is called an **eigenvector** of$T$ corresponding to the eigenvalue$\lambda$.
\end{definition}

The requirement that $v \neq 0$ is essential: the zero vector satisfies$T \cdot 0 = \lambda \cdot 0$ for every scalar$\lambda$, so it carries no information about$T$. Only nonzero vectors are eigenvectors.

The geometric content of the definition is clear: an eigenvector is a nonzero vector whose direction is preserved by $T$. The eigenvalue$\lambda$ is the factor by which$T$ scales that direction. If$\lambda = 1$, the vector is fixed outright. If$\lambda = -1$, it is reflected through the origin along that line. If$|\lambda| > 1$, it is stretched. If$0 < |\lambda| < 1$, it is contracted. If$\lambda = 0$, the vector is mapped to$0$, meaning$v \in \ker(T)$. These geometric interpretations make eigenvalues immediate to interpret in applied settings: they are the scaling factors of the natural axes.

A rotation of the plane by an angle $\theta$ other than$0$ or$\pi$ has no real eigenvectors, because no direction is left invariant — every nonzero vector is rotated to a genuinely different direction. However, complex eigenvalues$e^{\pm i\theta}$ still exist in$\mathbb{C}^2$, encoding the rotation's angle. A reflection across a line, by contrast, has eigenvectors along the mirror line ($\lambda = 1$) and perpendicular to it ($\lambda = -1$). A shear$T(x, y) = (x + y, y)$ has only the single eigenvalue$\lambda = 1$, with eigenvectors along the$x$-axis.

The eigenvalue equation $Tv = \lambda v$ can be rewritten as$(T - \lambda I)v = 0$. This says$v \in \ker(T - \lambda I)$. Finding eigenvectors for a given$\lambda$ is thus a question about the null space of the operator$T - \lambda I$: we need this null space to be nontrivial. Finding the eigenvalues themselves is a question about when$T - \lambda I$ fails to be injective — equivalently, when$\det(\lambda I - A) = 0$. This determinant condition is the bridge to the characteristic polynomial.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Let $A = \begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix}$. By inspection,$Ae_1 = 2e_1$ and$Ae_2 = 3e_2$. So$e_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue$2$, and$e_2 = \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue$3$. This is immediate for diagonal matrices: the standard basis vectors are always eigenvectors, and the eigenvalues are the diagonal entries.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Let $A = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ (the exchange matrix). Then$A\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}$ (eigenvalue$1$) and$A\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-1\\1\end{pmatrix} = -\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}$ (eigenvalue$-1$). Geometrically, the exchange matrix is a reflection across the line$y = x$: vectors along$y=x$ are fixed, vectors along$y=-x$ are reversed.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** A non-obvious eigenvector. Let $A = \begin{pmatrix}3&1\\2&4\end{pmatrix}$. Is$v = \begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}$ an eigenvector? Compute$Av = \begin{pmatrix}3+2\\2+8\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}5\\10\end{pmatrix} = 5\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}$. Yes:$v$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue$5$. Is$w = \begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}$ an eigenvector? Compute$Aw = \begin{pmatrix}3-1\\2-4\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2\\-2\end{pmatrix} = 2\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}$. Yes, eigenvalue$2$. These are the only eigenvalues (the characteristic polynomial is$(\lambda-5)(\lambda-2)$).
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** No real eigenvectors. The rotation matrix $R = \begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ satisfies$Rv = \lambda v$ only if$\lambda^2 + 1 = 0$ (from the characteristic polynomial), which has no real solutions. No nonzero real vector is simply scaled by a$90°$ rotation — any such vector is moved to an orthogonal direction. Over$\mathbb{C}$, the eigenvectors are$\begin{pmatrix}1\\-i\end{pmatrix}$ (eigenvalue$i$) and$\begin{pmatrix}1\\i\end{pmatrix}$ (eigenvalue$-i$).
\end{example}
