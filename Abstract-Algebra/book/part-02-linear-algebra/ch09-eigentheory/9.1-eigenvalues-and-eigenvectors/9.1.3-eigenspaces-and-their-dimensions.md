# 9.1.3 Eigenspaces and Their Dimensions

Once we have identified an eigenvalue $\lambda$ of$T$, we want to understand the full set of vectors that$T$ scales by$\lambda$. That set — together with the zero vector — forms a subspace: if$Tv = \lambda v$ and$Tw = \lambda w$, then$T(\alpha v + \beta w) = \alpha T v + \beta T w = \alpha \lambda v + \beta \lambda w = \lambda(\alpha v + \beta w)$ for all scalars$\alpha, \beta$. This subspace is the natural "stage" on which the eigenvalue$\lambda$ acts, and its dimension is the key invariant separating diagonalizable from non-diagonalizable operators.

\begin{definition}
Let $\lambda$ be an eigenvalue of$T: V \to V$. The **eigenspace** of$T$ corresponding to$\lambda$ is
$$E_\lambda = \ker(T - \lambda I) = \{v \in V : Tv = \lambda v\}.$$
The **geometric multiplicity** of $\lambda$ is$m_g(\lambda) = \dim E_\lambda$.
\end{definition}

The eigenspace $E_\lambda$ is a nonzero subspace of$V$ — nonzero because$\lambda$ being an eigenvalue guarantees that$T - \lambda I$ is not injective, so$\ker(T - \lambda I) \neq \{0\}$. Every nonzero element of$E_\lambda$ is an eigenvector, and the zero vector is included to make it a subspace.

The geometric multiplicity $m_g(\lambda) \geq 1$ always, by definition of eigenvalue. It measures how many independent directions$T$ scales by$\lambda$: geometrically,$E_\lambda$ is the$\lambda$-eigenspace, the subspace on which$T$ acts as$\lambda$ times the identity.

Each eigenspace $E_\lambda$ is **$T$-invariant**: if$v \in E_\lambda$, then$Tv = \lambda v \in E_\lambda$ (since$T(\lambda v) = \lambda T v = \lambda^2 v$, so$\lambda v$ is also in$E_\lambda$). More precisely, the restriction$T|_{E_\lambda}$ equals$\lambda \cdot \mathrm{id}_{E_\lambda}$.

A fundamental structural fact is that eigenspaces for distinct eigenvalues are **independent**: if $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_k$ are distinct eigenvalues and$v_i \in E_{\lambda_i}$ are nonzero vectors, then$v_1, \ldots, v_k$ are linearly independent. The proof is by induction on$k$: suppose$\sum_{i=1}^k c_i v_i = 0$. Apply$T - \lambda_1 I$ to both sides:$T(\sum c_i v_i) - \lambda_1 \sum c_i v_i = \sum c_i (\lambda_i - \lambda_1) v_i = 0$. This is a shorter relation with$c_1$ eliminated. By induction all$c_i = 0$ for$i \geq 2$, and then$c_1 = 0$ as well. This independence of eigenspaces is the cornerstone of diagonalization theory.

To compute $E_\lambda$, row-reduce the matrix$\lambda I - A$ and solve the homogeneous system$(\lambda I - A)v = 0$. The number of free variables in the solution is$\dim E_\lambda = m_g(\lambda) = n - \mathrm{rank}(\lambda I - A)$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Find the eigenspaces of $A = \begin{pmatrix}3&1\\2&4\end{pmatrix}$. From Section 9.1.2, the eigenvalues are$\lambda=2$ and$\lambda=5$. For$\lambda=2$:$(2I-A)=\begin{pmatrix}-1&-1\\-2&-2\end{pmatrix}\to\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$, giving$x_1+x_2=0$, so$E_2 = \mathrm{span}\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}\right\}$, dimension 1. For$\lambda=5$:$(5I-A)=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-2&1\end{pmatrix}\to\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$, giving$2x_1=x_2$, so$E_5=\mathrm{span}\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}\right\}$, dimension 1.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** A two-dimensional eigenspace. Let $A = \begin{pmatrix}3&0&0\\0&3&0\\0&0&5\end{pmatrix}$. The eigenvalues are$3$ (with algebraic multiplicity 2) and$5$ (with algebraic multiplicity 1). For$\lambda=3$:$(3I-A)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\0&0&0\\0&0&-2\end{pmatrix}\to\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\0&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}$, giving$x_3=0$,$x_1,x_2$ free. So$E_3 = \mathrm{span}\{e_1, e_2\}$, dimension 2. For$\lambda=5$:$(5I-A)=\begin{pmatrix}2&0&0\\0&2&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}$, giving$x_1=x_2=0$,$x_3$ free.$E_5 = \mathrm{span}\{e_3\}$, dimension 1.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Linear independence of eigenvectors from distinct eigenvalues. For the matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$, eigenvectors are$v_1=e_1$ (eigenvalue 1) and$v_2=e_2$ (eigenvalue 2). These are clearly independent. More generally, suppose$cv_1 + dv_2 = 0$, i.e.,$\begin{pmatrix}c\\d\end{pmatrix}=0$. Then$c=d=0$. This reflects the general theorem: eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are always independent.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** Computing eigenspaces for a $3\times 3$ matrix with a repeated eigenvalue. Let$A = \begin{pmatrix}2&1&0\\0&2&0\\0&0&2\end{pmatrix}$. The only eigenvalue is$\lambda = 2$ with$p_A(\lambda)=(\lambda-2)^3$. Compute$(2I-A)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\0&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}$. Row-reducing: one pivot in column 2, two free variables ($x_1$ and$x_3$). So$E_2 = \mathrm{span}\{e_1, e_3\}$, dimension 2. Here$m_g(2)=2 < m_a(2)=3$, so$A$ is not diagonalizable. The "missing" third eigenvector will be replaced by a generalized eigenvector in the Jordan theory of Chapter 10.
\end{example}
