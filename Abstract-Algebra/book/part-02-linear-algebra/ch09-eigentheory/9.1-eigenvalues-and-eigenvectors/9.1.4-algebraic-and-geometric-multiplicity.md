# 9.1.4 Algebraic and Geometric Multiplicity

An eigenvalue $\lambda$ can appear in two ways: as a repeated root of the characteristic polynomial, and as the source of a high-dimensional eigenspace. These two phenomena are measured by two different numbers, and the relationship between them controls whether the operator can be diagonalized. Understanding the distinction is the key to all of eigentheory beyond the basic definition.

\begin{definition}
Let $T : V \to V$ be a linear operator and$\lambda$ an eigenvalue of$T$. The **algebraic multiplicity**$m_a(\lambda)$ is the multiplicity of$\lambda$ as a root of the characteristic polynomial$p_T$. The **geometric multiplicity**$m_g(\lambda)$ is the dimension of the eigenspace$E_{\lambda} = \ker(T - \lambda I)$.
\end{definition}

If $p_T(\lambda) = (\lambda - \lambda_0)^2(\lambda - \mu)$ with$\lambda_0 \neq \mu$, then$m_a(\lambda_0) = 2$ and$m_a(\mu) = 1$. The algebraic multiplicity records how many times$\lambda$ is "counted" in the full degree-$n$ factorization of$p_T$. The geometric multiplicity$m_g(\lambda) = \dim E_{\lambda}$ measures the actual dimension of the eigenspace — the number of linearly independent eigenvectors for$\lambda$. These two multiplicities satisfy a fundamental inequality.

\begin{theorem}
For every eigenvalue $\lambda$ of$T$,
$$1 \leq m_g(\lambda) \leq m_a(\lambda).$$
\end{theorem}

The lower bound holds because $\lambda$ being an eigenvalue guarantees at least one eigenvector. The upper bound is a deeper result: the eigenspace cannot exceed the size of the "eigenvalue slot" that the characteristic polynomial allots to$\lambda$. The proof proceeds by choosing a basis adapted to$E_{\lambda}$, writing the matrix of$T$ in block form, and computing$p_T$ directly — the block structure forces the factor$(\lambda - x)^{m_g(\lambda)}$ into the characteristic polynomial, but the remaining block may contribute additional powers, so$m_g(\lambda) \leq m_a(\lambda)$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$. The characteristic polynomial is$p_A(\lambda) = (\lambda - 3)^2$, so$m_a(3) = 2$. The eigenspace is$E_3 = \ker(A - 3I) = \ker \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, which is spanned by$e_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}$. Thus$m_g(3) = 1 < 2 = m_a(3)$. The multiplicities disagree: this matrix is not diagonalizable.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Let $B = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = 3I_2$. The characteristic polynomial is again$p_B(\lambda) = (\lambda - 3)^2$, so$m_a(3) = 2$. But$E_3 = \ker(B - 3I) = \ker 0 = \mathbb{R}^2$, so$m_g(3) = 2 = m_a(3)$. The multiplicities agree, and$B$ is trivially diagonalizable (it is already diagonal).
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Let $C = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$. The characteristic polynomial is$p_C(\lambda) = (\lambda - 2)(\lambda - 5)^2$. For$\lambda = 2$:$E_2 = \ker(C - 2I)$ is spanned by$e_1$, so$m_g(2) = 1 = m_a(2)$. For$\lambda = 5$:$C - 5I = \begin{pmatrix} -3 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, so$E_5 = \ker(C - 5I)$ is spanned by$e_2$, giving$m_g(5) = 1 < 2 = m_a(5)$. The deficiency at$\lambda = 5$ makes$C$ non-diagonalizable.
\end{example}

The gap between the two multiplicities is precisely what can go wrong with diagonalization. When $m_g(\lambda) = m_a(\lambda)$ for every eigenvalue, the eigenspaces collectively span the whole space and$T$ is diagonalizable. When$m_g(\lambda) < m_a(\lambda)$ for some$\lambda$, the eigenspace for that value is too small — we have a repeated eigenvalue but not enough independent eigenvectors to match. This deficiency is not a failure of the eigenvalue itself but of the operator's structure around it.

\begin{remark}
The difference $m_a(\lambda) - m_g(\lambda) \geq 0$ is sometimes called the **defect** of the eigenvalue$\lambda$. An eigenvalue with defect zero is called **semisimple**; when every eigenvalue is semisimple,$T$ itself is called semisimple (the algebraic-geometry term for diagonalizable). Over an algebraically closed field, an operator is diagonalizable if and only if it is semisimple — a statement that generalizes to abstract group representations and Lie algebra modules, where it becomes the definition of complete reducibility.
\end{remark}

Resolving the deficiency leads to the theory of generalized eigenvectors and Jordan forms in Chapter 10. The key insight is that when $m_g(\lambda) < m_a(\lambda)$, there exist vectors$v$ satisfying$(T - \lambda I)^k v = 0$ for some$k > 1$ but$(T - \lambda I)^{k-1} v \neq 0$; these generalized eigenvectors fill the gap and assemble into Jordan chains. The sizes of those chains are controlled exactly by the multiplicities studied here.
