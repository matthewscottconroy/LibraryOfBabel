# 9.1 Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

The central question of this section is deceptively simple: for a linear operator $T : V \to V$, does there exist a nonzero vector $\mathbf{v}$ that $T$ merely scales? If $T(\mathbf{v}) = \lambda \mathbf{v}$ for some scalar $\lambda$, then the line through $\mathbf{v}$ is preserved by $T$ — it is an invariant direction. Such a vector is called an eigenvector of $T$, and the corresponding scalar $\lambda$ is its eigenvalue. The word "eigen" is German for "own" or "characteristic," reflecting the sense that these vectors belong to the operator in a special way: they reveal the intrinsic structure of $T$ that persists regardless of how coordinates are chosen.

To find the eigenvalues, we rewrite the condition as $(T - \lambda I)(\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{0}$ with $\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}$. This means $T - \lambda I$ must be singular — its null space must be nontrivial. In finite dimensions, singularity is equivalent to the determinant being zero, which gives the characteristic equation $\det(T - \lambda I) = 0$. Expanding this determinant produces the characteristic polynomial $p(\lambda) = \det(T - \lambda I)$, a monic polynomial of degree $n$ whose roots are exactly the eigenvalues of $T$. Over $\mathbb{C}$, the fundamental theorem of algebra guarantees that $p$ splits into linear factors, so every operator on a complex vector space has at least one eigenvalue. Over $\mathbb{R}$, this can fail: a rotation by $90°$ has no real eigenvalues because the characteristic polynomial $\lambda^2 + 1$ has no real roots.

Once the eigenvalues are known, the eigenspace for $\lambda$ is defined as $E_\lambda = \ker(T - \lambda I)$, the set of all vectors (including $\mathbf{0}$) satisfying $T(\mathbf{v}) = \lambda \mathbf{v}$. This is always a subspace of $V$. Its dimension is the geometric multiplicity of $\lambda$. The algebraic multiplicity, by contrast, is the multiplicity of $\lambda$ as a root of $p$. A fundamental inequality always holds: the geometric multiplicity is at most the algebraic multiplicity. When this inequality is strict for some eigenvalue — when the eigenspace is smaller than the characteristic polynomial predicts — the operator cannot be diagonalized, and we must look to the more general structure theory of Chapter 10 to understand what is happening. This gap between the two multiplicities is the first sign of the Jordan structure lurking beneath.
