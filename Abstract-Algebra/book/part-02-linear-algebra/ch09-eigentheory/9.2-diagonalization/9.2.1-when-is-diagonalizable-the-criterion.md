# 9.2.1 When Is $T$ Diagonalizable? The Criterion

The most computationally tractable situation in eigentheory is when the operator $T$ can be represented by a diagonal matrix in some basis. In a diagonal representation, every computation — powers, exponentials, inverses — reduces to scalar operations on the diagonal entries. The question, then, is precisely when such a basis exists, and the answer is a clean criterion in terms of the multiplicities studied in the previous section.

\begin{definition}
An operator $T : V \to V$ is called **diagonalizable** if there exists a basis of$V$ consisting entirely of eigenvectors of$T$. In matrix terms,$T$ is diagonalizable if and only if there exists an invertible matrix$P$ such that$P^{-1}AP = D$ is diagonal, with the eigenvalues of$T$ along the diagonal of$D$ and the corresponding eigenvectors as columns of$P$.
\end{definition}

The diagonal form is extraordinarily useful: once $A = PDP^{-1}$, we have$A^k = PD^kP^{-1}$, and$D^k$ is just the diagonal matrix of$k$-th powers of the eigenvalues. Polynomials, power series, and exponentials of$A$ reduce to the same operations on scalars.

\begin{theorem}[Diagonalizability Criterion]
Let $T : V \to V$ be a linear operator over a field$F$. The following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $T$ is diagonalizable (over$F$).
\item $V = \bigoplus_{\lambda} E_{\lambda}$, the direct sum of all eigenspaces.
\item $p_T$ splits into linear factors over$F$, and$m_g(\lambda) = m_a(\lambda)$ for every eigenvalue$\lambda$ of$T$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

The equivalence of (1) and (2) is essentially the definition: a basis of eigenvectors is the same as a basis assembled from bases of the individual eigenspaces, and these bases are independent across distinct eigenvalues (eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are always linearly independent). The equivalence of (2) and (3) follows because $\dim V = \sum_{\lambda} m_a(\lambda)$ always, so$V = \bigoplus_{\lambda} E_{\lambda}$ holds if and only if$\sum_{\lambda} \dim E_{\lambda} = \dim V$, i.e.,$\sum_{\lambda} m_g(\lambda) = \sum_{\lambda} m_a(\lambda)$, which forces$m_g(\lambda) = m_a(\lambda)$ at each eigenvalue.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** The matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ is already diagonal, with eigenvalues$1$ and$2$. Both have algebraic multiplicity$1$ and geometric multiplicity$1$: diagonalizable.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** The matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has characteristic polynomial$\lambda^2$. The sole eigenvalue$0$ has$m_a(0) = 2$ but$E_0 = \ker A = \mathrm{span}\{e_1\}$, so$m_g(0) = 1 < 2$. Not diagonalizable.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** The matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ has characteristic polynomial$(\lambda - 2)^2(\lambda - 3)$. For$\lambda = 2$:$E_2 = \ker(A - 2I)$ is spanned by$e_1$ and$e_2$, so$m_g(2) = 2 = m_a(2)$. For$\lambda = 3$:$m_g(3) = 1 = m_a(3)$. Both multiplicities agree, confirming diagonalizability. The matrix is diagonalized by$P = I_3$ (it is already diagonal).
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
The splitting condition — that $p_T$ factors into linear factors over$F$ — is automatic when$F$ is algebraically closed (e.g.,$F = \mathbb{C}$) but can fail over$\mathbb{R}$. A real rotation matrix$R_\theta = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$ for$\theta \notin \{0, \pi\}$ has characteristic polynomial$\lambda^2 - 2\cos\theta \cdot \lambda + 1$, which has no real roots. Over$\mathbb{R}$, this matrix is not diagonalizable; over$\mathbb{C}$, the eigenvalues$e^{\pm i\theta}$ exist and the matrix is diagonalizable.
\end{remark}

The key point is that failing at even one eigenvalue prevents diagonalization. If $T$ has eigenvalues$\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_k$ with$\sum_i m_a(\lambda_i) = n$ but$m_g(\lambda_j) < m_a(\lambda_j)$ for some$j$, then the combined eigenspaces span a proper subspace of$V$, leaving not enough eigenvectors for a full basis. Understanding when this failure occurs — and what canonical form replaces the diagonal one — is the subject of Chapter 10.
