# 9.2.2 The Diagonalization Algorithm

Given a concrete matrix $A$, determining whether it is diagonalizable and producing the diagonalization when it is follows a systematic procedure. The algorithm combines the root-finding of eigentheory with the linear algebra of null spaces, and it is worth seeing the steps clearly laid out before applying them.

**Step 1: Compute the characteristic polynomial and find eigenvalues.** Form $p_A(\lambda) = \det(\lambda I - A)$ and factor it over the base field. The roots are the eigenvalues; their multiplicities as roots give the algebraic multiplicities$m_a(\lambda)$. If$p_A$ does not factor completely into linear factors over the given field (for instance, if$A$ is a real matrix with complex eigenvalues), then$A$ is not diagonalizable over that field.

**Step 2: Find bases for all eigenspaces.** For each eigenvalue $\lambda$, find a basis for the eigenspace$E_{\lambda} = \ker(\lambda I - A)$ by row-reducing the matrix$\lambda I - A$ and solving the homogeneous system. The number of free variables equals$m_g(\lambda) = \dim E_{\lambda}$.

**Step 3: Apply the diagonalizability criterion.** If $m_g(\lambda) < m_a(\lambda)$ for any eigenvalue$\lambda$, the algorithm terminates:$A$ is not diagonalizable.

**Step 4: Assemble $P$ and$D$.** If the total count of eigenvectors equals$n = \dim V$, form the matrix$P$ whose columns are the eigenvectors — one basis for each eigenspace, in any order — and the diagonal matrix$D$ with the corresponding eigenvalues on the diagonal matching the column order of$P$. The result is the factorization$A = PDP^{-1}$.

To see why $A = PDP^{-1}$ holds: the$j$-th column equation of$AP = PD$ reads$Av_j = \lambda_j v_j$, which is precisely the eigenvector equation for the$j$-th column of$P$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Diagonalize $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$.
\par
*Step 1.* $p_A(\lambda) = (\lambda - 4)(\lambda - 3) - 2 = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = (\lambda - 2)(\lambda - 5)$. Eigenvalues:$\lambda_1 = 2$,$\lambda_2 = 5$.
\par
*Step 2.* For $\lambda = 2$:$2I - A = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & -1 \\ -2 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \to \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1/2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, giving$v_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\end{pmatrix}$.
For $\lambda = 5$:$5I - A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -2 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \to \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, giving$v_2 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}$.
\par
*Step 3.* Each eigenvalue has $m_g = m_a = 1$: diagonalizable.
\par
*Step 4.* $P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$,$D = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$. Check:$AP = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ -4 & 5 \end{pmatrix} = PD$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Attempt to diagonalize $A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
\par
*Step 1.* $p_A(\lambda) = (\lambda-3)^2(\lambda-2)$. Eigenvalues:$\lambda = 3$ with$m_a = 2$,$\lambda = 2$ with$m_a = 1$.
\par
*Step 2.* For $\lambda = 3$:$3I - A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, giving only$v = e_1$, so$m_g(3) = 1$.
\par
*Step 3.* $m_g(3) = 1 < 2 = m_a(3)$. Not diagonalizable.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Diagonalize $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
\par
$p_A(\lambda) = (1-\lambda)((\lambda)(\lambda-2)+1) = (1-\lambda)(\lambda^2 - 2\lambda + 1) = (1-\lambda)(\lambda-1)^2 = -(\lambda-1)^3$. So$\lambda = 1$ with$m_a = 3$. Checking$E_1 = \ker(A - I) = \ker\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\0&-1&-1\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix}$, whose row space has rank 1, giving$m_g(1) = 2 < 3$. Not diagonalizable.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
In practice, Step 1 (factoring the characteristic polynomial) is by far the most computationally intensive step for large matrices. For small matrices one factors directly; for large numerical matrices, eigenvalue-finding algorithms (QR iteration, power methods) are used instead, and the question of diagonalizability is replaced by questions about numerical conditioning.
\end{remark}
