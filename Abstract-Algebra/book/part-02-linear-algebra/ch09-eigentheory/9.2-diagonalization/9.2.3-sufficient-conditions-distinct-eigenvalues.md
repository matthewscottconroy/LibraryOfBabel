# 9.2.3 Sufficient Conditions: Distinct Eigenvalues

The multiplicity conditions in Section 9.2.1 are necessary and sufficient for diagonalizability, but checking them requires computing both algebraic and geometric multiplicities for every eigenvalue. In many practical situations, a simpler sufficient condition covers the case at hand and allows diagonalizability to be established with almost no work.

\begin{theorem}
If $T : V \to V$ has$n = \dim V$ distinct eigenvalues (that is,$p_T$ has$n$ distinct roots in$F$), then$T$ is automatically diagonalizable over$F$.
\end{theorem}

The proof rests on a fundamental independence result.

\begin{lemma}
Eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Suppose $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_k$ are distinct eigenvalues with eigenvectors$v_1, \ldots, v_k$. We proceed by induction on$k$. The base case$k = 1$ is clear since eigenvectors are nonzero. For the inductive step, assume$v_1, \ldots, v_{k-1}$ are independent and suppose$\sum_{i=1}^k c_i v_i = 0$. Applying$T$ gives$\sum_{i=1}^k c_i \lambda_i v_i = 0$, and subtracting$\lambda_k$ times the original relation gives$\sum_{i=1}^{k-1} c_i(\lambda_i - \lambda_k) v_i = 0$. By inductive hypothesis this forces$c_i(\lambda_i - \lambda_k) = 0$ for$i < k$; since$\lambda_i \neq \lambda_k$, we get$c_i = 0$ for$i < k$, and then$c_k = 0$ from the original relation.
\end{proof}

When all $n$ eigenvalues are distinct, this lemma produces$n$ linearly independent eigenvectors, which form a basis of the$n$-dimensional space$V$. Diagonalizability follows immediately.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 6 & -11 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$. The characteristic polynomial is$p_A(\lambda) = -(\lambda^3 - 6\lambda^2 + 11\lambda - 6) = -(\lambda-1)(\lambda-2)(\lambda-3)$. Since$A$ has three distinct eigenvalues ($1, 2, 3$) over$\mathbb{R}$, it is diagonalizable without further computation.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** A $5 \times 5$ matrix with characteristic polynomial$(\lambda-1)(\lambda-2)(\lambda-3)(\lambda-4)(\lambda-5)$ has five distinct eigenvalues and is automatically diagonalizable. No eigenspace dimension calculations are needed.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** The matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$ over$\mathbb{C}$ has characteristic polynomial$\lambda^2 - 2\cos\theta \cdot \lambda + 1$, with roots$e^{i\theta}$ and$e^{-i\theta}$. For$\theta \notin \{0, \pi\}$ these are distinct complex eigenvalues, so$A$ is diagonalizable over$\mathbb{C}$. Over$\mathbb{R}$, the polynomial has no real roots, so$A$ is not diagonalizable over$\mathbb{R}$.
\end{example}

Recall that the converse fails: a matrix may be diagonalizable even with repeated eigenvalues, provided the geometric multiplicities still match the algebraic ones. The identity matrix $I_n$ has the single eigenvalue$1$ with algebraic multiplicity$n$, but every vector is an eigenvector, so$m_g(1) = n = m_a(1)$ and it is trivially diagonalizable. The distinct-eigenvalue condition is sufficient but not necessary.

\begin{remark}
The proof of the lemma is sometimes called the "Vandermonde trick" because one can encode it as saying that a certain Vandermonde matrix is nonsingular. Specifically, if one arranges the coefficients of the linear dependence relation under repeated application of $T$, the resulting system has a Vandermonde matrix$V(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_k)$ as coefficient matrix, which is invertible when the$\lambda_i$ are distinct. This algebraic perspective connects the independence of eigenvectors directly to properties of the Vandermonde determinant$\prod_{i > j}(\lambda_i - \lambda_j) \neq 0$.
\end{remark}

In practice, the distinct-eigenvalue criterion is most useful for matrices arising in applications — for instance, in systems of differential equations, adjacency matrices of certain graphs, or matrices with entries that are generic (in a measure-theoretic sense, "most" real matrices have $n$ distinct eigenvalues). For matrices with structure that forces repeated eigenvalues — symmetric matrices, representation-theoretic matrices — one needs the full criterion with multiplicity checks.
