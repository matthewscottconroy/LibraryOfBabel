# 9.2 Diagonalization

The goal of diagonalization is to choose a basis of $V$ in which the matrix of $T$ becomes as transparent as possible — specifically, a basis of eigenvectors so that $T$ simply multiplies each basis vector by its eigenvalue. If such a basis exists, the matrix representing $T$ in that basis is diagonal, with eigenvalues running down the diagonal. The operator is then called diagonalizable. Working in this basis makes computations involving $T$ dramatically simpler: powers $T^k$ become diagonal matrices with entries $\lambda_i^k$, polynomial functions of $T$ become trivial to compute, and the long-run behavior of iterated application of $T$ is immediately visible.

The precise criterion for diagonalizability is the following: $T$ is diagonalizable if and only if the eigenspaces together span all of $V$, i.e., if the sum of the geometric multiplicities of all distinct eigenvalues equals $\dim V$. Equivalently, $T$ is diagonalizable if and only if for each eigenvalue $\lambda$, its geometric multiplicity (dimension of $E_\lambda$) equals its algebraic multiplicity (multiplicity as a root of the characteristic polynomial). The diagonalization algorithm proceeds in three steps: factor the characteristic polynomial to find all eigenvalues; for each eigenvalue, solve the system $(T - \lambda I)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ to find a basis for $E_\lambda$; check that the total number of basis vectors found equals $n$; and finally, form the matrix $P$ whose columns are the eigenvectors and verify that $P^{-1}AP = D$ is diagonal.

A key sufficient condition makes the criterion easy to apply in many cases: if $T$ has $n$ distinct eigenvalues in the base field, then $T$ is automatically diagonalizable. This follows from the fact that eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are always linearly independent, so $n$ distinct eigenvalues produce $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors, which must be a basis. The condition is sufficient but not necessary: a matrix can have repeated eigenvalues and still be diagonalizable (the identity matrix $I$ has only one eigenvalue but is already diagonal). Conversely, this section also develops concrete examples of non-diagonalizable operators — the prototypical case being the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} \lambda & 1 \\ 0 & \lambda \end{pmatrix}$, which has a repeated eigenvalue but only a one-dimensional eigenspace. These examples motivate everything that follows in Chapter 10.
