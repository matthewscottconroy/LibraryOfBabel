# 9.3.1 Definition and Existence

Among all polynomials $f$ with$f(T) = 0$, we ask: what is the smallest one? This question has a clean and important answer, and the polynomial it produces — the minimal polynomial — encodes the structural properties of$T$ in a more refined way than the characteristic polynomial. Where the characteristic polynomial sees all eigenvalue multiplicities, the minimal polynomial sees only the largest Jordan block at each eigenvalue.

\begin{definition}
Given a linear operator $T : V \to V$ over a field$F$, the **minimal polynomial**$m_T \in F[x]$ is the unique monic polynomial of least positive degree satisfying$m_T(T) = 0$, the zero operator.
\end{definition}

**Existence.** The operators $I, T, T^2, \ldots, T^{n^2}$ cannot all be linearly independent in$\mathrm{End}(V)$, which has dimension$n^2$ as a vector space. Hence some nontrivial polynomial relation$\sum_{k=0}^{N} c_k T^k = 0$ must hold for$N \leq n^2$. We may normalize to make it monic. Among all monic polynomials annihilating$T$, choose one of minimal degree — this is$m_T$. (In fact the Cayley–Hamilton theorem, proved in Section 9.3.2, provides a degree-$n$ annihilator immediately, so$\deg m_T \leq n$.)

**Uniqueness and the divisibility property.** The minimal polynomial is unique. Moreover, it satisfies a universal property:

\begin{theorem}
If $f \in F[x]$ satisfies$f(T) = 0$, then$m_T \mid f$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $f = q \cdot m_T + r$ with$\deg r < \deg m_T$. Then$r(T) = f(T) - q(T) m_T(T) = 0 - 0 = 0$, so$r$ annihilates$T$. If$r \neq 0$, then normalizing$r$ gives a monic annihilating polynomial of degree less than$\deg m_T$, contradicting minimality. Hence$r = 0$ and$m_T \mid f$.
\end{proof}

This means $m_T$ is the **generator** of the ideal$\{f \in F[x] : f(T) = 0\}$ in the principal ideal domain$F[x]$. The ideal$\mathrm{Ann}(T) = \{f : f(T) = 0\}$ is an ideal of$F[x]$, hence principal, and$m_T$ is its unique monic generator.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** For $T = \lambda_0 I$ (scalar multiple of the identity),$T - \lambda_0 I = 0$, so the polynomial$x - \lambda_0$ annihilates$T$. It has degree 1, so$m_T(x) = x - \lambda_0$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** For the nilpotent shift $N = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$, we have$N \neq 0$ but$N^2 = 0$. So$x$ does not annihilate$N$, but$x^2$ does. Hence$m_N(x) = x^2$. Note that$\deg m_N = 2 = \dim V$, which is the maximum possible.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Let $T$ have matrix$\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&2&0\\0&0&2\end{pmatrix}$. The polynomial$(x-1)(x-2)$ applied to$T$ gives$\mathrm{diag}(0, 0, 0) = 0$. Is there a smaller annihilator? The eigenvalues$1$ and$2$ must both be roots of$m_T$ (as we prove in Section 9.3.3), so$m_T$ must be divisible by$(x-1)(x-2)$. Since$(x-1)(x-2)$ already annihilates$T$ and has the minimum possible degree,$m_T(x) = (x-1)(x-2)$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** For $J = \begin{pmatrix}3&1&0\\0&3&1\\0&0&3\end{pmatrix}$, the characteristic polynomial is$(x-3)^3$. We check:$(J-3I)^2 = \begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\0&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix} \neq 0$, but$(J-3I)^3 = 0$. So$m_J(x) = (x-3)^3 = p_J(x)$: the minimal and characteristic polynomials coincide.
\end{example}

The minimal polynomial will reappear throughout this chapter and the next. Its roots are exactly the eigenvalues of $T$ (the same set of roots as the characteristic polynomial, though possibly with smaller multiplicities), and its factorization structure reveals whether$T$ is diagonalizable, as we see in Section 9.3.4. Over a general field, the minimal polynomial is the primary algebraic invariant distinguishing similar operators within a fixed characteristic polynomial class.
