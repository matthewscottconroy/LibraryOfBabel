# 9.3.2 The Cayley–Hamilton Theorem: $p_T(T) = 0$

One of the most striking results in linear algebra is that every square matrix satisfies its own characteristic polynomial. The statement is at once surprising — $p_T$ was defined using$\det(\lambda I - A)$, which seems to be a property of the scalar$\lambda$, not of the matrix$A$ — and enormously useful.

\begin{theorem}[Cayley–Hamilton]
Let $T : V \to V$ be a linear operator with characteristic polynomial$p_T$. Then$p_T(T) = 0$, the zero operator on$V$.
\end{theorem}

The naive "proof" $p_T(T) = \det(T - T) = \det(0) = 0$ is completely wrong: the expression$\det(\lambda I - A)$ is a polynomial in the scalar$\lambda$, not in the matrix$A$, and one cannot substitute a matrix for a scalar inside a determinant this way. The correct proof requires more care.

**Proof sketch via Jordan form.** Over an algebraically closed field, $T$ has a Jordan form$J = \mathrm{diag}(J_{k_1}(\lambda_1), \ldots, J_{k_r}(\lambda_r))$, and$p_T(x) = \prod_i (x - \lambda_i)^{k_i}$ (grouping equal eigenvalues). For each Jordan block$J_{k_i}(\lambda_i) = \lambda_i I + N$ where$N^{k_i} = 0$:
$$p_T(J_{k_i}(\lambda_i)) = \prod_j (J_{k_i}(\lambda_i) - \lambda_j I)^{k_j}.$$
The factor for $j = i$ contributes$(N)^{k_i} = 0$, making the whole product zero. Since$p_T(J) = \mathrm{diag}(p_T(J_{k_1}(\lambda_1)), \ldots) = 0$, and similarity preserves operator equations:$p_T(A) = P \cdot p_T(J) \cdot P^{-1} = 0$.

**Proof via the adjugate.** A field-independent proof uses the adjugate matrix. Let $B(\lambda) = \mathrm{adj}(\lambda I - A)$, the adjugate of$\lambda I - A$, whose entries are polynomials in$\lambda$ of degree at most$n-1$. Write$B(\lambda) = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \lambda^k B_k$ with$B_k \in M_n(F)$. The identity$(\lambda I - A) \cdot \mathrm{adj}(\lambda I - A) = \det(\lambda I - A) \cdot I = p_T(\lambda) I$ gives, upon expanding$p_T(\lambda) = \sum_{k=0}^n c_k \lambda^k$ and equating powers of$\lambda$, a sequence of matrix equations. Substituting$A$ for$\lambda$ and telescoping yields$p_T(A) = 0$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Let $A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4\end{pmatrix}$. Then$p_A(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - 5\lambda - 2$. By Cayley–Hamilton,$A^2 - 5A - 2I = 0$. Direct computation:$A^2 = \begin{pmatrix}7&10\\15&22\end{pmatrix}$, so$A^2 - 5A - 2I = \begin{pmatrix}7&10\\15&22\end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix}5&10\\15&20\end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$. Verified.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** For $N = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$,$p_N(\lambda) = \lambda^2$. Cayley–Hamilton says$N^2 = 0$, which is easily verified:$N^2 = \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** For $A = \begin{pmatrix}0&0&-6\\1&0&-11\\0&1&-6\end{pmatrix}$ (a companion matrix),$p_A(\lambda) = \lambda^3 + 6\lambda^2 + 11\lambda + 6$. Cayley–Hamilton gives$A^3 + 6A^2 + 11A + 6I = 0$, allowing any power of$A$ to be expressed in terms of$I$,$A$,$A^2$.
\end{example}

**Consequences.** First, $m_T \mid p_T$: the characteristic polynomial annihilates$T$, so by the universal divisibility property of$m_T$ (Section 9.3.1),$m_T$ divides$p_T$. This gives a practical upper bound$\deg m_T \leq n$.

Second, Cayley–Hamilton provides a way to express high powers of $T$ in terms of lower ones: since$T^n = -(c_{n-1} T^{n-1} + \cdots + c_0 I)$ from$p_T(T) = 0$, the operators$I, T, \ldots, T^{n-1}$ span the subalgebra$F[T] \subseteq \mathrm{End}(V)$.

\begin{corollary}
The algebra $F[T] = \{f(T) : f \in F[x]\}$ has dimension at most$n$ over$F$, and is isomorphic to$F[x]/(m_T)$.
\end{corollary}
