# 9.3.4 Diagonalizability via the Minimal Polynomial

We have seen two characterizations of diagonalizability: one in terms of a basis of eigenvectors, and one in terms of the agreement of algebraic and geometric multiplicities. The minimal polynomial offers a third characterization, arguably the most elegant, which makes the algebraic structure transparent and generalizes most naturally to other settings.

\begin{theorem}
Suppose $p_T$ splits into linear factors over$F$. Then$T$ is diagonalizable over$F$ if and only if$m_T$ has no repeated irreducible factors. Equivalently,$T$ is diagonalizable if and only if$m_T$ splits into distinct linear factors over$F$:
$$m_T(x) = (x - \lambda_1)(x - \lambda_2) \cdots (x - \lambda_k),\quad \lambda_i \text{ distinct}.$$
\end{theorem}

**Proof sketch.** By Section 9.3.3, the exponent of each factor $(x - \lambda)$ in$m_T$ equals the size of the largest Jordan block for$\lambda$. Diagonalizability requires every Jordan block to be$1 \times 1$, so the largest block at each eigenvalue has size 1. This is precisely the condition that$m_T$ is a product of distinct linear factors. Conversely, if$m_T$ has a repeated factor$(x - \lambda_0)^2$, then some Jordan block for$\lambda_0$ has size at least$2$, and$T$ is not diagonalizable.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** The nilpotent matrix $N = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}$ has$m_N(x) = x^2$, which has the repeated factor$x^2$. Conclusion: not diagonalizable (consistent with Example 1 of Section 9.2.4).
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** The identity matrix $I_n$ has$m_{I}(x) = x - 1$, a single distinct linear factor. Conclusion: diagonalizable (trivially, since$I$ is already diagonal).
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** A matrix with characteristic polynomial $p_T(x) = (x-1)(x-2)^2$ might be diagonalizable or not, depending on the Jordan structure. If$m_T(x) = (x-1)(x-2)$ (no repeated factors), then$T$ is diagonalizable: the Jordan block for$\lambda = 2$ must be$1 \times 1$, so$T$ has Jordan form$\mathrm{diag}(1, 2, 2)$. If$m_T(x) = (x-1)(x-2)^2$ (repeated factor), then$T$ is not diagonalizable: the Jordan block for$\lambda = 2$ has size$2$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** Let $A = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$ over$\mathbb{R}$. Then$p_A(x) = x^2 + 1$, which does not split over$\mathbb{R}$: no real eigenvalues. Over$\mathbb{C}$,$p_A(x) = (x-i)(x+i)$ splits into distinct linear factors, so$m_A(x) = (x-i)(x+i)$ (no repeated factors) and$A$ is diagonalizable over$\mathbb{C}$. Over$\mathbb{R}$, the theorem does not apply (splitting fails), and$A$ is not real-diagonalizable.
\end{example}

This criterion turns diagonalizability into a question purely about polynomial factorization, bypassing eigenspace dimension computations entirely. It also generalizes:

\begin{remark}
Over a field where polynomials need not split, $T$ is **semisimple** (the appropriate generalization of diagonalizable) if and only if$m_T$ is separable — meaning$m_T$ has no repeated irreducible factors in$F[x]$. This unifies the theory over all fields: over$\mathbb{F}_p$, semisimplicity means$m_T$ is not divisible by$p$-th powers of irreducibles (the "inseparable" case). In representation theory of groups and Lie algebras, semisimplicity of a representation corresponds exactly to the semisimplicity of the induced linear operators, and the minimal polynomial criterion carries over directly.
\end{remark}

The minimal polynomial therefore serves as a bridge: it encodes both the spectrum of $T$ (same roots as$p_T$) and the Jordan block structure (exponents equal to largest block sizes), and the single condition "no repeated factors" captures the complete story of diagonalizability in a remarkably compact form.
