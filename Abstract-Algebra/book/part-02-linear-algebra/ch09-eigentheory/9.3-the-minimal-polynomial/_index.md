# 9.3 The Minimal Polynomial

The characteristic polynomial $p(\lambda)$ of a linear operator $T$ encodes the eigenvalues and their algebraic multiplicities. But it does not tell the complete story about the structure of $T$: two operators can share the same characteristic polynomial yet behave very differently. A finer invariant is the minimal polynomial $m(x)$, defined as the monic polynomial of least degree such that $m(T) = 0$ (the zero operator). Such a polynomial exists because the $n^2 + 1$ matrices $I, T, T^2, \ldots, T^{n^2}$ cannot all be linearly independent in the space of $n \times n$ matrices, and it is unique because the set of all polynomials annihilating $T$ forms an ideal in $F[x]$, which is principal in a polynomial ring over a field.

The relationship between $m$ and $p$ is given by the Cayley–Hamilton theorem: $p(T) = 0$. This remarkable result — that every operator satisfies its own characteristic equation — implies immediately that $m(x)$ divides $p(x)$. Moreover, every eigenvalue of $T$ is a root of $m$, so $m$ and $p$ have the same roots; they differ only in the multiplicities. Specifically, the multiplicity of $\lambda$ as a root of $m$ equals the size of the largest Jordan block with eigenvalue $\lambda$, a fact that will be essential in Chapter 10. The proof of Cayley–Hamilton given here works directly with the matrix form, using the adjugate matrix and properties of polynomial multiplication.

The minimal polynomial provides an elegant and often practical criterion for diagonalizability: $T$ is diagonalizable if and only if $m(x)$ splits into distinct linear factors over the base field. In other words, diagonalizability is equivalent to $m(x) = (x - \lambda_1)(x - \lambda_2) \cdots (x - \lambda_k)$ with all $\lambda_i$ distinct. The intuition is clear: if $m$ has a repeated factor $(x - \lambda)^2$, then $(T - \lambda I)^2 = 0$ does not force $T - \lambda I = 0$, meaning there are generalized eigenvectors that are not honest eigenvectors, and the eigenspace for $\lambda$ is too small to span its part of $V$. This characterization is frequently faster to apply than computing all eigenspace dimensions, and it sets up the invariant-theoretic perspective — the study of the ideal $\{f \in F[x] : f(T) = 0\}$ — that will become central when canonical forms are developed via modules over principal ideal domains in Chapter 10.
