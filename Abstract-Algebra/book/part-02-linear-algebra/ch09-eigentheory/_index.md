# Chapter 9 — Eigentheory

Every linear operator $T : V \to V$ raises the same fundamental question: does there exist a basis of $V$ with respect to which $T$ looks as simple as possible? The answer depends entirely on whether $V$ can be decomposed into a family of one-dimensional subspaces that $T$ maps into themselves. These subspaces are precisely the eigenspaces, and the scalars by which $T$ stretches each one are the eigenvalues. This chapter develops the theory of eigenvalues and eigenvectors from scratch, building toward a complete criterion for when an operator admits such a decomposition — and introducing the tools needed to understand what happens when it does not.

The chapter begins with the definition: a nonzero vector $\mathbf{v}$ is an eigenvector of $T$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$ if $T(\mathbf{v}) = \lambda \mathbf{v}$. The condition that $\mathbf{v}$ is nonzero is essential, since the zero vector would satisfy the equation for any $\lambda$ and so carries no information. The eigenvalues of $T$ are exactly the roots of the characteristic polynomial $p(\lambda) = \det(T - \lambda I)$, a degree-$n$ polynomial whose factorization over the scalar field governs everything that follows. Once the eigenvalues are known, the eigenspace for each $\lambda$ — the null space of $T - \lambda I$ — can be computed directly. The dimension of this eigenspace is the geometric multiplicity of $\lambda$, which may differ from its algebraic multiplicity as a root of $p$.

The discrepancy between algebraic and geometric multiplicity is the central tension of eigentheory. When every eigenvalue has equal multiplicities, the eigenvectors span the entire space and $T$ is diagonalizable: there exists a basis $\mathcal{B}$ of eigenvectors, and in this basis $T$ is represented by a diagonal matrix with the eigenvalues along the diagonal. Section 9.2 establishes the diagonalization criterion precisely and provides the algorithm: factor the characteristic polynomial, compute each eigenspace, check that the dimensions sum to $n$, and assemble the eigenvectors as columns of a change-of-basis matrix $P$ so that $P^{-1}AP$ is diagonal. A key sufficient condition — that $T$ has $n$ distinct eigenvalues — guarantees diagonalizability immediately, since eigenvectors belonging to distinct eigenvalues are always linearly independent.

Section 9.3 introduces a more refined invariant: the minimal polynomial of $T$, the monic polynomial $m(x)$ of least degree satisfying $m(T) = 0$. The Cayley–Hamilton theorem, proved here, asserts that every operator satisfies its own characteristic polynomial, so $m$ always divides $p$. The minimal polynomial encodes the sizes of the Jordan blocks that will appear in Chapter 10: the multiplicity of $\lambda$ as a root of $m$ equals the size of the largest Jordan block for $\lambda$. More immediately useful is the characterization of diagonalizability entirely in terms of $m$: an operator is diagonalizable if and only if its minimal polynomial has no repeated roots. This criterion is often simpler to apply than checking multiplicities directly, and it connects eigentheory to the deeper structure theory that canonical forms will complete.

Together, these three sections establish the core toolkit: the characteristic polynomial identifies the eigenvalues, the eigenspaces measure their geometric structure, and the minimal polynomial captures the operator's annihilating ideal and predicts the Jordan structure lying beneath. Chapter 10 will take up the case where diagonalization fails, using precisely these ingredients to build the Jordan and rational canonical forms. Chapter 11 will add geometry to the picture, showing that when $T$ is self-adjoint the eigenspaces are not merely linearly independent but mutually orthogonal — a much stronger conclusion that yields the spectral theorem.
