# 10.1.1 Failure of Diagonalization and What Goes Wrong

Chapter 9 left us with a gap: many operators — indeed, most operators over $\mathbb{C}$ in some sense — cannot be diagonalized, and the obstruction is always the same. For a repeated eigenvalue$\lambda$, the eigenspace$E_{\lambda}$ may be too small to accommodate all the vectors that "want to" correspond to$\lambda$. We need a richer notion of associated vector to fill the space, and this section explains precisely where the failure originates.

Recall that $E_{\lambda} = \ker(T - \lambda I)$. When$m_g(\lambda) < m_a(\lambda)$, the first kernel is too small. The remedy is to look at larger kernels: define
$$K_j(\lambda) = \ker(T - \lambda I)^j \quad \text{for } j = 1, 2, \ldots$$
We always have the ascending chain $K_1(\lambda) \subseteq K_2(\lambda) \subseteq \cdots$ because if$(T - \lambda I)^j v = 0$, then$(T - \lambda I)^{j+1} v = (T - \lambda I)\bigl[(T - \lambda I)^j v\bigr] = 0$. This chain must stabilize once its dimension reaches$m_a(\lambda)$.

\begin{definition}
A **generalized eigenvector** for $\lambda$ of order$k$ is a vector$v \neq 0$ satisfying$(T - \lambda I)^k v = 0$ but$(T - \lambda I)^{k-1} v \neq 0$. Ordinary eigenvectors are generalized eigenvectors of order$1$.
\end{definition}

A generalized eigenvector of order $k > 1$ is not itself an eigenvector. Instead, it generates a **Jordan chain**: setting$v_k = v$,$v_{k-1} = (T - \lambda I)v$,$v_{k-2} = (T - \lambda I)^2 v$, and continuing down to$v_1 = (T - \lambda I)^{k-1} v$, we obtain a chain of vectors where$v_1$ is a genuine eigenvector ($Tv_1 = \lambda v_1$) and each$v_j$ satisfies$Tv_j = \lambda v_j + v_{j-1}$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** For $N = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$, the vector$e_2 = \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}$ satisfies$Ne_2 = e_1 \neq 0$ but$N^2 e_2 = 0$. So$e_2$ is a generalized eigenvector of order 2 for$\lambda = 0$. The Jordan chain is$v_2 = e_2$,$v_1 = N e_2 = e_1$. Note$Nv_1 = 0 = 0 \cdot v_1$ and$Nv_2 = e_1 = 0 \cdot v_2 + v_1$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** For $J_3(2) = \begin{pmatrix}2&1&0\\0&2&1\\0&0&2\end{pmatrix}$, let$v_3 = e_3$. Then$(J_3(2) - 2I)e_3 = e_2$ and$(J_3(2) - 2I)^2 e_3 = e_1$ and$(J_3(2) - 2I)^3 e_3 = 0$. The Jordan chain is$e_1, e_2, e_3$ with$(J_3(2)) e_j = 2e_j + e_{j-1}$ (where$e_0 = 0$).
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Consider $A = \begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$. The eigenvalue$\lambda = 1$ has$m_a = 3$. The kernel$K_1 = \ker(A - I) = \ker\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}$ is spanned by$e_1$ and$e_3$, so$m_g(1) = 2$. Now$K_2 = \ker(A-I)^2$:$(A-I)^2 = \begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\0&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix} = 0$, so$K_2 = \mathbb{F}^3$. The vector$e_2$ is a generalized eigenvector of order 2, with chain$e_1, e_2$.
\end{example}

The key insight is that chains of generalized eigenvectors furnish the additional vectors needed to fill the gap left by the failure of diagonalization. The chain structure is not arbitrary: each vector in the chain is exactly one application of $T - \lambda I$ away from the previous one, and the chain terminates at a genuine eigenvector. This rigid structure is what makes Jordan blocks the natural "building blocks" for non-diagonalizable operators.

\begin{remark}
The ascending chain $K_1(\lambda) \subseteq K_2(\lambda) \subseteq \cdots$ must strictly increase until it stabilizes, since each new vector in the chain adds a dimension. The chain stabilizes at$K_s(\lambda) = K_{s+1}(\lambda) = \cdots$ where$s$ is the size of the largest Jordan block for$\lambda$. This number$s$ equals the exponent of$(x - \lambda)$ in the minimal polynomial$m_T$, connecting the geometric picture of ascending kernels directly to the algebraic invariant$m_T$.
\end{remark}
