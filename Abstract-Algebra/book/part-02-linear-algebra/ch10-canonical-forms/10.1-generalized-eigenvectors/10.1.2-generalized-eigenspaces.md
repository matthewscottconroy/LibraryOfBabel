# 10.1.2 Generalized Eigenspaces: $J_{\lambda} = \ker(T - \lambda I)^n$

Having introduced generalized eigenvectors individually, we now collect them into a single subspace. For an operator $T$ on an$n$-dimensional space, the ascending chain$\ker(T - \lambda I) \subseteq \ker(T - \lambda I)^2 \subseteq \cdots$ stabilizes by dimension$n$ at the latest.

\begin{definition}
The **generalized eigenspace** of $\lambda$ is
$$J_{\lambda} = \ker(T - \lambda I)^n,$$
the eventual kernel of repeated applications of $T - \lambda I$. It consists of all vectors eventually annihilated by$T - \lambda I$.
\end{definition}

Notice that $J_{\lambda}$ contains$E_{\lambda}$ as a subspace, and the inclusion may be strict precisely when$\lambda$ is a defective eigenvalue (one where$m_g < m_a$). The generalized eigenspace collects all generalized eigenvectors of all orders, as well as the ordinary eigenvectors.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** For $N = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}$, the eigenspace$E_0 = \ker N = \mathrm{span}\{e_1\}$ has dimension 1, but$J_0 = \ker N^2 = \ker 0 = \mathbb{F}^2$ has dimension 2, filling the whole space. The generalized eigenspace is strictly larger.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** For a diagonalizable operator with distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$, each generalized eigenspace$J_{\lambda_i}$ equals the ordinary eigenspace$E_{\lambda_i}$, since the chain$\ker(T - \lambda_i I) \subseteq \ker(T - \lambda_i I)^2 \subseteq \cdots$ stabilizes immediately at$k = 1$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** For $A = \begin{pmatrix}3&1&0&0\\0&3&0&0\\0&0&3&1\\0&0&0&3\end{pmatrix} = \mathrm{diag}(J_2(3), J_2(3))$, we have$m_a(3) = 4$ and$m_g(3) = 2$. The generalized eigenspace$J_3 = \ker(A - 3I)^2 = \ker\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&0\\\ldots\end{pmatrix} = \mathbb{F}^4$: the entire space. In general for a single-eigenvalue operator,$J_{\lambda} = V$.
\end{example}

The generalized eigenspace $J_{\lambda}$ is$T$-invariant: if$(T - \lambda I)^n v = 0$, then$(T - \lambda I)^n(Tv) = T(T - \lambda I)^n v = 0$ (since$T$ commutes with$(T - \lambda I)^n$, which is a polynomial in$T$). Thus$Tv \in J_{\lambda}$ whenever$v \in J_{\lambda}$.

\begin{proposition}
The restriction of $T$ to$J_{\lambda}$ decomposes as$T|_{J_{\lambda}} = \lambda I + N_{\lambda}$, where$N_{\lambda} = (T - \lambda I)|_{J_{\lambda}}$ is nilpotent. The nilpotency index of$N_{\lambda}$ equals the size of the largest Jordan block for$\lambda$.
\end{proposition}

This is the key structural fact: each generalized eigenspace carries a nilpotent operator $N_{\lambda}$, and the entire complexity of the non-diagonalizable part of$T$ is captured by these nilpotent pieces on each$J_{\lambda}$.

The dimension of $J_{\lambda}$ equals the algebraic multiplicity$m_a(\lambda)$, not merely the geometric multiplicity:

\begin{theorem}
$\dim J_{\lambda} = m_a(\lambda)$.
\end{theorem}

This follows from the primary decomposition theorem (Section 10.1.3): the generalized eigenspaces partition $V$ into pieces of sizes exactly$m_a(\lambda_i)$.

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** For $A = \begin{pmatrix}2&1&0\\0&2&0\\0&0&5\end{pmatrix}$, the eigenvalues are$\lambda_1 = 2$ ($m_a = 2$) and$\lambda_2 = 5$ ($m_a = 1$). We have$J_2 = \ker(A - 2I)^3 = \ker\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\0&0&0\\0&0&27\end{pmatrix} = \mathrm{span}\{e_1, e_2\}$ and$J_5 = \ker(A - 5I)^3 = \mathrm{span}\{e_3\}$. Dimensions 2 and 1 match the algebraic multiplicities.
\end{example}
\par
The generalized eigenspaces are therefore the right objects to decompose $V$ when eigenspaces fall short. They fill the whole space (over an algebraically closed field), carry$T$-invariant structures, and reduce the study of$T$ to the study of nilpotent operators on each$J_{\lambda}$ — the content of the Jordan canonical form theorem.
