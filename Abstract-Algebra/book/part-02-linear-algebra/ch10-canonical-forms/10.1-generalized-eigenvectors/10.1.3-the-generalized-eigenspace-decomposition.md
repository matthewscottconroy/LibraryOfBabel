# 10.1.3 The Generalized Eigenspace Decomposition

We now arrive at the fundamental decomposition theorem that replaces the failed spectral decomposition $V = \bigoplus E_{\lambda}$ of diagonalization. Over an algebraically closed field, generalized eigenspaces always succeed where ordinary eigenspaces may fall short.

\begin{theorem}[Generalized Eigenspace Decomposition]
Let $F$ be algebraically closed and$T : V \to V$ a linear operator with distinct eigenvalues$\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_k$. Then
$$V = J_{\lambda_1} \oplus J_{\lambda_2} \oplus \cdots \oplus J_{\lambda_k},$$
where $J_{\lambda_i} = \ker(T - \lambda_i I)^n$ and$\dim J_{\lambda_i} = m_a(\lambda_i)$. Each summand is$T$-invariant.
\end{theorem}

The key tool is the Chinese Remainder Theorem for polynomials. The characteristic polynomial $p_T = \prod_i (x - \lambda_i)^{m_a(\lambda_i)}$ factors into pairwise coprime factors$q_i = (x - \lambda_i)^{m_a(\lambda_i)}$ over the algebraically closed field$F$. By Bézout's theorem in$F[x]$, there exist polynomials$r_i$ such that$\sum_i r_i(x) q_i(x) = 1$. Evaluating at$T$ (using Cayley–Hamilton to ensure$p_T(T) = 0$) gives projection operators$P_i = r_i(T) q_i(T) / (\text{appropriate normalization})$ onto the$J_{\lambda_i}$, satisfying$\sum_i P_i = I$ and$P_i P_j = 0$ for$i \neq j$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Let $T$ have characteristic polynomial$(\lambda - 2)^2(\lambda - 5)$ over$\mathbb{C}$. The generalized eigenspaces are$J_2$ (dimension 2) and$J_5$ (dimension 1), and$V = J_2 \oplus J_5$. On$J_2$,$T$ acts as$2I + N$ where$N^2 = 0$; on$J_5$,$T$ acts as scalar multiplication by 5.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** For $A = \begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&1\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$ over$\mathbb{C}$, the only eigenvalue is$\lambda = 1$ with$m_a = 3$. The single generalized eigenspace$J_1 = \ker(A-I)^3$:$(A-I)^2 = 0$, so$J_1 = \mathbb{C}^3$. There is only one summand; the decomposition is trivial.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** For $A = \begin{pmatrix}3&0\\0&5\end{pmatrix}$, eigenvalues$3$ and$5$ both have$m_a = 1$. The generalized eigenspaces$J_3 = \mathrm{span}\{e_1\}$ and$J_5 = \mathrm{span}\{e_2\}$ equal the ordinary eigenspaces. The decomposition$V = J_3 \oplus J_5 = E_3 \oplus E_5$ is the usual eigenspace decomposition for a diagonal matrix.
\end{example}

The algebraic foundation of this theorem is the theory of modules over a principal ideal domain, applied to $V$ as an$F[x]$-module via the action$f(x) \cdot v = f(T)v$. The decomposition corresponds to the **primary decomposition** of this module: since$p_T = \prod_i (x - \lambda_i)^{m_a(\lambda_i)}$ factors into pairwise coprime prime powers over$F$, the Chinese Remainder Theorem for modules splits$V$ into the corresponding primary components$J_{\lambda_i}$. We will revisit this perspective fully in Part V when we study modules over PIDs.

\begin{remark}
Each summand $J_{\lambda_i}$ is$T$-invariant, and the restriction of$T$ to$J_{\lambda_i}$ has the form$\lambda_i I + N_i$ where$N_i$ is nilpotent. The problem of understanding$T$ thus reduces to understanding nilpotent operators on each generalized eigenspace. This reduction is the conceptual backbone of the Jordan normal form: we analyze the nilpotent part$N_i$ on each$J_{\lambda_i}$ independently, and the Jordan blocks emerge from the structure of nilpotent operators on these spaces.
\end{remark}

Over a field that is not algebraically closed, the characteristic polynomial may not split into linear factors, and generalized eigenspaces for individual eigenvalues may not exist over $F$. In that setting, the primary decomposition runs over irreducible factors of$p_T$ rather than linear factors, leading to the rational canonical form of Section 10.3. The CRT machinery is identical; only the building blocks change from Jordan blocks to companion matrices.
