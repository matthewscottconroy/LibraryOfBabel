# 10.1 Generalized Eigenvectors

When an operator $T$ fails to be diagonalizable, the honest eigenspaces $E_\lambda = \ker(T - \lambda I)$ are not large enough to span $V$. The natural response is to enlarge them: instead of asking which vectors $\mathbf{v}$ satisfy $(T - \lambda I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$, we ask which vectors satisfy $(T - \lambda I)^k \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ for some positive integer $k$. A vector satisfying this condition for any $k \geq 1$ is called a generalized eigenvector of $T$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$. The collection of all such vectors forms the generalized eigenspace $K_\lambda = \ker(T - \lambda I)^n$, where $n = \dim V$; taking the $n$-th power is sufficient because the chain of kernels $\ker(T - \lambda I) \subseteq \ker(T - \lambda I)^2 \subseteq \cdots$ must stabilize by the time it has taken $n$ steps.

The central theorem of this section is the generalized eigenspace decomposition: if the characteristic polynomial of $T$ splits completely over the base field (which is automatic over $\mathbb{C}$), then $V = K_{\lambda_1} \oplus K_{\lambda_2} \oplus \cdots \oplus K_{\lambda_r}$ where $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_r$ are the distinct eigenvalues. This is a direct sum — the generalized eigenspaces are pairwise disjoint except for $\mathbf{0}$ — and the dimension of $K_\lambda$ is exactly the algebraic multiplicity of $\lambda$. The proof uses the fact that the polynomials $(x - \lambda_i)^{m_i}$ are pairwise coprime, where $m_i$ is the algebraic multiplicity of $\lambda_i$, so the Chinese Remainder Theorem (in its polynomial form) applies to give the decomposition.

What makes this decomposition so powerful is that each generalized eigenspace $K_\lambda$ is $T$-invariant, and the restriction $T|_{K_\lambda}$ has the form $\lambda I + N$ where $N = (T - \lambda I)|_{K_\lambda}$ is nilpotent. The problem of finding a canonical form for $T$ therefore reduces to finding a canonical form for nilpotent operators, one on each generalized eigenspace. This is the bridge to Jordan normal form: understanding what a nilpotent operator looks like in a well-chosen basis is exactly the problem Section 10.2 takes up. The generalized eigenvectors that go beyond the honest eigenspace capture the "mixing" behavior of $T$ near each eigenvalue, and making this mixing explicit is the purpose of the Jordan block structure.
