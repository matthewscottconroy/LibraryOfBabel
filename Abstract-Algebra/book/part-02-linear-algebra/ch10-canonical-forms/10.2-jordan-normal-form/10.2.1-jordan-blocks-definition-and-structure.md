# 10.2.1 Jordan Blocks: Definition and Structure

The generalized eigenspace decomposition reduces the study of any operator over an algebraically closed field to the study of operators of the form $\lambda I + N$ on a space where$N$ is nilpotent. To proceed, we need to understand nilpotent operators. The building blocks of nilpotent structure are the Jordan blocks.

\begin{definition}
The **Jordan block** $J_k(\lambda)$ is the$k \times k$ matrix with$\lambda$ on the main diagonal and$1$'s on the superdiagonal:
$$J_k(\lambda) = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \lambda & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & & & \ddots & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & \lambda \end{pmatrix}.$$
We write $J_k(\lambda) = \lambda I_k + N_k$, where$N_k$ is the$k \times k$ nilpotent shift matrix with$1$'s on the superdiagonal and$0$'s elsewhere.
\end{definition}

The structure of a Jordan block is worth internalizing. It has exactly one eigenvalue, $\lambda$, with algebraic multiplicity$k$ but geometric multiplicity$1$: the eigenspace is one-dimensional, spanned by the first standard basis vector$e_1$. The operator$J_k(\lambda) - \lambda I = N_k$ shifts the basis vectors:$N_k e_j = e_{j-1}$ for$j \geq 2$ and$N_k e_1 = 0$. Thus$N_k^k = 0$ but$N_k^{k-1} \neq 0$, confirming that$N_k$ has nilpotency index exactly$k$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** $J_1(\lambda) = (\lambda)$: a$1 \times 1$ matrix. A Jordan block of size 1 is simply a scalar, and the corresponding operator is diagonalizable.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** $J_2(0) = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$: the basic$2 \times 2$ nilpotent. Here$N_2 = J_2(0)$,$N_2^2 = 0$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** $J_3(5) = \begin{pmatrix}5&1&0\\0&5&1\\0&0&5\end{pmatrix}$. The eigenspace is$\ker(J_3(5) - 5I) = \ker N_3 = \mathrm{span}\{e_1\}$: one-dimensional. The chain$e_1, e_2, e_3$ satisfies$J_3(5) e_1 = 5e_1$,$J_3(5) e_2 = 5e_2 + e_1$,$J_3(5) e_3 = 5e_3 + e_2$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** The characteristic polynomial of $J_k(\lambda)$ is$(x - \lambda)^k$ and the minimal polynomial is also$(x - \lambda)^k$. To verify:$J_k(\lambda) - \lambda I = N_k$ has$N_k^{k-1} \neq 0$, so$(x - \lambda)^{k-1}$ does not annihilate$J_k(\lambda)$, confirming that$m_{J_k(\lambda)}(x) = (x-\lambda)^k$.
\end{example}

The reason Jordan blocks are the right building blocks is that on a **cyclic subspace** — one spanned by the chain $e_k, N_k e_k = e_{k-1}, \ldots, N_k^{k-1} e_k = e_1$ — the nilpotent shift takes the most efficient possible form. Any nilpotent operator on a cyclic space of dimension$k$ is represented by exactly this matrix in the right basis. 

\begin{proposition}
Let $N$ be a nilpotent operator on an$F$-vector space$W$ of dimension$k$ such that$N^{k-1} \neq 0$. Then there exists a basis of$W$ with respect to which$N$ is represented by the matrix$N_k$.
\end{proposition}

The proof is constructive: choose any $v$ with$N^{k-1}v \neq 0$, then$\{v, Nv, \ldots, N^{k-1}v\}$ is linearly independent (a consequence of$N^k = 0$ and the minimality of$k$), and in this basis$N$ acts as the shift$N_k$.

The full Jordan form theorem assembles these blocks into a direct sum, and we turn to that theorem in the next section. The upshot of this section is that Jordan blocks encode the two kinds of structure in a non-diagonalizable operator: the scalar $\lambda$ (the eigenvalue) and the nilpotent shift$N_k$ (the deficiency), combined by the "almost diagonal" form$\lambda I + N_k$.
