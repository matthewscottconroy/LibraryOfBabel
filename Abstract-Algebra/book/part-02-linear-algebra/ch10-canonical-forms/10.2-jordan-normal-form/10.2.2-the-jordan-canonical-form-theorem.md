# 10.2.2 The Jordan Canonical Form Theorem

With Jordan blocks defined and the generalized eigenspace decomposition in hand, we can now state the central theorem of the chapter. The Jordan canonical form theorem asserts that every linear operator over an algebraically closed field has a canonical matrix representation that is as close to diagonal as possible.

\begin{theorem}[Jordan Canonical Form]
Let $F$ be algebraically closed and$T : V \to V$ a linear operator. Then there exists a basis of$V$ with respect to which$T$ is represented by a block-diagonal matrix
$$J = \begin{pmatrix} J_{k_1}(\lambda_1) & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & J_{k_r}(\lambda_r) \end{pmatrix},$$
where each $J_{k_i}(\lambda_i)$ is a Jordan block. This matrix$J$ is unique up to permutation of the blocks, and is called the **Jordan canonical form** (or Jordan normal form) of$T$.
\end{theorem}

The proof proceeds in two stages. First, the generalized eigenspace decomposition (Section 10.1.3) splits $V = \bigoplus_{\lambda} J_{\lambda}$. Then on each$J_{\lambda}$, the operator$T - \lambda I$ is nilpotent, and the theory of nilpotent operators on a finite-dimensional space shows that any nilpotent operator is a direct sum of shift matrices$N_k$. The size sequence of these shift matrices is uniquely determined by the sequence of dimensions$\dim \ker N^j$ (the dimension sequence method of Section 10.2.4), establishing both existence and uniqueness.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Let $A = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$. The Jordan form is$J = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix} = J_2(2)$: a single$2 \times 2$ block. The Jordan basis consists of the Jordan chain$\{e_1, e_2\}$ where$e_1$ is the eigenvector and$e_2$ satisfies$(A - 2I)e_2 = e_1$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Let $A = \begin{pmatrix}3&0&0\\0&3&1\\0&0&3\end{pmatrix}$. Eigenvalue$\lambda = 3$ with$m_a = 3$. We have$m_g(3) = \dim \ker(A - 3I) = 2$ (spanned by$e_1$ and$e_2$). The Jordan form consists of 2 blocks summing to size 3: one$2 \times 2$ block and one$1 \times 1$ block:$J = \mathrm{diag}(J_1(3), J_2(3))$ (or equivalently$\mathrm{diag}(J_2(3), J_1(3))$).
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** A $4 \times 4$ matrix with characteristic polynomial$(\lambda - 1)^2(\lambda - 3)^2$ may have Jordan forms:$\mathrm{diag}(J_1(1), J_1(1), J_1(3), J_1(3))$ (diagonalizable),$\mathrm{diag}(J_2(1), J_1(3), J_1(3))$,$\mathrm{diag}(J_1(1), J_1(1), J_2(3))$, or$\mathrm{diag}(J_2(1), J_2(3))$. The actual form depends on the geometric multiplicities and minimal polynomial.
\end{example}

Notice that the Jordan form is diagonal if and only if all blocks have size $1 \times 1$, which is exactly the diagonalizability condition. In this sense, the Jordan form is the complete generalization of diagonalization: it tells us not just whether$T$ is diagonal but how far from diagonal it is, measured by the sizes of the off-diagonal shift blocks.

The basis vectors corresponding to a single Jordan block $J_k(\lambda)$ form a **Jordan chain**: a sequence$v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_k$ with$Tv_1 = \lambda v_1$ (an eigenvector),$Tv_2 = \lambda v_2 + v_1$, and in general$Tv_j = \lambda v_j + v_{j-1}$ for$j \geq 2$. Assembling all Jordan chains for all eigenvalues yields the Jordan basis for$V$.

\begin{remark}
Uniqueness of the Jordan form (up to block permutation) is the deepest part of the theorem. It follows from the fact that the dimension sequence $d_k(\lambda) = \dim \ker(T - \lambda I)^k$ is an invariant of$T$ independent of any choices, and this sequence determines the block sizes uniquely (Section 10.2.4). Two operators are similar over an algebraically closed field if and only if they have the same Jordan form, making the Jordan form the complete similarity invariant over such fields.
\end{remark}
