# 10.2.3 Reading the Jordan Form: Block Sizes and Multiplicities

Once we know that every operator has a Jordan canonical form, the next task is to read off the invariants of $T$ from its Jordan form — and conversely, to determine the Jordan form from information about$T$ without explicitly constructing a Jordan basis. This interplay between the Jordan form and the spectrum of$T$ is governed by a precise dictionary.

For a fixed eigenvalue $\lambda$, the Jordan blocks for$\lambda$ in the Jordan form encode three pieces of information simultaneously.

\begin{proposition}[The Jordan Dictionary]
Let $\lambda$ be an eigenvalue of$T$. In the Jordan form of$T$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The **number of Jordan blocks** with eigenvalue $\lambda$ equals$m_g(\lambda) = \dim E_{\lambda} = \dim \ker(T - \lambda I)$.
\item The **sum of the block sizes** for $\lambda$ equals$m_a(\lambda) = \dim J_{\lambda}$.
\item The **size of the largest Jordan block** for $\lambda$ equals the exponent of$(x - \lambda)$ in the minimal polynomial$m_T$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

**Why (1) holds:** Each Jordan block $J_k(\lambda)$ contributes exactly one eigenvector (from its first column, since$N_k e_1 = 0$), and eigenvectors from different blocks for the same eigenvalue are independent. So the total number of independent eigenvectors for$\lambda$ equals the block count, which equals$m_g(\lambda)$.

**Why (2) holds:** The generalized eigenspace $J_{\lambda}$ is the direct sum of the spaces spanned by the Jordan chains of each block, so$\dim J_{\lambda} = \sum (\text{block sizes}) = m_a(\lambda)$.

**Why (3) holds:** The minimal polynomial's exponent at $\lambda$ equals the nilpotency index of$(T - \lambda I)|_{J_{\lambda}}$, which is the size of the largest shift block$N_k$ in the direct sum decomposition of the nilpotent operator$(T - \lambda I)|_{J_{\lambda}}$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Suppose $T$ is$5 \times 5$ with a single eigenvalue$\lambda = 3$, algebraic multiplicity 5, geometric multiplicity 2, and minimal polynomial$m_T(x) = (x - 3)^3$. By the dictionary: 2 blocks (from$m_g = 2$), summing to 5 (from$m_a = 5$), with maximum size 3 (from exponent in$m_T$). The only partition of 5 into 2 parts with maximum part 3 is$3 + 2$. Jordan form:$\mathrm{diag}(J_3(3), J_2(3))$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Suppose $T$ is$6 \times 6$ with eigenvalue$\lambda = 0$ of algebraic multiplicity 6, geometric multiplicity 3, and$m_T(x) = x^2$. By the dictionary: 3 blocks, summing to 6, with maximum size 2. The only partition of 6 into 3 parts with maximum part 2 is$2 + 2 + 2$. Jordan form:$\mathrm{diag}(J_2(0), J_2(0), J_2(0))$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Suppose $T$ is$7 \times 7$ with eigenvalue$\lambda = 1$ of algebraic multiplicity 7, geometric multiplicity 3, and$m_T(x) = (x-1)^4$. Blocks: 3, summing to 7, maximum size 4. Partitions of 7 into 3 parts with max 4:$4 + 2 + 1$ or$4 + 1 + 2$ (same), or$3 + 2 + 2$... but wait, maximum must be exactly 4. So:$4 + 2 + 1$ gives 3 parts with max 4, and$4 + 1 + 2$ is the same partition. We also check$4 + 3 + 0$ is invalid (blocks must have positive size). So Jordan form:$\mathrm{diag}(J_4(1), J_2(1), J_1(1))$.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
When the Jordan form is not uniquely determined by $m_a$,$m_g$, and$m_T$ alone — for instance when there are multiple valid partitions — one must compute additional dimension data$\dim \ker(T - \lambda I)^k$ for$k = 2, 3, \ldots$ to distinguish cases. The full dimension sequence method (Section 10.2.4) handles this systematically by encoding all the partition data in the sequence$d_k = \dim \ker(T - \lambda I)^k$.
\end{remark}
\par
This dictionary turns Jordan form identification into an elementary arithmetic problem once the multiplicities and minimal polynomial degree are known, and it is the foundation for the computational method of the next section.
