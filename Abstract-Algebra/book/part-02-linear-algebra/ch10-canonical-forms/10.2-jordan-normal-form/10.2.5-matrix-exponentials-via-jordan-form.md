# 10.2.5 Matrix Exponentials via Jordan Form

One of the most compelling applications of the Jordan canonical form is the computation of the matrix exponential $e^{tA}$, which arises in the solution of linear systems of ordinary differential equations$x' = Ax$. The Jordan form makes what would otherwise be an unwieldy computation completely explicit.

Recall that the matrix exponential is defined by the power series $e^{tA} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(tA)^k}{k!}$. If$A = PJP^{-1}$ is the Jordan decomposition, then$e^{tA} = P e^{tJ} P^{-1}$, so the computation reduces to$e^{tJ}$. Since$J$ is block-diagonal,$e^{tJ}$ is block-diagonal with each block exponential computed independently.

For a single Jordan block $J_k(\lambda) = \lambda I + N_k$, the operators$\lambda I$ and$N_k$ commute, so$e^{t J_k(\lambda)} = e^{t\lambda I} e^{t N_k} = e^{\lambda t} e^{t N_k}$. The nilpotent exponential terminates after$k$ terms because$N_k^k = 0$:
$$e^{t N_k} = I + tN_k + \frac{t^2}{2!}N_k^2 + \cdots + \frac{t^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}N_k^{k-1}.$$
In matrix form, the $(i,j)$ entry of$e^{t J_k(\lambda)}$ is$e^{\lambda t} \frac{t^{j-i}}{(j-i)!}$ for$j \geq i$ and$0$ otherwise.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** For $J_1(\lambda) = (\lambda)$:$e^{t J_1(\lambda)} = (e^{\lambda t})$. A$1 \times 1$ block gives the expected scalar exponential.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** For $J_2(\lambda) = \begin{pmatrix}\lambda&1\\0&\lambda\end{pmatrix}$:
$$e^{t J_2(\lambda)} = e^{\lambda t} \begin{pmatrix}1 & t \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}.$$
The solution to $x' = J_2(\lambda) x$ with initial condition$x(0) = \begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}$ is$x(t) = e^{\lambda t}\begin{pmatrix}a + bt \\ b\end{pmatrix}$: a polynomial-times-exponential of the form typical in ODE systems with repeated eigenvalues.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** For $J_3(\lambda)$:
$$e^{t J_3(\lambda)} = e^{\lambda t} \begin{pmatrix}1 & t & t^2/2 \\ 0 & 1 & t \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}.$$
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** Solve $x' = Ax$ where$A = \begin{pmatrix}3&1\\0&3\end{pmatrix} = J_2(3)$. Then$e^{tA} = e^{3t}\begin{pmatrix}1&t\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$. The general solution is
$$x(t) = e^{3t}\begin{pmatrix}c_1 + c_2 t \\ c_2\end{pmatrix},$$
which is the standard "Jordan chain" form: one pure exponential solution $e^{3t}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}$ and one secular (polynomial-exponential) solution$e^{3t}\begin{pmatrix}t\\1\end{pmatrix}$.
\end{example}

The general result is:

\begin{theorem}
Solutions to $x' = Ax$ are linear combinations of functions of the form$t^m e^{\lambda t} v$ where$\lambda$ ranges over the eigenvalues of$A$,$v$ is a vector, and$m$ ranges from$0$ to$(\text{size of Jordan block for } \lambda) - 1$.
\end{theorem}

A single Jordan block of size $k$ for eigenvalue$\lambda$ contributes the$k$ linearly independent solutions$e^{\lambda t} v_1$,$e^{\lambda t}(tv_1 + v_2)$, and so on along the Jordan chain$v_1, \ldots, v_k$. This is precisely the form that every ODE textbook predicts for repeated-eigenvalue systems, but the Jordan form makes the connection rigorous and constructive.

\begin{remark}
The matrix exponential also illuminates the structure of Lie groups. If $A$ is the matrix of a Lie algebra element (infinitesimal generator),$e^{tA}$ is the corresponding one-parameter subgroup in the Lie group. The Jordan decomposition$A = S + N$ (semisimple plus nilpotent, where$S$ is diagonalizable and$N$ is nilpotent) gives$e^{tA} = e^{tS} e^{tN}$, and$e^{tN}$ is a polynomial (not just a formal power series) — one of the key reasons nilpotent Lie groups have tractable structure.
\end{remark}
