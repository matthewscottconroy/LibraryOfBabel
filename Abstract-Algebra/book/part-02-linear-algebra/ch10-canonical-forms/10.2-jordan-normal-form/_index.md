# 10.2 Jordan Normal Form

The Jordan normal form theorem is one of the crown jewels of linear algebra. It asserts that every square matrix over an algebraically closed field — in particular, every complex matrix — is similar to a block-diagonal matrix $J$ in which each block has a single eigenvalue $\lambda$ on the diagonal and 1s on the first superdiagonal. Such a block is called a Jordan block of size $k$ for eigenvalue $\lambda$:

$$J_k(\lambda) = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda & 1 & & \\ & \lambda & 1 & \\ & & \ddots & 1 \\ & & & \lambda \end{pmatrix}.$$

The diagonal matrix of the diagonalizable case is the special case where all Jordan blocks have size 1. The existence of the Jordan form follows from the generalized eigenspace decomposition of Section 10.1 together with a classification of nilpotent operators: every nilpotent operator on a finite-dimensional vector space is similar to a direct sum of nilpotent Jordan blocks $J_k(0)$, and the block sizes are uniquely determined by the rank-nullity data of successive powers.

To read off the Jordan form of a matrix $A$ without explicitly constructing a basis of generalized eigenvectors, one uses the dimension sequence: for each eigenvalue $\lambda$, compute $d_j = \dim \ker(A - \lambda I)^j$ for $j = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$ The differences $d_j - d_{j-1}$ give the number of Jordan blocks of size at least $j$, so the block sizes can be recovered from the increments of this sequence. The Jordan form is then unique up to permuting the blocks — this is the uniqueness clause, which is as important as the existence. Two matrices are similar over $\mathbb{C}$ if and only if they have the same Jordan form.

One of the most compelling applications is the computation of the matrix exponential. For a Jordan block $J_k(\lambda)$, one has $e^{tJ} = e^{\lambda t} e^{tN}$ where $N$ is nilpotent, and since $N^k = 0$, the exponential $e^{tN} = I + tN + \frac{t^2}{2!}N^2 + \cdots + \frac{t^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}N^{k-1}$ is a finite sum. The result is an upper-triangular matrix with polynomial-times-exponential entries, which is exactly the form of the general solution to the system of ODEs $\mathbf{y}' = A\mathbf{y}$. The Jordan form makes it explicit that repeated eigenvalues with non-trivial Jordan blocks generate solutions involving $te^{\lambda t}$, $t^2 e^{\lambda t}$, and so on — the resonance phenomenon familiar from the theory of second-order ODEs.
