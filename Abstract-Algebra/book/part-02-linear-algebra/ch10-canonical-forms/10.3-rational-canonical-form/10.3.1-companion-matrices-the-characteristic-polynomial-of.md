# 10.3.1 Companion Matrices; the Characteristic Polynomial of $C(f)$

The Jordan canonical form is defined over an algebraically closed field, but much of linear algebra takes place over $\mathbb{R}$,$\mathbb{Q}$, or finite fields, where polynomials need not factor completely into linear factors. To build a canonical form that works over any field, we need a different family of building blocks. The companion matrix provides them.

\begin{definition}
Given a monic polynomial $f(x) = x^k + a_{k-1}x^{k-1} + \cdots + a_1 x + a_0$ of degree$k$, the **companion matrix**$C(f)$ is the$k \times k$ matrix
$$C(f) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & -a_0 \\ 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & -a_1 \\ 0 & 1 & \cdots & 0 & -a_2 \\ \vdots & & \ddots & & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 & -a_{k-1} \end{pmatrix}.$$
\end{definition}

The defining property of $C(f)$ is that its characteristic polynomial is exactly$f$: a computation by cofactor expansion along the last column verifies$\det(xI - C(f)) = f(x)$. Moreover,$f$ is also the minimal polynomial of$C(f)$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** For $f(x) = x^2 - 5x + 6 = (x-2)(x-3)$:$C(f) = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -6 \\ 1 & 5\end{pmatrix}$. Eigenvalues are$2$ and$3$ (roots of$f$), and$C(f)$ is diagonalizable (distinct eigenvalues).
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** For $f(x) = x^2 + 1$ (irreducible over$\mathbb{R}$):$C(f) = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$. This is a real matrix with no real eigenvalues. Over$\mathbb{C}$, the eigenvalues are$\pm i$. The companion matrix represents the action of multiplication by$x$ on$\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2+1) \cong \mathbb{C}$, which is rotation by$\pi/2$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** For $f(x) = (x-3)^2 = x^2 - 6x + 9$:$C(f) = \begin{pmatrix}0&-9\\1&6\end{pmatrix}$. The characteristic and minimal polynomials are both$(x-3)^2$. Over any field, this companion matrix is similar to$J_2(3)$ (but not equal): the eigenvalue$3$ has algebraic multiplicity 2 and geometric multiplicity 1.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** For $f(x) = x^3 - 2$ over$\mathbb{Q}$:$C(f) = \begin{pmatrix}0&0&2\\1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}$. This represents the$\mathbb{Q}$-linear operator "multiply by$x$" on$\mathbb{Q}[x]/(x^3 - 2) = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$.
\end{example}

The reason $C(f)$ is the right building block is that it represents the action of multiplication by$x$ on the quotient ring$F[x]/(f(x))$. In the basis$\{1, x, x^2, \ldots, x^{k-1}\}$ of$F[x]/(f(x))$, multiplication by$x$ sends$x^j \mapsto x^{j+1}$ for$j < k-1$ and$x^{k-1} \mapsto -a_0 - a_1 x - \cdots - a_{k-1}x^{k-1}$ (from the relation$x^k = -a_{k-1}x^{k-1} - \cdots - a_0$ in the quotient). This is precisely the matrix$C(f)$.

\begin{proposition}
The characteristic polynomial and the minimal polynomial of $C(f)$ are both equal to$f$.
\end{proposition}

Notice that when $f(x) = (x - \mu)^k$, the companion matrix$C(f)$ is similar (though not equal) to the Jordan block$J_k(\mu)$. Over an algebraically closed field these two perspectives coincide, but over a general field only the companion matrix is defined without field extension. The rational canonical form builds on companion matrices in the same way that the Jordan form builds on Jordan blocks.
