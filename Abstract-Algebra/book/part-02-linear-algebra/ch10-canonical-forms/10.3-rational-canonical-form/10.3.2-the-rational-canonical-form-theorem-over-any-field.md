# 10.3.2 The Rational Canonical Form Theorem (Over Any Field)

The rational canonical form is the field-independent analogue of the Jordan canonical form. Whereas Jordan form requires the characteristic polynomial to split into linear factors, the rational canonical form exists over every field $F$ without exception.

\begin{theorem}[Rational Canonical Form]
Let $T : V \to V$ be a linear operator over any field$F$. Then there exist unique monic polynomials$f_1, f_2, \ldots, f_r \in F[x]$, each of degree at least$1$, satisfying the divisibility chain$f_1 \mid f_2 \mid \cdots \mid f_r$, such that$T$ is similar to the block-diagonal matrix
$$\mathrm{RCF}(T) = \mathrm{diag}(C(f_1), C(f_2), \ldots, C(f_r)).$$
The polynomials $f_1, \ldots, f_r$ are the **invariant factors** of$T$. Two operators over$F$ are similar if and only if they have the same invariant factors.
\end{theorem}

The name "rational" refers to the fact that the form can be computed using only operations within $F$: no field extensions are required. The invariant factors are polynomials in$F[x]$, and the companion matrices$C(f_i)$ have entries in$F$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Let $T$ have$p_T(x) = (x-2)(x-3)$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ (distinct roots). The only possibility is$r = 1$ and$f_1 = (x-2)(x-3)$, giving RCF$= C((x-2)(x-3)) = \begin{pmatrix}0&-6\\1&5\end{pmatrix}$.
\par
Alternatively, we could have $r = 2$ with$f_1 = (x-2)$,$f_2 = (x-3)$... but$f_1 \nmid f_2$ since$(x-2) \nmid (x-3)$. So the single-block form is the only one consistent with the divisibility chain. (In fact since the eigenvalues are distinct,$T$ is diagonalizable and its rational form coincides with the diagonal form: RCF =$\mathrm{diag}(C(x-2), C(x-3)) = \mathrm{diag}(2, 3)$, which has$f_1 = (x-2)$,$f_2 = (x-3)$, and$(x-2) \mid (x-3)$ fails over$\mathbb{Q}$, so...)
\par
*Correction:* For $T = \mathrm{diag}(2, 3)$, the invariant factors are$f_1 = x-2$ and$f_2 = x - 3$... but the divisibility$f_1 \mid f_2$ requires$(x-2) \mid (x-3)$ which fails. So the rational form must be a single$2 \times 2$ block$C((x-2)(x-3))$. Two distinct eigenvalues give a single companion block.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Let $T$ have$p_T(x) = (x-2)^2$ and$m_T(x) = (x-2)^2$ over any field. Then$r = 1$ and$f_1 = (x-2)^2$: the RCF is$C((x-2)^2) = \begin{pmatrix}0&-4\\1&4\end{pmatrix}$, which is similar to$J_2(2)$ (and over$\mathbb{C}$ they are similar).
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Let $T$ have$p_T(x) = (x-2)^2$ and$m_T(x) = (x-2)$. Then$T = 2I$, and$r = 2$ with$f_1 = f_2 = (x-2)$: RCF$= \mathrm{diag}(C(x-2), C(x-2)) = \mathrm{diag}(2, 2) = 2I$. The divisibility$f_1 \mid f_2$ holds trivially since$f_1 = f_2$.
\end{example}

The largest invariant factor $f_r$ is the minimal polynomial$m_T$: since$f_1 \mid f_2 \mid \cdots \mid f_r$, the factor$f_r$ annihilates every companion block (because the minimal polynomial of$C(f_i)$ is$f_i \mid f_r$), and no smaller polynomial does. The product$f_1 f_2 \cdots f_r = p_T$ equals the characteristic polynomial.

\begin{remark}
Over $\mathbb{R}$, the irreducible factors of$f_i$ are either linear$(x - \lambda)$ or quadratic$(x^2 + bx + c)$ with$b^2 - 4c < 0$. A real operator with non-real eigenvalues has quadratic factors in its invariant factors; the corresponding companion matrix blocks$C(x^2 + bx + c)$ represent$2 \times 2$ "rotation-scaling" blocks. This is the rational canonical form's way of encoding complex-eigenvalue structure in purely real matrices.
\end{remark}
