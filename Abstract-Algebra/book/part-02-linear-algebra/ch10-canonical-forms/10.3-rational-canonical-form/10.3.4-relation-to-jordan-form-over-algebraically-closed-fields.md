# 10.3.4 Relation to Jordan Form over Algebraically Closed Fields

The Jordan canonical form and the rational canonical form are two descriptions of the same underlying object — the $F[x]$-module structure of$V$ — viewed through different lenses. Over an algebraically closed field, the two descriptions are equivalent, and understanding how they relate illuminates both.

Over an algebraically closed field $F$ (such as$\mathbb{C}$), every polynomial in$F[x]$ factors completely into linear factors. In particular, each invariant factor$f_i$ factors as$f_i = \prod_j (x - \lambda_{ij})^{e_{ij}}$. By the Chinese Remainder Theorem for modules, the cyclic summand$F[x]/(f_i)$ in the rational decomposition splits further:
$$F[x]/(f_i) \cong \bigoplus_j F[x]/((x - \lambda_{ij})^{e_{ij}}).$$
Each factor $F[x]/((x - \mu)^k)$ corresponds to a single Jordan block$J_k(\mu)$. Thus the Jordan form is obtained from the rational form by refining each invariant factor block into its prime power components.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Over $\mathbb{C}$, let$T$ have single invariant factor$f_1 = (x-2)^2(x-3)$. The rational form is$C(f_1)$, a single$3 \times 3$ companion matrix. The CRT splits this into$F[x]/((x-2)^2) \oplus F[x]/(x-3)$, giving Jordan form$\mathrm{diag}(J_2(2), J_1(3))$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Over $\mathbb{R}$, let$T$ have invariant factor$f_1 = x^2 + 1$ (irreducible over$\mathbb{R}$). The rational form is$C(x^2+1) = \begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$. Over$\mathbb{C}$,$x^2+1 = (x-i)(x+i)$ splits, giving Jordan form$\mathrm{diag}(J_1(i), J_1(-i)) = \mathrm{diag}(i, -i)$ — a complex diagonal matrix with no real analogue. The rational form is genuinely preferable for this operator over$\mathbb{R}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Over $\mathbb{Q}$, let$T$ have$p_T(x) = x^3 - 2$. This is irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$ (by Eisenstein), so the single invariant factor is$f_1 = x^3 - 2$ and the rational form is the$3 \times 3$ companion matrix$C(x^3-2)$. Over$\mathbb{C}$, the polynomial splits as$(x - \sqrt[3]{2})(x - \omega\sqrt[3]{2})(x - \omega^2\sqrt[3]{2})$ where$\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$, giving a$3 \times 3$ diagonal Jordan form over$\mathbb{C}$.
\end{example}

The relationship between the two forms is one of granularity. The rational form groups all cyclic summands for all eigenvalues together under invariant factors (encoding the divisibility chain), while the Jordan form separates them by eigenvalue (encoding the primary decomposition). The Jordan form is "finer" over algebraically closed fields: each invariant factor block $C(f_i)$ breaks into as many Jordan blocks as$f_i$ has prime power factors.

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** A $4 \times 4$ operator over$\mathbb{C}$ with invariant factors$f_1 = (x-1)$ and$f_2 = (x-1)^2(x-2)$. Rational form:$\mathrm{diag}(C(x-1), C((x-1)^2(x-2)))$. Jordan form (refining$f_2$):$\mathrm{diag}(J_1(1), J_2(1), J_1(2))$ — a$4 \times 4$ block-diagonal matrix with three blocks.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
This comparison underscores the conceptual advantage of each form. The rational canonical form works uniformly over any field, is the natural invariant for similarity classification in general, and is the form produced by the Smith normal form algorithm. The Jordan form is simpler to read and compute with over algebraically closed fields, and its block structure directly reflects the eigenvalue structure of $T$. Both forms will reappear in Part V, where the module theory explains their construction via the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a PID.
\end{remark}
