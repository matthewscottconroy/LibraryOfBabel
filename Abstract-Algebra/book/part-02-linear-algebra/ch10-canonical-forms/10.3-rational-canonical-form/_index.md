# 10.3 Rational Canonical Form

Jordan normal form requires that all eigenvalues of the operator lie in the base field $F$. Over $\mathbb{C}$ this is guaranteed by the fundamental theorem of algebra, but over $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, or a finite field $\mathbb{F}_p$, a matrix may have no eigenvalues at all — yet one still wants a canonical form. The rational canonical form solves this problem by replacing eigenvalues with irreducible factors of the characteristic polynomial, and replacing Jordan blocks with companion matrices.

The companion matrix of a monic polynomial $f(x) = x^n + a_{n-1}x^{n-1} + \cdots + a_0$ is the $n \times n$ matrix that has 1s on the first subdiagonal and the negated coefficients $-a_0, -a_1, \ldots, -a_{n-1}$ in the last column. Its characteristic polynomial is $f(x)$ itself, and $f$ is also its minimal polynomial. The rational canonical form theorem states that every $n \times n$ matrix over any field $F$ is similar (over $F$) to a block-diagonal matrix whose blocks are companion matrices $C_{f_1}, C_{f_2}, \ldots, C_{f_k}$ for a sequence of monic polynomials $f_1 \mid f_2 \mid \cdots \mid f_k$ (each divides the next). These polynomials are the invariant factors of $T$; they are uniquely determined by $T$, so two matrices are similar over $F$ if and only if they have the same invariant factors.

The deepest proof of this theorem works via the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain. Viewing $V$ as a module over the polynomial ring $F[x]$ (where $f(x)$ acts on $\mathbf{v}$ as $f(T)(\mathbf{v})$), the structure theorem gives a decomposition $V \cong F[x]/(f_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus F[x]/(f_k)$ with $f_1 \mid \cdots \mid f_k$, and the rational canonical form is simply the matrix of $T$ acting on this decomposition. This perspective also clarifies the connection to Jordan form: over an algebraically closed field, each invariant factor $f_i$ can be factored into powers of linear polynomials, and refining the decomposition by these primary factors gives the Jordan block decomposition. The rational canonical form is thus the "primary" and the Jordan form the "elementary divisor" description of the same underlying module structure — two faces of a single algebraic theorem.
