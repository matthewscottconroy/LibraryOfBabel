# Chapter 10 — Canonical Forms

Chapter 9 left us with a question that applies to every non-diagonalizable operator: if the eigenvectors of $T$ do not span $V$, what is the simplest basis we can choose, and what does the matrix of $T$ look like in that basis? This chapter answers that question completely. It establishes two canonical forms — the Jordan normal form and the rational canonical form — each of which gives a canonical representative for each similarity class of matrices. The central theorem of the chapter is one of the most structurally powerful results in linear algebra: every square matrix over an algebraically closed field is similar to a matrix in Jordan form, and this form is unique up to reordering of blocks.

The solution begins with a conceptual move. When $T - \lambda I$ fails to be zero on all of $V$ but some power $(T - \lambda I)^k$ eventually kills everything associated with the eigenvalue $\lambda$, we should study the spaces on which these powers act. The generalized eigenspace $K_\lambda = \ker(T - \lambda I)^n$ (for large enough $n$) collects all vectors that $T$ sends toward the eigenvalue $\lambda$ in this iterated sense. Section 10.1 proves that $V$ decomposes as a direct sum of generalized eigenspaces, one for each distinct eigenvalue. This generalized eigenspace decomposition is the structural backbone of the Jordan form: it tells us that the problem of finding a canonical form for $T$ decomposes into one problem per eigenvalue, and each of those sub-problems involves a nilpotent operator $N = T - \lambda I$ restricted to $K_\lambda$.

Section 10.2 classifies nilpotent operators and uses that classification to construct Jordan normal form. The key insight is that a nilpotent operator $N$ on a $d$-dimensional space is determined — up to similarity — by the sizes of its Jordan blocks, which are in turn determined by the dimensions $\dim \ker N^j$ as $j$ ranges over $1, 2, \ldots$ These dimensions form a non-increasing sequence whose differences encode the block structure. The Jordan canonical form theorem states that every operator over $\mathbb{C}$ (or any algebraically closed field) is similar to a block-diagonal matrix $J = \mathrm{diag}(J_1, \ldots, J_r)$ where each Jordan block $J_i$ has a single eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ on the diagonal and 1s on the superdiagonal. This form is unique up to permuting the blocks. One of the most elegant applications is the computation of the matrix exponential $e^{tA}$ via Jordan form, which gives the general solution to a system of linear ODEs.

Section 10.3 introduces the rational canonical form, which works over any field, not just algebraically closed ones. Here the key algebraic tool is the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain: by viewing $V$ as a module over the polynomial ring $F[x]$ (where $x$ acts via $T$), one obtains a decomposition governed by invariant factors — monic polynomials $f_1 \mid f_2 \mid \cdots \mid f_k$ whose product is the characteristic polynomial. Each invariant factor corresponds to a companion matrix block, and the resulting block-diagonal form is the rational canonical form. It applies over $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{F}_p$, or any field where the characteristic polynomial may not split. Over an algebraically closed field, the two canonical forms are related by factoring the invariant factors, and the chapter concludes by making this relationship explicit.

Taken together, the Jordan and rational canonical forms solve the similarity classification problem: two matrices are similar if and only if they have the same Jordan form (over $\mathbb{C}$) or the same rational canonical form (over any field). This is a complete invariant, and it connects linear algebra to the deepest algebraic structures — polynomial rings, module theory, and the arithmetic of ideals — that will recur throughout Part 3 of this text.
