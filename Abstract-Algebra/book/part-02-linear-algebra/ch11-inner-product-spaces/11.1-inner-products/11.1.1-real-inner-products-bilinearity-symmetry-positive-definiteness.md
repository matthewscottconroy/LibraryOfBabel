# 11.1.1 Real Inner Products: Bilinearity, Symmetry, Positive Definiteness

Linear algebra as we have studied it so far is purely algebraic: vectors are elements of a set closed under addition and scalar multiplication, and linear maps are functions preserving those operations. There is no notion yet of length, angle, or distance. An inner product supplies all of this structure at once, transforming a bare vector space into a geometric space where these familiar concepts have precise meaning.

\begin{definition}
A **real inner product** on a vector space $V$ over$\mathbb{R}$ is a function$\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle: V \times V \to \mathbb{R}$ satisfying:
\begin{enumerate}
\item **Bilinearity:** $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ is linear in each argument:$\langle au + bv, w \rangle = a\langle u, w\rangle + b\langle v, w\rangle$ and similarly in the second argument.
\item **Symmetry:** $\langle u, v \rangle = \langle v, u \rangle$ for all$u, v \in V$.
\item **Positive definiteness:** $\langle v, v \rangle \geq 0$ for all$v \in V$, with equality if and only if$v = 0$.
\end{enumerate}
A vector space equipped with a real inner product is called a (real) **inner product space**.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
**Example 1 (Dot product).** On $\mathbb{R}^n$, set$\langle u, v \rangle = \sum_{i=1}^n u_i v_i$. Bilinearity and symmetry are immediate. Positive definiteness:$\sum u_i^2 = 0$ forces each$u_i = 0$, so$u = 0$. This is the **standard inner product** on$\mathbb{R}^n$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2 ($L^2$ inner product).** On the space$C([0,1])$ of continuous functions, set$\langle f, g \rangle = \int_0^1 f(x) g(x)\, dx$. Bilinearity follows from linearity of the integral; symmetry is clear; positive definiteness holds because$\int_0^1 f(x)^2\, dx = 0$ forces$f \equiv 0$ (since$f$ is continuous and non-negative). This is the starting point of Fourier analysis.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3 (Frobenius inner product).** On $M_n(\mathbb{R})$, set$\langle A, B \rangle = \mathrm{tr}(A^T B) = \sum_{i,j} A_{ij} B_{ij}$. This is the "flat" dot product treating the$n^2$ matrix entries as coordinates.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4 (Weighted inner product).** On $\mathbb{R}^n$ with positive weights$w_1, \ldots, w_n > 0$, set$\langle u, v \rangle_w = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i u_i v_i$. This is an inner product that assigns different "importance" to different coordinates.
\end{example}

The positive definiteness axiom is the load-bearing condition. Symmetry and bilinearity alone define a **symmetric bilinear form**, and such forms can assign zero or negative values to $\langle v, v \rangle$; the theory of indefinite forms is rich and will occupy us in Section 11.6. Positive definiteness is precisely the condition that prevents this, and it is what allows$\|v\| = \sqrt{\langle v, v \rangle}$ to serve as a genuine measure of size.

\begin{remark}
In a basis $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$, an inner product is completely determined by the$n \times n$ **Gram matrix**$G_{ij} = \langle e_i, e_j \rangle$. Symmetry forces$G = G^T$, and positive definiteness forces$G$ to be a positive definite matrix (all eigenvalues positive, equivalently$x^T G x > 0$ for all nonzero$x$). Conversely, any symmetric positive definite matrix defines an inner product on$F^n$ via$\langle u, v \rangle = u^T G v$. The standard inner product corresponds to$G = I_n$.
\end{remark}

With an inner product in hand, we can define orthogonality (Section 11.2), project vectors onto subspaces (Section 11.2.5), define the adjoint of a linear map (Section 11.3), and ultimately prove the spectral theorem classifying which operators can be diagonalized with respect to orthonormal bases (Section 11.4) — all of which the coming sections develop in turn.
