# 11.1.2 Complex (Hermitian) Inner Products: Sesquilinearity

When we pass from real to complex vector spaces, the definition of an inner product must be adjusted. A naive attempt to use a bilinear form $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle: V \times V \to \mathbb{C}$ runs into an immediate obstacle: if$\langle v, v \rangle = c$ for some nonzero$v$, then$\langle iv, iv \rangle = i^2 \langle v, v \rangle = -c$, so one of$\langle v, v \rangle$ and$\langle iv, iv \rangle$ must be negative or zero. A bilinear form therefore cannot be positive definite over$\mathbb{C}$.

The resolution is to break the symmetry between the two arguments.

\begin{definition}
A **Hermitian inner product** (or **complex inner product**) on a complex vector space $V$ is a function$\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle: V \times V \to \mathbb{C}$ satisfying:
\begin{enumerate}
\item **Sesquilinearity:** Conjugate-linear in the first argument and linear in the second: $\langle \lambda u, v \rangle = \overline{\lambda} \langle u, v \rangle$ and$\langle u, \lambda v \rangle = \lambda \langle u, v \rangle$.
\item **Conjugate symmetry:** $\langle u, v \rangle = \overline{\langle v, u \rangle}$.
\item **Positive definiteness:** $\langle v, v \rangle \geq 0$ with equality iff$v = 0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Notice that conjugate symmetry forces $\langle v, v \rangle = \overline{\langle v, v \rangle}$, so the diagonal values are automatically real — positive definiteness then makes sense. The adjective "sesquilinear" (from Latin "one-and-a-half linear") precisely captures what is needed: half the symmetry of bilinearity, with complex conjugation in one slot.

\begin{example}
**Example 1 (Standard Hermitian inner product).** On $\mathbb{C}^n$, set$\langle u, v \rangle = \sum_{i=1}^n \overline{u_i} v_i$. The conjugate on the first argument now saves us:$\langle iv, iv \rangle = \overline{i} \cdot i \cdot \langle v, v \rangle = (-i)(i) \langle v, v \rangle = \langle v, v \rangle > 0$ for$v \neq 0$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2 (Matrix trace inner product).** On $M_n(\mathbb{C})$, set$\langle A, B \rangle = \mathrm{tr}(A^* B)$ where$A^* = \overline{A}^T$ is the conjugate transpose. This is sesquilinear in the first argument (conjugate on$A$) and linear in the second.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3 (Hilbert space of sequences).** On $\ell^2(\mathbb{C})$ (sequences$(a_n)$ with$\sum |a_n|^2 < \infty$), set$\langle (a_n), (b_n) \rangle = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \overline{a_n} b_n$. This is the prototype of an infinite-dimensional complex Hilbert space.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4 (Weighted).** On $\mathbb{C}^2$ with weight matrix$W = \begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix}$, set$\langle u, v \rangle_W = u^* W v = 2\overline{u_1}v_1 + 3\overline{u_2}v_2$. This assigns different "sizes" to the two coordinate directions.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
Convention varies: some authors (particularly in physics) place the conjugation in the second argument rather than the first, writing $\langle u, v \rangle = \sum_i u_i \overline{v_i}$. This is equally valid but conjugate-linear in the second argument instead of the first. The "physicist's convention" and the "mathematician's convention" differ by complex conjugation of the inner product:$\langle u, v \rangle_{\mathrm{phys}} = \overline{\langle u, v \rangle_{\mathrm{math}}}$. We use the mathematician's convention (conjugate-linear in the first argument) throughout this text.
\end{remark}

All the geometric consequences of real inner products — norms, orthogonality, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, the spectral theorem — carry over from the real setting with only minor modifications to account for conjugation. The key formulas become: $\|v\| = \sqrt{\langle v, v \rangle}$, Cauchy-Schwarz$|\langle u, v \rangle| \leq \|u\|\|v\|$, and the matrix of the adjoint of$T$ is$A^* = \overline{A}^T$ in an orthonormal basis. The reader should keep the conjugate in mind throughout Chapter 11; in many formulas, the real and complex cases are unified simply by allowing conjugation.
