# 11.1.3 The Norm and Distance Induced by an Inner Product

Given an inner product, we immediately obtain a notion of size. The **norm** of a vector $v \in V$ is defined by$\|v\| = \sqrt{\langle v, v \rangle}$. Positive definiteness of the inner product ensures that$\langle v, v \rangle \geq 0$, so the square root is real and non-negative, and$\|v\| = 0$ if and only if$v = 0$. Homogeneity follows from (sesqui)linearity:$\|\lambda v\| = |\lambda| \|v\|$. What requires genuine work is the **triangle inequality**$\|u + v\| \leq \|u\| + \|v\|$, which is not at all obvious from the definition — it depends on the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality established in the next section.

The norm in turn yields a metric: the **distance** between $u$ and$v$ is$d(u,v) = \|u - v\|$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1 (Euclidean norm).** On $\mathbb{R}^n$ with the dot product,$\|v\| = \sqrt{\sum_i v_i^2}$ is the standard Euclidean length, and$d(u,v) = \sqrt{\sum_i (u_i - v_i)^2}$ is Euclidean distance. The triangle inequality is the familiar geometric statement that the sum of two sides of a triangle exceeds the third.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2 ($L^2$ norm).** On$C([0,1])$ with$\langle f, g \rangle = \int_0^1 f g\, dx$, the norm is$\|f\| = \sqrt{\int_0^1 f(x)^2\, dx}$. The triangle inequality becomes$\left(\int_0^1 (f+g)^2\right)^{1/2} \leq \left(\int_0^1 f^2\right)^{1/2} + \left(\int_0^1 g^2\right)^{1/2}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3 (Frobenius norm).** On $M_n(\mathbb{R})$ with$\langle A, B \rangle = \mathrm{tr}(A^T B)$, the norm is$\|A\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i,j} A_{ij}^2}$, the Frobenius (or Hilbert-Schmidt) norm. This satisfies$\|A\|_F^2 = \mathrm{tr}(A^T A) = \sum_i \sigma_i^2$, the sum of squares of singular values.
\end{example}

To see why the inner-product norm is special among all possible norms, recall that on $\mathbb{R}^n$ one can define other norms such as the$\ell^1$ norm$\|v\|_1 = \sum |v_i|$ or the$\ell^\infty$ norm$\|v\|_\infty = \max_i |v_i|$. Neither of these arises from an inner product.

\begin{theorem}[Parallelogram Law]
A norm $\|\cdot\|$ on a real vector space arises from an inner product if and only if it satisfies the **parallelogram law**:
$$\|u + v\|^2 + \|u - v\|^2 = 2\|u\|^2 + 2\|v\|^2.$$
When this holds, the inner product is recovered by the **polarization identity**:
$$\langle u, v \rangle = \frac{1}{4}\bigl(\|u+v\|^2 - \|u-v\|^2\bigr).$$
For complex spaces: $\langle u, v \rangle = \frac{1}{4}\sum_{k=0}^3 i^k \|u + i^k v\|^2$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** Check that $\|\cdot\|_1$ on$\mathbb{R}^2$ does not satisfy the parallelogram law. Take$u = (1,0)$ and$v = (0,1)$:$\|u+v\|_1^2 + \|u-v\|_1^2 = 2^2 + 2^2 = 8$, but$2\|u\|_1^2 + 2\|v\|_1^2 = 2 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 1 = 4 \neq 8$. So$\|\cdot\|_1$ does not come from an inner product.
\end{example}
\par
\begin{remark}
A complete inner product space (one where every Cauchy sequence converges) is called a **Hilbert space**. Finite-dimensional inner product spaces are automatically complete, but infinite-dimensional spaces such as $L^2([0,1])$ (square-integrable functions, not merely continuous) require careful completion. Much of functional analysis studies operators on Hilbert spaces, where the spectral theory of the finite-dimensional setting extends — with appropriate modifications — to infinite-dimensional operators such as differential and integral operators.
\end{remark}
