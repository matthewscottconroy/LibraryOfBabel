# 11.1.4 The Cauchy–Schwarz and Triangle Inequalities

Two inequalities sit at the foundation of inner-product geometry and reappear throughout analysis, probability, and physics. The first, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, relates the magnitude of the inner product to the norms of the two vectors.

\begin{theorem}[Cauchy–Schwarz Inequality]
For any $u, v \in V$ in an inner product space,
$$|\langle u, v \rangle| \leq \|u\| \|v\|,$$
with equality if and only if $u$ and$v$ are linearly dependent.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If $v = 0$ the inequality is trivial. For$v \neq 0$, consider$f(t) = \|u - tv\|^2 \geq 0$ for$t \in \mathbb{R}$ (for the real case; analogously for$\mathbb{C}$). Expanding:
$$f(t) = \|u\|^2 - 2t\langle u, v \rangle + t^2\|v\|^2.$$
This quadratic in $t$ is non-negative, so its discriminant is$\leq 0$:$4\langle u,v\rangle^2 - 4\|u\|^2\|v\|^2 \leq 0$, giving$|\langle u,v\rangle| \leq \|u\|\|v\|$. Equality$f(t^*) = 0$ at$t^* = \langle u,v\rangle/\|v\|^2$ means$u = t^* v$, i.e.,$u$ and$v$ are proportional.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{R}^n$:$|\sum_i u_i v_i| \leq \sqrt{\sum_i u_i^2} \cdot \sqrt{\sum_i v_i^2}$. In$\mathbb{R}^3$ with$u = (1, 2, 3)$ and$v = (4, 0, -1)$:$\langle u,v\rangle = 4 - 3 = 1$,$\|u\| = \sqrt{14}$,$\|v\| = \sqrt{17}$, and indeed$1 \leq \sqrt{14 \cdot 17} = \sqrt{238} \approx 15.4$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** In $C([0,1])$:$\left|\int_0^1 f(x)g(x)\, dx\right| \leq \sqrt{\int_0^1 f^2\, dx} \cdot \sqrt{\int_0^1 g^2\, dx}$. Taking$f(x) = x$ and$g(x) = 1$:$|\int_0^1 x\, dx| = 1/2$ and$\sqrt{\int_0^1 x^2\, dx} \cdot \sqrt{1} = 1/\sqrt{3}$. Indeed$1/2 < 1/\sqrt{3} \approx 0.577$.
\end{example}

In $\mathbb{R}^n$, the dot product can be written$\langle u, v \rangle = \|u\|\|v\|\cos\theta$, where$\theta$ is the angle between$u$ and$v$. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality is the statement that$|\cos\theta| \leq 1$. In the abstract setting, we can use the inequality in the other direction: it guarantees that$\langle u, v \rangle / (\|u\|\|v\|) \in [-1, 1]$ (in the real case), which defines a consistent notion of angle even in spaces where we had no prior geometric intuition.

\begin{theorem}[Triangle Inequality]
$\|u + v\| \leq \|u\| + \|v\|$ for all$u, v \in V$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Expand $\|u + v\|^2 = \|u\|^2 + 2\,\mathrm{Re}\langle u, v\rangle + \|v\|^2 \leq \|u\|^2 + 2|\langle u,v\rangle| + \|v\|^2 \leq \|u\|^2 + 2\|u\|\|v\| + \|v\|^2 = (\|u\| + \|v\|)^2$, where the last step uses Cauchy–Schwarz. Take square roots.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** In $\mathbb{R}^2$ with$u = (3, 0)$ and$v = (0, 4)$:$\|u + v\| = 5$ and$\|u\| + \|v\| = 7$. The triangle inequality$5 \leq 7$ holds, with strict inequality because$u$ and$v$ are not parallel.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4 (Equality in Cauchy–Schwarz).** In $\mathbb{R}^n$, equality$|\langle u,v\rangle| = \|u\|\|v\|$ holds iff$u = \lambda v$ for some scalar$\lambda$. For instance$u = (2,4)$ and$v = (1,2)$:$\langle u,v\rangle = 10 = \|(2,4)\| \cdot \|(1,2)\| = \sqrt{20} \cdot \sqrt{5} = \sqrt{100} = 10$.
\end{example}
\par
\begin{remark}
The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality has many equivalent forms. In probability: $|\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)| \leq \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X)\mathrm{Var}(Y)}$, where the covariance is an inner product on random variables with mean 0. In quantum mechanics:$\Delta A \cdot \Delta B \geq \frac{1}{2}|\langle[A,B]\rangle|$ (the Heisenberg uncertainty principle), derived from Cauchy–Schwarz. These two inequalities together — Cauchy–Schwarz and the triangle inequality — confirm that inner-product spaces are genuine metric spaces and underpin virtually every convergence argument in functional analysis.
\end{remark}
