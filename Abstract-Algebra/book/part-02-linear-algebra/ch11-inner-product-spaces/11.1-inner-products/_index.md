# 11.1 Inner Products

Vector spaces, as studied so far, carry no intrinsic notion of length or angle. Any two bases are algebraically equivalent, and there is no way to say one vector is "longer" than another without additional structure. An inner product supplies this structure by assigning to each ordered pair of vectors $(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v})$ a scalar $\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle$ subject to three axioms: it is linear in the first argument (bilinear over $\mathbb{R}$, sesquilinear over $\mathbb{C}$), it satisfies $\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle = \overline{\langle \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{u} \rangle}$ (symmetric over $\mathbb{R}$, conjugate-symmetric over $\mathbb{C}$), and it is positive definite: $\langle \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{v} \rangle > 0$ for all $\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}$. The positive definiteness condition is what distinguishes an inner product from a general bilinear form and ensures that the induced norm $\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{\langle \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{v} \rangle}$ is a genuine measure of magnitude.

The canonical examples are the dot product on $\mathbb{R}^n$ and the standard Hermitian inner product $\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle = \sum_i \bar{u}_i v_i$ on $\mathbb{C}^n$, but the definition applies to any vector space, including infinite-dimensional function spaces. On $C([a,b])$, the integral $\langle f, g \rangle = \int_a^b f(x) g(x) \, dx$ defines an inner product whose induced norm measures the "size" of a function in a way suited to approximation theory and Fourier analysis. The formal framework developed here thus simultaneously covers Euclidean geometry, quantum mechanics (where state vectors live in a complex Hilbert space and observables are self-adjoint operators), and the classical theory of orthogonal polynomials.

The first major theorem derived from the axioms alone is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality: $|\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle| \leq \|\mathbf{u}\| \cdot \|\mathbf{v}\|$, with equality if and only if $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are proportional. The proof is a slick computation: for any scalar $t$, expand $0 \leq \|\mathbf{u} - t\mathbf{v}\|^2$ and minimize over $t$. From Cauchy–Schwarz the triangle inequality $\|\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\| \leq \|\mathbf{u}\| + \|\mathbf{v}\|$ follows immediately, confirming that $\|\cdot\|$ is a proper norm on $V$. These inequalities are not merely computational tools — they encode the geometry that makes inner product spaces behave like the Euclidean plane, and they underpin every convergence argument in functional analysis.
