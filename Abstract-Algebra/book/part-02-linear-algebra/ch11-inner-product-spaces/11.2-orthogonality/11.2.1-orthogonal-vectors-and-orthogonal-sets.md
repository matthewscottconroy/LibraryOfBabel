# 11.2.1 Orthogonal Vectors and Orthogonal Sets

The most natural geometric concept that an inner product enables is perpendicularity. We say that two vectors $u, v \in V$ are **orthogonal**, written$u \perp v$, if$\langle u, v \rangle = 0$. In$\mathbb{R}^2$ or$\mathbb{R}^3$ this recovers the familiar notion of right angles: the dot product of two perpendicular vectors vanishes. The definition extends this to any inner product space. Note that the zero vector is orthogonal to every vector, since$\langle 0, v \rangle = 0$ always.

\begin{definition}
A set $S \subseteq V$ is called an **orthogonal set** if every pair of distinct vectors in$S$ is orthogonal:$\langle u, v \rangle = 0$ for all$u \neq v$ in$S$. If additionally every vector in$S$ has unit norm,$\|v\| = 1$ for all$v \in S$, the set is called **orthonormal**.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** The standard basis $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$ of$\mathbb{R}^n$ with the dot product is orthonormal:$\langle e_i, e_j \rangle = \delta_{ij}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** In $\mathbb{R}^2$, the vectors$u = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,1)$ and$v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,-1)$ are orthonormal:$\langle u, v \rangle = \frac{1}{2}(1 - 1) = 0$ and$\|u\| = \|v\| = 1$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** In $C([0, 2\pi])$ with$\langle f, g \rangle = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)g(x)\, dx$, the trigonometric functions$\{1/\sqrt{2}, \cos x, \sin x, \cos 2x, \sin 2x, \ldots\}$ form an orthonormal set. This is the starting point of classical Fourier analysis.
\end{example}

The key algebraic consequence of orthogonality is:

\begin{theorem}[Pythagorean Theorem]
If $u \perp v$, then$\|u + v\|^2 = \|u\|^2 + \|v\|^2$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$\|u+v\|^2 = \langle u+v, u+v \rangle = \|u\|^2 + \langle u,v\rangle + \langle v,u\rangle + \|v\|^2 = \|u\|^2 + 0 + 0 + \|v\|^2$.
\end{proof}

More importantly, orthogonality forces linear independence in a very clean way.

\begin{theorem}
An orthogonal set of nonzero vectors is linearly independent.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Suppose $\{v_1, \ldots, v_k\}$ is orthogonal with each$v_i \neq 0$, and$\sum_i c_i v_i = 0$. Take the inner product with$v_j$:
$$0 = \left\langle \sum_i c_i v_i, v_j \right\rangle = \sum_i c_i \langle v_i, v_j \rangle = c_j \|v_j\|^2.$$
Since $\|v_j\|^2 > 0$, we get$c_j = 0$ for each$j$.
\end{proof}

This is the key reason orthonormal bases are so computationally convenient: no row reduction or determinant calculation is needed to verify independence. The proof requires only one inner product computation per vector.

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** In $\mathbb{R}^3$, the vectors$u_1 = (1, 1, 0)/\sqrt{2}$,$u_2 = (1, -1, 1)/\sqrt{3}$,$u_3 = (1, -1, -2)/\sqrt{6}$ are mutually orthogonal (one can verify$\langle u_i, u_j \rangle = 0$ for$i \neq j$) and hence linearly independent. They form an orthonormal basis for$\mathbb{R}^3$.
\end{example}
\par
\begin{remark}
In the special case $k = n$ (an orthonormal set of size$n = \dim V$), linear independence immediately implies it is a basis. Thus: any orthonormal set of$n$ vectors in an$n$-dimensional inner product space is automatically an **orthonormal basis**. The Gram–Schmidt process (Section 11.2.3) guarantees that any basis can be replaced by an orthonormal one.
\end{remark}
