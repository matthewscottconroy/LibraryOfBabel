# 11.2.2 Orthonormal Bases; Coordinates via Inner Products

An **orthonormal basis** of an inner product space $V$ is a basis$\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$ that is simultaneously orthonormal:$\langle e_i, e_j \rangle = \delta_{ij}$. The existence of such a basis for every finite-dimensional inner product space is guaranteed by the Gram–Schmidt process. The real power of orthonormality is the extraordinary simplification it brings to coordinate computations.

\begin{theorem}
If $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$ is an orthonormal basis of$V$, then any vector$v \in V$ satisfies
$$v = \sum_{i=1}^n \langle e_i, v \rangle e_i \quad (\text{real case}) \quad \text{or} \quad v = \sum_{i=1}^n \langle e_i, v \rangle e_i \quad (\text{complex: use conjugate-linearity in first slot}).$$
The $i$-th coordinate of$v$ in the orthonormal basis is simply$\langle e_i, v \rangle$.
\end{theorem}

To verify: apply $\langle e_j, \cdot \rangle$ to both sides:$\langle e_j, v \rangle = \sum_i \langle e_i, v \rangle \langle e_j, e_i \rangle = \sum_i \langle e_i, v \rangle \delta_{ji} = \langle e_j, v \rangle$. Consistency confirmed.

This is striking: in a general basis, finding coordinates requires solving a linear system (inverting the change-of-basis matrix); in an orthonormal basis, coordinates are computed by $n$ inner products, each a direct calculation with no matrix inversion.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{R}^3$ with standard orthonormal basis$\{e_1, e_2, e_3\}$ and$v = (3, -1, 2)$: the coordinates are$\langle e_1, v \rangle = 3$,$\langle e_2, v \rangle = -1$,$\langle e_3, v \rangle = 2$. Trivial — but the formula$v = 3e_1 - e_2 + 2e_3$ is the expansion with coordinates given by inner products.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** In $\mathbb{R}^2$ with orthonormal basis$\{u_1, u_2\}$ where$u_1 = (1/\sqrt{2})(1,1)$ and$u_2 = (1/\sqrt{2})(1,-1)$, and$v = (3, 1)$: coordinates are$\langle u_1, v \rangle = (3+1)/\sqrt{2} = 4/\sqrt{2} = 2\sqrt{2}$ and$\langle u_2, v \rangle = (3-1)/\sqrt{2} = \sqrt{2}$. So$v = 2\sqrt{2} \cdot u_1 + \sqrt{2} \cdot u_2$. Verify:$2\sqrt{2} \cdot (1/\sqrt{2})(1,1) + \sqrt{2} \cdot (1/\sqrt{2})(1,-1) = (2,2) + (1,-1) = (3,1)$. Correct.
\end{example}

\begin{theorem}[Parseval's Identity]
For any $u, v \in V$ and orthonormal basis$\{e_i\}$:
$$\langle u, v \rangle = \sum_{i=1}^n \overline{\langle e_i, u \rangle} \langle e_i, v \rangle, \qquad \|v\|^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n |\langle e_i, v \rangle|^2.$$
\end{theorem}

The second formula (setting $u = v$) says the squared norm equals the sum of squared coordinate magnitudes — a generalization of the Pythagorean theorem to all dimensions.

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** For $v = (3, -1, 2) \in \mathbb{R}^3$ in the standard basis:$\|v\|^2 = 3^2 + (-1)^2 + 2^2 = 14$. Parseval:$\sum_i \langle e_i, v \rangle^2 = 9 + 1 + 4 = 14$. Consistent.
\end{example}

The matrix of a linear operator $T$ in an orthonormal basis has entries$T_{ij} = \langle e_i, Te_j \rangle$, and the matrix of any change from one orthonormal basis to another is an orthogonal (or unitary) matrix — one satisfying$Q^T Q = I$ (real) or$Q^* Q = I$ (complex). Working with orthonormal bases is not merely convenient — it is the natural language of inner product spaces, and it is the setting in which the spectral theorem takes its cleanest form.

\begin{remark}
The coordinate formula $v = \sum_i \langle e_i, v \rangle e_i$ is the finite-dimensional version of a general principle that holds in infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces: any element of a separable Hilbert space can be expanded in an orthonormal basis (a countable one). In$L^2([0, 2\pi])$, this is Fourier series; in$L^2(\mathbb{R})$, it is the theory of wavelets. The finite-dimensional theory developed here is the template for all of these.
\end{remark}
