# 11.2.3 The Gram–Schmidt Orthogonalization Process

The previous section established that orthonormal bases are computationally far more convenient than arbitrary ones: coordinates are computed by inner products, projections are transparent, and the Pythagorean theorem holds termwise. The standing question is whether every finite-dimensional inner product space actually admits an orthonormal basis. The **Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization process** answers this constructively and affirmatively, and does more: it shows that any basis can be replaced by an orthonormal one that spans exactly the same nested sequence of subspaces.

The geometric idea is simple. Given a collection of linearly independent vectors, one removes from each new vector all components it shares with the previously orthogonalized ones, isolating the "genuinely new" direction it contributes. The resulting residuals are mutually orthogonal, and normalizing them yields an orthonormal set. The span-preservation property — $\mathrm{span}\{e_1, \ldots, e_k\} = \mathrm{span}\{v_1, \ldots, v_k\}$ for every$k$ — is the key structural feature that makes Gram–Schmidt compatible with filtered and graded constructions throughout algebra.

\begin{theorem}[Gram–Schmidt]
Let $V$ be an inner product space over$\mathbb{F} \in \{\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}\}$, and let$\{v_1, \ldots, v_n\}$ be a linearly independent set in$V$. Then there exists an orthonormal set$\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$ in$V$ such that
$$\mathrm{span}\{e_1, \ldots, e_k\} = \mathrm{span}\{v_1, \ldots, v_k\}$$
for every $k = 1, \ldots, n$. In particular, every finite-dimensional inner product space has an orthonormal basis.
\end{theorem}

**The algorithm.** Define intermediate vectors $u_k$ and normalized vectors$e_k$ inductively. Set
$$u_1 = v_1, \qquad e_1 = \frac{u_1}{\|u_1\|}.$$
For $k = 2, 3, \ldots, n$, subtract from$v_k$ its projection onto the subspace already spanned by$e_1, \ldots, e_{k-1}$:
$$u_k = v_k - \sum_{j=1}^{k-1} \langle v_k, e_j \rangle\, e_j, \qquad e_k = \frac{u_k}{\|u_k\|}.$$
Each inner product $\langle v_k, e_j \rangle$ is the coefficient of$v_k$ along$e_j$; subtracting these projections leaves exactly the component of$v_k$ orthogonal to$\mathrm{span}\{e_1, \ldots, e_{k-1}\}$. Since$v_k \notin \mathrm{span}\{v_1, \ldots, v_{k-1}\} = \mathrm{span}\{e_1, \ldots, e_{k-1}\}$ (by linear independence),$u_k \neq 0$ and the normalization is valid. One verifies by induction that$\langle e_i, e_k \rangle = 0$ for all$i < k$: this follows because$u_k$ is orthogonal to each$e_i$ by construction, and$e_k$ is just a rescaling of$u_k$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Apply Gram–Schmidt to $v_1 = (1,1,0)$,$v_2 = (1,0,1)$,$v_3 = (0,1,1)$ in$\mathbb{R}^3$ with the standard inner product.
\par
**Step 1.** $u_1 = (1,1,0)$,$\|u_1\| = \sqrt{2}$, so$e_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,1,0)$.
\par
**Step 2.** Compute $\langle v_2, e_1 \rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$. Then
$$u_2 = (1,0,1) - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot \frac{(1,1,0)}{\sqrt{2}} = (1,0,1) - \left(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}, 0\right) = \left(\tfrac{1}{2}, -\tfrac{1}{2}, 1\right).$$
$\|u_2\|^2 = \tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{1}{4} + 1 = \tfrac{3}{2}$, so$\|u_2\| = \sqrt{3/2}$ and
$$e_2 = \frac{(1/2, -1/2, 1)}{\sqrt{3/2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}(1,-1,2).$$
\par
**Step 3.** Compute $\langle v_3, e_1 \rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(0 + 1 + 0) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ and$\langle v_3, e_2 \rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}(0 - 1 + 2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}$. Then
$$u_3 = (0,1,1) - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot \frac{(1,1,0)}{\sqrt{2}} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}} \cdot \frac{(1,-1,2)}{\sqrt{6}} = (0,1,1) - \left(\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2},0\right) - \left(\tfrac{1}{6},-\tfrac{1}{6},\tfrac{1}{3}\right) = \left(-\tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{2}{3}\right).$$
$\|u_3\| = \sqrt{4/9 + 4/9 + 4/9} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}$, so$e_3 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(-1,1,1)$.
\par
**Verification.** One checks directly that $\langle e_1, e_2 \rangle = \langle e_1, e_3 \rangle = \langle e_2, e_3 \rangle = 0$ and$\|e_i\| = 1$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** In $P_2(\mathbb{R})$ (polynomials of degree$\leq 2$) with the$L^2[-1,1]$ inner product$\langle f, g \rangle = \int_{-1}^1 f(x)g(x)\,dx$, apply Gram–Schmidt to the monomial basis$\{1, x, x^2\}$.
\par
**Step 1.** $u_1 = 1$.$\|1\|^2 = \int_{-1}^1 1\,dx = 2$, so$e_1 = 1/\sqrt{2}$.
\par
**Step 2.** $\langle x, e_1 \rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{-1}^1 x\,dx = 0$ (odd function on symmetric interval). Thus$u_2 = x$ and$\|x\|^2 = \int_{-1}^1 x^2\,dx = 2/3$, giving$e_2 = \sqrt{3/2}\,x$.
\par
**Step 3.** $\langle x^2, e_1 \rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{-1}^1 x^2\,dx = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot \frac{2}{3} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{3}$. And$\langle x^2, e_2 \rangle = \sqrt{3/2}\int_{-1}^1 x^3\,dx = 0$ (odd integrand). So
$$u_3 = x^2 - \frac{\sqrt{2}}{3} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = x^2 - \frac{1}{3}.$$
$\|x^2 - 1/3\|^2 = \int_{-1}^1(x^2-1/3)^2\,dx = \int_{-1}^1 \!\left(x^4 - \frac{2}{3}x^2 + \frac{1}{9}\right)\!dx = \frac{2}{5} - \frac{4}{9} + \frac{2}{9} = \frac{8}{45}$. Hence$e_3 = \sqrt{45/8}\,(x^2 - 1/3) = \frac{3}{\sqrt{8}}\cdot\frac{3x^2-1}{3} = \frac{\sqrt{5}}{2}\sqrt{2}\cdot\frac{3x^2-1}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}$.
\par
The resulting polynomial $x^2 - 1/3$ is (up to normalization) the **second Legendre polynomial**$P_2(x) = (3x^2 - 1)/2$. More generally, applying Gram–Schmidt to$\{1, x, x^2, \ldots\}$ on$[-1,1]$ produces the Legendre polynomials$P_0, P_1, P_2, \ldots$, an orthogonal basis for$L^2[-1,1]$ of fundamental importance in mathematical physics and approximation theory.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Any finite-dimensional subspace $W$ of an infinite-dimensional inner product space (such as$L^2[a,b]$) has an orthonormal basis, obtained by applying Gram–Schmidt to any basis of$W$. This underpins the method of choosing a finite-dimensional approximation space (e.g., polynomials up to degree$n$) and projecting a function onto it using orthonormal coordinates.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}
The orthonormal set $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$ produced by Gram–Schmidt is unique given the ordered basis$\{v_1, \ldots, v_n\}$ and the sign convention that each$R_{kk} = \|u_k\| > 0$. (Allowing negative diagonals introduces sign ambiguity at each step.)
\end{proposition}

The uniqueness here is the same uniqueness that will appear in the QR decomposition: the process establishes a canonical orthonormal frame adapted to the given basis.

\begin{remark}
The **modified Gram–Schmidt (MGS)** algorithm is an algebraically equivalent reformulation that is numerically superior. In classical Gram–Schmidt, one computes all inner products $\langle v_k, e_j\rangle$ against the original$v_k$ before modifying it. In MGS, after computing$e_1$ from$u_1$, one immediately subtracts the$e_1$-component from all remaining$v_j$, then repeats for$e_2$, and so on. Mathematically, the output is identical. Numerically, floating-point cancellation is significantly reduced: MGS produces vectors that are orthogonal to working precision, while classical Gram–Schmidt can lose orthogonality catastrophically for nearly dependent inputs. For large-scale numerical work (e.g., Krylov subspace methods such as GMRES and Arnoldi iteration), a reorthogonalization step is often added after each MGS pass. The classical algorithm, however, remains the correct choice for theoretical analysis, as its structure transparently yields the QR decomposition discussed in the next section.
\end{remark}
