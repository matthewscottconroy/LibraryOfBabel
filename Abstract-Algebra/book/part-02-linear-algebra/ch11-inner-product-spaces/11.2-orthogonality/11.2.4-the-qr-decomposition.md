# 11.2.4 The QR Decomposition

The Gram–Schmidt process, when applied to the columns of a matrix, produces a factorization of fundamental importance throughout numerical and theoretical linear algebra. The **QR decomposition** expresses any matrix with linearly independent columns as a product of an orthogonally-columned matrix $Q$ and an upper-triangular matrix$R$, packaging the entire Gram–Schmidt computation into a single matrix identity.

The decomposition is not merely a bookkeeping device. It transforms the problem of solving or approximating a linear system into one involving an isometry and a triangular system, each of which is as computationally benign as possible. The orthogonality of $Q$ ensures that left-multiplication by$Q^T$ (or$Q^*$ over$\mathbb{C}$) is a distance-preserving operation with condition number 1, while upper-triangular systems are solved by back-substitution in$O(n^2)$ operations. The combination makes QR the algorithm of choice for numerical least-squares and eigenvalue problems.

\begin{theorem}[QR Decomposition]
Let $A \in M_{m \times n}(\mathbb{F})$ have linearly independent columns, so$m \geq n$. There exists a unique factorization$A = QR$, where$Q \in M_{m \times n}(\mathbb{F})$ has orthonormal columns ($Q^*Q = I_n$) and$R \in M_{n \times n}(\mathbb{F})$ is upper triangular with strictly positive real diagonal entries.
\end{theorem}

**Construction via Gram–Schmidt.** Let $a_1, \ldots, a_n$ denote the columns of$A$. Apply Gram–Schmidt to produce orthonormal vectors$e_1, \ldots, e_n$ with$\mathrm{span}\{e_1, \ldots, e_k\} = \mathrm{span}\{a_1, \ldots, a_k\}$ for each$k$. The span preservation means that$a_k$ lies in$\mathrm{span}\{e_1, \ldots, e_k\}$, so there are unique coefficients$R_{jk}$ (with$R_{jk} = 0$ for$j > k$) such that
$$a_k = \sum_{j=1}^{k} R_{jk}\, e_j.$$
In fact $R_{jk} = \langle a_k, e_j \rangle$ for$j < k$ (the Gram–Schmidt inner products) and$R_{kk} = \|u_k\| > 0$ (the norm of the orthogonal residual). Setting$Q = [e_1 | \cdots | e_n]$ and$R = (R_{jk})$, the equation$A = QR$ holds columnwise. Since$R$ is upper triangular with positive diagonal, uniqueness follows from the factored polar decomposition for the positive definite matrix$A^*A = R^*Q^*QR = R^*R$.

**Full vs. reduced QR.** The **reduced** (thin) decomposition $A = QR$ has$Q \in M_{m \times n}$ with orthonormal columns. The **full** decomposition$A = \tilde{Q}\tilde{R}$ completes$Q$ to a unitary matrix$\tilde{Q} \in M_{m \times m}$ and pads$R$ with$m - n$ zero rows:$\tilde{R} = \begin{pmatrix} R \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$. The full form is useful for theoretical arguments (e.g., the proof of the SVD) while the thin form is preferred computationally.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Let $A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$.
\par
Apply Gram–Schmidt to $a_1 = (1,1,0)^T$ and$a_2 = (1,0,1)^T$.
\par
$e_1 = a_1/\|a_1\| = (1,1,0)^T/\sqrt{2}$.
\par
$\langle a_2, e_1 \rangle = (1 + 0 + 0)/\sqrt{2} = 1/\sqrt{2}$. Residual:$u_2 = (1,0,1)^T - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot \frac{(1,1,0)^T}{\sqrt{2}} = (1/2, -1/2, 1)^T$.$\|u_2\| = \sqrt{3/2}$,$e_2 = (1,-1,2)^T/\sqrt{6}$.
\par
The QR factorization is:
$$Q = \begin{pmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{6} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} & -1/\sqrt{6} \\ 0 & 2/\sqrt{6} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad R = \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 0 & \sqrt{3/2} \end{pmatrix}.$$
One verifies $QR = A$ and$Q^T Q = I_2$. The diagonal entries$R_{11} = \sqrt{2} > 0$ and$R_{22} = \sqrt{3/2} > 0$ confirm the normalization convention.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** The identity matrix $A = I_n$ has QR decomposition$Q = I_n$,$R = I_n$. More generally, any matrix with already-orthonormal columns satisfies$Q = A$ and$R = I_n$.
\end{example}

**Application: Least squares via QR.** Given an overdetermined system $Ax = b$ with$A \in M_{m \times n}$ ($m > n$) and$\mathrm{rank}(A) = n$, the least-squares solution minimizes$\|Ax - b\|^2$. The normal equations give$A^T A \hat{x} = A^T b$; substituting$A = QR$:
$$R^T Q^T Q R\hat{x} = R^T Q^T b \implies R^T R \hat{x} = R^T Q^T b \implies R\hat{x} = Q^T b$$
(using $Q^T Q = I_n$ and$R^T$ invertible). Thus the least-squares solution satisfies the upper-triangular system$R\hat{x} = Q^T b$, solved by back-substitution.

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Fit a line $y = \alpha + \beta x$ to data$(x_i, y_i)$:$(0,1)$,$(1,2)$,$(2,2)$. The overdetermined system is
$$A\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix} = b, \qquad A = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}, \quad b = \begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\2\end{pmatrix}.$$
Gram–Schmidt on columns of $A$:$a_1 = (1,1,1)^T$,$e_1 = (1,1,1)^T/\sqrt{3}$;$a_2 = (0,1,2)^T$,$\langle a_2, e_1\rangle = 3/\sqrt{3} = \sqrt{3}$,$u_2 = (0,1,2)^T - (1,1,1)^T = (-1,0,1)^T$,$e_2 = (-1,0,1)^T/\sqrt{2}$.
\par
Then $Q^T b = \begin{pmatrix} (1+2+2)/\sqrt{3} \\ (-1+0+2)/\sqrt{2}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}5/\sqrt{3} \\ 1/\sqrt{2}\end{pmatrix}$,$R = \begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{3} & \sqrt{3} \\ 0 & \sqrt{2}\end{pmatrix}$.
\par
Solving $R\hat{x} = Q^T b$: from the second row,$\sqrt{2}\,\hat{\beta} = 1/\sqrt{2}$, so$\hat{\beta} = 1/2$. From the first row,$\sqrt{3}\,\hat{\alpha} + \sqrt{3}\cdot(1/2) = 5/\sqrt{3}$, giving$\hat{\alpha} + 1/2 = 5/3$, so$\hat{\alpha} = 7/6$. The best-fit line is$y \approx 7/6 + x/2$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** The QR decomposition underlies the **QR algorithm** for eigenvalue computation. Starting from $A_0 = A$, one iterates: compute$A_k = Q_k R_k$ (QR factorization), then set$A_{k+1} = R_k Q_k$. Note that$A_{k+1} = R_k Q_k = Q_k^T A_k Q_k$, so each$A_k$ is unitarily similar to$A_0$ and has the same eigenvalues. Under mild conditions (no eigenvalues of equal modulus), the sequence$A_k$ converges to an upper-triangular (Schur) form, with eigenvalues appearing on the diagonal. With **shifts** and **deflation**, this iteration converges cubically for symmetric matrices and is the backbone of LAPACK's eigenvalue routines.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
The numerical stability advantage of QR over the normal equations is quantified by condition numbers. If $\kappa(A) = \|A\|\cdot\|A^+\|$ is the condition number of$A$ (where$A^+$ is the pseudoinverse), then the normal equations formulation has condition number$\kappa(A^T A) = \kappa(A)^2$. The QR approach yields a system with condition number$\kappa(R) = \kappa(A)$. For ill-conditioned problems (large$\kappa(A)$), squaring the condition number can make the normal equations approach unusable, while QR remains stable. This is why all production least-squares solvers use QR (or SVD for even greater robustness) rather than normal equations.
\end{remark}
