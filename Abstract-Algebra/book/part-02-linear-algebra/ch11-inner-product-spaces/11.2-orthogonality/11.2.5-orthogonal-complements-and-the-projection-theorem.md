# 11.2.5 Orthogonal Complements and the Projection Theorem

Given a subspace $W$ of an inner product space$V$, we can ask: what part of$V$ is "invisible" to$W$ in the sense of being orthogonal to every vector in it?

\begin{definition}
The **orthogonal complement** of $W$ is$W^\perp = \{v \in V \mid \langle v, w \rangle = 0 \text{ for all } w \in W\}$.
\end{definition}

That $W^\perp$ is a subspace follows from linearity: if$v_1, v_2 \perp W$, then$\langle c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2, w \rangle = c_1 \langle v_1, w \rangle + c_2 \langle v_2, w \rangle = 0$.

\begin{theorem}[Projection Theorem]
For a finite-dimensional subspace $W \subseteq V$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $V = W \oplus W^\perp$ (every vector decomposes uniquely as$v = w + w^\perp$ with$w \in W$,$w^\perp \in W^\perp$).
\item $\dim W + \dim W^\perp = \dim V$.
\item $(W^\perp)^\perp = W$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Choose an orthonormal basis $\{e_1, \ldots, e_k\}$ for$W$ and set$w = \sum_{i=1}^k \langle e_i, v \rangle e_i$ (the projection of$v$ onto$W$). Then$w^\perp = v - w$ satisfies$\langle w^\perp, e_j \rangle = \langle v, e_j \rangle - \langle v, e_j \rangle = 0$ for each$j$, so$w^\perp \in W^\perp$. Uniqueness: if$v = w + w^\perp = w' + (w')^\perp$, then$w - w' = (w')^\perp - w^\perp \in W \cap W^\perp$, and any$x \in W \cap W^\perp$ satisfies$\|x\|^2 = \langle x, x \rangle = 0$, so$x = 0$.
\end{proof}

The component $w = \mathrm{proj}_W v$ is the **orthogonal projection** of$v$ onto$W$, and it has a fundamental geometric property: it is the unique closest point in$W$ to$v$.

\begin{theorem}[Best Approximation]
$\mathrm{proj}_W v$ is the unique element of$W$ minimizing$\|v - u\|$ over all$u \in W$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For any $u \in W$:$\|v - u\|^2 = \|(v - w) + (w - u)\|^2 = \|v - w\|^2 + \|w - u\|^2$ (since$v - w \in W^\perp$ and$w - u \in W$ are orthogonal). This is minimized uniquely at$u = w$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{R}^3$, let$W = \mathrm{span}\{(1,0,0), (0,1,0)\} =$ the$xy$-plane. Then$W^\perp = \mathrm{span}\{(0,0,1)\} =$ the$z$-axis. For$v = (2, 3, 5)$:$\mathrm{proj}_W v = (2,3,0)$ and$v^\perp = (0,0,5)$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** In $C([-1,1])$ with the$L^2$ inner product, let$W = \{$constant functions$\}$. The projection of$f(x) = x^2$ onto$W$ is the constant$\langle 1, x^2 \rangle / \|1\|^2 = \frac{\int_{-1}^1 x^2\, dx}{\int_{-1}^1 1\, dx} = \frac{2/3}{2} = 1/3$. The "residual"$x^2 - 1/3$ is orthogonal to all constants.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** The projection formula underlies least-squares fitting. Given $Ax = b$ with$A \in M_{m \times n}$, the least-squares solution$\hat{x}$ satisfies$A\hat{x} = \mathrm{proj}_{\mathrm{Col}(A)} b$: the projection of$b$ onto the column space of$A$. The residual$b - A\hat{x}$ lies in$(\mathrm{Col}(A))^\perp = \ker(A^T)$, giving the normal equations$A^T(b - A\hat{x}) = 0$, i.e.,$A^T A \hat{x} = A^T b$.
\end{example}
\par
\begin{remark}
The orthogonal projection operator $P_W : V \to V$ defined by$P_W(v) = \mathrm{proj}_W v$ satisfies$P_W^2 = P_W$ (idempotent: projecting twice is the same as once) and$P_W^* = P_W$ (self-adjoint). Conversely, any operator satisfying$P^2 = P = P^*$ is an orthogonal projection onto its image. This characterization of orthogonal projections in terms of the adjoint connects the projection theorem directly to the spectral theory of self-adjoint operators developed in Section 11.4.
\end{remark}
