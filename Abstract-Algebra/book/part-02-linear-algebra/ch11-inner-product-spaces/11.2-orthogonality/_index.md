# 11.2 Orthogonality

Two vectors are orthogonal if their inner product is zero: $\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle = 0$. This simple condition is enormously productive. An orthogonal set is automatically linearly independent (a one-line proof from the inner product axioms), and an orthonormal basis — an orthogonal basis in which every vector has unit norm — gives coordinates by inner products alone: the coefficient of $e_i$ in the expansion of $\mathbf{v}$ is just $\langle \mathbf{v}, e_i \rangle$. There is no matrix to invert, no system to solve. This is why orthonormal bases are so much more computationally convenient than arbitrary bases, and why the first task of this section is to show that every finite-dimensional inner product space possesses one.

The Gram–Schmidt process constructs an orthonormal basis from any given basis $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_n\}$ by a sequential procedure: take $\mathbf{v}_1$ and normalize it to get $e_1$; subtract from $\mathbf{v}_2$ its projection onto $e_1$ and normalize the remainder to get $e_2$; continue, at each step subtracting all projections onto the previously constructed $e_i$ and normalizing. The formula for the projection of $\mathbf{v}$ onto a unit vector $e$ is $\text{proj}_e(\mathbf{v}) = \langle \mathbf{v}, e \rangle e$, and the residual $\mathbf{v} - \text{proj}_e(\mathbf{v})$ is the component of $\mathbf{v}$ orthogonal to $e$. The process terminates in $n$ steps with an orthonormal basis that spans the same subspaces as the original basis at each stage. Written in matrix terms, Gram–Schmidt on the columns of a matrix $A$ produces the $QR$ decomposition $A = QR$, where $Q$ is an orthogonal matrix and $R$ is upper triangular.

The orthogonal complement $W^\perp$ of a subspace $W$ is the set of all vectors orthogonal to every element of $W$. It is itself a subspace, and the projection theorem — one of the key structural results of this section — states that $V = W \oplus W^\perp$: every vector $\mathbf{v}$ decomposes uniquely as $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{w} + \mathbf{w}^\perp$ where $\mathbf{w} \in W$ and $\mathbf{w}^\perp \in W^\perp$. The vector $\mathbf{w}$ is the orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{v}$ onto $W$; it is the unique element of $W$ closest to $\mathbf{v}$ in the metric induced by the inner product. This minimum-distance characterization is the geometric core of least-squares approximation: when a linear system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has no solution, the best approximate solution minimizes $\|A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}\|$, and this minimum is achieved at the orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{b}$ onto the column space of $A$.
