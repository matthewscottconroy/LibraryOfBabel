# 11.3.1 The Adjoint $T^*$: Definition via the Inner Product

When studying linear maps between inner product spaces, the inner product structure gives rise to a new operation on maps that has no analogue in the purely linear-algebraic setting: the adjoint.

\begin{definition}
For a linear map $T: V \to W$ between inner product spaces, the **adjoint**$T^*: W \to V$ is the unique map characterized by
$$\langle Tv, w \rangle_{W} = \langle v, T^*w \rangle_{V} \quad \text{for all } v \in V,\, w \in W.$$
\end{definition}

This single equation defines $T^*$ entirely: it says that$T^*$ is the map that "moves$T$ from one side of the inner product to the other."

**Existence and uniqueness.** For each fixed $w \in W$, the map$v \mapsto \langle Tv, w \rangle_W$ is a linear functional on$V$. By the **Riesz Representation Theorem**: every linear functional on a finite-dimensional inner product space$V$ is of the form$v \mapsto \langle v, u \rangle_V$ for a unique vector$u \in V$. That vector is$T^*w$. Since$w$ was arbitrary, this defines a function$T^*: W \to V$, and linearity of$T^*$ follows from the linearity of the inner product.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** For $T: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ with matrix$A$ (in standard bases), the adjoint$T^*$ is represented by$A^T$:$\langle Au, v \rangle = (Au)^T v = u^T A^T v = \langle u, A^T v \rangle$. So$(T^*)$ has matrix$A^T$ — the ordinary transpose over$\mathbb{R}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** For $T: \mathbb{C}^n \to \mathbb{C}^m$ with matrix$A$ (in standard orthonormal bases), the adjoint$T^*$ has matrix$A^* = \overline{A}^T$ (conjugate transpose):$\langle Au, v \rangle = (Au)^* v = u^* A^* v = \langle u, A^* v \rangle$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** For the derivative operator $D: P_n(\mathbb{R}) \to P_{n-1}(\mathbb{R})$ defined by$Df = f'$, with$L^2([-1,1])$ inner products: the adjoint$D^*$ satisfies$\langle f', g \rangle = \langle f, D^*g \rangle$. Integration by parts gives$\langle f', g \rangle = \int_{-1}^1 f'g\, dx = [fg]_{-1}^1 - \int_{-1}^1 fg'\, dx$. So for polynomials vanishing at the boundary,$D^* = -D$ (the negative derivative): the derivative is skew-adjoint on this function space.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** For the multiplication operator $(M_f g)(x) = f(x)g(x)$ on$L^2([0,1])$ for a fixed real function$f$:$\langle M_f g, h \rangle = \int f(x)g(x)h(x)\, dx = \langle g, M_f h \rangle$. So$M_f^* = M_f$: multiplication by a real function is self-adjoint.
\end{example}
\par
Notice that the definition is intrinsic: it makes no reference to bases or matrices. This is the right way to think about the adjoint. The concrete matrix representation — that $[T^*] = [T]^*$ (conjugate transpose) in orthonormal bases — is a consequence of the definition, not the definition itself. The abstract characterization via the pairing equation is what allows us to reason about adjoints in infinite-dimensional spaces where matrices are not available.
\par
\begin{remark}
Basic properties: $(T^*)^* = T$;$(ST)^* = T^*S^*$ (order reverses);$(\alpha T)^* = \overline{\alpha} T^*$;$(S + T)^* = S^* + T^*$. These follow directly from the defining equation. The adjoint connects the inner product to the algebraic structure of$\mathrm{Hom}(V,W)$, and it is the foundation for the operator taxonomy (self-adjoint, unitary, normal) in Section 11.3.3 and the spectral theorem in Section 11.4.
\end{remark}
