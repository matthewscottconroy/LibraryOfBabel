# 11.3.2 The Matrix of the Adjoint: Conjugate Transpose

The abstract definition of the adjoint becomes concrete when we choose orthonormal bases. Suppose $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$ is an orthonormal basis of$V$ and$\{f_1, \ldots, f_m\}$ is an orthonormal basis of$W$, and let$A = [T]$ be the matrix of$T: V \to W$ in these bases, with entries$A_{ij} = \langle f_i, T e_j \rangle_W$. Then the matrix of$T^*: W \to V$ in the dual bases satisfies
$$(A^*)_{ji} = \langle e_j, T^* f_i \rangle_V = \langle T e_j, f_i \rangle_W = \overline{\langle f_i, T e_j \rangle_W} = \overline{A_{ij}}.$$
In other words, $[T^*] = \overline{A}^T$, the **conjugate transpose** of$A$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Let $T: \mathbb{C}^2 \to \mathbb{C}^2$ have matrix$A = \begin{pmatrix}1+i & 2 \\ 0 & 3-i\end{pmatrix}$ in the standard orthonormal basis. Then$T^*$ has matrix$A^* = \overline{A}^T = \begin{pmatrix}1-i & 0 \\ 2 & 3+i\end{pmatrix}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** For $A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4\end{pmatrix} \in M_2(\mathbb{R})$:$A^T = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 3 \\ 2 & 4\end{pmatrix}$ (over$\mathbb{R}$, conjugate transpose equals ordinary transpose).
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** The matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & i \\ i & -1\end{pmatrix}$ over$\mathbb{C}$:$A^* = \begin{pmatrix}1 & -i \\ -i & -1\end{pmatrix}$. Check:$A \neq A^*$, so$T$ is not self-adjoint.
\end{example}

Over $\mathbb{R}$, where conjugation is trivial, the matrix of the adjoint is simply the ordinary transpose$A^T$. This explains why the transpose is so ubiquitous in real linear algebra: it is the concrete avatar of the adjoint operation whenever orthonormal bases are in use.

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** Verify the adjoint property $\langle Au, v \rangle = \langle u, A^*v \rangle$ for$A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 2+i \\ 0 & 3\end{pmatrix}$,$u = (1, i)^T$,$v = (1, 0)^T$.
\par
$Au = (1 + i(2+i), 3i)^T = (1 + 2i - 1, 3i)^T = (2i, 3i)^T$.
$\langle Au, v \rangle = \overline{2i} \cdot 1 + \overline{3i} \cdot 0 = -2i$.
\par
$A^* = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 2-i & 3\end{pmatrix}$,$A^*v = (1, 2-i)^T$.
$\langle u, A^*v \rangle = \overline{1} \cdot 1 + \overline{i} \cdot (2-i) = 1 + (-i)(2-i) = 1 + (-2i + i^2) = 1 - 2i - 1 = -2i$. Consistent.
\end{example}
\par
The key point is not merely that $[T^*] = A^*$ as a formula, but why this is the correct generalization of the transpose. The ordinary transpose$A^T$ satisfies$\langle Au, v \rangle_{\mathrm{standard}} = \langle u, A^T v \rangle_{\mathrm{standard}}$ for the Euclidean inner product on$\mathbb{R}^n$ — it is the adjoint with respect to the standard inner product. When we change to a non-orthonormal basis or to a different inner product (say,$\langle u, v \rangle_G = u^T G v$ for a positive definite matrix$G$), the formula for the adjoint's matrix changes to$A^{\#} = G^{-1} A^T G$. The conjugate transpose formula is special to orthonormal bases.
\par
\begin{remark}
This perspective clarifies many formulas: whenever the conjugate transpose appears in a matrix equation, it is a sign that an inner product is lurking nearby. For instance, the normal equations $A^T A x = A^T b$ arise from projecting$b$ onto the column space of$A$ via the standard inner product on$\mathbb{R}^m$ — the$A^T$ is the adjoint of$A$ moving the inner product from the$\mathbb{R}^m$ side to the$\mathbb{R}^n$ side.
\end{remark}
