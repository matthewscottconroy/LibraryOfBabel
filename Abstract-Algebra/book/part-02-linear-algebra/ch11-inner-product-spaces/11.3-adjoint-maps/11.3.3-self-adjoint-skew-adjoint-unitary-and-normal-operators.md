# 11.3.3 Self-Adjoint, Skew-Adjoint, Unitary, and Normal Operators

With the adjoint in hand, we can classify linear operators $T: V \to V$ by their relationship to$T^*$. This classification has profound spectral consequences: it determines exactly which operators can be diagonalized by orthonormal bases.

\begin{definition}
An operator $T: V \to V$ is:
- **Self-adjoint** (or **Hermitian** over $\mathbb{C}$, **symmetric** over$\mathbb{R}$) if$T^* = T$, equivalently$\langle Tu, v \rangle = \langle u, Tv \rangle$ for all$u, v$.
- **Skew-adjoint** (or **skew-Hermitian**) if $T^* = -T$.
- **Unitary** (over $\mathbb{C}$) or **orthogonal** (over$\mathbb{R}$) if$T^*T = TT^* = I$, i.e.,$T^* = T^{-1}$.
- **Normal** if $T^*T = TT^*$.
\end{definition}

In matrix form (in an orthonormal basis): self-adjoint means $A = A^*$; skew-adjoint means$A = -A^*$; unitary/orthogonal means$A^*A = I$; normal means$A^*A = AA^*$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Real symmetric matrices ($A = A^T$) are self-adjoint over$\mathbb{R}$. Complex Hermitian matrices ($A = \overline{A}^T$) are self-adjoint over$\mathbb{C}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Rotation matrices $Q = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\\sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$ satisfy$Q^T Q = I$: orthogonal (real unitary). They are also normal.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** The matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -1\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$ over$\mathbb{R}$ is skew-symmetric ($A^T = -A$). Its eigenvalues over$\mathbb{C}$ are$\pm i$ (purely imaginary), consistent with the general fact that skew-adjoint operators have purely imaginary eigenvalues.
\end{example}

**Eigenvalues of self-adjoint operators are real.** If $Tv = \lambda v$ with$v \neq 0$:
$$\lambda \|v\|^2 = \langle Tv, v \rangle = \langle v, Tv \rangle = \overline{\lambda}\|v\|^2,$$
so $\lambda = \overline{\lambda}$, hence$\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$.

**Eigenspaces for distinct eigenvalues of a self-adjoint operator are orthogonal.** If $Tu = \lambda u$ and$Tv = \mu v$ with$\lambda \neq \mu$:
$$\lambda \langle u, v \rangle = \langle Tu, v \rangle = \langle u, Tv \rangle = \overline{\mu} \langle u, v \rangle = \mu \langle u, v \rangle$$
(using $\mu \in \mathbb{R}$). So$(\lambda - \mu)\langle u, v \rangle = 0$, forcing$\langle u, v \rangle = 0$.

**Unitary operators preserve the inner product:** $\langle Tu, Tv \rangle = \langle u, T^*Tv \rangle = \langle u, v \rangle$. Their eigenvalues all have modulus 1.

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** The $2 \times 2$ unitary matrix$U = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1 & i \\ i & 1\end{pmatrix}$:$U^*U = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1 & -i \\ -i & 1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1 & i \\ i & 1\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}2 & 0\\ 0 & 2\end{pmatrix} = I$. Its eigenvalues are$e^{\pm i\pi/4} = (\pm 1 \pm i)/\sqrt{2}$, both of modulus 1.
\end{example}

The largest natural class is that of **normal operators**, defined by $T^*T = TT^*$. Every self-adjoint, skew-adjoint, and unitary operator is normal. The importance of normality is precisely that it is the condition under which the spectral theorem holds: a complex operator is diagonalizable by a unitary basis if and only if it is normal. This is the content of Section 11.4.

\begin{remark}
Any operator decomposes uniquely as $T = T_s + T_a$ where$T_s = \frac{1}{2}(T + T^*)$ is self-adjoint and$T_a = \frac{1}{2}(T - T^*)$ is skew-adjoint. This is the operator analogue of the decomposition of a complex number into real and imaginary parts. The operator$T$ is normal iff$T_s$ and$T_a$ commute:$[T_s, T_a] = 0$.
\end{remark}
