# 11.3 Adjoint Maps

The inner product structure on $V$ allows us to associate to every linear map $T : V \to V$ a companion map called the adjoint. The adjoint $T^* : V \to V$ is defined by the identity $\langle T\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle = \langle \mathbf{u}, T^*\mathbf{v} \rangle$ for all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in V$. The existence and uniqueness of $T^*$ follow from the Riesz representation theorem: for each fixed $\mathbf{v}$, the map $\mathbf{u} \mapsto \langle T\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle$ is a linear functional on $V$, and in a finite-dimensional inner product space every linear functional is of the form $\langle \cdot, \mathbf{w} \rangle$ for a unique $\mathbf{w}$; this $\mathbf{w}$ is $T^*\mathbf{v}$. In an orthonormal basis, the matrix of $T^*$ is the conjugate transpose of the matrix of $T$: if $T$ has matrix $A$, then $T^*$ has matrix $\bar{A}^T = A^*$. Over $\mathbb{R}$, the adjoint corresponds simply to the transpose.

The adjoint gives rise to a fundamental taxonomy of operators. A self-adjoint (or Hermitian) operator satisfies $T^* = T$; over $\mathbb{R}$ these are the symmetric operators, represented by symmetric matrices. A skew-adjoint operator satisfies $T^* = -T$. A unitary operator (over $\mathbb{C}$) or orthogonal operator (over $\mathbb{R}$) satisfies $T^*T = TT^* = I$, meaning $T$ preserves the inner product and therefore all lengths and angles. The class that turns out to be most important for the spectral theorem is the class of normal operators: those satisfying $TT^* = T^*T$. Normality is a commutativity condition between $T$ and its adjoint, and it is equivalent to saying that $T$ and $T^*$ can be simultaneously diagonalized in an orthonormal basis. Every self-adjoint, skew-adjoint, and unitary operator is normal, so normality is the right hypothesis for the most general form of the spectral theorem.

Several properties of the adjoint are immediate from the definition and are frequently used: $(ST)^* = T^*S^*$, $(T^*)^* = T$, $\ker T^* = (\text{im}\, T)^\perp$, and $\text{im}\, T^* = (\ker T)^\perp$. This last pair, called the fundamental theorem of linear algebra in some presentations, gives a complete orthogonal decomposition of $V$ into the image of $T$ and the kernel of $T^*$ (and their complements). The relationship between $T$ and $T^*$ is entirely symmetric in this decomposition, which is why self-adjointness — the coincidence of $T$ and $T^*$ — is such a strong and useful condition.
