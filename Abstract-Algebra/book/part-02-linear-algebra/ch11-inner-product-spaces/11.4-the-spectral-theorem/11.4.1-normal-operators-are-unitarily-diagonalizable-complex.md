# 11.4.1 Normal Operators Are Unitarily Diagonalizable (Complex)

The spectral theorem is the central result of this chapter. Its complex version asserts that the class of operators for which unitary diagonalization is possible is exactly the class of normal operators.

\begin{theorem}[Complex Spectral Theorem]
A linear operator $T$ on a finite-dimensional complex inner product space$V$ is normal ($T^*T = TT^*$) if and only if there exists an orthonormal basis of$V$ consisting entirely of eigenvectors of$T$.
\end{theorem}

Over $\mathbb{C}$, every operator has at least one eigenvalue (by the fundamental theorem of algebra). The content of the spectral theorem is not that eigenvalues exist, but that the eigenvectors can be chosen to be mutually orthogonal and to span the whole space. For a general operator, this fails: a nilpotent operator$N$ with$N^2 = 0$ but$N \neq 0$ is not diagonalizable at all. Normality prevents this pathology.

**The diagonal entries** of $T$ in its orthonormal eigenbasis are its eigenvalues. For a self-adjoint operator these are real; for a unitary operator they lie on the unit circle; for a general normal operator they may be arbitrary complex numbers.

**One direction** (easy): If $T$ has an orthonormal eigenbasis$\{e_i\}$ with eigenvalues$\{\lambda_i\}$, then in that basis$T$ is represented by$D = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n)$ and$T^*$ by$\overline{D} = \mathrm{diag}(\overline{\lambda_1}, \ldots, \overline{\lambda_n})$. Diagonal matrices always commute, so$T^*T = TT^*$.

**The other direction** (harder): Suppose $T^*T = TT^*$. The key lemma is:

\begin{lemma}
If $T$ is normal and$Tv = \lambda v$, then$T^*v = \overline{\lambda}v$. In particular,$\ker(T - \lambda I) = \ker(T^* - \overline{\lambda}I)$, and eigenspaces of$T$ for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
From normality, $T - \lambda I$ is also normal (check:$(T - \lambda I)^*(T - \lambda I) = (T^* - \overline{\lambda}I)(T - \lambda I)$, and similarly the other order, and they agree by$T^*T = TT^*$). A normal operator$S$ satisfies$\|Sv\| = \|S^*v\|$ for all$v$ (since$\|Sv\|^2 = \langle S^*Sv, v \rangle = \langle SS^*v, v \rangle = \|S^*v\|^2$). Applying to$S = T - \lambda I$:$\|(T-\lambda I)v\| = 0$ implies$\|(T^* - \overline{\lambda}I)v\| = 0$.
\end{proof}

With this lemma, distinct eigenspaces are orthogonal: if $Tu = \lambda u$,$Tv = \mu v$,$\lambda \neq \mu$, then$\langle u, v \rangle = \langle u, \overline{\mu}^{-1}T^*v \rangle = \overline{\mu}^{-1}\langle Tu, v \rangle = \lambda \overline{\mu}^{-1} \langle u, v \rangle$... more directly, from the lemma$T^*v = \overline{\mu}v$, so$\lambda\langle u, v\rangle = \langle Tu, v\rangle = \langle u, T^*v\rangle = \mu\langle u,v\rangle$, giving$(\lambda - \mu)\langle u,v\rangle = 0$.

The proof now proceeds by induction on $\dim V$: take any eigenvalue$\lambda$ of$T$ (it exists over$\mathbb{C}$), choose an orthonormal basis for$E_{\lambda}$, and restrict$T$ to$E_{\lambda}^\perp$ (which is$T$-invariant since$T$ is normal and eigenvectors of$T^*$ for eigenvalue$\overline{\lambda}$ lie in$E_{\lambda}$).

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** The self-adjoint matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix}3 & 1 \\ 1 & 3\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues 2 and 4 with eigenvectors$(1,-1)/\sqrt{2}$ and$(1,1)/\sqrt{2}$. These are orthonormal. The spectral decomposition is$A = 2 \cdot \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}(1,-1) + 4 \cdot \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}(1,1)$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** The normal matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & i \\ -i & 1\end{pmatrix}$ over$\mathbb{C}$:$A^* = \begin{pmatrix}1 & i \\ -i & 1\end{pmatrix} = A$, so$A$ is self-adjoint. Eigenvalues:$\lambda = 0$ with eigenvector$(1, i)/\sqrt{2}$ and$\lambda = 2$ with eigenvector$(1, -i)/\sqrt{2}$. These are orthonormal, as predicted by the spectral theorem.
\end{example}

The spectral decomposition $T = \sum_i \lambda_i P_i$ (where$P_i$ is the orthogonal projection onto the$i$-th eigenspace) is the **spectral resolution** of$T$. Functions of$T$ are computed by$f(T) = \sum_i f(\lambda_i) P_i$ — an enormously powerful tool for computing matrix functions.
