# 11.4.2 Self-Adjoint Operators Are Orthogonally Diagonalizable (Real)

The real spectral theorem is in some ways more remarkable than its complex counterpart, because over $\mathbb{R}$ there is no guarantee that eigenvalues exist at all: a rotation matrix in$\mathbb{R}^2$ has no real eigenvalues. The theorem asserts that self-adjoint operators are the exception — they always have real eigenvalues and are orthogonally diagonalizable.

\begin{theorem}[Real Spectral Theorem]
If $T: V \to V$ is a self-adjoint operator on a finite-dimensional real inner product space, then all eigenvalues of$T$ are real and there exists an orthonormal basis of$V$ consisting of eigenvectors of$T$. Equivalently, every real symmetric matrix$A = A^T$ satisfies$A = Q D Q^T$ with$Q^T Q = I$ and$D$ real diagonal.
\end{theorem}

**Reality of eigenvalues.** Even though we work over $\mathbb{R}$, one shows the eigenvalues are real by temporarily allowing complex scalars: if$Tv = \lambda v$ for a complex eigenvalue$\lambda$ and complex vector$v \neq 0$, then$\lambda \|v\|^2 = \langle Tv, v \rangle = \langle v, T^*v \rangle = \langle v, Tv \rangle = \overline{\lambda}\|v\|^2$, forcing$\lambda = \overline{\lambda} \in \mathbb{R}$.

**Orthogonality of distinct eigenspaces.** For real $\lambda \neq \mu$ and$Tu = \lambda u$,$Tv = \mu v$:
$$\lambda \langle u, v \rangle = \langle Tu, v \rangle = \langle u, Tv \rangle = \mu \langle u, v \rangle$$
(using $T = T^*$). So$(\lambda - \mu)\langle u, v \rangle = 0$, giving$\langle u, v \rangle = 0$.

**Inductive proof of diagonalizability.** A key step: to find a real eigenvector. Consider $p_T(x) \in \mathbb{R}[x]$; it has at least one complex root$\lambda_0$. But$\lambda_0 \in \mathbb{R}$ (by reality of eigenvalues), so a real eigenvector$v \in V$ exists. The subspace$W = E_{\lambda_0}^\perp$ is$T$-invariant: if$\langle w, v \rangle = 0$ for all$v \in E_{\lambda_0}$, then$\langle Tw, v \rangle = \langle w, Tv \rangle = \lambda_0 \langle w, v \rangle = 0$. Restrict$T$ to$W$; it remains self-adjoint. Apply induction on$\dim V$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Let $A = \begin{pmatrix}5 & 4 \\ 4 & 5\end{pmatrix}$. Eigenvalues:$p_A(\lambda) = (\lambda-5)^2 - 16 = (\lambda-1)(\lambda-9)$, so$\lambda_1 = 1$,$\lambda_2 = 9$. Eigenvectors:$v_1 = (1,-1)/\sqrt{2}$ and$v_2 = (1,1)/\sqrt{2}$. Orthogonal decomposition:$A = 1 \cdot P_1 + 9 \cdot P_2$ where$P_1 = v_1 v_1^T$ and$P_2 = v_2 v_2^T$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Let $A = \begin{pmatrix}2&1&1\\1&2&1\\1&1&2\end{pmatrix}$. Characteristic polynomial:$p_A(\lambda) = (\lambda-1)^2(\lambda-4)$. Eigenvalue$\lambda = 1$: eigenspace$E_1 = \ker(A-I)$ has basis$\{(-1,1,0), (-1,0,1)\}$, Gram–Schmidt gives orthonormal basis$\{(-1,1,0)/\sqrt{2}, (-1,-1,2)/\sqrt{6}\}$. Eigenvalue$\lambda = 4$: eigenvector$(1,1,1)/\sqrt{3}$. Assembled, these form an orthogonal diagonalization$A = Q\mathrm{diag}(1,1,4)Q^T$.
\end{example}
\par
The real spectral theorem has enormous practical reach.
\par
\begin{remark}
**Applications:** (1) **Quadratic forms** (Section 11.6): classifying $Q(x) = x^T A x$ reduces to diagonalizing the symmetric matrix$A$. (2) **Principal curvatures** in differential geometry: the shape operator of a surface is self-adjoint, so its eigenvalues (principal curvatures) are real. (3) **Moments of inertia** in mechanics: the inertia tensor is symmetric, so the principal axes (eigenvectors) form an orthogonal frame. (4) The singular value decomposition (Section 11.5): the matrices$A^T A$ and$AA^T$ are symmetric positive semidefinite, and the real spectral theorem applied to them yields the SVD.
\end{remark}
