# 11.4.3 Positive Semidefinite Operators and Square Roots

Among self-adjoint operators, the positive semidefinite ones form a particularly important subclass with rich structure.

\begin{definition}
An operator $T: V \to V$ is **positive semidefinite** (PSD) if$\langle Tv, v \rangle \geq 0$ for all$v \in V$, and **positive definite** (PD) if$\langle Tv, v \rangle > 0$ for all$v \neq 0$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
By the spectral theorem, $T$ (self-adjoint) is PSD if and only if all eigenvalues$\lambda_i \geq 0$, and PD if and only if all$\lambda_i > 0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If $\lambda_i \geq 0$ and$v = \sum_i c_i e_i$ in the eigenbasis, then$\langle Tv, v \rangle = \sum_i \lambda_i |c_i|^2 \geq 0$. Conversely,$\langle Te_i, e_i \rangle = \lambda_i$, so if some$\lambda_i < 0$ then$T$ is not PSD.
\end{proof}

PSD operators arise naturally wherever inner products do.

\begin{example}
**Example 1 (Gram matrix).** For any set of vectors $v_1, \ldots, v_k \in V$, the Gram matrix$G_{ij} = \langle v_i, v_j \rangle$ is PSD:$\sum_{i,j} \overline{c_i} G_{ij} c_j = \langle \sum_i c_i v_i, \sum_j c_j v_j \rangle = \|\sum_i c_i v_i\|^2 \geq 0$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2 ($T^*T$ is PSD).** For any$T$:$\langle T^*T v, v \rangle = \langle Tv, Tv \rangle = \|Tv\|^2 \geq 0$. This is the starting point for the SVD.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** The covariance matrix $\Sigma = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(x_i - \bar{x})^T$ of a dataset is PSD: it is a sum of outer products of the form$vv^T$, each of which is PSD, and a sum of PSD matrices is PSD.
\end{example}

**PSD square roots.** The spectral theorem allows us to define functions of a PSD operator:

\begin{theorem}
Every PSD operator $T$ has a unique PSD square root$T^{1/2}$ satisfying$(T^{1/2})^2 = T$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $T = \sum_i \lambda_i P_i$ be the spectral decomposition with$\lambda_i \geq 0$. Define$T^{1/2} = \sum_i \sqrt{\lambda_i} P_i$. Then$(T^{1/2})^2 = \sum_i \lambda_i P_i = T$ (using$P_i P_j = \delta_{ij} P_i$). The operator$T^{1/2}$ is self-adjoint (eigenvalues$\sqrt{\lambda_i} \in \mathbb{R}$) and PSD (eigenvalues$\geq 0$). Uniqueness: if$S$ is any PSD square root with$S^2 = T$, then$S$ commutes with$T$ (since$ST = S \cdot S^2 = S^3 = S^2 \cdot S = TS$), so$S$ preserves each eigenspace of$T$. Within eigenspace$E_{\lambda_i}$,$S^2 = \lambda_i I$, and the unique PSD square root of$\lambda_i I$ on that space is$\sqrt{\lambda_i} I$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** For $A = \begin{pmatrix}5 & 4 \\ 4 & 5\end{pmatrix}$ with eigenvalues$1, 9$ and orthonormal eigenvectors$v_1 = (1,-1)/\sqrt{2}$,$v_2 = (1,1)/\sqrt{2}$:
$$A^{1/2} = 1 \cdot v_1 v_1^T + 3 \cdot v_2 v_2^T = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix} + \frac{3}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}.$$
Verify: $A^{1/2} \cdot A^{1/2} = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}^2 = \begin{pmatrix}5&4\\4&5\end{pmatrix} = A$. Correct.
\end{example}
\par
\begin{remark}
The PSD square root enables the **polar decomposition**: any invertible operator $T$ decomposes as$T = UP$ where$U$ is unitary and$P = (T^*T)^{1/2}$ is PSD. This is the operator analogue of$z = |z| e^{i\theta}$ for complex numbers:$P = |T|$ is the "absolute value" and$U = TP^{-1}$ is the "phase." For non-invertible$T$ one uses the pseudoinverse. The polar decomposition is used in continuum mechanics (decomposing deformation into rotation and stretch) and in quantum information (relating two density matrices).
\end{remark}
