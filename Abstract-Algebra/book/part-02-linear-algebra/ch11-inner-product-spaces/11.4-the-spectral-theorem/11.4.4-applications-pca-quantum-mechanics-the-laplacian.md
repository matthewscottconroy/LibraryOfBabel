# 11.4.4 Applications: PCA, Quantum Mechanics, the Laplacian

The spectral theorem is not merely an elegant structural result — it is one of the most applied theorems in all of mathematics. Three domains in particular illustrate its reach.

**Principal Component Analysis.** In statistics and data analysis, PCA is the spectral theorem in action. Given a dataset of vectors $x_1, \ldots, x_N \in \mathbb{R}^n$ (centered so$\sum x_i = 0$), form the **sample covariance matrix**$\Sigma = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N x_i x_i^T \in M_n(\mathbb{R})$. This matrix is real symmetric and positive semidefinite.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** For data points $(1,0)$,$(-1,0)$,$(0,1)$,$(0,-1)$ in$\mathbb{R}^2$:
$$\Sigma = \frac{1}{4}\left[\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}(1,0) + \begin{pmatrix}-1\\0\end{pmatrix}(-1,0) + \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}(0,1) + \begin{pmatrix}0\\-1\end{pmatrix}(0,-1)\right] = \frac{1}{4}\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{2}I.$$
The two principal components have equal variance $1/2$: no preferred direction.
\end{example}

The spectral theorem diagonalizes $\Sigma = Q D Q^T$. The eigenvectors (columns of$Q$) are the **principal components** — the directions of maximum variance in the data. The corresponding eigenvalues measure the variance in each direction. Projecting the data onto the first$k$ eigenvectors (those with largest eigenvalues) gives the best rank-$k$ approximation in the least-squares sense (the Eckart–Young theorem, Section 11.5.4).

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** For data with covariance $\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}4&2\\2&1\end{pmatrix}$, eigenvalues are$5$ and$0$. The first principal component (eigenvalue 5, eigenvector$(2,1)/\sqrt{5}$) captures all variance; the data lies in a one-dimensional subspace.
\end{example}

**Quantum Mechanics.** In quantum mechanics, physical observables — energy, momentum, spin — are represented by **self-adjoint operators** on a Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$. The spectral theorem is built into the foundations:

\begin{itemize}
\item The **eigenvalues** of the observable are the possible measurement outcomes (necessarily real, since the operator is self-adjoint).
\item The **eigenvectors** (or eigenspaces) are the states in which the observable has a definite value.
\item The **spectral decomposition** $A = \sum_i \lambda_i P_i$ gives the probability of observing$\lambda_i$ as$\|P_i \psi\|^2$ when the system is in state$\psi$ (normalized to$\|\psi\| = 1$).
\end{itemize}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** For a spin-$\frac{1}{2}$ particle, the spin operator in the$z$-direction is$S_z = \frac{\hbar}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$ (a Pauli matrix). Eigenvalues$\pm\hbar/2$; eigenvectors$e_1$ ("spin up") and$e_2$ ("spin down"). A state$\psi = c_1 e_1 + c_2 e_2$ has probability$|c_1|^2$ of measuring$+\hbar/2$ and$|c_2|^2$ of measuring$-\hbar/2$.
\end{example}
\par
**The Laplacian.** In analysis, the Laplacian $\Delta = \sum_i \partial^2/\partial x_i^2$ is a self-adjoint operator on$L^2(M)$ for a Riemannian manifold$M$ with appropriate boundary conditions. Its eigenfunctions$\Delta f = \lambda f$ are:
\par
\begin{itemize}
\item On $[0, \pi]$ with Dirichlet BC:$\sin(nx)$ with$\lambda = -n^2$.
\item On the circle $S^1$:$e^{in\theta}$ with$\lambda = -n^2$ — the foundation of Fourier series.
\item On the sphere $S^2$: spherical harmonics$Y_\ell^m$ — the basis for atomic orbital theory.
\end{itemize}
\par
The spectral theorem (in its infinite-dimensional form) guarantees these eigenfunctions form an orthonormal basis of $L^2(M)$, which is precisely the mathematical content of Fourier analysis and its generalizations.
\par
\begin{remark}
The unifying theme is that whenever a physical or geometric problem has a natural self-adjoint operator, the spectral theorem produces a canonical decomposition of the relevant function space into orthogonal eigenspaces, each representing a "mode" or "frequency" of the system. The eigenvalues measure the "energy" or "frequency" of each mode, and the expansion in the eigenbasis is the appropriate "frequency decomposition" of the problem.
\end{remark}
