# 11.4 The Spectral Theorem

The spectral theorem is the deepest result of this chapter and one of the central theorems in all of linear algebra. In its real form it states: every self-adjoint operator $T$ on a finite-dimensional real inner product space has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors with real eigenvalues. In its complex form it states: every normal operator $T$ on a finite-dimensional complex inner product space has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. In both cases, the key word is "orthonormal": not merely does an eigenbasis exist (which Chapter 9 addressed), but the eigenvectors can be chosen to be mutually orthogonal and of unit length. The matrix of $T$ in this basis is diagonal, and the change-of-basis matrix $P$ is orthogonal (or unitary), satisfying $P^T P = I$ (or $P^* P = I$).

The proof strategy over $\mathbb{C}$ is inductive on dimension. One first shows that every normal operator has at least one eigenvalue (immediate over $\mathbb{C}$ from the fundamental theorem of algebra) and that eigenvectors of a normal operator are also eigenvectors of $T^*$ (with conjugated eigenvalue). It follows that the eigenspace $E_\lambda$ is invariant under $T^*$ as well as $T$, and thus its orthogonal complement $E_\lambda^\perp$ is also $T$-invariant. Restricting $T$ to $E_\lambda^\perp$ gives a normal operator on a space of smaller dimension, and induction closes the argument. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the proof must also handle the fact that the characteristic polynomial may not split, but self-adjointness ensures all eigenvalues are real — a fact proved by the elegant argument that if $T\mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v}$, then $\lambda \|\mathbf{v}\|^2 = \langle T\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{v} \rangle = \langle \mathbf{v}, T\mathbf{v} \rangle = \bar{\lambda}\|\mathbf{v}\|^2$, giving $\lambda = \bar{\lambda}$.

The applications of the spectral theorem are vast. The theory of positive semidefinite operators — those with $\langle T\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{v} \rangle \geq 0$ for all $\mathbf{v}$ — is immediate: $T$ is positive semidefinite if and only if all its eigenvalues are non-negative, and in this case the unique positive semidefinite square root $T^{1/2}$ exists. Every self-adjoint operator can be written as $T = T_+ - T_-$ where $T_+, T_-$ are positive semidefinite and mutually orthogonal, the "positive and negative parts" of $T$. In quantum mechanics, the spectral theorem is the mathematical foundation of measurement theory: observables are self-adjoint operators, their eigenvalues are the possible measurement outcomes, and the eigenvectors are the definite states. In data analysis, the spectral theorem applied to the covariance matrix is the basis of principal component analysis.
