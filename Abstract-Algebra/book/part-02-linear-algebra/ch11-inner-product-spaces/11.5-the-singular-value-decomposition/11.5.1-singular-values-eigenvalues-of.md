# 11.5.1 Singular Values: Eigenvalues of $T^*T$

The spectral theorem gives a satisfying account of self-adjoint and normal operators: they are diagonalizable in an orthonormal basis, and their eigenvalues are real (respectively, complex with real parts encoding spectral data). But many of the most important linear maps in applications — a differentiation operator, a discretization of an integral equation, the Jacobian of a nonlinear map at a point — are neither square nor normal. For such operators, eigenvalues may be ill-defined or geometrically misleading. The **singular values** provide the correct replacement: they are coordinate-free, always non-negative and real, defined for maps between spaces of different dimension, and carry precise geometric meaning.

The key insight is that even when $T: V \to W$ is not self-adjoint, the composed operator$T^*T: V \to V$ always is. The eigenvalues of this positive semidefinite operator provide the raw material from which singular values are built.

\begin{definition}
Let $T: V \to W$ be a linear map between finite-dimensional inner product spaces. The **singular values** of$T$ are
$$\sigma_i(T) = \sqrt{\lambda_i(T^*T)},$$
where $\lambda_1(T^*T) \geq \lambda_2(T^*T) \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_n(T^*T) \geq 0$ are the eigenvalues of$T^*T$ listed in decreasing order with multiplicity, and$n = \dim V$.
\end{definition}

That this definition is sound requires verification. The operator $T^*T: V \to V$ is **self-adjoint**:$(T^*T)^* = T^*(T^*)^* = T^*T$. It is **positive semidefinite**: for any$v \in V$,
$$\langle T^*Tv, v\rangle = \langle Tv, Tv\rangle = \|Tv\|^2 \geq 0.$$
By the spectral theorem for self-adjoint operators, $T^*T$ is diagonalizable in an orthonormal basis with real, non-negative eigenvalues. The square roots$\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i} \geq 0$ are therefore well-defined.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** For a diagonal matrix $T = \mathrm{diag}(d_1, d_2, \ldots, d_n)$ with real entries,$T^*T = T^T T = \mathrm{diag}(d_1^2, \ldots, d_n^2)$. The eigenvalues are$d_i^2$ and the singular values are$\sigma_i = |d_i|$. For diagonal matrices, singular values are precisely the absolute values of the diagonal entries. This generalizes: for a unitary matrix$U$, all singular values equal 1, since$U^*U = I$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** For the upper-triangular matrix $T = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$, compute
$$T^T T = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}.$$
The characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^2 - 3\lambda + 1 = 0$, giving$\lambda = (3 \pm \sqrt{5})/2$. The singular values are
$$\sigma_1 = \sqrt{\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}} \approx 1.618, \qquad \sigma_2 = \sqrt{\frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}} \approx 0.618.$$
Notice that $\sigma_1 \sigma_2 = \sqrt{\lambda_1 \lambda_2} = \sqrt{\det(T^TT)} = |\det T| = 1$, consistent with$\det T = 1$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** For a rectangular matrix $A \in M_{m \times n}(\mathbb{R})$ with$m > n$ and$\mathrm{rank}(A) = r < n$, the matrix$A^T A$ is$n \times n$ of rank$r$, with exactly$r$ positive eigenvalues and$n - r$ zero eigenvalues. The corresponding singular values:$r$ positive values$\sigma_1 \geq \cdots \geq \sigma_r > 0$ and$n - r$ zeros. The positive singular values measure stretching factors along the$r$ independent directions in the image of$A$.
\end{example}

**Geometric interpretation.** Consider the image of the unit sphere $S^{n-1} = \{v \in V : \|v\| = 1\}$ under$T$. This image is an ellipsoid in$W$ (possibly degenerate, if$T$ has nontrivial kernel). The **singular values**$\sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \cdots$ are precisely the **lengths of the semi-axes** of this ellipsoid. More precisely, if$v_1, \ldots, v_r$ are orthonormal eigenvectors of$T^*T$ corresponding to the positive eigenvalues$\sigma_1^2 \geq \cdots \geq \sigma_r^2$, then$Tv_1, \ldots, Tv_r$ are orthogonal vectors of lengths$\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_r$ (orthogonality follows from the SVD construction of Section 11.5.2). The unit sphere in$V$ maps to an ellipsoid whose axes in$W$ have lengths$\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_r$.

Several operator-theoretic quantities can be read off from the singular values:
- **Operator norm**: $\|T\| = \sigma_1(T)$.
- **Rank**: $\mathrm{rank}(T)$ = number of positive singular values.
- **Frobenius norm**: $\|T\|_F^2 = \sum_i \sigma_i^2 = \mathrm{tr}(T^*T)$.
- **Absolute determinant** (for square $T$):$|\det T| = \prod_i \sigma_i$.
- **Condition number**: $\kappa(T) = \sigma_1/\sigma_n$ (for invertible square$T$).

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** The projection $P: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ onto the$xy$-plane is$P = \mathrm{diag}(1,1,0)$. Then$P^T P = \mathrm{diag}(1,1,0)$, with eigenvalues$1, 1, 0$ and singular values$\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 = 1$,$\sigma_3 = 0$. The unit sphere maps to the unit disk in the$xy$-plane: no stretching in the$xy$-directions ($\sigma = 1$), and the$z$-direction collapses to a point ($\sigma = 0$). This confirms$\mathrm{rank}(P) = 2$ and$\|P\| = 1$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 5.** Consider a rotation $R_\theta \in M_{2\times 2}(\mathbb{R})$ by angle$\theta$. Then$R_\theta^T R_\theta = I$, giving singular values$\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 = 1$. A rotation is an isometry: the unit circle maps to the unit circle, all radii preserved. The product of singular values$\sigma_1 \sigma_2 = 1 = |\det R_\theta|$.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
The symmetry $\sigma_i(T) = \sigma_i(T^*)$ holds for square$T$: the nonzero singular values of$T$ and$T^*$ coincide (they are square roots of the eigenvalues of$T^*T$ and$TT^*$ respectively, and$T^*T$ and$TT^*$ have the same nonzero eigenvalues). For non-square$T \in M_{m\times n}$ with$m \neq n$, the matrices$T^*T \in M_{n\times n}$ and$TT^* \in M_{m\times m}$ differ in size, but their nonzero eigenvalues still coincide — a fact central to the SVD proof. The singular values are the "correct" generalization of eigenvalues for arbitrary linear maps: invariant, real, non-negative, and geometrically transparent.
\end{remark}
