# 11.5.2 The SVD Theorem: $A = U\Sigma V^*$

The previous section established that any linear map $T: V \to W$ has a well-defined list of singular values$\sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \cdots \geq 0$, defined as square roots of eigenvalues of the positive semidefinite operator$T^*T$. The **Singular Value Decomposition** (SVD) makes this geometric picture fully explicit: it provides orthonormal bases for both$V$ and$W$ in which$T$ acts as purely diagonal scaling. Every matrix, without any symmetry or normality hypothesis, can be expressed in this canonical form.

The SVD is, in the words of many practitioners, the most important factorization in numerical linear algebra. It simultaneously encodes the rank of a matrix, its four fundamental subspaces, its best low-rank approximations, and its condition number. The spectral theorem for normal operators is a special case.

\begin{theorem}[Singular Value Decomposition]
For any $A \in M_{m \times n}(\mathbb{F})$, there exist unitary matrices$U \in M_{m \times m}(\mathbb{F})$ and$V \in M_{n \times n}(\mathbb{F})$, and a matrix$\Sigma \in M_{m \times n}(\mathbb{R})$ of the form
$$\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} D & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad D = \mathrm{diag}(\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_r),$$
with $\sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \cdots \geq \sigma_r > 0$ and$r = \mathrm{rank}(A)$, such that
$$A = U\Sigma V^*.$$
The $\sigma_i$ are the singular values of$A$, uniquely determined. The matrices$U$ and$V$ are not unique when singular values are repeated, but they can always be chosen to have real entries when$A$ is real.
\end{theorem}

**Proof sketch.** Let $v_1, \ldots, v_n$ be orthonormal eigenvectors of$A^*A \in M_{n \times n}$ corresponding to eigenvalues$\sigma_1^2 \geq \cdots \geq \sigma_r^2 > 0 = \sigma_{r+1}^2 = \cdots$. For$i = 1, \ldots, r$, define
$$u_i = \frac{Av_i}{\sigma_i} \in \mathbb{F}^m.$$
These vectors are orthonormal: for $i, j \leq r$,
$$\langle u_i, u_j\rangle = \frac{\langle Av_i, Av_j\rangle}{\sigma_i \sigma_j} = \frac{\langle A^*Av_i, v_j\rangle}{\sigma_i\sigma_j} = \frac{\sigma_i^2\,\delta_{ij}}{\sigma_i\sigma_j} = \delta_{ij}.$$
Extend $u_1, \ldots, u_r$ to a full orthonormal basis$u_1, \ldots, u_m$ of$\mathbb{F}^m$ (using Gram–Schmidt, say). The relations$Av_i = \sigma_i u_i$ for$i \leq r$ and$Av_i = 0$ for$i > r$ (since$A^*Av_i = 0$ implies$\|Av_i\|^2 = 0$) combine to give$AV = U\Sigma$, where$V = [v_1|\cdots|v_n]$,$U = [u_1|\cdots|u_m]$, and$\Sigma$ has the stated block form. Since$U$ and$V$ are unitary,$A = U\Sigma V^*$.

**The four fundamental subspaces.** The SVD provides the cleanest description of the four subspaces associated to $A$:
- $\mathrm{Col}(A) = \mathrm{span}\{u_1, \ldots, u_r\}$ (column space, spanned by first$r$ left singular vectors).
- $\mathrm{Null}(A) = \mathrm{span}\{v_{r+1}, \ldots, v_n\}$ (null space, spanned by last$n-r$ right singular vectors).
- $\mathrm{Row}(A) = \mathrm{span}\{v_1, \ldots, v_r\}$ (row space, spanned by first$r$ right singular vectors).
- $\mathrm{Null}(A^*) = \mathrm{span}\{u_{r+1}, \ldots, u_m\}$ (left null space).

These four subspaces are mutually orthogonal (the first two are orthogonal complements in $\mathbb{F}^n$; the last two in$\mathbb{F}^m$), confirming the fundamental theorem of linear algebra.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Let $A = \begin{pmatrix}3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix} \in M_{3\times 2}(\mathbb{R})$. Then$A^T A = \begin{pmatrix}9 & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}$ with eigenvalues$9$ and$0$, so$\sigma_1 = 3$,$\sigma_2 = 0$,$r = 1$. Taking$v_1 = e_1$,$v_2 = e_2$:$u_1 = Av_1/3 = e_1 \in \mathbb{R}^3$. Completing to an orthonormal basis of$\mathbb{R}^3$:$u_2 = e_2$,$u_3 = e_3$. The SVD is
$$A = \begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\0&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.$$
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Let $A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix} \in M_{2\times 2}(\mathbb{R})$.
\par
$A^T A = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$, eigenvalues$\lambda_1 = 2$,$\lambda_2 = 0$. Right singular vectors:$v_1 = (1,1)^T/\sqrt{2}$,$v_2 = (1,-1)^T/\sqrt{2}$.
\par
$\sigma_1 = \sqrt{2}$,$\sigma_2 = 0$.$u_1 = Av_1/\sigma_1 = (1,0)^T/\sqrt{2} \cdot \sqrt{2} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = (1,0)^T$. Extend:$u_2 = (0,1)^T$.
\par
$$A = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{2}&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1/\sqrt{2}&1/\sqrt{2}\\1/\sqrt{2}&-1/\sqrt{2}\end{pmatrix}.$$
Check: $U\Sigma V^T = \begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{2}&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1/\sqrt{2}&1/\sqrt{2}\\1/\sqrt{2}&-1/\sqrt{2}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix} = A$. \checkmark
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Let $A = \begin{pmatrix}2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3\end{pmatrix}$. Since$A$ is already diagonal with positive entries,$A^T A = \begin{pmatrix}4&0\\0&9\end{pmatrix}$ and$\sigma_1 = 3$,$\sigma_2 = 2$ (reordering by convention). With$V = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ (a permutation) and$U = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, we get$A = U\mathrm{diag}(3,2)V^T$. This shows that permuting singular values simultaneously permutes columns of$U$ and$V$.
\end{example}

**Reduced (thin) SVD.** For $A \in M_{m\times n}$ with$m \geq n$ and$r = \mathrm{rank}(A)$, the **reduced SVD** keeps only the$r$ nonzero singular values:
$$A = U_r \Sigma_r V_r^*,$$
where $U_r \in M_{m\times r}$,$\Sigma_r = \mathrm{diag}(\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_r) \in M_{r\times r}$,$V_r \in M_{n\times r}$. Equivalently, via the outer product expansion,
$$A = \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i\, u_i v_i^*.$$
This rank-$r$ decomposition expresses$A$ as a sum of$r$ rank-1 matrices. Truncating the sum to the first$k < r$ terms gives the best rank-$k$ approximation to$A$ in both the operator norm and the Frobenius norm — the Eckart–Young theorem (Section 11.5.4).

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** For any rank-1 matrix $A = uv^*$ (outer product of column vectors), the SVD has$r = 1$:$\sigma_1 = \|u\|\|v\|$, the single left singular vector is$u/\|u\|$, and the single right singular vector is$v/\|v\|$. This is the rank-1 case of the outer product expansion.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
The geometric content of the SVD is a factorization of any linear map $T: V \to W$ into three steps: (1) rotate/reflect in the domain ($V^*$), (2) scale along coordinate axes ($\Sigma$), (3) rotate/reflect in the codomain ($U$). Every linear map is, up to isometries on domain and codomain, just a rescaling of coordinates. This is the singular-value version of the polar decomposition: a square matrix$A = U\Sigma V^* = (UV^*)(V\Sigma V^*)$ factors as a unitary part$UV^*$ times a positive semidefinite part$V\Sigma V^*$ — the latter being the "shape" of$A$ stripped of rotational information. The SVD is the canonical form for arbitrary linear maps, playing the same role that Jordan normal form plays for endomorphisms, but applicable without any hypotheses on$A$.
\end{remark}
