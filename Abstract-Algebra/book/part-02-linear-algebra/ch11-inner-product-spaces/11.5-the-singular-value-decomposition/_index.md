# 11.5 The Singular Value Decomposition

The spectral theorem applies to normal operators from a space to itself, but many of the most important linear maps in applications are non-square: they map $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}^m$ with $m \neq n$, or are square but not normal. The singular value decomposition (SVD) provides the geometric analysis for this general setting. It states that every $m \times n$ real matrix $A$ can be written as $A = U\Sigma V^T$, where $U$ is an $m \times m$ orthogonal matrix, $V$ is an $n \times n$ orthogonal matrix, and $\Sigma$ is an $m \times n$ diagonal matrix with non-negative entries $\sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \cdots \geq \sigma_r > 0 = \cdots = 0$ (here $r = \text{rank}(A)$). The $\sigma_i$ are the singular values of $A$, uniquely determined by $A$.

The geometric meaning of the SVD is vivid: the columns of $V$ (the right singular vectors) are an orthonormal basis for $\mathbb{R}^n$ that $A$ maps to orthogonal vectors; the columns of $U$ (the left singular vectors) are an orthonormal basis for $\mathbb{R}^m$ in which those images have lengths $\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_r, 0, \ldots, 0$. Equivalently, $A$ maps the unit ball in $\mathbb{R}^n$ to an ellipsoid in $\mathbb{R}^m$, and the singular values are the lengths of the semi-axes of this ellipsoid. The derivation of the SVD follows from the spectral theorem: the symmetric positive semidefinite matrix $A^T A$ has an orthonormal eigenbasis $\{v_1, \ldots, v_n\}$ with non-negative eigenvalues $\sigma_1^2 \geq \cdots \geq \sigma_n^2$; setting $u_i = A v_i / \sigma_i$ (for $\sigma_i > 0$) gives orthonormal vectors in $\mathbb{R}^m$ that are completed to a full basis of $\mathbb{R}^m$.

The Eckart–Young theorem gives the SVD a striking extremal property: among all matrices of rank at most $k$, the matrix $A_k = \sum_{i=1}^k \sigma_i u_i v_i^T$ (the "truncated SVD") is the closest to $A$ in both the spectral norm and the Frobenius norm. This best low-rank approximation theorem is the theoretical foundation of principal component analysis, image compression, and dimensionality reduction. The pseudoinverse $A^+ = V \Sigma^+ U^T$ (where $\Sigma^+$ inverts the nonzero diagonal entries) solves least-squares problems directly: $\hat{x} = A^+ b$ gives the minimum-norm least-squares solution to $Ax = b$, providing a clean generalization of the inverse to non-square or rank-deficient matrices.
