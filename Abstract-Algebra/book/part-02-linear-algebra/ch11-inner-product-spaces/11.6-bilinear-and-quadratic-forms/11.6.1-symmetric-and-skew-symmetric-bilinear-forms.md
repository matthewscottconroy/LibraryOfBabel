# 11.6.1 Symmetric and Skew-Symmetric Bilinear Forms

An inner product is a bilinear form with extra structure (symmetry and positive definiteness). In this section and the two that follow, we study the broader class of bilinear forms, where positive definiteness is relaxed. This leads to a richer theory connecting linear algebra to geometry (quadratic curves and surfaces), number theory, and physics (the Minkowski metric of special relativity).

\begin{definition}
A **bilinear form** on a vector space $V$ over a field$F$ is a map$B: V \times V \to F$ that is linear in each argument separately. The form is **symmetric** if$B(u,v) = B(v,u)$ for all$u, v$, and **skew-symmetric** (or **alternating**) if$B(u,v) = -B(v,u)$ for all$u, v$.
\end{definition}

Note: skew-symmetry forces $B(v,v) = -B(v,v)$, so$B(v,v) = 0$ for all$v$ (assuming$\mathrm{char}(F) \neq 2$). Conversely,$B(v,v) = 0$ for all$v$ implies$B(u+v, u+v) = 0$ gives$B(u,v) + B(v,u) = 0$. So for$\mathrm{char}(F) \neq 2$, skew-symmetric and alternating are equivalent.

\begin{example}
**Example 1 (Symmetric, not PD).** The form $B(u,v) = u_1 v_1 - u_2 v_2$ on$\mathbb{R}^2$ is symmetric but not positive definite:$B(e_2, e_2) = -1 < 0$. This is the Minkowski metric in$1+1$ dimensions.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2 (Symplectic form).** On $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ with basis$\{e_1, \ldots, e_n, f_1, \ldots, f_n\}$, define$\omega(e_i, f_j) = \delta_{ij}$,$\omega(e_i, e_j) = 0$,$\omega(f_i, f_j) = 0$. This is skew-symmetric (and non-degenerate): the standard symplectic form, fundamental in classical mechanics and symplectic geometry.
\end{example}

**Matrix representation.** In a basis $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$, a bilinear form is represented by the$n \times n$ matrix$M_{ij} = B(e_i, e_j)$, so$B(u,v) = u^T M v$ (in column-vector notation). Symmetric forms correspond to symmetric matrices ($M = M^T$) and skew-symmetric forms to skew-symmetric matrices ($M = -M^T$).

Under a change of basis by an invertible matrix $P$, the representing matrix transforms by **congruence**:$M \mapsto P^T M P$. The theory of bilinear forms is therefore the theory of matrices up to congruence (not similarity$P^{-1}MP$).

**Canonical decomposition.** Any bilinear form $B$ decomposes as$B = B_s + B_a$ where$B_s(u,v) = \frac{1}{2}(B(u,v) + B(v,u))$ is symmetric and$B_a(u,v) = \frac{1}{2}(B(u,v) - B(v,u))$ is skew-symmetric.

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** The form $B(u,v) = 2u_1 v_2 + 3 u_2 v_1$ on$\mathbb{R}^2$ has$M = \begin{pmatrix}0&2\\3&0\end{pmatrix}$. The symmetric part has matrix$M_s = \begin{pmatrix}0&5/2\\5/2&0\end{pmatrix}$ and the skew-symmetric part has$M_a = \begin{pmatrix}0&-1/2\\1/2&0\end{pmatrix}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** The cross product on $\mathbb{R}^3$ is not bilinear into$\mathbb{R}$ but the triple product$B(u,v) = w \cdot (u \times v)$ (for fixed$w$) is a bilinear form in$u$ and$v$, and is alternating:$B(v,u) = w \cdot (v \times u) = -w \cdot (u \times v) = -B(u,v)$.
\end{example}
\par
\begin{remark}
The classification of skew-symmetric forms over $\mathbb{R}$ (or any field of characteristic$\neq 2$) is much simpler than for symmetric forms: every non-degenerate skew-symmetric form on an even-dimensional space is equivalent to the standard symplectic form. This **Darboux theorem** (in the algebraic setting) has no analogue for symmetric forms, which have many non-equivalent types classified by Sylvester's law of inertia (Section 11.6.2). Odd-dimensional skew-symmetric forms are always degenerate.
\end{remark}
