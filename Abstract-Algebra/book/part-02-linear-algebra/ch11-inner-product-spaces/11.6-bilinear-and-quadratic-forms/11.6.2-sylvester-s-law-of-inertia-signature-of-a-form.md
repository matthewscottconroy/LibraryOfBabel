# 11.6.2 Sylvester's Law of Inertia; Signature of a Form

We saw that an inner product is a symmetric bilinear form that is positive definite. What happens when we drop positive definiteness but keep symmetry? Over $\mathbb{R}$, Sylvester's law of inertia provides a complete classification.

**Diagonalization of symmetric bilinear forms.** Given any symmetric bilinear form $B$ on a real vector space$V$, one can always find a basis in which the associated matrix is diagonal. The procedure is an algebraic version of completing the square:
- If $B(e_i, e_i) \neq 0$ for some basis vector, use it as a pivot and subtract its contribution from all other basis vectors.
- If all diagonal entries vanish but some $B(e_i, e_j) \neq 0$, replace$(e_i, e_j)$ by$(e_i + e_j, e_i - e_j)$ to create nonzero diagonal entries.
- Rescale each basis vector to make each nonzero diagonal entry $\pm 1$.

\begin{theorem}[Sylvester's Law of Inertia]
For any symmetric bilinear form $B$ on a real vector space$V$, there exists a basis in which the associated matrix is$\mathrm{diag}(1, \ldots, 1, -1, \ldots, -1, 0, \ldots, 0)$ ($p$ ones,$q$ negative ones,$r$ zeros). The triple$(p, q, r)$, called the **signature** of$B$, is independent of the choice of diagonalizing basis. Two symmetric bilinear forms on$\mathbb{R}^n$ are congruent (equivalent under change of basis) if and only if they have the same signature.
\end{theorem}

\begin{example}
**Example 1 (Inner product).** Signature $(n, 0, 0)$:$p = n$,$q = r = 0$. This is the positive definite case; the form is an inner product.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2 (Minkowski metric).** On $\mathbb{R}^4$, the Minkowski metric$B(u,v) = -u_0 v_0 + u_1 v_1 + u_2 v_2 + u_3 v_3$ has signature$(3, 1, 0)$: three spatial directions and one time-like direction with opposite sign. Special relativity is formulated in this space.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** The form $B$ on$\mathbb{R}^3$ with matrix$M = \begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&-1&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}$ has signature$(1,1,1)$: one positive, one negative, one zero ("degenerate direction").
\end{example}
\par
**Proof of invariance sketch.** Suppose $B$ diagonalizes as$\mathrm{diag}(+1,\ldots,+1,-1,\ldots,-1,0,\ldots)$ with$p$ positive ones in one basis and$p'$ in another. If$p \neq p'$, WLOG$p > p'$. Let$V^+$ (dim$p$) be the subspace on which$B$ is positive definite in the first basis, and$V^{-}$ (dim$q' + r'$) the subspace on which$B \leq 0$ in the second. Then$\dim V^+ + \dim V^- > n$, so they intersect in a nonzero vector$v$. But$v \in V^+$ gives$B(v,v) > 0$, while$v \in V^-$ gives$B(v,v) \leq 0$: contradiction.
\par
\begin{example}
**Example 4.** The form $B$ on$\mathbb{R}^2$ with matrix$M = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}$. Eigenvalues of$M$:$\frac{5 \pm \sqrt{5}}{2}$, both positive. Signature$(2, 0, 0)$: positive definite.
\end{example}
\par
\begin{remark}
Sylvester's law of inertia says that the signature $(p, q, r)$ with$p + q + r = n$ is the complete congruence invariant of a real symmetric bilinear form. Compare with the similarity invariant for operators (Jordan form, or rational canonical form): similarity and congruence are different equivalence relations, and different invariants classify them. The signature is to bilinear forms what the characteristic polynomial is to linear operators, in the sense of being the fundamental classifying invariant.
\end{remark}
