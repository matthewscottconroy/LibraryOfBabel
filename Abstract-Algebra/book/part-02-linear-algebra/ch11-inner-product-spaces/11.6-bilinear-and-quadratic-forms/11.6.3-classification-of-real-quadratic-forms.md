# 11.6.3 Classification of Real Quadratic Forms

A **quadratic form** $Q: V \to \mathbb{R}$ is a function of the form$Q(v) = B(v,v)$ for some symmetric bilinear form$B$. The relationship between$Q$ and$B$ is two-to-one: given$Q$, the bilinear form is recovered by the polarization identity$B(u,v) = \frac{1}{2}(Q(u+v) - Q(u) - Q(v))$, so there is a canonical symmetric$B$ associated to each$Q$. In a basis,$Q(x) = x^T M x$ for the symmetric matrix$M = [B(e_i, e_j)]$.

In a diagonalizing basis, $Q(x) = \sum_{i=1}^p x_i^2 - \sum_{i=p+1}^{p+q} x_i^2$, where$(p,q,r)$ is the signature of$B$. By Sylvester's law, this signature completely classifies$Q$ up to change of basis (linear substitution).

\begin{definition}
A quadratic form $Q$ with signature$(p,q,r)$ is:
- **Positive definite** if $q = r = 0$:$Q(v) > 0$ for all$v \neq 0$.
- **Positive semidefinite** if $q = 0, r \geq 0$:$Q(v) \geq 0$ for all$v$.
- **Negative definite** if $p = r = 0$:$Q(v) < 0$ for all$v \neq 0$.
- **Negative semidefinite** if $p = 0, r \geq 0$.
- **Indefinite** if $p > 0$ and$q > 0$.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
**Example 1 (Positive definite).** $Q(x, y) = x^2 + 3y^2$ on$\mathbb{R}^2$. Signature$(2, 0, 0)$. Matrix$M = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix}$ is positive definite. Level sets$\{Q = c\}$ are ellipses centered at the origin.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2 (Indefinite).** $Q(x,y) = x^2 - y^2$ on$\mathbb{R}^2$. Signature$(1, 1, 0)$. Level sets$\{Q = c\}$ are hyperbolas for$c \neq 0$, and the pair of lines$x = \pm y$ for$c = 0$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3 (Positive semidefinite, degenerate).** $Q(x,y) = (x + y)^2 = x^2 + 2xy + y^2$ on$\mathbb{R}^2$. Matrix$M = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues$2$ and$0$. Signature$(1, 0, 1)$:$Q \geq 0$ but$Q(1,-1) = 0$ with$(1,-1) \neq 0$.
\end{example}

**Level sets and geometry.** The level sets $\{v \in V \mid Q(v) = c\}$ reveal the geometry:
- In $\mathbb{R}^2$: positive definite$\to$ ellipses; indefinite$\to$ hyperbolas; PSD/NSD$\to$ pairs of lines; degenerate$\to$ parallel lines.
- In $\mathbb{R}^3$: the six quadric surfaces (ellipsoid, hyperboloid of one sheet, hyperboloid of two sheets, elliptic paraboloid, hyperbolic paraboloid, cone) correspond to the various signatures.

\begin{example}
**Example 4 (Quadric surface).** $Q(x,y,z) = x^2 + 4y^2 - z^2$ on$\mathbb{R}^3$: signature$(2, 1, 0)$. Level set$\{Q = 1\}$ is a hyperboloid of one sheet. Level set$\{Q = -1\}$ is a hyperboloid of two sheets. Level set$\{Q = 0\}$ is a cone.
\end{example}

**Connection to eigenvalues.** If $Q(x) = x^T M x$ for a real symmetric matrix$M$ with eigenvalues$\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n$, then in the eigenbasis$Q(y) = \sum_i \lambda_i y_i^2$. The classification of$Q$ is determined by the signs of the eigenvalues of$M$:
- All $\lambda_i > 0$: positive definite.
- All $\lambda_i \geq 0$: positive semidefinite.
- Mixed signs: indefinite.

\begin{remark}
The classification of quadratic forms over $\mathbb{R}$ (by signature) is the "easy" version of a deep arithmetic theory. Over$\mathbb{Q}$ or finite fields, quadratic forms are classified by more refined invariants (the discriminant, Hasse–Minkowski invariants). The Hasse–Minkowski theorem asserts that two quadratic forms over$\mathbb{Q}$ are equivalent if and only if they are equivalent over$\mathbb{R}$ and over every$p$-adic completion$\mathbb{Q}_p$: a "local-global principle" connecting the real theory to number theory.
\end{remark}
