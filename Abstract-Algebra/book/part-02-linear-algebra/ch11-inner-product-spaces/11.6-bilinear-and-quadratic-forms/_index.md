# 11.6 Bilinear and Quadratic Forms

An inner product is a special bilinear form — one that is symmetric and positive definite. This section studies symmetric bilinear forms without the positivity requirement, and the associated quadratic forms $Q(\mathbf{x}) = B(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x})$. Every symmetric bilinear form $B : V \times V \to \mathbb{R}$ can be represented by a symmetric matrix $A$ via $B(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{x}^T A \mathbf{y}$, and the corresponding quadratic form is $Q(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^T A \mathbf{x}$. The central question is: when are two symmetric matrices $A$ and $A'$ related by a change of basis $A' = P^T A P$ for an invertible $P$? Answering this is the classification problem for quadratic forms, and it is solved by Sylvester's law of inertia.

Sylvester's theorem states that any real quadratic form can be brought, by a change of variables, to a diagonal form with entries $1$, $-1$, and $0$. The number of $1$s (the positive index of inertia, denoted $p$), the number of $-1$s (the negative index, $q$), and the number of $0$s (the nullity) are all invariants of the form: they do not depend on which diagonalizing basis is chosen. The pair $(p, q)$ is the signature of the form. Two symmetric matrices over $\mathbb{R}$ are congruent (related by $A' = P^T AP$ for invertible $P$) if and only if they have the same signature. The practical procedure for diagonalizing a quadratic form uses the spectral theorem: since $A$ is symmetric, it is orthogonally diagonalizable, and in the eigenbasis the form is $Q = \sum_i \lambda_i x_i^2$; the signs of the eigenvalues determine the signature directly.

The classification by signature captures the geometry of the quadratic form completely. If $p = n$ and $q = 0$ (all eigenvalues positive), then $Q$ is positive definite and defines an inner product; the form is that of an ellipsoid. If $p = n-1$ and $q = 1$, the form is the Lorentz metric of special relativity, defining a hyperboloid. Mixed signatures give indefinite forms, and the distinction between definite, semidefinite, and indefinite is fundamental in optimization (determining whether a critical point is a minimum, maximum, or saddle), in differential geometry (distinguishing Riemannian from pseudo-Riemannian metrics), and in number theory (classifying integer-valued quadratic forms). This classification problem foreshadows the study of bilinear forms over arbitrary fields in Chapter 12, where the tensor product language provides the natural framework.
