# Chapter 11 — Inner Product Spaces

The chapters on vector spaces, linear maps, and eigentheory developed algebra: the study of linear structure by itself, independent of any notion of length or angle. This chapter adds geometry. An inner product $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ on a vector space $V$ assigns a scalar to each pair of vectors in a way that is bilinear (or sesquilinear over $\mathbb{C}$), symmetric (or conjugate-symmetric), and positive definite. From these three axioms, everything geometrical flows: the norm $\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{\langle \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{v} \rangle}$ measures length, the expression $\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle = \|\mathbf{u}\| \|\mathbf{v}\| \cos\theta$ measures angles and recovers the familiar law of cosines, and two vectors are orthogonal precisely when their inner product is zero. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality $|\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle| \leq \|\mathbf{u}\| \|\mathbf{v}\|$ is the first theorem proved from the axioms alone, and it underlies every subsequent geometric argument.

The section on orthogonality develops the most powerful constructive tool of the chapter: the Gram–Schmidt process. Given any basis, Gram–Schmidt produces an orthonormal basis by a sequential projection-and-subtraction procedure. The elegance of an orthonormal basis $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$ is that coordinates are computed by inner products alone: $\mathbf{v} = \sum_i \langle \mathbf{v}, e_i \rangle e_i$. There is no need to solve a linear system. Gram–Schmidt also yields the $QR$ decomposition of a matrix: writing $A = QR$ where $Q$ is orthogonal and $R$ is upper triangular. This decomposition is the foundation of the $QR$ algorithm, the workhorse of numerical eigenvalue computation, and it connects the geometry of inner product spaces directly to applied matrix analysis.

The adjoint of a linear map is the unique map $T^* : V \to V$ satisfying $\langle T\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle = \langle \mathbf{u}, T^*\mathbf{v} \rangle$ for all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}$. In an orthonormal basis, the matrix of $T^*$ is the conjugate transpose of the matrix of $T$. The class of self-adjoint operators — those satisfying $T = T^*$ — plays the role in inner product spaces that symmetric matrices play in applied linear algebra. Normal operators, which satisfy $TT^* = T^*T$, form a broader class that includes self-adjoint, skew-adjoint, and unitary operators as special cases. The spectral theorem then delivers the climax of the chapter: every normal operator on a finite-dimensional complex inner product space is unitarily diagonalizable, and every self-adjoint operator on a real inner product space is orthogonally diagonalizable. Not only does the operator have an eigenbasis — the eigenbasis can be chosen orthonormal. The eigenvalues of a self-adjoint operator are always real, and eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are automatically orthogonal.

The singular value decomposition (SVD) extends this geometric analysis to non-square or non-normal operators. Every $m \times n$ real matrix $A$ can be written as $A = U \Sigma V^T$ where $U$ and $V$ are orthogonal matrices and $\Sigma$ is a rectangular diagonal matrix with non-negative entries — the singular values. The singular values are the square roots of the eigenvalues of $A^T A$, and they measure the amounts by which $A$ stretches the principal axes of an input ellipsoid. The SVD gives a complete geometric picture of any linear map: it decomposes $A$ into a rotation, a rescaling, and another rotation. The Eckart–Young theorem identifies the best rank-$k$ approximation to $A$ as the truncated SVD, making this decomposition fundamental in data science, signal processing, and numerical analysis. The chapter closes with bilinear and quadratic forms, including Sylvester's law of inertia, which classifies real quadratic forms by their signature — the number of positive and negative eigenvalues — and prepares the conceptual ground for the tensor algebra of Chapter 12.
