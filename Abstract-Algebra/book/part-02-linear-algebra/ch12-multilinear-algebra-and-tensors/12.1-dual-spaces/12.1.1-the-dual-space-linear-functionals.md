# 12.1.1 The Dual Space $V^* = \mathcal{L}(V, F)$; Linear Functionals

Every vector space $V$ carries with it a shadow space that encodes how$V$ interacts with its underlying field. A **linear functional** on$V$ is a linear map$\varphi: V \to F$, satisfying$\varphi(au + bv) = a\varphi(u) + b\varphi(v)$ for all$u, v \in V$ and scalars$a, b$. The collection of all linear functionals on$V$ forms a vector space under pointwise addition and scalar multiplication, called the **dual space**$V^* = \mathcal{L}(V, F)$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1 (Coordinate functionals).** On $\mathbb{R}^n$, every linear functional has the form$\varphi(x) = a_1 x_1 + \cdots + a_n x_n$ for some fixed vector$a = (a_1, \ldots, a_n)$. Identifying$\varphi$ with$a$ gives$(\mathbb{R}^n)^* \cong \mathbb{R}^n$ as vector spaces.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2 (Evaluation).** On the space $\mathbb{R}[x]_{\leq n}$ of polynomials of degree$\leq n$, evaluation at a point$\varphi_t(p) = p(t)$ is a linear functional. Integration$\varphi(p) = \int_0^1 p(x)\, dx$ is another.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3 (Trace).** On $M_n(\mathbb{R})$, the trace$\mathrm{tr}: M_n(\mathbb{R}) \to \mathbb{R}$ is a linear functional. It satisfies$\mathrm{tr}(A + B) = \mathrm{tr}(A) + \mathrm{tr}(B)$ and$\mathrm{tr}(cA) = c \mathrm{tr}(A)$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4 (Row of a matrix).** For a fixed row vector $r = (r_1, \ldots, r_n)$, the map$v \mapsto rv = \sum_i r_i v_i$ is a linear functional on$\mathbb{R}^n$. The matrix-vector product$Av$ computes$m$ linear functionals simultaneously, one for each row of$A$.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}
For a finite-dimensional vector space $V$,$\dim V^* = \dim V$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
In a basis $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$, a linear functional$\varphi \in V^*$ is completely determined by its values$\varphi(e_1), \ldots, \varphi(e_n) \in F$, which can be chosen freely. So$V^*$ is isomorphic (as a vector space) to$F^n$, which has dimension$n$.
\end{proof}

The dual space is thus isomorphic to $V$ when$V$ is finite-dimensional — but, crucially, **the isomorphism is not canonical**: it depends on the choice of basis. The only truly canonical relationship between$V$ and its dual is through the **evaluation pairing**$V \times V^* \to F$ defined by$(v, \varphi) \mapsto \varphi(v)$. This is a non-degenerate bilinear pairing and is the fundamental structure connecting$V$ and$V^*$ without any choice.

The distinction between "non-canonical isomorphism" and "canonical isomorphism" is one of the first places in linear algebra where the notion of **naturality** becomes essential. The isomorphism $V \cong V^*$ defined by$e_i \mapsto e^i$ (the corresponding dual basis functional) depends on the choice of$\{e_i\}$; the isomorphism$V \cong V^{**}$ defined by evaluation (Section 12.1.3) does not. In categories, a canonical map is one that commutes with all morphisms (linear maps) — and evaluation does, while$V \cong V^*$ does not.

\begin{remark}
Over an inner product space, there is a canonical isomorphism $V \cong V^*$ given by$v \mapsto \langle v, \cdot \rangle$ (the Riesz representation theorem). This isomorphism depends on the inner product, not on a basis — it is "natural" relative to the inner product structure. This is why the adjoint of a linear map (Section 11.3.1) "looks like" the dual map but requires an inner product to define: the inner product is precisely the canonical identification of$V$ with$V^*$.
\end{remark}
