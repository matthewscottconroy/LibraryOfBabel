# 12.1.2 Dual Bases; the Kronecker Delta

Given a basis $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$ of a finite-dimensional vector space$V$, we can construct a corresponding basis of the dual space$V^*$ in a canonical way.

\begin{definition}
The **dual basis** $\{e^1, \ldots, e^n\}$ of$V^*$ consists of the linear functionals defined by
$$e^i(e_j) = \delta^i_j \quad \text{(Kronecker delta)},$$
where $\delta^i_j = 1$ if$i = j$ and$\delta^i_j = 0$ if$i \neq j$. Explicitly,$e^i$ extracts the$i$-th coordinate: if$v = \sum_j v^j e_j$, then$e^i(v) = v^i$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
The dual basis $\{e^1, \ldots, e^n\}$ is a basis of$V^*$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
**Spanning:** Any $f \in V^*$ satisfies$f = \sum_i f(e_i) e^i$, since both sides agree on each basis vector:$\left(\sum_i f(e_i) e^i\right)(e_j) = \sum_i f(e_i) \delta^i_j = f(e_j)$.

**Independence:** If $\sum_i c_i e^i = 0$, then evaluating at$e_j$ gives$c_j = 0$ for each$j$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** For the standard basis $\{e_1, e_2, e_3\}$ of$\mathbb{R}^3$, the dual basis consists of the coordinate projections$e^1(x,y,z) = x$,$e^2(x,y,z) = y$,$e^3(x,y,z) = z$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** For the basis $\{v_1, v_2\} = \{(1,1), (1,0)\}$ of$\mathbb{R}^2$, the dual basis satisfies$e^1(v_1) = 1$,$e^1(v_2) = 0$,$e^2(v_1) = 0$,$e^2(v_2) = 1$. Since$v = av_1 + bv_2$ means$v = (a+b, a)$, we have$a = v_2$ (second coordinate) and$b = v_1 - v_2$ (first minus second). So$e^1(x,y) = y$ and$e^2(x,y) = x - y$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** In $P_2(\mathbb{R})$ with basis$\{1, x, x^2\}$, the dual basis consists of:$e^0(p) = p(0)$ (evaluation at 0),$e^1(p) = p'(0)$ (derivative at 0),$e^2(p) = p''(0)/2$. These extract the constant, linear, and quadratic coefficients respectively.
\end{example}

Notice the notational convention: basis vectors of $V$ carry lower indices ($e_j$) while dual basis vectors carry upper indices ($e^i$). This is not mere formality. The superscript-subscript pairing$e^i(e_j) = \delta^i_j$ reflects how upper and lower indices interact under contraction, a convention that pays dividends in the Einstein summation convention of Section 12.6. The dual basis makes the duality between$V$ and$V^*$ entirely explicit: each basis of one is paired with a unique basis of the other.

**Basis change and dual basis change.** If we change basis of $V$ by an invertible matrix$P$ (i.e., new basis$\tilde{e}_i = \sum_j P_{ji} e_j$), the dual basis transforms by$(P^T)^{-1} = (P^{-1})^T$: the two transformations are related by inverse-transpose.

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** If $P = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ (new basis$\tilde{e}_1 = e_1$,$\tilde{e}_2 = e_1 + e_2$), then the new dual basis transforms by$(P^{-1})^T = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}^T = \begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$. The new dual basis functionals are$\tilde{e}^1 = e^1 - e^2$ (extracting the first component minus the second) and$\tilde{e}^2 = e^2$.
\end{example}
\par
\begin{remark}
The inverse-transpose relationship between the transformation of a basis and the transformation of its dual basis is why column vectors (contravariant components, transforming by $P$) and row vectors or covectors (covariant components, transforming by$(P^T)^{-1}$) transform differently. This distinction will be formalized in the index-notation framework of Section 12.6.
\end{remark}
