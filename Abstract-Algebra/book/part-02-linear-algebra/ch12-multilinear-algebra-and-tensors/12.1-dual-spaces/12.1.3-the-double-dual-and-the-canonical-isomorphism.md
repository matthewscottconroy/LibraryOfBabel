# 12.1.3 The Double Dual and the Canonical Isomorphism $V \cong V^{**}$

Taking the dual of a vector space can be iterated. Having formed $V^*$, we can form its dual$V^{**} = (V^*)^*$, the space of all linear functionals on$V^*$. For a finite-dimensional$V$,$\dim V^{**} = \dim V^* = \dim V$, so$V$ and$V^{**}$ are isomorphic. The remarkable fact is that this isomorphism is **canonical** — independent of any choice of basis.

\begin{definition}
The **canonical map** $\iota: V \to V^{**}$ is defined by
$$\iota(v)(f) = f(v) \quad \text{for all } v \in V,\, f \in V^*.$$
The element $\iota(v) \in V^{**}$ is the evaluation functional: it sends each$f \in V^*$ to$f(v)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}
The canonical map $\iota: V \to V^{**}$ is an isomorphism (when$\dim V < \infty$).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$\iota$ is linear:$\iota(av + bw)(f) = f(av + bw) = af(v) + bf(w) = (a\iota(v) + b\iota(w))(f)$.

$\iota$ is injective: if$\iota(v) = 0$, then$f(v) = 0$ for all$f \in V^*$. In particular, the dual basis functionals$e^i$ satisfy$e^i(v) = v^i = 0$ for all$i$, so$v = 0$.

Since $\dim V^{**} = \dim V < \infty$, an injective linear map is an isomorphism.
\end{proof}

This definition requires **no basis, no inner product, no additional structure** — only the definition of $V^*$ itself. This is the prototypical example of a **natural isomorphism**.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** For $V = \mathbb{R}^2$ with standard basis$\{e_1, e_2\}$ and dual basis$\{e^1, e^2\}$, the double dual basis of$V^{**}$ is$\{(e^1)^*, (e^2)^*\}$ where$(e^i)^*(e^j) = \delta^i_j$. The canonical map sends$e_i \mapsto (e^i)^*$: evaluation at$e_i$ equals the dual basis functional$(e^i)^*$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Let $V = P_2(\mathbb{R})$ with dual space spanned by$\{$evaluation at$-1, 0, 1\}$. Then$\iota(p) =$ the "evaluation functional" on$V^*$ that sends each evaluation-functional$\mathrm{ev}_t$ to$p(t)$. The canonical identification allows us to think of$p$ as a function on the dual space, not just on the original space.
\end{example}
\par
The key property is the **absence of choice**: if we change basis in $V$, the map$\iota$ does not change, because its definition never referred to a basis. By contrast, the isomorphism$V \to V^*$ defined by$e_i \mapsto e^i$ depends entirely on the chosen basis — a different basis gives a different isomorphism. 
\par
\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Suppose we use $\iota$ to identify$V$ with$V^{**}$. Under a change of basis$e_i \mapsto \tilde{e}_i = P e_i$ in$V$, the dual basis changes by$(P^T)^{-1}$ and the double dual basis changes by... back to$P$. So$\iota$ intertwines the action of$P$ on$V$ with the action of$P$ on$V^{**}$, confirming naturality.
\end{example}
\par
\begin{remark}
The formal framework for "independence of choice" is the notion of a **natural transformation** in category theory. The canonical isomorphism $\iota$ is natural in the following sense: for any linear map$T: V \to W$, the diagram$V \xrightarrow{\iota_V} V^{**}$ and$W \xrightarrow{\iota_W} W^{**}$ commutes with the double dual map$T^{**}: V^{**} \to W^{**}$. This naturality is precisely what distinguishes$V \cong V^{**}$ from the non-natural$V \cong V^*$.
\par
In infinite dimensions, $\iota$ is injective but generally not surjective:$V \subsetneq V^{**}$. The difference between$V$ and$V^{**}$ is measured by various "reflexivity" conditions studied in functional analysis.
\end{remark}
