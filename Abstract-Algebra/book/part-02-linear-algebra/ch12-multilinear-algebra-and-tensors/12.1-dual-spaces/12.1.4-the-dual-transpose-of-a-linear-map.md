# 12.1.4 The Dual (Transpose) of a Linear Map

The dual construction is functorial: not only does every vector space have a dual, but every linear map $T: V \to W$ induces a linear map in the opposite direction between the duals.

\begin{definition}
The **dual map** (or **transpose**) of $T: V \to W$ is$T^*: W^* \to V^*$ defined by
$$(T^* f)(v) = f(Tv) \quad \text{for all } f \in W^*,\, v \in V.$$
In words: $T^*$ acts on a functional$f: W \to F$ by precomposing it with$T$.
\end{definition}

Note: we use $T^*$ for the dual map in the purely algebraic sense (no inner product needed); this is distinct from the adjoint of Section 11.3.1, which requires an inner product and goes$T^*: W \to V$ via the inner product pairing.

Notice that if $T: V \to W$ goes forward,$T^*: W^* \to V^*$ goes backward. This direction reversal is characteristic of **contravariant** constructions.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** For $T: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ given by$T(x,y) = (x, x+y, y)$, the dual map$T^*: (\mathbb{R}^3)^* \to (\mathbb{R}^2)^*$ sends$f \mapsto f \circ T$. If$f = e^1$ (first coordinate on$\mathbb{R}^3$), then$(T^* e^1)(x,y) = e^1(T(x,y)) = e^1(x, x+y, y) = x$. So$T^* e^1 = e^1_V$ (first coordinate on$\mathbb{R}^2$).
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** For a linear map $T$ with matrix$A$ in standard bases, the dual map$T^*$ has matrix$A^T$ in the dual bases. Specifically:$(T^* e^i)(e_j) = e^i(T e_j) = A_{ij}$, so the matrix of$T^*$ with rows indexed by$i$ and columns by$j$ is$A_{ij}$, but as a map from$(\mathbb{R}^m)^*$ to$(\mathbb{R}^n)^*$, it is represented by$A^T$.
\end{example}
\par
**Algebraic properties.** The dual map satisfies:
- $(ST)^* = T^* S^*$ (order reverses, like matrix transposition).
- $(\mathrm{id}_V)^* = \mathrm{id}_{V^*}$.
- $(S + T)^* = S^* + T^*$.
- $(\lambda T)^* = \lambda T^*$ (no conjugation, unlike the inner-product adjoint over$\mathbb{C}$).
\par
\begin{example}
**Example 3.** For the composition $T: \mathbb{R}^2 \xrightarrow{S} \mathbb{R}^3 \xrightarrow{R} \mathbb{R}^2$,$(RS)^* = S^* R^*$. If$S$ has matrix$A$ and$R$ has matrix$B$, then$RS$ has matrix$BA$, and$(BA)^T = A^T B^T = S^* R^*$ in matrix form.
\end{example}
\par
\begin{example}
**Example 4.** For the projection $P: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^3$,$P(x,y,z) = (x,y,0)$,$P$ is idempotent:$P^2 = P$. The dual map$P^*: (\mathbb{R}^3)^* \to (\mathbb{R}^3)^*$ satisfies$(P^*)^2 = (P^2)^* = P^*$: the dual is also idempotent. The dual projects covectors onto the span of$\{e^1, e^2\}$ in$(\mathbb{R}^3)^*$.
\end{example}
\par
The matrix connection: in standard bases $\{e_j\}$ and$\{f_i\}$ with dual bases$\{e^j\}$ and$\{f^i\}$, the matrix of$T$ has entries$A_{ij} = f^i(Te_j)$ and the matrix of$T^*$ is$A^T$. This is the abstract explanation of why matrix transposition is so natural: it is the coordinate expression of the dual map.
\par
\begin{remark}
The distinction between the dual map $T^*: W^* \to V^*$ (purely algebraic) and the adjoint$T^*: W \to V$ (requiring inner products to identify$V \cong V^*$ and$W \cong W^*$) is important. The inner product gives canonical identifications$\phi_V: V \to V^*$ (by$v \mapsto \langle v, \cdot \rangle$) and$\phi_W: W \to W^*$. The adjoint$T^{\dagger}: W \to V$ in the inner-product sense is then$T^{\dagger} = \phi_V^{-1} \circ T^* \circ \phi_W$. When we work in orthonormal bases,$\phi_V$ and$\phi_W$ become the identity maps (after identifying$V$ with$F^n$ via coordinates), and$T^{\dagger}$ corresponds to the conjugate transpose. The algebraic dual and the inner-product adjoint are thus unified by viewing$\phi$ as the bridge.
\end{remark}
