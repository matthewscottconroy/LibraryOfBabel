# 12.2.2 Existence: Constructing $V \otimes W$ via Quotients of Free Modules

The universal property tells us what the tensor product must do, but it does not guarantee that such an object exists. We now give a construction.

**Step 1: The free vector space.** Let $F(V \times W)$ denote the **free vector space** on the set$V \times W$ — the vector space whose basis is the set$V \times W$ itself, consisting of all formal finite linear combinations$\sum_i c_i (v_i, w_i)$ with$v_i \in V$,$w_i \in W$,$c_i \in F$. Elements of$V \times W$ are treated as formal symbols:$(v, w)$ is not the ordered pair but a basis vector labelled by the pair$(v, w)$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** For $V = W = \mathbb{R}$ (one-dimensional),$F(\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R})$ has a basis indexed by all pairs$(r, s) \in \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}$, so it is uncountably infinite-dimensional. The tensor product will be much smaller.
\end{example}
\par
**Step 2: Imposing bilinearity.** Let $R$ be the subspace of$F(V \times W)$ generated by all elements of the forms:
\begin{align*}
&(v_1 + v_2, w) - (v_1, w) - (v_2, w), \\
&(v, w_1 + w_2) - (v, w_1) - (v, w_2), \\
&(cv, w) - c(v, w), \quad (v, cw) - c(v, w).
\end{align*}
These are exactly the bilinearity relations that must hold.
\par
**Step 3: The quotient.** Define $V \otimes W = F(V \times W) / R$, and write$v \otimes w$ for the image of$(v, w)$ in this quotient. By construction,$(v, w) \mapsto v \otimes w$ is bilinear.
\par
**Verification of the universal property.** Given any bilinear $\varphi: V \times W \to U$, extend linearly to$\tilde{\varphi}: F(V \times W) \to U$ by$\tilde{\varphi}(\sum c_i(v_i, w_i)) = \sum c_i \varphi(v_i, w_i)$. The bilinearity of$\varphi$ ensures$\tilde{\varphi}$ vanishes on$R$, so it descends to a well-defined linear map$V \otimes W \to U$. Uniqueness holds since$v \otimes w$ generates$V \otimes W$.
\par
\begin{example}
**Example 2.** In $V \otimes W$, the bilinearity relations become algebraic identities:
\begin{align*}
(v_1 + v_2) \otimes w &= v_1 \otimes w + v_2 \otimes w, \\
v \otimes (w_1 + w_2) &= v \otimes w_1 + v \otimes w_2, \\
(cv) \otimes w &= c(v \otimes w) = v \otimes (cw).
\end{align*}
In particular, $0 \otimes w = 0$ and$v \otimes 0 = 0$.
\end{example}
\par
\begin{example}
**Example 3.** In $\mathbb{R}^2 \otimes \mathbb{R}^2$, we have:
$(e_1 + e_2) \otimes e_1 = e_1 \otimes e_1 + e_2 \otimes e_1$ and$e_1 \otimes (2e_2) = 2(e_1 \otimes e_2)$.
\end{example}
\par
\begin{example}
**Example 4.** The quotient construction modifies the free space by exactly the minimal relations needed for bilinearity. One can check that $V \otimes W$ is much smaller than$F(V \times W)$: if$V$ is$m$-dimensional and$W$ is$n$-dimensional, then$\dim(V \otimes W) = mn$ (proved in Section 12.2.3), compared to the infinite (or very large) dimension of$F(V \times W)$.
\end{example}
\par
\begin{remark}
This quotient construction is typical in algebra: one starts with a free object carrying no relations, then imposes the desired ones by quotienting. The same method is used to construct the free group (quotient of the free monoid), polynomial rings (quotient of the free noncommutative polynomial ring), and the exterior and symmetric algebras (quotients of the tensor algebra, Sections 12.4 and 12.5). The common thread is: start with the most general object, then impose just enough relations to get the desired properties, no more.
\end{remark}
