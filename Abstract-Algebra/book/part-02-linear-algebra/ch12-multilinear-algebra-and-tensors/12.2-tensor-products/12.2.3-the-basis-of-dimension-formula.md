# 12.2.3 The Basis of $V \otimes W$; Dimension Formula

Having constructed $V \otimes W$, we now identify its basis and dimension.

\begin{theorem}
Let $\{e_1, \ldots, e_m\}$ be a basis of$V$ and$\{f_1, \ldots, f_n\}$ a basis of$W$. Then the$mn$ elements$\{e_i \otimes f_j \mid 1 \leq i \leq m,\, 1 \leq j \leq n\}$ form a basis of$V \otimes W$, and
$$\dim(V \otimes W) = (\dim V)(\dim W).$$
\end{theorem}

**Spanning.** Any pure tensor $v \otimes w$ with$v = \sum_i a_i e_i$ and$w = \sum_j b_j f_j$ expands by bilinearity:
$$v \otimes w = \left(\sum_i a_i e_i\right) \otimes \left(\sum_j b_j f_j\right) = \sum_{i,j} a_i b_j (e_i \otimes f_j).$$
Every element of $V \otimes W$ is a finite sum of pure tensors, hence in the span of$\{e_i \otimes f_j\}$.

**Linear independence.** For each pair $(i_0, j_0)$, consider the bilinear map$\varphi_{i_0 j_0}: V \times W \to F$ defined by$\varphi_{i_0 j_0}(e_i, f_j) = \delta_{i, i_0}\delta_{j, j_0}$ (and extended bilinearly). By the universal property, there is a linear map$\tilde{\varphi}_{i_0 j_0}: V \otimes W \to F$ with$\tilde{\varphi}_{i_0 j_0}(e_i \otimes f_j) = \delta_{i,i_0}\delta_{j,j_0}$. If$\sum_{i,j} c_{ij}(e_i \otimes f_j) = 0$, applying$\tilde{\varphi}_{i_0 j_0}$ gives$c_{i_0 j_0} = 0$. So the set is independent.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** $\dim(\mathbb{R}^2 \otimes \mathbb{R}^3) = 2 \cdot 3 = 6$. Basis:$\{e_1 \otimes f_1, e_1 \otimes f_2, e_1 \otimes f_3, e_2 \otimes f_1, e_2 \otimes f_2, e_2 \otimes f_3\}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** $\dim(\mathbb{R}^m \otimes \mathbb{R}^n) = mn$. In terms of matrices:$\mathbb{R}^m \otimes \mathbb{R}^n \cong M_{m \times n}(\mathbb{R})$, where$e_i \otimes f_j$ corresponds to the elementary matrix$E_{ij}$ (with a 1 in position$(i,j)$).
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** $\dim(V \otimes V^*) = n^2$ for$n = \dim V$. Since$V \otimes V^* \cong \mathcal{L}(V, V) = \mathrm{End}(V)$ (Section 12.2.5), this is consistent: the space of linear operators on$V$ has dimension$n^2$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** For $P_2(\mathbb{R}) \otimes P_3(\mathbb{R})$ (polynomials of degree$\leq 2$ and$\leq 3$): dimensions are 3 and 4, so$\dim = 12$. Basis:$\{x^i \otimes x^j : 0 \leq i \leq 2, 0 \leq j \leq 3\}$. This is not the same as$P_5(\mathbb{R})$ (dimension 6) — the tensor product is bigger.
\end{example}
\par
The dimension formula $\dim(V \otimes W) = mn$ contrasts sharply with$\dim(V \times W) = m + n$: the tensor product is the **multiplicative** rather than additive combination of the two spaces. This multiplicative behavior is what makes tensor products appropriate for encoding independent "degrees of freedom" that interact multiplicatively.
\par
\begin{remark}
In quantum mechanics, the state space of two independent quantum systems is the tensor product of their individual state spaces, not the direct sum. If system $A$ has$m$ states and system$B$ has$n$ states, the combined system has$mn$ states. This is why quantum entanglement (the existence of states in$V \otimes W$ not of the form$v \otimes w$) is a genuinely new phenomenon: there are$mn$ dimensions of combined state space but only$m + n - 1$ "unentangled" dimensions (product states form a variety of much smaller dimension).
\end{remark}
