# 12.2.4 Pure and Non-Pure Tensors

The tensor product $V \otimes W$ is spanned by elements of the form$v \otimes w$, but not every element of$V \otimes W$ is of this form. This gap between generators and general elements is not an artifact of the construction — it is the defining structural feature of the tensor product and the source of much of its richness in geometry, algebra, and quantum mechanics.

\begin{definition}
An element $t \in V \otimes W$ is called a **pure tensor** (also **simple tensor** or **decomposable tensor**) if$t = v \otimes w$ for some$v \in V$ and$w \in W$. An element that is not pure is called a **non-pure tensor** (or **entangled**, in the language of quantum information).
\end{definition}

Every element of $V \otimes W$ is a finite sum of pure tensors — this is immediate from the definition of$V \otimes W$ as the span of all$v \otimes w$. But for$\dim V \geq 2$ and$\dim W \geq 2$, sums of two or more linearly independent pure tensors are generically non-pure.

The cleanest way to detect whether a tensor is pure is via a rank criterion. Fix bases $\{e_i\}$ for$V$ and$\{f_j\}$ for$W$; then every$t \in V \otimes W$ can be written as$t = \sum_{i,j} T_{ij}\, e_i \otimes f_j$ for a unique matrix of coefficients$T = (T_{ij}) \in M_{m \times n}(\mathbb{F})$.

\begin{proposition}
Under the identification $V \otimes W \cong M_{m\times n}(\mathbb{F})$ (via a choice of bases for$V$ and$W$), a tensor$t$ corresponds to a pure tensor if and only if the associated matrix$T$ has matrix rank$\leq 1$.
\end{proposition}

*Proof.* If $t = v \otimes w$ with$v = \sum_i a_i e_i$ and$w = \sum_j b_j f_j$, then$T_{ij} = a_i b_j$, which is the outer product$T = ab^T$, a matrix of rank 1 (assuming$v, w \neq 0$). Conversely, if$T = ab^T$, then$t = (\sum_i a_i e_i)\otimes(\sum_j b_j f_j)$ is pure. Thus purity corresponds exactly to matrix rank 1 (or rank 0 for the zero tensor).

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Take $V = W = \mathbb{R}^2$ with standard basis$\{e_1, e_2\}$. Consider$T = e_1 \otimes e_1 + e_2 \otimes e_2$, corresponding to the$2\times 2$ identity matrix$I_2$. Suppose$T = v \otimes w$ with$v = ae_1 + be_2$ and$w = ce_1 + de_2$. Then
$$v \otimes w = ac\,(e_1\otimes e_1) + ad\,(e_1\otimes e_2) + bc\,(e_2\otimes e_1) + bd\,(e_2\otimes e_2).$$
Matching coefficients against $T$:$ac = 1$,$ad = 0$,$bc = 0$,$bd = 1$. From$ad = 0$, either$a = 0$ or$d = 0$. If$a = 0$, then$ac = 0 \neq 1$; if$d = 0$, then$bd = 0 \neq 1$. Both cases give a contradiction. Therefore$T$ is non-pure.
\par
Equivalently, $I_2$ has rank 2 as a matrix, confirming non-purity.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** The matrix $\begin{pmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 1 & 2\end{pmatrix}$ has rank 1 (rows proportional), so it corresponds to a pure tensor. Indeed, it equals$\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}(1,2) = (e_1 + e_2)\otimes(e_1 + 2e_2)$. More generally, any rank-1 matrix corresponds to a pure tensor. Any matrix of rank$\geq 2$ corresponds to a non-pure tensor.
\par
For $\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$ (rank 2), one minimal decomposition as a sum of pure tensors is$e_1 \otimes e_1 + e_2 \otimes e_2$ (two pure tensors), as in Example 1.
\end{example}

**Tensor rank.** The general notion captures the minimum number of pure tensors required.

\begin{definition}
The **rank** of a tensor $t \in V \otimes W$ is
$$\mathrm{rank}(t) = \min\left\{r \geq 0 : t = \sum_{k=1}^r v_k \otimes w_k,\; v_k \in V,\; w_k \in W\right\}.$$
Pure tensors (and the zero tensor) have rank $\leq 1$. The rank of$T = e_1 \otimes e_1 + e_2 \otimes e_2 \in \mathbb{R}^2 \otimes \mathbb{R}^2$ is exactly 2.
\end{definition}

For tensors in $V \otimes W$ (two factors), tensor rank coincides with matrix rank under the basis identification, and is computable in polynomial time. For tensors in three or more factors — elements of$V_1 \otimes V_2 \otimes V_3$ — computing the rank becomes NP-hard in general, and even the maximum rank over all tensors in$(\mathbb{F}^n)^{\otimes 3}$ is not known for most$n$.

\begin{example}
**Example 3 (Schmidt decomposition).** In $V \otimes W$ over$\mathbb{C}$, with$\dim V = \dim W = n$, every tensor$t = \sum_{i,j} T_{ij}\, e_i \otimes f_j$ admits a **Schmidt decomposition**: applying SVD to the matrix$T = U\Sigma V^*$, we get
$$t = \sum_{k=1}^r \sigma_k\, \tilde{e}_k \otimes \tilde{f}_k,$$
where $\tilde{e}_k = \sum_i U_{ik}\, e_i$ and$\tilde{f}_k = \sum_j V_{jk}\, f_j$ form orthonormal sets in$V$ and$W$ respectively, and$r = \mathrm{rank}(T)$ is the tensor rank. The **Schmidt rank** of$t$ is$r$, and$t$ is pure iff$r = 1$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4 (Quantum entanglement).** In $\mathbb{C}^2 \otimes \mathbb{C}^2$ (the state space of two qubits), the **Bell states** are maximally entangled states. For instance,
$$|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(e_1 \otimes e_1 + e_2 \otimes e_2)$$
is non-pure by Example 1, with Schmidt rank 2 and Schmidt coefficients $\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 = 1/\sqrt{2}$. Equal Schmidt coefficients is the signature of maximal entanglement: no measurement on one qubit gives any information about the state of the other. In contrast, the **product state**$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(e_1 + e_2) \otimes \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(e_1 + e_2)$ is pure (Schmidt rank 1), corresponding to two independent qubits each in state$(e_1 + e_2)/\sqrt{2}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 5.** The tensor $t = e_1 \otimes e_1 + e_1 \otimes e_2 + e_2 \otimes e_1 \in \mathbb{R}^2 \otimes \mathbb{R}^2$ corresponds to the matrix$\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, which has rank 2. So$t$ is non-pure with rank 2. A Schmidt decomposition is obtained from the SVD of this matrix.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
The set of pure tensors (projective equivalence classes of rank-1 elements) forms an algebraic subvariety of $\mathbb{P}(V \otimes W)$ called the **Segre variety**$\Sigma_{m,n} \subset \mathbb{P}^{mn-1}$. Its equations are the$2 \times 2$ minors of the associated matrix — the vanishing of all$2 \times 2$ minors characterizes rank$\leq 1$. The$r$-th secant variety of$\Sigma_{m,n}$ (the closure of the set of rank-$r$ tensors) has expected dimension$r(m + n - r) - 1$ but may be deficient; classifying these secant varieties and determining tensor ranks is an active area of algebraic geometry with applications to fast matrix multiplication algorithms (Strassen-type), signal processing (independent component analysis), and quantum information theory.
\end{remark}
