# 12.3.1 The Tensor Algebra $T(V) = \bigoplus_{k \geq 0} V^{\otimes k}$

The tensor product allows us to combine two copies of a vector space. We can extend this idea indefinitely, forming the $k$-fold tensor power$V^{\otimes k} = V \otimes V \otimes \cdots \otimes V$ ($k$ factors) for any non-negative integer$k$.

\begin{definition}
The **tensor algebra** of a vector space $V$ is
$$T(V) = \bigoplus_{k \geq 0} V^{\otimes k} = F \oplus V \oplus (V \otimes V) \oplus (V \otimes V \otimes V) \oplus \cdots,$$
where $V^{\otimes 0} = F$ and$V^{\otimes 1} = V$. Multiplication is given by the tensor product: for$\alpha \in V^{\otimes j}$ and$\beta \in V^{\otimes k}$,$\alpha \cdot \beta = \alpha \otimes \beta \in V^{\otimes(j+k)}$.
\end{definition}

Elements of $V^{\otimes k}$ are called **$k$-tensors**. Elements of$T(V)$ are finite sums of homogeneous components, with one component from each$V^{\otimes k}$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** For $V = \mathbb{R}^2$ with basis$\{e_1, e_2\}$:
- $V^{\otimes 0} = \mathbb{R}$ (scalars).
- $V^{\otimes 1} = \mathbb{R}^2$ (vectors):$3e_1 - e_2$.
- $V^{\otimes 2}$ has basis$\{e_1 \otimes e_1, e_1 \otimes e_2, e_2 \otimes e_1, e_2 \otimes e_2\}$, dim 4.
- $V^{\otimes 3}$ has dim 8.
An element of $T(\mathbb{R}^2)$ might be$5 \cdot 1 + (3e_1 - e_2) + (e_1 \otimes e_2 - e_2 \otimes e_1)$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** For $V = \mathbb{R}$ (one-dimensional):$V^{\otimes k} \cong \mathbb{R}$ for all$k$ (since$\mathbb{R} \otimes \mathbb{R} \cong \mathbb{R}$). So$T(\mathbb{R}) \cong \mathbb{R} \oplus \mathbb{R} \oplus \mathbb{R} \oplus \cdots = \bigoplus_{k \geq 0} \mathbb{R} \cong \mathbb{R}[x]$ as a vector space.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Multiplication is non-commutative: for $v, w \in V$,$v \otimes w \in V^{\otimes 2}$ and$w \otimes v \in V^{\otimes 2}$, but$v \otimes w \neq w \otimes v$ in general (they are equal iff$v$ and$w$ are linearly dependent).
\end{example}

The tensor algebra is **infinite-dimensional** whenever $V \neq 0$, since it contains$V^{\otimes k}$ for every$k$ and$\dim V^{\otimes k} = (\dim V)^k$ grows without bound. Nevertheless, it is a well-defined associative algebra with unit$1 \in F = V^{\otimes 0}$.

One should think of $T(V)$ as the algebra of "words" in the elements of$V$: the alphabet is$V$ itself, and concatenation (tensor product) is the operation. No relations are imposed between different elements of$V$ —$v \otimes w$ and$w \otimes v$ are independent elements in$V^{\otimes 2}$. This is the precise meaning of "free": no commutativity, no nilpotency, nothing beyond what is required by the ring axioms.

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** Let $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$ be a basis of$V$. Then$T(V)$ is spanned by monomials$e_{i_1} \otimes e_{i_2} \otimes \cdots \otimes e_{i_k}$ for all$k \geq 0$ and all sequences$(i_1, \ldots, i_k)$ with each$i_j \in \{1, \ldots, n\}$. As a vector space this is isomorphic to$F\langle x_1, \ldots, x_n \rangle$, the free noncommutative polynomial algebra in$n$ variables.
\end{example}
\par
\begin{remark}
The exterior algebra $\bigwedge V$ and symmetric algebra$\mathrm{Sym}(V)$, the subjects of Sections 12.4 and 12.5, arise by imposing additional relations on$T(V)$: anti-commutativity gives$\bigwedge V$, and full commutativity gives$\mathrm{Sym}(V)$. The tensor algebra is thus the "mother algebra" from which all other natural graded algebras on$V$ descend as quotients.
\end{remark}
