# 12.3.2 Universal Property: $T(V)$ is the Free Associative Algebra on$V$

The tensor algebra $T(V) = \bigoplus_{k \geq 0} V^{\otimes k}$ is characterized, up to isomorphism, by a universal property that makes precise the sense in which$T(V)$ imposes no relations whatsoever on its generators. This "freeness" is the algebraic analogue of the free group or free monoid: just as the free group on a set$S$ is the most general group generated by elements of$S$ without any relations,$T(V)$ is the most general associative algebra generated by linear maps from$V$.

The key observation is that if $A$ is any associative algebra and$\varphi: V \to A$ is a linear map, then any product of elements in the image of$\varphi$ must satisfy whatever relations hold in$A$, but not more. Since$T(V)$ imposes no relations, there is exactly one algebra homomorphism$T(V) \to A$ extending$\varphi$ — no choices, no obstructions.

\begin{theorem}[Universal Property of $T(V)$]
Let $V$ be a vector space over$\mathbb{F}$, and let$T(V)$ denote its tensor algebra with inclusion$\iota: V \hookrightarrow T(V)$ (the degree-1 component). For any associative$\mathbb{F}$-algebra with unit$A$ and any$\mathbb{F}$-linear map$\varphi: V \to A$, there exists a unique algebra homomorphism$\tilde{\varphi}: T(V) \to A$ such that$\tilde{\varphi} \circ \iota = \varphi$, i.e., the following diagram commutes:
$$\begin{tikzcd} V \arrow[r, "\iota"] \arrow[dr, "\varphi"'] & T(V) \arrow[d, "\tilde{\varphi}", dashed] \\ & A \end{tikzcd}$$
\end{theorem}

**Proof.** *Existence.* Define $\tilde{\varphi}$ on each homogeneous component: on$V^{\otimes 0} = \mathbb{F}$, set$\tilde{\varphi}(c) = c \cdot 1_A$; on$V^{\otimes k}$, set
$$\tilde{\varphi}(v_1 \otimes v_2 \otimes \cdots \otimes v_k) = \varphi(v_1)\,\varphi(v_2)\cdots\varphi(v_k) \quad \text{(product in$A$)}.$$
This is well-defined and multilinear in each $v_i$, hence descends to the tensor product. Extending linearly to all of$T(V)$ and checking the algebra homomorphism property is straightforward:$\tilde{\varphi}(s \otimes t) = \tilde{\varphi}(s)\,\tilde{\varphi}(t)$ by construction. *Uniqueness.* Any algebra homomorphism$\psi: T(V) \to A$ extending$\varphi$ must satisfy$\psi(v_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes v_k) = \psi(v_1)\cdots\psi(v_k) = \varphi(v_1)\cdots\varphi(v_k) = \tilde{\varphi}(v_1\otimes\cdots\otimes v_k)$ (using that$\psi$ is an algebra map and agrees with$\varphi$ on degree-1 elements). Since the pure tensors span$T(V)$,$\psi = \tilde{\varphi}$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1 (Polynomial algebras).** Let $\dim V = 1$, say$V = \mathbb{F}\cdot x$. The only nonzero tensor powers are$V^{\otimes k} = \mathbb{F}\cdot x^{\otimes k}$, with$\dim V^{\otimes k} = 1$. Thus$T(V) = \bigoplus_{k \geq 0} \mathbb{F}\cdot x^{\otimes k}$ is 1-dimensional in each degree, and the product is$x^{\otimes j} \cdot x^{\otimes k} = x^{\otimes(j+k)}$. This is exactly the polynomial algebra$\mathbb{F}[t]$, with$x^{\otimes k} \leftrightarrow t^k$. The universal property then states: any linear map$\varphi: \mathbb{F}\cdot x \to A$ (i.e., any choice of element$a = \varphi(x) \in A$) extends uniquely to an algebra map$\mathbb{F}[t] \to A$ sending$t \mapsto a$. This is precisely the evaluation/substitution homomorphism$p(t) \mapsto p(a)$ — the universal property of the polynomial ring.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2 (Matrix representations).** Let $A = M_n(\mathbb{F})$ (the algebra of$n\times n$ matrices). Any linear map$\varphi: V \to M_n(\mathbb{F})$ extends uniquely to an algebra homomorphism$\tilde{\varphi}: T(V) \to M_n(\mathbb{F})$, sending$v_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes v_k \mapsto \varphi(v_1)\cdots\varphi(v_k)$ (matrix product). This is the universal way to "represent" linear maps from$V$ into matrices: any matrix representation of the vector space$V$ extends canonically to a representation of the entire tensor algebra. The module theory of$T(V)$ is thus the theory of linear maps$V \to \mathrm{End}(W)$, with no relation imposed.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3 (Multivariable polynomials).** Let $V = \mathbb{F}^n$ with basis$\{x_1, \ldots, x_n\}$, and let$A = \mathbb{F}[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ (commutative polynomial ring). The linear map$\varphi: V \to A$ sending$x_i \mapsto x_i$ (basis element to corresponding variable) extends to an algebra map$\tilde{\varphi}: T(V) \to \mathbb{F}[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ sending$x_{i_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes x_{i_k} \mapsto x_{i_1}\cdots x_{i_k}$. Since$\mathbb{F}[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ is commutative,$\tilde{\varphi}$ factors through the symmetric algebra:$T(V) \twoheadrightarrow \mathrm{Sym}(V) \xrightarrow{\sim} \mathbb{F}[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4 (Quotient algebras).** The universal property shows that any quotient of $T(V)$ by a two-sided ideal$I$ is the "freest" algebra satisfying the relations encoded by$I$. The **symmetric algebra**$\mathrm{Sym}(V) = T(V)/\langle v \otimes w - w \otimes v : v, w \in V\rangle$ has its own universal property: any linear$\varphi: V \to A$ (with$A$ commutative) extends uniquely to an algebra map$\mathrm{Sym}(V) \to A$. The **exterior algebra**$\bigwedge V = T(V)/\langle v \otimes v : v \in V\rangle$ is the free graded-anticommutative algebra on$V$. Both are quotients of$T(V)$, inheriting their universal properties as corollaries.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 5 (Universal enveloping algebra).** If $\mathfrak{g}$ is a Lie algebra (a vector space with a bracket$[\cdot,\cdot]$ satisfying antisymmetry and the Jacobi identity), the **universal enveloping algebra**$U(\mathfrak{g}) = T(\mathfrak{g})/\langle x\otimes y - y\otimes x - [x,y] : x,y \in \mathfrak{g}\rangle$ is the quotient of$T(\mathfrak{g})$ by the ideal generated by the relations$xy - yx = [x,y]$. By the universal property of$T(\mathfrak{g})$ (and its quotient), every Lie algebra representation$\mathfrak{g} \to \mathrm{End}(W)$ extends uniquely to an associative algebra representation$U(\mathfrak{g}) \to \mathrm{End}(W)$. The PBW (Poincaré–Birkhoff–Witt) theorem says$U(\mathfrak{g})$ has a basis of ordered monomials$x_{i_1}^{a_1}\cdots x_{i_k}^{a_k}$ — a far-reaching consequence of the free structure.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
In categorical terms, the tensor algebra functor $T: \mathbf{Vect}_{\mathbb{F}} \to \mathbf{Alg}_{\mathbb{F}}$ (from the category of$\mathbb{F}$-vector spaces to the category of associative$\mathbb{F}$-algebras with unit) is the **left adjoint** of the forgetful functor$U: \mathbf{Alg}_{\mathbb{F}} \to \mathbf{Vect}_{\mathbb{F}}$. The universal property asserts a natural bijection
$$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Alg}}(T(V),\, A) \cong \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Vect}}(V,\, U(A)),$$
saying that algebra maps out of $T(V)$ correspond precisely to linear maps from$V$ into the underlying vector space of$A$. This adjunction is one instance of a pervasive theme in algebra: "free" constructions are left adjoints to forgetful functors, and their universal properties encode exactly the data needed to map into any structure of the given type.
\end{remark}
