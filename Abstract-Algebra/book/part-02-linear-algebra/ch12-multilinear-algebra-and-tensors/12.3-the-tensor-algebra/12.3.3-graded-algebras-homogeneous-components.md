# 12.3.3 Graded Algebras; Homogeneous Components

The tensor algebra $T(V) = \bigoplus_{k \geq 0} V^{\otimes k}$ possesses more structure than simply being an associative algebra: its elements have degrees, and multiplication respects these degrees in the sharpest possible way. This structure is encoded by the notion of a graded algebra, one of the most broadly useful organizational principles in modern algebra. Graded algebras arise everywhere polynomial methods apply: in algebraic topology (cohomology rings), algebraic geometry (coordinate rings of projective varieties), commutative algebra (associated graded rings), representation theory (Hecke algebras, quantum groups), and homological algebra (Ext and Tor as graded modules).

\begin{definition}
A **graded algebra** over a field $\mathbb{F}$ is an associative$\mathbb{F}$-algebra$A$ together with a decomposition$A = \bigoplus_{k \geq 0} A_k$ as a direct sum of$\mathbb{F}$-vector subspaces, called the **homogeneous components** (or **graded pieces**), such that
$$A_j \cdot A_k \subseteq A_{j+k} \quad \text{for all } j, k \geq 0.$$
Elements of $A_k$ are called **homogeneous of degree$k$**. The unit element satisfies$1 \in A_0$, and$A_0$ is a subalgebra of$A$.
\end{definition}

The condition $A_j \cdot A_k \subseteq A_{j+k}$ means the multiplication is **degree-additive**: the product of a degree-$j$ element and a degree-$k$ element is homogeneous of degree$j+k$. This is the algebraic formalization of "the product of a degree-$j$ polynomial and a degree-$k$ polynomial has degree$j+k$."

For $T(V)$: the degree-$k$ component is$T^k V = V^{\otimes k}$, with$T^0 V = \mathbb{F}$ (scalar multiples of the unit) and$T^1 V = V$. The multiplication$T^j V \otimes T^k V = V^{\otimes j} \otimes V^{\otimes k} = V^{\otimes(j+k)} = T^{j+k}V$ precisely satisfies the graded algebra axiom.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** The polynomial ring $\mathbb{F}[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$, graded by total degree: the degree-$k$ component is the span of all monomials$x_1^{a_1}\cdots x_n^{a_n}$ with$a_1 + \cdots + a_n = k$. Its dimension is$\binom{n+k-1}{k}$. Multiplication satisfies$\mathbb{F}[x]_j \cdot \mathbb{F}[x]_k \subseteq \mathbb{F}[x]_{j+k}$. For$n = 1$:$\dim \mathbb{F}[x]_k = 1$ for all$k$, consistent with$T(\mathbb{F}) \cong \mathbb{F}[x]$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** The exterior algebra $\bigwedge V = \bigoplus_{k=0}^n \bigwedge^k V$ (where$n = \dim V$) is a graded algebra with$(\bigwedge V)_k = \bigwedge^k V$. Since$\bigwedge V$ is a quotient of$T(V)$ by a homogeneous ideal (generated by degree-2 elements$v \otimes v$), it inherits the grading. For$\dim V = n$:$\dim \bigwedge^k V = \binom{n}{k}$, so the total dimension of$\bigwedge V$ is$2^n$.
\par
The symmetric algebra $\mathrm{Sym}(V) = \bigoplus_{k \geq 0} \mathrm{Sym}^k V$ is graded with$\dim \mathrm{Sym}^k V = \binom{n+k-1}{k}$, isomorphic as a graded algebra to$\mathbb{F}[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ when$\dim V = n$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** The cohomology ring $H^*(M;\mathbb{Z}) = \bigoplus_{k \geq 0} H^k(M;\mathbb{Z})$ of a compact smooth manifold$M$ is a graded ring under the cup product, with$H^j \smile H^k \subseteq H^{j+k}$. Moreover, it satisfies **graded commutativity**:$\alpha \smile \beta = (-1)^{jk}\beta \smile \alpha$ for$\alpha \in H^j$ and$\beta \in H^k$. This makes it a **graded-commutative** algebra (also called a **supercommutative** algebra), of which$\bigwedge V$ is the canonical algebraic example.
\end{example}

**Homogeneous maps and graded morphisms.** If $A$ and$B$ are graded algebras, a linear map$f: A \to B$ is **homogeneous of degree$d$** if$f(A_k) \subseteq B_{k+d}$ for all$k$. A **graded algebra homomorphism** is a degree-0 algebra homomorphism:$f(A_k) \subseteq B_k$ for all$k$, and$f(ab) = f(a)f(b)$.

\begin{definition}
A **graded ideal** $I \subseteq A$ is a two-sided ideal that is also a graded subspace:$I = \bigoplus_{k \geq 0} (I \cap A_k)$. Equivalently,$I$ is generated by homogeneous elements. If$I$ is a graded ideal, the quotient$A/I$ inherits a grading:$(A/I)_k = A_k/(I \cap A_k)$.
\end{definition}

Both the symmetric algebra $\mathrm{Sym}(V)$ and the exterior algebra$\bigwedge V$ arise as quotients of$T(V)$ by graded ideals, and hence are themselves graded. The quotient map$T(V) \twoheadrightarrow \mathrm{Sym}(V)$ is a degree-0 graded algebra homomorphism; similarly for$T(V) \twoheadrightarrow \bigwedge V$.

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** For $V = \mathbb{F}^2$ with basis$\{e_1, e_2\}$, the graded components of the three main algebras built from$V$ are:
\par
- $T(V)$:$\dim T^k V = 2^k$. Dimensions:$1, 2, 4, 8, 16, \ldots$
- $\mathrm{Sym}(V)$:$\dim \mathrm{Sym}^k V = k+1$. Dimensions:$1, 2, 3, 4, 5, \ldots$ (isomorphic to$\mathbb{F}[x,y]$, graded by degree)
- $\bigwedge V$:$\dim \bigwedge^k V = \binom{2}{k}$. Dimensions:$1, 2, 1, 0, 0, \ldots$ (total dimension$4 = 2^2$)
\par
The quotient maps $T(V) \twoheadrightarrow \mathrm{Sym}(V)$ and$T(V) \twoheadrightarrow \bigwedge V$ collapse the exponentially growing tensor algebra onto its much smaller quotients by the imposed commutativity and alternating relations.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 5.** The exterior derivative $d: \Omega^k(M) \to \Omega^{k+1}(M)$ on differential forms is a graded map of degree$+1$ satisfying$d^2 = 0$. The de Rham complex$(\bigoplus_k \Omega^k(M), d)$ is a **differential graded algebra (DGA)**: a graded algebra with a degree-1 derivation$d$ satisfying$d^2 = 0$ and the graded Leibniz rule$d(\alpha \wedge \beta) = (d\alpha)\wedge\beta + (-1)^{|\alpha|}\alpha \wedge (d\beta)$. The cohomology of this complex is the de Rham cohomology, isomorphic (by de Rham's theorem) to singular cohomology with real coefficients. DGAs are a central tool in modern algebraic topology and homological algebra.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
The power of graded algebras lies in two features. First, a graded algebra is **controlled by finitely many finite-dimensional pieces**: one can often study it degree-by-degree, using the fact that $A_k$ is a finite-dimensional vector space when$V$ is finite-dimensional. Second, many structural theorems have graded analogues: the graded version of Nakayama's lemma, graded Noetherian rings, graded modules and their resolutions. In algebraic geometry, the **Proj construction** associates to any graded ring$A = \bigoplus_{k \geq 0} A_k$ (with$A_0 = \mathbb{F}$ and$A$ generated by$A_1$) a projective variety$\mathrm{Proj}(A)$, whose coordinate ring is exactly$A$. The graded algebra$\mathrm{Sym}(V)$ corresponds to projective space$\mathbb{P}(V^*)$ in this way. Thus the passage from$V$ to$\mathrm{Sym}(V)$ and then to$\mathrm{Proj}(\mathrm{Sym}(V))$ is the algebraic route from a vector space to its associated projective space — one of the foundational constructions of scheme theory.
\end{remark}
