# 12.4.1 Alternating Tensors and the Exterior Product $\wedge$

Among all multilinear maps, the **alternating** ones — those that change sign when two arguments are swapped — have special geometric significance. They measure oriented volume. A 2-form that computes the area of a parallelogram, or a $k$-form that computes the volume of a$k$-dimensional parallelepiped, is alternating: swapping two edge vectors reflects the parallelepiped and negates the orientation, hence negates the output.

\begin{definition}
The **exterior algebra** $\bigwedge V$ is the quotient of the tensor algebra$T(V)$ by the two-sided ideal$I$ generated by all elements of the form$v \otimes v$ for$v \in V$. The image of$v_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes v_k$ in$T(V)/I$ is written$v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_k$ and called an **exterior product** or **wedge product**. The defining relation is$v \wedge v = 0$ for all$v \in V$.
\end{definition}

**Anti-commutativity.** From $v \wedge v = 0$, set$v = u + w$:
$$0 = (u+w) \wedge (u+w) = u \wedge u + u \wedge w + w \wedge u + w \wedge w = u \wedge w + w \wedge u.$$
So $u \wedge w = -w \wedge u$. More generally, a transposition of any two adjacent factors negates the product, and a permutation$\sigma$ contributes the sign$\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)$:
$$v_{\sigma(1)} \wedge \cdots \wedge v_{\sigma(k)} = \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)\, v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_k.$$

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** In $\bigwedge^2 \mathbb{R}^3$:$e_1 \wedge e_2 = -e_2 \wedge e_1$, and$e_1 \wedge e_1 = 0$. A general element is$a(e_1 \wedge e_2) + b(e_1 \wedge e_3) + c(e_2 \wedge e_3)$ for$a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** For $u = e_1 + e_2$ and$v = e_1 - e_2$ in$\mathbb{R}^2$:
$u \wedge v = (e_1 + e_2) \wedge (e_1 - e_2) = e_1 \wedge e_1 - e_1 \wedge e_2 + e_2 \wedge e_1 - e_2 \wedge e_2 = 0 - e_1 \wedge e_2 - e_1 \wedge e_2 - 0 = -2(e_1 \wedge e_2)$.
The signed area is $-2$: the parallelogram with sides$u, v$ has area 2, and the orientation is negative (compared to$e_1, e_2$).
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** For $v_1, v_2, v_3 \in \mathbb{R}^3$:
$v_1 \wedge v_2 \wedge v_3 = \det[v_1 | v_2 | v_3] \cdot e_1 \wedge e_2 \wedge e_3$.
The wedge product computes the (signed) volume of the parallelepiped with edges $v_1, v_2, v_3$.
\end{example}
\par
The key consequence of $v \wedge v = 0$: if any two of the$v_i$ are equal,$v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_k = 0$. More generally, if any$v_i$ is a linear combination of the others, then$v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_k = 0$ (since by expanding and using anti-commutativity, each term involves a repeated factor). This will lead directly to the linear independence criterion of Section 12.4.4 and the determinant formula of Section 12.4.5.
\par
\begin{remark}
The exterior product is also the algebraic foundation of differential forms on manifolds. On a smooth $n$-manifold$M$, the cotangent space$T^*_p M$ at a point$p$ has an exterior algebra$\bigwedge(T^*_p M)$, and smooth sections of$\bigwedge^k T^*M$ are differential$k$-forms. The exterior derivative$d: \Omega^k(M) \to \Omega^{k+1}(M)$ is the unique graded derivation of degree 1 with$d^2 = 0$ agreeing with the usual differential on functions. Integration on manifolds, Stokes' theorem, de Rham cohomology, and much of modern differential geometry live in this framework.
\end{remark}
