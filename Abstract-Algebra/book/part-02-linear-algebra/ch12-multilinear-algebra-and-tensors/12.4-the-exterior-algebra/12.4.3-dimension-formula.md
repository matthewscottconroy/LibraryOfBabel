# 12.4.3 Dimension Formula: $\dim \bigwedge^k V = \binom{n}{k}$

The dimension of the $k$-th exterior power of an$n$-dimensional vector space is the binomial coefficient$\binom{n}{k}$. This follows directly from the basis theorem of Section 12.4.2: basis elements of$\bigwedge^k V$ are in bijection with strictly increasing$k$-element tuples$(i_1 < i_2 < \cdots < i_k)$ drawn from$\{1, \ldots, n\}$, and the number of such tuples is the number of$k$-element subsets of an$n$-element set, which is$\binom{n}{k}$.

\begin{theorem}
Let $V$ be an$n$-dimensional vector space over a field$F$. Then
$$\dim \bigwedge^k V = \binom{n}{k}$$
for each $0 \leq k \leq n$, and$\bigwedge^k V = 0$ for$k > n$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By the basis theorem (Section 12.4.2), the elements $e_{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{i_k}$ for$1 \leq i_1 < \cdots < i_k \leq n$ form a basis of$\bigwedge^k V$. The cardinality of this basis set equals the number of ways to choose an ordered strictly increasing$k$-tuple from$\{1, \ldots, n\}$, which is the same as the number of$k$-element subsets of$\{1, \ldots, n\}$, giving$\binom{n}{k}$. For$k > n$, no strictly increasing$k$-tuple exists (one would need$k$ distinct values in$\{1, \ldots, n\}$ but$k > n$), so the basis is empty and$\bigwedge^k V = 0$.
\end{proof}

Several special cases are immediately illuminating and worth examining carefully.

\begin{example}
**Example 1 (Degrees 0 and 1).** For any $n$:$\dim \bigwedge^0 V = \binom{n}{0} = 1$, consistent with$\bigwedge^0 V = F$ (the scalar multiples of the empty wedge product, which plays the role of the unit). And$\dim \bigwedge^1 V = \binom{n}{1} = n$, consistent with$\bigwedge^1 V = V$ itself.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2 (Top degree).** $\dim \bigwedge^n V = \binom{n}{n} = 1$. The$n$-th exterior power is one-dimensional, spanned by$e_1 \wedge e_2 \wedge \cdots \wedge e_n$. This one-dimensional space is called the **determinant line** of$V$. Any linear operator$T: V \to V$ acts on$\bigwedge^n V$ by a scalar — and that scalar is$\det(T)$ (see Section 12.4.5).
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3 (Middle degree, $n = 4$).** For$V = \mathbb{R}^4$ and$k = 2$:$\dim \bigwedge^2 \mathbb{R}^4 = \binom{4}{2} = 6$. The basis elements are$e_1 \wedge e_2$,$e_1 \wedge e_3$,$e_1 \wedge e_4$,$e_2 \wedge e_3$,$e_2 \wedge e_4$,$e_3 \wedge e_4$. For$k = 3$:$\dim \bigwedge^3 \mathbb{R}^4 = \binom{4}{3} = 4$. The dimensions$1, 4, 6, 4, 1$ for$k = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4$ match row 4 of Pascal's triangle.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4 (Total dimension).** The total dimension of $\bigwedge V = \bigoplus_{k=0}^n \bigwedge^k V$ is$\sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k} = 2^n$ (by the binomial theorem applied to$(1+1)^n$). For$n = 3$:$\dim \bigwedge V = 1 + 3 + 3 + 1 = 8 = 2^3$. In differential geometry, the$2^n$-dimensional space$\bigwedge(T^*_p M)$ of alternating forms on the tangent space at a point of an$n$-manifold$M$ is exactly this; on$\mathbb{R}^3$ one has$0$-forms (functions),$1$-forms (covectors),$2$-forms (pseudovectors), and$3$-forms (pseudoscalars), matching the classical objects of vector calculus.
\end{example}

The dimension formula has several important structural consequences. The symmetry $\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n}{n-k}$ implies a canonical isomorphism$\bigwedge^k V \cong \bigwedge^{n-k} V^*$ (via the **Hodge star** construction once an inner product is chosen), or more intrinsically$\bigwedge^k V \cong (\bigwedge^{n-k} V)^* \otimes \bigwedge^n V$. For$V = \mathbb{R}^3$, the Hodge star identifies$\bigwedge^1 V \cong \bigwedge^2 V$ (both three-dimensional), giving the classical identification of vectors and bivectors in$\mathbb{R}^3$, which underlies the cross product.

The vanishing $\bigwedge^k V = 0$ for$k > n$ is the algebraic expression of a geometric fact: any$k > n$ vectors in an$n$-dimensional space must be linearly dependent, so their wedge product is zero. The non-vanishing of$v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_k$ is therefore equivalent to linear independence of$\{v_1, \ldots, v_k\}$, which is the content of Section 12.4.4.

\begin{remark}
In the polynomial ring analogy, $\mathrm{Sym}^k V$ has dimension$\binom{n+k-1}{k}$ (Section 12.5.2), which grows without bound as$k$ increases — reflecting the fact that the symmetric algebra is infinite-dimensional (isomorphic to$F[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$). The exterior algebra, by contrast, is finite-dimensional of total dimension$2^n$, because the relation$v \wedge v = 0$ drastically restricts the allowed monomials. This finite-dimensionality is the algebraic reflection of the nilpotency of the exterior product.
\end{remark}
