# 12.4.4 Linear Independence via Exterior Products

One of the most elegant consequences of the exterior algebra is a clean algebraic criterion for linear independence: vectors $v_1, \ldots, v_k \in V$ are linearly independent if and only if their wedge product$v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_k \neq 0$ in$\bigwedge^k V$.

\begin{theorem}
Let $V$ be a vector space over a field$F$ and let$v_1, \ldots, v_k \in V$. Then$v_1, \ldots, v_k$ are linearly independent if and only if$v_1 \wedge v_2 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_k \neq 0$ in$\bigwedge^k V$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$(\Rightarrow)$ Suppose$v_1, \ldots, v_k$ are linearly independent. Extend them to a basis$\{v_1, \ldots, v_k, v_{k+1}, \ldots, v_n\}$ of$V$. In this new basis,$v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_k$ is one of the basis elements of$\bigwedge^k V$ (corresponding to the strictly increasing index tuple$(1, 2, \ldots, k)$), hence it is nonzero.

$(\Leftarrow)$ Suppose the vectors are linearly dependent: some$v_j = \sum_{i \neq j} c_i v_i$ is a linear combination of the others. Substituting into position$j$ of the wedge product and expanding by multilinearity:
$$v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_k = v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_{j-1} \wedge \Bigl(\sum_{i \neq j} c_i v_i\Bigr) \wedge v_{j+1} \wedge \cdots \wedge v_k.$$
Each term in the sum contains the factor $v_i$ for some$i \neq j$, which already appears in the product (since$i \in \{1, \ldots, k\} \setminus \{j\}$). A wedge product with a repeated factor is zero (since$w \wedge w = 0$ for any$w$). So every term is zero, hence the entire wedge product is zero.
\end{proof}

This criterion is conceptually important: it replaces the question "does this system of linear equations $\sum c_i v_i = 0$ have a nontrivial solution?" with the single computation of a wedge product. The dependence relation, if it exists, is encoded in the vanishing of a single element in an exterior power.

\begin{example}
**Example 1 (Standard basis).** In $\mathbb{R}^3$,$e_1 \wedge e_2 \wedge e_3 \neq 0$ since$\{e_1, e_2, e_3\}$ is linearly independent (it is the standard basis). Explicitly,$e_1 \wedge e_2 \wedge e_3$ is the unique basis element of$\bigwedge^3 \mathbb{R}^3$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2 (Dependent vectors vanish).** In $\mathbb{R}^2$, let$v_1 = (1, 2)$ and$v_2 = (2, 4) = 2v_1$. Then
$$v_1 \wedge v_2 = (e_1 + 2e_2) \wedge (2e_1 + 4e_2) = 2(e_1 \wedge e_1) + 4(e_1 \wedge e_2) + 4(e_2 \wedge e_1) + 8(e_2 \wedge e_2) = 0 + 4(e_1 \wedge e_2) - 4(e_1 \wedge e_2) + 0 = 0,$$
confirming dependence.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3 (Checking independence for three vectors).** Let $v_1 = e_1 + e_2$,$v_2 = e_2 + e_3$,$v_3 = e_1 + e_3$ in$\mathbb{R}^3$. Compute:
\begin{align*}
v_1 \wedge v_2 &= (e_1 + e_2) \wedge (e_2 + e_3) = e_1 \wedge e_2 + e_1 \wedge e_3 + e_2 \wedge e_3.
\end{align*}
Then $(v_1 \wedge v_2) \wedge v_3 = (e_1 \wedge e_2 + e_1 \wedge e_3 + e_2 \wedge e_3) \wedge (e_1 + e_3)$. Expanding:$e_1 \wedge e_2 \wedge e_1 = 0$,$e_1 \wedge e_2 \wedge e_3 \neq 0$,$e_1 \wedge e_3 \wedge e_1 = 0$,$e_1 \wedge e_3 \wedge e_3 = 0$,$e_2 \wedge e_3 \wedge e_1 = e_1 \wedge e_2 \wedge e_3 \cdot (-1)^2 = e_1 \wedge e_2 \wedge e_3$ (by two transpositions, sign$+1$... let us reorder:$e_2 \wedge e_3 \wedge e_1 = -e_2 \wedge e_1 \wedge e_3 = e_1 \wedge e_2 \wedge e_3$),$e_2 \wedge e_3 \wedge e_3 = 0$. Collecting:$e_1 \wedge e_2 \wedge e_3 + e_1 \wedge e_2 \wedge e_3 = 2(e_1 \wedge e_2 \wedge e_3) \neq 0$. So$v_1, v_2, v_3$ are linearly independent.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4 (The $k = n$ case and the determinant).** When$k = n$, the criterion says$v_1, \ldots, v_n$ are linearly independent if and only if$v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_n \neq 0 \in \bigwedge^n V$. Since$\dim \bigwedge^n V = 1$, we have$v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_n = c \cdot (e_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge e_n)$ for some scalar$c$. That scalar is exactly$c = \det[v_1 | \cdots | v_n]$ (the determinant of the matrix whose columns are the$v_i$). So the wedge product criterion is equivalent to the determinant criterion for invertibility of a square matrix.
\end{example}

The connection between wedge products, linear independence, and the determinant runs deep. In $\mathbb{R}^n$, linear independence of$k$ vectors$v_1, \ldots, v_k$ is equivalent to the$k \times n$ matrix with rows$v_i$ having rank$k$, which one typically checks by row reduction. The exterior product criterion bundles all of this into a single element in$\bigwedge^k V$ whose vanishing is the unique obstruction. When$k$ is small relative to$n$, this is often more efficient than row reduction.

More abstractly, $v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_k$ is a single object that "remembers" not only whether the vectors are independent, but also their$k$-dimensional span: two sets of$k$ independent vectors$\{v_1, \ldots, v_k\}$ and$\{w_1, \ldots, w_k\}$ have$v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_k = c \cdot w_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge w_k$ (a nonzero scalar multiple) if and only if they span the same$k$-dimensional subspace of$V$. This observation is the foundation of the **Plücker embedding**: the set of$k$-dimensional subspaces of$V$ (the Grassmannian$\mathrm{Gr}(k,V)$) embeds into the projectivization$\mathbb{P}(\bigwedge^k V)$ by sending a subspace to the line$[v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_k]$.

\begin{remark}
The same criterion holds over any field and for any $k \leq n$. Over an algebraically closed field, one can further characterize the decomposable (or **pure**) elements of$\bigwedge^k V$: these are exactly the elements of the form$v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_k$ (wedge products of$k$ vectors from$V$), and they satisfy the **Plücker relations** — a system of quadratic equations in the coordinates of$\bigwedge^k V$. Not every element of$\bigwedge^k V$ is decomposable for$2 \leq k \leq n-2$; a non-decomposable element does not correspond to any single$k$-dimensional subspace.
\end{remark}
