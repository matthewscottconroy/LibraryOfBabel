# 12.4.5 The Determinant as the Top Exterior Power $\bigwedge^n T$

The determinant of a linear operator $T: V \to V$ on an$n$-dimensional space has many equivalent definitions — as the product of eigenvalues, as the signed volume scaling factor, as the unique alternating multilinear function of the columns of a matrix that equals$1$ on the identity. The exterior algebra provides the most intrinsic and coordinate-free definition of all.

\begin{definition}
Let $V$ be an$n$-dimensional vector space and$T: V \to V$ a linear operator. The **determinant** of$T$ is the unique scalar$\det(T) \in F$ such that
$$(\bigwedge^n T)(v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_n) = \det(T) \cdot (v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_n)$$
for all $v_1, \ldots, v_n \in V$, where$\bigwedge^n T: \bigwedge^n V \to \bigwedge^n V$ is the linear map defined on pure tensors by$(\bigwedge^n T)(v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_n) = Tv_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge Tv_n$.
\end{definition}

The scalar $\det(T)$ is well-defined because$\dim \bigwedge^n V = 1$: any linear operator on a one-dimensional space is multiplication by a scalar. The definition makes no reference to matrices, bases, or explicit sums — it is completely intrinsic to$T$ and$V$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1 (Verification in coordinates).** Let $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$ be a basis and$A = (a^i{}_j)$ the matrix of$T$. Then$Te_j = \sum_i a^i{}_j e_i$, and
\begin{align*}
Te_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge Te_n &= \Bigl(\sum_{i_1} a^{i_1}{}_1 e_{i_1}\Bigr) \wedge \cdots \wedge \Bigl(\sum_{i_n} a^{i_n}{}_n e_{i_n}\Bigr) \\
&= \sum_{i_1, \ldots, i_n} a^{i_1}{}_1 \cdots a^{i_n}{}_n \, e_{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{i_n}.
\end{align*}
Any term with a repeated index vanishes. For terms with all distinct indices, $e_{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{i_n} = \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) \cdot e_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge e_n$ where$\sigma$ is the permutation$(i_1, \ldots, i_n)$. So the sum reduces to$\Bigl(\sum_{\sigma \in S_n} \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) \, a^{\sigma(1)}{}_1 \cdots a^{\sigma(n)}{}_n\Bigr) \cdot e_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge e_n$. The coefficient in parentheses is exactly the Leibniz formula for$\det(A)$, confirming that$\det(T) = \det(A)$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2 (Multiplicativity).** For two operators $S, T: V \to V$:
$$\bigwedge^n(ST) = \bigwedge^n S \circ \bigwedge^n T,$$
since $(ST)(v_1) \wedge \cdots \wedge (ST)(v_n) = S(Tv_1) \wedge \cdots \wedge S(Tv_n) = (\bigwedge^n S)(Tv_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge Tv_n)$. Applying$\bigwedge^n S = \det(S) \cdot \mathrm{id}$ and$\bigwedge^n T = \det(T) \cdot \mathrm{id}$, we get$\det(ST) = \det(S) \det(T)$. This is the cleanest proof of multiplicativity: it is immediate from the functorial composition law for the exterior power.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3 (Invertibility criterion).** $T$ is invertible if and only if$\det(T) \neq 0$. Proof:$\bigwedge^n T = \det(T) \cdot \mathrm{id}$. If$\det(T) \neq 0$, then$\bigwedge^n T$ is an isomorphism of$\bigwedge^n V$; since$T$ induces this isomorphism and$\dim \bigwedge^n V = 1 > 0$,$T$ itself must be injective (if$Tv = 0$ for some$v \neq 0$, extending$v$ to a basis gives$Tv \wedge \cdots = 0$, forcing$\det(T) = 0$). Conversely, if$\det(T) = 0$, then$Te_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge Te_n = 0$, which by Section 12.4.4 means$Te_1, \ldots, Te_n$ are linearly dependent, so$T$ is not injective.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4 (Volume scaling).** For $V = \mathbb{R}^n$ with the standard orientation,$\omega_0 = e_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge e_n$ is the unit volume form. The operator$T$ scales this form by$\det(T)$:$Te_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge Te_n = \det(T) \cdot e_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge e_n$. So$|\det(T)|$ is the factor by which$T$ scales$n$-dimensional volumes, and the sign of$\det(T)$ records whether$T$ preserves or reverses orientation. Geometrically: the image of the unit cube under$T$ has volume$|\det(T)|$.
\end{example}

The coordinate-free definition has theoretical advantages beyond elegance. It clarifies why $\det(T)$ is a well-defined property of the operator$T$ (not of any matrix representing$T$), why it is independent of the choice of basis, and why the formula$\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$ holds. The standard properties of the determinant follow naturally.

\begin{proposition}
The following properties hold for any linear operator $T: V \to V$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\det(\mathrm{id}) = 1$.
\item $\det(ST) = \det(S)\det(T)$.
\item $T$ is invertible if and only if$\det(T) \neq 0$, and in that case$\det(T^{-1}) = \det(T)^{-1}$.
\item $\det(cT) = c^n \det(T)$ for any scalar$c$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
All follow from the definition. (1) $\mathrm{id}$ acts trivially on$\bigwedge^n V$, so$\det(\mathrm{id}) = 1$. (2) Proved in Example 2. (3) If$T$ is invertible,$\det(T)\det(T^{-1}) = \det(TT^{-1}) = \det(\mathrm{id}) = 1$, so$\det(T^{-1}) = \det(T)^{-1}$. (4)$(cT)v_i = c(Tv_i)$, so$(cT)v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge (cT)v_n = c^n(Tv_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge Tv_n) = c^n \det(T) \cdot \omega$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The top exterior power $\bigwedge^n V$ also plays a central role in integration on manifolds. On an oriented$n$-manifold$M$, a nowhere-vanishing section of$\bigwedge^n T^*M$ is a **volume form** — it assigns an oriented volume to every$n$-dimensional parallelepiped of tangent vectors. The existence of such a form is equivalent to$M$ being orientable. The integral of an$n$-form over$M$ is then defined coordinate-free via the Stokes theorem framework. The determinant line$\bigwedge^n V$ is the algebraic prototype for this structure: it is the space of "scalings" of oriented volume on$V$.
\end{remark}
