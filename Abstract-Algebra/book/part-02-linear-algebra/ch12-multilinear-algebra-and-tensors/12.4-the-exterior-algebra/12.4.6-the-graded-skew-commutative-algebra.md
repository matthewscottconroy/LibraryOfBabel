# 12.4.6 The Graded Skew-Commutative Algebra $\bigwedge V$

The exterior algebra $\bigwedge V = \bigoplus_{k=0}^n \bigwedge^k V$ is not only a graded algebra but one with a characteristic commutativity rule: for homogeneous elements$\alpha \in \bigwedge^j V$ and$\beta \in \bigwedge^k V$,
$$\alpha \wedge \beta = (-1)^{jk} \beta \wedge \alpha.$$
This is called **graded commutativity** (or **graded skew-commutativity**, or **supercommutativity**). For $j = k = 1$ it reduces to the anti-commutativity$u \wedge v = -v \wedge u$ of ordinary vectors. For even-degree forms,$\alpha \wedge \beta = \beta \wedge \alpha$ when at least one of$j, k$ is even.

\begin{proposition}
For $\alpha \in \bigwedge^j V$ and$\beta \in \bigwedge^k V$,$\alpha \wedge \beta = (-1)^{jk} \beta \wedge \alpha$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
It suffices to verify this for pure tensors $\alpha = v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_j$ and$\beta = w_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge w_k$, and then extend by linearity. To move$\beta$ past$\alpha$, we must move each of$w_1, \ldots, w_k$ past all of$v_1, \ldots, v_j$. Moving a single$w_i$ past all$j$ vectors$v_1, \ldots, v_j$ requires$j$ transpositions, each introducing a sign$-1$, giving a factor of$(-1)^j$. Doing this for all$k$ factors$w_i$ gives a total sign of$(-1)^{jk}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
**Example 1 (Degree 1 and 2).** In $\bigwedge \mathbb{R}^4$, let$\alpha = e_1 \wedge e_2 \in \bigwedge^2 V$ and$\beta = e_3 \wedge e_4 \in \bigwedge^2 V$. Then$(-1)^{2 \cdot 2} = (-1)^4 = 1$, so$\alpha \wedge \beta = \beta \wedge \alpha$. Explicitly:$e_1 \wedge e_2 \wedge e_3 \wedge e_4 = e_3 \wedge e_4 \wedge e_1 \wedge e_2$. The move requires 4 transpositions ($e_3$ past$e_2$ and$e_1$, then$e_4$ past$e_2$ and$e_1$), giving$(-1)^4 = 1$. Indeed both are equal to$e_1 \wedge e_2 \wedge e_3 \wedge e_4$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2 (Degree 1 and 3).** Let $\alpha = e_1 \in \bigwedge^1 V$ and$\beta = e_2 \wedge e_3 \wedge e_4 \in \bigwedge^3 V$. Then$(-1)^{1 \cdot 3} = -1$, so$\alpha \wedge \beta = -\beta \wedge \alpha$. Explicitly:$e_1 \wedge e_2 \wedge e_3 \wedge e_4 = -(e_2 \wedge e_3 \wedge e_4 \wedge e_1) = -(e_2 \wedge e_3 \wedge e_4 \wedge e_1)$. Moving$e_1$ from position 4 to position 1 requires 3 transpositions, giving$(-1)^3 = -1$. Confirmed.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3 (Self-wedge of an even form).** For $\alpha \in \bigwedge^{2k} V$ (even degree),$\alpha \wedge \alpha = (-1)^{2k \cdot 2k} \alpha \wedge \alpha = \alpha \wedge \alpha$, so we cannot conclude$\alpha \wedge \alpha = 0$. Indeed, for$\alpha = e_1 \wedge e_2 + e_3 \wedge e_4 \in \bigwedge^2 \mathbb{R}^4$:
$$\alpha \wedge \alpha = (e_1 \wedge e_2 + e_3 \wedge e_4) \wedge (e_1 \wedge e_2 + e_3 \wedge e_4) = 0 + e_1 \wedge e_2 \wedge e_3 \wedge e_4 + e_3 \wedge e_4 \wedge e_1 \wedge e_2 + 0 = 2(e_1 \wedge e_2 \wedge e_3 \wedge e_4) \neq 0.$$
The vanishing $v \wedge v = 0$ holds only for odd-degree elements.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4 (Self-wedge of an odd form).** For $\alpha \in \bigwedge^{2k+1} V$ (odd degree),$\alpha \wedge \alpha = (-1)^{(2k+1)^2} \alpha \wedge \alpha = -\alpha \wedge \alpha$, which forces$2(\alpha \wedge \alpha) = 0$. Over a field of characteristic$\neq 2$, this gives$\alpha \wedge \alpha = 0$ for all odd-degree elements. In characteristic 2, this argument breaks down, and the exterior algebra behaves differently.
\end{example}

Graded skew-commutativity makes $\bigwedge V$ a **graded-commutative algebra** (also called a **supercommutative algebra** or a **commutative algebra in the category of graded modules**). This structure appears throughout modern algebra and geometry as a fundamental organizing principle.

The most significant geometric realization is the **algebra of differential forms**. On a smooth $n$-manifold$M$, the smooth sections of$\bigwedge^k T^*M$ are the **differential$k$-forms**$\Omega^k(M)$. Their wedge product satisfies the same graded-commutativity rule. The **exterior derivative**$d: \Omega^k(M) \to \Omega^{k+1}(M)$ is a degree-1 linear map satisfying:
\begin{itemize}
\item $d^2 = 0$ (nilpotency),
\item $d(\alpha \wedge \beta) = (d\alpha) \wedge \beta + (-1)^k \alpha \wedge (d\beta)$ (graded Leibniz rule).
\end{itemize}
An operator satisfying these two properties on a graded algebra is called a **derivation of degree 1**. The resulting cochain complex $(\Omega^\bullet(M), d)$ is the **de Rham complex**, and its cohomology$H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M) = \ker d / \mathrm{im}\, d$ is the **de Rham cohomology** — a topological invariant of$M$ that encodes its global shape purely through the algebraic structure of differential forms.

\begin{definition}
A **differential graded algebra (DGA)** is a graded algebra $A = \bigoplus_{k \geq 0} A_k$ together with a linear map$d: A_k \to A_{k+1}$ satisfying$d^2 = 0$ and the graded Leibniz rule$d(ab) = (da)b + (-1)^k a(db)$ for$a \in A_k$. The exterior algebra with$d = 0$ is a trivial DGA; the de Rham complex$(\Omega^\bullet(M), d)$ is a fundamental non-trivial example.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
The graded-commutativity rule $\alpha \wedge \beta = (-1)^{jk} \beta \wedge \alpha$ is the prototype for **superalgebra** in the sense of supersymmetric physics. If we declare elements of even degree "bosonic" and elements of odd degree "fermionic," then even-even and even-odd products commute, while odd-odd products anti-commute. This is exactly the algebraic structure underlying the algebra of fields in a supersymmetric quantum field theory. The Clifford algebra (a common generalization of the exterior algebra and the quaternion algebra) provides the algebraic foundation for spinors and the Dirac equation. Graded-commutativity is thus not merely a bookkeeping sign rule but the algebraic backbone of modern mathematical physics.
\end{remark}
