# 12.4 The Exterior Algebra

The exterior algebra $\Lambda(V)$ is the quotient of the tensor algebra $T(V)$ by the two-sided ideal generated by all elements of the form $v \otimes v$ for $v \in V$. The image of a pure tensor $v_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes v_k$ in this quotient is written $v_1 \wedge v_2 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_k$ and called the wedge product or exterior product. The defining relation $v \wedge v = 0$ immediately implies antisymmetry: $v \wedge w = -(w \wedge v)$ for all $v, w \in V$. More generally, swapping any two adjacent factors changes the sign of the wedge product, and if any two of the vectors $v_1, \ldots, v_k$ are equal then the product is zero. The exterior algebra thus encodes antisymmetry in the same universal way that the tensor algebra encodes no relations at all.

The $k$-th exterior power $\Lambda^k(V)$ is the degree-$k$ homogeneous part of $\Lambda(V)$. If $V$ has dimension $n$ with basis $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$, then a basis for $\Lambda^k(V)$ consists of all wedge products $e_{i_1} \wedge e_{i_2} \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{i_k}$ with $i_1 < i_2 < \cdots < i_k$. There are $\binom{n}{k}$ such products, giving $\dim \Lambda^k(V) = \binom{n}{k}$. In particular, $\dim \Lambda^n(V) = 1$ and $\Lambda^k(V) = 0$ for $k > n$. The single-dimensionality of $\Lambda^n(V)$ is the algebraic reason why the determinant is a scalar: a linear operator $T : V \to V$ acts on $\Lambda^n(V)$ by multiplication by a scalar, and that scalar is $\det(T)$.

The connection between exterior powers and linear independence is striking: vectors $v_1, \ldots, v_k$ are linearly independent if and only if $v_1 \wedge v_2 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_k \neq 0$. The proof is elegant — if any $v_i$ is a linear combination of the others, the wedge product vanishes because it becomes a sum of products each containing a repeated factor. This gives a clean algebraic criterion for linear independence that requires no coordinates and no row reduction. The exterior algebra also carries a graded algebra structure: the wedge product makes $\Lambda(V) = \bigoplus_{k=0}^n \Lambda^k(V)$ into a graded-commutative (or skew-commutative) algebra, satisfying $\alpha \wedge \beta = (-1)^{jk} \beta \wedge \alpha$ for $\alpha \in \Lambda^j(V)$ and $\beta \in \Lambda^k(V)$. This is the algebraic structure underlying differential forms in differential geometry.
