# 12.5.1 Symmetric Tensors; the Symmetric Product

Just as the exterior algebra arises by imposing anti-commutativity on $T(V)$, the symmetric algebra arises by imposing full commutativity. The idea is to quotient$T(V)$ by the smallest ideal that forces all products to commute.

\begin{definition}
The **symmetric algebra** $\mathrm{Sym}(V)$ is the quotient of the tensor algebra$T(V)$ by the two-sided ideal$I_{\mathrm{sym}}$ generated by all elements of the form$u \otimes v - v \otimes u$ for$u, v \in V$. The image of$v_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes v_k$ in$\mathrm{Sym}(V)$ is written$v_1 v_2 \cdots v_k$ (or$v_1 \vee v_2 \vee \cdots \vee v_k$ using the symmetric product$\vee$), and the defining relation is$uv = vu$ for all$u, v \in V$.
\end{definition}

Unlike the exterior algebra, where the vanishing relation $v \wedge v = 0$ was the key generating relation, here we impose commutativity directly. The element$v \cdot v = v^2$ is a perfectly good nonzero element of$\mathrm{Sym}^2(V)$; there is no nilpotency. This is the fundamental structural difference between the two quotients of$T(V)$.

The symmetric product is the algebraic encoding of **unordered multisets**: the element $v_1 v_2 \cdots v_k$ is completely symmetric under all permutations of its factors, by definition. Any permutation$\sigma \in S_k$ gives$v_{\sigma(1)} \cdots v_{\sigma(k)} = v_1 \cdots v_k$. This is to be contrasted with the exterior product, where$v_{\sigma(1)} \wedge \cdots \wedge v_{\sigma(k)} = \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) \cdot v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_k$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1 (Degree 2).** In $\mathrm{Sym}^2(V)$ with$V = \mathbb{R}^2$ and basis$\{e_1, e_2\}$: the elements$e_1^2$,$e_1 e_2 = e_2 e_1$, and$e_2^2$ form a basis of$\mathrm{Sym}^2(\mathbb{R}^2)$, giving dimension$3$. A general element is$a e_1^2 + b e_1 e_2 + c e_2^2$ for$a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}$. This corresponds to a homogeneous quadratic polynomial in two variables:$ax_1^2 + bx_1 x_2 + cx_2^2$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2 (Quadratic forms as symmetric tensors).** A quadratic form $Q(v) = v^T A v$ on$\mathbb{R}^n$ (with$A$ symmetric) corresponds to a symmetric bilinear form$B(u,v) = u^T A v$. Algebraically,$B \in (\mathbb{R}^n)^* \otimes_{\mathrm{sym}} (\mathbb{R}^n)^* = \mathrm{Sym}^2(V^*)$. The entries of the symmetric matrix$A$ are the components of this element of$\mathrm{Sym}^2(V^*)$ in the dual basis.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3 (Polarization preview).** Let $p: V \to F$ be the polynomial function$p(v) = B(v, v)$ for a symmetric bilinear form$B$. Expanding$p(u + v) - p(u) - p(v)$:
$$B(u+v, u+v) - B(u,u) - B(v,v) = 2B(u,v).$$
So $B(u,v) = \frac{1}{2}(p(u+v) - p(u) - p(v))$. This is the polarization identity: it recovers the bilinear form from the polynomial function. The symmetric algebra systematizes this recovery, and the full polarization for degree$k$ is the subject of Section 12.5.4.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4 (Riemannian metrics).** A Riemannian metric on a smooth manifold $M$ is a smoothly varying assignment of a positive definite symmetric bilinear form to each tangent space$T_p M$. Algebraically, it is a smooth section of the bundle$\mathrm{Sym}^2(T^*M)$ — a degree-2 symmetric tensor field. In local coordinates, it is written$g = g_{ij}\, dx^i \otimes dx^j$ (summed), but the symmetry$g_{ij} = g_{ji}$ means it is more naturally viewed as an element of$\mathrm{Sym}^2(T^*M)$, not all of$T^*M \otimes T^*M$.
\end{example}

The symmetric product is the algebraic foundation for polynomial functions on a vector space. The connection is as follows: given a basis $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$ of$V$, the symmetric algebra$\mathrm{Sym}(V)$ is isomorphic to the polynomial ring$F[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$, with$e_i$ playing the role of$x_i$. This isomorphism will be developed in Section 12.5.3. The key point for now is that commutativity of the symmetric product corresponds exactly to commutativity of polynomial multiplication.

The symmetric and exterior algebras are in a precise duality: $\mathrm{Sym}(V)$ is the "bosonic" algebra (associated to particles obeying Bose–Einstein statistics, whose many-particle states are symmetric under particle exchange), while$\bigwedge V$ is the "fermionic" algebra (associated to particles obeying Fermi–Dirac statistics, whose many-particle states are antisymmetric under particle exchange). In mathematics, this duality is made precise by the **Koszul complex**, in which the exterior algebra acts on the symmetric algebra by differentiation, providing a resolution of the residue field of a polynomial ring.

\begin{remark}
The symmetric algebra satisfies a universal property analogous to that of $T(V)$:$\mathrm{Sym}(V)$ is the **free commutative algebra** on$V$. That is, given any commutative$F$-algebra$A$ and any linear map$\varphi: V \to A$, there is a unique commutative algebra homomorphism$\tilde{\varphi}: \mathrm{Sym}(V) \to A$ extending$\varphi$. This is the algebraic fact underlying the interpretation of$\mathrm{Sym}(V)$ as the polynomial ring on$V$: the polynomial ring is the free commutative algebra on$n$ generators (where$n = \dim V$), and the choice of basis gives the identification of the generators with variables.
\end{remark}
