# 12.5.2 $\mathrm{Sym}^k(V)$ and Its Basis; Dimension Formula

The $k$-th symmetric power$\mathrm{Sym}^k(V)$ is the degree-$k$ component of the symmetric algebra: the subspace spanned by all symmetric$k$-fold products$v_1 v_2 \cdots v_k$ with$v_i \in V$. Since the product is commutative, the only information in a monomial is the multiset of its factors — the order does not matter. This combinatorial observation directly determines the basis and dimension.

\begin{theorem}
Let $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$ be a basis of$V$. A basis of$\mathrm{Sym}^k(V)$ consists of all monomials
$$e_{i_1} e_{i_2} \cdots e_{i_k} \quad \text{with } 1 \leq i_1 \leq i_2 \leq \cdots \leq i_k \leq n$$
(non-decreasing sequences). The dimension of $\mathrm{Sym}^k(V)$ is
$$\dim \mathrm{Sym}^k(V) = \binom{n + k - 1}{k}.$$
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
**Spanning.** Any monomial $v_1 \cdots v_k$ with$v_i = \sum_j a^j_i e_j$ expands by multilinearity into monomials in the basis vectors. Since multiplication is commutative, each such monomial$e_{i_1} \cdots e_{i_k}$ can be reordered to a non-decreasing sequence without any sign change (unlike the exterior case). Hence$\mathrm{Sym}^k(V)$ is spanned by non-decreasing monomials.

**Independence.** Define, for each non-decreasing tuple $(i_1, \ldots, i_k)$, a symmetric multilinear functional that evaluates to$1$ on$e_{i_1} \cdots e_{i_k}$ and to$0$ on all other non-decreasing basis monomials; such functionals exist by the universal property of$\mathrm{Sym}^k(V)$. This shows the spanning set is independent.

**Counting.** A non-decreasing $k$-tuple from$\{1, \ldots, n\}$ is the same as a$k$-element multiset from$\{1, \ldots, n\}$, counted by the stars-and-bars formula:$\binom{n + k - 1}{k}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
**Example 1 ($\mathrm{Sym}^2(\mathbb{R}^3)$).** For$n = 3$,$k = 2$:$\binom{3+2-1}{2} = \binom{4}{2} = 6$. The basis elements are$e_1^2$,$e_1 e_2$,$e_1 e_3$,$e_2^2$,$e_2 e_3$,$e_3^2$ — corresponding to the$6$ independent entries of a$3 \times 3$ symmetric matrix. This confirms that symmetric bilinear forms on$\mathbb{R}^3$ are parametrized by$6$ real numbers.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2 ($\mathrm{Sym}^3(\mathbb{R}^2)$).** For$n = 2$,$k = 3$:$\binom{2+3-1}{3} = \binom{4}{3} = 4$. Basis:$e_1^3$,$e_1^2 e_2$,$e_1 e_2^2$,$e_2^3$ — these are the four monomials of degree 3 in two variables, confirming$\mathrm{Sym}^3(\mathbb{R}^2) \cong F[x_1, x_2]_3$ (homogeneous cubic polynomials in 2 variables).
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3 (Comparison with exterior powers).** For $V = \mathbb{R}^4$, compare:
\begin{align*}
\dim \bigwedge^2 V &= \binom{4}{2} = 6, \\
\dim \mathrm{Sym}^2 V &= \binom{5}{2} = 10, \\
\dim \bigwedge^3 V &= \binom{4}{3} = 4, \\
\dim \mathrm{Sym}^3 V &= \binom{6}{3} = 20.
\end{align*}
The symmetric power grows much faster with $k$:$\dim \mathrm{Sym}^k V = O(k^{n-1})$, while$\dim \bigwedge^k V = 0$ for$k > n$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4 (Total dimension).** The total dimension of $\mathrm{Sym}(V) = \bigoplus_{k \geq 0} \mathrm{Sym}^k(V)$ is infinite (since$\binom{n+k-1}{k} > 0$ for all$k \geq 0$), consistent with$\mathrm{Sym}(V) \cong F[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ being an infinite-dimensional vector space. By contrast,$\bigwedge V$ is finite-dimensional of dimension$2^n$ (Section 12.4.3). The difference reflects the fundamental contrast between nilpotency ($e^2 = 0$ in$\bigwedge V$, which kills high degrees) and idempotency ($e^k \neq 0$ for all$k$ in$\mathrm{Sym}(V)$, allowing infinite growth).
\end{example}

The dimension formula $\binom{n+k-1}{k}$ has a natural interpretation: it counts the number of ways to place$k$ indistinguishable balls into$n$ labeled boxes (stars and bars), which is the same as choosing a monomial of degree$k$ in$n$ commuting variables. This is precisely the dimension of the space of degree-$k$ homogeneous polynomials in$n$ variables, confirming the isomorphism$\mathrm{Sym}^k(V) \cong F[x_1, \ldots, x_n]_k$.

\begin{remark}
In representation theory, the symmetric powers $\mathrm{Sym}^k(V)$ of a representation$V$ of a group$G$ carry natural$G$-actions (by acting on each factor). For$G = \mathrm{GL}(V)$, the symmetric powers are the "polynomial representations" of highest weight$k$ (in the one-dimensional case). For$G = \mathrm{SL}_2(F)$, the symmetric powers$\mathrm{Sym}^k(\mathbb{F}^2)$ are the irreducible representations of dimension$k+1$, and they are the prototype for the representation theory of all semisimple Lie algebras. The interplay between the symmetric algebra, polynomial functions, and highest-weight theory is one of the central themes of geometric representation theory.
\end{remark}
