# 12.5.3 The Symmetric Algebra $\mathrm{Sym}(V) \cong F[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$

Once a basis $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$ of$V$ is chosen, the symmetric algebra$\mathrm{Sym}(V)$ becomes isomorphic to the polynomial ring$F[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$, with$e_i$ playing the role of the variable$x_i$. The isomorphism sends the symmetric product$e_{i_1} e_{i_2} \cdots e_{i_k}$ to the monomial$x_{i_1} x_{i_2} \cdots x_{i_k}$, and extends linearly and multiplicatively to the entire algebra.

\begin{theorem}
Let $V$ be an$n$-dimensional vector space over$F$ with basis$\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$. There is a canonical algebra isomorphism$\mathrm{Sym}(V) \cong F[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ sending$e_i \mapsto x_i$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By the universal property of $\mathrm{Sym}(V)$ (it is the free commutative algebra on$V$), the linear map$\varphi: V \to F[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ sending$e_i \mapsto x_i$ extends uniquely to a commutative algebra homomorphism$\tilde{\varphi}: \mathrm{Sym}(V) \to F[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$. This map sends each non-decreasing monomial$e_{i_1} \cdots e_{i_k}$ to$x_{i_1} \cdots x_{i_k}$. Since the non-decreasing monomials in the$e_i$ form a basis of$\mathrm{Sym}(V)$ and the corresponding monomials in$x_1, \ldots, x_n$ form a basis of$F[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$, the map$\tilde{\varphi}$ is a bijective algebra homomorphism, hence an isomorphism.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
**Example 1 ($n = 1$).** For$V = F$ (one-dimensional, spanned by$e_1$):$\mathrm{Sym}(V) \cong F[x]$, the polynomial ring in one variable. The graded components are$\mathrm{Sym}^k(F) \cong F$ for all$k \geq 0$, matching$\dim F[x]_k = 1$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2 ($n = 2$).** For$V = \mathbb{R}^2$ with basis$\{e_1, e_2\}$:$\mathrm{Sym}(\mathbb{R}^2) \cong \mathbb{R}[x_1, x_2]$. The degree-2 component has basis$\{e_1^2, e_1 e_2, e_2^2\}$ mapping to$\{x_1^2, x_1 x_2, x_2^2\}$. A general element$2e_1^2 - 3e_1 e_2 + e_2^2$ maps to the polynomial$2x_1^2 - 3x_1 x_2 + x_2^2$, which represents a (degenerate) quadratic form with matrix$\begin{pmatrix}2 & -3/2 \\ -3/2 & 1\end{pmatrix}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3 (Change of basis changes the polynomial ring).** Suppose we change basis from $\{e_1, e_2\}$ to$\{f_1, f_2\} = \{e_1 + e_2, e_1 - e_2\}$. The isomorphism$\mathrm{Sym}(V) \cong F[y_1, y_2]$ using the new basis$\{f_i\}$ sends$f_1 \mapsto y_1$ and$f_2 \mapsto y_2$. The two polynomial rings$F[x_1, x_2]$ and$F[y_1, y_2]$ are related by$x_1 = \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2}$ and$x_2 = \frac{y_1 - y_2}{2}$, a linear change of variables. Thus the two identifications give isomorphic but "different-looking" polynomial rings; the symmetric algebra$\mathrm{Sym}(V)$ itself is the basis-independent object.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4 (Symmetric algebra of the dual).** The symmetric algebra of the dual space $\mathrm{Sym}(V^*)$ is particularly important: an element$f \in V^*$ acts on$V$ as a linear function, and a product$f_1 \cdots f_k \in \mathrm{Sym}^k(V^*)$ acts as a degree-$k$ polynomial function$V \to F$ by$v \mapsto f_1(v) \cdots f_k(v)$. In coordinates with dual basis$\{e^1, \ldots, e^n\}$:$\mathrm{Sym}(V^*) \cong F[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ where$x_i = e^i$, and$x_i(v) = v_i$ (the$i$-th coordinate). This is why the polynomial ring naturally acts on$V$: it is the symmetric algebra of the coordinate functions.
\end{example}

This identification is more than a notational convenience. It explains why polynomials in $n$ variables are the right algebraic object for describing polynomial functions on$V$: the polynomial ring$F[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ is the symmetric algebra of the$n$-dimensional coordinate space, and the commutativity of polynomial multiplication is the algebraic expression of the symmetry of the symmetric product.

The crucial caveat is that the isomorphism $\mathrm{Sym}(V) \cong F[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ depends on the choice of basis. The object$\mathrm{Sym}(V)$ is intrinsic to$V$; the polynomial ring with specific variable names requires coordinates. In algebraic geometry, the symmetric algebra$\mathrm{Sym}(V^*)$ is the coordinate ring of the affine space$V$ (thought of as a geometric object), and changing basis corresponds to a linear automorphism of this ring. The projective variety$\mathrm{Proj}(\mathrm{Sym}(V^*))$ is the projective space$\mathbb{P}(V)$, and the geometric study of subvarieties of$\mathbb{P}(V)$ defined by homogeneous polynomial equations is the subject of projective algebraic geometry.

\begin{remark}
The close relationship between $\mathrm{Sym}(V)$ and$F[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ has a non-commutative generalization. The tensor algebra$T(V)$, which imposes no commutativity, is isomorphic to the free noncommutative polynomial algebra$F\langle x_1, \ldots, x_n \rangle$ (once a basis is chosen). Both the symmetric and exterior algebras arise as quotients of this free algebra by imposing commutativity or anti-commutativity, respectively. In representation theory, the Weyl algebra (quotient of$T(V \oplus V^*)$ by the canonical commutation relations) plays the same role for the harmonic analysis of symmetric spaces that the polynomial ring plays for polynomial functions.
\end{remark}
