# 12.5.4 Polarization: Symmetric Functions and Polynomial Functions

A degree-$k$ polynomial function$p: V \to F$ and a symmetric$k$-linear form$\beta: V^k \to F$ are related but distinct objects. The **polarization** procedure recovers the multilinear form from the polynomial function, establishing (under mild characteristic hypotheses) a canonical bijection between the two.

\begin{definition}
Given a symmetric $k$-linear form$\beta: V^{\times k} \to F$, the **associated polynomial function** is$p(v) = \beta(v, v, \ldots, v)$ (restricting all$k$ arguments to the same vector). Given a degree-$k$ homogeneous polynomial function$p: V \to F$, the **polarization** of$p$ is the symmetric$k$-linear form$\hat{\beta}$ defined by
$$\hat{\beta}(v_1, \ldots, v_k) = \frac{1}{k!} \sum_{\emptyset \neq S \subseteq \{1,\ldots,k\}} (-1)^{k - |S|} p\!\left(\sum_{i \in S} v_i\right).$$
\end{definition}

The polarization formula uses inclusion-exclusion to "separate" the $k$ independent arguments of the multilinear form from the diagonal evaluation that produced$p$. One verifies directly that$\hat{\beta}$ is symmetric (by symmetry of the formula in$v_1, \ldots, v_k$) and$k$-linear (by expanding$p$'s linearity in each argument), and that$\hat{\beta}(v, \ldots, v) = p(v)$.

\begin{theorem}
Over a field $F$ of characteristic$0$ or characteristic$> k$, the maps$\beta \mapsto p$ (restriction to the diagonal) and$p \mapsto \hat{\beta}$ (polarization) are mutually inverse bijections between:
\begin{itemize}
\item symmetric $k$-linear forms$\beta: V^k \to F$, and
\item degree-$k$ homogeneous polynomial functions$p: V \to F$.
\end{itemize}
In particular, $\mathrm{Sym}^k(V^*)$ (which parametrizes symmetric$k$-linear forms on$V$) is canonically isomorphic to the space of degree-$k$ polynomial functions on$V$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{example}
**Example 1 ($k = 2$).** For a symmetric bilinear form$B: V \times V \to F$, the diagonal restriction$p(v) = B(v,v)$ is a quadratic polynomial function. The polarization recovers$B$:
$$\hat{B}(u,v) = \frac{1}{2}\bigl(p(u+v) - p(u) - p(v)\bigr) = \frac{1}{2}\bigl(B(u+v,u+v) - B(u,u) - B(v,v)\bigr) = B(u,v).$$
The polarization of $p(v) = v_1^2 + v_1 v_2 + v_2^2$ (for$v = (v_1, v_2) \in \mathbb{R}^2$) gives the symmetric bilinear form$B(u,v) = u_1 v_1 + \frac{1}{2}(u_1 v_2 + u_2 v_1) + u_2 v_2$, with matrix$\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1/2 \\ 1/2 & 1\end{pmatrix}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2 ($k = 3$).** For$p: V \to F$ a cubic polynomial, the polarization formula involves$2^3 - 1 = 7$ terms. For$p(v) = v_1^3$ (with$v = (v_1, v_2) \in \mathbb{R}^2$):
$$\hat{\beta}(u,v,w) = \frac{1}{6}\bigl(p(u+v+w) - p(u+v) - p(u+w) - p(v+w) + p(u) + p(v) + p(w)\bigr).$$
Computing: $(u_1+v_1+w_1)^3 - (u_1+v_1)^3 - (u_1+w_1)^3 - (v_1+w_1)^3 + u_1^3 + v_1^3 + w_1^3 = 6 u_1 v_1 w_1$. So$\hat{\beta}(u,v,w) = u_1 v_1 w_1$, which is indeed a symmetric trilinear form.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3 (Characteristic $p$ failure).** Over$F = \mathbb{F}_p$ (a field of characteristic$p$), the polarization formula for$k = p$ involves$1/p! = 1/0$ — a division by zero. Correspondingly, the map$\beta \mapsto p$ is not injective: the symmetric$p$-linear form$\beta(v_1, \ldots, v_p) = f(v_1)\cdots f(v_p)$ (for any linear functional$f$) has diagonal restriction$p(v) = f(v)^p = f^{(p)}(v)$ (the Frobenius), which is also the diagonal restriction of the zero form (since$f(v)^p = f^{(p)}(v)$ is the Frobenius endomorphism applied to$f$, not a symmetric bilinear form). The bijection genuinely fails.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4 (Symmetric functions).** The elementary symmetric polynomials $e_k(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \sum_{i_1 < \cdots < i_k} x_{i_1} \cdots x_{i_k}$ and power sum polynomials$p_k = \sum_i x_i^k$ are related by Newton's identities:
$$p_k - e_1 p_{k-1} + e_2 p_{k-2} - \cdots + (-1)^{k-1} e_{k-1} p_1 + (-1)^k k e_k = 0.$$
This is a polarization-type identity: the power sums $p_k$ (diagonal restrictions of the diagonal bilinear form) determine the elementary symmetric polynomials (which parametrize the symmetric multilinear forms) and vice versa. Over$\mathbb{Q}$, each can be expressed in terms of the other.
\end{example}

The practical upshot in characteristic $0$ is that symmetric tensors in$\mathrm{Sym}^k(V^*)$ and degree-$k$ polynomial functions$V \to F$ are the same thing, related by polarization. This identification is used constantly in representation theory — where highest-weight vectors of symmetric power representations label polynomial invariants under group actions — and in differential geometry, where jet prolongations and Taylor expansions are expressed in symmetric tensor terms.

\begin{remark}
The relationship between symmetric functions and polynomial functions is also the bridge to **invariant theory**: a polynomial function $p: V \to F$ is$G$-invariant (i.e.,$p(g \cdot v) = p(v)$ for all$g \in G$) if and only if the corresponding symmetric multilinear form$\hat{\beta}$ is$G$-invariant. The ring of$G$-invariant polynomial functions on$V$ is therefore the ring$\mathrm{Sym}(V^*)^G$ of$G$-invariant elements in the symmetric algebra of the dual. For example, the ring of invariant polynomials on$V = \mathbb{R}^n$ under$G = O(n)$ is$\mathbb{R}[r^2]$ (generated by the single invariant$r^2 = x_1^2 + \cdots + x_n^2$), reflecting the fact that the only$O(n)$-invariant quantity is the Euclidean distance from the origin. Invariant theory is the study of such rings of invariants, and polarization is its foundational tool.
\end{remark}
