# 12.6.1 Upper and Lower Indices; Contravariant and Covariant

The abstract definition of tensors as elements of iterated tensor products of $V$ and$V^*$ takes on a more explicit character when we work in coordinates. Physics and differential geometry have developed a powerful notational system for this, built around the placement of indices — upper or lower — and their transformation behavior under change of basis.

\begin{definition}
A **tensor of type $(p, q)$** is an element of$V^{\otimes p} \otimes (V^*)^{\otimes q}$: it has$p$ "vector slots" (taking covector inputs) and$q$ "covector slots" (taking vector inputs). In a basis$\{e_i\}$ of$V$ with dual basis$\{e^j\}$ of$V^*$, such a tensor is written with$p$ upper indices and$q$ lower indices:
$$T^{i_1 \cdots i_p}{}_{j_1 \cdots j_q}.$$
The upper indices are called **contravariant** and the lower indices are called **covariant**.
\end{definition}

The terminology "contravariant" and "covariant" reflects how the components of a tensor transform under a change of basis. If we replace the basis $\{e_i\}$ by$\{\tilde{e}_i\}$ with$\tilde{e}_i = P^j{}_i e_j$ (where$P = (P^j{}_i)$ is an invertible matrix), then:
\begin{itemize}
\item Components with **upper indices** transform by $(P^{-1})$: they transform "against" the direction of the basis change.
\item Components with **lower indices** transform by $P$: they transform "with" the basis change.
\end{itemize}
This is why upper-index components are called **contravariant** (contra = against) and lower-index components are called **covariant** (co = with).

\begin{example}
**Example 1 (Vectors: type $(1,0)$).** A vector$v = v^i e_i$ has components$v^i$ with one upper index. Under the basis change$\tilde{e}_i = P^j{}_i e_j$, the vector$v$ is unchanged but its components transform:$v = v^i e_i = v^i (P^{-1})^k{}_i \tilde{e}_k$, so$\tilde{v}^k = (P^{-1})^k{}_i v^i$. Upper-index components transform by$P^{-1}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2 (Covectors: type $(0,1)$).** A covector (linear functional)$\omega = \omega_j e^j$ has components$\omega_j$ with one lower index. Since the dual basis transforms as$\tilde{e}^i = P^i{}_j e^j$, a covector with$\omega = \omega_j e^j = \tilde{\omega}_k \tilde{e}^k$ gives$\tilde{\omega}_k = P^j{}_k \omega_j$. Lower-index components transform by$P$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3 (Linear operators: type $(1,1)$).** A linear operator$T: V \to V$ has components$T^i{}_j$ (one upper, one lower index) with respect to any basis:$T(e_j) = T^i{}_j e_i$. Under the basis change$\tilde{e}_i = P^k{}_i e_k$, the components transform as$\tilde{T}^i{}_j = (P^{-1})^i{}_k T^k{}_l P^l{}_j$ — one application of$P^{-1}$ for the upper index and one of$P$ for the lower index. This is precisely the similarity transformation$\tilde{A} = P^{-1} A P$ for a matrix.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4 (The metric tensor: type $(0,2)$).** A Riemannian metric$g$ has components$g_{ij}$ with two lower indices:$g_{ij} = g(e_i, e_j)$. Under basis change$\tilde{e}_i = P^k{}_i e_k$:$\tilde{g}_{ij} = g(\tilde{e}_i, \tilde{e}_j) = g(P^k{}_i e_k, P^l{}_j e_l) = P^k{}_i P^l{}_j g_{kl}$. Both lower indices transform by$P$. In matrix notation:$\tilde{G} = P^T G P$, which is the congruence transformation of a symmetric matrix.
\end{example}

To see why the upper/lower distinction matters: if we confuse a vector $v^i$ (upper index) with a covector$\omega_i$ (lower index), we will compute the wrong transformation behavior. In relativity, the four-velocity$u^\mu$ is contravariant while the electromagnetic potential$A_\mu$ is covariant, and mixing them yields a quantity that transforms neither as a vector nor a covector — it is physically meaningless. The index placement system is a built-in type system that enforces physical and geometric consistency.

More concretely, the distinction between contravariant and covariant is precisely the distinction between elements of $V$ and elements of$V^*$. A vector$v \in V$ is a contravariant object (its components transform by$P^{-1}$); a covector$f \in V^*$ is a covariant object (its components transform by$P$). A general$(p,q)$-tensor combines both. The contraction of a contravariant index with a covariant index (summing$T^i{}_i$ over$i$) produces a scalar — a coordinate-independent number — precisely because the$P$ and$P^{-1}$ transformations cancel. This cancellation is the algebraic foundation for coordinate independence in tensor calculus.

\begin{remark}
An inner product $g$ on$V$ provides a canonical isomorphism$V \cong V^*$ via$v \mapsto g(v, \cdot)$. In components:$v_i = g_{ij} v^j$ (lowering an index) and$v^i = g^{ij} v_j$ (raising an index), where$g^{ij}$ are the components of the inverse metric. This "musical isomorphism" between$V$ and$V^*$ is why, in Euclidean space with$g_{ij} = \delta_{ij}$, physicists often blur the distinction between vectors and covectors:$v^i = v_i$. In Riemannian or pseudo-Riemannian geometry (where$g_{ij} \neq \delta_{ij}$), this distinction is essential — for instance, in special relativity,$g_{\mu\nu} = \mathrm{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$ and$u^\mu \neq u_\mu$.
\end{remark}
