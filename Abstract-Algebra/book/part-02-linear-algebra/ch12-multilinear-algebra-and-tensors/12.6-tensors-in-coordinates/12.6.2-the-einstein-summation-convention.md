# 12.6.2 The Einstein Summation Convention

Tensor calculations involve a great many summations over indices. Einstein's summation convention is a notational device that suppresses the explicit summation symbol, making formulas significantly more compact and easier to read once the convention is internalized.

\begin{definition}[Einstein Summation Convention]
Whenever the same index letter appears exactly twice in a monomial expression — once as an upper index and once as a lower index — that index is implicitly summed over its full range $1, \ldots, n$ (or$0, \ldots, n-1$ in relativistic contexts). The repeated index is called a **dummy index** or **summation index**.
\end{definition}

For example: the matrix-vector product $y^i = A^i{}_j x^j$ means$y^i = \sum_{j=1}^n A^i{}_j x^j$; the trace$A^i{}_i$ means$\sum_{i=1}^n A^i{}_i = \mathrm{tr}(A)$; and the contraction$T^{ij}{}_j$ means$\sum_{j=1}^n T^{ij}{}_j$. The convention applies term by term in a sum; each term can have at most one pair of repeated indices for any given index name.

\begin{example}
**Example 1 (Matrix product).** The $(i,k)$-entry of the product$AB$ of two matrices is$(AB)^i{}_k = A^i{}_j B^j{}_k$. The index$j$ is a dummy index (appears once up, once down) and is summed over$j = 1, \ldots, n$. The free indices are$i$ (upper) and$k$ (lower). This is the standard matrix multiplication formula written without$\sum$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2 (Inner product and norm).** In $\mathbb{R}^n$ with the Euclidean metric$g_{ij} = \delta_{ij}$: the inner product$\langle u, v \rangle = g_{ij} u^i v^j = \delta_{ij} u^i v^j = u^i v_i = \sum_i u^i v^i$ (where$v_i = g_{ij} v^j = \delta_{ij} v^j = v^i$ by the flat metric). The norm squared is$\|v\|^2 = g_{ij} v^i v^j = v^i v_i$. In a general Riemannian metric:$\|v\|^2 = g_{ij} v^i v^j = g_{\mu\nu} v^\mu v^\nu$ in four-dimensional notation.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3 (Gradient and divergence).** In $\mathbb{R}^n$, the divergence of a vector field$X = X^i \partial_i$ is$\mathrm{div}(X) = \partial_i X^i$ (summed over$i$), where$\partial_i = \partial/\partial x^i$ acts with a lower index and$X^i$ carries an upper index. The gradient of a scalar$f$ is$\nabla f = (\partial_i f) e^i$, with lower-index components$\partial_i f = \partial f/\partial x^i$. The Laplacian$\Delta f = \partial_i \partial^i f = g^{ij} \partial_i \partial_j f$ uses the inverse metric$g^{ij}$ to raise one index.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4 (Christoffel symbols).** In Riemannian geometry, the Christoffel symbols $\Gamma^k{}_{ij}$ (a$(1,2)$-tensor-like object, though not strictly a tensor) encode the Levi-Civita connection. The covariant derivative of a vector field is$\nabla_j X^i = \partial_j X^i + \Gamma^i{}_{jk} X^k$. Here$k$ is a dummy index (once up in$X^k$, once down in$\Gamma^i{}_{jk}$), while$i$ and$j$ are free. The geodesic equation is$\frac{d^2 x^i}{dt^2} + \Gamma^i{}_{jk} \frac{dx^j}{dt} \frac{dx^k}{dt} = 0$, with$j$ and$k$ each summed over all values.
\end{example}

Notice the deliberate asymmetry in the convention: a repeated index must appear once upstairs and once downstairs. An index appearing twice in the same position (two upper or two lower) is either an error or a context where the convention is explicitly suspended. This asymmetry is not arbitrary — it encodes the pairing between $V$ and$V^*$: a contraction pairs a contravariant slot with a covariant slot, producing a coordinate-independent scalar, while summing two upper or two lower indices gives a coordinate-dependent quantity.

Free indices — those appearing exactly once in each term — represent one component at a time, and an equation must have the same free indices in the same positions on both sides. For instance, $T^i{}_j = S^i{}_j + R^i{}_j$ is valid (matching free index structure on both sides), while$T^i{}_j = S^j{}_i$ would be an error (the free indices are in different positions). These rules serve as a built-in consistency check: an equation that violates index-placement rules cannot represent a coordinate-independent tensorial statement.

\begin{remark}
The Einstein convention was introduced by Einstein in his 1916 paper on general relativity and is now standard in differential geometry, continuum mechanics, and theoretical physics. Its power lies in the fact that once one is fluent in the convention, the structure of a calculation is entirely captured by the index pattern: one can check correctness by inspection without computing any sums explicitly. A sum like $T^{ij}{}_k R^k{}_{lm}$ contracts two tensors of type$(2,1)$ and$(1,2)$ on the shared index$k$, producing a$(3,2)$-tensor — the index structure tells you everything about the type of the result before any computation begins.
\end{remark}
