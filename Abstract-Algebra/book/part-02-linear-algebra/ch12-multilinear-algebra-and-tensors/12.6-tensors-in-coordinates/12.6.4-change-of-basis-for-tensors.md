# 12.6.4 Change of Basis for Tensors

A central consistency requirement for the tensor index formalism is that physical and geometric quantities must be independent of the coordinate system used to compute them, even though their components change when the basis changes. The transformation laws for tensor components under a change of basis make this consistency explicit and provide a complete description of how tensor components behave.

**Setup.** Suppose we change from a basis $\{e_i\}$ of$V$ to a new basis$\{\tilde{e}_i\}$, related by
$$\tilde{e}_i = P^j{}_i e_j$$
for some invertible matrix $P = (P^j{}_i)$. The inverse relation is$e_i = (P^{-1})^j{}_i \tilde{e}_j$.

\begin{proposition}[Transformation Laws]
Under the basis change $\tilde{e}_i = P^j{}_i e_j$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A vector $v = v^i e_i = \tilde{v}^i \tilde{e}_i$ transforms as$\tilde{v}^i = (P^{-1})^i{}_j v^j$ (contravariant: transforms by$P^{-1}$).
\item A covector $\omega = \omega_i e^i = \tilde{\omega}_i \tilde{e}^i$ transforms as$\tilde{\omega}_i = P^j{}_i \omega_j$ (covariant: transforms by$P$).
\item A $(p,q)$-tensor$T^{i_1 \cdots i_p}{}_{j_1 \cdots j_q}$ transforms as
$$\tilde{T}^{i_1 \cdots i_p}{}_{j_1 \cdots j_q} = (P^{-1})^{i_1}{}_{k_1} \cdots (P^{-1})^{i_p}{}_{k_p} \, P^{l_1}{}_{j_1} \cdots P^{l_q}{}_{j_q} \, T^{k_1 \cdots k_p}{}_{l_1 \cdots l_q}.$$
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(1) Since $v = v^i e_i$ and$e_i = (P^{-1})^j{}_i \tilde{e}_j$, we get$v = v^i (P^{-1})^j{}_i \tilde{e}_j$, so$\tilde{v}^j = (P^{-1})^j{}_i v^i$. (2) The dual basis transforms as$\tilde{e}^i = P^i{}_j e^j$ (since$\tilde{e}^i(\tilde{e}_j) = \delta^i_j$ forces$P^i{}_k e^k(P^l{}_j e_l) = P^i{}_k P^l{}_j \delta^k_l = P^i{}_l P^l{}_j = \delta^i_j$). So$\omega = \omega_i e^i = \omega_i (P^{-1})^i{}_k \tilde{e}^k$... wait:$e^i = (P^{-1})^i{}_k \tilde{e}^k$ since$\tilde{e}^k = P^k{}_i e^i$ implies$e^i = (P^{-1})^i{}_k \tilde{e}^k$. Thus$\omega = \omega_i (P^{-1})^i{}_k \tilde{e}^k$, giving$\tilde{\omega}_k = \omega_i (P^{-1})^i{}_k$... The result follows by applying (1) and (2) to each index independently for (3).
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
**Example 1 (2D rotation).** Let $P = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$ be a rotation of the basis by angle$\theta$. Then$P^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$. A vector$v = (v^1, v^2)^T$ in the original basis has components$\tilde{v}^i = (P^{-1})^i{}_j v^j = (P^{-1} v)^i$ in the new basis. A covector$\omega = (\omega_1, \omega_2)$ transforms as$\tilde{\omega}_i = P^j{}_i \omega_j$. For example, the covector representing the$x_1$-coordinate functional has$\omega = (1, 0)$, and after rotation$\tilde{\omega} = (P^T)^j{}_i \omega_j = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ in the new basis.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2 (The metric in the new basis).** For the Euclidean metric $g_{ij} = \delta_{ij}$ and a rotation$P$ (satisfying$P^T P = I$, so$P^T = P^{-1}$):$\tilde{g}_{ij} = P^k{}_i P^l{}_j g_{kl} = P^k{}_i P^k{}_j = (P^T P)_{ij} = \delta_{ij}$. The Euclidean metric is preserved under orthogonal basis changes, consistent with the fact that rotations preserve distances. For a non-orthogonal basis change,$\tilde{g}_{ij} \neq \delta_{ij}$ in general.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3 (Trace is invariant).** For a $(1,1)$-tensor$T^i{}_j$:$\tilde{T}^i{}_i = (P^{-1})^i{}_k T^k{}_l P^l{}_i$. In matrix notation, this is$\mathrm{tr}(P^{-1} A P) = \mathrm{tr}(A)$, the invariance of trace under similarity. The transformation law for a$(1,1)$-tensor reproduces the well-known formula for how matrix components change under change of basis.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4 (Stress tensor in continuum mechanics).** The Cauchy stress tensor $\sigma^i{}_j$ (or$\sigma_{ij}$ using the metric to lower one index) describes the force per unit area in direction$e_i$ on a surface with normal$e_j$. Under a rotation of the reference frame$P \in O(3)$:$\tilde{\sigma}^i{}_j = (P^{-1})^i{}_k \sigma^k{}_l P^l{}_j$. For the tensor to represent the same physical state of stress in the new frame, its components must transform by exactly this law. The fact that physical quantities are frame-independent is enforced by the requirement that they be tensors.
\end{example}

The transformation laws are therefore not bookkeeping exercises — they are the algebraic guarantee that tensorial quantities carry genuine geometric or physical meaning independent of any observer's choice of coordinates. A well-formed tensor equation $T^{i_1 \cdots i_p}{}_{j_1 \cdots j_q} = S^{i_1 \cdots i_p}{}_{j_1 \cdots j_q}$ that holds in one basis automatically holds in every basis, because both sides transform by the same law. This is the **principle of general covariance**: the laws of physics can be written in terms of tensor equations, which are automatically valid in all coordinate systems.

\begin{remark}
The transformation law for tensors is the definition of a tensor in many physics textbooks (the "transformation law definition"), which defines a tensor as a multi-indexed object whose components transform by $P^{-1}$ for each upper index and$P$ for each lower index. The abstract definition (as an element of$V^{\otimes p} \otimes (V^*)^{\otimes q}$) and the transformation-law definition are equivalent, but the abstract approach emphasizes the coordinate-free nature of the object, while the transformation-law approach emphasizes how to compute with it. Both perspectives are essential: the abstract definition tells you what a tensor is, and the transformation law tells you how to work with it.
\end{remark}
