# 12.6 Tensors in Coordinates

The abstract constructions of the previous sections become concrete — and connect directly to the physics and differential geometry literature — when we express tensors in coordinates. A choice of basis $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$ for $V$ determines a dual basis $\{e^1, \ldots, e^n\}$ for $V^*$, and a mixed tensor of type $(p, q)$ — an element of $V^{\otimes p} \otimes (V^*)^{\otimes q}$ — is then represented by an array of components $T^{i_1 \cdots i_p}_{j_1 \cdots j_q}$ indexed by $p$ upper (contravariant) indices and $q$ lower (covariant) indices. The upper/lower index placement is not a notational quirk but carries information: upper indices transform one way under a change of basis, lower indices transform the other way, and only expressions in which the index placement is consistent — in the sense of the Einstein summation convention — represent genuinely coordinate-free (tensorial) quantities.

The Einstein summation convention is the agreement that whenever the same index appears once as an upper index and once as a lower index in a single term, it is summed over. So $T^i{}_i = \sum_{i=1}^n T^i{}_i$ is the trace, and $T^i{}_{jk} v^j w^k = \sum_{j,k} T^i{}_{jk} v^j w^k$ is a tensor contraction. Contraction is the coordinate version of applying a linear functional: pairing an upper index of a tensor with a lower index of the same or another tensor produces a new tensor of lower rank. The trace of a matrix is the simplest contraction, and it is invariant under change of basis precisely because the contraction consumes one upper and one lower index simultaneously.

The transformation law under change of basis is the coordinate expression of what it means to be a tensor. If $e_i' = \sum_j A^j_i e_j$ (a new basis expressed in terms of the old), then a contravariant vector transforms by the inverse matrix $v'^i = \sum_j (A^{-1})^i_j v^j$, while a covariant vector transforms by the original matrix $f_i' = \sum_j A^j_i f_j$. A general $(p,q)$-tensor transforms with $p$ factors of $A^{-1}$ and $q$ factors of $A$. This transformation law is the definition of a tensor in the physics tradition: a "tensor" is an array of components that transforms in the right way. The algebraic treatment in this chapter shows that this transformation law is exactly what results from expressing an abstract element of $V^{\otimes p} \otimes (V^*)^{\otimes q}$ in coordinates — the two definitions are equivalent, and the coordinate-free definition is the more fundamental one.
