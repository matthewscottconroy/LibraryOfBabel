# 13.1.1 The Four Axioms: Closure, Associativity, Identity, Inverses

Mathematics often proceeds by asking: what is the minimal list of rules that forces a structure to behave in a certain way? For the notion of symmetry, invertible operations, and algebraic "sameness," that minimal list is the four group axioms. Rather than treating groups as familiar objects dressed in abstraction, it is better to begin with the axioms themselves and let the structure emerge. The approach demands some patience — we will spend time proving things that might seem obvious — but the payoff is a theory of remarkable scope, applicable simultaneously to the symmetries of geometric objects, number-theoretic operations, polynomial roots, and much more.

The key question motivating the definition is this: what common structure underlies the integers under addition, the nonzero rationals under multiplication, the permutations of a set under composition, and the symmetries of a square under successive application? In each case there is a set of "operations" or "transformations," a way to combine two of them to get a third, a "do nothing" operation, and a way to undo each operation. Abstracting these four features yields the group axioms.

\begin{definition}
A \textbf{group} $(G, \cdot)$ is a set$G$ together with a binary operation$\cdot : G \times G \to G$ satisfying four conditions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Closure}: for all $a, b \in G$, the product$a \cdot b$ lies in$G$.
\item \textbf{Associativity}: $(a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)$ for all$a, b, c \in G$.
\item \textbf{Identity}: there exists an element $e \in G$ such that$e \cdot a = a \cdot e = a$ for every$a \in G$.
\item \textbf{Inverses}: for every $a \in G$, there exists$a^{-1} \in G$ satisfying$a \cdot a^{-1} = a^{-1} \cdot a = e$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Notice that the axioms say nothing about commutativity --- $a \cdot b$ need not equal$b \cdot a$. This is a deliberate omission. Many of the most important groups in mathematics are noncommutative, and insisting on commutativity would exclude them from the theory. Associativity, by contrast, cannot be relaxed: without it, products of more than two elements would be ambiguous depending on the order of parenthesization, and the theory would dissolve into meaninglessness.

The closure axiom might seem redundant if we think of the operation as already having codomain $G$. Its role is to remind us that when we specify a group, we must check that the operation stays within the designated set. A subset of a known group, equipped with the same operation, is not automatically a group — closure can fail. Similarly, the identity axiom requires the identity to work on both sides: there are algebraic structures where only a one-sided identity exists, but those are not groups.

These four axioms are the entire definition. Every theorem about groups, from the most elementary cancellation law to the deepest results of finite group theory, is a consequence of these four conditions alone. The power of the axiom system lies precisely in its parsimony: the axioms are few enough to admit an enormous variety of examples, yet strong enough to imply a rich common theory.

**Example 1.** The integers under addition $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ form a group. The operation$+$ is a binary operation on$\mathbb{Z}$. Closure: the sum of two integers is an integer. Associativity:$(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)$ for all$a, b, c \in \mathbb{Z}$ — this is a basic fact of arithmetic. Identity:$0$ satisfies$0 + a = a + 0 = a$ for all$a$. Inverses: for each$a \in \mathbb{Z}$, the element$-a$ satisfies$a + (-a) = 0$. All four axioms hold, so$(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ is a group. It is commutative:$a + b = b + a$ for all$a, b$, making it an abelian group.

**Example 2.** The natural numbers under addition $(\mathbb{N}, +)$ are \emph{not} a group. Closure and associativity hold, and$0$ is the identity, but inverses fail: the natural number$1$ has no additive inverse in$\mathbb{N}$, since$-1 \notin \mathbb{N}$. This example illustrates that the inverse axiom is nontrivial; it forces the set to be large enough to contain "undo" operations.

**Example 3.** The set of $2 \times 2$ invertible real matrices$GL_2(\mathbb{R}) = \{A \in M_2(\mathbb{R}) : \det A \neq 0\}$ under matrix multiplication forms a group. Matrix multiplication is associative (a general fact about function composition) and the identity matrix$I$ is the identity element. Each invertible matrix$A$ has an inverse$A^{-1}$ satisfying$AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I$. The product of two invertible matrices is invertible since$\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B) \neq 0$ when both determinants are nonzero. This group is nonabelian: the matrices$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ do not commute.

**Example 4.** The set $\{1, -1\}$ under multiplication forms a group of order 2. Closure: any product of two elements from$\{1, -1\}$ lies in$\{1, -1\}$. Associativity: multiplication of real numbers is associative. Identity:$1$ is the multiplicative identity. Inverses:$1^{-1} = 1$ and$(-1)^{-1} = -1$. This tiny group, despite its simplicity, is isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ and appears as the sign group in the theory of permutations.

**Example 5.** Let $X$ be any nonempty set and let$\mathrm{Sym}(X)$ be the set of all bijections from$X$ to$X$, with composition as the operation. This is a group: composition of bijections is a bijection (closure), composition of functions is associative, the identity function$\mathrm{id}_X$ is the identity element, and every bijection has an inverse bijection. When$X = \{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$, this is the symmetric group$S_n$ of order$n!$, the most fundamental nonabelian group family in the theory.
