# 13.1.2 Elementary Consequences Provable from the Axioms

Before exploring examples, it is worth pausing to extract what the four axioms guarantee on their own. This exercise has two purposes: it builds confidence that the definition is correctly stated, and it establishes the basic algebraic tools we will use constantly without further comment. None of the results in this section require anything beyond the axioms — no commutativity, no finiteness, no additional structure. They are the bedrock on which every theorem in the subject rests.

The first observation is that the identity element is **unique**. The axiom guarantees existence, but could there be two distinct identities? Suppose both $e$ and$e'$ satisfy the identity axiom. Then$e = e \cdot e' = e'$, where the first equality uses the fact that$e'$ is an identity and the second uses the fact that$e$ is an identity. Hence$e = e'$, and the identity is unique. From now on we write$e$ (or$1$, or$0$ in additive notation) for the unique identity without qualification.

Similarly, the inverse of each element is **unique**. If $b$ and$c$ are both inverses of$a$ — meaning$ab = ba = e$ and$ac = ca = e$ — then$b = b \cdot e = b \cdot (ac) = (ba) \cdot c = e \cdot c = c$. Hence$b = c$, and we are justified in writing$a^{-1}$ for the unique inverse of$a$.

From uniqueness of inverses, two further identities follow. First, the **inverse of an inverse**: $(a^{-1})^{-1} = a$. This is because$a$ itself satisfies the defining property of the inverse of$a^{-1}$:$a \cdot a^{-1} = e$ and$a^{-1} \cdot a = e$. By uniqueness,$(a^{-1})^{-1} = a$. Second, the **inverse of a product**:$(ab)^{-1} = b^{-1}a^{-1}$. Note the reversal of order, which is necessary whenever the group is nonabelian. To verify:$(ab)(b^{-1}a^{-1}) = a(bb^{-1})a^{-1} = aea^{-1} = aa^{-1} = e$, and$(b^{-1}a^{-1})(ab) = b^{-1}(a^{-1}a)b = b^{-1}eb = b^{-1}b = e$. The mnemonic is "socks and shoes": to undo putting on socks and then shoes, first remove the shoes, then the socks.

Finally, the **left and right cancellation laws** hold. If $ac = bc$, then$a = b$ (right cancellation), and if$ca = cb$, then$a = b$ (left cancellation). Both follow immediately by multiplying the appropriate side by the inverse of$c$. The cancellation laws mean that group equations can be solved uniquely: if$ax = b$ for known$a, b \in G$, the unique solution is$x = a^{-1}b$. This resembles division in a number system, even though the group need not be commutative.

**Example 1.** We verify $(ab)^{-1} = b^{-1}a^{-1}$ in a concrete nonabelian group. In$S_3$, let$a = (1\;2)$ and$b = (1\;3)$. Then$ab = (1\;2)(1\;3) = (1\;3\;2)$ (reading right to left:$1 \mapsto 3 \mapsto 3$,$3 \mapsto 1 \mapsto 2$,$2 \mapsto 2 \mapsto 1$, but more carefully: apply$(1\;3)$ first, then$(1\;2)$:$1 \to 3 \to 3$,$3 \to 1 \to 2$,$2 \to 2 \to 1$, so$ab = (1\;3\;2)$). The inverse is$(ab)^{-1} = (1\;2\;3)$. On the other side,$b^{-1} = (1\;3)$ and$a^{-1} = (1\;2)$ (both transpositions are their own inverses), and$b^{-1}a^{-1} = (1\;3)(1\;2)$:$1 \to 2 \to 2$,$2 \to 2 \to 1$,$3 \to 1 \to 3$... let us recompute carefully. Apply$(1\;2)$ first:$1 \mapsto 2$,$2 \mapsto 1$,$3 \mapsto 3$; then apply$(1\;3)$:$2 \mapsto 2$,$1 \mapsto 3$,$3 \mapsto 1$. So$b^{-1}a^{-1}$:$1 \mapsto 2 \mapsto 2$,$2 \mapsto 1 \mapsto 3$,$3 \mapsto 3 \mapsto 1$, giving$(2\;3\;1) = (1\;2\;3)$. Indeed$(ab)^{-1} = b^{-1}a^{-1}$. Note that$a^{-1}b^{-1} = (1\;2)(1\;3) = (1\;3\;2) \neq (1\;2\;3)$, confirming the reversal of order is essential.

**Example 2.** We use right cancellation to solve an equation in $GL_2(\mathbb{R})$. Suppose$XA = B$ where
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
By right cancellation, $X = BA^{-1}$. We compute$A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1/2 & -1/2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and then
$$X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1/2 & -1/2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1/2 & -1/2 \\ 1/2 & 1/2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
One verifies $XA = B$ directly. Note that we could not have written$X = A^{-1}B$, since matrix multiplication is not commutative.

**Example 3.** The cancellation law can fail for operations that are not group operations. Consider the integers under multiplication: $2 \cdot 3 = 2 \cdot 3$ trivially, but there is no issue there. However, consider$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ under multiplication:$2 \cdot 3 = 0 = 4 \cdot 3$ in$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, yet$2 \neq 4$. Cancellation of$3$ fails because$3$ is not a unit — it has no multiplicative inverse — and the integers modulo$6$ under multiplication are not a group. This contrast highlights why the inverse axiom is necessary for cancellation.

**Example 4.** We prove by induction that $\phi(g^n) = \phi(g)^n$ for any group homomorphism$\phi: G \to H$ and any integer$n$. For$n = 1$ this is trivial. For$n > 1$:$\phi(g^n) = \phi(g \cdot g^{n-1}) = \phi(g)\phi(g^{n-1}) = \phi(g)\phi(g)^{n-1} = \phi(g)^n$ by the homomorphism property and induction. For$n = 0$:$\phi(e) = e$, already proved. For$n < 0$:$\phi(g^n) = \phi((g^{-1})^{|n|}) = \phi(g^{-1})^{|n|} = (\phi(g)^{-1})^{|n|} = \phi(g)^n$, using$\phi(g^{-1}) = \phi(g)^{-1}$. This calculation uses uniqueness of inverses and is a small exercise in how the axioms propagate through homomorphisms.
