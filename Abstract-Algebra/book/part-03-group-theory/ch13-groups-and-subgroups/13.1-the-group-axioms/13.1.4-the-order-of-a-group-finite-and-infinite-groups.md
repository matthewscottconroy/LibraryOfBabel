# 13.1.4 The Order of a Group; Finite and Infinite Groups

One of the first questions we ask about any mathematical structure is: how big is it? For groups, "bigness" is measured by the **order** of the group, written $|G|$, which is simply the cardinality of the underlying set. A group is **finite** if$|G| < \infty$ and **infinite** otherwise. The integers$(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ are infinite; the symmetries of a square form a group of order 8; the cyclic group$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ has order$n$.

At first glance, the order of a group is a very crude invariant: it says nothing about the algebraic structure, only about the size. But in finite group theory, the order turns out to be remarkably informative. Lagrange's theorem (Chapter 14) shows that the order of any subgroup must divide the group order, which immediately constrains what subgroups can exist. The Sylow theorems (Chapter 19) sharpen this to show that groups of specific orders have specific types of subgroups. And the classification of finite simple groups — one of the monumental achievements of twentieth-century mathematics — shows that the order, together with other data, essentially determines the group.

The word "order" carries a second meaning in group theory, applying to individual elements. The **order of an element** $g \in G$ is the smallest positive integer$n$ such that$g^n = e$, or$\infty$ if no such$n$ exists. A rotation by$90°$ in the dihedral group$D_4$ has order 4; the integer$1$ in$(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ has infinite order since no finite multiple of$1$ equals$0$. These two uses of "order" — for groups and for elements — are distinguished by context, but both will appear constantly throughout the text.

These two notions of order are connected in a fundamental way. The cyclic subgroup $\langle g \rangle$ generated by$g$ has exactly$\mathrm{ord}(g)$ elements, and since$\langle g \rangle$ is a subgroup of$G$, Lagrange's theorem implies$\mathrm{ord}(g) \mid |G|$ in any finite group. This single consequence already has far-reaching implications: in a group of prime order$p$, every nonidentity element has order$p$, and the group must be cyclic (see Section 14.1.4).

Both notions of order appear in virtually every theorem that follows, and thinking about groups through the lens of orders --- asking "what orders are possible?", "what elements of order $p$ exist?", "how do the orders of elements constrain the group's structure?" --- is one of the core habits of mind in finite group theory. Building this habit now, at the level of the definition, will pay dividends throughout the text.

**Example 1.** The group $\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$ has order$|{\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}}| = 8$. The orders of its elements are:$\mathrm{ord}(\bar{0}) = 1$ (since$\bar{0}$ is the identity);$\mathrm{ord}(\bar{1}) = 8$ (since the smallest$n$ with$n \cdot \bar{1} = \bar{0}$ is$n = 8$);$\mathrm{ord}(\bar{2}) = 4$ (since$4 \cdot \bar{2} = \bar{8} = \bar{0}$ and$2 \cdot \bar{2} = \bar{4} \neq \bar{0}$);$\mathrm{ord}(\bar{4}) = 2$ (since$2 \cdot \bar{4} = \bar{8} = \bar{0}$);$\mathrm{ord}(\bar{3}) = 8$ (since$\gcd(3,8) = 1$, so$\bar{3}$ generates$\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$). Note all element orders divide$8 = |\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}|$, as guaranteed by Lagrange's theorem.

**Example 2.** The dihedral group $D_3$ (symmetries of an equilateral triangle) has order$|D_3| = 6$. It contains one identity$e$ of order$1$, two rotations$r, r^2$ of orders$3$ and$3$, and three reflections$s, rs, r^2s$ each of order$2$. The divisors of$6$ are$1, 2, 3, 6$, and indeed the element orders$\{1, 2, 3\}$ are all divisors of$6$. The group$D_3$ is isomorphic to$S_3$, the symmetric group on three elements; both have order$6$ and both are nonabelian.

**Example 3.** The group $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ is infinite, and its nonzero elements all have infinite order: for any$n \neq 0$, no positive integer multiple$kn$ equals$0$ (since$kn \neq 0$ for$k \geq 1$). Thus "infinite order" does not mean "very large finite order" but rather "the powers$\{g, g^2, g^3, \ldots\}$ are all distinct." By contrast, the group$(\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}, +)$ is infinite but every element has finite order: the class of$p/q$ (in lowest terms) has order$q$, since$q \cdot (p/q) = p \equiv 0$ in$\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 4.** In the quaternion group $Q_8 = \{\pm 1, \pm i, \pm j, \pm k\}$, we compute element orders. The identity$1$ has order$1$. The element$-1$ satisfies$(-1)^2 = 1$, so it has order$2$. For$i$:$i^2 = -1 \neq 1$,$i^3 = i^2 \cdot i = -i \neq 1$,$i^4 = (i^2)^2 = (-1)^2 = 1$, so$\mathrm{ord}(i) = 4$. Similarly$\mathrm{ord}(j) = \mathrm{ord}(k) = \mathrm{ord}(-i) = \mathrm{ord}(-j) = \mathrm{ord}(-k) = 4$. The order of$Q_8$ is$8$, and the element orders$\{1, 2, 4\}$ all divide$8$.

**Example 5.** Consider the group $GL_2(\mathbb{F}_2)$ of invertible$2 \times 2$ matrices over the field$\mathbb{F}_2 = \{0, 1\}$ with two elements. There are$16$ matrices in$M_2(\mathbb{F}_2)$ and those with nonzero determinant (computed mod$2$) are the invertible ones. A matrix$\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ is invertible iff$ad - bc \equiv 1 \pmod{2}$, i.e.,$ad + bc \equiv 1 \pmod{2}$. Counting gives$|GL_2(\mathbb{F}_2)| = (2^2 - 1)(2^2 - 2) = 3 \cdot 2 = 6$. In fact$GL_2(\mathbb{F}_2) \cong S_3$. This illustrates that the same abstract group of order$6$ can arise in very different contexts.
