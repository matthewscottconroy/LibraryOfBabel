# 13.1 The Group Axioms

The concept of a group abstracts a pattern that appears across nearly every branch of mathematics: a collection of objects that can be combined, where the combination is associative, there is a neutral element, and every element can be reversed. The four axioms — closure, associativity, identity, and inverses — are chosen to be precisely what is needed, no more. Closure ensures the operation stays within the set; associativity allows unambiguous iterated products $a_1 a_2 \cdots a_n$ without specifying how parentheses are placed; the identity $e$ provides a reference point satisfying $ea = ae = a$; and the existence of an inverse $a^{-1}$ for each $a$ means no element is a "dead end." Notably absent from the axioms is commutativity: a group in which $ab = ba$ for all $a, b$ is called abelian, but most groups that arise in nature are not abelian, and the general theory is richer for it.

From these four axioms alone, one can already prove non-trivial theorems. The identity element is unique: if $e$ and $e'$ both satisfy the identity axiom, then $e = ee' = e'$. The inverse of each element is unique: if $b$ and $c$ are both inverses of $a$, then $b = b(ac) = (ba)c = c$. One can prove the cancellation laws — $ab = ac$ implies $b = c$ — and the "shoes and socks" inversion formula $(ab)^{-1} = b^{-1}a^{-1}$, which reflects the reversal of order in a composition of transformations. These consequences are not merely exercises; they establish the rigidity of group structure and will be used constantly.

A group $G$ is finite if it contains finitely many elements; the number $|G|$ is called the order of $G$. Groups may be presented as multiplication tables (Cayley tables), as sets of transformations, or through generators and relations. The additive notation $(G, +)$ is conventional when the group is abelian (the integers $\mathbb{Z}$, the rationals $\mathbb{Q}$, residues $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$), while multiplicative notation $(G, \cdot)$ is used for permutation groups, matrix groups, and non-abelian groups in general. The two notations are purely a matter of convention, and fluency with both is essential. This section establishes the foundational language that every subsequent result will depend upon.
