# 13.2.2 The Symmetric Group $S_n$; Cycle Notation

Among all groups, the symmetric groups occupy a foundational position. The **symmetric group** $S_n$ is the set of all bijections from$\{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$ to itself, equipped with composition as the group operation. There are$n!$ such bijections, so$|S_n| = n!$. Composition of functions is always associative, the identity permutation serves as the identity element, and every bijection has an inverse bijection, so the group axioms are satisfied. For$n \geq 3$,$S_n$ is nonabelian --- a fact that can be checked directly by composing two specific permutations in either order and observing different results.

The importance of the symmetric groups extends far beyond their role as examples. Cayley's theorem (Section 16.3.2) shows that every group embeds as a subgroup of some $S_n$: abstract group theory is, in a precise sense, the study of subgroups of symmetric groups. In Galois theory, the Galois group of a polynomial acts faithfully on the roots, giving an embedding into$S_n$ where$n$ is the degree. The representation theory of$S_n$ --- the study of how$S_n$ acts on vector spaces --- is one of the richest and most beautiful chapters in all of algebra.

To work efficiently with permutations, we use **cycle notation**. A $k$-cycle$(a_1\; a_2\; \cdots\; a_k)$ is the permutation that sends$a_1 \mapsto a_2$,$a_2 \mapsto a_3$,$\ldots$,$a_{k-1} \mapsto a_k$, and$a_k \mapsto a_1$, fixing all other elements. A transposition is a$2$-cycle$(a\; b)$ that swaps two elements. Every permutation decomposes into a product of disjoint cycles --- cycles that act on disjoint sets of elements --- and this decomposition is unique up to the order in which we list the cycles and the choice of starting element within each cycle. The convention of omitting$1$-cycles (fixed points) from the notation is standard.

Composition of permutations in cycle notation requires care about the order convention. We follow the convention that $\sigma\tau$ means "apply$\tau$ first, then$\sigma$" --- i.e.,$(\sigma\tau)(x) = \sigma(\tau(x))$ --- which is the convention for function composition. Some texts use the opposite convention (left-to-right composition), so care is needed when reading other sources.

The order of a permutation written as a product of disjoint cycles is the least common multiple of the lengths of those cycles. This is because the cycle $(a_1\; \cdots\; a_k)$ has order$k$ — applying it$k$ times returns every element to its starting position — and disjoint cycles commute, so the order of their product is the lcm of their individual orders.

**Example 1.** Consider the permutation $\sigma \in S_5$ defined explicitly by$\sigma(1) = 3$,$\sigma(2) = 5$,$\sigma(3) = 4$,$\sigma(4) = 1$,$\sigma(5) = 2$. To find the cycle decomposition, start with$1$:$1 \to 3 \to 4 \to 1$, giving the cycle$(1\;3\;4)$. Now start with the smallest unaccounted element,$2$:$2 \to 5 \to 2$, giving$(2\;5)$. All elements accounted for. So$\sigma = (1\;3\;4)(2\;5)$. The order is$\mathrm{lcm}(3, 2) = 6$.

**Example 2.** Compute the product $\sigma\tau$ in$S_5$ where$\sigma = (1\;2\;3)$ and$\tau = (3\;4\;5)$. Using right-to-left composition: apply$\tau$ first, then$\sigma$.
\begin{align*}
1 &\xrightarrow{\tau} 1 \xrightarrow{\sigma} 2, \\
2 &\xrightarrow{\tau} 2 \xrightarrow{\sigma} 3, \\
3 &\xrightarrow{\tau} 4 \xrightarrow{\sigma} 4, \\
4 &\xrightarrow{\tau} 5 \xrightarrow{\sigma} 5, \\
5 &\xrightarrow{\tau} 3 \xrightarrow{\sigma} 1.
\end{align*}
So $\sigma\tau = (1\;2\;3\;4\;5)$, a single$5$-cycle of order$5$. Now compute$\tau\sigma$ (apply$\sigma$ first, then$\tau$):
\begin{align*}
1 &\xrightarrow{\sigma} 2 \xrightarrow{\tau} 2, \\
2 &\xrightarrow{\sigma} 3 \xrightarrow{\tau} 4, \\
3 &\xrightarrow{\sigma} 1 \xrightarrow{\tau} 1, \\
4 &\xrightarrow{\sigma} 4 \xrightarrow{\tau} 5, \\
5 &\xrightarrow{\sigma} 5 \xrightarrow{\tau} 3.
\end{align*}
So $\tau\sigma = (2\;4\;5\;3)(1)= (2\;4\;5\;3)$, a$4$-cycle. Since$\sigma\tau \neq \tau\sigma$,$S_5$ is nonabelian.

**Example 3.** Compute the inverse of $(1\;2\;3\;4\;5) \in S_5$. A$k$-cycle$(a_1\;a_2\;\cdots\;a_k)$ has inverse$(a_k\;a_{k-1}\;\cdots\;a_1)$, obtained by reversing the cycle. So$(1\;2\;3\;4\;5)^{-1} = (5\;4\;3\;2\;1) = (1\;5\;4\;3\;2)$ (rewritten to start from$1$). Verify: applying$(1\;2\;3\;4\;5)$ then$(1\;5\;4\;3\;2)$:$1 \to 2 \to 2$... wait, let us check$1$:$(1\;2\;3\;4\;5)$ sends$1 \to 2$, then$(1\;5\;4\;3\;2)$ sends$2 \to 1$. And$2 \to 3 \to 2$...$(1\;2\;3\;4\;5)$ sends$2 \to 3$,$(1\;5\;4\;3\;2)$ sends$3 \to 2$. For all elements in sequence: each is mapped to its successor then back to itself. So the composition is the identity. Confirmed.

**Example 4.** Express the permutation $(1\;2\;3\;4)$ as a product of transpositions. Using the formula$(a_1\;a_2\;\cdots\;a_k) = (a_1\;a_k)(a_1\;a_{k-1})\cdots(a_1\;a_2)$:$(1\;2\;3\;4) = (1\;4)(1\;3)(1\;2)$. Verify: applying right to left,$(1\;2)$ swaps$1$ and$2$, giving$2\;1\;3\;4$; then$(1\;3)$ swaps$1$ (currently at position$2$) and$3$... it is easier to track element values:$(1\;2)$:$1 \mapsto 2$,$2 \mapsto 1$,$3 \mapsto 3$,$4 \mapsto 4$; then$(1\;3)$:$2 \mapsto 2$,$1 \mapsto 3$,$3 \mapsto 1$,$4 \mapsto 4$; so after both:$1 \mapsto 2 \mapsto 2$,$2 \mapsto 1 \mapsto 3$,$3 \mapsto 3 \mapsto 1$,$4 \mapsto 4 \mapsto 4$; then$(1\;4)$:$2 \mapsto 2$,$3 \mapsto 3$,$1 \mapsto 4$,$4 \mapsto 1$; applying to the current state:$1 \mapsto 2$,$2 \mapsto 3$,$3 \mapsto 4$,$4 \mapsto 1$. This is$(1\;2\;3\;4)$. The decomposition uses$3 = k-1$ transpositions, which is an odd number — consistent with$(1\;2\;3\;4)$ being an odd permutation.

**Example 5.** The group $S_3$ has order$6$ and its elements in cycle notation are:$e$ (identity),$(1\;2)$,$(1\;3)$,$(2\;3)$,$(1\;2\;3)$,$(1\;3\;2)$. The three transpositions each have order$2$, the two$3$-cycles each have order$3$. The multiplication$(1\;2\;3)(1\;2) = (1\;3)$ (verify:$1 \to 2 \to 3$,$2 \to 1 \to 1$... apply$(1\;2)$ first:$1 \mapsto 2$,$2 \mapsto 1$,$3 \mapsto 3$; then$(1\;2\;3)$:$2 \mapsto 3$,$1 \mapsto 2$,$3 \mapsto 1$; net:$1 \mapsto 3$,$2 \mapsto 2$,$3 \mapsto 1$, which is$(1\;3)$). This demonstrates how to multiply permutations in cycle notation step by step, and confirms$S_3 \cong D_3$.
