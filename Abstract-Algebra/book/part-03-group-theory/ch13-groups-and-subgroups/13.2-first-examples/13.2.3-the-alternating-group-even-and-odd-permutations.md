# 13.2.3 The Alternating Group $A_n$; Even and Odd Permutations

Inside the symmetric group $S_n$ there is a distinguished subgroup of index two, the **alternating group**$A_n$, whose definition rests on a fundamental parity invariant of permutations. To see where this comes from, recall that every permutation can be expressed as a product of transpositions. A$k$-cycle, for instance, factors as$(a_1\;a_2\;\cdots\;a_k) = (a_1\;a_k)(a_1\;a_{k-1})\cdots(a_1\;a_2)$, a product of$k - 1$ transpositions. The key fact --- whose proof requires some care --- is that while a given permutation may be written as a product of transpositions in many different ways, the **parity** of the number of transpositions is always the same. A permutation is called **even** if any decomposition into transpositions uses an even number of them, and **odd** otherwise.

To prove that parity is well-defined, one constructs a homomorphism from $S_n$ to$\{+1, -1\}$ and verifies that it sends transpositions to$-1$. One standard approach: for a permutation$\sigma \in S_n$, define$\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) = \prod_{i < j} \frac{\sigma(i) - \sigma(j)}{i - j}$, the product over all pairs of the ratio$(\sigma(i) - \sigma(j))/(i-j)$. Each factor is$\pm 1$ (since$\sigma$ is a bijection, the numerator is a rearrangement of the denominator's values up to sign), and the product lies in$\{+1, -1\}$. One verifies that$\mathrm{sgn}$ is a group homomorphism and that$\mathrm{sgn}(a\;b) = -1$ for every transposition. Any decomposition of$\sigma$ into transpositions therefore gives$\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) = (-1)^k$ where$k$ is the number of transpositions, and since$\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)$ is well-defined, so is the parity of$k$.

\begin{definition}
The \textbf{alternating group} $A_n$ is the set of all even permutations in$S_n$. It is a subgroup: the identity is even, and the product of two even permutations is even (an even number plus an even number is even). The inverse of an even permutation is also even, since reversing a product of transpositions reverses their order but not their count. Since exactly half of all permutations are even,$|A_n| = n!/2$ for$n \geq 2$.
\end{definition}

The **sign homomorphism** $\mathrm{sgn}: S_n \to \{1, -1\}$, which assigns$+1$ to even permutations and$-1$ to odd ones, is a surjective group homomorphism, and$A_n$ is precisely its kernel. This is a clean example of how homomorphisms and normal subgroups interact, a theme central to Chapter 14. Since the index$[S_n : A_n] = 2$, and any subgroup of index$2$ is automatically normal (there are only two cosets, which must each be both a left and right coset),$A_n \trianglelefteq S_n$ without any further calculation.

The alternating groups become increasingly important as $n$ grows. For$n \geq 5$,$A_n$ is **simple** — it has no normal subgroups other than$\{e\}$ and itself. This result, proved later in the text, is the group-theoretic heart of Abel and Galois's theorem that the general degree-five polynomial cannot be solved by radicals. The simplicity of$A_5$ is thus not an isolated fact but the pivot of one of the great results in the history of mathematics.

**Example 1.** Determine the parity of $\sigma = (1\;2\;4)(3\;5) \in S_5$. The$3$-cycle$(1\;2\;4) = (1\;4)(1\;2)$, a product of$2$ transpositions (even). The$2$-cycle$(3\;5)$ is already a transposition (odd). So$\sigma = (1\;4)(1\;2)(3\;5)$, a product of$3$ transpositions. Hence$\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) = (-1)^3 = -1$, and$\sigma$ is odd. As a check:$|A_5| = 5!/2 = 60$, and$\sigma \notin A_5$.

**Example 2.** Show that $A_3 = \{e, (1\;2\;3), (1\;3\;2)\}$. The group$S_3$ has$6$ elements:$e$, three transpositions$(1\;2), (1\;3), (2\;3)$, and two$3$-cycles$(1\;2\;3), (1\;3\;2)$. The transpositions are odd ($1$ transposition each), and the$3$-cycles are even ($2$ transpositions each:$(1\;2\;3) = (1\;3)(1\;2)$). The identity is even. So$A_3 = \{e, (1\;2\;3), (1\;3\;2)\}$ has order$3$. Indeed$|A_3| = 3!/2 = 3$. The group$A_3$ is cyclic:$(1\;2\;3)^2 = (1\;3\;2)$ and$(1\;2\;3)^3 = e$, so$A_3 = \langle (1\;2\;3) \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 3.** Verify that the product of two odd permutations is even. Take $\sigma = (1\;2)$ and$\tau = (3\;4\;5)^{-1} = (3\;5\;4)$. Both are odd:$\sigma$ is a transposition (odd) and$\tau = (3\;5)(3\;4)$ is a product of$2$ transpositions (even). Wait ---$(3\;5\;4)$ is a$3$-cycle, hence even. Let us instead take$\sigma = (1\;2)$ and$\tau = (1\;3)$, both transpositions (odd). Their product: applying$(1\;3)$ first then$(1\;2)$:$1 \to 3 \to 3$,$3 \to 1 \to 2$,$2 \to 2 \to 1$. So$\sigma\tau = (1\;3\;2)$, a$3$-cycle, which is even. This confirms: odd$\times$ odd$=$ even, consistent with$\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma\tau) = \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)\cdot\mathrm{sgn}(\tau) = (-1)(-1) = 1$.

**Example 4.** We list all elements of $A_4$ and verify$|A_4| = 12$. The even permutations in$S_4$ are:$e$; the$3$-cycles$(1\;2\;3)$,$(1\;3\;2)$,$(1\;2\;4)$,$(1\;4\;2)$,$(1\;3\;4)$,$(1\;4\;3)$,$(2\;3\;4)$,$(2\;4\;3)$ (eight total); and the products of two disjoint transpositions$(1\;2)(3\;4)$,$(1\;3)(2\;4)$,$(1\;4)(2\;3)$ (three total). This gives$1 + 8 + 3 = 12$ even permutations, and$|A_4| = 12 = 4!/2$. Note$A_4$ has no subgroup of order$6$ despite$6 \mid 12$: this is a famous example showing that the converse of Lagrange's theorem fails.

**Example 5.** Show that $A_n$ is generated by the set of all$3$-cycles in$S_n$ (for$n \geq 3$). Any even permutation is a product of an even number of transpositions, so it suffices to show that every product of two transpositions can be written as a product of$3$-cycles. There are two cases: if the transpositions share one element,$(a\;b)(a\;c) = (a\;c\;b)$, a single$3$-cycle; if they are disjoint,$(a\;b)(c\;d) = (a\;b)(a\;c)(a\;c)(c\;d) = (a\;c\;b)(a\;d\;c)$, a product of two$3$-cycles. In either case, the product is expressible using$3$-cycles. Since every$3$-cycle is even and their products generate all even permutations,$A_n = \langle \text{all } 3\text{-cycles} \rangle$.
