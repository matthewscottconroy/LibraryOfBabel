# 13.2.4 Dihedral Groups $D_n$; Symmetries of the Regular$n$-gon

Some of the most geometrically intuitive groups arise as symmetry groups of regular polygons. Fix a regular $n$-gon in the plane, with vertices labeled$1, 2, \ldots, n$ in order around the polygon. A **symmetry** of the polygon is a rigid motion of the plane that maps the polygon to itself — equivalently, a bijection of the set of vertices that extends to an isometry of the plane. These symmetries form a group under composition, the **dihedral group**$D_n$, which has order$2n$.

The group $D_n$ consists of exactly$n$ rotations and$n$ reflections. The rotations are rotations by multiples of$2\pi/n$: they form a cyclic subgroup$\langle r \rangle$ of order$n$, generated by a single rotation$r$ by angle$2\pi/n$ counterclockwise. The reflections are reflections across the$n$ axes of symmetry of the polygon. Each reflection$s$ has order$2$. The interaction between rotations and reflections is captured by the fundamental relation$srs^{-1} = r^{-1}$, or equivalently$sr = r^{-1}s$. This says that conjugating a rotation by a reflection reverses its direction: reflecting, rotating forward, then reflecting back is the same as rotating backward. It is a geometrically transparent fact once you draw the picture.

\begin{definition}
The \textbf{dihedral group} $D_n$ (for$n \geq 3$) has the presentation
$$D_n = \langle r, s \mid r^n = s^2 = e,\; srs^{-1} = r^{-1} \rangle.$$
Its elements are $\{e, r, r^2, \ldots, r^{n-1}, s, rs, r^2 s, \ldots, r^{n-1}s\}$, and$|D_n| = 2n$.
\end{definition}

For $n \geq 3$, the group$D_n$ is **nonabelian**: a rotation followed by a reflection differs from the same reflection followed by the same rotation. The single relation$sr = r^{-1}s$ captures all the noncommutativity. The case$n = 3$ gives$D_3$, which has order$6$ and is in fact isomorphic to$S_3$, the smallest nonabelian group. The case$n = 4$ gives$D_4$ of order$8$, which we compare against the quaternion group$Q_8$ in the next section.

The dihedral groups are important for two reasons beyond their geometric appeal. First, they serve as the canonical illustration of groups given by generators and relations --- $D_n = \langle r, s \mid r^n = s^2 = e,\; srs^{-1} = r^{-1} \rangle$ --- a theme developed fully in Chapter 18 on presentations of groups. Second, their rich subgroup structure makes them ideal examples for testing conjectures about normality, cosets, and quotient groups: the cyclic subgroup$\langle r \rangle$ is always normal in$D_n$, but the reflection subgroups are not normal in general.

**Example 1.** Work out the group $D_3$ explicitly. The regular triangle has vertices$1, 2, 3$. Label$r = (1\;2\;3)$ (rotation by$120°$) and$s = (2\;3)$ (reflection across the axis through vertex$1$). Then:
\begin{align*}
e &= (), \quad r = (1\;2\;3), \quad r^2 = (1\;3\;2), \\
s &= (2\;3), \quad rs = (1\;2\;3)(2\;3) = (1\;2), \quad r^2 s = (1\;3\;2)(2\;3) = (1\;3).
\end{align*}
Verify $srs^{-1} = r^{-1}$:$s = (2\;3)$ and$r = (1\;2\;3)$, so$sr = (2\;3)(1\;2\;3)$: applying$(1\;2\;3)$ then$(2\;3)$:$1 \to 2 \to 3$,$2 \to 3 \to 2$,$3 \to 1 \to 1$, giving$(1\;3)$. And$r^{-1}s = (1\;3\;2)(2\;3)$:$1 \to 3 \to 3$,$2 \to 2 \to 3$... let us track:$(2\;3)$ first:$1 \to 1$,$2 \to 3$,$3 \to 2$; then$(1\;3\;2)$:$1 \to 3$,$3 \to 2$,$2 \to 1$; net:$1 \to 3$,$2 \to 2$... wait,$1 \to 1 \to 3$,$2 \to 3 \to 2$...$3 \to 2 \to 1$. So$r^{-1}s = (1\;3)$. And$sr = (1\;3)$ as computed, so indeed$sr = r^{-1}s$, confirming the relation.

**Example 2.** Find all subgroups of $D_4$. The group$D_4 = \{e, r, r^2, r^3, s, rs, r^2 s, r^3 s\}$ has order$8$. The possible subgroup orders are$1, 2, 4, 8$ by Lagrange. Order$1$:$\{e\}$. Order$8$:$D_4$ itself. Order$2$ subgroups (elements of order$2$):$r^2$ has order$2$, and$s, rs, r^2 s, r^3 s$ each have order$2$ (since$(r^k s)^2 = r^k s r^k s = r^k r^{-k} s s = e$). So the order-$2$ subgroups are$\{e, r^2\}$,$\{e, s\}$,$\{e, rs\}$,$\{e, r^2 s\}$,$\{e, r^3 s\}$ — five total. Order$4$ subgroups:$\langle r \rangle = \{e, r, r^2, r^3\}$ (cyclic of order$4$);$\{e, r^2, s, r^2 s\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$;$\{e, r^2, rs, r^3 s\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. The subgroup$\langle r \rangle$ is normal (index$2$); the two Klein four-subgroups are also normal (they are unions of conjugacy classes of$D_4$); the five order-$2$ subgroups generated by reflections are not all normal.

**Example 3.** Prove that the rotational subgroup $\langle r \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is normal in$D_n$. We use the criterion$gNg^{-1} = N$. It suffices to check$srs^{-1} \in \langle r \rangle$ and$r \cdot (r^k) \cdot r^{-1} \in \langle r \rangle$. The latter is$r^k \in \langle r \rangle$, trivially. For the former:$srs^{-1} = r^{-1} \in \langle r \rangle$ since$r^{-1} = r^{n-1}$. More generally,$s r^k s^{-1} = (srs^{-1})^k = r^{-k} \in \langle r \rangle$. So$s \langle r \rangle s^{-1} = \langle r \rangle$, confirming normality. The quotient$D_n / \langle r \rangle$ has order$2n/n = 2$, hence$D_n/\langle r \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 4.** In $D_5$ (symmetries of a regular pentagon, order$10$), find all elements and their orders. The rotations$e, r, r^2, r^3, r^4$ have orders$1, 5, 5, 5, 5$. The five reflections$s, rs, r^2 s, r^3 s, r^4 s$ each have order$2$. By Lagrange, subgroup orders divide$10$, so possible orders are$1, 2, 5, 10$. The cyclic subgroup$\langle r \rangle$ has order$5$. Since$5$ is prime,$\langle r \rangle$ is the unique subgroup of order$5$ (any group of prime order is cyclic and has no proper nontrivial subgroups, so there can be no other order-$5$ subgroup here). The Sylow$5$-subgroup is unique and hence normal.

**Example 5.** Compute the center of $D_n$. For$n$ odd: we need those$r^k$ or$r^j s$ commuting with all elements.$r^k$ commutes with all rotations automatically. Does$r^k$ commute with$s$? We need$r^k s = s r^k$, i.e.,$r^k s = r^{-k} s$ (using$sr^k = r^{-k}s$), so$r^{2k} = e$, meaning$n \mid 2k$. For$n$ odd, this forces$n \mid k$, so$r^k = e$. No nontrivial rotation is central. Similarly,$r^j s$ commutes with$r$ iff$r \cdot r^j s = r^j s \cdot r$, i.e.,$r^{j+1}s = r^j s r = r^j r^{-1} s = r^{j-1} s$, giving$r^2 = e$, impossible for$n \geq 3$ odd. So$Z(D_n) = \{e\}$ for$n$ odd. For$n$ even:$r^{n/2}$ satisfies$r^{n/2} s = s r^{-n/2} = s r^{n/2}$ (since$r^{-n/2} = r^{n/2}$ in$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ when$n$ is even). So$Z(D_n) = \{e, r^{n/2}\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ for$n$ even.
