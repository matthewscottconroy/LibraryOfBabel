# 13.2.5 Matrix Groups: $GL_n$,$SL_n$,$O(n)$,$U(n)$

Linear algebra and group theory intersect in the matrix groups, which are among the most important examples in all of mathematics. These groups encode the symmetries of geometric spaces, the transformations that preserve various structures (lengths, orientations, inner products), and the automorphisms of finite-dimensional vector spaces. They are simultaneously concrete enough to compute in and rich enough to illustrate the full depth of the theory.

The **general linear group** $GL_n(F)$ is the set of all invertible$n \times n$ matrices with entries in a field$F$, with matrix multiplication as the group operation. The product of two invertible matrices is invertible (since$\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B) \neq 0$), matrix multiplication is associative, the identity matrix$I$ serves as the group identity, and every invertible matrix has an inverse matrix --- so all four axioms hold. For$n \geq 2$,$GL_n(F)$ is nonabelian, as one can verify with any two non-commuting matrices. When$F = \mathbb{F}_q$ is the field with$q$ elements (a prime power),$GL_n(\mathbb{F}_q)$ is finite, of order$\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}(q^n - q^k)$.

Inside $GL_n(F)$ sits the **special linear group**$SL_n(F)$, the subgroup consisting of matrices with determinant$1$. The determinant function$\det: GL_n(F) \to F^*$ is a surjective group homomorphism, and$SL_n(F) = \ker(\det)$ --- a fact that immediately implies$SL_n(F)$ is a normal subgroup of$GL_n(F)$. The quotient$GL_n(F)/SL_n(F) \cong F^*$ by the first isomorphism theorem.

For real matrices, the geometry of lengths and angles leads to further important subgroups. The **orthogonal group** $O(n)$ consists of real$n \times n$ matrices satisfying$A^T A = I$; these are precisely the matrices that preserve the standard Euclidean inner product$\langle x, y \rangle = x^T y$, i.e., the isometries of$\mathbb{R}^n$ fixing the origin. The **special orthogonal group**$SO(n) = O(n) \cap SL_n(\mathbb{R})$ consists of orientation-preserving isometries --- in dimension$2$, these are rotations; in dimension$3$, they include rotations about all axes. The **unitary group**$U(n)$ is the complex analogue: matrices satisfying$A^* A = I$, where$A^*$ denotes the conjugate transpose. Notice that$O(n)$ and$U(n)$ are compact groups (closed and bounded as subsets of matrix space), a feature that makes them central in harmonic analysis and representation theory.

These matrix groups are the entry point into the vast theory of Lie groups, where the interplay between the algebraic group structure and the differential structure of a smooth manifold yields one of the richest areas of modern mathematics. For now, they serve as concrete, computable examples where the abstract group axioms take tangible form through matrix calculations.

**Example 1.** Verify that $SL_2(\mathbb{R})$ is a subgroup of$GL_2(\mathbb{R})$. We use the two-step subgroup test. Let$A, B \in SL_2(\mathbb{R})$, so$\det A = \det B = 1$. Then$\det(AB^{-1}) = \det(A)\det(B^{-1}) = \det(A)/\det(B) = 1/1 = 1$, so$AB^{-1} \in SL_2(\mathbb{R})$. The identity matrix$I$ has$\det I = 1$. By the two-step test,$SL_2(\mathbb{R}) \leq GL_2(\mathbb{R})$.

**Example 2.** Show $O(2)$ consists of rotations and reflections. A matrix$A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ satisfies$A^T A = I$ iff$a^2 + c^2 = 1$,$b^2 + d^2 = 1$, and$ab + cd = 0$. Write$a = \cos\theta$,$c = \sin\theta$; then$b = -\sin\theta$ or$b = \sin\theta$. The first gives$A = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$ with$\det A = 1$: a rotation by$\theta$. The second gives$A = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & -\cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$ with$\det A = -1$: a reflection. So$O(2)$ has two components:$SO(2)$ (rotations,$\det = 1$) and the reflections ($\det = -1$). Here$SO(2) \cong S^1 = \mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}$ as groups.

**Example 3.** Compute $|GL_2(\mathbb{F}_2)|$. The field$\mathbb{F}_2 = \{0, 1\}$ has$q = 2$ elements. By the formula,$|GL_2(\mathbb{F}_2)| = (2^2 - 1)(2^2 - 2) = 3 \cdot 2 = 6$. The six elements can be listed:$I$,$\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$,$\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$,$\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$,$\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$,$\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$. One verifies these are all nonsingular (over$\mathbb{F}_2$) and$GL_2(\mathbb{F}_2) \cong S_3$.

**Example 4.** Show that $U(1) \cong S^1$. We have$U(1) = \{z \in \mathbb{C} : |z| = 1\}$, the set of complex numbers of modulus$1$, with complex multiplication. The condition$A^* A = 1$ for a$1 \times 1$ complex matrix (a scalar)$z$ says$\bar{z} z = |z|^2 = 1$, i.e.,$|z| = 1$. The group operation is complex multiplication, which corresponds to addition of arguments:$e^{i\theta} \cdot e^{i\phi} = e^{i(\theta+\phi)}$. The map$\theta \mapsto e^{i\theta}$ is a surjective homomorphism from$(\mathbb{R}, +)$ to$U(1)$ with kernel$2\pi\mathbb{Z}$, so$U(1) \cong \mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 5.** Demonstrate that elements of $O(3)$ can be written as products of at most three reflections (through planes). A theorem of linear algebra (the Cartan-Dieudonné theorem) states that any orthogonal transformation in$\mathbb{R}^n$ is a product of at most$n$ reflections through hyperplanes. In$O(3)$: a rotation about an axis is the product of two reflections through planes containing that axis, and any element of$O(3)$ with determinant$-1$ is the product of either one or three reflections. For instance, the antipodal map$x \mapsto -x$ (which negates all coordinates) has$\det = -1$ and equals the product of reflections through the three coordinate planes. This illustrates how orthogonal groups serve as the natural setting for the geometry of reflections and rotations.
