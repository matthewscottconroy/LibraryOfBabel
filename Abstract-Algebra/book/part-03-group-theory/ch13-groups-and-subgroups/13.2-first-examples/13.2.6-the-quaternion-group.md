# 13.2.6 The Quaternion Group $Q_8$

Not every group of small order is easy to anticipate from geometric or number-theoretic considerations. The **quaternion group** $Q_8 = \{\pm 1, \pm i, \pm j, \pm k\}$ is a group of order$8$ defined by the multiplication rules$i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1$. It sits inside the quaternion algebra$\mathbb{H}$, discovered by Hamilton in 1843, and captures the multiplicative structure of the unit quaternions. Hamilton famously carved the defining relations into the stone of Brougham Bridge in Dublin, so struck was he by the discovery.

The quaternion algebra $\mathbb{H}$ is the real vector space with basis$\{1, i, j, k\}$ and multiplication defined by the rules$ij = k$,$jk = i$,$ki = j$, and the anti-commutative relations$ji = -k$,$kj = -i$,$ik = -j$. These can all be derived from the single relation$ijk = -1$. The element$-1$ has order$2$ and lies in the center of$Q_8$; every other non-identity element has order$4$. In particular,$i^4 = (i^2)^2 = (-1)^2 = 1$ and$i^3 = i^2 \cdot i = -i$. The group is manifestly nonabelian:$ij = k \neq -k = ji$.

The key point is that $Q_8$ provides one of the most instructive counterexamples in introductory group theory. Both$Q_8$ and the dihedral group$D_4$ are nonabelian groups of order$8$, yet they are **not isomorphic**. To see why, observe that$Q_8$ has exactly one element of order$2$, namely$-1$, whereas$D_4$ has five elements of order$2$ (the four reflections and the rotation by$180°$). Since any isomorphism must preserve orders of elements, the two groups cannot be isomorphic. This shows that knowing the order of a group is not enough to determine its isomorphism type, and that classifying groups of a given order requires more refined invariants.

The quaternion group reappears in several places later in the text. Its subgroup structure is clean: the subgroups of $Q_8$ are$\{1\}$,$\{±1\}$,$\langle i \rangle = \{1, i, -1, -i\}$,$\langle j \rangle = \{1, j, -1, -j\}$,$\langle k \rangle = \{1, k, -1, -k\}$, and$Q_8$ itself. Every subgroup is normal, making$Q_8$ a **Hamiltonian group** — a non-abelian group in which every subgroup is normal. This property, unusual among nonabelian groups, makes$Q_8$ the smallest and simplest example of a Hamiltonian group, and it plays a distinguished role in the classification of groups with this property.

The unit quaternions $S^3 = \{a + bi + cj + dk : a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + d^2 = 1\}$ form a Lie group that double-covers$SO(3)$. The finite group$Q_8 \subset S^3$ is a discrete subgroup and a finite analogue of this double cover. Understanding$Q_8$ is a natural first step toward the theory of spin groups and their role in physics.

**Example 1.** Verify the multiplication $jk = i$ in$Q_8$. From$ijk = -1$, multiply on the left by$i^{-1} = -i$ (since$i^2 = -1$ implies$i \cdot (-i) = -i^2 = 1$, so$i^{-1} = -i$):$(-i)(ijk) = (-i)(-1)$, giving$((-i)(i))jk = i$, so$(-i^2)jk = i$, so$(-(-1))jk = i$, so$jk = i$. Alternatively, one checks:$jk$ should equal$i$; verify$jk \cdot (jk)^{-1} = 1$. Since$(jk)^{-1} = k^{-1}j^{-1} = (-k)(-j) = kj = -i$ (from$ki = j$ multiply on right by$k^{-1} = -k$:$k \cdot i \cdot (-k) = j(-k)$... this gets complicated). The systematic approach: use the standard table to verify all products.

**Example 2.** List all subgroups of $Q_8$ and determine which are normal. The subgroups:$\{1\}$ (order$1$);$\{1, -1\}$ (order$2$);$\langle i \rangle = \{1, i, -1, -i\}$ (order$4$);$\langle j \rangle = \{1, j, -1, -j\}$ (order$4$);$\langle k \rangle = \{1, k, -1, -k\}$ (order$4$);$Q_8$ (order$8$). To check normality, we need$gHg^{-1} = H$ for all$g$. For$\langle i \rangle$:$j \cdot i \cdot j^{-1} = j \cdot i \cdot (-j) = -jij = -(ji)j = -(-k)j = kj = -i \in \langle i \rangle$. Similarly for all conjugates. Hence$\langle i \rangle \trianglelefteq Q_8$. By symmetry,$\langle j \rangle \trianglelefteq Q_8$ and$\langle k \rangle \trianglelefteq Q_8$. The subgroup$\{1, -1\} = Z(Q_8)$ is the center, hence normal. So every subgroup of$Q_8$ is normal, confirming the Hamiltonian property.

**Example 3.** Show $Q_8/\{1,-1\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ (the Klein four-group). The quotient$Q_8/\{1,-1\}$ has order$8/2 = 4$ and its four elements are the cosets$\{1,-1\}$,$\{i,-i\}$,$\{j,-j\}$,$\{k,-k\}$. Write these as$\bar{1}, \bar{i}, \bar{j}, \bar{k}$. In the quotient,$\bar{i}^2 = \overline{i^2} = \overline{-1} = \bar{1}$. Similarly$\bar{j}^2 = \bar{k}^2 = \bar{1}$. And$\bar{i}\bar{j} = \overline{ij} = \bar{k}$,$\bar{j}\bar{i} = \overline{ji} = \overline{-k} = \bar{k}$. So in the quotient,$\bar{i}\bar{j} = \bar{j}\bar{i}$ (the quotient is abelian), every nonidentity element has order$2$, and there are three nonidentity elements. This is exactly the Klein four-group$V_4 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 4.** Embed $Q_8$ in$GL_2(\mathbb{C})$. Define matrices:
$$\mathbf{1} = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{i} = \begin{pmatrix}i&0\\0&-i\end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{j} = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{k} = \begin{pmatrix}0&i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}.$$
One verifies: $\mathbf{i}^2 = \begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix} = -\mathbf{1}$;$\mathbf{j}^2 = \begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix} = -\mathbf{1}$;$\mathbf{i}\mathbf{j} = \begin{pmatrix}i&0\\0&-i\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0&i\\i&0\end{pmatrix} = \mathbf{k}$. So the subgroup$\{\pm\mathbf{1}, \pm\mathbf{i}, \pm\mathbf{j}, \pm\mathbf{k}\} \leq GL_2(\mathbb{C})$ is isomorphic to$Q_8$. These matrices are related to the Pauli matrices of quantum mechanics.

**Example 5.** Construct a surjective homomorphism $Q_8 \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ explicitly. Define$\varphi: Q_8 \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ by$\varphi(1) = \varphi(-1) = (0,0)$,$\varphi(i) = \varphi(-i) = (1,0)$,$\varphi(j) = \varphi(-j) = (0,1)$,$\varphi(k) = \varphi(-k) = (1,1)$. Check:$\varphi(ij) = \varphi(k) = (1,1) = (1,0) + (0,1) = \varphi(i) + \varphi(j)$. More generally,$\varphi(gh) = \varphi(g) + \varphi(h)$ for all$g, h$ (one checks all cases from the multiplication table). This is a surjective homomorphism with kernel$\{1, -1\}$, confirming$Q_8/\{1,-1\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.
