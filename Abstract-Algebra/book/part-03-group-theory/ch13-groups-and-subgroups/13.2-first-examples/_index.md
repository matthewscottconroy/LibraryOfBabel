# 13.2 First Examples

Abstract definitions become meaningful only when populated with examples, and the examples of group theory are among the most beautiful objects in mathematics. This section introduces the fundamental classes of groups that will recur throughout the entire text. Each family illuminates a different face of the general theory, and together they establish the enormous range of phenomena that the four group axioms encompass.

The most important single family of groups is the symmetric groups $S_n$, consisting of all bijections from $\{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$ to itself under composition. For $n \geq 3$, $S_n$ is non-abelian, and for $n = 3$ it is the smallest non-abelian group, of order 6. The cycle notation for permutations — writing $(1\,3\,5)(2\,4)$ for the permutation that cycles $1 \to 3 \to 5 \to 1$ and swaps $2 \leftrightarrow 4$ — makes products and orders easy to compute. Inside $S_n$ sits the alternating group $A_n$ of even permutations, a normal subgroup of index 2; the distinction between even and odd permutations, measured by the sign homomorphism, is one of the first examples of a homomorphism and provides the first glimpse of the isomorphism theorems.

The dihedral group $D_n$ is the symmetry group of a regular $n$-gon: it has $2n$ elements, consisting of $n$ rotations (including the identity) and $n$ reflections. Written with a rotation $r$ of order $n$ and a reflection $s$ of order 2, the entire group is determined by the relation $srs^{-1} = r^{-1}$, which reflects the geometric fact that conjugation by a reflection reverses the direction of rotation. The dihedral groups are non-abelian for $n \geq 3$ and are among the most hands-on examples in the subject; verifying group axioms and finding subgroups in $D_4$ or $D_6$ provides excellent training. The matrix groups $\text{GL}_n(F)$ (invertible matrices over a field $F$) and $\text{SL}_n(F)$ (those with determinant 1) connect group theory to linear algebra; $\text{GL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ alone contains as subgroups the orthogonal group, the group of rotations, all cyclic groups, and a host of other examples. Finally, the quaternion group $Q_8 = \{\pm 1, \pm i, \pm j, \pm k\}$, though of the same order as $D_4$, is structurally quite different: it has the surprising property that every subgroup of $Q_8$ is normal, and it cannot be written as a semidirect product in any non-trivial way.
