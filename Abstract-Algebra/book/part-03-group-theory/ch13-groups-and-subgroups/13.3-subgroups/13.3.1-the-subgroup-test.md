# 13.3.1 The Subgroup Test

Having defined groups and assembled a library of examples, we now turn to their internal structure. Given a group $G$, we frequently encounter subsets that are themselves groups under the same operation. Identifying these subsets is one of the primary activities of the subject: subgroups are the internal "pieces" from which the structure of$G$ is assembled, and understanding how they fit together is the central theme of group theory.

A nonempty subset $H \subseteq G$ is a **subgroup** of$G$, written$H \leq G$, if$H$ is closed under the group operation and under taking inverses — equivalently, if$H$ forms a group in its own right with the operation inherited from$G$. Associativity need not be re-checked: it holds in$H$ automatically because it holds in$G$ and$H$ uses the same operation. The identity of$H$ must be the identity of$G$ (not some other element), as one can show: if$e'$ is the identity of$H$, then$e' \cdot e' = e'$ in$H$, and multiplying both sides by$(e')^{-1}$ in$G$ gives$e' = e$.

Rather than checking all four axioms each time, we use the **two-step subgroup test**: a nonempty subset $H \subseteq G$ is a subgroup if and only if for all$a, b \in H$, the element$ab^{-1}$ also belongs to$H$. To see why this suffices, notice that taking$a = b$ gives$e = aa^{-1} \in H$, so the identity is present. Taking$a = e$ gives$b^{-1} \in H$ for any$b$, so inverses are present. Closure then follows because if$a, b \in H$ then$b^{-1} \in H$ and thus$a(b^{-1})^{-1} = ab \in H$. Associativity is inherited automatically from$G$ and need not be re-verified.

For finite groups, the test simplifies further: a nonempty finite subset $H \subseteq G$ is a subgroup if and only if it is **closed under the group operation**. The reason is that in a finite group, closure together with the structure of$G$ forces inverses to be present. If$h \in H$ and$H$ is closed under multiplication, then the sequence$h, h^2, h^3, \ldots$ all lie in$H$. Since$H$ is finite, some repetition occurs:$h^i = h^j$ with$i < j$, giving$h^{j-i} = e \in H$, and then$h^{-1} = h^{j-i-1} \in H$. One must be careful: this finite criterion fails for infinite groups (the positive integers under addition are closed under addition but form no group).

The subgroup test is the primary tool for confirming, without redundant checking, that specific subsets are subgroups. We will use it throughout: to verify that the center $Z(G)$ is a subgroup (Section 13.3.2), that the cyclic subgroup$\langle g \rangle$ is a subgroup (Section 13.3.3), and that kernels of homomorphisms are subgroups (Chapter 15). Every time, the two-step test reduces the verification to a single closure condition.

**Example 1.** Show that the set of even integers $2\mathbb{Z} = \{2k : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is a subgroup of$(\mathbb{Z}, +)$. The set is nonempty ($0 \in 2\mathbb{Z}$). For$a, b \in 2\mathbb{Z}$, write$a = 2m$,$b = 2n$. Then$a - b = 2m - 2n = 2(m - n) \in 2\mathbb{Z}$ (in additive notation, the test is$a - b \in H$). By the two-step test,$2\mathbb{Z} \leq \mathbb{Z}$. More generally, for any$n \in \mathbb{Z}$, the set$n\mathbb{Z} = \{nk : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is a subgroup of$\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 2.** Is the set $H = \{A \in GL_2(\mathbb{R}) : \det A > 0\}$ a subgroup of$GL_2(\mathbb{R})$? Check the two-step test. Let$A, B \in H$, so$\det A > 0$ and$\det B > 0$. Then$\det(AB^{-1}) = \det(A) \det(B)^{-1} = \det(A)/\det(B) > 0$ (ratio of positive reals is positive). So$AB^{-1} \in H$. The identity$I$ has$\det I = 1 > 0$, so$H$ is nonempty. By the two-step test,$H \leq GL_2(\mathbb{R})$. Note$H$ consists of matrices with positive determinant, including$SL_2(\mathbb{R})$ (where$\det = 1$) and much more.

**Example 3.** Show that the intersection of subgroups is a subgroup. Let $H_1, H_2 \leq G$ and let$H = H_1 \cap H_2$. Then$e \in H_1$ and$e \in H_2$ (since both are subgroups), so$e \in H$ and$H$ is nonempty. For$a, b \in H$:$a, b \in H_1$ implies$ab^{-1} \in H_1$;$a, b \in H_2$ implies$ab^{-1} \in H_2$; hence$ab^{-1} \in H_1 \cap H_2 = H$. By the two-step test,$H \leq G$. This generalizes: any arbitrary intersection$\bigcap_{i \in I} H_i$ of subgroups is a subgroup. This fact is the key ingredient in defining the subgroup generated by a set.

**Example 4.** Demonstrate that a subset can be closed under the operation yet fail to be a subgroup, when the ambient group is infinite. Consider $H = \mathbb{N} = \{0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots\}$ as a subset of$(\mathbb{Z}, +)$. The sum of two nonnegative integers is nonnegative, so$H$ is closed under$+$. But$-1 \notin H$, so$H$ does not contain inverses. The two-step test fails:$0 - 1 = -1 \notin H$. Hence$H$ is not a subgroup, despite being closed. The finite subgroup criterion does not apply here because$\mathbb{Z}$ is infinite.

**Example 5.** Use the finite subgroup criterion to show $SL_2(\mathbb{F}_p)$ is a subgroup of$GL_2(\mathbb{F}_p)$ for a prime$p$. Both groups are finite. The set$SL_2(\mathbb{F}_p) = \{A \in GL_2(\mathbb{F}_p) : \det A = 1\}$ is nonempty (the identity matrix is in it). Closure: if$\det A = 1$ and$\det B = 1$, then$\det(AB) = \det A \cdot \det B = 1 \cdot 1 = 1$, so$AB \in SL_2(\mathbb{F}_p)$. By the finite subgroup criterion,$SL_2(\mathbb{F}_p) \leq GL_2(\mathbb{F}_p)$. Its order is$|GL_2(\mathbb{F}_p)|/(p-1) = (p^2-1)(p^2-p)/(p-1) = p(p+1)(p-1)$.
