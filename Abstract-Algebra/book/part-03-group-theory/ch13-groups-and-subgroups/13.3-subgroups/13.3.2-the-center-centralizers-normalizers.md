# 13.3.2 The Center $Z(G)$; Centralizers$C_G(a)$; Normalizers

Three fundamental subgroup constructions arise by asking how much commutativity is present in a given group. Each measures a different aspect of how elements interact with one another, and together they encode much of the internal symmetry structure of $G$. These constructions appear constantly throughout group theory: in the class equation, in the definition of normal subgroups, in the analysis of$p$-groups, and in the study of automorphism groups.

The **center** of $G$ is$Z(G) = \{g \in G : gx = xg \text{ for all } x \in G\}$, the set of elements that commute with every element of the group. It is always a subgroup --- to apply the two-step test, take$a, b \in Z(G)$ and verify that$ab^{-1}$ commutes with every$x \in G$, which follows since both$a$ and$b$ do (check:$(ab^{-1})x = a(b^{-1}x) = a(xb^{-1}) = ax \cdot b^{-1} = xa \cdot b^{-1} = x(ab^{-1})$, using$a \in Z(G)$ and$b^{-1} \in Z(G)$ since$b \in Z(G)$) --- and it is always abelian. For an abelian group,$Z(G) = G$. For a nonabelian group,$Z(G)$ is a proper subgroup, and it may be trivial: for instance,$Z(S_n) = \{e\}$ for$n \geq 3$. The center is always a normal subgroup of$G$, because$g Z(G) g^{-1} = Z(G)$ for any$g$ (each element of$Z(G)$ commutes past$g$ and$g^{-1}$).

The **centralizer** of an element $a \in G$ is$C_G(a) = \{g \in G : ga = ag\}$, the set of elements that commute with the specific element$a$. This is also a subgroup (by the two-step test: if$ga = ag$ and$ha = ah$, then$(gh^{-1})a = g(h^{-1}a) = g(ah^{-1}) = (ga)h^{-1} = (ag)h^{-1} = a(gh^{-1})$). And$Z(G) = \bigcap_{a \in G} C_G(a)$ is the intersection of all centralizers. The centralizer of$a$ is at least as large as$Z(G)$ and at most$G$; the size of$C_G(a)$ is precisely$|G|/|\mathrm{cl}(a)|$ by the orbit-stabilizer theorem, where$\mathrm{cl}(a) = \{gag^{-1} : g \in G\}$ is the conjugacy class of$a$.

The **normalizer** of a subgroup $H \leq G$ is$N_G(H) = \{g \in G : gHg^{-1} = H\}$, the set of elements for which conjugation by$g$ maps$H$ to itself. The normalizer is a subgroup of$G$ containing$H$, and it is the largest subgroup in which$H$ is normal: if$H \trianglelefteq K \leq G$, then$K \leq N_G(H)$. Notice that$H \trianglelefteq G$ if and only if$N_G(H) = G$, so the normalizer precisely measures how far$H$ is from being normal in$G$. The index$[G : N_G(H)]$ counts the number of distinct conjugates of$H$ in$G$, a fact used constantly in the Sylow theorems.

These three constructions are related by containment: $Z(G) \leq C_G(a)$ for every$a$, and$H \leq N_G(H)$ for every subgroup$H$. When$H = \{a\}$ is a singleton,$N_G(\{a\}) = C_G(a)$, so normalizers of elements are their centralizers. The table of sizes of centralizers and the number of conjugacy classes are encoded in the **class equation** of Section 16.2.4, one of the most powerful tools in finite group theory.

**Example 1.** Compute $Z(D_4)$. The elements of$D_4 = \{e, r, r^2, r^3, s, rs, r^2 s, r^3 s\}$ with$r^4 = s^2 = e$ and$srs^{-1} = r^{-1}$. An element$g$ is central iff it commutes with$r$ and with$s$ (since$r$ and$s$ generate$D_4$). For$g = r^2$:$r \cdot r^2 = r^3 = r^2 \cdot r$ (rotations commute). And$s \cdot r^2 = sr^2$,$r^2 \cdot s = r^2 s$; using$sr^2 = r^{-2}s = r^2 s$ (since$r^{-2} = r^2$ in$D_4$ as$r^4 = e$). So$r^2$ commutes with$s$. Thus$r^2 \in Z(D_4)$. One checks$r \notin Z(D_4)$ since$sr \neq rs$. For reflections$r^k s$:$r(r^k s) = r^{k+1}s$ but$(r^k s)r = r^k (sr) = r^k(r^{-1}s) = r^{k-1}s \neq r^{k+1}s$ in general. So no reflection is central. Therefore$Z(D_4) = \{e, r^2\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 2.** Find $C_{S_4}((1\;2\;3))$. We need all$\sigma \in S_4$ such that$\sigma(1\;2\;3)\sigma^{-1} = (1\;2\;3)$. In$S_n$, conjugation by$\sigma$ sends the cycle$(a_1\;a_2\;\cdots\;a_k)$ to$(\sigma(a_1)\;\sigma(a_2)\;\cdots\;\sigma(a_k))$. So$\sigma(1\;2\;3)\sigma^{-1} = (\sigma(1)\;\sigma(2)\;\sigma(3))$. This equals$(1\;2\;3)$ iff$\{\sigma(1), \sigma(2), \sigma(3)\} = \{1, 2, 3\}$ in cyclic order, i.e.,$\sigma$ either fixes$4$ and permutes$\{1,2,3\}$ as a power of$(1\;2\;3)$, or fixes$4$ and... actually$\sigma$ must map the set$\{1,2,3\}$ to itself and act on it as a power of$(1\;2\;3)$. Since$\sigma(4)$ must be$4$ (as$1,2,3$ are mapped among themselves),$\sigma$ restricted to$\{1,2,3\}$ is a power of$(1\;2\;3)$. So$C_{S_4}((1\;2\;3)) = \{e, (1\;2\;3), (1\;3\;2)\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$, of order$3$. The conjugacy class of$(1\;2\;3)$ in$S_4$ has size$[S_4 : C_{S_4}((1\;2\;3))] = 24/3 = 8$.

**Example 3.** Compute $N_{S_4}(V)$ where$V = \{e, (1\;2)(3\;4), (1\;3)(2\;4), (1\;4)(2\;3)\}$ (the Klein four-group inside$S_4$). We need all$\sigma$ with$\sigma V \sigma^{-1} = V$. Each element of$V$ is a product of two disjoint transpositions. Conjugation sends$(1\;2)(3\;4) \mapsto (\sigma(1)\;\sigma(2))(\sigma(3)\;\sigma(4))$. For this to land in$V$, the resulting pair of disjoint transpositions must be one of the three elements of$V \setminus \{e\}$. One checks that any$\sigma \in S_4$ sends$V$ to itself: since$V$ is the unique subgroup of$S_4$ isomorphic to$V_4$ consisting of all products of two disjoint transpositions, any permutation must either map it to itself or to a different such subgroup. In fact$V$ is the unique such subgroup of$S_4$, so$N_{S_4}(V) = S_4$, i.e.,$V \trianglelefteq S_4$.

**Example 4.** Show $Z(Q_8) = \{1, -1\}$. An element$g \in Q_8$ is central iff it commutes with$i$ and$j$ (which generate$Q_8$). We have$i \cdot i = i^2 = -1 = i \cdot i$,$j \cdot i = -ij = -k$,$i \cdot j = k$: so$ij \neq ji$ and$i \notin Z(Q_8)$ (since$i$ fails to commute with$j$). Similarly$j, k, -i, -j, -k \notin Z(Q_8)$. The elements$1$ and$-1$:$(-1) \cdot x = -x = x \cdot (-1)$ for all$x$ since$-1$ is in the center of$\mathbb{H}$. So$Z(Q_8) = \{1, -1\}$.

**Example 5.** For $G = GL_2(\mathbb{R})$, identify$Z(G)$ explicitly. An element$A$ is central iff$AB = BA$ for all invertible matrices$B$. Taking$B = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$:$AB = BA$ forces$A$ to be... write$A = \begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$; then$AB = \begin{pmatrix}a&a+b\\c&c+d\end{pmatrix}$ and$BA = \begin{pmatrix}a+c&b+d\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$. Equating:$a+c = a$ gives$c = 0$;$b+d = a+b$ gives$d = a$;$c = c$ gives nothing new. Taking$B = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ similarly forces$b = 0$. So$A = \begin{pmatrix}a&0\\0&a\end{pmatrix} = aI$ for some$a \neq 0$. Hence$Z(GL_2(\mathbb{R})) = \{aI : a \in \mathbb{R}^*\}$, the scalar matrices, isomorphic to$\mathbb{R}^*$.
