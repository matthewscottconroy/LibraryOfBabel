# 13.3 Subgroups

A subgroup is a subset of a group that is itself a group under the inherited operation. Recognizing and working with subgroups is one of the central skills of group theory, because the internal structure of a group is entirely encoded in the lattice of its subgroups — which subgroups exist, how they contain one another, and how they interact. Two essential tests determine when a subset is a subgroup: the one-step test ($H$ is a subgroup if $a, b \in H$ implies $ab^{-1} \in H$) and the two-step test ($H$ is a subgroup if it is closed under the operation and under taking inverses). For finite groups, closure under the operation already forces the inverse condition, giving an even simpler criterion.

Among the most structurally important subgroups are those that measure how non-abelian a group is. The center $Z(G) = \{g \in G : gx = xg \text{ for all } x \in G\}$ is the subgroup of elements that commute with everyone; it is always an abelian normal subgroup, and a group is abelian if and only if $Z(G) = G$. The centralizer $C_G(a) = \{g \in G : ga = ag\}$ of a single element $a$ is the set of elements that commute with $a$; it is a subgroup containing both $a$ and $Z(G)$. The normalizer $N_G(H) = \{g \in G : gHg^{-1} = H\}$ of a subgroup $H$ measures how close $H$ is to being normal: $H \trianglelefteq G$ if and only if $N_G(H) = G$. These three constructions — center, centralizer, normalizer — will reappear constantly, especially in the proof of the Sylow theorems via the class equation.

Subgroups generated by sets are the bridges between individual elements and the group at large. The subgroup $\langle S \rangle$ generated by a subset $S \subseteq G$ is the smallest subgroup containing $S$; concretely, it consists of all finite products of elements of $S$ and their inverses. When $S = \{a\}$ is a single element, $\langle a \rangle = \{a^n : n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is a cyclic subgroup. The order of an element $a$, denoted $|a|$ or $\text{ord}(a)$, is the smallest positive integer $n$ such that $a^n = e$, or $\infty$ if no such $n$ exists; it equals $|\langle a \rangle|$. The study of element orders is the bridge to Lagrange's theorem: in a finite group, the order of every element must divide the order of the group.
