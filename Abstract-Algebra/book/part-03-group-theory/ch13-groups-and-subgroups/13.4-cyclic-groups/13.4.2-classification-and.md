# 13.4.2 Classification: $\mathbb{Z}$ and$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$

One of the most satisfying early theorems in group theory is the complete classification of cyclic groups: up to isomorphism, there are exactly two types. Every infinite cyclic group is isomorphic to $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$, and every finite cyclic group of order$n$ is isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. This means that knowing a group is cyclic tells us essentially everything about its structure — the only thing left to specify is whether it is infinite or, if finite, what its order is.

The proof proceeds by constructing an explicit isomorphism. Let $G = \langle g \rangle$ be cyclic. Define the map$\varphi: \mathbb{Z} \to G$ by$\varphi(k) = g^k$. This is a surjective group homomorphism — surjective because every element of$G$ is a power of$g$, and a homomorphism because$\varphi(j + k) = g^{j+k} = g^j g^k = \varphi(j)\varphi(k)$. The key is to identify the kernel. If all powers of$g$ are distinct, the kernel is$\{0\}$, and the first isomorphism theorem gives$G \cong \mathbb{Z}/\{0\} \cong \mathbb{Z}$. If some power is repeated, the kernel is a nonzero subgroup of$\mathbb{Z}$, which must be of the form$n\mathbb{Z}$ for a unique$n > 0$; then$G \cong \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$.

To see why the kernel of $\varphi$ must be of the form$n\mathbb{Z}$, recall that every subgroup of$\mathbb{Z}$ is cyclic — in fact, every subgroup of$\mathbb{Z}$ is of the form$m\mathbb{Z}$ for some$m \geq 0$ (a fact that follows easily from the division algorithm: if$H \leq \mathbb{Z}$ is nonempty, take$m$ to be the smallest positive element, and the division algorithm forces every element of$H$ to be a multiple of$m$). The integer$n$ that appears is exactly$\mathrm{ord}(g)$, the smallest positive integer with$g^n = e$.

\begin{theorem}[Classification of Cyclic Groups]
Let $G$ be a cyclic group.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $G$ is infinite, then$G \cong \mathbb{Z}$.
\item If $G$ is finite of order$n$, then$G \cong \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$.
\end{enumerate}
In either case, any two cyclic groups of the same order are isomorphic.
\end{theorem}

The classification theorem tells us that when we write down a specific cyclic group — say, the rotational symmetries of a regular hexagon, or the additive group of integers modulo 12 — we are, at the level of abstract group structure, looking at nothing more than $\mathbb{Z}$ or$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. All cyclic groups of the same order are the same group, even if they arise from different contexts.

**Example 1.** The group of rotations of a regular $n$-gon forms a cyclic group of order$n$, generated by rotation by$2\pi/n$. By the classification theorem, this group is isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. For$n = 4$, the four rotations$\{e, r, r^2, r^3\}$ with$r^4 = e$ correspond to$\{\bar{0}, \bar{1}, \bar{2}, \bar{3}\}$ under the isomorphism$r^k \mapsto \bar{k}$.

**Example 2.** The additive group $n\mathbb{Z} = \{nk : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is an infinite cyclic subgroup of$\mathbb{Z}$, generated by$n$. By the classification theorem,$n\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}$ for any$n \neq 0$. The isomorphism$n\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ is given by$nk \mapsto k$; it is a bijective homomorphism since$n(j+k) = nj + nk$.

**Example 3.** The subgroup $\langle (12345) \rangle$ of$S_5$ generated by the 5-cycle$(12345)$ is cyclic of order 5 (since$(12345)^5 = e$ and no smaller positive power is the identity). By the classification theorem, this subgroup is isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$. The isomorphism sends$(12345)^k \mapsto \bar{k}$.

**Example 4.** The subgroup $\langle i \rangle$ of$Q_8$ generated by$i$ consists of$\{1, i, -1, -i\}$ with$i^4 = 1$. This is cyclic of order 4 and hence isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$. Note that$\langle -i \rangle = \langle i \rangle$, so$i$ and$-i$ are both generators of this cyclic subgroup, consistent with the fact that$\phi(4) = 2$.

**Example 5.** Consider the matrix group generated by
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \in GL_2(\mathbb{Q}).$$
One computes $A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$,$A^3 = I$. So$\langle A \rangle = \{I, A, A^2\}$ is cyclic of order 3, isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$.

\begin{remark}
The classification has a structural consequence for subgroups: every subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic. This is because a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}$ has the form$m\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}$, and a subgroup of$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is the image of a subgroup of$\mathbb{Z}$ under the projection, hence cyclic. We will make this precise and count all subgroups in Section 13.4.4.
\end{remark}
