# 13.4 Cyclic Groups

Cyclic groups are the simplest groups — they are generated by a single element — yet they are rich enough to encode a remarkable amount of structure, and their theory is a perfect warm-up for the classification results that follow. A group $G$ is cyclic if $G = \langle a \rangle$ for some $a \in G$, meaning every element of $G$ is a power of $a$. The two fundamental examples are $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ (finite, of order $n$, generated by the class of 1) and $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ (infinite, generated by 1 or $-1$). The classification theorem states that every cyclic group is isomorphic to exactly one of these: $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ if the group has finite order $n$, or $\mathbb{Z}$ if infinite. In particular, all cyclic groups of the same order are isomorphic to each other.

The subgroup structure of cyclic groups is governed entirely by divisibility, which is one of the most elegant theorems at this level. For $G = \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, there is exactly one subgroup of order $d$ for each divisor $d$ of $n$, namely the subgroup generated by $n/d$. The subgroups are linearly ordered by divisibility: $\langle n/d_1 \rangle \leq \langle n/d_2 \rangle$ if and only if $d_1 \mid d_2$. This means the subgroup lattice of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is isomorphic to the divisor lattice of $n$, a fact that makes cyclic groups especially transparent. For the infinite cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}$, every subgroup is of the form $n\mathbb{Z}$ for a unique $n \geq 0$, and these are linearly ordered by inclusion (since $n\mathbb{Z} \subseteq m\mathbb{Z}$ iff $m \mid n$).

The generators of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ are exactly those elements $\bar{k}$ with $\gcd(k, n) = 1$, and Euler's totient function $\phi(n)$ counts them. This connection to number theory runs deep: $\phi(n)$ counts integers from 1 to $n$ that are coprime to $n$, and it equals $n\prod_{p \mid n}(1 - 1/p)$. The multiplicativity of $\phi$ and the identity $\sum_{d \mid n} \phi(d) = n$ (which follows immediately by partitioning $\{1, \ldots, n\}$ according to $\gcd(k,n)$) are both consequences of the subgroup structure of cyclic groups. These results set the stage for Lagrange's theorem and, much later, for the structure theorem for finitely generated abelian groups.
