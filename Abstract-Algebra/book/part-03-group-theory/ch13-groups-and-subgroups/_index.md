# Chapter 13 — Groups and Subgroups

The concept of a group is one of the most productive abstractions in all of mathematics, yet it emerges from an observation of stunning simplicity: many of the most important objects we encounter — symmetries of geometric figures, permutations of a finite set, invertible matrices, residues modulo an integer — all share the same underlying arithmetic structure. They can be combined, the combination is associative, there is a neutral element, and every element can be undone. When Évariste Galois first glimpsed this structure in 1831 while studying which polynomial equations could be solved by radicals, he had no name for what he was seeing. Arthur Cayley gave it one in 1854, and the abstract theory of groups was born.

This chapter lays the foundation. We begin with the four group axioms — closure, associativity, identity, and inverses — and immediately derive their elementary but non-obvious consequences: the identity is unique, inverses are unique, the cancellation laws hold, and the order in which we write inverses reverses under composition. These are the first theorems of group theory, proved from nothing but the axioms, and they illustrate the power of the abstract approach: a single argument applies equally to symmetries of a pentagon, to matrices over a field, and to the integers under addition.

From the axioms we move to examples, which are the lifeblood of the theory. The symmetric group $S_n$ of all permutations of $n$ objects is the richest early example: it is non-abelian for $n \geq 3$, every element admits a cycle decomposition, and Cayley's theorem will show (in Chapter 16) that every group embeds in some $S_n$. The dihedral group $D_n$ captures the symmetries of a regular $n$-gon — $n$ rotations and $n$ reflections — and provides a concrete laboratory for non-commutativity. The quaternion group $Q_8$ is more exotic: a non-abelian group of order 8 that is not dihedral, in which every subgroup is normal. The matrix groups $\text{GL}_n(F)$ and $\text{SL}_n(F)$ connect group theory to linear algebra and will reappear throughout the book. Together these examples exhibit the remarkable range of phenomena that a short list of axioms can capture.

The internal structure of a group is organized by its subgroups — subsets closed under the group operation and inverses. The subgroup tests give efficient criteria for recognizing subgroups, and the concepts of centralizer, normalizer, and center measure how non-abelian a group is: the center $Z(G)$ consists of elements that commute with everything, and its size is a first quantitative indicator of the group's structure. Subgroups generated by a single element lead to cyclic subgroups and the notion of the order of an element, one of the most important numerical invariants in finite group theory.

The chapter closes with cyclic groups, which are the simplest groups and yet surprisingly rich. Every cyclic group of order $n$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, and the subgroup structure of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is governed entirely by divisibility: for each divisor $d$ of $n$ there is exactly one subgroup of order $d$, generated by $n/d$. Euler's totient function $\phi(n)$ counts the generators of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. This clean lattice structure makes cyclic groups the template against which all other groups are measured, and their classification sets the stage for Lagrange's theorem in Chapter 14.
