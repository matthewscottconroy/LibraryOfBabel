# 14.1.3 Lagrange's Theorem and Its Proof

With the coset partition in hand, the central theorem of this chapter follows almost immediately from counting.

\begin{theorem}[Lagrange]
If $G$ is a finite group and$H \leq G$, then$|H|$ divides$|G|$. More precisely,$|G| = [G:H] \cdot |H|$.
\end{theorem}

The proof is a one-paragraph argument. By Section 14.1.1, the left cosets of $H$ partition$G$ into$[G:H]$ disjoint subsets, each of cardinality$|H|$ (since the map$h \mapsto gh$ is a bijection from$H$ to$gH$). Counting the elements of$G$ by adding up the sizes of the parts of the partition gives$|G| = [G:H] \cdot |H|$. Since$[G:H]$ is a positive integer,$|H|$ divides$|G|$.

The proof requires no normality assumption and applies to every subgroup. It is a testament to how much mileage can be extracted from a single clean observation about partitions.

It is worth appreciating what Lagrange's theorem does and does not say. It tells us that the order of any subgroup must divide the group order, and thus imposes strong restrictions on what subgroups can exist: a group of order 15 can have subgroups of order 1, 3, 5, or 15, and no others. What the theorem does not say is that a subgroup of each permitted order necessarily exists; that is a harder problem, addressed in part by the Sylow theorems. Lagrange's theorem is a necessary condition for subgroup existence, not a sufficient one.

**Example 1.** The symmetric group $S_4$ has order 24. Its divisors are$1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24$. Lagrange's theorem says any subgroup must have one of these orders. Indeed,$S_4$ contains subgroups of all these orders: the trivial group (order 1), the transpositions$\langle (12) \rangle$ (order 2), the 3-cycles$\langle (123) \rangle$ (order 3), the Klein four-group$\{e,(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)\}$ (order 4), the subgroup generated by$(123)$ and$(12)$ (order 6, isomorphic to$S_3$), a Sylow 2-subgroup such as$\langle (1234),(13) \rangle$ (order 8, isomorphic to$D_4$), the alternating group$A_4$ (order 12), and$S_4$ itself.

**Example 2.** A group of order 35 cannot have a subgroup of order 7 and a subgroup of order 5 that together generate a group of order other than 35 — any subgroup must have order dividing 35, so orders 1, 5, 7, 35. This is the first step toward proving that every group of order 35 is cyclic.

**Example 3.** Lagrange's theorem implies that in $\mathbb{Z}/15\mathbb{Z}$, any subgroup has order dividing 15. The divisors are 1, 3, 5, 15, so the subgroups have these orders. Explicitly:$\langle \bar{0} \rangle$ (order 1),$\langle \bar{5} \rangle = \{\bar{0},\bar{5},\bar{10}\}$ (order 3),$\langle \bar{3} \rangle = \{\bar{0},\bar{3},\bar{6},\bar{9},\bar{12}\}$ (order 5), and$\mathbb{Z}/15\mathbb{Z}$ itself (order 15).

**Example 4.** In $GL_2(\mathbb{F}_3)$, the order is$(3^2-1)(3^2-3) = 8 \cdot 6 = 48$. Any subgroup of$GL_2(\mathbb{F}_3)$ has order dividing 48. In particular, the subgroup$SL_2(\mathbb{F}_3)$ has order$|GL_2(\mathbb{F}_3)|/|(\mathbb{F}_3)^*| = 48/2 = 24$.

**Example 5.** Let $\sigma = (1 2 3 4 5)(6 7) \in S_7$. The order of$\sigma$ is$\mathrm{lcm}(5,2) = 10$. By Lagrange,$\langle \sigma \rangle$ is a subgroup of$S_7$ of order 10, and$10 \mid |S_7| = 5040$. One checks:$5040 / 10 = 504$, which is an integer, consistent with Lagrange.

\begin{remark}
The theorem is named after Joseph-Louis Lagrange, who observed the underlying counting principle in the context of polynomial roots in the 1770s, before the abstract concept of a group had been formulated. Its clean statement in the language of groups came later, as the group concept crystallized in the work of Galois, Cauchy, and others. The converse of Lagrange's theorem — that for every divisor $d$ of$|G|$ there exists a subgroup of order$d$ — fails in general:$A_4$ has order 12 but no subgroup of order 6. The Sylow theorems recover a partial converse for prime power divisors.
\end{remark}
