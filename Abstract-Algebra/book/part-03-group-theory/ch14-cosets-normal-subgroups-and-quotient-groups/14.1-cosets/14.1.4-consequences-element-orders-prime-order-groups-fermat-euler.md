# 14.1.4 Consequences: Element Orders, Prime-Order Groups, Fermat–Euler

Lagrange's theorem has immediate and striking consequences that reach from abstract group theory into classical number theory.

**Orders of elements.** Since $\langle g \rangle$ is a subgroup of$G$, its order$\mathrm{ord}(g) = |\langle g \rangle|$ must divide$|G|$ for any$g$ in a finite group. In particular,$g^{|G|} = e$ for every$g \in G$, since$g^{|G|} = (g^{\mathrm{ord}(g)})^{|G|/\mathrm{ord}(g)} = e$. This single observation subsumes a great deal of arithmetic.

\begin{corollary}
If $G$ is a finite group of order$n$, then$g^n = e$ for every$g \in G$.
\end{corollary}

**Classification of prime-order groups.** The second consequence classifies groups of prime order completely.

\begin{corollary}
If $|G| = p$ is prime, then$G \cong \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The only divisors of $p$ are$1$ and$p$, so the only subgroups of$G$ have order$1$ or$p$. For any nonidentity element$g \in G$, the cyclic subgroup$\langle g \rangle$ is nontrivial, hence has order$p$, so$\langle g \rangle = G$. Thus$G$ is cyclic, and every cyclic group of order$p$ is isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$.
\end{proof}

**Fermat–Euler from group theory.** The third consequence recovers the classical number-theoretic theorems of Euler and Fermat. The group $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^* = \{\bar{a} : \gcd(a,n)=1\}$ of units modulo$n$ is a finite group of order$\phi(n)$.

\begin{corollary}[Euler's Theorem]
If $\gcd(a,n) = 1$, then$a^{\phi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod{n}$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The element $\bar{a} \in (\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^*$ satisfies$\bar{a}^{\phi(n)} = \overline{a^{\phi(n)}} = \bar{1}$ by the corollary above, giving$a^{\phi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod{n}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Fermat's Little Theorem]
If $p$ is prime and$p \nmid a$, then$a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$.
\end{corollary}

This follows from Euler's theorem since $\phi(p) = p-1$ for a prime$p$.

**Example 1.** Let $G = \mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z}$ and$a = \bar{3}$. Then$\mathrm{ord}(\bar{3}) \mid |G| = 7$, so the order is 1 or 7. Since$\bar{3} \neq \bar{0}$, the order is 7, and$\bar{3}$ is a generator. One computes$3^7 \equiv 3 \pmod{7}$ (Fermat gives$3^6 \equiv 1$, then$3^7 \equiv 3$).

**Example 2.** For Euler's theorem with $n = 10$,$\phi(10) = \phi(2)\phi(5) = 1 \cdot 4 = 4$. Any integer$a$ with$\gcd(a,10)=1$ satisfies$a^4 \equiv 1 \pmod{10}$. Check:$3^4 = 81 \equiv 1$,$7^4 = 2401 \equiv 1$,$9^4 = 6561 \equiv 1 \pmod{10}$. All correct.

**Example 3.** A group of order $p$ has no proper nontrivial subgroups. As a consequence, every group of order 13 is cyclic. Moreover, the only homomorphisms$\varphi: G \to H$ from a group of prime order$p$ are the trivial homomorphism (mapping everything to$e_H$) or injections (when$p \mid |H|$).

**Example 4.** In $S_5$, every element satisfies$\sigma^{120} = e$ since$|S_5| = 120$. More precisely, the order of any permutation in$S_n$ is the least common multiple of its cycle lengths. For$\sigma = (12345)(678)(9,10) \in S_{10}$,$\mathrm{ord}(\sigma) = \mathrm{lcm}(5,3,2) = 30$, and$30 \mid |S_{10}| = 10!$, consistent with Lagrange.

**Example 5.** The RSA cryptosystem exploits Euler's theorem. Given an RSA modulus $n = pq$ (product of two primes),$\phi(n) = (p-1)(q-1)$. If$ed \equiv 1 \pmod{\phi(n)}$, then for any message$m$ with$\gcd(m,n)=1$:
$$(m^e)^d = m^{ed} = m^{1 + k\phi(n)} = m \cdot (m^{\phi(n)})^k \equiv m \cdot 1^k = m \pmod{n}.$$
The group-theoretic proof via Lagrange is precisely what guarantees this decryption step works.

\begin{remark}
Lagrange's theorem is the first instance of a principle that recurs throughout algebra: the global structure of an object is constrained by how it maps to its quotients. The size of a subgroup divides the size of the group; the size of an ideal quotient divides the size of the ring; the degree of a subfield extension divides the degree of a field extension. Each of these is a manifestation of the same counting argument.
\end{remark}
