# 14.1 Cosets

Given a subgroup $H$ of a group $G$, the left coset of $H$ determined by an element $g \in G$ is the set $gH = \{gh : h \in H\}$. Right cosets $Hg = \{hg : h \in H\}$ are defined similarly. The central theorem of this section is that the collection of distinct left cosets of $H$ partitions $G$ into disjoint, equal-sized subsets: two cosets $aH$ and $bH$ are either identical or disjoint (never partially overlapping), and every coset has exactly $|H|$ elements. The proof is clean and instructive: $aH = bH$ if and only if $b^{-1}a \in H$, which defines an equivalence relation on $G$ whose classes are precisely the left cosets. This partition is the coset decomposition of $G$ by $H$.

The number of distinct left cosets of $H$ in $G$ is the index $[G:H]$, also written $|G:H|$. For finite groups, counting the elements in the coset partition gives the fundamental relation $|G| = |H| \cdot [G:H]$, which is the content of Lagrange's theorem: the order of a subgroup divides the order of the group. The index $[G:H]$ can be finite even when $G$ is infinite (e.g., $[\mathbb{Z} : n\mathbb{Z}] = n$), and the product formula $[G:K] = [G:H][H:K]$ for nested subgroups $K \leq H \leq G$ is a useful consequence.

The coset partition carries immediate consequences for element orders. Since $\langle a \rangle$ is a subgroup of $G$ of order $\text{ord}(a)$, Lagrange's theorem implies $\text{ord}(a)$ divides $|G|$ for any finite group $G$. In particular, $a^{|G|} = e$ for every $a \in G$ — a fact that specializes to Fermat's little theorem (when $G = (\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^*$) and Euler's theorem (when $G = (\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^*$). These applications demonstrate that Lagrange's theorem is not an abstract curiosity but a theorem with direct computational consequences in number theory, and its proof is simply the observation that the group's elements are organized into equal-sized boxes by any subgroup.
