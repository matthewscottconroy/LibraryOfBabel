# 14.2.1 The Normality Condition: $gNg^{-1} = N$

Lagrange's theorem tells us that every subgroup partitions the group into cosets of equal size. But not every subgroup allows those cosets to be multiplied together in a sensible way. To understand which subgroups do, we need to isolate the precise condition that makes coset multiplication well-defined.

\begin{definition}
A subgroup $N \leq G$ is \textbf{normal} in$G$, written$N \trianglelefteq G$, if$gNg^{-1} = N$ for every$g \in G$. The set$gNg^{-1} = \{gng^{-1} : n \in N\}$ is called the \textbf{conjugate} of$N$ by$g$.
\end{definition}

Normality demands that conjugation by any group element maps $N$ to itself — the subgroup is stable under all inner automorphisms of$G$. An equivalent and often more intuitive formulation is that the left and right cosets of$N$ coincide:$gN = Ng$ for all$g \in G$. This does not mean that$g$ commutes with every element of$N$ individually; it means that the entire left coset$gN$ equals the entire right coset$Ng$ as sets. To see the equivalence:$gN = Ng$ iff$gNg^{-1} = N$ (multiply on the right by$g^{-1}$).

To understand why normality matters, attempt to define a multiplication on cosets by $(aN)(bN) = (ab)N$. For this to be well-defined, the product must be independent of the choice of coset representatives: if$aN = a'N$ and$bN = b'N$, we need$(ab)N = (a'b')N$. Writing$a' = an_1$ and$b' = bn_2$ for some$n_1, n_2 \in N$, we compute$a'b' = an_1 bn_2 = a(n_1 b)n_2$. For$(a'b')N = (ab)N$, we need$n_1 b \in bN$, i.e.,$b^{-1}n_1 b \in N$ — exactly the normality condition. Without it, coset multiplication is undefined, and the entire quotient group construction collapses.

**Example 1.** Every subgroup of an abelian group is normal, since $gNg^{-1} = \{gng^{-1} : n \in N\} = \{n : n \in N\} = N$ whenever$G$ is abelian (as$gng^{-1} = n$ for all$n$). So in$\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$, every subgroup is normal.

**Example 2.** In $S_3$, the subgroup$\langle (12) \rangle = \{e, (12)\}$ is not normal. Conjugating by$(13)$:$(13)(12)(13)^{-1} = (23) \notin \langle (12) \rangle$. So$gNg^{-1} \neq N$ for$g = (13)$.

**Example 3.** The alternating group $A_n \trianglelefteq S_n$ for all$n$. The sign homomorphism$\mathrm{sgn}: S_n \to \{\pm 1\}$ has$A_n$ as its kernel, and every kernel is normal.

**Example 4.** The center $Z(G)$ is always normal in$G$, since for$z \in Z(G)$ and any$g$,$gzg^{-1} = z$ (by definition of the center). Thus$gZ(G)g^{-1} = Z(G)$.

**Example 5.** In $Q_8 = \{\pm 1, \pm i, \pm j, \pm k\}$, consider$H = \langle i \rangle = \{1, i, -1, -i\}$. To check normality:$jij^{-1} = j \cdot i \cdot (-j) = -ji \cdot j = -(ji)j$. Using$ji = -k$ and$(-k)j = -kj = -(-i) = i$, so$jij^{-1} = i \in H$. One checks that$jHj^{-1} = H$,$kHk^{-1} = H$, and similarly for$-j, -k$. So$\langle i \rangle \trianglelefteq Q_8$. In fact every subgroup of$Q_8$ is normal, because$Q_8$ has a unique element of order 2 (namely$-1$) and all subgroups of index 2 are normal.

\begin{proposition}
A subgroup $N \leq G$ of index 2 is always normal.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
There are exactly two left cosets of $N$ in$G$:$N$ itself and$G \setminus N$. The same holds for right cosets. For any$g \in G$, if$g \in N$ then$gN = N = Ng$. If$g \notin N$ then$gN = G \setminus N = Ng$. In either case$gN = Ng$, so$N \trianglelefteq G$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The normality condition is about conjugation stabilizing the subgroup set-wise, not element-wise. A normal subgroup $N$ can have nontrivial conjugation action on its own elements ($gng^{-1}$ need not equal$n$ for$n \in N$,$g \notin N$), but the set$N$ itself is mapped to itself by every conjugation. This is weaker than requiring$N \leq Z(G)$, which would force every element of$N$ to be individually fixed by conjugation.
\end{remark}
