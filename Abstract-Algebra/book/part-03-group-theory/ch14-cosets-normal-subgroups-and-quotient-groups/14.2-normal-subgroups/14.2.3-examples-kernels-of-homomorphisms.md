# 14.2.3 Examples: $A_n$,$Z(G)$,$SL_n$, Kernels of Homomorphisms

The abstract definition of normality becomes vivid through examples, and the most important examples arise as kernels of naturally defined homomorphisms. The pattern is systematic: whenever mathematicians define a natural map from a group to a simpler structure, the kernel is automatically a normal subgroup that encodes the "invisible" part.

The alternating group $A_n$ is normal in$S_n$. The **sign homomorphism**$\mathrm{sgn}: S_n \to \{1, -1\}$, which assigns$+1$ to even permutations and$-1$ to odd ones, is a surjective group homomorphism (for$n \geq 2$), and$A_n = \ker(\mathrm{sgn})$. Since every kernel is normal,$A_n \trianglelefteq S_n$. The index$[S_n : A_n] = 2$, and any subgroup of index 2 is automatically normal — since then$G$ is partitioned into only two left cosets,$H$ and$G \setminus H$, and the same partition arises from right cosets.

The center $Z(G)$ is always normal in$G$. For any$z \in Z(G)$ and$g \in G$, we have$gzg^{-1} = z \in Z(G)$, so conjugation by any element fixes every element of the center. Thus$gZ(G)g^{-1} = Z(G)$ for all$g$.

The special linear group $SL_n(\mathbb{F})$ is normal in$GL_n(\mathbb{F})$. The determinant$\det: GL_n(\mathbb{F}) \to \mathbb{F}^*$ is a surjective group homomorphism, and$SL_n(\mathbb{F}) = \ker(\det)$. The quotient$GL_n(\mathbb{F})/SL_n(\mathbb{F}) \cong \mathbb{F}^*$ will follow from the first isomorphism theorem.

**Example 1.** The sign homomorphism on $S_4$ gives$A_4 \trianglelefteq S_4$ with$|A_4| = 12$. The four cosets of$A_4$ in$S_4$ do not exist (there are only two:$A_4$ and the set of odd permutations). Elements of$A_4$: all products of an even number of transpositions — the 3-cycles, double transpositions, and the identity.

**Example 2.** In $GL_2(\mathbb{F}_5)$, the determinant takes values in$\mathbb{F}_5^* = \{1,2,3,4\}$ (order 4). The kernel$SL_2(\mathbb{F}_5)$ has order$|GL_2(\mathbb{F}_5)|/4 = (5^2-1)(5^2-5)/4 = 24 \cdot 20/4 = 120$. This group$SL_2(\mathbb{F}_5)$ contains a normal subgroup$\{\pm I\}$ of order 2, and the quotient$PSL_2(\mathbb{F}_5) = SL_2(\mathbb{F}_5)/\{\pm I\}$ is isomorphic to$A_5$ — the smallest nonabelian simple group.

**Example 3.** In $D_n = \langle r, s : r^n = s^2 = e, srs = r^{-1} \rangle$, the rotation subgroup$\langle r \rangle$ is normal of index 2. The map$D_n \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ sending rotations to$\bar{0}$ and reflections to$\bar{1}$ is a surjective homomorphism with kernel$\langle r \rangle$.

**Example 4.** The commutator subgroup $[G,G]$ is always normal in$G$. It is generated by commutators$[a,b] = aba^{-1}b^{-1}$, and the map$G \to G/[G,G]$ is the universal abelianization — the kernel of any homomorphism from$G$ to an abelian group contains$[G,G]$.

**Example 5.** In $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, every subgroup is normal (since$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is abelian). The subgroup$d\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} = \{\bar{0}, \bar{d}, \overline{2d}, \ldots\}$ for each divisor$d$ of$n$ is the kernel of the homomorphism$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}$ given by$\bar{k} \mapsto \bar{k} \bmod d$.

These examples illustrate a general principle: the most natural subgroups in mathematics tend to appear as kernels of naturally defined homomorphisms. Conversely, every normal subgroup is the kernel of the projection to the corresponding quotient group. This tight connection between normality and homomorphisms is one of the organizing themes of the entire theory.

\begin{remark}
Not every normal subgroup is interesting or "geometric" in the way that $A_n$ and$SL_n$ are. In particular, a group is called \textbf{simple} if it has no normal subgroups other than$\{e\}$ and$G$ itself. The classification of finite simple groups — completed in the late 20th century and spanning thousands of journal pages — is the deepest structural theorem in finite group theory. Understanding which groups are simple is in some sense the foundational problem of the subject.
\end{remark}
