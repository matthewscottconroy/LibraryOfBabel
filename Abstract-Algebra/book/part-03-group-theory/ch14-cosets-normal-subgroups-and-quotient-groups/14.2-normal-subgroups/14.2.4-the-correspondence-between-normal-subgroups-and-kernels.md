# 14.2.4 The Correspondence Between Normal Subgroups and Kernels

We have seen that every kernel of a group homomorphism is a normal subgroup. The converse is equally true: every normal subgroup is a kernel. Together, these two facts establish a perfect bijective correspondence between normal subgroups of $G$ and (surjective) group homomorphisms out of$G$, up to isomorphism of the codomain.

\begin{theorem}
A subgroup $N \leq G$ is normal if and only if there exists a group homomorphism$\varphi: G \to H$ such that$N = \ker \varphi$.
\end{theorem}

The forward direction ($\Rightarrow$) is proved constructively. Given$N \trianglelefteq G$, the canonical projection$\pi: G \to G/N$ defined by$\pi(g) = gN$ is a surjective group homomorphism with$\ker(\pi) = N$. We verify:$\pi(ab) = (ab)N = (aN)(bN) = \pi(a)\pi(b)$, so$\pi$ is a homomorphism. Surjectivity is immediate. And$\ker(\pi) = \{g : gN = N\} = \{g : g \in N\} = N$.

The backward direction ($\Leftarrow$) was proved in Section 15.1.3: if$\varphi: G \to H$ is any homomorphism, then$\ker \varphi$ is normal because for any$g \in G$ and$k \in \ker \varphi$,$\varphi(gkg^{-1}) = \varphi(g)\varphi(k)\varphi(g)^{-1} = \varphi(g) e_H \varphi(g)^{-1} = e_H$, so$gkg^{-1} \in \ker\varphi$.

This bijection — normal subgroups correspond exactly to kernels of surjective homomorphisms — has a direct analogue in ring theory, where ideals correspond exactly to kernels of ring homomorphisms. The parallel is not a coincidence: both are instances of the general algebraic principle that "quotient structures" are characterized by the substructures that collapse to the identity.

**Example 1.** The three normal subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ of various orders all arise as kernels: the subgroup$\langle \bar{6} \rangle$ of order 2 is$\ker(\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z})$; the subgroup$\langle \bar{4} \rangle$ of order 3 is$\ker(\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z})$ (via$\bar{k} \mapsto \bar{k} \bmod 4$); and so on.

**Example 2.** The trivial subgroup $\{e\} \trianglelefteq G$ is always normal — it is the kernel of any injective homomorphism from$G$. The whole group$G \trianglelefteq G$ is the kernel of the trivial homomorphism$G \to \{e\}$. So the two "degenerate" normal subgroups correspond to the two degenerate homomorphisms.

**Example 3.** In $S_3$, the normal subgroups are$\{e\}$,$A_3 = \langle (123) \rangle$, and$S_3$ itself. Each is a kernel:$\{e\} = \ker(\mathrm{id})$,$A_3 = \ker(\mathrm{sgn}: S_3 \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})$,$S_3 = \ker(S_3 \to \{e\})$.

**Example 4.** In $\mathbb{Z}$, the normal subgroups are exactly$n\mathbb{Z}$ for$n \geq 0$. Each is a kernel:$n\mathbb{Z} = \ker(\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})$. The correspondence gives a bijection between nonneg integers$n$ and normal subgroups of$\mathbb{Z}$, matching the subgroup lattice of$\mathbb{Z}$ with the quotients$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 5.** Consider $G = D_4$ with its normal subgroup$N = \langle r^2 \rangle = \{e, r^2\}$ of order 2. The corresponding surjective homomorphism is$D_4 \to D_4/N \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ (since$D_4/\langle r^2 \rangle$ has order 4 and is abelian, but not cyclic — one can check it has three elements of order 2).

The correspondence has an important amplification: the **correspondence theorem** (also called the fourth isomorphism theorem) describes the lattice of subgroups of $G/N$ in terms of subgroups of$G$ containing$N$. Specifically, there is an order-preserving bijection between subgroups of$G$ that contain$N$ and subgroups of$G/N$, with normal subgroups corresponding to normal subgroups. This correspondence theorem will be stated and proved in Chapter 15 alongside the isomorphism theorems.

\begin{remark}
The correspondence between normal subgroups and kernels means that studying all group homomorphisms out of $G$ is equivalent to studying all normal subgroups of$G$. This motivates the program of representation theory: to understand a group$G$, study all of its homomorphisms into well-understood groups (such as$GL_n(\mathbb{C})$, the group of invertible complex matrices). Each such homomorphism reveals a normal subgroup (its kernel) and a quotient of$G$ that maps into$GL_n(\mathbb{C})$.
\end{remark}
