# 14.2 Normal Subgroups

For a general subgroup $H \leq G$, the left cosets and right cosets of $H$ need not coincide. When they do — when $gH = Hg$ for every $g \in G$ — the subgroup $H$ is called normal, written $H \trianglelefteq G$. This condition admits several equivalent formulations: $gHg^{-1} = H$ for all $g$ (invariance under conjugation); $gHg^{-1} \subseteq H$ for all $g$ (the two-sided condition is automatic by a cardinality argument in the finite case); or $ghg^{-1} \in H$ for all $g \in G$ and $h \in H$. Each formulation is useful in different contexts, and converting fluently between them is an essential skill.

The structural significance of normality is that it is exactly the condition needed to make the set of cosets into a group. If we attempt to multiply cosets by $(aH)(bH) = (ab)H$, the question is whether this is well-defined: if $aH = a'H$ and $bH = b'H$, is $(ab)H = (a'b')H$? A direct computation shows that well-definedness holds for all choices of $a, a', b, b'$ if and only if $H$ is normal in $G$. This is the precise theorem: $G/H$ is a group under coset multiplication if and only if $H \trianglelefteq G$. Normality is not just a sufficient condition but a necessary one, which explains why it is the correct notion and not some stronger or weaker variant.

Among the most important normal subgroups are kernels of homomorphisms. If $\phi: G \to H$ is any group homomorphism, then $\ker(\phi) = \{g \in G : \phi(g) = e_H\}$ is a normal subgroup of $G$: for any $k \in \ker(\phi)$ and $g \in G$, $\phi(gkg^{-1}) = \phi(g)\phi(k)\phi(g)^{-1} = \phi(g)e_H\phi(g)^{-1} = e_H$. The First Isomorphism Theorem (Chapter 15) will show that this is not just an example but the universal source of normal subgroups: every normal subgroup is the kernel of some homomorphism, namely the projection $G \to G/H$. This correspondence between normal subgroups and kernels is the cornerstone of the entire theory of quotient groups.
