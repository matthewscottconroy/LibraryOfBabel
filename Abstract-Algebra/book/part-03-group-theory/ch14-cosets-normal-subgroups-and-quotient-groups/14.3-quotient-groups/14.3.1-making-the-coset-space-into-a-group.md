# 14.3.1 Making the Coset Space Into a Group

We have established that the cosets of any subgroup $H \leq G$ partition$G$. The natural next question is whether the set of cosets can itself be given a group structure. As we saw in Section 14.2.1, attempting to define coset multiplication by$(aN)(bN) = (ab)N$ runs into a well-definedness problem: the result must be independent of which representatives$a$ and$b$ we choose for the cosets$aN$ and$bN$. Normality is exactly the condition that resolves this problem.

\begin{theorem}
Let $N \trianglelefteq G$. The set$G/N$ of left cosets of$N$ in$G$, equipped with the multiplication$(aN)(bN) = (ab)N$, is a group. It is called the \textbf{quotient group} of$G$ by$N$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\textbf{Well-definedness.} Suppose $aN = a'N$ and$bN = b'N$, so$a' = an_1$ and$b' = bn_2$ for some$n_1, n_2 \in N$. Then
$$a'b' = an_1 bn_2 = a(n_1 b)n_2.$$
Since $N \trianglelefteq G$, the element$b^{-1}n_1 b \in N$, so$n_1 b = b(b^{-1}n_1 b) = bn_3$ for some$n_3 \in N$. Therefore$a'b' = ab n_3 n_2 \in (ab)N$, so$(a'b')N = (ab)N$.

\textbf{Associativity.} $((aN)(bN))(cN) = (ab)N \cdot cN = (abc)N = (aN)((bc)N) = (aN)((bN)(cN))$, which holds by associativity in$G$.

\textbf{Identity.} The coset $N = eN$ is the identity:$(eN)(aN) = (ea)N = aN$ and$(aN)(eN) = (ae)N = aN$.

\textbf{Inverses.} The inverse of $aN$ is$a^{-1}N$:$(aN)(a^{-1}N) = (aa^{-1})N = eN = N$.
\end{proof}

The normality of $N$ was used once and only once in this argument: to prove well-definedness. This confirms that normality is not merely sufficient but necessary for the quotient construction to succeed. More precisely: the coset multiplication is well-defined if and only if$N$ is normal.

**Example 1.** Let $G = \mathbb{Z}$ and$N = n\mathbb{Z}$. Since$\mathbb{Z}$ is abelian,$n\mathbb{Z}$ is normal. The quotient group$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ has elements$\{\bar{0}, \bar{1}, \ldots, \overline{n-1}\}$ with addition$\bar{a} + \bar{b} = \overline{a+b}$ (reducing modulo$n$). This is exactly the familiar modular arithmetic.

**Example 2.** Let $G = S_3$ and$N = A_3 = \langle (123) \rangle = \{e, (123), (132)\}$ (normal since$[S_3:A_3] = 2$). The two cosets are$A_3$ and$(12)A_3 = \{(12),(23),(13)\}$. The quotient$S_3/A_3$ has order 2 with multiplication:$A_3 \cdot A_3 = A_3$,$A_3 \cdot (12)A_3 = (12)A_3$,$(12)A_3 \cdot (12)A_3 = (12)(12) A_3 = A_3$. So$S_3/A_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 3.** Let $G = D_4$ and$N = \langle r^2 \rangle = \{e, r^2\}$. The four cosets are$N = \{e,r^2\}$,$rN = \{r, r^3\}$,$sN = \{s, sr^2\}$,$srN = \{sr, sr^3\}$. The quotient$D_4/N$ has order 4. One checks:$(rN)^2 = r^2 N = N$ (identity),$(sN)^2 = s^2 N = N$,$(rN)(sN) = rsN = (sr^{-1})N = srN$ (using$rs = sr^{-1}$). So$D_4/N \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 4.** Let $G = GL_n(\mathbb{F})$ and$N = SL_n(\mathbb{F})$. The cosets are the sets of matrices with a given determinant:$\lambda N = \{A \in GL_n(\mathbb{F}) : \det A = \lambda\}$. Multiplication:$(\lambda N)(\mu N) = (\lambda\mu)N$. So$GL_n(\mathbb{F})/SL_n(\mathbb{F}) \cong (\mathbb{F}^*, \cdot)$ via$\lambda N \mapsto \lambda$.

**Example 5.** Let $G = \mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ and$N = \langle \bar{4} \rangle = \{\bar{0},\bar{4},\bar{8}\}$. The cosets are$N$,$\bar{1}+N$,$\bar{2}+N$,$\bar{3}+N$. The quotient$G/N$ has order 4, and$(\bar{1}+N) + (\bar{1}+N) = \bar{2}+N$,$(\bar{1}+N)^4 = \bar{4}+N = N$. Since$\bar{1}+N$ has order 4 in$G/N$, we get$G/N \cong \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$.

\begin{remark}
The quotient group construction is the group-theoretic analogue of modding out by an equivalence relation: two elements $a, b \in G$ become equal in$G/N$ if and only if$a^{-1}b \in N$, i.e.,$a$ and$b$ lie in the same coset. The group structure on$G/N$ is the unique one making the projection$\pi: G \to G/N$ a group homomorphism — a statement that will be made precise by the universal property in Section 14.3.3.
\end{remark}
