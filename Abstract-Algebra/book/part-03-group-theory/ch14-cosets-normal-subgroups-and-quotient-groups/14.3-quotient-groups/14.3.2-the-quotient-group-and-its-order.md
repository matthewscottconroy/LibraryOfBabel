# 14.3.2 The Quotient Group $G/N$ and Its Order

The group constructed in the previous section — the set of cosets of $N$ in$G$, with multiplication$(aN)(bN) = (ab)N$ — is called the **quotient group** of$G$ by$N$, and is written$G/N$ (read "$G$ mod$N$"). Its elements are the cosets$\{aN : a \in G\}$, its identity is$N = eN$, and the inverse of a coset$aN$ is the coset$a^{-1}N$.

The order of the quotient group is the number of cosets of $N$ in$G$, which is the index$[G:N]$. When$G$ is finite, Lagrange's theorem gives:
$$|G/N| = [G:N] = \frac{|G|}{|N|}.$$

The quotient group is in a precise sense "simpler" than $G$: it has fewer elements, and those elements are coarser objects (entire cosets) rather than individual group elements. The passage from$G$ to$G/N$ systematically forgets all distinctions within each coset while preserving the multiplication structure between them.

The intuition is best captured by analogy with modular arithmetic. When we work modulo $n$, we declare all multiples of$n$ to be equivalent to zero and study only the residues. The resulting structure$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is the quotient of$\mathbb{Z}$ by the subgroup$n\mathbb{Z}$. The quotient group construction generalizes this: given any normal subgroup$N$, we "collapse"$N$ to the identity and study how the rest of$G$ behaves relative to this identification.

**Example 1.** $|S_4/A_4| = 24/12 = 2$, and indeed$S_4/A_4 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ — the only group of order 2.

**Example 2.** $|D_6/\langle r \rangle| = 12/6 = 2$. The quotient$D_6/\langle r \rangle$ has elements$\langle r \rangle$ (all rotations) and$s\langle r \rangle$ (all reflections), and is isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 3.** $|\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}| = n$. We can continue: given$d \mid n$, the quotient$(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})/(\langle \bar{d} \rangle)$ has order$n/(n/d) = d$ and is isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}$. Explicitly for$n=12$,$d=4$: the quotient$(\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z})/\langle \bar{3} \rangle$ has order$12/4 = 3$ (since$|\langle \bar{3} \rangle| = 4$), so it is isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 4.** In $Q_8$, the center$Z(Q_8) = \{1, -1\}$ is normal of order 2. The quotient$Q_8/Z(Q_8)$ has order$8/2 = 4$. Its elements are$\{1,-1\}$,$\{i,-i\}$,$\{j,-j\}$,$\{k,-k\}$. Multiplication:$\{i,-i\}^2 = \{i^2, (-i)^2, i(-i), (-i)i\} = \{-1, -1, 1, 1\}$... but in the quotient,$\{i,-i\} \cdot \{i,-i\} = \{i \cdot i, i \cdot (-i), (-i) \cdot i, (-i)(-i)\} = \{-1,1,1,-1\}$, which is the coset$\{1,-1\} = Z(Q_8)$. So every nonidentity element of$Q_8/Z(Q_8)$ has order 2, and the quotient is isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 5.** The quotient $GL_n(\mathbb{F})/Z(GL_n(\mathbb{F}))$ is called the **projective general linear group**$PGL_n(\mathbb{F})$. Its center$Z(GL_n(\mathbb{F})) = \{\lambda I : \lambda \in \mathbb{F}^*\}$ (scalar matrices) has order$|\mathbb{F}^*|$. For$\mathbb{F} = \mathbb{F}_q$ (field with$q$ elements),$|PGL_n(\mathbb{F}_q)| = |GL_n(\mathbb{F}_q)|/(q-1)$.

\begin{remark}
The order formula $|G/N| = |G|/|N|$ immediately constrains which quotient groups are possible. In particular, if$|G| = p$ is prime, the only quotients are$G/\{e\} \cong G$ (of order$p$) and$G/G \cong \{e\}$ (of order 1), reflecting that groups of prime order are simple. More generally, understanding the quotient groups of$G$ is equivalent to understanding the normal subgroups of$G$ and their lattice of containments.
\end{remark}
