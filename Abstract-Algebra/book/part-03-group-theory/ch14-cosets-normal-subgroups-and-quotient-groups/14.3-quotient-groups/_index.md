# 14.3 Quotient Groups

When $N \trianglelefteq G$, the set $G/N$ of left cosets becomes a group under the multiplication $(aN)(bN) = (ab)N$. This group is the quotient group (or factor group) of $G$ by $N$. Forming a quotient group is the group-theoretic analogue of passing to residue classes in arithmetic: just as $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ identifies integers that differ by a multiple of $n$, the quotient $G/N$ identifies elements of $G$ that differ by a factor in $N$. The identity of $G/N$ is the coset $N$ itself (the coset of the identity), the inverse of $aN$ is $a^{-1}N$, and the order of $G/N$ is the index $[G:N] = |G|/|N|$ for finite groups. All group axioms are inherited from $G$ precisely because $N$ is normal.

The canonical projection $\pi: G \to G/N$ defined by $\pi(g) = gN$ is a surjective group homomorphism with kernel $N$. This map packages the quotient construction into the language of homomorphisms, which is the right framework for the isomorphism theorems. The canonical projection is universal in the following sense: any homomorphism $\phi: G \to H$ with $N \subseteq \ker(\phi)$ factors uniquely through $\pi$, meaning there exists a unique homomorphism $\bar{\phi}: G/N \to H$ such that $\phi = \bar{\phi} \circ \pi$. This universal property characterizes the quotient group up to isomorphism, independent of the coset construction.

Quotient groups should be understood as "controlled collapsing": passing to $G/N$ loses information (the structure of $N$ and how it sits in $G$), but retains information about the "large-scale" structure of $G$ modulo $N$. The examples make this vivid. The quotient $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ collapses $\mathbb{Z}$ to its residues, retaining only remainders on division by $n$. The quotient $S_n/A_n \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ collapses the symmetric group to parity, retaining only whether a permutation is even or odd. The quotient $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$ collapses the real line to the circle, retaining only fractional parts. In each case, the structure of $G/N$ reflects a particular coarse-grained feature of $G$, and the isomorphism theorems of Chapter 15 will make this intuition precise.
