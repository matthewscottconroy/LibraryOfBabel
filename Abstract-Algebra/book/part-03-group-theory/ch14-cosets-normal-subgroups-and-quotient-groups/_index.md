# Chapter 14 — Cosets, Normal Subgroups, and Quotient Groups

One of the first questions one asks about a group is: how does a subgroup sit inside the ambient group? The answer begins with cosets. If $H$ is a subgroup of $G$, then the left cosets of $H$ — the sets $gH = \{gh : h \in H\}$ as $g$ ranges over $G$ — partition $G$ into disjoint, equal-sized pieces. This partition is not merely a curiosity; it encodes deep information about the relationship between $H$ and $G$, and from it flows the most fundamental numerical theorem in all of group theory.

Lagrange's theorem states that if $G$ is a finite group and $H$ is a subgroup, then $|H|$ divides $|G|$. The proof is immediate once the coset partition is understood: the cosets all have the same size $|H|$ and cover $G$ without overlap, so $|G| = |H| \cdot [G:H]$, where $[G:H]$ is the number of distinct cosets, called the index of $H$ in $G$. The consequences are immediate and striking. Every element of a finite group has order dividing the group's order. Groups of prime order are automatically cyclic. Fermat's little theorem and Euler's theorem are corollaries — the order of any element in $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^*$ divides $p-1$. Lagrange's theorem also sets hard constraints on which subgroups can exist: a group of order 15, for instance, can have subgroups only of orders 1, 3, 5, and 15. While the converse is not true in general (there is no guarantee that a divisor of $|G|$ is the order of some subgroup), Lagrange's theorem remains the indispensable first tool in any structural analysis.

The coset partition of $G$ by $H$ raises a natural follow-up question: can the set of cosets be made into a group? The naive attempt defines multiplication by $(aH)(bH) = (ab)H$, but this is well-defined only if the choice of representatives $a$ and $b$ does not affect the result. Working through the details reveals that well-definedness is equivalent to a single condition: $H$ must be invariant under conjugation, meaning $gHg^{-1} = H$ for all $g \in G$. Subgroups satisfying this condition are called normal, written $H \trianglelefteq G$, and they are the subgroups that truly "blend into" the group's structure rather than merely sitting inside it. Galois understood this condition implicitly when he identified which subgroups of the Galois group correspond to field extensions that can be built by radicals; normal subgroups correspond to Galois extensions.

When $H$ is normal, the set $G/H$ of cosets becomes a group called the quotient group or factor group, with multiplication $(aH)(bH) = (ab)H$. The quotient group collapses all distinctions between elements of $G$ that differ by a factor in $H$, producing a simpler group that retains certain structural features of $G$. The canonical example is $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, which is the quotient of $(\mathbb{Z},+)$ by the subgroup $n\mathbb{Z}$: the coset $a + n\mathbb{Z}$ is the residue class of $a$ modulo $n$, and coset addition is exactly modular arithmetic. More dramatically, the quotient $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$ is the circle group, in which two real numbers are identified if they have the same fractional part. Quotient groups are the group-theoretic analogue of modular arithmetic, and they are the machinery that makes the isomorphism theorems of Chapter 15 possible.
