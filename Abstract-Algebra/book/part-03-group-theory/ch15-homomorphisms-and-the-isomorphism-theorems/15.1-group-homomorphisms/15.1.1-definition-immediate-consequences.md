# 15.1.1 Definition; Immediate Consequences

One of the central aims of algebra is to compare different algebraic structures and determine when two groups, despite looking different, are "really the same." The tool for making such comparisons precise is the notion of a structure-preserving map.

\begin{definition}
A \textbf{group homomorphism} $\varphi: G \to H$ is a function satisfying
$$\varphi(ab) = \varphi(a)\varphi(b) \quad \text{for all } a, b \in G.$$
\end{definition}

The single condition says that $\varphi$ respects multiplication: performing the group operation in$G$ and then mapping to$H$ gives the same result as mapping first and then multiplying in$H$. From this one condition, a cluster of structural properties follow automatically.

\begin{proposition}
Let $\varphi: G \to H$ be a group homomorphism. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\varphi(e_G) = e_H$.
\item $\varphi(g^{-1}) = \varphi(g)^{-1}$ for all$g \in G$.
\item $\varphi(g^n) = \varphi(g)^n$ for all$g \in G$ and$n \in \mathbb{Z}$.
\item The image $\varphi(K)$ of any subgroup$K \leq G$ is a subgroup of$H$.
\item The preimage $\varphi^{-1}(L)$ of any subgroup$L \leq H$ is a subgroup of$G$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For (1): $\varphi(e_G) = \varphi(e_G e_G) = \varphi(e_G)\varphi(e_G)$; canceling$\varphi(e_G)$ on the left gives$e_H = \varphi(e_G)$. For (2):$\varphi(g)\varphi(g^{-1}) = \varphi(gg^{-1}) = \varphi(e_G) = e_H$, so$\varphi(g^{-1}) = \varphi(g)^{-1}$. Properties (3)--(5) follow similarly.
\end{proof}

**Example 1.** The sign homomorphism $\mathrm{sgn}: S_n \to \{1,-1\}$ defined by$\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) = (-1)^{\mathrm{inv}(\sigma)}$ (where$\mathrm{inv}(\sigma)$ is the number of inversions) satisfies$\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma\tau) = \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)\mathrm{sgn}(\tau)$. It sends the identity to 1, inverses to inverses (since$\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma^{-1}) = \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)^{-1} = \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)$, as sign is$\pm 1$).

**Example 2.** The determinant $\det: GL_n(\mathbb{F}) \to \mathbb{F}^*$ is a homomorphism since$\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$. It sends$I$ to$1 = e_{\mathbb{F}^*}$, and$\det(A^{-1}) = \det(A)^{-1}$, as predicted.

**Example 3.** The exponential map $\exp: (\mathbb{R},+) \to (\mathbb{R}_{>0}, \cdot)$ defined by$\exp(x) = e^x$ is a homomorphism:$e^{x+y} = e^x \cdot e^y$. It maps$0 \mapsto 1$ (identity to identity), and$-x \mapsto e^{-x} = (e^x)^{-1}$.

**Example 4.** For a fixed $n \in \mathbb{Z}$, the map$\varphi: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ defined by$\varphi(k) = nk$ is a homomorphism (of abelian groups). It sends$0 \mapsto 0$,$(-k) \mapsto -nk = -(nk)$, and$\varphi(j+k) = n(j+k) = nj+nk = \varphi(j)+\varphi(k)$.

**Example 5.** The map $\varphi: \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ defined by$\varphi(\bar{k}) = \bar{mk}$ for a fixed$m$ is a homomorphism if and only if it is well-defined, which requires$n \mid m \cdot (nk)$ for all$k$ — which always holds. So multiplication by$\bar{m}$ is always an endomorphism of$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$.

The key point is that all structural consequences flow from the single equation $\varphi(ab) = \varphi(a)\varphi(b)$. A homomorphism need not be injective or surjective, and the extent to which it fails each of these will be measured precisely by the kernel and image introduced in Section 15.1.3.

\begin{remark}
The subgroup structure of $G$ and$H$ is constrained by any homomorphism between them. The preimage of a normal subgroup$L \trianglelefteq H$ is normal in$G$: for$g \in G$ and$k \in \varphi^{-1}(L)$,$\varphi(gkg^{-1}) = \varphi(g)\varphi(k)\varphi(g)^{-1} \in L$ (since$L$ is normal), so$gkg^{-1} \in \varphi^{-1}(L)$. This will be used crucially in the proof of the correspondence theorem.
\end{remark}
