# 15.1.3 Kernel and Image as Subgroups

Every homomorphism $\varphi: G \to H$ comes equipped with two canonically associated subgroups, one in each group. These subgroups capture, in a precise way, how far$\varphi$ is from being an isomorphism.

\begin{definition}
Let $\varphi: G \to H$ be a group homomorphism.
\begin{itemize}
\item The \textbf{kernel} of $\varphi$ is$\ker \varphi = \{g \in G : \varphi(g) = e_H\}$.
\item The \textbf{image} of $\varphi$ is$\mathrm{im}\,\varphi = \{\varphi(g) : g \in G\} \subseteq H$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
$\ker \varphi$ is a normal subgroup of$G$, and$\mathrm{im}\,\varphi$ is a subgroup of$H$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\textbf{Kernel.} $\ker\varphi$ contains$e_G$ (since$\varphi(e_G) = e_H$). If$\varphi(a) = \varphi(b) = e_H$ then$\varphi(ab^{-1}) = \varphi(a)\varphi(b)^{-1} = e_H$, so$\ker\varphi$ is a subgroup. Normality: for any$g \in G$ and$k \in \ker\varphi$,$\varphi(gkg^{-1}) = \varphi(g)\varphi(k)\varphi(g)^{-1} = \varphi(g)e_H\varphi(g)^{-1} = e_H$, so$gkg^{-1} \in \ker\varphi$.

\textbf{Image.} $e_H = \varphi(e_G) \in \mathrm{im}\,\varphi$. If$\varphi(a), \varphi(b) \in \mathrm{im}\,\varphi$ then$\varphi(a)\varphi(b) = \varphi(ab) \in \mathrm{im}\,\varphi$. And$\varphi(a)^{-1} = \varphi(a^{-1}) \in \mathrm{im}\,\varphi$.
\end{proof}

The key point is that these two subgroups measure exactly the two possible failures of an isomorphism. The homomorphism $\varphi$ is injective if and only if$\ker \varphi = \{e_G\}$: if$\varphi$ is injective, only$e_G$ can map to$e_H$; conversely, if$\varphi(a) = \varphi(b)$ then$\varphi(ab^{-1}) = e_H$, so$ab^{-1} \in \ker \varphi = \{e\}$ and$a = b$. Similarly,$\varphi$ is surjective if and only if$\mathrm{im}\,\varphi = H$.

**Example 1.** For $\det: GL_n(\mathbb{F}) \to \mathbb{F}^*$:$\ker(\det) = SL_n(\mathbb{F})$ (normal, as verified before), and$\mathrm{im}(\det) = \mathbb{F}^*$ (surjective, since for any$\lambda \in \mathbb{F}^*$ the diagonal matrix$\mathrm{diag}(\lambda, 1, \ldots, 1)$ has determinant$\lambda$).

**Example 2.** For $\mathrm{sgn}: S_n \to \{1,-1\}$ (for$n \geq 2$):$\ker(\mathrm{sgn}) = A_n$ (normal of index 2), and$\mathrm{im}(\mathrm{sgn}) = \{1,-1\}$ (surjective since$(12) \mapsto -1$).

**Example 3.** For $\varphi: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$,$k \mapsto \bar{k}$:$\ker\varphi = n\mathbb{Z}$ (normal in$\mathbb{Z}$, since$\mathbb{Z}$ is abelian), and$\mathrm{im}\,\varphi = \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ (surjective).

**Example 4.** For the conjugation map $\phi: G \to \mathrm{Aut}(G)$,$g \mapsto \phi_g$ where$\phi_g(x) = gxg^{-1}$:$\ker\phi = Z(G)$ (elements fixing every$x$ by conjugation are exactly central elements), and$\mathrm{im}\,\phi = \mathrm{Inn}(G)$ (the group of inner automorphisms). The First Isomorphism Theorem gives$G/Z(G) \cong \mathrm{Inn}(G)$.

**Example 5.** For $\varphi: D_4 \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ sending all rotations to$\bar{0}$ and all reflections to$\bar{1}$:$\ker\varphi = \langle r \rangle = \{e, r, r^2, r^3\}$ (the rotation subgroup, normal of index 2), and$\mathrm{im}\,\varphi = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ (surjective). The kernel is confirmed normal since$[D_4:\langle r \rangle] = 2$.

**Example 6.** For the abelianization map $\varphi: G \to G/[G,G]$ (where$[G,G]$ is the commutator subgroup), the kernel is$[G,G]$ (normal in$G$) and the image is$G/[G,G]$, the largest abelian quotient of$G$. For$G = S_3$:$[S_3, S_3] = A_3$ (the normal subgroup generated by all commutators), so the image is$S_3/A_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

\begin{remark}
The normality of $\ker\varphi$ is the conceptual basis for the entire quotient group and isomorphism theorem machinery. Once we know every kernel is normal, we can form the quotient$G/\ker\varphi$, and the First Isomorphism Theorem identifies this quotient with$\mathrm{im}\,\varphi$. The kernel measures exactly the "information lost" by$\varphi$, and the quotient$G/\ker\varphi$ is the "information retained." There is a useful exactness perspective:$1 \to \ker\varphi \to G \to \mathrm{im}\,\varphi \to 1$ is a short exact sequence, and the isomorphism theorems describe exactly how to split, reconstruct, or compare groups appearing in such sequences.
\end{remark}
