# 15.1.4 Inner Automorphisms; the Automorphism Group $\mathrm{Aut}(G)$

Among all automorphisms of a group $G$, the most natural ones arise from the group's own multiplication. For each$g \in G$, the map$\phi_g: G \to G$ defined by$\phi_g(x) = gxg^{-1}$ is called **conjugation by$g$**, or an **inner automorphism**.

\begin{proposition}
For each $g \in G$, the map$\phi_g: G \to G$,$x \mapsto gxg^{-1}$ is an automorphism of$G$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Homomorphism: $\phi_g(xy) = g(xy)g^{-1} = (gxg^{-1})(gyg^{-1}) = \phi_g(x)\phi_g(y)$. Bijective:$\phi_g \circ \phi_{g^{-1}} = \phi_{g^{-1}} \circ \phi_g = \mathrm{id}_G$.
\end{proof}

The full collection of automorphisms of $G$ forms a group$\mathrm{Aut}(G)$ under composition. The inner automorphisms form a subgroup$\mathrm{Inn}(G) \leq \mathrm{Aut}(G)$:$\phi_g \circ \phi_h = \phi_{gh}$ (verify:$(\phi_g \circ \phi_h)(x) = g(hxh^{-1})g^{-1} = (gh)x(gh)^{-1} = \phi_{gh}(x)$), and$\phi_g^{-1} = \phi_{g^{-1}}$. In fact,$\mathrm{Inn}(G)$ is **normal** in$\mathrm{Aut}(G)$: for any$\alpha \in \mathrm{Aut}(G)$,
$$(\alpha \circ \phi_g \circ \alpha^{-1})(x) = \alpha(g \cdot \alpha^{-1}(x) \cdot g^{-1}) = \alpha(g) \cdot x \cdot \alpha(g)^{-1} = \phi_{\alpha(g)}(x).$$
So $\alpha \circ \phi_g \circ \alpha^{-1} = \phi_{\alpha(g)} \in \mathrm{Inn}(G)$.

\begin{proposition}
The map $\Phi: G \to \mathrm{Inn}(G)$,$g \mapsto \phi_g$, is a surjective group homomorphism with$\ker\Phi = Z(G)$. Therefore$\mathrm{Inn}(G) \cong G/Z(G)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\Phi$ is a homomorphism since$\Phi(gh) = \phi_{gh} = \phi_g \circ \phi_h = \Phi(g) \circ \Phi(h)$. An element$g$ lies in$\ker\Phi$ iff$\phi_g = \mathrm{id}_G$, i.e.,$gxg^{-1} = x$ for all$x$, i.e.,$g \in Z(G)$. The First Isomorphism Theorem gives$G/Z(G) \cong \mathrm{Inn}(G)$.
\end{proof}

The quotient $\mathrm{Out}(G) = \mathrm{Aut}(G)/\mathrm{Inn}(G)$ is called the **outer automorphism group** of$G$. Its elements are cosets of inner automorphisms and measure the symmetries of$G$ that cannot be realized by conjugation.

**Example 1.** For $G = \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$: every automorphism is multiplication by some unit$\bar{m}$ with$\gcd(m,n) = 1$. Since$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is abelian, all inner automorphisms are trivial ($Z(G) = G$, so$\mathrm{Inn}(G) = \{1\}$). Therefore$\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}) \cong (\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^*$ and$\mathrm{Out}(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})$.

**Example 2.** For $G = S_3$:$Z(S_3) = \{e\}$, so$\mathrm{Inn}(S_3) \cong S_3/\{e\} \cong S_3$. One can show$\mathrm{Aut}(S_3) \cong S_3$, so$\mathrm{Inn}(S_3) = \mathrm{Aut}(S_3)$ and$\mathrm{Out}(S_3) = \{1\}$ — all automorphisms of$S_3$ are inner.

**Example 3.** For $G = Q_8$:$Z(Q_8) = \{1,-1\}$, so$\mathrm{Inn}(Q_8) \cong Q_8/\{1,-1\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ (order 4). One computes$|\mathrm{Aut}(Q_8)| = 24$, so$|\mathrm{Out}(Q_8)| = 24/4 = 6$, and$\mathrm{Out}(Q_8) \cong S_3$.

**Example 4.** For $G = \mathbb{Z}$: the only automorphisms are$n \mapsto n$ and$n \mapsto -n$, giving$\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. Since$\mathbb{Z}$ is abelian,$\mathrm{Inn}(\mathbb{Z}) = \{1\}$ and$\mathrm{Out}(\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 5.** For $G = S_n$ with$n \neq 6$: every automorphism of$S_n$ is an inner automorphism, so$\mathrm{Inn}(S_n) = \mathrm{Aut}(S_n)$ and$\mathrm{Out}(S_n) = \{1\}$. The exceptional case$n = 6$ is one of the most surprising facts in finite group theory:$S_6$ has an outer automorphism of order 2, coming from the exceptional isomorphism between two conjugacy classes of subgroups isomorphic to$S_5$. For$n \neq 6$, there is essentially one conjugacy class of "standard" transpositions, but for$n = 6$ there is a second class of elements with the same cycle structure that cannot be mapped to the first by any inner automorphism.

\begin{remark}
The automorphism group $\mathrm{Aut}(G)$ is itself a group whose structure can be much more complex than$G$. For finite abelian groups,$\mathrm{Aut}(G)$ is computed by the theory of the Smith normal form and involves a product of general linear groups over residue fields. For nonabelian groups, computing$\mathrm{Aut}(G)$ is in general a hard problem.
\end{remark}
