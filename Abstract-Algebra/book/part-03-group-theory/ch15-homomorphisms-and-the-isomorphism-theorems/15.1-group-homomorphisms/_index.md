# 15.1 Group Homomorphisms

A group homomorphism is a function $\phi: G \to H$ between groups that respects the group operation: $\phi(ab) = \phi(a)\phi(b)$ for all $a, b \in G$. This single condition is deceptively powerful. It immediately implies $\phi(e_G) = e_H$ (apply the condition to $a = b = e_G$), $\phi(a^{-1}) = \phi(a)^{-1}$ (apply to $a$ and $a^{-1}$), and $\phi(a^n) = \phi(a)^n$ for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}$. Homomorphisms preserve all group-theoretic structure: they send subgroups to subgroups, and their preimages of subgroups are subgroups. The category of groups, with groups as objects and homomorphisms as morphisms, is the natural setting for all of group theory.

A homomorphism $\phi: G \to H$ is an isomorphism if it is bijective. When an isomorphism exists, $G$ and $H$ are isomorphic, written $G \cong H$; they are the same group up to relabeling of elements. An endomorphism is a homomorphism from $G$ to itself, and an automorphism is a bijective endomorphism. The set $\text{Aut}(G)$ of all automorphisms of $G$ is itself a group under composition, the automorphism group of $G$, which measures the symmetries of the group structure. For $G = \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, every automorphism is multiplication by a unit modulo $n$, giving $\text{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}) \cong (\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^*$, a group whose structure connects back to number theory via Euler's totient function.

Particularly important are the inner automorphisms: for each $g \in G$, conjugation by $g$ defines an automorphism $\phi_g: G \to G$, $\phi_g(x) = gxg^{-1}$. The map $g \mapsto \phi_g$ is itself a homomorphism from $G$ to $\text{Aut}(G)$, with kernel $Z(G)$; its image is the subgroup $\text{Inn}(G)$ of inner automorphisms, a normal subgroup of $\text{Aut}(G)$. The quotient $\text{Out}(G) = \text{Aut}(G)/\text{Inn}(G)$ is the outer automorphism group. Two foundational structural invariants of any homomorphism $\phi: G \to H$ are its kernel $\ker(\phi) = \phi^{-1}(e_H)$, a normal subgroup of $G$, and its image $\text{im}(\phi) = \phi(G)$, a subgroup of $H$. The First Isomorphism Theorem, which follows in Section 15.2, shows these two invariants determine the homomorphism up to isomorphism.
