# 15.2.1 First Isomorphism Theorem: $G/\ker\varphi \cong \mathrm{im}\,\varphi$

Every homomorphism $\varphi: G \to H$ collapses a normal subgroup — namely$\ker\varphi$ — to the identity, and the question is whether the rest of$G$ faithfully mirrors$\mathrm{im}\,\varphi$. The First Isomorphism Theorem gives a definitive answer.

\begin{theorem}[First Isomorphism Theorem]
Let $\varphi: G \to H$ be a group homomorphism. Then$\ker\varphi \trianglelefteq G$ and there is a well-defined isomorphism
$$\bar{\varphi}: G/\ker\varphi \xrightarrow{\;\sim\;} \mathrm{im}\,\varphi, \quad g\ker\varphi \mapsto \varphi(g).$$
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We have already shown $\ker\varphi \trianglelefteq G$. Define$\bar{\varphi}(g\ker\varphi) = \varphi(g)$. \textbf{Well-defined}: if$g\ker\varphi = g'\ker\varphi$ then$g'^{-1}g \in \ker\varphi$, so$\varphi(g'^{-1}g) = e_H$, hence$\varphi(g) = \varphi(g')$. \textbf{Homomorphism}:$\bar{\varphi}((gK)(g'K)) = \bar{\varphi}((gg')K) = \varphi(gg') = \varphi(g)\varphi(g') = \bar{\varphi}(gK)\bar{\varphi}(g'K)$ (where$K = \ker\varphi$). \textbf{Surjective}: every element of$\mathrm{im}\,\varphi$ is$\varphi(g)$ for some$g$. \textbf{Injective}:$\bar{\varphi}(gK) = e_H$ implies$\varphi(g) = e_H$, so$g \in K$, meaning$gK = K = eK$ is the identity coset.
\end{proof}

The theorem is the first and most fundamental of the isomorphism theorems. It says every homomorphic image of $G$ — every group that$G$ maps onto — is realized up to isomorphism as a quotient of$G$. To understand all homomorphic images of$G$, it suffices to understand all normal subgroups of$G$ and the resulting quotients.

**Example 1.** The determinant $\det: GL_n(\mathbb{F}) \to \mathbb{F}^*$ is surjective with$\ker(\det) = SL_n(\mathbb{F})$. By the First Isomorphism Theorem,$GL_n(\mathbb{F})/SL_n(\mathbb{F}) \cong \mathbb{F}^*$. For$\mathbb{F} = \mathbb{F}_q$, this gives$GL_n(\mathbb{F}_q)/SL_n(\mathbb{F}_q) \cong \mathbb{F}_q^* \cong \mathbb{Z}/(q-1)\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 2.** The map $\varphi: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$,$k \mapsto \bar{k}$, is surjective with$\ker\varphi = n\mathbb{Z}$. The First Isomorphism Theorem gives$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ — trivially, but this shows that$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is indeed a quotient of$\mathbb{Z}$ in the precise sense.

**Example 3.** The conjugation map $\Phi: G \to \mathrm{Aut}(G)$,$g \mapsto \phi_g$, has kernel$Z(G)$ and image$\mathrm{Inn}(G)$. The First Isomorphism Theorem gives$G/Z(G) \cong \mathrm{Inn}(G)$. For$G = D_4$:$Z(D_4) = \{e, r^2\}$, so$D_4/Z(D_4)$ has order 4 and$\mathrm{Inn}(D_4)$ is a group of order 4 — isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 4.** Define $\varphi: S_4 \to S_3$ by restricting permutations to the action on the three "pairings"$\{(12)(34), (13)(24), (14)(23)\}$. This is a surjective homomorphism with$\ker\varphi = V = \{e,(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)\}$ (the Klein four-group). The First Isomorphism Theorem gives$S_4/V \cong S_3$.

**Example 5.** For $G = \mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ and the map$\varphi: \mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$,$\bar{k} \mapsto \bar{k} \bmod 4$ (i.e.,$\varphi(\bar{k}) = \overline{k \bmod 4}$): this is surjective with$\ker\varphi = \{\bar{k} : 4 \mid k\} = \{\bar{0}, \bar{4}, \bar{8}\} = \langle \bar{4} \rangle$ (order 3). The First Isomorphism Theorem gives$(\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z})/\langle\bar{4}\rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$.

\begin{remark}
The pattern generalizes far beyond groups. Every algebraic structure satisfying the appropriate axioms — rings, modules, Lie algebras, vector spaces — has an analogue of this theorem, and the proof is always the same: define the map on cosets/classes, check it is well-defined and bijective. Recognizing this pattern is a step toward the more unified language of universal algebra and category theory, where the First Isomorphism Theorem is an immediate consequence of the universal property of quotient objects.
\end{remark}
