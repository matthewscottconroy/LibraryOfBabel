# 15.3.2 Recognizing Internal Direct Products: Three Conditions

In practice, we often encounter a group $G$ together with two subgroups$H$ and$K$ and need to determine whether$G$ truly decomposes as$H \times K$. The three-condition recognition theorem gives a clean and checkable criterion.

\begin{theorem}[Internal Direct Product Recognition]
A group $G$ is an internal direct product of subgroups$H$ and$K$ if and only if all three of the following hold:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $H \trianglelefteq G$ and$K \trianglelefteq G$;
\item $H \cap K = \{e\}$;
\item $HK = G$.
\end{enumerate}
When these hold, $G \cong H \times K$ via$(h,k) \mapsto hk$.
\end{theorem}

All three conditions are necessary. Condition (1) alone does not force commutativity between $H$ and$K$ — for that we need (2). And conditions (1) and (2) together do not guarantee that every element of$G$ has a decomposition — for that we need (3). To see why (1) and (2) together imply commutativity: the commutator$[h,k] = hkh^{-1}k^{-1}$ lies in$K$ (since$K$ is normal:$hkh^{-1} \in K$, so$hkh^{-1}k^{-1} \in K$) and in$H$ (since$H$ is normal:$k^{-1}hk \in H$, so$hkh^{-1}k^{-1} = h(kh^{-1}k^{-1}) \in H$). Therefore$[h,k] \in H \cap K = \{e\}$, giving$hk = kh$.

**Example 1.** $G = \mathbb{Z}/15\mathbb{Z}$,$H = \langle \bar{5} \rangle = \{0,5,10\}$ (order 3),$K = \langle \bar{3} \rangle = \{0,3,6,9,12\}$ (order 5). Check: (1) Both normal (abelian group). (2)$H \cap K$: only$\bar{0}$, since$|H|=3$ and$|K|=5$ are coprime and$H \cap K \leq H$ has order dividing 3, while$H \cap K \leq K$ has order dividing 5, so$|H \cap K|$ divides$\gcd(3,5) = 1$. (3)$|HK| = |H||K|/|H \cap K| = 15/1 = 15 = |G|$. Conclusion:$\mathbb{Z}/15\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 2.** $G = D_6$ (order 12),$H = \langle r^3 \rangle = \{e, r^3\}$ (order 2, center of$D_6$),$K = \langle r^2, s \rangle$ (the dihedral group$D_3$, order 6). Check: (1)$H \subseteq Z(D_6)$ so$H \trianglelefteq D_6$;$K \trianglelefteq D_6$ (index 2). (2)$H \cap K$:$r^3 \in K$?$K = \langle r^2, s\rangle$ contains$r^2, r^4, e, s, sr^2, sr^4$ — does it contain$r^3$? Not listed, so$H \cap K = \{e\}$. (3)$|HK| = 2 \cdot 6 / 1 = 12 = |G|$. Conclusion:$D_6 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times D_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times S_3$.

**Example 3.** Can we write $D_4$ as an internal direct product? Suppose$D_4 = H \times K$ nontrivially. Then$|H||K| = 8$ with$H,K \trianglelefteq D_4$,$H \cap K = \{e\}$, so the only possibility is$|H|=2$,$|K|=4$ or vice versa. The only normal subgroup of order 2 is$Z(D_4) = \{e, r^2\}$. The subgroups of order 4 containing$\{e,r^2\}$ are$\langle r \rangle$,$\langle r^2, s\rangle$,$\langle r^2, rs\rangle$. Check$Z(D_4) \cap \langle r \rangle = \{e, r^2\} \neq \{e\}$. So condition (2) fails for all such pairs. Therefore$D_4$ is not an internal direct product.

**Example 4.** $G = A_4$ (order 12). Let$H = V = \{e,(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)\}$ (order 4, normal) and a Sylow 3-subgroup$K = \langle (123) \rangle$ (order 3). Check: (1)$V \trianglelefteq A_4$ (it is the unique Sylow 2-subgroup).$K$ is not normal in$A_4$ (Sylow 3-subgroups are not unique). So condition (1) fails for$K$.

**Example 5.** Consider groups of order $p^2$ for prime$p$. By the classification, they are either cyclic ($\cong \mathbb{Z}/p^2\mathbb{Z}$) or$\cong \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$. For the second type, take two distinct subgroups$H = \langle (\bar{1},\bar{0}) \rangle$ and$K = \langle (\bar{0},\bar{1}) \rangle$, both of order$p$. All three conditions are satisfied: both normal (abelian),$H \cap K = \{0\}$ (coprime orders),$H + K = G$.

\begin{remark}
This recognition theorem is the workhorse for classifying groups of small order. For groups of order $pq$ (with$p < q$ primes), the Sylow theorems produce a normal Sylow$q$-subgroup$N$ and a Sylow$p$-subgroup$H$. If$p \nmid (q-1)$, then$H$ is also normal and$N \cap H = \{e\}$,$NH = G$, so$G \cong \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/pq\mathbb{Z}$. If$p \mid (q-1)$, then$H$ may not be normal, and we need the semidirect product instead.
\end{remark}
