# Chapter 15 — Homomorphisms and the Isomorphism Theorems

To understand a mathematical structure, one must understand the maps that respect it. For groups, these are the homomorphisms: functions $\phi: G \to H$ satisfying $\phi(ab) = \phi(a)\phi(b)$ for all $a, b \in G$. This single condition forces a cascade of consequences — the identity maps to the identity, inverses map to inverses, subgroups map to subgroups — and the collection of all group homomorphisms organizes the category of groups. When a homomorphism is also a bijection, it is an isomorphism, and two groups linked by an isomorphism are, from the abstract point of view, identical: they have the same structure, the same multiplication table, and satisfy the same theorems. The central project of group theory is the classification of groups up to isomorphism.

Every homomorphism $\phi: G \to H$ carries two intrinsic subgroups: the kernel $\ker(\phi) = \{g \in G : \phi(g) = e_H\}$ and the image $\text{im}(\phi) = \phi(G) \leq H$. The kernel measures how far $\phi$ is from being injective — it is trivial if and only if $\phi$ is injective — and it is always a normal subgroup of $G$. The image measures how far $\phi$ is from being surjective. Emmy Noether recognized in the 1920s that these two invariants are linked by a theorem of elegant precision: the First Isomorphism Theorem states that $G/\ker(\phi) \cong \text{im}(\phi)$. Every homomorphic image of $G$ is, up to isomorphism, a quotient of $G$, and every quotient of $G$ is a homomorphic image. This theorem is the Rosetta Stone of group theory, translating between quotient groups and subgroups.

The Second and Third Isomorphism Theorems extend this correspondence. The Second Isomorphism Theorem — sometimes called the diamond theorem for the shape of the subgroup lattice it describes — states that if $H \leq G$ and $N \trianglelefteq G$, then $H \cap N \trianglelefteq H$ and $H/(H \cap N) \cong HN/N$. The two "paths" through the diamond of subgroups $H$, $N$, $H \cap N$, $HN$ yield isomorphic quotients, a fact about the geometry of subgroup lattices that becomes essential when studying composition series. The Third Isomorphism Theorem handles nested normal subgroups: if $N \trianglelefteq K \trianglelefteq G$, then $(G/N)/(K/N) \cong G/K$, formalizing the intuition that "canceling in stages" gives the same result as canceling all at once.

The chapter also develops the tools for building new groups from old ones. Direct products $G \times H$ assemble groups side by side, and the recognition theorem characterizes when an abstract group is an internal direct product of its subgroups — conditions involving trivial intersection and commutativity between the factors. More flexible is the semidirect product $G \rtimes_\phi H$, which arises when $H$ acts on $G$ by automorphisms, twisting the multiplication law. The dihedral group $D_n \cong \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \rtimes \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is the prototypical example, with $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ acting on $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ by inversion. Semidirect products will be indispensable in Chapter 17, where the Sylow theorems force group structures that are often semidirect products, and they provide the language for the group extensions that appear throughout representation theory.
