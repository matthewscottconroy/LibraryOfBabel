# 16.1.1 Definition: A Homomorphism $G \to \mathrm{Sym}(X)$

So far, groups have been studied as abstract structures. One of the deepest insights in group theory is that groups are most naturally understood by how they *act* — that is, by the ways in which group elements permute the elements of some set. This perspective turns abstract group theory into a concrete study of symmetry.

\begin{definition}
A \textbf{(left) group action} of $G$ on a set$X$ is a group homomorphism$\rho: G \to \mathrm{Sym}(X)$, where$\mathrm{Sym}(X)$ denotes the group of all bijections$X \to X$. Equivalently, it is a function$G \times X \to X$, written$(g, x) \mapsto g \cdot x$, satisfying:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $e \cdot x = x$ for all$x \in X$ (identity acts trivially);
\item $(gh) \cdot x = g \cdot (h \cdot x)$ for all$g, h \in G$ and$x \in X$ (compatibility with group multiplication).
\end{enumerate}
When $G$ acts on$X$, we say$X$ is a \textbf{$G$-set}.
\end{definition}

The equivalence between these two formulations is straightforward: given $\rho: G \to \mathrm{Sym}(X)$, define$g \cdot x = \rho(g)(x)$; the two axioms follow from$\rho$ being a homomorphism. Conversely, given a function satisfying the two axioms, set$\rho(g)(x) = g \cdot x$; the homomorphism condition$\rho(gh) = \rho(g) \circ \rho(h)$ follows from axiom (2).

The second axiom deserves attention. It says that applying $h$ first and then$g$ gives the same result as applying$gh$ directly. Without this compatibility, we would merely have a function$G \times X \to X$ with no algebraic content. The associativity of actions is what makes them structurally meaningful: it means that a sequence of group elements acts on$X$ the same way regardless of how we parenthesize the product.

The action axioms encode the idea that each $g \in G$ acts on$X$ as a genuine symmetry — an invertible transformation — and that the group structure governs how these symmetries compose. A single group can act on many different sets, and the choice of set often illuminates different features of the group.

**Example 1.** $S_n$ acts on$\{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$ by$\sigma \cdot i = \sigma(i)$. This is the canonical action, and it is the definition of$S_n$: a permutation is precisely a bijection of the$n$-element set.

**Example 2.** $GL_n(\mathbb{F})$ acts on$\mathbb{F}^n$ by$A \cdot \mathbf{v} = A\mathbf{v}$ (matrix-vector multiplication). Identity:$I \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}$. Compatibility:$(AB)\mathbf{v} = A(B\mathbf{v})$ (by associativity of matrix multiplication). This is the standard linear representation.

**Example 3.** Any group $G$ acts on itself by left multiplication:$g \cdot x = gx$. This is Cayley's action, and it is the basis of Cayley's theorem (Section 16.3.2).

**Example 4.** $G$ acts on itself by conjugation:$g \cdot x = gxg^{-1}$. Identity:$e \cdot x = x$. Compatibility:$(gh) \cdot x = (gh)x(gh)^{-1} = g(hxh^{-1})g^{-1} = g \cdot (h \cdot x)$. The orbits of this action are conjugacy classes.

**Example 5.** $D_n$ acts on the vertices$\{v_1, \ldots, v_n\}$ of a regular$n$-gon. A rotation by$2\pi k/n$ sends$v_j \mapsto v_{j+k \pmod n}$, and a reflection sends each vertex to its mirror image. This action captures the geometric symmetry of the$n$-gon and is the original motivation for the definition of$D_n$.

The rest of this chapter is devoted to exploiting the action perspective systematically. The key tools are orbits (what a point can be moved to), stabilizers (what fixes a point), and the orbit-stabilizer theorem (connecting these two invariants). Together they yield some of the most striking results in finite group theory.

\begin{remark}
The homomorphism $\rho: G \to \mathrm{Sym}(X)$ need not be injective. The kernel of$\rho$ is$\{g \in G : g \cdot x = x \; \forall x \in X\}$, the set of elements that act trivially on every point. When$\ker\rho = \{e\}$, the action is called \textbf{faithful} and$G$ embeds into$\mathrm{Sym}(X)$. The action is therefore, up to this distinction, a study of subgroups of$\mathrm{Sym}(X)$.
\end{remark}
