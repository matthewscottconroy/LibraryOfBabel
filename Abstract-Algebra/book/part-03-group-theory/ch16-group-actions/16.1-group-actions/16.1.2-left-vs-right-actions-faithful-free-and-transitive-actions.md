# 16.1.2 Left vs. Right Actions; Faithful, Free, and Transitive Actions

Actions come in two flavors depending on which side the group elements are applied, and they are further classified by how "effective" the action is. These distinctions are important for matching the right notion of action to the problem at hand.

A **left action** satisfies $(gh) \cdot x = g \cdot (h \cdot x)$: the group acts on the left and elements compose in the natural order. A **right action** satisfies$x \cdot (gh) = (x \cdot g) \cdot h$: the group acts on the right and the order is preserved. The two notions are interchangeable — given a left action of$G$ on$X$, one obtains a right action by setting$x \cdot g = g^{-1} \cdot x$. In most of what follows we work with left actions and write$g \cdot x$ or simply$gx$.

\begin{definition}
Let $G$ act on a set$X$ via$\rho: G \to \mathrm{Sym}(X)$.
\begin{itemize}
\item The action is \textbf{faithful} if $\ker\rho = \{e\}$, i.e.,$g \cdot x = x$ for all$x$ implies$g = e$.
\item The action is \textbf{free} if $g \cdot x = x$ for some$x$ implies$g = e$ (no non-identity element fixes any point).
\item The action is \textbf{transitive} if for every pair of points $x, y \in X$ there exists$g \in G$ with$g \cdot x = y$ (there is only one orbit).
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

Freeness is strictly stronger than faithfulness: if an action is free, it is automatically faithful (taking any fixed point and applying the definition). The converse fails: conjugation is faithful if and only if $Z(G) = \{e\}$, but elements other than the identity can fix individual points (any element fixes its own conjugacy class representatives trivially if they lie in the center).

An action can be transitive without being free (the action of $S_3$ on$\{1,2,3\}$ is transitive, but$(12)$ fixes 3) and free without being transitive (the regular action of$G$ on itself is free, but it is transitive only if$G$ acts on a single-element set or we use a specific quotient action).

**Example 1.** The action of $S_n$ on$\{1,\ldots,n\}$ is faithful (distinct permutations do different things to some element), transitive (for any$i,j$ the transposition or$n$-cycle can send$i$ to$j$), but not free (the transposition$(12)$ fixes 3, 4, ..., n).

**Example 2.** The action of $G$ on itself by left multiplication is faithful, free, and transitive: (1)$gx = x$ for all$x$ gives$g = e$ (take$x = e$); (2)$gx = x$ implies$g = e$ immediately; (3) for any$x, y$, take$g = yx^{-1}$ to get$gx = y$.

**Example 3.** Conjugation of $G$ on itself: the kernel is$Z(G)$ (faithful iff$Z(G)=\{e\}$), not free (the identity fixes everything), and not transitive in general (there are multiple conjugacy classes).

**Example 4.** The action of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ on$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ by addition$\bar{k} \cdot \bar{m} = \bar{k}+\bar{m}$ is the regular action: faithful (if$\bar{k}+\bar{m} = \bar{m}$ for all$\bar{m}$, then$\bar{k} = \bar{0}$), free, and transitive.

**Example 5.** The action of $D_n$ on the$n$ vertices of a regular$n$-gon is faithful (distinct symmetries do different things to vertices), transitive (any vertex can be sent to any other by a rotation), but not free (a reflection fixes the vertices on its axis when$n$ is even).

\begin{proposition}
Suppose $G$ acts transitively on$X$. Then for any two points$x, y \in X$, the stabilizers$G_x$ and$G_y$ are conjugate subgroups of$G$:$G_y = gG_xg^{-1}$ for any$g$ with$g \cdot x = y$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If $h \in G_y$, then$h \cdot y = y$. Since$y = g \cdot x$, this gives$h \cdot (g \cdot x) = g \cdot x$, so$(g^{-1}hg) \cdot x = x$, meaning$g^{-1}hg \in G_x$, i.e.,$h \in gG_xg^{-1}$. The reverse inclusion is symmetric.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
In the theory of linear representations, a representation of $G$ on a vector space$V$ is a group action on$V$ by linear maps, i.e., a homomorphism$\rho: G \to GL(V)$. The distinction between faithful and non-faithful representations is central: a faithful representation provides a concrete realization of$G$ as a matrix group, while a non-faithful one factors through a quotient$G/\ker\rho$.
\end{remark}
