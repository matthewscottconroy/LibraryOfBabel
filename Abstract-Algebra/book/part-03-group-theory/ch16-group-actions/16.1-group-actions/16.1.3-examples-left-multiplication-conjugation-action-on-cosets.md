# 16.1.3 Examples: Left Multiplication, Conjugation, Action on Cosets

Abstract definitions become meaningful through examples. Three actions of a group on itself — or on a set derived from it — are fundamental enough to be considered canonical, and each leads to a major theorem.

**Left multiplication** (Cayley action). The action of $G$ on itself defined by$g \cdot x = gx$ is faithful (if$gx = x$ for all$x$, taking$x=e$ gives$g=e$), transitive (for any$x,y$, take$g = yx^{-1}$), and free ($gx = x \Rightarrow g = e$ immediately). The associated homomorphism$\rho: G \to \mathrm{Sym}(G)$ is injective, and this is the content of Cayley's theorem (Section 16.3.2): every group embeds in a symmetric group.

**Conjugation.** The action of $G$ on itself defined by$g \cdot x = gxg^{-1}$. This action is neither free nor generally transitive. The orbit of$x$ under conjugation is the **conjugacy class** of$x$, written$\mathrm{cl}(x) = \{gxg^{-1} : g \in G\}$. The stabilizer of$x$ is the **centralizer**$C_G(x) = \{g \in G : gx = xg\}$. The conjugation action is faithful if and only if$Z(G) = \{e\}$: the kernel is$Z(G)$.

**Action on cosets.** Given a subgroup $H \leq G$, define the action of$G$ on$G/H = \{aH : a \in G\}$ by$g \cdot (aH) = (ga)H$. This is well-defined (if$aH = a'H$ then$gaH = ga'H$), satisfies the action axioms, and is automatically transitive (any coset$aH$ can be reached from$H$ by$g = a$). The kernel of this action is the core$\mathrm{Core}_G(H) = \bigcap_{g \in G} gHg^{-1}$, the largest normal subgroup of$G$ contained in$H$.

**Example 1.** Cayley action on $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$. Label the elements$0,1,2,3$. Then$\bar{1}$ acts by$0 \mapsto 1 \mapsto 2 \mapsto 3 \mapsto 0$, corresponding to the 4-cycle$(0123) \in S_4$. The embedding$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow S_4$ sends$\bar{k} \mapsto (0,1,2,3)^k$.

**Example 2.** Conjugacy classes in $S_3$. The conjugacy classes are$\{e\}$,$\{(12),(13),(23)\}$ (all transpositions), and$\{(123),(132)\}$ (all 3-cycles). The centralizer$C_{S_3}((12))$ consists of elements commuting with$(12)$:$e$ and$(12)$ itself (since$(13)(12)(13) = (23) \neq (12)$ and$(23)(12)(23) = (13) \neq (12)$). So$|C_{S_3}((12))| = 2$, and by orbit-stabilizer,$|\mathrm{cl}((12))| = |S_3|/|C_{S_3}((12))| = 6/2 = 3$.

**Example 3.** Action on cosets in $S_4$. Let$H = \langle (1234) \rangle$ (cyclic of order 4). The index$[S_4:H] = 6$, so$G$ acts on 6 cosets. This gives a homomorphism$\rho: S_4 \to S_6$. The kernel is$\mathrm{Core}_{S_4}(H) = \bigcap_{g \in S_4} gHg^{-1}$. Since$H$ is not normal in$S_4$ (conjugating$(1234)$ by$(12)$ gives$(2134)$, which is not in$H$), the core is a proper subgroup of$H$.

**Example 4.** Conjugation in $D_4 = \langle r, s \rangle$. The conjugacy classes are:$\{e\}$,$\{r^2\}$,$\{r, r^3\}$,$\{s, sr^2\}$,$\{sr, sr^3\}$. The center$Z(D_4) = \{e, r^2\}$ consists of the two singleton conjugacy classes. The class equation:$8 = 1 + 1 + 2 + 2 + 2$.

**Example 5.** Action of $D_4$ on vertices of a square (labeled$1,2,3,4$ clockwise). The rotation$r$ corresponds to$(1234)$, the reflection$s$ (across horizontal axis) to$(14)(23)$. The action is faithful (different symmetries give different permutations) and transitive (any vertex can be sent to any other by a rotation). The stabilizer of vertex 1: only$e$ (since any nontrivial rotation or reflection that fixes vertex 1 would have to fix all vertices, but no such element exists except$e$). By orbit-stabilizer: orbit of 1 has size$|D_4|/|G_1| = 8/1 = 8$... but there are only 4 vertices. Error check: actually$G_1$ has order$8/4 = 2$, and the stabilizer of vertex 1 is$\{e, s'\}$ where$s'$ is the reflection across the diagonal through vertex 1.

\begin{remark}
These three canonical actions together yield the three most important theorems about finite groups via group actions: Cayley's theorem (from left multiplication), the class equation and $p$-group theory (from conjugation), and the embedding$G \hookrightarrow S_{[G:H]}$ with kernel$\mathrm{Core}_G(H)$ (from the action on cosets). We treat each in detail in Sections 16.3.2, 16.2.4, and 16.3.4.
\end{remark}
