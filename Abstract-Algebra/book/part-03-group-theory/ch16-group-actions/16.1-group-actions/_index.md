# 16.1 Group Actions

A group action formalizes the idea of a group operating on a set by symmetries. A left action of a group $G$ on a set $X$ is a function $G \times X \to X$, written $(g, x) \mapsto g \cdot x$, satisfying two axioms: the identity acts trivially ($e \cdot x = x$ for all $x \in X$) and the action is compatible with the group law ($g \cdot (h \cdot x) = (gh) \cdot x$ for all $g, h \in G$ and $x \in X$). Equivalently — and this is the most conceptually important reformulation — a group action is a group homomorphism $\phi: G \to \text{Sym}(X)$, where $\phi(g)$ is the permutation $x \mapsto g \cdot x$ of $X$. This reformulation connects actions to homomorphism theory and shows that every action "is" a representation of $G$ as permutations of $X$.

The three standard families of actions are fundamental. Left multiplication: $G$ acts on itself by $g \cdot x = gx$; this is the action used in Cayley's theorem. Conjugation: $G$ acts on itself by $g \cdot x = gxg^{-1}$; the orbits are conjugacy classes and the stabilizers are centralizers. Action on cosets: $G$ acts on $G/H$ by $g \cdot (aH) = (ga)H$; this action encodes the relationship between $G$ and its subgroups and is the key ingredient in proofs of the Sylow theorems. Right actions are defined analogously with $(x \cdot g) \cdot h = x \cdot (gh)$; one converts between left and right actions by $g \cdot x = x \cdot g^{-1}$.

Three qualitative properties classify actions. An action is faithful if the only $g \in G$ with $g \cdot x = x$ for all $x \in X$ is $g = e$ — no non-identity element acts trivially on every point. It is free if no non-identity element fixes any point — the stabilizer of every point is trivial. It is transitive if for any two points $x, y \in X$ there exists $g \in G$ with $g \cdot x = y$ — the group can move any point to any other. These properties interact in subtle ways: a free action is faithful, but not conversely; a transitive free action is called regular. Recognizing which actions have these properties is the first step in applying the orbit-stabilizer theorem, which is the main theorem of the next section.
