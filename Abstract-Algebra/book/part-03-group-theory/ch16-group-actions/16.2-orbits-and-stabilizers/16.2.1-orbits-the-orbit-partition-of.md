# 16.2.1 Orbits; the Orbit Partition of $X$

When a group $G$ acts on a set$X$, the set$X$ naturally decomposes into pieces that the action cannot mix together. These pieces are the orbits, and their existence is the first structural fact about any action.

\begin{definition}
The \textbf{orbit} of a point $x \in X$ under the action of$G$ is
$$G \cdot x = \{g \cdot x : g \in G\}.$$
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
The orbits of a $G$-action on$X$ partition$X$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Declare $x \sim y$ if there exists$g \in G$ with$g \cdot x = y$. Reflexivity:$e \cdot x = x$. Symmetry:$g \cdot x = y$ implies$g^{-1} \cdot y = x$. Transitivity:$g \cdot x = y$ and$h \cdot y = z$ imply$(hg) \cdot x = z$. So$\sim$ is an equivalence relation, and its equivalence classes are the orbits.
\end{proof}

Understanding a group action reduces to understanding each orbit separately, since each orbit $G \cdot x$ is itself a transitive$G$-set (the action of$G$ on the orbit$G \cdot x$ is transitive by definition). The action is transitive if and only if there is exactly one orbit, i.e.,$X$ itself is the single orbit.

**Example 1.** $S_3$ acting on$X = \{1,2,3\}$ by permutation. For any$i,j \in X$ and any$i \neq j$, the transposition$(ij)$ sends$i \mapsto j$. So the action is transitive — there is one orbit:$S_3 \cdot 1 = \{1,2,3\}$.

**Example 2.** $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ acting on$X = \{1,2,3,4\}$ by$\bar{k} \cdot n = n + k \pmod{4}$ (with elements labeled$1,2,3,4$ instead of$0,1,2,3$). This is transitive:$\bar{1} \cdot 1 = 2$,$\bar{2} \cdot 1 = 3$,$\bar{3} \cdot 1 = 4$. One orbit:$X$.

**Example 3.** $G = \{e, (12), (34), (12)(34)\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ acting on$\{1,2,3,4\}$. The orbit of 1:$e \cdot 1 = 1$,$(12) \cdot 1 = 2$,$(34) \cdot 1 = 1$,$(12)(34) \cdot 1 = 2$. So orbit of 1 is$\{1,2\}$. Similarly, orbit of 3 is$\{3,4\}$. Two orbits of size 2.

**Example 4.** $D_4$ acting by conjugation on$D_4 = \{e, r, r^2, r^3, s, sr, sr^2, sr^3\}$. The orbits (conjugacy classes) are:$\{e\}$,$\{r^2\}$,$\{r, r^3\}$,$\{s, sr^2\}$,$\{sr, sr^3\}$. Five orbits, sizes$1,1,2,2,2$. Total:$1+1+2+2+2 = 8 = |D_4|$.

**Example 5.** $GL_2(\mathbb{R})$ acting on$\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}$ by matrix multiplication. For any two nonzero vectors$\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^2$, there is an invertible matrix sending$\mathbf{v}$ to$\mathbf{w}$ (extend to a basis, extend to basis containing$\mathbf{w}$, take the change-of-basis matrix). So the action is transitive: one orbit$\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}$.

\begin{remark}
When $G$ acts on itself by conjugation, the orbit partition is the partition into conjugacy classes. In this case the orbit structure captures deep information about the group: elements in the same conjugacy class are "algebraically equivalent" from the group's perspective. The center consists precisely of the singleton orbits — elements whose conjugacy class has size 1.
\end{remark}
