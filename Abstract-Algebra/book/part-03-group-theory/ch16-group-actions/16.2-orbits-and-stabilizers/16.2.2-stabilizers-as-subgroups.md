# 16.2.2 Stabilizers as Subgroups

If orbits tell us where a point can go, stabilizers tell us how much of the group keeps it fixed. The two concepts are complementary, and their relationship — made precise in the next section — is the engine of orbit counting.

\begin{definition}
The \textbf{stabilizer} of a point $x \in X$, also called the \textbf{isotropy group} of$x$, is
$$G_x = \mathrm{Stab}_G(x) = \{g \in G : g \cdot x = x\}.$$
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
$G_x$ is a subgroup of$G$ for each$x \in X$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$e \in G_x$ since$e \cdot x = x$. If$g, h \in G_x$, then$(gh) \cdot x = g \cdot (h \cdot x) = g \cdot x = x$, so$gh \in G_x$. If$g \in G_x$, then$g^{-1} \cdot x = g^{-1} \cdot (g \cdot x) = (g^{-1}g) \cdot x = x$, so$g^{-1} \in G_x$.
\end{proof}

A key structural fact is that stabilizers of points in the same orbit are conjugate.

\begin{proposition}
If $y = g \cdot x$ for some$g \in G$, then$G_y = gG_xg^{-1}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$h \in G_y$ means$h \cdot (g \cdot x) = g \cdot x$, i.e.,$(g^{-1}hg) \cdot x = x$, so$g^{-1}hg \in G_x$, giving$h \in gG_xg^{-1}$. The reverse inclusion is symmetric.
\end{proof}

The stabilizers along a single orbit therefore form a conjugacy class of subgroups in $G$. The action is free if and only if every stabilizer is trivial —$G_x = \{e\}$ for all$x$.

**Example 1.** $G = S_4$ acting on$\{1,2,3,4\}$. The stabilizer of 1 is$\{\sigma \in S_4 : \sigma(1) = 1\} \cong S_3$ (permutations of$\{2,3,4\}$). Its order is$3! = 6$. By orbit-stabilizer (next section): orbit size$= 4 = 24/6$. Consistent.

**Example 2.** $D_4$ acting on the vertices$\{1,2,3,4\}$ of a square (labeled clockwise). The stabilizer of vertex 1 is$\{e, s_1\}$ where$s_1$ is the reflection across the diagonal through vertex 1. So$|G_1| = 2$, orbit of 1 has size$8/2 = 4$ (all four vertices). The stabilizers$G_1, G_2, G_3, G_4$ are pairwise conjugate subgroups of order 2.

**Example 3.** $GL_2(\mathbb{R})$ acting on$\mathbb{R}^2$. The stabilizer of$\mathbf{e}_1 = (1,0)^T$ consists of matrices$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & b \\ 0 & d \end{pmatrix}$ with$d \neq 0$: these are upper triangular matrices with 1 in the$(1,1)$ position and nonzero$(2,2)$ entry.

**Example 4.** $G$ acting on itself by conjugation. The stabilizer of$x$ is$C_G(x) = \{g : gxg^{-1} = x\} = \{g : gx = xg\}$, the centralizer of$x$. The center$Z(G) = \bigcap_{x \in G} C_G(x)$ is the intersection of all centralizers.

**Example 5.** $S_n$ acting on$k$-element subsets of$\{1,\ldots,n\}$ (for$0 \leq k \leq n$) by$\sigma \cdot S = \{\sigma(s) : s \in S\}$. The stabilizer of the subset$\{1,\ldots,k\}$ consists of permutations preserving this set, i.e.,$\sigma$ such that$\sigma(\{1,\ldots,k\}) = \{1,\ldots,k\}$. This stabilizer is$S_k \times S_{n-k}$ (permutations of$\{1,\ldots,k\}$ times permutations of$\{k+1,\ldots,n\}$), of order$k!(n-k)!$. By orbit-stabilizer, the orbit size is$n!/k!(n-k)! = \binom{n}{k}$ — the number of$k$-element subsets.

\begin{remark}
The stabilizer $G_x$ measures the "symmetry of the point$x$" within the action. Points with large stabilizers are highly symmetric — much of the group fixes them — while points with trivial stabilizers are in some sense "generic." In geometry, highly symmetric points correspond to fixed points of the action (stabilizer is all of$G$) or to points with special position. The study of stabilizers is the beginning of the theory of fixed-point sets and equivariant geometry.
\end{remark}
