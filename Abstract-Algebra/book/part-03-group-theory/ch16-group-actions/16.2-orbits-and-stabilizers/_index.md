# 16.2 Orbits and Stabilizers

When a group $G$ acts on a set $X$, the first thing to determine is which elements of $X$ can be reached from a given starting point by applying group elements. The orbit of $x \in X$ under $G$ is $\text{Orb}(x) = \{g \cdot x : g \in G\}$, the set of all points reachable from $x$. The orbits partition $X$: the relation "$y$ is reachable from $x$" is an equivalence relation (reflexive because $e \cdot x = x$, symmetric because $g \cdot x = y$ implies $g^{-1} \cdot y = x$, transitive because $h \cdot y = z$ and $g \cdot x = y$ imply $(hg) \cdot x = z$), and the equivalence classes are exactly the orbits. The action is transitive if and only if there is a single orbit — all of $X$.

The stabilizer of $x \in X$ is $\text{Stab}(x) = \{g \in G : g \cdot x = x\}$, the set of group elements that fix $x$. It is a subgroup of $G$ (a basic verification), and it measures how redundant the action is at $x$: a large stabilizer means many group elements "do nothing" to $x$. The Orbit-Stabilizer Theorem relates these two quantities: $|\text{Orb}(x)| = [G : \text{Stab}(x)]$, so $|G| = |\text{Orb}(x)| \cdot |\text{Stab}(x)|$ for finite groups. The proof is a clean bijection: the map $g\text{Stab}(x) \mapsto g \cdot x$ is a well-defined bijection from the coset space $G/\text{Stab}(x)$ to the orbit $\text{Orb}(x)$.

When the action is conjugation, the Orbit-Stabilizer Theorem gives the class equation. The orbit of $g \in G$ under conjugation is its conjugacy class $\text{Cl}(g) = \{xgx^{-1} : x \in G\}$, and the stabilizer is the centralizer $C_G(g)$. Since the orbits partition $G$, summing the orbit sizes gives $|G| = \sum_{[g]} [G : C_G(g)]$, where the sum is over one element $g$ per conjugacy class. Separating the central elements (those with $\text{Cl}(g) = \{g\}$, i.e., $C_G(g) = G$, i.e., $g \in Z(G)$) gives the class equation: $|G| = |Z(G)| + \sum_i [G : C_G(g_i)]$, where the sum runs over the non-central conjugacy classes. This equation is one of the most powerful tools in finite group theory, immediately implying that every $p$-group has a non-trivial center and driving the proof of the Sylow theorems.
