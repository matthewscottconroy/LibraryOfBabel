# 16.3.1 $p$-Groups Have Non-Trivial Centers (via Class Equation)

The class equation, derived from the conjugation action, yields one of the most important structural results in finite group theory: every nontrivial $p$-group has a nontrivial center. The proof is a single elegant application of divisibility.

Recall that a **$p$-group** is a finite group whose order is a power of a prime:$|G| = p^n$ for some prime$p$ and$n \geq 1$. The class equation reads$|G| = |Z(G)| + \sum_i [G : C_G(x_i)]$, where the sum runs over conjugacy classes of size greater than 1. Since$n \geq 1$, we have$p \mid |G|$. Each term$[G : C_G(x_i)]$ divides$|G| = p^n$ and is greater than 1, so each is divisible by$p$. Therefore$p$ divides the entire sum$\sum_i [G : C_G(x_i)]$, and since$p \mid |G|$, it follows that$p \mid |Z(G)|$. In particular,$|Z(G)| \geq p > 1$, so$Z(G) \neq \{e\}$.

\begin{theorem}
Every nontrivial $p$-group has nontrivial center: if$|G| = p^n$ with$n \geq 1$, then$p \mid |Z(G)|$ and in particular$Z(G) \neq \{e\}$.
\end{theorem}

As a first application: **any group of order $p^2$ is abelian.**

\begin{corollary}
If $|G| = p^2$, then$G \cong \mathbb{Z}/p^2\mathbb{Z}$ or$G \cong \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By the theorem, $|Z(G)| \geq p$. Either$|Z(G)| = p^2$ and$G = Z(G)$ is abelian, or$|Z(G)| = p$ and$G/Z(G)$ has order$p$, making it cyclic. But if$G/Z(G)$ is cyclic, then$G$ is abelian: let$G/Z(G) = \langle gZ(G) \rangle$; any element of$G$ is$g^i z$ for some$i$ and$z \in Z(G)$; then$(g^i z_1)(g^j z_2) = g^{i+j}z_1 z_2 = (g^j z_2)(g^i z_1)$. So$G$ is abelian, contradicting$|Z(G)| < |G|$ unless$G = Z(G)$. Thus$G = Z(G)$ in either case and$G$ is abelian.
\end{proof}

**Example 1.** $|G| = 9 = 3^2$. By the corollary,$G \cong \mathbb{Z}/9\mathbb{Z}$ or$G \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$. Both are abelian with nontrivial centers (the whole group). To distinguish:$\mathbb{Z}/9\mathbb{Z}$ has an element of order 9, while$\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ does not.

**Example 2.** $|G| = 4 = 2^2$. By the corollary,$G \cong \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ or$G \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. These are the only two groups of order 4, and both are abelian.

**Example 3.** $|G| = 8 = 2^3$. The center is nontrivial ($|Z(G)| \in \{2,4,8\}$). For$D_4$:$Z(D_4) = \{e, r^2\}$, order 2. For$Q_8$:$Z(Q_8) = \{1,-1\}$, order 2. For$\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$:$Z = G$, order 8.

**Example 4.** The inductive structure of $p$-groups. Since$Z(G) \neq \{e\}$ for any$p$-group$G$, we can form$G/Z(G)$, which is again a$p$-group (of smaller order). If$G$ has order$p^n$, then$G/Z(G)$ has order at most$p^{n-1}$. Applying the theorem inductively gives a chain$G \supsetneq Z_1(G) \supsetneq Z_2(G) \supsetneq \cdots \supsetneq \{e\}$ (the upper central series), showing$G$ is nilpotent.

**Example 5.** The theorem is sharp: there exist groups of order $p^2$ with center of order exactly$p^2$ (all abelian groups), and no groups of order$p^2$ with trivial center. For groups of order$p^3$, the center can be exactly$p$ (as in$D_4$ and$Q_8$, both order 8 with$|Z| = 2$) or can be the whole group (abelian$p$-groups of order$p^3$).

\begin{remark}
This theorem is the base step in an inductive structure that permeates $p$-group theory. Since$Z(G) \neq \{e\}$, one can pass to$G/Z(G)$, which is again a$p$-group of smaller order. This inductive approach — collapsing the center repeatedly — is the foundation for understanding the lower and upper central series, nilpotent groups, and the classification of$p$-groups of small order. It also underlies the Sylow theorems and is one of the key tools in the classification of finite simple groups.
\end{remark}
