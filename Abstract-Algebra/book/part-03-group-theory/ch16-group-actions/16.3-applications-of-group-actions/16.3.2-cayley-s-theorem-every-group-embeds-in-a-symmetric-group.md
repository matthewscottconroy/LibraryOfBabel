# 16.3.2 Cayley's Theorem: Every Group Embeds in a Symmetric Group

One of the earliest and most conceptually important results in group theory is Cayley's theorem: every group, however abstract, is isomorphic to a group of permutations. This means that the study of abstract groups is, in principle, no more general than the study of permutation groups.

\begin{theorem}[Cayley]
Every group $G$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of$\mathrm{Sym}(G)$. In particular, every group of order$n$ embeds in$S_n$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Define $\rho: G \to \mathrm{Sym}(G)$ by$\rho(g)(x) = gx$ (left multiplication). Each$\rho(g)$ is a bijection:$\rho(g^{-1})$ is its inverse. The map$\rho$ is a homomorphism:$\rho(gh)(x) = (gh)x = g(hx) = \rho(g)(\rho(h)(x)) = (\rho(g) \circ \rho(h))(x)$. And$\rho$ is injective: if$\rho(g) = \rho(h)$, then$gx = hx$ for all$x$; taking$x = e$ gives$g = h$. Therefore$G \cong \rho(G) \leq \mathrm{Sym}(G)$.
\end{proof}

For a finite group of order $n$, this gives an embedding$G \hookrightarrow S_n$. The embedding is rarely optimal in terms of size — a group of order 6 embeds in$S_6$ via Cayley, but$S_3$ itself has order 6 and can be embedded more efficiently — but the theorem's value is existential: it guarantees that every abstract group has a concrete permutation model.

**Example 1.** $G = \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z} = \{\bar{0}, \bar{1}, \bar{2}\}$. The Cayley action sends$\bar{1}$ to the permutation$\bar{0} \mapsto \bar{1} \mapsto \bar{2} \mapsto \bar{0}$, i.e., the 3-cycle$(012)$ in$S_3$. The image$\rho(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}) = \{e, (012), (021)\} \cong A_3 \leq S_3$. So$\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ embeds in$S_3$, not$S_3$ via Cayley but in a more efficient way.

**Example 2.** $G = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. Cayley embeds this in$S_4$ (since$|G|=4$). The four elements act by left multiplication, giving four permutations of the four-element set$G$. The image in$S_4$ is a subgroup isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ (the Klein four-group$V_4$).

**Example 3.** $G = D_3 \cong S_3$. Cayley embeds$D_3$ in$S_6$ (since$|D_3| = 6$), but we can also embed it in$S_3$ directly via its action on the 3 vertices of a triangle. This more efficient embedding has image equal to all of$S_3$.

**Example 4.** $G = Q_8$. Cayley embeds$Q_8$ in$S_8$ (order 8). But$Q_8$ also acts faithfully on a 4-element set:$Q_8$ acts on$\{1,i,j,k\}$ (left cosets of$\langle -1 \rangle$) by$\pm 1 \mapsto \mathrm{id}$,$\pm i \mapsto$ the permutation of cosets induced by multiplication. This gives an embedding$Q_8 \hookrightarrow S_4$.

**Example 5.** $G = A_5$ (order 60). Cayley embeds$A_5$ in$S_{60}$. But$A_5 \leq S_5$ naturally via its definition, giving a much more efficient embedding in$S_5$. This embedding shows that$A_5$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of$S_5$ of index 1 (it is a subgroup of$S_5$, not just embedded in it).

Cayley's theorem is more than a curiosity. It shows that abstract group theory and the theory of permutation groups are equivalent in expressive power: any theorem about permutation groups applies, in principle, to all groups. It also motivates the study of more efficient representations — actions on smaller sets, linear representations over fields — as ways of getting a handle on the structure of $G$ via a smaller and more tractable group than$S_{|G|}$.

\begin{remark}
The proof of Cayley's theorem is essentially the statement that every group acts faithfully on itself by left multiplication. More efficient representations correspond to faithful actions on smaller sets. The minimum $n$ such that$G$ embeds in$S_n$ is related to the minimum index of a proper subgroup of$G$. For simple groups, where the only normal subgroups are$\{e\}$ and$G$, this leads to bounds that play a role in the classification of finite simple groups.
\end{remark}
