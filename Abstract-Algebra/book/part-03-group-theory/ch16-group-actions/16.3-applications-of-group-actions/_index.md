# 16.3 Applications of Group Actions

The machinery of orbits, stabilizers, and the class equation becomes most impressive through its applications. This section presents four major applications: a structural result about $p$-groups (forced by the class equation), Cayley's embedding theorem, Burnside's orbit-counting lemma, and the use of the coset action to produce homomorphisms into symmetric groups. Together they show that the abstract formalism of group actions has both structural depth and direct computational utility.

The class equation immediately forces non-trivial centers in $p$-groups. If $|G| = p^n$ with $p$ prime and $n \geq 1$, then every term $[G : C_G(g_i)]$ in the class equation is a non-trivial power of $p$ (for non-central $g_i$), so $|Z(G)| \equiv |G| \equiv 0 \pmod{p}$, which means $Z(G)$ is non-trivial. This result has immediate consequences: groups of order $p^2$ are abelian (a short argument via the quotient $G/Z(G)$), and every $p$-group has a normal subgroup of every order dividing $|G|$ (by induction on $|G|/p$ using $Z(G)$). Cayley's theorem — every group $G$ embeds in $S_{|G|}$ — follows directly from the regular action (left multiplication), which is both free and transitive, giving a faithful representation of $G$ as permutations of its own elements.

Burnside's lemma turns the orbit count into an average fixed-point count: the number of orbits of $G$ acting on $X$ equals $\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g \in G}|X^g|$, where $X^g = \{x \in X : g \cdot x = x\}$ is the fixed-point set of $g$. This is proved by double-counting the pairs $(g, x)$ with $g \cdot x = x$: summing over $g$ gives $\sum_{g}|X^g|$, while summing over $x$ and applying the orbit-stabilizer theorem gives $\sum_x |\text{Stab}(x)| = |G| \cdot |\text{Orbits}|$. The result is widely used to count colorings, configurations, and geometric objects up to symmetry. Finally, when $G$ acts on the coset space $G/H$ by left multiplication, the associated homomorphism $\phi: G \to S_{[G:H]}$ has kernel $\text{Core}(H) = \bigcap_{g \in G} gHg^{-1}$, the largest normal subgroup of $G$ contained in $H$. If $|G|$ does not divide $[G:H]!$, then $\phi$ cannot be injective, so $\text{Core}(H)$ is non-trivial, meaning $G$ is not simple. This argument is one of the standard tools for proving groups of small order are not simple.
