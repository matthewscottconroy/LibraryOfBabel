# Chapter 16 — Group Actions

The most natural way for a group to arise in mathematics is not as an abstract set with a multiplication table, but as a group of transformations — permutations of some set that preserve structure. Felix Klein's Erlangen Program of 1872 declared that every geometry is characterized by the group of transformations that preserve its features, and this perspective transformed not only geometry but the whole of mathematics. Group actions are the formalization of this idea: a group $G$ acts on a set $X$ when each $g \in G$ induces a permutation of $X$, and these permutations are consistent with the group law.

Precisely, a left action of $G$ on $X$ is a function $G \times X \to X$, written $(g, x) \mapsto g \cdot x$, satisfying $e \cdot x = x$ and $(gh) \cdot x = g \cdot (h \cdot x)$. Equivalently, an action is a homomorphism $\phi: G \to \text{Sym}(X)$, and this formulation makes the connection to homomorphism theory immediate. An action is faithful if $\phi$ is injective — no non-identity element acts trivially — and transitive if $X$ consists of a single orbit. The three standard examples — left multiplication of $G$ on itself, conjugation of $G$ on itself, and left multiplication on the coset space $G/H$ — each illuminate a different face of the group's structure.

The central structural theorem of this chapter is the Orbit-Stabilizer Theorem: if $G$ acts on $X$ and $x \in X$, then $|G| = |\text{Orb}(x)| \cdot |\text{Stab}(x)|$. The orbit $\text{Orb}(x) = \{g \cdot x : g \in G\}$ and the stabilizer $\text{Stab}(x) = \{g \in G : g \cdot x = x\}$ together perfectly account for the size of the group. This theorem unifies Lagrange's theorem (take $X = G/H$ with left multiplication) and will power the proof of the Sylow theorems in Chapter 17. When the action is conjugation, the orbits are conjugacy classes, the stabilizers are centralizers, and summing the orbit sizes gives the class equation: $|G| = |Z(G)| + \sum_i [G : C_G(g_i)]$, where the sum runs over one representative from each non-central conjugacy class. The class equation is a powerful tool for analyzing $p$-groups, immediately implying that every non-trivial $p$-group has a non-trivial center.

The applications culminate in two celebrated theorems. Cayley's theorem states that every group embeds into a symmetric group: the left multiplication action of $G$ on itself gives an injective homomorphism $G \hookrightarrow S_{|G|}$, realizing $G$ concretely as a permutation group. While this embedding is rarely efficient — it represents a group of order $n$ inside $S_n$ — it provides a universal representation that underlies much of finite group theory. Burnside's lemma, meanwhile, turns group actions into a counting tool: the number of orbits of $G$ acting on $X$ equals $\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g \in G}|X^g|$, where $X^g$ is the set of elements fixed by $g$. This formula has become indispensable in combinatorics for counting colorings, configurations, and chemical isomers up to symmetry, and it previews the deeper theory of group representations and characters.
