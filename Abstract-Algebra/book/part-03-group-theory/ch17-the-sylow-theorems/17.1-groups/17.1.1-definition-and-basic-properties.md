# 17.1.1 Definition and Basic Properties

Before we can state the Sylow theorems, we need to understand the objects they are about: $p$-groups. The concept is simple to state but rich in consequences. A *$p$-group* is a group in which every element has order a power of the prime$p$. For finite groups, this condition admits a strikingly clean equivalent formulation: a finite group is a$p$-group if and only if$|G| = p^n$ for some non-negative integer$n$. The forward direction follows immediately from Lagrange's theorem, which forces every element's order to divide$|G| = p^n$, hence every element order is a power of$p$. The converse follows from Cauchy's theorem: if some prime$q \neq p$ divided$|G|$, the group would contain an element of order$q$, contradicting the assumption that all element orders are powers of$p$.

Why do $p$-groups deserve special attention? The answer is that they are, in a precise sense, the fundamental building blocks of all finite groups. The Sylow theorems — the main theorems of this chapter — guarantee that inside any finite group$G$, there exist large$p$-group subgroups for every prime$p$ dividing$|G|$. The structure of$G$ is then determined, to a significant degree, by how these$p$-power pieces fit together and interact. This viewpoint, of studying a group through its prime-power order subgroups, pervades all of finite group theory.

The definition of a $p$-group should feel natural in light of Lagrange's theorem. The most basic fact about subgroups is that their orders divide the group order. When$|G| = p^n$, this constraint forces a remarkable rigidity: the only available subgroup orders are$1, p, p^2, \ldots, p^n$. There are no "mixed" divisors, no elements of composite order not already explained by the prime$p$. The group is, in this arithmetic sense, monochromatic — all its structure is governed by a single prime.

$p$-groups themselves exhibit a rich and varied internal structure. The trivial group$\{e\}$ is a$p$-group of order$p^0 = 1$. The cyclic group$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ is the unique$p$-group of order$p$. At order$p^2$, there are exactly two isomorphism types: the cyclic group$\mathbb{Z}/p^2\mathbb{Z}$ and the elementary abelian group$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$. At order$p^3$, the count jumps to five groups (for$p$ odd), including two non-abelian ones. As$n$ grows, the number of isomorphism classes of$p$-groups of order$p^n$ grows superexponentially — it is known to be$p^{(2/27)n^3 + O(n^{8/3})}$. So$p$-groups are plentiful, varied, and by no means fully classified.

The next two subsections establish two foundational structural facts about $p$-groups that serve as essential tools throughout this chapter. The first is that every nontrivial$p$-group has a nontrivial center — a consequence of the class equation and prime arithmetic. The second is that every group of order$p^2$ is abelian — a consequence of the first fact and a clever quotient argument. These results show that despite their variety,$p$-groups satisfy deep structural constraints that arbitrary groups do not.

\begin{definition}
A group $G$ is a \emph{$p$-group} for a prime$p$ if every element of$G$ has order a power of$p$ (including$p^0 = 1$). A finite group is a$p$-group if and only if$|G| = p^n$ for some$n \geq 0$.
\end{definition}

**Example 1.** The cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$ is a$2$-group of order$2^3$. Its elements have orders$1, 2, 4, 8$, all powers of$2$. The generator$\bar{1}$ has order$8$, the element$\bar{2}$ has order$4$, the element$\bar{4}$ has order$2$, and$\bar{0}$ has order$1$. Note that no element has order$3$ or$6$: such an element would contradict$|G| = 8$.

**Example 2.** The elementary abelian group $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^n = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} \times \cdots \times \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ ($n$ factors) is a$p$-group of order$p^n$ in which every non-identity element has order exactly$p$. This group is the additive group of an$n$-dimensional vector space over$\mathbb{F}_p$, and it is as "spread out" as a$p$-group of its order can be. The extreme opposite is$\mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$, where a single generator element has order$p^n$. These two families represent the two poles of the world of$p$-groups.

**Example 3.** The dihedral group $D_4$ of order$8$ is a non-abelian$2$-group. Its elements$\{e, r, r^2, r^3, s, sr, sr^2, sr^3\}$ have orders$1, 4, 2, 4, 2, 2, 2, 2$ respectively — all powers of$2$. The center is$Z(D_4) = \{e, r^2\}$, which is nontrivial (illustrating the theorem of the next subsection). The group is not abelian:$rs = sr^{-1} = sr^3 \neq sr$.

**Example 4.** The quaternion group $Q_8 = \{\pm 1, \pm i, \pm j, \pm k\}$ is another non-abelian$2$-group of order$8$. Its elements have orders$1, 2, 4, 4, 4, 4, 4, 4$. Like$D_4$, it has center$Z(Q_8) = \{1, -1\}$. However,$Q_8$ and$D_4$ are not isomorphic: in$Q_8$, every subgroup of order$4$ is cyclic and normal, whereas$D_4$ has a non-normal subgroup of order$4$ (namely, any subgroup generated by a reflection). This distinction illustrates that even among$p$-groups of the same order, the isomorphism type is determined by fine structural data.

**Example 5.** Consider the Sylow context: if $|G| = 360 = 2^3 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5$, then the Sylow$2$-subgroups are$2$-groups of order$8$, the Sylow$3$-subgroups are$3$-groups of order$9$, and the Sylow$5$-subgroups are$5$-groups of order$5$. Each Sylow subgroup is itself a$p$-group, and the Sylow theorems will tell us how many of each there are, how they relate to each other, and how they generate$G$. The three families of$p$-groups — for$p = 2, 3, 5$ — are the raw material from which the group of order$360$ is assembled.
