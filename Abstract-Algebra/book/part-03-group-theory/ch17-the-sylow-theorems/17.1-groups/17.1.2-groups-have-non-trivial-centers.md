# 17.1.2 $p$-Groups Have Non-Trivial Centers

One of the most fundamental structural facts about $p$-groups is that they cannot have trivial center. This might seem surprising at first: after all, there are plenty of groups with trivial center — the symmetric group$S_n$ for$n \geq 3$, most matrix groups, and countless others. The restriction to$p$-groups introduces a arithmetic rigidity that forces commutativity, at least in the center.

The proof uses the *class equation*, a powerful counting identity that decomposes a group based on the sizes of its conjugacy classes. Recall that the conjugacy class of $x \in G$ is$\{gxg^{-1} : g \in G\}$, whose size equals the index$[G : C_G(x)]$ of the centralizer of$x$. Elements in the center$Z(G)$ form singleton conjugacy classes (since$gxg^{-1} = x$ for all$g$ when$x \in Z(G)$). Collecting the remaining conjugacy classes, we obtain the class equation:
$$|G| = |Z(G)| + \sum_{i} [G : C_G(x_i)],$$
where the sum is over representatives $x_i$ of the non-central conjugacy classes (those with more than one element).

Now specialize to a $p$-group:$|G| = p^n$ with$n \geq 1$. For each non-central conjugacy class, the size$[G : C_G(x_i)]$ is a divisor of$|G| = p^n$ that is strictly greater than$1$ (since$x_i \notin Z(G)$), hence it is divisible by$p$. Therefore every term in the sum on the right is divisible by$p$. Since$p \mid |G|$ and$p$ divides the entire sum, we conclude$p \mid |Z(G)|$. In particular,$|Z(G)| \geq p > 1$, so$Z(G)$ is nontrivial.

\begin{theorem}
Every nontrivial $p$-group has nontrivial center: if$|G| = p^n$ with$n \geq 1$, then$Z(G) \neq \{e\}$.
\end{theorem}

The implication for simplicity is immediate. If $|G| = p^n$ with$n \geq 2$, then$Z(G)$ is a proper nontrivial normal subgroup of$G$ (it is normal because$gZ(G)g^{-1} = Z(G)$ for all$g$). Therefore no$p$-group of order$p^n$ with$n \geq 2$ is simple. The only simple$p$-groups are the cyclic groups$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ of order$p$.

The true power of the center theorem lies in the inductive foothold it provides. If $G$ is a$p$-group, then$Z(G)$ is a nontrivial normal subgroup, and$G/Z(G)$ is again a$p$-group of strictly smaller order. We can therefore reason about$p$-groups by induction on$|G|$, reducing questions about$G$ to questions about the smaller$p$-group$G/Z(G)$. This inductive strategy — descend via the center, apply the induction hypothesis to the quotient, then lift back — is the engine behind the proof in the next subsection and recurs throughout the theory.

Notice also that this result has no analogue for arbitrary finite groups. The symmetric group $S_3$ has trivial center despite being perfectly finite. The center theorem is genuinely special to$p$-groups, a consequence of the arithmetic rigidity imposed by having prime-power order: every conjugacy class size is forced to be a power of$p$, and the class equation then becomes a divisibility constraint on$|Z(G)|$.

**Example 1.** Let $G = D_4$, the dihedral group of order$8 = 2^3$. The conjugacy classes of$D_4$ (with$r^4 = s^2 = e$,$srs = r^{-1}$) are:$\{e\}$,$\{r^2\}$,$\{r, r^3\}$,$\{s, sr^2\}$,$\{sr, sr^3\}$. The class equation is$8 = 1 + 1 + 2 + 2 + 2$. Reading off the singletons,$Z(D_4) = \{e, r^2\}$ has order$2$, confirming$Z(D_4) \neq \{e\}$. The quotient$D_4/Z(D_4)$ has order$4$ and is isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, another$2$-group.

**Example 2.** Let $G = Q_8$ be the quaternion group. Its conjugacy classes are$\{1\}$,$\{-1\}$,$\{i, -i\}$,$\{j, -j\}$,$\{k, -k\}$. The class equation is$8 = 1 + 1 + 2 + 2 + 2$. We read$Z(Q_8) = \{1, -1\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, nontrivial as guaranteed. Compare with$D_4$: both have center of order$2$, but$Q_8/Z(Q_8) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ as well, showing that the center theorem alone does not distinguish these two groups.

**Example 3.** Let $G$ be the Heisenberg group over$\mathbb{F}_p$: the group of upper-triangular$3 \times 3$ matrices over$\mathbb{F}_p$ with$1$s on the diagonal. Explicitly,$G$ consists of matrices
$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & a & c \\ 0 & 1 & b \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad a, b, c \in \mathbb{F}_p.$$
This group has order $p^3$ and is non-abelian for$p \geq 2$. A direct calculation shows that two such matrices commute if and only if they have the same "diagonal entries"$a$ and$b$. The center consists of matrices with$a = b = 0$, forming a subgroup isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$. The class equation confirms$p \mid |Z(G)| = p$.

**Example 4.** Suppose $|G| = p^2$. The class equation gives$p^2 = |Z(G)| + \sum [G : C_G(x_i)]$. Each term in the sum divides$p^2$ and is at least$p$. If$|Z(G)| = 1$ (suppose for contradiction), then$p^2 - 1 = \sum [G : C_G(x_i)]$, a sum of terms each divisible by$p$. But$p^2 - 1 \equiv -1 \not\equiv 0 \pmod{p}$, a contradiction. Hence$|Z(G)| \geq p$, which means$|Z(G)| \in \{p, p^2\}$. This sets up the classification argument of Section 17.1.3.

**Example 5.** For an infinite $p$-group, the center theorem does not apply directly, and the center can in principle be trivial. However, for *finitely generated* infinite$p$-groups, the question of whether the center is nontrivial is much more subtle, and such groups (including the famous Grigorchuk group) are an active research area. The theorem's strength lies entirely in the finite setting, where arithmetic divisibility arguments have full force.
