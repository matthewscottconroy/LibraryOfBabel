# 17.1.3 Groups of Order $p^2$ Are Abelian

We now put the center theorem to work in a concrete and elegant application. The claim is that every group of order $p^2$ is abelian. This is a result with no analogue at most other orders: groups of order$p^3$ need not be abelian (the Heisenberg group over$\mathbb{F}_p$ has order$p^3$ and is non-abelian), and groups of order$pq$ with distinct primes$p, q$ can be non-abelian. The order$p^2$ is special precisely because the center theorem, combined with a short argument about cyclic quotients, forces full commutativity.

\begin{theorem}
Every group of order $p^2$, for any prime$p$, is abelian.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $|G| = p^2$. By the center theorem (Section 17.1.2),$Z(G)$ is nontrivial, so$|Z(G)| \in \{p, p^2\}$. If$|Z(G)| = p^2$, then$Z(G) = G$, meaning$G$ is abelian. Suppose for contradiction that$|Z(G)| = p$. Then$G/Z(G)$ has order$p^2/p = p$, so$G/Z(G)$ is cyclic of order$p$. Write$G/Z(G) = \langle gZ(G) \rangle$ for some$g \in G$. Every element of$G$ is of the form$g^k z$ for some$k \in \{0, 1, \ldots, p-1\}$ and$z \in Z(G)$. Given any two elements$g^k z$ and$g^m w$ of$G$ (with$z, w \in Z(G)$), we compute:
$$(g^k z)(g^m w) = g^k g^m z w = g^{k+m} z w = g^{m+k} w z = (g^m w)(g^k z),$$
where we used that $z$ and$w$ commute with everything (since$z, w \in Z(G)$) and that$g^k$ and$g^m$ commute with each other (they are both powers of$g$). Thus every two elements of$G$ commute, so$G$ is abelian. But an abelian group has$Z(G) = G$, contradicting$|Z(G)| = p < p^2$. This contradiction shows the case$|Z(G)| = p$ is impossible, and$G$ must be abelian.
\end{proof}

The key lemma used in the proof is worth stating explicitly: *if $G/Z(G)$ is cyclic, then$G$ is abelian*. This lemma has the flavor of a "cyclic implies commuting" argument: whenever you can express all elements of$G$ as powers of a single element times central elements, commutativity becomes transparent.

Since $G$ is abelian of order$p^2$, the structure theorem for finitely generated abelian groups (Chapter 19) immediately identifies the possibilities: either$G \cong \mathbb{Z}/p^2\mathbb{Z}$ (if$G$ has an element of order$p^2$) or$G \cong \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ (if every non-identity element has order$p$). These are the only two abelian groups of order$p^2$ up to isomorphism. The key distinction is the maximum order of an element:$\mathbb{Z}/p^2\mathbb{Z}$ has an element of order$p^2$, while$(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^2$ does not.

This argument is a prototype for inductive classification arguments in the theory of $p$-groups. The center theorem provides a nontrivial normal subgroup; a short combinatorial argument forces commutativity; and then the general structure theorem identifies the isomorphism types. The same strategy — center theorem, quotient by center, induction — recurs throughout Sylow theory and beyond, though the conclusions become more complex as the order grows.

**Example 1.** Let $p = 2$. The groups of order$4$ are$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ and$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. In$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$, the element$\bar{1}$ has order$4 = 2^2$, so this is the cyclic case. In$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, every non-identity element$(\bar{1}, \bar{0})$,$(\bar{0}, \bar{1})$,$(\bar{1}, \bar{1})$ has order$2$, so no element has order$4$. These are the only two groups of order$4$, and both are abelian.

**Example 2.** Let $p = 3$. The groups of order$9$ are$\mathbb{Z}/9\mathbb{Z}$ and$\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$. In$\mathbb{Z}/9\mathbb{Z}$, the element$\bar{1}$ generates the whole group. In$(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})^2$, the element$(1, 0)$ has order$3$, and the element$(1, 1)$ also has order$3$; there is no element of order$9$, because$3 \cdot (a, b) = (3a, 3b) = (0, 0)$ for all$(a, b)$. To distinguish these groups concretely: ask whether the group contains an element of order$9$. If yes, it is$\mathbb{Z}/9\mathbb{Z}$; if not, it is$(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})^2$.

**Example 3.** Suppose $G$ is a group of order$p^2$ and we want to determine the structure of its subgroups. Since$G$ is abelian of order$p^2$, Lagrange's theorem constrains subgroup orders to$\{1, p, p^2\}$. In$\mathbb{Z}/p^2\mathbb{Z}$, there is a unique subgroup of each order:$\{0\}$,$\langle p \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$, and$\mathbb{Z}/p^2\mathbb{Z}$ itself. In$(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^2$, there are$p + 1$ subgroups of order$p$ (corresponding to the$p + 1$ one-dimensional subspaces of the vector space$\mathbb{F}_p^2$). This difference in the number of order-$p$ subgroups is a complete invariant distinguishing the two groups of order$p^2$.

**Example 4.** The proof that $G/Z(G)$ cyclic implies$G$ abelian extends to a slightly more general statement: if$G/Z(G)$ is cyclic, then$G = \langle g \rangle \cdot Z(G)$ for any$g$ whose coset generates$G/Z(G)$, and the group law in$G$ is entirely determined. In particular, two elements$g^a z_1$ and$g^b z_2$ satisfy$(g^a z_1)(g^b z_2) = g^{a+b} z_1 z_2$, which is symmetric in the two factors. This shows concretely why$G$ must be abelian: the "interaction" between any two elements reduces to addition of exponents and multiplication in$Z(G)$, both of which are abelian operations.

**Example 5.** The result fails at order $p^3$. The Heisenberg group$H_p$ of$3 \times 3$ upper-unitriangular matrices over$\mathbb{F}_p$ has order$p^3$ and is non-abelian: the matrices with$(1,2)$-entry equal to$1$ and all others zero, and the matrix with$(2,3)$-entry equal to$1$, do not commute. Their commutator is the matrix with only the$(1,3)$-entry equal to$1$, which is nontrivial. The group$H_p$ has center$Z(H_p) \cong \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ (the scalar matrices with only$(1,3)$-entry possibly nonzero), and the quotient$H_p/Z(H_p) \cong (\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^2$ is abelian but not cyclic. This is why the argument breaks at order$p^3$: the quotient by the center need not be cyclic, and without cyclicity of the quotient, the commutativity argument fails.
