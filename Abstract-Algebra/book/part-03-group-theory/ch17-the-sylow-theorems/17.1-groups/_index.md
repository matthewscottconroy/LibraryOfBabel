# 17.1 $p$-Groups

A $p$-group is a group whose order is a power of the prime $p$, that is, $|G| = p^n$ for some $n \geq 0$. Equivalently (by Lagrange's theorem and its partial converse in the abelian case), a finite group is a $p$-group if and only if every element has order a power of $p$. These groups are among the most intensively studied objects in finite group theory: they are the building blocks that the Sylow theorems extract from arbitrary finite groups, and their structural properties are rich despite — or because of — their constrained nature. The theory of $p$-groups is in some ways more subtle than the theory of larger groups, because there is no simple classification even for $p$-groups of modest order.

The first fundamental theorem about $p$-groups is that every non-trivial $p$-group has a non-trivial center. The proof is a direct application of the class equation: writing $|G| = |Z(G)| + \sum_i [G: C_G(g_i)]$, every term $[G : C_G(g_i)]$ is a power of $p$ greater than 1 (since $g_i \notin Z(G)$ means the class size is more than 1 and must divide $|G| = p^n$), so $p \mid |Z(G)|$, hence $Z(G) \neq \{e\}$. This result is used repeatedly in inductive arguments about $p$-groups. The second key theorem is that groups of order $p^2$ are abelian: if $|Z(G)| = p^2$ we are done; if $|Z(G)| = p$, then $G/Z(G)$ has order $p$, hence is cyclic, but a group with cyclic quotient by its center must be abelian, a contradiction.

The structure of $p$-groups of small order is illuminating. Groups of order $p$ are cyclic ($\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$). Groups of order $p^2$ are abelian and isomorphic to either $\mathbb{Z}/p^2\mathbb{Z}$ or $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$. For order $p^3$, there are 5 isomorphism classes (3 abelian and 2 non-abelian) when $p$ is odd. The non-abelian groups of order $p^3$ include the Heisenberg group of upper triangular matrices with 1s on the diagonal, which is a central extension of $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^2$ by $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ and serves as a key example in representation theory. The general classification of $p$-groups is wide open: the number of isomorphism classes of groups of order $p^n$ grows extremely rapidly with $n$ (roughly $p^{2n^3/27}$), and there is no hope for a complete classification even in principle.
