# 17.2.1 Definition: A Sylow $p$-Subgroup of Order$p^a$ When$|G| = p^a m$

Lagrange's theorem tells us that every subgroup's order must divide the group's order, but it says nothing about which divisors actually occur as subgroup orders. Cauchy's theorem goes one step further, guaranteeing elements of prime order $p$ whenever$p \mid |G|$ — in other words, cyclic subgroups of prime order. But what about subgroups of order$p^2$,$p^3$, or the full prime-power piece of$|G|$? This is a genuinely different question, and it is the question the Sylow theorems answer.

The fundamental setup is to write $|G| = p^a m$ where$p$ is a prime,$a \geq 1$, and$\gcd(p, m) = 1$ — that is,$p^a$ is the *exact* power of$p$ dividing$|G|$. With this notation:

\begin{definition}
A \emph{Sylow $p$-subgroup} of$G$ is a subgroup of order exactly$p^a$.
\end{definition}

The definition singles out the maximal $p$-power piece of a finite group. The key word is "maximal": a Sylow$p$-subgroup is not just any$p$-subgroup, but one of the largest possible order. Any$p$-subgroup of$G$ has order$p^k$ for some$k \leq a$, by Lagrange's theorem, and a Sylow$p$-subgroup achieves the maximum$k = a$.

It is worth being explicit: Lagrange's theorem does not, by itself, guarantee that a subgroup of order $p^a$ exists. Lagrange only tells us the order must *divide*$|G|$ if the subgroup exists; it says nothing about existence. Without Sylow's first theorem (the next subsection), we could not be sure Sylow$p$-subgroups exist at all. Their existence is a theorem, not a tautology.

The notation $\mathrm{Syl}_p(G)$ is standard for the set of all Sylow$p$-subgroups of$G$, and$n_p = |\mathrm{Syl}_p(G)|$ for the number of them. The Sylow theorems will give us complete information about$n_p$: it exists and is at least$1$ (Sylow I), all elements of$\mathrm{Syl}_p(G)$ are conjugate (Sylow II), and$n_p \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$ with$n_p \mid m$ (Sylow III).

A Sylow $p$-subgroup is also characterized by maximality among$p$-subgroups: a subgroup$H$ is a Sylow$p$-subgroup if and only if$H$ is a$p$-subgroup not properly contained in any larger$p$-subgroup of$G$. This equivalence is useful for recognizing Sylow subgroups without computing orders directly.

**Example 1.** Let $|G| = 60 = 2^2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5$. Then:
- Sylow $2$-subgroups have order$p^a = 2^2 = 4$.
- Sylow $3$-subgroups have order$p^a = 3^1 = 3$.
- Sylow $5$-subgroups have order$p^a = 5^1 = 5$.

For $G = A_5$: the Sylow$2$-subgroups are subgroups isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ (generated by two disjoint transpositions); the Sylow$3$-subgroups are cyclic subgroups of order$3$ (generated by$3$-cycles); the Sylow$5$-subgroups are cyclic subgroups of order$5$ (generated by$5$-cycles). Sylow III will tell us$n_5 \in \{1, 6\}$; since$A_5$ is simple, we need$n_5 = 6$.

**Example 2.** Let $|G| = 12 = 2^2 \cdot 3$. The Sylow$2$-subgroups have order$4$ and the Sylow$3$-subgroups have order$3$. In the cyclic group$\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$, the unique subgroup of order$4$ is$\langle \bar{3} \rangle = \{0, 3, 6, 9\}$ and the unique subgroup of order$3$ is$\langle \bar{4} \rangle = \{0, 4, 8\}$. Here$n_2 = n_3 = 1$, so both Sylow subgroups are normal (as expected in an abelian group).

**Example 3.** Let $|G| = 168 = 2^3 \cdot 3 \cdot 7$. The Sylow$7$-subgroups have order$7$. Since$7$ is prime, each such subgroup is cyclic. Sylow III gives$n_7 \mid 24$ and$n_7 \equiv 1 \pmod{7}$, so$n_7 \in \{1, 8\}$. The group$\mathrm{PSL}(2, \mathbb{F}_7)$ of order$168$ is simple, so it cannot have a unique (hence normal) Sylow subgroup; thus$n_7 = 8$.

**Example 4.** Let $|G| = p^a$ itself (so$m = 1$). Then the only Sylow$p$-subgroup is$G$ itself. Every$p$-group is its own (unique) Sylow$p$-subgroup. This is the degenerate case: all the action of Sylow theory happens when$m > 1$, when there is a genuinely non-$p$ piece of the group order.

**Example 5.** Let $|G| = 30 = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5$. Here$a = 1$ for every prime, so each Sylow subgroup is cyclic of prime order. The Sylow$5$-subgroups are cyclic of order$5$; Sylow III gives$n_5 \mid 6$ and$n_5 \equiv 1 \pmod{5}$. The divisors of$6$ are$1, 2, 3, 6$; among these,$1 \equiv 1 \pmod{5}$ and$6 \equiv 1 \pmod{5}$. So$n_5 \in \{1, 6\}$. As we will see in the applications section, a counting argument forces$n_5 = 1$, making the Sylow$5$-subgroup normal. Similar analysis applies to the other primes, eventually showing$G$ is cyclic or a specific semidirect product.
