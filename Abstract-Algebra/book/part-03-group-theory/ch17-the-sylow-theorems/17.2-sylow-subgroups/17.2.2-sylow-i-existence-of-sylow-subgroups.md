# 17.2.2 Sylow I: Existence of Sylow $p$-Subgroups

The first and most fundamental of the Sylow theorems guarantees that Sylow subgroups are never a vacuous concept.

\begin{theorem}[Sylow I]
Let $G$ be a finite group and$p$ a prime dividing$|G|$. Write$|G| = p^a m$ with$\gcd(p, m) = 1$. Then$G$ has at least one subgroup of order$p^a$ — that is,$\mathrm{Syl}_p(G)$ is nonempty.
\end{theorem}

The existence of a subgroup of the right size is far from obvious. Lagrange's theorem is a *necessary* condition on subgroup orders but is nowhere near *sufficient* — there are integers dividing a group's order for which no subgroup of that order exists (the alternating group $A_4$ has no subgroup of order$6$ despite$6 \mid 12$). The Sylow theorem is asserting something much deeper: for prime-power divisors of the maximal power, existence is guaranteed.

The most elegant proof of Sylow I uses a group action on sets. Let $\mathcal{X}$ be the collection of all subsets of$G$ of cardinality$p^a$:
$$\mathcal{X} = \{ T \subseteq G : |T| = p^a \}.$$
The group $G$ acts on$\mathcal{X}$ by left multiplication:$g \cdot T = gT = \{gt : t \in T\}$. By the orbit-stabilizer theorem, the size of the orbit of any$T \in \mathcal{X}$ equals$[G : G_T]$, where$G_T = \{g \in G : gT = T\}$ is the stabilizer. The crucial observation is that$G_T \subseteq T \cdot T^{-1}$ (if$gT = T$ then$g = g(t)t^{-1} \in T \cdot T^{-1}$ for any fixed$t \in T$), but more precisely one shows$|G_T|$ divides$p^a$. Combining with$|G_T| = |G| / |\mathrm{Orb}(T)|$, the key step is showing that$|\mathrm{Orb}(T)|$ is divisible by exactly the same power of$p$ as$m$.

The total count $|\mathcal{X}| = \binom{p^a m}{p^a}$ drives the argument. A standard combinatorial identity gives
$$\binom{p^a m}{p^a} \equiv \binom{m}{1} = m \pmod{p},$$
so $p \nmid |\mathcal{X}|$. Since$|\mathcal{X}|$ is not divisible by$p$, at least one orbit must have size not divisible by$p$. For any$T$ in such an orbit,$|G_T|$ divides$|G| = p^a m$ and$[G : G_T]$ is not divisible by$p$, so$p^a \mid |G_T|$. But also$|G_T| \leq p^a$ (the stabilizer cannot be larger than$T$, which has$p^a$ elements). Therefore$|G_T| = p^a$, and$G_T$ is the desired Sylow$p$-subgroup.

Existence is the most important of the three Sylow theorems because it guarantees that the $p$-primary structure of a finite group is never vacuous. No matter how$G$ is constructed, its Sylow$p$-subgroups are present and available for analysis. The second and third theorems describe how Sylow subgroups relate to one another and constrain how many there can be, but without the first theorem those questions would be meaningless.

A classical corollary recovers Cauchy's theorem: if $p \mid |G|$, then$G$ has an element of order$p$. Indeed, any Sylow$p$-subgroup$P$ has order$p^a \geq p$, so$P$ is a nontrivial$p$-group and therefore (by the center theorem of Section 17.1.2) has nontrivial center; any nonidentity element in its center has order dividing$p^a$, hence has some power of prime order$p$.

In fact, Sylow I generalizes Cauchy fully: if $p^k \mid |G|$ for any$k \leq a$, then$G$ has a subgroup of order$p^k$. This follows by taking a Sylow$p$-subgroup$P$ of order$p^a$ and applying the following lemma to$P$: every$p$-group of order$p^n$ has a subgroup of order$p^k$ for every$0 \leq k \leq n$. This lemma is proved by induction using the center theorem, exactly as in the discussion of$p$-groups in Section 17.1.

**Example 1.** Let $G = S_5$,$|G| = 120 = 2^3 \cdot 3 \cdot 5$. Sylow I guarantees a subgroup of order$8$, a subgroup of order$3$, and a subgroup of order$5$. Explicitly:$\langle (12), (34), (1324) \rangle \cong D_4$ is one Sylow$2$-subgroup of order$8$;$\langle (123) \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ is a Sylow$3$-subgroup;$\langle (12345) \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$ is a Sylow$5$-subgroup. The theorem guarantees these exist; finding them explicitly may require additional work.

**Example 2.** Let $G = \mathrm{GL}(2, \mathbb{F}_3)$. We have$|G| = (9-1)(9-3) = 8 \cdot 6 = 48 = 2^4 \cdot 3$. Sylow I guarantees a subgroup of order$16$ (Sylow$2$-subgroup) and a subgroup of order$3$ (Sylow$3$-subgroup). A Sylow$3$-subgroup is generated by a matrix of order$3$, for instance$\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ (whose characteristic polynomial$x^2 + x + 1$ has roots of order$3$ in$\mathbb{F}_9$).

**Example 3.** The theorem shows more: by applying the argument to the $p$-group$P$ itself, every$p$-group has subgroups of all orders$p^0, p^1, \ldots, p^n$. For instance,$D_4$ (order$8$) has subgroups of orders$1, 2, 4, 8$:
\begin{align*}
&|H| = 1: \quad \{e\} \\
&|H| = 2: \quad \{e, r^2\}, \{e, s\}, \{e, sr\}, \{e, sr^2\}, \{e, sr^3\} \\
&|H| = 4: \quad \{e, r, r^2, r^3\}, \{e, r^2, s, sr^2\}, \{e, r^2, sr, sr^3\} \\
&|H| = 8: \quad D_4.
\end{align*}
This comprehensive subgroup structure is a consequence of Sylow I applied to $D_4$ as a$2$-group.

**Example 4.** Let $|G| = 35 = 5 \cdot 7$. Sylow I gives subgroups of order$5$ and$7$. Since both subgroups have prime order, both are cyclic. Sylow III (which we will prove shortly) gives$n_5 \mid 7$ and$n_5 \equiv 1 \pmod{5}$, so$n_5 = 1$; and$n_7 \mid 5$ and$n_7 \equiv 1 \pmod{7}$, so$n_7 = 1$. Both Sylow subgroups are unique and hence normal. The group$G$ is therefore the direct product$\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/35\mathbb{Z}$. Without Sylow I guaranteeing the subgroups exist, this argument could not begin.

**Example 5.** The proof of Sylow I via the binomial coefficient estimate $\binom{p^a m}{p^a} \equiv m \pmod{p}$ is worth verifying for a small case. Take$p = 2$,$a = 2$,$m = 3$, so$|G| = 12$. Then$|\mathcal{X}| = \binom{12}{4} = 495 = 5 \cdot 9 \cdot 11$. Is$2 \nmid 495$? Yes:$495$ is odd. So the set of$4$-element subsets has odd cardinality, hence some orbit has odd size, and the stabilizer of any element in that orbit is a subgroup of order$4$.
