# 17.2.3 Sylow II: All Sylow $p$-Subgroups Are Conjugate

The second Sylow theorem is, in some ways, the most surprising. Sylow I guarantees existence; Sylow II asserts a far more striking uniqueness-up-to-conjugacy: all maximal $p$-power subgroups of a finite group are isomorphic to each other, and not merely isomorphic but conjugate. That is, given any two Sylow $p$-subgroups $P$ and $Q$, there exists an element $g \in G$ such that $Q = gPg^{-1}$. They are not just abstractly the same; they are the same subgroup seen from a different vantage point inside $G$.

## Statement and Proof

\begin{theorem}[Sylow II]
Let $G$ be a finite group, $p$ a prime, and write $|G| = p^a m$ with $\gcd(p,m) = 1$. If $P$ and $Q$ are any two Sylow $p$-subgroups of $G$, then $Q = gPg^{-1}$ for some $g \in G$.
\end{theorem}

*Proof.* We use the action of $Q$ on the set $G/P$ of left cosets of $P$, acting by left multiplication: $q \cdot (xP) = (qx)P$. This is a genuine action of $Q$ on the finite set $G/P$.

The total number of cosets is $[G:P] = m$, which is not divisible by $p$ (since $|P| = p^a$ is the full $p$-part of $|G|$). By the orbit-stabiliser theorem, each orbit has size equal to the index of its stabiliser in $Q$. Since $|Q| = p^a$, every orbit has size a power of $p$, including $p^0 = 1$.

The sum of orbit sizes is $m$. Since $p \nmid m$ and each orbit has $p$-power size, the number of orbits of size $1$ (fixed points) must satisfy:
$$\#\{\text{fixed points}\} \equiv m \not\equiv 0 \pmod{p}.$$
In particular, there is at least one fixed point: a coset $xP$ such that $q \cdot (xP) = xP$ for all $q \in Q$, i.e., $x^{-1}qx \in P$ for all $q \in Q$. This means $x^{-1}Qx \subseteq P$.

Since $|x^{-1}Qx| = |Q| = p^a = |P|$ and $x^{-1}Qx \subseteq P$, we conclude $x^{-1}Qx = P$, hence $Q = xPx^{-1}$. Setting $g = x$ completes the proof. $\square$

## Consequences

**Conjugacy is an equivalence relation.** Since conjugation by $g$ is an automorphism of $G$, all Sylow $p$-subgroups are pairwise isomorphic as abstract groups. In a group of order $p^a m$, you cannot have Sylow $p$-subgroups of different abstract structure.

**Normality criterion.** A Sylow $p$-subgroup $P$ is normal in $G$ if and only if $n_p = 1$. Indeed, $P \trianglelefteq G$ means $gPg^{-1} = P$ for all $g$, so all conjugates of $P$ equal $P$, leaving $P$ as the only Sylow $p$-subgroup. Conversely, if $n_p = 1$, the unique Sylow $p$-subgroup is fixed by all conjugation, hence normal.

**Containment of $p$-subgroups.** Sylow II implies something stronger: *every $p$-subgroup of $G$ is contained in some Sylow $p$-subgroup*. The proof is analogous: if $H$ is any $p$-subgroup, act with $H$ on $G/P$; a fixed point gives $xPx^{-1} \supseteq H$.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1: $G = S_4$, $p = 2$.** We have $|S_4| = 24 = 2^3 \cdot 3$, so Sylow $2$-subgroups have order $8$. The Sylow $2$-subgroups of $S_4$ are the three subgroups isomorphic to $D_4$ (dihedral group of order $8$), one for each pair of elements to act on:
$$P_1 = \langle (1234), (13) \rangle, \quad P_2 = \langle (1243), (14) \rangle, \quad P_3 = \langle (1324), (12) \rangle.$$
These three groups are conjugate: for instance, the permutation $(34)$ conjugates $P_1$ to $P_2$. Sylow II guarantees this; we can verify it directly. All three are isomorphic to $D_4$, and none is normal in $S_4$ (since $n_2 = 3 \neq 1$).

**Example 2: $G = A_4$, $p = 2$.** We have $|A_4| = 12 = 2^2 \cdot 3$, so Sylow $2$-subgroups have order $4$. It turns out $n_2 = 1$: the unique Sylow $2$-subgroup is
$$V_4 = \{e, (12)(34), (13)(24), (14)(23)\} \trianglelefteq A_4,$$
the Klein four-group. Any other subgroup of order $4$ would have to be conjugate to $V_4$, so it would also equal $V_4$. This is a case where Sylow II gives a strong conclusion: the unique Sylow $2$-subgroup is automatically normal.

**Example 3: $G = \mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{F}_3)$, $p = 2$.** We have $|G| = (9-1)(9-3) = 8 \cdot 6 = 48 = 2^4 \cdot 3$, so Sylow $2$-subgroups have order $16$. There is one conjugacy class of Sylow $2$-subgroups, each isomorphic to the generalized quaternion group $Q_{16}$. Sylow II tells us these groups — all maximal $2$-power subgroups of $G$ — are conjugate within $G$, hence isomorphic as abstract groups.

## Connection to Sylow III

Sylow II says all Sylow $p$-subgroups are conjugate. Sylow III says their number $n_p$ satisfies $n_p \mid m$ and $n_p \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$. These two theorems interact: the orbit of $P$ under conjugation by $G$ has exactly $n_p$ elements (since conjugate subgroups are exactly the Sylow subgroups, by Sylow II), and the stabilizer of $P$ under conjugation is $N_G(P)$ (the normalizer). The orbit-stabilizer theorem gives:
$$n_p = [G : N_G(P)].$$
This connects the number of Sylow subgroups to the size of the normalizer: the larger the normalizer, the fewer Sylow subgroups. When $P \trianglelefteq G$, we have $N_G(P) = G$ and $n_p = 1$, consistent with the normality criterion above.

## Theoretical Significance

Sylow II is the key ingredient in showing that certain groups cannot be simple. If $n_p = 1$, the unique Sylow $p$-subgroup is normal, giving a nontrivial proper normal subgroup (provided the group has order $> p^a$). The simplicity obstruction from Sylow theory is entirely based on showing $n_p = 1$ is forced by Sylow III's constraints, and Sylow II then delivers the normal subgroup.

For example, every group of order $p^2$ for a prime $p$ has $n_p = 1$ (the whole group is a $p$-group and is its own Sylow subgroup). More substantially: for a group of order $pq$ with primes $p < q$ and $p \nmid q-1$, both $n_p = 1$ and $n_q = 1$ are forced, and the group must be cyclic.

The theorem also places constraints on how normal subgroups intersect Sylow subgroups: if $N \trianglelefteq G$ and $P \in \mathrm{Syl}_p(G)$, then $N \cap P \in \mathrm{Syl}_p(N)$ and $NP/N \in \mathrm{Syl}_p(G/N)$ — a pair of statements that require Sylow II to state and Sylow III to count, and that are essential in the Schur-Zassenhaus theorem, Sylow's most powerful extension.
