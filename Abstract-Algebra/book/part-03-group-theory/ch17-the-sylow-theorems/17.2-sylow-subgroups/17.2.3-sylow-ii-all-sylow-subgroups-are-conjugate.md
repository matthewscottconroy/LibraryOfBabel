# 17.2.3 Sylow II: All Sylow $p$-Subgroups Are Conjugate

The first theorem guarantees existence; the second theorem reveals a remarkable uniformity among all Sylow $p$-subgroups.

\begin{theorem}[Sylow II]
Let $G$ be a finite group,$p$ a prime, and$P, Q \in \mathrm{Syl}_p(G)$. Then$Q = gPg^{-1}$ for some$g \in G$. In other words,$G$ acts transitively on$\mathrm{Syl}_p(G)$ by conjugation, so all Sylow$p$-subgroups form a single conjugacy class of subgroups.
\end{theorem}

Before proving this, appreciate why it is striking. In a group of order $12 = 2^2 \cdot 3$, there might be one or four Sylow$2$-subgroups of order$4$. The theorem says that if there are four of them, any one can be obtained from any other by conjugation by some element of the group. The four subgroups are not four independent copies — they are, from$G$'s perspective, completely interchangeable.

\begin{proof}
Let $P \in \mathrm{Syl}_p(G)$ and let$P$ act on$\mathrm{Syl}_p(G)$ by conjugation:$x \cdot Q = xQx^{-1}$ for$x \in P$,$Q \in \mathrm{Syl}_p(G)$. We identify the fixed points of this action, i.e., the$Q$ with$xQx^{-1} = Q$ for all$x \in P$. This condition means$P \leq N_G(Q)$, so$PQ$ is a subgroup of$G$. We claim$P = Q$.

Since $Q \trianglelefteq N_G(Q)$ and$P \leq N_G(Q)$, the product$PQ$ is a subgroup. We have$|PQ| = |P||Q|/|P \cap Q|$, and$PQ \leq G$, so$|PQ| \mid |G| = p^a m$. Since$|P| = |Q| = p^a$ and$|PQ|$ is a power of$p$ (as$PQ$ is a$p$-group contained in$G$), we get$|PQ| = p^k$ for some$k$. But$p^a \mid |PQ|$ (as$P \leq PQ$), so$|PQ| = p^a$ and thus$PQ = P = Q$.

Therefore $P$ is the *only* fixed point of the action of$P$ on$\mathrm{Syl}_p(G)$. Since$|P| = p^a$ is a power of$p$, the number of elements in each non-trivial orbit is divisible by$p$. The total number of Sylow subgroups satisfies$n_p \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$ (the one fixed point, plus orbits of size divisible by$p$). Moreover, all Sylow subgroups not equal to$P$ lie in non-fixed orbits of$P$, hence lie in the orbit of$P$ under the full$G$-action (by the argument above applied to any two Sylow subgroups). This shows$G$ acts transitively.
\end{proof}

The consequence of conjugacy is both structural and practical. Structurally, it means all Sylow $p$-subgroups are isomorphic: they are literally conjugate, hence isomorphic as groups. There is no "preferred" Sylow$p$-subgroup. Practically, any property of a Sylow$p$-subgroup that is invariant under isomorphism — being abelian, cyclic, having a certain number of elements of a given order — is automatically shared by all Sylow$p$-subgroups.

More concretely: a Sylow $p$-subgroup$P$ is normal in$G$ if and only if$gPg^{-1} = P$ for all$g \in G$. By Sylow II, the only way this holds is if$P$ is the *unique* Sylow$p$-subgroup, i.e.,$n_p = 1$. This is a key criterion: *a Sylow$p$-subgroup is normal if and only if it is unique*.

**Example 1.** In $S_3$ (order$6 = 2 \cdot 3$), the Sylow$3$-subgroups are the subgroups of order$3$. There is only one such subgroup, namely$A_3 = \{e, (123), (132)\}$. This must be normal (as the unique Sylow$3$-subgroup), which we can verify:$A_3 \trianglelefteq S_3$ because it has index$2$. Now consider the Sylow$2$-subgroups, which have order$2$: they are$\{e, (12)\}$,$\{e, (13)\}$,$\{e, (23)\}$. There are three of them, and$n_2 = 3 \equiv 1 \pmod{2}$ (consistent with Sylow III). Any two are conjugate:$(123)\{e,(12)\}(132) = \{e, (13)\}$, etc.

**Example 2.** In $A_4$ (order$12 = 2^2 \cdot 3$), the Sylow$2$-subgroups have order$4$. There is exactly one subgroup of order$4$ in$A_4$, namely the Klein four-group$V = \{e, (12)(34), (13)(24), (14)(23)\}$. This is normal in$A_4$ (it is the unique Sylow$2$-subgroup). Meanwhile, there are four Sylow$3$-subgroups, generated by the four$3$-cycles. By Sylow II, each pair of these is conjugate, which one can verify:$(12)(34)\langle(123)\rangle(12)(34) = \langle(134)\rangle$, etc.

**Example 3.** In $G = \mathrm{GL}(2, \mathbb{F}_5)$, which has order$(25-1)(25-5) = 24 \cdot 20 = 480 = 2^5 \cdot 3 \cdot 5$, the Sylow$5$-subgroups have order$5$. Two matrices of order$5$ in$\mathrm{GL}(2, \mathbb{F}_5)$ are conjugate if and only if they have the same minimal polynomial (by rational canonical form theory). Since there are several irreducible quadratics over$\mathbb{F}_5$ with roots of order$5$, there are multiple conjugacy classes of such matrices, but Sylow II guarantees all Sylow$5$-subgroups (as subgroups, not individual elements) are conjugate.

**Example 4.** Let $G$ be any group with$|G| = p$ (prime). Then the only Sylow$p$-subgroup is$G$ itself. The uniqueness is trivial, and normality is automatic since$G \trianglelefteq G$. Sylow II is vacuously satisfied: "all one Sylow subgroup is conjugate to itself" is trivially true.

**Example 5.** The conjugacy of Sylow subgroups has important consequences for the structure of group homomorphisms. If $\phi: G \to H$ is a surjective homomorphism and$P \in \mathrm{Syl}_p(G)$, then$\phi(P)$ is a$p$-subgroup of$H$. It need not be a Sylow$p$-subgroup of$H$ (since$|H|$ may have a smaller$p$-part than$|G|$), but if$|\phi(P)| = p^b$ where$p^b \| |H|$, then$\phi(P)$ is a Sylow$p$-subgroup of$H$. Moreover, as$P$ ranges over$\mathrm{Syl}_p(G)$, the images$\phi(P)$ are all conjugate in$H$ (since$\phi(gPg^{-1}) = \phi(g)\phi(P)\phi(g)^{-1}$), confirming that Sylow II is compatible with homomorphisms.
