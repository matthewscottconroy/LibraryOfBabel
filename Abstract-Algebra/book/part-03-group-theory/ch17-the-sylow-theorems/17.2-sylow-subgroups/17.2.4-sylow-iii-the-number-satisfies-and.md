# 17.2.4 Sylow III: The Number $n_p$ Satisfies$n_p \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$ and$n_p \mid m$

The third Sylow theorem places sharp arithmetic constraints on the number of Sylow $p$-subgroups, turning what might otherwise be a completely open question into a manageable finite set of possibilities.

\begin{theorem}[Sylow III]
Let $G$ be a finite group with$|G| = p^a m$,$\gcd(p, m) = 1$, and let$n_p = |\mathrm{Syl}_p(G)|$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $n_p \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$.
\item $n_p \mid m$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

These two conditions together are remarkably powerful. The congruence $n_p \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$ comes directly from the Sylow II proof:$P$ acts on$\mathrm{Syl}_p(G)$ by conjugation with exactly one fixed point (namely$P$ itself), so$n_p \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$. The divisibility$n_p \mid m$ has a clean explanation via normalizers.

\begin{proof}[Proof of $n_p \mid m$]
By Sylow II, $G$ acts transitively on$\mathrm{Syl}_p(G)$ by conjugation. Therefore$n_p = [G : \mathrm{Stab}(P)]$ for any fixed$P \in \mathrm{Syl}_p(G)$, and the stabilizer is precisely the normalizer$N_G(P) = \{g \in G : gPg^{-1} = P\}$. By Lagrange's theorem,$n_p = [G : N_G(P)]$ divides$|G| = p^a m$. Since$P \leq N_G(P)$, we have$p^a \mid |N_G(P)|$, hence$n_p = |G|/|N_G(P)|$ satisfies$n_p \mid m$.
\end{proof}

Together, the two constraints $n_p \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$ and$n_p \mid m$ often leave only a very small set of possibilities for$n_p$. The standard template for applying Sylow III is:

1. List all divisors of $m$.
2. Among those divisors, retain only those congruent to $1 \pmod{p}$.
3. The actual value of $n_p$ is one of the remaining candidates.

If only one candidate remains, we have $n_p$ uniquely, and if it equals$1$, the unique Sylow$p$-subgroup is normal.

The interplay between the two constraints is where the power lies. The divisibility condition eliminates large candidate values; the congruence condition eliminates values that are multiples of $p$. Together they can squeeze$n_p$ to be$1$ in many cases of interest, giving us a normal Sylow subgroup "for free."

**Example 1.** Let $|G| = 30 = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5$. For$p = 5$:$a = 1$,$m = 6$. We need$n_5 \mid 6$ and$n_5 \equiv 1 \pmod{5}$. Divisors of$6$:$1, 2, 3, 6$. Which are$\equiv 1 \pmod{5}$? Only$1$ and$6$. So$n_5 \in \{1, 6\}$. If$n_5 = 6$, there are$6 \cdot 4 = 24$ elements of order$5$. For$p = 3$:$n_3 \mid 10$ and$n_3 \equiv 1 \pmod{3}$, so$n_3 \in \{1, 10\}$. If$n_3 = 10$, there are$20$ elements of order$3$. But$24 + 20 = 44 > 30$, a contradiction. So at least one of$n_3, n_5$ must equal$1$. In fact, a more careful argument shows both must equal$1$, so$G$ has normal subgroups of orders$3$ and$5$, and$G \cong \mathbb{Z}/30\mathbb{Z}$ or a related semidirect product.

**Example 2.** Let $|G| = 56 = 2^3 \cdot 7$. For$p = 7$:$n_7 \mid 8$ and$n_7 \equiv 1 \pmod{7}$, so$n_7 \in \{1, 8\}$. For$p = 2$:$n_2 \mid 7$ and$n_2 \equiv 1 \pmod{2}$, so$n_2 \in \{1, 7\}$. Suppose$n_7 = 8$. Then there are$8 \cdot 6 = 48$ elements of order$7$. The remaining$56 - 48 = 8$ elements must form a single Sylow$2$-subgroup (since the Sylow$2$-subgroup has order$8$ and the$48$ elements of order$7$ account for everything else). So$n_2 = 1$ and the Sylow$2$-subgroup is normal. Either way ($n_7 = 1$ or$n_2 = 1$), there is a normal Sylow subgroup, so$G$ is not simple.

**Example 3.** Let $|G| = 48 = 2^4 \cdot 3$. For$p = 2$:$n_2 \mid 3$ and$n_2 \equiv 1 \pmod{2}$, so$n_2 \in \{1, 3\}$. For$p = 3$:$n_3 \mid 16$ and$n_3 \equiv 1 \pmod{3}$, so$n_3 \in \{1, 4, 16\}$. If$n_2 = 3$, the three Sylow$2$-subgroups of order$16$ together contain at least$3 \cdot 1 = 3$ elements (at minimum the identity in each), but their union can be large. The constraint does not immediately rule out any case, and indeed groups of order$48$ are not all simple — but the argument requires a bit more work than Sylow III alone.

**Example 4.** Let $|G| = pq$ with$p < q$ both prime. For the Sylow$q$-subgroup:$n_q \mid p$ and$n_q \equiv 1 \pmod{q}$. The divisors of$p$ are$1$ and$p$. Since$p < q$,$p \not\equiv 1 \pmod{q}$ (as$p < q$ means$p - 1 < q - 1 < q$, so$p \not\equiv 1 \pmod{q}$ unless$p = 1$, which is impossible). Therefore$n_q = 1$, and the Sylow$q$-subgroup is always normal in a group of order$pq$.

**Example 5.** Sylow III gives $n_p \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$ and$n_p \mid m$. A common mistake is to overlook that$n_p \mid m$ (not$n_p \mid |G|$). For$|G| = 60 = 2^2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5$ and$p = 2$:$m = 15$, so$n_2 \mid 15$ and$n_2 \equiv 1 \pmod{2}$. Divisors of$15$:$1, 3, 5, 15$; all are odd, so all satisfy$n_2 \equiv 1 \pmod{2}$. Therefore$n_2 \in \{1, 3, 5, 15\}$. For$A_5$, one can verify$n_2 = 5$: the five Sylow$2$-subgroups of$A_5$ are the stabilizers of the five vertices of an icosahedron (under the standard geometric realization of$A_5$ as icosahedral symmetries).
