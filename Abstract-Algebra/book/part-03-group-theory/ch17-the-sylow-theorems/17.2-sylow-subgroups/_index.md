# 17.2 Sylow Subgroups

Let $G$ be a finite group of order $n = p^a m$ where $p$ is prime and $\gcd(p, m) = 1$. A Sylow $p$-subgroup of $G$ is a subgroup of order exactly $p^a$ — the largest possible prime-power order at the prime $p$. Lagrange's theorem guarantees that any subgroup has order dividing $|G|$, but it gives no information about which orders actually occur. The first Sylow theorem fills this gap: every finite group has at least one Sylow $p$-subgroup for each prime $p$ dividing its order.

The standard proof of Sylow I proceeds via a group action. Consider the action of $G$ by left multiplication on the set $\mathcal{X}$ of all subsets of $G$ of size $p^a$. The number $|\mathcal{X}| = \binom{p^a m}{p^a}$ is not divisible by $p$ (a combinatorial fact provable by expanding the binomial coefficient and tracking $p$-adic valuations). Since the orbits of the action partition $\mathcal{X}$, at least one orbit must have size not divisible by $p$. By the orbit-stabilizer theorem, the size of this orbit equals $|G|$ divided by the stabilizer of some set $S$ in that orbit; if the orbit size is not divisible by $p$, then the stabilizer has order divisible by $p^a$, and a Sylow subgroup is extracted. This proof is elegant because it reduces existence to a counting argument.

Sylow II states that any two Sylow $p$-subgroups are conjugate: if $P$ and $Q$ are both Sylow $p$-subgroups, then $Q = gPg^{-1}$ for some $g \in G$. This is proved by letting $P$ act by conjugation on the set of Sylow $p$-subgroups and showing that $Q$ lies in the orbit of $P$. The conjugacy implies in particular that any Sylow $p$-subgroup is normal if and only if it is the unique Sylow $p$-subgroup. Sylow III constrains the number $n_p$ of Sylow $p$-subgroups: it must divide $m = |G|/p^a$ and satisfy $n_p \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$. The divisibility condition comes from the orbit-stabilizer theorem; the congruence condition is a subtler counting argument involving the action of a Sylow subgroup on the set of all Sylow subgroups. These two conditions together are often the key tool for classifying groups of specific orders or proving non-simplicity.
