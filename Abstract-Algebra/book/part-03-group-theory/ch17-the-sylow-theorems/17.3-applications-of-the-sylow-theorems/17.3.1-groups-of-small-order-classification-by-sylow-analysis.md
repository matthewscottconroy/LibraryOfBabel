# 17.3.1 Groups of Small Order: Classification by Sylow Analysis

The Sylow theorems transform the classification of finite groups of small order from an ad hoc collection of puzzles into a systematic procedure. Before Sylow's work, determining the groups of a given order required ingenuity specific to that order. With the three theorems in hand, we have a uniform algorithm: apply Sylow III to determine the possible values of $n_p$ for each prime, use element counting or normality arguments to eliminate cases, and assemble the surviving cases as products or semidirect products.

The strategy in outline: for a group $G$ of order$n = p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_k^{a_k}$, for each prime$p_i$, Sylow III restricts$n_{p_i}$ to a finite list. If$n_{p_i} = 1$ for some$i$, the unique Sylow$p_i$-subgroup$P_i$ is normal in$G$. If several Sylow subgroups are forced to be normal,$G$ is often a direct product$P_1 \times \cdots \times P_k$, or a semidirect product, and the classification reduces to understanding the smaller groups$P_i$ and the possible actions of$P_j$ on$P_i$. If no$n_{p_i} = 1$ is forced directly, element counting is the next tool: if distinct Sylow subgroups contribute disjoint sets of elements of particular orders, one can count elements and show the set is "too large" to accommodate more than one Sylow subgroup of a second prime.

Let us work through groups of order $12 = 2^2 \cdot 3$ in detail. The constraints are:
- $n_3 \mid 4$ and$n_3 \equiv 1 \pmod{3}$: so$n_3 \in \{1, 4\}$.
- $n_2 \mid 3$ and$n_2 \equiv 1 \pmod{2}$: so$n_2 \in \{1, 3\}$.

**Case $n_3 = 4$.** There are$4$ Sylow$3$-subgroups, each of order$3$ and cyclic, contributing$4 \cdot 2 = 8$ elements of order$3$. The remaining$12 - 8 = 4$ elements must include the identity plus$3$ more elements forming the unique Sylow$2$-subgroup (since only$4$ elements remain and the Sylow$2$-subgroup has order$4$). So$n_2 = 1$, and the unique Sylow$2$-subgroup$P_2 \cong \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ or$P_2 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is normal. This gives two possible internal structures, leading to$A_4$ (with$P_2 = V_4$) and$D_6$ or$\mathrm{Dic}_3$ depending on the action.

**Case $n_3 = 1$.** The unique Sylow$3$-subgroup$Q \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ is normal. Then$G$ is an extension of$Q$ by a group of order$4$, and the two choices for$P_2$ give further cases. Working through all possible actions of$P_2$ on$Q$ yields the remaining groups of order$12$.

The complete list of groups of order $12$ is:$\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$,$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$,$A_4$,$D_6$ (dihedral of order$12$), and the dicyclic group$\mathrm{Dic}_3$. Each arises from one of the cases above.

This process extends to all orders up to $15$ (excluding$16 = 2^4$, where the entire group is a$2$-group and Sylow theory provides no constraint). The result in each case is a complete, systematic classification using a uniform framework.

**Example 1.** Groups of order $6 = 2 \cdot 3$. We have$n_3 \mid 2$ and$n_3 \equiv 1 \pmod{3}$, so$n_3 = 1$. The unique Sylow$3$-subgroup$Q = \langle b \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ is normal. Let$P = \langle a \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ be any Sylow$2$-subgroup. Then$G = P Q$ (since$|PQ| = 6/|P \cap Q| = 6$). The action of$a$ on$Q$ by conjugation is a homomorphism$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to \mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. The trivial action gives$G \cong \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$; the nontrivial action (with$aba^{-1} = b^{-1}$) gives$G \cong S_3$. These are the only two groups of order$6$.

**Example 2.** Groups of order $15 = 3 \cdot 5$. Here$n_5 \mid 3$ and$n_5 \equiv 1 \pmod{5}$, so$n_5 = 1$. Also$n_3 \mid 5$ and$n_3 \equiv 1 \pmod{3}$, so$n_3 = 1$. Both Sylow subgroups are normal and cyclic of coprime orders, so$G \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/15\mathbb{Z}$. There is exactly one group of order$15$.

**Example 3.** Groups of order $21 = 3 \cdot 7$. We have$n_7 \mid 3$ and$n_7 \equiv 1 \pmod{7}$, so$n_7 = 1$. The Sylow$7$-subgroup$Q \cong \mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z}$ is normal. For$n_3$:$n_3 \mid 7$ and$n_3 \equiv 1 \pmod{3}$, so$n_3 \in \{1, 7\}$. If$n_3 = 1$, then$G \cong \mathbb{Z}/21\mathbb{Z}$. If$n_3 = 7$, there are$7 \cdot 2 = 14$ elements of order$3$ and$7 - 1 = 6$ elements of order$7$, filling all$21 = 1 + 14 + 6$ elements. Since$3 \mid 7 - 1 = 6$, there exists a nontrivial homomorphism$\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z} \to \mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, and the corresponding semidirect product$\mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z} \rtimes \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ is non-abelian of order$21$. There are exactly two groups of order$21$.

**Example 4.** Groups of order $8 = 2^3$. Here the entire group is a$2$-group. Sylow theory gives no information (there is only one Sylow$2$-subgroup, which is$G$ itself). Classification must proceed by other methods — center theorem, quotient arguments — and yields five groups:$\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$,$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$,$(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^3$,$D_4$, and$Q_8$. This illustrates the limit of Sylow theory: at prime-power orders, it provides no constraints.

**Example 5.** Groups of order $10 = 2 \cdot 5$. We have$n_5 \mid 2$ and$n_5 \equiv 1 \pmod{5}$, so$n_5 = 1$. The Sylow$5$-subgroup$Q \cong \mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$ is normal. The Sylow$2$-subgroup$P \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ acts on$Q$ by a homomorphism$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to \mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$. The trivial action gives$\mathbb{Z}/10\mathbb{Z}$; the unique nontrivial action (via inversion$b \mapsto b^{-1} = b^4$) gives$D_5$, the dihedral group of the pentagon. Since$|\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z})| = 4$ and$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ maps to the unique element of order$2$, these are the only two options.
