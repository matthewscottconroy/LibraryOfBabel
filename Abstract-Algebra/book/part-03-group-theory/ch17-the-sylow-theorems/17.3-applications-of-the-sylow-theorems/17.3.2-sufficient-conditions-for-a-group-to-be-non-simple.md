# 17.3.2 Sufficient Conditions for a Group to Be Non-Simple

Recall that a group $G$ is *simple* if$|G| > 1$ and$G$ has no proper nontrivial normal subgroups. Proving that a specific group is not simple — that it must possess a nontrivial proper normal subgroup — is often the first step in any structural analysis. The Sylow theorems provide the cleanest general technique for establishing non-simplicity, and this technique applies to a wide range of group orders.

The fundamental criterion is: if $n_p = 1$ for any prime$p$ dividing$|G|$, then the unique Sylow$p$-subgroup is normal in$G$. This follows directly from Sylow II: since all Sylow$p$-subgroups are conjugate, if there is only one, it must equal all of its conjugates, hence it is normal. And since a Sylow$p$-subgroup is a proper subgroup when$p^a < |G|$ (i.e., when$m > 1$), the group$G$ is not simple.

The most direct sufficient condition for non-simplicity is: *if $|G| = p^a m$ with$m > 1$ and the only positive integer satisfying$n \mid m$ and$n \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$ is$n = 1$, then$n_p = 1$ and$G$ is not simple*. This condition is satisfied, for instance, when$m < p$: the only divisor of$m$ that can be$\equiv 1 \pmod{p}$ is$1$ itself, since all positive divisors of$m$ are at most$m < p \leq p + 1$.

A more nuanced argument combines the two constraints from Sylow III with element counting. If $n_p$ Sylow$p$-subgroups each contribute a certain number of elements not shared with other Sylow subgroups, and this count exhausts almost all of$|G|$, then a second prime's Sylow subgroup is forced to be unique. The counting method is especially effective when the Sylow subgroups for different primes intersect trivially (as happens when they have coprime or prime orders).

\begin{proposition}
If $|G| = p^a m$ with$p > m$, then$n_p = 1$, so$G$ has a normal Sylow$p$-subgroup and is not simple (provided$|G| > p^a$, i.e.,$m > 1$).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $n_p \mid m$ and$n_p \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$, and the only positive divisor of$m$ that is$\equiv 1 \pmod{p}$ when$m < p$ is$1$ (since all positive divisors of$m$ are at most$m < p$), we have$n_p = 1$.
\end{proof}

The Sylow non-simplicity criterion is the standard first step in any group classification argument. The cases where it fails — where $n_p > 1$ for all primes$p$ — include the actual finite simple groups, which require considerably deeper tools for their analysis.

**Example 1.** Let $|G| = 45 = 3^2 \cdot 5$. For$p = 5$:$n_5 \mid 9$ and$n_5 \equiv 1 \pmod{5}$. Divisors of$9$ are$1, 3, 9$; only$1 \equiv 1 \pmod{5}$ among these (since$3 \not\equiv 1 \pmod 5$ and$9 \not\equiv 1 \pmod 5$). So$n_5 = 1$, the Sylow$5$-subgroup is normal, and$G$ is not simple. Here$m = 5 < p^a = 9$ but more precisely$m = 5$ and the unique divisor of$9$ congruent to$1 \pmod{5}$ is$1$.

**Example 2.** Let $|G| = 36 = 2^2 \cdot 3^2$. For$p = 3$:$n_3 \mid 4$ and$n_3 \equiv 1 \pmod{3}$, so$n_3 \in \{1, 4\}$. For$p = 2$:$n_2 \mid 9$ and$n_2 \equiv 1 \pmod{2}$, so$n_2 \in \{1, 3, 9\}$. If$n_3 = 4$, there are$4 \cdot (9 - 3) = 24$ elements of order$3$ or$9$ (from the four Sylow$3$-subgroups of order$9$, each with$8$ non-identity elements, but possible intersections complicate this). In fact, if two distinct Sylow$3$-subgroups intersect in a group of order$3$, the four Sylow$3$-subgroups contribute at most$4 \cdot 8 - 3 \cdot 1 = 29$ non-identity elements, but this is already more than$36 - 1 = 35$ when counted naively — so at least some Sylow$3$-subgroups must share non-identity elements. The argument requires care, but ultimately one can show$G$ is not simple.

**Example 3.** Let $|G| = 56 = 2^3 \cdot 7$. For$p = 7$:$n_7 \mid 8$ and$n_7 \equiv 1 \pmod{7}$, so$n_7 \in \{1, 8\}$. For$p = 2$:$n_2 \mid 7$ and$n_2 \equiv 1 \pmod{2}$, so$n_2 \in \{1, 7\}$. Suppose$n_7 = 8$. Each Sylow$7$-subgroup has order$7$ (prime), so any two distinct ones intersect only in$\{e\}$. Thus the$8$ Sylow$7$-subgroups contribute$8 \cdot 6 = 48$ distinct elements of order$7$. The remaining$56 - 48 = 8$ elements must include all of the Sylow$2$-subgroup (which has order$8$), so$n_2 = 1$ and the unique Sylow$2$-subgroup is normal. In either case ($n_7 = 1$ or$n_2 = 1$),$G$ has a normal proper nontrivial subgroup, so no group of order$56$ is simple.

**Example 4.** Let $|G| = 100 = 2^2 \cdot 5^2$. For$p = 5$:$n_5 \mid 4$ and$n_5 \equiv 1 \pmod{5}$, so$n_5 = 1$. (The divisors of$4$ are$1, 2, 4$; none of$2$ or$4$ is$\equiv 1 \pmod{5}$.) So the unique Sylow$5$-subgroup of order$25$ is normal, and no group of order$100$ is simple. This illustrates the proposition above:$m = 4 < 5 = p$.

**Example 5.** The Sylow embedding technique (Section 17.3.4) handles cases where Sylow III alone does not force $n_p = 1$. For$|G| = 60$:$n_5 \in \{1, 6\}$ and$n_2 \in \{1, 3, 5, 15\}$. Neither constraint alone forces a Sylow subgroup to be normal. If$G$ is simple,$n_5 = 6$ and$n_2 \geq 3$. The conjugation action on the$6$ Sylow$5$-subgroups embeds$G \hookrightarrow S_6$. Further analysis using the fact that$60 \nmid |S_5| = 120$ forces$G \hookrightarrow A_6$, and ultimately$G \cong A_5$ is the unique simple group of order$60$. Simplicity here requires a positive proof, not just ruling out normality.
