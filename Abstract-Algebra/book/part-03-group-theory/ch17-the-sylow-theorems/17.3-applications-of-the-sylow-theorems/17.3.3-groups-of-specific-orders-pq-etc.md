# 17.3.3 Groups of Specific Orders ($pq$,$p^2 q$, etc.)

The most instructive applications of the Sylow theorems come from working out the complete classification for families of group orders defined by their prime factorization structure. Rather than treating each order separately, we identify patterns that persist across all orders of a given shape. These results are theorems in their own right and serve as prototypical Sylow arguments that every mathematician should internalize.

**Groups of order $pq$ ($p < q$ distinct primes).**

\begin{theorem}
Let $G$ be a group of order$pq$ with$p < q$ both prime. Then the Sylow$q$-subgroup is always normal. If$p \nmid q - 1$, then$G \cong \mathbb{Z}/pq\mathbb{Z}$ is cyclic. If$p \mid q - 1$, there is, up to isomorphism, exactly one additional group: the nonabelian semidirect product$\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z} \rtimes \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The constraint $n_q \mid p$ and$n_q \equiv 1 \pmod{q}$ gives$n_q \in \{1, p\}$. Since$p < q$, we have$p \not\equiv 1 \pmod{q}$ (any positive integer$\leq p < q$ can only be$\equiv 1 \pmod q$ if it equals$1$, since$1$ is the only positive integer$< q$ with that property and$p$ can only equal$1$ if$p$ is not prime). So$n_q = 1$, and the unique Sylow$q$-subgroup$Q \cong \mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}$ is normal.

Let $P \cong \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ be any Sylow$p$-subgroup. Since$Q \trianglelefteq G$ and$P \cap Q = \{e\}$ (as$|P \cap Q|$ must divide$\gcd(p, q) = 1$), we have$G = PQ$ with$P \cap Q = \{e\}$, so$G$ is a semidirect product$\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z} \rtimes_\phi \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ where$\phi: \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} \to \mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/(q-1)\mathbb{Z}$ is a group homomorphism. The homomorphisms from$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ to$\mathbb{Z}/(q-1)\mathbb{Z}$ correspond to elements of order dividing$p$ in$\mathbb{Z}/(q-1)\mathbb{Z}$. Since$\mathbb{Z}/(q-1)\mathbb{Z}$ is cyclic, there is a subgroup of order$p$ if and only if$p \mid q - 1$. If$p \nmid q-1$, the only homomorphism is trivial, giving$G \cong \mathbb{Z}/pq\mathbb{Z}$. If$p \mid q-1$, there are$p-1$ nontrivial homomorphisms, all yielding isomorphic semidirect products (they differ by an automorphism of$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$), so there is exactly one nonabelian group of order$pq$.
\end{proof}

**Groups of order $p^2 q$ ($p < q$ distinct primes).**

Here the analysis is more involved but follows the same logic. We have:
- $n_q \mid p^2$ and$n_q \equiv 1 \pmod{q}$: so$n_q \in \{1, p, p^2\}$ restricted to values$\equiv 1 \pmod{q}$.
- $n_p \mid q$ and$n_p \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$: so$n_p \in \{1, q\}$.

In many cases, at least one of these is forced to equal $1$ by element counting. The complete classification depends on the relationship between$p$,$q$, and divisors of$p^2 - 1$ and$q - 1$. For example, at order$12 = 2^2 \cdot 3$, the five groups arise from the different possibilities for$n_2$ and$n_3$ and the different actions of one Sylow subgroup on the other.

**Example 1.** $|G| = 6 = 2 \cdot 3$. By the theorem above ($p = 2, q = 3$):$n_3 = 1$, and since$2 \mid 3 - 1 = 2$, there is a nonabelian group. The two groups of order$6$ are$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ and$S_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z} \rtimes \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. In$S_3$, the generator of$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ acts on$\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ by inversion: if$b$ generates$\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ and$a$ generates$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, then$aba^{-1} = b^{-1}$.

**Example 2.** $|G| = 15 = 3 \cdot 5$. Here$p = 3, q = 5$:$n_5 \mid 3$ and$n_5 \equiv 1 \pmod{5}$, so$n_5 = 1$. Since$3 \nmid 5 - 1 = 4$ (as$3 \nmid 4$), the only group of order$15$ is$\mathbb{Z}/15\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 3.** $|G| = 35 = 5 \cdot 7$. Here$p = 5, q = 7$:$n_7 = 1$ always (since$5 < 7$ forces$n_7 = 1$). For$n_5$:$n_5 \mid 7$ and$n_5 \equiv 1 \pmod{5}$, so$n_5 \in \{1, 7\}$. Since$5 \nmid 7 - 1 = 6$, we get$n_5 = 1$. Both Sylow subgroups are normal and the group is$\mathbb{Z}/35\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 4.** $|G| = 77 = 7 \cdot 11$. Here$p = 7, q = 11$:$n_{11} = 1$ (since$7 < 11$). For$n_7$:$n_7 \mid 11$ and$n_7 \equiv 1 \pmod{7}$, so$n_7 \in \{1, 11\}$. Is$7 \mid 11 - 1 = 10$? No, so$n_7 = 1$ and$G \cong \mathbb{Z}/77\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 5.** $|G| = 2p$ for an odd prime$p$. We have$n_p \mid 2$ and$n_p \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$, so$n_p \in \{1, 2\}$. Since$2 < p$ (as$p$ is an odd prime$\geq 3$), we have$2 \not\equiv 1 \pmod{p}$ for$p \geq 3$, so$n_p = 1$. The unique Sylow$p$-subgroup$Q \cong \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ is normal. Since$2 \mid p - 1$ always (as$p$ is odd,$p - 1$ is even), there is always a nontrivial action of$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ on$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ (by inversion). Thus for every odd prime$p$, there are exactly two groups of order$2p$: the cyclic group$\mathbb{Z}/2p\mathbb{Z}$ and the dihedral group$D_p$.
