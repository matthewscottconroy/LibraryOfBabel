# 17.3.4 Embedding $G$ in$S_{n_p}$ via the Conjugation Action

The Sylow theorems do more than produce normal subgroups: they produce group actions, and group actions produce homomorphisms into symmetric groups. This "embedding via conjugation" technique is one of the central tools for studying groups that resist simpler analysis, including the finite simple groups themselves.

The setup is as follows. The group $G$ acts on the set$\mathrm{Syl}_p(G)$ by conjugation:$g \cdot P = gPg^{-1}$. By Sylow II, this action is transitive. The action defines a group homomorphism
$$\varphi: G \longrightarrow \mathrm{Sym}(\mathrm{Syl}_p(G)) \cong S_{n_p}.$$
The kernel of $\varphi$ is the set of elements$g \in G$ such that$gPg^{-1} = P$ for *every* Sylow$p$-subgroup$P$. This is precisely the intersection of all the normalizers:
$$\ker \varphi = \bigcap_{P \in \mathrm{Syl}_p(G)} N_G(P),$$
which is the largest normal subgroup of $G$ contained in every$N_G(P)$. It is called the *$p$-core* of$G$, often written$O_p(G)$ or$\mathrm{Core}_G(N_G(P))$.

If $G$ is simple,$\ker \varphi$ is a normal subgroup of$G$, hence either$\{e\}$ or$G$. If$\ker \varphi = G$, every element normalizes every Sylow$p$-subgroup, meaning every Sylow$p$-subgroup is normal, hence$n_p = 1$ — but that would mean the unique Sylow$p$-subgroup is normal, contradicting simplicity (assuming$1 < p^a < |G|$). Therefore$\ker \varphi = \{e\}$ for a simple group, and$\varphi$ is injective. This gives:

\begin{proposition}
If $G$ is a simple group and$p^a \| |G|$ with$p^a < |G|$, then$G$ embeds into$S_{n_p}$, giving the divisibility constraint$|G| \mid (n_p)!$.
\end{proposition}

This constraint is often dramatically restrictive. For a group of order $|G|$ to embed in$S_{n_p}$, one needs$|G| \mid (n_p)!$. If$n_p$ is small, this forces$|G|$ to divide a small factorial, which can be incompatible with the assumed order of$G$.

Furthermore, since $\varphi(G) \leq S_{n_p}$ and$|G|$ is odd or has various specific properties,$\varphi(G)$ may be forced to lie in$A_{n_p}$: if$|G|$ is odd, then$|G|$ divides$|\varphi(G)|$ and every element of$\varphi(G)$ is an even permutation (by a parity argument), so$\varphi(G) \leq A_{n_p}$ and$|G| \mid (n_p)!/2$.

**Example 1.** Suppose $|G| = 60$ and$G$ is simple. From Sylow III:$n_5 \in \{1, 6\}$. Since$G$ is simple and a normal Sylow$5$-subgroup would violate simplicity,$n_5 = 6$. The conjugation action gives$\varphi: G \to S_6$ with$\ker \varphi = \{e\}$, so$G$ embeds in$S_6$. Since$|G| = 60$ is even but not divisible by$4 \cdot 3 = 12$, further analysis using the sign homomorphism shows$G \leq A_6$. Using the simplicity of$G$ and the fact that$|A_6| = 360 = 6 \cdot |G|$, one can show$G \cong A_5$. This is the key step in the classification of simple groups of order$60$.

**Example 2.** Suppose $|G| = 168$ and$G$ is simple. Then$168 = 2^3 \cdot 3 \cdot 7$. Sylow analysis gives$n_7 \in \{1, 8\}$; since$G$ is simple,$n_7 = 8$. The conjugation action gives$G \hookrightarrow S_8$. But$|G| = 168$ and$|S_7| = 5040 = 30 \cdot 168$, so$G$ could in principle lie in$S_7$ — and indeed the action of$G$ on cosets of a Sylow$7$-subgroup (of index$24$) gives an embedding into$S_{24}$. The group$G = \mathrm{PSL}(2, \mathbb{F}_7)$ is the unique simple group of order$168$, and its simplicity can be established using the embedding technique together with explicit matrix calculations.

**Example 3.** Let $|G| = 120$ with$G$ simple. Then$120 = 2^3 \cdot 3 \cdot 5$ and$n_5 \in \{1, 6\}$. Since$G$ is simple,$n_5 = 6$, giving$G \hookrightarrow S_6$. Now$|G| = 120 = |S_5|$. We get$\varphi(G) \leq S_6$ with$|\varphi(G)| = 120$. One checks that$\varphi(G) \leq A_6$ (since$G$ has no subgroup of index$2$, being simple), and a subgroup of order$120$ in$A_6$ turns out to be isomorphic to$S_5$. But$S_5$ is not simple (it has$A_5$ as a normal subgroup), so no simple group of order$120$ embeds in$A_6$, giving a contradiction. Therefore *no simple group of order$120$ exists*.

**Example 4.** The embedding gives useful structural information even without simplicity. If $G$ is any group of order$pq^2$ (with$p < q$) and$n_q > 1$, then$G \hookrightarrow S_{n_q}$, and the bound$|G| \leq (n_q)!$ constrains the possible values of$n_q$. This kind of argument is the backbone of the classification of groups of many specific small orders.

**Example 5.** The $p$-core$O_p(G) = \bigcap_{P \in \mathrm{Syl}_p(G)} N_G(P)$ is an important invariant beyond its role in the embedding argument. It is the largest normal$p$-subgroup of$G$ (or is trivial). In a group whose Sylow$p$-subgroups intersect nontrivially — for instance, when all Sylow$p$-subgroups are equal (i.e.,$n_p = 1$) — the$p$-core equals the Sylow$p$-subgroup. In general, computing the$p$-core is a first step in the analysis of groups of Lie type and in the modular representation theory of finite groups.
