# 17.3 Applications of the Sylow Theorems

The Sylow theorems are not merely existence and uniqueness results — they are a working toolkit for classifying groups of small order and proving non-simplicity. The procedure is standard: given a group $G$ of order $n = p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_k^{a_k}$, apply the constraints $n_{p_i} \mid n/p_i^{a_i}$ and $n_{p_i} \equiv 1 \pmod{p_i}$ to each prime. When both conditions force $n_{p_i} = 1$, the Sylow $p_i$-subgroup $P_i$ is normal. When all Sylow subgroups are normal and have pairwise trivial intersection, the group decomposes as a direct product of its Sylow subgroups. Even when $n_{p_i} > 1$, element-counting arguments — counting elements of each prime power order and checking whether there is room for all of them — often yield contradictions that force normality.

The applications to groups of specific orders illustrate the method. Groups of order 15: the constraints force $n_3 = 1$ and $n_5 = 1$, so both Sylow subgroups are normal and $G \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/15\mathbb{Z}$. Groups of order 35, 77, and more generally $pq$ with $p < q$ and $p \nmid q-1$: analogously forced to be cyclic. Groups of order 12: more interesting, since $n_3 \in \{1, 4\}$; if $n_3 = 4$ then a counting argument forces a unique Sylow 2-subgroup, leading to $A_4$; if $n_3 = 1$ and the Sylow 2-subgroup is $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ or $V_4$, other groups arise. The complete list of groups of order 12 is $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, $A_4$, $D_6$, and the dicyclic group $\text{Dic}_3$.

The deepest application is embedding $G$ into a symmetric group via a conjugation action. If $P$ is a Sylow $p$-subgroup of $G$, then $G$ acts by conjugation on the set of $n_p$ Sylow $p$-subgroups, giving a homomorphism $G \to S_{n_p}$. The kernel is the largest normal subgroup of $G$ contained in every Sylow $p$-subgroup (the core). If $|G|$ does not divide $n_p!$, this kernel must be non-trivial, so $G$ is not simple. For example, a group of order 48 has $n_2 \in \{1, 3\}$; if $n_2 = 3$, then $G$ maps to $S_3$ with kernel of order at least $48/6 = 8$, providing a normal subgroup. This technique is one of the most powerful in the Sylow toolkit and is used constantly in the proof of the Feit–Thompson theorem and the Classification of Finite Simple Groups.
