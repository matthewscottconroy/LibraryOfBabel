# Chapter 17 — The Sylow Theorems

Among all the theorems in finite group theory, the Sylow theorems stand in a class of their own. In a single 1872 paper, Peter Ludwig Sylow proved three theorems that together give the most precise general structural information about finite groups: every finite group contains subgroups of each prime-power order dividing the full group order, all such maximal prime-power subgroups are conjugate to one another, and the number of such subgroups satisfies sharp divisibility and congruence conditions. These results are at once existence theorems, uniqueness theorems (up to conjugacy), and counting theorems, and their proofs are masterclasses in the use of group actions. With Lagrange's theorem controlling what subgroups can exist by size, the Sylow theorems control what must exist.

The story begins with $p$-groups — groups whose order is a power of the prime $p$. These are the building blocks the Sylow theorems are designed to extract from larger groups. The class equation immediately implies that every non-trivial $p$-group has a non-trivial center: if $|G| = p^n$, then $|Z(G)|$ must be divisible by $p$, so $Z(G) \neq \{e\}$. This forces $p$-groups to be quite constrained — in particular, every group of order $p^2$ is abelian, since if $G/Z(G)$ were cyclic then $G$ would be abelian, and $Z(G)$ cannot have order $p$ without creating that situation. These properties make $p$-groups simultaneously the simplest and most tractable ingredients in any structural decomposition.

The three Sylow theorems concern a group $G$ of order $p^a m$ where $p \nmid m$. A Sylow $p$-subgroup is a subgroup of order exactly $p^a$. Sylow I guarantees at least one exists. The standard proof proceeds via the action of $G$ on the set of all subsets of $G$ of size $p^a$, exploiting the fact that the number of such subsets is not divisible by $p$. Sylow II establishes that any two Sylow $p$-subgroups are conjugate, meaning they are "the same up to perspective" — there is no canonical choice, but they are all equivalent. Sylow III pins down the number $n_p$ of Sylow $p$-subgroups: it divides $m$ and satisfies $n_p \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$. The two conditions together are often severely restrictive: for many group orders, only $n_p = 1$ is consistent, forcing the Sylow subgroup to be normal.

The power of the Sylow theorems lies in their application to classification. To classify groups of a given order $n$, one applies Sylow analysis prime by prime, constraining $n_p$ for each prime $p$ dividing $n$. If $n_p = 1$ for some $p$, the unique Sylow $p$-subgroup is normal, and one can attempt to decompose $G$ as a product or semidirect product. Groups of order 15 must be cyclic; groups of order $pq$ with $p < q$ are either cyclic or a specific metacyclic group depending on whether $p \mid q-1$; groups of order 12, 20, and 30 each admit complete classification by these methods. Beyond explicit examples, Sylow analysis provides obstructions to simplicity: a group $G$ is simple only if $n_p > 1$ for all primes dividing $|G|$, and this condition is so restrictive that ruling out simplicity is often straightforward. The Sylow theorems thus set the agenda for everything that follows in Chapters 18 and 19.
