# 18.1.1 Simple Groups: Definition and Small Examples

In number theory, prime numbers serve as atomic constituents: every positive integer factors uniquely into primes, and the primes cannot themselves be factored further. Finite group theory has a precise analogue of this concept. Understanding a finite group means, at least in part, understanding the irreducible pieces from which it is assembled — and identifying what those pieces are is both one of the deepest questions in mathematics and one of the most celebrated achievements of twentieth-century algebra.

\begin{definition}
A group $G$ is \emph{simple} if$|G| > 1$ and$G$ has no proper nontrivial normal subgroups. Equivalently, the only normal subgroups of$G$ are$\{e\}$ and$G$ itself.
\end{definition}

The condition $|G| > 1$ excludes the trivial group, which would otherwise satisfy the definition vacuously. The condition on normal subgroups (not all subgroups) is crucial: a group can have many proper subgroups while still being simple. The alternating group$A_5$, the smallest nonabelian simple group, has subgroups isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$,$\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$,$\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$,$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$,$D_3 \cong S_3$,$A_4$, and$D_5$, but none of these are normal in$A_5$.

Why does simplicity capture the notion of "atomic"? The answer comes from composition series (Section 18.1.2): every finite group can be decomposed into a chain of subgroups, and the successive quotients in this chain are simple groups. These simple quotients are the group-theoretic primes — the building blocks that cannot be broken down further. The Jordan–Hölder theorem (Section 18.1.3) shows that the multiset of these simple quotients is an invariant of the group, independent of the decomposition chosen. So the simple groups are, in a precise sense, the atoms from which all finite groups are built.

The simplest examples of simple groups are the cyclic groups $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ for primes$p$. These are abelian, and in an abelian group every subgroup is normal (since$gHg^{-1} = H$ when$g$ and$h$ commute). A cyclic group of prime order has no proper nontrivial subgroups at all (by Lagrange's theorem, subgroup orders divide$p$, so subgroups have order$1$ or$p$), hence certainly no proper nontrivial normal subgroups. These are the only abelian simple groups: if$G$ is abelian and simple, every subgroup is normal, so$G$ has no proper nontrivial subgroups at all, which forces$|G|$ to be prime.

For nonabelian simple groups, one must look further. By the center theorem for $p$-groups (Section 17.1.2), no$p$-group of order$p^n$ with$n \geq 2$ is simple (the center is a nontrivial proper normal subgroup). And the Sylow-based non-simplicity arguments of Section 17.3.2 show that most "mixed" orders are also ruled out. The first genuinely nonabelian simple group is$A_5$, of order$60$.

To verify $A_5$ is simple, we compute its conjugacy classes. The permutations in$A_5$ split into five conjugacy classes:$\{e\}$ (size$1$), products of two disjoint transpositions (size$15$),$3$-cycles (size$20$), and two classes of$5$-cycles (sizes$12$ and$12$). A normal subgroup must be a union of conjugacy classes containing$e$, so its order is a sum of elements from$\{1, 15, 20, 12, 12\}$ that includes$1$ and divides$60$. Checking all such sums:$1$,$1+15=16$,$1+20=21$,$1+12=13$,$1+15+20=36$,$1+15+12=28$,$1+20+12=33$,$1+12+12=25$,$1+15+20+12=48$,$1+15+12+12=40$,$1+20+12+12=45$,$1+15+20+12+12=60$. The only sums that divide$60$ are$1$ (giving$\{e\}$) and$60$ (giving$A_5$). Therefore$A_5$ is simple.

Beyond $A_5$, the classification of finite simple groups reveals three infinite families and twenty-six exceptions. The alternating groups$A_n$ for$n \geq 5$ are all simple (proved by a direct argument with$3$-cycles). The groups of Lie type are families of groups built from linear algebra over finite fields: the projective special linear groups$\mathrm{PSL}(n, \mathbb{F}_q)$, the orthogonal, symplectic, and unitary groups, and the exceptional groups of types$G_2, F_4, E_6, E_7, E_8, {}^2E_6, {}^3D_4, {}^2F_4, {}^2G_2, {}^2B_2$. Finally, there are twenty-six sporadic groups — those fitting no infinite family — ranging from the Mathieu group$M_{11}$ (order$7920$) to the Monster group (order approximately$8 \times 10^{53}$).

The complete classification of all finite simple groups, announced in 1983 and with a gap finally closed in 2004, is the central achievement of twentieth-century group theory. Its proof spans thousands of journal pages and represents the combined work of hundreds of mathematicians over several decades.

**Example 1.** $\mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z}$ is simple. Its only subgroups have order$1$ or$7$, so there is no room for a proper nontrivial subgroup at all, let alone a normal one.

**Example 2.** $A_4$ is *not* simple. The Klein four-group$V = \{e, (12)(34), (13)(24), (14)(23)\}$ is a subgroup of order$4$ in$A_4$. One checks$\sigma V \sigma^{-1} = V$ for all$\sigma \in A_4$: for instance,$(123)V(123)^{-1} = \{e, (13)(24), (14)(23), (12)(34)\} = V$. So$V \trianglelefteq A_4$, and$A_4$ is not simple.

**Example 3.** $\mathrm{PSL}(2, \mathbb{F}_2) \cong S_3$ has order$6$ and is not simple ($A_3 \trianglelefteq S_3$). But$\mathrm{PSL}(2, \mathbb{F}_3) \cong A_4$ has order$12$ and is not simple (for the reason above). However,$\mathrm{PSL}(2, \mathbb{F}_4) \cong \mathrm{PSL}(2, \mathbb{F}_5) \cong A_5$ has order$60$ and *is* simple, as the smallest nonabelian simple group. The groups$\mathrm{PSL}(2, \mathbb{F}_q)$ are simple for all prime powers$q \geq 4$.

**Example 4.** The Mathieu group $M_{11}$ is a sporadic simple group of order$7920 = 2^4 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5 \cdot 11$. It arises as a multiply transitive permutation group on$11$ points (specifically, it acts$4$-transitively). Its simplicity can be verified by checking that the conjugacy class sizes —$1, 55, 66, 165, 990, 1980, 720, 720, 660, 880, 144$ — cannot be summed to give a proper divisor of$7920$ that includes$1$.

**Example 5.** The question "which orders $n$ support a simple group?" is fully answered by the classification:$n = 1$ gives only the trivial group;$n = p$ (prime) gives$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$;$n = 60$ gives$A_5$;$n = 168$ gives$\mathrm{PSL}(2, \mathbb{F}_7)$; and so on. Many orders support no simple group at all. For instance, by Section 17.3.2, no group of order$pq$ with$p < q$ is simple (the Sylow$q$-subgroup is always normal). The pattern of which orders support simple groups is deeply irregular and fully understood only through the complete classification.
