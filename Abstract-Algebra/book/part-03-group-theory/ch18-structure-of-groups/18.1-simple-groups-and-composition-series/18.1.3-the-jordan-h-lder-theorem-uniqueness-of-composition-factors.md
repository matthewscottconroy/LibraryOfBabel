# 18.1.3 The Jordan–Hölder Theorem: Uniqueness of Composition Factors

The existence of composition series, proved in the previous section, raises an immediate question: does the choice of series matter? Different chains of subgroups can yield different composition factors in different orders — could they also yield different factors? The Jordan–Hölder theorem answers definitively: no.

\begin{theorem}[Jordan–Hölder]
Any two composition series of a finite group $G$ have the same length, and their composition factors are the same up to reordering and isomorphism. That is, if
$$G = G_0 \supset G_1 \supset \cdots \supset G_s = \{e\} \quad \text{and} \quad G = H_0 \supset H_1 \supset \cdots \supset H_t = \{e\}$$
are two composition series, then $s = t$ and there is a bijection between$\{G_i/G_{i+1}\}$ and$\{H_j/H_{j+1}\}$ such that corresponding factors are isomorphic.
\end{theorem}

The theorem elevates the composition factors from a byproduct of a particular choice of series to a genuine invariant of the group $G$. We may speak of "the composition factors of$G$" without ambiguity.

The proof proceeds by induction on $|G|$ (or equivalently on the length of a composition series). For the base case, if$G$ is simple, both composition series must be$G \supset \{e\}$, and the unique factor is$G$ itself.

For the inductive step, let $G_1$ and$H_1$ be the first terms of the two series. If$G_1 = H_1$, the two series restricted to$G_1$ are both composition series of$G_1$; by induction they have the same factors, and prepending$G/G_1 = G/H_1$ to both gives matching factors for the full series.

If $G_1 \neq H_1$, then since both are maximal normal subgroups of$G$, the subgroup$G_1 H_1$ satisfies$G_1 \subsetneq G_1 H_1 \leq G$; by maximality of$G_1$, we get$G_1 H_1 = G$. The second isomorphism theorem then gives:
$$G_1 / (G_1 \cap H_1) \cong G_1 H_1 / H_1 = G / H_1 \quad \text{and} \quad H_1 / (G_1 \cap H_1) \cong G / G_1.$$
So the factors $G/G_1$ and$G/H_1$ appear as quotients within the$G_1$ and$H_1$ chains respectively, just one step down. Applying induction to both$G_1$ and$H_1$ (which share the subgroup$G_1 \cap H_1$ as a common "meeting point"), one constructs a bijection between all the factors of the two series.

The analogy with the fundamental theorem of arithmetic is exact and illuminating. Just as every positive integer $n > 1$ has a unique prime factorization$n = p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_k^{a_k}$ (up to reordering), every finite group$G$ has a unique multiset of simple composition factors. The group$G$ is "built from" these simple groups in the same way that$n$ is built from its prime factors. The Jordan–Hölder theorem is the group-theoretic fundamental theorem of arithmetic.

The theorem reshapes the program of understanding finite groups into two sub-problems. First: classify all finite simple groups (the "prime factorization" of group theory). Second: solve the *extension problem* — given simple groups $S_1, \ldots, S_k$, understand all finite groups$G$ with those composition factors. The first problem is now complete: the classification of finite simple groups gives a full list. The second problem is far from complete and represents a major open area of research. Even for composition factors as simple as$\{\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\}$, there are two groups with those factors:$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ (a non-split extension) and$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ (a split extension). In general, extensions are parametrized by group cohomology, a subtle invariant.

**Example 1.** $G = S_3$. The composition series$S_3 \supset A_3 \supset \{e\}$ has factors$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$. Is there another composition series? The normal subgroups of$S_3$ are$\{e\}$,$A_3$, and$S_3$ itself — there are no other proper nontrivial normal subgroups. So the only composition series is$S_3 \supset A_3 \supset \{e\}$, and Jordan–Hölder is vacuously confirmed (there is no second series to compare).

**Example 2.** $G = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. The three composition series, inserting each of the three order-$2$ subgroups:
\begin{align*}
&\mathbb{Z}^2/2\mathbb{Z}^2 \supset \langle (\bar{1}, \bar{0}) \rangle \supset \{(0,0)\}: \text{ factors } \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}. \\
&\mathbb{Z}^2/2\mathbb{Z}^2 \supset \langle (\bar{0}, \bar{1}) \rangle \supset \{(0,0)\}: \text{ factors } \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}. \\
&\mathbb{Z}^2/2\mathbb{Z}^2 \supset \langle (\bar{1}, \bar{1}) \rangle \supset \{(0,0)\}: \text{ factors } \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}.
\end{align*}
All three give the same factors, confirming Jordan–Hölder.

**Example 3.** Two groups can have the same composition factors without being isomorphic. Both $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ and$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ have composition factors$\{\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\}$. But$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ has an element of order$4$, while$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ does not. Same atoms, different structure: this is the extension problem.

**Example 4.** $G = S_4$. The composition series$S_4 \supset A_4 \supset V_4 \supset \langle (12)(34) \rangle \supset \{e\}$ gives factors$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. An alternative starting point:$S_4 \supset A_4 \supset V_4 \supset \langle (13)(24) \rangle \supset \{e\}$. The middle factors are isomorphic (both$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$) even though the specific subgroups differ. Jordan–Hölder guarantees this match.

**Example 5.** A non-example: for infinite groups, Jordan–Hölder can fail. The group $\mathbb{Z}$ has composition series$\mathbb{Z} \supset 2\mathbb{Z} \supset 4\mathbb{Z} \supset \cdots$ (if we insist on infinite chains), but$\mathbb{Z}$ has no composition series in the classical sense because there is no finite chain from$\mathbb{Z}$ to$\{0\}$ with simple factors (since$\mathbb{Z}$ has no maximal subgroups). The Jordan–Hölder theorem is fundamentally a theorem about finite groups, and its proof uses finiteness in an essential way.
