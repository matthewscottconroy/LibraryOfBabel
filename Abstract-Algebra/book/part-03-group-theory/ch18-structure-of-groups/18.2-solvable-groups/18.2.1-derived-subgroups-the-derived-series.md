# 18.2.1 Derived Subgroups: $G' = [G, G]$; the Derived Series

One of the most natural ways to measure how far a group departs from commutativity is to track the "failure to commute" in a systematic, iterative way. The commutator formalism does exactly this, and its iteration produces the derived series — a canonical descending chain that captures the cumulative effect of all non-commutativity in the group.

The *commutator* of two elements $a, b \in G$ is the element
$$[a, b] = aba^{-1}b^{-1}.$$
Note that $[a, b] = e$ if and only if$ab = ba$ — commutators measure the deviation from commutativity. The *derived subgroup* (also called the *commutator subgroup*) is
$$G' = [G, G] = \langle [a, b] : a, b \in G \rangle,$$
the subgroup generated by all commutators in $G$. The derived subgroup is a normal subgroup of$G$ — and in fact it is *characteristic*, meaning it is preserved by every automorphism of$G$, not just by conjugation. Characteristic subgroups are more than normal: they cannot be moved by any automorphism of the group.

The significance of $G'$ is captured by a universal property:$G/G'$ is the *largest abelian quotient* of$G$. More precisely:

\begin{proposition}
If $N \trianglelefteq G$ and$G/N$ is abelian, then$G' \leq N$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$G/N$ abelian means$(aN)(bN) = (bN)(aN)$ for all$a, b \in G$, i.e.,$abN = baN$, i.e.,$b^{-1}a^{-1}ba \in N$. But$b^{-1}a^{-1}ba = [b^{-1}, a^{-1}]$, which is a commutator, so every generator of$G'$ lies in$N$, hence$G' \leq N$.
\end{proof}

This says: to kill all commutators (make the group abelian), you must quotient out at least $G'$. The group$G^{\mathrm{ab}} = G/G'$ is called the *abelianization* of$G$, and it is the universal abelian quotient of$G$.

We now iterate. Define the *derived series* by:
$$G^{(0)} = G, \quad G^{(i+1)} = (G^{(i)})' = [G^{(i)}, G^{(i)}].$$
Each term $G^{(i)}$ is characteristic in$G$ (since the commutator subgroup of a characteristic subgroup is characteristic). The derived series is a descending chain:
$$G = G^{(0)} \supseteq G^{(1)} \supseteq G^{(2)} \supseteq \cdots$$
It may or may not reach $\{e\}$ in finitely many steps. Whether it does is the definition of solvability, treated in the next subsection.

For abelian groups, $G^{(1)} = \{e\}$ immediately (since$[a, b] = aba^{-1}b^{-1} = e$ for all$a, b$). The series terminates after one step. For$S_3$:$S_3^{(1)} = [S_3, S_3]$ is the subgroup generated by all commutators; one computes$[(12), (123)] = (12)(123)(12)^{-1}(123)^{-1} = (23)(132) = (12)$, wait — let us be careful. The commutators in$S_3$ include$(12)(123)(12)(132) = (12)(123)(12)(132)$. Computing:$(12)(123) = (23)$, then$(23)(12) = (132)$, then$(132)(132) = (123)^{-1} \cdot \ldots$ — the key point is that$[S_3, S_3] = A_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$. Then$A_3^{(1)} = [A_3, A_3] = \{e\}$ since$A_3$ is abelian. So$S_3^{(2)} = \{e\}$.

For $A_5$: every commutator$[a, b]$ lies in$A_5$ (since$A_5$ is closed), and since$A_5$ is simple and$A_5' \trianglelefteq A_5$, we must have$A_5' = \{e\}$ or$A_5' = A_5$. But if$A_5' = \{e\}$, then$A_5$ would be abelian, which it is not. So$A_5' = A_5$, and the derived series is constant:$A_5^{(n)} = A_5$ for all$n \geq 1$.

**Example 1.** $G = \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ (abelian). Every commutator$[a, b] = a + b - a - b = 0$, so$G' = \{0\}$. The derived series terminates immediately at step$1$:$G^{(1)} = \{e\}$.

**Example 2.** $G = S_3$. The commutators include$[(12), (13)] = (12)(13)(12)^{-1}(13)^{-1} = (12)(13)(12)(13) = (123)^{-1} \cdot \ldots$ Let us compute directly:$(12)(13) = (132)$ and$(12)(13)(12)^{-1}(13)^{-1} = (12)(13)(12)(13)$.$(12)(13) = (132)$,$(132)(12) = (23)$,$(23)(13) = (123)$. So$[(12),(13)] = (123) \in A_3$. Since all commutators are even permutations,$G' \leq A_3$. Since$A_3 = \langle (123) \rangle$ and$(123) \in G'$, we get$G' = A_3$. Then$G^{(2)} = (A_3)' = \{e\}$.

**Example 3.** $G = D_4 = \langle r, s \mid r^4 = s^2 = e, srs = r^{-1} \rangle$. We compute$[r, s] = rs r^{-1} s^{-1} = r \cdot s r^{-1} s = r \cdot r = r^2$ (using$sr^{-1} s^{-1} = r$). So$r^2 \in D_4'$. Also$D_4' \leq \langle r^2 \rangle$ since$D_4/\langle r^2 \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is abelian. Therefore$D_4' = \{e, r^2\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. Since$D_4'$ is abelian,$D_4^{(2)} = \{e\}$.

**Example 4.** $G = \mathrm{GL}(2, \mathbb{F}_p)$. The commutator subgroup is$G' = \mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{F}_p)$ (matrices of determinant$1$), since$\mathrm{GL}(2, \mathbb{F}_p)/\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{F}_p) \cong \mathbb{F}_p^*$ is abelian, and one shows every element of$\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{F}_p)$ is a commutator. For$p \geq 5$,$\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{F}_p)' = \mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{F}_p)$ (since$\mathrm{PSL}(2, \mathbb{F}_p)$ is simple), so the derived series stabilizes:$\mathrm{GL}(2, \mathbb{F}_p)^{(2)} = \mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{F}_p)$ and the series never reaches$\{e\}$.

**Example 5.** The universal property of the abelianization has a concrete consequence: for any group $G$ and any abelian group$A$, there is a natural bijection$\mathrm{Hom}(G, A) \cong \mathrm{Hom}(G/G', A) = \mathrm{Hom}(G^{\mathrm{ab}}, A)$. This means: to understand all homomorphisms from$G$ to abelian groups, it suffices to understand the single group$G^{\mathrm{ab}} = G/G'$. For$G = S_n$, the abelianization is$S_n / A_n \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, so the only homomorphisms from$S_n$ to abelian groups are the trivial map and the sign homomorphism (and their compositions with homomorphisms from$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$).
