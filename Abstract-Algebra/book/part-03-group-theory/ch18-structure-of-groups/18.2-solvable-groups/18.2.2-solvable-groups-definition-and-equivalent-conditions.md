# 18.2.2 Solvable Groups: Definition and Equivalent Conditions

The derived series of a group — iterating the commutator subgroup construction — either reaches the trivial group in finitely many steps, or it stabilizes at a nontrivial subgroup and stays there forever. The groups for which the derived series terminates are called solvable, and they form one of the most important and well-studied classes in all of group theory.

\begin{definition}
A group $G$ is \emph{solvable} (or \emph{soluble}) if its derived series terminates at the trivial group:$G^{(n)} = \{e\}$ for some$n \geq 0$.
\end{definition}

The name "solvable" is not arbitrary. As Galois showed (and as we will make precise in Chapter 32), a polynomial equation over a field of characteristic $0$ is solvable by radicals if and only if its Galois group is solvable. The groups$S_1, S_2, S_3, S_4$ are all solvable (corresponding to the classical solution formulas for polynomials of degrees$1, 2, 3, 4$), while$S_5$ is not (and this is why no general quintic formula exists).

Every abelian group is solvable with $n = 1$. The symmetric group$S_3$ is solvable with$n = 2$ (since$S_3' = A_3$ and$A_3' = \{e\}$). The symmetric group$S_4$ is solvable with$n = 4$: the derived series is$S_4 \supset A_4 \supset V_4 \supset \{e\}$, and we need to add one more step since$V_4' = \{e\}$ (as$V_4$ is abelian). Wait —$V_4$ is abelian, so$V_4' = \{e\}$, meaning$S_4^{(3)} = V_4' = \{e\}$ and the derived series terminates at step$3$. By contrast,$A_5$ is not solvable:$A_5' = A_5$ (since$A_5$ is nonabelian simple), so$A_5^{(n)} = A_5$ for all$n \geq 1$.

There are several equivalent characterizations of solvability, each illuminating a different aspect of the concept:

\begin{theorem}
For a finite group $G$, the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $G$ is solvable (the derived series reaches$\{e\}$).
\item $G$ has a composition series with all composition factors isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ for primes$p$ (not necessarily the same prime at each step).
\item $G$ has a subnormal series$G = H_0 \supset H_1 \supset \cdots \supset H_k = \{e\}$ with each quotient$H_i/H_{i+1}$ abelian.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

The equivalence of (1) and (3) is essentially tautological: if the derived series terminates, it is a subnormal series with abelian factors (each $G^{(i)}/G^{(i+1)}$ is abelian by the universal property of$G^{(i+1)}$ as the commutator subgroup of$G^{(i)}$). Conversely, if such a series exists, the universal property forces$G^{(i)} \leq H_i$ for each$i$ (by induction: the abelian quotient$H_i/H_{i+1}$ forces$(G^{(i)})' \leq H_{i+1}$ by the universal property, hence$G^{(i+1)} \leq H_{i+1}$). The equivalence with (2) follows from the fact that any abelian group can be refined to a series with cyclic prime-order factors (the simple abelian groups).

The class of solvable groups is closed under subgroups, quotients, and extensions. These closure properties are the content of the next subsection and make inductive arguments about solvability tractable.

**Example 1.** $G = S_3$. The derived series:$S_3 \supset S_3' = A_3 \supset A_3' = \{e\}$. At each step the quotient$S_3/A_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ and$A_3/\{e\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ are abelian. So$S_3$ is solvable of derived length$2$.

**Example 2.** $G = A_4$. The derived series:$A_4 \supset A_4' = V_4 \supset V_4' = \{e\}$. Why is$A_4' = V_4$? The quotient$A_4/V_4 \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ is abelian, so$A_4' \leq V_4$. Conversely,$(12)(34) = [(123), (124)]$ (a computation one should verify), so$V_4 \leq A_4'$. Thus$A_4' = V_4$. Since$V_4$ is abelian,$A_4^{(2)} = \{e\}$, so$A_4$ is solvable of derived length$2$.

**Example 3.** $G = S_4$. The derived series:$S_4 \supset A_4 \supset V_4 \supset \{e\}$. Quotients:$S_4/A_4 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$,$A_4/V_4 \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$,$V_4/\{e\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. All abelian. So$S_4$ is solvable. The composition factors (after refining$V_4$) are$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 4.** The Feit–Thompson theorem (1963) states that every group of odd order is solvable. This is one of the deepest results in finite group theory: its proof, by Walter Feit and John Thompson, is 255 pages long and occupies an entire issue of the *Pacific Journal of Mathematics*. The theorem implies that every nonabelian simple group has even order, which is a key input to the classification of finite simple groups.

**Example 5.** $G = \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ for any$n$. Being abelian,$G' = \{0\}$ and$G$ is solvable of derived length$1$. More generally, every nilpotent group (Section 18.3) is solvable, and every$p$-group is solvable (since$p$-groups are nilpotent). The solvable groups are thus a large and natural class including all$p$-groups, all abelian groups, and many other groups including all groups whose order has at most two distinct prime factors (by Burnside's$p^a q^b$ theorem, proved using character theory).
