# 18.2.3 Subgroups, Quotients, and Extensions of Solvable Groups

The class of solvable groups has excellent closure properties, which make inductive arguments tractable. A priori, solvability is defined via the derived series reaching the identity, and it is not obvious that this structural condition is inherited by subgroups, projected onto quotients, or reconstructed from extensions. All three of these properties hold, and together they form the technical backbone of virtually every inductive proof about solvable groups.

\begin{theorem}
Let $G$ be a solvable group. Then:
1. Every subgroup $H \leq G$ is solvable.
2. Every quotient $G/N$ is solvable.
3. If $N \trianglelefteq G$ and both$N$ and$G/N$ are solvable, then$G$ is solvable.
\end{theorem}

**Subgroups of solvable groups are solvable.** Suppose $G^{(n)} = \{e\}$ and$H \leq G$. We claim$H^{(i)} \leq G^{(i)}$ for all$i$, by induction on$i$. The base case$H = H^{(0)} \leq G = G^{(0)}$ is clear. For the inductive step,$H^{(i+1)} = [H^{(i)}, H^{(i)}]$ is generated by commutators of elements of$H^{(i)}$, all of which lie in$G^{(i)}$ by the inductive hypothesis. Hence$H^{(i+1)} \leq [G^{(i)}, G^{(i)}] = G^{(i+1)}$. In particular$H^{(n)} \leq G^{(n)} = \{e\}$, so$H$ is solvable.

**Quotients of solvable groups are solvable.** Let $\pi: G \twoheadrightarrow G/N$ be the quotient map. We claim$\pi(G^{(i)}) = (G/N)^{(i)}$ for all$i$. The base case is$\pi(G) = G/N$. For the inductive step, since$\pi$ is a surjective homomorphism and$\pi([a,b]) = [\pi(a), \pi(b)]$, we have$\pi(G^{(i+1)}) = \pi([G^{(i)}, G^{(i)}]) = [\pi(G^{(i)}), \pi(G^{(i)})] = [(G/N)^{(i)}, (G/N)^{(i)}] = (G/N)^{(i+1)}$. Therefore$(G/N)^{(n)} = \pi(G^{(n)}) = \pi(\{e\}) = \{\bar{e}\}$, so$G/N$ is solvable.

**Extensions of solvable groups are solvable.** This is the deepest and most important of the three properties. Suppose $N \trianglelefteq G$ with$N^{(m)} = \{e\}$ and$(G/N)^{(k)} = \{e\}$. Write$\pi: G \to G/N$. From the quotient result applied in reverse,$\pi(G^{(k)}) = (G/N)^{(k)} = \{\bar{e}\}$, so$G^{(k)} \leq \ker\pi = N$. Now applying the subgroup result,$G^{(k)}$ is a subgroup of$N$ and thus solvable; its derived series satisfies$(G^{(k)})^{(m)} = G^{(k+m)} \leq N^{(m)} = \{e\}$. Hence$G^{(k+m)} = \{e\}$, and$G$ is solvable.

\begin{remark}
The extension property is what makes solvability useful for inductive proofs. To show that a group $G$ is solvable, it suffices to find a normal subgroup$N$ for which both$N$ and$G/N$ are solvable by some inductive hypothesis. This divide-and-conquer strategy underlies Galois's proof that solvability of a polynomial by radicals is equivalent to solvability of the Galois group.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** $S_4$ is solvable. The normal series$S_4 \supset A_4 \supset V_4 \supset \{e\}$ (where$V_4 = \{e, (12)(34), (13)(24), (14)(23)\}$ is the Klein four-group) has successive quotients$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$,$\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$, and$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, all abelian. Alternatively,$V_4$ is abelian (hence solvable),$A_4/V_4 \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ is abelian, and$S_4/A_4 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is abelian. By the extension theorem applied twice,$A_4$ is solvable and then$S_4$ is solvable.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Every $p$-group is solvable. We prove this by induction on$|G| = p^n$. The base case$n = 1$ gives$G \cong \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$, which is abelian. For$n > 1$, recall that every$p$-group has a nontrivial center$Z(G)$. The quotient$G/Z(G)$ has order$p^{n-k}$ where$k = |Z(G)| \geq p$, so by induction$G/Z(G)$ is solvable. The subgroup$Z(G)$ is abelian and hence solvable. By the extension theorem,$G$ is solvable.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** The group $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \times S_3$ is solvable. Here$N = \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \times \{e\}$ is a normal abelian subgroup, and the quotient is$S_3$. Since$S_3$ has the normal series$S_3 \supset A_3 \supset \{e\}$ with abelian factors,$S_3$ is solvable. Both$N$ and$G/N \cong S_3$ are solvable, so$G$ is solvable. The derived series of$G$ satisfies$G' = \{0\} \times A_3$ and$G'' = \{e\}$.
\end{example}

Notice that the extension property is the analogue, for group structure, of the statement in number theory that a tower of abelian extensions is again an abelian-type extension. It fails dramatically for simplicity: an extension of simple groups need not be simple (for instance, $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ extended by itself gives$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ or$V_4$, neither of which is simple). The well-behaved nature of solvability under extensions is one of the reasons solvable groups are far more tractable than arbitrary finite groups, and it is the reason that Galois theory can reduce the question of solving a polynomial to a question about a specific group rather than a specific group and all of its subgroups simultaneously.
