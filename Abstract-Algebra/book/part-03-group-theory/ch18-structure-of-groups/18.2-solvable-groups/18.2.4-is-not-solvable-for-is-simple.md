# 18.2.4 $S_n$ Is Not Solvable for$n \geq 5$;$A_5$ Is Simple

We now establish the central example of a non-solvable group, which also serves as the linchpin connecting group theory to the insolubility of the quintic. The argument proceeds in two steps: first proving $A_5$ is simple, then deducing non-solvability. The simplicity of$A_5$ is not obvious from its definition — it requires a detailed analysis of the conjugacy class structure of even permutations.

\begin{theorem}
The alternating group $A_5$ is simple.
\end{theorem}

**Proof sketch.** We compute the conjugacy classes of $A_5$. Recall that in$S_5$, elements are conjugate if and only if they have the same cycle type. Within$A_5$, a conjugacy class of$S_5$ either remains a single conjugacy class of$A_5$, or splits into two classes of equal size (this splitting occurs precisely when the centralizer in$S_5$ of an element$g$ is contained in$A_5$). The cycle types in$A_5$ (even permutations of$\{1,2,3,4,5\}$) are: the identity ($1$ element), products of two disjoint transpositions ($15$ elements),$3$-cycles ($20$ elements), and$5$-cycles ($24$ elements split into two classes of$12$). The class sizes are therefore$1, 15, 20, 12, 12$.

A normal subgroup $N \trianglelefteq A_5$ must be a union of conjugacy classes including the identity class$\{e\}$. Its order must divide$|A_5| = 60$. The possible sums of subsets of$\{1, 15, 20, 12, 12\}$ containing$1$ are:$1$,$1+15 = 16$,$1+20 = 21$,$1+12 = 13$,$1+15+20 = 36$,$1+12+12 = 25$,$1+15+12 = 28$,$1+20+12 = 33$,$1+15+20+12 = 48$,$1+15+12+12 = 40$,$1+20+12+12 = 45$, and$60$. Among these, only$1$ and$60$ divide$60$. Hence the only normal subgroups of$A_5$ are$\{e\}$ and$A_5$, and$A_5$ is simple.$\square$

\begin{corollary}
$A_5$ is not solvable.
\end{corollary}

Since $A_5$ is simple and nonabelian, the only normal subgroups are$\{e\}$ and$A_5$. The derived subgroup$A_5' = [A_5, A_5]$ is a normal subgroup of$A_5$. Since$A_5$ is nonabelian,$A_5' \neq \{e\}$; since$A_5$ is simple,$A_5' = A_5$. The derived series is therefore constant:$A_5^{(n)} = A_5$ for all$n \geq 1$, so it never reaches$\{e\}$. This means$A_5$ is not solvable.

\begin{theorem}
For $n \geq 5$,$S_n$ is not solvable.
\end{theorem}

For $n \geq 5$, the alternating group$A_n$ contains a copy of$A_5$ (acting on any five-element subset of$\{1, \ldots, n\}$). By Section 18.2.3, a subgroup of a solvable group is solvable. Since$A_5$ is not solvable,$A_n$ cannot be solvable for$n \geq 5$. Since$A_n \trianglelefteq S_n$ with$S_n/A_n \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ solvable, and$A_n$ is not solvable, the extension property (Section 18.2.3, property 3) — applied in its contrapositive form — gives that$S_n$ is not solvable: if$S_n$ were solvable, then its normal subgroup$A_n$ would be solvable, a contradiction.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** We verify directly that $A_4$ is solvable but$A_5$ is not. For$A_4$: the Klein four-group$V_4 = \{e, (12)(34), (13)(24), (14)(23)\}$ is normal in$A_4$, with$A_4/V_4 \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ abelian and$V_4$ abelian. The derived series is$A_4 \supset V_4 \supset \{e\}$. For$A_5$: any normal subgroup has order dividing$60$ and equal to a sum of class sizes including$1$. No such sum in$\{1, 15, 20, 12, 12\}$ other than$1$ and$60$ divides$60$. So$A_5$ is simple, hence non-solvable.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** The group $A_6$ is also simple (and hence non-solvable). The conjugacy classes of$A_6$ have sizes$1, 15, 40, 45, 72, 90$ (or, more precisely, accounting for splitting,$1, 15, 40, 45, 36, 36, 90$). As before, no nontrivial proper subset of these sizes sums to a divisor of$|A_6| = 360$, confirming simplicity.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** By contrast, $S_4$ is solvable. The derived series is$S_4 \supset A_4 \supset V_4 \supset \{e\}$. Since$A_5 \not\leq S_4$ (as$|S_4| = 24 < 60 = |A_5|$), the obstruction to solvability does not appear.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
This is the group-theoretic heart of the impossibility of solving the general degree-$5$ polynomial by radicals. Galois theory (Chapter 32) will make precise the correspondence: a polynomial$f$ of degree$n$ is solvable by radicals if and only if its Galois group (a subgroup of$S_n$) is solvable. The general degree-$5$ polynomial has Galois group$S_5$, which is not solvable. The key step in Abel–Ruffini's theorem is precisely the non-solvability of$S_5$ that we have just proved, which in turn rests on the simplicity of$A_5$. The fact that$A_4$ is solvable (while$A_5$ is not) explains why degree$4$ is the last case where the general polynomial is solvable by radicals.
\end{remark}
