# 18.2 Solvable Groups

A group $G$ is solvable if it has a subnormal series $G = G_0 \supset G_1 \supset \cdots \supset G_r = \{e\}$ with each quotient $G_i/G_{i+1}$ abelian. Equivalently, $G$ is solvable if and only if its derived (commutator) series $G^{(0)} = G \supset G^{(1)} = [G,G] \supset G^{(2)} = [G^{(1)},G^{(1)}] \supset \cdots$ eventually reaches the trivial group. The commutator subgroup $[G,G]$ is the subgroup generated by all commutators $[a,b] = aba^{-1}b^{-1}$; it is normal in $G$, and $G/[G,G]$ is the largest abelian quotient of $G$. A group is abelian if and only if $[G,G] = \{e\}$, making the derived series a measure of "how far from abelian" the group is.

The connection to Galois theory is one of the most celebrated theorems in mathematics: a polynomial $f(x)$ over a field of characteristic zero is solvable by radicals if and only if its Galois group (viewed as a permutation group of the roots) is solvable. The symmetric groups $S_n$ for $n \leq 4$ are solvable — $S_4$ has derived series $S_4 \supset A_4 \supset V_4 \supset \{e\}$ — which explains why polynomials of degree at most 4 can be solved by radicals (the quadratic, cubic, and quartic formulas). For $n \geq 5$, the alternating group $A_n$ is simple and non-abelian, so the derived series $S_n \supset A_n = A_n \supset \cdots$ never terminates, and $S_n$ is not solvable. This is why there is no general radical formula for polynomials of degree 5 or more.

The class of solvable groups is closed under subgroups, quotients, and extensions. If $N \trianglelefteq G$ and both $N$ and $G/N$ are solvable, then $G$ is solvable; this "extension property" is proved by combining the solvability series for $N$ and $G/N$. Two major theorems establish the breadth of the solvable class. Burnside's $p^a q^b$ theorem (1904) states that every group of order $p^a q^b$ (for primes $p, q$) is solvable, proved using character theory. The Feit–Thompson theorem (1963) establishes the far deeper result that every group of odd order is solvable. Together these results imply that every non-abelian simple group has even order and order involving at least three distinct primes — constraints that are central to the Classification of Finite Simple Groups.
