# 18.3.1 The Lower and Upper Central Series

Just as the derived series measures how far a group is from being abelian by tracking commutators with itself, one can build sequences of subgroups that track commutativity with the whole group $G$ at each step. This leads to two natural and complementary series, both of which are more sensitive to the "depth" of non-commutativity than the derived series alone, and both of which converge to the same criterion: nilpotency.

\begin{definition}
The **lower central series** of $G$ is the sequence of characteristic subgroups defined by
$$\gamma_1(G) = G, \qquad \gamma_{i+1}(G) = [G, \gamma_i(G)],$$
where $[G, H]$ denotes the subgroup generated by all commutators$[g, h] = g^{-1}h^{-1}gh$ with$g \in G$ and$h \in H$.
\end{definition}

Each term $\gamma_{i+1}(G) = [G, \gamma_i(G)]$ is normal in$G$ (in fact characteristic), and the series is decreasing:
$$G = \gamma_1(G) \supseteq \gamma_2(G) \supseteq \gamma_3(G) \supseteq \cdots$$
Note that $\gamma_2(G) = [G, G] = G'$, the derived subgroup. So the lower central series and the derived series agree at the first step and diverge thereafter: the lower central series takes$[G, \gamma_i(G)]$ at each step, while the derived series takes$[\gamma_i(G), \gamma_i(G)]$. Since$[\gamma_i, \gamma_i] \subseteq [G, \gamma_i]$, the lower central series descends at least as fast as the derived series:$\gamma_{i+1}(G) \supseteq G^{(i+1)}$ for all$i$.

\begin{definition}
The **upper central series** of $G$ is the ascending chain
$$Z_0(G) = \{e\}, \quad Z_1(G) = Z(G), \quad Z_{i+1}(G)/Z_i(G) = Z(G/Z_i(G)).$$
Equivalently, $Z_{i+1}(G)$ is the preimage in$G$ under the quotient map$G \to G/Z_i(G)$ of the center of that quotient.
\end{definition}

The upper central series is non-decreasing:
$$\{e\} = Z_0(G) \subseteq Z_1(G) \subseteq Z_2(G) \subseteq \cdots$$
Each $Z_i(G)$ is characteristic in$G$. An element$g \in G$ lies in$Z_{i+1}(G)$ if and only if$[g, x] \in Z_i(G)$ for all$x \in G$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Compute the lower and upper central series of the dihedral group $D_4 = \langle r, s \mid r^4 = s^2 = e,\, srs^{-1} = r^{-1} \rangle$ of order$8$. We have$[r, s] = r^{-1} s^{-1} r s = r^{-1} \cdot r^{-1} = r^{-2} \cdot r^{0}$. More carefully:$srs = r^{-1}$, so$s^{-1}r^{-1}sr = (r^{-1})^{-1} = r^{-2+2}$... Let us use the formula$[r, s] = r^{-1} s^{-1} r s$. Since$s^{-1} = s$ and$sr = r^{-1}s$, we get$s^{-1}rs = r^{-1}$, so$[r,s] = r^{-1} \cdot r^{-1} = r^{-2} = r^2$. Thus$\gamma_2(D_4) = [D_4, D_4] = \langle r^2 \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. Then$\gamma_3(D_4) = [D_4, \langle r^2 \rangle]$: since$r^2$ is central in$D_4$ (it commutes with$r$ and with$s$ because$sr^2s = r^{-2} = r^2$), we get$[g, r^2] = e$ for all$g$, so$\gamma_3(D_4) = \{e\}$.
\par
For the upper central series: $Z_1(D_4) = Z(D_4) = \{e, r^2\} = \langle r^2 \rangle$. Then$D_4/Z_1(D_4) \cong V_4 = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, which is abelian. Its center is itself, so$Z_2(D_4)/Z_1(D_4) = V_4$, giving$Z_2(D_4) = D_4$. The series terminates:$\{e\} \subset \langle r^2 \rangle \subset D_4$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** For an abelian group $A$, every commutator$[a,b] = e$, so$\gamma_2(A) = \{e\}$ and the lower central series terminates immediately. The upper central series satisfies$Z_1(A) = A$, so it reaches$A$ in one step. Both confirm that$A$ is nilpotent of class$1$ (or$0$ if$A = \{e\}$).
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** For $S_3$, we have$S_3' = A_3 = \langle (123) \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ and$[S_3, A_3]$: since$A_3$ is abelian and$[s, a]$ for$s \in S_3 \setminus A_3$ and$a \in A_3$ gives$sa s^{-1} a^{-1} = a^{-1} a^{-1} = a^{-2} \neq e$... More directly,$\gamma_2(S_3) = A_3$ and$\gamma_3(S_3) = [S_3, A_3] = A_3$ (since$A_3$ is normal and$S_3$ acts on it nontrivially: conjugation by a transposition sends$(123) \mapsto (132)$, so the commutator$[(12), (123)] = (12)(132)(12)(123) = (132)(123) = (13)$... wait, that is not in$A_3$). More carefully,$[S_3, A_3]$ is generated by$[s, a]$ for$s \in S_3$,$a \in A_3$. Since$A_3 \subset S_3$ and$[(123),(132)] = (123)(132)(132)(123) = e$ (both are in the center of$A_3$)... The key point is$\gamma_3(S_3) = A_3 \neq \{e\}$, so the lower central series never reaches$\{e\}$, confirming$S_3$ is not nilpotent.
\end{example}

These two series are, in a precise sense, dual to each other. A group is nilpotent if and only if the lower central series reaches $\{e\}$ — that is,$\gamma_{c+1}(G) = \{e\}$ for some$c$ — and this is equivalent to the upper central series reaching$G$:$Z_c(G) = G$ for the same$c$. The integer$c$ is called the **nilpotency class** of$G$, and the equivalence of the two conditions is proved by a mutual induction showing that$\gamma_{k+1}(G) = \{e\}$ implies$Z_k(G) = G$ and vice versa.
