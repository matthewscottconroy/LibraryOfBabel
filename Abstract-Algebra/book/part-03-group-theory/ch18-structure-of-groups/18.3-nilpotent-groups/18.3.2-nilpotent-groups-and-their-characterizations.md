# 18.3.2 Nilpotent Groups and Their Characterizations

A group $G$ is **nilpotent** if its lower central series reaches$\{e\}$ in finitely many steps:$\gamma_{c+1}(G) = \{e\}$ for some$c \geq 0$, called the **nilpotency class** of$G$. Equivalently, the upper central series reaches$G$:$Z_c(G) = G$ for the same$c$. The class measures the depth of non-commutativity: abelian groups have class$1$ (or$0$ for the trivial group), the non-abelian groups of order$p^3$ have class$2$, and so on.

Nilpotent groups are, in a precise sense, "more abelian" than merely solvable groups. The hierarchy is:
$$\text{abelian} \subsetneq \text{nilpotent} \subsetneq \text{solvable} \subsetneq \text{finite groups}.$$

Every abelian group is nilpotent (class $\leq 1$). Every nilpotent group is solvable, since the lower central series terminates at$\{e\}$ with abelian successive quotients$\gamma_i(G)/\gamma_{i+1}(G)$ (proved in Section 18.3.4). But$S_3$ is solvable and not nilpotent, since its center is trivial and the upper central series is constant at$\{e\}$.

\begin{theorem}
For a finite group $G$, the following are equivalent:
1. $G$ is nilpotent.
2. Every Sylow subgroup of $G$ is normal.
3. $G$ is the direct product of its Sylow subgroups.
4. Every maximal subgroup of $G$ is normal.
5. Every subgroup of $G$ is subnormal.
\end{theorem}

We defer the full proof to Section 18.3.3. Here we develop the intuition for each condition.

**Condition 2** says that $p$-power-order elements do not "mix" across different primes in any nontrivial way: each Sylow subgroup sits as a normal piece of$G$. **Condition 3** is the strongest:$G$ breaks apart entirely as a direct product. **Condition 4** is algebraically elementary to state but non-obvious to connect to nilpotency. **Condition 5** (subnormality) means every$H \leq G$ sits inside a chain$H = H_0 \trianglelefteq H_1 \trianglelefteq \cdots \trianglelefteq H_k = G$; this is a strong form of the condition that every subgroup interacts "nicely" with the ambient group.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Every $p$-group is nilpotent. We prove by induction on$|G| = p^n$. For$n = 1$,$G \cong \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ is abelian. For$n > 1$,$G$ has a nontrivial center$Z(G)$ (the class equation forces$|Z(G)| \geq p$). The quotient$G/Z(G)$ is a$p$-group of strictly smaller order, hence nilpotent by induction; say$Z_c(G/Z(G)) = G/Z(G)$. Then$Z_{c+1}(G) = G$ by definition of the upper central series, so$G$ is nilpotent of class at most$c+1$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** The group $G = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}$ for distinct primes$p, q$ is abelian and hence nilpotent of class$1$. Its unique Sylow$p$-subgroup is$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} \times \{0\}$ (normal) and its unique Sylow$q$-subgroup is$\{0\} \times \mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}$ (normal), and$G$ is their direct product. This illustrates Condition 3.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** The group $D_4$ is nilpotent of class$2$. We computed in Section 18.3.1 that$\gamma_3(D_4) = \{e\}$ and$\gamma_2(D_4) = \langle r^2 \rangle \neq \{e\}$. Indeed$D_4$ is a$2$-group of order$8$, confirming that all$p$-groups are nilpotent.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** The dihedral group $D_6$ of order$12$ is not nilpotent. We can verify this using Condition 2:$D_6$ has a Sylow$3$-subgroup of order$3$ and a Sylow$2$-subgroup of order$4$. The number of Sylow$3$-subgroups divides$4$ and is$\equiv 1 \pmod{3}$, so it could be$1$ or$4$. In$D_6$ (symmetries of a hexagon), the$120°$ and$240°$ rotations generate the unique Sylow$3$-subgroup (which is normal, being the rotation subgroup of order$6$'s unique Sylow$3$-subgroup)... actually,$D_6$ has center$\langle r^3 \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, and$Z_2(D_6) = D_6$ does not hold; more directly,$S_3 \cong D_3$ is a quotient of$D_6$,$S_3$ is not nilpotent, and quotients of nilpotent groups are nilpotent, so$D_6$ is not nilpotent.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 5.** Classify all groups of order $p^2$ for a prime$p$. Such a group is a$p$-group of order$p^2$, hence nilpotent. By the structure theorem for abelian groups (or directly), the abelian groups of order$p^2$ are$\mathbb{Z}/p^2\mathbb{Z}$ and$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$. Since every group of order$p^2$ is abelian (a standard consequence of the class equation:$|G|/|Z(G)|$ divides$|G|$, and if$Z(G)$ is nontrivial and proper, then$G/Z(G)$ is cyclic, forcing$G$ to be abelian), these are all groups of order$p^2$ up to isomorphism.
\end{example}

The practical import of nilpotency is that it reduces the study of a finite nilpotent group entirely to the study of its prime-power-order constituents. Each Sylow subgroup is a $p$-group, and$p$-groups are among the most intensively studied objects in finite group theory, with a rich structure theory in their own right.
