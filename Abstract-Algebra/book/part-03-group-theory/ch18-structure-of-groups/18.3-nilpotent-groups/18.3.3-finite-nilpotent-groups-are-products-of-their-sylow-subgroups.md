# 18.3.3 Finite Nilpotent Groups Are Products of Their Sylow Subgroups

One of the most elegant structural theorems in finite group theory states that a finite group $G$ is nilpotent if and only if it is the internal direct product of its Sylow subgroups. This completely reduces the structure of a finite nilpotent group to that of its$p$-primary components, and it is the nonabelian analogue of the primary decomposition of finite abelian groups.

\begin{theorem}
A finite group $G$ is nilpotent if and only if$G \cong P_1 \times P_2 \times \cdots \times P_k$, where$P_i$ are the Sylow subgroups of$G$ for the distinct primes$p_1, \ldots, p_k$ dividing$|G|$.
\end{theorem}

**Proof ($\Rightarrow$): Nilpotent implies Sylow subgroups are normal.** We use the characterization that in a nilpotent group, every maximal subgroup is normal. Let$P$ be a Sylow$p$-subgroup of$G$ and consider its normalizer$N_G(P)$. We claim$N_G(P) = G$.

Suppose for contradiction that $N_G(P) \subsetneq G$. Then$N_G(P)$ is contained in some maximal subgroup$M$ of$G$. Since$G$ is nilpotent,$M \trianglelefteq G$. But now the Frattini argument applies: for any$g \in G$,$gPg^{-1}$ is a Sylow$p$-subgroup of$M$ (since$P \leq M$ and conjugation preserves orders), so by Sylow II applied in$M$,$gPg^{-1}$ is conjugate to$P$ inside$M$: there exists$m \in M$ with$gPg^{-1} = mPm^{-1}$, i.e.,$m^{-1}g \in N_G(P) \leq M$. Thus$g \in M$, contradicting$M \subsetneq G$. So$N_G(P) = G$, meaning$P \trianglelefteq G$.

**Proof ($\Rightarrow$) continued: Direct product decomposition.** Once all Sylow subgroups$P_1, \ldots, P_k$ are normal, the recognition criterion for internal direct products applies. Two normal subgroups of coprime order always have trivial intersection and commute elementwise: if$|P_i| = p_i^{a_i}$ and$|P_j| = p_j^{a_j}$ with$p_i \neq p_j$, then$P_i \cap P_j$ has order dividing$\gcd(p_i^{a_i}, p_j^{a_j}) = 1$. For commutativity: the commutator$[x, y] = x^{-1}y^{-1}xy \in P_i \cap P_j = \{e\}$ for$x \in P_i$,$y \in P_j$ (since$[x,y] \in P_i$ as$P_i \trianglelefteq G$ and$[x,y] \in P_j$ as$P_j \trianglelefteq G$). Since$|P_1 \cdots P_k| = p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_k^{a_k} = |G|$, we obtain$G = P_1 \times \cdots \times P_k$ (internal direct product).

**Proof ($\Leftarrow$): Direct products of$p$-groups are nilpotent.** Every$p$-group is nilpotent (Section 18.3.2, Example 1). Direct products of nilpotent groups are nilpotent: if$\gamma_{c_i+1}(P_i) = \{e\}$ for each$i$, then$\gamma_{c+1}(P_1 \times \cdots \times P_k) = \{e\}$ for$c = \max_i c_i$.$\square$

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** The group $G = \mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$. The Sylow$2$-subgroup is$P_2 = \langle 3 \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ and the Sylow$3$-subgroup is$P_3 = \langle 4 \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$. Since$G$ is abelian (hence nilpotent), both are normal and$G = P_2 \times P_3$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** The group $D_4$ of order$8$ is nilpotent (it is a$2$-group). Its unique Sylow$2$-subgroup is$D_4$ itself. The product decomposition is trivially$D_4 = D_4$. The theorem's nontrivial content appears when$|G|$ has multiple prime factors.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Let $G$ be a group of order$45 = 3^2 \cdot 5$. The number of Sylow$3$-subgroups divides$5$ and is$\equiv 1 \pmod{3}$, so it is$1$; similarly the number of Sylow$5$-subgroups divides$9$ and is$\equiv 1 \pmod{5}$, so it is$1$. Both Sylow subgroups are unique and hence normal. Thus$G = P_3 \times P_5$ where$P_3$ has order$9$ and$P_5 \cong \mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$. Since$P_3$ is a group of order$p^2$, it is abelian (isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}/9\mathbb{Z}$ or$\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$). Therefore all groups of order$45$ are abelian and there are exactly two isomorphism classes:$\mathbb{Z}/45\mathbb{Z}$ and$\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/15\mathbb{Z}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** The group $S_3$ of order$6$ is not nilpotent. Indeed,$S_3$ has a non-normal Sylow$2$-subgroup: the Sylow$2$-subgroups are$\langle (12) \rangle$,$\langle (13) \rangle$,$\langle (23) \rangle$, none of which are normal. So the theorem correctly predicts that$S_3$ is not nilpotent.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
This theorem has a beautiful corollary: every finite abelian group is nilpotent (since abelian groups are nilpotent of class $\leq 1$), and the structure theorem for finite abelian groups — proved in Chapter 19 — is a special case of this decomposition into$p$-primary pieces. The Sylow-product theorem for nilpotent groups is thus a nonabelian generalization of the primary decomposition of finite abelian groups.
\end{remark}
