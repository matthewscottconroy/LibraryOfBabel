# 18.3.4 Nilpotent $\Rightarrow$ Solvable; the Hierarchy

We now step back and place nilpotent and solvable groups in their proper context within the larger landscape of finite group theory. We establish that every nilpotent group is solvable, and we examine the strict containments and the prototypical examples witnessing them.

\begin{theorem}
Every nilpotent group is solvable.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $G$ be nilpotent of class$c$, so$\gamma_{c+1}(G) = \{e\}$. We claim that$G^{(i)} \leq \gamma_{i+1}(G)$ for all$i \geq 0$. For$i = 0$,$G^{(0)} = G = \gamma_1(G)$. Inductively,$G^{(i+1)} = [G^{(i)}, G^{(i)}] \leq [\gamma_{i+1}(G), \gamma_{i+1}(G)] \leq [G, \gamma_{i+1}(G)] = \gamma_{i+2}(G)$, where the last inequality uses$\gamma_{i+1}(G) \leq G$. In particular$G^{(c)} \leq \gamma_{c+1}(G) = \{e\}$, so$G$ is solvable.
\end{proof}

The converse fails. The group $S_3$ is solvable (with derived series$S_3 \supset A_3 \supset \{e\}$) but has trivial center, so$Z_1(S_3) = \{e\}$ and the upper central series is constant at$\{e\}$, never reaching$S_3$. Thus$S_3$ is not nilpotent.

The full hierarchy of group classes is:
$$\text{abelian} \subsetneq \text{nilpotent} \subsetneq \text{solvable} \subsetneq \text{finite groups}.$$

**Witnesses to strict containments:**
- Abelian but... all such groups are trivially nilpotent. The non-abelian group $D_4$ of order$8$ is nilpotent but not abelian (its center$\langle r^2 \rangle$ is proper).
- Nilpotent but not abelian: $D_4$, or any non-abelian$p$-group, e.g., the quaternion group$Q_8$.
- Solvable but not nilpotent: $S_3$, or any non-nilpotent group with a solvable composition series, e.g.,$A_4$.
- Finite but not solvable: $S_5$,$A_5$, and all groups containing them.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** The dihedral group $D_4$ (order$8$) is nilpotent of class$2$. It is not abelian since$rs \neq sr$. The lower central series is$D_4 \supset \langle r^2 \rangle \supset \{e\}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** The symmetric group $S_3$ (order$6$) is solvable with derived series$S_3 \supset A_3 \supset \{e\}$. Since$Z(S_3) = \{e\}$, the upper central series is$\{e\} \subseteq \{e\} \subseteq \cdots$, never reaching$S_3$. Not nilpotent.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** The group $A_4$ (order$12$) is solvable with series$A_4 \supset V_4 \supset \{e\}$ ($V_4$ is the Klein four-group). The center of$A_4$ is trivial, so$A_4$ is not nilpotent. Alternatively,$A_4$ has no normal Sylow$3$-subgroup (there are four Sylow$3$-subgroups), so the characterization of nilpotency via Sylow subgroups confirms this.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** The group $S_5$ (order$120$) is not solvable, since$A_5$ is a simple non-abelian normal subgroup. Hence$S_5$ lies outside the solvable world entirely.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}
The class of nilpotent groups is closed under subgroups, quotients, and finite direct products. The class of solvable groups likewise has these closure properties (as proved in Section 18.2.3).
\end{proposition}

The practical import of this hierarchy is substantial. Nilpotent groups decompose into $p$-groups and can be analyzed prime by prime (Section 18.3.3). Solvable groups, while not so cleanly decomposable, still admit inductive arguments via their composition factors: the Jordan–Hölder theorem (Section 18.1.3) guarantees that all composition factors are simple, and for solvable groups all composition factors are isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ for various primes$p$. This makes solvable groups amenable to inductive proofs that proceed factor by factor through a composition series.

The non-solvable simple groups — the alternating groups $A_n$ for$n \geq 5$, the groups of Lie type, and the twenty-six sporadic groups — lie entirely outside the solvable world. Their classification required over a century of work and thousands of pages of proof, culminating in the classification of finite simple groups (CFSG) in the 1980s.

Both nilpotency and solvability reappear prominently in Galois theory (Chapter 32), where the solvability of a polynomial by radicals is equivalent to the solvability of its Galois group. Towers of cyclic extensions correspond precisely to subnormal series with cyclic composition factors, and the intermediate fields of a solvable extension correspond to subgroups in the derived or lower central series.
