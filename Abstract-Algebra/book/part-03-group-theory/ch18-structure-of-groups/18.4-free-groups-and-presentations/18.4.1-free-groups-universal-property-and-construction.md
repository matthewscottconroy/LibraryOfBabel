# 18.4.1 Free Groups: Universal Property and Construction

All of the groups studied so far come with some ambient structure — an explicit operation rule, an embedding into a symmetric group, or a multiplication table. The concept of a free group takes a fundamentally different approach: we ask for the group with the fewest possible relations, the one that captures the bare combinatorial essence of having a given set of generators with no constraints whatsoever beyond the group axioms themselves.

\begin{definition}
The **free group** $F_S$ on a set$S$ is a group together with a function$\iota: S \to F_S$ satisfying the following **universal property**: for every group$G$ and every function$f: S \to G$, there exists a unique group homomorphism$\phi: F_S \to G$ such that$\phi \circ \iota = f$.
$$\begin{array}{ccc} S & \xrightarrow{\iota} & F_S \\ & \searrow^{f} & \downarrow^{\exists! \phi} \\ & & G \end{array}$$
\end{definition}

In other words, maps out of $F_S$ correspond bijectively with maps out of$S$ into any group$G$: specifying where the generators go uniquely determines the homomorphism, because there are no relations in$F_S$ to check for compatibility. This universal property determines$F_S$ uniquely up to isomorphism (as with any object defined by a universal property: if$F$ and$F'$ both satisfy it, the universal property gives mutually inverse maps between them).

The concrete construction confirms the intuition. Define $F_S$ to be the set of all **reduced words** in the alphabet$S \cup S^{-1}$ — finite sequences$s_1^{\epsilon_1} s_2^{\epsilon_2} \cdots s_k^{\epsilon_k}$ with$s_i \in S$,$\epsilon_i \in \{+1, -1\}$, and no consecutive cancellation: the sequence contains no adjacent pair$s^{+1}s^{-1}$ or$s^{-1}s^{+1}$ for any$s \in S$. The group operation is concatenation followed by reduction (cancellation of adjacent inverse pairs). The identity is the empty word$\varepsilon$.

\begin{proposition}
The group $F_S$ constructed above satisfies the universal property for free groups.
\end{proposition}

Given $f: S \to G$, define$\phi: F_S \to G$ by$\phi(s_1^{\epsilon_1} \cdots s_k^{\epsilon_k}) = f(s_1)^{\epsilon_1} \cdots f(s_k)^{\epsilon_k}$. This is the only possible definition that is a homomorphism extending$f$ (since every element of$F_S$ is a product of generators and their inverses), and it is well-defined because reduction in$F_S$ corresponds to the group axiom$f(s)f(s)^{-1} = e$ in$G$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** The free group $F_1 = F_{\{a\}}$ on one generator is isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}$: it consists of all words$a^n$ for$n \in \mathbb{Z}$ (using the convention$a^{-k} = (a^{-1})^k$), and the operation is addition of exponents. The universal property says: a group homomorphism$F_1 \to G$ is determined by the image of$a$, which can be any element of$G$ — this is equivalent to saying$\mathbb{Z}$ is the free abelian group on one generator.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** The free group $F_2 = F_{\{a,b\}}$ on two generators contains, for instance, the distinct reduced words$a$,$b$,$ab$,$ba$,$a^2$,$aba^{-1}$,$b^{-1}a^2b$, and so on. It is infinite (words of any length are distinct elements). The free group$F_2$ is non-abelian:$ab \neq ba$ (these are distinct reduced words). In fact$F_2$ is a remarkably rich object: it contains a free subgroup of rank$n$ for every$n \geq 1$ (and even a free subgroup of countably infinite rank).
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Every finitely generated group is a quotient of a free group. If $G = \langle g_1, \ldots, g_n \rangle$, the map$f: \{s_1, \ldots, s_n\} \to G$ with$f(s_i) = g_i$ extends by the universal property to a surjective homomorphism$\phi: F_n \to G$, where$F_n = F_{\{s_1,\ldots,s_n\}}$. Then$G \cong F_n / \ker\phi$. The kernel$\ker\phi$ is the normal subgroup of$F_n$ generated by all relations satisfied by the$g_i$ in$G$. This is the foundation for group presentations.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** The free group on $n \geq 2$ generators contains a copy of every countable group. More precisely, every countable group embeds into a quotient of$F_2$ (trivially, since$F_n$ embeds in$F_2$ for every$n$ via the Nielsen–Schreier theorem). The free group$F_2$ is, in a precise sense, the "generic" finitely generated group.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
The Nielsen–Schreier theorem states that every subgroup of a free group is free. This is a nontrivial result: it says that the free property is inherited by subgroups. The rank can increase: a subgroup of index $k$ in$F_n$ is free of rank$1 + k(n-1)$. For example, the commutator subgroup$[F_2, F_2] \leq F_2$ is free of infinite rank, even though$F_2$ has rank$2$.
\end{remark}

The free group on two or more generators is an extraordinarily rich object: it is infinite, non-abelian, torsion-free, and contains embedded copies of virtually every "freely behaving" algebraic structure. This last property, following from the universal property, is why every finitely generated group can be described by specifying generators and relations — the subject of the next section.
