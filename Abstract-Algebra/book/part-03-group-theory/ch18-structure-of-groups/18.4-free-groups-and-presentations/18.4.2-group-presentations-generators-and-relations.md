# 18.4.2 Group Presentations: Generators and Relations

Every group is a quotient of a free group, so every group can be described by specifying a free group and a normal subgroup to quotient out. A **group presentation** makes this description explicit and concise.

\begin{definition}
A **presentation** of a group $G$ is an expression$\langle S \mid R \rangle$ where$S$ is a set of **generators**,$R$ is a set of **relators** (words in$F_S$ that are required to equal the identity), and$G \cong F_S / N(R)$, where$N(R)$ is the **normal closure** of$R$ in$F_S$ — the smallest normal subgroup of$F_S$ containing all elements of$R$.
\end{definition}

A **relation** $r = 1$ (with$r \in R$) means that in the presented group, the element represented by the word$r$ equals the identity. Often relations are written in the form$u = v$, which is shorthand for the relator$uv^{-1}$. For example, the relation$ab = ba$ is the relator$aba^{-1}b^{-1} = [a,b]$, asserting that$a$ and$b$ commute.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** The cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ has presentation$\langle a \mid a^n \rangle$. The single relator$a^n$ forces$a$ to have order dividing$n$. Since the free group$F_{\{a\}} \cong \mathbb{Z}$ and$N(a^n) = n\mathbb{Z}$, the quotient is$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. With no relations at all,$\langle a \mid \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** The free abelian group $\mathbb{Z}^2$ has presentation$\langle a, b \mid aba^{-1}b^{-1} \rangle = \langle a, b \mid [a,b] \rangle$. The single relation forces commutativity of the two generators. In$F_2$, the normal closure of$[a,b]$ is the commutator subgroup$F_2' = [F_2, F_2]$, and$F_2 / F_2' \cong \mathbb{Z}^2$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** The dihedral group $D_n$ has presentation
$$\langle r, s \mid r^n, s^2, (sr)^2 \rangle \quad \text{or equivalently} \quad \langle r, s \mid r^n = s^2 = e,\; srs^{-1} = r^{-1} \rangle.$$
The three relators say: $r$ has order dividing$n$,$s$ has order dividing$2$, and$s$ inverts$r$ by conjugation. One verifies that the presented group has order$2n$ by showing every element reduces to$r^k$ or$sr^k$ for$0 \leq k < n$, and these$2n$ elements are distinct.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** The quaternion group $Q_8 = \{\pm 1, \pm i, \pm j, \pm k\}$ has presentation$\langle i, j \mid i^4, i^2 j^{-2}, ijij^{-1} \rangle$. One way to read this:$i$ has order dividing$4$,$i^2 = j^2$ (which forces both to be the element$-1$), and$ij = ji^{-1} = ji^3$ (non-commutativity). From this presentation one derives$k = ij$,$k^2 = i^2 = j^2 = -1$, and the full multiplication table of$Q_8$.
\end{example}

The power of presentations lies in making the structure of a group explicit and checkable in principle. Given a presentation, one can:
- **Read off generators**: the group is generated by $S$ with the listed relations.
- **Identify quotients**: adding more relators to a presentation gives a quotient group.
- **Recognize subgroups**: specifying which words in $F_S$ generate a subgroup, together with the inherited relations, presents that subgroup.
- **Compute abelianizations**: the abelianization $G^{\mathrm{ab}} = G/[G,G]$ is presented by$\langle S \mid R \cup \{[s_i, s_j] : s_i, s_j \in S\} \rangle$, and then simplified by the theory of finitely generated abelian groups (Chapter 19).

\begin{remark}
Presentations are the natural language for geometric group theory, the theory of Coxeter groups, and fundamental groups in topology. The fundamental group $\pi_1(\Sigma_g)$ of a closed orientable surface of genus$g$ has presentation
$$\langle a_1, b_1, \ldots, a_g, b_g \mid [a_1, b_1][a_2, b_2] \cdots [a_g, b_g] \rangle,$$
a single-relator group that encodes the topology of the surface. In this context, generators correspond to loops and relations encode how loops compose in the surface.
\end{remark}

Working within a presented group can be subtle. Verifying that two words $u, v \in F_S$ represent the same element of$\langle S \mid R \rangle$ requires showing$uv^{-1} \in N(R)$, which may require an arbitrarily long sequence of relation applications. As Section 18.4.4 will explain, this "word problem" is not algorithmically solvable for general finite presentations — a deep result connecting abstract algebra to the theory of computation.
