# 18.4.3 Examples: Dihedral, Quaternion, Triangle Groups

Concrete presentations make the structure of familiar groups transparent and demonstrate how relations constrain a group's behavior. We examine three important families in detail, showing how to extract structural information directly from a presentation.

## The Dihedral Groups $D_n$

The dihedral group $D_n$ — the symmetry group of a regular$n$-gon — has presentation
$$D_n = \langle r, s \mid r^n = s^2 = e,\; srs^{-1} = r^{-1} \rangle.$$
Here $r$ is a counterclockwise rotation by$2\pi/n$ and$s$ is a reflection. The relation$srs^{-1} = r^{-1}$ encodes the geometric fact that conjugating a rotation by a reflection reverses its orientation. Every element of$D_n$ can be written uniquely as$r^k$ or$sr^k$ for$0 \leq k < n$, giving$|D_n| = 2n$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** From the presentation of $D_4$, compute the center and the derived subgroup. The center consists of elements commuting with both$r$ and$s$. For$r^k$:$sr^ks^{-1} = r^{-k}$, so$r^k$ is central iff$r^{-k} = r^k$, i.e.,$r^{2k} = e$, i.e.,$n \mid 2k$. For$n = 4$, this gives$k \in \{0, 2\}$, so$Z(D_4) = \{e, r^2\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. For the derived subgroup:$[r, s] = r^{-1}s^{-1}rs = r^{-1} \cdot r^{-1} = r^{-2}$ (using$s^{-1}rs = r^{-1}$). So$D_4' \ni r^2$. Since$r^2$ has order$2$ and$D_4/\langle r^2 \rangle \cong V_4$ is abelian,$D_4' = \langle r^2 \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** The presentation is minimal for $n \geq 3$: removing any single relation gives an infinite group. Without$r^n = e$: the group$\langle r, s \mid s^2 = e, srs = r^{-1} \rangle$ is the infinite dihedral group$D_\infty$, the symmetry group of$\mathbb{Z}$ under reflections and translations. Without$s^2 = e$: the presented group is infinite. Without$srs^{-1} = r^{-1}$: we get$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, which is abelian and larger. All three relations are needed to pin down the finite group$D_n$.
\end{example}

## The Quaternion Group $Q_8$

The quaternion group $Q_8 = \{\pm 1, \pm i, \pm j, \pm k\}$ with$i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1$ has presentation
$$Q_8 = \langle i, j \mid i^4 = e,\; i^2 = j^2,\; ij = ji^{-1} \rangle.$$
Setting $k = ij$, one derives$k^2 = ijij = i(ji)j = i(i^{-1}j^{-1})j = j^{-1}j = \ldots$ more carefully,$k^2 = (ij)^2 = ijij$, and using$ji = i^{-1}j$ (from$ij = ji^{-1}$ rearranged) gives$ijij = i(ji)j = i(i^{-1}j)j = j^2 = i^2$. With$i^4 = e$ and$i^2 = j^2$, we get$i^2 = -1$ (interpreting order-$4$ generator).

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Compare $D_4$ and$Q_8$: both have order$8$, both have center of order$2$, and both are non-abelian. Yet they are not isomorphic. From the presentations:$D_4$ has an element of order$2$ outside the center (any reflection$sr^k$ has order$2$), while$Q_8$ has only one element of order$2$, namely$-1 = i^2 = j^2 = k^2$. This is detectable from the presentations: in$D_4$, the relation$s^2 = e$ produces many order-$2$ elements, while in$Q_8$ the relation$i^4 = e$ with$i^2 = j^2$ implies all non-identity elements of order$\leq 2$ lie in$\langle i^2 \rangle$.
\end{example}

## Triangle Groups

The **triangle group** $\Delta(p, q, r)$ with$p, q, r \geq 2$ (integers or$\infty$) is defined by
$$\Delta(p,q,r) = \langle a, b, c \mid a^p = b^q = c^r = abc = e \rangle.$$
Geometrically, $\Delta(p,q,r)$ is the symmetry group of a tiling of a simply connected surface by triangles with angles$\pi/p$,$\pi/q$,$\pi/r$.

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** The group $\Delta(2, 3, 3)$: the angle sum is$\pi/2 + \pi/3 + \pi/3 = 7\pi/6 > \pi$, so this is a spherical triangle group. Indeed$\Delta(2,3,3) \cong A_4$ (the rotation group of a tetrahedron), of order$12$. We can verify: with$a^2 = b^3 = c^3 = abc = e$, the presented group has order$12$ (by a coset enumeration), and its composition factors are$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ and$\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$, matching$A_4$.
\par
The other finite triangle groups correspond to the Platonic solids: $\Delta(2,3,4) \cong S_4$ (octahedron/cube, order$24$),$\Delta(2,3,5) \cong A_5$ (icosahedron/dodecahedron, order$60$), and$\Delta(2,2,n) \cong D_n$ (order$2n$).
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 5.** The group $\Delta(2,3,6)$: the angle sum is$\pi/2 + \pi/3 + \pi/6 = \pi$, exactly. This is a Euclidean triangle group — the symmetry group of the triangular tiling of the plane, an infinite group. The group$\Delta(2,3,7)$ with$1/2 + 1/3 + 1/7 = 41/42 < 1$ is a hyperbolic triangle group, infinite and related to the remarkable$84(g-1)$ bound on automorphisms of a compact Riemann surface of genus$g$.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
These examples illustrate the general principle: presentations link group theory to geometry. The angle-sum condition $1/p + 1/q + 1/r > 1$,$= 1$, or$< 1$ determines whether the group is finite (spherical), infinite and Euclidean, or infinite and hyperbolic. From the algebraic side, the fineness of the presentation determines the richness of the group — more relators constrain the group more, producing smaller groups (or more relations among the generators).
\end{remark}
