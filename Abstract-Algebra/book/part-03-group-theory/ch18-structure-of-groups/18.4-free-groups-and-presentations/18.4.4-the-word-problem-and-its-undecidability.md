# 18.4.4 The Word Problem and Its Undecidability

Given a finitely presented group $G = \langle S \mid R \rangle$ and a word$w$ in the generators$S \cup S^{-1}$, the **word problem** asks: does$w$ represent the identity element in$G$? Equivalently, does$w$ lie in the normal closure$N(R) \leq F_S$? Can$w$ be transformed into the empty word by applying the relations in$R$ and their consequences a finite number of times?

\begin{definition}
The word problem for a finitely presented group $G = \langle S \mid R \rangle$ is the decision problem: given a word$w \in (S \cup S^{-1})^*$, determine whether$w =_G e$.
\end{definition}

## Groups with Solvable Word Problems

For many specific groups, the word problem is effectively decidable by an algorithm.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** In the free group $F_S$, the word problem is trivially solvable: reduce$w$ by cancelling adjacent inverse pairs. The resulting reduced word equals$e$ if and only if$w = e$ in$F_S$. This runs in time linear in the length of$w$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** In $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} = \langle a \mid a^n \rangle$, the word$a^k$ represents the identity iff$n \mid k$. Reduce$w = a^k$ by computing$k \bmod n$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** For finite groups, the word problem is trivially solvable: compute the element represented by $w$ by repeated multiplication in the (finite) group, and check whether the result is the identity. This algorithm terminates in time polynomial in$|w|$ and$|G|$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** The word problem is solvable for all **hyperbolic groups** in the sense of Gromov — a large, geometrically natural class of infinite groups including free groups, fundamental groups of closed hyperbolic manifolds, and many Coxeter groups. The algorithm exploits the "thin triangles" property of the Cayley graph, which gives an effective normal form for elements.
\end{example}

## Undecidability: Novikov and Boone

The situation for general finitely presented groups is dramatically different.

\begin{theorem}[Novikov 1955; Boone 1958]
There exists a finitely presented group $G$ whose word problem is undecidable: no algorithm can, given an arbitrary word$w \in (S \cup S^{-1})^*$, correctly determine in finite time whether$w =_G e$.
\end{theorem}

The proof encodes computations of a Turing machine into the relations of a group. Specifically, one constructs a group $G$ such that$w =_G e$ if and only if a given Turing machine halts on a given input. Since the halting problem is undecidable (Turing, 1936), the word problem for$G$ is undecidable. This is not merely a practical limitation — it is a mathematical theorem that no algorithm exists, provable from Gödel-style incompleteness arguments.

\begin{remark}
The undecidability is deep in the following sense: it is not that we have not yet found an algorithm; rather, the existence of such an algorithm would imply the decidability of the halting problem, which is provably impossible. The construction of $G$ involves Higman's embedding theorem (every recursively presented group embeds in a finitely presented group) as a key ingredient.
\end{remark}

## Consequences for Group Theory

The undecidability of the word problem cascades to make many other natural group-theoretic decision problems undecidable.

\begin{proposition}
The following decision problems are undecidable for finitely presented groups:
1. The **isomorphism problem**: given presentations $\langle S \mid R \rangle$ and$\langle S' \mid R' \rangle$, are the presented groups isomorphic?
2. The **conjugacy problem**: given words $u, v$, does there exist$w$ with$w u w^{-1} =_G v$?
3. The **subgroup membership problem**: given words $u, g_1, \ldots, g_k$, is$u$ in the subgroup generated by$g_1, \ldots, g_k$?
\end{proposition}

The isomorphism problem is undecidable because the trivial group has an undecidable word problem (as a quotient of any group with undecidable word problem), and recognizing the trivial group — determining whether a given presentation presents the trivial group — is itself undecidable.

\begin{example}
**Example 5.** The group $G = \langle a, b, c, d \mid a^{-1}ba = b^2,\; b^{-1}cb = c^2,\; c^{-1}dc = d^2,\; d^{-1}ad = a^2 \rangle$ (the Baumslag–Solitar-type group) has an undecidable word problem. In contrast, each individual relation has a clear interpretation, and the group looks manageable — yet no algorithm can determine whether an arbitrary word equals the identity.
\end{example}

The contrast between the richness of group theory's structural results — the Sylow theorems, the Jordan–Hölder theorem, the classification of finite simple groups — and the algorithmic intractability of even basic questions about presented groups is one of the most intellectually striking features of modern algebra. Structural theorems tell us what groups look like from a bird's-eye view; decision problems ask whether we can navigate the group algorithmically, and often the answer is no.
