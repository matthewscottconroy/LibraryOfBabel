# 18.4 Free Groups and Presentations

Every group we have encountered so far has been presented concretely — as permutations, matrices, or residues. But there is a universal approach: describing a group by the symbols one uses to generate it and the algebraic relations those generators must satisfy. This approach requires first constructing the "freest possible" group on a given set of generators, one with no relations at all beyond those forced by the group axioms.

The free group $F_S$ on a set $S$ is characterized by a universal property: any function $f: S \to G$ from $S$ to any group $G$ extends uniquely to a group homomorphism $F_S \to G$. Concretely, $F_S$ consists of all reduced words — finite sequences of symbols $s$ and $s^{-1}$ for $s \in S$, with no cancellable adjacent pairs — under concatenation followed by reduction. The free group on two generators $F_2 = \langle a, b \rangle$ is a remarkable object: it is countably infinite, non-abelian, contains every countable group as a quotient, and contains every free group as a subgroup. Subgroups of free groups are themselves free (the Nielsen–Schreier theorem), and the rank of a subgroup of index $n$ in $F_r$ is $1 + n(r-1)$ — a formula that has analogs in the theory of Riemann surfaces and covering spaces.

A group presentation $\langle S \mid R \rangle$ defines a group as the quotient $F_S / \langle\langle R \rangle\rangle$, where $\langle\langle R \rangle\rangle$ is the normal closure of the set of relators $R \subseteq F_S$ (words in $F_S$ that are declared to equal the identity). Every group has a presentation (possibly with infinitely many generators and relators), and finite presentations — those with finitely many of both — are the focus for decidability questions. The fundamental decision problem of combinatorial group theory, the word problem, asks: given a finite presentation and a word $w$ in the generators, does $w = e$ in the group? Novikov and Boone proved in the 1950s that the word problem is undecidable in general — there exist finitely presented groups for which no algorithm can determine whether a word represents the identity. This result connects group theory directly to the theory of computation and Turing's undecidability theorems.
