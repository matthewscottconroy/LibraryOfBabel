# Chapter 18 — Structure of Groups

The Sylow theorems tell us where to look for subgroups of prime-power order; this chapter asks the deeper question of how a group is assembled from its irreducible pieces. The guiding analogy is the factorization of integers: every integer greater than 1 factors uniquely into primes, and primes are precisely the integers with no non-trivial factorizations. For groups, the role of primes is played by simple groups — groups with no proper non-trivial normal subgroups — and the role of unique factorization is played by the Jordan–Hölder theorem. Understanding how groups are built from simple groups is the program that has driven finite group theory from Galois to the present day, culminating in the Classification of Finite Simple Groups, one of the greatest collective achievements in mathematical history.

A composition series for a group $G$ is a chain $G = G_0 \supset G_1 \supset \cdots \supset G_r = \{e\}$ where each $G_{i+1}$ is normal in $G_i$ and each quotient $G_i/G_{i+1}$ is simple. The quotients $G_i/G_{i+1}$ are the composition factors, and they are the "prime factors" of $G$. The Jordan–Hölder theorem guarantees that any two composition series of $G$ have the same length and the same multiset of composition factors up to isomorphism. This is the group-theoretic analogue of unique prime factorization: while the group $G$ may not be uniquely recoverable from its composition factors (the extension problem is in general unsolvable), the factors themselves are a complete invariant of the series. For $S_4$, for instance, the composition factors are $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, and $\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ — appearing in every composition series, whatever order they are encountered.

Solvable groups are those whose composition factors are all cyclic of prime order. Equivalently, $G$ is solvable if and only if its derived series $G \supset [G,G] \supset [[G,G],[G,G]] \supset \cdots$ eventually reaches the trivial group. The connection to Galois theory is direct: a polynomial equation is solvable by radicals if and only if its Galois group is solvable. Every group of order $p^a q^b$ is solvable (Burnside's theorem, proved using character theory), and the Feit–Thompson theorem of 1963 — proved in 255 pages — established that every group of odd order is solvable. Together these results mean that the only obstacles to solvability are even-order simple groups, and the smallest of these is $A_5$ of order 60. Nilpotent groups are an even more restricted class: they satisfy the stronger condition that the lower central series $G \supset [G,G_0] \supset [G,G_1] \supset \cdots$ reaches $\{e\}$. Finite nilpotent groups are characterized by the beautiful theorem that they are precisely the direct products of their Sylow subgroups, placing $p$-groups at the center of nilpotency theory.

The chapter closes with free groups and presentations, which provide the language for defining and studying groups by generators and relations. The free group $F_S$ on a set $S$ is the "most general" group generated by $S$: it satisfies no relations except those forced by the group axioms, and every group generated by $S$ is a quotient of $F_S$. A presentation $\langle S \mid R \rangle$ defines a group as the quotient of $F_S$ by the normal closure of a set of relators $R$, and this language captures everything from cyclic groups ($\langle a \mid a^n \rangle$) to dihedral groups ($\langle r, s \mid r^n, s^2, srs^{-1}r \rangle$) to the triangle groups that appear in the theory of Riemann surfaces. The word problem — whether two words in a presentation represent the same group element — is in general algorithmically undecidable, a profound connection between group theory and mathematical logic.
