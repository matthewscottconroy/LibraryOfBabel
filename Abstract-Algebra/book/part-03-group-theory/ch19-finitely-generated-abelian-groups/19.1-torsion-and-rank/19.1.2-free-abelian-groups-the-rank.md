# 19.1.2 Free Abelian Groups; the Rank

The simplest torsion-free abelian groups are the free abelian groups. These are the abelian analogues of free groups: they are generated by a set of elements with no relations among them beyond those forced by the abelian group axioms (commutativity and the group laws). They are the "$\mathbb{Z}$-vector spaces" of the theory.

\begin{definition}
A **free abelian group of rank $r$** is a group isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}^r = \mathbb{Z} \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathbb{Z}$ ($r$ copies). A **basis** for a free abelian group$F$ is a set$\{e_1, \ldots, e_r\}$ of generators such that every element of$F$ can be written uniquely as$n_1 e_1 + \cdots + n_r e_r$ with$n_i \in \mathbb{Z}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
Any two bases of a free abelian group $F$ have the same cardinality, called the **rank**$\mathrm{rank}(F)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For a prime $p$, the quotient$F/pF$ is a vector space over$\mathbb{F}_p = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$: if$F \cong \mathbb{Z}^r$, then$pF \cong p\mathbb{Z}^r$ and$F/pF \cong (\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^r$, a vector space of dimension$r$ over$\mathbb{F}_p$. Since the dimension of a vector space is a well-defined isomorphism invariant,$r$ is independent of the choice of basis.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** The group $\mathbb{Z}^3$ has rank$3$, with standard basis$\{(1,0,0), (0,1,0), (0,0,1)\}$. Another basis is$\{(1,0,0), (1,1,0), (1,1,1)\}$: any$(a,b,c) \in \mathbb{Z}^3$ is written as$(a-b)(1,0,0) + (b-c)(1,1,0) + c(1,1,1)$, and this expression is unique. Both are valid bases of the same free abelian group.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** The group $2\mathbb{Z} \oplus 3\mathbb{Z} \leq \mathbb{Z}^2$ is a free abelian group of rank$2$ with basis$\{(2,0),(0,3)\}$. It is a subgroup of$\mathbb{Z}^2$ (also rank$2$), illustrating that subgroups of free abelian groups are free abelian of rank at most equal to the ambient rank.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** The group $\mathbb{Z}$ itself is free abelian of rank$1$. Its only bases are$\{1\}$ and$\{-1\}$. The subgroup$n\mathbb{Z}$ for$n \geq 1$ is free abelian of rank$1$ with basis$\{n\}$ (or$\{-n\}$). The quotient$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is not free abelian (it has torsion), consistent with the fact that quotients of free abelian groups need not be free.
\end{example}

\begin{theorem}
Every subgroup of a free abelian group of rank $r$ is free abelian of rank at most$r$. Equivalently, every finitely generated torsion-free abelian group is free abelian.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
By induction on $r$. For$r = 1$: every subgroup of$\mathbb{Z}$ is of the form$n\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}$ (free of rank$1$) or$\{0\}$ (free of rank$0$). For$r > 1$: let$\pi: \mathbb{Z}^r \to \mathbb{Z}$ be projection onto the first coordinate. The image$\pi(H)$ is a subgroup of$\mathbb{Z}$, hence free of rank$\leq 1$. The kernel$H \cap \ker\pi \leq \mathbb{Z}^{r-1}$ is free of rank$\leq r-1$ by induction. The short exact sequence$0 \to H \cap \ker\pi \to H \to \pi(H) \to 0$ splits (since$\pi(H)$ is free, being a subgroup of$\mathbb{Z}$), so$H \cong (H \cap \ker\pi) \oplus \pi(H)$ is free of rank$\leq r$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** Subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}^2$: the subgroup$H = \{(a,b) : 2 \mid a+b\}$ is the kernel of the map$(a,b) \mapsto (a+b) \bmod 2$, hence has index$2$ in$\mathbb{Z}^2$. By the theorem,$H$ is free abelian of rank$2$. A basis is$\{(2,0),(1,1)\}$: any$(a,b) \in H$ (with$a \equiv b \pmod{2}$) writes as$\frac{a-b}{2}(2,0) + b(1,1)$, unique since the matrix$\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ has determinant$2 \neq 0$.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
The rank of a finitely generated torsion-free abelian group equals the dimension of the rational vector space $A \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{Q}$. This provides a coordinate-free definition of rank and connects abelian group theory to commutative algebra. In the language of modules, free abelian groups of rank$r$ are exactly the free$\mathbb{Z}$-modules of rank$r$, and their submodules are again free — reflecting the fact that$\mathbb{Z}$ is a principal ideal domain (and in particular, a hereditary ring).
\end{remark}
