# 19.1.3 Finitely Generated Groups: Generators and Relations

A finitely generated abelian group $A$ is one expressible as$A = \langle a_1, \ldots, a_n \rangle$, meaning every element is an integer linear combination$k_1 a_1 + \cdots + k_n a_n$. Choosing such a generating set defines a surjective homomorphism$\phi: \mathbb{Z}^n \to A$ by$\phi(e_i) = a_i$, and the kernel of$\phi$ encodes all relations among the generators.

\begin{definition}
Let $A$ be a finitely generated abelian group with generating set$\{a_1, \ldots, a_n\}$. A **relation** among the generators is a tuple$(k_1, \ldots, k_n) \in \mathbb{Z}^n$ such that$k_1 a_1 + \cdots + k_n a_n = 0$ in$A$. The set of all relations is$\ker\phi \leq \mathbb{Z}^n$.
\end{definition}

Since $\mathbb{Z}$ is a principal ideal domain, every subgroup of$\mathbb{Z}^n$ is free abelian of rank at most$n$ (Section 19.1.2). In particular,$\ker\phi \cong \mathbb{Z}^m$ for some$m \leq n$, and we can choose a basis$f_1, \ldots, f_m$ for$\ker\phi$. Writing$f_j = \sum_{i=1}^n M_{ij} e_i$ expresses the relations in terms of the standard basis of$\mathbb{Z}^n$, producing an$n \times m$ integer matrix$M$ with$A \cong \mathbb{Z}^n / \mathrm{im}(M)$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Let $A = \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, generated by$\bar{1}$. Then$n = 1$,$\phi: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ sends$k \mapsto \bar{k}$, and$\ker\phi = 6\mathbb{Z}$. The relation matrix is$M = (6)$, a$1 \times 1$ matrix. Indeed$A \cong \mathbb{Z}/\mathrm{im}(6) = \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Let $A = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$, with generators$a_1 = (\bar{1}, \bar{0})$ and$a_2 = (\bar{0}, \bar{1})$. Then$\phi: \mathbb{Z}^2 \to A$ by$\phi(e_1) = a_1$,$\phi(e_2) = a_2$. The kernel is generated by$f_1 = (2, 0)$ (the relation$2a_1 = 0$) and$f_2 = (0, 3)$ (the relation$3a_2 = 0$). The relation matrix is$M = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$. This is already in Smith normal form (diagonal with$2 \mid 3$... actually we need$d_1 \mid d_2$, and$2 \nmid 3$, so we would need to reorder or reduce). More precisely, we should apply Smith normal form to get$\mathrm{diag}(1, 6)$, giving$\mathbb{Z}/1\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, which is correct since$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ by the Chinese Remainder Theorem.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Let $A = \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, with generators$a_1 = (\bar{1},\bar{0})$ and$a_2 = (\bar{0},\bar{1})$. The relation matrix is$M = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$. Applying column operations: add column 2 to column 1, giving$\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 0 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, then subtract twice row 2 from row 1:$\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -4 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$... This is getting complicated; the systematic Smith normal form algorithm is described in Section 19.2.4. The key point is that different choices of generating sets correspond to row and column operations on$M$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** The group $A = \mathbb{Z}^2 / H$ where$H$ is generated by$(3, 1)$ and$(1, 2)$. The relation matrix is$M = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$. To find$A$, we compute the Smith normal form: swap columns to get$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, subtract twice row 1 from row 2:$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 0 & -5 \end{pmatrix}$, subtract three times column 1 from column 2:$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -5 \end{pmatrix}$, negate column 2:$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$. Hence$A \cong \mathbb{Z}/1\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$.
\end{example}

The key point is that choosing different generating sets for $A$ and different bases for$\ker\phi$ corresponds precisely to performing row and column operations on$M$. Row operations correspond to changing the basis of$\mathbb{Z}^n$ (i.e., changing the generators of$A$); column operations correspond to changing the basis of$\ker\phi$ (i.e., choosing different "fundamental relations"). Since we work over$\mathbb{Z}$ rather than a field, the available operations are integer row and column operations (adding multiples of one row/column to another, swapping, negating), but this is sufficient to bring$M$ to a canonical diagonal form — **Smith normal form** — as explained in Section 19.2.4.

\begin{remark}
The presentation $A \cong \mathbb{Z}^n / \mathrm{im}(M)$ connects the classification of finitely generated abelian groups to the theory of integer matrices. The invariants of$A$ — rank and torsion — become properties of$M$ that are preserved under integer row and column operations. This reduction of abstract algebra to concrete linear algebra over$\mathbb{Z}$ is what makes the structure theorem both provable and computationally effective.
\end{remark}
