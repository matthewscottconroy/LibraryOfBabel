# 19.2.1 Statement: $\mathbb{Z}^r \oplus \mathbb{Z}/d_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathbb{Z}/d_k$

The fundamental theorem of finitely generated abelian groups is one of the most complete classification results in all of algebra. It asserts that every finitely generated abelian group is, up to isomorphism, one of a specific list, and that no two groups on the list are isomorphic. This reduces the classification problem — which might seem intractable given the variety of possible generating sets and relations — to a finite combinatorial problem.

\begin{theorem}[Fundamental Theorem of Finitely Generated Abelian Groups]
Every finitely generated abelian group $A$ is isomorphic to a direct sum
$$A \cong \mathbb{Z}^r \oplus \mathbb{Z}/d_1\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/d_2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathbb{Z}/d_k\mathbb{Z}$$
where $r \geq 0$ is a non-negative integer and$d_1, d_2, \ldots, d_k \geq 2$ are integers satisfying$d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_k$. Moreover, the pair$(r; d_1, \ldots, d_k)$ is uniquely determined by$A$.
\end{theorem}

The integer $r$ is the **rank** (or **Betti number**) of$A$; it equals the rank of the free part$A/T(A)$. The integers$d_1, \ldots, d_k$ are the **invariant factors** of$A$. The divisibility condition$d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_k$ is the canonical ordering condition that makes the decomposition unique. A term$\mathbb{Z}/d_i\mathbb{Z}$ with$d_i = 1$ contributes trivially (the trivial group), so we require$d_i \geq 2$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** The group $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$. Here$r = 0$ and the single invariant factor is$d_1 = 12$. By the Chinese Remainder Theorem,$\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$. This is the primary decomposition form; the invariant factor form$\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ is a single summand.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Abelian groups of order $36 = 2^2 \cdot 3^2$. The invariant factor form requires the invariant factors$d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots$ with product$36$ and each$d_i \geq 2$. The possibilities:
- $d_1 = 36$: the cyclic group$\mathbb{Z}/36\mathbb{Z}$.
- $d_1 = 2, d_2 = 18$: the group$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/18\mathbb{Z}$ (check$2 \mid 18$: yes).
- $d_1 = 6, d_2 = 6$: the group$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ (check$6 \mid 6$: yes).
- $d_1 = 2, d_2 = 2, \ldots$: we need product$36$, but$2 \cdot 2 \cdot d = 36$ gives$d = 9$, and$2 \mid 2 \mid 9$ fails since$2 \nmid 9$. So this does not give a valid invariant factor sequence.
\par
Hence there are exactly three abelian groups of order $36$:$\mathbb{Z}/36\mathbb{Z}$,$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/18\mathbb{Z}$, and$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** The group $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}$ has rank$r = 1$ and invariant factors$d_1 = 2$,$d_2 = 4$ (since$2 \mid 4$). It is distinct from$\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}$ (single invariant factor$8$) and$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}$ (which is not valid since$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ has product$8$ but we would need$2 \mid 2 \mid 2$, which holds, so this is valid with invariant factors$2,2,2$). The three groups$\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$,$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$,$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ are the three distinct abelian groups of order$8 = 2^3$, corresponding to the partitions$\{3\}$,$\{1,2\}$,$\{1,1,1\}$ of$3$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** An infinite finitely generated abelian group: $A = \mathbb{Z}^2 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$. Here$r = 2$ and$d_1 = 3$. The torsion subgroup$T(A) = \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ and the free part has rank$2$. This group cannot be isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}^2 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/9\mathbb{Z}$ (different invariant factor) or$\mathbb{Z}^3 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ (different rank).
\end{example}

To appreciate the theorem's scope: the two groups $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ and$\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$ both have order$8$, both are abelian, but they are non-isomorphic because their invariant factors differ ($2,4$ vs.$8$). The key distinguishing feature is that$\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$ has an element of order$8$, while the maximum order in$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ is$4$ (achieved by$(0,1)$). The invariant factors encode exactly this kind of information.
