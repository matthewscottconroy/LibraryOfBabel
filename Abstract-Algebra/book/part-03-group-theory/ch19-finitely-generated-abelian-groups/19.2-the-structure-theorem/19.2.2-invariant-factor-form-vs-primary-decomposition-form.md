# 19.2.2 Invariant Factor Form vs. Primary Decomposition Form

The structure theorem can be stated in two equivalent forms, each with its own advantages. Fluency in both, and the ability to convert between them, is essential for applying the theorem in practice.

The **invariant factor form** expresses the torsion part of $A$ as
$$T(A) \cong \mathbb{Z}/d_1\mathbb{Z} \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathbb{Z}/d_k\mathbb{Z}, \quad d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_k \geq 2.$$
This form is canonical: the divisibility chain uniquely pins down the group. It is most useful for theoretical arguments involving the entire group simultaneously, such as computing $\mathrm{Hom}(A, B)$ or checking whether one group is a quotient of another.

The **primary decomposition form** (or **elementary divisor form**) breaks each invariant factor into prime-power components using the Chinese Remainder Theorem: if $d_i = p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_m^{a_m}$, then$\mathbb{Z}/d_i\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/p_1^{a_1}\mathbb{Z} \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathbb{Z}/p_m^{a_m}\mathbb{Z}$. Collecting all prime-power summands:
$$A \cong \mathbb{Z}^r \oplus \bigoplus_p \left(\mathbb{Z}/p^{e_{p,1}}\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/p^{e_{p,2}}\mathbb{Z} \oplus \cdots\right), \quad e_{p,1} \leq e_{p,2} \leq \cdots$$
The integers $p^{e_{p,j}}$ are the **elementary divisors**. This form is most convenient when analyzing one prime at a time, since the summands for different primes are completely independent.

\begin{theorem}[Equivalence]
The invariant factor form and the elementary divisor form determine each other, and each uniquely determines $A$ up to isomorphism.
\end{theorem}

**Converting from elementary divisors to invariant factors** (Frobenius method): Arrange the elementary divisors by prime and by increasing exponent. Form the invariant factors by taking, at each stage, the least common multiple of one entry from each prime's list (taking the largest available at each step).

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Elementary divisors: $2, 4, 3, 9$. Sort by prime:$2$-primary$= \{2^1, 2^2\}$,$3$-primary$= \{3^1, 3^2\}$. Take the largest from each prime at the last step:$\mathrm{lcm}(4, 9) = 36$. Take the next largest:$\mathrm{lcm}(2, 3) = 6$. Invariant factors:$6 \mid 36$. Check:$6 \cdot 36 = 216 = 2^3 \cdot 3^3$... but our elementary divisors give order$2 \cdot 4 \cdot 3 \cdot 9 = 216$. And indeed$6 \mid 36$. The group is$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/36\mathbb{Z}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Elementary divisors: $4, 4, 2, 3, 3$. Sort:$2$-primary$= \{2, 4, 4\}$ (sorted:$2 \leq 4 \leq 4$),$3$-primary$= \{3, 3\}$ (sorted:$3 \leq 3$). The$3$-primary list has only two entries, so we can have at most two invariant factors (pad the shorter lists with$1$'s):
- Step 1 (largest): $\mathrm{lcm}(4, 3) = 12$.
- Step 2 (next): $\mathrm{lcm}(4, 3) = 12$.
- Step 3 (remaining): $\mathrm{lcm}(2, 1) = 2$ (no more$3$-primary entries, use$1$).
Invariant factors: $2 \mid 12 \mid 12$. The group is$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$. Check: order$= 2 \cdot 12 \cdot 12 = 288 = 2^5 \cdot 3^2$, and our elementary divisors give$4 \cdot 4 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 = 288$. Consistent.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Invariant factors $\to$ elementary divisors:$A = \mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/60\mathbb{Z}$ (check$12 \mid 60$: yes). Factor each:$12 = 2^2 \cdot 3$ and$60 = 2^2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5$. Elementary divisors:$4, 4$ (from$2^2$ in each),$3, 3$ (from$3^1$ in each),$5$ (from$5^1$ in second only). Primary decomposition:$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** Abelian groups of order $p^4$ for a prime$p$. The elementary divisors are a multiset of prime powers$p^{a_1}, p^{a_2}, \ldots$ with$\sum a_i = 4$. This corresponds to partitions of$4$:
| Partition of $4$ | Elementary divisors | Invariant factors | Group |
|---|---|---|---|
| $4$ |$p^4$ |$p^4$ |$\mathbb{Z}/p^4\mathbb{Z}$ |
| $3+1$ |$p^3, p$ |$p \mid p^3$ |$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/p^3\mathbb{Z}$ |
| $2+2$ |$p^2, p^2$ |$p^2 \mid p^2$ |$\mathbb{Z}/p^2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/p^2\mathbb{Z}$ |
| $2+1+1$ |$p^2, p, p$ |$p \mid p \mid p^2$ |$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/p^2\mathbb{Z}$ |
| $1+1+1+1$ |$p,p,p,p$ |$p \mid p \mid p \mid p$ |$(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^4$ |
\par
Five isomorphism classes in all, one for each partition of $4$.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
In practice, the elementary divisor form is most convenient for classifying all groups of a given order (since different primes factor independently), while the invariant factor form is most convenient for computing homomorphisms and quotients. Converting between them is a routine combinatorial exercise using the Frobenius method above.
\end{remark}
