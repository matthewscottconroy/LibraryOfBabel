# 19.2.3 Uniqueness of the Invariant Factors

The structure theorem asserts not only that a finitely generated abelian group decomposes as $\mathbb{Z}^r \oplus \mathbb{Z}/d_1\mathbb{Z} \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathbb{Z}/d_k\mathbb{Z}$ but also that this decomposition is **unique**: the rank$r$ and the invariant factor sequence$d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_k$ are invariants of$A$, not artifacts of a particular choice of basis or generating set. Without uniqueness, we would have a decomposition theorem but not a genuine classification theorem.

\begin{theorem}[Uniqueness]
If $A \cong \mathbb{Z}^r \oplus \mathbb{Z}/d_1\mathbb{Z} \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathbb{Z}/d_k\mathbb{Z}$ and$A \cong \mathbb{Z}^s \oplus \mathbb{Z}/e_1\mathbb{Z} \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathbb{Z}/e_\ell\mathbb{Z}$ with$d_1 \mid \cdots \mid d_k$ and$e_1 \mid \cdots \mid e_\ell$, then$r = s$ and$k = \ell$ and$d_i = e_i$ for all$i$.
\end{theorem}

**Proof of uniqueness of $r$.** The torsion subgroup$T(A)$ is an invariant of$A$ (it does not depend on the decomposition). Passing to$A/T(A) \cong \mathbb{Z}^r$ recovers the free part, and the rank of a free abelian group is well-defined (Section 19.1.2). So$r = s$.

**Proof of uniqueness of invariant factors via $p$-ranks.** Work one prime$p$ at a time. Define$A[p] = \{a \in A : pa = 0\}$, the **$p$-torsion** subgroup of$A$. This is an$\mathbb{F}_p$-vector space. In the decomposition$A \cong \mathbb{Z}^r \oplus \bigoplus_i \mathbb{Z}/d_i\mathbb{Z}$, the$p$-torsion is$A[p] \cong \bigoplus_{\{i\,:\, p \mid d_i\}} \mathbb{F}_p$. Thus:
$$\dim_{\mathbb{F}_p} A[p] = |\{i : p \mid d_i\}|.$$
This quantity is intrinsic to $A$ (it does not depend on the decomposition), and it equals the number of invariant factors divisible by$p$. More refined: for each prime$p$ and each$j \geq 1$, define
$$f_j(p) = \dim_{\mathbb{F}_p}\!\left(p^{j-1}A[p^j] / p^j A[p^{j+1}]\right),$$
where $A[p^j] = \{a \in A : p^j a = 0\}$. Then$f_j(p)$ counts the number of elementary divisors equal to$p^j$, and all these quantities are intrinsic to$A$.

\begin{proposition}
The elementary divisors of $A$ are exactly the prime powers$p^j$ appearing with multiplicity$f_j(p)$, for all primes$p$ and$j \geq 1$.
\end{proposition}

Since the elementary divisors determine the invariant factors (by the Frobenius method of Section 19.2.2), and the elementary divisors are intrinsic to $A$, the invariant factors are uniquely determined by$A$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Let $A = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$. Then$A[2] = \{(a,b) : 2a = 0, 2b = 0\} = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \{0, 2\} \cong (\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^2$, so$\dim_{\mathbb{F}_2} A[2] = 2$. And$A[4] = A$ since$4$ kills both summands. We get$f_1(2) = \dim A[2] / (2 A[4]/A[2]) = \ldots$ concretely: the elementary divisors are$2$ and$4$, and$f_1(2) = 1$ (one divisor equal to$2^1$) and$f_2(2) = 1$ (one divisor equal to$2^2$). The invariant factors are$2 \mid 4$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Distinguish $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ and$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. Both have order$4$. For$B = \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$:$B[2] = \{0, 2\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$,$\dim_{\mathbb{F}_2} B[2] = 1$. For$C = (\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^2$:$C[2] = C \cong (\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^2$,$\dim_{\mathbb{F}_2} C[2] = 2$. Since the$p$-ranks differ,$B \not\cong C$. The structure theorem confirms:$B$ has invariant factor$\{4\}$ and$C$ has invariant factors$\{2, 2\}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Verify that $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ and$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/18\mathbb{Z}$ are non-isomorphic (though both have order$36$). For$A = (\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z})^2$:$A[3] \cong (\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})^2$ (since$3a = 0$ in$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ iff$a \in \{0, 2, 4\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$... actually$A[3] = \{a \in A : 3a = 0\}$; in$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$,$3 \cdot \bar{0} = 0$,$3 \cdot \bar{2} = 6 = 0$,$3 \cdot \bar{4} = 12 = 0$, so$A[3]$ in each factor is$\{0, 2, 4\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$). So$\dim_{\mathbb{F}_3} A[3] = 2$. For$B = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/18\mathbb{Z}$:$B[3]$ in$\mathbb{Z}/18\mathbb{Z}$ is$\{0, 6, 12\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$, and$B[3] \cap (\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}) = \{0\}$. So$\dim_{\mathbb{F}_3} B[3] = 1$. Different$3$-ranks, so$A \not\cong B$.
\end{example}
\par
The uniqueness theorem is what makes the structure theorem a **complete classification**: two finitely generated abelian groups are isomorphic if and only if they have the same rank and the same multiset of invariant factors. This turns the isomorphism problem for finitely generated abelian groups into a purely combinatorial check, a striking contrast to the isomorphism problem for general finitely presented groups (Section 18.4.4), which is undecidable.
