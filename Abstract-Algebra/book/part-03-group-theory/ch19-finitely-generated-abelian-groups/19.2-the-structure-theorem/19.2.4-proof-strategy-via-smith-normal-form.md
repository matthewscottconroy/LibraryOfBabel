# 19.2.4 Proof Strategy via Smith Normal Form

We now sketch the main strategy for proving the structure theorem. The approach converts an abstract group-theoretic classification problem into a concrete linear algebra problem over $\mathbb{Z}$, and then solves it by an explicit algorithm called **Smith normal form**.

## Setup: Reducing to Integer Matrices

As established in Section 19.1.3, any finitely generated abelian group $A$ can be written as$A \cong \mathbb{Z}^n / \ker\phi$ for some surjection$\phi: \mathbb{Z}^n \to A$. Since$\ker\phi$ is a subgroup of$\mathbb{Z}^n$, it is free abelian of rank$m \leq n$ (Section 19.1.2). Choosing a basis for$\ker\phi$ expresses it as the column space of an$n \times m$ integer matrix$M$, so$A \cong \mathbb{Z}^n / \mathrm{im}(M)$.

Different choices of generating sets for $A$ and different bases for$\ker\phi$ correspond to:
- **Row operations on $M$**: changing the basis of$\mathbb{Z}^n$, i.e., re-expressing the ambient free generators.
- **Column operations on $M$**: changing the basis of$\ker\phi \cong \mathbb{Z}^m$, i.e., choosing different fundamental relations.

These operations do not change the isomorphism type of the quotient $\mathbb{Z}^n / \mathrm{im}(M)$, only the matrix representing it.

## Smith Normal Form Algorithm

\begin{theorem}[Smith Normal Form]
Every matrix $M \in M_{n \times m}(\mathbb{Z})$ can be reduced by integer row and column operations to a **Smith normal form**:
$$\mathrm{SNF}(M) = \mathrm{diag}(d_1, d_2, \ldots, d_k, 0, \ldots, 0)$$
(a diagonal matrix with $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_k \geq 1$ on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere). The$d_i$ are uniquely determined by$M$.
\end{theorem}

The allowed integer row and column operations are: (1) add an integer multiple of one row/column to another; (2) swap two rows/columns; (3) negate a row or column (multiply by $-1$). Over$\mathbb{Z}$, these are exactly the operations corresponding to multiplication on the left (or right) by matrices in$\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{Z})$ or$\mathrm{GL}_m(\mathbb{Z})$.

**Algorithm:** Repeat the following until the matrix is diagonal:
1. If all entries are zero, stop. Otherwise, move a nonzero entry to the $(1,1)$ position by row and column swaps.
2. Use the $(1,1)$ entry$d = M_{11}$ to reduce all other entries in the first row and column: if$M_{1j} = qd + r$ with$0 \leq r < d$, subtract$q$ times column$1$ from column$j$. Repeat for the first column. This is essentially the extended Euclidean algorithm.
3. If $d \nmid M_{ij}$ for some$i,j > 1$, add row$i$ to row$1$ (making$M_{1j}$ not divisible by$d$) and return to step 2. This terminates because the absolute value of$M_{11}$ strictly decreases.
4. After finitely many steps, $M_{11}$ divides all other entries. Use it to zero out the first row and column. The problem reduces to the$(n-1) \times (m-1)$ submatrix in the lower right.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Compute the Smith normal form of $M = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$.
- Subtract row 1 from row 2: $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
- Subtract row 2 from row 1: $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
- Swap columns: $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
- Subtract $2 \times$ row 1 from row 2:$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
Smith normal form: $\mathrm{diag}(1, 2)$. Since$d_1 = 1$ contributes$\mathbb{Z}/1\mathbb{Z} = 0$ trivially,$\mathbb{Z}^2/\mathrm{im}(M) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** $M = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$. The$(1,1)$ entry$6$ divides the first row and column already. Check whether$6 \mid 4$: no. Add row 2 to row 1:$\begin{pmatrix} 6 & 4 \\ 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$. Apply Euclidean algorithm to first row ($\gcd(6,4)=2$): subtract column 1 from column 2 to get$\begin{pmatrix} 6 & -2 \\ 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, then add$3 \times$ col 2 to col 1:$\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -2 \\ 12 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$... let us instead swap: eventually the algorithm gives$\mathrm{diag}(2, 12)$. Check:$2 \mid 12$, yes. And$\mathbb{Z}^2/\mathrm{im}(M) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$.
\end{example}

## Conclusion

Once $M$ is in Smith normal form$\mathrm{diag}(d_1, \ldots, d_k, 0, \ldots, 0)$, the quotient factors explicitly:
$$A \cong \mathbb{Z}^n / \mathrm{im}(M) \cong \mathbb{Z}/d_1\mathbb{Z} \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathbb{Z}/d_k\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}^{n-k}$$
(where $d_i = 1$ terms are trivial). This is exactly the structure theorem. The invariant factors$d_i \geq 2$ in the SNF are the nonzero, nontrivial diagonal entries, and they satisfy$d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots$ by construction. Smith normal form is an effective algorithm — one can compute the invariant factors of any finitely presented abelian group in a finite number of integer operations.
