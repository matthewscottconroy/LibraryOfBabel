# 19.3 Applications

The Structure Theorem is not merely an abstract classification — it immediately resolves a range of concrete questions about specific groups, and its methods and results extend across mathematics from number theory to algebraic topology to linear algebra.

The most direct application is the classification of abelian groups of a given finite order. To list all abelian groups of order $n = p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_k^{a_k}$, one uses the primary decomposition: the abelian groups of order $n$ are in bijection with the tuples of partitions, one partition of each $a_i$. For example, the abelian groups of order $360 = 2^3 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5$ correspond to partitions of 3 for the prime 2 (giving $\mathbb{Z}/8$, $\mathbb{Z}/4 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2$, or $\mathbb{Z}/2 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2$), partitions of 2 for the prime 3 (giving $\mathbb{Z}/9$ or $\mathbb{Z}/3 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3$), and the unique partition of 1 for the prime 5 (giving $\mathbb{Z}/5$), for a total of $3 \times 2 \times 1 = 6$ groups. Computing the group of units $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^*$ is a further application: the Chinese Remainder Theorem gives $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^* \cong \prod_{p^a \| n} (\mathbb{Z}/p^a\mathbb{Z})^*$, and for odd primes $p$, $(\mathbb{Z}/p^a\mathbb{Z})^*$ is cyclic of order $\phi(p^a) = p^{a-1}(p-1)$.

The connection to module theory over $\mathbb{Z}$ (and more generally over PIDs) explains why the structure theorem matters so broadly. A finitely generated abelian group is the same as a finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-module, and the structure theorem is a special case of the classification of finitely generated modules over a PID. This perspective makes explicit the link to linear algebra: the rational canonical form of a matrix $A$ over a field $k$ is the structure theorem applied to the $k[x]$-module $k^n$ with $x$ acting as $A$. The homology groups of topological spaces — including the homology groups of surfaces, which are determined entirely by their genus — are finitely generated abelian groups, and the structure theorem identifies their free and torsion parts directly. The homomorphism group $\text{Hom}(A, B)$ for finitely generated abelian groups $A$ and $B$ is computable from the structure theorem: $\text{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/\gcd(m,n)\mathbb{Z}$ and $\text{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}, B) \cong B$, and these together with bilinearity determine $\text{Hom}(A,B)$ for all finitely generated $A$ and $B$.
