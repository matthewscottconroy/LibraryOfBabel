# 20.1.1 Additive Group, Multiplicative Monoid, and Distributivity

The integers, the real numbers, and the polynomials with real coefficients all share a common algebraic pattern: they each support two operations — addition and multiplication — that interact in a specific, disciplined way. This pattern is ubiquitous in mathematics: it appears in matrix algebras, in function spaces, in the coordinate rings of geometric objects, and in the arithmetic of number fields. The ring axioms isolate precisely this pattern, distilling into a short list of conditions the essence of what it means for two operations to coexist coherently on a single set.

The motivating question is this: what are the minimal rules one must impose on addition and multiplication to recover the kind of algebra we do every day? We want $a(b + c) = ab + ac$ (the distributive law), we want addition to be commutative and associative with an identity and inverses, and we want multiplication to at least be associative. Remarkably, these conditions alone are sufficient to derive a rich theory. Everything else — cancellation in domains, the existence of inverses for units, the structure of ideals — follows from these few axioms.

\begin{definition}
A \emph{ring} $(R, +, \cdot)$ is a set$R$ equipped with two binary operations satisfying three clusters of conditions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $(R, +)$ is an abelian group: addition is associative, commutative, admits an identity element$0$, and every element has an additive inverse$-a$.
\item $(R, \cdot)$ is a monoid: multiplication is associative and admits an identity element$1$ satisfying$1 \cdot a = a \cdot 1 = a$ for all$a$.
\item Multiplication distributes over addition from both sides: $a(b + c) = ab + ac$ and$(a + b)c = ac + bc$ for all$a, b, c \in R$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

The first cluster places $(R, +)$ in familiar territory — it is a group with commutativity. The second cluster makes$(R, \cdot)$ somewhat weaker: it is a monoid rather than a group, because we do not in general require multiplicative inverses. The absence of inverses is not an oversight; it is deliberate and fundamental. The integers have no multiplicative inverse for$2$ within$\mathbb{Z}$, yet the integers are one of the most important rings in mathematics.

The distributive laws are not a convenience — they are the structural glue that binds the two operations into a coherent whole. Without them, we would simply have two independent algebraic structures on the same set, coexisting without interacting. The distributive law is what allows us to "expand brackets," to write $2(x + y) = 2x + 2y$, to factor out common terms, and to perform every algebraic manipulation that calculus and linear algebra rely on. Strip away the distributive law, and the ring structure collapses into two unrelated monoids.

Notice that the axioms do not require multiplication to be commutative. This is deliberate: matrix rings, group rings, and many algebras arising in geometry and physics are noncommutative, yet they fit perfectly within this framework. The ring axioms thus provide a common language for an enormous range of mathematical objects, from the most familiar number systems to the most exotic algebraic constructions. The commutative and noncommutative theories diverge significantly, and we will see both.

Several elementary consequences follow immediately from the axioms. First, $a \cdot 0 = 0$ for all$a$: indeed,$a \cdot 0 = a(0 + 0) = a \cdot 0 + a \cdot 0$, so cancelling$a \cdot 0$ from both sides gives$0 = a \cdot 0$. Second,$(-a)b = -(ab)$ and$a(-b) = -(ab)$: these follow from distributivity and the additive inverse axioms. Third,$(-1)a = -a$: the element$-1$ acts as the additive inverse of the multiplicative identity. These small facts are used constantly without comment.

**Example 1.** The integers $\mathbb{Z}$ form a ring under ordinary addition and multiplication. The additive group is$(\mathbb{Z}, +)$, the identity is$0$, and the multiplicative identity is$1$. Every ring axiom is satisfied; this is the prototypical ring against which all others are measured.

**Example 2.** The set $M_2(\mathbb{R})$ of$2 \times 2$ real matrices, under matrix addition and multiplication, is a ring. Addition is componentwise (hence commutative), multiplication is the usual matrix product (associative but not commutative), and the distributive laws hold because they hold for linear maps. The multiplicative identity is the identity matrix$I_2$. Note that$AB \neq BA$ in general: take
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Then $AB = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ while$BA = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, so$AB \neq BA$.

**Example 3.** The integers modulo $n$, written$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, form a ring. The elements are residue classes$\{0, 1, \ldots, n-1\}$, addition and multiplication are performed modulo$n$, and the distributive law holds because it holds in$\mathbb{Z}$ before reduction. For$n = 6$:$2 \cdot 3 = 6 \equiv 0$, so the product of two nonzero elements can be zero. This is a qualitatively different phenomenon from the integers.

**Example 4.** The polynomial ring $R[x]$, consisting of all formal sums$a_0 + a_1 x + \cdots + a_n x^n$ with coefficients in a ring$R$, is a ring under componentwise addition and polynomial multiplication. The distributive law holds by the way multiplication is defined: each coefficient of a product$fg$ is a finite sum of products of coefficients of$f$ and$g$, and expanding brackets commutes with collecting like powers.

**Example 5.** The set $\{0\}$ with$0 + 0 = 0$ and$0 \cdot 0 = 0$ is a ring called the **zero ring** or **trivial ring**. It has$1 = 0$. This is the only ring in which$1 = 0$: indeed, if$1 = 0$ in any ring$R$, then for all$a \in R$,$a = 1 \cdot a = 0 \cdot a = 0$, so$R = \{0\}$. A convention adopted throughout this text is that$1 \neq 0$, which excludes the zero ring and aligns with the practice of most modern authors.
