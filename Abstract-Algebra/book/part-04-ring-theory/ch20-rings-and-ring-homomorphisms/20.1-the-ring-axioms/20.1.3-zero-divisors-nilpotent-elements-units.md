# 20.1.3 Zero Divisors; Nilpotent Elements; Units

Not all nonzero elements of a ring behave like the nonzero elements of a field. The ring axioms allow for three qualitatively different kinds of nonzero elements, and distinguishing between them is one of the first tasks in understanding the arithmetic of a ring. These distinctions — zero divisors, nilpotents, and units — collectively determine whether a ring supports cancellation, whether it has "infinitesimal" elements, and how richly invertible its elements are.

The simplest contrast to make is with the integers. In $\mathbb{Z}$, if$ab = 0$ then$a = 0$ or$b = 0$. This **cancellation property** is so familiar that it feels inevitable, but in a general ring it can fail. The ring$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ provides an immediate counterexample:$2 \cdot 3 = 6 \equiv 0$, yet$2 \neq 0$ and$3 \neq 0$. The elements$2$ and$3$ are "zero divisors," and their presence is the signature of arithmetic that does not behave like the integers.

\begin{definition}
A nonzero element $a \in R$ is a \emph{zero divisor} if there exists a nonzero$b \in R$ such that$ab = 0$ or$ba = 0$. A ring with no zero divisors (and with$1 \neq 0$) is an \emph{integral domain}.
\end{definition}

Zero divisors are precisely the elements that obstruct cancellation: if $ab = ac$ and$a$ is a zero divisor, we cannot in general cancel$a$ to conclude$b = c$. Specifically, cancellation fails because$a(b - c) = 0$ does not force$b = c$ when$a$ might be a zero divisor paired with$b - c$. Integral domains are exactly the rings where such cancellation holds, and the integers$\mathbb{Z}$ are the canonical example.

\begin{definition}
An element $a \in R$ is \emph{nilpotent} if$a^n = 0$ for some positive integer$n$. The smallest such$n$ is the \emph{nilpotency index} of$a$. The set of all nilpotent elements of$R$ is called the \emph{nilradical} of$R$.
\end{definition}

Every nilpotent element is a zero divisor: if $a^n = 0$ and$n$ is minimal, then$a \cdot a^{n-1} = 0$ with$a^{n-1} \neq 0$ (by minimality of$n$). The converse fails:$2 \in \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ is a zero divisor ($2 \cdot 3 = 0$) but$2^n \neq 0$ for all$n$ in$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$. Nilpotent elements arise naturally in algebraic geometry as the "infinitesimal" elements of a ring — they are the algebraic analogue of functions that vanish to high order at a point. The nilradical is always an ideal, and the quotient$R/\sqrt{0}$ (where$\sqrt{0}$ denotes the nilradical) is a ring with no nilpotent elements, called the **reduced** ring associated to$R$.

\begin{definition}
A \emph{unit} is an element $u \in R$ for which there exists$v \in R$ with$uv = vu = 1$. The element$v$ is the \emph{multiplicative inverse} of$u$. The set of all units forms a group under multiplication, denoted$R^\times$ and called the \emph{group of units} of$R$.
\end{definition}

The group of units captures the "invertible" part of the ring's multiplicative structure. In $\mathbb{Z}$, the units are$\pm 1$; in a field, every nonzero element is a unit, so$F^\times = F \setminus \{0\}$. In$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, the units are exactly the residue classes$\bar{a}$ with$\gcd(a, n) = 1$, and$(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$ has order$\phi(n)$ (Euler's totient function). In$M_n(F)$, the units are the invertible matrices$GL_n(F)$.

A unit cannot be a zero divisor in a ring with $1 \neq 0$: if$u$ is a unit and$uv = 0$, then$v = u^{-1}(uv) = u^{-1} \cdot 0 = 0$. Thus in an integral domain, where zero divisors are absent, the only "almost-units" among nonzero elements are the units themselves and the irreducible elements that cannot be inverted. This stratification — units, irreducibles, and non-units — determines much of the ring's arithmetic character.

**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$: The units are$\{1, 5, 7, 11\}$ (those coprime to$12$), forming a group of order$\phi(12) = 4$. The zero divisors include$2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 10$ (those sharing a factor with$12$). The element$6$ satisfies$6^2 = 36 \equiv 0 \pmod{12}$, so$6$ is nilpotent (of index$2$). Note$6 = 2 \cdot 3$ in$\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ and both$2$ and$3$ divide$12$.

**Example 2.** In the polynomial ring $\mathbb{R}[x]$: The units are the nonzero constant polynomials$\mathbb{R}^\times = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$. There are no zero divisors, since$\mathbb{R}$ is a domain and$\deg(fg) = \deg f + \deg g$ for nonzero polynomials. There are no nilpotent elements, since$f^n = 0$ would require$\deg f^n = n \deg f = -\infty$, impossible for a nonzero polynomial.

**Example 3.** In the ring $R = \mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^2)$: Elements are of the form$a + bx$ with$a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$ and the relation$x^2 = 0$. The element$x$ is nilpotent of index$2$:$x^2 = 0$. The element$2$ is not nilpotent (powers of$2$ in$\mathbb{Z}$ never vanish) but is also not a unit. The element$1 + x$ is a unit with inverse$1 - x$:$(1 + x)(1 - x) = 1 - x^2 = 1$. This ring models the "dual numbers" used in synthetic differential geometry and automatic differentiation.

**Example 4.** In $M_2(\mathbb{R})$: The units are the invertible matrices (those with nonzero determinant), forming the group$GL_2(\mathbb{R})$. The matrix$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ is a zero divisor:$A \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 0$. The matrix$B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ is nilpotent of index$2$:$B^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Every nilpotent matrix is a zero divisor.

**Example 5.** A **field** is precisely a commutative ring with $1 \neq 0$ in which every nonzero element is a unit:$F^\times = F \setminus \{0\}$. Fields have no zero divisors (if$ab = 0$ and$a \neq 0$, then$b = a^{-1}(ab) = 0$) and no nonzero nilpotent elements ($a^n = 0$ and$a \neq 0$ gives$a^{-n} \cdot a^n = 1 = 0$, contradicting$1 \neq 0$). The field condition is thus the strongest possible: units are maximal and zero divisors and nilpotents are completely absent.
