# 20.1.4 The Characteristic of a Ring

Every ring carries a numerical invariant that captures the most basic additive behavior of its multiplicative identity. This invariant, the *characteristic*, is both easy to define and surprisingly far-reaching in its consequences. It determines what kind of arithmetic is possible in the ring, dictates the structure of the smallest subring, and is fundamental to the study of fields, particularly in positive characteristic.

The question the characteristic answers is: how many times do we have to add $1$ to itself before reaching$0$? In$\mathbb{Z}$, the answer is never — no finite sum of ones yields zero. In$\mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z}$, the answer is seven:$1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 7 \equiv 0$. This count is the characteristic.

\begin{definition}
The \emph{characteristic} of a unital ring $R$, written$\mathrm{char}(R)$, is the smallest positive integer$n$ such that
$$\underbrace{1 + 1 + \cdots + 1}_{n} = 0.$$
If no such $n$ exists, we set$\mathrm{char}(R) = 0$.
\end{definition}

The characteristic is well-defined because the set $S = \{n \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0} : n \cdot 1 = 0\}$ is either empty (characteristic$0$) or has a least element (the characteristic). One checks that if$n \cdot 1 = 0$ and$m \cdot 1 = 0$, then$(n + m) \cdot 1 = 0$ and$(nm) \cdot 1 = (n \cdot 1)(m \cdot 1) = 0 \cdot 0 = 0$, so$S$ is closed under addition and multiplication: it forms an ideal of$\mathbb{Z}$, which is always of the form$n\mathbb{Z}$.

The characteristic is tightly constrained in the presence of no zero divisors. If $R$ is an integral domain and$n \cdot 1 = 0$ for some$n > 0$, then writing$n = ab$ gives$(a \cdot 1)(b \cdot 1) = ab \cdot 1 = n \cdot 1 = 0$. Since$R$ has no zero divisors,$a \cdot 1 = 0$ or$b \cdot 1 = 0$. The minimality of$n$ then forces$n$ to be prime — no proper factorization$n = ab$ can have$a \cdot 1 = 0$ or$b \cdot 1 = 0$ with$a, b < n$.

\begin{theorem}
The characteristic of any integral domain (and in particular, of any field) is either $0$ or a prime$p$.
\end{theorem}

This is not merely a curiosity: the characteristic of a field determines its prime subfield and hence the entire ambient arithmetic. A field of characteristic $0$ contains a copy of$\mathbb{Q}$; a field of characteristic$p$ contains a copy of$\mathbb{F}_p = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$. The characteristic thus divides the universe of fields into two fundamentally different worlds, with distinct geometry, analysis, and number theory.

In characteristic $p$, a remarkable phenomenon occurs: the **Frobenius map**$\varphi: R \to R$ defined by$\varphi(a) = a^p$ is a ring homomorphism whenever$R$ is a commutative ring of characteristic$p$. This follows from the binomial theorem:
$$
(a + b)^p = \sum_{k=0}^{p} \binom{p}{k} a^k b^{p-k}.
$$
All intermediate binomial coefficients $\binom{p}{k}$ for$0 < k < p$ are divisible by$p$ and therefore vanish in characteristic$p$, leaving$(a + b)^p = a^p + b^p$. Combined with the obvious$\varphi(ab) = (ab)^p = a^p b^p$, we see that$\varphi$ is a ring endomorphism. The Frobenius is central to the arithmetic of finite fields, the theory of algebraic varieties over fields of positive characteristic, and the Galois theory of such fields.

**Example 1.** $\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{Z}) = 0$: no finite sum of$1$'s yields$0$ in the integers. Similarly$\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{Q}) = \mathrm{char}(\mathbb{R}) = \mathrm{char}(\mathbb{C}) = 0$.

**Example 2.** $\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}) = n$: the$n$-fold sum$\bar{1} + \cdots + \bar{1} = \bar{n} = \bar{0}$, and no smaller positive integer achieves this. Note that$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ has characteristic$6$, which is not prime, consistent with the fact that$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ is not an integral domain.

**Example 3.** The field $\mathbb{F}_p = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ for a prime$p$ has$\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F}_p) = p$. The Frobenius map$a \mapsto a^p$ is a field automorphism of$\mathbb{F}_p$ (in fact the identity, since$a^p \equiv a \pmod{p}$ by Fermat's little theorem). For the field$\mathbb{F}_{p^2}$, the Frobenius is a nontrivial automorphism of order$2$ generating the Galois group$\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{F}_{p^2}/\mathbb{F}_p) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 4.** The product ring $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ has characteristic$\mathrm{lcm}(2, 3) = 6$: the element$(\bar{1}, \bar{1})$ requires$6$ additions to reach$(\bar{0}, \bar{0})$. This is an example of a ring whose characteristic is not prime, consistent with the existence of zero divisors:$(\bar{1}, \bar{0}) \cdot (\bar{0}, \bar{1}) = (\bar{0}, \bar{0})$.

**Example 5.** In any ring $R$, the characteristic can be detected via the prime subring. The prime subring is the image of the unique ring homomorphism$\mathbb{Z} \to R$ (sending$n \mapsto n \cdot 1_R$), and its isomorphism type is$\mathbb{Z}/\mathrm{char}(R)\mathbb{Z}$. For$\mathrm{char}(R) = 0$, the prime subring is isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}$; for$\mathrm{char}(R) = p$, it is isomorphic to$\mathbb{F}_p$. In this sense, the characteristic tells us whether the ring "sees" integer arithmetic or modular arithmetic at the most fundamental level.
