# 20.2.1 $\mathbb{Z}$,$\mathbb{Q}$,$\mathbb{R}$,$\mathbb{C}$; the Integers Modulo$n$

Before developing the general theory, it is essential to have a firm grasp of the most familiar examples. These examples are not merely illustrations — they are the benchmarks against which we measure every abstract ring, and returning to them will ground every new concept we introduce. The family of rings $\mathbb{Z}$,$\mathbb{Q}$,$\mathbb{R}$,$\mathbb{C}$, and$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ encompasses both the deepest objects in number theory and the simplest finite rings, and understanding the differences among them is the first order of business in ring theory.

The integers $\mathbb{Z}$ are the prototypical ring. They are commutative and unital, with the familiar operations of addition and multiplication. Crucially,$\mathbb{Z}$ is an **integral domain**: the product of two nonzero integers is always nonzero. However,$\mathbb{Z}$ is not a field — most elements lack multiplicative inverses. The integer$2$, for instance, has no multiplicative inverse in$\mathbb{Z}$: there is no integer$n$ with$2n = 1$. This gap between "domain" and "field" will be central to Chapters 22 and 23, where we study when one can build a field from a domain and what factorization properties the intermediate rings possess.

Passing to $\mathbb{Q}$,$\mathbb{R}$, or$\mathbb{C}$, we obtain fields: every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse. These rings are also integral domains, since a field has no zero divisors (if$ab = 0$ and$a \neq 0$, then$b = a^{-1}(ab) = 0$). The hierarchy$\mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R} \subset \mathbb{C}$ is an ascending chain of rings, each embedding into the next by the identity map, with each step adding more invertible elements. The complex numbers$\mathbb{C}$ are algebraically closed — every nonconstant polynomial with complex coefficients splits into linear factors — and this completeness property will appear repeatedly in Galois theory and representation theory.

The integers modulo $n$, written$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, form a finite commutative ring whose arithmetic is governed entirely by divisibility properties of$n$. The elements are the residue classes$\{0, 1, \ldots, n-1\}$ (or more properly,$\{\bar{0}, \bar{1}, \ldots, \overline{n-1}\}$), with addition and multiplication performed and then reduced modulo$n$.

When $n$ is composite, say$n = ab$ with$1 < a, b < n$, the classes$\bar{a}$ and$\bar{b}$ are nonzero yet satisfy$\bar{a} \cdot \bar{b} = \overline{ab} = \bar{n} = \bar{0}$, so$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ has zero divisors. When$n$ is prime, no such factorization exists and$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is an integral domain; since it is finite, every nonzero element is a unit (this follows from the fact that multiplication by a nonzero element is an injective map on a finite set, hence surjective, hence the element is a unit). Thus$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is a field if and only if$n$ is prime.

The rings $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ for various$n$ collectively exhibit almost every ring-theoretic phenomenon one might encounter: zero divisors, nilpotent elements, units in abundance, and the transition to field behavior at prime values of$n$. They will appear as examples throughout the text.

**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$:$2 \cdot 3 = 6 \equiv 0$, so$2$ and$3$ are zero divisors. The units are$\{1, 5\}$ (those coprime to$6$), forming a group of order$\phi(6) = 2$. Note$5 \equiv -1$, so the units are$\pm 1$. The ring is not a domain and not a field.

**Example 2.** In $\mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z}$ (prime): Every nonzero element is a unit. Explicitly:$1^{-1} = 1$,$2^{-1} = 4$ (since$2 \cdot 4 = 8 \equiv 1$),$3^{-1} = 5$ (since$3 \cdot 5 = 15 \equiv 1$),$6^{-1} = 6$ (since$6^2 = 36 \equiv 1$). This ring is the field$\mathbb{F}_7$.

**Example 3.** In $\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$: The units are$\{1, 3, 5, 7\}$ (odd residues), with$3^2 = 9 \equiv 1$, so$3^{-1} = 3$. The element$2$ is a zero divisor:$2 \cdot 4 = 8 \equiv 0$. The element$4$ is nilpotent:$4^2 = 16 \equiv 0$. The polynomial$x^2 - 1$ has four roots in$\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$:$x = 1, 3, 5, 7$. This illustrates the failure of the bound "a degree-$n$ polynomial has at most$n$ roots" when the coefficient ring is not a domain.

**Example 4.** The ring $\mathbb{Z}$ has characteristic$0$, while$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ has characteristic$n$. For the field$\mathbb{Q}$, the characteristic is$0$: the map$\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Q}$ sending$n \mapsto n/1$ is injective, so no finite sum of ones in$\mathbb{Q}$ vanishes.

**Example 5.** The Chinese Remainder Theorem gives an isomorphism of rings $\mathbb{Z}/mn\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ when$\gcd(m, n) = 1$. For instance,$\mathbb{Z}/15\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$. This isomorphism reflects the splitting of the prime factorization$15 = 3 \cdot 5$ into coprime factors, and it will be the model for the abstract Chinese Remainder Theorem in Chapter 21.
