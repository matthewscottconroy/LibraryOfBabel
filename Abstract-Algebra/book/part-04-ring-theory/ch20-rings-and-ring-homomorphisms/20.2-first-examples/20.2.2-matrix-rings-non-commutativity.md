# 20.2.2 Matrix Rings $M_n(R)$; Non-Commutativity

The most important source of noncommutative rings in algebra is the ring of matrices. Matrix rings demonstrate concretely that the ring axioms admit far more exotic behavior than the integers or the rationals might suggest, and they are not merely examples — they are the fundamental building blocks from which all semisimple rings are constructed, a deep fact that we will approach via the Artin–Wedderburn theorem later in the text.

For any ring $R$ and any positive integer$n$, the set$M_n(R)$ of all$n \times n$ matrices with entries in$R$, equipped with componentwise addition and the usual matrix multiplication, is a ring. Componentwise addition makes$(M_n(R), +)$ into an abelian group (it is a direct sum of$n^2$ copies of the additive group of$R$). Matrix multiplication is defined by$(AB)_{ij} = \sum_k A_{ik} B_{kj}$, and one verifies associativity by computing$(AB)C$ and$A(BC)$ and checking that both equal$\sum_{k,l} A_{ik} B_{kl} C_{lj}$. The multiplicative identity is the identity matrix$I_n$ with ones on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere. The distributive laws hold because multiplication is bilinear:$A(B + C) = AB + AC$ follows from$\sum_k A_{ik}(B_{kj} + C_{kj}) = \sum_k A_{ik} B_{kj} + \sum_k A_{ik} C_{kj}$.

For $n \geq 2$, even when$R = \mathbb{R}$, this ring is noncommutative. The failure of commutativity is not incidental; it reflects the non-commutativity of composition of linear maps. If$f, g: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$ are linear, then$f \circ g$ and$g \circ f$ are typically different. Matrices are simply representations of linear maps in a chosen basis, so the ring structure of$M_n(\mathbb{R})$ encodes the composition algebra of linear endomorphisms.

Matrix rings also exhibit zero divisors and non-units that have no analogue in fields. A matrix with determinant zero (a singular matrix) is not a unit: it has no two-sided inverse. Moreover, singular matrices can be zero divisors. For example, in $M_2(\mathbb{R})$:
$$
\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.
$$
Both factors are nonzero (rank-1) matrices, yet their product is zero. The set of invertible matrices, $GL_n(R) = M_n(R)^\times$, consists precisely of those matrices with a two-sided inverse — over a field, these are exactly the matrices with nonzero determinant.

The deeper significance of matrix rings appears in the Artin–Wedderburn theorem: every semisimple ring (a ring with no nonzero nilpotent ideals and satisfying the descending chain condition on one-sided ideals) decomposes as a finite direct product of matrix rings over division algebras. This makes $M_n(R)$ not merely an example but a fundamental building block of the entire classification theory of rings. Whenever one encounters a representation of a group or algebra, one is secretly working inside a matrix ring: a representation of a group$G$ is a homomorphism$G \to GL_n(F)$, which extends to a ring homomorphism$F[G] \to M_n(F)$.

**Example 1.** The ring $M_2(\mathbb{R})$ contains the zero divisors$E_{11} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and$E_{22} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ with$E_{11} \cdot E_{22} = 0$. Neither is nilpotent:$E_{11}^2 = E_{11} \neq 0$. But the matrix$N = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ is nilpotent of index$2$:$N^2 = 0$.

**Example 2.** Commutativity fails in $M_2(\mathbb{R})$: take
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Then $AB = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and$BA = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, so$AB \neq BA$.

**Example 3.** The ring $M_n(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})$ for a prime$p$ is a finite ring. The number of elements is$p^{n^2}$. The group of units is$GL_n(\mathbb{F}_p)$, and its order is$(p^n - 1)(p^n - p)(p^n - p^2) \cdots (p^n - p^{n-1})$: the first row can be any nonzero vector ($p^n - 1$ choices), the second row must be outside the span of the first ($p^n - p$ choices), and so on.

**Example 4.** When $R$ is commutative, the **center** of$M_n(R)$ (the set of matrices commuting with all matrices) is$\{cI_n : c \in R\} \cong R$. Scalar matrices commute with everything because$cI_n \cdot A = cA = A \cdot cI_n$. For$n \geq 2$, the center is strictly smaller than$M_n(R)$, confirming noncommutativity.

**Example 5.** The ring $M_1(R) \cong R$ for any ring$R$:$1 \times 1$ matrices are just scalars. This provides a trivial but useful base case: every ring embeds into a matrix ring, and the theory of matrix rings is the theory of rings "made noncommutative."
