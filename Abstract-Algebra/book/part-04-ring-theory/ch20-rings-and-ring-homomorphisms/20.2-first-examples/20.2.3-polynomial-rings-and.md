# 20.2.3 Polynomial Rings $R[x]$ and$R[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$

Polynomial rings are the primary tool for constructing new rings from old ones, and they appear throughout algebra, geometry, and number theory. Understanding their ring-theoretic properties is indispensable for everything that follows: quotients of polynomial rings give field extensions, ideals in polynomial rings correspond to algebraic varieties, and the unique factorization properties of polynomial rings are the algebraic backbone of irreducibility theory.

The polynomial ring $R[x]$ consists of all formal sums$a_0 + a_1 x + \cdots + a_n x^n$ with coefficients$a_i \in R$. The word "formal" is important:$x$ is not a number or a function, but a symbol, and two polynomials are equal precisely when all their corresponding coefficients are equal. Addition is componentwise, and multiplication follows the rule for multiplying polynomials: the coefficient of$x^k$ in the product$fg$ is
$$\sum_{i+j=k} a_i b_j,$$
where $f = \sum a_i x^i$ and$g = \sum b_j x^j$. One verifies that$R[x]$ satisfies all ring axioms; the distributive law corresponds to the familiar rule for expanding brackets in polynomial arithmetic.

The key structural property of $R[x]$ is captured by a universal property: for any ring$S$ containing$R$ as a subring and any element$s \in S$, there is a unique ring homomorphism$\mathrm{ev}_s: R[x] \to S$ that fixes$R$ pointwise and sends$x \mapsto s$, namely evaluation at$s$:$\mathrm{ev}_s(f) = f(s)$. This **substitution principle** is what makes polynomial rings so useful: they encode the freest possible way to adjoin one new element to a ring.

When $R$ is an integral domain, so is$R[x]$. Suppose$f = a_m x^m + \cdots$ and$g = b_n x^n + \cdots$ are nonzero with leading coefficients$a_m$ and$b_n$ respectively. The leading coefficient of$fg$ is$a_m b_n$, which is nonzero because$R$ has no zero divisors. Thus$fg \neq 0$, confirming that$R[x]$ is again a domain. The converse also holds: if$R[x]$ is a domain, then$R$ must be one (since$R$ embeds into$R[x]$ as the constant polynomials). This hereditary property will be crucial when we prove in Chapter 22 that$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is a UFD.

The degree function $\deg: R[x] \setminus \{0\} \to \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ satisfies$\deg(fg) \leq \deg f + \deg g$ in general, with equality when$R$ is a domain. It also satisfies$\deg(f + g) \leq \max(\deg f, \deg g)$ with equality when$\deg f \neq \deg g$. These estimates make the degree a useful norm-like function that controls the size of polynomials.

The multivariable polynomial ring $R[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ is constructed iteratively:$R[x_1, \ldots, x_n] = R[x_1, \ldots, x_{n-1}][x_n]$. Its elements are finite$R$-linear combinations of monomials$x_1^{e_1} \cdots x_n^{e_n}$. The multivariable ring encodes the algebra of polynomial functions on$n$-dimensional affine space, and its ideal theory is the algebraic foundation of algebraic geometry. The quotient ring$F[x_1, \ldots, x_n]/I$, for an ideal$I$ in a polynomial ring over a field$F$, is the coordinate ring of the variety defined by$I$ — a connection we will see more clearly when we study prime and maximal ideals in Chapter 21.

**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{Z}[x]$: the polynomial$f = 2x^3 - 3x + 5$ has degree$3$ and leading coefficient$2$. The polynomial$g = x^2 + 1$ has degree$2$. Their product is$fg = 2x^5 - 3x^3 + 5x^2 + 2x^3 - 3x + 5 = 2x^5 - x^3 + 5x^2 - 3x + 5$, which has degree$5 = 3 + 2$, confirming the degree formula in a domain.

**Example 2.** In $(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})[x]$: the polynomials$f = x^2 + x + 1$ and$g = x + 1$ satisfy$fg = x^3 + x^2 + x + x^2 + x + 1 = x^3 + 1$ (using$2 \equiv 0$). This ring has characteristic$2$, so many polynomials that are distinct over$\mathbb{Q}$ become equal mod$2$: for example,$x^2 + 2x + 1 \equiv x^2 + 1 = (x + 1)^2$ in$(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})[x]$, since$2x \equiv 0$.

**Example 3.** The evaluation map $\mathrm{ev}_2: \mathbb{Z}[x] \to \mathbb{Z}$ sending$f \mapsto f(2)$ is a ring homomorphism (evaluation at$2$). Its kernel is the set of polynomials vanishing at$2$, namely$(x - 2)$ — the principal ideal generated by$x - 2$. By the first isomorphism theorem,$\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x - 2) \cong \mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 4.** In $\mathbb{R}[x_1, x_2]$: the ideal$I = (x_1^2 + x_2^2 - 1)$ consists of all polynomials vanishing on the unit circle. The quotient$\mathbb{R}[x_1, x_2]/I$ is the coordinate ring of the unit circle, the algebraic object encoding all polynomial functions on the circle. This is a fundamental example in algebraic geometry: the geometry of the circle is encoded in the algebra of this quotient ring.

**Example 5.** The ideal $(x_1, x_2)$ in$F[x_1, x_2]$ (for any field$F$) consists of all polynomials with zero constant term. This ideal is not principal: if$(x_1, x_2) = (f)$ for some$f$, then$f$ would divide both$x_1$ and$x_2$, but$\deg f \leq 1$ and a linear polynomial cannot simultaneously divide both$x_1$ and$x_2$ unless$f$ is a nonzero constant — but then$(f) = F[x_1, x_2]$. This failure of principality distinguishes multivariable polynomial rings from the one-variable case and from$\mathbb{Z}$.
