# 20.2.5 Group Rings $R[G]$; Quaternions$\mathbb{H}$

Two further examples push us firmly into noncommutative territory and illustrate how the ring axioms accommodate structures arising from symmetry and geometry. The group ring unifies ring theory with representation theory, while the quaternions exhibit the surprising phenomenon of a division ring that is not a field. Together, they complete the essential panorama of ring-theoretic examples encountered in the first part of the subject.

The **group ring** $R[G]$ is constructed from a ring$R$ and a group$G$ as follows. Its elements are formal$R$-linear combinations$\sum_{g \in G} a_g g$, where each$a_g \in R$ and all but finitely many are zero. Addition is componentwise:$(\sum a_g g) + (\sum b_g g) = \sum (a_g + b_g) g$. Multiplication is defined by extending the group law$R$-bilinearly:
$$\left(\sum_{g \in G} a_g g\right)\left(\sum_{h \in G} b_h h\right) = \sum_{g, h \in G} a_g b_h (gh).$$
After collecting terms, the coefficient of a group element $k$ in the product is$\sum_{gh = k} a_g b_h$ — a convolution product. One checks all ring axioms: the additive identity is the zero combination$\sum 0 \cdot g$, the multiplicative identity is$1_R \cdot e$ (where$e$ is the identity of$G$), and the distributive laws hold by bilinearity of multiplication.

The group ring is commutative if and only if both $R$ is commutative and$G$ is abelian. For nonabelian$G$, we get$(\sum a_g g)(\sum b_h h) \neq (\sum b_h h)(\sum a_g g)$ in general, since$gh \neq hg$ for some$g, h \in G$.

The group ring is not merely an algebraic curiosity. A **representation** of $G$ over$R$ — that is, a group homomorphism$\rho: G \to GL_n(R)$ — extends uniquely to a ring homomorphism$R[G] \to M_n(R)$ by$\sum a_g g \mapsto \sum a_g \rho(g)$. Conversely, any ring homomorphism$R[G] \to M_n(R)$ that restricts to the standard embedding$R \hookrightarrow M_n(R)$ (via scalar matrices) determines a representation of$G$. This identification — representations of$G$ are the same as$R[G]$-modules — makes group rings the organizing algebraic structure of representation theory: to understand all representations of$G$, one must understand all modules over$R[G]$.

The **quaternion algebra** $\mathbb{H} = \{a + bi + cj + dk : a,b,c,d \in \mathbb{R}\}$ is a four-dimensional real vector space equipped with multiplication determined by the rules$i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1$. These imply$ij = k$,$jk = i$,$ki = j$, and$ji = -k$,$kj = -i$,$ik = -j$. One verifies that$\mathbb{H}$ satisfies all the ring axioms (the associativity of multiplication is a computation using the basis rules), with real scalars central:$a \cdot q = q \cdot a$ for$a \in \mathbb{R}$ and$q \in \mathbb{H}$.

Every nonzero element of $\mathbb{H}$ has a multiplicative inverse: for$q = a + bi + cj + dk \neq 0$, the **conjugate** is$\bar{q} = a - bi - cj - dk$, and the **norm** is$|q|^2 = q\bar{q} = a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + d^2 > 0$. The inverse is$q^{-1} = \bar{q}/|q|^2$. A ring in which every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse (but multiplication may not be commutative) is called a **division ring** or **skew field**. The quaternions$\mathbb{H}$ are the unique real associative division algebra that is neither$\mathbb{R}$ nor$\mathbb{C}$; this is the content of **Frobenius's theorem** (1877). The quaternions also arise as the group ring$\mathbb{R}[\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}]$ modulo certain relations, connecting the two constructions of this section.

**Example 1.** The group ring $\mathbb{Z}[C_2]$ for the cyclic group$C_2 = \{e, g\}$ with$g^2 = e$: elements are$a + bg$ with$a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$, and multiplication satisfies$(a + bg)(c + dg) = (ac + bd) + (ad + bc)g$. The ring homomorphism$\mathbb{Z}[C_2] \to \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$ defined by$a + bg \mapsto (a + b, a - b)$ is an isomorphism (verify: it is a bijection and preserves addition and multiplication). Thus$\mathbb{Z}[C_2] \cong \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 2.** The group ring $\mathbb{C}[G]$ for a finite group$G$ of order$n$: by Maschke's theorem and the Artin–Wedderburn theorem,$\mathbb{C}[G] \cong \prod_i M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})$ where the product ranges over the irreducible representations of$G$ and$n_i$ is the dimension of the$i$-th irreducible. For$G = S_3$, we have$\mathbb{C}[S_3] \cong \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C} \times M_2(\mathbb{C})$, corresponding to the two degree-1 irreducibles and one degree-2 irreducible of$S_3$.

**Example 3.** In $\mathbb{H}$: the element$q = 1 + i + j + k$ has norm$|q|^2 = 4$, so$q^{-1} = (1 - i - j - k)/4$. Check:$q \cdot q^{-1} = (1 + i + j + k)(1 - i - j - k)/4$. Computing:$(1 + i + j + k)(1 - i - j - k) = 1 - i - j - k + i - i^2 - ij - ik + j - ji - j^2 - jk + k - ki - kj - k^2 = 1 + 1 - k + j + 1 - i + 1 + \ldots$. Using the multiplication rules and$i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = -1$:$= 4$. So$q \cdot q^{-1} = 1$.

**Example 4.** The quaternion group $Q_8 = \{\pm 1, \pm i, \pm j, \pm k\}$ of order$8$ can be identified with the eight unit quaternions with entries$\pm 1$ and the rest$0$. The real group ring$\mathbb{R}[Q_8]$ decomposes by the Artin–Wedderburn theorem as$\mathbb{R}[Q_8] \cong \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{H}$, where the last factor corresponds to the two-dimensional representation of$Q_8$ that gives a copy of$\mathbb{H}$.

**Example 5.** The **Hamilton quaternions** $\mathbb{H}$ model rotations of three-dimensional space. A unit quaternion$q$ with$|q| = 1$ acts on the pure imaginary quaternions$\{bi + cj + dk\}$ (which are identified with$\mathbb{R}^3$) by conjugation:$v \mapsto qvq^{-1}$. This action is a rotation of$\mathbb{R}^3$, and the map$q \mapsto (v \mapsto qvq^{-1})$ gives a surjective group homomorphism$S^3 \to SO(3)$ with kernel$\{\pm 1\}$, exhibiting$S^3$ as the double cover of$SO(3)$. This is the spinor cover fundamental to quantum mechanics and Riemannian geometry.
