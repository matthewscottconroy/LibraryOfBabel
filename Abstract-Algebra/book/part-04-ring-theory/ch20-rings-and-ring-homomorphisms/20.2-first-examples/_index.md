# 20.2 First Examples

The abstract axioms of a ring gain their meaning through a wide and varied collection of examples, and the purpose of this section is to survey the most important families. The diversity of these examples is not accidental: it reflects the deliberate generality of the ring axioms, which were designed precisely to encompass all of them. By examining each example, we calibrate our intuition and discover which properties — commutativity, unique factorization, the presence or absence of zero divisors — depend on more than just the ring axioms.

The integers $\mathbb{Z}$ are the prototype of a commutative ring. Their quotients $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ provide a family of finite rings that displays the full range of behavior depending on whether $n$ is prime, prime power, or composite. The matrix rings $M_n(k)$ over a field $k$ are the fundamental examples of non-commutative rings; they are simple in the sense of having no proper two-sided ideals, which will be explained by the Artin–Wedderburn theorem in a later part of the text. Polynomial rings $R[x]$ and $R[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ are the universal examples: they are free in the sense that specifying a ring homomorphism out of $R[x]$ amounts to specifying a ring homomorphism out of $R$ and choosing where $x$ goes. Power series rings $R[[x]]$, function rings such as $C(X, \mathbb{R})$, and group rings $RG$ extend the family further.

Two examples merit special attention for the conceptual work they do throughout the text. The Gaussian integers $\mathbb{Z}[i] = \{a + bi : a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ are a subring of $\mathbb{C}$ that behaves in many ways like $\mathbb{Z}$: they have unique factorization, they are a Euclidean domain, and they encode facts about which integers are sums of two squares. The ring $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, by contrast, provides the essential cautionary example: it is an integral domain in which unique factorization fails, motivating the introduction of ideals in Chapter 21 and the hierarchy of domains in Chapter 22. Having both examples in hand from the start prevents the false impression that all "nice" rings must behave like $\mathbb{Z}$.
