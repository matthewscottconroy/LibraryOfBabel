# 20.3.1 Definition; Preservation of All Ring Structure

A ring homomorphism is the natural notion of a structure-preserving map between rings. As with group homomorphisms, the definition requires that every piece of ring structure be preserved — and in a ring, there are two operations plus an identity, so there are three conditions to impose. Getting all three right is essential; missing even one leads to maps that ignore important structure and fail to form a well-behaved category.

Before stating the definition, it is worth recalling the analogous situation for groups. A group homomorphism $\phi: G \to H$ is required only to preserve the operation:$\phi(ab) = \phi(a)\phi(b)$. From this single condition, the preservation of the identity and inverses is automatic. For rings, the situation is similar for the additive structure but requires an extra condition for the multiplicative identity.

\begin{definition}
A \emph{ring homomorphism} $\phi: R \to S$ is a function satisfying:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\phi(a + b) = \phi(a) + \phi(b)$ for all$a, b \in R$ \quad (preserves addition),
\item $\phi(ab) = \phi(a)\phi(b)$ for all$a, b \in R$ \quad (preserves multiplication),
\item $\phi(1_R) = 1_S$ \quad (preserves the multiplicative identity).
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

The first condition says $\phi$ is a group homomorphism for the additive groups; from it, one immediately deduces$\phi(0) = 0$ (substitute$b = 0$) and$\phi(-a) = -\phi(a)$ (substitute$b = -a$). The second says$\phi$ preserves multiplication. The third, which is sometimes omitted in less careful treatments, ensures that the multiplicative identity maps to the multiplicative identity.

The third condition is not redundant. Consider the map $\phi: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$ defined by$\phi(n) = (n, 0)$. This function satisfies conditions (1) and (2):$\phi(m + n) = (m + n, 0) = (m, 0) + (n, 0) = \phi(m) + \phi(n)$, and$\phi(mn) = (mn, 0) = (m, 0)(n, 0) = \phi(m)\phi(n)$. However,$\phi(1_{\mathbb{Z}}) = (1, 0) \neq (1, 1) = 1_{\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}}$. Without the identity condition, such maps would count as homomorphisms, obscuring important structural information.

A ring homomorphism that is also bijective is a **ring isomorphism**. An isomorphism from a ring to itself is a **ring automorphism**. The automorphisms of a ring $R$ form a group$\mathrm{Aut}(R)$ under composition, and understanding this group is fundamental to Galois theory.

One important consequence: a ring homomorphism $\phi: R \to S$ automatically satisfies$\phi(r^n) = \phi(r)^n$ for all$n \geq 1$ (by induction on$n$) and$\phi(n \cdot a) = n \cdot \phi(a)$ for all$n \in \mathbb{Z}$ (by the additive group homomorphism property). In characteristic$p$, the Frobenius$\phi(a) = a^p$ is a ring homomorphism for any commutative ring of characteristic$p$, as we verified in §20.1.4.

**Example 1.** The canonical map $\pi: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ sending$k \mapsto \bar{k}$ is a ring homomorphism:$\pi(j + k) = \overline{j + k} = \bar{j} + \bar{k}$,$\pi(jk) = \overline{jk} = \bar{j}\bar{k}$, and$\pi(1) = \bar{1}$. This map is surjective with kernel$n\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 2.** The evaluation map $\mathrm{ev}_a: R[x] \to R$ defined by$f \mapsto f(a)$ is a ring homomorphism for any$a \in R$:$\mathrm{ev}_a(f + g) = f(a) + g(a)$,$\mathrm{ev}_a(fg) = f(a)g(a)$, and$\mathrm{ev}_a(1) = 1$. The kernel of$\mathrm{ev}_a$ is the ideal$(x - a)$ of polynomials vanishing at$a$.

**Example 3.** Complex conjugation $\phi: \mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}$,$\phi(a + bi) = a - bi$, is a ring automorphism: it is bijective, preserves addition ($\overline{z + w} = \bar{z} + \bar{w}$), preserves multiplication ($\overline{zw} = \bar{z}\bar{w}$), and fixes the identity ($\bar{1} = 1$). It has order$2$:$\phi^2 = \mathrm{id}$. This is the Galois automorphism of$\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R}$.

**Example 4.** The determinant map $\det: M_n(F) \to F$ is **not** a ring homomorphism (for$n \geq 2$):$\det(A + B) \neq \det A + \det B$ in general. For example,$\det(I + I) = \det(2I) = 2^n$ but$\det I + \det I = 2$, and$2^n \neq 2$ for$n \geq 2$. The determinant is a multiplicative map of monoids (a ring morphism from the multiplicative structure only), not a ring homomorphism.

**Example 5.** The inclusion $\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Q}$,$n \mapsto n/1$, is an injective ring homomorphism. It sends$1_{\mathbb{Z}} = 1$ to$1/1 = 1_{\mathbb{Q}}$, preserves addition and multiplication, and is injective (its kernel is$\{0\}$). This is the prototype for the fraction field construction in Chapter 24: any injective ring homomorphism from an integral domain to a field extends uniquely through the fraction field.
