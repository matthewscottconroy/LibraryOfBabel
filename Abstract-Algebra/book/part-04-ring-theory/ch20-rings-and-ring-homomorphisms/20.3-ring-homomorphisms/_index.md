# 20.3 Ring Homomorphisms

A ring homomorphism is a map $\phi \colon R \to S$ that respects both operations of the ring: $\phi(a + b) = \phi(a) + \phi(b)$, $\phi(ab) = \phi(a)\phi(b)$, and $\phi(1_R) = 1_S$. The first two conditions say that $\phi$ is simultaneously a group homomorphism of the additive groups and a monoid homomorphism of the multiplicative monoids; the third condition, sending multiplicative identities to multiplicative identities, is independent and essential for avoiding degenerate maps such as the zero map from $\mathbb{Z}$ to $\mathbb{Z}$. These three requirements together mean that $\phi$ preserves all the arithmetic of the ring.

The kernel of a ring homomorphism, $\ker \phi = \{r \in R : \phi(r) = 0\}$, is more than a subgroup of the additive group: it is an ideal. The absorption property — if $x \in \ker \phi$ and $r \in R$, then $rx, xr \in \ker \phi$ — follows immediately from the homomorphism condition. Conversely, every ideal $I \subseteq R$ arises as the kernel of the canonical projection $\pi \colon R \to R/I$. This exact correspondence between ideals and kernels is the starting point of Chapter 21 and explains why ideals, not arbitrary subgroups, are the right notion of "normal subring." The image $\phi(R)$ is a subring of $S$, and the First Isomorphism Theorem states $R/\ker\phi \cong \phi(R)$, exactly mirroring the group-theoretic result.

An important application of ring homomorphisms is the characterization of characteristic via a canonical map. For any unital ring $R$, there is a unique ring homomorphism $\chi \colon \mathbb{Z} \to R$ sending $n \mapsto n \cdot 1_R$; its kernel is $n\mathbb{Z}$ where $n$ is the characteristic of $R$. This makes the characteristic a structural invariant of $R$ visible at the level of ring maps, and shows that $\mathbb{Z}$ is the initial object in the category of unital rings — every ring receives exactly one map from $\mathbb{Z}$. The notion of ring isomorphism, when $\phi$ is bijective and both $\phi$ and $\phi^{-1}$ are homomorphisms (the latter being automatic for bijective ring maps), gives the correct notion of sameness for rings and will be used throughout the text to identify quotient rings with familiar objects.
