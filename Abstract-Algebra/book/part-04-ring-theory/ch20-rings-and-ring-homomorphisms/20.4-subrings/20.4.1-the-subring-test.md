# 20.4.1 The Subring Test

Just as a subset of a group can inherit group structure, a subset of a ring can inherit ring structure. The **subring test** gives an efficient criterion for verifying this inheritance, packaging several closure conditions into a practical checklist. Understanding subrings is essential both for identifying the "pieces" of a ring (such as the prime subring and the center) and for understanding how ring constructions produce new rings from old ones.

The definition of a subring must be stated carefully. We want a subset $S \subseteq R$ that is itself a ring under the operations of$R$, and we want the multiplicative identity of$S$ to be the multiplicative identity of$R$. This last requirement is a convention: some authors allow subrings to have their own identity different from$1_R$, but this leads to awkward situations where the inclusion map is not a ring homomorphism (it fails to send$1_S$ to$1_R$). By requiring$1_R \in S$, we ensure that the inclusion$S \hookrightarrow R$ is a ring homomorphism in the full sense.

\begin{definition}
A subset $S \subseteq R$ is a \emph{subring} of$R$ if:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $1_R \in S$,
\item $S$ is closed under subtraction:$a, b \in S \Rightarrow a - b \in S$,
\item $S$ is closed under multiplication:$a, b \in S \Rightarrow ab \in S$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

This is the **subring test**. The second condition encodes both closure under addition (take $b = 0$, which is forced to be in$S$ by the first two conditions:$1 - 1 = 0 \in S$) and the existence of additive inverses ($0 - a = -a \in S$, then$a + b = a - (-b) \in S$). So a subset passes the subring test if and only if it forms a ring under the restricted operations with the same multiplicative identity.

Recall from group theory that the analogous subgroup test did not require checking the identity separately, since it followed from closure under the group operation and inverses. For subrings, the identity must be checked explicitly — or equivalently absorbed into the condition "$1_R \in S$." This reflects the richer structure of rings compared to groups: the multiplicative identity is not determined by the additive structure, so it must be verified separately.

The center of a ring $R$, defined by$Z(R) = \{z \in R : zr = rz \text{ for all } r \in R\}$, is always a subring. It contains$1$ ($1 \cdot r = r = r \cdot 1$), is closed under subtraction (if$z_1$ and$z_2$ commute with all$r$, so does$z_1 - z_2$), and is closed under multiplication (if$z_1 r = r z_1$ and$z_2 r = r z_2$, then$(z_1 z_2) r = z_1(z_2 r) = z_1(r z_2) = (z_1 r) z_2 = (r z_1) z_2 = r(z_1 z_2)$). The center plays the role in ring theory that the center of a group plays in group theory: it is the "commutative part" of the ring.

**Example 1.** The Gaussian integers $\mathbb{Z}[i] = \{a + bi : a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\} \subseteq \mathbb{C}$:$1 = 1 + 0i \in \mathbb{Z}[i]$. For differences:$(a + bi) - (c + di) = (a - c) + (b - d)i \in \mathbb{Z}[i]$ since$a - c, b - d \in \mathbb{Z}$. For products:$(a + bi)(c + di) = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i \in \mathbb{Z}[i]$ since$ac - bd, ad + bc \in \mathbb{Z}$. All three conditions hold, so$\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is a subring of$\mathbb{C}$.

**Example 2.** The ring $\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{2}] = \{a + b\sqrt{2} : a, b \in \mathbb{Q}\}$ is a subring of$\mathbb{R}$:$1 = 1 + 0\sqrt{2} \in \mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{2}]$, differences are of the same form, and$(a + b\sqrt{2})(c + d\sqrt{2}) = (ac + 2bd) + (ad + bc)\sqrt{2} \in \mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{2}]$ since$\mathbb{Q}$ is closed under addition and multiplication.

**Example 3.** The set of upper triangular matrices in $M_n(R)$ (with zeros below the main diagonal) is a subring:$I_n$ is upper triangular, differences of upper triangular matrices are upper triangular, and products of upper triangular matrices are upper triangular (this follows from the formula$(AB)_{ij} = \sum_k A_{ik} B_{kj}$: if$A_{ik} = 0$ for$k < i$ and$B_{kj} = 0$ for$k < j$, then$(AB)_{ij} = 0$ for$j < i$).

**Example 4.** The set $n\mathbb{Z}$ of multiples of$n$ (for$n \geq 2$) is **not** a subring of$\mathbb{Z}$ because$1 \notin n\mathbb{Z}$. However,$n\mathbb{Z}$ is an ideal of$\mathbb{Z}$: it is a subgroup closed under multiplication by all of$\mathbb{Z}$. This illustrates the critical distinction between subrings (which must contain$1$) and ideals (which need not).

**Example 5.** The center of $M_n(F)$ (for a field$F$ and$n \geq 1$) is the set of scalar matrices$\{cI_n : c \in F\} \cong F$. This is a subring isomorphic to$F$. The subring test confirms it:$I_n \in Z(M_n(F))$, differences and products of scalar matrices are scalar matrices, and scalar matrices commute with everything.
