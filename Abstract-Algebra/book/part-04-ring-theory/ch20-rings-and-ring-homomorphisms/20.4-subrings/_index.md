# 20.4 Subrings

A subring of a ring $R$ is a subset $S \subseteq R$ that is itself a ring under the operations inherited from $R$. The subring test gives a concise criterion: a nonempty subset $S$ is a subring if and only if it is closed under subtraction (equivalently, it is a subgroup of the additive group) and closed under multiplication, and contains the multiplicative identity $1_R$. The closure conditions ensure that $S$ inherits all the ring axioms automatically from $R$, since associativity and distributivity hold for all elements of $R$ and hence for all elements of $S$. The test is the subring analogue of the subgroup test from group theory, and it is used constantly when verifying that specific subsets are subrings.

The most canonical subring of any ring is the prime subring, defined as the intersection of all subrings — equivalently, the smallest subring, which is the subring generated by $1_R$. For a ring of characteristic $0$, the prime subring is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}$; for a ring of characteristic $p$, it is isomorphic to $\mathbb{F}_p = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$. This gives a precise sense in which $\mathbb{Z}$ and the fields $\mathbb{F}_p$ are the most fundamental rings: every ring contains a copy of exactly one of them, determined by the characteristic. More generally, for any element $s \in R$, the subring generated by $s$ over the prime subring consists of all polynomial expressions in $s$ with integer coefficients; when $s$ satisfies a polynomial relation over the prime subring, this generated subring is a quotient of a polynomial ring, connecting back to the Kronecker construction.

The relationship between subrings and ideals deserves emphasis. An ideal $I \subseteq R$ is always an additive subgroup, and it is closed under multiplication by $R$, but it need not be a subring in the sense defined above: it may fail to contain $1$. In fact, a nonzero ideal $I$ of a ring $R$ contains $1$ if and only if $I = R$. This means that ideals and subrings are genuinely different concepts, and understanding the distinction is crucial for what follows. Subrings are the "same type of thing" as the ambient ring — another ring, possibly without some features. Ideals are the substructures designed not for similarity but for quotient formation, and it is ideals, not subrings, that are the central objects of Chapter 21.
