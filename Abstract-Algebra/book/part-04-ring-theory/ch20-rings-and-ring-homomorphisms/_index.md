# Chapter 20 — Rings and Ring Homomorphisms

The study of algebra up through groups captures one kind of arithmetic symmetry: a single operation with rich structural consequences. But the mathematics we actually encounter — the integers, polynomials, matrices, continuous functions — nearly always carries two interacting operations: addition and multiplication. A ring is the structure that makes this interaction precise. With a ring, we can add and subtract freely (the additive group) while multiplying with the associativity of a monoid, and the two are bound together by the distributive laws. This minimal package is already enough to do serious arithmetic, and it encompasses an astonishing range of objects.

The ring axioms are deliberately parsimonious. We do not require multiplication to be commutative, nor do we require multiplicative inverses to exist. This generality is not laziness — it is a recognition that some of the most important rings, such as $M_n(k)$, the ring of $n \times n$ matrices over a field, are genuinely non-commutative, and that the failure of invertibility (as in $\mathbb{Z}$) is precisely what makes divisibility theory interesting. A ring in which every nonzero element is invertible is a division ring; if it is also commutative, it is a field. At the other extreme, a ring may harbor zero divisors: nonzero elements $a, b$ with $ab = 0$. A ring with no zero divisors and at least two elements is an integral domain, the setting in which the deeper divisibility theory of Chapter 22 unfolds.

Among the first examples, the integers $\mathbb{Z}$ serve as the universal commutative ring: there is exactly one ring homomorphism from $\mathbb{Z}$ to any unital ring. The quotient rings $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ provide a finite laboratory where the interplay between addition and multiplication is fully visible. Polynomial rings $R[x]$ build new rings from old by adjoining an indeterminate; they will be the central object of Chapters 23. Matrix rings $M_n(k)$ exhibit the full force of non-commutativity. Each example teaches a different lesson about what rings can do, and together they justify the breadth of the axioms.

The correct notion of structure-preserving map between rings is the ring homomorphism: a function $\phi \colon R \to S$ that preserves both addition and multiplication, and sends the multiplicative identity of $R$ to that of $S$. The kernel $\ker \phi = \{r \in R : \phi(r) = 0\}$ is not merely a subgroup of the additive group; it is an ideal, closed under multiplication by arbitrary ring elements. This absorption property is precisely what is needed to form a quotient ring, and the First Isomorphism Theorem for rings mirrors the group-theoretic result: $R/\ker\phi \cong \text{im}(\phi)$. Kernels and ideals are thus two faces of the same phenomenon, and Chapter 21 develops the ideal theory that this observation opens.

The characteristic of a ring $R$ is defined as the smallest positive integer $n$ such that $n \cdot 1 = 0$, or $0$ if no such integer exists. It can be understood as a ring homomorphism from $\mathbb{Z}$: the unique map $\mathbb{Z} \to R$ has kernel $n\mathbb{Z}$, and the characteristic is the generator $n$. In any integral domain, the characteristic is either $0$ or a prime $p$, a constraint that will resurface repeatedly when we study polynomial rings over finite fields and field extensions. This chapter lays the definitional and motivational groundwork for all of ring theory; the structures introduced here — domains, units, zero divisors, homomorphisms — are the vocabulary in which Chapters 21 through 24 are written.
