# 21.1.1 Left, Right, and Two-Sided Ideals; the Absorption Property

Ideals play the same role in ring theory that normal subgroups play in group theory: they are precisely the subsets from which quotient structures can be built, and they are precisely the kernels of homomorphisms. But to appreciate why this is true — why ideals and not subrings are the right objects — one must understand what ideals add beyond the subring condition, and why this extra structure is exactly what the quotient construction requires.

The intuition begins with a problem. We want to form a "quotient ring" in which a given subset $I$ "becomes zero." In group theory, the analogous construction requires a **normal** subgroup — a subgroup stable under conjugation by arbitrary group elements. In ring theory, the analogous requirement is the **absorption property**: an ideal is not just a subset closed under the ring's own multiplication, but one that absorbs multiplication from the entire ambient ring.

\begin{definition}
A subset $I \subseteq R$ is:
\begin{itemize}
\item a \emph{left ideal} if $(I, +)$ is a subgroup of$(R, +)$ and$RI \subseteq I$ (i.e.,$ra \in I$ for all$r \in R$,$a \in I$);
\item a \emph{right ideal} if $(I, +)$ is a subgroup of$(R, +)$ and$IR \subseteq I$ (i.e.,$ar \in I$ for all$a \in I$,$r \in R$);
\item a \emph{two-sided ideal} (or simply \emph{ideal}), written $I \trianglelefteq R$, if it is both a left and right ideal.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

In a commutative ring, left and right ideals coincide, so one simply speaks of ideals. In a noncommutative ring, the distinction matters: one-sided ideals control one-sided module structures, and the theory becomes more intricate.

The critical condition is the **absorption property** $RI \subseteq I$. To appreciate why this matters, contrast it with the subring condition. A subring$S$ is closed under multiplication of its own elements: if$a, b \in S$ then$ab \in S$. An ideal$I$ is closed under multiplication from the entire ambient ring: if$a \in I$ and$r \in R$ (with$r$ possibly not in$I$), then$ra \in I$. The ideal absorbs arbitrary ring elements, not just its own. This much stronger property is what allows us to form a quotient ring.

To see why subrings are not enough, consider $\mathbb{Z}$ as a subring of$\mathbb{Q}$. The coset$3 + \mathbb{Z}$ and the coset$7 + \mathbb{Z}$ are the same coset in$(\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}, +)$. But$\frac{1}{2} \cdot 3 = \frac{3}{2}$ and$\frac{1}{2} \cdot 7 = \frac{7}{2}$ lie in different cosets of$\mathbb{Z}$ in$\mathbb{Q}$, so multiplying cosets by$\frac{1}{2}$ is not well-defined. The issue is that$\mathbb{Z}$ does not absorb multiplication by$\frac{1}{2}$. Ideals, by contrast, prevent exactly this failure by requiring absorption from the entire ring.

The analogy with normal subgroups is precise: ideals are to rings as normal subgroups are to groups, for exactly the same structural reason. A normal subgroup $N \trianglelefteq G$ is one where$gNg^{-1} \subseteq N$ for all$g$ — conjugation by any group element stabilizes$N$. An ideal is one where$RI \subseteq I$ — multiplication by any ring element stabilizes$I$. In both cases, the stability condition is exactly what makes the quotient construction well-defined.

**Example 1.** The ideal $(n) = n\mathbb{Z}$ in$\mathbb{Z}$ is a two-sided ideal: it is a subgroup of$(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ (multiples of$n$ are closed under addition and negation), and$m \cdot nk = n(mk) \in n\mathbb{Z}$ for any$m \in \mathbb{Z}$ and$nk \in n\mathbb{Z}$. The quotient ring$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is the familiar ring of residues.

**Example 2.** In $M_2(F)$ (for a field$F$), the set of matrices whose first column is zero,$I = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 0 & * \\ 0 & * \end{pmatrix} \right\}$, is a **right ideal** but not a left ideal. To check: for$A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & a \\ 0 & b \end{pmatrix} \in I$ and$B = \begin{pmatrix} c & d \\ e & f \end{pmatrix} \in M_2(F)$, we have$AB = \begin{pmatrix} ae & af \\ be & bf \end{pmatrix}$, which need not have zero first column. But$BA = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & ca + db \\ 0 & ea + fb \end{pmatrix} \in I$ — wait, that is left multiplication. Actually$A \cdot B$ need not be in$I$, confirming$I$ is a right ideal but not left.

**Example 3.** In $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, the set$I = (2, x) = \{2f(x) + xg(x) : f, g \in \mathbb{Z}[x]\}$ is a two-sided ideal. It consists of all polynomials with even constant term. The absorption property holds: if$h \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ and$p = 2f + xg \in I$, then$hp = h \cdot 2f + h \cdot xg = 2(hf) + x(hg) \in I$.

**Example 4.** The zero ideal $\{0\}$ and the unit ideal$R$ are always ideals. The zero ideal corresponds to the "do nothing" quotient (we force only$0$ to equal$0$, giving$R/\{0\} \cong R$), and the unit ideal corresponds to the zero ring (we force every element to equal$0$, giving$R/R = 0$). All other ideals are called **proper**, and it is the proper ideals that carry interesting information.

**Example 5.** In a field $F$, the only ideals are$\{0\}$ and$F$. If$I$ is a nonzero ideal, it contains some$a \neq 0$; then$a^{-1} \in F$ and$a^{-1} \cdot a = 1 \in I$ (by absorption), so$I$ contains$1$ and hence contains$r \cdot 1 = r$ for all$r \in F$, giving$I = F$. This explains why fields are "simple rings" — they have no nontrivial two-sided ideals — and it is the ring-theoretic reason why fields have no interesting quotients.
