# 21.1.2 The Kernel of a Ring Homomorphism is an Ideal

The connection between ideals and ring homomorphisms is not incidental — it is a perfect biconditional that gives ideals their fundamental importance. Just as normal subgroups are precisely the kernels of group homomorphisms, ideals are precisely the kernels of ring homomorphisms. Understanding this equivalence is the key to using ideals effectively.

If $\phi: R \to S$ is a ring homomorphism, then$\ker \phi = \{r \in R : \phi(r) = 0\}$ is a two-sided ideal of$R$. The additive subgroup condition is immediate from the fact that$\phi$ is a homomorphism of additive groups: if$\phi(r) = \phi(r') = 0$, then$\phi(r - r') = \phi(r) - \phi(r') = 0$, so$r - r' \in \ker \phi$. For the absorption property, take$r \in \ker \phi$ and any$s \in R$. Then$\phi(sr) = \phi(s)\phi(r) = \phi(s) \cdot 0 = 0$, so$sr \in \ker \phi$. Similarly$\phi(rs) = \phi(r)\phi(s) = 0 \cdot \phi(s) = 0$, so$rs \in \ker \phi$. Thus kernels are two-sided ideals.

The converse is equally important: every ideal arises as a kernel. Given any ideal $I \trianglelefteq R$, the natural projection$\pi: R \to R/I$ defined by$\pi(r) = r + I$ is a ring homomorphism (as we will verify in §21.2.1), and$\ker \pi = I$. The ring$R/I$ is called the quotient ring of$R$ by$I$, and the whole point of the quotient construction is precisely to force the elements of$I$ to become zero. So the ideals of$R$ are precisely the kernels of ring homomorphisms out of$R$ — the biconditional is exact.

This perfect correspondence mirrors the group-theoretic situation. In group theory, the normal subgroups of $G$ are precisely the kernels of group homomorphisms out of$G$, and the quotient$G/N$ is the universal group in which$N$ collapses to the identity. The same logic applies here: the quotient ring$R/I$ is the universal ring in which the elements of$I$ become zero. Every ring map from$R$ that kills$I$ factors through$R/I$. This universal role of quotient rings will be made precise in §21.2.2.

It is worth pausing to appreciate why the kernel is a two-sided ideal and not merely a left or right ideal. The calculation $\phi(rs) = \phi(r)\phi(s) = 0 \cdot \phi(s) = 0$ shows right absorption, and$\phi(sr) = \phi(s) \cdot 0 = 0$ shows left absorption. Both calculations follow from$\phi$ respecting multiplication and$\phi(r) = 0$ — and a ring homomorphism must respect multiplication from both sides. So the symmetry of the ideal condition (absorbing ring elements from both sides) is exactly what the symmetry of a ring homomorphism (respecting products in both positions) produces.

**Example 1.** Let $R = \mathbb{Z}$ and let$\phi: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ be the canonical reduction map$n \mapsto n \bmod 6$. Then$\ker \phi = 6\mathbb{Z} = \{0, \pm 6, \pm 12, \ldots\}$, the set of multiples of$6$. This is the ideal$(6) \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$. The fact that$\ker \phi$ is an ideal is evident: a multiple of 6 plus another multiple of 6 is a multiple of 6, and any integer times a multiple of 6 is a multiple of 6.

**Example 2.** Let $\phi: \mathbb{Z}[x] \to \mathbb{Z}$ be the evaluation map at$2$:$\phi(f) = f(2)$. This is a ring homomorphism (evaluation at any fixed ring element always is). The kernel is$\ker \phi = \{f \in \mathbb{Z}[x] : f(2) = 0\}$, the set of polynomials that vanish at$2$. One checks that$\ker \phi = (x - 2) = \{(x-2) \cdot g : g \in \mathbb{Z}[x]\}$: certainly every multiple of$(x-2)$ vanishes at$2$, and conversely, dividing$f$ by$(x-2)$ and using$f(2) = 0$ forces the remainder to be zero (by the Factor Theorem). The kernel is the principal ideal generated by$x - 2$.

**Example 3.** Consider $\phi: \mathbb{R}[x] \to \mathbb{C}$ defined by$\phi(f) = f(i)$ (evaluating at$i = \sqrt{-1}$). This is a ring homomorphism. The kernel is$\ker \phi = \{f \in \mathbb{R}[x] : f(i) = 0\}$. Since$i$ satisfies$x^2 + 1 = 0$, we have$(x^2 + 1) \mid f$ for any$f \in \ker \phi$ over$\mathbb{R}[x]$ (because$x^2 + 1$ is the minimal polynomial of$i$ over$\mathbb{R}$). Conversely, every multiple of$x^2 + 1$ vanishes at$i$. So$\ker \phi = (x^2 + 1)$. The image of$\phi$ is all of$\mathbb{C}$ (since$\phi$ is surjective: any$a + bi \in \mathbb{C}$ is$\phi(a + bx)$). The first isomorphism theorem will then give$\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2 + 1) \cong \mathbb{C}$.

**Example 4.** Let $R = M_2(\mathbb{R})$ be the ring of$2 \times 2$ real matrices, and let$\phi: M_2(\mathbb{R}) \to \mathbb{R}$ be the function$\phi(A) = \mathrm{tr}(A)$ (the trace). This is NOT a ring homomorphism, because$\mathrm{tr}(AB) \neq \mathrm{tr}(A)\mathrm{tr}(B)$ in general. Hence there is no reason to expect that$\ker(\mathrm{tr}) = \{A : \mathrm{tr}(A) = 0\}$ is an ideal — and indeed it is not (the trace-zero matrices do not form an ideal, since the product of two trace-zero matrices need not have trace zero). This example reinforces that "kernel of a ring homomorphism" is a stronger condition than "kernel of an additive group homomorphism."

**Example 5.** Let $\phi: \mathbb{Z}[x] \to (\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})[x]$ be the coefficient reduction map, sending each coefficient modulo$2$. For instance,$\phi(3x^2 - 5x + 1) = x^2 + x + 1$ in$(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})[x]$. This is a ring homomorphism (reduction modulo 2 commutes with addition and multiplication of polynomials). The kernel is$\ker \phi = \{f \in \mathbb{Z}[x] : \text{all coefficients of } f \text{ are even}\} = 2\mathbb{Z}[x]$, the ideal generated by$2$ in$\mathbb{Z}[x]$. One verifies:$2\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is closed under addition (sums of even-coefficient polynomials have even coefficients) and absorbs ring multiplication (any polynomial times an even-coefficient polynomial has even coefficients). The quotient$\mathbb{Z}[x]/2\mathbb{Z}[x] \cong (\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})[x]$, as the first isomorphism theorem will confirm.

The rest of this chapter develops the quotient construction systematically, making precise the sense in which $R/I$ is the universal ring that kills$I$.
