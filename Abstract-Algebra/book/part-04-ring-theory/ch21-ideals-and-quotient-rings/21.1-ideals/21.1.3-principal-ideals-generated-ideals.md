# 21.1.3 Principal Ideals $(a) = aR$; Generated Ideals

Not every ideal requires a complicated description. In many of the rings we care about most — the integers, polynomial rings over fields — every ideal can be specified by a single element. Understanding principal ideals is therefore a starting point for understanding the ideal structure of a ring, and the failure of principality will be the key distinction separating the levels of the domain hierarchy in Chapter 22.

For an element $a$ in a commutative ring$R$, the *principal ideal generated by$a$* is the set$(a) = aR = \{ra : r \in R\}$. This is the smallest ideal of$R$ containing$a$: it contains$a$ (take$r = 1$), it is closed under addition ($ra + sa = (r+s)a$), and it absorbs multiplication from$R$ ($s \cdot (ra) = (sr)a$). The notation$(a)$ emphasizes that the ideal is determined by one element; we also write$\langle a \rangle$ in some contexts. The principal ideal$(0) = \{0\}$ is the zero ideal, and$(1) = R$ is the entire ring — so every ideal lies between$(0)$ and$(1)$.

In a noncommutative ring, the situation is more nuanced. The ideal generated by a single element $a$ must absorb multiplication from both sides, so one must take all finite sums of elements of the form$ras$ with$r, s \in R$. This is the *two-sided ideal* generated by$a$, denoted$(a) = RaR$. When$R$ is commutative (as will be the case in most of our applications), the distinction disappears.

More generally, the ideal generated by a finite set $\{a_1, \ldots, a_n\}$ in a commutative ring is
$$(a_1, \ldots, a_n) = \{r_1 a_1 + \cdots + r_n a_n : r_i \in R\}.$$
This is the smallest ideal containing all $a_i$ simultaneously. The set is closed under addition and absorbs ring multiplication term-by-term. One can also describe it as the intersection of all ideals containing$a_1, \ldots, a_n$, which shows it is the smallest such ideal.

The relationship between the ideal-theoretic language and divisibility is direct: in a commutative ring, $(b) \subseteq (a)$ if and only if$a \mid b$. This is because$b \in (a)$ means$b = ra$ for some$r \in R$, which is precisely the definition of$a \mid b$. Thus the lattice of principal ideals (ordered by reverse divisibility) is isomorphic to the divisibility preorder on$R/\!\!\sim$ (elements modulo the associate relation). This dictionary between ideals and divisibility will be central to the domain hierarchy of Chapter 22.

**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{Z}$, every ideal is principal. The ideal$(n) = n\mathbb{Z} = \{\ldots, -2n, -n, 0, n, 2n, \ldots\}$ is the set of all multiples of$n$. The ideal$(6)$ consists of multiples of$6$. The sum$(2) + (3)$: since$1 = (-1) \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 3$, we have$1 \in (2) + (3)$, so$(2) + (3) = (1) = \mathbb{Z}$ — a direct reflection of$\gcd(2,3) = 1$. More generally,$(m) + (n) = (\gcd(m,n))$ in$\mathbb{Z}$, because the GCD is the smallest positive integer expressible as an integer linear combination of$m$ and$n$ (Bézout's theorem).

**Example 2.** In $\mathbb{Q}[x]$, the ideal generated by$x^2 - 1$ is$(x^2 - 1) = \{f \cdot (x^2-1) : f \in \mathbb{Q}[x]\}$, consisting of all polynomials divisible by$x^2-1$. Since$x^2 - 1 = (x-1)(x+1)$, this is the set of polynomials with both$1$ and$-1$ as roots (counted with multiplicity$\geq 1$). Note$(x-1) \supset (x^2-1)$ since$x^2-1 = (x-1)(x+1)$, meaning$(x-1)$ is a "larger" ideal (contains more elements).

**Example 3.** In $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, the ideal$(2, x) = \{2f + xg : f, g \in \mathbb{Z}[x]\}$ consists of all polynomials with even constant term. This ideal is *not* principal. If$(2, x) = (h)$ for some$h \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$, then$h \mid 2$ and$h \mid x$. Since$h \mid 2$, we have$h \in \{1, 2, -1, -2\}$ (the divisors of 2 in$\mathbb{Z}$, which embed into$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ as constants). But$h \mid x$ forces the degree of$h$ to be at most the degree of$x$, so$h$ is a constant dividing$x$, meaning$h = \pm 1$. But then$(h) = \mathbb{Z}[x] \neq (2, x)$, since$(2, x)$ does not contain the polynomial$1$ (the constant$1$ has odd constant term$1 \neq 0$, and no element$2f + xg$ equals$1$ unless$2 \mid 1$, which is false). So no single polynomial generates$(2, x)$.

**Example 4.** The ideal $(x^2, y)$ in$\mathbb{R}[x, y]$ requires two generators. It consists of all polynomials of the form$f \cdot x^2 + g \cdot y$ with$f, g \in \mathbb{R}[x, y]$. Intuitively, these are polynomials that vanish, together with their$x$-derivative, along the line$\{y = 0\}$ — that is, that vanish to order$\geq 2$ when restricted to the$x$-axis. One can show$(x^2, y)$ is not principal by a degree argument similar to that above.

**Example 5.** In a field $F$, every nonzero element is a unit, so$(a) = F$ for any$a \neq 0$ and$(0) = \{0\}$. There are only two ideals in a field, and both are principal. This is consistent with the fact that fields sit at the top of the domain hierarchy — they have the simplest possible ideal structure.

The failure of principal generation at the level of $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ — exemplified by the ideal$(2, x)$ — is the key distinction between$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ and the PID$\mathbb{Z}$. This points ahead to the domain hierarchy in Chapter 22, where we will see that the condition "every ideal is principal" (the PID condition) is strictly stronger than the condition "unique factorization holds" (the UFD condition).
