# 21.1.4 Operations on Ideals: Sum, Product, Intersection

Ideals can be combined in multiple ways, giving the collection of all ideals of a ring a rich internal structure. The three fundamental operations — sum, intersection, and product — each serve a distinct purpose and behave differently from one another. Understanding these operations is essential for working with quotient rings, localization, and the arithmetic of ideals in Dedekind domains.

The *sum* $I + J = \{a + b : a \in I, b \in J\}$ is the smallest ideal containing both$I$ and$J$. It is an ideal: if$a + b$ and$a' + b'$ are elements (with$a, a' \in I$ and$b, b' \in J$), then$(a + b) + (a' + b') = (a + a') + (b + b') \in I + J$ since$a + a' \in I$ and$b + b' \in J$. For absorption:$r(a + b) = ra + rb$ with$ra \in I$ (since$I$ is an ideal) and$rb \in J$ (since$J$ is an ideal), so$r(a+b) \in I + J$. In$\mathbb{Z}$, the sum of ideals$(m) + (n) = (\gcd(m, n))$, which encodes Bézout's identity: the smallest positive integer expressible as$um + vn$ is$\gcd(m,n)$.

The *intersection* $I \cap J$ is also an ideal (the largest ideal contained in both). Closure under addition and absorption follow immediately from the ideal property of$I$ and$J$ separately. In$\mathbb{Z}$,$(m) \cap (n) = (\mathrm{lcm}(m,n))$: an integer is a multiple of both$m$ and$n$ exactly when it is a multiple of their least common multiple. Together, sum and intersection give the ideals of a ring the structure of a *lattice*, with$I + J$ as the join (least upper bound) and$I \cap J$ as the meet (greatest lower bound).

The *product* $IJ$ is the ideal generated by all products$ab$ with$a \in I$ and$b \in J$. Explicitly,
$$IJ = \left\{\sum_{i=1}^n a_i b_i : a_i \in I,\, b_i \in J,\, n \geq 1\right\}.$$
Note that we must take finite sums of products, not merely individual products $ab$, in order to ensure closure under addition. The product$IJ$ is always contained in$I \cap J$ (since$ab \in I$ by left absorption of$J$ into$I$, and$ab \in J$ by right absorption of$I$ into$J$), but the containment can be strict. In$\mathbb{Z}$,$(m)(n) = (mn)$ while$(m) \cap (n) = (\mathrm{lcm}(m,n))$, and these agree only when$\gcd(m,n) = 1$.

The *quotient ideal* (or colon ideal) $I : J = \{r \in R : rJ \subseteq I\}$ is also an ideal, measuring how much of$J$ can be "divided" into$I$. One verifies it is an ideal: if$r, r' \in I : J$ and$s \in J$, then$(r + r')s = rs + r's \in I + I = I$, and$(t \cdot r)s = t(rs) \in I$ for any$t \in R$.

**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{Z}$, compute$(12) + (8)$. Since$\gcd(12, 8) = 4$, we have$(12) + (8) = (4)$. Explicitly,$4 = 12 \cdot (-1) + 8 \cdot 2$, confirming$4 \in (12) + (8)$, and any element$12a + 8b = 4(3a + 2b)$ is a multiple of$4$. Also$(12) \cap (8) = (\mathrm{lcm}(12,8)) = (24)$. And$(12)(8) = (96)$, which equals$(96) \neq (24)$; note$24 \nmid 96$... wait:$(96) \subsetneq (24)$ since every multiple of$96$ is a multiple of$24$, confirming$IJ \subseteq I \cap J$.

**Example 2.** In $\mathbb{Z}$, take$I = (6)$ and$J = (10)$. Then$\gcd(6,10) = 2$ so$I + J = (2)$. The lcm is$30$, so$I \cap J = (30)$. The product$IJ = (60)$. We have$(60) \subsetneq (30) \subsetneq (2)$, illustrating all three operations giving distinct ideals. The quotient ideal$(6) : (10) = \{n \in \mathbb{Z} : 10n \in (6)\} = \{n : 6 \mid 10n\} = \{n : 3 \mid 5n\} = \{n : 3 \mid n\} = (3)$.

**Example 3.** In $F[x]$ for a field$F$, let$I = (x-1)$ and$J = (x^2 - 1) = (x-1)(x+1)$. Then$I + J = (x-1)$ (since$J \subseteq I$, the sum is just$I$),$I \cap J = J$ (since$J \subseteq I$), and$IJ = ((x-1)^2(x+1)) = ((x-1)(x^2-1))$. The product ideal$IJ$ is strictly smaller than$I \cap J = J$ in this case.

**Example 4.** In $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, the ideals$(2)$ and$(x)$ (the multiples of$2$ and the multiples of$x$) satisfy$(2) + (x) = (2, x)$ (polynomials with even constant term),$(2) \cap (x) = (2x)$ (multiples of$2x$), and$(2)(x) = (2x)$ as well. Here the product equals the intersection:$\gcd(2, x) = 1$ in the UFD$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ (these have no common irreducible factor), so$IJ = I \cap J$. This is the multivariable analogue of the$\gcd = 1$ case for integers.

**Example 5.** The *primary decomposition* of ideals uses these operations systematically. In $\mathbb{Z}$, any ideal$(n) = (p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_k^{a_k})$ can be expressed as an intersection$(n) = (p_1^{a_1}) \cap \cdots \cap (p_k^{a_k})$. Each$(p_i^{a_i})$ is a primary ideal (a power of a prime ideal in a PID). This decomposition is the ideal-theoretic version of the fundamental theorem of arithmetic, and it generalizes to Noetherian rings via the Lasker–Noether theorem on primary decomposition.

These four operations together equip the ideal lattice with a structure that mirrors, in the commutative case, the lattice of divisors of an integer — a perspective that becomes precise in the theory of Dedekind domains in §22.5, where every ideal factors uniquely as a product of prime ideals.
