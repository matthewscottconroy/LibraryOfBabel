# 21.1 Ideals

An ideal of a ring $R$ is a nonempty subset $I \subseteq R$ that is closed under addition, closed under subtraction, and satisfies the absorption property: for all $r \in R$ and $x \in I$, both $rx$ and $xr$ belong to $I$. In a commutative ring, the two absorption conditions coincide, and an ideal is simply an additive subgroup that is "swallowed" by multiplication from outside. The absorption property is the feature that distinguishes ideals from arbitrary subrings: a subring contains $1$ and is closed under multiplication within itself, while an ideal absorbs multiplication from the entire ambient ring and typically does not contain $1$. This difference means that ideals are designed not to sit inside $R$ as rings in their own right, but to serve as the kernels of ring homomorphisms and as the building blocks of quotient rings.

The simplest ideals are principal: for any $a \in R$, the principal ideal $(a) = \{ra : r \in R\}$ (in a commutative ring) is the smallest ideal containing $a$. In $\mathbb{Z}$, every ideal is principal — this is the defining property of a principal ideal domain — and the principal ideals $(n)$ correspond exactly to the multiples of $n$, recovering the familiar structure of integer divisibility. In polynomial rings $F[x]$ over a field, every ideal is similarly principal, generated by the GCD of all polynomials in the ideal. In rings like $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, however, the ideal $(2, x)$ consisting of polynomials with even constant term requires two generators and is not principal: this non-principality is a meaningful structural property of the ring, not a pathology.

Ideals can be combined in several ways that reflect arithmetic intuitions. The sum $I + J = \{a + b : a \in I, b \in J\}$ is the smallest ideal containing both $I$ and $J$; the intersection $I \cap J$ is the largest ideal contained in both; the product $IJ$, consisting of all finite sums of products $ab$ with $a \in I$ and $b \in J$, is contained in $I \cap J$ but may be strictly smaller. These operations on ideals give the set of ideals of $R$ a rich algebraic structure of its own, and in Dedekind domains — the class of rings studied in Chapter 22 — the product operation makes the nonzero ideals into a free abelian group, giving a perfect analogue of unique factorization at the level of ideals.
