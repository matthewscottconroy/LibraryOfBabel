# 21.2.1 Constructing $R/I$; Well-Definedness Requires the Ideal Property

The quotient ring construction formalizes the idea of "working modulo $I$": we declare the elements of$I$ to be zero and ask what ring structure survives. The construction is straightforward, but the verification that it works reveals precisely why ideals — and not merely subrings — are the correct objects for forming quotients.

Given an ideal $I \trianglelefteq R$, the underlying set of the *quotient ring*$R/I$ is the set of cosets$\{a + I : a \in R\}$ of$I$ as a subgroup of the additive group$(R, +)$. Since$I$ is a normal subgroup of the abelian group$(R, +)$ (and every subgroup of an abelian group is normal), the quotient group$(R/I, +)$ is already a well-defined abelian group. Addition of cosets is given by$(a + I) + (b + I) = (a + b) + I$. The question is whether multiplication on cosets is well-defined.

We define multiplication by $(a + I)(b + I) = ab + I$. To check well-definedness, we must verify that if$a + I = a' + I$ (so$a' - a \in I$) and$b + I = b' + I$ (so$b' - b \in I$), then$a'b' + I = ab + I$. Writing$a' = a + s$ and$b' = b + t$ with$s, t \in I$, we compute
$$a'b' = (a + s)(b + t) = ab + at + sb + st.$$
The terms $at$,$sb$, and$st$ all lie in$I$:$at \in RI \subseteq I$ because$I$ is a left ideal,$sb \in IR \subseteq I$ because$I$ is a right ideal, and$st \in I$ because$I$ is closed under multiplication. Thus$a'b' - ab = at + sb + st \in I$, confirming$a'b' + I = ab + I$.

This is the precise reason we need $I$ to be an ideal rather than merely a subgroup or a subring. The terms$at$ and$sb$ require the absorption property from both sides:$at$ requires right absorption ($RI \subseteq I$) and$sb$ requires left absorption ($IR \subseteq I$). A subring that is not an ideal would fail this check. For instance,$\mathbb{Z}$ is a subring of$\mathbb{Q}$, but the coset multiplication on$\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$ is not well-defined as a ring structure (though it is a well-defined abelian group quotient). With an ideal in hand,$R/I$ inherits a well-defined ring structure, and the projection$\pi: R \to R/I$,$\pi(r) = r + I$, is a surjective ring homomorphism with kernel$I$.

Once we know multiplication is well-defined, verifying the ring axioms for $R/I$ is routine: commutativity (if$R$ is commutative), associativity, and distributivity all follow from the corresponding properties in$R$, since the coset representatives are just elements of$R$ and the coset operation reduces to the operation on$R$. The multiplicative identity is$1 + I$ (since$(1 + I)(a + I) = a + I$).

**Example 1.** The most familiar example: $R = \mathbb{Z}$,$I = (n) = n\mathbb{Z}$. The quotient ring$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ has elements$\{0 + n\mathbb{Z}, 1 + n\mathbb{Z}, \ldots, (n-1) + n\mathbb{Z}\}$, written simply$\{0, 1, \ldots, n-1\}$ where arithmetic is done modulo$n$. Multiplication is well-defined because$n\mathbb{Z}$ absorbs: if$a \equiv a' \pmod{n}$ and$b \equiv b' \pmod{n}$, then$ab \equiv a'b' \pmod{n}$. The quotient ring$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ contains zero divisors:$2 \cdot 3 = 6 \equiv 0$, corresponding to the fact that$(6) = (2)(3)$ is a product of two proper ideals.

**Example 2.** Let $R = \mathbb{R}[x]$ and$I = (x^2 + 1)$. The cosets are$f + (x^2+1)$ for$f \in \mathbb{R}[x]$, and every coset has a unique representative of degree$\leq 1$ (by dividing by$x^2 + 1$). So elements of$\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2+1)$ look like$a + bx$ with$a, b \in \mathbb{R}$. Multiplication:$(a + bx)(c + dx) = ac + (ad + bc)x + bd \cdot x^2$. Since$x^2 \equiv -1$ in the quotient (because$x^2 + 1 \equiv 0$), this becomes$(ac - bd) + (ad + bc)x$. This is precisely the multiplication law for complex numbers! So$\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2+1) \cong \mathbb{C}$.

**Example 3.** Take $R = \mathbb{Z}[x]$ and$I = (2, x^2 + x + 1)$. The cosets are represented by polynomials modulo both$2$ and$x^2 + x + 1$. First reduce coefficients mod$2$ (using$I \ni 2$), then reduce the degree mod$x^2 + x + 1$ (using$x^2 \equiv -x - 1 \equiv x + 1$ in$\mathbb{F}_2[x]$, since we're working mod 2). Elements of$R/I$ are thus represented as$a + bx$ with$a, b \in \{0, 1\}$, giving a ring of 4 elements. This is in fact the field$\mathbb{F}_4$: the polynomial$x^2 + x + 1$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{F}_2$, so the quotient is a field.

**Example 4.** Let $R = \mathbb{Z}$ and consider the subring$S = 2\mathbb{Z}$ (the even integers). This is a subring (closed under addition, subtraction, and multiplication) but it is not an ideal of$\mathbb{Z}$: for instance,$1 \cdot 2 = 2 \in S$ but there is no absorption failure — actually$2\mathbb{Z}$ is an ideal of$\mathbb{Z}$. Let us instead try$S = \{0, 2, 4\} \subset \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$. This is a subring of$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ (it is the cyclic subgroup$\langle 2 \rangle$ under addition, and$2 \cdot 2 = 4 \in S$,$4 \cdot 4 = 16 \equiv 4 \in S$,$2 \cdot 4 = 8 \equiv 2 \in S$). But$S$ is not an ideal:$1 \cdot 2 = 2 \in S$, but$3 \cdot 2 = 6 \equiv 0 \notin S$ — wait,$0 \in S$. Actually$S$ is an ideal here (it absorbs:$r \cdot s$ for$r \in \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ and$s \in S = \{0,2,4\}$ lands in$S$). The quotient$(\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z})/S$ has 2 elements and is isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, consistent with the correspondence theorem.
