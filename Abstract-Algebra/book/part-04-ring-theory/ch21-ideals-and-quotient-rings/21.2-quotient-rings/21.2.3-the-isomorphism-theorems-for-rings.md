# 21.2.3 The Isomorphism Theorems for Rings

The isomorphism theorems for rings are direct analogues of those for groups, and their proofs follow the same logic. They are best understood not as isolated results but as a coherent package that describes how ring homomorphisms interact with quotient constructions. Each theorem identifies a naturally arising isomorphism, and knowing these isomorphisms saves considerable work in explicit calculations.

The *First Isomorphism Theorem* is the most fundamental: for any ring homomorphism $\phi: R \to S$, the induced map$\bar\phi: R/\ker\phi \to \mathrm{im}\,\phi$ is a ring isomorphism. The proof is immediate from the universal property:$\bar\phi$ is injective because$\ker\bar\phi = \ker\phi/\ker\phi = 0$ (the only coset that maps to zero is the zero coset), and it is surjective onto$\mathrm{im}\,\phi$ by definition. In diagram form, any ring homomorphism$\phi$ factors as$R \xrightarrow{\pi} R/\ker\phi \xrightarrow{\sim} \mathrm{im}\,\phi \hookrightarrow S$, where the middle map is an isomorphism. This factorization is the canonical way to decompose a ring map into a surjection followed by an isomorphism followed by an injection.

The *Second Isomorphism Theorem* involves a subring and an ideal simultaneously: if $S \leq R$ is a subring and$I \trianglelefteq R$ is an ideal, then$S \cap I$ is an ideal of$S$, and$(S + I)/I \cong S/(S \cap I)$. To see this, consider the composite map$S \hookrightarrow R \xrightarrow{\pi} R/I$. The image of$S$ under$\pi$ is$(S + I)/I$, and the kernel is$\{s \in S : s \in I\} = S \cap I$. By the first isomorphism theorem applied to this composite,$S/(S \cap I) \cong (S + I)/I$. This theorem says: the portion of the quotient$R/I$ contributed by$S$ is isomorphic to$S$ modulo the part of$I$ that intersects$S$.

The *Third Isomorphism Theorem* handles nested quotients: if $I \subseteq J$ are both ideals of$R$, then$J/I$ is an ideal of$R/I$ and$(R/I)/(J/I) \cong R/J$. Intuitively, quotienting out$I$ first and then$J/I$ has the same effect as quotienting out$J$ directly. The proof: the canonical map$\pi: R \to R/J$ has kernel$J$. Composing with the projection$R \to R/I$ gives a map$R/I \to R/J$ with kernel$J/I$. The first isomorphism theorem then gives the result. In everyday language: if we first forget the difference between elements that are$I$-equivalent, and then further forget the difference between elements that are$J$-equivalent, we end up forgetting all$J$-equivalences — regardless of the order of the two forgettings.

**Example 1.** For the first isomorphism theorem: the evaluation map $\phi: \mathbb{R}[x] \to \mathbb{C}$ defined by$\phi(f) = f(i)$ is surjective (any$a + bi = \phi(a + bx)$) and has$\ker\phi = (x^2 + 1)$ (the polynomials vanishing at$i$, which are multiples of the minimal polynomial$x^2 + 1$ of$i$ over$\mathbb{R}$). The first isomorphism theorem gives$\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2+1) \cong \mathbb{C}$. This is the cleanest construction of$\mathbb{C}$ as a quotient ring.

**Example 2.** Another instance of the first isomorphism theorem: the map $\phi: \mathbb{Z}[x] \to \mathbb{F}_p[x]$ sending coefficients mod$p$ is a surjective ring homomorphism with$\ker\phi = (p)$, so$\mathbb{Z}[x]/(p) \cong \mathbb{F}_p[x]$. More generally, the map$\phi: \mathbb{Z}[x] \to \mathbb{F}_p[x]/(f)$ (for an irreducible$f \in \mathbb{F}_p[x]$) has kernel$(p, \tilde f)$ where$\tilde f$ is any lift of$f$ to$\mathbb{Z}[x]$, so$\mathbb{Z}[x]/(p, \tilde f) \cong \mathbb{F}_p[x]/(f)$, a finite field of order$p^{\deg f}$.

**Example 3.** For the second isomorphism theorem in $R = \mathbb{Z}$: let$S = 3\mathbb{Z}$ (the subring of multiples of 3) and$I = 6\mathbb{Z}$. Then$S \cap I = 6\mathbb{Z}$ and$S + I = 3\mathbb{Z} + 6\mathbb{Z} = 3\mathbb{Z}$ (since$6\mathbb{Z} \subseteq 3\mathbb{Z}$). So$(S + I)/I = 3\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ and$S/(S \cap I) = 3\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$. Both are cyclic groups (rings, in fact) of order 2, and the theorem confirms they are isomorphic. In this case the isomorphism is trivial, but in more complex examples the theorem saves explicit computations.

**Example 4.** For the third isomorphism theorem in $R = \mathbb{Z}$: let$I = (12)$ and$J = (4)$. Then$I \subseteq J$ (since$12 = 4 \cdot 3$ so$(12) \subseteq (4)$),$J/I = 4\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ (three cosets:$\{4\mathbb{Z}, 4+12\mathbb{Z}, 8+12\mathbb{Z}\}$), and$(R/I)/(J/I) = (\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z})/(\{0,4,8\}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$. On the other hand,$R/J = \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$. The theorem confirms$(\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z})/(\{0,4,8\}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ — quotienting out the subgroup of multiples of 4 in$\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ gives$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$.

Each of these theorems reflects the categorical nature of ring homomorphisms and would hold in any algebraic category with normal subobjects — a perspective made precise in homological algebra. The first isomorphism theorem in particular is the workhorse result: it is used whenever we want to identify a quotient ring with a more familiar ring, by finding a surjective homomorphism with the right kernel.
