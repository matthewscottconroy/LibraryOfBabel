# 21.2 Quotient Rings

Given an ideal $I$ in a ring $R$, the quotient ring $R/I$ is constructed by forming the additive cosets $r + I = \{r + x : x \in I\}$ and defining addition and multiplication on cosets by $(r + I) + (s + I) = (r + s) + I$ and $(r + I)(s + I) = rs + I$. The critical point is well-definedness: the product of cosets depends on representatives, and the calculation that a different choice $r' \in r + I$ and $s' \in s + I$ yields the same product coset uses the absorption property of $I$ in an essential way. This is exactly why arbitrary subgroups do not yield quotient rings — the absorption property of an ideal is precisely the condition needed for coset multiplication to be independent of representatives.

The quotient $R/I$ inherits all the ring axioms from $R$, with additive identity $0 + I = I$ and multiplicative identity $1 + I$. The canonical projection $\pi \colon R \to R/I$ sending $r \mapsto r + I$ is a surjective ring homomorphism with $\ker \pi = I$. This establishes the tight correspondence: ideals are kernels of homomorphisms, and kernels of homomorphisms are ideals. The First Isomorphism Theorem then states that for any ring homomorphism $\phi \colon R \to S$, there is an isomorphism $R/\ker\phi \cong \phi(R)$ given by $r + \ker\phi \mapsto \phi(r)$. The second and third isomorphism theorems have direct analogues: if $I \subseteq J$ are ideals of $R$, then $J/I$ is an ideal of $R/I$ and $(R/I)/(J/I) \cong R/J$.

The Correspondence Theorem (or Fourth Isomorphism Theorem) is indispensable in practice: there is an order-preserving bijection between ideals of $R$ that contain $I$ and ideals of $R/I$, given by $J \mapsto J/I$. This bijection preserves primeness and maximality: $J$ is prime (respectively maximal) in $R$ if and only if $J/I$ is prime (respectively maximal) in $R/I$. The theorem allows one to "lift" ideal-theoretic questions from $R/I$ back to $R$ and to reduce questions about $R$ to questions about a simpler quotient. For instance, to find all ideals of $\mathbb{Z}/(n)$, one need only find all multiples of $n$ that are divisors of $n$, reducing a question about the quotient ring to pure arithmetic in $\mathbb{Z}$.
