# 21.3.1 Prime Ideals: $ab \in P \Rightarrow a \in P$ or$b \in P$

Among all ideals of a ring, the prime ideals occupy a special place. They are the algebraic counterpart of prime numbers, and in commutative algebra and algebraic geometry, the totality of prime ideals in a ring encodes its geometric structure. The definition looks simple — a multiplicative "either/or" condition — but its consequences ramify throughout the whole of algebra.

An ideal $P \subsetneq R$ is *prime* if whenever$ab \in P$ for elements$a, b \in R$, then$a \in P$ or$b \in P$. The condition$P \neq R$ is necessary to exclude the whole ring, which would otherwise trivially satisfy the implication (vacuously, since there are no elements to worry about). This definition is the ideal-theoretic generalization of a classical arithmetic condition: an integer$p$ is prime if and only if$p \mid ab$ implies$p \mid a$ or$p \mid b$, and the principal ideal$(p)$ in$\mathbb{Z}$ is prime if and only if$p$ is a prime number (or$p = 0$, since$\mathbb{Z}$ is a domain).

There are several equivalent reformulations. An ideal $P$ is prime if and only if the complement$R \setminus P$ is closed under multiplication — that is,$R \setminus P$ is a *multiplicative set*. This perspective, which moves attention away from$P$ and toward its complement, is central in localization theory: localizing$R$ at a prime$P$ means inverting all elements of$R \setminus P$. Still another equivalent formulation: the product$IJ \subseteq P$ of two ideals implies$I \subseteq P$ or$J \subseteq P$ — the prime condition extends from elements to ideals.

The most important equivalent condition links prime ideals to quotient rings: $P$ is prime if and only if$R/P$ is an integral domain. We will prove this carefully in §21.3.3. The biconditional makes prime ideals the algebraic objects through which domains are manufactured: every domain arises as$R/P$ for some ring$R$ and prime ideal$P$.

Notice that in $\mathbb{Z}$, the ideal$(0)$ is prime (since$\mathbb{Z}$ is a domain: if$ab = 0$ then$a = 0$ or$b = 0$), but$(0)$ is not maximal (it is properly contained in$(p)$ for every prime$p$). The prime ideals$(p)$ for prime$p$ are both prime and maximal. More generally, the set of all prime ideals of$R$, called the *prime spectrum*$\mathrm{Spec}(R)$, organizes the ring's arithmetic into geometric data — a theme developed in §21.3.6.

**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{Z}$: the prime ideals are$(0)$ and$(p)$ for every prime$p \geq 2$. Check$(6)$ is not prime:$2 \cdot 3 = 6 \in (6)$ but$2 \notin (6)$ and$3 \notin (6)$. Check$(5)$ is prime: if$5 \mid ab$ then$5 \mid a$ or$5 \mid b$ (since$5$ is a prime number). The quotient$\mathbb{Z}/(5) = \mathbb{F}_5$ is indeed a domain (in fact a field). The ideal$(0)$ is prime because$\mathbb{Z}/(0) = \mathbb{Z}$ is a domain.

**Example 2.** In $\mathbb{Z}[x]$: the ideal$(x)$ is prime. If$f \cdot g \in (x)$, then$(fg)(0) = f(0)g(0) = 0$, so$f(0) = 0$ or$g(0) = 0$, meaning$f \in (x)$ or$g \in (x)$. The quotient$\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ is a domain. The ideal$(x^2 - 1) = (x-1)(x+1)$ is not prime:$(x-1)(x+1) \in (x^2-1)$ but$x - 1 \notin (x^2-1)$ (since$x^2-1$ does not divide$x-1$).

**Example 3.** In $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, the ideal$(p, f)$ for a prime$p$ and an irreducible polynomial$f \in \mathbb{F}_p[x]$ (lifted to$\mathbb{Z}[x]$) is a maximal ideal, hence prime. For example,$(2, x^2 + x + 1)$ is a maximal ideal of$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ because$\mathbb{Z}[x]/(2, x^2+x+1) \cong \mathbb{F}_2[x]/(x^2+x+1) \cong \mathbb{F}_4$, a field.

**Example 4.** In $k[x, y]$ for an algebraically closed field$k$: the ideal$(x)$ is prime (since$k[x,y]/(x) \cong k[y]$ is a domain). The ideal$(x, y)$ is maximal (since$k[x,y]/(x,y) \cong k$ is a field). The ideal$(xy) = (x)(y)$ is not prime:$xy \in (xy)$ but$x \notin (xy)$ and$y \notin (xy)$ (since neither$x/x = 1$ nor$y/y = 1$ is a multiple of$xy$ as a polynomial). This corresponds geometrically to the fact that the variety$V(xy) = \{x = 0\} \cup \{y = 0\}$ is reducible (the union of two lines).

**Example 5.** In $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$: the ideal$\{0, 2, 4\}$ corresponds under the correspondence theorem to the ideal$(2) \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$ containing$(6)$. The quotient is$(\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z})/\{0,2,4\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/(2) = \mathbb{F}_2$, a domain, so$\{0,2,4\}$ is prime (in fact maximal). Similarly$\{0, 3\}$ corresponds to$(3)$ and quotient$\cong \mathbb{F}_3$, so it is also prime and maximal. The ideal$\{0, 2, 4\} \cap \{0, 3\} = \{0\}$ — the zero ideal of$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ — is not prime, because the quotient$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ itself has zero divisors ($2 \cdot 3 = 0$).
