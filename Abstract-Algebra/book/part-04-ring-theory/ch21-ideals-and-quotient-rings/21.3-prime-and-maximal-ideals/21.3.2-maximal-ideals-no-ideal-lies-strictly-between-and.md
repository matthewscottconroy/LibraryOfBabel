# 21.3.2 Maximal Ideals: No Ideal Lies Strictly Between $M$ and$R$

Maximal ideals are the largest proper ideals of a ring. They represent the "end of the line" in the ideal lattice — there is nothing strictly between a maximal ideal and the whole ring — and their quotient rings are fields. This field quotient property gives maximal ideals their central role in constructing fields from rings, and makes them the algebraic counterpart of "points" in algebraic geometry.

An ideal $M \subsetneq R$ is *maximal* if there is no ideal$I$ satisfying$M \subsetneq I \subsetneq R$. In other words,$M$ is a proper ideal, and the only ideal strictly larger than$M$ is$R$ itself. Equivalently (by the correspondence theorem),$R/M$ has no proper nontrivial ideals — any ideal of$R/M$ is either$(0)$ or$R/M$ itself. A nonzero ring with no proper nontrivial ideals is a field, so$M$ maximal$\Leftrightarrow$ $R/M$ is a field. We will prove this cleanly in §21.3.4.

Every maximal ideal is prime. Here is the argument directly: suppose $M$ is maximal and$ab \in M$ but$a \notin M$. The ideal$M + (a)$ strictly contains$M$ (since$a \in M + (a)$ but$a \notin M$), so by maximality$M + (a) = R$. Write$1 = m + ra$ for some$m \in M$ and$r \in R$. Then$b = b \cdot 1 = b(m + ra) = bm + r(ab)$. Since$m \in M$ and$M$ absorbs multiplication,$bm \in M$. Since$ab \in M$ and$M$ absorbs,$r(ab) \in M$. Thus$b = bm + r(ab) \in M$, confirming that$M$ is prime.

The converse fails: not every prime ideal is maximal. In $\mathbb{Z}$, the ideal$(0)$ is prime (since$\mathbb{Z}$ is a domain) but not maximal (it is properly contained in$(p)$ for every prime$p$). The relationship between prime and maximal ideals depends on the ring's Krull dimension (§24.4.1): in a field,$(0)$ is both prime and maximal; in a PID like$\mathbb{Z}$, the nonzero prime ideals are maximal; in$\mathbb{Z}[x]$, neither$(0)$ nor$(p)$ alone is maximal (they are properly contained in$(p, f)$ for an irreducible$f \bmod p$).

**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{Z}$, the maximal ideals are exactly$(p)$ for prime$p$. The quotient$\mathbb{Z}/(p) = \mathbb{F}_p$ is a field, confirming maximality. The ideal$(0)$ is prime but not maximal. The ideal$(6)$ is neither prime nor maximal:$(6) \subsetneq (2) \subsetneq \mathbb{Z}$ and$(6) \subsetneq (3) \subsetneq \mathbb{Z}$, and$(6)$ is not prime since$2 \cdot 3 = 6 \in (6)$ but$2, 3 \notin (6)$.

**Example 2.** In $\mathbb{Q}[x]$: the maximal ideals are$(f)$ for irreducible polynomials$f$. For instance,$(x - a)$ is maximal for every$a \in \mathbb{Q}$ (quotient$\cong \mathbb{Q}$), and$(x^2 + 1)$ is maximal (quotient$\cong \mathbb{Q}(i)$, a field). The ideal$(x^2 - 1) = (x-1)(x+1)$ is not maximal:$(x^2-1) \subsetneq (x-1) \subsetneq \mathbb{Q}[x]$.

**Example 3.** In $\mathbb{Z}[x]$: the maximal ideals have the form$(p, f(x))$ where$p$ is a prime and$f$ is a polynomial whose image in$\mathbb{F}_p[x]$ is irreducible. The quotient is$\mathbb{F}_p[x]/(f \bmod p)$, a finite field. For example,$(3, x^2 + 1)$ is maximal since$x^2 + 1$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{F}_3$ (checking:$0^2+1 = 1 \neq 0$,$1^2+1 = 2 \neq 0$,$2^2+1 = 5 \equiv 2 \neq 0$), so the quotient is$\mathbb{F}_9$.

**Example 4.** In the ring $C([0,1])$ of continuous real-valued functions on$[0,1]$: for each point$t \in [0,1]$, the ideal$\mathfrak{m}_t = \{f \in C([0,1]) : f(t) = 0\}$ is maximal. The quotient map$f \mapsto f(t)$ is a surjective ring homomorphism$C([0,1]) \to \mathbb{R}$ with kernel$\mathfrak{m}_t$. By the first isomorphism theorem,$C([0,1])/\mathfrak{m}_t \cong \mathbb{R}$, a field. A theorem of analysis (Gelfand–Kolmogorov) says that every maximal ideal of$C([0,1])$ is of this form. This is a prototypical example of the "points as maximal ideals" philosophy of algebraic geometry.

**Example 5.** The distinction between prime and maximal is sharp in $k[x, y]$ for a field$k$. The ideal$(x)$ is prime (quotient$\cong k[y]$, a domain) but not maximal ($(x) \subsetneq (x, y) \subsetneq k[x,y]$). The ideal$(x, y)$ is maximal (quotient$\cong k$, a field). The ideal$(x, y - 1)$ is another maximal ideal, corresponding to the point$(0, 1) \in \mathbb{A}^2_k$. The prime-but-not-maximal ideal$(x)$ corresponds geometrically to the$y$-axis, an irreducible curve — a subvariety of positive dimension, not a point.

Existence of maximal ideals in every nonzero ring will be established in §21.3.5 using Zorn's Lemma.
