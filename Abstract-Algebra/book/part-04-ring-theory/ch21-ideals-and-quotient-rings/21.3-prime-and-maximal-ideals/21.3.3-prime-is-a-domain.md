# 21.3.3 $P$ Prime$\Leftrightarrow$ $R/P$ Is a Domain

The connection between prime ideals and integral domains is not merely analogical — it is a precise biconditional that allows us to read off properties of an ideal from properties of its quotient ring, and vice versa. This equivalence is the conceptual heart of prime ideal theory: it says that prime ideals are exactly the algebraic gadgets that produce domains when you quotient by them.

An ideal $P \subsetneq R$ is prime if and only if the quotient ring$R/P$ is an integral domain. The proof is a direct translation of definitions, and the two directions are completely symmetric. By definition,$R/P$ has a zero divisor precisely when there exist cosets$a + P \neq 0 + P$ and$b + P \neq 0 + P$ with$(a + P)(b + P) = 0 + P$, i.e., there exist$a \notin P$ and$b \notin P$ with$ab \in P$. This is exactly the negation of the prime condition. Stated contrapositively:$R/P$ has no zero divisors$\iff$ for all$a, b \in R$, if$a \notin P$ and$b \notin P$ then$ab \notin P$ $\iff$ $ab \in P$ implies$a \in P$ or$b \in P$ $\iff$ $P$ is prime.

Also needed: $R/P$ is a ring (which we have already verified) with no zero divisors. The extra condition that$R/P$ is an integral domain requires$R/P$ to be nonzero (so$P \neq R$) and to have no zero divisors. The nonzero condition corresponds to$P \neq R$, which is already required in the definition of a prime ideal.

This equivalence is extraordinarily useful in both directions. To prove an ideal is prime, one can instead show the quotient is a domain — a condition that is often easier to verify. To prove a quotient ring is a domain, one shows the ideal being quotiented by is prime. Every theorem about integral domains then applies to prime ideal quotients: for instance, domains have the cancellation law, so $R/P$ does too, which translates back into useful divisibility information about$P$.

In algebraic geometry, the correspondence runs even deeper. If $R = k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ is a polynomial ring over an algebraically closed field$k$, then the prime ideals correspond to the irreducible subvarieties of affine$n$-space$k^n$: a prime ideal$P$ corresponds to the irreducible variety$V(P) = \{(a_1, \ldots, a_n) \in k^n : f(a_1, \ldots, a_n) = 0 \text{ for all } f \in P\}$. The fact that$R/P$ is a domain corresponds geometrically to$V(P)$ being irreducible — not decomposable as a union of proper closed subvarieties.

**Example 1.** The ideal $(x^2 - 1) \subset \mathbb{Q}[x]$: since$x^2 - 1 = (x-1)(x+1)$, the quotient$\mathbb{Q}[x]/(x^2-1) \cong \mathbb{Q} \times \mathbb{Q}$ (by CRT), which has zero divisors:$(1,0) \cdot (0,1) = (0,0)$. Therefore$(x^2-1)$ is not prime. Confirming directly:$(x-1)(x+1) \in (x^2-1)$ but$x - 1 \notin (x^2-1)$.

**Example 2.** The ideal $(x^2 + 1) \subset \mathbb{Q}[x]$: the polynomial$x^2+1$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$ (no rational roots, degree 2). Thus$(x^2+1)$ is a prime (even maximal) ideal, and$\mathbb{Q}[x]/(x^2+1) \cong \mathbb{Q}(i)$, which is a field (in particular a domain). Confirming: if$(x^2+1) \mid fg$ in$\mathbb{Q}[x]$, then since$x^2+1$ is irreducible (hence prime in the PID$\mathbb{Q}[x]$),$x^2+1 \mid f$ or$x^2+1 \mid g$.

**Example 3.** In $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, the ideal$(x)$: the quotient$\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ (by the evaluation-at-$0$ map), which is a domain. So$(x)$ is prime. But$(x)$ is not maximal:$(x) \subsetneq (x, 2) \subsetneq \mathbb{Z}[x]$, and$(x, 2)/( x) \cong 2\mathbb{Z}/0 = \{0,2\}$... more precisely$\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x,2) \cong \mathbb{F}_2$, so$(x,2)$ is maximal while$(x)$ is merely prime.

**Example 4.** In $\mathbb{Z}$, the ideal$(0)$:$\mathbb{Z}/(0) = \mathbb{Z}$ is a domain, so$(0)$ is prime. But every nonzero prime$(p)$ with$p$ prime strictly contains$(0)$, so$(0)$ is not maximal. The ideal$(p)$ for$p$ prime:$\mathbb{Z}/(p) = \mathbb{F}_p$ is a field, hence a domain, so$(p)$ is prime. The ideal$(p)$ is also maximal in$\mathbb{Z}$ (no ideal lies strictly between$(p)$ and$\mathbb{Z}$, since a PID has the property that$(p) \subset I \subset \mathbb{Z}$ implies$I = (d)$ with$d \mid p$, so$d = 1$ and$I = \mathbb{Z}$). Thus in a PID, every nonzero prime ideal is maximal.

**Example 5.** In $\mathbb{R}[x, y]$: the ideal$(x^2 + y^2 - 1)$ (a circle). The quotient$\mathbb{R}[x, y]/(x^2 + y^2 - 1)$ is the coordinate ring of the unit circle. This ring is a domain (the circle is an irreducible variety over$\mathbb{R}$): if$fg \equiv 0$ modulo$x^2 + y^2 - 1$, then$(x^2+y^2-1) \mid fg$ in$\mathbb{R}[x,y]$. Since$x^2+y^2-1$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{R}$ (it has no real linear factors), it is prime in the UFD$\mathbb{R}[x,y]$, so$(x^2+y^2-1)$ is a prime ideal and the quotient is a domain.
