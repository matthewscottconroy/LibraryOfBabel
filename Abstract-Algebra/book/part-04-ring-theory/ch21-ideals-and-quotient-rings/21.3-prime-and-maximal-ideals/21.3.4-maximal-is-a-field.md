# 21.3.4 $M$ Maximal$\Leftrightarrow$ $R/M$ Is a Field

The parallel with prime ideals is exact: just as prime ideals correspond to quotient rings that are domains, maximal ideals correspond to quotient rings that are fields. This equivalence is one of the most useful and frequently applied results in ring theory. It transforms the abstract condition of maximality into a concrete structural property of the quotient, and it provides a systematic factory for constructing fields.

An ideal $M \subsetneq R$ is maximal if and only if the quotient ring$R/M$ is a field. The proof proceeds in both directions. If$M$ is maximal, we show$R/M$ is a field by proving every nonzero element of$R/M$ is a unit. Take$a + M \neq 0 + M$, so$a \notin M$. The ideal$M + (a)$ strictly contains$M$ (since$a \in M + (a)$ but$a \notin M$), so by maximality$M + (a) = R$. Write$1 = m + ra$ for some$m \in M$,$r \in R$. In$R/M$, we compute$1 + M = (m + M) + (r + M)(a + M) = 0 + (r + M)(a + M)$, so$(r + M)(a + M) = 1 + M$. Thus$a + M$ is a unit, and since$a$ was arbitrary,$R/M$ is a field.

Conversely, if $R/M$ is a field, then$R/M$ has no proper nontrivial ideals (any field is simple as a ring: the only ideals of a field$F$ are$(0)$ and$F$ itself, because any nonzero ideal contains a unit and therefore equals$F$). By the Correspondence Theorem, the ideals of$R$ containing$M$ correspond bijectively to the ideals of$R/M$. Since$R/M$ has only$\{0\}$ and itself,$R$ has only$M$ and$R$ above$M$. This means$M$ is maximal.

Combining with the prime ideal theorem: since every field is a domain, $M$ maximal implies$R/M$ is a field implies$R/M$ is a domain implies$M$ is prime. So every maximal ideal is prime, consistent with §21.3.2. The converse fails in general —$(0) \subsetneq (2)$ in$\mathbb{Z}$ shows a prime ideal that is not maximal.

The practical power of this theorem is hard to overstate. To construct a field containing $F$, adjoin an element satisfying an irreducible polynomial: the quotient$F[x]/(f)$ is a field if and only if$(f)$ is maximal in$F[x]$, which happens precisely when$f$ is irreducible (since$F[x]$ is a PID and in a PID the prime ideals are exactly the ideals generated by irreducible elements, and prime ideals in a PID are maximal). The entire theory of field extensions can thus be organized around this single equivalence.

**Example 1.** $F = \mathbb{Q}$,$f = x^2 - 2$. Since$\sqrt{2} \notin \mathbb{Q}$, the polynomial$x^2 - 2$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$ (it has degree 2 and no rational roots). So$(x^2-2)$ is maximal in$\mathbb{Q}[x]$ and$\mathbb{Q}[x]/(x^2-2)$ is a field. Elements are of the form$a + b\sqrt{2}$ (where$\sqrt{2}$ denotes the image of$x$), arithmetic is done by reducing$(\sqrt{2})^2 = 2$, and the multiplicative inverse of$a + b\sqrt{2} \neq 0$ is$\frac{a - b\sqrt{2}}{a^2 - 2b^2}$ (the denominator is nonzero because$\sqrt{2}$ is irrational). This gives$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$.

**Example 2.** $F = \mathbb{F}_2$,$f = x^2 + x + 1$. Checking:$f(0) = 1 \neq 0$ and$f(1) = 3 = 1 \neq 0$ in$\mathbb{F}_2$, so$f$ has no roots and is irreducible of degree 2. The quotient$\mathbb{F}_2[x]/(x^2+x+1)$ is a field with$2^2 = 4$ elements. Its elements are$\{0, 1, \alpha, \alpha+1\}$ where$\alpha$ satisfies$\alpha^2 + \alpha + 1 = 0$, i.e.,$\alpha^2 = \alpha + 1$. This is the field$\mathbb{F}_4$, the unique field with 4 elements.

**Example 3.** $F = \mathbb{F}_3$,$f = x^2 + 1$. Check:$0^2 + 1 = 1 \neq 0$,$1^2 + 1 = 2 \neq 0$,$2^2 + 1 = 5 \equiv 2 \neq 0$ in$\mathbb{F}_3$. So$x^2 + 1$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{F}_3$ and$\mathbb{F}_3[x]/(x^2+1)$ is a field with$3^2 = 9$ elements, which is$\mathbb{F}_9$.

**Example 4.** Not all quotients by irreducible polynomials are "new." Over $\mathbb{C}$, every irreducible polynomial has degree 1 (by the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra). So the maximal ideals of$\mathbb{C}[x]$ are exactly$(x - a)$ for$a \in \mathbb{C}$, and$\mathbb{C}[x]/(x-a) \cong \mathbb{C}$. There are no new fields to be obtained from$\mathbb{C}$ by this construction —$\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed. This is an algebraic restatement of the FTA.

**Example 5.** The theorem applies beyond polynomial rings. In the ring $R = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, the ideal$\mathfrak{p} = (2, 1 + \sqrt{-5})$ is maximal: the quotient$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]/(2, 1+\sqrt{-5})$ can be shown to be isomorphic to$\mathbb{F}_2$ (the image of$\sqrt{-5}$ is$1$ modulo$2$, and the image of$1 + \sqrt{-5}$ is$0$, so the quotient has only$2$ elements). Since$\mathbb{F}_2$ is a field,$\mathfrak{p}$ is maximal (and hence prime) in$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$. This prime ideal will play a central role in §22.5 when we study the failure of unique factorization.
