# 21.3.5 Existence of Maximal Ideals via Zorn's Lemma

We know what maximal ideals are and what they do. The question we have not yet answered is: do they always exist? In a finite ring, or a ring with an explicit finite list of ideals (like $\mathbb{Z}$), one can find maximal ideals by inspection. In a general ring — which may be uncountably infinite with an unmanageably complex ideal lattice — no such search is feasible, and the answer requires a set-theoretic principle from the foundations of mathematics.

The theorem is: in any nonzero ring $R$, every proper ideal$I$ is contained in some maximal ideal. In particular, taking$I = (0)$, every nonzero ring has at least one maximal ideal. The proof proceeds by Zorn's Lemma, the standard set-theoretic tool for establishing the existence of maximal objects among partially ordered families.

Recall that Zorn's Lemma states: if $(\mathcal{P}, \leq)$ is a nonempty partially ordered set in which every chain (totally ordered subset) has an upper bound in$\mathcal{P}$, then$\mathcal{P}$ has a maximal element. To apply it here, let$\mathcal{P}$ be the set of all proper ideals of$R$ that contain$I$, partially ordered by inclusion. This is nonempty since$I \in \mathcal{P}$. We must verify that every chain in$\mathcal{P}$ has an upper bound in$\mathcal{P}$.

If $\{I_\alpha\}$ is a chain of proper ideals containing$I$ (so$I_\alpha \subseteq I_\beta$ or$I_\beta \subseteq I_\alpha$ for all$\alpha, \beta$), then their union$J = \bigcup_\alpha I_\alpha$ is an ideal: if$x, y \in J$ then$x \in I_\alpha$ and$y \in I_\beta$ for some$\alpha, \beta$, and since the chain is totally ordered, one contains the other, say$I_\alpha \subseteq I_\beta$, so$x, y \in I_\beta$ and$x + y \in I_\beta \subseteq J$; absorption is similar. The crucial point is that$J$ is *proper*: if$1 \in J$, then$1 \in I_\alpha$ for some$\alpha$, giving$I_\alpha = R$ — contradicting the properness of each$I_\alpha$. Thus$J \in \mathcal{P}$ is an upper bound for the chain. By Zorn's Lemma,$\mathcal{P}$ has a maximal element, which is a maximal ideal of$R$ containing$I$.

The key detail deserves emphasis: the union of a chain of proper ideals is again proper *because* $1$ is not in any of the ideals in the chain. This is why Zorn's argument works for ideals and not for arbitrary subsets: ideals carry the additional "not containing$1$" condition, and this condition is preserved under directed unions. If we merely took a union of arbitrary subrings (rather than proper ideals), the union might well contain$1$.

This existence theorem has sweeping consequences. Every nonzero ring has a quotient that is a field, obtained by quotienting by a maximal ideal. Every element not in any maximal ideal must be a unit (since the ideal generated by a non-unit is proper, hence contained in a maximal ideal; so if $a$ is not a unit, there is a maximal ideal containing$a$, hence$a$ is not a unit iff$a$ lies in some maximal ideal). In commutative algebra, the Zorn's Lemma argument underlies the theory of local rings, the Nullstellensatz, and the construction of algebraic closures.

**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{Z}$: the ideal$(6)$ is a proper ideal. The proper ideals containing$(6)$ are$(6)$,$(3)$,$(2)$, and$(1) = \mathbb{Z}$ — but$(1)$ is not proper. The maximal ones among the proper ideals containing$(6)$ are$(2)$ and$(3)$. So$(6)$ is contained in at least two maximal ideals. Zorn's Lemma guarantees at least one; the specific structure of$\mathbb{Z}$ gives us both.

**Example 2.** In $\mathbb{Z}[x]$: the ideal$(x)$ is proper (it consists of polynomials with zero constant term, and$1 \notin (x)$). The chain$(x) \subsetneq (x, 2) \subsetneq \mathbb{Z}[x]$ does not yet reach a maximal ideal, but$(x, 2)$ is maximal (since$\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x,2) \cong \mathbb{F}_2$, a field). Zorn guarantees that starting from$(x)$, there exists a maximal ideal above it; finding it explicitly requires knowing the ring's structure.

**Example 3.** In $\mathbb{Q}[x, y]/(xy - 1)$: this is the coordinate ring of the hyperbola$xy = 1$ in$\mathbb{A}^2_{\mathbb{Q}}$. By Zorn, there exist maximal ideals. The elements$x$ and$y$ satisfy$xy = 1$, so$x$ is a unit (with inverse$y$). However,$x - a$ for$a \in \mathbb{Q}^*$ generates a maximal ideal, since the quotient is$\mathbb{Q}[x,y]/(xy-1, x-a) \cong \mathbb{Q}[y]/(ay - 1) \cong \mathbb{Q}$ (as$ay - 1$ is linear). The existence of these maximal ideals is guaranteed by Zorn; their explicit form requires analysis.

**Example 4.** In $\mathbb{R}[x_1, x_2, x_3, \ldots]$ (a polynomial ring in countably many variables): the ideal$I = (x_1, x_2, x_3, \ldots)$ consists of all polynomials with zero constant term. It is a proper ideal (not containing$1$). By Zorn,$I$ is contained in a maximal ideal$M$. But$M$ must contain all$x_i$, and the quotient$\mathbb{R}[x_1, x_2, \ldots]/M$ is a field extending$\mathbb{R}$. One cannot write down$M$ explicitly — it is a genuinely non-constructive object whose existence requires the Axiom of Choice (via Zorn's Lemma).

This is arguably the most important application of Zorn's Lemma in ring theory, and it illustrates a broader principle: existence theorems in algebra often require transfinite methods, while concrete computations use explicit algorithms. Both perspectives are essential.
