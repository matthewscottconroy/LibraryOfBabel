# 21.3.6 The Spectrum $\mathrm{Spec}(R)$: A Preview of Algebraic Geometry

The prime ideals of a commutative ring $R$ can be assembled into a topological space, turning the algebraic structure of$R$ into a geometric object. This construction — the prime spectrum — is the starting point of modern algebraic geometry, and understanding it even briefly illuminates the deep connections between algebra and geometry.

The *prime spectrum* $\mathrm{Spec}(R)$ is the set of all prime ideals of$R$, topologized by the *Zariski topology*. The closed sets are defined by$V(I) = \{P \in \mathrm{Spec}(R) : I \subseteq P\}$ for ideals$I \subseteq R$ — the set of prime ideals containing$I$. One checks the axioms for closed sets:$V(R) = \emptyset$ (no prime contains$R$ since$R \neq R$ is impossible —$1 \in P$ would force$P = R$, but primes are proper),$V((0)) = \mathrm{Spec}(R)$, and$V(I) \cup V(J) = V(IJ) = V(I \cap J)$ (a prime contains$IJ$ iff it contains$I$ or$J$, because primes pull products apart), and$\bigcap_\alpha V(I_\alpha) = V(\sum_\alpha I_\alpha)$ (a prime contains all the$I_\alpha$ iff it contains their sum). So the collection$\{V(I) : I \unlhd R\}$ defines the closed sets of a topology on$\mathrm{Spec}(R)$.

The Zariski topology is in general not Hausdorff. A point $P$ of$\mathrm{Spec}(R)$ is closed if and only if$\{P\} = V(P)$ is already closed, which requires that no other prime contains$P$ — i.e.,$P$ is a maximal ideal. So the closed points of$\mathrm{Spec}(R)$ are exactly the maximal ideals: the "geometric points" of the variety. Non-maximal primes are not closed; they are "generic points" of subvarieties, a concept with no topological analogue in classical geometry but entirely natural here.

The open sets $D(f) = \mathrm{Spec}(R) \setminus V((f)) = \{P : f \notin P\}$ form a basis for the Zariski topology. These are the "distinguished open sets." On$D(f)$, the ring$R$ behaves as if$f$ were invertible, and this is made precise by localization: the ring of "functions on$D(f)$" is$R[1/f] = R_f$. This observation is the seed of sheaf theory.

To see how geometry arises, consider $R = k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ where$k$ is an algebraically closed field such as$\mathbb{C}$. By Hilbert's Nullstellensatz (§24.4.3), the maximal ideals of$R$ correspond bijectively to points of affine$n$-space$k^n$: the maximal ideal corresponding to$(a_1, \ldots, a_n) \in k^n$ is$(x_1 - a_1, \ldots, x_n - a_n)$. More generally, the prime ideals correspond to the irreducible subvarieties of$k^n$: a prime ideal$P$ corresponds to the irreducible variety$V(P) = \{(a_1, \ldots, a_n) \in k^n : f(a_1, \ldots, a_n) = 0 \text{ for all } f \in P\}$. The fact that$R/P$ is a domain corresponds geometrically to$V(P)$ being irreducible — not decomposable as a union of proper closed subvarieties.

**Example 1.** $\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{Z})$: the prime ideals are$(0)$ and$(p)$ for each prime$p$. The closed point$(p)$ closes off into the singleton$\{(p)\}$. The "generic point"$(0)$ is not closed: its closure is$V((0)) = \mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{Z})$ — the entire space. This corresponds to the fact that$\mathbb{Z}$ is an integral domain, and the ideal$(0)$ is "generic" in the sense that$\mathbb{Z}/(0) = \mathbb{Z}$ sees the whole spectrum.

**Example 2.** $\mathrm{Spec}(k[x])$ for an algebraically closed field$k$: the prime ideals are$(0)$ (the generic point, corresponding to the "generic point" of the affine line$\mathbb{A}^1_k$) and$(x - a)$ for each$a \in k$ (the closed points, corresponding to the points$a \in k$). This is the algebraic analogue of the affine line. A closed set$V(I) = V(f)$ for a nonzero polynomial$f$ is a finite set of points (the roots of$f$).

**Example 3.** $\mathrm{Spec}(k[x, y])$: prime ideals include$(0)$ (generic point of$\mathbb{A}^2_k$),$(f)$ for irreducible polynomials$f$ (generic points of irreducible curves), and$(x - a, y - b)$ for points$(a, b) \in k^2$ (closed points). The topology encodes the incidence relations: a point$(a, b)$ lies on the curve$V(f)$ iff$(x-a, y-b) \supseteq (f)$ iff$(f) \in \overline{\{(x-a,y-b)\}}$... more precisely, the generic point$(f)$ specializes to a closed point$(x-a, y-b)$ iff$(f) \subseteq (x-a, y-b)$ iff$f(a, b) = 0$.

**Example 4.** $\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{Z}[x])$: this is the prototype for arithmetic geometry. The prime ideals include$(0)$,$(p)$ for rational primes,$(f)$ for irreducible$f \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ with no fixed prime divisor of coefficients, and$(p, f)$ for a prime$p$ and$f$ irreducible in$\mathbb{F}_p[x]$. Geometrically,$\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{Z}[x])$ is a "two-dimensional" arithmetic space fibered over$\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{Z})$, the "arithmetic line." The fiber over the prime$(p)$ is$\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{F}_p[x]) = \mathbb{A}^1_{\mathbb{F}_p}$, the affine line over$\mathbb{F}_p$. This perspective, due to Grothendieck, is the foundation of the Langlands program and much of modern number theory.

This perspective — that the geometry of a variety is encoded in the commutative algebra of its coordinate ring — is the foundational idea of algebraic geometry in the sense of Grothendieck. Every statement about the geometry of varieties translates into a statement about rings and ideals, and conversely. The prime spectrum is the dictionary that mediates between the two languages.
