# Chapter 21 — Ideals and Quotient Rings

In group theory, the subgroups that permit a well-defined quotient are exactly the normal subgroups: those closed under conjugation. The analogous concept for rings is the ideal, and its defining property is absorption rather than normality. An ideal $I$ in a ring $R$ is a subgroup of the additive group that is also closed under multiplication by arbitrary elements of $R$: if $r \in R$ and $x \in I$, then $rx \in I$ and $xr \in I$. This absorption property is precisely the condition needed to make coset multiplication well defined, and without it the quotient $R/I$ would not inherit a ring structure. The ideal is thus the natural answer to the question: which subsets of $R$ allow us to pass to a quotient ring?

The connection to homomorphisms is immediate and important. If $\phi \colon R \to S$ is a ring homomorphism, then $\ker \phi$ is always an ideal of $R$, and conversely every ideal arises as the kernel of some homomorphism — namely, the canonical projection $R \to R/I$. This reciprocity, formalized in the First Isomorphism Theorem, gives a clean classification of quotient rings: up to isomorphism, every quotient of $R$ is determined by an ideal, and every ideal determines a quotient. The isomorphism theorems for rings — the second, third, and correspondence theorem — mirror those for groups and form the technical backbone of the chapter.

Not all ideals are created equal, and the chapter's deepest results concern two special classes. A prime ideal $\mathfrak{p}$ is one for which $ab \in \mathfrak{p}$ implies $a \in \mathfrak{p}$ or $b \in \mathfrak{p}$; equivalently, the quotient $R/\mathfrak{p}$ is an integral domain. A maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$ is one that is not properly contained in any proper ideal; equivalently, $R/\mathfrak{m}$ is a field. Since every field is a domain, every maximal ideal is prime, but the converse fails: in $\mathbb{Z}$, the zero ideal $(0)$ is prime but not maximal, since $\mathbb{Z}/(0) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ is a domain but not a field. The distinction between prime and maximal ideals encodes, algebraically, the distinction between domains and fields.

The existence of maximal ideals in every nonzero unital ring is a theorem that requires Zorn's lemma, and therefore the axiom of choice. This seemingly abstract point has concrete consequences: it guarantees that every proper ideal is contained in a maximal ideal, and thus that the intersection of all maximal ideals — the Jacobson radical — is a meaningful invariant. Similarly, the intersection of all prime ideals is the nilradical, consisting precisely of the nilpotent elements. These two radicals measure, in complementary ways, how far a ring is from being semisimple or reduced. The collection of all prime ideals forms the prime spectrum $\text{Spec}(R)$, a topological space under the Zariski topology. This spectrum is the bridge from ring theory to algebraic geometry, and its study anticipates the full treatment of dimension and geometry in Chapter 24.
