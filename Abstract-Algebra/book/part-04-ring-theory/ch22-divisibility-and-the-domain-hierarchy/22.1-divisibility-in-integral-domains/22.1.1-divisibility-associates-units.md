# 22.1.1 Divisibility; Associates; Units

The familiar arithmetic of the integers — the notion that $2$ divides$6$, that$-3$ and$3$ are "essentially the same" divisor, that$\pm 1$ are trivial — generalizes cleanly to any integral domain. Getting the definitions right requires only a small amount of care to eliminate the ambiguities that arise from units. Once these definitions are in place, the divisibility theory of an arbitrary domain looks like a precise generalization of the arithmetic of$\mathbb{Z}$.

In an integral domain $R$, we say$a$ *divides*$b$, written$a \mid b$, if$b = ac$ for some$c \in R$. Divisibility is a preorder on$R$: it is reflexive ($a = a \cdot 1$, so$a \mid a$) and transitive (if$b = ac$ and$c = a'c'$ then$b = a(a'c') = (aa')c'$, so$a \mid b$). It is not in general antisymmetric, because$a \mid b$ and$b \mid a$ does not force$a = b$: in$\mathbb{Z}$,$2 \mid -2$ and$-2 \mid 2$, but$2 \neq -2$. The antisymmetry fails exactly because of units.

This motivates the notion of *associates*. Elements $a$ and$b$ are associates if$a = ub$ for some unit$u \in R^\times$. Associates divide each other, and the divisibility relation is antisymmetric modulo the associate relation: if$a \mid b$ and$b \mid a$, then$a$ and$b$ are associates. Equivalently,$a$ and$b$ are associates if and only if$(a) = (b)$ as principal ideals — they generate the same ideal. This equivalence makes the dictionary between ideals and divisibility very clean: divisibility of elements translates to containment of principal ideals, and the associate equivalence corresponds to equality of principal ideals.

The units of $R$ are exactly the elements that divide every element of$R$ (since$u \mid b$ for any$b$ because$b = u \cdot (u^{-1}b)$). They form the trivial associate class$R^\times$. In developing factorization theory, units play the role of "trivial factors" that we wish to ignore. The correct statement of unique factorization will be "unique up to order and associates," and this phrase gains its precise meaning from the definitions we have just established.

**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{Z}$: the units are$R^\times = \{\pm 1\}$. Associates of$n$ are$\{n, -n\}$. The divisors of$12$ are$\pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 3, \pm 4, \pm 6, \pm 12$, but up to associates these are just$\{1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12\}$. The divisibility preorder has the associate equivalence classes as its "elements," and the partial order on these classes is:$[a] \leq [b]$ iff$a \mid b$. For positive integers, this is the usual divisibility partial order.

**Example 2.** In $F[x]$ for a field$F$: the units are$R^\times = F^\times$ (all nonzero constants). Associates of a polynomial$f$ are all scalar multiples$\{cf : c \in F^\times\}$. Conventionally, we pick the monic representative from each associate class (the unique scalar multiple with leading coefficient$1$). The monic associates are the "canonical" representatives, just as positive integers are canonical in$\mathbb{Z}$. Divisibility:$f \mid g$ iff$g = f \cdot q$ for some$q \in F[x]$.

**Example 3.** In $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ (Gaussian integers): the units are$R^\times = \{1, -1, i, -i\}$ (the elements of norm$1$:$N(a + bi) = a^2 + b^2 = 1$ iff$(a,b) \in \{(\pm 1, 0), (0, \pm 1)\}$). Associates of$3 + i$ are$\{3+i, -(3+i), i(3+i), -i(3+i)\} = \{3+i, -3-i, -1+3i, 1-3i\}$. Two Gaussian integers are associates iff one is obtained from the other by multiplication by$1, -1, i$, or$-i$ — i.e., by rotation by a multiple of$90°$.

**Example 4.** In the ring $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$: the units are again$R^\times = \{\pm 1\}$ (since$N(a + b\sqrt{-5}) = a^2 + 5b^2 = 1$ forces$a = \pm 1, b = 0$). So associates of$2$ are$\{2, -2\}$. Check: does$2 \mid (1 + \sqrt{-5})$? We would need$(1 + \sqrt{-5}) = 2c$ for some$c = a + b\sqrt{-5} \in \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, giving$2a = 1$ and$2b = 1$, which has no integer solution. So$2 \nmid (1 + \sqrt{-5})$ — they are not related by divisibility in either direction.

**Example 5.** The ideal-theoretic translation: in any commutative ring, $a \mid b \Leftrightarrow (b) \subseteq (a)$ and$a, b$ are associates$\Leftrightarrow (a) = (b)$. This lets us import inclusion-based reasoning into divisibility arguments. For instance, if$a \mid b$ and$a \mid c$, then$(b) \subseteq (a)$ and$(c) \subseteq (a)$, so$(b + c) \subseteq (a)$... wait, we need$b+c \in (a)$, not$(b+c) \subseteq (a)$. Since$b = ar$ and$c = as$,$b + c = a(r+s) \in (a)$, so$a \mid (b+c)$. This confirms the elementary fact that a common divisor of$b$ and$c$ divides their sum, now proved without calculation.
