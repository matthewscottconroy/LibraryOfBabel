# 22.1.2 Irreducible Elements and Prime Elements

In the integers, the prime numbers play two roles simultaneously: they are the atoms of multiplication (elements that cannot be factored nontrivially), and they are the elements that impose divisibility conditions on products. These two roles, which coincide in $\mathbb{Z}$, diverge in more general domains — and understanding the divergence is the key to the rest of this chapter.

An element $p \in R$ (nonzero, non-unit) is *irreducible* if whenever$p = ab$ for some$a, b \in R$, then$a$ or$b$ is a unit. Irreducibility captures the idea of an "atom":$p$ cannot be written as a product of two nontrivial factors. Equivalently, if$p = ab$ then either$(a) = (p)$ (meaning$a$ and$p$ are associates) or$(b) = (p)$ (meaning$b$ and$p$ are associates) — one of the factors is as "large" as$p$ itself (up to units). In$\mathbb{Z}$, the irreducible elements are exactly the prime numbers and their negatives.

An element $p$ is *prime* if whenever$p \mid ab$, then$p \mid a$ or$p \mid b$. Equivalently, the principal ideal$(p)$ is a prime ideal of$R$. This is the divisibility condition:$p$ imposes an "either/or" on any product it divides, just as a prime number does in elementary number theory. The ideal-theoretic reformulation is useful:$p$ is prime iff$R/(p)$ is an integral domain.

Notice that these are two genuinely different conditions. Every prime is irreducible: if $p$ is prime and$p = ab$, then$p \mid ab$, so$p \mid a$ or$p \mid b$. Suppose$p \mid a$, say$a = pc$. Then$p = pcb$, giving$pcb - p = 0$, so$p(cb - 1) = 0$. In a domain, since$p \neq 0$, we get$cb = 1$, so$b$ is a unit. Similarly if$p \mid b$ then$a$ is a unit. Either way, one factor is a unit, confirming irreducibility. This direction requires only the definition of a prime and the cancellation law in a domain.

The converse is not automatic: irreducible elements need not be prime in all domains. The failure of irreducibles to be prime is the signature of non-unique factorization, and we will see a concrete example in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ in §22.1.3. For now, we note that the implication "irreducible$\Rightarrow$ prime" holds in all UFDs (§22.2.2) and in particular in PIDs and Euclidean domains. It is precisely the domains where this converse holds that have well-behaved factorization theory.

**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{Z}$: the element$p = 5$ is both irreducible (no factorization$5 = ab$ with$|a|, |b| < 5$) and prime ($5 \mid ab$ implies$5 \mid a$ or$5 \mid b$, by the fundamental theorem of arithmetic). The element$4 = 2 \cdot 2$ is neither irreducible nor prime.

**Example 2.** In $\mathbb{Q}[x]$: the polynomial$f = x^2 - 2$ is irreducible (degree 2, no rational roots, since$\pm\sqrt{2} \notin \mathbb{Q}$). It is also prime, since$\mathbb{Q}[x]/(x^2-2) \cong \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ is a field, hence a domain. For prime: if$f \mid gh$, then since$f$ is irreducible in the PID$\mathbb{Q}[x]$, the ideal$(f)$ is prime, so$f \mid g$ or$f \mid h$.

**Example 3.** In $\mathbb{Z}[x]$: the element$x$ is irreducible (if$x = fg$ with$\deg f, \deg g \geq 1$ then$\deg f = \deg g = 0$... no wait:$\deg(fg) = 1$ requires$\deg f + \deg g = 1$, so one has degree$0$ (a constant) and the other has degree$1$; a degree-$0$ polynomial in$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is a unit iff it equals$\pm 1$, so$f = \pm 1$, making$x$ irreducible). Also$x$ is prime in$\mathbb{Z}[x]$:$\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ is a domain. The element$2$ is irreducible in$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ (it's a prime integer) and prime ($\mathbb{Z}[x]/(2) \cong \mathbb{F}_2[x]$ is a domain).

**Example 4.** In $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$: the element$3$ is irreducible (by a norm argument:$N(3) = 9$, and$9 = N(\alpha)N(\beta)$ with neither factor being$1$ would require elements of norm$3$, but$a^2 + 5b^2 = 3$ has no integer solution). But$3$ is not prime:$3 \mid (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5}) = 6$ but$3 \nmid (1 \pm \sqrt{-5})$ (since$1 \pm \sqrt{-5} = 3\alpha$ would give$3 \mid 1$ in$\mathbb{Z}$). So$3$ is an irreducible non-prime, witnessing that$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is not a UFD.

**Example 5.** In $F[x,y]$ for a field$F$: the polynomial$x$ is irreducible (degree 1 in$x$, cannot factor in$F[x,y]$ since any factor would have to divide$x$ in$F[x]$ first). It is also prime:$F[x,y]/(x) \cong F[y]$ is a domain. The polynomial$xy - 1$ is irreducible in$F[x,y]$: the variety$\{xy = 1\}$ is irreducible (a hyperbola, not decomposable as a union of varieties). Is it prime? Yes,$F[x,y]/(xy-1)$ is a domain (it's the coordinate ring of an irreducible variety).
