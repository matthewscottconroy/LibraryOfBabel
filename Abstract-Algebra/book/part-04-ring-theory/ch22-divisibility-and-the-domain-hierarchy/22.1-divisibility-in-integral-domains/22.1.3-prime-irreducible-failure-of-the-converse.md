# 22.1.3 Prime $\Rightarrow$ Irreducible; Failure of the Converse

The implication "prime implies irreducible" holds in all integral domains, but the converse is a special property that distinguishes the well-behaved domains in our hierarchy. Understanding exactly where and why the converse fails is instructive — it points to the heart of the failure of unique factorization and motivates the ideal-theoretic repair in §22.5.

We verified in §22.1.2 that every prime element is irreducible in an integral domain. The proof was a direct application of the divisibility condition: if $p$ is prime and$p = ab$, then$p \mid ab$, so$p \mid a$ or$p \mid b$; in either case the remaining factor must be a unit, confirming irreducibility. This direction requires only the definition of a prime and the cancellation law in a domain.

The converse fails already in the ring $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}] = \{a + b\sqrt{-5} : a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\}$, a subring of$\mathbb{C}$. Consider the element$3$. Using the norm$N(a + b\sqrt{-5}) = a^2 + 5b^2$, one checks that$3$ is irreducible in$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$: any factorization$3 = \alpha\beta$ gives$N(3) = 9 = N(\alpha)N(\beta)$, and an element of norm$3$ would satisfy$a^2 + 5b^2 = 3$, which has no integer solution (since$5b^2 \geq 5$ for$|b| \geq 1$, and$a^2 = 3$ has no integer solution). So$N(\alpha) = 1$ or$N(\beta) = 1$, making one of them a unit ($N = 1$ means$\pm 1$ are the only units since units have norm$1$).

Yet $3$ is not prime. We have$6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5})$, so$3 \mid (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5})$. But$3 \nmid (1 \pm \sqrt{-5})$: if$3 \mid (1 + \sqrt{-5})$, we would have$1 + \sqrt{-5} = 3(a + b\sqrt{-5})$ for integers$a, b$, giving$3a = 1$ — impossible in$\mathbb{Z}$. So$3$ divides the product$(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ without dividing either factor, violating the prime condition.

The failure of primeness of $3$ is intimately related to the non-unique factorization$6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$. In fact, this is not a coincidence: in any integral domain,$R$ is a UFD if and only if every irreducible is prime (Theorem 22.2.2). The irreducibility-but-non-primeness of$3$ is thus exactly what prevents$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ from being a UFD.

**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, the element$2$ is also irreducible but not prime. Irreducibility:$N(2) = 4$, and$4 = N(\alpha)N(\beta)$ with neither factor$1$ would require an element of norm$2$ (since$4 = 2 \cdot 2$), but$a^2 + 5b^2 = 2$ has no integer solution. So$2$ is irreducible. Non-primeness:$2 \mid (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5}) = 6$ but$2 \nmid (1 \pm \sqrt{-5})$ since$1/(2) \notin \mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 2.** Similarly, $1 + \sqrt{-5}$ is irreducible (norm$6 = N(1+\sqrt{-5})$; the factorizations$6 = 1 \cdot 6 = 2 \cdot 3$ would require elements of norm$2$ or$3$ in$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, but$a^2 + 5b^2 = 2$ and$a^2 + 5b^2 = 3$ have no solutions). Non-primeness of$1 + \sqrt{-5}$:$(1+\sqrt{-5}) \mid 6 = 2 \cdot 3$, but$(1+\sqrt{-5}) \nmid 2$ (since$2/(1+\sqrt{-5}) = 2(1-\sqrt{-5})/6 = (1-\sqrt{-5})/3 \notin \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$) and$(1+\sqrt{-5}) \nmid 3$ (similar computation).

**Example 3.** In $\mathbb{Z}[x]$: all irreducibles are prime. This is because$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is a UFD (Gauss's theorem:$\mathbb{Z}$ is a UFD implies$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is a UFD, and in a UFD all irreducibles are prime). The element$x + 2$ is irreducible in$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ (it is a polynomial of degree 1 with content 1). Is it prime? We need$\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x+2) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ to be a domain — yes, evaluation at$-2$ gives an isomorphism$\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x+2) \cong \mathbb{Z}$, which is a domain.

**Example 4.** In $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-3}] = \{a + b\sqrt{-3} : a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\}$: the element$2$ is irreducible (norm$4$, same norm argument as$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ since$a^2 + 3b^2 = 2$ requires$b = 0$ and$a^2 = 2$, no integer solution). Is$2$ prime? We have$4 = 2 \cdot 2 = (1+\sqrt{-3})(1-\sqrt{-3})$. Does$2 \mid (1+\sqrt{-3})$? That would require$a + b\sqrt{-3}$ with$2a = 1$, no. So$2$ is irreducible but not prime in$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-3}]$ either. (Note:$\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$ where$\omega = e^{2\pi i/3} = (-1+\sqrt{-3})/2$ is the "correct" ring of integers of$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})$, which is a PID. The ring$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-3}]$ is a proper subring that is not integrally closed.)

**Example 5.** In a general UFD like $\mathbb{Q}[x, y]$: the element$x$ is prime ($(x)$ is prime since$\mathbb{Q}[x,y]/(x) \cong \mathbb{Q}[y]$ is a domain), and$y$ is prime. The product$xy$ is reducible (it factors as$x \cdot y$). Now consider$f = x^2 + y^2$ — is it prime in$\mathbb{R}[x,y]$? The quotient$\mathbb{R}[x,y]/(x^2+y^2)$ is the coordinate ring of the variety$\{x^2 + y^2 = 0\}$ over$\mathbb{R}$. Over$\mathbb{R}$ this variety is just the origin (since$x^2 + y^2 = 0$ iff$x = y = 0$), so the coordinate ring is not a domain — it has the zero divisors$(x + iy)$ and$(x - iy)$ in the complexification. Over$\mathbb{R}$,$x^2 + y^2$ is irreducible but the ideal$(x^2 + y^2)$ is not prime in$\mathbb{R}[x,y]$.
