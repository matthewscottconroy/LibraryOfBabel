# 22.1.4 GCDs and LCMs; When They Exist

Greatest common divisors and least common multiples are central tools in elementary arithmetic, but in a general integral domain, they need not exist. Understanding when they do — and what their existence implies — is part of what distinguishes the levels of the domain hierarchy. The existence of GCDs is guaranteed in UFDs (via the exponent formula) and in PIDs (via the sum-of-ideals formula), but fails in domains like $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$.

A *greatest common divisor* of elements $a$ and$b$ in a domain$R$ is an element$d$ satisfying:$d \mid a$ and$d \mid b$, and if$d'$ is any element dividing both$a$ and$b$, then$d' \mid d$. In other words,$d$ is a common divisor that is divisible by every common divisor — it is the "largest" in the divisibility order. The GCD, if it exists, is unique up to associates: if$d$ and$d'$ are both GCDs of$a$ and$b$, then$d \mid d'$ and$d' \mid d$, so$d$ and$d'$ are associates. We write$\gcd(a, b) = d$, understanding that this denotes an associate class.

Similarly, a *least common multiple* of $a$ and$b$ is an element$\ell$ satisfying:$a \mid \ell$ and$b \mid \ell$, and if$\ell'$ is any element divisible by both$a$ and$b$, then$\ell \mid \ell'$. The LCM, if it exists, is unique up to associates.

In the domain $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ from §22.1.3, the GCD of$6$ and$2(1 + \sqrt{-5})$ does not exist in any meaningful sense: both$2$ and$1 + \sqrt{-5}$ are common divisors of$6$ and$2(1+\sqrt{-5})$ (since$6 = 2 \cdot 3$ and$6 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$, and$2 \mid 2(1+\sqrt{-5})$ and$(1+\sqrt{-5}) \mid 2(1+\sqrt{-5})$), but neither$2$ divides$1+\sqrt{-5}$ nor$1+\sqrt{-5}$ divides$2$. So neither candidate is the GCD. This failure of GCDs is tightly connected to the failure of unique factorization.

In a PID, GCDs always exist and have a particularly clean description: $\gcd(a,b)$ is the generator of the ideal$(a) + (b)$. Writing$(a) + (b) = (d)$ gives$d = ua + vb$ for some$u, v \in R$ — the Bézout identity, which is a powerful tool for working in$\mathbb{Z}$,$F[x]$, and the Gaussian integers. We will develop this systematically in §22.3.

**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{Z}$:$\gcd(12, 18) = 6$. Check:$6 \mid 12$ and$6 \mid 18$, and any common divisor$d'$ divides both$12$ and$18$, hence divides$18 - 12 = 6$, so$d' \mid 6$. The Bézout identity:$6 = 12 \cdot (-1) + 18 \cdot 1$. Similarly$\mathrm{lcm}(12, 18) = 36$:$12 \mid 36$,$18 \mid 36$, and if$12 \mid m$ and$18 \mid m$ then$36 \mid m$ (by prime factorization:$12 = 2^2 \cdot 3$,$18 = 2 \cdot 3^2$, so$\mathrm{lcm} = 2^2 \cdot 3^2 = 36$). Note$\gcd(12,18) \cdot \mathrm{lcm}(12, 18) = 6 \cdot 36 = 216 = 12 \cdot 18$.

**Example 2.** In $\mathbb{Q}[x]$:$\gcd(x^2 - 1, x^2 - x)$. Factor:$x^2 - 1 = (x-1)(x+1)$ and$x^2 - x = x(x-1)$. The common factor is$x - 1$, so$\gcd = x - 1$ (monic). Bézout: we need$u(x^2-1) + v(x^2-x) = x - 1$. Using the extended Euclidean algorithm:$x^2 - 1 = 1 \cdot (x^2 - x) + (x-1)$, so$x - 1 = (x^2-1) - (x^2-x)$, giving$u = 1$,$v = -1$.

**Example 3.** In $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ (Gaussian integers):$\gcd(3+i, 1+3i)$. Note$N(3+i) = 10$ and$N(1+3i) = 10$. Since both have the same norm, if$d \mid (3+i)$ and$d \mid (1+3i)$, then$N(d) \mid 10$. Also,$d \mid (3+i) - (1+3i) = 2 - 2i = 2(1-i)$ and$d \mid (3+i) + i(1+3i) = 3 + i + i - 3 = 2i$. From$d \mid 2i$ and$N(2i) = 4$, we get$N(d) \mid 4$ and$N(d) \mid 10$, so$N(d) \mid 2$. The elements of norm$1$ are units; the element$1 + i$ has norm$2$. Check:$(1+i) \mid (3+i)$? Compute$(3+i)/(1+i) = (3+i)(1-i)/2 = (3 - 3i + i - i^2)/2 = (3 - 2i + 1)/2 = (4-2i)/2 = 2 - i \in \mathbb{Z}[i]$. Yes! And$(1+i) \mid (1+3i)$:$(1+3i)/(1+i) = (1+3i)(1-i)/2 = (1 - i + 3i - 3i^2)/2 = (1 + 2i + 3)/2 = (4+2i)/2 = 2 + i \in \mathbb{Z}[i]$. Yes. So$\gcd(3+i, 1+3i) = 1+i$ (up to units).

**Example 4.** In $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$: consider$a = 6$ and$b = 2\sqrt{-5}$. Common divisors include$2$ (since$6 = 2 \cdot 3$ and$2\sqrt{-5} = 2 \cdot \sqrt{-5}$). Also$(1+\sqrt{-5})$ divides$6 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ and divides$2\sqrt{-5}$? Check:$2\sqrt{-5}/(1+\sqrt{-5}) = 2\sqrt{-5}(1-\sqrt{-5})/6 = 2\sqrt{-5}(1-\sqrt{-5})/6 = (2\sqrt{-5} - 2 \cdot (-5))/6 = (10 + 2\sqrt{-5})/6 = (5+\sqrt{-5})/3 \notin \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$. So$(1+\sqrt{-5}) \nmid 2\sqrt{-5}$. The only candidates for a GCD appear to reduce to divisors of$2$, but the analysis shows no single element serves as GCD.

**Example 5.** In a UFD, the GCD formula using prime factorizations works uniformly. In $\mathbb{Z}$: write$a = \prod p_i^{e_i}$ and$b = \prod p_i^{f_i}$. Then$\gcd(a, b) = \prod p_i^{\min(e_i, f_i)}$ and$\mathrm{lcm}(a, b) = \prod p_i^{\max(e_i, f_i)}$. For example,$a = 2^3 \cdot 3 \cdot 5^2 = 600$ and$b = 2 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5 = 90$. Then$\gcd = 2^1 \cdot 3^1 \cdot 5^1 = 30$ and$\mathrm{lcm} = 2^3 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5^2 = 1800 = 600 \cdot 90 / 30$, confirming$\gcd \cdot \mathrm{lcm} = ab$.

In a PID, GCDs always exist and have a particularly clean description: $\gcd(a,b)$ is the generator of the ideal$(a) + (b)$. This Bézout identity is a powerful tool and will be developed systematically in §22.3.
