# 22.2.1 UFDs: Unique Factorization into Irreducibles (up to Associates and Order)

The fundamental theorem of arithmetic states that every integer greater than $1$ factors uniquely into primes, up to the order in which we write the factors and up to signs. The definition of a unique factorization domain captures this property in full generality. UFDs are exactly the domains where the most basic feature of integer arithmetic — the uniqueness of prime factorization — remains intact.

An integral domain $R$ is a *unique factorization domain (UFD)* if every nonzero non-unit element satisfies two conditions. First, *existence of factorization*: it can be written as a finite product of irreducibles:$a = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_n$ for some irreducible elements$p_i \in R$. Second, *uniqueness of factorization*: if$a = p_1 \cdots p_n = q_1 \cdots q_m$ are two factorizations into irreducibles, then$n = m$ and after reordering,$p_i$ and$q_i$ are associates for each$i$.

The integers $\mathbb{Z}$ are the canonical example: the fundamental theorem of arithmetic is precisely the statement that$\mathbb{Z}$ is a UFD. The polynomial ring$\mathbb{Q}[x]$ is another UFD, with the irreducibles being the irreducible polynomials over$\mathbb{Q}$. We will see in §22.2.4 that the UFD property is preserved when passing to polynomial rings: if$R$ is a UFD, then so is$R[x]$. This gives a large class of UFDs including$\mathbb{Z}[x]$,$\mathbb{Z}[x,y]$,$\mathbb{Q}[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$, and so on.

The condition "up to associates and order" is not a weak caveat — it is essential. Without it, the definition would be too restrictive to apply to any ring with nontrivial units. In $\mathbb{Z}$, the factorizations$6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (-2) \cdot (-3) = 3 \cdot 2$ are all considered "the same" unique factorization because$2$ and$-2$ are associates, and the order of factors is immaterial. The UFD definition captures the precise content of unique factorization once we quotient out these harmless ambiguities.

Strategically, when checking whether a domain is a UFD, the two conditions split naturally. Existence of factorization often follows from a chain condition (ascending chain condition on principal ideals — see §22.3.2). Uniqueness of factorization is equivalent (in a domain where factorizations exist) to the condition that every irreducible is prime (§22.2.2). So the practical route to proving a domain is a UFD is usually: (1) verify the ACC on principal ideals to get existence, and (2) show every irreducible is prime to get uniqueness.

**Example 1.** $\mathbb{Z}$ is a UFD. Every positive integer$n > 1$ factors as$n = p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_k^{a_k}$, and this factorization is unique. For instance,$360 = 2^3 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5$. Any other factorization of$360$ into primes (or their negatives) must use the same primes with the same exponents (up to sign and order). This is the fundamental theorem of arithmetic, proven rigorously by Euclid's method (every prime is prime in the divisibility sense) or by the Euclidean algorithm.

**Example 2.** $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ is a UFD. The irreducibles are the irreducible polynomials over$\mathbb{Q}$: linear polynomials$ax + b$ (associate to$x - c$), and polynomials of degree$\geq 2$ with no rational roots and no factorization over$\mathbb{Q}$. Every polynomial$f \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ of degree$\geq 1$ factors uniquely (up to units = nonzero constants) into irreducibles:$f = c \cdot f_1^{a_1} \cdots f_k^{a_k}$ with$c \in \mathbb{Q}^\times$ and each$f_i$ monic irreducible. For example,$2x^4 - 2 = 2(x^4 - 1) = 2(x-1)(x+1)(x^2+1)$.

**Example 3.** $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ (Gaussian integers) is a UFD. The Gaussian primes (irreducibles up to units$\{\pm 1, \pm i\}$) are: (a)$1 + i$ (the unique prime above the rational prime$2$, up to associates), (b) Gaussian integers$a + bi$ with$a^2 + b^2 = p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ for rational primes$p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, and (c) rational primes$p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ (which remain prime in$\mathbb{Z}[i]$). Every Gaussian integer factors uniquely into these.

**Example 4.** $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is NOT a UFD: the element$6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ has two distinct factorizations into irreducibles (as verified in §22.1.3 and to be elaborated in §22.5.1). This failure shows that not all rings of integers of number fields are UFDs.

**Example 5.** The polynomial ring $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is a UFD (Gauss's theorem, §22.2.4). The irreducibles of$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ are: (a) primes$p \in \mathbb{Z}$ (viewed as constant polynomials), and (b) primitive polynomials (GCD of coefficients$= 1$) that are irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$. For instance,$2x^2 + 3x + 1 = (2x+1)(x+1)$ factors, while$x^2 + x + 1$ is irreducible in$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ (no rational roots, and primitive). The factorization$6x^2 + 3x = 3x(2x+1)$ is unique up to order and associates.
