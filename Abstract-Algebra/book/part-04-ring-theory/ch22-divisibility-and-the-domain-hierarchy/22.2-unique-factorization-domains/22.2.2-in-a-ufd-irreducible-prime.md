# 22.2.2 In a UFD, Irreducible $\Leftrightarrow$ Prime

In §22.1.3 we saw that prime always implies irreducible, but that irreducible need not imply prime in a general domain. The UFD condition is precisely strong enough to restore the equivalence. This equivalence is the key structural feature that makes unique factorization work: it is the bridge between the "atomic" condition (cannot be factored further) and the "divisibility" condition (imposes an either/or on products).

In a UFD, every irreducible element is prime. Suppose $p$ is irreducible and$p \mid ab$. Write$ab = pc$ for some$c \in R$. Now factor$a$,$b$, and$c$ into irreducibles:$a = u \cdot p_1 \cdots p_r$,$b = v \cdot q_1 \cdots q_s$,$c = w \cdot r_1 \cdots r_t$ (where$u, v, w$ are units). The left side$ab$ factors into irreducibles as$uv \cdot p_1 \cdots p_r q_1 \cdots q_s$, and the right side$pc$ factors as$w \cdot p \cdot r_1 \cdots r_t$. By uniqueness of factorization,$p$ must be an associate of some$p_i$ or some$q_j$. If$p$ is an associate of$p_i$, then$p \mid p_1 \cdots p_r$, hence$p \mid a$. Similarly if$p$ is an associate of some$q_j$, then$p \mid b$. Either way,$p \mid a$ or$p \mid b$, confirming$p$ is prime.

The key point is that this argument uses uniqueness of factorization in an essential way: we must know that the factorization of $ab$ is essentially the concatenation of the factorizations of$a$ and$b$, so that$p$ must appear as a factor of$a$ or$b$. Without uniqueness, this conclusion fails, as the example of$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ in §22.1.3 demonstrates.

Combining with the general fact that prime implies irreducible (§22.1.2), we get: in a UFD, an element is irreducible if and only if it is prime. This equivalence does not hold in all domains, only in UFDs. Indeed, we can take it as an equivalent characterization: a domain in which factorizations exist is a UFD if and only if every irreducible is prime. (Proof of the "if" direction: given two factorizations $p_1 \cdots p_n = q_1 \cdots q_m$, since$p_1$ is prime and divides the right side, it divides some$q_j$; since$q_j$ is irreducible and$p_1$ is not a unit,$p_1$ and$q_j$ are associates; cancel and iterate by induction on$n$.)

**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{Z}$: every irreducible is prime, which is precisely the fundamental lemma used in the proof of unique factorization: if$p$ is prime and$p \mid ab$, then$p \mid a$ or$p \mid b$. This is proved from the Euclidean algorithm (Euclid's lemma), or it follows from our theorem since$\mathbb{Z}$ is a UFD.

**Example 2.** In $\mathbb{Q}[x]$: the polynomial$x^2 + 1$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$ (no rational roots). By the UFD property of$\mathbb{Q}[x]$, it is also prime: if$(x^2+1) \mid fg$ in$\mathbb{Q}[x]$, then$(x^2+1) \mid f$ or$(x^2+1) \mid g$. Concretely, if$f(i) = 0$ then$(x-i) \mid f$ over$\mathbb{C}$, and the minimal polynomial of$i$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ is$x^2+1$, so$(x^2+1) \mid f$ over$\mathbb{Q}$.

**Example 3.** In $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$: the element$3$ is irreducible but not prime (§22.1.3), so$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is not a UFD. The failure of the equivalence "irreducible$\Leftrightarrow$ prime" is a characterizing symptom of the non-UFD nature of this ring.

**Example 4.** In the ring $\mathbb{Z}[2i] = \{a + 2bi : a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ (a proper subring of$\mathbb{Z}[i]$): is$2$ prime? Note$2 = (1+i)(1-i)$ in$\mathbb{Z}[i]$, but$1+i \notin \mathbb{Z}[2i]$ since the imaginary part would need to be$1$, which is not a multiple of$2$. So$2 = (-1) \cdot 2 \cdot (-1)$ is a factorization into associates. Let's check:$4 = 2 \cdot 2 = (2i)(-2i)$ in$\mathbb{Z}[2i]$. Are$2$ and$2i$ associates?$2i = u \cdot 2$ would require$u = i$, but$i \notin \mathbb{Z}[2i]$. So$2$ and$2i$ are not associates but have the same norm, and$4 = 2 \cdot 2 = (2i) \cdot (-2i)$ might give two essentially different factorizations... this would show$\mathbb{Z}[2i]$ is not a UFD. (Note$\mathbb{Z}[2i]$ is not integrally closed, confirming it lies outside the Dedekind domain framework.)

**Example 5.** In $\mathbb{Z}[x]$: the polynomial$2x + 2 = 2(x+1)$. Here$2$ and$x+1$ are both irreducible (the first is a prime, the second is a primitive degree-1 polynomial). Both are also prime in$\mathbb{Z}[x]$:$(2)$ generates a prime ideal since$\mathbb{Z}[x]/(2) \cong \mathbb{F}_2[x]$ is a domain;$(x+1)$ generates a prime ideal since$\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x+1) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ (evaluation at$-1$) is a domain. This illustrates the equivalence in a UFD: every irreducible ($2$ and$x+1$) is prime.
