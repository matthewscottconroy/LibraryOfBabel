# 22.2.3 GCDs Exist in UFDs

One of the most useful consequences of unique factorization is that greatest common divisors always exist. In a general integral domain, GCDs may fail to exist (as we noted in §22.1.4), but in a UFD, the factorization into irreducibles provides an explicit recipe for computing them. This recipe generalizes the elementary formula $\gcd(2^3 \cdot 3, 2 \cdot 3^2) = 2^1 \cdot 3^1$ from integer arithmetic to any UFD.

In a UFD $R$, let$a$ and$b$ be nonzero non-units (the cases where one is zero or a unit are trivial). Write$a = u \cdot p_1^{e_1} p_2^{e_2} \cdots p_k^{e_k}$ and$b = v \cdot p_1^{f_1} p_2^{f_2} \cdots p_k^{f_k}$, where$u, v$ are units, the$p_i$ are pairwise non-associate irreducibles (representatives from their associate classes), and the exponents$e_i, f_i \geq 0$ (with exponent$0$ indicating that$p_i$ does not appear). Then
$$\gcd(a, b) = p_1^{\min(e_1, f_1)} p_2^{\min(e_2, f_2)} \cdots p_k^{\min(e_k, f_k)}.$$

To see this, observe that an element $d$ divides$a$ if and only if$d = w \cdot \prod p_i^{g_i}$ with$g_i \leq e_i$ for all$i$. Similarly$d \mid b$ iff$g_i \leq f_i$ for all$i$. The conditions for$d$ to be a common divisor of$a$ and$b$ are thus$g_i \leq \min(e_i, f_i)$ for all$i$, and the largest such$d$ (up to units) is obtained by taking$g_i = \min(e_i, f_i)$. This element is the GCD by definition.

The same formula gives $\mathrm{lcm}(a, b) = p_1^{\max(e_1, f_1)} \cdots p_k^{\max(e_k, f_k)}$, and the identity$\gcd(a,b) \cdot \mathrm{lcm}(a,b) \sim ab$ follows because$\min(e_i, f_i) + \max(e_i, f_i) = e_i + f_i$ for all$i$. (Here$\sim$ means equality up to a unit.)

This explicit formula explains why unique factorization is so powerful in number theory. The GCD is a complete and computable invariant of the divisibility relationship between two elements. In a UFD, all divisibility questions reduce to comparing exponent vectors in the prime factorization.

**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{Z}$: compute$\gcd(360, 252)$. Factor:$360 = 2^3 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5$ and$252 = 2^2 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 7$. GCD:$2^{\min(3,2)} \cdot 3^{\min(2,2)} \cdot 5^{\min(1,0)} \cdot 7^{\min(0,1)} = 2^2 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5^0 \cdot 7^0 = 4 \cdot 9 = 36$. LCM:$2^3 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 = 8 \cdot 9 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 = 2520$. Check:$36 \cdot 2520 = 90720 = 360 \cdot 252$.

**Example 2.** In $\mathbb{Q}[x]$: compute$\gcd(x^4 - 1, x^3 - 1)$. Factor:$x^4 - 1 = (x-1)(x+1)(x^2+1)$ and$x^3 - 1 = (x-1)(x^2+x+1)$. GCD:$(x-1)^{\min(1,1)} \cdot (x+1)^{\min(1,0)} \cdot (x^2+1)^{\min(1,0)} \cdot (x^2+x+1)^{\min(0,1)} = (x-1)$. So$\gcd(x^4-1, x^3-1) = x - 1$ (monic representative). This can be confirmed by the Euclidean algorithm for polynomials.

**Example 3.** In $\mathbb{Z}[i]$: compute$\gcd(10, 3+i)$. Factor$10 = 2 \cdot 5 = (-i)(1+i)^2(2+i)(2-i)$ (using the Gaussian prime factorizations:$2 = -i(1+i)^2$,$5 = (2+i)(2-i)$). Factor$3+i$:$N(3+i) = 10 = 2 \cdot 5$.$(3+i)/(1+i) = (3+i)(1-i)/2 = (3 - 3i + i + 1)/2 = (4-2i)/2 = 2-i \in \mathbb{Z}[i]$. Is$2-i$ prime?$N(2-i) = 5$, and elements of norm$5$ are Gaussian primes (since$5 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ and$5 = (2+i)(2-i)$). So$3+i = (1+i)(2-i)$. Both$10$ and$3+i$ are divisible by$(1+i)$ (norm$2$ prime) and by$(2-i)$ (norm$5$ prime). Check:$(1+i)(2+i) = 2 + i + 2i - 1 = 1 + 3i$, and$(1+i)(2-i) = 2 - i + 2i + 1 = 3 + i$. So$3+i = (1+i)(2-i)$, and$10 = (1+i)(1-i) \cdot (2+i)(2-i) = (-i)(1+i)^2(2+i)(2-i)$ (various orderings). So$\gcd(10, 3+i) = (1+i)(2-i) = 3 + i$ — meaning$3+i \mid 10$! Let's verify:$10/(3+i) = 10(3-i)/10 = 3 - i \in \mathbb{Z}[i]$. Yes, so$(3+i) \mid 10$ and$(3+i) \mid (3+i)$, meaning$\gcd = 3+i$ (up to units).

**Example 4.** In $\mathbb{Z}[x]$: compute$\gcd(4x^2 + 4x, 6x)$. First extract integer content:$4x^2 + 4x = 4x(x+1)$ and$6x = 6x$. GCD of contents:$\gcd(4, 6) = 2$. GCD of primitive parts:$\gcd(x(x+1), x) = x$ (since$x \mid x(x+1)$ and$x \mid x$, and$x$ is irreducible, so the GCD of the primitive parts involves the common factor$x$). Thus$\gcd(4x^2+4x, 6x) = 2x$. Check:$4x^2+4x = 2x \cdot (2x+2)$ and$6x = 2x \cdot 3$, confirming$2x$ divides both; and$\gcd(2x+2, 3) = 1$ in appropriate sense, so$2x$ is indeed the GCD.

**Example 5.** In $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$: the failure of GCDs to exist here is now explicable. The ring is not a UFD, so the exponent formula does not apply. The elements$6$ and$2(1+\sqrt{-5})$ have two natural "common divisor candidates" — namely$2$ and$1+\sqrt{-5}$ — with neither dividing the other. The non-existence of a GCD is a direct consequence of the non-UFD nature of the ring.
