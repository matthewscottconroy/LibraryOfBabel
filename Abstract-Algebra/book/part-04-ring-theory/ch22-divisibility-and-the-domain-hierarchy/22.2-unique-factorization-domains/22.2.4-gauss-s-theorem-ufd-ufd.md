# 22.2.4 Gauss's Theorem: $R$ UFD$\Rightarrow$ $R[x]$ UFD

The UFD property is robust under polynomial extension. Gauss's theorem states that if $R$ is a UFD, then so is$R[x]$ — and by induction, so is$R[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ for any number of variables. This is one of the most important structural results in ring theory, and it places polynomial rings squarely in the UFD class of the domain hierarchy.

The proof has two components. The first is *Gauss's lemma*: the product of primitive polynomials is primitive. A polynomial $f \in R[x]$ is *primitive* if the GCD of its coefficients is$1$ (or more precisely, a unit — since GCDs are defined up to associates in a UFD). Gauss's lemma asserts that if$f$ and$g$ are primitive, so is$fg$. The proof is by contradiction: if some irreducible$p \in R$ divides all coefficients of$fg$, reduce modulo$p$. In the domain$R/(p)$ (which is a domain because$p$ is prime in$R$, being irreducible in the UFD$R$), the equation$\bar{f}\bar{g} = \overline{fg} = 0$ forces$\bar{f} = 0$ or$\bar{g} = 0$ (since$R/(p)[x]$ is a domain as a polynomial ring over a domain). But$\bar{f} = 0$ means$p$ divides all coefficients of$f$, contradicting primitivity of$f$; similarly$\bar{g} = 0$ contradicts primitivity of$g$.

The second component uses Gauss's lemma to describe the irreducibles of $R[x]$. Let$F = \mathrm{Frac}(R)$ be the fraction field of$R$. The irreducibles of$R[x]$ are exactly: (a) the irreducibles of$R$ (viewed as constant polynomials), and (b) the primitive polynomials in$R[x]$ that are irreducible over$F$. With this characterization in hand, one verifies the two conditions for a UFD: existence of factorization (using the UFD property of both$R$ and$F[x]$, together with primitivity arguments to clear denominators) and uniqueness (using Gauss's lemma to compare factorizations at the coefficient level).

As a consequence, $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is a UFD: the integers are a UFD, so Gauss's theorem applies. By induction,$\mathbb{Z}[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ is a UFD for any$n$. This places polynomial rings in the UFD class of the domain hierarchy, even though (as we will see in §22.3)$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is not a PID.

**Example 1.** Gauss's lemma in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$: take$f = 2x + 3$ (primitive, GCD of coefficients is$1$) and$g = 3x + 2$ (primitive). Then$fg = 6x^2 + 4x + 9x + 6 = 6x^2 + 13x + 6$. GCD of coefficients of$fg$:$\gcd(6, 13, 6) = 1$. Primitive, as Gauss's lemma predicts.

**Example 2.** Gauss's lemma in action for a non-primitive case: take $f = 2x^2 + 4 = 2(x^2 + 2)$ and$g = 3x + 6 = 3(x + 2)$. The content of$f$ is$2$ and the content of$g$ is$3$. Then$fg = 6x^3 + 12x^2 + 12x + 24 = 6(x^3 + 2x^2 + 2x + 4)$. The content of$fg$ is$6 = 2 \cdot 3 = \mathrm{content}(f) \cdot \mathrm{content}(g)$. The general statement of Gauss's lemma says$\mathrm{content}(fg) = \mathrm{content}(f) \cdot \mathrm{content}(g)$, of which the primitive case is the key special instance.

**Example 3.** Using Gauss's theorem to factor in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$: is$f = x^4 + x^2 + 1$ irreducible in$\mathbb{Z}[x]$? By Gauss's theorem,$f$ is irreducible in$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ iff it is irreducible in$\mathbb{Q}[x]$ (since$f$ is primitive). In$\mathbb{Q}[x]$:$f(x) = x^4 + x^2 + 1 = (x^2 + x + 1)(x^2 - x + 1)$. Verify:$(x^2+x+1)(x^2-x+1) = x^4 - x^3 + x^2 + x^3 - x^2 + x + x^2 - x + 1 = x^4 + x^2 + 1$. So$f$ factors in$\mathbb{Q}[x]$ (and hence in$\mathbb{Z}[x]$). Both factors$x^2 + x + 1$ and$x^2 - x + 1$ are irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$ (discriminants$1 - 4 = -3 < 0$), so the factorization in$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is$f = (x^2+x+1)(x^2-x+1)$.

**Example 4.** Using Gauss's theorem combined with Eisenstein (§23.2.3): the polynomial $f = x^3 + 2 \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ is primitive and Eisenstein at$p = 2$:$2 \nmid 1$ (leading coefficient),$2 \mid 0$ (coefficient of$x^2$),$2 \mid 0$ (coefficient of$x$),$2 \mid 2$ (constant term), and$4 \nmid 2$. So$f$ is irreducible in$\mathbb{Q}[x]$ and hence in$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ (by Gauss's theorem, since$f$ is primitive). The extension$\mathbb{Q}[x]/(x^3+2) \cong \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ is a degree-$3$ field extension of$\mathbb{Q}$.

**Example 5.** Gauss's theorem fails for non-domains: consider the ring $R = \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, which is not a domain. In$R[x]$, we have$2 \cdot 3 = 0$, so$(2x + 1)(3x + 1) = 6x^2 + 2x + 3x + 1 = 0 \cdot x^2 + 5x + 1 = 5x + 1$, but also$3 \cdot (2x+1) = 6x + 3 = 3$ and$2 \cdot (3x+1) = 6x+2 = 2$. Unique factorization fails, and Gauss's argument breaks down because$R/(p)$ may not be a domain when$p$ is not prime.
