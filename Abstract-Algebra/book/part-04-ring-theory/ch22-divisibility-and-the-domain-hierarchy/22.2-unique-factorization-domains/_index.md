# 22.2 Unique Factorization Domains

A unique factorization domain (UFD) is an integral domain in which every nonzero non-unit element can be written as a product of irreducibles, and that product is unique up to order and replacement of factors by associates. This definition axiomatizes exactly the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic: $\mathbb{Z}$ is a UFD, and the positive integers are the canonical representatives of the associate classes of nonzero elements. The definition splits into two parts — existence of an irreducible factorization, and uniqueness — and each part has a distinct algebraic characterization that makes UFDs tractable.

Existence of factorization is guaranteed by the ascending chain condition on principal ideals (ACCP): the ring $R$ satisfies ACCP if every ascending chain $(a_1) \subseteq (a_2) \subseteq \cdots$ of principal ideals eventually stabilizes. This condition prevents infinite factorization chains and ensures that any non-unit can be factored into irreducibles in finitely many steps. Uniqueness is guaranteed by the prime-equals-irreducible condition: in a domain where every irreducible is prime, any two irreducible factorizations of the same element must coincide up to order and associates. This is the key: in a UFD, irreducibles and primes are the same, and it is this equivalence, not the existence of a norm or a Euclidean algorithm, that drives uniqueness.

Gauss's theorem — that if $R$ is a UFD then the polynomial ring $R[x]$ is also a UFD — is one of the most useful results in algebra. Its proof uses Gauss's lemma: a product of primitive polynomials is primitive, where a polynomial is primitive if its coefficients have no common factor. The theorem implies, by induction, that $\mathbb{Z}[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ and $k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ are UFDs for any field $k$ and any $n$, giving unique factorization in the polynomial rings most important for geometry and number theory. However, $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ fails to be a PID, so the class of UFDs is strictly larger than the class of PIDs, and the irreducible factorization in UFDs is a genuinely more general notion than primary decomposition in PIDs.
