# 22.3.1 PIDs: Every Ideal Is Principal

Principal ideal domains occupy the second tier of the domain hierarchy, sitting strictly between Euclidean domains and UFDs. Their defining property — that every ideal is generated by a single element — gives them an exceptionally clean and tractable algebraic structure. In a PID, the entire ideal theory reduces to the theory of divisibility among elements, and divisibility theory reduces to principal ideal containment.

An integral domain $R$ is a *principal ideal domain (PID)* if every ideal of$R$ is principal, that is, of the form$(a) = aR$ for some$a \in R$. The condition applies to all ideals, including$(0) = \{0\}$ and$(1) = R$, which are trivially principal. The content of the definition lies in the non-trivial ideals in between.

The integers $\mathbb{Z}$ are the prototypical PID. Every ideal of$\mathbb{Z}$ is of the form$n\mathbb{Z} = (n)$ for some$n \geq 0$: this follows because if$I$ is any nonzero ideal, the smallest positive integer in$I$ generates all of$I$ by the division algorithm. The polynomial ring$F[x]$ over a field$F$ is likewise a PID: one uses the degree of a polynomial in place of the size of an integer, and the same division algorithm argument applies. The Gaussian integers$\mathbb{Z}[i]$ are a PID, as we will verify in §22.4 by showing they form a Euclidean domain.

In a PID, the ideal structure is in bijection with the associate classes of elements. Two elements generate the same ideal if and only if they are associates: $(a) = (b) \Leftrightarrow a$ and$b$ are associates. So the principal ideals are parameterized by associate classes. This means the entire divisibility theory of$R$ is captured by the inclusion relations among principal ideals:$a \mid b$ if and only if$(b) \subseteq (a)$ (since$b \in (a)$ iff$a \mid b$). The PID condition thus reduces the study of divisibility to the study of ideal containment.

A crucial feature of PIDs: prime and maximal ideals coincide for nonzero ideals. In a PID $R$, if$(p) \neq (0)$ and$(p)$ is prime, then$(p)$ is maximal. Proof: suppose$(p) \subsetneq (a) \subsetneq R$. Then$p \in (a)$ so$a \mid p$. Since$p$ is irreducible (irreducible$\Leftrightarrow$ prime in a PID, as we will prove in §22.3.2), either$a$ is a unit (giving$(a) = R$, contradiction) or$a$ is an associate of$p$ (giving$(a) = (p)$, contradiction). So no such$(a)$ exists, and$(p)$ is maximal.

**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{Z}$: every ideal$(n)$ is principal by definition. The ideal$(6)$ is the set of multiples of$6$. The ideal$(0) = \{0\}$ and$(1) = \mathbb{Z}$. The prime ideals$(p)$ for primes$p$ are maximal (as just argued). The ideal lattice of$\mathbb{Z}$ is isomorphic to the divisibility lattice of positive integers (ordered by divisibility, with$(m) \subseteq (n)$ iff$n \mid m$).

**Example 2.** In $\mathbb{Q}[x]$: the ideal generated by$x^2 - x - 6 = (x-3)(x+2)$ is the set of polynomials divisible by$(x-3)(x+2)$. Since$\gcd(x-3, x+2) = 1$, the sum$(x-3) + (x+2) = (1) = \mathbb{Q}[x]$. The ideal$(x^2-x-6)$ is not prime (since$(x-3)(x+2) \in (x^2-x-6)$ but neither factor is in the ideal), confirming that$x^2 - x - 6$ is reducible.

**Example 3.** In $\mathbb{Z}[i]$: the ideal$(2+i)$ is principal, generated by the Gaussian prime$2+i$ (which has norm$5$). Every element of$(2+i)$ is of the form$(2+i)(a+bi) = (2a-b) + (a+2b)i$ for integers$a, b$. The ideal$(2+i)$ is prime (since$N(2+i) = 5$ is a rational prime and$\mathbb{Z}[i]/(2+i) \cong \mathbb{F}_5$), hence maximal.

**Example 4.** In $\mathbb{Z}[x]$: the ideal$(2, x)$ is NOT principal (as shown in §21.1.3). This is the key reason$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is not a PID, even though$\mathbb{Z}$ is. The failure of principality is what distinguishes$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ from$\mathbb{Z}$ and$\mathbb{Q}[x]$ in the hierarchy.

**Example 5.** An example of a PID that is not "obviously" Euclidean: the ring $\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1+\sqrt{-19}}{2}\right]$ is a PID. This ring (the ring of integers of$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-19})$) has class number$1$ — every ideal is principal — but it can be shown that no Euclidean norm exists on it. The existence of PIDs that are not Euclidean domains confirms that the inclusions in the hierarchy$\text{ED} \subsetneq \text{PID} \subsetneq \text{UFD}$ are strict.
