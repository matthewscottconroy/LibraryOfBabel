# 22.3.2 PIDs Are UFDs (via the Ascending Chain Condition)

The proof that every PID is a UFD is one of the jewels of the domain hierarchy. It proceeds in two steps — existence of factorization and uniqueness of factorization — and the key tool for the first step is a chain condition on ideals. This argument exhibits the interplay between the ideal-theoretic and divisibility-theoretic perspectives that characterizes PID theory.

The *ascending chain condition (ACC)* for a ring $R$ says that every ascending chain of ideals$I_1 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq I_3 \subseteq \cdots$ eventually stabilizes: there exists$N$ such that$I_n = I_N$ for all$n \geq N$. Every PID satisfies the ACC. To see this, suppose$I_1 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq \cdots$ is an ascending chain in a PID$R$. The union$J = \bigcup_{n} I_n$ is an ideal (one checks closure under addition and absorption by noting that any two elements lie in a common$I_n$), so$J = (a)$ for some$a \in R$. Since$a \in J$, we have$a \in I_N$ for some$N$, and therefore$(a) \subseteq I_N \subseteq J = (a)$. Hence$I_n = J$ for all$n \geq N$, and the chain stabilizes.

From the ACC, we deduce that every nonzero non-unit in a PID has a factorization into irreducibles. Suppose $a$ is a nonzero non-unit that cannot be factored into irreducibles. Then$a$ itself is not irreducible (being a non-unit that cannot be factored into irreducibles), so$a = bc$ with neither$b$ nor$c$ a unit. Since neither is a unit, we have$(a) \subsetneq (b)$ and$(a) \subsetneq (c)$ (strictly, because if$(a) = (b)$ then$a$ and$b$ are associates, making$c$ a unit). At least one of$b, c$ — say$b$ — also cannot be factored into irreducibles (otherwise$a = bc$ would have a factorization). So$b = b_1 c_1$ with$(b) \subsetneq (b_1)$, giving the strictly ascending chain$(a) \subsetneq (b) \subsetneq (b_1) \subsetneq \cdots$. The ACC says this chain must stabilize, contradiction. So every non-unit has a factorization into irreducibles.

For uniqueness, one shows that irreducibles in a PID are prime. If $p$ is irreducible and$p \mid ab$ with$p \nmid a$, then the ideal$(p, a) = (d)$ for some$d$ (since$R$ is a PID). Since$p \in (d)$,$d \mid p$, and since$p$ is irreducible and$d \nmid 1$ (as$a \notin (p)$, so$(d) \neq (p)$ if$d \sim p$... let us be more careful:$p = d \cdot q$ for some$q$; since$p$ is irreducible, either$d$ is a unit or$q$ is a unit. If$d$ is a unit then$(d) = R$, so$1 \in (p, a)$, giving$1 = rp + sa$ for some$r, s \in R$, and then$b = rpb + s(ab) \in (p)$... but we wanted$p \mid b$. Let us redo: since$d \mid p$ and$p$ is irreducible, either$d$ is a unit (so$(d) = R$) or$d$ and$p$ are associates (so$(d) = (p)$). Case 1:$(d) = R$, so$1 \in (p, a)$, say$1 = xp + ya$. Multiply by$b$:$b = xpb + y(ab) \in (p)$ (since$p \mid ab$). So$p \mid b$. Case 2:$(d) = (p)$, so$a \in (d) = (p)$, meaning$p \mid a$ — contradiction with$p \nmid a$. So only Case 1 applies, giving$p \mid b$. This confirms$p$ is prime. Primeness of irreducibles then gives uniqueness of factorization exactly as in §22.2.2.

**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{Z}$: the ACC holds because any ascending chain$(n_1) \supseteq (n_2) \supseteq \ldots$ of ideals (remember: containment is reversed for ideals of$\mathbb{Z}$ since$(a) \subseteq (b)$ iff$b \mid a$) with$n_1 \mid n_2 \mid \ldots$ must eventually stabilize (integers cannot be divisible by a strictly longer chain of prime powers). The ACC for ascending$I_1 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq \ldots$ means$n_1 \geq n_2 \geq \ldots$ (in terms of divisibility), which stabilizes since positive integers are well-ordered.

**Example 2.** Existence of factorization via ACC: in $F[x]$ (a PID), suppose$f$ has no factorization into irreducibles. Then$f = gh$ with neither a unit, so$\deg g < \deg f$ and$\deg h < \deg f$, giving$(f) \subsetneq (g)$ (since$\deg g < \deg f$ means$g \nmid f$... wait,$(f) \subsetneq (g)$ requires$g \mid f$ and$f \nmid g$). Since$f = gh$ and$h$ is not a unit,$g \mid f$ strictly:$g \mid f$ but$f \nmid g$ (since$\deg g < \deg f$). So$(f) \subsetneq (g)$. One of$g, h$ cannot be factored into irreducibles; iterate. The chain$(f) \subsetneq (g) \subsetneq \ldots$ of strictly ascending ideals must stabilize (ACC), contradiction.

**Example 3.** Irreducibles are prime in $F[x]$ (a PID): take irreducible$p$ and suppose$p \mid fg$. The ideal$(p, f) = (\gcd(p, f))$ in the PID$F[x]$. Since$p$ is irreducible,$\gcd(p, f)$ is either a unit or an associate of$p$. If$\gcd(p,f)$ is a unit (i.e.,$p$ and$f$ are coprime), then$1 \in (p, f)$, so$1 = up + vf$, giving$g = upg + v(fg)$; since$p \mid fg$, we get$p \mid g$. If$\gcd(p,f) \sim p$, then$p \mid f$. Either way,$p \mid f$ or$p \mid g$.

**Example 4.** The ACC approach to existence of factorization also works in Noetherian rings more generally. The reason PIDs satisfy the ACC is that all ideals are principal, and a strictly ascending chain of principal ideals $(a_1) \subsetneq (a_2) \subsetneq \ldots$ corresponds to a sequence of elements where each properly divides the previous:$a_1 = a_2 \cdot c_2$ with$c_2$ not a unit,$a_2 = a_3 \cdot c_3$ with$c_3$ not a unit, etc. In the integers or polynomial rings, this process must terminate because the norms (absolute values or degrees) are decreasing positive integers.

**Example 5.** A domain where ACC fails and factorization need not exist: $R = \mathbb{Z}[x, y, y/x, y/x^2, y/x^3, \ldots]$, a localization of$\mathbb{Z}[x,y]$ at powers of$x$. Here$y = x \cdot (y/x)$, and$y/x = x \cdot (y/x^2)$, and so on, giving an infinite strictly ascending chain$(y) \subsetneq (y/x) \subsetneq (y/x^2) \subsetneq \ldots$ such that$y$ has no irreducible factorization (it can always be divided by$x$ one more time). This is an extreme example of ACC failure.
