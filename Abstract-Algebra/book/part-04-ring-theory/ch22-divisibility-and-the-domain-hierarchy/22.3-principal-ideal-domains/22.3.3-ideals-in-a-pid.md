# 22.3.3 Ideals in a PID: $(a) + (b) = (\gcd(a,b))$

In a PID, the arithmetic of GCDs and LCMs translates directly into ideal-theoretic operations, giving ideal-theoretic proofs of classical number-theoretic identities. This connection is more than notational: it shows that the ideal structure of a PID is a complete and faithful encoding of its divisibility theory. Every divisibility fact about elements becomes a containment or equality of ideals, and vice versa.

Let $R$ be a PID and$a, b \in R$. The sum of ideals$(a) + (b)$ is an ideal of$R$ (as we verified in §21.1.4), and since$R$ is a PID, this sum must be principal:$(a) + (b) = (d)$ for some$d \in R$. The element$d$ is a GCD of$a$ and$b$. To see this: since$a \in (d)$, we have$d \mid a$; similarly$d \mid b$. And if$d'$ divides both$a$ and$b$, then$a, b \in (d')$, so$(a) + (b) \subseteq (d')$, giving$d \in (d')$, hence$d' \mid d$. So$d$ satisfies the definition of a GCD.

Writing $(d) = (a) + (b)$ means$d = ua + vb$ for some$u, v \in R$ — this is *Bézout's identity*, generalizing the familiar integer version to all PIDs. In$\mathbb{Z}$,$\gcd(12, 8) = 4$ and$4 = 12 \cdot (-1) + 8 \cdot 2$. In$F[x]$, if$\gcd(f, g) = 1$ (coprime polynomials), then there exist polynomials$u, v$ with$uf + vg = 1$ — a fact central to the Chinese Remainder Theorem for polynomial rings.

Similarly, $(a) \cap (b) = (\ell)$ where$\ell = \mathrm{lcm}(a, b)$: the intersection of principal ideals is principal, generated by the LCM. The formula$\gcd(a,b) \cdot \mathrm{lcm}(a,b) \sim ab$ (where$\sim$ denotes equality up to a unit) follows from$\min(e,f) + \max(e,f) = e + f$ for each prime exponent, and holds in all PIDs.

These relations between GCDs, LCMs, and ideal operations make PIDs the algebraic framework within which classical number theory lives most naturally. The ideal $(a, b)$ encodes$\gcd(a,b)$; the intersection$(a) \cap (b)$ encodes$\mathrm{lcm}(a,b)$; and the product$(a)(b)$ encodes$ab$.

**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{Z}$:$(12) + (18) = (6)$, since$\gcd(12, 18) = 6$ and Bézout gives$6 = 12 \cdot (-1) + 18 \cdot 1$. Verify:$6 \in (12) + (18)$ (by the Bézout expression), and every element of$(12) + (18)$ is of the form$12a + 18b = 6(2a + 3b)$, which is a multiple of$6$. Also$(12) \cap (18) = (36)$ since$\mathrm{lcm}(12, 18) = 36$.

**Example 2.** In $\mathbb{Q}[x]$:$(x^2-1) + (x^2+x) = ?$. Compute$\gcd(x^2-1, x^2+x)$.$x^2-1 = (x-1)(x+1)$ and$x^2+x = x(x+1)$. GCD$= x+1$. So$(x^2-1) + (x^2+x) = (x+1)$. Bézout:$(x+1) = (x^2-1) - (x-1) \cdot 1$?$x^2-1 = (x+1)(x-1)$, so$x+1 = (x^2-1)/(x-1)$... more usefully:$x+1 = (x^2+x) - x(x-1)$... need a Bézout combination. Extended Euclidean:$x^2-1 = 1 \cdot (x^2+x) + (-x-1)$, so$x^2-1 - (x^2+x) = -x-1 = -(x+1)$. Thus$x+1 = (x^2+x) - (x^2-1) = 1 \cdot (x^2+x) + (-1)(x^2-1)$.

**Example 3.** In $\mathbb{Z}[i]$: compute$(3+4i) + (4+3i)$ as ideals.$\gcd(3+4i, 4+3i)$: norms are$N(3+4i) = 25 = N(4+3i)$. Difference:$(3+4i) - (4+3i) = -1+i$,$N(-1+i) = 2$. So$\gcd(3+4i, 4+3i)$ has norm dividing$\gcd(25, 25, 2) = 1$, meaning$\gcd = 1$. Thus$(3+4i) + (4+3i) = (1) = \mathbb{Z}[i]$. Bézout: find$u, v \in \mathbb{Z}[i]$ with$u(3+4i) + v(4+3i) = 1$. One can find these via the Euclidean algorithm in$\mathbb{Z}[i]$.

**Example 4.** Coprimality and the Chinese Remainder Theorem: if $\gcd(a, b) = 1$ in a PID$R$, then$(a) + (b) = (1) = R$, so$(a)$ and$(b)$ are comaximal ideals. The Chinese Remainder Theorem then gives$R/(ab) \cong R/(a) \times R/(b)$. In$\mathbb{Z}$:$\gcd(6, 35) = 1$, so$\mathbb{Z}/210 \cong \mathbb{Z}/6 \times \mathbb{Z}/35 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2 \times \mathbb{Z}/3 \times \mathbb{Z}/5 \times \mathbb{Z}/7$. This decomposition is entirely a consequence of the ideal structure of$\mathbb{Z}$ as a PID.

**Example 5.** The ideal $(n) \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}$: the ideals of$\mathbb{Z}$ containing$(n)$ are exactly$(d)$ for$d \mid n$, and by the correspondence theorem, the ideals of$\mathbb{Z}/(n)$ correspond bijectively to the divisors of$n$. For$n = 12 = 2^2 \cdot 3$: the divisors are$1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$, giving 6 ideals in$\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$. These are$(1) = \mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$,$(2) = \{0,2,4,6,8,10\}$,$(3) = \{0,3,6,9\}$,$(4) = \{0,4,8\}$,$(6) = \{0,6\}$,$(12) = \{0\}$. The GCD formula confirms these form the lattice of divisors of 12 ordered by divisibility.
