# 22.3.4 Examples: $\mathbb{Z}$,$F[x]$,$\mathbb{Z}[i]$ (Gaussian Integers)

The abstract theory of PIDs is grounded in three important families of examples, each with a distinct flavor and its own applications. Together they illustrate the range of phenomena that the PID axiom captures, from the classical arithmetic of integers through polynomial algebra to the geometry of the Gaussian plane.

The integers $\mathbb{Z}$ are the foundational PID. Every ideal is$n\mathbb{Z}$ for a unique$n \geq 0$, the nonzero prime ideals are$(p)$ for primes$p$, and the maximal ideals are exactly the prime ideals. The theory of$\mathbb{Z}$ as a PID is the algebraic content of elementary number theory: divisibility, the division algorithm, Bézout's identity, and the Chinese Remainder Theorem. The proof that$\mathbb{Z}$ is a PID uses the minimum-positive-integer argument: given any nonzero ideal$I$, let$d$ be the smallest positive integer in$I$; then for any$a \in I$, write$a = dq + r$ with$0 \leq r < d$; since$r = a - dq \in I$ and$r < d$, minimality forces$r = 0$, so$a \in (d)$.

The polynomial ring $F[x]$ over a field$F$ is a PID for any field$F$. The division algorithm for polynomials (dividing by the leading term, which is always a unit over a field) shows that every ideal is generated by the unique monic polynomial of smallest degree in that ideal. This makes$F[x]$ behave like$\mathbb{Z}$ with the degree of a polynomial playing the role of the absolute value of an integer. The analogy is more than superficial: both$\mathbb{Z}$ and$F[x]$ are Euclidean domains with the respective norms$|n|$ and$\deg(f)$, and both are PIDs by the same argument (take the element of minimum norm in any nonzero ideal).

The Gaussian integers $\mathbb{Z}[i] = \{a + bi : a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\} \subset \mathbb{C}$ form a PID, as we will verify in §22.4 by exhibiting them as a Euclidean domain. The key ingredient is the norm$N(a + bi) = a^2 + b^2$, which is multiplicative and satisfies$N(\alpha) = 1$ iff$\alpha$ is a unit ($\alpha \in \{\pm 1, \pm i\}$). The Gaussian integers have a beautiful application to classical number theory: an ordinary prime$p$ remains prime in$\mathbb{Z}[i]$ if and only if$p \equiv 3 \pmod{4}$, and factors as$p = \pi\bar\pi$ for a Gaussian prime$\pi$ if$p \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$ or$p = 2$. Since$p = (a+bi)(a-bi) = a^2 + b^2$, this gives the classical theorem that a prime$p \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$ is a sum of two integer squares.

**Example 1.** Bézout in $\mathbb{Z}$:$\gcd(56, 15) = 1$, and$1 = 56 \cdot 4 + 15 \cdot (-15)$? Check:$56 \cdot 4 = 224$ and$15 \cdot (-15) = -225$, giving$224 - 225 = -1 \neq 1$. Let us redo:$56 = 3 \cdot 15 + 11$,$15 = 1 \cdot 11 + 4$,$11 = 2 \cdot 4 + 3$,$4 = 1 \cdot 3 + 1$,$3 = 3 \cdot 1$. Back-substitute:$1 = 4 - 1 \cdot 3 = 4 - (11 - 2 \cdot 4) = 3 \cdot 4 - 11 = 3(15 - 11) - 11 = 3 \cdot 15 - 4 \cdot 11 = 3 \cdot 15 - 4(56 - 3 \cdot 15) = 15 \cdot 15 - 4 \cdot 56$. So$1 = (-4) \cdot 56 + 15 \cdot 15$.

**Example 2.** In $\mathbb{Q}[x]$: find$\gcd(x^3-1, x^4-1)$ and Bézout coefficients.$x^4-1 = (x+1)(x^3-1) + (x-1)$, so$x^4 - 1 - (x+1)(x^3-1) = x-1$. Then$x^3-1 = (x^2+x+1)(x-1)$, so$x-1 \mid x^3-1$. Thus$\gcd(x^3-1, x^4-1) = x-1$. Bézout:$x-1 = 1 \cdot (x^4-1) + (-(x+1))(x^3-1)$.

**Example 3.** The Gaussian primes: which rational primes $p$ remain prime in$\mathbb{Z}[i]$? 
- $p = 2$:$2 = -i(1+i)^2$, so$2$ splits (and even ramifies). The prime$2$ becomes$(1+i)^2$ up to a unit.
- $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$: by Fermat's theorem on sums of two squares,$p = a^2 + b^2 = (a+bi)(a-bi)$, with$a + bi$ a Gaussian prime of norm$p$. So$p$ splits.
- $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$:$p$ remains prime in$\mathbb{Z}[i]$. If$p = \alpha\beta$ with$N(\alpha) N(\beta) = p^2$, then$N(\alpha) = p$ or$N(\alpha) = 1$. But$N(\alpha) = p$ means$a^2 + b^2 = p$, which is impossible for$p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ (a sum of two squares is$\equiv 0, 1, 2 \pmod 4$, never$3$). So no such$\alpha$ exists, and$p$ is prime.

**Example 4.** The application to sums of two squares: $p = 5 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$. Find Gaussian prime factor:$5 = 2^2 + 1^2 = (2+i)(2-i)$. So$5$ splits into$(2+i)(2-i)$ in$\mathbb{Z}[i]$, and$5$ is a sum of two squares. For$p = 13 = 4 + 9 = (2+3i)(2-3i)$, also$\equiv 1 \pmod 4$. For$p = 7 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$:$7$ is a Gaussian prime, and$7 \neq a^2 + b^2$ for any integers$a, b$ (check:$7 - 0 = 7$ not a perfect square;$7 - 1 = 6$ not a square;$7 - 4 = 3$ not a square; done).

**Example 5.** The ideal structure of $\mathbb{Z}[i]$: the prime ideals are$(0)$,$(1+i)$ (above$2$),$(a+bi)$ for$a + bi$ a Gaussian prime above$p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, and$(p)$ for$p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$. The quotient$\mathbb{Z}[i]/(p)$ for$p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$: since$(p)$ is prime (as$p$ is a Gaussian prime),$\mathbb{Z}[i]/(p) \cong \mathbb{F}_{p^2}$ (a field with$p^2$ elements, since$\mathbb{Z}[i]/(p) \cong \mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^2+1, p) \cong \mathbb{F}_p[x]/(x^2+1)$, and$x^2+1$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{F}_p$ when$p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$). For$p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$:$(a+bi)$ is a maximal ideal with$\mathbb{Z}[i]/(a+bi) \cong \mathbb{F}_p$.
