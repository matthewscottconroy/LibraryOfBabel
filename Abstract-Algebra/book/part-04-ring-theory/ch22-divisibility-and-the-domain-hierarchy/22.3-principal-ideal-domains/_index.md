# 22.3 Principal Ideal Domains

A principal ideal domain (PID) is an integral domain in which every ideal is principal, meaning generated by a single element. The simplest examples are $\mathbb{Z}$ and the polynomial ring $F[x]$ over any field $F$, both of which admit a Euclidean algorithm. PIDs are a strictly stronger condition than being a UFD: every PID is a UFD, but $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is a UFD that fails to be a PID. The key step in proving that PIDs are UFDs invokes the ascending chain condition: in a PID, any ascending chain of ideals $(a_1) \subseteq (a_2) \subseteq \cdots$ corresponds to a chain of divisibility relations $a_1 \mid a_2 \mid \cdots$, and since the union of a chain of ideals is an ideal, hence principal $(a) = \bigcup (a_i)$, the chain eventually reaches $(a_i) = (a)$.

The ideal structure of a PID is especially transparent. The prime ideals are exactly the ideals generated by irreducible elements (since irreducible equals prime in a PID) together with the zero ideal. Moreover, irreducible elements generate maximal ideals: since $(p)$ is prime and there are no ideals properly between $(p)$ and $(1) = R$ in a PID (any such ideal $(a)$ would give $p = ab$ with $a \nmid p$ and $b \nmid p$, contradicting irreducibility), $(p)$ is maximal. This means that in a PID, the prime ideals other than $(0)$ are exactly the maximal ideals, a remarkable simplification of the ideal structure.

The Gaussian integers $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ are the paradigmatic example of a PID that is not $\mathbb{Z}$ or $F[x]$. They admit a Euclidean norm $N(a+bi) = a^2 + b^2$ that satisfies $N(\alpha\beta) = N(\alpha)N(\beta)$ and permits division with remainder — the complex plane allows one to round any Gaussian rational to the nearest Gaussian integer with remainder of norm less than the divisor. This makes $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ a Euclidean domain, hence a PID, and the unique factorization in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is the algebraic engine behind the theorem that a prime $p \in \mathbb{Z}$ is a sum of two squares if and only if $p = 2$ or $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$.
