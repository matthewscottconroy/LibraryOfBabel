# 22.4.1 Definition: Euclidean Norms and the Division Algorithm

The highest tier of the domain hierarchy below fields consists of the Euclidean domains. Their defining property — the existence of a division algorithm — makes them the most computationally accessible class of domains and provides the richest structure for explicit arithmetic. Everything that can be done in $\mathbb{Z}$ by hand — the Euclidean algorithm, Bézout's identity, explicit GCD computations — generalizes immediately to any Euclidean domain.

An integral domain $R$ is a *Euclidean domain* if there exists a function$N: R \setminus \{0\} \to \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$, called a *Euclidean norm* (or *Euclidean function*), such that for any$a \in R$ and any nonzero$b \in R$, there exist elements$q, r \in R$ with$a = bq + r$ and either$r = 0$ or$N(r) < N(b)$. The elements$q$ and$r$ are called the *quotient* and *remainder*, respectively.

The division algorithm is the heart of the definition: it says we can always divide $a$ by$b$, obtaining a quotient and a remainder that is either zero or strictly smaller than$b$ as measured by the norm. The crucial point is that the norm decreases with each division step — this is what guarantees the Euclidean algorithm terminates. Note that the quotient and remainder need not be unique; what matters is their existence.

The three most important Euclidean domains are:
- $\mathbb{Z}$ with$N(n) = |n|$: the standard absolute value gives$a = bq + r$ with$0 \leq r < |b|$.
- $F[x]$ with$N(f) = \deg(f)$: polynomial division gives$a = bq + r$ with$\deg r < \deg b$ or$r = 0$.
- $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ with$N(a + bi) = a^2 + b^2$: one uses the geometry of the Gaussian integers as a lattice in$\mathbb{C}$ to verify division with remainder.

For $\mathbb{Z}[i]$, the division algorithm works as follows. Given$\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{Z}[i]$ with$\beta \neq 0$, compute$\alpha/\beta = \alpha\bar\beta/N(\beta) = x + yi$ for some$x, y \in \mathbb{Q}$. Choose integers$m, n$ with$|x - m| \leq 1/2$ and$|y - n| \leq 1/2$, and set$q = m + ni$. Then$r = \alpha - q\beta$, and$N(r) = N(\alpha - q\beta) = N(\beta) \cdot N(\alpha/\beta - q) = N(\beta) \cdot ((x-m)^2 + (y-n)^2) \leq N(\beta) \cdot (1/4 + 1/4) = N(\beta)/2 < N(\beta)$.

The Euclidean norm itself need not be unique: different norms on the same domain may each witness its Euclidean property. And not every "natural" choice of norm is Euclidean: there exist domains where the division algorithm can be performed with some norm, but the most obvious candidate fails.

**Example 1.** Division in $\mathbb{Z}$: divide$a = 37$ by$b = 7$.$37 = 7 \cdot 5 + 2$, so$q = 5$,$r = 2$, and$N(2) = 2 < 7 = N(7)$. Or$37 = 7 \cdot 6 - 5$, giving$r = -5$ with$N(-5) = 5 < 7$. Both are valid (the remainder need not be unique for the definition, though for the standard Euclidean algorithm we use$0 \leq r < b$).

**Example 2.** Division in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$: divide$f = 3x^3 + 2x - 1$ by$g = x^2 + 1$.$3x^3 \div x^2 = 3x$;$3x \cdot (x^2+1) = 3x^3 + 3x$;$f - 3x \cdot g = (3x^3 + 2x - 1) - (3x^3 + 3x) = -x - 1$. So$f = (3x) \cdot (x^2+1) + (-x-1)$, with$q = 3x$,$r = -x-1$, and$\deg(r) = 1 < 2 = \deg(g)$.

**Example 3.** Division in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$: divide$\alpha = 11 + 3i$ by$\beta = 2 + i$. Compute$\alpha/\beta = (11+3i)(2-i)/N(2+i) = (22 - 11i + 6i - 3i^2)/5 = (22 + 3 - 5i)/5 = (25 - 5i)/5 = 5 - i$. This is already a Gaussian integer! So$q = 5 - i$ and$r = 0$:$(11+3i) = (5-i)(2+i) = 10 + 5i - 2i - i^2 = 10 + 3i + 1 = 11 + 3i$. Division is exact.

**Example 4.** Division in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ with nonzero remainder: divide$\alpha = 7 + 2i$ by$\beta = 3 + i$.$\alpha/\beta = (7+2i)(3-i)/N(3+i) = (21 - 7i + 6i - 2i^2)/10 = (23 - i)/10 = 2.3 - 0.1i$. Round to nearest Gaussian integer:$q = 2 + 0i = 2$. Then$r = (7+2i) - 2(3+i) = 7+2i - 6 - 2i = 1$. So$7 + 2i = 2 \cdot (3+i) + 1$, with$N(1) = 1 < 10 = N(3+i)$.

**Example 5.** The Eisenstein integers $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$ where$\omega = e^{2\pi i/3} = (-1+\sqrt{-3})/2$ are also a Euclidean domain with norm$N(a + b\omega) = a^2 - ab + b^2$. Division works because the Eisenstein integers tile the plane with regular hexagons, and the nearest lattice point to any complex number is always within distance$1/\sqrt{3} < 1$ (adjusting for the norm). This gives a division algorithm, making$\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$ Euclidean. As the ring of integers of$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})$, the Eisenstein integers arise naturally in Fermat's Last Theorem for the exponent$3$.
