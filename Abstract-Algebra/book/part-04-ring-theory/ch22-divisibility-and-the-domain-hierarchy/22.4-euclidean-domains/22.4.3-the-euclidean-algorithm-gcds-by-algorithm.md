# 22.4.3 The Euclidean Algorithm; GCDs by Algorithm

The division algorithm defines Euclidean domains, but its real power emerges when applied repeatedly. The Euclidean algorithm — iterating the division algorithm — provides an effective computational procedure for finding GCDs and Bézout coefficients in any Euclidean domain. This algorithm is one of the oldest in mathematics (attributed to Euclid, ca. 300 BCE) and remains computationally efficient (polynomial time) in all known implementations.

Given nonzero elements $a$ and$b$ in a Euclidean domain$R$, the algorithm proceeds as follows. Divide$a$ by$b$:$a = b q_1 + r_1$. If$r_1 = 0$, then$b \mid a$ and$\gcd(a, b) = b$ (up to a unit). If$r_1 \neq 0$, divide$b$ by$r_1$:$b = r_1 q_2 + r_2$. Continue, dividing each remainder by the next:$r_{k-1} = r_k q_{k+1} + r_{k+1}$. The algorithm terminates because$N(r_1) > N(r_2) > \cdots \geq 0$ is a strictly decreasing sequence in$\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$, so it must reach$0$ in finitely many steps. The last nonzero remainder$r_k$ is a GCD of$a$ and$b$.

To see why, observe that $\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(b, r_1)$ because any common divisor of$a$ and$b$ also divides$r_1 = a - bq_1$, and vice versa. The same reasoning gives$\gcd(b, r_1) = \gcd(r_1, r_2) = \cdots = \gcd(r_{k-1}, r_k) = r_k$.

Bézout's identity $\gcd(a,b) = ua + vb$ is obtained by back-substitution: express$r_k$ in terms of$r_{k-1}$ and$r_{k-2}$, then substitute recursively until$a$ and$b$ appear. The extended Euclidean algorithm performs this back-substitution systematically.

**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{Z}$:$\gcd(35, 13)$.
- $35 = 2 \cdot 13 + 9$
- $13 = 1 \cdot 9 + 4$
- $9 = 2 \cdot 4 + 1$
- $4 = 4 \cdot 1$

Last nonzero remainder: $1$. So$\gcd(35, 13) = 1$. Back-substitution for Bézout:
\begin{align*}
1 &= 9 - 2 \cdot 4 \\
&= 9 - 2(13 - 9) = 3 \cdot 9 - 2 \cdot 13 \\
&= 3(35 - 2 \cdot 13) - 2 \cdot 13 = 3 \cdot 35 - 8 \cdot 13.
\end{align*}
Check: $3 \cdot 35 - 8 \cdot 13 = 105 - 104 = 1$. So$u = 3$,$v = -8$.

**Example 2.** In $\mathbb{Q}[x]$:$\gcd(x^3 - x, x^2 - 1)$.
- $x^3 - x = x \cdot (x^2 - 1) + 0$

Last nonzero remainder: $x^2 - 1$ divides$x^3 - x$ exactly (since$x^3 - x = x(x^2-1)$). So$\gcd(x^3-x, x^2-1) = x^2 - 1$. Bézout:$x^2 - 1 = 0 \cdot (x^3-x) + 1 \cdot (x^2-1)$, giving$u = 0$,$v = 1$.

**Example 3.** In $\mathbb{Q}[x]$:$\gcd(x^4 - 1, x^3 + x^2 - x - 1)$.
- $x^4 - 1 = x \cdot (x^3 + x^2 - x - 1) + (-x^3 + x^2 + x - 1) \cdot ...$

Let me compute more carefully. $x^4 - 1 - x(x^3 + x^2 - x - 1) = x^4 - 1 - x^4 - x^3 + x^2 + x = -x^3 + x^2 + x - 1$. So first remainder is$r_1 = -x^3 + x^2 + x - 1 = -(x^3 - x^2 - x + 1) = -(x-1)^2(x+1)$.

Note $x^3 + x^2 - x - 1 = x^2(x+1) - (x+1) = (x+1)(x^2-1) = (x+1)^2(x-1)$.

So $\gcd(x^4-1, x^3+x^2-x-1) = \gcd((x-1)(x+1)(x^2+1), (x+1)^2(x-1)) = (x+1)(x-1) = x^2-1$.

**Example 4.** In $\mathbb{Z}[i]$:$\gcd(11 + 3i, 1 + 8i)$.
$N(11+3i) = 121 + 9 = 130$,$N(1+8i) = 1 + 64 = 65$.

Divide: $(11+3i)/(1+8i) = (11+3i)(1-8i)/65 = (11 - 88i + 3i - 24i^2)/65 = (11 + 24 - 85i)/65 = (35 - 85i)/65 = (7 - 17i)/13 \approx 0.54 - 1.31i$.

Round to $q = 1 - i$ (nearest Gaussian integer).$r = (11+3i) - (1-i)(1+8i) = 11+3i - (1 + 8i - i - 8i^2) = 11+3i - (1 + 7i + 8) = 11+3i - 9 - 7i = 2 - 4i$.

$N(2-4i) = 4 + 16 = 20 < 65$. Continue: divide$(1+8i)$ by$(2-4i)$:
$(1+8i)/(2-4i) = (1+8i)(2+4i)/20 = (2 + 4i + 16i + 32i^2)/20 = (2-32+20i)/20 = (-30+20i)/20 = -3/2 + i$.

Round to $q = -1 + i$.$r = (1+8i) - (-1+i)(2-4i) = 1+8i - (-2+4i+2i-4i^2) = 1+8i - (-2 + 6i + 4) = 1+8i - 2 - 6i = -1+2i$.

$N(-1+2i) = 1 + 4 = 5 < 20$. Continue: divide$(2-4i)$ by$(-1+2i)$:
$(2-4i)/(-1+2i) = (2-4i)(-1-2i)/5 = (-2-4i+4i+8i^2)/5 = (-2-8)/5 = -10/5 = -2$. Exact!

So $r = 0$ and$\gcd(11+3i, 1+8i) = -1+2i$ (up to units; the associate$1-2i$ is the "monic" representative in some sense).

**Example 5.** The efficiency of the Euclidean algorithm: in $\mathbb{Z}$, the number of steps needed to compute$\gcd(a, b)$ with$a > b > 0$ is at most$2\log_\phi(b)$ where$\phi = (1+\sqrt{5})/2$ is the golden ratio. The worst case is consecutive Fibonacci numbers:$\gcd(F_{n+1}, F_n)$ requires exactly$n-1$ divisions. This is the slowest the algorithm can be, and it is still logarithmic in$b$ — much faster than trial division.
