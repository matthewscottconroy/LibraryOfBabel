# 22.4.4 The Hierarchy: $\text{Fields} \subset \text{ED} \subset \text{PID} \subset \text{UFD} \subset \text{Domain}$

The results of this chapter can now be assembled into a coherent picture: the domain hierarchy. Each level has a defining property that implies the level below it, and the inclusions are all strict — no two levels collapse into one. This hierarchy is one of the central organizing structures of algebra, and knowing where a given ring sits within it immediately reveals what kind of arithmetic is available.

At the top sit the *fields*: every nonzero element is a unit. Fields are trivially Euclidean (set $N(a) = 1$ for all$a \neq 0$ — the division algorithm becomes$a = a \cdot 1 + 0$, with zero remainder), trivially PIDs (their only ideals are$(0)$ and$(1) = R$), and trivially UFDs (there are no nonzero non-units to factorize, so the conditions are vacuously satisfied).

*Euclidean domains* carry a division algorithm (§22.4.1), and every Euclidean domain is a PID (§22.4.2). Examples: $\mathbb{Z}$,$F[x]$ for any field$F$,$\mathbb{Z}[i]$,$\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$ (Eisenstein integers). *Principal ideal domains* have all ideals principal (§22.3.1), and every PID is a UFD (§22.3.2). Examples:$\mathbb{Z}$,$F[x]$,$\mathbb{Z}[i]$, and also$\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1+\sqrt{-19}}{2}]$ (which is a PID but not Euclidean). *Unique factorization domains* admit unique factorization into irreducibles (§22.2.1). Examples:$\mathbb{Z}$,$F[x]$,$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ (by Gauss's theorem),$k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ for any field$k$. All UFDs are integral domains by definition.

Each inclusion is strict. The ring $\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1+\sqrt{-19}}{2}\right]$ is a PID but not Euclidean. The ring$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is a UFD but not a PID: the ideal$(2, x)$ is not principal (since$\gcd(2, x) = 1$ in$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ but$(2, x) \neq (1)$, as$2$ is not a unit; and no single polynomial generates an ideal containing both$2$ and$x$). The ring$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is a domain but not a UFD: the element$6$ has two distinct factorizations into irreducibles.

The hierarchy organizes the arithmetic of integral domains from the most structured and computationally explicit (Euclidean domains, where algorithms work) to the most general (domains, where they may not). At each level, additional structure brings additional computational power: in a field, every linear system has a solution; in a Euclidean domain, the Euclidean algorithm runs; in a PID, GCDs are Bézout sums; in a UFD, GCDs exist by the exponent formula.

**Example 1.** Where do the rings of integers of quadratic fields sit? For $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})$, the ring of integers$\mathcal{O}_K$ is always a Dedekind domain (§22.5.2), but its position in the hierarchy depends on the class number$h_K$.$\mathcal{O}_K$ is a PID iff$h_K = 1$; it is a UFD iff it is a PID (for Dedekind domains, PID and UFD coincide); and it is Euclidean iff a Euclidean norm exists. The imaginary quadratic fields$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ with$d < 0$ for which$\mathcal{O}_K$ is a UFD are exactly those with$d \in \{-1, -2, -3, -7, -11, -19, -43, -67, -163\}$ (Heegner's theorem).

**Example 2.** $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is a UFD by Gauss's theorem, but not a PID. This is a good example of a UFD without the PID property. The ideal$(2, x)$ in$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ witnesses the non-principality:$\gcd_{\mathbb{Z}[x]}(2, x) = 1$ (the elements$2$ and$x$ have no common nontrivial factor in$\mathbb{Z}[x]$), but$(2, x) \neq (1)$ (the ideal of polynomials with even constant term is a proper ideal). In a PID, coprime elements would generate the unit ideal. The failure here is a PID failure, not a UFD failure.

**Example 3.** $F[x_1, x_2]$ for a field$F$ is a UFD (Gauss's theorem applied twice) but not a PID:$(x_1, x_2)$ is not principal. It is also not Euclidean: there is no sensible "degree" function for multivariable polynomials that supports a division algorithm giving a unique remainder.

**Example 4.** $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{2}]$: this is the ring of integers of$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$, a real quadratic field with class number$1$. It is a PID and in fact Euclidean, with norm$N(a + b\sqrt{2}) = |a^2 - 2b^2|$. The units are$\pm(1+\sqrt{2})^n$ for$n \in \mathbb{Z}$ — infinitely many! This contrasts with the imaginary quadratic cases, where the unit group is always finite.

**Example 5.** The failure hierarchy: to show a domain is not a UFD, exhibit two distinct factorizations into irreducibles ($\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$:$6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$). To show a UFD is not a PID, exhibit a non-principal ideal ($\mathbb{Z}[x]$:$(2, x)$). To show a PID is not Euclidean, prove no Euclidean norm exists (requires non-trivial argument for$\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1+\sqrt{-19}}{2}]$). To show an Euclidean domain is not a field, exhibit a non-unit ($\mathbb{Z}$:$2$ is not a unit). Each level requires a different type of counterexample.

A central theme of algebraic number theory is determining where a given ring of integers $\mathcal{O}_K$ sits in this hierarchy — a question that connects to deep arithmetic invariants such as the class number, which we discuss in §22.5.
