# 22.5.1 $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$: A Domain That Is Not a UFD

The failure of unique factorization is not a pathological exception confined to artificial examples — it is a phenomenon that arises naturally in the rings of integers of number fields, and it drove the development of ideal theory in the nineteenth century. The ring $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ provides the simplest and most instructive example.

In $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}] = \{a + b\sqrt{-5} : a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\}$, consider the element$6$. We have two factorizations:$6 = 2 \cdot 3$ and$6 = (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5})$. To show these are genuinely different — not merely reorderings of the same factors up to units — we must show that$2, 3, 1 + \sqrt{-5}$, and$1 - \sqrt{-5}$ are all irreducible and that no two of them are associates.

The norm $N(a + b\sqrt{-5}) = a^2 + 5b^2$ is multiplicative:$N(\alpha\beta) = N(\alpha)N(\beta)$. The units of$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ are exactly the elements of norm$1$, namely$\pm 1$ (since$a^2 + 5b^2 = 1$ forces$b = 0$ and$a = \pm 1$). To check that$2$ is irreducible: if$2 = \alpha\beta$, then$N(2) = 4 = N(\alpha)N(\beta)$. The value$2$ is not in the range of the norm (since$a^2 + 5b^2 = 2$ has no integer solution:$b = 0$ gives$a^2 = 2$, impossible;$|b| \geq 1$ gives$a^2 + 5 \geq 5 > 2$). So$N(\alpha) = 1$ or$N(\beta) = 1$, meaning one of them is a unit, and$2$ is irreducible.

Similarly: $N(3) = 9$, and$a^2 + 5b^2 = 3$ has no integer solution ($b = 0$:$a^2 = 3$, impossible;$|b| \geq 1$:$a^2 + 5 \geq 5 > 3$). So$3$ is irreducible. For$1 \pm \sqrt{-5}$:$N(1 + \sqrt{-5}) = 1 + 5 = 6$. If$1 + \sqrt{-5} = \alpha\beta$ then$N(\alpha)N(\beta) = 6$. The possible factorizations$6 = 1 \cdot 6 = 2 \cdot 3$ would require an element of norm$2$ or norm$3$, but neither is achievable. So$1 \pm \sqrt{-5}$ are irreducible.

Since $2$ and$3$ are real while$1 \pm \sqrt{-5}$ are not real, none of them can be associates (associates differ only by a unit$\pm 1$, so their norms are equal, but$N(2) = 4 \neq 6 = N(1+\sqrt{-5})$ and$N(3) = 9 \neq 6$). The four elements are pairwise non-associate irreducibles, and$6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ gives two genuinely distinct factorizations of$6$ into irreducibles, confirming that$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is not a UFD.

**Example 1.** The same phenomenon appears in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-6}]$: consider$10 = 2 \cdot 5 = (2 + \sqrt{-6})(2 - \sqrt{-6})$. Norm:$N(2 \pm \sqrt{-6}) = 4 + 6 = 10$. Elements of norm$2$:$a^2 + 6b^2 = 2$ has no integer solution. Elements of norm$5$:$a^2 + 6b^2 = 5$ has no solution ($b=0$:$a^2 = 5$, no;$|b| \geq 1$:$a^2 + 6 \geq 6 > 5$). So$2, 5, 2\pm\sqrt{-6}$ are all irreducible, and$10$ has two factorizations.

**Example 2.** The relationship between non-UFD and non-prime irreducibles: in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, the irreducible$2$ is not prime. Proof:$2 \mid 6 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$. But$2 \nmid (1+\sqrt{-5})$: if$(1+\sqrt{-5}) = 2(a+b\sqrt{-5})$ then$2a = 1$, impossible in$\mathbb{Z}$. Similarly$2 \nmid (1-\sqrt{-5})$. So$2$ is an irreducible non-prime, and this is precisely what makes the ring non-UFD.

**Example 3.** A more striking example: in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, the element$21 = 3 \cdot 7 = (1+2\sqrt{-5})(1-2\sqrt{-5})$. Check:$(1 \pm 2\sqrt{-5})$ has norm$1 + 4 \cdot 5 = 21$. An element of norm$3$ would satisfy$a^2 + 5b^2 = 3$: impossible. An element of norm$7$:$a^2 + 5b^2 = 7$:$b = 0 \Rightarrow a^2 = 7$ (no);$b = \pm 1 \Rightarrow a^2 = 2$ (no). So$3, 7, 1\pm 2\sqrt{-5}$ are all irreducible, and$21$ has two factorizations.

**Example 4.** Historical context: Kummer in the 1840s attempted to prove Fermat's Last Theorem using the ring $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]$ (where$\zeta_p$ is a primitive$p$-th root of unity). His initial arguments assumed unique factorization in$\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]$, which fails for$p \geq 23$. Recognizing this failure, Kummer introduced "ideal numbers" — precursors to Dedekind's ideals — to restore unique factorization at the ideal level. This historical episode was one of the main drivers of the development of ideal theory.

**Example 5.** Norm forms and quadratic fields: the ring $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{d}]$ (or more generally$\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})}$) fails to be a UFD precisely when the class number$h > 1$. For$d = -5$:$h = 2$, and the failure of unique factorization is minimal — there are two "classes" of fractional ideals, corresponding to the two factorizations of$6$. For$d = -23$:$h = 3$, and there are even more exotic non-unique factorizations. The class number thus measures the "size of the failure" of unique factorization in a precise group-theoretic sense.
