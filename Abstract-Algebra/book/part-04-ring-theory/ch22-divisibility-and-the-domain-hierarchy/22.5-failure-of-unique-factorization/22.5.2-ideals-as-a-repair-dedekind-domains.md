# 22.5.2 Ideals as a Repair: Dedekind Domains

The failure of unique factorization in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ suggested to Kummer and Dedekind that elements themselves might be the wrong objects to factorize. The correct objects, it turns out, are ideals. In a suitably well-behaved class of rings — the *Dedekind domains* — unique factorization holds for ideals even when it fails for elements. This is the ideal-theoretic repair of non-unique factorization.

A *Dedekind domain* is an integral domain satisfying three conditions: it is Noetherian (every ideal is finitely generated), it is integrally closed in its field of fractions (every element of the fraction field satisfying a monic polynomial with coefficients in $R$ already lies in$R$), and it has Krull dimension$1$ (every nonzero prime ideal is maximal). This may seem like an abstract definition, but the key examples are the rings of integers$\mathcal{O}_K$ of number fields$K$, and these are the rings that originally motivated the theory.

In a Dedekind domain, every nonzero ideal factors uniquely as a product of prime ideals. Let us see how this repairs the failure in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$. Although$6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ gives two element factorizations, the principal ideal$(6)$ has a unique prime ideal factorization:$(6) = \mathfrak{p}_2^2 \cdot \mathfrak{p}_3 \cdot \mathfrak{p}_3'$, where$\mathfrak{p}_2 = (2, 1+\sqrt{-5})$,$\mathfrak{p}_3 = (3, 1+\sqrt{-5})$, and$\mathfrak{p}_3' = (3, 1-\sqrt{-5})$ are prime ideals. The ideals$(2) = \mathfrak{p}_2^2$ and$(3) = \mathfrak{p}_3 \mathfrak{p}_3'$ split into products of prime ideals, and the two element factorizations of$6$ correspond to two ways of grouping the prime ideal factors — but the underlying prime ideal factorization is unique.

One can verify that $\mathfrak{p}_2$ and$\mathfrak{p}_3$ are genuinely non-principal (they are not of the form$(\alpha)$ for any$\alpha \in \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$), which is exactly why the element factorization breaks down. The factorization$6 = 2 \cdot 3$ corresponds to grouping as$(2)(3) = \mathfrak{p}_2^2 \cdot \mathfrak{p}_3\mathfrak{p}_3'$, while$6 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ corresponds to grouping as$(\mathfrak{p}_2 \mathfrak{p}_3)(\mathfrak{p}_2 \mathfrak{p}_3') = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$. The two groupings are different but the underlying prime ideal factorization$\mathfrak{p}_2^2 \mathfrak{p}_3 \mathfrak{p}_3'$ is unique.

The analogy with integers is precise: just as $\mathbb{Z}$ has unique factorization of elements, Dedekind domains have unique factorization of ideals. The failure at the element level is "repaired" by replacing elements with the ideals they generate. Dedekind's insight was that the "ideal numbers" of Kummer — the objects that restore unique factorization — are precisely the ideals of the ring.

**Example 1.** Verification that $\mathfrak{p}_2 = (2, 1+\sqrt{-5})$ is a prime ideal of$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$. The quotient$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]/\mathfrak{p}_2$: modding out by$2$ gives$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]/(2) \cong \mathbb{F}_2[\sqrt{-5}] \cong \mathbb{F}_2[x]/(x^2+5) \cong \mathbb{F}_2[x]/(x^2+1) \cong \mathbb{F}_2[x]/((x+1)^2)$. Further modding out by$1+\sqrt{-5}$ sets$x = 1$ in$\mathbb{F}_2[x]/(x+1)^2$, giving$\mathbb{F}_2$. So$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]/\mathfrak{p}_2 \cong \mathbb{F}_2$, a field, confirming$\mathfrak{p}_2$ is maximal (hence prime).

**Example 2.** The factorization $(2) = \mathfrak{p}_2^2$: compute$\mathfrak{p}_2^2 = (2, 1+\sqrt{-5})^2 = (4, 2(1+\sqrt{-5}), (1+\sqrt{-5})^2)$. Now$(1+\sqrt{-5})^2 = 1 + 2\sqrt{-5} - 5 = -4 + 2\sqrt{-5}$. So$\mathfrak{p}_2^2$ contains$4$,$2+2\sqrt{-5}$, and$-4 + 2\sqrt{-5}$. Then$(2+2\sqrt{-5}) - (2)(-4+2\sqrt{-5})/...$: notice$4 \in \mathfrak{p}_2^2$ and$-4+2\sqrt{-5} \in \mathfrak{p}_2^2$, so$2\sqrt{-5} = (-4+2\sqrt{-5}) + 4 \in \mathfrak{p}_2^2$. Also$2+2\sqrt{-5} \in \mathfrak{p}_2^2$, so$(2+2\sqrt{-5}) - 2\sqrt{-5} = 2 \in \mathfrak{p}_2^2$. And$2\sqrt{-5}/...$ Hmm, let's just note:$2 \in \mathfrak{p}_2^2$ (since$4 - 2 \cdot 2 + 2\sqrt{-5}(-1) + ...$). One verifies directly that$\mathfrak{p}_2^2 = (2)$.

**Example 3.** The three conditions for Dedekind domains and why they are all needed: (a) Noetherian ensures ideals are finitely generated, giving the finiteness needed for the factorization theorem. (b) Integrally closed ensures the ring has "no missing algebraic integers," which is needed for the prime ideal decomposition. Without it, a ring like $\mathbb{Z}[2i]$ (which is not integrally closed, since$i = (2i)/2$ has norm formula$i^2 = -1$, a monic polynomial, but$i \notin \mathbb{Z}[2i]$) can have non-unique ideal factorization. (c) Dimension 1 (every nonzero prime maximal) ensures the prime ideal structure is simple enough for the factorization theorem to hold.

**Example 4.** Every PID is a Dedekind domain: a PID is Noetherian (by the ACC, §22.3.2), integrally closed (a PID is a UFD, and every UFD is integrally closed: if $a/b$ in lowest terms satisfies a monic polynomial over$R$, then substituting and multiplying by$b^n$ shows$b \mid a$, so$b$ is a unit), and has Krull dimension$\leq 1$ (every nonzero prime is maximal, as shown in §22.3.1). In a PID, moreover, every ideal is principal, so the unique factorization of ideals reduces to the unique factorization of elements. The Dedekind domain picture is thus a genuine generalization.

**Example 5.** Smooth affine curves: if $C$ is a smooth irreducible affine curve over a field$k$, its coordinate ring$k[C]$ is a Dedekind domain. The prime ideals (other than$(0)$) correspond to points of$C$, the "closed points." Unique factorization of ideals corresponds to the fact that divisors on a smooth curve are just integer-valued functions on the set of points — the divisor group is the free abelian group on the set of closed points. The Dedekind domain axioms are precisely the conditions on$k[C]$ that guarantee this clean point-divisor correspondence.
