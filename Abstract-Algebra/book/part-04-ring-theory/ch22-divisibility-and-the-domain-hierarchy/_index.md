# Chapter 22 — Divisibility and the Domain Hierarchy

One of the oldest questions in mathematics is how integers factor: can every positive integer be expressed as a product of primes, and is that expression unique? The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic answers yes, and its proof — usually encountered early in number theory — can feel nearly obvious. But the moment one moves from $\mathbb{Z}$ to other rings of algebraic integers, the comfortable certainty of unique factorization evaporates. In the ring $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, the element $6$ factors as $2 \cdot 3$ and also as $(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$, with all four factors irreducible, and neither factorization refines to the other. Understanding why unique factorization holds in $\mathbb{Z}$ but fails here — and what structural properties must be present for it to hold — is the central project of this chapter.

The hierarchy of integral domains organizes the answer. At the broadest level, an integral domain is simply a commutative ring in which the product of two nonzero elements is nonzero. Within this class, we distinguish several increasingly well-behaved species. A unique factorization domain (UFD) is one in which every nonzero non-unit element factors into irreducibles, and that factorization is unique up to order and associates. A principal ideal domain (PID) is one in which every ideal is generated by a single element; it turns out that PIDs are always UFDs, but the converse fails. A Euclidean domain is one equipped with a norm function that permits division with remainder, generalizing the familiar algorithm for integers; Euclidean domains are always PIDs. The inclusions are all strict: $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is a UFD but not a PID (the ideal $(2, x)$ is not principal), and there exist PIDs that admit no Euclidean norm at all, such as $\mathbb{Z}\!\left[\frac{1+\sqrt{-19}}{2}\right]$.

The key to understanding this hierarchy is the relationship between two concepts that coincide in $\mathbb{Z}$ but diverge in general domains: irreducible elements and prime elements. An element $p$ is prime if $p \mid ab$ implies $p \mid a$ or $p \mid b$; it is irreducible if it cannot be written as a product of two non-units. In a UFD, these notions are equivalent, and this equivalence is what makes unique factorization work. In a domain that fails to be a UFD — precisely because the implication "irreducible implies prime" breaks down — elements that look like they should be building blocks of factorization turn out not to have the divisibility behavior of genuine primes. The ascending chain condition on principal ideals provides the standard mechanism for proving existence of factorization, while the prime-equals-irreducible condition ensures uniqueness.

The chapter culminates by confronting what happens when unique factorization genuinely fails, and finding that ideals provide the correct remedy. In a Dedekind domain — an integrally closed Noetherian domain of Krull dimension one, the class that includes all rings of integers of number fields — unique factorization of elements may fail, but unique factorization of ideals into prime ideals always holds. The ideal class group, which measures the obstruction to every ideal being principal, quantifies how far a Dedekind domain strays from being a PID. When the class group is trivial, the domain is a PID and unique factorization of elements is restored. This perspective, originating with Kummer and Dedekind, transformed both ring theory and algebraic number theory, and points directly toward the commutative algebra of Chapter 24.
