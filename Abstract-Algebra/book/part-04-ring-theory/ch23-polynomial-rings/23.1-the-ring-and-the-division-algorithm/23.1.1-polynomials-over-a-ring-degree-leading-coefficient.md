# 23.1.1 Polynomials over a Ring; Degree; Leading Coefficient

The most natural way to build new rings from old ones is to adjoin a formal variable. Starting from a ring $R$, we form the polynomial ring$R[x]$: its elements are formal expressions$f = a_0 + a_1 x + \cdots + a_n x^n$ where the coefficients$a_i$ lie in$R$ and$x$ is an indeterminate, meaning it carries no algebraic relations beyond those forced by the ring axioms. Addition and multiplication are performed in the evident way — add coefficients of like powers, multiply by distributing and collecting — making$R[x]$ into a ring that contains$R$ as the constant polynomials.

The **degree** of a nonzero polynomial $f$ is$\deg f = n$, the largest index$n$ for which$a_n \neq 0$; the **leading coefficient** is that$a_n$. We leave the degree of the zero polynomial undefined, or assign it$-\infty$ by convention to make degree formulas cleaner. Notice that if$R$ is an integral domain, then$\deg(fg) = \deg f + \deg g$, because neither leading coefficient can be killed by a zero divisor. Over a general ring this identity can fail: in$(\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z})[x]$, the product$2x \cdot 3x = 6x^2 = 0$ loses degree entirely.

The ring $R[x]$ enjoys a universal property: it is the free$R$-algebra on one generator. Concretely, specifying a ring homomorphism$R[x] \to S$ that is the identity on$R$ is the same as choosing an element$s \in S$ (the image of$x$). This is the *substitution principle* — we may substitute any ring element for$x$ — and it underlies every evaluation map$f \mapsto f(a)$ used throughout algebra.

The passage from $R$ to$R[x]$ preserves many properties. If$R$ is commutative, so is$R[x]$. If$R$ is an integral domain, so is$R[x]$ (since the leading coefficient of$fg$ is the product of those of$f$ and$g$, which is nonzero in a domain). If$R$ is a UFD, so is$R[x]$ (Gauss's theorem, §22.2.4). However, if$R$ is a PID,$R[x]$ need not be a PID:$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is not a PID even though$\mathbb{Z}$ is.

**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{Z}[x]$: the polynomial$f = 3x^4 - 2x^2 + 7$ has degree$4$ and leading coefficient$3$. The product with$g = 2x^3 + x$:$\deg(fg) = 4 + 3 = 7$ (since leading coefficient$3 \cdot 2 = 6 \neq 0$ in$\mathbb{Z}$). The degree formula holds because$\mathbb{Z}$ is a domain.

**Example 2.** In $(\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z})[x]$: take$f = 2x^2 + 1$ (degree 2, leading coefficient$2$) and$g = 3x + 4$ (degree 1, leading coefficient$3$). Then$fg = 6x^3 + 8x^2 + 3x + 4 = 0 \cdot x^3 + 2x^2 + 3x + 4 = 2x^2 + 3x + 4$ (since$6 \equiv 0 \pmod 6$ and$8 \equiv 2 \pmod 6$). The degree of$fg$ is$2$, not$3 = \deg f + \deg g$. The degree formula fails because the leading coefficient$2 \cdot 3 = 6 \equiv 0 \pmod 6$.

**Example 3.** The universal property: define a ring map $\phi: \mathbb{Z}[x] \to \mathbb{Z}$ by specifying$\phi(x) = 5$. This is the evaluation map$f \mapsto f(5)$. It is the unique ring homomorphism$\mathbb{Z}[x] \to \mathbb{Z}$ that fixes$\mathbb{Z}$ and sends$x \mapsto 5$. For instance,$\phi(x^2 - 3x + 2) = 25 - 15 + 2 = 12$.

**Example 4.** The universal property in the non-commutative case: if $R$ is a ring (possibly noncommutative) and$S$ is an$R$-algebra with a central element$s$ (meaning$s$ commutes with everything in$S$), then there is a unique$R$-algebra map$R[x] \to S$ sending$x \mapsto s$. Without centrality, the map would not be well-defined (since in a noncommutative ring,$x$ commutes with all coefficients, but an arbitrary element need not). The center of$S$ plays the role of the "commutative locus" where polynomial substitution makes sense.

**Example 5.** Polynomials versus polynomial functions: over an infinite field $F$, the polynomial ring$F[x]$ is in bijection with polynomial functions$F \to F$ (two different polynomials give different functions). Over a finite field$\mathbb{F}_q$, this fails:$x^q - x$ and$0$ define the same function (Fermat's little theorem:$a^q = a$ for all$a \in \mathbb{F}_q$), but they are different polynomials. The ring$\mathbb{F}_q[x]$ is thus richer than the ring of polynomial functions$\mathbb{F}_q \to \mathbb{F}_q$. This distinction is important in coding theory and cryptography over finite fields.
