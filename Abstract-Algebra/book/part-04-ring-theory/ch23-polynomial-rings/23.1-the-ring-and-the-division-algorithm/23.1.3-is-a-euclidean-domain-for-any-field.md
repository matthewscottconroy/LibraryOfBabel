# 23.1.3 $F[x]$ Is a Euclidean Domain for Any Field$F$

When $F$ is a field, every nonzero polynomial has an invertible leading coefficient. This means the division algorithm from the previous subsection applies without restriction: given any$f$ and any nonzero$g$ in$F[x]$, we can always divide to get$f = qg + r$ with$\deg r < \deg g$ or$r = 0$. This is precisely the condition that makes$F[x]$ a **Euclidean domain**, with Euclidean norm$N(f) = \deg f$.

The consequences of being Euclidean unfold in a familiar chain. A Euclidean domain is a principal ideal domain (PID): every ideal $I \subseteq F[x]$ is generated by a single polynomial, namely the nonzero element of$I$ of minimal degree. And a PID is a unique factorization domain (UFD): every nonzero, non-unit polynomial in$F[x]$ factors into irreducibles, and this factorization is unique up to units (nonzero scalar multiples) and reordering. The monic irreducibles play the role that the prime numbers play in$\mathbb{Z}$.

The Euclidean algorithm also works in $F[x]$: given$f$ and$g$, we repeatedly divide and replace, and the last nonzero remainder is the GCD$\gcd(f,g)$, which we typically normalize to be monic. Moreover, Bézout's identity holds: there exist$u, v \in F[x]$ with$uf + vg = \gcd(f, g)$. Two polynomials are coprime (GCD equal to$1$) if and only if they generate the unit ideal$F[x]$, i.e.,$uf + vg = 1$ for some$u, v$. The analogy with$\mathbb{Z}$ is essentially complete, and this parallel structure makes$F[x]$ one of the most tractable and important examples of a PID.

The field hypothesis is essential in two ways. First, it ensures every nonzero leading coefficient is invertible, making the division algorithm work. Second, the units of $F[x]$ are exactly the nonzero constant polynomials$F^\times$, so the "irreducible" and "prime" conditions are both sensible. If$F$ is not a field (e.g.,$F = \mathbb{Z}$), then$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is still a UFD but not a PID, as the ideal$(2, x)$ witnesses.

**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{Q}[x]$: the ideal$I$ generated by$\{x^2 - 1, x^3 - 1\}$. Compute$\gcd(x^2-1, x^3-1)$ using the Euclidean algorithm:
- $x^3 - 1 = x \cdot (x^2-1) + (x-1)$
- $x^2 - 1 = (x+1) \cdot (x-1) + 0$

So $\gcd = x - 1$ (monic). The ideal$(x^2-1, x^3-1) = (x-1)$. Any polynomial vanishing at both$1$ and all cube roots of$1$ that lie in$\mathbb{Q}$ (only$1$ itself) is divisible by$x - 1$.

**Example 2.** In $\mathbb{F}_2[x]$: every nonzero polynomial has leading coefficient$1$ (since$1$ is the only nonzero element of$\mathbb{F}_2$), so every polynomial is monic. The degree function gives the Euclidean structure. Divide$f = x^4 + x + 1$ by$g = x^2 + x + 1$:
- $x^4 \div x^2 = x^2$;$x^2(x^2+x+1) = x^4 + x^3 + x^2$; remainder:$(x^4+x+1) - (x^4+x^3+x^2) = x^3+x^2+x+1$.
- $x^3 \div x^2 = x$;$x(x^2+x+1) = x^3+x^2+x$; remainder:$(x^3+x^2+x+1) - (x^3+x^2+x) = 1$.

So $\gcd(x^4+x+1, x^2+x+1) = \gcd(x^2+x+1, 1) = 1$. They are coprime.

**Example 3.** The ideal $(f, g) = F[x]$ when$\gcd(f,g) = 1$: Chinese Remainder Theorem. If$f$ and$g$ are coprime in$F[x]$, then$F[x]/(fg) \cong F[x]/(f) \times F[x]/(g)$. For$F = \mathbb{Q}$,$f = x-1$,$g = x+1$:$F[x]/((x-1)(x+1)) \cong \mathbb{Q} \times \mathbb{Q}$ (one copy for each root).

**Example 4.** In $\mathbb{C}[x]$: every polynomial factors completely into linear factors (by the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra). So the monic irreducibles are exactly$\{x - a : a \in \mathbb{C}\}$. Every ideal in$\mathbb{C}[x]$ is$(x - a_1)^{n_1} \cdots (x - a_k)^{n_k}$ for distinct$a_i \in \mathbb{C}$ and positive integers$n_i$. The quotients$\mathbb{C}[x]/(x-a)^n$ are the "fat point" rings, with a distinguished nonzero nilpotent element$(x-a) + (x-a)^n$.

**Example 5.** The fundamental theorem of algebra as a statement about $\mathbb{C}[x]$: the only irreducible polynomials over$\mathbb{C}$ are the linear ones. Over$\mathbb{R}$, the irreducibles are linear polynomials$x - a$ and quadratic polynomials$x^2 + bx + c$ with$b^2 - 4c < 0$ (negative discriminant, so no real roots). Over$\mathbb{Q}$, there are irreducibles of every degree$n \geq 1$ (e.g.,$x^n - 2$ is irreducible by Eisenstein). Over$\mathbb{F}_p$, the number of monic irreducibles of degree$n$ is$\frac{1}{n} \sum_{d \mid n} \mu(n/d) p^d$ (by Möbius inversion on the factorization of$x^{p^n} - x$ over$\mathbb{F}_p$), confirming there are irreducibles of every degree.
