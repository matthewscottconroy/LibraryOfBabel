# 23.1.4 Roots and Factors: $(x-a) \mid f \Leftrightarrow f(a) = 0$

An element $a \in R$ is a **root** of a polynomial$f \in R[x]$ if$f(a) = 0$, where$f(a)$ denotes the result of substituting$a$ for$x$. The connection between roots and linear factors is clean and fundamental:$(x - a) \mid f$ if and only if$f(a) = 0$.

To see why, apply the division algorithm to divide $f$ by$(x - a)$, noting that$x - a$ is monic hence has a unit leading coefficient. We get$f = (x - a)q + r$ where$r$ is a constant (degree$0$ or$r = 0$). Evaluating at$x = a$ gives$f(a) = 0 \cdot q(a) + r = r$. So$r = f(a)$, and$(x - a) \mid f$ if and only if this remainder is zero, i.e.,$f(a) = 0$.

In an integral domain, a nonzero product is nonzero. This gives us a useful bound: a polynomial of degree $n$ over an integral domain has **at most$n$ roots**. For if$a_1, \ldots, a_k$ are distinct roots, then$(x - a_1) \cdots (x - a_k)$ divides$f$, forcing$k \leq n$. This bound is sharp —$x^n - 1$ has exactly$n$ roots in$\mathbb{C}$ — and plays a vital role in proving results about groups of units, characteristic of fields, and the structure of finite fields.

The hypothesis of integral domain is essential. In $\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$, the polynomial$x^2 - 1$ is satisfied by$x = 1, 3, 5, 7$ — four roots for a degree-$2$ polynomial — because$\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$ has zero divisors ($3 \cdot 3 - 1 = 8 \equiv 0$, etc.). The root bound is thus a diagnostic tool: if we can exhibit more than$n$ roots, we know the coefficient ring is not an integral domain.

**Example 1.** Factor Theorem in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$:$f = x^3 - 6x^2 + 11x - 6$. Check$f(1) = 1 - 6 + 11 - 6 = 0$, so$(x-1) \mid f$. Divide:$x^3 - 6x^2 + 11x - 6 = (x-1)(x^2 - 5x + 6) = (x-1)(x-2)(x-3)$. The roots are$1, 2, 3$, and the degree-3 polynomial has exactly 3 roots over$\mathbb{Q}$ (a domain).

**Example 2.** Roots in a non-domain: $f = x^2 - 1$ in$\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$. Check:$1^2 - 1 = 0$,$3^2 - 1 = 8 \equiv 0$,$5^2 - 1 = 24 \equiv 0$,$7^2 - 1 = 48 \equiv 0$. So$f$ has 4 roots:$\{1, 3, 5, 7\}$. The bound fails because$\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$ has zero divisors.

**Example 3.** Roots and cyclotomic polynomials: the $n$-th cyclotomic polynomial$\Phi_n(x)$ has as its roots exactly the primitive$n$-th roots of unity$e^{2\pi i k/n}$ with$\gcd(k,n) = 1$. For$n = p$ prime,$\Phi_p(x) = x^{p-1} + x^{p-2} + \cdots + 1$, a polynomial of degree$p-1$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ with$\phi(p) = p-1$ distinct roots in$\mathbb{C}$. The bound is sharp.

**Example 4.** Consequence for finite fields: in $\mathbb{F}_q$ (a field with$q$ elements), the multiplicative group$\mathbb{F}_q^\times$ is cyclic of order$q-1$. Proof sketch:$\mathbb{F}_q^\times$ is a finite abelian group. If it had exponent$d < q-1$, then every element would satisfy$x^d = 1$, meaning$x^d - 1$ would have$q-1$ roots — but$q-1 > d$ is impossible for a degree-$d$ polynomial over the field$\mathbb{F}_q$ (using the root bound). So the exponent of$\mathbb{F}_q^\times$ equals$q-1$, and a finite abelian group whose exponent equals its order is cyclic.

**Example 5.** Multiple roots and the derivative: in $F[x]$ for a field$F$, the element$a$ is a *multiple root* of$f$ if$(x-a)^2 \mid f$. This happens if and only if$f(a) = 0$ and$f'(a) = 0$, where$f'$ is the formal derivative. To see why: write$f = (x-a)^k g$ with$g(a) \neq 0$. Then$f' = k(x-a)^{k-1}g + (x-a)^k g'$, so$f'(a) = k \cdot g(a)$. If$\mathrm{char}(F) = 0$ or$\mathrm{char}(F) \nmid k$, then$f'(a) = 0$ iff$k \geq 2$. Over a field of characteristic$p$, the polynomial$x^p - a$ has derivative$0$ (since$px^{p-1} = 0$ in characteristic$p$), so every root is a "multiple" root — but in the sense that$x^p - a = (x - a^{1/p})^p$ in the algebraic closure, where$a^{1/p}$ is the unique$p$-th root of$a$ (in characteristic$p$, the Frobenius is injective).
