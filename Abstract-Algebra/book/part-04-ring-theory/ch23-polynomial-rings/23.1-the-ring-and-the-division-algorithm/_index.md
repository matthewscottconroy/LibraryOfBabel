# 23.1 The Ring $R[x]$ and the Division Algorithm

The polynomial ring $R[x]$ consists of all formal finite sums $a_0 + a_1 x + \cdots + a_n x^n$ with coefficients $a_i \in R$, added and multiplied according to the familiar rules. The word "formal" is important: $x$ is not a number or an element of $R$ but an indeterminate, a symbol whose powers serve to index the coefficients. Two polynomials are equal if and only if all their corresponding coefficients are equal. The degree of a nonzero polynomial is the index of its leading (highest-indexed nonzero) coefficient; the degree function satisfies $\deg(fg) \leq \deg(f) + \deg(g)$ in general, with equality when $R$ is a domain (since in a domain, the leading coefficient of $fg$ is the product of the leading coefficients, which is nonzero). This means that $R[x]$ is a domain whenever $R$ is.

The division algorithm in $R[x]$ requires care about when it applies. If $R$ is a field, or more generally if the leading coefficient of the divisor is a unit in $R$, then for any $f, g \in R[x]$ with $g \neq 0$, there exist unique $q, r \in R[x]$ with $f = qg + r$ and $\deg r < \deg g$ (or $r = 0$). The proof mimics long division: iteratively subtract multiples of $g$ from $f$ to reduce the degree, using the fact that the leading coefficient of $g$ is invertible to cancel the leading term of $f$ at each step. When $F$ is a field, this makes $F[x]$ a Euclidean domain with norm $\deg$, hence a PID and a UFD. The classification of ideals is immediate: every ideal of $F[x]$ is principal, generated by the monic GCD of all its elements.

An immediate and fundamental consequence of the division algorithm is the Factor Theorem: $a \in F$ is a root of $f \in F[x]$ if and only if $(x - a) \mid f$. This follows by dividing $f$ by $(x - a)$ and observing that the remainder is a constant equal to $f(a)$. The theorem implies that a polynomial of degree $n$ over a field has at most $n$ roots in that field, a constraint with far-reaching consequences: it implies that a finite subgroup of the multiplicative group of a field is cyclic (since the polynomial $x^n - 1$ has at most $n$ roots), and it is the first step toward understanding the splitting field and Galois theory. The interplay between arithmetic properties of $f$ and the location of its roots is the thread that connects this section to the irreducibility theory of Section 23.2.
