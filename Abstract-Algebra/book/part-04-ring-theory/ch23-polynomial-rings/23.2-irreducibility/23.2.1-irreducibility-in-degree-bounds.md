# 23.2.1 Irreducibility in $F[x]$; Degree Bounds

Having established that $F[x]$ is a UFD, we need to understand its irreducible elements — the "primes" of the polynomial world. A nonzero polynomial$f \in F[x]$ of degree at least$1$ is **irreducible** over$F$ if, whenever$f = gh$ in$F[x]$, one of$g$ or$h$ must be a unit, that is, a nonzero constant. Equivalently,$f$ cannot be written as a product of two polynomials each of strictly smaller positive degree. Every polynomial of positive degree factors uniquely (up to units and order) into irreducibles, by the UFD property of$F[x]$.

A key observation makes checking irreducibility tractable for small degrees. If $f = gh$ with$\deg f \geq 1$, then$\deg g + \deg h = \deg f$, so one factor must have degree at most$\lfloor \deg f / 2 \rfloor$. In particular, **a polynomial of degree$2$ or$3$ is irreducible over$F$ if and only if it has no root in$F$**: any factorization would require a linear factor$(x - a)$ with$a \in F$, which corresponds precisely to a root. The absence of roots is therefore both necessary and sufficient for irreducibility in degrees$2$ and$3$.

For degree $4$ and beyond, the degree-bound argument breaks down. A degree-$4$ polynomial could factor as a product of two quadratics. The polynomial$x^4 + 4x^2 + 4 = (x^2 + 2)^2$ factors over$\mathbb{Q}$ into two quadratics, yet has no rational roots. For higher degrees, we need the more systematic criteria developed in the next subsections: the rational root test (§23.2.2), Eisenstein's criterion (§23.2.3), and reduction modulo primes (§23.2.4).

Notice also that irreducibility depends on the field: $x^2 - 2$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$ (no rational root$\pm\sqrt{2}$), but factors over$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ as$(x - \sqrt{2})(x + \sqrt{2})$. And$x^2 + 1$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{R}$ (no real root), but factors over$\mathbb{C}$ as$(x-i)(x+i)$. The field matters.

**Example 1.** Over $\mathbb{Q}$, degree 2:$f = x^2 - 5$. Possible roots:$\pm\sqrt{5}$, neither rational. So$f$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$. Over$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$,$f = (x - \sqrt{5})(x+\sqrt{5})$, reducible.

**Example 2.** Over $\mathbb{Q}$, degree 3:$f = x^3 - 3x + 1$. Rational root candidates:$\pm 1$.$f(1) = 1-3+1 = -1 \neq 0$;$f(-1) = -1+3+1 = 3 \neq 0$. No rational roots$\Rightarrow$ irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$.

**Example 3.** Over $\mathbb{F}_2$, degree 2:$f = x^2 + x + 1$. Elements of$\mathbb{F}_2$:$\{0, 1\}$.$f(0) = 1 \neq 0$,$f(1) = 1+1+1 = 3 = 1 \neq 0$ (in$\mathbb{F}_2$). No roots$\Rightarrow$ irreducible over$\mathbb{F}_2$.

**Example 4.** Over $\mathbb{F}_3$, degree 3:$f = x^3 - x + 1$. Check all elements:$f(0) = 1$,$f(1) = 1-1+1 = 1$,$f(2) = 8 - 2 + 1 = 7 \equiv 1 \pmod 3$. No roots$\Rightarrow$ irreducible over$\mathbb{F}_3$.

**Example 5.** Degree 4, the subtlety: $f = x^4 + 1$ over$\mathbb{Q}$. No rational roots (check$\pm 1$:$f(1) = 2 \neq 0$,$f(-1) = 2 \neq 0$). But$x^4+1$ is reducible over every finite field$\mathbb{F}_p$ (a classical result). Over$\mathbb{Q}$: is it irreducible? The factorization$x^4 + 1 = (x^2 + \sqrt{2}x + 1)(x^2 - \sqrt{2}x + 1)$ shows it's reducible over$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$, but to check irreducibility over$\mathbb{Q}$, we need to rule out quadratic factors with rational coefficients. A quadratic factor$x^2 + bx + c$ would give$x^4 + 1 = (x^2+bx+c)(x^2-bx+d)$, expanding:$x^4 + (c+d-b^2)x^2 + b(d-c)x + cd$. Matching:$c+d-b^2 = 0$,$b(d-c) = 0$,$cd = 1$. From$b(d-c) = 0$: either$b=0$ or$d = c$. If$b = 0$:$c+d = 0$ and$cd = 1$, so$c$ and$d$ are roots of$t^2 + 1 = 0$, no real solution. If$d = c$:$2c - b^2 = 0$ and$c^2 = 1$, so$c = \pm 1$ and$b = \pm\sqrt{2}$, irrational. So no rational factorization into quadratics. Thus$x^4+1$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$.
