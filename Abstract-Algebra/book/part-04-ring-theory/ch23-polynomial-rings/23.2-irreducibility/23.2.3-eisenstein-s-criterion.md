# 23.2.3 Eisenstein's Criterion

Eisenstein's criterion gives a clean sufficient condition for irreducibility that applies to many polynomials the rational root test cannot handle. It is perhaps the most widely used single irreducibility criterion in algebra and number theory.

The statement: suppose $f = a_n x^n + \cdots + a_1 x + a_0 \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$, and there exists a prime$p$ such that$p \nmid a_n$,$p \mid a_i$ for all$0 \leq i \leq n-1$, and$p^2 \nmid a_0$. Then$f$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$.

The key point is that reducing modulo $p$ collapses$f$ to$\bar{a}_n x^n$, a monomial. Any factorization$f = gh$ over$\mathbb{Z}$ would reduce to$\bar{g}\bar{h} = \bar{a}_n x^n$ over$\mathbb{F}_p$. Since$\mathbb{F}_p[x]$ is a UFD and$x$ is the only irreducible dividing$\bar{a}_n x^n$ (up to units), both$\bar{g}$ and$\bar{h}$ must be monomials in$x$. In particular, their constant terms are divisible by$p$. But then$p^2 \mid a_0 = g(0)h(0)$, contradicting$p^2 \nmid a_0$.

The criterion applies directly to $f$ only when the prime$p$ witnesses the conditions. Often, a substitution$x \mapsto x + c$ for a suitable constant$c$ transforms a polynomial into one where Eisenstein applies. The criterion does not require the polynomial to be monic or primitive (though primitivity must be checked separately to conclude irreducibility over$\mathbb{Q}$ from irreducibility over$\mathbb{Z}$).

**Example 1.** $f = x^5 - 6x + 3$. Try$p = 3$:$p \nmid 1$ (leading),$p \mid -6$ and$p \mid 3$ (all lower coefficients),$p^2 = 9 \nmid 3$. Eisenstein applies at$p = 3$:$f$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$.

**Example 2.** $f = x^4 + 4x^3 + 2$. Try$p = 2$:$p \nmid 1$ (leading),$p \mid 4$ and$p \mid 0$ (coefficient of$x^2$) and$p \mid 0$ (coefficient of$x$) and$p \mid 2$ (constant),$p^2 = 4 \nmid 2$. Eisenstein at$p = 2$:$f$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$.

**Example 3.** The cyclotomic polynomial $\Phi_p(x) = x^{p-1} + x^{p-2} + \cdots + x + 1$ for a prime$p$. Eisenstein does not apply directly. Substitute$x \mapsto x + 1$:
$$\Phi_p(x+1) = \frac{(x+1)^p - 1}{(x+1) - 1} = \frac{(x+1)^p - 1}{x}.$$
By the binomial theorem, $(x+1)^p = x^p + \binom{p}{1}x^{p-1} + \cdots + \binom{p}{p-1}x + 1$. So$(x+1)^p - 1 = x^p + \binom{p}{1}x^{p-1} + \cdots + \binom{p}{p-1}x$, and dividing by$x$ gives$\Phi_p(x+1) = x^{p-1} + \binom{p}{1}x^{p-2} + \cdots + \binom{p}{p-1}$. Now apply Eisenstein at$p$: the leading coefficient is$1$ (not divisible by$p$), all intermediate coefficients$\binom{p}{k}$ for$1 \leq k \leq p-1$ are divisible by$p$, and the constant term$\binom{p}{p-1} = p$ satisfies$p^2 \nmid p$. So$\Phi_p(x+1)$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$, hence$\Phi_p(x)$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$.

**Example 4.** $f = x^n - p$ for any prime$p$ and any$n \geq 1$. Eisenstein at$p$: leading coefficient$1$ (not divisible by$p$), all lower coefficients are$0$ (divisible by$p$), constant term$-p$ has$p \mid (-p)$ but$p^2 \nmid (-p)$ (as long as$p \geq 2$). So$x^n - p$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$ for all primes$p$ and all$n \geq 1$. This shows$\mathbb{Q}$ has algebraic extensions of every degree.

**Example 5.** Eisenstein over other rings: the criterion works over any UFD, not just $\mathbb{Z}$. In$\mathbb{Z}[y][x]$ (polynomials in$x$ over$\mathbb{Z}[y]$), the prime$y \in \mathbb{Z}[y]$ gives Eisenstein. For instance,$f = x^2 - y \in \mathbb{Z}[y][x]$ is Eisenstein at the prime$y$: leading coefficient$1$ (not divisible by$y$), no$x$-term (coefficient$0$, divisible by$y$), constant$-y$ divisible by$y$ but not$y^2$. So$x^2 - y$ is irreducible in$\mathbb{Z}[y][x] = \mathbb{Z}[x,y]$, confirming the variety$\{z^2 = y\}$ is irreducible.
