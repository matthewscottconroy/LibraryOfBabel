# 23.2.5 Irreducibility over $\mathbb{R}$ and$\mathbb{C}$; the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

The question of irreducibility takes on a definitive answer when we work over the classical number fields $\mathbb{R}$ and$\mathbb{C}$. The **Fundamental Theorem of Algebra** — proved in complex analysis using properties of the complex plane — asserts that every nonconstant polynomial with complex coefficients has at least one root in$\mathbb{C}$. The algebraic consequence is immediate: if$f \in \mathbb{C}[x]$ had degree$\geq 2$, it would have a root$a$ and therefore a linear factor$(x - a)$, contradicting irreducibility. Thus the only irreducible polynomials over$\mathbb{C}$ are the linear ones$x - a$.

Over $\mathbb{R}$ the situation is only slightly more involved. A nonreal complex root of a real polynomial always comes paired with its complex conjugate: if$f \in \mathbb{R}[x]$ and$f(z) = 0$ with$z = a + bi$ ($b \neq 0$), then$f(\bar{z}) = 0$ as well. The product$(x - z)(x - \bar{z}) = x^2 - 2ax + (a^2 + b^2)$ has real coefficients and is a real factor of$f$. This quadratic has negative discriminant$4b^2 - 4(a^2+b^2) \cdot 1 < 0$... let us recompute: discriminant of$x^2 - 2ax + (a^2+b^2)$ is$(2a)^2 - 4(a^2+b^2) = 4a^2 - 4a^2 - 4b^2 = -4b^2 < 0$ when$b \neq 0$. So the quadratic has no real roots and is therefore irreducible over$\mathbb{R}$.

It follows that the irreducible polynomials over $\mathbb{R}$ are precisely the linear polynomials$x - a$ (with$a \in \mathbb{R}$) and the quadratics$x^2 + bx + c$ with$b^2 - 4c < 0$. Every real polynomial of positive degree factors uniquely into a product of these. Notice that this is a much more constrained situation than over$\mathbb{Q}$, where irreducibles of arbitrarily large degree exist.

**Example 1.** Over $\mathbb{C}$:$x^4 - 1 = (x-1)(x+1)(x-i)(x+i)$ — all factors are linear.$x^4 + 1 = (x - e^{i\pi/4})(x - e^{3i\pi/4})(x - e^{5i\pi/4})(x - e^{7i\pi/4})$ — four linear factors over$\mathbb{C}$.

**Example 2.** Over $\mathbb{R}$:$x^4 + 1 = (x^2 + \sqrt{2}x + 1)(x^2 - \sqrt{2}x + 1)$. Check discriminant of$x^2 + \sqrt{2}x + 1$:$2 - 4 = -2 < 0$. Irreducible over$\mathbb{R}$. Both quadratic factors are irreducible, and this is the complete factorization into irreducibles over$\mathbb{R}$.

**Example 3.** Over $\mathbb{R}$:$x^5 - 1 = (x-1)(x^4+x^3+x^2+x+1)$. The remaining factor$x^4+x^3+x^2+x+1 = \Phi_5(x)$ (5th cyclotomic polynomial). Its roots are$e^{2\pi ik/5}$ for$k = 1,2,3,4$. These come in conjugate pairs:$e^{2\pi i/5}$ and$e^{-2\pi i/5} = e^{8\pi i/5}$, and$e^{4\pi i/5}$ and$e^{-4\pi i/5} = e^{6\pi i/5}$. So$\Phi_5(x) = (x^2 - 2\cos(2\pi/5)x + 1)(x^2 - 2\cos(4\pi/5)x + 1)$, the product of two irreducible real quadratics.

**Example 4.** The algebraic proof of FTA (partial): suppose $f \in \mathbb{R}[x]$ has no root in$\mathbb{R}$. Then all roots of$f$ in$\mathbb{C}$ are nonreal (occur in conjugate pairs), so$\deg f$ is even. Write$\deg f = 2^a m$ with$m$ odd. By induction on$a$: base case$a = 0$ (odd degree) is handled by the intermediate value theorem ($f$ changes sign). For general$a$: form$g(x) = \prod_{i < j} (x - (r_i + r_j) - c r_i r_j)$ for a suitable constant$c$, reducing the problem to$\deg g = \binom{n}{2}$ which has smaller$2$-adic valuation. This Galois-theoretic argument shows the FTA follows from the IVT plus the existence of square roots in$\mathbb{R}$.

**Example 5.** Contrast with $\mathbb{Q}$: while$\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed and$\mathbb{R}$ has only quadratics as "hard" irreducibles, over$\mathbb{Q}$ one has irreducibles of every degree. For instance,$\Phi_n(x)$ (the$n$-th cyclotomic polynomial) is irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$ for all$n$ (a theorem requiring substantial work), and$\deg \Phi_n = \phi(n)$ takes arbitrarily large values. The polynomial$x^p - 2$ (Eisenstein at$2$) is irreducible of degree$p$ for every prime$p$. This richness of irreducibles over$\mathbb{Q}$ is the algebraic manifestation of the arithmetic complexity of$\mathbb{Q}$ compared to$\mathbb{R}$ and$\mathbb{C}$.
