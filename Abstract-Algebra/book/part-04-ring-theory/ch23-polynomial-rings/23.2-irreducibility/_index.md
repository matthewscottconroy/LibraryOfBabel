# 23.2 Irreducibility

An irreducible polynomial over a field $F$ is a non-constant polynomial that cannot be written as a product of two polynomials of strictly smaller degree. Since $F[x]$ is a UFD, irreducibility is the polynomial analogue of primality — and in $F[x]$, unlike in some other domains, the two notions coincide. The irreducible polynomials over $F$ are the primes of $F[x]$, the building blocks from which all polynomials factor uniquely. The question of which polynomials are irreducible depends heavily on the field: over $\mathbb{C}$, only the linear polynomials are irreducible (the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra); over $\mathbb{R}$, the irreducibles are the linear polynomials and the quadratics with negative discriminant; over $\mathbb{Q}$ and finite fields, the situation is more complex and requires dedicated criteria.

Several criteria for irreducibility over $\mathbb{Q}$ are available. For polynomials of degree two or three, the rational root test suffices: $f \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ has a rational root only if that root is a ratio of a divisor of the constant term to a divisor of the leading coefficient, giving finitely many candidates to check. For higher degrees, Eisenstein's criterion is often decisive: if $f(x) = a_n x^n + \cdots + a_0 \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ and there exists a prime $p$ such that $p \nmid a_n$, $p \mid a_i$ for all $i < n$, and $p^2 \nmid a_0$, then $f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$. Eisenstein's criterion applies directly to $x^n - p$ for any prime $p$ and any $n \geq 1$, and to cyclotomic polynomials $\Phi_p(x)$ after the substitution $x \mapsto x+1$.

Reduction modulo a prime provides yet another tool: if the reduction $\bar{f} \in \mathbb{F}_p[x]$ is irreducible and $\deg \bar{f} = \deg f$, then $f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$. This criterion is especially useful for polynomials where Eisenstein does not apply; one tries various primes $p$ and checks irreducibility in the finite field $\mathbb{F}_p[x]$, which is a finite computation. Over finite fields $\mathbb{F}_q$, there is an explicit formula for the number of monic irreducible polynomials of degree $n$: it is $\frac{1}{n}\sum_{d \mid n} \mu(n/d) q^d$, where $\mu$ is the Möbius function. This count is always positive, ensuring that irreducibles exist in every degree, and it is the polynomial analogue of the prime counting function.
