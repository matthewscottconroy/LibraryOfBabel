# 23.3.3 The Chinese Remainder Theorem for $F[x]$

Recall that the classical Chinese Remainder Theorem for $\mathbb{Z}$ says: if$m_1, \ldots, m_k$ are pairwise coprime integers, then$\mathbb{Z}/(m_1 \cdots m_k) \cong \mathbb{Z}/(m_1) \times \cdots \times \mathbb{Z}/(m_k)$. The same statement holds word-for-word in any PID, and in particular in$F[x]$. If$f_1, \ldots, f_k \in F[x]$ are pairwise coprime polynomials (meaning$\gcd(f_i, f_j) = 1$ for$i \neq j$), then
$$F[x]/(f_1 \cdots f_k) \cong F[x]/(f_1) \times \cdots \times F[x]/(f_k).$$
Coprimality guarantees that the ideals $(f_i)$ and$(f_j)$ together generate all of$F[x]$ (i.e., they are comaximal:$(f_i) + (f_j) = F[x]$), which is the hypothesis needed for the general CRT.

The proof: define the map $\phi: F[x] \to \prod_{i=1}^k F[x]/(f_i)$ by$\phi(g) = (g \bmod f_1, \ldots, g \bmod f_k)$. This is a ring homomorphism with kernel$(f_1 \cdots f_k)$ (by coprimality, the$\text{lcm}$ of the$f_i$ equals their product). Surjectivity: for each$j$, find$e_j \in F[x]$ with$e_j \equiv 1 \pmod{f_j}$ and$e_j \equiv 0 \pmod{f_i}$ for$i \neq j$ — these are the "idempotents," constructed using Bézout on the coprime pairs.

The most significant case is when each $f_i$ is irreducible: then each quotient$F[x]/(f_i)$ is a field extension of$F$, and the product decomposes the quotient ring into a product of fields. A polynomial$f$ that splits into distinct irreducible factors$f = f_1 \cdots f_k$ (a squarefree polynomial) yields the decomposition$F[x]/(f) \cong K_1 \times \cdots \times K_k$ where each$K_i = F[x]/(f_i)$ is a field. When$f$ splits completely over$F$ (all$f_i = x - a_i$ are linear), this gives$F[x]/(f) \cong F^k$.

**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{Q}[x]$:$f = x^2 - 1 = (x-1)(x+1)$. Since$\gcd(x-1, x+1) = 1$ (over$\mathbb{Q}$, the Euclidean algorithm gives$\gcd = 1$ since$(x+1) - (x-1) = 2 \in \mathbb{Q}^\times$), CRT gives$\mathbb{Q}[x]/(x^2-1) \cong \mathbb{Q}[x]/(x-1) \times \mathbb{Q}[x]/(x+1) \cong \mathbb{Q} \times \mathbb{Q}$.

The idempotents: $e_1 = (1-x)/(-2)$ and$e_2 = (1+x)/2$ satisfy$e_1 + e_2 = 1$,$e_1^2 \equiv e_1$,$e_2^2 \equiv e_2$, and$e_1 e_2 \equiv 0$ modulo$x^2-1$. Verify:$e_1 e_2 = (1-x)(1+x)/(-4) = (1-x^2)/(-4) \equiv (1-1)/(-4) = 0$ when$x^2 = 1$.

**Example 2.** In $\mathbb{Q}[x]$:$f = x^3 - x = x(x-1)(x+1)$. The three factors are pairwise coprime, so$\mathbb{Q}[x]/(x^3-x) \cong \mathbb{Q} \times \mathbb{Q} \times \mathbb{Q}$. The three projections are:$g \mapsto g(0)$,$g \mapsto g(1)$,$g \mapsto g(-1)$. The "evaluation at roots" decomposition.

**Example 3.** In $\mathbb{F}_2[x]$:$f = x^4 - 1 = (x-1)^4 = (x+1)^4$ over$\mathbb{F}_2$ (since$-1 = 1$ in$\mathbb{F}_2$). So$x^4 - 1 = (x+1)^4$. This is NOT squarefree, so CRT does not apply in the same way. Instead,$\mathbb{F}_2[x]/(x^4-1) = \mathbb{F}_2[x]/(x+1)^4$, which is a local ring with maximal ideal$(x+1)/(x+1)^4$ and a unique simple module. This illustrates that the CRT requires coprimality (squarefreeness over$F$) — without it, the ring is not a product of fields.

**Example 4.** In $\mathbb{F}_2[x]$:$f = x^4 + x^3 + x^2 + x + 1 = \Phi_5(x)$. Over$\mathbb{F}_2$: the polynomial factors as$x^4+x^3+x^2+x+1 = (x^2+x+1)^2$ over$\mathbb{F}_4$... actually over$\mathbb{F}_2$ it is$(x^2+x+1)^2$ if$5 \mid 2^2-1 = 3$? No,$5 \nmid 3$. In fact over$\mathbb{F}_2$:$\text{ord}(2 \bmod 5) = 4$ (since$2^4 = 16 \equiv 1 \pmod 5$ and$2^1 = 2$,$2^2 = 4$,$2^3 = 3$, not$1$). So$\Phi_5$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{F}_2$ (the factorization pattern corresponds to the orbit structure under Frobenius). Thus$\mathbb{F}_2[x]/\Phi_5 \cong \mathbb{F}_{16}$ is a field extension of degree$4$.

**Example 5.** The CRT for $F[x]$ is the algebraic backbone of the structure theory of semisimple rings. By the Artin–Wedderburn theorem, a commutative semisimple ring is a product of fields. For a commutative ring$R = F[x]/(f)$, semisimplicity is equivalent to$f$ being squarefree (no repeated irreducible factors). This is because semisimplicity means$R$ has no nonzero nilpotent elements: if$g + (f)$ is nilpotent,$(g)^n \equiv 0 \pmod{f}$, so$f \mid g^n$, and if$f$ is squarefree this forces$f \mid g$. The CRT decomposition$R \cong \prod F[x]/(f_i)$ (product of fields) is the Artin–Wedderburn decomposition.
