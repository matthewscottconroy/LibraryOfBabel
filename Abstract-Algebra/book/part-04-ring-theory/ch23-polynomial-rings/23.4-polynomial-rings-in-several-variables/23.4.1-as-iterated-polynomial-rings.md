# 23.4.1 $R[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ as Iterated Polynomial Rings

Moving from one variable to several is conceptually straightforward: the polynomial ring $R[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ is defined inductively as$R[x_1, \ldots, x_{n-1}][x_n]$. At each step, we adjoin one new indeterminate to the ring already constructed. The elements are finite$R$-linear combinations of **monomials**$x_1^{a_1} \cdots x_n^{a_n}$, where the exponent tuple$(a_1, \ldots, a_n)$ ranges over$\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^n$. Addition is termwise and multiplication distributes, collecting like monomials.

The iterated construction ensures that familiar properties propagate. If $R$ is an integral domain, so is$R[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ (since each step preserves the domain property: a polynomial ring over a domain is a domain). If$R$ is a UFD, so is$R[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ (by Gauss's theorem applied$n$ times). The degree function generalizes: the **total degree** of a monomial$x_1^{a_1}\cdots x_n^{a_n}$ is$a_1 + \cdots + a_n$, and the total degree of a polynomial is the maximum total degree of its monomials.

One essential difference from the single-variable case stands out: for $n \geq 2$, the ring$F[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ over a field$F$ is no longer a PID. The ideal$(x_1, x_2)$ in$F[x_1, x_2]$ — consisting of all polynomials with zero constant term — requires both$x_1$ and$x_2$ as generators and cannot be generated by a single element. The failure of the principal ideal property means we cannot naively apply the one-variable theory of GCDs and the Euclidean algorithm. Understanding and computing with ideals in multivariable polynomial rings requires new tools, developed in the next subsections.

The iterated structure gives an important feature: $R[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ is the same ring regardless of the order in which we adjoin the variables. This follows from the universal property: a ring map from$R[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ is determined by its values on the generators$x_1, \ldots, x_n$, and these can be chosen independently. In particular,$R[x_1][x_2] \cong R[x_2][x_1]$ as$R$-algebras.

**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{Z}[x, y]$: the polynomial$f = 3x^2y - 2xy^2 + 5y^3 + x - 1$ has total degree$3$ (the term$3x^2y$ has total degree$3$, and similarly$-2xy^2$ and$5y^3$). Viewed as a polynomial in$y$ over$\mathbb{Z}[x]$:$f = 5y^3 - 2xy^2 + (3x^2+1) y \cdot ...$ wait:$f = (-1) + x + (3x^2)y + (-2x)y^2 + 5y^3$. As a polynomial in$y$ with coefficients in$\mathbb{Z}[x]$:$f = 5y^3 + (-2x)y^2 + 3x^2 y + (x-1)$.

**Example 2.** The ring $\mathbb{Q}[x, y]$ is a UFD: Gauss's theorem applied to$\mathbb{Q}$ (a field, hence a UFD) gives$\mathbb{Q}[x]$ is a UFD, and applying again gives$\mathbb{Q}[x][y] = \mathbb{Q}[x,y]$ is a UFD. The irreducibles are: (a) nonzero constants$c \in \mathbb{Q}^\times$ (units, not actually irreducibles), (b) irreducible polynomials in$x$ alone (like$x^2+1$ or$x-2$), (c) irreducible polynomials in$y$ alone, and (d) polynomials in both$x$ and$y$ that are irreducible in the UFD sense. For example,$x^2 + y^2 - 1$ is irreducible in$\mathbb{Q}[x,y]$: it defines the unit circle, an irreducible variety.

**Example 3.** Symmetric polynomials: the fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials states that the ring of symmetric polynomials $\mathbb{Z}[x_1,\ldots,x_n]^{S_n}$ (polynomials invariant under all permutations of the variables) is itself a polynomial ring$\mathbb{Z}[e_1, \ldots, e_n]$, where$e_k = \sum_{i_1 < \cdots < i_k} x_{i_1} \cdots x_{i_k}$ is the$k$-th elementary symmetric polynomial. This result uses the iterated structure: one adjoin the$e_k$ one by one, using the Newton identities to express power sums in terms of elementary symmetric polynomials.

**Example 4.** The Hilbert–Serre theorem (preview): the Hilbert function of a finitely generated graded $k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$-module$M$ is eventually polynomial. The grading on$k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ is by total degree: the degree-$d$ piece is the span of all monomials of total degree$d$, which has dimension$\binom{n+d-1}{n-1}$. For a quotient ring$k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]/I$, the Hilbert function$H(d) = \dim_k (R/I)_d$ is eventually a polynomial in$d$ (the Hilbert polynomial), whose degree is the Krull dimension of$R/I$ minus$1$.

**Example 5.** The ideal $(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ in$k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$: this is the maximal ideal corresponding to the origin in$\mathbb{A}^n_k$. The quotient$k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]/(x_1,\ldots,x_n) \cong k$, confirming it is maximal. Any polynomial with zero constant term lies in this ideal. The powers$(x_1,\ldots,x_n)^d$ consist of polynomials with no terms of degree$< d$, and$k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]/(x_1,\ldots,x_n)^d$ is the "fat point" ring, recording the first$d$ terms of a Taylor expansion at the origin. These local rings are fundamental in deformation theory.
