# 23.4.2 Monomial Orders; Division Algorithm in Several Variables

To generalize the division algorithm to $F[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$, we first need to decide which monomial is "largest" in a given polynomial — the multivariable analog of the leading term. A **monomial order** is a total ordering on the set of monomials in$x_1,\ldots,x_n$ that is compatible with multiplication (multiplying both sides of a comparison by a monomial preserves the order) and is a well-ordering (every nonempty set of monomials has a smallest element). The well-ordering condition ensures that any reduction process eventually terminates.

The two most common monomial orders are **lexicographic order** (lex) and **graded reverse lexicographic order** (grlex, also called degrevlex). In lex order, $x^\alpha > x^\beta$ if the leftmost nonzero entry of$\alpha - \beta$ is positive — essentially dictionary order. In grlex order, higher total degree wins, with ties broken by reverse lex (so among monomials of equal total degree, the one with the smallest last variable wins). For example, under lex with$x_1 > x_2 > x_3$:$x_1^3 > x_1^2 x_2 > x_1 x_2^2 > \ldots > x_2^3 > \ldots > x_1 > x_2 > x_3 > 1$; in particular$x_1 > x_2^{100}$ since$x_1$ is "lexicographically larger." Under grlex,$x_2^{100}$ far outranks$x_1$ since total degree$100 > 1$.

Given a monomial order, every nonzero polynomial has a well-defined **leading monomial** $\mathrm{LM}(f)$ (the largest monomial present), a **leading coefficient**$\mathrm{LC}(f)$ (the coefficient of the leading monomial), and a **leading term**$\mathrm{LT}(f) = \mathrm{LC}(f) \cdot \mathrm{LM}(f)$. We can then attempt a division algorithm: given$f$ and a list$(g_1, \ldots, g_m)$, we successively reduce$f$ by each$g_i$ whenever the leading term of$g_i$ divides the leading term of the current remainder.

A fundamental difficulty arises: the result depends on the order in which we apply the divisors. Two different orderings of the same list $(g_1, g_2)$ can yield different remainders for the same$f$. This non-uniqueness of the remainder is the core problem that Gröbner bases are designed to solve.

**Example 1.** Lex order with $x > y$ in$F[x,y]$: order the monomials$x^a y^b$ by$x^a y^b >_{\text{lex}} x^c y^d$ if$a > c$, or$a = c$ and$b > d$. So:$x^2 > xy > x > y^3 > y^2 > y > 1$. The polynomial$f = 3xy - 2y^3 + x + 5$ has leading monomial$xy$ and leading term$3xy$.

**Example 2.** Grlex order with $x > y$ in$F[x,y]$:$x^a y^b >_{\text{grlex}} x^c y^d$ if$a+b > c+d$, or$a+b = c+d$ and$b < d$ (less of the "smaller" variable). So:$x^3 > x^2y > xy^2 > y^3 > x^2 > xy > y^2 > x > y > 1$. The polynomial$f = 3xy - 2y^3 + x + 5$: monomials are$xy$ (degree 2),$y^3$ (degree 3),$x$ (degree 1),$1$ (degree 0). Leading term under grlex:$y^3$ has degree 3,$xy$ has degree 2, so$\mathrm{LT}(f) = -2y^3$.

**Example 3.** Division in $\mathbb{Q}[x,y]$ by$\{g_1 = xy - 1, g_2 = y^2 - 1\}$ using lex order ($x > y$). Divide$f = x^2 y + xy^2 + y^2$. Leading term of$f$:$x^2 y$. Does$\mathrm{LT}(g_1) = xy$ divide$x^2 y$? Yes:$x^2 y / xy = x$. Subtract$x \cdot g_1 = x^2 y - x$: new remainder$f_1 = xy^2 + x + y^2$. Leading term$xy^2$: does$xy$ divide$xy^2$? Yes: divide by$y$. Subtract$y \cdot g_1 = xy^2 - y$:$f_2 = x + y^2 + y$. Leading term$x$: does$xy$ divide$x$? No (would need$y^{-1}$). Does$y^2$ divide$x$? No. So$x$ goes to the remainder. Continue with$y^2 + y$:$\mathrm{LT}(g_2) = y^2$ divides$y^2$: subtract$1 \cdot g_2 = y^2 - 1$: remainder$y + 1 + y + 1 = ...$ wait:$y^2 + y - (y^2 - 1) = y + 1$. Final remainder:$x + y + 1$. So$f = (x+y)(xy-1) + 1 \cdot (y^2-1) + (x+y+1)$.

**Example 4.** The order-dependence problem: divide the same $f = xy^2 - x$ by$\{g_1 = xy-1, g_2 = y^2-1\}$ in two orderings. Order (i): try$g_1$ first, then$g_2$.$\mathrm{LT}(g_1) = xy$ divides$xy^2$: subtract$y \cdot g_1 = xy^2 - y$: remainder$-x+y$.$xy$ doesn't divide$-x+y$.$y^2$ doesn't divide. Remainder:$-x+y$. Order (ii): try$g_2$ first.$\mathrm{LT}(g_2) = y^2$ divides$xy^2$: subtract$x \cdot g_2 = xy^2 - x$: remainder$0$. The remainder is$0$ with order (ii) but$-x+y$ with order (i), even though$f \in (g_1, g_2)$ (since$f = x(y^2-1)$). The non-uniqueness of the remainder is a real obstacle.

**Example 5.** Why lex is useful for elimination: under lex with $x > y$, the reduction process "eliminates"$x$ as much as possible. If we have a Gröbner basis for an ideal$I \subseteq F[x, y]$ under lex order, then the intersection$I \cap F[y]$ (the elimination ideal) is generated by the Gröbner basis elements involving only$y$. This makes lex the standard order for solving polynomial systems by elimination (the multivariable analogue of Gaussian elimination).
