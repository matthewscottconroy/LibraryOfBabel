# 23.4.3 Gröbner Bases and Buchberger's Algorithm (Introduction)

The obstacle identified in the previous subsection — that division by multiple polynomials in several variables yields a remainder that depends on the order of division — calls for a better kind of generating set. A **Gröbner basis** for an ideal $I \subseteq F[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ (with respect to a fixed monomial order) is a finite generating set$\{g_1,\ldots,g_m\}$ of$I$ with the following key property: the leading monomials of the$g_i$ generate the **ideal of leading monomials**$\mathrm{LM}(I) = (\mathrm{LM}(f) : f \in I)$. In other words, the leading terms of$I$ are already "seen" by the leading terms of the basis.

The payoff is that division by a Gröbner basis produces a unique remainder, independent of the order in which reductions are applied. This means the remainder of $f$ on division by the Gröbner basis of$I$ is zero if and only if$f \in I$, making **ideal membership decidable** — a computation that is impossible with an arbitrary generating set.

**Buchberger's algorithm** produces a Gröbner basis starting from any finite generating set. The key construction is the **S-polynomial** of two polynomials $f$ and$g$:
$$S(f,g) = \frac{L}{\mathrm{LT}(f)} \cdot f - \frac{L}{\mathrm{LT}(g)} \cdot g,$$
where $L = \mathrm{lcm}(\mathrm{LM}(f), \mathrm{LM}(g))$ is the least common multiple of the leading monomials. The S-polynomial is engineered to cancel the leading terms of$f$ and$g$, revealing potential "new" leading monomials in the ideal. **Buchberger's criterion** says a generating set is already a Gröbner basis if and only if the S-polynomial of every pair reduces to zero on division by the set. The algorithm iterates: compute all S-polynomials, reduce them, and adjoin any nonzero remainders; repeat until all S-polynomials reduce to zero.

Termination follows from the Noetherian property of the monomial ordering: the sequence of leading monomial ideals is strictly ascending until it stabilizes.

**Example 1.** Gröbner basis of $I = (xy - 1, y^2 - 1)$ in$\mathbb{Q}[x,y]$ (lex,$x > y$). Compute$S(g_1, g_2)$ where$g_1 = xy-1$ and$g_2 = y^2-1$.$\mathrm{LM}(g_1) = xy$,$\mathrm{LM}(g_2) = y^2$.$L = \mathrm{lcm}(xy, y^2) = xy^2$.$S(g_1,g_2) = (xy^2)/(xy) \cdot g_1 - (xy^2)/(y^2) \cdot g_2 = y \cdot g_1 - x \cdot g_2 = y(xy-1) - x(y^2-1) = xy^2 - y - xy^2 + x = x - y$. Reduce$x - y$ by$\{g_1, g_2\}$:$\mathrm{LT}(g_1) = xy$ doesn't divide$x$;$\mathrm{LT}(g_2) = y^2$ doesn't divide$x$. So the remainder is$x - y \neq 0$. Add$g_3 = x - y$ to the basis.

Now continue with pairs involving $g_3$.$S(g_1, g_3)$:$\mathrm{LM}(g_1) = xy$,$\mathrm{LM}(g_3) = x$.$L = xy$.$S = y \cdot g_3 - g_1 = y(x-y) - (xy-1) = xy - y^2 - xy + 1 = 1 - y^2 = -(y^2-1) = -g_2$. Reduces to$0$ on division by$\{g_1, g_2, g_3\}$. Good.

$S(g_2, g_3)$:$\mathrm{LM}(g_2) = y^2$,$\mathrm{LM}(g_3) = x$.$L = xy^2$.$S = y^2 \cdot g_3 - x \cdot g_2 = y^2(x-y) - x(y^2-1) = xy^2 - y^3 - xy^2 + x = x - y^3$. Reduce by$\{g_1, g_2, g_3\}$:$x$ is the leading term;$g_3 = x - y$ has$\mathrm{LT} = x$ which divides$x$. So$x - y^3 - 1 \cdot g_3 = x - y^3 - (x-y) = y - y^3 = y(1-y^2) = -y \cdot g_2$. So$x-y^3$ reduces to$0$ via$g_3$ then$g_2$. Good.

The Gröbner basis is $\{xy-1, y^2-1, x-y\}$. The variety$V(I) = \{(x,y) : xy=1, y^2=1\}$ consists of points with$y = \pm 1$ and$x = 1/y = y$: so$\{(1,1), (-1,-1)\}$.

**Example 2.** The lexicographic Gröbner basis solves polynomial systems. Given $I = (f_1, \ldots, f_k)$ with$f_i \in k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$, the lex Gröbner basis contains polynomials in$x_n$ alone, then in$x_{n-1}, x_n$, etc. Solving the system reduces to solving univariate polynomials (back-substitution), analogous to Gaussian elimination for linear systems.

**Example 3.** Ideal membership: is $f = x^2 y - y^3$ in the ideal$I = (x^2+y^2-1, xy)$? Compute a Gröbner basis of$I$ and reduce$f$ by it. If the remainder is$0$, then$f \in I$. This is a concrete, finite computation that Buchberger's algorithm enables.

**Example 4.** Reduced Gröbner bases: a Gröbner basis is **reduced** if the leading coefficient of each element is $1$ (over a field), and no monomial of any element is divisible by the leading monomial of another element. Every ideal has a unique reduced Gröbner basis (for a fixed monomial order). This makes the reduced Gröbner basis a canonical form for ideals — two ideals are equal iff they have the same reduced Gröbner basis.

**Example 5.** Complexity: Buchberger's algorithm terminates in finite time but can be exponential in the worst case. The degrees of intermediate polynomials can grow doubly-exponentially in $n$ (the number of variables). In practice, with grlex ordering and optimizations (Gebauer-Möller criteria to skip redundant S-pairs), it runs efficiently on most examples. Faugère's$F_4$ and$F_5$ algorithms use linear algebra techniques to speed up the computation substantially.
