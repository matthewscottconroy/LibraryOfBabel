# 23.4.4 Hilbert's Basis Theorem: $R$ Noetherian$\Rightarrow$ $R[x]$ Noetherian

Gröbner bases are finite objects, and Buchberger's algorithm terminates in a finite number of steps. But why should a finite Gröbner basis exist at all? The answer is Hilbert's Basis Theorem: if $R$ is a Noetherian ring, then$R[x]$ is also Noetherian. Since fields are Noetherian, it follows by induction that$F[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ is Noetherian for any field$F$ and any$n$. Every ideal in$F[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ is finitely generated, so every ideal has a finite Gröbner basis.

The proof is a clever use of the Noetherian hypothesis on $R$. Given any ideal$I \subseteq R[x]$, for each$n \geq 0$ let$J_n$ be the set of leading coefficients of degree-$n$ polynomials in$I$, together with$0$. Each$J_n$ is an ideal of$R$, and$J_0 \subseteq J_1 \subseteq J_2 \subseteq \cdots$ is an ascending chain (multiplying a degree-$n$ polynomial by$x$ gives a degree-$(n+1)$ polynomial with the same leading coefficient). Since$R$ is Noetherian, this chain stabilizes:$J_N = J_{N+1} = \cdots$ for some$N$. Each$J_n$ for$n \leq N$ is finitely generated over$R$. Choosing preimages in$I$ for all these generators produces a finite set of polynomials that generates all of$I$.

The historical significance of this theorem cannot be overstated. When Hilbert proved it in 1890, he showed that certain invariant rings — which had been studied by explicitly constructing generators one by one — were automatically finitely generated without exhibiting the generators. Gordan, who had spent years constructing such bases by hand, reportedly called Hilbert's proof "theology, not mathematics." The Noetherian condition — every ideal finitely generated — is now recognized as the correct framework for the finiteness properties needed throughout commutative algebra and algebraic geometry.

**Example 1.** $\mathbb{Z}$ is Noetherian (it is a PID, and every PID is Noetherian). So$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is Noetherian,$\mathbb{Z}[x,y] = \mathbb{Z}[x][y]$ is Noetherian, and so on. Every ideal in$\mathbb{Z}[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ is finitely generated. This is not obvious: for example, the ideal$(2, x, x^2, x^3, \ldots)$ would require infinitely many generators if the chain didn't stabilize — but the Hilbert Basis Theorem ensures all chains of leading coefficient ideals stabilize.

**Example 2.** A non-Noetherian ring: $R = F[x_1, x_2, x_3, \ldots]$ (polynomial ring in countably many variables). The ideal$I = (x_1, x_2, x_3, \ldots)$ requires infinitely many generators. The chain$J_n =$ (leading coefficients of degree-$n$ polynomials in$I$): here$J_n = R$ for all$n \geq 1$ (since$x_1^n \in I$ for all$n$), but the original chain of leading-coefficient ideals for the specific ideal$I$ doesn't stabilize in the right way. The key point is that$R$ itself is not Noetherian, so the hypothesis of HBT fails.

**Example 3.** Applying HBT to invariant rings: let $G = \mathbb{Z}/2$ act on$\mathbb{C}[x,y]$ by$(x,y) \mapsto (-x,-y)$. The invariant ring$R^G = \mathbb{C}[x,y]^G$ consists of polynomials with all terms of even total degree. Hilbert's theorem (in the form of HBT for the action of a finite group on a finitely generated algebra) guarantees that$R^G$ is finitely generated as a$\mathbb{C}$-algebra. Explicitly:$R^G = \mathbb{C}[x^2, xy, y^2]$, which is isomorphic to$\mathbb{C}[u,v,w]/(uw-v^2)$ via$u = x^2, v = xy, w = y^2$ (the relation is$uw = x^2 y^2 = (xy)^2 = v^2$).

**Example 4.** The Noetherian property and algebraic geometry: Hilbert's Basis Theorem implies that every algebraic variety in $\mathbb{A}^n_k$ (over an algebraically closed field$k$) is defined by a finite set of polynomial equations. Without this, varieties could require infinitely many defining equations, and the theory would be unmanageable. The Noetherian condition is thus the foundational finiteness property that makes algebraic geometry possible.

**Example 5.** The proof strategy for generating $I$: let us make the argument more explicit. Given$I \subseteq R[x]$, form$J_n$ as above. Since$J_N = J_{N+1} = \ldots$, write$J_n = (c_{n,1}, \ldots, c_{n,r_n})$ for$n = 0, 1, \ldots, N$ (finitely generated by the Noetherian hypothesis on$R$). For each$c_{n,j}$, pick a polynomial$f_{n,j} \in I$ of degree$n$ with leading coefficient$c_{n,j}$. Claim: the finite set$\{f_{n,j} : 0 \leq n \leq N, 1 \leq j \leq r_n\}$ generates$I$. Proof by strong induction on degree: given$f \in I$ of degree$m$, the leading coefficient of$f$ lies in$J_m = J_{\min(m,N)}$, so it is a linear combination of the$c_{\min(m,N), j}$'s. Subtract the appropriate linear combination of the$f_{n,j}$'s (multiplied by$x^{m-n}$) to reduce the degree. Iterate until degree$< 0$, reaching$0$.
