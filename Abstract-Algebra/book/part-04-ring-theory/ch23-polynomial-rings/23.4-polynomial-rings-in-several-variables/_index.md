# 23.4 Polynomial Rings in Several Variables

The polynomial ring $R[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ in $n$ variables can be constructed iteratively as $R[x_1, \ldots, x_{n-1}][x_n]$. When $R = F$ is a field, each $F[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ is a Noetherian UFD — the Noetherian property from Hilbert's basis theorem, the UFD property from Gauss's theorem iterated. However, unlike the single-variable case, $F[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ for $n \geq 2$ is not a PID (the ideal $(x_1, x_2)$ requires two generators), and there is no simple Euclidean algorithm. Polynomial arithmetic in several variables requires a more sophisticated framework.

The central difficulty in several variables is that the division algorithm requires a choice: in $F[x]$, the remainder of $f$ upon division by $g$ is uniquely determined, but in $F[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$, dividing by an ordered tuple of polynomials $(g_1, \ldots, g_m)$ yields a remainder that depends on the order of division. To resolve this ambiguity, one fixes a monomial order — a total order on monomials compatible with multiplication — such as the lexicographic or degree-lexicographic order. With a fixed monomial order, one can define a canonical remainder, but a polynomial $f$ in the ideal $(g_1, \ldots, g_m)$ need not have remainder $0$ with respect to an arbitrary generating set.

A Gröbner basis for an ideal $I$ is a generating set $\{g_1, \ldots, g_m\}$ with the additional property that a polynomial $f$ belongs to $I$ if and only if its remainder upon division by the basis is $0$, for any fixed monomial order. Buchberger's algorithm computes Gröbner bases by iteratively adjoining $S$-polynomials: the $S$-polynomial of $f$ and $g$ is the combination $\text{lcm}(\text{lm}(f), \text{lm}(g)) / \text{lm}(f) \cdot f - \text{lcm}(\text{lm}(f), \text{lm}(g)) / \text{lm}(g) \cdot g$, designed to cancel leading terms and detect hidden membership. If all $S$-polynomials reduce to $0$, the generating set is already a Gröbner basis; otherwise, any nonzero remainder is adjoined and the process repeats. Gröbner bases transform ideal membership from an art to an algorithm, and they underlie computer algebra systems for polynomial computation. Hilbert's basis theorem guarantees that the algorithm terminates, since the ideal of leading monomials stabilizes after finitely many steps.
