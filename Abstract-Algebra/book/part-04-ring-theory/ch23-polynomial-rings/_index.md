# Chapter 23 — Polynomial Rings

Polynomials are among the oldest objects in mathematics, but their role in abstract algebra goes far beyond providing examples. The polynomial ring $R[x]$ over a ring $R$ is the universal construction for adjoining a new element to a ring subject to no relations whatsoever: given any ring $S$, a ring element $s \in S$, and a homomorphism $\phi \colon R \to S$, there is a unique ring homomorphism $R[x] \to S$ that extends $\phi$ and sends $x$ to $s$. This universal property means that polynomial rings are the free objects in an important sense, and it explains why they appear everywhere — from basic number theory to algebraic geometry to representation theory.

When the coefficient ring $R$ is a field $F$, the polynomial ring $F[x]$ is a Euclidean domain with the degree function serving as the norm. Division with remainder works exactly as in $\mathbb{Z}$: given $f, g \in F[x]$ with $g \neq 0$, there exist unique $q, r$ with $f = qg + r$ and $\deg r < \deg g$. This means $F[x]$ is a PID and a UFD, and the irreducible elements play the role of primes. A polynomial $f \in F[x]$ of degree $n$ has at most $n$ roots in $F$, and over an algebraically closed field such as $\mathbb{C}$, the irreducibles are exactly the linear polynomials. Over $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, and finite fields, the classification of irreducibles is more subtle and is the subject of the chapter's central section.

Determining whether a polynomial is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ is, in general, a delicate problem. The rational root theorem eliminates candidates when the polynomial has integer coefficients and low degree. Eisenstein's criterion gives a powerful sufficient condition: if $f(x) = a_n x^n + \cdots + a_0 \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ and there exists a prime $p$ dividing every $a_i$ with $i < n$ but not dividing $a_n$, and with $p^2$ not dividing $a_0$, then $f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$. Reduction modulo $p$ provides another tool: if $\bar{f} \in \mathbb{F}_p[x]$ is irreducible and $\deg \bar{f} = \deg f$, then $f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$. These criteria combine to handle most polynomials encountered in practice.

The quotient rings $F[x]/(f)$ are where polynomial rings reveal their deepest power. When $f$ is irreducible over $F$, the ideal $(f)$ is maximal, and $F[x]/(f)$ is a field extension of $F$ in which $f$ acquires a root. This is the Kronecker construction: rather than asserting that a root of $f$ exists, we build a field that contains one. The image of $x$ in $F[x]/(f)$ is a root, and every element of the extension can be written as a polynomial in this root of degree less than $\deg f$. The construction unifies all algebraic field extensions and will be the foundation of Galois theory. When $f$ factors over $F$, the Chinese Remainder Theorem for rings gives a product decomposition of $F[x]/(f)$, decomposing the quotient ring into a product of simpler pieces. Moving to polynomials in several variables introduces new complexity — there is no division algorithm — but Gröbner bases, introduced by Buchberger, provide a canonical form that makes ideal membership decidable and reduces multivariable problems to single-variable computations.
