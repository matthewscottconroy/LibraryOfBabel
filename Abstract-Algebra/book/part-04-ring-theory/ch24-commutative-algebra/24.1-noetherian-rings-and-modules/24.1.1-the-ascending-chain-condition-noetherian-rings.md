# 24.1.1 The Ascending Chain Condition; Noetherian Rings

Much of commutative algebra is built on a single finiteness condition. A ring $R$ satisfies the **ascending chain condition (ACC)** on ideals if every ascending chain$I_1 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq I_3 \subseteq \cdots$ of ideals eventually stabilizes — that is, there exists$N$ such that$I_N = I_{N+1} = I_{N+2} = \cdots$. A ring satisfying ACC is called **Noetherian**, after Emmy Noether, who identified this condition as the key to making ring theory work. Noether's insight in her 1921 paper was that the ACC hypothesis, rather than any specific algebraic property of the ring, is what underlies the main structural theorems: primary decomposition, the theory of associated primes, and the whole machinery of ideal theory in commutative rings.

Three conditions turn out to be equivalent for a commutative ring $R$. First, every ascending chain of ideals stabilizes (ACC). Second, every ideal of$R$ is finitely generated. Third, every nonempty collection of ideals in$R$ has a maximal element (with respect to inclusion). To see the equivalence of the first two: if every ideal is finitely generated, any ascending chain$I_1 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq \cdots$ has union$I = \bigcup I_n$, which is again an ideal. If$I$ is finitely generated by$a_1, \ldots, a_k$, each$a_j$ lies in some$I_{n_j}$, so taking$N = \max\{n_j\}$ gives$I \subseteq I_N$, hence$I = I_N$ and the chain stabilizes. Conversely, if some ideal$I$ is not finitely generated, we can build a strictly increasing chain by adding one generator at a time: pick$a_1 \in I$, then$a_2 \in I \setminus (a_1)$, then$a_3 \in I \setminus (a_1, a_2)$, and so forth, producing an infinite strictly ascending chain$(a_1) \subsetneq (a_1, a_2) \subsetneq (a_1, a_2, a_3) \subsetneq \cdots$ that never stabilizes. Thus ACC fails if and only if some ideal is not finitely generated.

The equivalence with the maximal element condition (sometimes called the maximum condition) is also straightforward. If ACC holds, then given any nonempty collection $\mathcal{F}$ of ideals, we can start with any$I_1 \in \mathcal{F}$ and, if$I_1$ is not maximal in$\mathcal{F}$, find$I_2 \supsetneq I_1$ in$\mathcal{F}$, and so on. This process must terminate by ACC, yielding a maximal element. Conversely, if every nonempty collection has a maximal element, then the collection$\{I_n : n \geq 1\}$ of terms of any ascending chain has a maximal element$I_N$, and since$I_N \subseteq I_{N+1} \subseteq \cdots$, maximality forces$I_N = I_{N+1} = \cdots$.

\begin{definition}
A commutative ring $R$ is **Noetherian** if it satisfies the ascending chain condition on ideals: for every chain$I_1 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq \cdots$ of ideals in$R$, there exists$N$ such that$I_n = I_N$ for all$n \geq N$.
\end{definition}

The class of Noetherian rings is large enough to encompass essentially all the rings that appear naturally in number theory and algebraic geometry. Any field is Noetherian (its only ideals are $(0)$ and$(1)$). The integers$\mathbb{Z}$ are Noetherian because they form a PID, and every PID is Noetherian: in a PID, every ideal is principal, hence generated by a single element, so every ideal is certainly finitely generated. By Hilbert's Basis Theorem (§24.1.2), every finitely generated algebra over a Noetherian ring is Noetherian: in particular, the polynomial rings$\mathbb{Z}[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ and$k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ (for a field$k$) are Noetherian. Quotients of Noetherian rings are Noetherian: the ideals of$R/I$ correspond bijectively to ideals of$R$ containing$I$ via the correspondence theorem, so a chain of ideals in$R/I$ lifts to a chain in$R$ that stabilizes. Localizations$S^{-1}R$ of Noetherian rings are Noetherian: every ideal of$S^{-1}R$ is of the form$S^{-1}J$ for some ideal$J$ of$R$, so a chain in$S^{-1}R$ produces a chain in$R$ that stabilizes.

The Noetherian condition is the minimal hypothesis needed for most theorems of commutative algebra to hold cleanly. In a non-Noetherian ring, serious pathologies arise: there can be ideals that are not finitely generated, prime ideals whose behavior resists any finiteness argument, and modules without useful finiteness properties. The Noetherian condition rules all of this out, providing a coherent framework in which the full apparatus of ideal theory, primary decomposition, and dimension theory can be deployed. It is, in a precise sense, the weakest condition that ensures the main theorems of classical commutative algebra.

The third equivalent condition — every nonempty set of ideals has a maximal element — is particularly powerful in producing existence results by what is sometimes called the "maximal element trick." For instance, one shows that annihilator ideals $\mathrm{Ann}(m) = \{r \in R : rm = 0\}$ for nonzero$m \in M$ have maximal elements, and that maximal annihilator ideals are prime (a key step in primary decomposition theory). In the Noetherian setting, such arguments are finitary and explicit; in the non-Noetherian setting they require Zorn's lemma and yield weaker results.

**Example 1.** The integers $\mathbb{Z}$: every ideal is$(n)$ for some$n \geq 0$ (since$\mathbb{Z}$ is a PID). Inclusion$(n) \supseteq (m)$ is equivalent to$n \mid m$. An ascending chain$(n_1) \subseteq (n_2) \subseteq \cdots$ corresponds to$n_1 \mid n_2 \mid \cdots$, a divisibility chain. Since$|n_1|$ has only finitely many divisors, any such chain eventually repeats, so the chain stabilizes. More explicitly, if$(n_1) \subseteq (n_2) \subseteq \cdots$, then$|n_1| \geq |n_2| \geq \cdots$ (each divisor is at most as large in absolute value), and a non-increasing sequence of non-negative integers must eventually be constant. So$\mathbb{Z}$ is Noetherian.

**Example 2.** A field $k$: the only ideals of$k$ are$(0)$ and$k$ itself. Any chain of ideals is either eventually$(0)$ or eventually$k$; it stabilizes immediately. The field is trivially Noetherian. More generally, any division ring (ring in which every nonzero element is a unit) is Noetherian for the same reason.

**Example 3.** The polynomial ring $k[x_1, x_2, x_3, \ldots]$ in countably many variables over a field$k$ is NOT Noetherian. The chain$(x_1) \subsetneq (x_1, x_2) \subsetneq (x_1, x_2, x_3) \subsetneq \cdots$ is a strictly ascending chain of ideals that never stabilizes. The ideal$I = (x_1, x_2, x_3, \ldots)$ is not finitely generated: any finite subset$\{f_1, \ldots, f_m\}$ of$I$ involves only finitely many variables, and any polynomial in those variables lies in some$(x_1, \ldots, x_N)$, which doesn't contain all of$I$. This ring is the canonical example of a non-Noetherian ring.

**Example 4.** $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$: this is a subring of the ring of integers of$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})$ and is Noetherian. The ring$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is a finitely generated$\mathbb{Z}$-module — it is spanned by$\{1, \sqrt{-5}\}$ as a$\mathbb{Z}$-module — so any ascending chain of ideals in$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ lifts to an ascending chain of$\mathbb{Z}$-submodules of$\mathbb{Z}^2$, which stabilizes since$\mathbb{Z}^2$ is a Noetherian$\mathbb{Z}$-module. In this ring, the ideal$(2, 1+\sqrt{-5})$ is not principal (this is the failure of unique factorization), but it is finitely generated — generated by exactly two elements — confirming Noetherianness.

**Example 5.** The ACC in practice — a maximal element argument. Let $R = k[x_1, x_2]$ (Noetherian by HBT). Let$\mathcal{S}$ be the set of all proper ideals of$R$ that do not contain a polynomial involving only$x_1$. This set is nonempty (it contains$(0)$) and consists of ideals. Since$R$ is Noetherian,$\mathcal{S}$ has a maximal element$P$. We claim$P$ is prime: if$f, g \in R$ with$fg \in P$ but$f \notin P$, then$P + (f) \supsetneq P$, so by maximality of$P$ in$\mathcal{S}$, the ideal$P + (f)$ is not in$\mathcal{S}$, meaning it contains a polynomial in$x_1$ alone, say$h(x_1) \in P + (f)$. Write$h = p + rf$ for$p \in P$. Then$h(x_1) g = pg + rfg \in P$ (since$pg \in P$ and$rfg \in P$ as$fg \in P$), so$h(x_1) g \in P$. By the same argument applied to$g$, a similar polynomial$\ell(x_1) \in P + (g)$; and$\ell(x_1) \cdot h(x_1)$ is a polynomial in$x_1$ alone in$P$, contradicting$P \in \mathcal{S}$. Therefore$P$ is prime. This style of argument — "take a maximal element of a set of ideals, prove it is prime" — pervades commutative algebra and is valid precisely because$R$ is Noetherian.
