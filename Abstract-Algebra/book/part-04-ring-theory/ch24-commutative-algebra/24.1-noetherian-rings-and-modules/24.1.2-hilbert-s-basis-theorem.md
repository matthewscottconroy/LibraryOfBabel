# 24.1.2 Hilbert's Basis Theorem

Hilbert's Basis Theorem is the engine that drives the Noetherian property through the rings of algebraic geometry and number theory. The statement is clean: if $R$ is a Noetherian ring, then the polynomial ring$R[x]$ is also Noetherian. By induction on the number of variables,$R[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ is Noetherian whenever$R$ is. Taking$R = k$ a field (trivially Noetherian) gives Noetherianness of all polynomial rings$k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$; taking$R = \mathbb{Z}$ gives Noetherianness of$\mathbb{Z}[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$; and taking quotients gives Noetherianness of every finitely generated algebra over a Noetherian ring. This single theorem thus certifies the Noetherian property for the vast majority of rings appearing in practice.

The proof is an elegant use of the ACC hypothesis on $R$. Given any ideal$I \subseteq R[x]$, we want to exhibit a finite generating set. For each$n \geq 0$, define$J_n$ to be the set of all leading coefficients of polynomials in$I$ of degree exactly$n$, together with$0$. A direct check shows that$J_n$ is an ideal of$R$: if$f, g \in I$ both have degree$n$ with leading coefficients$a$ and$b$, then$f + g \in I$ and has leading coefficient$a + b$ (unless cancellation reduces the degree, in which case$a + b$ is still in$J_n$ since$0 \in J_n$); and if$r \in R$ then$rf \in I$ has leading coefficient$ra$. The key structural observation is that$J_n \subseteq J_{n+1}$ for all$n$: if$f \in I$ has degree$n$ and leading coefficient$a$, then$xf \in I$ has degree$n+1$ and the same leading coefficient$a$, so$a \in J_{n+1}$ as well. Thus$J_0 \subseteq J_1 \subseteq J_2 \subseteq \cdots$ is an ascending chain of ideals in$R$.

\begin{theorem}[Hilbert's Basis Theorem]
If $R$ is a Noetherian ring, then$R[x]$ is Noetherian. Consequently, if$k$ is a field or$R = \mathbb{Z}$, then$R[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ is Noetherian for every$n \geq 0$.
\end{theorem}

Since $R$ is Noetherian, the chain$J_0 \subseteq J_1 \subseteq \cdots$ stabilizes: there exists$N$ with$J_N = J_{N+1} = J_{N+2} = \cdots$. Each$J_n$ for$0 \leq n \leq N$ is finitely generated (since$R$ is Noetherian), say$J_n = (c_{n,1}, \ldots, c_{n,r_n})$. For each generator$c_{n,j}$, choose a polynomial$f_{n,j} \in I$ of degree$n$ whose leading coefficient is$c_{n,j}$. We claim the finite collection$\mathcal{G} = \{f_{n,j} : 0 \leq n \leq N,\, 1 \leq j \leq r_n\}$ generates$I$.

The proof that $\mathcal{G}$ generates$I$ proceeds by strong induction on the degree. Let$f \in I$ be arbitrary, with$\deg f = m$. The leading coefficient$a$ of$f$ lies in$J_m$. If$m \leq N$, then$a \in J_m = (c_{m,1}, \ldots, c_{m,r_m})$, so write$a = \sum_j \lambda_j c_{m,j}$ with$\lambda_j \in R$, and subtract$\sum_j \lambda_j f_{m,j}$ from$f$: this new polynomial is in$I$ (as both$f$ and the$f_{m,j}$ lie in$I$), has strictly smaller degree (the leading term$ax^m$ cancels), so induction applies. If$m > N$, then$J_m = J_N$, so$a \in J_N = (c_{N,1}, \ldots, c_{N,r_N})$; write$a = \sum_j \lambda_j c_{N,j}$ and subtract$\sum_j \lambda_j x^{m-N} f_{N,j}$ from$f$, again reducing the degree strictly. Iterating until the degree reaches$-\infty$ (i.e., the polynomial becomes$0$) shows$f$ lies in the ideal generated by$\mathcal{G}$.

The historical weight of this proof is substantial. Before Hilbert's 1890 paper, algebraic invariant theory — the study of polynomial expressions invariant under group actions — was pursued by explicitly constructing generators for invariant rings. Gordan had invested enormous effort in finding generating sets for the invariants of binary forms, working case by case. Hilbert's proof showed at a stroke that invariant rings of finite groups acting on polynomial rings are finitely generated — without producing a single generator. Gordan's reaction, "Das ist Theologie und nicht Mathematik" (this is theology, not mathematics), captured the shock of seeing an existence proof with no explicit content. Today we recognize this as the birth of what we call abstract or structural algebra: the assertion that an object exists because its nonexistence would violate a structural property of the ambient system. The Noetherian hypothesis plays precisely this structural role.

Several important ring-theoretic operations preserve the Noetherian property, and it is useful to have them gathered. Quotients $R/I$ of Noetherian rings are Noetherian: the ideals of$R/I$ correspond bijectively (via the correspondence theorem) to ideals of$R$ containing$I$, so a chain in$R/I$ lifts to a chain in$R$ that stabilizes. Localizations$S^{-1}R$ are Noetherian: every ideal of$S^{-1}R$ is the extension$S^{-1}J$ of some ideal$J \subseteq R$, so chains in$S^{-1}R$ correspond to chains among a subset of ideals of$R$. If$S$ is an$R$-algebra that is finitely generated as an$R$-module, and$R$ is Noetherian, then$S$ is a Noetherian ring (submodules of a finitely generated module over a Noetherian ring are finitely generated; a fortiori, ideals of$S$ are finitely generated$S$-modules, hence finitely generated$R$-submodules of$S$). Rings of integers$\mathcal{O}_K$ of number fields are Noetherian by this last criterion: they are free$\mathbb{Z}$-modules of rank$[K : \mathbb{Q}]$, hence module-finite over$\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 1.** $\mathbb{Q}[x, y, z]/(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - 1)$: the polynomial ring$\mathbb{Q}[x, y, z]$ is Noetherian by three applications of HBT (start from$\mathbb{Q}$, a field, hence Noetherian; apply HBT to get$\mathbb{Q}[x]$ Noetherian; then$\mathbb{Q}[x][y] = \mathbb{Q}[x,y]$ Noetherian; then$\mathbb{Q}[x,y,z]$ Noetherian). The quotient by$(x^2+y^2+z^2-1)$ is Noetherian as a quotient of a Noetherian ring. This ring is the coordinate ring of the unit sphere$S^2$ over$\mathbb{Q}$, and its Noetherianness implies that every ideal of functions vanishing on a given algebraic subset of$S^2$ is finitely generated.

**Example 2.** The ring of integers $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_n]$ of the cyclotomic field$\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$: here$\zeta_n = e^{2\pi i/n}$. The minimal polynomial of$\zeta_n$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ is the cyclotomic polynomial$\Phi_n(x)$, so$\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_n] \cong \mathbb{Z}[x]/(\Phi_n(x))$. Since$\mathbb{Z}$ is Noetherian,$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is Noetherian by HBT, and the quotient$\mathbb{Z}[x]/(\Phi_n(x))$ is Noetherian. Every ideal in$\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_n]$ is finitely generated (in fact, as a Dedekind domain, every ideal is generated by at most two elements). This is used constantly in algebraic number theory when working with cyclotomic fields.

**Example 3.** Termination of Buchberger's algorithm: the Gröbner basis algorithm for an ideal $I \subseteq k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ produces, at each step, a new set of generators by appending nonzero S-polynomial reductions to the current basis. Each new addition strictly increases the ideal of leading monomials$\mathrm{LM}(I')$ of the current generating set$I'$. Since$k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ is Noetherian (by HBT), the ascending chain of leading monomial ideals must stabilize, which is precisely when the algorithm terminates. Without the Noetherian property, this termination argument would not be available.

**Example 4.** The power series ring $k[[x]]$ is Noetherian: its ideals are$(0)$ and$(x^n)$ for$n \geq 0$. A chain$(x^{n_1}) \subseteq (x^{n_2}) \subseteq \cdots$ requires$n_1 \geq n_2 \geq \cdots$ (since$(x^a) \subseteq (x^b)$ iff$b \leq a$), a non-increasing sequence of non-negative integers, which must stabilize. More generally,$k[[x_1, \ldots, x_n]]$ is Noetherian — this is a theorem analogous to HBT for power series rings — and these local rings are the algebraic models for neighborhoods of points in algebraic varieties.

**Example 5.** HBT requires the Noetherian hypothesis on $R$. If$R$ is not Noetherian,$R[x]$ need not be Noetherian. Let$R = k[y_1, y_2, y_3, \ldots]$ (polynomial ring in countably many variables). The chain$(y_1) \subsetneq (y_1, y_2) \subsetneq \cdots$ in$R$ is strictly ascending, so$R$ is not Noetherian. The same chain, viewed inside$R[x]$, shows$R[x]$ is also not Noetherian. Furthermore, there is no way to rescue Noetherianness by a more careful argument: the failure is intrinsic to$R$. This confirms that HBT's conclusion is equivalent to the hypothesis — we cannot prove$R[x]$ is Noetherian for all$R$.
