# 24.1.3 Noetherian Modules; Submodules of Finitely Generated Modules

The Noetherian condition extends naturally from rings to modules. A module $M$ over a ring$R$ is **Noetherian** if every ascending chain of submodules$M_1 \subseteq M_2 \subseteq M_3 \subseteq \cdots$ eventually stabilizes. Equivalently, every submodule of$M$ is finitely generated. The same equivalence proof as for rings applies here, with submodules replacing ideals.

\begin{definition}
An $R$-module$M$ is **Noetherian** if every ascending chain of submodules$M_1 \subseteq M_2 \subseteq \cdots$ stabilizes, i.e., there exists$N$ such that$M_n = M_N$ for all$n \geq N$. Equivalently,$M$ is Noetherian if and only if every submodule of$M$ is finitely generated.
\end{definition}

When $R$ is a Noetherian ring, the Noetherian property propagates to all finitely generated$R$-modules. The argument proceeds by induction on the number of generators. A cyclic module$M = Rm$ is a quotient of$R$ (via$r \mapsto rm$), and quotients of Noetherian modules are Noetherian. If$M$ is generated by$m_1,\ldots,m_k$, we have a surjection$R^k \to M$, and since$R$ is Noetherian,$R^k$ is Noetherian. Noetherianness of$R^k$ follows from the short exact sequences$0 \to R^{j} \to R^{j+1} \to R \to 0$: extensions of Noetherian modules by Noetherian modules are Noetherian (the submodule condition for the extension follows from the conditions for the pieces). The quotient$M$ of the Noetherian module$R^k$ is therefore Noetherian.

The key structural consequence is this: **over a Noetherian ring, every submodule of a finitely generated module is finitely generated**. This is not obvious — in general, a submodule of a finitely generated module need not be finitely generated (over non-Noetherian rings). The Noetherian hypothesis ensures it. The proof: if $M$ is finitely generated over the Noetherian ring$R$, then by the above,$M$ is Noetherian, so every ascending chain of submodules stabilizes, and therefore every submodule is finitely generated.

The three equivalent forms of "Noetherian" for modules mirror those for rings: (1) ACC on submodules, (2) every submodule finitely generated, (3) every nonempty collection of submodules has a maximal element. These are all equivalent by the same argument as in §24.1.1, with ideals replaced by submodules.

In number theory, this means every ideal in the ring of integers $\mathcal{O}_K$ of a number field$K$ is finitely generated (in fact, generated by at most two elements — this sharper bound comes from the structure of Dedekind domains). In algebraic geometry, it means that coherent sheaves on a Noetherian scheme have finitely generated sections over affine pieces — the algebraic incarnation of "coherence." These finiteness properties are invoked constantly and are what make the algebra of Noetherian rings so tractable.

**Example 1.** $\mathbb{Z}$ as a$\mathbb{Z}$-module: every submodule (i.e., every subgroup) of$\mathbb{Z}$ is of the form$n\mathbb{Z}$ for some$n \geq 0$. A chain$n_1 \mathbb{Z} \subseteq n_2 \mathbb{Z} \subseteq \cdots$ means$n_1 \mid n_2 \mid \cdots$... wait, actually$n_1\mathbb{Z} \subseteq n_2\mathbb{Z}$ means$n_2 \mid n_1$ (larger ideal, smaller generator). So a strictly ascending chain corresponds to$n_1 > n_2 > \cdots > 0$, which stabilizes. Confirms$\mathbb{Z}$ is Noetherian as a module over itself.

**Example 2.** $\mathbb{Z}^2$ as a$\mathbb{Z}$-module: every submodule (subgroup) is isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}^r$ for$r \leq 2$ (free abelian of rank$\leq 2$), by the structure theorem for finitely generated abelian groups. In particular, every submodule of$\mathbb{Z}^2$ is finitely generated (in fact, by at most$2$ elements), confirming that$\mathbb{Z}^2$ is Noetherian.

**Example 3.** The submodule $M = \bigoplus_{n \geq 1} \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ over$\mathbb{Z}$: this is an infinite direct sum, hence not finitely generated as a$\mathbb{Z}$-module. Indeed, the chain$M_k = \bigoplus_{n=1}^k \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is strictly ascending, so$M$ is not Noetherian. This is a module over the Noetherian ring$\mathbb{Z}$ that is not Noetherian — illustrating that the Noetherian property does not automatically pass to all modules, only to finitely generated ones.

**Example 4.** The module $k[x_1, x_2, \ldots]$ over itself (the polynomial ring in infinitely many variables as a module over itself) is not Noetherian: the submodule generated by$\{x_1, x_2, \ldots\}$ keeps growing. Compare with the quotient module$k[x_1, x_2, \ldots]/(x_1^2, x_2^2, \ldots)$ — even this is not Noetherian. The Noetherian property of the ring is needed as a hypothesis.

**Example 5.** Application to primary decomposition: over a Noetherian ring, every submodule $N \subseteq M$ of a finitely generated module has a primary decomposition$N = Q_1 \cap \cdots \cap Q_k$ where each$Q_i$ is$\mathfrak{p}_i$-primary (meaning$M/Q_i$ has a unique associated prime$\mathfrak{p}_i$). This is the module-theoretic analogue of primary decomposition for ideals (§22.5) and is one of the main structural theorems of Noetherian module theory. The Noetherian hypothesis ensures that the intersection is finite.
