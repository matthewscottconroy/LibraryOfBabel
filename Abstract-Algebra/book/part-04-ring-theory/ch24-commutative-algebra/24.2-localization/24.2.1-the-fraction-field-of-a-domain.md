# 24.2.1 The Fraction Field of a Domain

The familiar construction of the rational numbers from the integers generalizes to any integral domain. Starting from an integral domain $R$, we want to build the "smallest field containing$R$." The construction is concrete: form the set of formal symbols$a/b$ with$a, b \in R$ and$b \neq 0$, and declare$a/b \sim c/d$ whenever$ad = bc$. The absence of zero divisors ensures this is an equivalence relation: the cross-multiplication check$ad = bc$ and$cf = de$ gives$adf = bcf = bde$, so$af = be$ (canceling$d$, which is nonzero), with no need to divide by anything that might be zero. The equivalence classes form a field$\mathrm{Frac}(R)$ under the usual rules for adding and multiplying fractions:
$$\frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d} = \frac{ad+bc}{bd}, \qquad \frac{a}{b} \cdot \frac{c}{d} = \frac{ac}{bd}.$$
The product $bd$ is nonzero in the denominator because$R$ is a domain.

\begin{definition}
Let $R$ be an integral domain. The **fraction field** (or **field of fractions**) of$R$, written$\mathrm{Frac}(R)$ or$Q(R)$, is the set of equivalence classes of pairs$(a, b)$ with$a, b \in R$,$b \neq 0$, under the relation$(a,b) \sim (c,d) \iff ad = bc$, equipped with the addition and multiplication induced by the fraction rules above.
\end{definition}

The natural map $\iota: R \to \mathrm{Frac}(R)$ sending$a \mapsto a/1$ is an injective ring homomorphism, so$R$ embeds into its fraction field. The fraction field is universal for this property: any injective ring homomorphism from$R$ to a field$F$ extends uniquely to a ring homomorphism$\mathrm{Frac}(R) \to F$. In this sense,$\mathrm{Frac}(R)$ is the smallest field containing$R$.

The key reason $R$ must be a domain is to ensure the equivalence relation is well-defined and transitive, and to guarantee that every nonzero element$a/b$ (with$a \neq 0$) is invertible: its inverse is$b/a$. In a ring with zero divisors, there would be nonzero$a$ with$ab = 0$ for some$b \neq 0$; such$a$ could not have an inverse in any ring (since$1 = (a^{-1}a)b = a^{-1}(ab) = 0$ would follow). The domain condition is therefore necessary, not just convenient.

The fraction field construction is the first instance of a more general technique — localization — that inverts not all nonzero elements, but only those in a chosen multiplicative set (§24.2.2). The fraction field is localization at $S = R \setminus \{0\}$.

**Example 1.** $\mathrm{Frac}(\mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Q}$: the integers are a domain, and the fraction field is exactly the rationals. Every rational$p/q$ (in lowest terms,$q > 0$) is an equivalence class.

**Example 2.** $\mathrm{Frac}(F[x]) = F(x)$: the fraction field of the polynomial ring over a field is the field of rational functions. Elements are$p(x)/q(x)$ with$p, q \in F[x]$ and$q \neq 0$. This field is the natural domain for partial fraction decompositions.

**Example 3.** $\mathrm{Frac}(\mathbb{Z}[i]) = \mathbb{Q}(i)$: the Gaussian integers have fraction field the Gaussian rationals$\{a + bi : a, b \in \mathbb{Q}\}$. More generally,$\mathrm{Frac}(\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{d}]) = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ for any squarefree integer$d$.

**Example 4.** $\mathrm{Frac}(k[[x]]) = k((x))$: the fraction field of the formal power series ring is the field of formal Laurent series$\sum_{n \geq N} a_n x^n$ (finitely many negative powers). This is a complete valued field central to$p$-adic and local analytic methods.

**Example 5.** The universality more carefully: suppose $\phi: R \to F$ is an injective ring map into a field$F$. Define$\tilde{\phi}: \mathrm{Frac}(R) \to F$ by$\tilde{\phi}(a/b) = \phi(a)/\phi(b)$. This is well-defined (since$\phi$ is injective,$\phi(b) \neq 0$ when$b \neq 0$), and is the unique ring homomorphism extending$\phi$. If$\phi$ is not injective (say$\phi(r) = 0$ for some nonzero$r$), then$r/1$ would need to map to$0$ but$r/1$ is a unit (invertible) in$\mathrm{Frac}(R)$, contradiction. So injectivity of$\phi$ is required.
