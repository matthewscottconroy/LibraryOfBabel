# 24.2.2 Localization at a Multiplicative Set $S^{-1}R$

The fraction field inverts every nonzero element of a domain. More generally, we may wish to invert only a selected subset of ring elements — perhaps just the powers of a single element $f$, or all elements outside a given prime ideal. This flexibility is captured by the notion of a multiplicative set.

\begin{definition}
A **multiplicative set** (or **multiplicative subset**) in a commutative ring $R$ is a subset$S \subseteq R$ that is closed under multiplication ($s, t \in S \Rightarrow st \in S$), contains 1, and does not contain 0.
\end{definition}

Given such $S$, the **localization**$S^{-1}R$ is constructed exactly as the fraction field: take formal symbols$a/s$ with$a \in R$ and$s \in S$, and declare$a/s \sim b/t$ if there exists$u \in S$ with$u(at - bs) = 0$. The extra factor$u$ is needed when$R$ has zero divisors, to ensure the relation is transitive. When$R$ is a domain,$u$ can always be taken to be 1 and the condition simplifies to$at = bs$.

There is a canonical ring homomorphism $\phi: R \to S^{-1}R$ sending$a \mapsto a/1$. Its kernel consists of all$a \in R$ for which$sa = 0$ for some$s \in S$; in particular,$\phi$ is injective when$R$ is a domain. The map$\phi$ has a universal property: if$f: R \to T$ is a ring homomorphism such that$f(s)$ is invertible in$T$ for every$s \in S$, then$f$ factors uniquely through$S^{-1}R$.

Two important special cases illustrate the range of the construction. Taking $S = R \setminus \{0\}$ (for a domain) recovers the fraction field$\mathrm{Frac}(R)$. Taking$S = \{1, f, f^2, f^3, \ldots\}$ for a nonzero non-nilpotent element$f$ gives the **localization at$f$**, written$R_f$ or$R[1/f]$, which adjoins a multiplicative inverse for$f$ and all its powers. For instance,$\mathbb{Z}[1/2]$ is the ring of rational numbers with only powers of 2 in the denominator; it inverts all powers of 2 while leaving odd-denominator fractions out of reach.

The ideals of $S^{-1}R$ are in bijection with the ideals$I$ of$R$ satisfying$I \cap S = \emptyset$, via the map$I \mapsto S^{-1}I = \{a/s : a \in I, s \in S\}$. This **correspondence of ideals** is fundamental: prime ideals of$S^{-1}R$ correspond exactly to prime ideals of$R$ that do not meet$S$. More precisely, there is an order-preserving bijection:
$$\{\text{prime ideals of } S^{-1}R\} \longleftrightarrow \{\text{prime ideals } P \text{ of } R \text{ with } P \cap S = \emptyset\}.$$

**Example 1.** $\mathbb{Z}[1/2] = \{a/2^n : a \in \mathbb{Z}, n \geq 0\}$. This ring contains$\mathbb{Z}$ and inverts$2$, but not$3$ or$5$. The prime ideals of$\mathbb{Z}[1/2]$ correspond to prime ideals of$\mathbb{Z}$ not meeting$S = \{1, 2, 4, \ldots\}$: these are the primes$(p)$ with$p \neq 2$, i.e.,$(3), (5), (7), \ldots$. There is no prime$(2)$ in$\mathbb{Z}[1/2]$ because$2$ is a unit there.

**Example 2.** $\mathbb{Z}_{(p)} = \{a/b \in \mathbb{Q} : p \nmid b\}$ for a prime$p$. This is the localization of$\mathbb{Z}$ at$S = \mathbb{Z} \setminus (p)$ (all integers not divisible by$p$). The ring$\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$ has a unique maximal ideal$(p)\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$ and inverts all primes$\ell \neq p$. This is the foundational construction of$p$-adic arithmetic:$\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$ sees only the prime$p$.

**Example 3.** $k[x]_f = k[x, 1/f]$ for$f \in k[x]$ nonzero. If$f = x^2 - 1 = (x-1)(x+1)$, then$k[x, 1/(x^2-1)]$ inverts$x^2-1$ and all its powers. The prime ideals of$k[x]$ not meeting$\{1, f, f^2, \ldots\}$ are the primes not dividing$f$: i.e., all$(x-a)$ with$f(a) \neq 0$. Geometrically,$\text{Spec}(k[x]_{f})$ is the affine line with the zeros of$f$ removed — the complement of the vanishing locus of$f$.

**Example 4.** Localization of modules: if $M$ is an$R$-module, define$S^{-1}M = \{m/s : m \in M, s \in S\}$ with$m/s \sim m'/s'$ if$u(s'm - sm') = 0$ for some$u \in S$. Then$S^{-1}M$ is an$S^{-1}R$-module, and$S^{-1}(-)$ is an exact functor: it preserves short exact sequences. This is the key technical property used in the local-global principles.

**Example 5.** The total quotient ring: when $R$ is not a domain,$S = R \setminus \{\text{zero divisors}\}$ is a multiplicative set (the set of non-zero-divisors). The localization$S^{-1}R$ is the **total quotient ring** of$R$. For$R = k[x,y]/(xy)$ (the coordinate ring of two lines crossing), the total quotient ring is$k \times k$ (one field for each irreducible component). This generalizes the fraction field to rings with zero divisors.
