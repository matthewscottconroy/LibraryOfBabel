# 24.2.3 Localizing at a Prime: The Local Ring $R_{P}$

Among all localizations, the most important is localization at a prime ideal. If $P \subseteq R$ is a prime ideal, then the complement$S = R \setminus P$ is a multiplicative set:$1 \notin P$ so$1 \in S$, and if$a, b \notin P$ then$ab \notin P$ (since$P$ is prime), so$S$ is closed under multiplication. The localization$R_{P} = (R \setminus P)^{-1}R$ is called the **local ring of$R$ at$P$**.

\begin{definition}
A commutative ring $R$ is **local** if it has exactly one maximal ideal$\mathfrak{m}$. The pair$(R, \mathfrak{m})$ is called a local ring, and the quotient$R/\mathfrak{m}$ is the **residue field**.
\end{definition}

The ring $R_{P}$ is local in the technical sense: it has a unique maximal ideal, namely$PR_{P} = \{a/s : a \in P,\ s \notin P\}$. To see this, note that an element$a/s \in R_{P}$ is a unit if and only if$a \notin P$ (in which case$a \in S$ and$s/a$ is the inverse). So the non-units are exactly those$a/s$ with$a \in P$, which form the ideal$PR_{P}$; since all non-units lie in a single ideal, that ideal is the unique maximal ideal.

The residue field of $R_{P}$ is$R_{P}/PR_{P} \cong \mathrm{Frac}(R/P)$, since the composite$R \to R_{P} \to R_{P}/PR_{P}$ has kernel$P$ and the image satisfies the universal property of the fraction field of the domain$R/P$.

Geometrically, the prime ideal $P$ corresponds to an irreducible subvariety of$\mathrm{Spec}(R)$, and$R_{P}$ is the ring of "rational functions defined in a neighborhood of$P$." When$P = \mathfrak{m}$ is a maximal ideal corresponding to a single point,$R_{\mathfrak{m}}$ keeps track of local behavior near that point while discarding everything happening elsewhere. This principle of isolating one prime at a time is indispensable throughout number theory and algebraic geometry.

**Example 1.** $\mathbb{Z}_{(p)} = \{a/b \in \mathbb{Q} : p \nmid b\}$ for a prime$p$. This is the localization of$\mathbb{Z}$ at the prime ideal$(p)$. The unique maximal ideal is$(p)\mathbb{Z}_{(p)} = \{a/b : p \mid a, p \nmid b\}$, and the residue field is$\mathbb{F}_p = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$. The ring$\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$ sees only the$p$-adic behavior: its unit group consists of rationals$a/b$ with$p \nmid a$ and$p \nmid b$.

**Example 2.** $k[x]_{(x-a)}$ for a field$k$ and$a \in k$. Localizing the polynomial ring at the maximal ideal$(x-a)$ gives the ring of rational functions$f(x)/g(x)$ with$g(a) \neq 0$. This is the ring of "germs of rational functions at$a$." Its unique maximal ideal is$(x-a) \cdot k[x]_{(x-a)}$, consisting of rational functions vanishing at$a$. The residue field is$k$.

**Example 3.** $k[x,y]_{(x,y)}$ for a field$k$. Localization at the maximal ideal$(x,y)$ (corresponding to the origin) gives the ring of rational functions$f(x,y)/g(x,y)$ with$g(0,0) \neq 0$ — the local ring at the origin of the affine plane. The unique maximal ideal is$(x, y) \cdot k[x,y]_{(x,y)}$. This ring contains all the "local" information about the plane near the origin: its elements are power series-like objects (actually rational functions, but they can be expanded as power series). Its completion is the formal power series ring$k[[x,y]]$.

**Example 4.** Localization and prime ideal structure. In $R = k[x,y]$, the prime ideal$P = (x)$ corresponds to the$y$-axis. The local ring$R_{(x)}$ has elements$f(x,y)/g(x,y)$ with$x \nmid g$ (i.e.,$g$ is not divisible by$x$ in$k[x,y]$). Its prime ideals correspond to primes of$k[x,y]$ contained in$(x)$: these are$(0)$ and$(x)$ itself (and the maximal ideal$(x, y-a)$ for each$a$, which contains$x$, so these correspond to primes of$R_{(x)}$). The local ring$R_{(x)}$ is not a field (its maximal ideal is nontrivial), but it is a discrete valuation ring: every nonzero element has a well-defined order of vanishing along the$y$-axis.

**Example 5.** Nakayama's lemma: in a local ring $(R, \mathfrak{m})$, if$M$ is a finitely generated$R$-module and$\mathfrak{m}M = M$, then$M = 0$. This is the key tool for detecting when a finitely generated module is zero, or when a map is surjective, using local information. For example: if$M$ is finitely generated over$(R, \mathfrak{m})$ and$M/\mathfrak{m}M$ is generated by$n$ elements (as an$R/\mathfrak{m}$-vector space), then$M$ is generated by$n$ elements as an$R$-module. Nakayama's lemma is one of the most-used tools in local commutative algebra.
