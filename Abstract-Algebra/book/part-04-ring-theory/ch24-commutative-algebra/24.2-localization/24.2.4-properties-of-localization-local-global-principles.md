# 24.2.4 Properties of Localization; Local–Global Principles

Localization interacts cleanly with exact sequences, one of its most important technical properties. If $0 \to M' \to M \to M'' \to 0$ is an exact sequence of$R$-modules, then applying$S^{-1}(-)$ yields an exact sequence$0 \to S^{-1}M' \to S^{-1}M \to S^{-1}M'' \to 0$. We say that localization is an **exact functor**. In particular, it preserves both injections and surjections. This reflects the intuition that localization only "forgets" zero divisors and "remote" information; it does not introduce new relations among the remaining elements.

The exactness of localization underpins the **local–global principles**, which allow global questions about modules and maps to be decomposed into local questions.

\begin{theorem}[Local–Global Principle]
Let $R$ be a commutative ring and$M$ an$R$-module.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $M = 0$ if and only if$M_P = 0$ for every prime ideal$P \subseteq R$.
\item A homomorphism $f: M \to N$ is injective (resp. surjective, an isomorphism) if and only if$f_P: M_P \to N_P$ is injective (resp. surjective, an isomorphism) for every prime$P$.
\item It suffices to check at maximal ideals: $M = 0$ iff$M_{\mathfrak{m}} = 0$ for every maximal$\mathfrak{m}$, and similarly for$f$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

The proof of (1): if $M \neq 0$, pick any nonzero$m \in M$. The annihilator ideal$\mathrm{Ann}(m) = \{r \in R : rm = 0\}$ is a proper ideal, contained in some maximal ideal$\mathfrak{m}$. Then$m/1 \neq 0$ in$M_{\mathfrak{m}}$: if$m/1 = 0$ in$M_{\mathfrak{m}}$, there would exist$s \notin \mathfrak{m}$ with$sm = 0$, i.e.,$s \in \mathrm{Ann}(m) \subseteq \mathfrak{m}$, contradiction. So$M_{\mathfrak{m}} \neq 0$.

The power of these principles lies in the simplicity of local rings. A local ring $(R_P, PR_P)$ has a unique maximal ideal, so many complications — like the existence of nontrivial idempotents, or the failure of modules to be free — cannot occur locally. For instance, a finitely generated module over a local ring is free if and only if it is projective (Nakayama's lemma). By checking the local conditions (often straightforward), one deduces global conclusions that would be difficult to verify directly.

The ideal correspondence under localization provides another structural tool. The ideals of $S^{-1}R$ are in bijection with those ideals$I \subseteq R$ satisfying$I \cap S = \emptyset$: the maps$I \mapsto S^{-1}I$ and$J \mapsto J \cap R$ are inverse bijections between$\{\text{ideals of } S^{-1}R\}$ and$\{\text{ideals of } R \text{ not meeting } S\}$. For prime ideals this becomes the key correspondence:$\mathrm{Spec}(S^{-1}R) \cong \{P \in \mathrm{Spec}(R) : P \cap S = \emptyset\}$.

**Example 1.** Exactness of localization in practice. Consider $0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\cdot 2} \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to 0$. Localize at$S = \{1, 3, 9, 27, \ldots\}$ (powers of 3):$0 \to \mathbb{Z}[1/3] \xrightarrow{\cdot 2} \mathbb{Z}[1/3] \to \mathbb{Z}[1/3]/2\mathbb{Z}[1/3] \to 0$. Now$2$ is a unit in$\mathbb{Z}[1/3]$ (since$\gcd(2,3)=1$), so$\mathbb{Z}[1/3]/2\mathbb{Z}[1/3] = 0$. The localized sequence is$0 \to \mathbb{Z}[1/3] \xrightarrow{\sim} \mathbb{Z}[1/3] \to 0$, which is exact with an isomorphism in the middle. Compare with localization at$(2)$:$\mathbb{Z}_{(2)}/(2)\mathbb{Z}_{(2)} \cong \mathbb{F}_2$, nontrivial.

**Example 2.** Local-global for torsion-free. An $R$-module$M$ is torsion-free if$rm = 0 \Rightarrow r = 0$ or$m = 0$. For finitely generated modules over a Noetherian domain$R$:$M$ is torsion-free if and only if$M_P$ is a free$R_P$-module for every prime$P$ of height 0 (the minimal primes, which in a domain is just$(0)$). More practically: a finitely generated module$M$ over a Noetherian ring$R$ is projective if and only if$M_P$ is a free$R_P$-module for every prime$P$. This reduces projectivity to a local check.

**Example 3.** The local-global principle for flatness: an $R$-module$M$ is flat if and only if$M_P$ is a flat$R_P$-module for every prime$P$. Over a local ring$(R, \mathfrak{m})$, flat finitely generated modules are free (by Nakayama). So flatness of finitely generated modules is locally free, and the local-global principle says this is equivalent to global flatness. This is the algebraic underpinning of vector bundles: a locally free sheaf corresponds to a projective (equivalently, locally free) module.

**Example 4.** Gluing via localization. Suppose $R = k[x, y]$ and$f_1 = x$,$f_2 = y$,$f_1 + f_2 = x + y$ generate the unit ideal (as$x \cdot 1 + y \cdot 0 + \ldots$ can generate with appropriate combination... actually$1 \in (x,y)$ if we allow rational coefficients). Over$\{f_1 = x, f_2 = 1-x\}$: then$R_{f_1} = k[x, x^{-1}, y]$ and$R_{f_2} = k[(1-x)^{-1}, y]$. The local-global principle: a module over$R$ is determined by its restrictions to$R_{f_1}$ and$R_{f_2}$ together with how they agree on$R_{f_1 f_2}$. This is the algebraic version of "sheaves are determined by open covers," and is the foundation of scheme theory.

**Example 5.** Localization and depth. The **depth** of a module $M$ over a local ring$(R, \mathfrak{m})$ is the length of the longest regular sequence in$\mathfrak{m}$ on$M$. This local invariant governs when$M$ satisfies Cohen-Macaulay or Gorenstein properties. The global version: depth$(M, I)$ (for an ideal$I$) is the infimum of depths of$M_P$ over primes$P \supseteq I$. The local-global principle ensures that depth can be computed prime by prime, making it accessible via localization even for non-local rings. Auslander–Buchsbaum's formula ($\text{proj.dim}(M) + \text{depth}(M) = \text{depth}(R)$ for finitely generated modules over local rings) is one of the deepest applications of localization in homological commutative algebra.
