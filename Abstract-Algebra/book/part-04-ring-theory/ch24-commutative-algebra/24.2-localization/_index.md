# 24.2 Localization

Localization is the algebraic operation of formally inverting a subset of a ring's elements. Given a commutative ring $R$ and a multiplicative set $S \subseteq R$ (a subset closed under multiplication containing $1$), the localization $S^{-1}R$ is the ring of fractions $\{a/s : a \in R, s \in S\}$ with the equivalence relation $a/s = b/t$ if and only if $u(at - bs) = 0$ for some $u \in S$. The natural map $R \to S^{-1}R$ sending $a \mapsto a/1$ is a ring homomorphism, and its kernel is $\{a \in R : sa = 0 \text{ for some } s \in S\}$. When $R$ is a domain and $S = R \setminus \{0\}$, the localization is the fraction field $\text{Frac}(R)$, the smallest field containing $R$.

The most important case in algebraic geometry and number theory is localization at a prime ideal $\mathfrak{p}$: take $S = R \setminus \mathfrak{p}$ (which is multiplicative because $\mathfrak{p}$ is prime), and form $R_{\mathfrak{p}} = S^{-1}R$. The ring $R_{\mathfrak{p}}$ is a local ring — it has a unique maximal ideal $\mathfrak{p} R_{\mathfrak{p}} = \{a/s : a \in \mathfrak{p}, s \notin \mathfrak{p}\}$ — and the residue field $R_{\mathfrak{p}} / \mathfrak{p} R_{\mathfrak{p}}$ is isomorphic to the fraction field of $R/\mathfrak{p}$. Localization at $\mathfrak{p}$ "zooms in" on the ring's behavior near the prime $\mathfrak{p}$, discarding all information about other primes. For $R = \mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathfrak{p} = (p)$, the localization $\mathbb{Z}_{(p)} = \{a/b : p \nmid b\}$ is the subring of $\mathbb{Q}$ in which only multiples of $p$ are non-invertible.

The power of localization lies in the local-global principle: many properties of a ring $R$ or a module $M$ can be checked locally, meaning by verifying them for every localization $R_{\mathfrak{p}}$ (or $M_{\mathfrak{p}}$) at a prime ideal $\mathfrak{p}$. For instance, an $R$-module homomorphism $f \colon M \to N$ is injective (respectively surjective) if and only if the localized map $f_{\mathfrak{p}} \colon M_{\mathfrak{p}} \to N_{\mathfrak{p}}$ is injective (respectively surjective) for every prime $\mathfrak{p}$. This reduces global questions — which may be hard to verify directly — to local questions about the simpler rings $R_{\mathfrak{p}}$, and is a foundational technique in the proof of the Nullstellensatz, in the structure theory of flat modules, and in the definition of regularity and depth.
