# 24.3.1 Integral Elements; Integral Closure

The notion of integrality generalizes algebraic numbers from field extensions to ring extensions. Suppose $R \subseteq S$ is an inclusion of commutative rings. An element$\alpha \in S$ is **integral over$R$** if it satisfies a monic polynomial equation with coefficients in$R$: there exist$a_0, \ldots, a_{n-1} \in R$ with
$$\alpha^n + a_{n-1}\alpha^{n-1} + \cdots + a_1\alpha + a_0 = 0.$$
The monic condition is essential — without it, every element of every ring would trivially "satisfy" some polynomial by dividing through by the leading coefficient — and it is what makes integrality a ring-theoretic condition rather than a field-theoretic one.

\begin{definition}
Let $R \subseteq S$ be an extension of commutative rings. An element$\alpha \in S$ is **integral over$R$** if there exist$n \geq 1$ and$a_0, \ldots, a_{n-1} \in R$ such that$\alpha^n + a_{n-1}\alpha^{n-1} + \cdots + a_0 = 0$. The **integral closure** of$R$ in$S$, written$\bar{R}^S$ or$\overline{R}$ when$S$ is understood, is the set of all elements of$S$ integral over$R$. The extension$R \subseteq S$ is **integral** if every element of$S$ is integral over$R$.
\end{definition}

Several equivalent characterizations illuminate what integrality really means. An element $\alpha \in S$ is integral over$R$ if and only if the subring$R[\alpha] \subseteq S$ is a finitely generated$R$-module (spanned by$1, \alpha, \ldots, \alpha^{n-1}$ where$n$ is the degree of the monic relation). Equivalently,$\alpha$ is integral over$R$ if and only if$\alpha$ is contained in some subring of$S$ that contains$R$ and is finitely generated as an$R$-module. This last formulation makes it easy to prove that the integral elements form a ring: if$\alpha$ and$\beta$ are integral over$R$, then$R[\alpha, \beta]$ is a finitely generated$R$-module (as a product of finitely generated modules over a Noetherian ring) containing$\alpha + \beta$ and$\alpha\beta$, so those are integral too.

The integral closure of $R$ in$S$ is a subring of$S$ containing$R$. If$\bar{R}^S = R$ (no new integral elements exist in$S$ beyond those already in$R$), we say$R$ is **integrally closed** in$S$. If$R$ is a domain and is integrally closed in its own fraction field$\mathrm{Frac}(R)$, we say$R$ is **integrally closed** (or **normal**) without further qualification.

The paradigm example: the ring of integers $\mathbb{Z}$ is integrally closed in its fraction field$\mathbb{Q}$ (any rational number satisfying a monic integer polynomial is already an integer), and for a number field$K$, the ring of integers$\mathcal{O}_K$ is the integral closure of$\mathbb{Z}$ in$K$. This is the foundational setup of algebraic number theory.

Integrality satisfies a transitivity property: if $R \subseteq S \subseteq T$ with$S$ integral over$R$ and$T$ integral over$S$, then$T$ is integral over$R$. This follows because any monic polynomial over$S$ satisfied by$\alpha \in T$ has coefficients integral over$R$, and one can form a larger finitely generated$R$-module containing$\alpha$.

**Example 1.** $\mathbb{Z} \subseteq \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{2}]$: the element$\sqrt{2}$ satisfies$x^2 - 2 = 0$ (monic, coefficients in$\mathbb{Z}$), so$\sqrt{2}$ is integral over$\mathbb{Z}$. More generally,$a + b\sqrt{2}$ for$a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$ is integral over$\mathbb{Z}$ (it satisfies$x^2 - 2ax + (a^2 - 2b^2) = 0$, which is monic with integer coefficients). So$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{2}]$ is integral over$\mathbb{Z}$, and in this case$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{2}]$ is the full ring of integers of$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ (since 2 is squarefree).

**Example 2.** A non-integral element: is $1/2 \in \mathbb{Q}$ integral over$\mathbb{Z}$? If$(1/2)^n + a_{n-1}(1/2)^{n-1} + \cdots + a_0 = 0$, multiply through by$2^n$:$1 + a_{n-1} \cdot 2 + \cdots + a_0 \cdot 2^n = 0$. But the left side is$1 \pmod 2$, hence odd, hence nonzero. Contradiction. So$1/2$ is not integral over$\mathbb{Z}$. This confirms$\mathbb{Z}$ is integrally closed: the only rationals integral over$\mathbb{Z}$ are the integers themselves.

**Example 3.** Integral closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ in$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})$: the ring of integers$\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})} = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$. Elements$a + b\sqrt{-5}$ with$a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$ satisfy$(x - (a+b\sqrt{-5}))(x - (a - b\sqrt{-5})) = x^2 - 2ax + (a^2 + 5b^2)$, monic with integer coefficients. So every element of$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is integral over$\mathbb{Z}$. (For$d \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, the ring of integers of$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ is$\mathbb{Z}[(1+\sqrt{d})/2]$, slightly larger; but for$d = -5 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, it is$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$.)

**Example 4.** Non-integrally closed ring: $R = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-3}]$. The element$\omega = (1+\sqrt{-3})/2$ satisfies$\omega^2 - \omega + 1 = 0$ (monic over$\mathbb{Z}$!), so$\omega$ is integral over$\mathbb{Z}$, hence over$R$. But$\omega \notin R = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-3}]$. So$R$ is NOT integrally closed: the integral closure of$R$ in$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})$ strictly contains$R$, and equals$\mathbb{Z}[\omega] = \mathbb{Z}[(1+\sqrt{-3})/2]$, the Eisenstein integers.

**Example 5.** Integral closure of a polynomial ring: the integral closure of $k[t^2, t^3] \subseteq k[t]$. The element$t \in k[t]$ satisfies$x^2 - t^2 = 0$ (monic over$k[t^2, t^3]$?). Actually$t^2 \in k[t^2, t^3]$ but we need a monic polynomial over$k[t^2, t^3]$:$t$ satisfies$x^2 - t^2 = 0$ where$t^2 \in k[t^2, t^3]$. So$t$ is integral over$k[t^2, t^3]$, and the integral closure of$k[t^2, t^3]$ in$k(t)$ contains$t$. Since$k[t]$ is a UFD (hence integrally closed) and$k[t^2, t^3] \subseteq k[t] \subseteq k(t)$, the integral closure of$k[t^2, t^3]$ in$k(t)$ is exactly$k[t]$. Geometrically,$k[t^2, t^3]$ is the coordinate ring of the cuspidal cubic$\{y^2 = x^3\}$, and the normalization (integral closure) corresponds to resolving the cusp: the normalization map$k[t] \to k[t^2, t^3]$ corresponds to the map$t \mapsto (t^2, t^3)$ parametrizing the curve.
