# 24.3.2 The Going-Up and Going-Down Theorems

When $S$ is integral over$R$, the prime ideals of$S$ lie over those of$R$ in a controlled way. Recall that a prime$Q \subseteq S$ **lies over** a prime$P \subseteq R$ if$Q \cap R = P$. The going-up and going-down theorems describe how these "lying-over" relationships behave as we move along chains of primes.

\begin{theorem}[Lying-Over Theorem]
Let $R \subseteq S$ be an integral extension. For every prime ideal$P \subseteq R$, there exists a prime ideal$Q \subseteq S$ with$Q \cap R = P$.
\end{theorem}

The proof uses localization: pass to $R_P \subseteq S_P = (R \setminus P)^{-1}S$ (which is still integral over$R_P$), and find a maximal ideal$Q'$ in$S_P$; its contraction to$R_P$ is the maximal ideal$PR_P$, so$Q' \cap R_P = PR_P$, and pulling back to$S$ gives a prime lying over$P$.

\begin{theorem}[Going-Up Theorem]
Let $S$ be integral over$R$, and let$P \subseteq P'$ be primes in$R$. If$Q \subseteq S$ lies over$P$, then there exists a prime$Q' \supseteq Q$ in$S$ lying over$P'$.
\end{theorem}

The proof reduces to the lying-over theorem. Pass to $R/P \subseteq S/Q$ (which is integral since quotients of integral extensions are integral and domains), and apply the lying-over theorem to find a prime of$S/Q$ lying over$P'/P \subseteq R/P$. Lifting back to$S$ gives the desired$Q'$.

\begin{theorem}[Going-Down Theorem]
Let $R$ be an integrally closed domain and$S$ an integral extension that is also a domain. Let$P' \subseteq P$ be primes in$R$ and let$Q \subseteq S$ lie over$P$. Then there exists a prime$Q' \subseteq Q$ in$S$ lying over$P'$.
\end{theorem}

The integrally closed condition on $R$ is genuinely necessary — the theorem fails without it. The standard counterexample: take$R = k[t^2, t^3] \subseteq S = k[t]$. Here$R$ is not integrally closed (its integral closure is$k[t]$ as discussed in §24.3.1). The prime$(t) \cap R = (t^2, t^3)$, and one can construct chains in$\mathrm{Spec}(R)$ that cannot be lifted downward in$\mathrm{Spec}(S)$.

Together, these theorems show that the map $\mathrm{Spec}(S) \to \mathrm{Spec}(R)$ (induced by the inclusion$R \subseteq S$) is surjective: every prime of$R$ is the image of some prime of$S$. More precisely, they control the fiber structure: chains of primes in$R$ can always be lifted to chains of primes in$S$ (both upward and downward, under appropriate hypotheses). A key consequence is the equality of Krull dimensions:

\begin{corollary}
If $S$ is integral over$R$ and both are domains, then$\dim R = \dim S$.
\end{corollary}

The proof: any chain $P_0 \subsetneq P_1 \subsetneq \cdots \subsetneq P_n$ in$\mathrm{Spec}(R)$ lifts by going-up to a chain$Q_0 \subsetneq Q_1 \subsetneq \cdots \subsetneq Q_n$ in$\mathrm{Spec}(S)$ (each$Q_i$ properly contains$Q_{i-1}$ because$Q_i \cap R = P_i \supsetneq P_{i-1} = Q_{i-1} \cap R$). Similarly, chains in$S$ contract to chains of the same length in$R$ (if$Q_i \cap R = Q_j \cap R$, then$Q_i$ and$Q_j$ lie over the same prime in$R$, but they are incomparable, contradicting...). The argument requires going-down for the other direction.

**Example 1.** $\mathbb{Z} \subseteq \mathbb{Z}[i]$ (Gaussian integers). The lying-over theorem: for each prime$p \in \mathbb{Z}$, there is a prime of$\mathbb{Z}[i]$ lying over$(p)$. Concretely:
- $p = 2$:$(1+i)$ lies over$(2)$, since$(1+i) \cap \mathbb{Z} = (2)$ (as$2 = -i(1+i)^2$, so$(2) \subseteq (1+i)$, and$(1+i)$ is prime of norm 2).
- $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$:$(p)$ splits as$(a+bi)(a-bi)$ in$\mathbb{Z}[i]$, giving two primes over$(p)$.
- $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$:$(p)$ remains prime in$\mathbb{Z}[i]$ (inert).

**Example 2.** Going-up in $\mathbb{Z} \subseteq \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$. The chain$(0) \subsetneq (2)$ in$\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{Z})$. Take$Q = (0)$ in$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, lying over$(0) \in \mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{Z})$. Going-up says we can find$Q' \supseteq (0)$ lying over$(2)$. The prime$(2, 1+\sqrt{-5})$ of$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ satisfies$(2, 1+\sqrt{-5}) \cap \mathbb{Z} = (2)$ and contains$(0)$. So$Q' = (2, 1+\sqrt{-5})$.

**Example 3.** Going-down in $\mathbb{Z} \subseteq \mathbb{Z}[i]$. The chain$(0) \subsetneq (5)$ in$\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{Z})$. The prime$5 = (2+i)(2-i)$ factors in$\mathbb{Z}[i]$. Take$Q = (2+i)$, lying over$(5)$. Going-down says there exists$Q' \subseteq (2+i)$ lying over$(0)$. Clearly$Q' = (0)$ works (the zero ideal of$\mathbb{Z}[i]$ lies over$(0) \in \mathbb{Z}$). The theorem guarantees such a$Q'$ exists; here it's trivial.

**Example 4.** Consequence for dimension: $\mathbb{Z} \subseteq \mathbb{Z}[i]$ both have Krull dimension 1 (both are Dedekind domains:$\dim \mathbb{Z} = 1$ since every nonzero prime is maximal). The corollary confirms$\dim \mathbb{Z}[i] = \dim \mathbb{Z} = 1$.

**Example 5.** Going-up in algebraic geometry. Let $k$ be a field,$R = k[x]$,$S = k[x,y]/(y^2 - x)$. The inclusion$R \hookrightarrow S$ (where$x$ maps to the image of$x$) is integral:$y$ satisfies$y^2 - x = 0$ (monic over$R$). Geometrically,$\mathrm{Spec}(S) \to \mathrm{Spec}(R)$ is the map$\mathbb{A}^1 \to \mathbb{A}^1$ given by$t \mapsto t^2$ (the parabola$y^2 = x$ projected onto the$x$-axis). The chain$(0) \subsetneq (x-1)$ in$\mathrm{Spec}(R)$: over$(0)$ lies$(0)$ in$\mathrm{Spec}(S)$; going-up to$(x-1)$ gives$(x-1, y-1)$ and$(x-1, y+1)$ (two primes lying over$(x-1)$, corresponding to the two preimage points$y = \pm 1$ above$x = 1$). Both lie over$(x-1)$ and contain$(0)$, consistent with going-up.
