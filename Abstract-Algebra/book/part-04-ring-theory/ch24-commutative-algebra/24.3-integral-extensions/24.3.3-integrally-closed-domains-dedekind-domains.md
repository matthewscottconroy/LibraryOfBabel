# 24.3.3 Integrally Closed Domains; Dedekind Domains

An integral domain $R$ is **integrally closed** (also called **normal**) if every element of$\mathrm{Frac}(R)$ that is integral over$R$ already belongs to$R$. Intuitively,$R$ contains all the "algebraic numbers" it is forced to contain by its own arithmetic. The class of integrally closed domains includes all UFDs: in a UFD, if$a/b$ (written in lowest terms, so$\gcd(a,b) = 1$) satisfies a monic polynomial$x^n + c_{n-1}x^{n-1} + \cdots + c_0$ with coefficients in$R$, then substituting$a/b$ and multiplying by$b^n$ gives$a^n + c_{n-1}a^{n-1}b + \cdots + c_0 b^n = 0$, so$b \mid a^n$; since$\gcd(a,b)=1$, we get$b \mid 1$, so$b$ is a unit, and$a/b \in R$. In particular,$\mathbb{Z}$,$F[x]$, and$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ are all integrally closed.

\begin{definition}
An integral domain $R$ is **integrally closed** if the integral closure of$R$ in$\mathrm{Frac}(R)$ equals$R$ itself: every$r \in \mathrm{Frac}(R)$ satisfying a monic polynomial over$R$ lies in$R$.
\par
A **Dedekind domain** is a Noetherian integrally closed domain of Krull dimension 1.
\end{definition}

The Krull dimension 1 condition means that every nonzero prime ideal is maximal. Equivalently, the only prime ideals are $(0)$ and the maximal ideals — so the "spectrum" of a Dedekind domain consists of a generic point and a discrete set of closed points, one for each maximal ideal. This is the simplest possible nontrivial prime ideal structure for a domain.

The key theorem characterizing Dedekind domains is the unique factorization of ideals:

\begin{theorem}
Let $R$ be a Dedekind domain. Every nonzero proper ideal$I \subseteq R$ factors uniquely (up to order) as a product of prime ideals:$I = P_1^{a_1} \cdots P_k^{a_k}$ with each$P_i$ a nonzero prime and each$a_i \geq 1$. Moreover, the nonzero fractional ideals form a group under multiplication (the **ideal group**), freely generated by the nonzero prime ideals.
\end{theorem}

The principal fractional ideals form a subgroup, and the quotient is the **ideal class group** $\mathrm{Cl}(R)$. When$\mathrm{Cl}(R) = 0$, every ideal is principal and$R$ is a PID (and hence a UFD). The class group measures how far$R$ is from being a PID: it is the "defect" of unique factorization in$R$.

Dedekind domains arise everywhere in number theory and algebraic geometry. For any number field $K$ (a finite extension of$\mathbb{Q}$), the ring of integers$\mathcal{O}_K$ is a Dedekind domain: it is Noetherian (as a finitely generated$\mathbb{Z}$-module), integrally closed by definition (it is the integral closure of$\mathbb{Z}$ in$K$), and has Krull dimension 1 because every nonzero prime of$\mathcal{O}_K$ lies over a prime$(p) \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$ and is therefore maximal (primes lying over a maximal ideal in an integral extension are maximal, by the going-up theorem). The unique factorization of ideals in$\mathcal{O}_K$ is the foundation of algebraic number theory: it replaces the failure of unique element factorization (in rings like$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$) with unique ideal factorization.

**Example 1.** $\mathbb{Z}$: Noetherian (PID), integrally closed (UFD), Krull dimension 1 (all nonzero primes$(p)$ are maximal). Dedekind domain. In$\mathbb{Z}$, every nonzero ideal is a power of a prime ideal:$(12) = (2)^2 \cdot (3)$ — this is the content of unique prime factorization.

**Example 2.** $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})} = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$: Dedekind domain, but NOT a PID. The ideal$(2, 1+\sqrt{-5})$ is not principal. The class group is$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ (class number 2): the only nontrivial ideal class is$[(2, 1+\sqrt{-5})]$, and its square is principal:$(2, 1+\sqrt{-5})^2 = (2)$. The unique ideal factorization:$(6) = (2)(3) = (2, 1+\sqrt{-5})^2 (3, 1+\sqrt{-5})(3, 1-\sqrt{-5})$.

**Example 3.** $k[x]$ for a field$k$: Noetherian, integrally closed (UFD), Krull dimension 1. Dedekind domain. Every ideal is principal (it is a PID). Class group is trivial.

**Example 4.** $k[x, y]/(y^2 - x^3)$ (coordinate ring of the cuspidal cubic): this ring is NOT integrally closed. The element$t = y/x$ in the fraction field satisfies$t^2 = x$ (since$y^2 = x^3$), so$t$ is integral over the ring, but$t \notin k[x,y]/(y^2-x^3)$. The integral closure is$k[t]$, corresponding to the normalization (desingularization) of the cusp. This ring has dimension 1 and is Noetherian, but is not integrally closed — hence not Dedekind.

**Example 5.** Smooth affine curves as Dedekind domains: if $C$ is a smooth (nonsingular) affine algebraic curve over an algebraically closed field$k$, its coordinate ring$k[C]$ is a Dedekind domain. The smoothness ensures integrally closed: at each point, the local ring is a DVR (discrete valuation ring), and a domain all of whose localizations are DVRs is integrally closed. The Noetherian and dimension-1 conditions are geometric (curves have dimension 1). The class group$\mathrm{Cl}(k[C])$ is related to the Picard group of$C$, connecting commutative algebra directly to algebraic geometry.
