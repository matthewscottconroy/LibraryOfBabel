# 24.4.2 Dimension of $F[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ Is$n$

The polynomial ring $F[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ over a field$F$ has Krull dimension exactly$n$, matching the intuitive dimension of affine$n$-space$\mathbb{A}^n_F$. This is one of the fundamental results of commutative algebra, confirming that the purely ring-theoretic definition of dimension (chains of primes) agrees with geometric intuition.

Establishing the lower bound is easy: the chain
$$(0) \subsetneq (x_1) \subsetneq (x_1, x_2) \subsetneq \cdots \subsetneq (x_1, \ldots, x_n)$$
is a chain of prime ideals of length $n$. Each ideal$(x_1,\ldots,x_k)$ is prime because the quotient$F[x_1,\ldots,x_n]/(x_1,\ldots,x_k) \cong F[x_{k+1},\ldots,x_n]$ is an integral domain (a polynomial ring over a field). So$\dim F[x_1,\ldots,x_n] \geq n$.

The upper bound — that no chain of primes can have length greater than $n$ — is the harder direction. It follows from the **Krull principal ideal theorem** generalized to several generators: in a Noetherian ring, a prime$P$ that is minimal over an ideal generated by$r$ elements has height$\mathrm{ht}(P) \leq r$. Since every maximal ideal of$F[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ is generated by$n$ elements (of the form$(x_1 - a_1, \ldots, x_n - a_n)$ when$F$ is algebraically closed, or more generally has height$\leq n$), no chain can exceed length$n$.

A more elegant approach uses the **Noether normalization theorem**: for any finitely generated $F$-algebra$A$ (not necessarily a domain), there exist algebraically independent elements$y_1,\ldots,y_d \in A$ such that$A$ is integral over$F[y_1,\ldots,y_d]$. Applying the going-up theorem (§24.3.2),$\dim A = \dim F[y_1,\ldots,y_d] = d$. For$A = F[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ itself (a domain), taking$y_i = x_i$ gives$d = n$.

\begin{theorem}[Noether Normalization]
Let $A$ be a finitely generated algebra over a field$F$, and let$A$ be a domain. Then there exist$d \geq 0$ and algebraically independent elements$y_1,\ldots,y_d \in A$ such that$A$ is a finite integral extension of$F[y_1,\ldots,y_d]$. Moreover,$d = \dim A$.
\end{theorem}

To see why this matches geometry: the prime ideals of $F[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ correspond (when$F$ is algebraically closed) to the irreducible subvarieties of$\mathbb{A}^n_F$. A chain of primes$P_0 \subsetneq \cdots \subsetneq P_n$ corresponds to a chain of irreducible varieties$V(P_0) \supsetneq \cdots \supsetneq V(P_n)$, from a subvariety of dimension$n$ down to a point. The statement$\dim F[x_1,\ldots,x_n] = n$ is the algebraic statement that the longest chains of subvarieties of$\mathbb{A}^n_F$ have length$n$, which is exactly the geometric dimension.

**Example 1.** $F[x]$: dimension 1. The chain$(0) \subsetneq (f)$ for any irreducible$f$ has length 1. Since$(f)$ is maximal (it is a prime and$F[x]$ is a PID, hence all nonzero primes are maximal), no longer chain exists.

**Example 2.** $F[x,y]$: dimension 2. The chain$(0) \subsetneq (x) \subsetneq (x, y)$ has length 2. In geometric terms:$\{(0)\}$ corresponds to all of$\mathbb{A}^2_F$,$(x)$ corresponds to the line$x = 0$, and$(x,y)$ corresponds to the origin.

**Example 3.** $F[x_1,x_2,x_3]/(x_1^2 + x_2^2 + x_3^2 - 1)$: the coordinate ring of the unit sphere$S^2$. By Noether normalization, the sphere is integral over$F[y_1, y_2]$ for some algebraically independent elements; the dimension is 2 (a surface). A chain$(0) \subsetneq P_1 \subsetneq \mathfrak{m}$ (where$P_1$ corresponds to a "line on the sphere," e.g., the great circle$x_1 = 0$, and$\mathfrak{m}$ is a point on that circle) witnesses$\dim \geq 2$.

**Example 4.** $F[x_1,\ldots,x_n]/I$ for a prime ideal$I$: dimension$n - \mathrm{ht}(I)$ for a "good" ideal. More precisely, by Noether normalization applied to the domain$F[x_1,\ldots,x_n]/I$, the dimension is$n - \mathrm{ht}(I)$ when$I$ is equidimensional (all minimal primes of$I$ have the same height). For example,$F[x,y,z]/(xy) = F[x,y,z]/(xy)$ has dimension 2 (it is the coordinate ring of the union of two planes in$\mathbb{A}^3$, which is a surface), and indeed$\mathrm{ht}(xy) = 1$ and$3 - 1 = 2$.

**Example 5.** The catenary property: a Noetherian ring $R$ is **catenary** if for every pair of primes$P \subsetneq Q$, all maximal chains of primes between$P$ and$Q$ have the same length. The ring$F[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ is catenary (in fact, universally catenary: all its finitely generated extensions are catenary). This means Krull dimension is well-behaved:$\mathrm{ht}(P) + \dim R/P = \dim R$ for every prime$P$ in an equidimensional catenary ring. For$R = F[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ and$P = (x_1,\ldots,x_k)$:$\mathrm{ht}(P) = k$ and$\dim R/P = \dim F[x_{k+1},\ldots,x_n] = n-k$, confirming$k + (n-k) = n = \dim R$.
