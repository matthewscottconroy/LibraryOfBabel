# 24.4.3 Hilbert's Nullstellensatz (Statement)

The Nullstellensatz is the theorem that makes algebraic geometry algebraic. It establishes a precise dictionary between the geometry of polynomial zero sets and the algebra of polynomial ideals, and without it the two subjects would remain loosely connected analogies rather than a unified theory.

To state it, let $k$ be an algebraically closed field and let$I \subseteq k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ be an ideal. Define the **zero set**$V(I) = \{a \in \mathbb{A}^n_k : f(a) = 0 \text{ for all } f \in I\}$, the common vanishing locus of all polynomials in$I$. Define also the **ideal of vanishing**$\mathcal{I}(V) = \{f \in k[x_1,\ldots,x_n] : f(a) = 0 \text{ for all } a \in V\}$ for any subset$V \subseteq \mathbb{A}^n_k$.

\begin{theorem}[Hilbert's Nullstellensatz]
Let $k$ be an algebraically closed field. For any ideal$I \subseteq k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{(Weak Nullstellensatz)} If $V(I) = \emptyset$, then$I = k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ (i.e.,$1 \in I$).
\item \textbf{(Strong Nullstellensatz)} $\mathcal{I}(V(I)) = \sqrt{I}$, where$\sqrt{I} = \{f : f^m \in I \text{ for some } m \geq 1\}$ is the radical of$I$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

The weak Nullstellensatz is a consequence of the fact that $k$ is algebraically closed: if$I \neq (1)$, then$I$ is contained in a maximal ideal$\mathfrak{m}$, and any maximal ideal of$k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ is of the form$(x_1 - a_1, \ldots, x_n - a_n)$ for some$a_i \in k$ (this is the content of the Nullstellensatz for maximal ideals, provable by Zariski's lemma: if$k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]/\mathfrak{m}$ is a field that is a finitely generated$k$-algebra, it must equal$k$). So$V(I) \supseteq V(\mathfrak{m}) = \{(a_1,\ldots,a_n)\} \neq \emptyset$.

The strong Nullstellensatz follows from the weak one by a trick due to Rabinowitsch: to show $f \in \mathcal{I}(V(I))$ implies$f \in \sqrt{I}$, introduce a new variable$t$ and consider the ideal$J = I + (1 - tf) \subseteq k[x_1,\ldots,x_n,t]$. The variety$V(J)$ is empty: any point$(a,b)$ in$V(J)$ would have$a \in V(I)$ (so$f(a) = 0$) and$1 - bf(a) = 0$ (so$f(a) = 1/b \neq 0$), contradiction. By the weak Nullstellensatz,$J = (1)$, so$1 \in J$: write$1 = \sum g_i f_i + h(1-tf)$ in$k[x_1,\ldots,x_n,t]$ with$f_i \in I$. Set$t = 1/f$ and clear denominators (multiply by a high power of$f$):$f^N = \sum g_i(x, 1/f) f^N f_i$, which shows$f^N \in I$.

The radical condition is necessary and tight. The one-directional inclusion $\sqrt{I} \subseteq \mathcal{I}(V(I))$ is clear: if$g^m \in I$ then$g^m$ vanishes on$V(I)$, so$g$ vanishes on$V(I)$ too. The Nullstellensatz says the opposite inclusion holds — and specifically, the algebraic closed field hypothesis is needed. Over$\mathbb{R}$, the ideal$(x^2 + 1) \subseteq \mathbb{R}[x]$ has$V(x^2+1) = \emptyset$ but$1 \notin (x^2+1)$, so the weak Nullstellensatz fails.

A key corollary gives the fundamental bijection of classical algebraic geometry:

\begin{corollary}
There is an inclusion-reversing bijection between:
\begin{itemize}
\item radical ideals $I \subseteq k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ (those with$\sqrt{I} = I$), and
\item algebraic subvarieties $V \subseteq \mathbb{A}^n_k$ (zero sets of polynomial ideals),
\end{itemize}
given by $I \mapsto V(I)$ and$V \mapsto \mathcal{I}(V)$. Under this bijection, prime ideals correspond to irreducible varieties, and maximal ideals correspond to points.
\end{corollary}

**Example 1.** In $\mathbb{C}[x,y]$: the ideal$I = (x^2, y)$. The zero set$V(I) = \{(0,0)\}$ (both$x^2$ and$y$ vanish only at the origin). The radical$\sqrt{I} = (x, y)$ (since$x^2 \in I$ implies$x \in \sqrt{I}$). And$\mathcal{I}(\{(0,0)\}) = (x,y)$. So$\mathcal{I}(V(I)) = (x,y) = \sqrt{I}$. The Nullstellensatz confirms this.

**Example 2.** The ring $\mathbb{C}[x,y]/(xy) \cong \mathbb{C}[x,y]/(xy)$: the variety$V(xy) = V(x) \cup V(y)$ (the two coordinate axes in$\mathbb{A}^2_{\mathbb{C}}$). The radical of$(xy)$: since$xy$ is squarefree,$\sqrt{(xy)} = (xy)$. The Nullstellensatz says$\mathcal{I}(V(xy)) = (xy)$, meaning the only polynomials vanishing on both axes are multiples of$xy$.

**Example 3.** The Nullstellensatz and maximal ideals. In $\mathbb{C}[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$, every maximal ideal is of the form$(x_1 - a_1, \ldots, x_n - a_n)$ for some$(a_1,\ldots,a_n) \in \mathbb{C}^n$. This is not true over$\mathbb{R}$: in$\mathbb{R}[x]$, the ideal$(x^2+1)$ is maximal with quotient$\mathbb{C} \neq \mathbb{R}$.

**Example 4.** Over a non-algebraically closed field, the Nullstellensatz fails. In $\mathbb{Q}[x]$, the ideal$(x^2 - 2)$ has$V(x^2-2) = \emptyset$ in$\mathbb{Q}$ (no rational root), but$(x^2-2) \neq (1)$. And$(x^2-2)$ is already radical (it is prime:$\mathbb{Q}[x]/(x^2-2)$ is a domain), but$\mathcal{I}(\emptyset) = \mathbb{Q}[x] \neq (x^2-2)$. The algebraically closed hypothesis is essential.

**Example 5.** Application to systems of polynomial equations. Given polynomials $f_1, \ldots, f_m \in \mathbb{C}[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$, the system$f_1 = \cdots = f_m = 0$ has no solution in$\mathbb{C}^n$ if and only if$1 \in (f_1,\ldots,f_m)$, i.e., there exist polynomials$g_1,\ldots,g_m$ with$\sum g_i f_i = 1$. This certificate of infeasibility (a "Nullstellensatz certificate") can be computed by Gröbner basis methods. If$1 \notin (f_1,\ldots,f_m)$, the system has a solution — finding it then requires further work.
