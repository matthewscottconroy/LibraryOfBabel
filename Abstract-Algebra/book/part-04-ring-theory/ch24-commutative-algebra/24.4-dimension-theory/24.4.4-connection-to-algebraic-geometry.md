# 24.4.4 Connection to Algebraic Geometry: $\mathrm{Spec}(R)$

The tools developed throughout this chapter — Noetherian rings, localization, integral extensions, Krull dimension, the Nullstellensatz — do not merely provide examples and techniques for commutative algebra in isolation. They are the algebraic foundation of modern algebraic geometry, where geometric objects are defined and studied through their rings of functions.

The central construction is the **spectrum**. For any commutative ring $R$, the set$\mathrm{Spec}(R)$ of prime ideals of$R$ is given a topology — the **Zariski topology** — in which the closed sets are the sets$V(I) = \{P \in \mathrm{Spec}(R) : I \subseteq P\}$ for ideals$I \subseteq R$. Under this topology, maximal ideals correspond to "closed points" (the geometric points of the variety), while non-maximal primes correspond to "generic points" of irreducible subvarieties. The nilradical$\bigcap_{P} P$ (the intersection of all primes) represents the "nilpotent functions" — those that vanish everywhere but are not identically zero in the ring.

\begin{definition}
The **spectrum** of a commutative ring $R$, written$\mathrm{Spec}(R)$, is the set of prime ideals of$R$ equipped with the Zariski topology, in which the closed sets are$V(I) = \{P \in \mathrm{Spec}(R) : P \supseteq I\}$ for ideals$I \subseteq R$.
\end{definition}

The dictionary between algebra and geometry is functorial: a ring homomorphism $\phi: R \to S$ induces a continuous map$\phi^*: \mathrm{Spec}(S) \to \mathrm{Spec}(R)$ by$Q \mapsto \phi^{-1}(Q)$. This pullback goes backward, just as in the correspondence between manifolds and function rings in differential geometry. The various ring-theoretic constructions we have studied correspond to geometric operations:
- Quotient $R \to R/I$: restriction to the closed subscheme$V(I) \hookrightarrow \mathrm{Spec}(R)$.
- Localization $R \to S^{-1}R$: restriction to the open subscheme$\mathrm{Spec}(S^{-1}R) \hookrightarrow \mathrm{Spec}(R)$ (the complement of$V(\mathrm{Ann}(s))$ for$s \in S$).
- Integral extension $R \hookrightarrow S$: a finite (surjective on$\mathrm{Spec}$) map of spaces.
- Tensor product $R \otimes_k S$: fiber product$\mathrm{Spec}(R) \times_k \mathrm{Spec}(S)$ of schemes.

The Krull dimension of $R$ equals the topological dimension of$\mathrm{Spec}(R)$ in the sense of the combinatorial dimension: the supremum of lengths of chains of irreducible closed subsets. The Nullstellensatz identifies$\mathrm{Spec}(k[x_1,\ldots,x_n])$ with affine$n$-space when$k$ is algebraically closed: the maximal ideals (closed points) are in bijection with$\mathbb{A}^n_k$, and the non-maximal primes encode the higher-dimensional loci.

The scheme-theoretic perspective goes further than classical algebraic geometry. Classical algebraic geometry works with varieties, which are reduced (no nilpotents) and often irreducible. The scheme perspective allows nilpotents: $\mathrm{Spec}(k[x]/(x^2))$ is a "fat point" that is geometrically a single point but algebraically retains information about first-order infinitesimals. This is crucial for moduli problems, deformation theory, and the study of intersection multiplicities. Two curves tangent at a point have intersection multiplicity$\geq 2$, encoded algebraically by the ring$k[x]/(x^2)$ appearing in their scheme-theoretic intersection.

**Example 1.** $\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{Z})$: the spectrum of$\mathbb{Z}$ consists of$(0)$ (the generic point, dense in$\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{Z})$) and$(p)$ for each prime$p$ (the closed points). The Zariski topology: the closed sets are$\emptyset$,$\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{Z})$ itself, and finite sets of closed points$\{(p_1),\ldots,(p_k)\} = V(p_1\cdots p_k)$. The structure sheaf assigns to the open set$D(f) = \mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{Z}[1/f])$ the ring$\mathbb{Z}[1/f]$. This is the archetype of an arithmetic scheme, and algebraic geometry over$\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{Z})$ is the language of modern arithmetic geometry.

**Example 2.** $\mathrm{Spec}(k[x])$ for an algebraically closed field$k$: the closed points are$(x-a)$ for$a \in k$, in bijection with$\mathbb{A}^1_k = k$. The generic point is$(0)$. The Zariski topology on$\mathbb{A}^1_k$: the closed sets are finite sets of points and the whole space, making it a cofinite topology. This is the algebraic analogue of the complex line.

**Example 3.** $\mathrm{Spec}(k[x,y])$: the prime ideals are$(0)$ (generic point of the plane),$(f)$ for irreducible$f$ (generic point of the curve$\{f=0\}$), and maximal ideals$(x-a, y-b)$ (the points$(a,b) \in \mathbb{A}^2_k$). The Zariski topology: closed sets are algebraic subvarieties (finite unions of curves and points). The localization at a maximal ideal$(x-a, y-b)$ is the local ring$\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{A}^2,\,(a,b)}$, keeping track of behavior near the point.

**Example 4.** Schemes with nilpotents: $\mathrm{Spec}(k[x]/(x^2))$. As a topological space, this has a single point (the unique prime$(x)/(x^2)$), same as$\mathrm{Spec}(k)$. But the ring$k[x]/(x^2)$ is not$k$: it has a nilpotent element$\epsilon = x + (x^2)$ with$\epsilon^2 = 0, \epsilon \neq 0$. Geometrically, this is a "double point" or first-order thickening of a point: it "sees" tangent directions. Scheme-theoretic intersections: the$x$-axis$y=0$ and the parabola$y=x^2$ in$\mathbb{A}^2_k$ meet at the origin with intersection ring$k[x]/(x^2)$ (eliminating$y$), so scheme-theoretically they meet with multiplicity 2, even though they share only the single point$(0,0)$.

**Example 5.** Morphisms of schemes from ring maps. The map $\phi: k[y] \to k[x]$,$y \mapsto x^2$, corresponds geometrically to the map$\phi^*: \mathbb{A}^1_k \to \mathbb{A}^1_k$,$a \mapsto a^2$ (squaring). The preimage of the closed point$(y-b)$ is the set of primes of$k[x]$ lying over$(y-b)$: these are the ideals$(x^2 - b)$. For$b \neq 0$ (and$k = \mathbb{C}$),$(x^2 - b) = (x-\sqrt{b})(x+\sqrt{b})$, giving two closed points. For$b = 0$,$(x^2) = (x)^2$, giving a single point with multiplicity 2. The scheme-theoretic fiber over$b = 0$ is$\mathrm{Spec}(k[x]/(x^2))$ — the double point, not just the single point$\{0\}$ — capturing the ramification of the squaring map at$0$.
