# Chapter 24 — Commutative Algebra

Commutative algebra is the study of commutative rings in the depth and generality needed for algebraic geometry and algebraic number theory. Where the earlier chapters built the language — rings, ideals, quotients, domains, polynomial rings — this chapter develops the structural theorems that allow one to reason globally about rings through local data, to measure complexity by chains of prime ideals, and to connect rings to the geometric spaces they implicitly define. The subject crystallized in the mid-twentieth century through the work of Noether, Krull, and Zariski, and its language is now indispensable throughout mathematics.

The Noetherian condition — that every ascending chain of ideals stabilizes — is at once a finiteness hypothesis and a remarkably strong structural constraint. Rings satisfying this condition, called Noetherian rings, include all fields, all principal ideal domains, and, by Hilbert's basis theorem, all polynomial rings $R[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ over a Noetherian ring $R$. Hilbert's original proof was nonconstructive and caused controversy when it appeared in 1890, but it settled the finite generation question for polynomial invariants and inaugurated modern abstract algebra. In a Noetherian ring, every ideal is finitely generated, submodules of finitely generated modules are finitely generated, and primary decomposition of ideals is possible. These properties underpin nearly every finiteness argument in commutative algebra.

Localization is the operation of inverting a multiplicative set $S \subseteq R$ to produce a new ring $S^{-1}R$ in which elements of $S$ become units. The construction mimics the passage from $\mathbb{Z}$ to $\mathbb{Q}$, but the richness lies in localizing at a prime ideal $\mathfrak{p}$: the ring $R_{\mathfrak{p}} = (R \setminus \mathfrak{p})^{-1}R$ has a unique maximal ideal $\mathfrak{p} R_{\mathfrak{p}}$ and is therefore a local ring. Local rings are simpler than global ones — modules over them are "almost free" — and the local-global principle asserts that many properties of $R$ can be checked by verifying them for each $R_{\mathfrak{p}}$. This decomposition of global questions into local ones is one of the most powerful ideas in the subject and foreshadows sheaf theory and scheme theory.

Integral extensions generalize algebraic extensions of fields to the setting of arbitrary rings. An element $s$ in a ring $S \supseteq R$ is integral over $R$ if it satisfies a monic polynomial with coefficients in $R$. The integral closure of $R$ in $S$ is the set of all such elements; when $R$ equals its own integral closure in its fraction field, $R$ is called integrally closed, a property enjoyed by UFDs and central to the definition of Dedekind domains. The going-up and going-down theorems describe how prime ideals behave under integral extensions, showing that prime ideals "lift" and "descend" along the ring extension in a controlled way. Krull dimension, defined as the supremum of lengths of chains of prime ideals, provides the correct algebraic analogue of the geometric notion of dimension. For the polynomial ring $k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ over a field, the Krull dimension is exactly $n$, a fact proved using integral extensions and the Noether normalization lemma. Hilbert's Nullstellensatz then establishes the fundamental bijection between radical ideals in $k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ and algebraic subsets of affine $n$-space over an algebraically closed field $k$, making the entire preceding theory visible as the algebraic shadow of geometry.
