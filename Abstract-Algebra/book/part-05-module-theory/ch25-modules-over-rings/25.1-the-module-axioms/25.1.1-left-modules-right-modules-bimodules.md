# 25.1.1 Left $R$-Modules; Right Modules; Bimodules

Vector spaces over a field are among the best-behaved algebraic structures we know: every vector space has a basis, every subspace has a complement, and the entire structure is determined up to isomorphism by a single cardinal number, the dimension. But most of algebra does not take place over fields. Integers, polynomial rings, matrix algebras, and rings of algebraic integers are equally natural arenas for linear algebra, and in each of these settings the naive vector-space intuition breaks down in instructive ways. The correct framework for linear algebra over an arbitrary ring is the theory of modules, and this section lays its foundations.

The motivating question is simple: what does it mean to "scale" elements of an abelian group by elements of a ring? The answer is given by the module axioms, which are precisely the axioms that ensure scalar multiplication interacts coherently with both the additive structure of the group and the multiplicative structure of the ring.

\begin{definition}
Let $R$ be a ring with identity$1_{R}$. A \emph{left$R$-module} is an abelian group$(M, +)$ equipped with a scalar multiplication$R \times M \to M$, written$(r, m) \mapsto rm$, satisfying the following four axioms for all$r, s \in R$ and all$m, m' \in M$:
\begin{align*}
r(m + m') &= rm + rm', \\
(r + s)m &= rm + sm, \\
(rs)m &= r(sm), \\
1_{R} \cdot m &= m.
\end{align*}
\end{definition}

The first two axioms say that scalar multiplication is distributive over addition in both arguments — it is bilinear in the sense appropriate to the ring-group interaction. The third axiom is the associativity of scaling: first scaling by $s$ then by$r$ is the same as scaling by the product$rs$ computed first in$R$. Because the product$rs$ is formed in$R$ with$r$ written on the left, this is a \emph{left} module: the ring acts from the left. The fourth axiom says the identity element of$R$ acts as the identity transformation on$M$.

A \emph{right $R$-module} places the scalar on the right: the action is$(m, r) \mapsto mr$, and the associativity axiom reads$m(rs) = (mr)s$. When$R$ is commutative, the distinction between left and right vanishes — we may pass freely between the two conventions — but for noncommutative rings the two notions are genuinely different. A map between left$R$-modules must intertwine left actions; it cannot mix left and right.

A \emph{bimodule} bridges the two frameworks: an $(R, S)$-bimodule$M$ carries a left$R$-action and a right$S$-action that commute in the sense that$r(ms) = (rm)s$ for all$r \in R$,$s \in S$,$m \in M$. The commutativity condition ensures that the two actions are independent: scaling by$R$ on the left and by$S$ on the right do not interfere with each other. Any ring$R$ is itself an$(R, R)$-bimodule under left and right multiplication, and bimodules over two rings form the appropriate notion of "morphism" in a bicategory whose objects are rings — a perspective that becomes essential in Morita theory.

\begin{example}
Every abelian group $(M, +)$ is a$\mathbb{Z}$-module in exactly one way: a positive integer$n$ acts by$n \cdot m = m + m + \cdots + m$ ($n$ summands), a negative integer acts by$(-n) \cdot m = -(n \cdot m)$, and$0 \cdot m = 0$. The module axioms are automatically satisfied, and no choices are involved. Thus the category of$\mathbb{Z}$-modules is \emph{equal} to the category of abelian groups: not merely analogous, but identical.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
When $R = F$ is a field, every$F$-module is an$F$-vector space. Conversely, every$F$-vector space is an$F$-module. The theory of$F$-modules is therefore exactly the theory of vector spaces, with all the familiar results: every module over a field has a basis, every submodule has a complement, and finitely generated modules are characterized up to isomorphism by their dimension. The field case is therefore the "nicest" instance of module theory.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $R = M_n(F)$ be the ring of$n \times n$ matrices over a field$F$. The column space$F^n$, consisting of column vectors, is a left$R$-module under the action of matrix-vector multiplication. This is a faithful module — distinct matrices act differently on$F^n$ — and it is the prototypical example of a module over a matrix ring. We will see in the theory of semisimple modules that$F^n$ is in fact a simple$M_n(F)$-module.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $R$ be any ring. A left ideal$I \leq R$ (that is, a subset closed under addition and under left multiplication by$R$) is a left$R$-module under the restriction of$R$'s multiplication. Similarly, the quotient$R/I$ is a left$R$-module, where$r \cdot (s + I) = rs + I$. These two families of modules — ideals and their quotients — are the cyclic modules, which we study in Section 25.5.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $G$ be a group and$k$ a field. The \emph{group algebra}$k[G]$ is the$k$-vector space with basis$\{g : g \in G\}$, made into a ring by the multiplication$(\sum_{g} a_g g)(\sum_h b_h h) = \sum_{g,h} a_g b_h (gh)$. A left$k[G]$-module is precisely a$k$-linear representation of$G$: an abelian group$M$ that is simultaneously a$k$-vector space and carries a$G$-action by$k$-linear maps. The module axioms encode both linearity and the group homomorphism condition. This connection makes module theory the natural language for representation theory.
\end{example}

The left/right distinction is not merely pedantic. Consider the group algebra $R = k[G]$ for a non-abelian group$G$. A left$R$-module is a representation of$G$ on a$k$-vector space where$G$ acts on the left; a right$R$-module has$G$ acting on the right. These give inequivalent representation theories unless$G$ has some special symmetry (such as every element being conjugate to its inverse). The bimodule structure on$R$ itself — where$R$ acts on itself by left and right multiplication simultaneously — reflects the full two-sided structure of the ring, and bimodules over two different rings are the appropriate setting for Morita equivalence, which asks when two rings have "the same" module theory despite being non-isomorphic as rings.
