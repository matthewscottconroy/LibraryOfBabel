# 25.1.2 The Correspondence: $R$-Modules$\leftrightarrow$ Ring Maps$R \to \mathrm{End}(M)$

The definition of a module in terms of four axioms is correct but somewhat opaque. There is a second, more structural formulation that clarifies the relationship between modules and representations, and that makes the parallel with group representations transparent. This reformulation will recur throughout the text, especially when we study semisimple modules and their connection to semisimple rings.

The key observation is that for any abelian group $(M, +)$, the set$\mathrm{End}(M)$ of group endomorphisms carries a natural ring structure: addition is pointwise,$(f + g)(m) = f(m) + g(m)$, and multiplication is composition,$(f \circ g)(m) = f(g(m))$. The zero map is the additive identity and the identity map$\mathrm{id}_{M}$ is the multiplicative identity. The ring axioms for$\mathrm{End}(M)$ follow directly from associativity of composition and distributivity of composition over pointwise addition.

\begin{proposition}
A left $R$-module structure on an abelian group$M$ is equivalent to a ring homomorphism$\rho: R \to \mathrm{End}(M)$.
\end{proposition}

The correspondence is given by $\rho(r)(m) = rm$. To verify the equivalence: the axiom$r(m + m') = rm + rm'$ says that$\rho(r)$ is an additive group endomorphism of$M$. The axiom$(r + s)m = rm + sm$ says that$r \mapsto \rho(r)$ is additive as a map$R \to \mathrm{End}(M)$. The axiom$(rs)m = r(sm)$ says$\rho(rs) = \rho(r) \circ \rho(s)$, i.e.,$\rho$ is multiplicative. Finally,$1_{R} \cdot m = m$ says$\rho(1_{R}) = \mathrm{id}_{M}$. These together are exactly the conditions that$\rho: R \to \mathrm{End}(M)$ is a ring homomorphism (with$\rho(1_{R}) = \mathrm{id}_{M}$, i.e., a unital homomorphism).

This reformulation identifies $R$-modules with \emph{representations of$R$}: a module is precisely an action of$R$ on an abelian group by ring endomorphisms. The analogy with group representations is not merely aesthetic. For a group$G$, a representation over a field$k$ is a group homomorphism$G \to \mathrm{GL}(V)$ for some$k$-vector space$V$. For a ring$R$, a module is a ring homomorphism$R \to \mathrm{End}(M)$ for some abelian group$M$. The structures are parallel: the ring$R$ plays the role of the group$G$, and the endomorphism ring$\mathrm{End}(M)$ plays the role of$\mathrm{GL}(V)$.

The connection is more than analogical: it is literal. If $k$ is a field and$G$ is a group, the group ring$k[G]$ is the ring formed by taking formal finite$k$-linear combinations of elements of$G$ and extending the group multiplication bilinearly. A$k[G]$-module is the same as a$k$-linear representation of$G$: the ring homomorphism$k[G] \to \mathrm{End}(M)$ restricts to a group homomorphism$G \to \mathrm{GL}(M)$ (the invertible endomorphisms, since group elements have inverses in$G$) and to a$k$-algebra map on the scalar part. Thus the theory of modules over group rings subsumes the theory of group representations, making module theory the unified framework for studying symmetry through linear algebra.

\begin{example}
Let $R = \mathbb{Z}$ and$M = \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. The corresponding ring homomorphism$\rho: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathrm{End}(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})$ sends an integer$k$ to the endomorphism$\bar{m} \mapsto k\bar{m}$. The kernel of$\rho$ is$n\mathbb{Z}$, since multiplication by$n$ acts as zero on$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. Thus$\rho$ factors through$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \to \mathrm{End}(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})$, and the image is the ring of multiplications by integers modulo$n$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $R = F[x]$ (a polynomial ring over a field$F$) and let$V$ be a finite-dimensional$F$-vector space equipped with a linear operator$T: V \to V$. Define$\rho: F[x] \to \mathrm{End}_{F}(V)$ by$\rho(f) = f(T)$ — evaluate the polynomial at the operator$T$. For instance,$\rho(x^2 + 1) = T^2 + \mathrm{id}_{V}$. This is a ring homomorphism because the polynomial ring$F[x]$ is commutative, and polynomials in$T$ compose as expected. The kernel of$\rho$ is the ideal$(m_{T})$ generated by the minimal polynomial$m_{T}$ of$T$. This example is the foundation of the rational canonical form theory in Chapter 27.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $R = M_n(k)$, the$n \times n$ matrix ring over a field$k$. The inclusion$R \hookrightarrow \mathrm{End}_{k}(k^n)$ — viewing matrices as linear maps on column vectors — is a ring homomorphism, making$k^n$ an$R$-module. The trivial ring homomorphism$R \to \mathrm{End}(\{0\})$ gives the zero module. Every ring homomorphism$M_n(k) \to \mathrm{End}(M)$ is either zero or injective (since$M_n(k)$ is simple as a ring), which constrains the possible module structures dramatically.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
A ring homomorphism $R \to \mathrm{End}(M)$ need not be injective. When the kernel is a two-sided ideal$I \subseteq R$, the module$M$ is annihilated by$I$ in the sense that$rm = 0$ for all$r \in I$ and$m \in M$. The module then factors through the quotient ring$R/I$: the original$R$-module structure descends to an$(R/I)$-module structure. This observation will be important when we study modules annihilated by a fixed ideal, as in the torsion theory of finitely generated modules over PIDs.
\end{example}

The representation-theoretic perspective also clarifies what a module homomorphism is: it is a map between abelian groups that intertwines the two ring-action representations. If $\rho: R \to \mathrm{End}(M)$ and$\sigma: R \to \mathrm{End}(N)$ are two module structures, an$R$-linear map$f: M \to N$ is a group homomorphism satisfying$f \circ \rho(r) = \sigma(r) \circ f$ for all$r \in R$. This is precisely the condition that$f$ is a natural transformation between the two representations, a concept that points toward the categorical foundations of module theory and eventually to the Yoneda lemma and representable functors.
