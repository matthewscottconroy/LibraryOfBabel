# 25.1.3 Key Examples: Abelian Groups ($R = \mathbb{Z}$), Vector Spaces ($R$ a Field),$F[x]$-Modules

The definition of a module is deliberately broad, intended to unify many structures that appear in different parts of mathematics. The three families of examples in this section — $\mathbb{Z}$-modules,$F$-modules for a field$F$, and$F[x]$-modules — are the ones that will drive the theory for the rest of Part V. Each family corresponds to a different flavor of "linear algebra," and each will eventually lead to a classification theorem: the Fundamental Theorem of Finitely Generated Abelian Groups, the dimension theorem for vector spaces, and the theory of rational canonical form.

**Abelian groups as $\mathbb{Z}$-modules.** Every abelian group$(M, +)$ is a$\mathbb{Z}$-module in a unique way. For a positive integer$n$, define$n \cdot m = m + m + \cdots + m$ ($n$ summands). For$n < 0$, define$n \cdot m = -(|n| \cdot m)$, and$0 \cdot m = 0$. These rules are not a choice — they are \emph{forced} by the module axioms applied to$R = \mathbb{Z}$: since$n = 1 + 1 + \cdots + 1$, the axiom$(r+s)m = rm + sm$ forces$n \cdot m$ to equal the$n$-fold sum. There is no other possible$\mathbb{Z}$-module structure on$M$. Consequently, the category of$\mathbb{Z}$-modules is equal to the category of abelian groups: the two are the same, not merely in bijection.

\begin{example}
The abelian group $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} = \{0,1,2,3,4,5\}$ is a$\mathbb{Z}$-module. The submodules of$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ as a$\mathbb{Z}$-module are exactly its subgroups:$\{0\}$,$\{0,2,4\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$,$\{0,3\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, and$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ itself. Note that$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ as$\mathbb{Z}$-modules (by the Chinese Remainder Theorem), which is the first instance of the primary decomposition that Chapter 27 will systematize.
\end{example}

**Vector spaces as $F$-modules.** When$R = F$ is a field, every$F$-module is an$F$-vector space and conversely. The defining feature of a field — that every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse — forces the module to be free: every finitely generated$F$-module has a basis, and all bases have the same cardinality (the dimension). This is the content of the standard dimension theorem from linear algebra. Over a field, therefore, the module-theoretic theory collapses to vector space theory, and every module is isomorphic to$F^n$ for some$n \geq 0$. The theory of$F$-modules is thus completely classified, serving as both the motivating example and the easy case for everything that follows.

\begin{example}
The $\mathbb{R}$-module$\mathbb{R}^3$ has a basis$\{e_1, e_2, e_3\}$. Every element is a unique$\mathbb{R}$-linear combination$\alpha_1 e_1 + \alpha_2 e_2 + \alpha_3 e_3$. The submodules (subspaces) include lines through the origin, planes through the origin, and the zero subspace. Every subspace has a complement: for any subspace$W \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3$, we can find$W'$ with$W \cap W' = 0$ and$W + W' = \mathbb{R}^3$. This complementation property — which holds over any field — will fail dramatically for modules over general rings.
\end{example}

**$F[x]$-modules and linear operators.** The most important new example — and the one that drives Chapter 27 — comes from polynomial rings. Let$F$ be a field,$V$ a finite-dimensional$F$-vector space, and$T: V \to V$ a linear operator. Define an$F[x]$-module structure on$V$ by
$$f \cdot v = f(T)(v), \quad f \in F[x],\; v \in V.$$
Here $f(T)$ is the operator obtained by substituting$T$ for the indeterminate: if$f = a_n x^n + \cdots + a_1 x + a_0$, then$f(T) = a_n T^n + \cdots + a_1 T + a_0 \cdot \mathrm{id}_{V}$. The module axioms follow from the fact that$F[x]$ is commutative and that the evaluation map$f \mapsto f(T)$ is a ring homomorphism$F[x] \to \mathrm{End}_{F}(V)$.

The key feature of this construction is that it captures similarity of operators in module-theoretic terms. Two operators $T: V \to V$ and$T': V' \to V'$ are \emph{similar} (conjugate by an invertible linear map) if and only if the resulting$F[x]$-modules$V$ and$V'$ are isomorphic. Thus the classification of operators up to similarity is equivalent to the classification of finitely generated$F[x]$-modules up to isomorphism — exactly the content of the structure theorem for modules over the PID$F[x]$.

\begin{example}
Let $V = F^2$ and$T = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ (the nilpotent Jordan block). Then$T^2 = 0$, so the minimal polynomial of$T$ is$x^2$. The$F[x]$-module structure has$x \cdot e_1 = T e_1 = e_2$ and$x \cdot e_2 = T e_2 = 0$. The annihilator of$e_1$ is the ideal$(x^2)$, so the cyclic submodule$F[x] \cdot e_1 = V$ and$V \cong F[x]/(x^2)$ as$F[x]$-modules. The operator$T$ thus corresponds to the primary cyclic module$F[x]/(x^2)$, which is the primary decomposition form for this module.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $V = F^2$ and$T = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda & 0 \\ 0 & \mu \end{pmatrix}$ with$\lambda \neq \mu$. Then$V = F e_1 \oplus F e_2$, each summand is invariant under$T$, and each summand$F e_i$ is isomorphic as an$F[x]$-module to$F[x]/(x - \lambda_i)$ (where$\lambda_1 = \lambda$,$\lambda_2 = \mu$). Thus$V \cong F[x]/(x-\lambda) \oplus F[x]/(x-\mu)$. The invariant factors of$T$ are$(x-\lambda)(x-\mu)$ (the characteristic polynomial, since we have two distinct eigenvalues with one summand each). When$\lambda = \mu$, the structure changes:$V$ could be$F[x]/(x-\lambda)^2$ (the Jordan block) or$F[x]/(x-\lambda) \oplus F[x]/(x-\lambda)$ (the diagonalizable case), and these are not isomorphic as$F[x]$-modules.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $R = F[x, y]$ be the polynomial ring in two variables, and let$V = F^2$ be equipped with two commuting operators$S$ and$T$ (that is,$ST = TS$). This determines an$F[x,y]$-module structure on$V$ by$(f(x,y)) \cdot v = f(S, T)(v)$. The commutativity of$S$ and$T$ is precisely the condition that this is well-defined as an$F[x,y]$-module. However,$F[x,y]$ is \emph{not} a PID (it is a UFD but not a PID), so the structure theorem does not apply directly. The classification of finitely generated$F[x,y]$-modules is far more complicated, and this contrast highlights why the PID hypothesis in Chapter 27 is essential.
\end{example}

These three families illustrate the two extremes and the important middle ground of module theory. At one extreme, $\mathbb{Z}$-modules are as general as abelian groups, with all the complexity that entails. At the other extreme,$F$-modules are vector spaces, which are completely tame. The$F[x]$-modules occupy the middle: more structured than general$\mathbb{Z}$-modules (because$F[x]$ is a PID), but richer than vector spaces (because$F[x]$ is not a field). The classification theorem for$F[x]$-modules is the theory of canonical forms for linear operators, and it is the deepest theorem in this part of the text.
