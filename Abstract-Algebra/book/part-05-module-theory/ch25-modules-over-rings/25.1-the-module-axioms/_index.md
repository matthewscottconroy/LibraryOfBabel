# 25.1 The Module Axioms

What exactly is required to "act on" an abelian group with a ring? The module axioms answer this question precisely, and their simplicity conceals their reach. A left $R$-module $M$ is an abelian group $(M, +)$ together with a ring homomorphism $R \to \text{End}_{\mathbb{Z}}(M)$; unpacking this, each element $r \in R$ determines an endomorphism $m \mapsto r \cdot m$ of $M$, and the axioms require that this assignment is compatible with both the ring structure of $R$ and the group structure of $M$: $(r + s) \cdot m = r \cdot m + s \cdot m$, $r \cdot (m + n) = r \cdot m + r \cdot n$, $(rs) \cdot m = r \cdot (s \cdot m)$, and $1_R \cdot m = m$. These are not arbitrary conditions but exactly what is needed for $R$ to act on $M$ in a way consistent with all the ring operations.

The key examples immediately reveal the breadth of the definition. When $R = F$ is a field, an $R$-module is precisely a vector space over $F$: every element of $F$ is invertible, so the scalar action is as rich as possible. When $R = \mathbb{Z}$, the ring homomorphism $\mathbb{Z} \to \text{End}_{\mathbb{Z}}(M)$ is entirely determined: $n \mapsto (m \mapsto nm)$ where $nm$ means $m$ added to itself $n$ times (and negated for $n < 0$). There is no choice, which means every abelian group is a $\mathbb{Z}$-module in exactly one way. A third fundamental example arises when $R = F[x]$: an $F[x]$-module structure on an $F$-vector space $V$ is determined by a choice of where $x$ acts, and since $x$ must act as an $F$-linear endomorphism of $V$, an $F[x]$-module structure is equivalent to a pair $(V, T)$ consisting of $V$ and a linear operator $T \in \text{End}_F(V)$. This identification is the seed of the entire theory of canonical forms in Chapter 27.

There is an important distinction between left and right modules, as well as bimodules on which $R$ acts on the left and a second ring $S$ acts on the right compatibly. For commutative rings $R$, left and right modules coincide, and in that case the category of $R$-modules is symmetric. The correspondence between module structures and ring homomorphisms into endomorphism rings also suggests why noncommutative rings naturally give rise to both left and right modules — the opposite ring $R^{\text{op}}$, with multiplication reversed, governs right modules in the same way $R$ governs left modules. This distinction will matter when we discuss tensor products in Chapter 28, where the construction $M \otimes_R N$ requires $M$ to be a right $R$-module and $N$ a left $R$-module.
