# 25.2.1 Submodules; the Submodule Test

Once we have the basic objects — modules — the next step is to identify the naturally occurring sub-objects and the maps between them. The principle in algebra is always the same: a sub-object is a subset that is closed under all the operations of the ambient structure. For modules, the operations are addition, additive inverses (or equivalently subtraction), and scalar multiplication, so a submodule is a subset that is closed under all three.

The formal definition synthesizes the subgroup and subspace conditions in a natural way. Let $M$ be a left$R$-module.

\begin{definition}
A subset $N \subseteq M$ is a \emph{submodule} of$M$ if:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $N$ is a subgroup of$(M, +)$: it contains$0$, is closed under addition, and is closed under negation; and
\item $N$ is closed under the$R$-action: for all$r \in R$ and$n \in N$, we have$rn \in N$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

As with subgroups and subspaces, the two conditions can be compressed into a practical test.

\begin{proposition}[Submodule Test]
A nonempty subset $N \subseteq M$ is a submodule if and only if for all$m, n \in N$ and$r \in R$, we have$m - n \in N$ and$rm \in N$.
\end{proposition}

The proof is straightforward: the condition $m - n \in N$ (taking$m = n$ first to get$0 \in N$, then using it freely) gives the subgroup condition, while$rm \in N$ gives scalar closure.

When $R = F$ is a field, this recovers exactly the subspace criterion from linear algebra — and indeed, any submodule of an$F$-module is a subspace. When$R = \mathbb{Z}$, the scalar closure condition$rm \in N$ for all$r \in \mathbb{Z}$ is automatically satisfied by any subgroup$N$ of the abelian group$M$, since$\mathbb{Z}$ acts by repeated addition and every subgroup is closed under addition. Thus for$\mathbb{Z}$-modules (abelian groups), the notions of submodule and subgroup coincide.

The key examples of submodules are worth cataloguing explicitly, as they will recur throughout the chapter.

\begin{example}
In any module $M$, the subset$\{0\}$ (containing only the zero element) and$M$ itself are always submodules. These are the \emph{trivial} submodules. A module with no submodules other than these two is called \emph{simple}, and will be studied in Section 25.5.3.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For any ring $R$, the ring$R$ itself is a left$R$-module (with action given by left multiplication). A submodule of$R$ as a left$R$-module is precisely a \emph{left ideal} of$R$: a nonempty subset$I \subseteq R$ such that$a - b \in I$ for$a, b \in I$ and$ra \in I$ for all$r \in R$. This shows that the theory of left ideals is a special case of the theory of submodules, and that results about submodules apply immediately to ideals.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For any $R$-linear map$f: M \to N$, the kernel$\ker f = \{m \in M : f(m) = 0\}$ is a submodule of$M$. Indeed, if$f(m) = f(m') = 0$ and$r \in R$, then$f(m - m') = f(m) - f(m') = 0$ and$f(rm) = rf(m) = r \cdot 0 = 0$. Similarly, the image$\mathrm{im}\, f = \{f(m) : m \in M\}$ is a submodule of$N$. These are the module-theoretic kernels and images, to be studied in Section 25.3.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For any element $m \in M$, the \emph{cyclic submodule} generated by$m$ is$Rm = \{rm : r \in R\}$. This is a submodule:$r_1 m - r_2 m = (r_1 - r_2)m \in Rm$ and$s(rm) = (sr)m \in Rm$. It is the smallest submodule of$M$ containing$m$. More generally, for any subset$S \subseteq M$, the submodule generated by$S$ is$\langle S \rangle = \{r_1 s_1 + r_2 s_2 + \cdots + r_k s_k : r_i \in R, s_i \in S, k \geq 0\}$, the set of all finite$R$-linear combinations of elements of$S$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $M = \mathbb{Z}$ viewed as a$\mathbb{Z}$-module. The submodules of$\mathbb{Z}$ are precisely the ideals of$\mathbb{Z}$, namely the sets$n\mathbb{Z} = \{nm : m \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ for$n \geq 0$. The submodule$2\mathbb{Z}$ is a submodule of$\mathbb{Z}$ that has no complement in$\mathbb{Z}$: there is no submodule$N$ of$\mathbb{Z}$ with$2\mathbb{Z} \cap N = \{0\}$ and$2\mathbb{Z} + N = \mathbb{Z}$. (If$N$ contains an odd integer$k$, then$N$ also contains$2\mathbb{Z}$ and hence$\mathbb{Z}$; the two conditions cannot be simultaneously satisfied.) This failure of complementation is a fundamental contrast with the vector space case and will motivate the study of projective modules in Chapter 26.
\end{example}

Submodules can be combined in two standard ways. The \emph{intersection} $N_1 \cap N_2$ of two submodules is again a submodule (check the test: if$m, m' \in N_1 \cap N_2$, then$m - m'$ and$rm$ lie in both$N_1$ and$N_2$). The \emph{sum}$N_1 + N_2 = \{n_1 + n_2 : n_1 \in N_1, n_2 \in N_2\}$ is also a submodule, and it is the smallest submodule containing both$N_1$ and$N_2$. These two operations make the collection of submodules of$M$ into a \emph{lattice}, a partially ordered set closed under binary meets (intersections) and joins (sums). The lattice structure of submodules is a rich invariant of the module, encoding information about how the module can be decomposed and how submodules relate to each other. It will be studied further in the context of the correspondence theorem (Section 25.2.4) and in the theory of composition series.
