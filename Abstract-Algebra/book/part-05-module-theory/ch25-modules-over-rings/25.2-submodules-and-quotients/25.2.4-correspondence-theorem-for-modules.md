# 25.2.4 Correspondence Theorem for Modules

When we form the quotient $M/N$, we gain a simpler module but potentially lose information. The correspondence theorem tells us precisely what we have not lost: the submodule structure of$M$ that lies "above$N$" is completely preserved in$M/N$. This theorem is the module-theoretic version of the lattice isomorphism theorem for groups and rings, and it is equally indispensable.

The intuition is this: any submodule $L$ of$M/N$ must "come from somewhere" in$M$. The cosets that comprise$L$ are cosets of$N$ in$M$, so their union is a subset of$M$. It turns out this union is always a submodule of$M$ that contains$N$, and this gives the inverse map from submodules of$M/N$ to submodules of$M$ above$N$.

\begin{theorem}[Correspondence Theorem]
Let $N \leq M$ be a submodule and let$\pi: M \to M/N$ be the quotient map. There is an inclusion-preserving bijection
$$\{L \leq M : N \subseteq L\} \longleftrightarrow \{\text{submodules of } M/N\},$$
given in one direction by $L \mapsto L/N = \pi(L)$, and in the other by$K \mapsto \pi^{-1}(K)$.
\end{theorem}

Both maps are well-defined: if $N \subseteq L \leq M$, then$L/N = \{l + N : l \in L\}$ is a submodule of$M/N$ (check: for$l_1 + N, l_2 + N \in L/N$ and$r \in R$, we have$(l_1 + N) - (l_2 + N) = (l_1 - l_2) + N \in L/N$ since$l_1 - l_2 \in L$, and$r(l + N) = rl + N \in L/N$ since$rl \in L$). Conversely, if$K \leq M/N$, then$\pi^{-1}(K) = \{m \in M : m + N \in K\}$ is a submodule of$M$ containing$N$.

The two maps are mutually inverse: $\pi^{-1}(L/N) = L$ (for any$L \supseteq N$), and$\pi(\pi^{-1}(K)) = K$ (for any submodule$K \leq M/N$). Both directions preserve inclusion: if$L_1 \subseteq L_2$ then$L_1/N \subseteq L_2/N$, and if$K_1 \subseteq K_2$ then$\pi^{-1}(K_1) \subseteq \pi^{-1}(K_2)$. Thus the lattice of submodules of$M/N$ is isomorphic (as a poset) to the lattice of submodules of$M$ lying above$N$.

\begin{example}
Let $M = \mathbb{Z}$ and$N = 6\mathbb{Z}$. The submodules of$M/N = \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ are the subgroups of$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$: they are$\{0\}$,$\{0,2,4\}$,$\{0,3\}$, and$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ itself, corresponding to$6\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$,$2\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$,$3\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, and$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$. The submodules of$\mathbb{Z}$ containing$6\mathbb{Z}$ are exactly$6\mathbb{Z}$,$2\mathbb{Z}$,$3\mathbb{Z}$, and$\mathbb{Z}$, which correspond under the bijection to the four submodules of$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ listed above.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $R = F[x]$,$M = F[x]$, and$N = (f)$ for a polynomial$f = f_1 f_2$ with$\gcd(f_1, f_2) = 1$. The submodules (ideals) of$F[x]/(f)$ correspond to ideals of$F[x]$ containing$(f)$, i.e., divisors of$f$ in$F[x]$. The divisors of$f_1 f_2$ are (up to units)$1$,$f_1$,$f_2$, and$f_1 f_2$, giving four submodules of$F[x]/(f_1 f_2)$: the whole ring,$f_1 F[x]/(f) \cong F[x]/(f_2)$,$f_2 F[x]/(f) \cong F[x]/(f_1)$, and$\{0\}$. This is the submodule lattice of$F[x]/(f_1 f_2)$, which by the Chinese Remainder Theorem is isomorphic to$F[x]/(f_1) \times F[x]/(f_2)$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
A particularly useful application is the following. Suppose we want to show that $M/N$ is a simple module (has no proper nonzero submodules). By the correspondence theorem, this is equivalent to showing that the only submodules of$M$ containing$N$ are$N$ itself and$M$ — that is,$N$ is a \emph{maximal} submodule of$M$. In the case$M = R$ (viewed as a left module over itself) and$N = \mathfrak{m}$ a maximal left ideal, the quotient$R/\mathfrak{m}$ is simple. This gives a concrete construction of all simple left$R$-modules: they are precisely the quotients$R/\mathfrak{m}$ for maximal left ideals$\mathfrak{m}$.
\end{example}

The correspondence theorem is a powerful tool for inductive arguments on module structure. A typical strategy: to understand $M$, choose a submodule$N$ that is "small" (e.g., cyclic or simple), understand$N$ and the quotient$M/N$ separately, and use the correspondence theorem to stitch the submodule lattices of$N$ and$M/N$ together into the lattice of$M$. This "extension problem" — how does knowing the submodule structure of$N$ and$M/N$ constrain the submodule structure of$M$? — is one of the central problems of module theory, and its solution in the semisimple case (where every module is a direct sum of simples) is the content of Chapter 42.

The correspondence theorem also underlies the proof of the following key fact: if $M$ is a finitely generated module and$N$ is a submodule, then$M/N$ is finitely generated (by the images of the generators of$M$ under$\pi$). The converse fails:$N$ need not be finitely generated even if$M$ and$M/N$ are, unless$R$ is Noetherian. When$R$ is Noetherian, every submodule of a finitely generated module is finitely generated, which is why Noetherian rings provide the most tractable setting for finitely generated module theory.
