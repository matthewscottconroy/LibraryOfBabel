# 25.2 Submodules and Quotients

The internal structure of a module is organized by its submodules — those subsets closed under both the additive group operation and the ring action. The submodule test reduces to two conditions: nonemptiness (or containment of the zero element) together with closure under subtraction and closure under multiplication by ring elements. This economy of conditions mirrors the subgroup and subring tests and will be used constantly throughout the chapter. The collection of all submodules of $M$ forms a lattice under inclusion, with intersection as meet and sum $N_1 + N_2 = \{n_1 + n_2 : n_i \in N_i\}$ as join. Understanding this lattice is often the first step toward understanding the module itself.

Given a submodule $N \subseteq M$, the quotient module $M/N$ is constructed exactly as a quotient group: elements are cosets $m + N$, addition is $(m + N) + (m' + N) = (m + m') + N$, and the $R$-action is $r \cdot (m + N) = rm + N$. Well-definedness of the scalar action uses nothing more than the submodule axioms — the key point is that for $m + N = m' + N$ (i.e., $m - m' \in N$) one needs $rm - rm' = r(m - m') \in N$, which holds because $N$ is closed under the ring action. This simplicity, compared with the quotient construction in nonabelian groups where normality must be verified separately, is one benefit of working in the abelian setting.

The isomorphism theorems transfer verbatim from groups to modules, with the same proofs. The First Isomorphism Theorem asserts that any surjective module homomorphism $f : M \to M'$ induces an isomorphism $M/\ker f \cong M'$; this is the fundamental factoring result that will be used in virtually every proof of the form "construct a module by showing it is the image of a known module." The Second and Third Isomorphism Theorems describe the submodule structure of quotients, and the Correspondence (Fourth) Isomorphism Theorem establishes a bijection between submodules of $M/N$ and submodules of $M$ containing $N$, preserving inclusion in both directions. This correspondence theorem is indispensable when analyzing the module structure of quotients — it means that understanding $M/N$ is equivalent to understanding the portion of the submodule lattice of $M$ that lies above $N$.
