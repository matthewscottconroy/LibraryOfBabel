# 25.3.1 $R$-Linear Maps;$\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(M,N)$ as an Abelian Group

Maps that preserve the structure of an algebraic object are the lifeblood of algebra. For modules, the structure-preserving maps are the $R$-linear maps, which simultaneously respect addition and scalar multiplication. These maps — also called module homomorphisms — are the morphisms of the category of$R$-modules, and their study is inseparable from the study of modules themselves.

\begin{definition}
Let $M$ and$N$ be left$R$-modules. An \emph{$R$-linear map} (or \emph{$R$-module homomorphism}) from$M$ to$N$ is a function$f: M \to N$ satisfying:
\begin{align*}
f(m + m') &= f(m) + f(m'), \\
f(rm) &= rf(m),
\end{align*}
for all $m, m' \in M$ and all$r \in R$. An$R$-linear bijection is an \emph{isomorphism}, and we write$M \cong N$ if an isomorphism exists.
\end{definition}

The first condition says $f$ is a group homomorphism of the underlying abelian groups; the second says$f$ intertwines the$R$-actions. When$R$ is a field, this is precisely the definition of a linear map between vector spaces. When$R = \mathbb{Z}$, the condition$f(rm) = rf(m)$ is automatic for any group homomorphism$f$, since the$\mathbb{Z}$-action is just iterated addition. Thus$\mathbb{Z}$-linear maps are precisely the group homomorphisms.

The two conditions can be combined: $f$ is$R$-linear if and only if$f(rm + n) = rf(m) + f(n)$ for all$r \in R$ and$m, n \in M$. This single condition captures both additivity (set$r = 1$) and scalar compatibility (set$n = 0$).

The set of all $R$-linear maps from$M$ to$N$ is denoted$\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(M, N)$. This set has a natural abelian group structure:

\begin{proposition}
With pointwise addition $(f + g)(m) = f(m) + g(m)$, the set$\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(M, N)$ is an abelian group.
\end{proposition}

To see that $f + g$ is$R$-linear:$(f + g)(rm) = f(rm) + g(rm) = rf(m) + rg(m) = r(f(m) + g(m)) = r(f+g)(m)$. The zero map$0: M \to N$,$0(m) = 0$, is the additive identity, and the additive inverse of$f$ is the map$(-f)(m) = -f(m)$.

When $R$ is commutative,$\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(M, N)$ is not merely an abelian group but an$R$-module in its own right. The$R$-action is$(r \cdot f)(m) = r \cdot f(m)$. To check that$r \cdot f$ is$R$-linear:$(r \cdot f)(sm) = r \cdot f(sm) = r \cdot sf(m) = rs \cdot f(m)$. For this to equal$s \cdot (r \cdot f)(m) = s \cdot rf(m) = sr \cdot f(m)$, we need$rs = sr$, which holds when$R$ is commutative. Over a non-commutative ring, this$R$-module structure does not generally exist, and$\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(M, N)$ is only an abelian group.

\begin{example}
Let $R = \mathbb{Z}$,$M = \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$,$N = \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$. A$\mathbb{Z}$-linear map$f: M \to N$ is determined by$f(1)$. Since$6 \cdot 1 = 0$ in$M$, we need$6f(1) = 0$ in$N$, so$4 \mid 6f(1)$, which means$2 \mid f(1)$ (since$4 \mid 6k$ iff$2 \mid 3k$ iff$2 \mid k$, as$\gcd(2,3)=1$). Thus$f(1) \in \{0, 2\}$ and$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $R = F$ be a field,$M = F^m$,$N = F^n$. A linear map$f: F^m \to F^n$ is determined by an$n \times m$ matrix:$f(e_j) = \sum_i a_{ij} e_i$ gives the matrix$(a_{ij})$. Pointwise addition of maps corresponds to entry-wise addition of matrices. Thus$\mathrm{Hom}_{F}(F^m, F^n) \cong M_{n \times m}(F)$ as abelian groups (and as$F$-vector spaces). The dimension of this Hom space is$mn$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For any module $M$,$\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(R, M) \cong M$ as abelian groups (and as$R$-modules when$R$ is commutative). The isomorphism sends$f: R \to M$ to$f(1) \in M$. To see this: an$R$-linear map$f: R \to M$ is determined by$f(1)$, since$f(r) = f(r \cdot 1) = rf(1)$, and any$m \in M$ gives the map$r \mapsto rm$. This is the Yoneda lemma in this context: maps out of$R$ are the same as elements of$M$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $R = \mathbb{Z}$,$M = \mathbb{Z}$,$N = \mathbb{Z}$. Then$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ via$f \mapsto f(1)$: every endomorphism of$\mathbb{Z}$ is multiplication by some integer. The ring structure on$\mathrm{End}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z})$ is the ring$\mathbb{Z}$ itself, with composition corresponding to multiplication of integers.
\end{example}

The $\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(M, N)$ construction is functorial in both$M$ and$N$. It is \emph{contravariant} in$M$: a map$\phi: M' \to M$ induces a map$\phi^*: \mathrm{Hom}_{R}(M, N) \to \mathrm{Hom}_{R}(M', N)$ by$\phi^*(f) = f \circ \phi$ (precomposition). It is \emph{covariant} in$N$: a map$\psi: N \to N'$ induces a map$\psi_*: \mathrm{Hom}_{R}(M, N) \to \mathrm{Hom}_{R}(M, N')$ by$\psi_*(f) = \psi \circ f$ (postcomposition). This bifunctoriality — contravariant in the first argument and covariant in the second — is central to homological algebra. In particular,$\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(-, N)$ is a left-exact contravariant functor, and$\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(M, -)$ is a left-exact covariant functor; the failure of these to be exact in general gives rise to the$\mathrm{Ext}$ functors.
