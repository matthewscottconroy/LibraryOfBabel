# 25.3.2 Kernel, Image, and the First Isomorphism Theorem

The kernel and image of a linear map encode the map's "failure to be injective" and "failure to be surjective" respectively. Together with the First Isomorphism Theorem, they give the fundamental decomposition of any $R$-linear map into a canonical factorization that exposes its essential structure.

\begin{proposition}
Let $f: M \to N$ be an$R$-linear map. Then$\ker f = \{m \in M : f(m) = 0\}$ is a submodule of$M$, and$\mathrm{im}\, f = \{f(m) : m \in M\}$ is a submodule of$N$.
\end{proposition}

The proof is a direct application of the submodule test. For $\ker f$: if$f(m) = f(m') = 0$, then$f(m - m') = f(m) - f(m') = 0$, so$m - m' \in \ker f$. And$f(rm) = rf(m) = r \cdot 0 = 0$, so$rm \in \ker f$. For$\mathrm{im}\, f$: if$f(m), f(m') \in \mathrm{im}\, f$, then$f(m) - f(m') = f(m - m') \in \mathrm{im}\, f$. And$rf(m) = f(rm) \in \mathrm{im}\, f$.

Knowing the kernel completely determines injectivity:

\begin{proposition}
An $R$-linear map$f: M \to N$ is injective if and only if$\ker f = 0$.
\end{proposition}

This is because $f$ is a group homomorphism of the underlying abelian groups, and a group homomorphism is injective if and only if its kernel is trivial.

The First Isomorphism Theorem is the statement that every $R$-linear map factors canonically as a surjection followed by an isomorphism:

\begin{theorem}[First Isomorphism Theorem]
Let $f: M \to N$ be$R$-linear. The map$\bar{f}: M/\ker f \to \mathrm{im}\, f$ defined by$\bar{f}(m + \ker f) = f(m)$ is an isomorphism of$R$-modules. In particular,$M/\ker f \cong \mathrm{im}\, f$.
\end{theorem}

The factorization is: $M \xrightarrow{\pi} M/\ker f \xrightarrow{\bar{f}} \mathrm{im}\, f \hookrightarrow N$, where$\pi$ is the quotient map and the last map is the inclusion. The map$f$ is "essentially" the isomorphism$\bar{f}$, with the surjection$\pi$ accounting for the many-to-one behavior of$f$ (the kernel) and the inclusion accounting for the fact that$f$ may not surject onto all of$N$.

\begin{example}
Let $f: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ be the natural map$f(n) = \bar{n}$. Then$\ker f = 6\mathbb{Z}$ and$\mathrm{im}\, f = \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$. The First Isomorphism Theorem gives$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, confirming the quotient definition.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $R = F[x]$,$M = F[x]$, and define$f: F[x] \to F^n$ by$f(g) = (g(0), g'(0), \ldots, g^{(n-1)}(0))$ — the first$n$ Taylor coefficients. This is$F$-linear (it is the map sending$g$ to the truncation of its Taylor series at degree$n-1$). The kernel is$(x^n)$ (polynomials divisible by$x^n$), and the image is all of$F^n$. The First Isomorphism Theorem gives$F[x]/(x^n) \cong F^n$ as$F$-vector spaces.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $R = M_2(\mathbb{R})$, the ring of$2 \times 2$ real matrices, and let$M = \mathbb{R}^2$ (column vectors),$N = \mathbb{R}^2$. For a matrix$A \in M_2(\mathbb{R})$, the map$f_A: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ defined by$f_A(v) = Av$ is$\mathbb{R}$-linear (in fact,$R$-linear). The kernel is the null space$\ker A$ and the image is the column space$\mathrm{im}\, A$. The rank-nullity theorem from linear algebra,$\dim(\ker A) + \dim(\mathrm{im}\, A) = 2$, is a consequence of the First Isomorphism Theorem over the field$\mathbb{R}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $V$ be an$F[x]$-module (a vector space with operator$T$), and let$p(x) \in F[x]$. The map$f: V \to V$ defined by$f(v) = p(T)(v)$ is$F[x]$-linear (since$p(T)$ commutes with any element of$F[x]$ acting on$V$, because$F[x]$ is commutative). The kernel$\ker f = \{v \in V : p(T)(v) = 0\}$ is the$p(T)$-null space, a$T$-invariant subspace. The image$\mathrm{im}\, f = p(T)(V)$ is also$T$-invariant. The First Isomorphism Theorem gives$V/\ker f \cong \mathrm{im}\, f$ as$F[x]$-modules.
\end{example}

The First Isomorphism Theorem is used constantly in practice via the following strategy: to show $M/N \cong P$, construct a surjective$R$-linear map$f: M \to P$ with$\ker f = N$, then invoke the theorem. This is far more efficient than constructing an explicit isomorphism$M/N \to P$ directly, since the coset representatives in$M/N$ can be unwieldy.

A map $f: M \to N$ is an isomorphism if and only if it is simultaneously injective and surjective. In the language of the theorem, this means$\ker f = 0$ and$\mathrm{im}\, f = N$, so$M \cong M/0 = M \cong N$. Two modules are isomorphic if and only if they are connected by a bijective$R$-linear map, and the First Isomorphism Theorem tells us how to systematically look for such maps: find a surjection$M \to N$ and check its kernel.
