# 25.3.3 Endomorphism Rings $\mathrm{End}_{R}(M)$

Among all $R$-linear maps, the self-maps$f: M \to M$ carry an especially rich structure: they can be composed with each other, giving an additional multiplicative operation on top of pointwise addition. This additional structure makes the collection of all endomorphisms into a ring, the \emph{endomorphism ring}, which encodes fundamental information about the module and connects module theory to ring theory in a deep way.

\begin{definition}
The \emph{endomorphism ring} of an $R$-module$M$ is the set$\mathrm{End}_{R}(M) = \mathrm{Hom}_{R}(M, M)$, equipped with pointwise addition$(f + g)(m) = f(m) + g(m)$ and multiplication by composition$(f \circ g)(m) = f(g(m))$.
\end{definition}

This is indeed a ring: $(\mathrm{End}_{R}(M), +)$ is the abelian group$\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(M, M)$ from Section 25.3.1; composition is associative (composition of functions always is); the identity map$\mathrm{id}_{M}$ is the multiplicative identity; and both distributive laws hold because$f \circ (g + h) = f \circ g + f \circ h$ (since$f$ is additive) and$(f + g) \circ h = f \circ h + g \circ h$ (by definition of pointwise addition). Note that$\mathrm{End}_{R}(M)$ is not generally commutative: composition of linear maps is not commutative in general.

The module $M$ is itself a left$\mathrm{End}_{R}(M)$-module, with the action given by$f \cdot m = f(m)$. This gives a ring homomorphism$\mathrm{End}_{R}(M)^{\mathrm{op}} \to \mathrm{End}(M)$ (we need the opposite ring because function composition is conventionally written from right to left). More importantly, knowing$\mathrm{End}_{R}(M)$ often allows us to read off structural information about$M$ itself.

\begin{example}
For the free module $R^n$ with standard basis$\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$, any endomorphism$f: R^n \to R^n$ is determined by the$n$ images$f(e_j) = \sum_{i=1}^n a_{ij} e_i$. The assignment$f \mapsto (a_{ij})$ is an isomorphism of rings$\mathrm{End}_{R}(R^n) \cong M_{n}(R)^{\mathrm{op}}$, where$M_n(R)$ is the ring of$n \times n$ matrices over$R$. (The opposite arises because composing maps corresponds to multiplying matrices in the opposite order if we use column vectors and the standard convention.) Over commutative rings,$M_n(R) \cong M_n(R)^{\mathrm{op}}$ via transposition, so the distinction often disappears. This is the module-theoretic foundation of matrix algebra: matrices are endomorphisms of the free module$R^n$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For the $\mathbb{Z}$-module$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, every endomorphism is determined by the image of$\bar{1}$. Since$n \cdot \bar{1} = \bar{0}$, the image$f(\bar{1})$ must satisfy$n \cdot f(\bar{1}) = 0$, which means$f(\bar{1}) \in \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. Any element works, so$\mathrm{End}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ as rings (via$f \mapsto f(\bar{1})$, and the ring structure on$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ corresponds to composition). In particular, the endomorphism ring is commutative.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For a simple module $M$ (one with no proper nonzero submodules), Schur's lemma applies. If$f: M \to M$ is$R$-linear and$f \neq 0$, then$\ker f$ is a proper submodule of$M$ (since$f \neq 0$ implies$\ker f \neq M$) and$\mathrm{im}\, f$ is a nonzero submodule. By simplicity,$\ker f = 0$ and$\mathrm{im}\, f = M$, so$f$ is an isomorphism — hence invertible. This shows that every nonzero element of$\mathrm{End}_{R}(M)$ is invertible, so$\mathrm{End}_{R}(M)$ is a \emph{division ring} (a skew field). When$R$ is a$k$-algebra over an algebraically closed field$k$ and$M$ is finite-dimensional over$k$, the only finite-dimensional division algebra over$k$ is$k$ itself (by elementary algebra), giving$\mathrm{End}_{R}(M) \cong k$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For the $\mathbb{Z}$-module$\mathbb{Q}$, every endomorphism$f: \mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{Q}$ is determined by$f(1) \in \mathbb{Q}$: since$f(n) = nf(1)$ for integers and$nf(1/n) = f(1)$ implies$f(1/n) = f(1)/n$, we get$f(p/q) = (p/q)f(1)$. Thus$\mathrm{End}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Q}) \cong \mathbb{Q}$ as rings. In general, for any localization$S^{-1}R$, the endomorphism ring as an$R$-module is$S^{-1}R$ itself.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For a direct sum $M = M_1 \oplus M_2$ where$\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(M_1, M_2) = 0 = \mathrm{Hom}_{R}(M_2, M_1)$, the endomorphism ring decomposes as a matrix ring:
$$\mathrm{End}_{R}(M) \cong \begin{pmatrix} \mathrm{End}_{R}(M_1) & 0 \\ 0 & \mathrm{End}_{R}(M_2) \end{pmatrix} \cong \mathrm{End}_{R}(M_1) \times \mathrm{End}_{R}(M_2).$$
This is the module-theoretic reflection of a direct sum decomposition: when the two summands do not interact (no maps between them), the endomorphisms split accordingly.
\end{example}

The connection between module decompositions and idempotents in the endomorphism ring is fundamental. An element $e \in \mathrm{End}_{R}(M)$ is \emph{idempotent} if$e^2 = e$. Every idempotent$e$ gives a decomposition$M = \mathrm{im}\, e \oplus \ker e$ (since for any$m$,$m = e(m) + (m - e(m))$, and$e(m - e(m)) = e(m) - e^2(m) = 0$). Conversely, every decomposition$M = P \oplus Q$ gives the idempotent$e$ projecting onto$P$ along$Q$. Thus the decompositions of$M$ as a direct sum are in bijection with idempotents in$\mathrm{End}_{R}(M)$. A module$M$ is \emph{indecomposable} (cannot be written as a direct sum of two nonzero submodules) if and only if$\mathrm{End}_{R}(M)$ has no nontrivial idempotents — a condition that plays a key role in the Krull-Schmidt theorem for module decompositions.
