# 25.4.1 External Direct Sum $\bigoplus M_{i}$; Universal Property

The central strategy of algebra is to decompose complicated objects into simpler pieces. For modules, the most natural way to assemble simpler modules into a larger one — while keeping them "independent" of each other — is the direct sum. The direct sum is both a concrete construction and a categorical notion characterized by a universal property, and understanding both descriptions is essential for using it effectively.

Suppose we are given a family of $R$-modules$\{M_{i}\}_{i \in I}$, indexed by some set$I$ (finite or infinite). The \emph{external direct sum} is designed to produce a new module that "contains a copy of each$M_i$" with the copies being "orthogonal" — no element of one copy interacts with any element of another.

\begin{definition}
The \emph{external direct sum} $\bigoplus_{i \in I} M_{i}$ is the set of all tuples$(m_{i})_{i \in I}$ with$m_{i} \in M_{i}$ such that$m_{i} = 0$ for all but finitely many$i$. Addition and$R$-action are componentwise:$(m_{i}) + (m_{i}') = (m_{i} + m_{i}')$ and$r(m_{i}) = (rm_{i})$.
\end{definition}

The finiteness condition is essential: it ensures that sums of elements remain well-defined (an element of a direct sum has only finitely many nonzero components, so adding finitely many such elements again yields a finitely-supported tuple), and that the canonical injections $\iota_{j}: M_{j} \to \bigoplus_{i} M_{i}$, sending$m_{j}$ to the tuple that is$m_{j}$ in position$j$ and$0$ elsewhere, have well-defined images.

The crucial characterization of the direct sum is its universal property as the \emph{coproduct} in the category of $R$-modules:

\begin{theorem}[Universal Property of the Direct Sum]
For any family of $R$-linear maps$f_{i}: M_{i} \to N$ into a common target module$N$, there exists a unique$R$-linear map$f: \bigoplus_{i \in I} M_{i} \to N$ satisfying$f \circ \iota_{i} = f_{i}$ for every$i \in I$.
\end{theorem}

The unique map is given explicitly by $f((m_{i})) = \sum_{i \in I} f_{i}(m_{i})$, which is a finite sum because all but finitely many$m_{i}$ are zero. Well-definedness and$R$-linearity are immediate from the componentwise operations.

The universal property says: to map out of the direct sum, it suffices to specify where each summand goes, independently of all other summands. This is the "no interaction" property: specifying $f_{i}: M_{i} \to N$ for each$i$ determines$f$ completely, with no constraints linking different components. This is exactly the right notion of "assembling modules with no interactions."

\begin{example}
For a finite family $\{M_1, M_2\}$, the universal property specializes to: given maps$f_1: M_1 \to N$ and$f_2: M_2 \to N$, there is a unique map$f: M_1 \oplus M_2 \to N$ with$f(m_1, 0) = f_1(m_1)$ and$f(0, m_2) = f_2(m_2)$. Explicitly,$f(m_1, m_2) = f_1(m_1) + f_2(m_2)$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
The $\mathbb{Z}$-module$\mathbb{Z}^n = \mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z} \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathbb{Z}$ ($n$ copies) is the free abelian group of rank$n$. Its elements are$n$-tuples of integers. A$\mathbb{Z}$-linear map$f: \mathbb{Z}^n \to M$ to any abelian group$M$ is determined by the$n$ values$f(e_1), \ldots, f(e_n) \in M$, with no constraints. This is a special case of the universal property:$\mathbb{Z}$-linear maps out of$\mathbb{Z}^n$ are the same as tuples of elements in$M$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $I = \mathbb{N}$ and$M_n = \mathbb{Z}$ for each$n \in \mathbb{N}$. The direct sum$\bigoplus_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \mathbb{Z}$ consists of sequences$(a_0, a_1, a_2, \ldots)$ of integers with$a_n = 0$ for all sufficiently large$n$. This is the free abelian group on countably many generators. A$\mathbb{Z}$-linear map from$\bigoplus_{n} \mathbb{Z}$ to$M$ is the same as a sequence$(m_0, m_1, m_2, \ldots) \in M^{\mathbb{N}}$ with any values — unconstrained — but evaluated at a finitely-supported sequence.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $R = F$ be a field and$V_i$ be$F$-vector spaces. The direct sum$\bigoplus_{i} V_i$ of vector spaces is again a vector space, and its dimension equals$\sum_i \dim V_i$ (for finite families, or the appropriate infinite cardinal for infinite families). The universal property specializes to: linear maps out of$\bigoplus V_i$ are the same as families of linear maps from each$V_i$.
\end{example}

The universal property characterizes the direct sum up to isomorphism: if $P$ is any$R$-module equipped with$R$-linear maps$\iota_i: M_i \to P$ satisfying the same universal property, then$P \cong \bigoplus_{i} M_i$. This categorical characterization is often the most useful: to prove something about direct sums, it suffices to use the universal property rather than working with explicit tuples.

The direct sum is the correct building block for free modules: the free module $R^{(S)} = \bigoplus_{s \in S} R$ is the direct sum of$|S|$ copies of$R$, and every$R$-module is a quotient of such a free module (Section 26.1.4). The universal property then says: to define an$R$-linear map out of$R^{(S)}$, it suffices to specify where each basis vector$e_s$ goes, with no constraints — which is exactly the statement that free modules have bases.
