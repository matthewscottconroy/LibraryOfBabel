# 25.4.2 Direct Product $\prod M_{i}$; Difference from Direct Sum

The direct product is the "dual" construction to the direct sum: while the direct sum has the universal property of a coproduct (maps \emph{out} of the direct sum), the direct product has the universal property of a product (maps \emph{into} the direct product). For finite index sets the two constructions agree, but for infinite families they diverge in important ways, and understanding the difference is crucial for homological computations.

\begin{definition}
The \emph{direct product} $\prod_{i \in I} M_{i}$ is the set of all tuples$(m_{i})_{i \in I}$ with$m_{i} \in M_{i}$ for every$i \in I$, with no finiteness restriction. Addition and$R$-action are componentwise.
\end{definition}

For a finite index set $I$, every tuple is finitely-supported, so$\bigoplus_{i \in I} M_{i} = \prod_{i \in I} M_{i}$. Both constructions give the same module. For infinite index sets, the direct sum is a proper submodule of the direct product:$\bigoplus_{i} M_{i} \subsetneq \prod_{i} M_{i}$ whenever infinitely many$M_{i}$ are nonzero.

\begin{theorem}[Universal Property of the Direct Product]
For any family of $R$-linear maps$f_{i}: N \to M_{i}$ from a common source module$N$, there exists a unique$R$-linear map$f: N \to \prod_{i \in I} M_{i}$ satisfying$\pi_{i} \circ f = f_{i}$ for every$i$, where$\pi_{i}: \prod_{j} M_{j} \to M_{i}$ is the$i$-th projection.
\end{theorem}

The unique map is $f(n) = (f_{i}(n))_{i \in I}$: to map into a product, we specify the$i$-th component independently for each$i$. This is dual to the direct sum: maps \emph{into}$\prod M_{i}$ correspond to families of maps$N \to M_{i}$, while maps \emph{out of}$\bigoplus M_{i}$ correspond to families of maps$M_{i} \to N$.

\begin{example}
$\bigoplus_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \mathbb{Z}$ consists of eventually-zero sequences of integers, while$\prod_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \mathbb{Z} = \mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}$ consists of all integer sequences. The sequence$(1, 2, 3, 4, \ldots)$ belongs to the product but not the direct sum. The abelian group$\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}$ is far more complicated than$\bigoplus_n \mathbb{Z}$: it is not free abelian (a deep theorem of Specker and Baer), whereas$\bigoplus_n \mathbb{Z}$ is free abelian of countable rank.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $R = F$ be a field. The direct product$\prod_{i \in I} F$ is the$F$-vector space of all$I$-indexed families of elements of$F$. The direct sum$\bigoplus_{i \in I} F$ is the subspace of finitely-supported families. A linear map$f: \bigoplus_{i} F \to F$ is a finite linear functional, given by a sequence with finitely many nonzero entries. A linear map$f: \prod_{i} F \to F$ is more constrained — it must be continuous in an appropriate sense if$I$ is infinite — and the two Hom groups are generally different.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
The direct product $\prod_{n \geq 1} \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is the abelian group of sequences$(a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots)$ with$a_n \in \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. The direct sum$\bigoplus_{n \geq 1} \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ consists of such sequences with$a_n = 0$ for all large enough$n$. Both appear naturally in number theory: the direct product is related to profinite completions, while the direct sum classifies torsion-free quotients of finite abelian groups.
\end{example}

The distinction between direct sum and direct product becomes critical when applying the Hom functor:
\begin{align*}
\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(M, \prod_{i} N_{i}) &\cong \prod_{i} \mathrm{Hom}_{R}(M, N_{i}), \\
\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(\bigoplus_{i} M_{i}, N) &\cong \prod_{i} \mathrm{Hom}_{R}(M_{i}, N).
\end{align*}
These isomorphisms follow immediately from the respective universal properties. Note that $\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(\bigoplus M_{i}, N)$ is a \emph{product} (not a direct sum): a map out of an infinite direct sum is a choice of where each summand goes, with no finiteness constraint on the choices. Dually,$\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(M, \prod N_{i})$ is also a product: a map into an infinite product is a choice of component maps, again with no finiteness constraint.

\begin{example}
The formula $\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\bigoplus_n \mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}) \cong \prod_n \mathbb{Z}$ shows that the "dual" of the free module of countable rank is the uncountable module$\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}$. This is in sharp contrast with the finite-rank case, where$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}^n, \mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}^n$ — duality is an involution for finitely generated free modules. For infinite rank, the double dual$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}, \mathbb{Z})$ is even larger than$\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}$, and the canonical map$\bigoplus_n \mathbb{Z} \to \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\bigoplus_n \mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}), \mathbb{Z})$ is not an isomorphism. This failure of reflexivity for infinite-rank free modules is one of the reasons that finitely generated modules are easier to work with.
\end{example}

In homological algebra, the direct product is the right notion for building injective resolutions (injective modules arise as products), while the direct sum is the right notion for projective and free resolutions. The rule is: left adjoints (like tensor product and free module) commute with colimits (direct sums, cokernels), while right adjoints (like Hom) commute with limits (direct products, kernels). This fundamental principle, arising from the tensor-Hom adjunction (Chapter 28), explains the different roles of direct sums and direct products throughout homological algebra.
