# 25.4.3 Internal Direct Sums; Complementary Submodules

The external direct sum $M_1 \oplus M_2$ assembles two separate modules into a new one. But often we encounter a module$M$ that is already "built from" two submodules sitting inside it, and we want to recognize when those submodules constitute a direct sum decomposition. The notion of an internal direct sum captures this situation without requiring us to step outside$M$ to form a new module.

The key question is: when does $M$ split as a direct sum of two of its submodules? The answer requires both that the submodules together generate$M$ and that they are "independent" — no element lies in both except zero.

\begin{definition}
Let $M$ be an$R$-module and$N_1, N_2 \leq M$ submodules. We say$M$ is the \emph{internal direct sum} of$N_1$ and$N_2$, written$M = N_1 \oplus N_2$, if:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $N_1 + N_2 = M$ (the submodules generate$M$); and
\item $N_1 \cap N_2 = \{0\}$ (the submodules intersect trivially).
\end{enumerate}
In this case, $N_1$ and$N_2$ are \emph{complementary submodules}, and each is a \emph{complement} of the other in$M$.
\end{definition}

When $M = N_1 \oplus N_2$ (internally), there is a canonical isomorphism$M \cong N_1 \oplus N_2$ with the external direct sum: every element$m \in M$ has a unique decomposition$m = n_1 + n_2$ with$n_1 \in N_1$ and$n_2 \in N_2$. Uniqueness follows from condition (2): if$n_1 + n_2 = n_1' + n_2'$, then$n_1 - n_1' = n_2' - n_2 \in N_1 \cap N_2 = \{0\}$, so$n_1 = n_1'$ and$n_2 = n_2'$.

The uniqueness of the decomposition is not merely a convenience but is what makes the internal direct sum a genuine decomposition into independent parts. If $M = N_1 + N_2$ with$N_1 \cap N_2 \neq 0$, then some element$n \neq 0$ lies in both, and$n = n + 0 = 0 + n$ gives two different "decompositions" — the module is not a direct sum.

\begin{example}
Let $R = F$ be a field,$M = F^2$,$N_1 = F \cdot e_1$ (the$x$-axis),$N_2 = F \cdot e_2$ (the$y$-axis). Then$N_1 + N_2 = F^2$ and$N_1 \cap N_2 = \{0\}$, so$F^2 = N_1 \oplus N_2$ internally. More generally, any subspace$W \leq V$ in a vector space has a complement$W'$ (obtained by extending a basis of$W$ to a basis of$V$ and taking$W' =$ span of the additional basis vectors). Thus over a field, every submodule has a complement.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $R = \mathbb{Z}$,$M = \mathbb{Z}$,$N = 2\mathbb{Z}$. Does$N$ have a complement in$M$? We need a subgroup$N'$ with$2\mathbb{Z} + N' = \mathbb{Z}$ and$2\mathbb{Z} \cap N' = \{0\}$. If$N'$ contains an odd integer$k$, then$N'$ contains all multiples$mk$ and in particular$2k - k \cdot 2 = 0$ — but this doesn't give a contradiction directly. However,$2\mathbb{Z} + N' = \mathbb{Z}$ requires that$1 \in 2\mathbb{Z} + N'$, so$1 = 2j + n'$ for some$j \in \mathbb{Z}$ and$n' \in N'$, giving$n' = 1 - 2j$ is odd. If$N'$ contains such an$n'$, then$2n' \in N' \cap 2\mathbb{Z}$, and$2n' \neq 0$ (since$n' \neq 0$), contradicting$N' \cap 2\mathbb{Z} = \{0\}$. Thus$N = 2\mathbb{Z}$ has no complement in$\mathbb{Z}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $M = \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$,$N_1 = \{0, 3\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$,$N_2 = \{0, 2, 4\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$. Check:$N_1 + N_2 = \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ (since$3 + 4 = 7 \equiv 1$, so$1 \in N_1 + N_2$) and$N_1 \cap N_2 = \{0\}$ (the only common element is$0$). Thus$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} = N_1 \oplus N_2$ internally, and we recover the Chinese Remainder Theorem isomorphism$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional$F$-vector space with operator$T$, viewed as an$F[x]$-module. If$T$ has minimal polynomial$m_T = f_1 f_2$ with$\gcd(f_1, f_2) = 1$ (over$F[x]$, so$f_1$ and$f_2$ are coprime), then$V = \ker f_1(T) \oplus \ker f_2(T)$ as$F[x]$-modules. The two submodules$\ker f_1(T)$ and$\ker f_2(T)$ are complementary: their sum is all of$V$ (by the Bezout relation$af_1 + bf_2 = 1$ in$F[x]$) and their intersection is zero (since any$v$ in both satisfies$f_1(T)v = 0$ and$f_2(T)v = 0$, hence$(a(T)f_1(T) + b(T)f_2(T))v = v = 0$). This is the primary decomposition theorem for linear operators, recoverable from the structure theorem in Chapter 27.
\end{example}

The failure of arbitrary submodules to have complements is precisely what distinguishes module theory over general rings from linear algebra over fields. The submodules that \emph{do} have complements in every ambient module are the \emph{projective} modules (Chapter 26). A module $P$ is projective if and only if every surjection$M \to P$ splits — equivalently,$P$ is always a direct summand of any module it sits inside as a direct summand of a free module. The existence of complements for every subspace in a vector space is therefore the statement that every$F$-module is projective — true over fields but not over general rings. This contrast is the starting point for the entire theory of projective, injective, and flat modules in Chapter 26.
