# 25.4 Direct Sums and Products

Building complex modules out of simpler ones via direct sums and products is one of the central operations of module theory, and the distinction between the two constructions — identical for finite families but divergent for infinite ones — carries significant content. The direct product $\prod_{i \in I} M_i$ consists of all tuples $(m_i)_{i \in I}$ with $m_i \in M_i$ for every $i$, with componentwise operations; it is characterized by the universal property for maps into it, meaning that any family of homomorphisms $f_i : N \to M_i$ factors uniquely through the projections $\pi_j : \prod M_i \to M_j$. The direct sum $\bigoplus_{i \in I} M_i$ consists of those tuples with only finitely many nonzero components; it is characterized by the universal property for maps out of it, meaning that any family of homomorphisms $f_i : M_i \to N$ factors uniquely through the inclusions $\iota_j : M_j \to \bigoplus M_i$.

For finite index sets, $\bigoplus_{i=1}^n M_i = \prod_{i=1}^n M_i$, and the common object is denoted $M_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus M_n$. An external direct sum is the construction of a new module from separate pieces; an internal direct sum identifies $M$ as $\bigoplus N_i$ when $M$ is the sum of submodules $N_i$ that intersect trivially — meaning $N_j \cap \sum_{i \neq j} N_i = 0$ for each $j$. Internally and externally defined direct sums are isomorphic, but the internal condition is the more natural one when analyzing the structure of a given module. A submodule $N \subseteq M$ is a direct summand if there exists a complementary submodule $N'$ with $M = N \oplus N'$; the existence of such a complement is a strong condition and is precisely the defining characteristic of projective modules, as Chapter 26 will discuss.

The direct sum decomposition plays a central role in classification. The structure theorem of Chapter 27 asserts that every finitely generated module over a PID is a direct sum of cyclic modules; this means the problem of classifying such modules reduces to understanding the individual summands and how many of each type appear. The endomorphism ring of a direct sum $M \oplus N$ is the ring of $2 \times 2$ matrices with entries $\text{End}(M)$, $\text{Hom}(N,M)$, $\text{Hom}(M,N)$, and $\text{End}(N)$ in the four positions, which is the algebraic basis for the matrix representation of linear maps once a direct sum decomposition has been chosen. Direct sums also interact cleanly with the other constructions of the chapter: $\text{Hom}_R(M_1 \oplus M_2, N) \cong \text{Hom}_R(M_1, N) \oplus \text{Hom}_R(M_2, N)$, expressing that Hom turns direct sums in the first variable into direct products.
