# 25.5.2 Cyclic Modules $R/I$; Annihilators

At the simplest end of the spectrum of finitely generated modules sits the \emph{cyclic module}: a module generated by a single element. Cyclic modules are the module-theoretic analogue of cyclic groups, and they play the same foundational role: just as every finitely generated abelian group decomposes into cyclic groups (the Fundamental Theorem), every finitely generated module over a PID decomposes into cyclic modules (the Structure Theorem of Chapter 27). Understanding cyclic modules in detail is therefore the key to understanding finitely generated modules over PIDs.

\begin{definition}
An $R$-module$M$ is \emph{cyclic} if there exists$m \in M$ with$M = Rm = \{rm : r \in R\}$.
\end{definition}

For any element $m \in M$, define the \emph{annihilator} of$m$ to be
$$\mathrm{Ann}_{R}(m) = \{r \in R : rm = 0\}.$$
This is a left ideal of $R$: if$r_1 m = r_2 m = 0$, then$(r_1 - r_2)m = 0$, and for any$s \in R$,$(sr)m = s(rm) = 0$. The annihilator measures how much of$R$ annihilates$m$, or equivalently, how many relations the element$m$ satisfies.

\begin{theorem}
Every cyclic module is isomorphic to $R/I$ for some left ideal$I$. Specifically,$Rm \cong R/\mathrm{Ann}_{R}(m)$.
\end{theorem}

The isomorphism is given by the surjective map $\phi: R \to Rm$,$\phi(r) = rm$, whose kernel is exactly$\mathrm{Ann}_{R}(m)$. By the First Isomorphism Theorem,$Rm \cong R/\mathrm{Ann}_{R}(m)$.

Conversely, for any left ideal $I \leq R$, the quotient$R/I$ is a cyclic$R$-module generated by the coset$\bar{1} = 1 + I$, and$\mathrm{Ann}_{R}(\bar{1}) = I$. Thus the cyclic$R$-modules are exactly (up to isomorphism) the modules of the form$R/I$ for some left ideal$I$, and the annihilator of the generator is$I$. This gives a complete classification of cyclic modules: two cyclic modules$R/I$ and$R/J$ are isomorphic if and only if$I$ and$J$ are isomorphic as left$R$-modules (which for two-sided ideals in a commutative ring means$I = J$).

\begin{example}
For $R = \mathbb{Z}$: the cyclic$\mathbb{Z}$-modules are$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ for$n \geq 0$. When$n = 0$,$\mathbb{Z}/0\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}$ is the cyclic module with trivial annihilator (generator of infinite order). When$n \geq 1$,$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ has generator$\bar{1}$ with$\mathrm{Ann}(\bar{1}) = n\mathbb{Z}$, corresponding to an element of "order$n$." The classification of cyclic$\mathbb{Z}$-modules is thus: one for each$n \geq 0$, indexed by the annihilator ideal.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For $R = F[x]$: the cyclic$F[x]$-modules are$F[x]/(f)$ for polynomials$f \in F[x]$. The annihilator of the generator$\bar{1}$ is$(f)$. Two such modules$F[x]/(f)$ and$F[x]/(g)$ are isomorphic if and only if$f$ and$g$ are associate (differ by a unit, i.e., a nonzero scalar in$F$). The dimension of$F[x]/(f)$ as an$F$-vector space is$\deg f$ (with basis$\{1, x, x^2, \ldots, x^{\deg f - 1}\}$). When$f$ is irreducible,$F[x]/(f)$ is a field — the splitting field of$f$ over$F$ (if$f$ has degree$n$, this is a degree-$n$ extension of$F$).
\end{example}

\begin{example}
In the $F[x]$-module$V = F^2$ with operator$T = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, take$m = e_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Then$x \cdot e_1 = Te_1 = e_2$ and$x^2 \cdot e_1 = T^2 e_1 = Te_2 = 0$. So$\mathrm{Ann}_{F[x]}(e_1) = (x^2)$ and$F[x] e_1 = \{e_1, e_2\} = V$. Thus$V$ is cyclic, generated by$e_1$, and$V \cong F[x]/(x^2)$. This is the module corresponding to the$2 \times 2$ nilpotent Jordan block.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $R = M_2(F)$ (the matrix ring) and$M = F^2$ (column vectors). The cyclic submodule generated by$e_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ is$M_2(F) e_1 = F^2$ (all column vectors, since any column vector$\begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a & 0 \\ b & 0 \end{pmatrix} e_1$). The annihilator of$e_1$ in$M_2(F)$ is$\left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 0 & a \\ 0 & b \end{pmatrix} : a, b \in F \right\}$, the left ideal of matrices with first column zero. Thus$F^2 \cong M_2(F) / \mathrm{Ann}(e_1)$ is cyclic as an$M_2(F)$-module.
\end{example}

The annihilator of the entire module $M$ is$\mathrm{Ann}_{R}(M) = \{r \in R : rm = 0 \text{ for all } m \in M\} = \bigcap_{m \in M} \mathrm{Ann}_{R}(m)$. This is a two-sided ideal of$R$ (if$R$ is commutative): if$ra = 0$ for all$a \in M$ and$s \in R$, then$r(sm) = r(sm)$; for commutative$R$, this gives$(rs)(m) = r(s(m)) = r(sm) = s(rm) = 0$, so$rs \in \mathrm{Ann}_{R}(M)$. For cyclic modules$M = R/I$, the annihilator of$M$ is$I$ (when$I$ is a two-sided ideal). The annihilator is thus the "order ideal" of the module, measuring the ring-theoretic constraints that$M$ imposes on scalar multiplication.

The structure theorem for modules over a PID (Chapter 27) expresses every finitely generated module as a direct sum of cyclic modules $R/(d_i)$, with the annihilators$(d_i)$ satisfying$d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_k$. The invariant factors$d_i$ are essentially the annihilators of the generators of the cyclic summands, arranged in order of divisibility. This is why understanding cyclic modules and their annihilators is the key prerequisite for the structure theorem.
