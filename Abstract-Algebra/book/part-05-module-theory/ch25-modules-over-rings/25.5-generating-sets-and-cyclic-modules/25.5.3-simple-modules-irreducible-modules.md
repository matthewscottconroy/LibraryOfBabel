# 25.5.3 Simple Modules (Irreducible Modules)

Having defined the basic objects of module theory, we now identify the "indecomposable atoms" — the modules that cannot be broken down any further. A nonzero module $M$ is *simple* (also called *irreducible*) if it has no submodules other than$0$ and$M$ itself. In other words, there is no proper nontrivial submodule: any nonzero submodule is all of$M$.

Simple modules are automatically cyclic. For any nonzero element $m \in M$, the cyclic submodule$Rm$ is a nonzero submodule of$M$, so by simplicity$Rm = M$. Moreover, the annihilator$\mathrm{Ann}(m) = \{r \in R : rm = 0\}$ must be a maximal left ideal of$R$ — precisely because$R/\mathrm{Ann}(m) \cong Rm = M$ has no proper nonzero submodules, which corresponds exactly to$\mathrm{Ann}(m)$ being maximal. Conversely, every quotient$R/\mathfrak{m}$ by a maximal left ideal$\mathfrak{m}$ is a simple module. This gives a complete description: the simple$R$-modules are precisely the modules of the form$R/\mathfrak{m}$ for maximal left ideals$\mathfrak{m} \subseteq R$.

The most fundamental theorem about simple modules is *Schur's Lemma*: any nonzero $R$-linear map$f: M \to N$ between simple modules is an isomorphism. To see this, note that$\ker f$ is a submodule of$M$; since$f \neq 0$, we have$\ker f \neq M$, so$\ker f = 0$ by simplicity. Similarly,$\mathrm{im}\, f$ is a nonzero submodule of$N$, hence$\mathrm{im}\, f = N$. Thus$f$ is bijective. An important corollary is that$\mathrm{End}_R(M)$ is a division ring whenever$M$ is simple: every nonzero endomorphism is an automorphism, so$\mathrm{End}_R(M)$ satisfies the division ring axiom. When$R$ is a$k$-algebra and$M$ is a finite-dimensional simple module over an algebraically closed field$k$, Schur's Lemma forces$\mathrm{End}_R(M) \cong k$ — a fact that is essential in representation theory.

Simple modules are the correct notion of "irreducible representation" in the context of ring theory. Just as irreducible representations of a group $G$ are the atomic building blocks from which all representations are assembled, simple$R$-modules are the atoms of module theory. The question of when every module decomposes as a direct sum of simples — and what happens when such a decomposition fails — is the central theme of the theory of semisimple modules and rings. The Jordan–Hölder theorem ensures that any composition series of a module has well-defined simple composition factors regardless of how the series is chosen, making the multiset of simple factors a genuine invariant.

The structural role of simple modules extends in multiple directions. In the representation theory of finite groups, the simple $\mathbb{C}[G]$-modules are precisely the irreducible complex representations of$G$, and their number equals the number of conjugacy classes of$G$. In commutative algebra, the simple modules over a commutative ring$R$ are the fields$R/\mathfrak{m}$ for maximal ideals$\mathfrak{m}$. In homological algebra, injective modules (Section 26.3) are built up from injective hulls of simple modules, and these injective hulls are the "atoms" of the injective side of module theory.

\begin{definition}
Let $R$ be a ring. An$R$-module$M$ is \textit{simple} (or \textit{irreducible}) if$M \neq 0$ and the only submodules of$M$ are$0$ and$M$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Schur's Lemma]
Let $M$ and$N$ be simple$R$-modules. Then every nonzero$R$-module homomorphism$f: M \to N$ is an isomorphism. In particular,$\mathrm{End}_R(M)$ is a division ring.
\end{theorem}

**Example 1.** Let $R = \mathbb{Z}$ and$M = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ for a prime$p$. The submodules of$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ as a$\mathbb{Z}$-module are its subgroups, which are$\{0\}$ and$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ since$p$ is prime. Thus$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ is a simple$\mathbb{Z}$-module. Any$\mathbb{Z}$-module map$f: \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ is determined by$f(\bar{1}) \in \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$, and it is nonzero if and only if$f(\bar{1}) \neq 0$. In that case$f$ is multiplication by a nonzero element of$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$, hence an automorphism. We have$\mathrm{End}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$, which is indeed a field.

**Example 2.** Let $R = k$ be a field. Then the only simple$k$-module is$k$ itself (up to isomorphism). Indeed, any nonzero module over a field is a vector space, and a simple vector space must have dimension 1 — any vector spans the whole space. Correspondingly,$\mathrm{End}_k(k) \cong k$, consistent with Schur's Lemma. The simple modules over$k$ are in bijection with the maximal ideals of$k$, and$k$ itself has only one maximal ideal, namely$0$.

**Example 3.** Let $R = M_n(k)$ be the ring of$n \times n$ matrices over a field$k$. The standard module$k^n$ (column vectors, with$R$ acting by left multiplication) is simple: if$v \in k^n$ is nonzero, then for any$w \in k^n$ there exists a matrix sending$v$ to$w$, so$M_n(k) \cdot v = k^n$. Thus$k^n$ is the unique simple$M_n(k)$-module up to isomorphism, and$\mathrm{End}_{M_n(k)}(k^n) \cong k$. Note that$R = M_n(k)$ is a simple ring with a unique simple module, exemplifying the Artin–Wedderburn classification.

**Example 4.** Let $G = \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ and$R = \mathbb{C}[G]$ the complex group algebra. Then$R \cong \mathbb{C}[x]/(x^3 - 1) \cong \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C}$ since$x^3 - 1 = (x-1)(x - \omega)(x - \omega^2)$ splits completely over$\mathbb{C}$, where$\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$. The three simple$\mathbb{C}[G]$-modules correspond to the three irreducible complex representations of$G$: the trivial representation ($g \mapsto 1$), and the two nontrivial one-dimensional representations ($g \mapsto \omega$ and$g \mapsto \omega^2$). Their count matches the number of conjugacy classes of the abelian group$G$.

**Example 5.** Let $R = \mathbb{Z}[G]$ for the same group$G = \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$, but now over$\mathbb{Z}$. The module$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ for any prime$p$ is a simple$\mathbb{Z}$-module, but as a$\mathbb{Z}[G]$-module it may or may not be simple depending on whether$G$ acts trivially or not. If$g$ acts as the identity on$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$, the module is simple as a$\mathbb{Z}[G]$-module (same argument as the trivial$\mathbb{Z}$-module). If$p = 3$, then$\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}[G] \cong \mathbb{F}_3[x]/(x^3 - 1) = \mathbb{F}_3[x]/(x-1)^3$, which is a local ring, and the unique simple module is$\mathbb{F}_3$. This illustrates how the field characteristic interacts with the group structure to determine the simple modules.

Simple modules are the correct notion of "irreducible representation" in the context of ring theory, and their systematic study — including when a module decomposes as a direct sum of simples — is the content of the theory of semisimple modules treated in Chapters 42–43.
