# 25.5 Generating Sets and Cyclic Modules

A module $M$ is generated by a subset $S \subseteq M$ if every element of $M$ can be written as a finite $R$-linear combination of elements of $S$: $m = \sum_{i=1}^k r_i s_i$ for some $r_i \in R$ and $s_i \in S$. The submodule generated by $S$ is the smallest submodule of $M$ containing $S$, and it equals the set of all such finite combinations. A module is finitely generated if it admits a finite generating set, which is the natural finiteness condition in module theory. Over a Noetherian ring — one in which every ideal is finitely generated — every submodule of a finitely generated module is again finitely generated; this is Noether's chain condition at work, and it guarantees that the ascending chain condition on submodules holds. Much of the structure theory in Chapter 27 operates under the hypothesis of finite generation over a PID.

The simplest nonzero modules are the cyclic ones. A module $M$ is cyclic if it is generated by a single element $m_0$: $M = Rm_0 = \{r \cdot m_0 : r \in R\}$. The map $\phi : R \to M$ defined by $\phi(r) = r \cdot m_0$ is a surjective $R$-module homomorphism, so by the First Isomorphism Theorem $M \cong R/\ker\phi$. The kernel $\text{Ann}_R(m_0) = \{r \in R : r \cdot m_0 = 0\}$ is called the annihilator of $m_0$, and it is a left ideal of $R$. Thus every cyclic module is isomorphic to $R/I$ for some left ideal $I$. The annihilator of the entire module $M$ is $\text{Ann}_R(M) = \{r \in R : r \cdot m = 0 \text{ for all } m \in M\}$, which is a two-sided ideal; it measures the extent to which the ring action is "trivial" on $M$ and is the analogue of the exponent of an abelian group.

A simple module is one that has no submodules other than $0$ and $M$ itself. Simple modules are the building blocks of the Jordan–Hölder theory for modules — they are the module-theoretic analogues of simple groups — and they are always cyclic. Indeed, any nonzero element $m$ of a simple module generates all of $M$ (since $Rm$ is a nonzero submodule), so $M \cong R/I$ for some maximal left ideal $I$. Conversely, $R/\mathfrak{m}$ is simple whenever $\mathfrak{m}$ is a maximal left ideal, because the submodules of $R/\mathfrak{m}$ correspond to left ideals of $R$ containing $\mathfrak{m}$, and there are none between $\mathfrak{m}$ and $R$. The classification of simple modules over a given ring is often the first step in understanding its representation theory, as Chapter 26 will illustrate through the endomorphism rings of direct sums of simples.
