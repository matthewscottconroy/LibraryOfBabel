# Chapter 25 — Modules over Rings

The concept of a module is one of the most productive generalizations in modern algebra. At first glance, the axioms look like those of a vector space — an abelian group $M$ equipped with a scalar multiplication by elements of a ring $R$, satisfying distributivity and compatibility conditions. But the shift from a field to an arbitrary ring is not merely cosmetic. It collapses the distinction between several structures that had previously appeared unrelated: abelian groups are $\mathbb{Z}$-modules; vector spaces are $F$-modules for a field $F$; representations of a group $G$ over a field $F$ are $F[G]$-modules; and ideals of a ring $R$ are $R$-modules. The module axioms are the common skeleton of all these theories, and once you see this, the earlier chapters on groups, rings, and linear algebra reorganize themselves around a single underlying concept.

The key examples are instructive precisely because they are so different on the surface. A $\mathbb{Z}$-module carries no "scalar multiplication" beyond what comes freely from the additive structure of an abelian group: $n \cdot m$ is just $m$ added to itself $n$ times, or negated if $n < 0$. No new data is needed; every abelian group is automatically a $\mathbb{Z}$-module in exactly one way. At the other extreme, an $F[x]$-module on a finite-dimensional vector space $V$ is precisely a choice of a linear operator $T : V \to V$, since $x$ must act by some endomorphism and the rest of the $F[x]$-action is forced. This identification — $F[x]$-module structures on $V$ correspond bijectively to linear operators on $V$ — is one of the organizing ideas of Chapter 27, where it enables the classification of linear operators up to similarity via the structure theorem.

Within any module one finds the expected substructures. A submodule is a subgroup closed under the ring action, and the quotient $M/N$ is constructed exactly as one constructs a quotient group, with well-definedness following from the normality built into the abelian group structure. The isomorphism theorems — first, second, third, and the correspondence theorem — hold for modules in precise analogy with their group-theoretic counterparts, and the proofs transfer with little change. Module homomorphisms, which must respect both the additive group structure and the scalar multiplication, form an abelian group $\text{Hom}_R(M, N)$ and in the case $M = N$ an endomorphism ring $\text{End}_R(M)$. This endomorphism ring is often noncommutative and carries significant information about the internal symmetry of $M$.

The chapter also introduces the tools needed to build modules out of pieces. The direct sum $M_1 \oplus M_2 \oplus \cdots \oplus M_n$ is characterized by a universal property for maps out of it, while the direct product is characterized by maps into it; over finite index sets these coincide, but the distinction matters for infinite families. A module is cyclic if it is generated by a single element, in which case it is isomorphic to $R/I$ for some left ideal $I$ called the annihilator. Simple modules — those with no proper nonzero submodules — are the analogue of simple groups and are always cyclic: they are isomorphic to $R/\mathfrak{m}$ for a maximal left ideal $\mathfrak{m}$. The interplay between generating sets, annihilators, and the ring structure is the foundation on which the entire structure theory of the following chapters is built.
