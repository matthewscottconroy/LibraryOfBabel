## Important Figures

- **Richard Dedekind (1831–1916)** — the module concept (*Modul*) appeared in his work on algebraic integers; Dedekind studied $\mathbb{Z}$-modules (abelian groups) and $R$-modules (ideals and their generalizations)
- **Emmy Noether (1882–1935)** — reframed all of ring theory and linear algebra in terms of modules; the isomorphism theorems for modules; the structure of Noetherian modules
- **Bartel van der Waerden (1903–1996)** — *Moderne Algebra* (1930–1931) systematized and disseminated the module-theoretic approach

## References and Primary Sources

- **E. Noether, "Abstrakter Aufbau der Idealtheorie..." (1927)** — modules as the unifying language
- **S. Lang, *Algebra* (rev. 3rd ed., Springer, 2002)**, Chs. III, XIII — modules over rings; structure theorem

## Examples, Applications, and Thought Experiments

- **Vector spaces are $F$-modules** — every $R$-module over a field $F = R$ is a vector space; modules over non-fields generalize by allowing $R$ to have zero divisors and by not requiring all nonzero elements to be invertible; the loss of "division" introduces torsion
- **$\mathbb{Z}$-modules are abelian groups** — a $\mathbb{Z}$-module is exactly an abelian group; the action of $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ is $n \cdot a = a + a + \cdots + a$ ($n$ times); the module structure on an abelian group is uniquely determined; no extra data needed
- **An ideal as a module** — every left ideal $I \subseteq R$ is an $R$-module; $\mathbb{Z}/(n)$ is a $\mathbb{Z}$-module; a maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$ gives a simple module $R/\mathfrak{m}$; the module-theoretic perspective on ideals unifies ring theory
- **Torsion** — in $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module, $6 \cdot x = 0$ for all $x$; torsion elements satisfy $r \cdot m = 0$ for some nonzero $r \in R$; over a field, no torsion occurs (free = no torsion); torsion is the obstruction to a module behaving like a vector space

## Exercises

1. Let $R$ be a ring and $M$ an abelian group. Suppose you are given a ring homomorphism $\phi : R \to \text{End}_{\mathbb{Z}}(M)$. Show directly from the module axioms that defining $r \cdot m := \phi(r)(m)$ makes $M$ into a left $R$-module, and that every left $R$-module structure on $M$ arises this way from a unique such homomorphism. Conclude that left $R$-module structures on $M$ correspond bijectively to ring homomorphisms $R \to \text{End}_{\mathbb{Z}}(M)$.

2. State and prove the submodule test: a nonempty subset $N \subseteq M$ of a left $R$-module $M$ is a submodule if and only if $x - y \in N$ for all $x, y \in N$ and $r \cdot n \in N$ for all $r \in R$, $n \in N$. Use this criterion to verify that the kernel of any $R$-module homomorphism $f : M \to M'$ is a submodule of $M$.

3. Let $M = \mathbb{Z}^2$ viewed as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module, and let $N$ be the submodule generated by $(2, 0)$ and $(0, 3)$. Describe $N$ explicitly, identify the quotient module $M/N$ as a familiar abelian group, and determine the annihilator $\text{Ann}_{\mathbb{Z}}(M/N)$.

4. Prove the First Isomorphism Theorem for modules: if $f : M \to M'$ is a homomorphism of $R$-modules, then $M/\ker f \cong \text{im}\, f$ as $R$-modules. Be careful to show that the natural map is well-defined and is an $R$-module homomorphism, not just a group homomorphism.

5. Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over a field $F$ and let $T : V \to V$ be a linear operator. Show that the definition $p(x) \cdot v := p(T)(v)$ for $p(x) \in F[x]$ and $v \in V$ makes $V$ into an $F[x]$-module. What is the annihilator $\text{Ann}_{F[x]}(V)$ in terms of $T$? Under what condition on $T$ is $V$ a cyclic $F[x]$-module?

6. A module $M$ is called simple if it has no submodules other than $0$ and $M$ itself. Prove that every simple $R$-module is isomorphic to $R/\mathfrak{m}$ for some maximal left ideal $\mathfrak{m} \subseteq R$. (Hint: fix any nonzero element $m \in M$ and consider the map $R \to M$ sending $r \mapsto r \cdot m$.)

7. Let $R$ be a commutative ring and $M$, $N$ left $R$-modules. Show that $\text{Hom}_R(M, N)$ is itself an $R$-module under pointwise addition and the action $(r \cdot f)(m) := r \cdot f(m)$. Give an example where $\text{Hom}_R(M, N) = 0$ even though both $M$ and $N$ are nonzero.

8. (Challenge) Let $M = \mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module. Determine all submodules of $M$, draw the submodule lattice, and identify each quotient $M/N$ as a familiar cyclic group. Use the correspondence theorem to match the submodules of $M$ with the ideals of $\mathbb{Z}$ that contain $12\mathbb{Z}$, and verify that the lattice of submodules of $M$ is anti-isomorphic to the lattice of divisors of $12$.
