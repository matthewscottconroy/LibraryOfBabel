# 26.1.1 Bases and Free Modules; the Universal Property

In linear algebra, every vector space has a basis: a linearly independent spanning set from which every element is uniquely recoverable as a finite linear combination of basis vectors. The analogous notion for modules — when it exists — is the central object of this section.

A subset $B \subseteq M$ is a *basis* for the$R$-module$M$ if every element$m \in M$ has a unique expression as a finite$R$-linear combination of elements of$B$: for every$m$, there is a unique choice of distinct elements$b_1, \ldots, b_k \in B$ and coefficients$r_1, \ldots, r_k \in R$ with$m = r_1 b_1 + \cdots + r_k b_k$. A module that has a basis is called *free*. The key word is "unique": a generating set without the uniqueness condition is just a generating set, but a basis forces no redundancy among the generators.

The power of having a basis lies in the *universal property*: for any $R$-module$N$ and any function$f: B \to N$, there exists a unique$R$-linear map$\tilde{f}: M \to N$ extending$f$. That is,$\tilde{f}(b) = f(b)$ for all$b \in B$, and$\tilde{f}$ is determined on all of$M$ by its values on$B$ via$\tilde{f}(r_1 b_1 + \cdots + r_k b_k) = r_1 f(b_1) + \cdots + r_k f(b_k)$. This property characterizes free modules entirely: a module is free on the set$B$ if and only if$B$ satisfies this universal property. In particular, free modules are the "most flexible" modules — maps out of a free module can be defined arbitrarily on a basis, with no constraints whatsoever.

The prototype of a free module is the direct sum $R^{(B)} = \bigoplus_{b \in B} R$: one copy of$R$ for each element of$B$, with the canonical basis$\{e_b\}_{b \in B}$ consisting of the elements with a$1$ in position$b$ and zeros elsewhere. Any free$R$-module of rank$|B|$ is isomorphic to$R^{(B)}$ via the unique isomorphism sending$b \mapsto e_b$. For$B = \{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$, we write$R^n$ for the free module of rank$n$; its elements are$n$-tuples$(r_1, \ldots, r_n)$ with componentwise addition and$R$-action. This is the direct analogue of$\mathbb{R}^n$ in the setting of an arbitrary ring.

The distinction between free and non-free modules is one of the defining features of module theory that has no counterpart in linear algebra. Many natural modules are not free. The cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ as a$\mathbb{Z}$-module is not free: the element$1 \in \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ generates the module, but it is not a basis element because$n \cdot 1 = 0$, violating linear independence. An ideal$I \subseteq R$ is a submodule of$R$ but is generally not free as an$R$-module unless it is principal and generated by a non-zero-divisor. Thus the category of$R$-modules is genuinely richer than the category of vector spaces.

\begin{definition}
Let $R$ be a ring. A left$R$-module$F$ is \textit{free} with basis$B \subseteq F$ if every element of$F$ can be written uniquely as a finite$R$-linear combination of elements of$B$. Equivalently,$F \cong R^{(B)} = \bigoplus_{b \in B} R$ via the map$e_b \mapsto b$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Universal Property of Free Modules]
Let $F$ be a free$R$-module with basis$B$. For any$R$-module$N$ and any set map$f: B \to N$, there exists a unique$R$-linear map$\tilde{f}: F \to N$ with$\tilde{f}(b) = f(b)$ for all$b \in B$.
\end{theorem}

**Example 1.** The polynomial ring $R[x]$ is a free$R$-module with basis$\{1, x, x^2, x^3, \ldots\}$: every polynomial$r_0 + r_1 x + \cdots + r_n x^n$ is uniquely a finite$R$-linear combination of monomials. This is an infinite free module (of countable rank). The universal property says: any$R$-module map$R[x] \to N$ is determined by the image of$x$ (subject to no constraints); this recovers the familiar fact that polynomial maps are determined by where the variable goes.

**Example 2.** The module $\mathbb{Z}^2$ is free of rank 2 over$\mathbb{Z}$ with basis$\{(1,0),(0,1)\}$. A$\mathbb{Z}$-linear map$f: \mathbb{Z}^2 \to M$ (for any abelian group$M$) is determined by$(f(1,0), f(0,1)) \in M^2$, and any pair of elements of$M$ works. This is the universal property in action:$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}^2, M) \cong M^2$.

**Example 3.** The ideal $2\mathbb{Z} \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$ is a free$\mathbb{Z}$-module of rank 1 with basis$\{2\}$: every element is uniquely$2k$ for$k \in \mathbb{Z}$, and$2$ generates$2\mathbb{Z}$ with no relations. More generally, any principal ideal$(r) \subseteq R$ generated by a non-zero-divisor is a free$R$-module of rank 1.

**Example 4.** The ideal $I = (2, x) \subseteq \mathbb{Z}[x]$ (generated by 2 and$x$) is *not* free as a$\mathbb{Z}[x]$-module. To see this: if$I$ were free of rank 1, it would be a principal ideal, but one can check that$I$ is not principal in$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ (since$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is not a PID). If it were free of rank 2 or higher, then$I$ would have rank 2 over$\mathbb{Z}[x]$, but$I \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}[x]} \mathbb{Q}[x] \cong \mathbb{Q}[x]$ has rank 1, a contradiction. Thus$I$ is a non-free module, illustrating that the distinction matters.

**Example 5.** As a $\mathbb{Z}$-module,$\mathbb{Q}$ is not free. Any element$q \in \mathbb{Q}$ satisfies relations with other elements (e.g.,$2 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 1 \cdot 1$), so no spanning set is linearly independent. More formally, if$\{q_\alpha\}$ were a basis, then$q_\alpha$ and$q_\beta$ would satisfy$q_\beta \cdot q_\alpha - q_\alpha \cdot q_\beta = 0$ only if one is a multiple of the other, but in$\mathbb{Q}$ every nonzero element is a rational multiple of every other. This shows that a torsion-free module need not be free.
