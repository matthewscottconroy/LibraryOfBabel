# 26.1.2 $\mathrm{Hom}_R(R^n, M) \cong M^n$; Free Modules Are the Most General

The universal property of free modules has an immediate and elegant consequence when we apply it to $R^n$, the free module of rank$n$ with standard basis$\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$. An$R$-linear map$f: R^n \to M$ is completely determined by the$n$ images$f(e_1), f(e_2), \ldots, f(e_n)$, which can be any elements of$M$. Given any tuple$(m_1, \ldots, m_n) \in M^n$, the assignment$e_j \mapsto m_j$ extends uniquely to an$R$-linear map. This establishes a bijection$\mathrm{Hom}_R(R^n, M) \leftrightarrow M^n$ sending$f$ to$(f(e_1), \ldots, f(e_n))$.

This bijection is natural: it is an isomorphism of abelian groups, and when $R$ is commutative it is an isomorphism of$R$-modules. The identification$\mathrm{Hom}_R(R^n, M) \cong M^n$ says that$R^n$ *represents* the functor$M \mapsto M^n$: to give a linear map out of$R^n$ into$M$ is the same as to choose$n$ elements of$M$. This is the prototypical example of a representable functor, and it explains why free modules play the role that they do in homological constructions: they are the modules for which computing$\mathrm{Hom}$ is as explicit as possible.

The key point is that free modules impose no constraints on maps out of them. Any assignment of values to basis elements works. In contrast, maps out of a module with relations must respect those relations — for instance, any map $f: \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \to M$ must satisfy$nf(1) = f(n \cdot 1) = f(0) = 0$, which constrains$f(1)$ to be an$n$-torsion element. Free modules are the "no-constraint" modules, and this is precisely why every module is a quotient of a free module (Section 26.1.4): the free module is built to be surjected onto anything.

The isomorphism $\mathrm{Hom}_R(R, M) \cong M$ (the case$n = 1$) is the most basic instance: a module map out of$R$ is determined by the image of$1 \in R$, which can be any element of$M$. This says$R$ represents the identity functor on$\mathrm{Mod}_R$ — a property called *Yoneda representability*. It also gives the foundational identity$\mathrm{Hom}_R(R, M) \cong M$ that underlies the Yoneda lemma in category theory.

The "most general" aspect of free modules appears most clearly in the following sense: for any property of modules that is preserved under direct sums and quotients, one can check the property on free modules first and then deduce it for all modules. This is the "free → quotient" proof strategy. Many theorems in homological algebra are proved by taking a free resolution $F_\bullet \to M \to 0$, verifying a claim on the free modules$F_i$ (where it is easy), and then deriving the claim for$M$ (where it may be hard) using exactness arguments.

\begin{proposition}
For any ring $R$, any$R$-module$M$, and any$n \geq 1$, there is a natural isomorphism of abelian groups
$$\mathrm{Hom}_R(R^n, M) \cong M^n,$$
given by $f \mapsto (f(e_1), \ldots, f(e_n))$. When$R$ is commutative, this is an isomorphism of$R$-modules.
\end{proposition}

**Example 1.** Take $R = \mathbb{Z}$,$n = 1$. Then$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}, M) \cong M$ for any abelian group$M$: the unique map sending$1 \mapsto m$ corresponds to$m \in M$. Composition with multiplication by$k$ gives$k \cdot m$, matching the$\mathbb{Z}$-module structure on$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}, M) \cong M$.

**Example 2.** Take $R = \mathbb{Z}$,$n = 2$,$M = \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$. Then$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}^2, \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}) \cong (\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z})^2$. An explicit map is$(a, b) \mapsto 2a + 3b$ (taken mod 6), corresponding to the pair$(2, 3) \in (\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z})^2$.

**Example 3.** Take $R = k[x]$ for a field$k$, and$M = k[x]/(x^2)$. Then$\mathrm{Hom}_{k[x]}(k[x], k[x]/(x^2)) \cong k[x]/(x^2)$, since the map is determined by the image of$1$, which can be any element of$k[x]/(x^2)$.

**Example 4.** Compare with $\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}, M)$: this is *not* all of$M$, but rather the$n$-torsion submodule$M[n] = \{m \in M : nm = 0\}$. For example,$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}) \cong \{0, 3\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ (the 2-torsion in$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$). This contrasts with the free case: maps out of a non-free module are constrained, not free to take any value.

**Example 5.** The isomorphism $\mathrm{Hom}_R(R^m, R^n) \cong M_{n \times m}(R)$: a map$R^m \to R^n$ is determined by the$n$-tuples that are the images of the$m$ standard basis vectors, forming an$n \times m$ matrix with entries in$R$. Composition of maps corresponds to matrix multiplication, making this isomorphism an isomorphism of rings between$\mathrm{End}_R(R^n) \cong M_n(R)$ and the matrix ring.
