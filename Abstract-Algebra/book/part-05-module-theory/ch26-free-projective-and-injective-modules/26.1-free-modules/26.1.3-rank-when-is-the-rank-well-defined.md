# 26.1.3 Rank; When Is the Rank Well-Defined?

The *rank* of a free module $M$ is the cardinality of a basis. For vector spaces, this is simply the dimension, and a fundamental theorem of linear algebra guarantees that any two bases of a vector space have the same cardinality. The question is whether this uniqueness extends from fields to arbitrary rings.

For commutative rings, rank is well-defined: any two bases of a free module $R^n$ have the same cardinality$n$. The cleanest proof proceeds by reduction to the vector space case. Pick any maximal ideal$\mathfrak{m} \subset R$; the residue field$R/\mathfrak{m}$ is a field, and the natural map$R^n \to (R/\mathfrak{m})^n$ converts any basis of$R^n$ to a basis of the vector space$(R/\mathfrak{m})^n$. Since the dimension of a vector space is unique, all bases of$R^n$ must have size$n$.

For noncommutative rings, the situation is more delicate. There exist rings $R$ for which$R \cong R^2$ as left$R$-modules, meaning$R$ has a basis of size 1 and also a basis of size 2. Such rings are said to lack the *invariant basis number* (IBN) property. Fortunately, most rings encountered in practice — commutative rings, Noetherian rings, group rings over a field, and all rings satisfying mild finiteness conditions — do satisfy IBN. Under this assumption, the rank of a free module is a well-defined invariant, and we write$\mathrm{rk}(M) = n$ for$M \cong R^n$ without ambiguity. Rank is the module-theoretic replacement for dimension, and it governs the free part of the structure theorem in Chapter 27.

The IBN property is closely related to the stability of rank under ring maps. If $\phi: R \to S$ is any ring map (e.g.,$R$ surjects onto a field), and$R$ has IBN, then the induced map on free modules respects rank: if$R^m \cong R^n$, tensoring with$S$ gives$S^m \cong S^n$, so$m = n$ by IBN for$S$. This is exactly the proof that commutative rings have IBN: the quotient map$R \to R/\mathfrak{m}$ reduces to the field case.

An explicit example of a ring lacking IBN: let $V$ be an infinite-dimensional vector space and$R = \mathrm{End}_k(V)$. Then$R \cong R^2$ as left$R$-modules:$V \cong V \oplus V$ as vector spaces (since$V$ is infinite-dimensional), and applying$\mathrm{Hom}_k(V, -)$ gives$R = \mathrm{Hom}_k(V, V) \cong \mathrm{Hom}_k(V, V \oplus V) \cong R \oplus R$. So$R$ has rank 1 and rank 2 simultaneously — IBN fails dramatically.

\begin{definition}
A ring $R$ has the \textit{invariant basis number} (IBN) property if$R^m \cong R^n$ as left$R$-modules implies$m = n$. The \textit{rank} of a free$R$-module$F$ is the cardinality of any basis, well-defined when$R$ has IBN.
\end{definition}

**Example 1.** Any field $k$ has IBN:$k^m \cong k^n$ iff$m = n$ (dimension theorem for vector spaces). The rank of$k^n$ as a$k$-module equals its dimension as a vector space.

**Example 2.** $\mathbb{Z}$ has IBN. If$\mathbb{Z}^m \cong \mathbb{Z}^n$, tensor with$\mathbb{Q}$:$\mathbb{Q}^m \cong \mathbb{Q}^n$ as$\mathbb{Q}$-vector spaces, so$m = n$. More generally, any commutative ring has IBN by the residue field argument.

**Example 3.** The free $\mathbb{Z}[x]$-module$\mathbb{Z}[x]^3$ has rank 3. Any basis must have exactly 3 elements. If we tensor with the quotient map$\mathbb{Z}[x] \to \mathbb{Z}[x]/(x) \cong \mathbb{Z}$, the rank is preserved since$\mathbb{Z}^3$ has rank 3.

**Example 4.** Let $R = M_2(\mathbb{Q})$, the ring of$2 \times 2$ rational matrices. Then$R \cong \mathbb{Q}^2 \oplus \mathbb{Q}^2$ as left$R$-modules (the two columns), so$R^1 \cong R_{\mathrm{col}}^2$ where$R_{\mathrm{col}} = \mathbb{Q}^2$ with the natural$R$-action. This means$R$ has IBN (rank is still well-defined since$M_2(\mathbb{Q})$ is Morita equivalent to$\mathbb{Q}$), but the "column module" is rank 1 as a projective module. This illustrates the subtle interplay between IBN and Morita equivalence.

**Example 5.** For a PID $R$ with fraction field$K$, the rank of a finitely generated torsion-free$R$-module$M$ (not necessarily free) is defined as$\dim_K(M \otimes_R K)$. When$M$ is free, this coincides with the basis-cardinality definition. The structure theorem (Chapter 27) shows that over a PID, finitely generated torsion-free modules are free, so the two definitions agree in that setting.
