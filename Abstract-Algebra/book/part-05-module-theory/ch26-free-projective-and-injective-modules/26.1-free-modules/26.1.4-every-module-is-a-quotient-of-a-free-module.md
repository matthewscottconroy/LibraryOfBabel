# 26.1.4 Every Module Is a Quotient of a Free Module

One of the most useful facts about free modules is that they are "universal" in a global sense: every $R$-module, no matter how complicated, is a quotient of a free module. This gives us a systematic way to present any module by generators and relations — a presentation that is the starting point for many computations.

Let $M$ be any$R$-module and let$S$ be a generating set for$M$ (in the extreme case, we may take$S = M$ itself). Form the free module$R^{(S)} = \bigoplus_{s \in S} R$, with one copy of$R$ for each element of$S$, and define the map$\pi: R^{(S)} \to M$ by$\pi(e_s) = s$ on basis elements and extending$R$-linearly. By construction$\pi$ is surjective, since$S$ generates$M$. The kernel$K = \ker \pi$ is a submodule of$R^{(S)}$ called the *module of relations* or the *first syzygy* of$M$ with respect to$S$. By the First Isomorphism Theorem,$M \cong R^{(S)}/K$.

This construction is the starting point for *free resolutions*: one can iterate by replacing $K$ with a quotient of a free module, obtaining a chain of free modules that "resolves"$M$ step by step. The length and structure of the minimal free resolution are deep invariants of the module, measuring how far$M$ is from being free. The immediate application, however, is conceptual: to prove a statement about all$R$-modules, it suffices to prove it for free modules and then check that the property is preserved by taking quotients. This strategy — prove on free modules, descend to quotients — is one of the workhorses of homological algebra, and its foundation is the universal surjection$R^{(S)} \twoheadrightarrow M$.

A *presentation* of $M$ is a choice of surjection$R^n \twoheadrightarrow M$ together with a choice of surjection$R^m \twoheadrightarrow K = \ker(R^n \twoheadrightarrow M)$. This yields a sequence$R^m \to R^n \to M \to 0$, which is exact at$R^n$ and at$M$. Such a presentation can be encoded by an$n \times m$ matrix$A$ (the presentation matrix) whose columns are the images of the basis vectors of$R^m$ in$R^n$. Different choices of generating sets give different matrices, but over a PID any two presentation matrices for the same module have the same Smith normal form (up to invertible row and column operations), making the Smith normal form a canonical presentation.

\begin{theorem}
For any ring $R$ and any$R$-module$M$, there exists a surjective$R$-linear map$\pi: F \twoheadrightarrow M$ from a free module$F$ to$M$.
\end{theorem}

**Example 1.** Present $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ as a$\mathbb{Z}$-module. Take$S = \{1\}$ (a single generator), so$F = \mathbb{Z}$ and$\pi: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ sends$n \mapsto n \bmod 6$. The kernel is$K = 6\mathbb{Z}$, and indeed$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$. The presentation is$\mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 6} \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \to 0$.

**Example 2.** Present $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ with two generators$e_1 = (1, 0)$ and$e_2 = (0, 1)$. The map$\pi: \mathbb{Z}^2 \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ sends$(a, b) \mapsto (a \bmod 2, b \bmod 3)$. The kernel is$K = 2\mathbb{Z} \oplus 3\mathbb{Z}$, generated by$(2, 0)$ and$(0, 3)$. The presentation matrix is$\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$, already in Smith normal form.

**Example 3.** Present $M = k[x]/(x^2 + 1)$ as a$k[x]$-module over a field$k$. Use$S = \{1\}$: the map$\pi: k[x] \to k[x]/(x^2 + 1)$ is the quotient map with kernel$K = (x^2 + 1)$. The presentation is$k[x] \xrightarrow{\times(x^2+1)} k[x] \to M \to 0$.

**Example 4.** The ring $R$ itself is free of rank 1, presented with no relations: the identity$R \xrightarrow{=} R$ has kernel 0. Any quotient$R/I$ has a one-generator presentation with kernel$I$.

**Example 5.** The minimal free resolution of $k$ over$k[x]/(x^n)$ (for a field$k$ and$n \geq 2$) is infinite:$\cdots \to k[x]/(x^n) \xrightarrow{\times x} k[x]/(x^n) \xrightarrow{\times x^{n-1}} k[x]/(x^n) \to k \to 0$. This shows that the projective dimension of$k$ over$k[x]/(x^n)$ is infinite for$n \geq 2$, a fact that distinguishes the ring$k[x]/(x^n)$ from a PID.
