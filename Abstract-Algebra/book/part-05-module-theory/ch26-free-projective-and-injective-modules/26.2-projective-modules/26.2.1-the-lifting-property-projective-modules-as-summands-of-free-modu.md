# 26.2.1 The Lifting Property; Projective Modules as Summands of Free Modules

Free modules are wonderfully flexible, but many naturally occurring modules — for example, ideals in a ring — are not free yet still behave "freely enough" in a key respect. Projective modules capture exactly this intermediate notion.

An $R$-module$P$ is *projective* if it has the following *lifting property*: for any surjective$R$-linear map$g: M \twoheadrightarrow N$ and any$R$-linear map$f: P \to N$, there exists an$R$-linear map$\tilde{f}: P \to M$ with$g \circ \tilde{f} = f$. Informally, any map from$P$ into a quotient can be "lifted" to a map into the module before quotienting. For free modules, this lifting is easy to construct by choosing preimages of the basis elements; the remarkable feature of projective modules is that the same lifting property holds even without a basis.

The connection to free modules is tight: $P$ is projective if and only if it is a *direct summand* of a free module — that is, there exists a module$Q$ and a free module$F$ with$F = P \oplus Q$. To see why the lifting property implies this, apply it to the surjection$\pi: F \twoheadrightarrow P$ of Section 26.1.4: the identity$\mathrm{id}_P: P \to P$ lifts to a map$\sigma: P \to F$ with$\pi \circ \sigma = \mathrm{id}_P$, making$\sigma$ a *splitting* of$\pi$ and$P$ a direct summand of$F$. Conversely, any direct summand of a free module inherits the lifting property because free modules have it. Projective modules are thus precisely those modules that "sit inside free modules as direct summands" — a characterization that is often the most useful one in practice.

There is a third equivalent characterization: $P$ is projective if and only if every short exact sequence$0 \to M' \to M \to P \to 0$ splits. This says the surjection$M \to P$ always has a right inverse — a section$s: P \to M$ with the composition$P \xrightarrow{s} M \to P$ being the identity. The splitting gives$M \cong M' \oplus P$. The equivalence of the lifting property and the splitting of short exact sequences follows by setting$N = P$,$f = \mathrm{id}_P$ in the lifting definition.

\begin{definition}
An $R$-module$P$ is \textit{projective} if for every surjection$g: M \twoheadrightarrow N$ and every map$f: P \to N$, there exists a lift$\tilde{f}: P \to M$ with$g \circ \tilde{f} = f$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}
The following are equivalent for an $R$-module$P$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $P$ is projective.
\item $P$ is a direct summand of a free module.
\item Every short exact sequence $0 \to M' \to M \to P \to 0$ splits.
\item The functor $\mathrm{Hom}_R(P, -)$ is exact.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

**Example 1.** Every free module is projective. For $F = R^n$: given$g: M \twoheadrightarrow N$ and$f: R^n \to N$, define$\tilde{f}$ by choosing for each basis element$e_i$ a preimage$m_i \in M$ with$g(m_i) = f(e_i)$, then extending linearly. The fact that we can choose these preimages freely (since$g$ is surjective) is what makes this work.

**Example 2.** Let $R = \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$. Then$P = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is a projective$R$-module. Indeed,$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ as$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$-modules (via the Chinese Remainder Theorem), so$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is a direct summand of the free module$R = \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, hence projective. Note:$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is not free as a$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$-module (it has 2 elements while$R$ has 6), so projective$\neq$ free here.

**Example 3.** The ideal $I = (1 + \sqrt{-5}, 2)$ in$R = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is a projective$R$-module of rank 1 that is not free. One can verify:$I \oplus I \cong R^2$ (as$R$-modules), so$I$ is a summand of a rank-2 free module. But$I$ is not principal (hence not free of rank 1), since$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is not a PID.

**Example 4.** Let $k$ be a field and$R = k \times k$. The module$P = k \times 0$ is projective:$R = (k \times 0) \oplus (0 \times k)$, so$P$ is a direct summand of the free module$R$. The lifting property: given any surjection$g: M \to N$ and$f: P \to N$, one lifts$f$ by choosing preimages. The idempotent$e = (1, 0) \in R$ satisfies$P = Re$.

**Example 5.** The rank-1 projective modules over a Dedekind domain $R$ (which is a generalization of a PID allowing non-principal ideals) are precisely the invertible fractional ideals$I \subseteq K = \mathrm{Frac}(R)$. Two such modules$I$ and$J$ are isomorphic as$R$-modules if and only if they represent the same class in the ideal class group$\mathrm{Cl}(R)$. Thus$K_0(R) \cong \mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathrm{Cl}(R)$, measuring the failure of all projectives to be free.
