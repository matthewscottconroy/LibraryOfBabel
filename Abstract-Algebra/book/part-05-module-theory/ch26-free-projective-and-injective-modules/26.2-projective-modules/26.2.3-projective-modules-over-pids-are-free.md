# 26.2.3 Projective Modules over PIDs Are Free

Over a principal ideal domain, the gap between projective and free modules collapses entirely: every projective $R$-module is free. This is a nontrivial theorem that depends essentially on the arithmetic structure of PIDs, and it is one of the reasons PIDs provide such a clean setting for module theory.

To appreciate why the result is nontrivial, consider the ring $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, which fails to be a PID (it is not a unique factorization domain). The ideal$I = (2, 1 + \sqrt{-5})$ is projective as a$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$-module but is not free: it has rank 1 as a module but is not isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ itself, since$I^2 = (2)$ is principal but$I$ is not. The failure of unique factorization in$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is precisely what allows nonfree projectives to exist, and the presence of nonfree projective modules is measured by the ideal class group of the ring.

For PIDs, the proof that projective modules are free proceeds by noting that a projective module $P$ is a submodule of a free module (it is a summand of one, hence a fortiori a submodule), and that submodules of free modules over a PID are free — a theorem that follows from the structure of PIDs by transfinite induction or by using the fact that PIDs are Noetherian and hereditary. The conclusion is that over a PID, free, projective, and "submodule of a free module" are all equivalent conditions. This result is essential for the structure theorem of Chapter 27: it ensures that when we write a finitely generated module as$R^n/K$, the submodule$K$ of the free module$R^n$ is itself free, which is the starting point for the Smith normal form argument.

The key lemma underlying the theorem is:

\begin{lemma}
Let $R$ be a PID. Every submodule of a free$R$-module is free.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
By induction on rank and Zorn's lemma for the infinite case. For a free module $F = R^n$, consider the projection$\pi_1: R^n \to R$ onto the first coordinate. For any submodule$N \leq R^n$, the image$\pi_1(N)$ is an ideal in$R$, hence principal:$\pi_1(N) = (d_1)$ for some$d_1 \in R$. Choose$n_1 \in N$ with$\pi_1(n_1) = d_1$. Then$N = Rn_1 \oplus (N \cap \ker \pi_1)$, and$N \cap \ker \pi_1$ is a submodule of$R^{n-1}$, to which induction applies.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}
Let $R$ be a PID. Every projective$R$-module is free.
\end{theorem}

**Example 1.** Over $R = \mathbb{Z}$: every projective$\mathbb{Z}$-module is free. In particular, every torsion-free finitely generated abelian group is free — a fact we used in Chapter 19 (FTFGAB). The projective$\mathbb{Z}$-modules are exactly$\mathbb{Z}^n$ for$n \geq 0$.

**Example 2.** Over $R = k[x]$ for a field$k$: every projective$k[x]$-module is free. This is the Quillen–Suslin theorem in the case of a polynomial ring in one variable (the one-variable case is easy; the full Quillen–Suslin theorem handles$k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$). A rank-1 projective over$k[x]$ is a rank-1 submodule of$k[x]^n$, hence a nonzero ideal, hence principal (since$k[x]$ is a PID), hence free of rank 1.

**Example 3.** Over a Dedekind domain $R$ that is not a PID (e.g.,$R = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$), nonfree rank-1 projectives exist and are classified by the ideal class group$\mathrm{Cl}(R)$. The ideal$I = (2, 1 + \sqrt{-5})$ has order 2 in$\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, confirming it is nonfree. The structure theorem fails for modules over such rings precisely because submodules of free modules need not be free.

**Example 4.** The Serre conjecture (proved by Quillen and Suslin in 1976): every finitely generated projective module over $k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ is free. This generalizes the PID case. The geometric intuition:$k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ is the coordinate ring of affine space$\mathbb{A}^n$, which is contractible, so all vector bundles are trivial — hence all projectives are free.

**Example 5.** Over $\mathbb{Z}$ (a PID), the module$\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is not projective (it has torsion). Every projective$\mathbb{Z}$-module is torsion-free and free. The non-projective module$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ fits into a non-split short exact sequence$0 \to 2\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to 0$; if$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ were projective, this would split — but any section$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ would require an element of order 2 in$\mathbb{Z}$, which doesn't exist.
