# 26.3.1 The Extension Property; Injective Modules

Projective modules are defined by a lifting property for surjections: maps from $P$ into a quotient can be lifted to maps into the total module. Injective modules are the categorical dual: they satisfy an *extension property* for injections. The duality between the two definitions is precise and will recur throughout homological algebra.

An $R$-module$Q$ is *injective* if for any injective$R$-linear map$i: M \hookrightarrow N$ and any$R$-linear map$f: M \to Q$, there exists an$R$-linear map$\tilde{f}: N \to Q$ with$\tilde{f} \circ i = f$. In other words, any map from a submodule$M$ of$N$ into$Q$ can be extended to all of$N$. One can picture this as filling in the dotted arrow in the diagram: given$M \hookrightarrow N$ and$f: M \to Q$, the map$f$ extends over the larger module$N$.

As with projective modules, injectivity has a splitting characterization: $Q$ is injective if and only if every short exact sequence$0 \to Q \to M \to M'' \to 0$ splits. This means any injection of$Q$ into a larger module always has a left inverse — a retraction$r: M \to Q$ with$r \circ i = \mathrm{id}_Q$. The precise duality with projectivity is then clear: projective modules split out of sequences that surject onto them, while injective modules split off from sequences that inject out of them. These two conditions are exchanged by applying the functor$\mathrm{Hom}_R(-, R)$ (or more generally by any duality functor), making injective modules the "duals" of projective modules in a precise categorical sense.

The extension property is dual to the lifting property not just metaphorically but in precise categorical terms: if one reverses all arrows in the diagram for projectives ($P \to N \twoheadrightarrow M$, lift to$P \to N$), one gets exactly the diagram for injectives ($M \hookrightarrow N$, extend$M \to Q$ to$N \to Q$). This arrow-reversal duality is the hallmark of projective-injective duality throughout homological algebra, and it explains why projective and injective resolutions play symmetric roles in computing$\mathrm{Ext}$ (Chapter 40).

\begin{definition}
An $R$-module$Q$ is \textit{injective} if for every injection$i: M \hookrightarrow N$ and every map$f: M \to Q$, there exists a map$\tilde{f}: N \to Q$ with$\tilde{f} \circ i = f$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}
The following are equivalent for an $R$-module$Q$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $Q$ is injective.
\item Every short exact sequence $0 \to Q \to M \to M'' \to 0$ splits.
\item The functor $\mathrm{Hom}_R(-, Q)$ is exact (i.e., right exact as well as left exact).
\item Baer's criterion (Section 26.3.2): every $R$-linear map$I \to Q$ from a left ideal$I \subseteq R$ extends to a map$R \to Q$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

**Example 1.** The $\mathbb{Z}$-module$\mathbb{Q}$ is injective. Given a subgroup$H \hookrightarrow A$ (a submodule of an abelian group) and a homomorphism$f: H \to \mathbb{Q}$, one can extend$f$ to$A$ using the divisibility of$\mathbb{Q}$: for any$a \in A$, if$na \in H$ for some$n$, define$\tilde{f}(a) = f(na)/n$.

**Example 2.** The $\mathbb{Z}$-module$\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$ is injective (it is divisible). Given$f: H \to \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$ and$H \hookrightarrow A$, one extends using the fact that in$\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$ every element has$n$th roots (divisibility).

**Example 3.** $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ over$\mathbb{Z}$ is NOT injective. The inclusion$p\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Z}$ and the map$p\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ sending$p \mapsto 1$ (i.e.,$pk \mapsto k \bmod p$) cannot be extended to$\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$: such an extension would send$1 \mapsto$ some element$m$, and then$p \mapsto pm = 0 \neq 1$. The obstruction is that$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ has$p$-torsion, so$p$ times anything is 0, but the map on$p\mathbb{Z}$ requires$p \mapsto 1 \neq 0$.

**Example 4.** The Prüfer $p$-group$\mathbb{Z}(p^\infty) = \bigcup_{n \geq 1} \langle 1/p^n + \mathbb{Z} \rangle \subseteq \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$ is injective over$\mathbb{Z}$. It is divisible: given$x \in \mathbb{Z}(p^\infty)$ and$n \geq 1$, we can find$y$ with$ny = x$ because$\mathbb{Z}(p^\infty)$ is closed under division by integers.

**Example 5.** Over a field $k$, every module (= vector space) is both projective and injective. Any module map from a subspace$V \hookrightarrow W$ to$k$ extends to$W$: this is the dual basis lemma for vector spaces, and it characterizes fields as rings over which every module is injective (the *self-injective* or *Frobenius* property).
