# 26.3.2 Baer's Criterion for Injectivity

The definition of injectivity requires extension over arbitrary inclusions $M \hookrightarrow N$, which at first glance involves an overwhelming variety of data. Baer's criterion provides a remarkable simplification: it reduces injectivity to the much more manageable task of extending maps defined on ideals.

*Baer's criterion* states that an $R$-module$Q$ is injective if and only if for every left ideal$I \subseteq R$ and every$R$-linear map$f: I \to Q$, there exists an element$q \in Q$ such that$f(r) = rq$ for all$r \in I$. The condition "$f(r) = rq$" says that$f$ extends to the map$r \mapsto rq$ on all of$R$, which is simply the$R$-linear map$R \to Q$ given by left multiplication by$q$.

To see why this is sufficient, suppose $Q$ satisfies Baer's criterion and we wish to extend a map$f: M \to Q$ over an inclusion$M \hookrightarrow N$. By Zorn's lemma, there is a maximal partial extension of$f$ to some submodule$M' \subseteq N$ containing$M$. If$M' \neq N$, pick$n \in N \setminus M'$ and consider the ideal$I = \{r \in R : rn \in M'\}$. The map$r \mapsto f'(rn)$ from$I$ to$Q$ is$R$-linear, so by Baer's criterion it extends to some$q \in Q$. Defining the extension of$f'$ to$M' + Rn$ by$f'(m' + rn) = f'(m') + rq$ contradicts maximality of$M'$. Hence$M' = N$, completing the proof.

Baer's criterion is the main tool for proving that specific modules are injective. It is especially powerful for PIDs and Noetherian rings, where every ideal is principal (or at least finitely generated), making the condition even easier to check: over a PID $R$, a module$Q$ is injective if and only if for every nonzero$r \in R$ and every$q \in Q$, there exists$q' \in Q$ with$rq' = q$ — which is the divisibility condition of Section 26.3.4 in the case$R = \mathbb{Z}$.

\begin{theorem}[Baer's Criterion]
An $R$-module$Q$ is injective if and only if for every left ideal$I \subseteq R$ and every$R$-linear map$f: I \to Q$, there exists$q \in Q$ such that$f(r) = rq$ for all$r \in I$.
\end{theorem}

**Example 1.** $\mathbb{Q}$ is an injective$\mathbb{Z}$-module. Ideals of$\mathbb{Z}$ have the form$n\mathbb{Z}$. Given$f: n\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Q}$, we have$f(nk) = kf(n)$ for all$k$. Setting$q = f(n)/n \in \mathbb{Q}$, we get$f(m) = mq$ for all$m \in n\mathbb{Z}$ (check:$f(nk) = kf(n) = k(nq) = (nk)q$). Baer's criterion is satisfied.

**Example 2.** $\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$ is an injective$\mathbb{Z}$-module. For ideal$n\mathbb{Z}$ and$f: n\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$:$f$ is determined by$f(n) = [\alpha] \in \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$. Set$q = [\alpha/n] \in \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$. Then$nq = n[\alpha/n] = [\alpha] = f(n)$, so$f(nk) = k[\alpha] = (nk)[\alpha/n] = (nk)q$. Criterion satisfied.

**Example 3.** $\mathbb{Z}$ is NOT an injective$\mathbb{Z}$-module. Consider the ideal$2\mathbb{Z}$ and the map$f: 2\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ sending$2k \mapsto k$. For Baer's criterion, we need$q \in \mathbb{Z}$ with$f(2) = 2q$, i.e.,$1 = 2q$. But$2q = 1$ has no solution in$\mathbb{Z}$. Hence the criterion fails.

**Example 4.** Over a field $k$, every$k$-module (= vector space) is injective. Ideals of$k$ are$\{0\}$ and$k$ itself. For the zero ideal, the only map is the zero map, which extends trivially. For the ideal$I = k$: any$k$-linear map$f: k \to Q$ sends$r \mapsto r \cdot f(1)$, i.e.,$f(r) = r \cdot f(1)$. Setting$q = f(1)$ gives$f(r) = rq$. Baer's criterion is satisfied, confirming that all$k$-modules are injective.

**Example 5.** Over a PID $R$, applying Baer's criterion shows$Q$ is injective iff it is divisible: for every principal ideal$(r) = rR$ and every map$f: rR \to Q$ (determined by$f(r) = q_0$), we need$q \in Q$ with$rq = q_0$. This is exactly the statement that every element of$Q$ is divisible by every nonzero$r \in R$. Thus over PIDs, Baer's criterion simplifies to the divisibility condition, recovering the characterization of injective$\mathbb{Z}$-modules as divisible abelian groups (Section 26.3.4).
