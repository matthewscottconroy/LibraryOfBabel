# 26.3.4 Injective Modules over $\mathbb{Z}$: Divisible Abelian Groups

The abstract definition of injectivity becomes wonderfully concrete for $\mathbb{Z}$-modules, which are simply abelian groups. The injective$\mathbb{Z}$-modules are precisely the *divisible* abelian groups: those in which every element can be "divided" by any nonzero integer.

Formally, an abelian group $D$ is *divisible* if for every$d \in D$ and every nonzero integer$n$, there exists$d' \in D$ with$nd' = d$. The group$\mathbb{Q}$ is divisible — any rational number can be divided by any integer. The quotient$\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$ is divisible: given$[q] \in \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$ and$n \geq 1$, the element$[q/n]$ satisfies$n \cdot [q/n] = [q]$. The *Prüfer$p$-group*$\mathbb{Z}(p^\infty) = \mathbb{Z}[1/p]/\mathbb{Z}$, consisting of all fractions with$p$-power denominators modulo$\mathbb{Z}$, is also divisible.

The equivalence between injectivity and divisibility for abelian groups follows directly from Baer's criterion: a map from a nonzero ideal $n\mathbb{Z}$ to$D$ is determined by the image of$n$, say$f(n) = d$. Extending this to a map$\mathbb{Z} \to D$ requires finding$d' \in D$ with$nd' = d$, which is precisely divisibility. Thus$D$ is injective as a$\mathbb{Z}$-module if and only if it is divisible.

The injective hull of $\mathbb{Z}/p^k\mathbb{Z}$ is the Prüfer group$\mathbb{Z}(p^\infty)$, and the injective hull of$\mathbb{Z}$ is$\mathbb{Q}$. Every abelian group embeds into a divisible group, and divisible groups play the role for abelian groups that algebraically closed fields play for integral domains: they are the "complete" objects into which everything else maps. This concrete characterization of injectives over$\mathbb{Z}$ both motivates and illuminates the abstract definition for general rings.

The structure of injective $\mathbb{Z}$-modules is completely known. By the theory of divisible groups, every divisible abelian group decomposes uniquely as
$$D \cong \mathbb{Q}^{(\kappa)} \oplus \bigoplus_{p \text{ prime}} \mathbb{Z}(p^\infty)^{(\kappa_p)}$$
for some cardinal $\kappa$ (the "rational part") and cardinals$\kappa_p$ (the "$p$-primary parts"). This classification of all injective$\mathbb{Z}$-modules into copies of$\mathbb{Q}$ and Prüfer groups is the abelian group analogue of the structure theorem for modules over a PID, viewed from the injective side.

\begin{theorem}
A $\mathbb{Z}$-module (abelian group)$D$ is injective if and only if it is divisible: for every$d \in D$ and every nonzero$n \in \mathbb{Z}$, there exists$d' \in D$ with$nd' = d$.
\end{theorem}

**Example 1.** $\mathbb{Q}$ is divisible: given$q \in \mathbb{Q}$ and$n \neq 0$, set$d' = q/n \in \mathbb{Q}$. Injective as a$\mathbb{Z}$-module.

**Example 2.** $\mathbb{Z}(p^\infty) = \{[a/p^n] : a \in \mathbb{Z}, n \geq 0\} \subseteq \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$ is divisible. Given$[a/p^k] \in \mathbb{Z}(p^\infty)$ and$m \geq 1$: if$\gcd(m, p) = 1$, find$m^{-1} \bmod p^k$ (exists by CRT) and set$d' = [a \cdot m^{-1}/p^k]$; if$m = p^j$, set$d' = [a/p^{k+j}]$. In both cases,$md' = [a/p^k]$.

**Example 3.** $\mathbb{Z}$ is not divisible:$1 \in \mathbb{Z}$ has no "half" ($2d' = 1$ has no integer solution). Similarly,$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ for$n \geq 2$ is not divisible: the element$1$ cannot be divided by$n$ (as$n \cdot 0 = 0 \neq 1$).

**Example 4.** The direct sum $\mathbb{Q} \oplus \mathbb{Z}(p^\infty)$ is divisible (direct sums of divisible groups are divisible), hence injective. It is the injective hull of$\mathbb{Q} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ over$\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 5.** For a module $M$ over a PID$R$ (not just$\mathbb{Z}$), the analogous condition is:$Q$ is injective iff for every nonzero$r \in R$ and every$q \in Q$, there exists$q' \in Q$ with$rq' = q$. Such modules over$R$ are called *divisible$R$-modules*. For$R = k[x]$, divisible means: for every nonzero polynomial$f(x)$ and element$q$, we can "divide$q$ by$f$." The injective hull of$k[x]/(x-\lambda)$ is$k[x]/(x-\lambda)$-localized fraction module, i.e., the formal Laurent series at$\lambda$.
