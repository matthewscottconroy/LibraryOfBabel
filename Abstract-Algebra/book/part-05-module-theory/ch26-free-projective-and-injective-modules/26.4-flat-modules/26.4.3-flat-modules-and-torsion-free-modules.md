# 26.4.3 Flat Modules and Torsion-Free Modules

Over domains and PIDs, flatness admits a very concrete description in terms of the absence of torsion — a condition far easier to verify than the abstract tensor product criterion. An element $m$ of an$R$-module$M$ is *torsion* if there exists a nonzero$r \in R$ with$rm = 0$; the module$M$ is *torsion-free* if it has no nonzero torsion elements.

Over a PID, the equivalence is complete: a module $M$ is flat if and only if it is torsion-free. The forward direction — flat implies torsion-free — holds over any domain: if$rm = 0$ for nonzero$r$ and$m$, then tensoring the injection$R \xrightarrow{\times r} R$ (multiplication by$r$) with$M$ gives the map$M \xrightarrow{\times r} M$, which sends$m \mapsto rm = 0$, so$m$ is in the kernel of a map that should be injective, contradicting flatness. The reverse — torsion-free implies flat over a PID — follows because over a PID, any torsion-free module is a filtered union of finitely generated torsion-free submodules, each of which is free (being a finitely generated submodule of a free module), hence projective, hence flat.

Over a general ring, torsion-free does not imply flat. For example, over the ring $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$, the module$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ has no "$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$-torsion" in the relevant sense, yet it is not flat: tensoring the injection$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 2} \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ fails. The equivalence of flatness and torsion-freeness over PIDs and Dedekind domains makes flatness computable in those settings, which is why it appears so naturally in the arithmetic of number fields and algebraic curves.

The structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a PID (Chapter 27) separates any such module into a free (torsion-free) part and a torsion part: $M \cong R^r \oplus T$ where$T$ is the torsion submodule. This decomposition is compatible with the flat/non-flat dichotomy:$R^r$ is flat (free), while$T$ is never flat unless$T = 0$. The flat part is precisely the torsion-free part, confirming the equivalence.

\begin{theorem}
Let $R$ be a PID. A finitely generated$R$-module$M$ is flat if and only if it is torsion-free, if and only if it is free.
\end{theorem}

**Example 1.** $\mathbb{Z}$ is torsion-free as a$\mathbb{Z}$-module, hence flat, hence free (rank 1). All three conditions coincide.

**Example 2.** $\mathbb{Z}^n$ is torsion-free for any$n \geq 0$, hence flat over$\mathbb{Z}$. As a finitely generated torsion-free module over a PID, it is free.

**Example 3.** $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ (for$n \geq 2$) is not torsion-free ($n \cdot 1 = 0$), hence not flat over$\mathbb{Z}$. Verified directly: tensoring$\mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times n} \mathbb{Z}$ with$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ gives the zero map, a non-injective map from the injected copy.

**Example 4.** Over $R = k[x]$ (a PID for any field$k$):$k[x]/(f)$ is torsion (multiplied by$f$ gives 0), hence not flat.$k[x]$ itself is free of rank 1, hence flat. The localization$k[x, x^{-1}]$ (inverting$x$) is torsion-free over$k[x]$, hence flat.

**Example 5.** Over $R = \mathbb{Z}$: the torsion-free part of$\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ is$\mathbb{Z}$ (the$\mathbb{Z}$-summand), and the torsion part is$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$. The module is not flat (due to the torsion summand). The flat modules over$\mathbb{Z}$ are precisely the torsion-free abelian groups, which include$\mathbb{Z}^n$,$\mathbb{Q}$,$\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$ (localization at$p$), and subgroups of$\mathbb{Q}$ such as$\mathbb{Z}[1/p]$.
