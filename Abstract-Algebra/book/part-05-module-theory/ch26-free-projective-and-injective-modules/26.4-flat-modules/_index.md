# 26.4 Flat Modules

Flat modules are defined by an exactness condition on the tensor product functor rather than by a lifting or extension property, and they occupy a conceptually distinct position from the projective and injective classes. A right $R$-module $M$ is flat if the functor $M \otimes_R -$ (applied to left $R$-modules) is exact: whenever $0 \to A \to B \to C \to 0$ is a short exact sequence of left $R$-modules, the tensored sequence $0 \to M \otimes_R A \to M \otimes_R B \to M \otimes_R C \to 0$ remains exact. Since tensoring always preserves right exactness (the last two terms of exactness are automatic), flatness is the condition that tensoring also preserves injections. Projective modules are flat, but the converse fails: $\mathbb{Q}$ is a flat $\mathbb{Z}$-module (since localizations of integral domains are flat) that is not projective (since projective $\mathbb{Z}$-modules are free, and $\mathbb{Q}$ is not finitely generated, let alone free over $\mathbb{Z}$).

The flatness condition is most naturally understood through the language of torsion. Over an integral domain $R$, a finitely generated module is flat if and only if it is torsion-free — there are no elements $m \neq 0$ annihilated by any nonzero $r \in R$. This equivalence explains why $\mathbb{Q}$ is flat ($\mathbb{Q}$ has no $\mathbb{Z}$-torsion) and why $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is not flat for $n \geq 2$ (every element is annihilated by $n$). For general rings, flatness does not reduce to torsion-freeness, and one must work directly with the exactness condition or use the Lazard criterion: a module is flat if and only if it is a filtered colimit (direct limit) of free modules. This characterization, due to Lazard, shows that flat modules are "approximately free" in a precise categorical sense.

In commutative algebra and algebraic geometry, flatness is the correct notion of a "continuously varying" family of modules. A flat family of modules over a base ring $R$ is one where the fibers $M \otimes_R R/\mathfrak{p}$ (at prime ideals $\mathfrak{p}$) vary without abrupt jumps in rank or structure. The flatness condition appears prominently in the theory of base change: for a ring map $R \to S$, the functor $M \mapsto M \otimes_R S$ (extension of scalars) sends flat $R$-modules to flat $S$-modules, and short exact sequences of flat modules remain exact after any base change. The connection to tensor products will be developed in Chapter 28, where right exactness of $\otimes$ is proved and the failure of left exactness is measured by the Tor functor — whose vanishing characterizes flatness.
