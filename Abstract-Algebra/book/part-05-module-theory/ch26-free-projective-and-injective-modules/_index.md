# Chapter 26 — Free, Projective, and Injective Modules

Among all modules over a ring $R$, the free modules occupy the simplest and most concrete position. A free module $R^n$ has a basis — a set of generators over which every element has a unique expression as a finite $R$-linear combination — and this property makes free modules entirely amenable to matrix-theoretic methods. Every module is a quotient of a free module, which means free modules provide a universal way to present any module by generators and relations. Over a field, every module is free (this is just the statement that every vector space has a basis), but over a general ring the situation is richer and the distinction between free and non-free modules becomes a source of deep geometry and algebra.

Projective modules arise naturally once one asks about the uniqueness of presentations. A module $P$ is projective if, whenever one has a surjection $M \twoheadrightarrow N$ and a map $P \to N$, the map lifts to a map $P \to M$. Equivalently, $P$ is projective if and only if it is a direct summand of a free module: there exists some module $Q$ such that $P \oplus Q \cong R^n$. Every free module is projective, but the converse fails in general. The prototypical example is the ring $R = \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$, where each summand is a direct summand of $R$ itself and hence projective, yet neither is free. The question of when projective implies free is subtle and deep: Serre conjectured in 1955 that projective modules over polynomial rings $k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ over a field are free, and this was proved by Quillen and Suslin independently in 1976 — a landmark in algebraic $K$-theory.

Injective modules are, in a precise sense, the dual notion. A module $Q$ is injective if every injective map $M \hookrightarrow N$ and every map $M \to Q$ extends to a map $N \to Q$. Injective modules are harder to construct concretely, but Baer's criterion (1940) gives a manageable characterization: $Q$ is injective if and only if every homomorphism from a left ideal $I \subseteq R$ into $Q$ extends to a map from all of $R$. Over $\mathbb{Z}$, the injective modules are precisely the divisible abelian groups — those $M$ in which for every $m \in M$ and every nonzero $n \in \mathbb{Z}$, there exists $m' \in M$ with $n m' = m$. The archetypal example is $\mathbb{Q}$, and more generally $\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$ and its subgroups $\mathbb{Z}[p^{-1}]/\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}(p^\infty)$. Every module embeds into an injective module, and the smallest such embedding — the injective hull — is unique up to isomorphism.

The fourth class treated in this chapter is that of flat modules. A module $M$ is flat if the functor $- \otimes_R M$ is exact, meaning it preserves short exact sequences. Free modules are flat, projective modules are flat, and flat modules over an integral domain are torsion-free. The converse implications are all false in general, and each class is strictly larger than the one before it. These four classes — free, projective, injective, flat — are the fundamental module types in homological algebra. A projective resolution of a module $M$ is a long exact sequence of projective modules mapping onto $M$, and the derived functors of $\text{Hom}$ and $\otimes$ — namely Ext and Tor — measure, respectively, how far $M$ is from being projective and how far it is from being flat. This chapter thus lays the essential foundation for the homological machinery developed in Part VI.
