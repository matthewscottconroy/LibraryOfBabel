# 27.1.1 The Setting: $R$ a PID,$M$ Finitely Generated

The structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain is one of the crown jewels of algebra. It simultaneously generalizes two fundamental classification theorems — the classification of finitely generated abelian groups and the Jordan normal form of a linear operator — by subsuming both as special cases of a single, elegant result. To appreciate why PIDs are the right setting, one needs to see how the structural properties of PIDs constrain the module-theoretic possibilities.

Let $R$ be a PID and let$M$ be a finitely generated$R$-module. Since every module is a quotient of a free module (Section 26.1.4), we can write$M \cong R^n/K$ for some integer$n$ and some submodule$K \leq R^n$. The crucial input from the PID hypothesis comes here: over a PID, every submodule of a free module is itself free (Section 26.2.3). Thus$K$ is a free module of rank$m \leq n$, and the problem reduces to understanding how a free module of rank$m$ sits inside a free module of rank$n$ via the inclusion map.

The key point is that this inclusion can be described by an $n \times m$ matrix over$R$. The structure theorem asks: by choosing bases for$K$ and$R^n$ judiciously, can we simplify this matrix? The answer — supplied by the theory of Smith normal form — is yes, and the resulting simplified presentation directly reveals the isomorphism type of$M$. The theorem holds for all finitely generated modules over any PID, a class that includes$\mathbb{Z}$, polynomial rings$F[x]$ over a field, and the ring of integers in any Euclidean domain.

Why do we need $R$ to be a PID, and not just a commutative ring? Three properties of PIDs are used in the proof:
(1) Every submodule of a free module is free (the "hereditary" property, Section 26.2.3).
(2) Any two elements have a greatest common divisor, and the gcd can be written as a linear combination of the two elements (Bézout's theorem).
(3) Every ideal is principal, so a submodule $K \leq R$ (an ideal) is generated by a single element.

Together, these allow the Smith normal form algorithm (Section 27.1.2) to reduce a presentation matrix to diagonal form by performing Euclidean-algorithm-style row and column operations. Over a non-PID — say $R = \mathbb{Z}[x]$ — the algorithm breaks down because not every ideal is principal, and the structure theorem fails: there exist finitely generated$\mathbb{Z}[x]$-modules that are not direct sums of cyclic modules.

\begin{theorem}[Structure Theorem for f.g. Modules over a PID — Existence]
Let $R$ be a PID and$M$ a finitely generated$R$-module. Then
$$M \cong R^r \oplus R/(d_1) \oplus R/(d_2) \oplus \cdots \oplus R/(d_k)$$
for some $r \geq 0$ and nonzero non-units$d_1, \ldots, d_k \in R$ with$d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_k$.
\end{theorem}

**Example 1.** The abelian group $M = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ is a finitely generated$\mathbb{Z}$-module. Since$2 \mid 6$, this is already in invariant factor form with$r = 0$,$d_1 = 2$,$d_2 = 6$.

**Example 2.** The abelian group $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ is isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ (by CRT, since$\gcd(2,3) = 1$), so the invariant factor form is$r = 0$,$d_1 = 6$. Two different-looking direct sums can be isomorphic when the invariant factor forms coincide.

**Example 3.** The module $M = \mathbb{Z}^2 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ has$r = 2$, and torsion part$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. But$2 \nmid 4$ in the correct divisibility direction: we need$d_1 \mid d_2$, so the invariant factor form requires$d_1 = 2$,$d_2 = 4$ (since$2 \mid 4$). Thus the correct invariant factor form is$r = 2$,$d_1 = 2$,$d_2 = 4$.

**Example 4.** Over $R = F[x]$: the$F[x]$-module$F[x]/(x^2 - 1) \oplus F[x]/(x-1)$ needs to be put in invariant factor form. We have$d_1 = x-1$ and$d_2 = x^2 - 1 = (x-1)(x+1)$. Check:$(x-1) \mid (x^2-1)$? Yes. So this is already in invariant factor form.

**Example 5.** The module $\mathbb{Z}^3$ over$\mathbb{Z}$ is free of rank 3, with$r = 3$ and no torsion summands ($k = 0$). This is the trivial case of the structure theorem: a finitely generated free module.
