# 27.1.2 Smith Normal Form of a Matrix over a PID

The computational heart of the structure theorem is the reduction of a matrix to Smith normal form. This process is the analogue of Gaussian elimination over a field, extended to the setting of a PID where we can perform row and column operations but cannot divide freely.

Suppose $K$ is a free$R$-module of rank$m$ and$K \hookrightarrow R^n$ is the inclusion. Choosing bases for both$K$ and$R^n$, we represent the inclusion by an$n \times m$ matrix$A$ with entries in$R$. Row operations on$A$ — adding a multiple of one row to another, swapping rows, multiplying a row by a unit — correspond to changing the basis of$R^n$; they are encoded by left multiplication by an invertible matrix in$\mathrm{GL}_n(R)$. Column operations correspond to changing the basis of$K$; they are encoded by right multiplication by a matrix in$\mathrm{GL}_m(R)$.

By performing a sequence of such operations — much as in the Euclidean algorithm, using the fact that every principal ideal domain admits gcd computations — one reduces $A$ to *Smith normal form*: a diagonal matrix$\mathrm{diag}(d_1, d_2, \ldots, d_r, 0, \ldots, 0)$ where$d_1, d_2, \ldots, d_r \in R$ are nonzero elements satisfying$d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$. The key step in the reduction is using the Euclidean algorithm (or gcd computations in$R$) to clear off-diagonal entries one at a time, starting from the entry of smallest "size." The divisibility chain$d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$ ensures the diagonal entries are ordered in a canonical way.

The algorithm proceeds as follows. Starting with matrix $A$:
(1) Find the entry $a_{ij}$ of smallest "size" (e.g., smallest absolute value for$\mathbb{Z}$, smallest degree for$F[x]$). Bring it to position$(1,1)$ by row/column swaps.
(2) Use the Euclidean algorithm to reduce all other entries in row 1 and column 1 modulo $a_{11}$: for each$a_{1j}$, write$a_{1j} = q \cdot a_{11} + r$ and subtract$q$ times column 1 from column$j$, replacing$a_{1j}$ by$r$. Repeat until row 1 and column 1 are zero except at$(1,1)$.
(3) If $a_{11}$ does not divide some off-diagonal entry$a_{ij}$, use row operations to move$a_{ij}$ into row 1 and repeat step 2. Eventually$a_{11}$ divides all entries.
(4) Recurse on the $(n-1) \times (m-1)$ submatrix.

\begin{theorem}[Smith Normal Form]
Let $R$ be a PID and$A$ an$n \times m$ matrix over$R$. There exist invertible matrices$P \in \mathrm{GL}_n(R)$ and$Q \in \mathrm{GL}_m(R)$ such that$PAQ = \mathrm{diag}(d_1, \ldots, d_r, 0, \ldots, 0)$ with$d_i \neq 0$ and$d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$.
\end{theorem}

**Example 1.** Reduce $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 6 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ over$\mathbb{Z}$ to Smith normal form.
- The smallest entry is $2$; it is already at position$(1,1)$.
- Column operation: $C_2 \leftarrow C_2 - 2C_1$ gives$\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 6 & -9 \end{pmatrix}$.
- Row operation: $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 3R_1$ gives$\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & -9 \end{pmatrix}$.
- Multiply row 2 by $-1$:$\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 9 \end{pmatrix}$.
- Check: $2 \mid 9$? No! So we need more work. Add row 2 to row 1:$\begin{pmatrix} 11 & 0 \\ 0 & 9 \end{pmatrix}$. Hmm, let us restart.

Starting over: $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 6 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$. Subtract$2 \times C_1$ from$C_2$:$\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 6 & -9 \end{pmatrix}$.$R_2 - 3R_1$:$\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & -9 \end{pmatrix}$. Now$2 \nmid 9$, so:$R_1 + R_2$:$\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -9 \\ 0 & -9 \end{pmatrix}$. The off-diagonal$-9$:$\gcd(2, 9) = 1$. Use Euclidean algorithm:$C_2 + 4C_1$: entry becomes$-9 + 8 = -1$. Swap columns:$\begin{pmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ -9 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Multiply$C_1$ by$-1$:$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 9 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.$C_2 - 2C_1$:$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 9 & -18 \end{pmatrix}$.$R_2 - 9R_1$:$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -18 \end{pmatrix}$. Smith normal form:$\mathrm{diag}(1, 18)$.

**Example 2.** Over $\mathbb{Z}$:$A = \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 4 \\ 10 \end{pmatrix}$ (a$3 \times 1$ matrix). The gcd of the entries is$\gcd(6, 4, 10) = 2$. By row operations (Euclidean algorithm on rows), reduce to$\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Smith normal form is$\mathrm{diag}(2, 0, 0)$ (with the understanding that the$0$'s mean no further nonzero diagonal entries).

**Example 3.** Over $\mathbb{Z}$: the presentation matrix$\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ for$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ has$d_1 = 2$,$d_2 = 3$. But$2 \nmid 3$, so this is NOT yet in Smith normal form! Row/column operations:$R_1 + R_2$, then$R_2 - R_1$, etc. In fact,$\mathrm{diag}(2, 3)$ reduces to$\mathrm{diag}(1, 6)$ by the algorithm (since$\gcd(2,3) = 1$). This recovers$\mathbb{Z}/\gcd = \mathbb{Z}/1 = 0$ and$\mathbb{Z}/6$, consistent with$\mathbb{Z}/2 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/6$.

**Example 4.** Over $F[x]$: reduce$A = \begin{pmatrix} x & x^2 \\ 1 & x \end{pmatrix}$.$R_1 - xR_2$:$\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & x \end{pmatrix}$. Swap rows:$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & x \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.$C_2 - xC_1$:$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Smith normal form:$\mathrm{diag}(1, 0)$.

**Example 5.** The Smith normal form of the zero matrix is the zero matrix; the Smith normal form of the identity matrix $I_n$ is$I_n$ itself. For a diagonal matrix$\mathrm{diag}(d_1, \ldots, d_n)$ already satisfying$d_1 \mid \cdots \mid d_n$, no operations are needed.
