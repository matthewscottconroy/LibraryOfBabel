# 27.1.4 The Structure Theorem: Primary Decomposition Form

The invariant factor form of the structure theorem — expressing $M$ as a direct sum$R^r \oplus R/(d_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus R/(d_k)$ — is the most natural output of the Smith normal form algorithm. But there is a second form, the *primary decomposition form*, which is better suited for classification problems and which connects directly to the prime factorization in$R$.

The key is the Chinese Remainder Theorem for modules. If $d = p_1^{a_1} p_2^{a_2} \cdots p_s^{a_s}$ is the prime factorization of$d_i$ in$R$ (with distinct primes$p_j$), then$R/(d_i) \cong R/(p_1^{a_1}) \oplus R/(p_2^{a_2}) \oplus \cdots \oplus R/(p_s^{a_s})$. Applying this to each torsion summand in the invariant factor form and collecting terms by prime, we arrive at the primary decomposition form: every finitely generated$R$-module$M$ is isomorphic to a direct sum
$$M \cong R^r \oplus \bigoplus_p \bigoplus_{i} R/(p^{a_{p,i}}),$$
where $p$ ranges over primes of$R$ and$a_{p,i} \geq 1$. The summands$R/(p^a)$ are called *primary cyclic modules* or *elementary divisors*.

The two forms carry the same information — one can convert between them — but each has its advantages. The invariant factor form is more natural when $M$ arises as a presentation (e.g., from a matrix), while the primary decomposition form is easier to use when classifying modules of a given "order." For example, enumerating all finitely generated$R$-modules satisfying some condition on their primary components is transparent in the primary decomposition form, as is the connection to the elementary divisor theory familiar from linear algebra.

To convert from invariant factors to elementary divisors: factor each $d_i$ as a product of prime powers and list all resulting prime-power factors. To convert from elementary divisors back to invariant factors: for each prime$p$, sort the$p$-power factors in non-decreasing order of exponent; the$j$-th invariant factor$d_j$ is the product of the$j$-th largest$p$-power factor over all primes$p$.

\begin{theorem}[Structure Theorem — Primary Decomposition Form]
Let $R$ be a PID and$M$ a finitely generated$R$-module. Then
$$M \cong R^r \oplus \bigoplus_{p \text{ prime}} \left( R/(p^{e_1}) \oplus \cdots \oplus R/(p^{e_{n_p}}) \right)$$
where for each prime $p$ the exponents$e_1 \leq e_2 \leq \cdots \leq e_{n_p}$ are positive integers (the \textit{$p$-elementary divisors} of$M$). The free rank$r$ and the elementary divisors are uniquely determined by$M$.
\end{theorem}

**Example 1.** Convert $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ to primary decomposition over$\mathbb{Z}$:$12 = 2^2 \cdot 3$, so$\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$. Elementary divisors:$4 = 2^2$ and$3 = 3^1$.

**Example 2.** Convert $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ (invariant factor form with$d_1 = 2$,$d_2 = 6$) to primary decomposition:$d_1 = 2 = 2^1$,$d_2 = 6 = 2 \cdot 3$. Primary decomposition:$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ (grouping by prime: two copies of$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ and one copy of$\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$).

**Example 3.** Convert back: suppose elementary divisors are $2, 4, 3, 9$ (i.e.,$2^1, 2^2, 3^1, 3^2$). Sort by prime: at$p=2$, exponents are$1, 2$; at$p=3$, exponents are$1, 2$. The invariant factors:$d_1 = 2^1 \cdot 3^1 = 6$ and$d_2 = 2^2 \cdot 3^2 = 36$. Check:$6 \mid 36$? Yes. So the invariant factor form is$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/36\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 4.** Over $F[x]$: the primary decomposition uses irreducible polynomials in place of primes. For the$F[x]$-module$M = F[x]/(x^2(x+1)^3)$, the primary decomposition is$F[x]/(x^2) \oplus F[x]/((x+1)^3)$.

**Example 5.** The module $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$ is already in primary decomposition form at the prime$p = 2$ (elementary divisors$2, 4, 8$). To find the invariant factor form: three invariant factors, with$d_1 = 2$,$d_2 = 4$,$d_3 = 8$. Check:$2 \mid 4 \mid 8$? Yes. So the invariant factor form is the same:$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$.
