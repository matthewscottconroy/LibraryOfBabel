# 27.1.5 Uniqueness of the Invariant Factors

The structure theorem asserts that every finitely generated $R$-module over a PID is isomorphic to a module of the form$R^r \oplus R/(d_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus R/(d_k)$ with$d_1 \mid \cdots \mid d_k$. But existence alone does not make this a classification: we also need to know that the invariant factors$r$ and$d_1, \ldots, d_k$ are uniquely determined by the isomorphism type of$M$. Without uniqueness, two different-looking lists of invariant factors might describe the same module.

The uniqueness is proved by exhibiting the invariant factors as intrinsic invariants of $M$, independent of any presentation or Smith normal form computation. The key observation is that the product$d_1 d_2 \cdots d_i$ is, up to units in$R$, the GCD of all$i \times i$ minors of any presentation matrix for$M$. These GCDs are determined by$M$ alone (not by the choice of presentation), since any two presentations are related by invertible row and column operations that do not change the GCDs of minors. Specifically, elementary row/column operations change the minors by multiplication by units, so the GCDs are preserved up to associates. From the products, one recovers the individual invariant factors:$d_i = (d_1 \cdots d_i)/(d_1 \cdots d_{i-1})$ (the ratio of consecutive determinantal divisors).

The uniqueness theorem completes the structure theorem as a genuine *classification*: two finitely generated $R$-modules are isomorphic if and only if they have the same free rank and the same invariant factors (up to associates). This is a complete and computable invariant — to determine whether two modules are isomorphic, compute their presentation matrices, reduce to Smith normal form, and compare the diagonal entries. The invariant factors are not merely a bookkeeping device but the definitive invariants of the module, encoding all isomorphism-type information in a single ordered list.

The *determinantal divisors* (or *fitting ideals*) of $M$ are the principal ideals$D_i = (d_1 \cdots d_i) \subseteq R$ generated by the gcd of all$i \times i$ minors of a presentation matrix. They satisfy$D_1 \supseteq D_2 \supseteq \cdots$ (decreasing sequence of ideals). The$i$-th Fitting ideal$F_i(M)$ is defined as the ideal generated by all$(n - i) \times (n - i)$ minors of a presentation matrix for an$n$-generator module. These are intrinsic module-theoretic invariants (not just of the presentation), and they generalize the invariant factors to the non-PID setting.

\begin{theorem}[Uniqueness of Invariant Factors]
Let $R$ be a PID. If$R^r \oplus R/(d_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus R/(d_k) \cong R^{r'} \oplus R/(d'_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus R/(d'_{k'})$ (with$d_1 \mid \cdots \mid d_k$ and$d'_1 \mid \cdots \mid d'_{k'}$), then$r = r'$,$k = k'$, and$d_i$ is an associate of$d'_i$ for all$i$.
\end{theorem}

**Example 1.** Two modules $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ and$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$: these are clearly isomorphic (direct sum is commutative). Their invariant factors are the same:$d_1 = 2$,$d_2 = 6$.

**Example 2.** Are $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ and$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ isomorphic? Invariant factors of first:$2 \mid 12$, so$d_1 = 2$,$d_2 = 12$. Second:$4 \nmid 6$ and$6 \nmid 4$; primary decompositions:$\mathbb{Z}/4 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3$ vs.$\mathbb{Z}/2 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/4 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3$? Wait:$\mathbb{Z}/4 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/6 \cong \mathbb{Z}/4 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3$; invariant factors:$d_1 = 2$,$d_2 = 12$ (same as first). So they ARE isomorphic.

**Example 3.** Are $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ and$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ isomorphic? Both have order 4. First has invariant factors$d_1 = 2$,$d_2 = 2$; second has invariant factor$d_1 = 4$. Since$2 \neq 4$, they are NOT isomorphic. (Also: the first has exponent 2, the second has exponent 4.)

**Example 4.** Invariant factors of the presentation $\mathbb{Z}^3 \xrightarrow{A} \mathbb{Z}^3$ where$A = \mathrm{diag}(2, 6, 30)$: determinantal divisors are$D_1 = \gcd(2, 6, 30) = 2$;$D_2 = \gcd(\text{all } 2\times 2 \text{ minors}) = \gcd(12, 60, 180) = 12$;$D_3 = |\det A| = 360$. Invariant factors:$d_1 = D_1 = 2$,$d_2 = D_2/D_1 = 6$,$d_3 = D_3/D_2 = 30$. Check:$2 \mid 6 \mid 30$? Yes.

**Example 5.** Uniqueness for the primary decomposition form: the elementary divisors of a module are also uniquely determined. For $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$, the elementary divisors are$2, 4, 3$. Any other module with the same elementary divisors must be isomorphic.
