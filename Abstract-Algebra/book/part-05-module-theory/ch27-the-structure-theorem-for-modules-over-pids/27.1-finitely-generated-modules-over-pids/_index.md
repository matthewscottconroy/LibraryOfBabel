# 27.1 Finitely Generated Modules over PIDs

The main theorem of this section is one of the gems of algebra: every finitely generated module $M$ over a principal ideal domain $R$ is isomorphic to a direct sum $R^r \oplus R/(d_1) \oplus R/(d_2) \oplus \cdots \oplus R/(d_k)$, where $r \geq 0$ and $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_k$ are nonzero nonunit elements of $R$, uniquely determined up to associates. The integer $r$ is the rank of the free part, and the elements $d_1, \ldots, d_k$ are the invariant factors. Alternatively, each $R/(d_i)$ can be further decomposed via the Chinese Remainder Theorem into primary cyclic pieces $R/(p^a)$ for prime elements $p \in R$; these are the elementary divisors. Both decompositions are isomorphism invariants and together determine $M$ completely.

The proof proceeds via Smith normal form, named after H.J.S. Smith who developed the integer matrix version in 1861. Begin with a finite presentation: choose generators $m_1, \ldots, m_n$ for $M$ to get a surjective map $R^n \to M$, then observe that the kernel is a submodule of $R^n$ and hence (by the submodule theorem for free modules over a PID) is itself free of some rank $m \leq n$. The kernel is generated by rows of an $m \times n$ matrix $A$ over $R$, and changing generators in the source and target corresponds to multiplying $A$ on the left and right by invertible matrices over $R$. By performing a finite sequence of such elementary row and column operations — adding multiples of rows/columns to one another, scaling by units, and swapping rows/columns — one can reduce $A$ to diagonal form $\text{diag}(d_1, \ldots, d_r, 0, \ldots, 0)$ with $d_i \mid d_{i+1}$. The module $M$ is then the cokernel of this diagonal matrix, which is $R/(d_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus R/(d_r) \oplus R^{n-r}$.

Uniqueness of the invariant factors is the harder half of the theorem. One approach uses determinantal divisors: define $D_k(A)$ to be the ideal generated by all $k \times k$ minors of $A$; over a PID this is a principal ideal $(d_k)$, and the generators $d_k$ are isomorphism invariants of $M$ independent of the presentation. The invariant factors are then recovered as $\delta_i = d_i / d_{i-1}$ (the ratios of successive determinantal divisors), which are well-defined up to units. This uniqueness proof also shows that the structure theorem gives a complete set of invariants: two finitely generated modules over a PID are isomorphic if and only if they have the same rank and the same invariant factors.
