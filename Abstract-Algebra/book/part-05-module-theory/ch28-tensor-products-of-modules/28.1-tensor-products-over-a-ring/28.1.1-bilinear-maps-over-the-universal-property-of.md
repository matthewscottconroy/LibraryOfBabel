# 28.1.1 Bilinear Maps over $R$; the Universal Property of$M \otimes_R N$

Many constructions in algebra require a way to "multiply" elements from two different modules together and land in a third module, respecting the ring action on both sides. The tensor product is the universal solution to this problem.

Let $M$ be a right$R$-module and$N$ a left$R$-module. A map$f: M \times N \to A$ (where$A$ is an abelian group) is *$R$-balanced* if it is biadditive — meaning$f(m + m', n) = f(m, n) + f(m', n)$ and$f(m, n + n') = f(m, n) + f(m, n')$ — and satisfies the balance condition$f(mr, n) = f(m, rn)$ for all$r \in R$. The balance condition ensures that the ring action on$M$ from the right and on$N$ from the left are compatible: moving a scalar from one argument to the other does not change the output. When$R$ is commutative and both modules are left$R$-modules, this reduces to bilinearity in the usual sense.

The *tensor product* $M \otimes_R N$ is defined by the following universal property: it is an abelian group together with an$R$-balanced map$\otimes: M \times N \to M \otimes_R N$, written$(m, n) \mapsto m \otimes n$, such that for every$R$-balanced map$f: M \times N \to A$ there exists a unique group homomorphism$\bar{f}: M \otimes_R N \to A$ with$\bar{f}(m \otimes n) = f(m, n)$. The elements$m \otimes n$ are called *pure tensors*; they generate$M \otimes_R N$ but not every element of$M \otimes_R N$ is a pure tensor in general. Two different-looking expressions may represent the same element, since the group$M \otimes_R N$ is defined by generators and relations — pure tensors as generators, and the bilinearity and balance conditions as relations.

The universal property characterizes $M \otimes_R N$ uniquely up to isomorphism: if$(T, \phi)$ and$(T', \phi')$ both satisfy the universal property for$R$-balanced maps from$M \times N$, then there is a unique isomorphism$T \to T'$ intertwining$\phi$ and$\phi'$. The existence of the tensor product (with this universal property) follows from an explicit construction as a quotient of a free abelian group (Section 28.1.2).

Why define things this way? The point is that bilinear algebra — multilinear maps, products of modules, representation-theoretic constructions — all naturally involve maps that are linear in each variable separately but not in their joint input. The tensor product linearizes such maps: an $R$-balanced map$M \times N \to A$ is the same as a group homomorphism$M \otimes_R N \to A$. This "linearization" principle is fundamental in algebra, topology (cup products), and physics (quantum mechanics, where tensor products of Hilbert spaces represent composite systems).

\begin{definition}
The \textit{tensor product} $M \otimes_R N$ of a right$R$-module$M$ and a left$R$-module$N$ is an abelian group equipped with an$R$-balanced map$M \times N \to M \otimes_R N$,$(m, n) \mapsto m \otimes n$, with the universal property: every$R$-balanced$f: M \times N \to A$ factors uniquely as$M \times N \to M \otimes_R N \xrightarrow{\bar{f}} A$.
\end{definition}

**Example 1.** The tensor product $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z} \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. An$\mathbb{Z}$-balanced map$\mathbb{Z}/m \times \mathbb{Z}/n \to A$ is a bilinear map satisfying$f(a, b) = 0$ whenever$ma = 0$ or$nb = 0$ (automatic from bilinearity). By the formula derived in Section 28.2.2,$\mathbb{Z}/m \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{Z}/n \cong \mathbb{Z}/\gcd(m,n)$.

**Example 2.** Complexification: $V \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{C}$ for a real vector space$V$. The$\mathbb{R}$-balanced map$V \times \mathbb{C} \to V \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{C}$ sends$(v, z) \mapsto v \otimes z$. The result$V_{\mathbb{C}} = V \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{C}$ is the complexification of$V$: if$V = \mathbb{R}^n$, then$V_{\mathbb{C}} = \mathbb{C}^n$.

**Example 3.** Extension of scalars: $M \otimes_R S$ for a ring map$R \to S$. This converts an$R$-module$M$ into an$S$-module. For$M = \mathbb{Z}$,$R = \mathbb{Z}$,$S = \mathbb{Q}$:$\mathbb{Z} \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{Q} \cong \mathbb{Q}$ (the module of rank-1 free$\mathbb{Z}$-module tensored with$\mathbb{Q}$ is$\mathbb{Q}$ itself).

**Example 4.** The tensor product over a non-commutative ring. Let $R = M_2(k)$ and$M = k^2$ (row vectors, right$R$-module),$N = k^2$ (column vectors, left$R$-module). An$R$-balanced map$k^2 \times k^2 \to A$ must satisfy$f(v A, w) = f(v, Aw)$ for all$2 \times 2$ matrices$A$. One can show$M \otimes_R N \cong k$ (a 1-dimensional space), with the pure tensor$e_1 \otimes e_1 + e_2 \otimes e_2$ spanning it (the "trace" element).

**Example 5.** The tensor product of two free modules: $R^m \otimes_R R^n \cong R^{mn}$ (for commutative$R$). The pure tensors$e_i \otimes e_j$ (for$1 \leq i \leq m$,$1 \leq j \leq n$) form a basis. This is the module-theoretic version of the fact that an$m \times n$ matrix has$mn$ entries.
