# Chapter 28 — Tensor Products of Modules

The tensor product is the universal solution to the problem of turning bilinear maps into linear ones. Given a right $R$-module $M$ and a left $R$-module $N$, a bilinear map $b : M \times N \to P$ into an abelian group $P$ is one that is additive in each variable and satisfies the balance condition $b(mr, n) = b(m, rn)$. The tensor product $M \otimes_R N$ is an abelian group together with a universal bilinear map $M \times N \to M \otimes_R N$, written $(m, n) \mapsto m \otimes n$, through which every bilinear map uniquely factors. This universal property completely determines $M \otimes_R N$ up to isomorphism, and it is the correct definition — the explicit construction as a quotient of a free abelian group on the set $M \times N$ is merely a proof of existence. Tensor products formalize the intuition behind "scalar extension": passing from a $\mathbb{Q}$-vector space to a $\mathbb{R}$-vector space, or from a module over a subring to a module over a larger ring, is accomplished by tensoring.

Concrete computations with tensor products reveal immediately that they can behave in surprising ways. The tensor product $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z} \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/\gcd(m,n)\mathbb{Z}$; in particular, when $m$ and $n$ are coprime the tensor product is zero even though both factors are nonzero. This happens because the torsion in each factor interacts through the balance condition: $m \cdot (a \otimes b) = (ma) \otimes b = 0 \otimes b = 0$ and $n \cdot (a \otimes b) = a \otimes (nb) = 0$, so every element is annihilated by both $m$ and $n$, hence by $\gcd(m, n)$. More generally, tensoring with $R/I$ computes $M/IM$, which is the module $M$ with the submodule $IM$ collapsed to zero. Scalar extension from $R$ to a larger ring $S$ via $S \otimes_R M$ is the algebraic analogue of restricting and extending scalars in representation theory.

The tensor product functor is right exact: given a short exact sequence $0 \to A \to B \to C \to 0$, tensoring with any module $M$ yields an exact sequence $A \otimes M \to B \otimes M \to C \otimes M \to 0$, but the map on the left need not be injective. The failure of left exactness is measured by the derived functor $\text{Tor}_1^R(C, M)$, which vanishes precisely when $M$ is flat. This failure is not an anomaly to be corrected but rather a source of information: $\text{Tor}$ detects torsion in modules and captures obstructions to lifting maps across extensions. The calculation $\text{Tor}_1^{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}/m, \mathbb{Z}/n) \cong \mathbb{Z}/\gcd(m,n)$ quantifies how far the tensor product fails to be exact in this case.

The Hom–Tensor adjunction is the structural theorem that organizes the relationship between tensor and Hom. It states that there is a natural isomorphism $\text{Hom}_R(M \otimes_R N, P) \cong \text{Hom}_R(M, \text{Hom}_R(N, P))$, expressing the fact that the functors $- \otimes_R N$ and $\text{Hom}_R(N, -)$ are adjoint. This adjunction has immediate consequences: left adjoints preserve colimits and hence tensor product is right exact; right adjoints preserve limits and hence $\text{Hom}$ is left exact. The adjunction also explains the passage between Tor and Ext via derived functors, and it is a prototype for adjunctions throughout algebra and topology. In the language of Chapter 29, the Hom–Tensor adjunction is the algebraic shadow of the deeper adjunction between derived tensor product $\otimes^{\mathbf{L}}$ and derived Hom $\mathbf{R}\text{Hom}$ in the derived category, where the two functors govern all of derived algebra.
