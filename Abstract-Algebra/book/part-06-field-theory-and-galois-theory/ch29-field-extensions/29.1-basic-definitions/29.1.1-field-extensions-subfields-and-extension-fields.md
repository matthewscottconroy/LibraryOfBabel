# 29.1.1 Field Extensions $E/F$; Subfields and Extension Fields

The most basic question of field theory is deceptively simple: given a field $F$, what fields contain it? Every field we know lives inside a larger one. The rationals$\mathbb{Q}$ live inside the reals$\mathbb{R}$, which live inside the complex numbers$\mathbb{C}$. A finite field$\mathbb{F}_p$ lives inside$\mathbb{F}_{p^2}$, which lives inside$\mathbb{F}_{p^4}$, and so on. In each case, the larger field "extends" the smaller one by making room for new elements, new roots, new solutions. The study of this containment — its structure, its symmetries, its quantitative size — is the subject of field theory and ultimately of Galois theory.

A *field extension* $E/F$ is a pair where$F$ is a subfield of$E$: both$E$ and$F$ are fields,$F \subseteq E$ as sets, and the inclusion map$\iota: F \hookrightarrow E$ is a ring homomorphism (automatically injective, since fields have no nonzero ideals). We write$E/F$, pronounced "$E$ over$F$," and call$E$ the *extension field* and$F$ the *base field* or *ground field*. The notation is deliberately suggestive of a quotient, but$E/F$ is not a quotient in any sense; it is a containment. The slash simply names the pair, and the convention of writing the larger field on top will be consistent with later fraction-like notation for the degree$[E:F]$.

The key insight that unlocks the entire theory is that a field extension $E/F$ automatically makes$E$ into a vector space over$F$. The$F$-vector space structure on$E$ is the one you expect: the addition on$E$ serves as vector addition, and for a scalar$c \in F$ and a vector$v \in E$, the scalar multiplication$c \cdot v$ is simply the field multiplication in$E$. The field axioms of$E$ together with the fact that$F \subseteq E$ as a subfield imply all of the vector space axioms without further work. This is not a coincidence or a trick — it reflects the deep fact that a field is, at its core, an abelian group under addition equipped with a compatible multiplicative structure, and the subfield$F$ acts on$E$ in exactly the way a field of scalars acts on a vector space.

Once we have the vector space structure, all of the tools of linear algebra become available: bases, dimension, linear independence, linear maps, and dimension-counting arguments. The dimension of $E$ over$F$ as a vector space — called the *degree* of the extension and written$[E:F]$ — is the fundamental numerical invariant. It measures, in a precise linear-algebraic sense, how much larger$E$ is than$F$. The entire architecture of Galois theory rests on this: the Galois group has order equal to the degree, the Galois correspondence matches subgroups with sub-extensions in a dimension-exact way, and the impossibility results for classical geometric constructions reduce to degree calculations.

To see why this matters concretely, consider several examples at once. The extension $\mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ is infinite-dimensional: the reals contain$\mathbb{Q}$-linearly independent subsets of every finite size, so$[\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{Q}] = \infty$. The extension$\mathbb{R} \subseteq \mathbb{C}$ is two-dimensional: every complex number has the form$a + bi$ with$a, b \in \mathbb{R}$, so$\{1, i\}$ is an$\mathbb{R}$-basis for$\mathbb{C}$ and$[\mathbb{C}:\mathbb{R}] = 2$. The extension$\mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$, where$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}) = \{a + b\sqrt{2} : a, b \in \mathbb{Q}\}$, is also two-dimensional with basis$\{1, \sqrt{2}\}$. In each case, the vector space dimension captures something geometrically and algebraically meaningful about how the extension was formed.

The notion of a subfield deserves to be made precise. A subset $F \subseteq E$ is a *subfield* of$E$ if it is closed under addition, subtraction, multiplication, and taking nonzero inverses, and if it contains$1_E$. Equivalently,$F$ is a subfield if the inclusion$F \hookrightarrow E$ is a field homomorphism. By convention, a field homomorphism must send$1$ to$1$, so any subfield shares the identity element with the ambient field. Every field$E$ contains a smallest subfield, its *prime subfield*, which is isomorphic to$\mathbb{Q}$ if$\mathrm{char}(E) = 0$ or to$\mathbb{F}_p$ if$\mathrm{char}(E) = p > 0$. Any field extension$E/F$ therefore sits on top of this prime subfield, and the prime subfield provides the "floor" beneath all field theory.

**Example 1.** The simplest example of a field extension is $\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R}$. Here$\mathbb{R}$ is the base field and$\mathbb{C} = \{a + bi : a, b \in \mathbb{R}\}$ is the extension. The$\mathbb{R}$-vector space structure on$\mathbb{C}$ has$\{1, i\}$ as a basis (since every element is uniquely$a \cdot 1 + b \cdot i$), so$[\mathbb{C}:\mathbb{R}] = 2$. Arithmetic in$\mathbb{C}$ reduces to arithmetic in$\mathbb{R}$ together with the relation$i^2 = -1$; there are no other constraints.

**Example 2.** Consider $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3}) = \{a + b\sqrt{3} : a, b \in \mathbb{Q}\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}$. This is a subfield of$\mathbb{R}$ containing$\mathbb{Q}$: the sum and product of two elements$a + b\sqrt{3}$ and$c + d\sqrt{3}$ are$(a+c) + (b+d)\sqrt{3}$ and$(ac + 3bd) + (ad + bc)\sqrt{3}$, both in the required form; the inverse of$a + b\sqrt{3} \neq 0$ is$\frac{a - b\sqrt{3}}{a^2 - 3b^2}$, which has rational coefficients since$a^2 - 3b^2 \neq 0$ for$a, b \in \mathbb{Q}$ not both zero (as$\sqrt{3}$ is irrational). The extension$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3})/\mathbb{Q}$ has degree$2$ with basis$\{1, \sqrt{3}\}$.

**Example 3.** The extension $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3})/\mathbb{Q}$ is obtained by adjoining both$\sqrt{2}$ and$\sqrt{3}$. We have$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}) = \{a + b\sqrt{2} + c\sqrt{3} + d\sqrt{6} : a, b, c, d \in \mathbb{Q}\}$, and one can verify that$\{1, \sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}, \sqrt{6}\}$ is a$\mathbb{Q}$-basis, giving degree$4$. This is an example of a field obtained by successive adjunctions:$\mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3})$, each step of degree$2$.

**Example 4.** Over $\mathbb{F}_p$, the extension$\mathbb{F}_{p^n}/\mathbb{F}_p$ is a field extension of degree$n$. The field$\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ can be constructed as$\mathbb{F}_p[x]/(f)$ where$f$ is any irreducible polynomial of degree$n$ over$\mathbb{F}_p$; the elements of$\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ are the residue classes of polynomials of degree less than$n$, so$\{1, \bar{x}, \bar{x}^2, \ldots, \bar{x}^{n-1}\}$ is a$\mathbb{F}_p$-basis and$[\mathbb{F}_{p^n}:\mathbb{F}_p] = n$.

**Example 5.** The field $\mathbb{Q}(\pi)$ — the smallest subfield of$\mathbb{R}$ containing$\mathbb{Q}$ and$\pi$ — is an example of an *infinite* extension. Since$\pi$ is transcendental over$\mathbb{Q}$ (no nonzero polynomial with rational coefficients vanishes at$\pi$), the elements$1, \pi, \pi^2, \pi^3, \ldots$ are all$\mathbb{Q}$-linearly independent in$\mathbb{Q}(\pi)$, so$[\mathbb{Q}(\pi):\mathbb{Q}] = \infty$. The field$\mathbb{Q}(\pi)$ is isomorphic to the rational function field$\mathbb{Q}(t)$, the field of all rational expressions$p(t)/q(t)$ with$p, q \in \mathbb{Q}[t]$ and$q \neq 0$.

The vector space perspective on field extensions is not merely an organizational device. It is the reason Galois theory works at all. When the Galois group $\mathrm{Gal}(E/F)$ acts on$E$, it acts by$F$-linear automorphisms of the vector space$E/F$ — so group theory and linear algebra interlock at every step. The degree$[E:F]$ will turn out to equal the order of the Galois group, a quantitative miracle that follows directly from the vector space structure. The entire subject is founded on this one observation: a field extension is a vector space.
