# 29.1.2 The Degree $[E:F]$; Finite and Infinite Extensions

Once we know that $E$ is a vector space over$F$, the most natural numerical invariant to attach to$E/F$ is the dimension of that vector space. The *degree* (or *index*) of the extension is defined to be$[E:F] = \dim_F E$. If this dimension is finite, we call$E/F$ a *finite extension*; if the dimension is infinite, the extension is *infinite*. The degree is the fundamental quantitative invariant of a field extension, playing a role analogous to the order of a group or the rank of a module over a ring.

The range of behavior is wide, and even simple examples reveal the full spectrum. At the finite end, $[\mathbb{C}:\mathbb{R}] = 2$ because$\{1, i\}$ is an$\mathbb{R}$-basis for$\mathbb{C}$: every complex number$a + bi$ is a unique$\mathbb{R}$-linear combination of$1$ and$i$. Similarly,$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}):\mathbb{Q}] = 2$ with basis$\{1, \sqrt{2}\}$, and$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}):\mathbb{Q}] = 3$ with basis$\{1, \sqrt[3]{2}, \sqrt[3]{4}\}$. At the infinite end,$[\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{Q}] = \infty$: the reals have uncountably many elements that are$\mathbb{Q}$-linearly independent (any set of algebraically independent real numbers works), and the dimension exceeds any finite bound. For extensions of infinite degree, the exact cardinality of the dimension is often less important than the simple fact that the extension is infinite.

The degree is far more than a bookkeeping device. It governs divisibility constraints, controls which intermediate fields can exist, and will later determine the order of Galois groups. Even the coarsest reading of the degree — whether it is prime, composite, a power of two, or infinite — places strong constraints on the structure of the extension. A finite extension of prime degree has no proper intermediate fields (by the Tower Law, any intermediate field would have degree dividing a prime, forcing it to equal either $F$ or$E$). A Galois extension of degree$n$ has exactly one subgroup of every order dividing$n$... but that is getting ahead of ourselves.

Let us be more precise about what it means to have a basis for an extension. An $F$-basis of$E$ is a set$\mathcal{B} \subseteq E$ that is$F$-linearly independent (no nontrivial$F$-linear combination is zero) and$F$-spanning (every element of$E$ is an$F$-linear combination of elements of$\mathcal{B}$). The degree$[E:F]$ is the cardinality of any such basis. For finite extensions, this cardinality is a positive integer; for infinite extensions, it is an infinite cardinal. A fundamental theorem of linear algebra guarantees that any two bases have the same cardinality, so the degree is well-defined.

The degree interacts multiplicatively with towers of extensions — a fact we will prove in detail as the Tower Law — and this multiplicativity is the key to most degree calculations. To compute $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}):\mathbb{Q}]$, for instance, one inserts the intermediate field$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ and computes the two degrees$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}):\mathbb{Q}] = 2$ and$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}):\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})] = 2$ separately, then multiplies to get$4$. Without the multiplicative structure, even this simple calculation would require finding a$\mathbb{Q}$-basis of$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3})$ directly.

**Example 1.** The extension $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ has degree$2$. The elements of$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ are$\{a + b\sqrt{2} : a, b \in \mathbb{Q}\}$. The set$\{1, \sqrt{2}\}$ is$\mathbb{Q}$-linearly independent (if$a + b\sqrt{2} = 0$ with$a, b \in \mathbb{Q}$, then since$\sqrt{2}$ is irrational we must have$b = 0$ and hence$a = 0$) and spans$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ by definition. So$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}):\mathbb{Q}] = 2$.

**Example 2.** The extension $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ has degree$3$. The minimal polynomial of$\sqrt[3]{2}$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ is$x^3 - 2$ (irreducible by Eisenstein at$p=2$), so the simple extension theorem gives$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}):\mathbb{Q}] = 3$ with basis$\{1, \sqrt[3]{2}, \sqrt[3]{4}\}$. Note that$\sqrt[3]{4} = (\sqrt[3]{2})^2$ is a distinct basis element; it cannot be written as a$\mathbb{Q}$-linear combination of$1$ and$\sqrt[3]{2}$.

**Example 3.** The extension $\mathbb{F}_4/\mathbb{F}_2$ has degree$2$. The field$\mathbb{F}_4 = \mathbb{F}_2[x]/(x^2 + x + 1)$ (since$x^2 + x + 1$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{F}_2$) has$\mathbb{F}_2$-basis$\{1, \omega\}$ where$\omega$ is the image of$x$, satisfying$\omega^2 + \omega + 1 = 0$. The four elements are$\{0, 1, \omega, \omega+1\}$, and$[\mathbb{F}_4:\mathbb{F}_2] = 2$.

**Example 4.** The extension $\mathbb{Q}(i, \sqrt{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ has degree$4$. Here$i = \sqrt{-1}$ and$\sqrt{2}$ are both algebraic over$\mathbb{Q}$ of degree$2$, and one can check that$\mathbb{Q}(i) \cap \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}) = \mathbb{Q}$ (since one is real and the other is not). The Tower Law gives$[\mathbb{Q}(i, \sqrt{2}):\mathbb{Q}] = [\mathbb{Q}(i, \sqrt{2}):\mathbb{Q}(i)] \cdot [\mathbb{Q}(i):\mathbb{Q}] = 2 \cdot 2 = 4$, with$\mathbb{Q}$-basis$\{1, i, \sqrt{2}, i\sqrt{2}\}$.

**Example 5.** For an infinite example: $[\mathbb{Q}(\pi):\mathbb{Q}] = \infty$ because$\pi$ is transcendental. The elements$1, \pi, \pi^2, \pi^3, \ldots$ are$\mathbb{Q}$-linearly independent (a nontrivial$\mathbb{Q}$-linear relation would give a nonzero polynomial vanishing at$\pi$, contradicting transcendence), so no finite set spans$\mathbb{Q}(\pi)$ over$\mathbb{Q}$.

The degree is also the key to the first impossibility proofs in mathematics. Whether $\sqrt[3]{2}$ is constructible by ruler and compass reduces to whether$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}):\mathbb{Q}] = 3$ can be realized as a power of$2$ — and it cannot, since$3$ divides no power of$2$. The quantitative precision of the degree transforms geometric impossibility into an elementary number-theoretic fact.
