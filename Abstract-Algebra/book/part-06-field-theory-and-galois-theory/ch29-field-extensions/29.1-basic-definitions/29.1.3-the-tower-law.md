# 29.1.3 The Tower Law: $[E:F] = [E:K][K:F]$

When you have a chain of field extensions $F \subseteq K \subseteq E$, it is natural to ask how the three degrees$[E:F]$,$[E:K]$, and$[K:F]$ relate to one another. The intermediate field$K$ sits between the two, contributing its own layer of structure, and one might worry that the relationship between the degrees is complicated. In fact, it is as clean as possible: the degrees multiply.

\begin{theorem}[Tower Law]
Let $F \subseteq K \subseteq E$ be field extensions. Then
$$[E:F] = [E:K] \cdot [K:F].$$
In particular, $[E:F]$ is finite if and only if both$[E:K]$ and$[K:F]$ are finite.
\end{theorem}

The proof makes the multiplicative structure transparent and is worth following in detail. Let $\{v_i\}_{i \in I}$ be a$K$-basis of$E$ and$\{w_j\}_{j \in J}$ be an$F$-basis of$K$. We claim the set of products$\{v_i w_j\}_{i \in I, j \in J}$ is an$F$-basis of$E$.

*Spanning:* Let $e \in E$. Since$\{v_i\}$ is a$K$-basis of$E$, we can write$e = \sum_i k_i v_i$ with$k_i \in K$. Since$\{w_j\}$ is an$F$-basis of$K$, each$k_i = \sum_j f_{ij} w_j$ with$f_{ij} \in F$. Substituting gives
$$e = \sum_i \Bigl(\sum_j f_{ij} w_j\Bigr) v_i = \sum_{i,j} f_{ij} (w_j v_i),$$
so the products span $E$ over$F$.

*Linear independence:* Suppose $\sum_{i,j} f_{ij} v_i w_j = 0$ with$f_{ij} \in F$. Regrouping,$\sum_i \bigl(\sum_j f_{ij} w_j\bigr) v_i = 0$. Since$\{v_i\}$ is$K$-linearly independent and each$\sum_j f_{ij} w_j \in K$, we conclude$\sum_j f_{ij} w_j = 0$ for each$i$. Since$\{w_j\}$ is$F$-linearly independent,$f_{ij} = 0$ for all$i, j$.

Counting basis elements gives $[E:F] = |I \times J| = |I| \cdot |J| = [E:K] \cdot [K:F]$, with appropriate conventions for infinite cardinals. The proof is complete.

The practical power of the Tower Law is that it reduces degree calculations to manageable pieces. Complex extensions can be built from simple ones, and the degree of the result is the product of the degrees of the steps. But the Tower Law does more than compute degrees; it constrains the structure of intermediate fields.

A critical corollary is: if $F \subseteq K \subseteq E$ and$[E:F]$ is finite, then$[K:F]$ divides$[E:F]$. This divisibility constraint is a powerful obstruction tool. If no integer of the right size divides$[E:F]$, no intermediate field of that degree can exist. For example, if$[E:F] = p$ is prime, then any intermediate field$K$ satisfies$[K:F] \in \{1, p\}$, forcing either$K = F$ or$K = E$. Extensions of prime degree have no proper intermediate fields — they are "indecomposable" in the field-tower sense.

**Example 1.** Compute $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}):\mathbb{Q}]$. Insert the intermediate field$K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$:
$$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}):\mathbb{Q}] = [\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}):\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})] \cdot [\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}):\mathbb{Q}].$$
The second factor is $2$. For the first: is$\sqrt{3} \in \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$? Any element of$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ has the form$a + b\sqrt{2}$, and$(a + b\sqrt{2})^2 = 3$ gives$a^2 + 2b^2 = 3$ and$2ab = 0$. If$a = 0$ then$2b^2 = 3$, impossible rationally; if$b = 0$ then$a^2 = 3$, impossible rationally. So$\sqrt{3} \notin \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$, meaning$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}):\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})] = 2$. Therefore$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}):\mathbb{Q}] = 2 \cdot 2 = 4$.

**Example 2.** Compute $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2}):\mathbb{Q}]$. The tower is$\mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2})$. The minimal polynomial of$\sqrt[4]{2}$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ is$x^4 - 2$ (irreducible by Eisenstein), giving$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2}):\mathbb{Q}] = 4$. The Tower Law checks:$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2}):\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})] \cdot [\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}):\mathbb{Q}] = 2 \cdot 2 = 4$. Indeed$(\sqrt[4]{2})^2 = \sqrt{2} \in \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$, so$\sqrt[4]{2}$ has degree$\leq 2$ over$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$, and since$\sqrt[4]{2} \notin \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ (the latter is real but does not contain$\sqrt[4]{2}$... let us verify: elements of$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ are$a + b\sqrt{2}$, and$(a + b\sqrt{2})^2 = \sqrt{2}$ has no solution in$\mathbb{Q}$), the degree is exactly$2$.

**Example 3.** The Tower Law shows that $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega):\mathbb{Q}] = 6$ where$\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$. One path:$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}):\mathbb{Q}] = 3$ and$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega):\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})] = 2$ (since$\omega$ satisfies$x^2 + x + 1 = 0$, which is irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}) \subset \mathbb{R}$). Thus the total degree is$3 \cdot 2 = 6$.

**Example 4.** For finite fields: $[\mathbb{F}_{p^{mn}}:\mathbb{F}_p] = mn$. By the Tower Law through$\mathbb{F}_{p^m}$:
$$[\mathbb{F}_{p^{mn}}:\mathbb{F}_p] = [\mathbb{F}_{p^{mn}}:\mathbb{F}_{p^m}] \cdot [\mathbb{F}_{p^m}:\mathbb{F}_p] = n \cdot m.$$
This confirms that $[\mathbb{F}_{p^{mn}}:\mathbb{F}_{p^m}] = n$, which will be used when we classify subfields of$\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$.

**Example 5.** An application of the divisibility obstruction: the extension $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ has degree$3$. Any intermediate field$K$ with$\mathbb{Q} \subsetneq K \subsetneq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ would satisfy$[K:\mathbb{Q}]$ dividing$3$ and$[K:\mathbb{Q}] > 1$, forcing$[K:\mathbb{Q}] = 3$. But then$[K:\mathbb{Q}] = [\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}):\mathbb{Q}]$ and$K \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$, which forces$K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$, a contradiction. So$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ has no proper intermediate fields — an extension of prime degree is always irreducible in this sense.

The Tower Law is one of those theorems that seems almost too simple to be useful, but it is the backbone of virtually every degree computation in field theory. It will reappear in the proof of the fundamental theorem of Galois theory (where it translates between the order of a subgroup and the degree of the corresponding fixed field), in the analysis of composite extensions, and in the classification of constructible numbers. Its utility comes precisely from its generality: it holds for any tower, with no assumptions beyond the containment $F \subseteq K \subseteq E$.
