# 29.1 Basic Definitions

A field extension is, at its most elementary, an inclusion: a field $F$ sitting inside a larger field $K$, written $K/F$ (read "$K$ over $F$"). This simple setup generates an enormous amount of structure. The larger field $K$ is automatically an $F$-vector space — you can add elements of $K$ and scale them by elements of $F$ — and the dimension of this vector space, called the degree $[K : F]$, is the first and most important invariant of the extension. When $[K:F]$ is finite, we call the extension finite; when it is infinite, the extension is still well-defined but requires different tools to analyze.

The vocabulary established in this section underpins everything that follows. An intermediate field between $F$ and $K$ is a field $E$ satisfying $F \subseteq E \subseteq K$; these intermediate fields are precisely the objects that the Galois correspondence will classify in Chapter 31. A subfield $F$ of $K$ is also determined by specifying which elements of $K$ it contains, and the prime subfield — the smallest subfield of any field — is either isomorphic to $\mathbb{Q}$ (in characteristic zero) or to $\mathbb{F}_p$ (in characteristic $p$). The characteristic of a field is therefore a coarse but fundamental invariant.

The Tower Law is the section's central theorem: if $F \subseteq K \subseteq L$, then $[L:F] = [L:K][K:F]$. Its proof is a clean linear-algebra argument — a basis for $L$ over $K$ and a basis for $K$ over $F$ combine to give a basis for $L$ over $F$ — but its consequences are far-reaching. The Tower Law implies that the degree of any subextension divides the degree of the whole extension, constraining the lattice of intermediate fields. It also means that a degree computation can always be broken into smaller, more manageable steps by inserting an intermediate field. In practice, nearly every degree calculation in Galois theory proceeds by choosing a clever tower.
