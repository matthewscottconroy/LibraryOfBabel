# 29.2.1 Algebraic Elements; the Minimal Polynomial $\mathrm{min}_{F,\alpha}$

Among all elements of an extension field $E/F$, the most important and tractable are those that satisfy polynomial equations over the base field. An element$\alpha \in E$ is *algebraic over$F$* if there exists a nonzero polynomial$f \in F[x]$ with$f(\alpha) = 0$. In other words,$\alpha$ is algebraic if it satisfies some algebraic relation with coefficients drawn from$F$. The element$\sqrt{2}$ satisfies$x^2 - 2 = 0$; the element$i$ satisfies$x^2 + 1 = 0$; the element$\sqrt[5]{3} + \sqrt{7}$ satisfies a more complicated polynomial, but it is algebraic nonetheless. If no such polynomial exists,$\alpha$ is *transcendental over$F$*. The numbers$\pi$ and$e$ are transcendental over$\mathbb{Q}$.

To see why algebraic elements are so tractable, consider the *evaluation homomorphism* $\mathrm{ev}_\alpha: F[x] \to E$ defined by$f \mapsto f(\alpha)$. This sends each polynomial$\sum a_n x^n$ to the element$\sum a_n \alpha^n \in E$, and it is a ring homomorphism. If$\alpha$ is algebraic, the kernel of$\mathrm{ev}_\alpha$ is a nonzero ideal in$F[x]$. Since$F[x]$ is a principal ideal domain (every ideal is generated by a single element), the kernel is generated by some monic polynomial:$\ker(\mathrm{ev}_\alpha) = (f)$ for a unique monic$f \in F[x]$. This monic generator is the *minimal polynomial* of$\alpha$ over$F$, written$\mathrm{min}_{F,\alpha}$. By construction, it is the unique monic polynomial of least degree in$F[x]$ that vanishes at$\alpha$.

The minimal polynomial is necessarily irreducible. If $\mathrm{min}_{F,\alpha} = gh$ with$g, h \in F[x]$ both of smaller degree, then$g(\alpha)h(\alpha) = 0$ in$E$. Since$E$ is a field and hence an integral domain, either$g(\alpha) = 0$ or$h(\alpha) = 0$. But both$g$ and$h$ have degree less than$\mathrm{min}_{F,\alpha}$, contradicting the minimality of the minimal polynomial. The minimal polynomial is therefore irreducible, and every other polynomial vanishing at$\alpha$ is a multiple of$\mathrm{min}_{F,\alpha}$.

The degree of the minimal polynomial completely determines the size of the simple extension $F(\alpha)$. By the first isomorphism theorem applied to$\mathrm{ev}_\alpha$, the image is$F[x]/(\mathrm{min}_{F,\alpha})$, and this image is exactly$F[\alpha]$ — the subring of$E$ generated by$F$ and$\alpha$. Since$\mathrm{min}_{F,\alpha}$ is irreducible and$F[x]$ is a PID, the ideal$(\mathrm{min}_{F,\alpha})$ is maximal, so$F[x]/(\mathrm{min}_{F,\alpha})$ is a field. This means$F[\alpha]$ is already a field, so$F[\alpha] = F(\alpha)$ (no further denominators are needed). If$n = \deg(\mathrm{min}_{F,\alpha})$, then the elements$1, \alpha, \alpha^2, \ldots, \alpha^{n-1}$ form an$F$-basis of$F(\alpha)$, giving$[F(\alpha):F] = n$.

The minimal polynomial is the bridge between the algebraic properties of $\alpha$ and the linear-algebraic structure of$F(\alpha)/F$. Its degree tells us the dimension of the extension; its irreducibility tells us that$F(\alpha)$ is a field; its coefficients are all in$F$, so the relation$\mathrm{min}_{F,\alpha}(\alpha) = 0$ provides an explicit way to reduce powers$\alpha^k$ for$k \geq n$ to$F$-linear combinations of$1, \alpha, \ldots, \alpha^{n-1}$. Arithmetic in$F(\alpha)$ reduces to polynomial arithmetic modulo$\mathrm{min}_{F,\alpha}$.

**Example 1.** Find $\mathrm{min}_{\mathbb{Q}, \sqrt{2}}$. The polynomial$x^2 - 2 \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ vanishes at$\sqrt{2}$ and is irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$ (it has no rational roots by the rational root theorem). Therefore$\mathrm{min}_{\mathbb{Q}, \sqrt{2}} = x^2 - 2$, and$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}):\mathbb{Q}] = 2$.

**Example 2.** Find $\mathrm{min}_{\mathbb{Q}, \zeta_3}$ where$\zeta_3 = e^{2\pi i/3}$. We know$\zeta_3^3 = 1$ and$\zeta_3 \neq 1$, so$\zeta_3$ is a root of$x^3 - 1 = (x-1)(x^2 + x + 1)$. Since$\zeta_3 \neq 1$, it satisfies$x^2 + x + 1 = 0$. This polynomial has no rational roots (checking:$f(1) = 3 \neq 0$,$f(-1) = 1 \neq 0$), so it is irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$. Therefore$\mathrm{min}_{\mathbb{Q}, \zeta_3} = x^2 + x + 1$ and$[\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_3):\mathbb{Q}] = 2$.

**Example 3.** Find $\mathrm{min}_{\mathbb{Q}, \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}}$. Set$\alpha = \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}$. Then$\alpha^2 = 5 + 2\sqrt{6}$, so$\alpha^2 - 5 = 2\sqrt{6}$, and$(\alpha^2 - 5)^2 = 24$, giving$\alpha^4 - 10\alpha^2 + 1 = 0$. The polynomial$f = x^4 - 10x^2 + 1$ has rational coefficients and vanishes at$\alpha$. Is it the minimal polynomial? Its degree is$4$, and one can verify irreducibility by checking it has no rational roots and does not factor as a product of two quadratics over$\mathbb{Q}$ (any factorization$f = (x^2 + ax + b)(x^2 - ax + c)$ leads to$b + c - a^2 = -10$,$a(c - b) = 0$,$bc = 1$ with no rational solution). Therefore$\mathrm{min}_{\mathbb{Q}, \sqrt{2}+\sqrt{3}} = x^4 - 10x^2 + 1$ and$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{3}):\mathbb{Q}] = 4$.

**Example 4.** The minimal polynomial of $\alpha = \sqrt[3]{2}$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ is$x^3 - 2$, irreducible by Eisenstein with$p = 2$. The minimal polynomial of the same$\alpha$ over$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ is$x - \sqrt[3]{2}$ (since$\alpha$ is already in the field). The minimal polynomial depends on the base field: as the base field grows and "knows more about"$\alpha$, the minimal polynomial can only stay the same or shrink.

**Example 5.** Over $\mathbb{F}_2$, find$\mathrm{min}_{\mathbb{F}_2, \omega}$ where$\omega$ is a root of$x^2 + x + 1 \in \mathbb{F}_2[x]$. This polynomial has no root in$\mathbb{F}_2$ (evaluating at$0$:$0 + 0 + 1 = 1 \neq 0$; at$1$:$1 + 1 + 1 = 1 \neq 0$ in$\mathbb{F}_2$). So$x^2 + x + 1$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{F}_2$, and$\mathrm{min}_{\mathbb{F}_2, \omega} = x^2 + x + 1$. The extension$\mathbb{F}_2(\omega) = \mathbb{F}_4$ has degree$2$ over$\mathbb{F}_2$.

The minimal polynomial also controls which other elements of $\bar{F}$ are "algebraically indistinguishable" from$\alpha$ over$F$: they are precisely the other roots of$\mathrm{min}_{F,\alpha}$. These conjugates of$\alpha$ over$F$ are the elements that any$F$-automorphism of$\bar{F}$ could send$\alpha$ to. When the minimal polynomial has$n = [F(\alpha):F]$ distinct roots in$\bar{F}$, there are exactly$n$ ways to embed$F(\alpha)$ into$\bar{F}$ over$F$ — one for each choice of where to send$\alpha$ — and this counting of embeddings is a precursor to the order theorem for Galois groups.
