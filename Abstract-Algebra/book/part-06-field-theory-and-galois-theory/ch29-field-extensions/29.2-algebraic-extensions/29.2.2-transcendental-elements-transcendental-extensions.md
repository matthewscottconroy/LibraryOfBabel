# 29.2.2 Transcendental Elements; Transcendental Extensions

An element $\alpha \in E$ is *transcendental over$F$* if it satisfies no nonzero polynomial with coefficients in$F$. Equivalently, in terms of the evaluation homomorphism$\mathrm{ev}_\alpha: F[x] \to E$, transcendence is exactly injectivity: the kernel is trivial, meaning$f(\alpha) \neq 0$ for every nonzero$f \in F[x]$. The element$\alpha$ does not satisfy any algebraic relation over$F$ whatsoever — it might as well be a formal indeterminate.

The simplest consequence of transcendence is that the subring $F[\alpha] = \{f(\alpha) : f \in F[x]\} \subseteq E$ is isomorphic to the polynomial ring$F[x]$ itself, via the injective evaluation map. Passing to the field of fractions, the subfield$F(\alpha) \subseteq E$ generated by$F$ and$\alpha$ is isomorphic to$F(x)$, the field of rational functions in one variable over$F$. In this sense,$\alpha$ is a "free variable" living inside$E$: there are no polynomial constraints tying it to the elements of$F$. The degree$[F(\alpha):F]$ is infinite — the elements$1, \alpha, \alpha^2, \ldots$ are$F$-linearly independent in$F(\alpha)$, forming an infinite$F$-linearly independent set.

This makes transcendental extensions fundamentally different in character from algebraic ones. An algebraic extension has a finite degree (if it is a simple extension), and all arithmetic in it reduces to polynomial arithmetic modulo the minimal polynomial. A transcendental extension has infinite degree and its arithmetic is the arithmetic of rational functions — there is no moding out, no reduction, no relation to use. The theory of algebraic extensions is, in a precise sense, the theory of finite-dimensional vector spaces with extra multiplicative structure; the theory of transcendental extensions is the theory of function fields.

The classical transcendental numbers over $\mathbb{Q}$ are$\pi$ and$e$. That$e$ is transcendental was proved by Hermite in 1873 and that$\pi$ is transcendental by Lindemann in 1882. These proofs use analytic methods — estimates on integrals, convergence arguments — that are well outside the scope of purely algebraic field theory. The transcendence of$e + \pi$ and$e\pi$ remain open problems; it is known that they cannot both be algebraic (since$e$ and$\pi$ are roots of$x^2 - (e+\pi)x + e\pi$, and if both coefficients were algebraic then both roots would be algebraic, contradicting the transcendence of$e$ and$\pi$), but the individual transcendence of$e + \pi$ and$e\pi$ is unknown.

Transcendental extensions serve as the background for algebraic geometry and the theory of function fields. A function field in one variable over $F$ is a finite extension of$F(t)$ for a transcendental element$t$; it plays the role in geometry that the coordinate ring plays in commutative algebra. The study of function fields mirrors the theory of algebraic curves: the "points" of a curve correspond to discrete valuations on the function field, and the structure of the Galois group of the function field extension reflects the topology of the curve. Although classical Galois theory focuses on algebraic extensions, transcendence provides the geometric setting in which algebraic extensions are best understood.

**Example 1.** The element $\pi \in \mathbb{R}$ is transcendental over$\mathbb{Q}$. The simple extension$\mathbb{Q}(\pi)$ is isomorphic to$\mathbb{Q}(t)$, the field of rational functions with rational coefficients, via the map$t \mapsto \pi$. The elements of$\mathbb{Q}(\pi)$ are all rational expressions$p(\pi)/q(\pi)$ with$p, q \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ and$q(\pi) \neq 0$ (the latter is automatic since$\pi$ is transcendental, so no nonzero$q$ vanishes at$\pi$).

**Example 2.** The element $t$ in the rational function field$\mathbb{F}_p(t)$ is transcendental over$\mathbb{F}_p$. The extension$\mathbb{F}_p(t)/\mathbb{F}_p$ is infinite: the elements$1, t, t^2, \ldots$ are$\mathbb{F}_p$-linearly independent. The element$t$ is also transcendental over$\mathbb{F}_p(t^2)$: if$t$ satisfied a polynomial$\sum a_i(t^2) t^i = 0$ with$a_i(t^2) \in \mathbb{F}_p(t^2)$, then$t$ would be algebraic over$\mathbb{F}_p(t^2)$ and$[\mathbb{F}_p(t):\mathbb{F}_p(t^2)] = 2$ would be the degree. But in fact$t$ satisfies$x^2 - t^2 = 0$ over$\mathbb{F}_p(t^2)$, so$t$ is algebraic of degree$2$ over$\mathbb{F}_p(t^2)$. This shows that whether an element is algebraic or transcendental depends on the base field:$t$ is transcendental over$\mathbb{F}_p$ but algebraic over$\mathbb{F}_p(t^2)$.

**Example 3.** The element $\sqrt{2}$ is algebraic over$\mathbb{Q}$ (degree$2$) but transcendental over any subfield of$\mathbb{Q}$ that does not contain a root of$x^2 - 2$. More interestingly,$\sqrt{2}$ is transcendental over the prime field$\mathbb{F}_p$ for each prime$p$ in the following sense:$\sqrt{2}$ does not live in the algebraic closure$\overline{\mathbb{F}_p}$, since every element of$\overline{\mathbb{F}_p}$ has finite characteristic-$p$ structure, whereas$\sqrt{2}$ is a real number of characteristic zero. The notion of "transcendental over$F$" is entirely dependent on$F$.

**Example 4.** Consider $F = \mathbb{C}(t)$, the field of rational functions over$\mathbb{C}$. The element$t^2$ is algebraic over$F$ (satisfying$x - t^2 = 0$, trivially), but$t$ is algebraic over$\mathbb{C}(t^2)$ with minimal polynomial$x^2 - t^2$ and degree$2$. The element$\sin(t)$, if we embed things into a larger field of functions, would be transcendental over$\mathbb{C}(t)$ — it satisfies no polynomial equation over$\mathbb{C}(t)$. This illustrates that transcendence is about purely algebraic independence, not analytic independence.

**Example 5.** The transcendence degree of $\mathbb{R}$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ is uncountable: there exists an uncountable algebraically independent subset of$\mathbb{R}$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ (a "transcendence basis"). The existence follows from Zorn's Lemma: consider maximal algebraically independent subsets of$\mathbb{R}$ over$\mathbb{Q}$; any such maximal set is a transcendence basis. Since$\mathbb{R}$ is uncountable and$\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$ (the algebraic numbers) is countable, the transcendence degree must be uncountable (more precisely, it equals$|\mathbb{R}| = 2^{\aleph_0}$). Once a transcendence basis$\mathcal{B}$ is fixed,$\mathbb{R}$ is algebraic over$\mathbb{Q}(\mathcal{B})$, and the algebraic part of$\mathbb{R}$ over$\mathbb{Q}(\mathcal{B})$ can be studied using algebraic extension theory.

Transcendental extensions and algebraic extensions together form the two extremes of all field extensions: any extension can be "factored" as a transcendental base extension followed by an algebraic extension. The algebraic part is well-understood by the theory of this and the next several chapters; the transcendental part is governed by the theory of transcendence bases and function fields. The interplay between the two, studied in the subject of model theory and valued field theory, reaches deep into logic and arithmetic.
