# 29.2.5 Every Finite Extension Is Algebraic

A fundamental bridge between the quantitative notion of degree and the qualitative notion of algebraicity is the theorem that every finite extension is algebraic. This is a clean, perhaps surprising result: it says that finiteness of dimension — a linear-algebraic condition — forces every element to satisfy a polynomial equation, an algebraic condition. The two notions of "finite" and "algebraic" are related, and here is how.

\begin{theorem}
If $[E:F] = n < \infty$, then$E/F$ is algebraic.
\end{theorem}

The proof is a direct application of linear algebra. Let $\alpha \in E$ be arbitrary. Consider the$n+1$ elements$1, \alpha, \alpha^2, \ldots, \alpha^n \in E$. Since$E$ is an$n$-dimensional vector space over$F$, any collection of$n+1$ elements is$F$-linearly dependent: there exist$a_0, a_1, \ldots, a_n \in F$, not all zero, such that
$$a_0 + a_1 \alpha + a_2 \alpha^2 + \cdots + a_n \alpha^n = 0.$$
This is precisely a polynomial relation with coefficients in $F$ that$\alpha$ satisfies. Since not all$a_i$ are zero, the polynomial$f = \sum_{i=0}^n a_i x^i \in F[x]$ is nonzero, and$f(\alpha) = 0$. Therefore$\alpha$ is algebraic over$F$. Since$\alpha$ was arbitrary,$E/F$ is algebraic.

The proof is worth pausing over because it reveals why the vector space structure is so powerful. The argument is a general-position argument — the pigeonhole principle applied to a vector space. We do not need to know anything about $\alpha$ or the structure of$E$ beyond the fact that$E$ is$n$-dimensional over$F$. The finiteness of dimension automatically forces every element to satisfy a polynomial relation, without any computation or special structure. This is the mathematical content of the slogan "finite extensions are algebraic."

The bound on the degree of the relation is also useful. The element $\alpha \in E$ satisfies a polynomial of degree at most$n = [E:F]$. The minimal polynomial$\mathrm{min}_{F,\alpha}$ therefore has degree$\leq n$, and by the Tower Law$[F(\alpha):F] \leq [E:F]$. This bounds the size of any simple subextension by the size of the whole extension.

The converse fails, and the failure is instructive. There exist algebraic extensions of infinite degree. The most important example is the algebraic closure $\bar{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q}$: every element is algebraic over$\mathbb{Q}$ by definition, yet the extension is infinite because$[\bar{\mathbb{Q}}:\mathbb{Q}]$ is infinite (as$\sqrt[n]{2}$ has degree$n$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ for every$n \geq 1$). The theorem thus establishes a one-way implication: finite implies algebraic, but algebraic does not imply finite.

**Example 1.** The extension $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ has degree$2$, so every element is algebraic of degree at most$2$ over$\mathbb{Q}$. The element$3 + 5\sqrt{2}$ is algebraic:$(3 + 5\sqrt{2} - 3)^2 = 50$, so it satisfies$(x - 3)^2 - 50 = x^2 - 6x - 41 = 0$. The minimal polynomial is$x^2 - 6x - 41$ (irreducible, since discriminant$= 36 + 164 = 200$ is not a perfect square).

**Example 2.** In $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$, which has degree$3$ over$\mathbb{Q}$, every element satisfies a polynomial of degree$\leq 3$. The element$\alpha = 1 + \sqrt[3]{2} + \sqrt[3]{4}$ has degree exactly$3$: if it had degree$1$ it would be rational, but$\alpha - 1 = \sqrt[3]{2}(1 + \sqrt[3]{2})$, which is manifestly irrational; if it had degree$2$, then$[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}] = 2$ would not divide$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}):\mathbb{Q}] = 3$, contradicting the Tower Law. So$[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}] = 3$ and$\mathbb{Q}(\alpha) = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$.

**Example 3.** In $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$, which has degree$n$ over$\mathbb{F}_p$, every element$\alpha$ satisfies$\alpha^{p^n} = \alpha$ (from the field structure) and therefore every element is algebraic over$\mathbb{F}_p$ of degree dividing$n$. More precisely, the minimal polynomial$\mathrm{min}_{\mathbb{F}_p, \alpha}$ is an irreducible divisor of$x^{p^n} - x$, and its degree$d$ satisfies$d \mid n$.

**Example 4.** The infinite algebraic extension $\bigcup_{n \geq 1} \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[n]{2})$ is algebraic over$\mathbb{Q}$ (every element lies in some$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[n]{2})$ and is hence algebraic over$\mathbb{Q}$) but infinite (since$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[n]{2}):\mathbb{Q}] = n$ grows without bound). This is the simplest example showing that "algebraic" and "finite" are genuinely different properties.

**Example 5.** If $E/F$ is a finite extension and$F \subseteq K \subseteq E$ is any intermediate field, then both$K/F$ and$E/K$ are finite (by the Tower Law:$[K:F]$ and$[E:K]$ divide$[E:F]$), and hence both are algebraic. So the algebraicity of a finite extension passes to all of its sub-extensions and tower-extensions automatically.

The theorem "finite implies algebraic" is used constantly in field theory, often implicitly. Whenever we have a finitely generated algebraic extension — one obtained by adjoining finitely many algebraic elements — we know by repeated applications of the Tower Law that the degree is finite, and hence that all elements are algebraic. This allows us to work with explicit finite extensions rather than worrying about infinite algebraic extensions, at least when a single polynomial is in play.

In the context of Galois theory, the most important extensions are both finite and algebraic. The fundamental theorem requires finite Galois extensions (both normal and separable), and the "finite" part is guaranteed by the algebraic construction we use. Infinite Galois extensions — like $\bar{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q}$ — exist and have a rich theory governed by profinite groups and the Krull topology, but they require additional machinery beyond what the finite theory needs.
