# 29.3.1 Algebraically Closed Fields; the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

A persistent limitation of working over an arbitrary field is that polynomial equations may fail to have solutions within that field. Over $\mathbb{Q}$, the equation$x^2 - 2 = 0$ has no rational solution. Over$\mathbb{R}$, the equation$x^2 + 1 = 0$ has no real solution. Over$\mathbb{F}_p$, many polynomials fail to have roots. One might wonder: is there a "universal" field over which every polynomial equation has a solution? The answer is yes, and such fields are called algebraically closed.

\begin{definition}
A field $k$ is *algebraically closed* if every nonconstant polynomial$f \in k[x]$ has at least one root in$k$.
\end{definition}

This single condition is equivalent to several others. A field $k$ is algebraically closed if and only if every polynomial$f \in k[x]$ of degree$n \geq 1$ factors completely as$f = c(x - \alpha_1)\cdots(x - \alpha_n)$ with$\alpha_i \in k$. It is equivalent to: the only irreducible polynomials in$k[x]$ are the linear ones$(x - \alpha)$. And it is equivalent to:$k$ has no nontrivial algebraic extensions, meaning the only algebraic extension of$k$ is$k$ itself. All three conditions capture the same idea — an algebraically closed field is one where polynomial algebra is as simple as possible, with no irreducible polynomials of degree greater than one.

The canonical example is $\mathbb{C}$, the complex numbers, whose algebraic closure is asserted by the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra.

\begin{theorem}[Fundamental Theorem of Algebra]
Every nonconstant polynomial with complex coefficients has a complex root.
\end{theorem}

This theorem is remarkable for several reasons. It is a statement about algebra — about polynomials and their roots — but every known proof uses some analysis or topology. The proofs fall into several families: those using complex analysis (Liouville's theorem: a bounded entire function is constant, applied to $1/f$ if$f$ has no root), those using topology (the degree of a map from$S^2$ to itself), and those using real analysis (the intermediate value theorem). The absence of a purely algebraic proof is not an oversight; it reflects the fundamentally analytic content of the statement.

The Galois-theoretic proof deserves special mention. It proceeds by showing that no algebraic extension of $\mathbb{C}$ can be nontrivial. If$\mathbb{C} \subsetneq E$ were a finite extension, then$[E:\mathbb{R}]$ would be even (since$[\mathbb{C}:\mathbb{R}] = 2$ divides$[E:\mathbb{R}]$). One shows that any Galois closure of$E/\mathbb{R}$ would have degree a power of$2$, with Galois group a$2$-group. Such groups have nontrivial centers and nontrivial quotients, leading by induction to a real element satisfying$x^2 = -1$, which is absurd, or to a degree-$2$ extension of$\mathbb{C}$, which is ruled out because$\mathbb{C}$ contains square roots of all complex numbers. The proof uses the intermediate value theorem for the existence of real roots of odd-degree polynomials.

Algebraically closed fields are the "perfect" setting for polynomial algebra. Over such a field, every polynomial of degree $n$ has exactly$n$ roots (counted with multiplicity), and there are no surprises from irreducible polynomials of higher degree. This makes algebraically closed fields the natural ambient setting for algebraic geometry: algebraic varieties over$\bar{k}$ have all the points they "should" have, and the Nullstellensatz takes its cleanest form.

**Example 1.** The polynomial $x^4 - 1 \in \mathbb{C}[x]$ factors as$(x-1)(x+1)(x-i)(x+i)$, with all four roots$\{1, -1, i, -i\} \subseteq \mathbb{C}$. Over$\mathbb{R}$, it factors as$(x-1)(x+1)(x^2+1)$, with only two real roots and one irreducible quadratic factor. This illustrates the complete factorization available over an algebraically closed field.

**Example 2.** The polynomial $x^3 - 2 \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ has three complex roots:$\sqrt[3]{2}$,$\sqrt[3]{2}\zeta_3$, and$\sqrt[3]{2}\zeta_3^2$ where$\zeta_3 = e^{2\pi i/3}$. All three lie in$\mathbb{C}$, confirming that$\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed. None of the non-real roots lie in$\mathbb{R}$, which is not algebraically closed.

**Example 3.** The algebraic closure of $\mathbb{F}_p$ (constructed in §29.3.2) is algebraically closed. It contains a root of every irreducible polynomial over$\mathbb{F}_p$, and in fact contains roots of every polynomial over any of its subfields. The algebraic closure$\overline{\mathbb{F}_p} = \bigcup_{n \geq 1} \mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ is the union of all finite fields of characteristic$p$.

**Example 4.** A subfield of $\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed if and only if it contains all algebraic numbers. The algebraic closure$\bar{\mathbb{Q}} \subseteq \mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed: any polynomial with coefficients in$\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$ has coefficients that are algebraic over$\mathbb{Q}$, hence its roots are algebraic over$\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$... and hence algebraic over$\mathbb{Q}$ (by transitivity of algebraicity), and hence lie in$\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$. So$\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$ is algebraically closed, even though it is much smaller than$\mathbb{C}$.

**Example 5.** No finite field is algebraically closed. If $\mathbb{F}_q$ is a finite field with$q$ elements, consider the polynomial$f(x) = 1 + \prod_{\alpha \in \mathbb{F}_q}(x - \alpha) \in \mathbb{F}_q[x]$. For any$\alpha \in \mathbb{F}_q$,$f(\alpha) = 1 + 0 = 1 \neq 0$. So$f$ has no root in$\mathbb{F}_q$, and$\mathbb{F}_q$ is not algebraically closed. This is the field-theoretic analogue of Euclid's proof that there are infinitely many primes.

Algebraically closed fields are the endpoint of the algebraic closure construction: given any field $F$, one can build an algebraic extension$\bar{F}/F$ that is algebraically closed and algebraic over$F$. The existence and uniqueness of such a closure is the content of the next two subsections, and the resulting field$\bar{F}$ is the universe within which all the Galois theory of$F$ takes place.
