# 29.3.2 Existence of Algebraic Closures (via Zorn's Lemma)

Given an arbitrary field $F$, does there exist an algebraically closed field$\bar{F}$ that is algebraic over$F$? The answer is yes, and such a field is called an *algebraic closure* of$F$. But the existence is genuinely non-trivial: unlike the case of$\mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{C}$, where the algebraic closure sits visibly inside an already-familiar ambient field, the general case requires building the ambient field from scratch. This is where set-theoretic tools — specifically Zorn's Lemma — are essential.

The difficulty is not algebraic but set-theoretic. We want to simultaneously adjoin roots of all irreducible polynomials over $F$, then adjoin roots of all irreducible polynomials over the resulting extension, and iterate this process. The product of infinitely many steps requires justification that the process terminates and produces a well-defined field. The two main approaches are Artin's construction and the Zorn's Lemma maximality argument.

\textbf{Artin's construction} proceeds in stages. In the first stage, for each monic irreducible polynomial $f \in F[x]$, introduce a formal variable$x_f$. Form the polynomial ring$R = F[x_f : f \text{ monic irreducible in } F[x]]$ in one variable per irreducible polynomial. The ideal$I$ generated by all$\{f(x_f) : f \text{ monic irreducible}\}$ is a proper ideal (one argues using the existence of maximal ideals, which itself requires Zorn's Lemma). Take a maximal ideal$\mathfrak{m} \supseteq I$; then$F_1 = R/\mathfrak{m}$ is a field extension of$F$ in which every irreducible polynomial over$F$ has a root. Repeat this process with$F_1$ in place of$F$ to get$F_2$, and so on. The union$\bar{F} = \bigcup_{n=0}^\infty F_n$ is a field (the union of a directed system of fields is a field) that contains a root for every polynomial over every$F_n$, hence is algebraically closed. Since each step was algebraic,$\bar{F}/F$ is algebraic.

\textbf{The maximality argument} is more concise, though it also uses Zorn's Lemma. Consider the class of all algebraic extensions of $F$ (this requires some set-theoretic care to ensure we have a set rather than a proper class, which is why the presentation is often given by bounding the cardinality of extensions; we suppress these details). Partially order these extensions by inclusion:$E_1 \leq E_2$ if$E_1 \subseteq E_2$ as fields over$F$. Any chain$E_1 \subseteq E_2 \subseteq \cdots$ has an upper bound: its union$\bigcup E_i$ is an algebraic extension (any element lies in some$E_i$, hence is algebraic over$F$). By Zorn's Lemma, there exists a maximal element$\bar{F}$. If$\bar{F}$ were not algebraically closed, some irreducible polynomial$g \in \bar{F}[x]$ of degree$>1$ would have no root; adjoining a root gives a strictly larger algebraic extension, contradicting maximality. Hence$\bar{F}$ is algebraically closed.

\begin{theorem}[Existence of Algebraic Closures]
Every field $F$ has an algebraic closure: a field$\bar{F}$ that is algebraically closed and algebraic over$F$.
\end{theorem}

The role of Zorn's Lemma (equivalently, the Axiom of Choice) in this theorem is unavoidable. It has been shown that the existence of algebraic closures is equivalent to a weak form of the Axiom of Choice. Over specific fields like $\mathbb{Q}$, the algebraic closure can be constructed more concretely (as the subfield of algebraic numbers in$\mathbb{C}$), but the abstract existence for arbitrary fields requires the full machinery of set theory.

**Example 1.** The algebraic closure of $\mathbb{Q}$ is$\bar{\mathbb{Q}} = \{\alpha \in \mathbb{C} : \alpha \text{ is algebraic over } \mathbb{Q}\}$. It is algebraically closed (as shown in §29.3.1, example 4) and algebraic over$\mathbb{Q}$ (by definition). This is a concrete construction that avoids Zorn's Lemma, since we can embed everything inside$\mathbb{C}$.

**Example 2.** The algebraic closure of $\mathbb{F}_p$ is$\overline{\mathbb{F}_p} = \bigcup_{n \geq 1} \mathbb{F}_{p^n}$. This is a concrete construction: for each$n$, the finite field$\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ exists and is unique up to isomorphism (§32.3.1); the inclusions$\mathbb{F}_{p^m} \subseteq \mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ for$m \mid n$ make this a directed system; the union is a field. Any polynomial over$\overline{\mathbb{F}_p}$ has finitely many coefficients, hence lies in some$\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$, and any polynomial over$\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ has a root in some larger finite field (by existence of finite fields of any prime power degree). So$\overline{\mathbb{F}_p}$ is algebraically closed.

**Example 3.** The algebraic closure of $\mathbb{R}$ is$\mathbb{C}$. Indeed,$\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R}$ is algebraic (degree$2$) and$\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed (Fundamental Theorem of Algebra). So$\mathbb{C} = \bar{\mathbb{R}}$. This is the one case where the algebraic closure is a proper finite extension of the original field.

**Example 4.** For the field $\mathbb{Q}(t)$ of rational functions, the algebraic closure$\overline{\mathbb{Q}(t)}$ is much more complicated. It contains, for each irreducible$f \in \mathbb{Q}(t)[x]$, a root. Elements include$\sqrt{t}$,$t^{1/3}$, and roots of polynomials like$x^2 - (t^3 + 1) \in \mathbb{Q}(t)[x]$. These roots generate an enormous algebraic extension with a rich Galois theory related to the theory of algebraic curves over$\mathbb{Q}$.

**Example 5.** An algebraically closed field cannot be a finite extension of its base. If $[\bar{F}:F] = n < \infty$ and$n > 1$, then$\bar{F}$ cannot be algebraically closed over itself (it is its own algebraic closure, which is immediate). But the question is whether$\bar{F}$ is algebraically closed. This is consistent —$\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R}$ has degree$2$ and$\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed. The point is that if$F$ itself is algebraically closed, then$\bar{F} = F$, and any proper finite algebraic extension of$F$ would contradict algebraic closure.

The existence of algebraic closures is a foundational result that underlies all of field theory. Without it, one cannot even state properly what it means for a polynomial to "split" or for an extension to be normal — these concepts require an ambient algebraically closed field in which all roots live. The algebraic closure $\bar{F}$ is the universe within which the Galois theory of$F$ takes place, and its existence, though non-constructive in general, is what makes the theory coherent.
