# 29.3.4 $\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$: The Field of Algebraic Numbers

The algebraic closure of $\mathbb{Q}$ is the field$\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$ of *algebraic numbers*: those complex numbers$\alpha$ for which there exists a nonzero polynomial$f \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ with$f(\alpha) = 0$. Because$\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed and contains$\mathbb{Q}$, the algebraic numbers live inside the already-familiar complex plane — no abstract construction is required to find them. They are a concrete subfield of$\mathbb{C}$, and much can be said about their structure and size.

The field $\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$ is countable. There are only countably many polynomials with rational coefficients: for each degree$n$, there are countably many polynomials of degree$n$ (a polynomial is determined by its finitely many rational coefficients, and$\mathbb{Q}^{n+1}$ is countable). The union of countably many degree classes is countable. Each polynomial has finitely many roots in$\mathbb{C}$, and the set$\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$ is a countable union of finite sets, hence countable. This is striking:$\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$ is a countably infinite, algebraically complete field, sitting inside the uncountable field$\mathbb{C}$.

The countability of $\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$ makes it "small" inside$\mathbb{C}$. The transcendental numbers — the elements of$\mathbb{C} \setminus \bar{\mathbb{Q}}$ — form a set of uncountable cardinality$|\mathbb{C}|$. In a measure-theoretic sense, a randomly chosen complex number is transcendental with probability$1$:$\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$ is countable and hence has Lebesgue measure zero in$\mathbb{R}^2$. Yet despite its small size,$\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$ is algebraically complete — it is its own algebraic closure, and every polynomial with algebraic coefficients splits entirely into linear factors over$\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$.

Some well-known elements of $\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$:
\begin{itemize}
\item All rational numbers.
\item All $n$th roots of rational numbers:$\sqrt{2}$,$\sqrt[3]{5}$,$\sqrt[7]{-1}$.
\item All roots of unity: $e^{2\pi i/n}$ for any$n \geq 1$.
\item All numbers expressible by nested radicals over $\mathbb{Q}$:$\sqrt{2 + \sqrt{3}}$,$\sqrt[3]{1 + \sqrt{2}}$, etc.
\item The roots of any polynomial with integer coefficients.
\end{itemize}

Elements of $\mathbb{C}$ that do not lie in$\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$ include$\pi$,$e$,$\log(2)$,$\Gamma(1/3)$, and most other classically studied transcendental constants. Whether specific combinations like$\pi + e$ or$\pi^e$ are transcendental remains unknown.

**Example 1.** The element $\alpha = \sqrt{2 + \sqrt{3}} \in \mathbb{R}$ is algebraic over$\mathbb{Q}$. Set$\alpha = \sqrt{2 + \sqrt{3}}$; then$\alpha^2 = 2 + \sqrt{3}$, so$\alpha^2 - 2 = \sqrt{3}$, and$(\alpha^2 - 2)^2 = 3$, giving$\alpha^4 - 4\alpha^2 + 1 = 0$. The polynomial$f = x^4 - 4x^2 + 1$ has rational coefficients and vanishes at$\alpha$. One checks it is irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$ (no rational roots; no factorization into quadratics over$\mathbb{Q}$), so$\mathrm{min}_{\mathbb{Q},\alpha} = x^4 - 4x^2 + 1$ and$[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}] = 4$.

**Example 2.** The element $\zeta_7 = e^{2\pi i/7}$ is algebraic: it is a root of the$7$th cyclotomic polynomial$\Phi_7 = x^6 + x^5 + x^4 + x^3 + x^2 + x + 1$, which is irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$ (by the Eisenstein criterion applied to$\Phi_7(x+1)$). So$[\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_7):\mathbb{Q}] = 6$, and$\zeta_7 \in \bar{\mathbb{Q}}$.

**Example 3.** Countability of $\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$ implies the existence of transcendental numbers by a cardinality argument, independent of any explicit construction. The reals$\mathbb{R}$ are uncountable (Cantor), while$\bar{\mathbb{Q}} \cap \mathbb{R}$ is countable. Therefore$\mathbb{R} \setminus \bar{\mathbb{Q}} \neq \emptyset$, and in fact$|\mathbb{R} \setminus \bar{\mathbb{Q}}| = |\mathbb{R}|$. This is the non-constructive proof of the existence of transcendental numbers, predating both Liouville's explicit construction (1851) and Hermite's/Lindemann's specific results.

**Example 4.** The structure of the lattice of finite subextensions of $\bar{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q}$ is the subject of algebraic number theory. For each prime$p$, there is a unique extension$\mathbb{Q}_p^{\mathrm{unr}} \cap \bar{\mathbb{Q}}$... more directly, the finite Galois extensions of$\mathbb{Q}$ inside$\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$ include:$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ for all squarefree$d \in \mathbb{Z}$ (degree$2$),$\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$ for all$n$ (degree$\phi(n)$), splitting fields of various cubics and quartics, and so on. The absolute Galois group$G_{\mathbb{Q}} = \mathrm{Gal}(\bar{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q})$ is the profinite group controlling all of these simultaneously.

**Example 5.** The field $\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$ is the "smallest algebraically closed field of characteristic$0$" in the following sense: any algebraically closed field of characteristic$0$ contains a subfield isomorphic to$\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$. This is because any algebraically closed field of characteristic$0$ contains$\mathbb{Q}$, and the algebraic closure of$\mathbb{Q}$ in that field is a copy of$\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$. So$\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$ is, up to isomorphism, the unique algebraically closed field of characteristic$0$ that is algebraic over its prime field.

The deepest reason to care about $\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$ is its absolute Galois group$G_{\mathbb{Q}} = \mathrm{Gal}(\bar{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q})$. This group encodes all algebraic symmetries over$\mathbb{Q}$: every finite Galois extension of$\mathbb{Q}$ arises as a fixed field of an open subgroup of$G_{\mathbb{Q}}$ (under the Krull topology), and understanding the representations of$G_{\mathbb{Q}}$ on finite modules is essentially the same as understanding all Galois representations over$\mathbb{Q}$. The Langlands program, one of the central organizing frameworks of modern number theory, can be viewed as a vast project to classify and relate these representations. The field$\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$, seemingly a simple object, is thus the arena for some of the most profound open problems in all of mathematics.
