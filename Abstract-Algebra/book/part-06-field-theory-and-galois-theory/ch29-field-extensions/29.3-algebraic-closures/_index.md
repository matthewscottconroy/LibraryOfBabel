# 29.3 Algebraic Closures

A field $F$ is algebraically closed if every nonconstant polynomial in $F[x]$ has a root in $F$ — equivalently, every nonconstant polynomial splits completely into linear factors over $F$. The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra asserts that $\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed, a result whose cleanest proofs draw on complex analysis or algebraic topology rather than pure algebra, reflecting the depth of the statement. The finite fields $\mathbb{F}_p$ are very far from algebraically closed: $x^p - x - 1$ has no roots in $\mathbb{F}_p$. The algebraic closure construction is how we remedy this deficiency for any field.

An algebraic closure of a field $F$ is an algebraically closed field $\overline{F}$ that is algebraic over $F$ — the smallest algebraically closed field containing $F$. The existence of $\overline{F}$ is a non-trivial theorem; it requires Zorn's Lemma applied to a suitably ordered collection of algebraic extensions, building an extension that is both large enough to contain roots of every polynomial and no larger than necessary. The uniqueness of $\overline{F}$ up to $F$-isomorphism (any two algebraic closures of $F$ are isomorphic via an isomorphism that fixes $F$ pointwise) is a separate theorem, proved by showing any algebraic extension embeds into $\overline{F}$.

The most natural example is $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$, the field of algebraic numbers inside $\mathbb{C}$. Every polynomial with rational coefficients splits completely over $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$, yet $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ is a countable field, in stark contrast to the uncountable field $\mathbb{C}$ it lives in. The elements of $\mathbb{C}$ not in $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ — numbers like $\pi$, $e$, and most real numbers you could name — are the transcendentals. For finite fields, the algebraic closure $\overline{\mathbb{F}_p}$ is an infinite field containing $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ for every $n \geq 1$; it is a union of an ascending chain of finite fields, and its Galois group over $\mathbb{F}_p$ is the profinite completion $\hat{\mathbb{Z}}$, a preview of the rich interaction between Galois theory and number theory that lies beyond this course.
