# Chapter 29 — Field Extensions

Every number you encountered before university lived in a field without your knowing it: the rationals $\mathbb{Q}$, the reals $\mathbb{R}$, the complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$. What was hidden from view was the architecture connecting them — the precise sense in which $\mathbb{R}$ is vastly larger than $\mathbb{Q}$, and $\mathbb{C}$ only a small step beyond $\mathbb{R}$. This chapter builds that architecture from the ground up. A field extension $K/F$ is simply a field $K$ containing a subfield $F$; the power of the concept lies in how much arithmetic information is encoded in this containment.

The most immediate invariant is the degree $[K : F]$, the dimension of $K$ as a vector space over $F$. When $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$, every element has a unique representation $a + b\sqrt{2}$ with $a, b \in \mathbb{Q}$, so $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}) : \mathbb{Q}] = 2$. When $K = \mathbb{C}$ and $F = \mathbb{R}$, the basis $\{1, i\}$ witnesses $[\mathbb{C} : \mathbb{R}] = 2$ as well — two very different extensions sharing the same degree. The Tower Law makes this count multiplicative: if $F \subseteq K \subseteq L$, then $[L : F] = [L : K][K : F]$. This single formula has enormous reach; it constrains which extensions can exist inside which, and later it will be the engine that converts Galois-group information into field-theoretic information.

An element $\alpha$ in an extension $K$ of $F$ is called algebraic over $F$ if it satisfies some nonzero polynomial with coefficients in $F$, and transcendental otherwise. The distinction is profound. Every algebraic element $\alpha$ has a unique monic irreducible minimal polynomial $m_\alpha \in F[x]$, and the simple extension $F(\alpha) \cong F[x]/(m_\alpha(x))$ is completely determined by this polynomial. The degree $[F(\alpha) : F]$ is exactly $\deg m_\alpha$. Transcendental elements, like $\pi$ or $e$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, generate extensions isomorphic to a pure rational function field $F(t)$ — infinite-dimensional over $F$ with no polynomial relations. The contrast between these two cases pervades all of field theory.

The chapter culminates with algebraic closures. A field $F$ is algebraically closed if every nonconstant polynomial in $F[x]$ has a root in $F$ — equivalently, splits completely over $F$. The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra asserts that $\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed. Every field $F$ has an algebraic closure $\overline{F}$: an algebraically closed field containing $F$ with the property that every element of $\overline{F}$ is algebraic over $F$. The existence of $\overline{F}$ requires Zorn's Lemma, and its uniqueness up to $F$-isomorphism is a theorem of real content. The field $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ of all algebraic numbers — a countable algebraically closed field sitting inside $\mathbb{C}$ — is the natural home of the whole Galois-theoretic story that unfolds in Chapters 30 through 32.
