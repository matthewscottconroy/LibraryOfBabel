# 30.1.1 Normal Extensions: Every Irreducible with a Root in $E$ Splits in$E$

For the Galois correspondence to function correctly, extensions need to satisfy two independent conditions: normality and separability. These conditions together make up the Galois condition, and each does a distinct job. Separability ensures that the automorphism group is large enough; normality ensures that the extension is closed under all symmetries of the base field. The present section develops normality; separability is treated in §30.2.

To understand normality, consider what can go wrong. Take $F = \mathbb{Q}$ and$E = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$. The element$\sqrt[3]{2}$ is a root of the irreducible polynomial$x^3 - 2 \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$. This polynomial has three roots in$\mathbb{C}$: the real root$\sqrt[3]{2}$ and the two complex conjugates$\sqrt[3]{2} \cdot e^{2\pi i/3}$ and$\sqrt[3]{2} \cdot e^{4\pi i/3}$. The field$E = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ is a subfield of$\mathbb{R}$ and contains only the first root. The other two roots, which are complex, do not lie in$E$. So$x^3 - 2$ has a root in$E$ but does not split completely in$E$. This is the phenomenon that normality rules out.

\begin{definition}
An algebraic extension $E/F$ is *normal* if for every irreducible polynomial$f \in F[x]$ that has at least one root in$E$, every root of$f$ lies in$E$ — equivalently,$f$ splits completely into linear factors in$E[x]$.
\end{definition}

The geometric intuition is that $E$ should be a symmetric object over$F$: the roots of any irreducible polynomial are "conjugate" over$F$, and normality says that if one conjugate lives in$E$, then all conjugates do. The extension$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ fails normality because it contains$\sqrt[3]{2}$ but not the other conjugates, making it an asymmetric slice of the full picture.

Normality connects directly to automorphisms. Any $F$-automorphism$\sigma$ of$\bar{F}$ (the algebraic closure) must send each root of an irreducible polynomial$f \in F[x]$ to another root of$f$, since$\sigma$ fixes the coefficients of$f$. If$E/F$ is normal and$\alpha \in E$, then every$F$-conjugate of$\alpha$ is a root of$\mathrm{min}_{F,\alpha}$, and normality guarantees that all these roots lie in$E$. Therefore$\sigma(\alpha) \in E$ for every$\sigma \in \mathrm{Aut}(\bar{F}/F)$: the field$E$ is stable under all$F$-automorphisms of$\bar{F}$. This stability is the key property. Conversely, if$E$ is stable under all$F$-automorphisms of$\bar{F}$, then for any irreducible$f$ with a root$\alpha \in E$, all other roots are$F$-conjugates of$\alpha$ and therefore lie in$\sigma(\alpha) \in E$ for appropriate$\sigma$.

\begin{proposition}
A finite algebraic extension $E/F$ is normal if and only if$E$ is stable under every$F$-automorphism of$\bar{F}$.
\end{proposition}

The non-normality of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ has a concrete consequence: the automorphism group$\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})/\mathbb{Q})$ has only one element. The only$\mathbb{Q}$-automorphism of$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ is the identity, because any such automorphism must send$\sqrt[3]{2}$ to another root of$x^3 - 2$, but the other roots are not in$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$. With only one automorphism and degree$3$, the automorphism group is much smaller than the degree — the ratio$|\mathrm{Aut}|/[E:F] = 1/3$ shows exactly how far$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ falls short of being Galois.

**Example 1.** The extension $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ is normal. The minimal polynomial$x^2 - 2$ has roots$\pm\sqrt{2}$, and$-\sqrt{2} = -1 \cdot \sqrt{2} \in \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ since$-1 \in \mathbb{Q}$. So both roots lie in$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ and the extension is normal. The Galois group$\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})/\mathbb{Q}) = \{id, \sigma\}$ where$\sigma(\sqrt{2}) = -\sqrt{2}$ has order$2 = [\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}):\mathbb{Q}]$.

**Example 2.** The extension $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ is not normal:$x^3 - 2$ has a root$\sqrt[3]{2} \in \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ but the other roots$\sqrt[3]{2} e^{2\pi i/3}$ and$\sqrt[3]{2} e^{4\pi i/3}$ are complex, hence not in$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}) \subset \mathbb{R}$.

**Example 3.** The splitting field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$ of$x^3 - 2$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ (where$\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$) is normal. It is the splitting field of$x^3 - 2$, which means all roots$\{\sqrt[3]{2}, \sqrt[3]{2}\omega, \sqrt[3]{2}\omega^2\}$ lie in it. For any irreducible$g \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ with a root in this field, all roots of$g$ also lie in the field (by the characterization in §30.1.2). The degree is$6$.

**Example 4.** Every quadratic extension $F(\sqrt{d})/F$ (where$\mathrm{char}(F) \neq 2$ and$d \in F \setminus F^2$) is normal. The minimal polynomial of$\sqrt{d}$ is$x^2 - d$, with roots$\pm\sqrt{d}$. The element$-\sqrt{d} = (-1)\sqrt{d} \in F(\sqrt{d})$ since$-1 \in F$. So both roots lie in$F(\sqrt{d})$ and the extension is normal. More generally, every extension of degree$2$ is normal (since a degree-$2$ polynomial with one root in$E$ must split — the other root is determined by Vieta's formulas and lies in$E$).

**Example 5.** The extension $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ is not normal. The minimal polynomial of$\sqrt[4]{2}$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ is$x^4 - 2$, which has roots$\pm\sqrt[4]{2}$ and$\pm i\sqrt[4]{2}$. The field$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2}) \subset \mathbb{R}$ contains the real roots$\pm\sqrt[4]{2}$ but not the complex roots$\pm i\sqrt[4]{2}$. So$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ is not normal, even though it contains two of the four roots.

The failure of normality is not a pathology but a warning: without normality, the extension lacks the symmetry needed for the Galois correspondence. Normal extensions are exactly those where the symmetry group $\mathrm{Aut}(E/F)$ acts transitively on the roots of every irreducible polynomial over$F$, and this transitivity is what guarantees the bijection between subgroups and intermediate fields. Normality will be characterized constructively in the next section: finite normal extensions are precisely the splitting fields of polynomials over$F$.
