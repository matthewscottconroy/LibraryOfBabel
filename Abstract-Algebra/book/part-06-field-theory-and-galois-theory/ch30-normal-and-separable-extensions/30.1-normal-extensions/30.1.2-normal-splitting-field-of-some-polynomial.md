# 30.1.2 Normal $\Leftrightarrow$ Splitting Field of Some Polynomial

The definition of normality in terms of irreducible polynomials is clean but abstract: one must check a condition for all irreducible polynomials over $F$, which is not directly constructive. For finite extensions, there is a more practical and constructive characterization: normality is equivalent to being a splitting field.

\begin{definition}
The *splitting field* of $f \in F[x]$ over$F$ is the smallest extension of$F$ in which$f$ factors completely into linear factors. If$f = c(x - \alpha_1)\cdots(x - \alpha_n)$ in$\bar{F}[x]$, the splitting field is$F(\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n)$.
\end{definition}

Splitting fields exist (as subfields of $\bar{F}$), are finite over$F$ (by repeated application of the Tower Law, since each$\alpha_i$ is algebraic over$F$), and are unique up to$F$-isomorphism. Two splitting fields of the same polynomial are isomorphic over$F$ by an isomorphism that sends each root to a root — the isomorphism is not unique (there may be many ways to match the roots), but the fields themselves are isomorphic.

\begin{theorem}
A finite algebraic extension $E/F$ is normal if and only if$E$ is the splitting field of some polynomial$f \in F[x]$.
\end{theorem}

\textit{Proof sketch.} For the forward direction ($\Rightarrow$): suppose$E/F$ is normal and finite. By the primitive element theorem (or by direct observation),$E$ is generated over$F$ by finitely many elements$\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_k$. Each minimal polynomial$\mathrm{min}_{F,\alpha_i}$ is irreducible over$F$ with a root$\alpha_i \in E$; by normality,$\mathrm{min}_{F,\alpha_i}$ splits completely in$E$. Therefore$E$ contains all roots of the product$f = \prod_{i=1}^k \mathrm{min}_{F,\alpha_i}$, and$E$ is generated by these roots, so$E$ is the splitting field of$f$.

For the backward direction ($\Leftarrow$): suppose$E$ is the splitting field of$f \in F[x]$ over$F$, and let$g \in F[x]$ be any irreducible polynomial with a root$\alpha \in E$. We must show all roots of$g$ lie in$E$. Let$\beta$ be any root of$g$ in$\bar{F}$. Since$g$ is irreducible and both$\alpha$ and$\beta$ are roots of$g$, there is an$F$-isomorphism$F(\alpha) \to F(\beta)$ sending$\alpha \mapsto \beta$ (both are isomorphic to$F[x]/(g)$). This isomorphism extends to an embedding$\sigma: E \hookrightarrow \bar{F}$ (by the extension theorem for algebraic closures). Since$E$ is the splitting field of$f$,$\sigma(E)$ is also a splitting field of$f$ over$F$, and all splitting fields of the same polynomial are$F$-isomorphic. In particular$\sigma(E) = E$ (both are splitting fields of$f$ inside$\bar{F}$, and the splitting field inside$\bar{F}$ is unique). Since$\beta = \sigma(\alpha) \in \sigma(E) = E$, we conclude$\beta \in E$.$\square$

This characterization is the practical definition of normality: to construct a normal extension, build a splitting field. And splitting fields are explicit — one writes down a polynomial, lists its roots, and adjoins them all.

**Example 1.** The splitting field of $x^2 - 2$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ is$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, -\sqrt{2}) = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$, since$-\sqrt{2} \in \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$. This is a normal extension of degree$2$.

**Example 2.** The splitting field of $x^3 - 2$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ is$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$ where$\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$. The three roots are$\sqrt[3]{2}$,$\sqrt[3]{2}\omega$,$\sqrt[3]{2}\omega^2$, and the splitting field is the smallest field containing all three. Since$\omega = \sqrt[3]{2}\omega / \sqrt[3]{2}$, the field$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$ contains all three roots. The degree$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega):\mathbb{Q}] = 6$, computed via the tower$\mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$ with degrees$3$ and$2$.

**Example 3.** The splitting field of $x^4 - 2$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ is$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2}, i)$. The four roots are$\pm\sqrt[4]{2}$ and$\pm i\sqrt[4]{2}$. Adjoining$\sqrt[4]{2}$ gives$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2}) \subset \mathbb{R}$; adjoining$i$ (which satisfies$x^2 + 1 = 0$, irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2})$ since$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2}) \subset \mathbb{R}$) gives the splitting field of degree$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2}, i):\mathbb{Q}] = 4 \cdot 2 = 8$.

**Example 4.** The splitting field of $x^p - 1$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ (for prime$p$) is$\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)$ where$\zeta_p = e^{2\pi i/p}$. The roots of$x^p - 1$ are$1, \zeta_p, \zeta_p^2, \ldots, \zeta_p^{p-1}$. Since$\zeta_p^k = (\zeta_p)^k \in \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)$ for all$k$, the field$\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)$ is the splitting field. The degree is$[\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p):\mathbb{Q}] = p - 1$ (the degree of the$p$th cyclotomic polynomial$\Phi_p = 1 + x + \cdots + x^{p-1}$, which is irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$).

**Example 5.** The splitting field of $f = x^4 + 1$ over$\mathbb{Q}$. The roots are the primitive$8$th roots of unity$\zeta_8^k$ for$k \in \{1, 3, 5, 7\}$ where$\zeta_8 = e^{i\pi/4}$. The splitting field is$\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_8) = \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_8)$, which has degree$[\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_8):\mathbb{Q}] = \phi(8) = 4$. Interestingly,$\zeta_8 = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(1 + i)$, and one can check$\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_8) = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, i)$. This is already normal and has$4$ elements in its Galois group, which will be computed as$(\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z})^* \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

The splitting field characterization of normality is the entry point for all computations with Galois extensions. In practice, whenever we want a normal (and Galois) extension of $F$, we write down a separable polynomial$f \in F[x]$ and take its splitting field. The degree of the splitting field, the structure of the Galois group, and the lattice of intermediate fields are then computed from the structure of the roots of$f$ and the algebraic relations among them.
