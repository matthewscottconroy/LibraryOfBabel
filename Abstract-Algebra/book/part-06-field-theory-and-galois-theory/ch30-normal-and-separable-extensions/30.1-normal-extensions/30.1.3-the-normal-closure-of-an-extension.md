# 30.1.3 The Normal Closure of an Extension

Not every finite extension is normal, but every finite extension can be enlarged to a normal one by including whatever roots are missing. The *normal closure* $\tilde{E}$ of a finite extension$E/F$ is the smallest normal extension of$F$ that contains$E$. Its existence and uniqueness follow from the characterization of normal extensions as splitting fields: since$E/F$ is finite,$E$ is generated over$F$ by finitely many elements$\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_k$, and we define$\tilde{E}$ to be the splitting field over$F$ of the product$\prod_{i=1}^k \mathrm{min}_{F,\alpha_i}$.

This construction has a clear conceptual meaning. The extension $E/F$ may fail to be normal precisely because some minimal polynomial$\mathrm{min}_{F,\alpha_i}$ has roots outside$E$. The normal closure patches this by adjoining all missing roots of all minimal polynomials of generators of$E$. The result is normal by the splitting field criterion, it contains$E$ since the generators$\alpha_i$ are among the adjoined roots, and any normal extension of$F$ containing$E$ must already contain all the roots of each$\mathrm{min}_{F,\alpha_i}$ (by normality and the definition of minimal polynomials), so$\tilde{E}$ is minimal among all normal extensions of$F$ that contain$E$.

The degree $[\tilde{E}:F]$ is finite whenever$[E:F]$ is finite, which follows by induction on the degree and the Tower Law. More precisely,$[\tilde{E}:F]$ divides$[E:F]!$, since$\tilde{E}$ is obtained from$F$ by adjoining at most$[E:F]$ conjugates of the generators, each step increasing the degree by at most the degree of the minimal polynomial involved. This bound is rarely sharp but shows that the normal closure cannot be catastrophically larger than the original extension.

In the canonical non-normal example, the extension $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ fails to be normal because the minimal polynomial$x^3 - 2$ has three roots —$\sqrt[3]{2}$,$\omega\sqrt[3]{2}$, and$\omega^2\sqrt[3]{2}$, where$\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$ is a primitive cube root of unity — but only the first lies in$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}) \subset \mathbb{R}$. The normal closure is$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$, the splitting field of$x^3 - 2$ over$\mathbb{Q}$, which has degree$6$ over$\mathbb{Q}$, twice the degree of the original extension.

The Galois group $\mathrm{Gal}(\tilde{E}/F)$ acts transitively on the roots of each minimal polynomial$\mathrm{min}_{F,\alpha_i}$, since any two roots of an irreducible polynomial are conjugate via a field automorphism extending to$\tilde{E}$. This transitivity is a key structural feature: it means the Galois group "sees" all the conjugates of every generator of$E$. The subgroup$\mathrm{Gal}(\tilde{E}/E) \leq \mathrm{Gal}(\tilde{E}/F)$ corresponds, under the Galois correspondence for$\tilde{E}/F$, to the intermediate field$E$ itself. When$E/F$ is not Galois, this subgroup need not be normal in$\mathrm{Gal}(\tilde{E}/F)$ — indeed, the normality of$\mathrm{Gal}(\tilde{E}/E)$ in$\mathrm{Gal}(\tilde{E}/F)$ is exactly equivalent to$E/F$ being a normal extension.

The normal closure construction makes precise the sense in which every finite extension embeds into a Galois extension in the most efficient way. Given any finite $E/F$, the normal closure$\tilde{E}$ is the smallest Galois extension of$F$ containing$E$ (provided$E/F$ is separable; if not, one may need to also arrange separability). All questions about the symmetry of$E$ over$F$ can then be phrased in terms of the subgroup$H = \mathrm{Gal}(\tilde{E}/E)$ of the full Galois group$G = \mathrm{Gal}(\tilde{E}/F)$: the degree$[E:F] = [G:H]$, and the conjugates of$E$ in$\tilde{E}$ correspond to the conjugates of$H$ in$G$.

**Example 1.** Let $E = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$. The minimal polynomial of$\sqrt{2}$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ is$x^2 - 2$, which splits as$(x - \sqrt{2})(x + \sqrt{2})$ over$E$ since$-\sqrt{2} \in E$. Both roots lie in$E$, so$E/\mathbb{Q}$ is already normal. The normal closure is$\tilde{E} = E = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ with$[\tilde{E}:\mathbb{Q}] = 2$.

**Example 2.** Let $E = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$. The polynomial$x^3 - 2$ has roots$\sqrt[3]{2},\, \omega\sqrt[3]{2},\, \omega^2\sqrt[3]{2}$ where$\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$. Since$\omega \notin \mathbb{R}$, only$\sqrt[3]{2}$ lies in$E \subset \mathbb{R}$. The normal closure is the splitting field
$$\tilde{E} = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega), \quad [\tilde{E}:\mathbb{Q}] = 6.$$
The Galois group $\mathrm{Gal}(\tilde{E}/\mathbb{Q}) \cong S_3$. The subgroup$H = \mathrm{Gal}(\tilde{E}/E)$ has order$2$ (generated by complex conjugation restricted to$\tilde{E}$) and is not normal in$S_3$, confirming that$E/\mathbb{Q}$ is not normal.

**Example 3.** Let $E = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2}) \subset \mathbb{R}$, a root of$f(x) = x^4 - 2$. The four roots of$f$ are$\sqrt[4]{2},\, -\sqrt[4]{2},\, i\sqrt[4]{2},\, -i\sqrt[4]{2}$. Since$E \subset \mathbb{R}$, only two roots lie in$E$. The normal closure must adjoin$i\sqrt[4]{2}$, and since$i = (i\sqrt[4]{2})/\sqrt[4]{2}$, we get$\tilde{E} = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2}, i)$ with$[\tilde{E}:\mathbb{Q}] = 8$ and$\mathrm{Gal}(\tilde{E}/\mathbb{Q}) \cong D_4$.

**Example 4.** Let $E = \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_5)$ where$\zeta_5 = e^{2\pi i/5}$. The minimal polynomial of$\zeta_5$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ is the cyclotomic polynomial$\Phi_5(x) = x^4 + x^3 + x^2 + x + 1$. The four roots$\zeta_5, \zeta_5^2, \zeta_5^3, \zeta_5^4$ are all powers of$\zeta_5$ and hence all lie in$E$. So$\Phi_5$ splits completely in$E$, meaning$E/\mathbb{Q}$ is normal (it is a splitting field). The normal closure is$\tilde{E} = E$ itself.
