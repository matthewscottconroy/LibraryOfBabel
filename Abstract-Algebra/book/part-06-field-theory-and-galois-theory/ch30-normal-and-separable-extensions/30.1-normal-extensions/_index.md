# 30.1 Normal Extensions

An algebraic extension $K/F$ is normal if, whenever an irreducible polynomial $f \in F[x]$ has at least one root in $K$, it has all of its roots in $K$. Intuitively, a normal extension does not favor one root of an irreducible polynomial over its conjugates: if one conjugate is present, all are. The failure of normality is visible and concrete: $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ is not normal because $x^3 - 2$ has a real root $\sqrt[3]{2}$ in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ but its complex conjugate roots $\omega\sqrt[3]{2}$ and $\omega^2\sqrt[3]{2}$, where $\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$, are absent. To make the extension normal, one must adjoin $\omega$ as well, arriving at the splitting field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$.

This example generalizes to the characterization: $K/F$ is normal if and only if $K$ is the splitting field of some polynomial (or family of polynomials) in $F[x]$. Every splitting field is normal, and every normal extension arises this way. The splitting field perspective makes normality concrete and computable: to test normality of $K/F$, one checks whether every irreducible polynomial over $F$ with a root in $K$ factors completely in $K[x]$. The splitting field construction shows that for every algebraic extension $K/F$, there is a smallest normal extension of $F$ containing $K$, called the normal closure.

Normal extensions interact with the Galois group in a precise way. If $K/F$ is a Galois extension with group $G = \text{Gal}(K/F)$, and $E$ is an intermediate field, then $E/F$ is normal if and only if the subgroup $\text{Gal}(K/E)$ is normal in $G$. This is one of the deepest clauses of the fundamental theorem of Galois theory: the group-theoretic concept of normality of subgroups exactly shadows the field-theoretic concept of normality of extensions. Without this correspondence between the two notions of "normal," the Galois correspondence would tell only half the story.
