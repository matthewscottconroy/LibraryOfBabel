# 30.2.1 Separable Polynomials: No Repeated Roots in $\bar{F}$

Alongside normality, separability is the second condition an extension must satisfy to admit a well-behaved Galois correspondence. While normality concerns which polynomials split, separability concerns how they split — specifically, whether their roots are distinct. A polynomial $f \in F[x]$ is *separable* if it has no repeated roots in$\bar{F}$. A polynomial that fails this condition, having some root$\alpha$ with multiplicity greater than one, is called *inseparable*.

Over familiar fields like $\mathbb{Q}$,$\mathbb{R}$, or any finite field, separability is automatic for irreducible polynomials, and one might wonder why it needs attention at all. The reason is characteristic$p$: in characteristic$p > 0$, the formal derivative of a polynomial$f(x^p)$ is identically zero, making it possible for an irreducible polynomial to have a zero derivative — a strong signal of repeated roots. The polynomial$x^p - a$ over a field where$a$ is not a$p$th power provides the canonical example: it is irreducible, yet its derivative is$px^{p-1} = 0$, and its single root$\alpha = a^{1/p}$ in the algebraic closure has multiplicity$p$.

Repeated roots are the algebraic obstruction to the Galois correspondence working correctly. If the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$ over$F$ has a repeated root, then distinct$F$-automorphisms of$\bar{F}$ may send$\alpha$ to the same root, since repeated roots are indistinguishable algebraically from one another. This collapses the Galois group below the expected size: instead of$[E:F]$ automorphisms, one gets fewer, and the bijection between subgroups and intermediate fields fails. Separability is the condition that rules out this collapse, ensuring that the Galois group is large enough to recover the full lattice of intermediate fields.

It is worth making explicit what "no repeated roots in $\bar{F}$" means. When one writes$f(x) = c\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x - \alpha_i)$ in$\bar{F}[x]$, the polynomial is separable if and only if all$\alpha_i$ are distinct — equivalently, if the factorization has no repeated linear factors. The degree of$f$ over$F$ may be less than$n$ (if we count without multiplicity) but equals$n$ in the separable case. A reducible polynomial is declared separable if each of its irreducible factors is separable; the main concern is the irreducible case.

The definition is formulated over $\bar{F}$ rather than over$F$ itself to ensure that all roots are visible. If one checked only for repeated roots in$F$ or in some intermediate extension, one might miss repetitions that appear only in the algebraic closure. The algebraically closed field$\bar{F}$ is the correct universe in which to decide whether a polynomial has a repeated root, and all separability statements are ultimately statements about factorizations in$\bar{F}[x]$.

Understanding separability requires distinguishing two sources of potential repetition. A reducible polynomial $f = gh$ may acquire apparent repetition simply from$g$ and$h$ sharing a common root, but this is a statement about the factorization of$f$, not about the irreducible factors. The genuinely interesting case is when an *irreducible* polynomial is inseparable — when an irreducible$f$ has a repeated root in$\bar{F}$. As we will see in the next subsection, this is detectable using the formal derivative:$f$ is separable if and only if$\gcd(f, f') = 1$.

**Example 1.** Over $\mathbb{Q}$, the polynomial$f(x) = x^2 - 2$ is irreducible with roots$\pm\sqrt{2}$ in$\bar{\mathbb{Q}} = \overline{\mathbb{Q}}$. The two roots are distinct, so$f$ is separable.

**Example 2.** The polynomial $f(x) = (x^2 - 2)^2 = x^4 - 4x^2 + 4$ is not separable over$\mathbb{Q}$, because the root$\sqrt{2}$ appears with multiplicity$2$. However,$f$ is reducible, and its irreducible factor$x^2 - 2$ is separable. In the theory, we say$f$ itself is inseparable as a polynomial, but it splits into separable irreducible factors.

**Example 3.** Let $F = \mathbb{F}_p(t)$, the rational function field over$\mathbb{F}_p$. The polynomial$f(x) = x^p - t \in F[x]$ is irreducible (by Eisenstein at the prime$t$ in$\mathbb{F}_p[t]$). In the algebraic closure$\bar{F}$, there exists a unique element$\alpha$ with$\alpha^p = t$, and$f(x) = (x - \alpha)^p$ in$\bar{F}[x]$. Thus$f$ is irreducible and inseparable: the single root$\alpha$ appears with multiplicity$p$. This is the canonical example of an inseparable irreducible polynomial.

**Example 4.** Over $\mathbb{F}_5$, consider$f(x) = x^5 - x$. Then$f'(x) = 5x^4 - 1 = -1$ in$\mathbb{F}_5[x]$, so$\gcd(f, f') = \gcd(f, -1) = 1$. Thus$f$ is separable. Indeed,$x^5 - x = x(x^4-1) = x(x-1)(x+1)(x-2)(x+2)$ in$\mathbb{F}_5[x]$, with all five roots$0, 1, 2, 3, 4$ distinct.
