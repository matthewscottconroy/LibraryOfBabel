# 30.2.2 Separability via the Formal Derivative: $\gcd(f, f') = 1$

To check whether a polynomial has repeated roots, one needs a computable criterion that works over any field, including those where limits and analysis are unavailable. The tool is the *formal derivative*. For $f = \sum_{n} a_n x^n \in F[x]$, the formal derivative is defined by the usual formula$f' = \sum_n n a_n x^{n-1}$, where$n a_n$ means$a_n + a_n + \cdots + a_n$ ($n$ times) in the field$F$. No limits are involved; this is a purely algebraic operation defined by the power rule. The formal derivative satisfies the standard rules: linearity, the product rule$(fg)' = f'g + fg'$, and the chain rule, all verified by direct computation with monomials.

The key theorem is that $f$ is separable — has no repeated roots in$\bar{F}$ — if and only if$\gcd(f, f') = 1$ in$F[x]$. The proof runs through$\bar{F}$: if$\alpha$ is a repeated root of$f$, then$f = (x - \alpha)^2 g$ in$\bar{F}[x]$, and differentiating gives$f' = 2(x-\alpha)g + (x-\alpha)^2 g'$, so$(x - \alpha)$ divides$f'$. Thus$f$ and$f'$ share the common factor$(x - \alpha)$, making$\gcd(f, f') \neq 1$ in$\bar{F}[x]$, and hence in$F[x]$. Conversely, if$f$ and$f'$ share a common factor$h \in F[x]$ of degree at least one, then any root of$h$ in$\bar{F}$ is a repeated root of$f$.

This criterion is practical and computable: given an explicit polynomial, one computes $f'$ by the power rule and then the gcd using the Euclidean algorithm in$F[x]$. The algorithm operates entirely within$F[x]$ and does not require knowing the roots of$f$. This is a significant practical advantage: the roots may live in a large or complicated extension, but separability is detected by a computation that stays in$F$.

The criterion also illuminates precisely when inseparability can occur. If $f$ is irreducible and$f' \neq 0$, then since$\deg f' < \deg f$ and$f$ is irreducible (hence has no proper divisors of positive degree),$f$ cannot divide$f'$, so$\gcd(f, f') = 1$ and$f$ is separable. Inseparability of an irreducible polynomial therefore requires$f' = 0$. When does a nonzero polynomial have zero formal derivative? Writing$f = \sum_{k=0}^{n} a_k x^k$, we have$f' = \sum_{k=1}^{n} k a_k x^{k-1}$. This vanishes identically if and only if$k a_k = 0$ in$F$ for all$k \geq 1$. In characteristic$0$,$k \neq 0$ for all$k \geq 1$, so$f' = 0$ forces$a_k = 0$ for all$k \geq 1$, meaning$f$ is a constant — impossible for an irreducible polynomial of degree$\geq 1$. In characteristic$p > 0$,$k a_k = 0$ whenever$p \mid k$, so$f' = 0$ precisely when all terms with exponent not divisible by$p$ vanish, i.e.,$f$ is a polynomial in$x^p$.

This analysis pins down precisely where inseparability can hide: only in characteristic $p > 0$, and only for polynomials of the form$f(x) = g(x^p)$ for some$g \in F[x]$. If in addition$g(x^p) = g(y)$ evaluated at$y = x^p$ is irreducible in$F[x]$, then$f$ is an inseparable irreducible polynomial.

**Example 1.** Let $f(x) = x^3 - 3x + 1 \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$. Then$f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3 = 3(x^2 - 1)$. Computing$\gcd(f, f')$ by the Euclidean algorithm in$\mathbb{Q}[x]$: one finds that$f$ and$f'$ have no common roots (since$\pm 1$ are not roots of$f$), so$\gcd(f, f') = 1$ and$f$ is separable.

**Example 2.** Let $F = \mathbb{F}_2$ and$f(x) = x^2 + x + 1$. Then$f'(x) = 2x + 1 = 1$ in$\mathbb{F}_2[x]$ (since$2 = 0$). So$\gcd(f, f') = \gcd(f, 1) = 1$ and$f$ is separable. Indeed,$f$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{F}_2$ (it has no roots in$\mathbb{F}_2$) and its roots in$\mathbb{F}_4$ are distinct.

**Example 3.** Let $F = \mathbb{F}_p(t)$ and$f(x) = x^p - t$. Then$f'(x) = px^{p-1} = 0$ in characteristic$p$. So$\gcd(f, f') = \gcd(x^p - t, 0) = x^p - t$, which has degree$p > 0$. The gcd is not$1$, confirming that$f$ is inseparable. The root$t^{1/p}$ in$\bar{F}$ has multiplicity$p$: indeed,$f(x) = (x - t^{1/p})^p$ in$\bar{F}[x]$.

**Example 4.** Over $\mathbb{F}_3$, consider$f(x) = x^6 - x^3 + 1$. Then$f'(x) = 6x^5 - 3x^2 = 0$ in$\mathbb{F}_3[x]$. Substituting$u = x^3$ gives$f(x) = u^2 - u + 1 = g(u)$, and the polynomial$g(u) = u^2 - u + 1$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{F}_3$ (discriminant$1 - 4 = -3 = 0$ mod$3$... actually checking:$g(0) = 1$,$g(1) = 1$,$g(2) = 3 = 0$, so$u = 2$ is a root). Thus$g(u) = (u-2)(u-?)$ — refactoring reveals$g(u) = (u-2)^2$ over$\mathbb{F}_3$ since$g = u^2 - u + 1$ and$g(2) = 4 - 2 + 1 = 3 = 0$,$g'(2) = 4 - 1 = 3 = 0$. So$f(x) = (x^3 - 2)^2$ and the repeated factor$x^3 - 2$ confirms inseparability.
