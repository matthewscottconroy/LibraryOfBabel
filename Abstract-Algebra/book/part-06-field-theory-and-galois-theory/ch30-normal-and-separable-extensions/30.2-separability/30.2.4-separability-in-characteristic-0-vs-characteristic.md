# 30.2.4 Separability in Characteristic 0 vs. Characteristic $p$

The behavior of separability divides sharply along characteristic lines, and this division is one of the most significant structural features of field theory. In characteristic $0$, inseparability is impossible for irreducible polynomials. The argument is brief: if$f$ is irreducible and$f' \neq 0$, then because$\deg f' < \deg f$ and$f$ is irreducible, the gcd$\gcd(f, f')$ must be$1$ (no nonunit divisor of$f$ can have degree less than$\deg f$ when$f$ is irreducible). Thus$f$ is separable. In characteristic$0$, the derivative$f'$ of a nonzero polynomial is automatically nonzero (since the leading coefficient$na_n$ is nonzero when$n \neq 0$ in characteristic$0$). Therefore, every irreducible polynomial over a characteristic-$0$ field is separable, and consequently every algebraic extension is separable.

\begin{theorem}
Let $F$ be a field of characteristic$0$. Then every irreducible polynomial$f \in F[x]$ is separable, and every algebraic extension of$F$ is separable.
\end{theorem}

In characteristic $p > 0$, the situation changes fundamentally because$n \cdot 1 = 0$ in$F$ when$p \mid n$. The formal derivative of a polynomial$f(x) = \sum a_i x^{ip}$ (a polynomial only in$x^p$) is$f'(x) = \sum ip \cdot a_i x^{ip-1} = 0$: the derivative vanishes identically because every exponent is a multiple of$p$. Since$\gcd(f, 0) = f$, any such polynomial shares a non-trivial common factor with its (zero) derivative, and must have repeated roots in$\bar{F}$. The standard example is$x^p - a$ over a field$F$ of characteristic$p$, where$a \notin F^p$: this polynomial is irreducible but its only root in$\bar{F}$ is$a^{1/p}$, appearing with multiplicity$p$, since$x^p - a = (x - a^{1/p})^p$ in$\bar{F}[x]$.

\begin{proposition}
Let $F$ be a field of characteristic$p > 0$ and$f \in F[x]$ irreducible. Then$f$ is inseparable if and only if$f' = 0$, which occurs if and only if$f(x) = g(x^p)$ for some$g \in F[x]$.
\end{proposition}

More generally, the only way an irreducible polynomial $f \in F[x]$ can be inseparable is if$f' = 0$, which forces$f$ to be a polynomial in$x^p$. Write$f(x) = g(x^p)$ for some$g \in F[x]$. If$g$ itself has a root$b \in \bar{F}$, then$b^{1/p}$ is a root of$f$ with multiplicity at least$p$. The irreducibility of$f = g(x^p)$ then forces$g$ to be irreducible over$F$, and the roots of$f$ are exactly the$p$th roots of the roots of$g$, each with multiplicity$p$.

This contrast explains why inseparability is purely a characteristic-$p$ phenomenon, and why Galois theory over$\mathbb{Q}$ or$\mathbb{R}$ encounters no separability issues. When working in characteristic$p$, one must actively distinguish separable from inseparable extensions. The Frobenius endomorphism is the key map for understanding inseparability, and the concept of a perfect field precisely identifies when the characteristic-$p$ pathology can be avoided.

**Example 1.** Over $\mathbb{Q}$ (characteristic$0$), every polynomial of the form$x^n - a$ with$a \in \mathbb{Q}$ is separable. Its derivative is$nx^{n-1}$, which is nonzero (since$n \neq 0$ in$\mathbb{Q}$), and$\gcd(x^n - a, nx^{n-1}) = 1$ because any common factor would divide$x^{n-1}$, hence be a power of$x$, but$x^n - a$ evaluated at$0$ is$-a \neq 0$. All extensions of$\mathbb{Q}$ are separable, and there is no separability obstruction in classical algebraic number theory.

**Example 2.** Over $\mathbb{F}_p$, the polynomial$x^{p^2} - x$ is separable. Computing the derivative:$\frac{d}{dx}(x^{p^2} - x) = p^2 x^{p^2 - 1} - 1 = -1$ in characteristic$p$ (since$p^2 \equiv 0 \pmod{p}$). So the derivative is$-1 \neq 0$, and$\gcd(x^{p^2} - x, -1) = 1$. The polynomial$x^{p^2} - x$ is separable, with$p^2$ distinct roots forming exactly$\mathbb{F}_{p^2}$.

**Example 3.** Over $\mathbb{F}_2$, the polynomial$f(x) = x^4 + x^2 + 1$ is inseparable. Computing:$f'(x) = 4x^3 + 2x = 0$ in$\mathbb{F}_2[x]$ (since$4 = 2 = 0$ mod$2$). Writing$f(x) = (x^2)^2 + x^2 + 1 = g(x^2)$ where$g(u) = u^2 + u + 1$, we see$f$ is a polynomial in$x^2 = x^p$. Over$\mathbb{F}_2$,$g(0) = 1 \neq 0$ and$g(1) = 1 \neq 0$, so$g$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{F}_2$. Thus$f(x) = g(x^2)$ is inseparable: its roots in$\bar{\mathbb{F}}_2$ are the square roots of the roots of$g$, each appearing with multiplicity$2$.

**Example 4.** Over any perfect field $F$ of characteristic$p$ (for instance any finite field$\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$), every irreducible polynomial is separable. If$f$ is irreducible and$f' = 0$, then$f(x) = g(x^p)$. Since$F$ is perfect, every coefficient of$g$ is a$p$th power: write$g(u) = \sum c_i^p u^i$. By the Frobenius identity,$g(x^p) = \sum c_i^p x^{ip} = \left(\sum c_i x^i\right)^p = h(x)^p$ for$h = \sum c_i x^i \in F[x]$. So$f = h^p$, contradicting irreducibility. Hence no irreducible polynomial over a perfect field can be inseparable.

**Example 5.** Over $F = \mathbb{F}_p(t)$, consider the tower$F \subseteq F(t^{1/p}) \subseteq F(t^{1/p^2})$. Each step is purely inseparable of degree$p$:$t^{1/p}$ has minimal polynomial$x^p - t$ over$F$, and$t^{1/p^2}$ has minimal polynomial$x^p - t^{1/p}$ over$F(t^{1/p})$, which is inseparable since$t^{1/p} \notin F(t^{1/p})^p$. The full extension$F(t^{1/p^2})/F$ has degree$p^2$ and is purely inseparable. Characteristic$0$ admits no such towers.

\begin{remark}
The sharp dichotomy between characteristic $0$ and characteristic$p$ for separability is one reason that algebraic geometry in positive characteristic is substantially more delicate. Many results automatic in characteristic$0$ require an explicit separability hypothesis in characteristic$p$. Grothendieck's theory of étale morphisms is, in part, a systematic treatment of separability in geometric terms: a morphism of schemes is étale if and only if it is flat and its fibers are geometrically reduced, the latter condition being the geometric avatar of separability.
\end{remark}
