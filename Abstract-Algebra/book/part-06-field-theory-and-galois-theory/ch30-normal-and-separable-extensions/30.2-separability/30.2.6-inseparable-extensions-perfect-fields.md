# 30.2.6 Inseparable Extensions; Perfect Fields

A field $F$ is *perfect* if every algebraic extension of$F$ is separable. The condition is equivalent to either of two simpler criteria:$F$ has characteristic$0$, or$F$ has characteristic$p > 0$ and the Frobenius$\phi: a \mapsto a^p$ is surjective, meaning$F = F^p$ (every element of$F$ is already a$p$th power). The equivalence follows from the analysis of inseparability: an inseparable irreducible polynomial must have zero formal derivative, which forces it to be a polynomial in$x^p$, which forces the existence of a root of the form$a^{1/p}$ with$a \notin F^p$. If no such element$a$ exists, then no inseparable irreducible polynomial exists and every algebraic extension is separable.

\begin{definition}
A field $F$ is *perfect* if every irreducible polynomial in$F[x]$ is separable, equivalently if every algebraic extension of$F$ is separable.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}
A field $F$ is perfect if and only if either$\mathrm{char}(F) = 0$, or$\mathrm{char}(F) = p > 0$ and the Frobenius$\phi: F \to F$ given by$\phi(a) = a^p$ is surjective, i.e.,$F = F^p$.
\end{theorem}

The most important examples of perfect fields are all fields of characteristic $0$ and all finite fields. For finite fields$\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$, the Frobenius is an automorphism (any injective map from a finite set to itself is surjective), hence surjective, so every element is a$p$th power. This makes all finite fields perfect — a fact with significant consequences for the theory of finite Galois extensions. Algebraic closures of any field are also perfect, since an algebraically closed field contains$p$th roots of all its elements by definition.

\begin{corollary}
Every finite field $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ is perfect. Every algebraically closed field is perfect.
\end{corollary}

The non-perfect case does arise and has a rich, if more technical, theory. A field of the form $F = k(t)$, the rational function field in one variable over a perfect field$k$ of characteristic$p$, is not perfect: the element$t \in F$ is not a$p$th power, since any$p$th power in$k(t)$ is a rational function of$t^p$, not of$t$. The extension$F(t^{1/p})/F$ is purely inseparable of degree$p$, with trivial Galois group.

An extension $E/F$ is called *purely inseparable* if every element of$E$ not in$F$ is inseparable over$F$ — equivalently, if the only separable sub-extension of$E/F$ is$F$ itself. In a purely inseparable extension, every element$\alpha \in E$ satisfies a polynomial of the form$x^{p^k} - a$ with$a \in F$, and the Galois group is trivial. The degree$[E:F]$ in the purely inseparable case is a power of$p$. More generally, for any finite algebraic extension, the *separable degree*$[E:F]_{s}$ (number of$F$-embeddings into$\bar{F}$) and the *inseparable degree*$[E:F]_{i}$ (the$p$-power "defect") satisfy$[E:F] = [E:F]_{s} \cdot [E:F]_{i}$.

**Example 1.** The field $\mathbb{Q}$ is perfect (characteristic$0$). So are$\mathbb{R}$,$\mathbb{C}$, and any number field$K/\mathbb{Q}$. All algebraic extensions of these fields are separable, and there is no inseparability obstruction anywhere in classical algebraic number theory or complex analysis.

**Example 2.** Every finite field $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ is perfect. Since$|\mathbb{F}_{p^n}^*| = p^n - 1$, the Frobenius$x \mapsto x^p$ is injective on a finite set, hence bijective. More explicitly: given$a \in \mathbb{F}_{p^n}$, the element$a^{p^{n-1}}$ satisfies$(a^{p^{n-1}})^p = a^{p^n} = a$ (using$a^{p^n} = a$ for all$a \in \mathbb{F}_{p^n}$). So$a = (a^{p^{n-1}})^p$ is a$p$th power in$\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$, confirming perfectness.

**Example 3.** Let $F = \mathbb{F}_p(t)$. Then$t \in F$ is not a$p$th power in$F$, since any element of$F^p$ has the form$(f(t)/g(t))^p = f(t^p)/g(t^p)$ — a rational function of$t^p$ only — and$t$ cannot be expressed in this form (comparing degrees in numerator and denominator shows the$t$-degree would be a multiple of$p$). The extension$F(t^{1/p})/F$ is purely inseparable of degree$p$, with minimal polynomial$x^p - t$ for$t^{1/p}$.

**Example 4.** An algebraically closed field $\Omega$ is perfect. Given any$a \in \Omega$, the polynomial$x^p - a$ has a root in$\Omega$ (since$\Omega$ is algebraically closed), so$a$ is a$p$th power. The condition$\Omega = \Omega^p$ holds, and all algebraic extensions of$\Omega$ are trivial (separable vacuously). The algebraic closure$\overline{\mathbb{F}_p(t)}$ is perfect, even though$\mathbb{F}_p(t)$ is not.

**Example 5.** Let $F = \mathbb{F}_p(s, t)$ with$E = F(s^{1/p}, t^{1/p})$. Then$E/F$ is purely inseparable of degree$p^2$: the separable degree$[E:F]_{s} = 1$ and the inseparable degree$[E:F]_{i} = p^2$. As computed in §30.3.3, this extension is not simple — there is no primitive element — and$\mathrm{Gal}(E/F) = \{\mathrm{id}\}$ is trivial, illustrating the complete breakdown of the Galois correspondence in the purely inseparable case.

Understanding inseparable extensions in general requires the machinery of $p$-bases and Hasse–Schmidt derivations, which generalize the role of bases and derivations to the inseparable setting. A$p$-basis of$E$ over$F$ is a set$\{t_i\}$ such that$E = F(t_i^{1/p} : i)$ and this generation is as efficient as possible; in Example 5,$\{s, t\}$ is a$p$-basis for$E/F$. For most of the classical Galois theory developed in the next chapter, we work over perfect fields — chiefly over$\mathbb{Q}$ and finite fields — where separability is automatic and the full Galois correspondence applies without modification.

\begin{remark}
The theory of imperfect fields and inseparable extensions is not merely a technical curiosity: it arises naturally in the geometry of algebraic varieties over fields of characteristic $p$. A smooth variety over a perfect field has separable function field extensions, but the function field of a non-smooth variety (or of a variety over an imperfect field) may be inseparable. The concept of a$p$-basis for a field extension is the algebraic shadow of the sheaf of differentials$\Omega^1_{X/k}$ in algebraic geometry, and the theory of inseparable extensions underlies the study of Frobenius-split varieties and the classification of$p$-groups in modular representation theory.
\end{remark}
