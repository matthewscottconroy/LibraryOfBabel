# Chapter 30 — Normal and Separable Extensions

Not every field extension is a Galois extension, and the distinction matters enormously. A Galois extension is one that is simultaneously normal and separable, and this chapter is devoted to understanding both conditions thoroughly — not as arbitrary technicalities, but as natural and independently motivated properties that together guarantee the perfect correspondence between subgroups and subfields that Chapter 31 establishes. Stripping either condition away breaks the theory in a precise and illuminating way.

Normality captures the idea that an extension is "closed under conjugation." An extension $K/F$ is normal if whenever an irreducible polynomial $f \in F[x]$ has at least one root in $K$, it has all of its roots in $K$. The extension $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ is not normal: the polynomial $x^3 - 2$ has the real root $\sqrt[3]{2}$ in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$, but its other two roots, $\omega\sqrt[3]{2}$ and $\omega^2\sqrt[3]{2}$ where $\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$, are complex and absent. The correct way to fix this deficiency is to form the splitting field — the smallest extension of $F$ over which a given polynomial factors completely into linear factors. Normal extensions are precisely the splitting fields of polynomials, and this equivalence gives them their geometric meaning: a normal extension is one that does not discriminate among conjugate roots.

Separability is the subtler condition, invisible in characteristic zero but decisive in characteristic $p$. A polynomial is separable if it has no repeated roots in its splitting field; equivalently, if it is coprime to its formal derivative $f'$. In characteristic zero, every irreducible polynomial is automatically separable — its derivative is of strictly lower degree and hence coprime to it. In characteristic $p$, irreducible polynomials of the form $x^p - a$ satisfy $f' = 0$ identically, so the formal derivative test breaks down entirely, and a polynomial may factor as a perfect $p$-th power with all roots coinciding. The Frobenius endomorphism $\phi: x \mapsto x^p$ is the central actor here: it is always a ring homomorphism, and its behavior determines which elements are separable and which are not. An extension is separable if every element has a separable minimal polynomial, and a field is perfect — having no inseparable extensions at all — if every element of its algebraic closure is separable over it. All fields of characteristic zero and all finite fields are perfect.

The chapter closes with the Primitive Element Theorem, one of the most satisfying results in the subject: every finite separable extension $K/F$ is a simple extension, $K = F(\alpha)$ for a single element $\alpha$. The proof constructs $\alpha$ explicitly as a linear combination of generators, a surprisingly clean argument once the right lemma about the number of intermediate fields is in hand. The theorem fails for inseparable extensions, and this failure points precisely to the pathological cases where the Galois correspondence does not apply. Understanding why the theorem holds when it does, and fails when it does not, is the best preparation for seeing why separability is not merely a technical assumption in Chapter 31 but a structural necessity.
