# 31.1.1 Galois Extensions: Normal and Separable

Not every field extension carries enough symmetry to support a clean correspondence between subgroups and intermediate fields. The Galois condition is precisely the combination of properties that ensures the symmetry group of an extension is as large as possible — large enough to account for every intermediate field and no more. An algebraic extension $E/F$ is called *Galois* if it is both normal and separable: normal meaning every irreducible polynomial over$F$ that has one root in$E$ splits completely in$E$, and separable meaning that every element of$E$ is a root of a polynomial over$F$ with no repeated roots. Either condition alone is insufficient; the interplay of both is what makes the theory work.

\begin{definition}
A finite algebraic extension $E/F$ is *Galois* if it is both normal and separable. The *Galois group* of$E/F$ is$\mathrm{Gal}(E/F) = \mathrm{Aut}(E/F)$, the group of field automorphisms of$E$ fixing$F$ pointwise.
\end{definition}

For finite extensions, there is a cleaner equivalent formulation: $E/F$ is Galois if and only if$E$ is the splitting field of a separable polynomial over$F$. This equivalence is both practically useful and conceptually illuminating. It says that Galois extensions are exactly those obtained by adjoining all the roots of some well-behaved polynomial. The separability condition rules out the pathologies of characteristic-$p$ extensions where inseparable polynomials can cause the automorphism group to collapse. The splitting field characterization also makes it easy to produce Galois extensions: take any separable polynomial$f \in F[x]$, form its splitting field$E$, and$E/F$ is automatically Galois.

\begin{theorem}
Let $E/F$ be a finite extension. The following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $E/F$ is Galois (normal and separable).
\item $E$ is the splitting field of a separable polynomial$f \in F[x]$.
\item $|\mathrm{Aut}(E/F)| = [E:F]$.
\item The fixed field $E^{\mathrm{Aut}(E/F)}$ equals$F$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

The key insight is that the Galois condition is exactly what guarantees $|\mathrm{Gal}(E/F)| = [E:F]$. In a non-Galois extension, the automorphism group may be strictly smaller than the degree. For example,$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ has degree$3$ but$|\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})/\mathbb{Q})| = 1$: the only automorphism is the identity, because the other two roots of$x^3 - 2$ lie outside$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}) \subset \mathbb{R}$. With both normality and separability, the automorphism group is as large as it can possibly be, and the fundamental theorem becomes available.

It is instructive to see normality and separability fail independently. A normal but non-separable extension: over $F = \mathbb{F}_p(t)$, the splitting field of$x^p - t$ is$F(t^{1/p})$, which is normal (the unique root$t^{1/p}$ means the polynomial splits completely in the extension) but not separable. A separable but non-normal extension:$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ is separable (characteristic$0$) but not normal ($x^3 - 2$ has a root in$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ but does not split there). In each case, the Galois correspondence breaks down.

**Example 1.** The extension $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ is Galois. It is the splitting field of$x^2 - 2$ (a separable polynomial), and both roots$\pm\sqrt{2}$ lie in$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$. The Galois group is$\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})/\mathbb{Q}) = \{\mathrm{id}, \sigma\}$ where$\sigma: \sqrt{2} \mapsto -\sqrt{2}$, a group of order$2 = [\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}):\mathbb{Q}]$.

**Example 2.** The splitting field of $x^3 - 2$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ is$E = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$,$\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$. This is the splitting field of a separable polynomial, hence Galois. The degree is$[E:\mathbb{Q}] = 6$. The Galois group$\mathrm{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q}) \cong S_3$, the symmetric group on the three roots$\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega^2\sqrt[3]{2}$ of$x^3 - 2$.

**Example 3.** The cyclotomic field $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$,$\zeta_n = e^{2\pi i/n}$, is Galois over$\mathbb{Q}$: it is the splitting field of$x^n - 1$ (whose derivative$nx^{n-1} \neq 0$ in characteristic$0$, so$x^n-1$ is separable). The Galois group is$\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)/\mathbb{Q}) \cong (\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^*$, an abelian group of order$\phi(n)$.

**Example 4.** Every finite field extension $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}/\mathbb{F}_p$ is Galois. It is the splitting field of$x^{p^n} - x$ over$\mathbb{F}_p$, and this polynomial is separable (its derivative is$-1 \neq 0$). The Galois group is$\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{F}_{p^n}/\mathbb{F}_p) \cong \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, generated by the Frobenius$\phi: x \mapsto x^p$.

**Example 5.** The extension $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ is not Galois: it is the splitting field of$x^4 - 2$ only if we include the complex root$i\sqrt[4]{2}$, but$i \notin \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2}) \subset \mathbb{R}$. So$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ is separable but not normal. The Galois closure of$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2})$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ is$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2}, i)$, the full splitting field of$x^4 - 2$, which is a degree-$8$ Galois extension with$\mathrm{Gal} \cong D_4$ (dihedral group of order$8$).

The Galois condition is therefore not an arbitrary technical requirement but the precise algebraic statement that the extension's symmetry is complete. For infinite algebraic extensions $E/F$, the Galois condition is the same (normal and separable), but the Galois group$\mathrm{Gal}(E/F)$ is an infinite group equipped with the Krull topology, and the fundamental theorem must be modified: only closed subgroups correspond to intermediate fields. This connects to profinite group theory and the arithmetic of the absolute Galois group$\mathrm{Gal}(\bar{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q})$.

\begin{remark}
Over fields of characteristic $0$ or over perfect fields, the normality condition alone characterizes Galois extensions (since separability is automatic). Over imperfect fields of characteristic$p$, one must verify both conditions. In algebraic geometry, the Galois condition for function field extensions corresponds to a Galois cover: a finite morphism$f: X \to Y$ of varieties is Galois if the function field extension$k(X)/k(Y)$ is Galois, and the Galois group acts as deck transformations. The geometric language makes visible why normality (all fibers are geometrically complete) and separability (fibers are reduced, no ramification) both contribute to the Galois property.
\end{remark}
