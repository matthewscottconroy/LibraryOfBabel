# 31.1.4 Fixed Fields: $E^H$ for$H \leq \mathrm{Gal}(E/F)$

Given a subgroup $H \leq \mathrm{Gal}(E/F)$, the elements of$E$ that every automorphism in$H$ fixes form a subfield. Formally, the *fixed field* of$H$ is$E^H = \{e \in E : \sigma(e) = e \text{ for all } \sigma \in H\}$. One checks that$E^H$ is closed under addition and multiplication and contains all inverses, so it is indeed a subfield of$E$, and it contains$F$ since every$\sigma \in \mathrm{Gal}(E/F)$ fixes$F$ by definition. The fixed field construction is the map that sends subgroups of$G = \mathrm{Gal}(E/F)$ to intermediate fields of$E/F$, and it is the inverse direction of the Galois correspondence.

\begin{definition}
Let $H$ be a group of automorphisms of a field$E$. The *fixed field* of$H$ is
$$E^H = \{e \in E : \sigma(e) = e \text{ for all } \sigma \in H\}.$$
\end{definition}

The key technical result underlying the entire theory is *Artin's theorem*: if $H$ is a finite group of automorphisms of a field$E$, then$[E:E^H] = |H|$, and moreover$\mathrm{Gal}(E/E^H) = H$ — the automorphisms of$E$ fixing$E^H$ are exactly the elements of$H$, no more and no less. The proof uses the linear independence of characters (Dedekind's theorem): distinct field homomorphisms from a field into any extension are linearly independent over that extension. Applying this with the elements of$H$ as the characters, any$F$-linear relation$\sum_{h \in H} c_h h = 0$ (as functions on$E$) forces all$c_h = 0$. This linear independence is used to show$[E:E^H] \geq |H|$; the other inequality follows by a direct construction.

\begin{theorem}[Artin's Theorem]
Let $H$ be a finite group of field automorphisms of$E$, and let$E^H$ be its fixed field. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $[E : E^H] = |H|$.
\item $E/E^H$ is a Galois extension.
\item $\mathrm{Gal}(E/E^H) = H$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

The consequence that $\mathrm{Gal}(E/E^H) = H$ — not merely containing$H$, but equal to it — is what makes the correspondence invertible. It guarantees that starting with$H$, forming its fixed field$E^H$, and then taking the Galois group of$E$ over$E^H$ recovers$H$ exactly. No information is lost.

The degree formula from Artin's theorem gives a key piece of structural information: for any subgroup $H \leq G = \mathrm{Gal}(E/F)$ and the corresponding fixed field$K = E^H$,
$$[E:K] = |H|, \qquad [K:F] = [G:H],$$
where $[G:H]$ is the index of$H$ in$G$. These equalities connect the lattice of subgroups (with their orders and indices) to the lattice of intermediate fields (with their degrees over$F$ and$E$), and they constitute the quantitative content of the Galois correspondence.

**Example 1.** Let $E = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3})$ and$G = \mathrm{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ with generators$\sigma: \sqrt{2} \mapsto -\sqrt{2}$ (fixing$\sqrt{3}$) and$\tau: \sqrt{3} \mapsto -\sqrt{3}$ (fixing$\sqrt{2}$). For$H = \langle \sigma \rangle = \{\mathrm{id}, \sigma\}$: the fixed field$E^H$ consists of elements$a + b\sqrt{2} + c\sqrt{3} + d\sqrt{6}$ (with$a,b,c,d \in \mathbb{Q}$) satisfying$\sigma(\cdot) = \cdot$. Since$\sigma$ negates$\sqrt{2}$, we need$b = d = 0$, giving$E^H = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3})$. Check:$[E:E^H] = 2 = |H|$. Similarly,$E^{\langle\tau\rangle} = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ and$E^{\langle\sigma\tau\rangle} = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{6})$.

**Example 2.** Let $E = \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_8)$ where$\zeta_8 = e^{2\pi i/8}$, a primitive$8$th root of unity. The Galois group$G = \mathrm{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q}) \cong (\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z})^* = \{1, 3, 5, 7\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. The subgroup$H = \{\sigma_1, \sigma_5\}$ where$\sigma_5: \zeta_8 \mapsto \zeta_8^5$. The fixed field$E^H$ consists of elements fixed by$\sigma_5$. Since$\zeta_8 + \zeta_8^5 = \zeta_8 - \zeta_8 = 0$... let us compute:$\zeta_8 = e^{i\pi/4} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(1+i)$,$\zeta_8^5 = e^{5i\pi/4} = -\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(1+i) = -\zeta_8$. So$\sigma_5(\zeta_8) = -\zeta_8$. Elements fixed by$\sigma_5$ include$\zeta_8 + \zeta_8^5 = 0$ and$\zeta_8 \cdot \zeta_8^5 = \zeta_8^6 = -\zeta_8^2$. The fixed field is$E^H = \mathbb{Q}(i)$ (or$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-1})$): one checks$[E:\mathbb{Q}(i)] = 2 = |H|$.

**Example 3.** For $E = \mathbb{F}_{p^{12}}$ and$G = \mathrm{Gal}(E/\mathbb{F}_p) \cong \mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ generated by the Frobenius$\phi$: the subgroups of$\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ correspond to divisors of$12$. For$H = \langle \phi^4 \rangle$ (subgroup of order$3$, index$4$): the fixed field is$E^H = \mathbb{F}_{p^4}$ (elements fixed by$\phi^4$, i.e., satisfying$x^{p^4} = x$). Check:$[E:E^H] = 3 = |H|$ and$[E^H:\mathbb{F}_p] = 4 = [G:H]$.

**Example 4.** Let $E = \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_7)$ and$G \cong (\mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z})^* \cong \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$. The unique subgroup of order$2$ is$H = \{\sigma_1, \sigma_6\}$ where$\sigma_6: \zeta_7 \mapsto \zeta_7^6 = \zeta_7^{-1}$. The fixed field$E^H$ consists of elements invariant under$\zeta_7 \mapsto \zeta_7^{-1}$, which are the real parts:$E^H = \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_7 + \zeta_7^{-1}) = \mathbb{Q}(2\cos(2\pi/7))$. This is a totally real cubic subfield of$\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_7)$ with$[E^H:\mathbb{Q}] = 3 = [G:H]$.

**Example 5.** For $E = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$ (Galois over$\mathbb{Q}$,$G \cong S_3$): the subgroup$H = A_3 = \{e, (123), (132)\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ (the rotation subgroup of$S_3$, normal in$S_3$). The fixed field$E^H$ is fixed by all cyclic permutations of the three roots$\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega^2\sqrt[3]{2}$. These permutations fix the base field$\mathbb{Q}(\omega)$: the automorphism cycling the cube roots fixes$\omega$ (it is determined by$\sqrt[3]{2} \mapsto \omega\sqrt[3]{2}$,$\omega \mapsto \omega$). So$E^H = \mathbb{Q}(\omega)$. Check:$[E:E^H] = 3 = |H|$ and$[E^H:\mathbb{Q}] = 2 = [G:H] = [S_3 : A_3]$.

\begin{remark}
Artin's theorem is often stated as: "a field $E$ is a Galois extension of$E^H$ for any finite group$H$ of automorphisms of$E$." This is the starting point for Artin's own elegant development of Galois theory, which begins with groups of automorphisms and derives the field theory, rather than beginning with field extensions and constructing the automorphism group. The two approaches are equivalent but philosophically different: Artin's approach makes the group structure primary and the field structure secondary, while the classical approach (via splitting fields and polynomial roots) makes the field structure primary. Both perspectives are valuable.
\end{remark}
