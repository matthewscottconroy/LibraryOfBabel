# 31.2.1 Statement: The Galois Correspondence

The Fundamental Theorem of Galois Theory is the central organizing result of the subject — the theorem that makes Galois theory a theory rather than a collection of facts about field extensions. In its simplest form, it asserts that the internal structure of a Galois extension $E/F$ is completely encoded in its Galois group. Specifically, every intermediate field between$F$ and$E$ corresponds to exactly one subgroup of$G = \mathrm{Gal}(E/F)$, and vice versa.

\begin{theorem}[Fundamental Theorem of Galois Theory]
Let $E/F$ be a finite Galois extension with Galois group$G = \mathrm{Gal}(E/F)$. There is a bijection
$$\Phi: \{\text{intermediate fields } F \subseteq K \subseteq E\} \longleftrightarrow \{\text{subgroups } H \leq G\} :\Psi$$
given by $\Phi(K) = \mathrm{Gal}(E/K)$ and$\Psi(H) = E^H$. These maps are mutually inverse. Moreover:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The correspondence is inclusion-reversing: $K_1 \subseteq K_2 \Leftrightarrow \mathrm{Gal}(E/K_2) \subseteq \mathrm{Gal}(E/K_1)$.
\item Degree equals index: $[E:K] = |\mathrm{Gal}(E/K)|$ and$[K:F] = [G:\mathrm{Gal}(E/K)]$.
\item Normality correspondence: $H \trianglelefteq G$ if and only if$E^H/F$ is Galois, in which case$\mathrm{Gal}(E^H/F) \cong G/H$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

The power of this statement lies in its completeness: it is not merely that the maps exist in both directions, but that they account for every intermediate field and every subgroup, with no gaps or collisions. The structure of the lattice of intermediate fields — which fields sit inside which others, how they intersect and compose — is entirely mirrored in the lattice of subgroups of $G$. The correspondence is inclusion-reversing: larger subgroups correspond to smaller fields (fixed by more automorphisms means fewer elements survive), and smaller subgroups correspond to larger fields.

Questions about extensions, which belong to field theory, become questions about groups. Deciding whether an equation is solvable by radicals becomes deciding whether a group is solvable; classifying intermediate extensions becomes classifying subgroups; understanding which extensions are Galois over $F$ becomes understanding which subgroups are normal in$G$.

**Example 1.** For $E = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3})$ and$G = \{\mathrm{id}, \sigma, \tau, \sigma\tau\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ (where$\sigma: \sqrt{2}\mapsto -\sqrt{2}$ and$\tau: \sqrt{3}\mapsto -\sqrt{3}$), the Galois correspondence gives:

| Subgroup $H$ | Order | Fixed field$E^H$ |$[E^H:\mathbb{Q}]$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\{\mathrm{id}\}$ |$1$ |$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2},\sqrt{3})$ |$4$ |
| $\langle\sigma\rangle$ |$2$ |$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3})$ |$2$ |
| $\langle\tau\rangle$ |$2$ |$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ |$2$ |
| $\langle\sigma\tau\rangle$ |$2$ |$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{6})$ |$2$ |
| $G$ |$4$ |$\mathbb{Q}$ |$1$ |

All five subgroups of $G$ correspond bijectively to the five intermediate fields. Since$G$ is abelian, all subgroups are normal, and all intermediate extensions are Galois over$\mathbb{Q}$.

**Example 2.** For $E = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$ and$G = S_3 \cong \mathrm{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q})$, the six subgroups of$S_3$ are:$\{e\}$ (trivial), three subgroups$\langle (12) \rangle$,$\langle (13) \rangle$,$\langle (23) \rangle$ of order$2$, the normal subgroup$A_3 = \langle (123) \rangle$ of order$3$, and$S_3$ itself. The corresponding intermediate fields are:$E$, and three intermediate fields of degree$3$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ (the fixed fields of the three order-$2$ subgroups, each being a real cubic field$\mathbb{Q}(\omega^k \sqrt[3]{2})$... more precisely$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$,$\mathbb{Q}(\omega\sqrt[3]{2})$,$\mathbb{Q}(\omega^2\sqrt[3]{2})$), and$E^{A_3} = \mathbb{Q}(\omega)$ (degree$2$), and$\mathbb{Q}$ itself. The normal subgroup$A_3 \trianglelefteq S_3$ corresponds to$\mathbb{Q}(\omega)/\mathbb{Q}$, which is Galois with$\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\omega)/\mathbb{Q}) \cong S_3/A_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 3.** For $\mathbb{F}_{p^{12}}/\mathbb{F}_p$ with$G = \mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ generated by the Frobenius$\phi$: the subgroups correspond to divisors$d$ of$12$ ($d = 1,2,3,4,6,12$), and the Galois correspondence gives:

| Divisor $d$ | Subgroup$H = \langle\phi^d\rangle$,$|H| = 12/d$ | Fixed field$E^H = \mathbb{F}_{p^d}$ |
|---|---|---|
| $1$ |$\langle\phi\rangle = G$, order$12$ |$\mathbb{F}_p$ |
| $2$ |$\langle\phi^2\rangle$, order$6$ |$\mathbb{F}_{p^2}$ |
| $3$ |$\langle\phi^3\rangle$, order$4$ |$\mathbb{F}_{p^3}$ |
| $4$ |$\langle\phi^4\rangle$, order$3$ |$\mathbb{F}_{p^4}$ |
| $6$ |$\langle\phi^6\rangle$, order$2$ |$\mathbb{F}_{p^6}$ |
| $12$ |$\{\mathrm{id}\}$, order$1$ |$\mathbb{F}_{p^{12}}$ |

Since $G$ is cyclic (and abelian), all subgroups are normal, and every intermediate field is Galois over$\mathbb{F}_p$.

The statement also includes two further features beyond the mere bijection. The normality correspondence — that $H \trianglelefteq G$ if and only if$E^H/F$ is Galois — connects normal subgroups and Galois sub-extensions, and the quotient group identification$\mathrm{Gal}(E^H/F) \cong G/H$ shows that every quotient of$G$ is realized as a Galois group of some sub-extension. These features complete the theorem from a bijection of sets to a full correspondence of algebraic structures.

\begin{remark}
The Fundamental Theorem is the algebraic analogue of the covering space correspondence in topology: for a connected space $X$ with basepoint, there is a bijection between connected covering spaces of$X$ and subgroups of the fundamental group$\pi_1(X)$. Normal subgroups correspond to regular (Galois) covers, and the deck transformation group of a regular cover is$\pi_1(X)/H$. The Galois correspondence is precisely the algebraic version of this correspondence, with field extensions playing the role of covering spaces and the Galois group playing the role of the fundamental group. This analogy, made precise by Grothendieck's theory of the étale fundamental group, has become one of the organizing principles of modern arithmetic geometry.
\end{remark}
