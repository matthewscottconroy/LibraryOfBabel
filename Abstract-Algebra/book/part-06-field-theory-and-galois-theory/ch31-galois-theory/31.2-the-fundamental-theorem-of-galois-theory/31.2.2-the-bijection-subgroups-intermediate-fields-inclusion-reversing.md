# 31.2.2 The Bijection: Subgroups $\leftrightarrow$ Intermediate Fields (Inclusion-Reversing)

One of the most important structural features of the Galois correspondence is that it reverses the order of inclusion. The lattice of intermediate fields and the lattice of subgroups of $G$ are anti-isomorphic as posets: larger fields correspond to smaller groups, and smaller fields correspond to larger groups. To see why, observe that if$K_1 \subseteq K_2$ are two intermediate fields, then any automorphism fixing$K_2$ certainly fixes$K_1$, so$\mathrm{Gal}(E/K_2) \leq \mathrm{Gal}(E/K_1)$ — fixing a larger field is a stricter condition, yielding a smaller group. Reversing the logic, if$H_1 \leq H_2$ are subgroups, then$E^{H_2} \subseteq E^{H_1}$: more automorphisms impose more constraints, leaving fewer elements fixed.

\begin{proposition}
Under the Galois correspondence, the maps $K \mapsto \mathrm{Gal}(E/K)$ and$H \mapsto E^H$ are order-reversing bijections between the poset of intermediate fields (ordered by inclusion) and the poset of subgroups of$G$ (ordered by inclusion). Specifically:
$$K_1 \subseteq K_2 \iff \mathrm{Gal}(E/K_2) \subseteq \mathrm{Gal}(E/K_1), \qquad H_1 \subseteq H_2 \iff E^{H_2} \subseteq E^{H_1}.$$
\end{proposition}

The inclusion-reversing feature carries degree information as well. For any intermediate field $K$ corresponding to the subgroup$H = \mathrm{Gal}(E/K)$, the Galois correspondence gives:
$$[E:K] = |\mathrm{Gal}(E/K)| = |H|, \qquad [K:F] = [G:H] \quad (\text{the index of } H \text{ in } G).$$
These two equalities together reproduce the Tower Law:
$$[E:F] = [E:K] \cdot [K:F] = |H| \cdot [G:H] = |G|,$$
consistent with $|G| = |\mathrm{Gal}(E/F)| = [E:F]$ from the Order Theorem.

The anti-isomorphism of lattices is more than a coincidence: it is a precise dictionary between two different organizational principles. In the lattice of subgroups of $G$, the operations are intersection ($H_1 \cap H_2$) and join ($\langle H_1, H_2 \rangle$). In the lattice of intermediate fields, the corresponding operations are compositum ($K_1 K_2$, the smallest field containing both) and intersection ($K_1 \cap K_2$). The anti-isomorphism sends:
$$H_1 \cap H_2 \longleftrightarrow K_1 K_2, \qquad \langle H_1, H_2 \rangle \longleftrightarrow K_1 \cap K_2.$$
Thus the intersection of two subgroups corresponds to the compositum of the corresponding fields, and the subgroup generated by two subgroups corresponds to the intersection of the fields.

**Example 1.** For $G = S_3 \cong \mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2},\omega)/\mathbb{Q})$, the lattice of subgroups (ordered by inclusion) runs from$\{e\}$ at the bottom up through the three order-$2$ subgroups$\langle (12)\rangle$,$\langle (13)\rangle$,$\langle (23)\rangle$, then$A_3$ and$S_3$. The anti-isomorphic lattice of intermediate fields runs from$\mathbb{Q}$ at the bottom up through$\mathbb{Q}(\omega)$ (degree$2$), then the three degree-$3$ fields$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$,$\mathbb{Q}(\omega\sqrt[3]{2})$,$\mathbb{Q}(\omega^2\sqrt[3]{2})$, then$E = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2},\omega)$ at the top. The smallest subgroup$\{e\}$ corresponds to the largest field$E$; the largest subgroup$S_3$ corresponds to$\mathbb{Q}$.

The anti-isomorphism between lattices: the three order-$2$ subgroups have join$S_3$, and the corresponding three degree-$3$ fields have intersection$\mathbb{Q}$. The intersection of two order-$2$ subgroups, say$\langle(12)\rangle \cap \langle(13)\rangle = \{e\}$, corresponds to the compositum$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}) \cdot \mathbb{Q}(\omega\sqrt[3]{2}) = E$. One can verify: the subgroup generated by$(12)$ and$(13)$ is$S_3$, corresponding to$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}) \cap \mathbb{Q}(\omega\sqrt[3]{2}) = \mathbb{Q}$.

**Example 2.** For $G = \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_5)/\mathbb{Q})$ (since$(\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z})^* \cong \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$): the subgroups are$\{0\}$ (trivial),$\{0,2\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, and$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$. The intermediate fields are$\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_5)$ (degree$4$),$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$ (the unique quadratic subfield, degree$2$), and$\mathbb{Q}$. The subgroup$\{0,2\}$ of index$2$ corresponds to$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$ of degree$2$: the automorphisms fixing$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$ are$\sigma_1$ (identity) and$\sigma_4$ (since$\sigma_4(\zeta_5) = \zeta_5^4 = \overline{\zeta_5}$ and$\sigma_4(\sqrt{5}) = \sqrt{5}$).

**Example 3.** For $\mathbb{F}_{p^{12}}/\mathbb{F}_p$ with$G = \mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$: the subgroup of order$d$ corresponds to the field$\mathbb{F}_{p^{12/d}}$. Anti-isomorphism of lattice operations: the intersection of subgroups of orders$4$ and$6$ in$\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ is the subgroup of order$\gcd(4,6) = 2$, corresponding to the compositum$\mathbb{F}_{p^3} \cdot \mathbb{F}_{p^2} = \mathbb{F}_{p^{\mathrm{lcm}(3,2)}} = \mathbb{F}_{p^6}$. The subgroup generated by subgroups of orders$4$ and$6$ has order$\mathrm{lcm}(4,6) = 12$, corresponding to the intersection$\mathbb{F}_{p^3} \cap \mathbb{F}_{p^2} = \mathbb{F}_{p^{\gcd(3,2)}} = \mathbb{F}_p$.

**Example 4.** For $E/F$ with$[E:F] = pq$,$p \neq q$ prime, and$G = \mathrm{Gal}(E/F) \cong \mathbb{Z}/pq\mathbb{Z}$ (cyclic): the subgroups of$\mathbb{Z}/pq\mathbb{Z}$ are$\{0\}$,$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ (order$p$, index$q$),$\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}$ (order$q$, index$p$), and$\mathbb{Z}/pq\mathbb{Z}$. The Galois correspondence gives: the subgroup of order$p$ (index$q$) corresponds to the unique intermediate field of degree$q$ over$F$, and the subgroup of order$q$ (index$p$) corresponds to the unique intermediate field of degree$p$ over$F$. The lattice is a diamond shape with$F$ at bottom and$E$ at top.

**Example 5.** For $G = (\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^3 \cong \mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2},\sqrt{3},\sqrt{5})/\mathbb{Q})$: the group has$2^4 - 1 = 15$ proper nontrivial subgroups (subgroups of orders$1, 2, 4, 8$ other than the full group). The anti-isomorphism sends: the seven subgroups of order$2$ to the seven intermediate fields of degree$4$; the seven subgroups of order$4$ to the seven intermediate fields of degree$2$; and the unique subgroup of order$8$ (the full group$G$) to$\mathbb{Q}$. One can list the seven degree-$2$ intermediate fields:$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$,$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3})$,$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$,$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{6})$,$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{10})$,$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{15})$,$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{30})$ — exactly$7 = 2^3 - 1$ fields corresponding to the non-identity elements of$(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^3$.

\begin{remark}
The anti-isomorphism of lattices is the algebraic analogue of Galois duality for covers in topology. In the covering space theory of a connected topological space $X$, larger covering spaces correspond to smaller subgroups of$\pi_1(X)$ (a cover with smaller deck transformation group covers "more" of$X$). The Galois correspondence between field extensions and subgroups is precisely this duality, realized algebraically. Understanding the full lattice structure — not just the individual correspondences but the meets, joins, and their interactions — is central to applications such as computing the Galois group of a composite field and understanding ramification in algebraic number theory.
\end{remark}
