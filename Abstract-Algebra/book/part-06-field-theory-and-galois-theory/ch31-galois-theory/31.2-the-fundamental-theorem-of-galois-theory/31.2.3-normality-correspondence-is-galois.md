# 31.2.3 Normality Correspondence: $H \trianglelefteq G \Leftrightarrow E^H/F$ Is Galois

The Galois correspondence does not merely match up subgroups with intermediate fields; it also translates structural properties of the subgroups into field-theoretic properties of the extensions. The most important instance of this translation concerns normality. A subgroup $H \leq G$ is normal in$G$ if and only if the corresponding fixed field$E^H$ is a Galois extension of$F$ — not just an extension lying inside the Galois extension$E/F$, but Galois over the base field$F$ itself. This parallel between normal subgroups and Galois sub-extensions is one of the deepest features of the correspondence.

\begin{theorem}[Normality Correspondence]
Let $E/F$ be a finite Galois extension with Galois group$G$, and let$H \leq G$ be a subgroup with corresponding fixed field$K = E^H$. Then$H \trianglelefteq G$ if and only if$K/F$ is a Galois extension.
\end{theorem}

To understand why, consider what normality of $H$ in$G$ means: the subgroup$H$ is closed under conjugation, so$\sigma H \sigma^{-1} = H$ for all$\sigma \in G$. Now suppose$e \in E^H$, so$\tau(e) = e$ for all$\tau \in H$. Consider$\sigma(e) \in E$ for any$\sigma \in G$. For any$\tau \in H$:$\tau(\sigma(e)) = \sigma(\sigma^{-1}\tau\sigma(e))$. Since$H \trianglelefteq G$, the element$\sigma^{-1}\tau\sigma \in H$, so$\sigma^{-1}\tau\sigma(e) = e$, giving$\tau(\sigma(e)) = \sigma(e)$. Thus$\sigma(e) \in E^H$. This shows that$\sigma$ maps$E^H$ into itself for every$\sigma \in G$: the field$E^H$ is stable (invariant) under the action of the entire group$G$.

The stability of $E^H$ under$G$ is precisely what is needed for$E^H/F$ to be a Galois extension:
- *Normal*: For any irreducible $f \in F[x]$ with a root in$E^H$, all roots of$f$ in$\bar{F}$ lie in$E$ (since$E/F$ is Galois), and by stability of$E^H$ under$G$, these roots lie in$E^H$. So$f$ splits completely in$E^H$.
- *Separable*: Every element of $E^H \subseteq E$ is separable over$F$, inherited from$E/F$.

Conversely, if $E^H/F$ is normal, then for any$\sigma \in G$, the restriction$\sigma|_{E^H}$ is an$F$-embedding of$E^H$ into$\bar{F}$. Normality forces$\sigma(E^H) \subseteq E^H$ for all$\sigma$, which implies$\sigma H \sigma^{-1} \subseteq H$ for all$\sigma$, and since$H$ is finite,$\sigma H \sigma^{-1} = H$, so$H \trianglelefteq G$.

**Example 1.** For $E = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2},\omega)$ with$G = S_3$: the subgroup$A_3 = \{1, (123), (132)\}$ is the unique normal subgroup of index$2$ in$S_3$. The fixed field$E^{A_3}$ consists of elements fixed by all cyclic permutations of the three roots of$x^3 - 2$. One finds$E^{A_3} = \mathbb{Q}(\omega)$ (since the cyclic automorphisms permute the cube roots but fix$\omega$). Indeed,$\mathbb{Q}(\omega)/\mathbb{Q}$ is Galois — it is the splitting field of$x^2 + x + 1$, a separable polynomial. The normality correspondence is confirmed:$A_3 \trianglelefteq S_3 \Leftrightarrow \mathbb{Q}(\omega)/\mathbb{Q}$ is Galois.

**Example 2.** The order-$2$ subgroups of$S_3$, say$H = \{1, \tau\}$ where$\tau: \sqrt[3]{2} \mapsto \sqrt[3]{2}$,$\omega \mapsto \omega^2$: these are not normal in$S_3$ (they are not closed under conjugation:$(123)(12)(132) = (23) \notin \{1,(12)\}$). The corresponding fixed field is$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ — a degree-$3$ extension of$\mathbb{Q}$ that is NOT Galois: the polynomial$x^3 - 2$ has a root$\sqrt[3]{2}$ in$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ but does not split there (the other roots are complex). The non-normality of$H$ in$G$ is reflected in the non-Galois nature of the fixed field extension over$\mathbb{Q}$.

**Example 3.** For $G = (\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^2$ (abelian), every subgroup is normal. Correspondingly, for$E = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2},\sqrt{3})/\mathbb{Q}$, every intermediate field —$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$,$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3})$,$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{6})$ — is Galois over$\mathbb{Q}$. This illustrates the general principle: an abelian Galois group implies that every intermediate extension is Galois over the base field.

**Example 4.** For $G = D_4 \cong \mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2},i)/\mathbb{Q})$ (dihedral group of order$8$): the center$Z(D_4) = \{e, r^2\}$ (where$r$ is a rotation of order$4$) is a normal subgroup. The fixed field$E^{Z(D_4)}$ is a degree-$4$ Galois extension of$\mathbb{Q}$ with Galois group$D_4/Z(D_4) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ — in fact,$E^{Z(D_4)} = \mathbb{Q}(i, \sqrt{2})$, which is Galois over$\mathbb{Q}$ with the Klein four-group as Galois group. By contrast, the non-normal subgroup$\langle r \rangle$ (cyclic of order$4$, index$2$)... actually$\langle r \rangle$ is normal in$D_4$ since it has index$2$. The non-normal subgroups of$D_4$ are the reflections, corresponding to non-Galois degree-$4$ sub-extensions like$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2})$.

**Example 5.** For $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}/\mathbb{F}_p$ with$G = \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ (cyclic, hence abelian): every subgroup is normal, and every intermediate field$\mathbb{F}_{p^d}$ (for$d \mid n$) is Galois over$\mathbb{F}_p$. This is expected: in a cyclic group, all subgroups are characteristic (invariant under all automorphisms), hence normal. The Galois group$\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{F}_{p^d}/\mathbb{F}_p) \cong \mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}$ is the quotient of$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ by the subgroup corresponding to$\mathbb{F}_{p^d}$, consistent with$\mathrm{Gal}(E^H/F) \cong G/H$.

\begin{remark}
The normality correspondence has a beautiful reformulation: the intermediate fields that are Galois over $F$ are precisely those that are "invariant" under the$G$-action on the lattice of intermediate fields, where$\sigma \in G$ acts on an intermediate field$K$ by$\sigma \cdot K = \sigma(K)$. A field$K$ is Galois over$F$ if and only if$\sigma(K) = K$ for all$\sigma \in G$, which is exactly the stability condition. This perspective makes the normality correspondence a theorem about group actions: the fixed fields of normal subgroups are exactly the$G$-stable intermediate fields.
\end{remark}
