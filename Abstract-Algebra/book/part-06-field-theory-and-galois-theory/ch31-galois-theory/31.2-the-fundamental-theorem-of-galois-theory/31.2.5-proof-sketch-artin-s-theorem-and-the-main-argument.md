# 31.2.5 Proof Sketch: Artin's Theorem and the Main Argument

The proof of the Fundamental Theorem of Galois Theory can be decomposed into two distinct tasks: establishing the degree formula that makes the maps well-behaved, and verifying that the maps $K \mapsto \mathrm{Gal}(E/K)$ and$H \mapsto E^H$ are mutually inverse bijections. The technical engine for both is Artin's theorem (§31.1.4): if$H$ is a finite group of automorphisms of a field$E$, then$[E:E^H] = |H|$ and$\mathrm{Gal}(E/E^H) = H$.

\begin{theorem}[Fundamental Theorem — Full Statement]
Let $E/F$ be a finite Galois extension with Galois group$G$. The maps$\Phi: K \mapsto \mathrm{Gal}(E/K)$ and$\Psi: H \mapsto E^H$ are mutually inverse bijections between intermediate fields and subgroups of$G$. Furthermore,$[E:K] = |\Phi(K)|$,$[K:F] = [G:\Phi(K)]$, and$H \trianglelefteq G \Leftrightarrow \Psi(H)/F$ is Galois.
\end{theorem}

**Step 1: The degree formula.** Let $G = \mathrm{Gal}(E/F)$ and$H \leq G$ any subgroup, with fixed field$K = E^H$. Artin's theorem gives$[E:K] = |H|$ immediately. Since$[E:F] = |G|$ (by the Order Theorem), the Tower Law yields:
$$[K:F] = \frac{[E:F]}{[E:K]} = \frac{|G|}{|H|} = [G:H].$$
Thus the degree of the sub-extension $K/F$ equals the index of the corresponding subgroup. This is the quantitative heart of the correspondence: every degree-$d$ subextension corresponds to a subgroup of index$d$.

**Step 2: Injectivity of $H \mapsto E^H$.** Two distinct subgroups$H_1 \neq H_2$ have distinct fixed fields. For if$E^{H_1} = E^{H_2}$, then by Artin's theorem$H_i = \mathrm{Gal}(E/E^{H_i})$, so$H_1 = \mathrm{Gal}(E/E^{H_1}) = \mathrm{Gal}(E/E^{H_2}) = H_2$, a contradiction.

**Step 3: Composition $H \mapsto E^H \mapsto \mathrm{Gal}(E/E^H)$ is the identity.** This is exactly the content of Artin's theorem:$\mathrm{Gal}(E/E^H) = H$.

**Step 4: Composition $K \mapsto \mathrm{Gal}(E/K) \mapsto E^{\mathrm{Gal}(E/K)}$ is the identity.** The extension$E/K$ is Galois (it is the splitting field over$K$ of any separable polynomial over$F$ that splits in$E$, and$K \subseteq E$ means$E$ remains a splitting field over$K$). By the Order Theorem applied to$E/K$:$|\mathrm{Gal}(E/K)| = [E:K]$. So$[E:E^{\mathrm{Gal}(E/K)}] = |\mathrm{Gal}(E/K)| = [E:K]$ by Artin's theorem. Since$K \subseteq E^{\mathrm{Gal}(E/K)}$ (every element of$K$ is fixed by automorphisms that fix$K$) and$[E:E^{\mathrm{Gal}(E/K)}] = [E:K]$, the Tower Law forces$[E^{\mathrm{Gal}(E/K)}:K] = 1$, hence$E^{\mathrm{Gal}(E/K)} = K$.

Steps 3 and 4 together show the two maps are mutually inverse bijections. The bijection is inclusion-reversing by the degree formula (Step 1), and the normality correspondence follows from §31.2.3.

**Proof of Artin's Theorem.** The proof proceeds in two parts.

*Upper bound.* For any $\alpha \in E$, consider the polynomial$f_\alpha(x) = \prod_{\sigma \in H}(x - \sigma(\alpha)) \in E[x]$. The coefficients of$f_\alpha$ are elementary symmetric polynomials in$\{\sigma(\alpha) : \sigma \in H\}$, and since$H$ acts on itself by right multiplication (permuting the elements of$H$), these symmetric functions are fixed by every element of$H$, hence lie in$E^H$. So$\alpha$ is algebraic over$E^H$ of degree at most$|H|$ (the degree of$f_\alpha$). This shows$E$ is algebraic over$E^H$ with each element of degree at most$|H|$, giving$[E:E^H] \leq |H|$.

*Lower bound.* Dedekind's theorem on linear independence of characters: distinct field homomorphisms $\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_m: E \to \Omega$ (into any field$\Omega$) are linearly independent over$\Omega$. In particular, the$|H|$ elements of$H$, viewed as homomorphisms$E \to E$, are linearly independent over$E$. If$[E:E^H] = n < |H|$, one can find a nontrivial$E^H$-linear combination of the$\sigma \in H$ that vanishes on all of$E$, contradicting independence. Hence$[E:E^H] \geq |H|$, and combining with the upper bound:$[E:E^H] = |H|$.

**Example 1.** $E = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3})$,$H = \{1, \sigma\}$ with$\sigma: \sqrt{2} \mapsto -\sqrt{2}$,$\sqrt{3} \mapsto \sqrt{3}$. Artin's theorem gives$[E:E^H] = 2$, and$E^H = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3})$. Verify:$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2},\sqrt{3}):\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3})] = 2 = |H|$. The composition$H \mapsto E^H \mapsto \mathrm{Gal}(E/E^H) = \mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2},\sqrt{3})/\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3})) = \{1,\sigma\} = H$ recovers$H$ exactly.

**Example 2.** $E = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$,$K = \mathbb{Q}(\omega)$. Verify Step 4:$\mathrm{Gal}(E/K) = A_3$ (the rotations fixing$\omega$). Then$E^{A_3}$ is the fixed field of$A_3$, which we claimed is$\mathbb{Q}(\omega)$. Check:$[E:E^{A_3}] = |A_3| = 3$ and$[E:\mathbb{Q}(\omega)] = 3$, consistent. The composition$K = \mathbb{Q}(\omega) \mapsto A_3 \mapsto E^{A_3} = \mathbb{Q}(\omega) = K$ recovers$K$.

**Example 3.** Upper bound in Artin's theorem: for $E = \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_5)$ and$H = \mathrm{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$, the polynomial$f_{\zeta_5}(x) = (x-\zeta_5)(x-\zeta_5^2)(x-\zeta_5^3)(x-\zeta_5^4) = \Phi_5(x) = x^4+x^3+x^2+x+1 \in \mathbb{Q}[x] = (E^H)[x]$. This polynomial has coefficients in$\mathbb{Q} = E^H$ and$\zeta_5$ satisfies it: the degree of$\zeta_5$ over$E^H$ is exactly$4 = |H|$, achieving the upper bound.

**Example 4.** Linear independence of characters: for $H = \{1, \sigma\}$ acting on$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$, the claim is that$1$ and$\sigma$ are linearly independent over$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$. Indeed, if$c_1 \cdot \mathrm{id} + c_2 \cdot \sigma = 0$ (as functions on$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$), then evaluating at$\sqrt{2}$:$c_1 \sqrt{2} + c_2(-\sqrt{2}) = 0$, i.e.,$(c_1 - c_2)\sqrt{2} = 0$, giving$c_1 = c_2$. But evaluating at$1$:$c_1 + c_2 = 0$, so$c_1 = -c_2$. Together$c_1 = c_2 = 0$. This confirms linear independence.

**Example 5.** For $E = \mathbb{F}_{p^{12}}$ and$H = \langle \phi^4 \rangle$ (Frobenius iterated$4$ times, subgroup of order$3$): Artin gives$[E:E^H] = 3$. The fixed field$E^H$ consists of$x \in E$ with$\phi^4(x) = x$, i.e.,$x^{p^4} = x$, which is$\mathbb{F}_{p^4}$. Check:$[\mathbb{F}_{p^{12}}:\mathbb{F}_{p^4}] = 3 = |H|$. The degree formula$[E^H:\mathbb{F}_p] = [G:H] = 12/3 = 4$ gives$[\mathbb{F}_{p^4}:\mathbb{F}_p] = 4$. All consistent.

\begin{remark}
The proof strategy — first establishing Artin's theorem for groups of automorphisms, then deriving everything else — is Artin's own approach, which he developed in the 1940s. It has the advantage of being self-contained and clean: the Galois correspondence follows from a single theorem about the relationship between a finite automorphism group and the degree of the extension over its fixed field. The earlier classical approach, going back to Galois and later systematized by Weber, built from the polynomial side (splitting fields, roots, discriminants), and while equivalent, is less transparent. Artin's formulation is now standard and has the further advantage of generalizing cleanly to infinite Galois extensions via the Krull topology.
\end{remark}
