# 31.2 The Fundamental Theorem of Galois Theory

The fundamental theorem of Galois theory establishes a perfect duality between the subgroup lattice of $\text{Gal}(K/F)$ and the lattice of intermediate fields between $F$ and $K$. Precisely: there is an inclusion-reversing bijection $H \mapsto K^H$ from subgroups of $\text{Gal}(K/F)$ to intermediate fields $F \subseteq E \subseteq K$, with inverse $E \mapsto \text{Gal}(K/E)$. Larger subgroups correspond to smaller intermediate fields, and vice versa: the whole group $\text{Gal}(K/F)$ corresponds to the smallest intermediate field $F$, while the trivial subgroup $\{e\}$ corresponds to the largest, $K$ itself. This anti-isomorphism of lattices is the theorem's central assertion.

The proof has two parts. The first part, following from Artin's theorem in Section 31.1, shows that the two maps are inverses of each other: starting with $H$, passing to $K^H$, and then taking $\text{Gal}(K/K^H)$ returns $H$; starting with an intermediate field $E$, passing to $\text{Gal}(K/E)$, and then taking the fixed field returns $E$. Both directions require careful dimensional bookkeeping using the Tower Law. The second part identifies the additional structure: $E/F$ is a Galois extension if and only if $\text{Gal}(K/E)$ is a normal subgroup of $\text{Gal}(K/F)$, in which case $\text{Gal}(E/F) \cong \text{Gal}(K/F)/\text{Gal}(K/E)$. This is the isomorphism theorem for Galois groups, and it converts questions about subextensions into group quotient calculations.

The theorem's power comes from the structural vocabulary it imports. Transitive group actions, normal subgroups, solvable groups, abelian groups — all of these group-theoretic concepts now correspond to field-theoretic ones. Cyclic Galois groups correspond to the simplest possible Galois extensions; abelian Galois groups characterize abelian extensions, which are central to class field theory. The lattice of subgroups of a finite group can in principle be written down explicitly, so the fundamental theorem reduces the classification of intermediate fields — a priori a problem about infinite algebraic objects — to a finite combinatorial problem. The worked examples in this section, including the full correspondence for $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2},\sqrt{3})/\mathbb{Q}$ and for cyclotomic extensions, make the theorem's reach concrete before Chapter 32 applies it to classical problems.
