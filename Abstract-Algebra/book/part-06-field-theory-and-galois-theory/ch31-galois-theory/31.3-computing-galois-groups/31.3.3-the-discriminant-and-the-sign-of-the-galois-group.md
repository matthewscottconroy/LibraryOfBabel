# 31.3.3 The Discriminant and the Sign of the Galois Group

When the Galois group $\mathrm{Gal}(f) \leq S_n$ is viewed as a permutation group on the roots$\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n$ of a polynomial$f$, a natural question is whether the permutations are even or odd — whether the group is contained in the alternating group$A_n$. The discriminant provides a definitive algebraic answer to this question directly from the coefficients of$f$, without needing to know the roots explicitly.

\begin{definition}
Let $f \in F[x]$ be a separable polynomial with roots$\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n$ in$\bar{F}$. Define$\delta = \prod_{i < j}(\alpha_i - \alpha_j)$. The *discriminant* of$f$ is$\Delta(f) = \delta^2 = \prod_{i < j}(\alpha_i - \alpha_j)^2$.
\end{definition}

The discriminant $\Delta(f)$ always lies in the base field$F$: it is a symmetric function of the roots (any permutation of the roots changes an even number of signs in$\delta$, and the square$\delta^2$ absorbs all sign changes), hence expressible in terms of the coefficients of$f$ via Newton's identities. However,$\delta$ itself may not lie in$F$ — it lies in$F$ if and only if$\Delta(f)$ is a perfect square in$F$.

\begin{theorem}[Discriminant Test]
Let $f \in F[x]$ be separable and irreducible, with Galois group$G = \mathrm{Gal}(f/F) \leq S_n$. Then:
$$G \subseteq A_n \iff \delta \in F \iff \Delta(f) \text{ is a perfect square in } F.$$
\end{theorem}

*Proof sketch.* Any $\sigma \in S_n$ acts on$\delta$ by$\sigma(\delta) = \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) \cdot \delta$ (a transposition negates exactly one factor$\alpha_i - \alpha_j$ and hence negates$\delta$; the sign function is the unique homomorphism$S_n \to \{\pm 1\}$). So$\sigma(\delta) = \delta$ iff$\sigma$ is even. Thus$G \subseteq A_n$ iff every$\sigma \in G$ fixes$\delta$ iff$\delta$ is fixed by the full Galois group iff$\delta \in F$ (since$F$ is the fixed field of$G$) iff$\delta = \pm\sqrt{\Delta(f)} \in F$ iff$\Delta(f)$ is a square.$\square$

For irreducible cubics $f = x^3 + px + q$ over$\mathbb{Q}$: the discriminant is$\Delta = -4p^3 - 27q^2$, and the Galois group is$A_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ iff$\Delta$ is a perfect square in$\mathbb{Q}$, and$S_3$ otherwise. The discriminant also has a geometric interpretation over$\mathbb{R}$:$\Delta > 0$ means three distinct real roots;$\Delta < 0$ means one real and two complex conjugate roots;$\Delta = 0$ means a repeated root.

**Example 1.** $f(x) = x^3 - 3x + 1$ over$\mathbb{Q}$, a depressed cubic with$p = -3$,$q = 1$. Discriminant:$\Delta = -4(-3)^3 - 27(1)^2 = 108 - 27 = 81 = 9^2$. This is a perfect square in$\mathbb{Q}$, so$\mathrm{Gal}(f) \subseteq A_3$. Since$f$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$ (no rational roots:$f(0) = 1, f(1) = -1, f(-1) = 3, f(3) = 19$, and by the rational root theorem no rational root exists) and$\mathrm{Gal}(f)$ is transitive,$|\mathrm{Gal}(f)| \geq 3$. The only transitive subgroup of$S_3$ inside$A_3$ is$A_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$. So$\mathrm{Gal}(x^3 - 3x + 1/\mathbb{Q}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 2.** $f(x) = x^3 - 2$ over$\mathbb{Q}$, with$p = 0$,$q = -2$. Discriminant:$\Delta = -4(0)^3 - 27(-2)^2 = -108$. Since$\Delta = -108 < 0$ is not a perfect square in$\mathbb{Q}$ (in particular,$\sqrt{-108} \notin \mathbb{Q}$),$\mathrm{Gal}(f) \not\subseteq A_3$. Since$f$ is irreducible and the Galois group is transitive of order$6$ in$S_3$, we conclude$\mathrm{Gal}(x^3-2/\mathbb{Q}) \cong S_3$. Note:$\Delta = -108$ confirms one real root ($\sqrt[3]{2}$) and two complex roots ($\omega\sqrt[3]{2}$,$\omega^2\sqrt[3]{2}$), geometrically consistent with$\Delta < 0$.

**Example 3.** $f(x) = x^3 + 3x + 1$ over$\mathbb{Q}$, with$p = 3$,$q = 1$. Discriminant:$\Delta = -4(27) - 27 = -108 - 27 = -135$. Not a perfect square, so$\mathrm{Gal}(f) \cong S_3$. Alternatively,$f$ has one real root (discriminant negative), and the Galois group is$S_3$.

**Example 4.** $f(x) = x^3 - 7$ over$\mathbb{Q}$, with$p = 0$,$q = -7$. Discriminant:$\Delta = -27 \cdot 49 = -1323$. Not a square (negative), so$\mathrm{Gal}(f) \cong S_3$. This is consistent with the splitting field$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{7}, \omega)$ having degree$6$ over$\mathbb{Q}$.

**Example 5.** For an irreducible quartic $f = x^4 + bx^2 + c$ (a biquadratic), the discriminant is$\Delta = 16c(b^2 - 4c)^2$. If$c$ and$b^2 - 4c$ are both positive,$\Delta > 0$ and is a product of squares times$16c$:$\Delta$ is a perfect square iff$c$ is a perfect square in$F$. For$f = x^4 - 5$:$b = 0$,$c = -5$,$\Delta = 16(-5)(0-4(-5))^2 = -80 \cdot 400 = -32000 = -2^7 \cdot 5^3$. Not a perfect square (negative, and the odd power of$5$ prevents a perfect square). So$\mathrm{Gal}(f) \not\subseteq A_4$. Combined with the order-$8$ structure from §31.3.1 ($\mathrm{Gal}(f) \cong D_4$, which is not a subgroup of$A_4$ since$D_4$ contains reflections, which are odd permutations), this is consistent.

\begin{remark}
For polynomials of degree $n \geq 5$, the discriminant alone does not determine the Galois group — it only tells whether the group is in$A_n$ or not. More refined tools are needed: for cubics, the discriminant is enough; for quartics, one needs the resolvent cubic (§31.3.4); for quintics and higher degrees, one uses a combination of the discriminant, Dedekind's theorem (factorization patterns mod$p$ give the cycle type of Frobenius), and more specific invariants. The discriminant remains a fundamental first step: it is easily computable from the coefficients and immediately rules out half the transitive subgroups of$S_n$ (those not in$A_n$).
\end{remark}
