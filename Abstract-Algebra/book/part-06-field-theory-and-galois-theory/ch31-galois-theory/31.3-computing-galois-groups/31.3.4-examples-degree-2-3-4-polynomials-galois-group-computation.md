# 31.3.4 Examples: Degree 2, 3, 4 Polynomials; Galois Group Computation

Working through low-degree examples is the best way to see the general theory become concrete. The tools developed in this section — transitivity of the action on roots, the discriminant, and degree constraints — combine to determine the Galois group in each case. In each case, the Galois group is a transitive subgroup of $S_n$ where$n = \deg f$, and the question is which one.

**Degree 2.** For a separable irreducible quadratic $f \in F[x]$, the splitting field$E$ has degree$2$ over$F$. The only transitive subgroup of$S_2$ is$S_2 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ itself. There is no ambiguity:$\mathrm{Gal}(f) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ always, generated by the automorphism swapping the two roots.

**Degree 3.** For an irreducible cubic $f$, the Galois group$\mathrm{Gal}(f)$ is a transitive subgroup of$S_3$. The transitive subgroups of$S_3$ are$A_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ and$S_3$ itself. The discriminant resolves the ambiguity:$\mathrm{Gal}(f) \cong A_3$ if and only if$\Delta(f)$ is a perfect square in$F$ (§31.3.3).

**Degree 4.** The degree-4 case is richer. The transitive subgroups of $S_4$ (up to conjugacy) are five:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $S_4$ (order$24$): the full symmetric group.
\item $A_4$ (order$12$): even permutations.
\item $D_4$ (order$8$): dihedral group (symmetries of a square).
\item $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ (order$4$): cyclic group.
\item $V_4 = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ (order$4$): Klein four-group (embedded in$A_4$).
\end{enumerate}

The principal additional tool is the *resolvent cubic*. For a quartic $f(x) = x^4 + bx^2 + cx + d$ (depressed form, leading coefficient$1$), the resolvent cubic is$g(y) = y^3 - by^2 - 4dy + (4bd - c^2)$. Its roots are the three expressions$\alpha_1\alpha_2 + \alpha_3\alpha_4$,$\alpha_1\alpha_3 + \alpha_2\alpha_4$,$\alpha_1\alpha_4 + \alpha_2\alpha_3$ (pairings of the four roots of$f$). These lie in the splitting field of$f$ but may or may not lie in$F$. The Galois group of$f$ is determined by:

| $\mathrm{Gal}(g/F)$ |$\Delta(f)$ a square in$F$? |$\mathrm{Gal}(f/F)$ |
|---|---|---|
| $S_3$ | No |$S_4$ |
| $S_3$ | Yes |$A_4$ |
| $\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ | No |$D_4$ |
| $\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ | Yes |$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ |
| $\{1\}$ | (either) |$V_4$ |

**Example 1.** $f(x) = x^3 - 2$ over$\mathbb{Q}$. Irreducible (no rational roots). Discriminant:$\Delta = -4(0)^3 - 27(-2)^2 = -108$, not a perfect square (negative). Therefore$\mathrm{Gal}(x^3 - 2/\mathbb{Q}) \cong S_3$. The splitting field is$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$ of degree$6$, with Galois group$S_3$ generated by the$3$-cycle$\sigma: \sqrt[3]{2} \mapsto \omega\sqrt[3]{2}$ and the transposition$\tau: \omega \mapsto \omega^2$.

**Example 2.** $f(x) = x^3 - 3x + 1$ over$\mathbb{Q}$. Irreducible (no rational roots). Discriminant:$\Delta = -4(-3)^3 - 27(1)^2 = 108 - 27 = 81 = 9^2$, a perfect square. Therefore$\mathrm{Gal}(x^3-3x+1/\mathbb{Q}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$. The three roots are$2\cos(2\pi/9)$,$2\cos(8\pi/9)$,$2\cos(14\pi/9)$, all real, and the splitting field$\mathbb{Q}(\cos(2\pi/9))$ has degree$3$ over$\mathbb{Q}$.

**Example 3.** $f(x) = x^4 - 2$ over$\mathbb{Q}$. Irreducible (Eisenstein,$p=2$). Roots:$\sqrt[4]{2}$,$i\sqrt[4]{2}$,$-\sqrt[4]{2}$,$-i\sqrt[4]{2}$. Splitting field:$E = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2}, i)$,$[E:\mathbb{Q}] = 8$. Resolvent cubic: for$f = x^4 + 0 \cdot x^2 + 0 \cdot x + (-2)$ (with$b=0$,$c=0$,$d=-2$):$g(y) = y^3 + 8y = y(y^2 + 8)$. Over$\mathbb{Q}$,$y^2 + 8$ is irreducible (no real roots, irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$), so$g$ has one rational root ($y=0$) and two irrational ones. The Galois group of$g$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ is$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ (not$S_3$), but wait: since$g = y(y^2+8)$ splits into a linear and irreducible quadratic over$\mathbb{Q}$,$\mathrm{Gal}(g/\mathbb{Q}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. However, the degree of$E$ is$8$, and$|\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}| = 2 \neq 8$. A more careful analysis: the resolvent should be computed from the monic depressed form. The Galois group is$D_4$, confirmed by the explicit automorphisms$\sigma: \sqrt[4]{2} \mapsto i\sqrt[4]{2}$ (order$4$) and$\tau: i \mapsto -i$ (order$2$) satisfying$\tau\sigma\tau^{-1} = \sigma^{-1}$.

**Example 4.** $f(x) = x^4 - 5x^2 + 5 \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$. This is the minimal polynomial of$2\cos(2\pi/5)$ (verifiable: the identity$\cos(2\pi/5) = (\sqrt{5}-1)/4$... more precisely,$\zeta_5 + \zeta_5^{-1} = 2\cos(2\pi/5)$ satisfies a degree-$2$ polynomial over$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$, and the minimal polynomial of$2\cos(2\pi/5)$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ has degree$[\mathbb{Q}(\cos(2\pi/5)):\mathbb{Q}] = 2$). Actually$2\cos(2\pi/5)$ has minimal polynomial$x^2 - x - 1$ of degree$2$... let us instead use the correct example:$f(x) = \Phi_5(x) = x^4 + x^3 + x^2 + x + 1$ over$\mathbb{Q}$. The Galois group is$\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_5)/\mathbb{Q}) \cong (\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z})^* \cong \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$, the cyclic case.

**Example 5.** $f(x) = x^4 + 1 \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$. This is$\Phi_8(x)$, the$8$th cyclotomic polynomial. Galois group:$\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_8)/\mathbb{Q}) \cong (\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z})^* \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} = V_4$. The four roots$\zeta_8, \zeta_8^3, \zeta_8^5, \zeta_8^7$ are all primitive$8$th roots of unity. The discriminant of$x^4+1$ is$\Delta = 256 = 16^2$, a perfect square. The resolvent cubic of$x^4+1$ (writing$x^4 + 0 \cdot x^3 + 0 \cdot x^2 + 0 \cdot x + 1$, i.e.,$b=0$,$c=0$,$d=1$):$g(y) = y^3 - 4y = y(y^2-4) = y(y-2)(y+2)$, which splits completely over$\mathbb{Q}$. So$\mathrm{Gal}(g/\mathbb{Q}) = \{1\}$, and from the table:$\mathrm{Gal}(f) \cong V_4$, consistent with$(\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z})^* \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

\begin{remark}
The degree-$4$ analysis via the resolvent cubic is a classical result going back to Ferrari (quartic formula) and Lagrange (resolvent theory). The resolvent cubic construction generalizes: for any degree$n$, there is an analogous construction of the *resolvent polynomial*, whose Galois group captures a quotient of the Galois group of the original polynomial. The systematic theory of resolvents, combined with the classification of transitive subgroups of$S_n$ for small$n$, gives a complete algorithm for computing Galois groups over$\mathbb{Q}$ for degrees$n \leq 6$ or so; for higher degrees, computer algebra systems use probabilistic methods based on the Frobenius density theorem.
\end{remark}
