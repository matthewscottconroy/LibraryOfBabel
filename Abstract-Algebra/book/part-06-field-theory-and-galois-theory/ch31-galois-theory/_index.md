# Chapter 31 — Galois Theory

The fundamental theorem of Galois theory is one of the great achievements of nineteenth-century mathematics — a perfect dictionary between two seemingly unrelated worlds. On one side sits a group: the Galois group $\text{Gal}(K/F)$, consisting of all automorphisms of $K$ that fix $F$ pointwise, with composition as the group operation. On the other side sit the intermediate fields between $F$ and $K$: all fields $E$ with $F \subseteq E \subseteq K$. The theorem asserts that these two collections stand in an exact, inclusion-reversing bijection. Larger subgroups correspond to smaller intermediate fields; normal subgroups correspond to Galois intermediate extensions; quotient groups appear as Galois groups of subextensions. The entirety of Chapter 32 will consist of consequences drawn from this single correspondence.

For the correspondence to work perfectly, the extension $K/F$ must be Galois — that is, both normal and separable, the conditions developed in Chapter 30. The Galois group then has order exactly $[K : F]$, a fact that looks like a coincidence until one appreciates that it is simultaneously a theorem about vector-space dimension (a linear-algebraic fact about the extension) and a theorem about orbit sizes (a group-theoretic fact about the automorphisms). The proof of this key equality, due to Artin, passes through the independence of characters: distinct field homomorphisms into an extension are linearly independent over that extension, a lemma whose proof is a masterpiece of brevity. Artin's approach — starting with a finite group of automorphisms and defining the fixed field — actually reverses the historical development, but it has the virtue of making every step of the argument transparent.

The correspondence itself is described by two maps: given a subgroup $H \leq \text{Gal}(K/F)$, take its fixed field $K^H = \{ x \in K : \sigma(x) = x \text{ for all } \sigma \in H\}$; given an intermediate field $E$, take the subgroup $\text{Gal}(K/E)$ of automorphisms fixing $E$. These maps are inverses of each other, and their inverse relationship is the theorem's content. The normality clause adds the sharpest statement: the intermediate field $E$ is Galois over $F$ if and only if the corresponding subgroup $H = \text{Gal}(K/E)$ is normal in $\text{Gal}(K/F)$, in which case $\text{Gal}(E/F) \cong \text{Gal}(K/F)/H$. This is where abstract group theory — normal subgroups, quotient groups, the isomorphism theorems — flows directly back into field theory.

The chapter also develops the substantial art of computing Galois groups in practice. The Galois group acts faithfully on the roots of any polynomial in $F[x]$ that splits over $K$, embedding $\text{Gal}(K/F)$ into a symmetric group $S_n$. The image must be a transitive subgroup (if the polynomial is irreducible), and the discriminant of the polynomial detects whether the image lies inside the alternating group $A_n$. Cyclotomic fields provide the most structured family of examples: $\text{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)/\mathbb{Q}) \cong (\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^*$, an abelian group whose structure encodes the arithmetic of $n$. With the tools of this chapter in hand, the classical problems of Chapter 32 — solvability by radicals, ruler-and-compass constructions, and the structure of finite fields — become straightforward applications of an elegant and powerful theory.
