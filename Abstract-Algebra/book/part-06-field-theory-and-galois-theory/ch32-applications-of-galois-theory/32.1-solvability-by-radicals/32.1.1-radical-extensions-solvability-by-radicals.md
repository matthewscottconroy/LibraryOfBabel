# 32.1.1 Radical Extensions; Solvability by Radicals

For millennia, mathematicians sought to express the roots of polynomial equations in terms of the coefficients using only arithmetic operations and the extraction of roots. The quadratic formula accomplishes this for degree 2; cubic and quartic formulas, discovered in sixteenth-century Italy, handle degrees 3 and 4. The question for degree 5 and beyond remained open until Galois theory resolved it definitively. To frame the question precisely, one needs the notion of a radical extension.

\begin{definition}
A *radical extension* of a field $F$ is a field extension$R/F$ obtained by a succession of root extractions: a tower
$$F = F_0 \subseteq F_1 \subseteq \cdots \subseteq F_k = R$$
where each $F_{i+1} = F_i(\alpha_i)$ with$\alpha_i^{n_i} \in F_i$ for some positive integer$n_i$. Each step adjoins a single$n_i$th root of an element already present in the previous field. A polynomial$f \in F[x]$ is *solvable by radicals* over$F$ if its splitting field is contained in some radical extension of$F$, or equivalently, if every root of$f$ is expressible by iterated root extractions starting from elements of$F$.
\end{definition}

Solvability by radicals is the precise algebraic formulation of the intuitive notion of "expressibility by a formula using $+, -, \times, \div$ and$\sqrt[n]{\phantom{x}}$." The key subtlety is that one need not require the splitting field itself to be a radical extension — only that it is *contained* in one. A radical extension may be much larger than the splitting field, introducing auxiliary roots of unity needed for the intermediate steps of the formula.

The quadratic formula $\alpha = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$ is the simplest example: starting from the coefficients$a, b, c \in F$, the formula constructs the roots by a single square root extraction, giving the radical tower$F \subseteq F(\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac})$. The cubic formula of Cardano–Ferrari involves a square root and two cube root extractions: for the depressed cubic$x^3 + px + q$, one takes the quantity$D = \frac{q^2}{4} + \frac{p^3}{27}$ (related to the discriminant), forms$u = \sqrt[3]{-q/2 + \sqrt{D}}$ and$v = \sqrt[3]{-q/2 - \sqrt{D}}$ with$uv = -p/3$, and expresses the roots as$u + v$,$\omega u + \omega^2 v$,$\omega^2 u + \omega v$ where$\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$. The resulting radical tower has the form$F \subseteq F(\sqrt{D}) \subseteq F(\sqrt{D}, \omega) \subseteq F(\sqrt{D}, \omega, \sqrt[3]{-q/2 + \sqrt{D}})$, of length 3 or more.

\begin{theorem}[Galois Criterion for Solvability by Radicals]
Let $F$ be a field of characteristic zero and$f \in F[x]$ a separable polynomial with splitting field$E/F$. Then$f$ is solvable by radicals over$F$ if and only if$\mathrm{Gal}(E/F)$ is a solvable group.
\end{theorem}

The proof occupies §32.1.3; the present section establishes the definitions and motivating examples. The algebraic conditions for building the radical tower step by step — that each $F_{i+1}/F_i$ is a cyclic extension (after adjoining appropriate roots of unity) — translate precisely into the condition that the Galois group has a subnormal series with cyclic (hence abelian) factors. This is exactly the definition of a solvable group.

\begin{remark}
The assumption of characteristic zero (or more precisely, that the characteristic does not divide any of the $n_i$) is essential for the theorem as stated. In characteristic$p$, purely inseparable extensions of the form$F(\alpha)$ with$\alpha^p \in F$ cannot be handled by the standard Kummer theory, and one needs the theory of Artin–Schreier extensions (which handle$p$-power roots via equations$x^p - x = a$) to complete the picture. Over characteristic-zero fields, every finite radical extension is separable, so the Galois theory applies directly.
\end{remark}

**Example 1.** The quadratic $x^2 - 2$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ is solvable by radicals: the root$\sqrt{2}$ lies in the radical extension$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$, obtained by a single square root extraction from$2 \in \mathbb{Q}$. The Galois group is$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, which is solvable (the subnormal series is$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \supset \{0\}$ with quotient$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, which is abelian). More generally, any quadratic polynomial over any field of characteristic$\neq 2$ is solvable by radicals, since$\mathrm{Gal}(f) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 2.** The cubic $x^3 - 2$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ is solvable by radicals. The splitting field is$E = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$ where$\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$, with$[E:\mathbb{Q}] = 6$ and$\mathrm{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q}) \cong S_3$. An explicit radical tower is:
$$\mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\omega) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\omega, \sqrt[3]{2}).$$
Each step is a radical extension: $\mathbb{Q}(\omega)/\mathbb{Q}$ adjoins the square root$\omega = (-1 + \sqrt{-3})/2$ (since$\omega$ satisfies$x^2 + x + 1 = 0$ over$\mathbb{Q}$, and$\omega = (-1 \pm \sqrt{-3})/2$). Then$\mathbb{Q}(\omega, \sqrt[3]{2})/\mathbb{Q}(\omega)$ adjoins a cube root. The Galois group$S_3$ is solvable via$S_3 \supset A_3 \supset \{e\}$, with quotients$S_3/A_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ and$A_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$, both abelian.

**Example 3.** The quartic $x^4 - 2$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ is solvable by radicals. An explicit radical tower is:
$$\mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(i) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(i, \sqrt{2}) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(i, \sqrt[4]{2}).$$
The steps are: $\mathbb{Q}(i)/\mathbb{Q}$ adjoins$\sqrt{-1}$ (a square root);$\mathbb{Q}(i, \sqrt{2})/\mathbb{Q}(i)$ adjoins$\sqrt{2}$ (a square root of$2 \in \mathbb{Q}(i)$);$\mathbb{Q}(i, \sqrt[4]{2})/\mathbb{Q}(i, \sqrt{2})$ adjoins$\sqrt[4]{2} = \sqrt{\sqrt{2}}$ (a square root of$\sqrt{2}$). The splitting field of$x^4 - 2$ is$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2}, i)$, which equals the top of the tower. The Galois group is$D_4$ of order$8$, which is solvable:$D_4 \supset \langle r \rangle \supset \langle r^2 \rangle \supset \{e\}$ with quotients$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$,$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$,$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, all abelian.

**Example 4.** The *casus irreducibilis* for cubics: consider $f(x) = x^3 - 3x + 1$ over$\mathbb{Q}$, with discriminant$\Delta = 81 = 9^2 > 0$. The three roots are real:$2\cos(2\pi/9)$,$2\cos(8\pi/9)$,$2\cos(14\pi/9)$. The Galois group is$A_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ (cyclic, hence solvable). The Cardano formula for this polynomial, however, requires computing$\sqrt{D}$ where$D = (q/2)^2 + (p/3)^3 = 1/4 - 1 = -3/4 < 0$. Thus$\sqrt{D} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} i$ is non-real, even though the roots are real. The formula gives$\sqrt[3]{\text{(complex number)}} + \sqrt[3]{\text{(complex conjugate)}} = \text{real}$, so the complex intermediate steps are unavoidable. This phenomenon — real roots expressible only via complex radicals — illustrates that the radical extension containing the splitting field may involve intermediate fields not contained in$\mathbb{R}$.

**Example 5.** A degree-$6$ extension solvable by radicals:$E = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt[3]{3})$ is obtained by the tower$\mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt[3]{3})$, with steps of degrees$2$ and$3$. This is already a radical extension, though not the splitting field of any particular polynomial over$\mathbb{Q}$ unless roots of unity are involved. The Galois closure is$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt[3]{3}, \omega)$ where$\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$ is a primitive cube root of unity. Since$\sqrt{2}$ and$\sqrt[3]{3}$ satisfy polynomials over$\mathbb{Q}$ with solvable Galois groups ($\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ and$S_3$ respectively, or$\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ if$\omega$ is already adjoined), and since products of solvable groups are solvable, the full Galois group is solvable.

The question Galois resolved is: can every polynomial be solved by radicals? For degrees $\leq 4$, the answer is yes — explicit formulas exist, and the Galois groups ($\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, subgroups of$S_3$, subgroups of$S_4$) are all solvable. For degree$5$ and beyond, the answer is no in general, because the symmetric group$S_5$ is not solvable (its only normal subgroup is$A_5$, and$A_5$ is simple and non-abelian). A specific polynomial of degree$5$ with Galois group$S_5$ — such as$x^5 - 4x + 2$ over$\mathbb{Q}$, which is irreducible with two complex roots (hence Galois group containing a transposition and a 5-cycle, generating$S_5$) — is therefore not solvable by radicals.
