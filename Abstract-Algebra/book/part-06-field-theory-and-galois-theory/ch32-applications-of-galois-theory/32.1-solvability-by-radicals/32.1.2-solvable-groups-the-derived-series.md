# 32.1.2 Solvable Groups; the Derived Series

To state the algebraic criterion for solvability by radicals, one needs a notion of "tameness" for groups that captures the idea of being built from abelian pieces. The correct notion is solvability, defined through the derived series, a canonical descending chain of normal subgroups whose successive quotients measure how far the group is from being abelian. A group $G$ is solvable if and only if this chain eventually reaches the trivial group.

\begin{definition}
The *derived series* (or *commutator series*) of a group $G$ is the descending chain
$$G = G^{(0)} \supset G^{(1)} \supset G^{(2)} \supset \cdots$$
where $G^{(k+1)} = [G^{(k)}, G^{(k)}]$ is the *commutator subgroup* of$G^{(k)}$, defined as the subgroup generated by all commutators$[a,b] = aba^{-1}b^{-1}$ with$a, b \in G^{(k)}$. The group$G$ is *solvable* if$G^{(n)} = \{e\}$ for some$n \geq 0$.
\end{definition}

The commutator subgroup $[H,H]$ is always normal in$H$, and$H/[H,H]$ is always abelian (in fact, it is the largest abelian quotient of$H$, called the *abelianization* of$H$). Therefore consecutive quotients$G^{(k)}/G^{(k+1)}$ in the derived series are automatically abelian. A solvable group is one that can be systematically dismantled into finitely many abelian layers.

\begin{theorem}[Equivalent Characterizations of Solvability]
For a finite group $G$, the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(1)] $G$ is solvable (the derived series terminates at$\{e\}$).
\item[(2)] There exists a subnormal series $G = H_0 \supseteq H_1 \supseteq \cdots \supseteq H_m = \{e\}$ with each$H_i \trianglelefteq H_{i-1}$ and each quotient$H_{i-1}/H_i$ abelian.
\item[(3)] There exists a subnormal series with each factor $H_{i-1}/H_i$ cyclic of prime order.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

The derived series is the canonical choice, but any subnormal series with abelian factors witnesses solvability. The finest choice is a composition series (where all factors are simple), and for solvable groups the simple factors are all cyclic of prime order $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ — exactly the abelian simple groups. The closure properties of solvable groups are important: subgroups of solvable groups are solvable; quotients of solvable groups are solvable; extensions of solvable groups by solvable groups are solvable (if$N \trianglelefteq G$ with$N$ and$G/N$ both solvable, then$G$ is solvable).

\begin{proposition}
All abelian groups are solvable. All groups of order $p^k$ (a prime power) are solvable. The symmetric group$S_n$ is solvable for$n \leq 4$ and non-solvable for$n \geq 5$.
\end{proposition}

*Proof sketch.* For abelian $G$:$G^{(1)} = [G,G] = \{e\}$. For$p$-groups: by induction on$|G|$, using that every$p$-group has a non-trivial center, so$G/Z(G)$ is a strictly smaller$p$-group. For$S_4$: the chain$S_4 \supset A_4 \supset V_4 \supset \{e\}$ has abelian factors$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$,$\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$,$V_4$. For$S_5$:$[S_5, S_5] = A_5$, and$A_5$ is simple and non-abelian, so$A_5^{(1)} = A_5$, giving a non-terminating derived series.$\square$

The connection to radical extensions is provided by Kummer theory. If $F$ contains a primitive$n$th root of unity$\zeta_n$ and$a \in F^{\times}$, then$F(\sqrt[n]{a})/F$ is a cyclic Galois extension of degree dividing$n$. This is because all$n$ roots of$x^n - a$ are$\alpha, \zeta_n\alpha, \zeta_n^2\alpha, \ldots, \zeta_n^{n-1}\alpha$ (where$\alpha^n = a$), all lying in$F(\alpha)$, so the extension is Galois; and the Galois group is cyclic, generated by$\alpha \mapsto \zeta_n \alpha$. A radical tower therefore gives rise to a chain of cyclic Galois extensions (after adjoining roots of unity), producing a solvable Galois group. Conversely, any solvable group can be decomposed into cyclic extensions by Kummer theory, yielding a radical tower.

\begin{remark}
The Feit–Thompson theorem (1963) proves that every group of odd order is solvable. This deep result implies that every group of odd order can be realized as a Galois group of a solvable extension, and it played a central role in the classification of finite simple groups. Combined with the criterion for solvability by radicals, it shows that polynomials whose Galois groups have odd order are always solvable by radicals — though finding the explicit radical expression may still be difficult.
\end{remark}

**Example 1.** $G = \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$: abelian, hence solvable. The derived series is$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \supset \{0\}$, with quotient$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ abelian. The commutator subgroup is trivial since the group is abelian:$[a,b] = a + b - a - b = 0$ for all$a, b$ (using additive notation). Any Galois extension with this Galois group is solvable by radicals; for example,$\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_7)/\mathbb{Q}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, and$\zeta_7$ is a radical expression.

**Example 2.** $G = S_3$ of order$6$: derived series$S_3 \supset A_3 \supset \{e\}$. The commutator subgroup$[S_3, S_3] = A_3$: the commutator of the transposition$(12)$ and the 3-cycle$(123)$ is$(12)(123)(12)^{-1}(123)^{-1} = (132)(132) \cdot (123) = (13)(132) \cdot \text{...}$; more directly, the abelianization$S_3/[S_3,S_3]$ must equal$S_3^{\text{ab}}$, the largest abelian quotient. The sign map$\mathrm{sgn}: S_3 \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ has kernel$A_3$, and$S_3/A_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is the largest abelian quotient (since$A_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ has no proper normal subgroups in$S_3$ besides itself and$\{e\}$). Then$A_3^{(1)} = [A_3, A_3] = \{e\}$ since$A_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ is abelian. Quotients:$S_3/A_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ and$A_3/\{e\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$, both abelian.

**Example 3.** $G = S_4$ of order$24$: the derived series is$S_4 \supset A_4 \supset V_4 \supset \{e\}$, where$V_4 = \{e, (12)(34), (13)(24), (14)(23)\}$ is the Klein four-group. Step 1:$[S_4, S_4] = A_4$ (since$S_4/A_4 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is the largest abelian quotient). Step 2:$[A_4, A_4] = V_4$ (since$A_4/V_4 \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ is the largest abelian quotient of$A_4$). Step 3:$[V_4, V_4] = \{e\}$ (since$V_4 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is abelian). The factors are$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$,$\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$,$V_4$, all abelian. So$S_4$ is solvable, confirming that quartic polynomials (with Galois group inside$S_4$) are solvable by radicals.

**Example 4.** $G = A_5$ of order$60$:$A_5$ is simple — its only normal subgroups are$\{e\}$ and$A_5$ itself. Since$A_5$ is non-abelian,$[A_5, A_5] = A_5$. The derived series is$A_5 \supset A_5 \supset A_5 \supset \cdots$, which never reaches$\{e\}$. Therefore$A_5$ is not solvable. Consequently,$S_5$ is also not solvable:$[S_5, S_5] = A_5$, and since$A_5$ is not solvable, neither is$S_5$. Any degree-$5$ polynomial over$\mathbb{Q}$ with Galois group$S_5$ is therefore not solvable by radicals.

**Example 5.** $G = D_4$ of order$8$ (dihedral group, symmetries of a square):$D_4 = \langle r, s \mid r^4 = s^2 = e,\, srs^{-1} = r^{-1} \rangle$. The commutator subgroup:$[r, s] = rsr^{-1}s^{-1}$. Since$srs^{-1} = r^{-1}$, we have$rs = sr^{-1}$, so$[r,s] = rsr^{-1}s^{-1} = (sr^{-1})r^{-1}s^{-1} = sr^{-2}s^{-1}$. Since$sr^{-2}s^{-1} = r^{2}$ (as$srs^{-1} = r^{-1}$ implies$sr^2s^{-1} = r^{-2} = r^2$ since$r^4 = e$), we get$[r,s] = r^2$. Thus$[D_4, D_4] = \{e, r^2\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. Since$\{e,r^2\}$ is abelian,$[\{e,r^2\}, \{e,r^2\}] = \{e\}$. Derived series:$D_4 \supset \{e,r^2\} \supset \{e\}$, with quotients$D_4/\{e,r^2\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ and$\{e,r^2\}/\{e\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. So$D_4$ is solvable. This confirms the quartic$x^4 - 2$ (with$\mathrm{Gal} \cong D_4$) is solvable by radicals.
