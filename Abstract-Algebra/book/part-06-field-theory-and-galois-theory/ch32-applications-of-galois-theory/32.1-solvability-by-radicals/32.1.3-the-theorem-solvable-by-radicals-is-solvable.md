# 32.1.3 The Theorem: $f$ Solvable by Radicals$\Leftrightarrow$ $\mathrm{Gal}(f/F)$ Is Solvable

*Galois's theorem* states: a separable polynomial $f \in F[x]$, where$\mathrm{char}(F) = 0$, is solvable by radicals over$F$ if and only if its Galois group$\mathrm{Gal}(f/F)$ is a solvable group. This is the central achievement of Galois theory and the original motivation for the entire subject. It transforms an analytic question — can the roots be expressed by a formula involving nested radicals? — into a purely algebraic one about the structure of a finite group.

\begin{theorem}[Galois]
Let $F$ be a field of characteristic zero and$f \in F[x]$ a separable polynomial with splitting field$E$ and Galois group$G = \mathrm{Gal}(E/F)$. Then$f$ is solvable by radicals over$F$ if and only if$G$ is a solvable group.
\end{theorem}

The proof proceeds in two directions. For the forward direction, if $f$ is solvable by radicals, then by definition the roots of$f$ lie in a radical tower$F = F_0 \subseteq F_1 \subseteq \cdots \subseteq F_k$ with each$F_{i+1} = F_i(\alpha_i)$ and$\alpha_i^{n_i} \in F_i$. One first enlarges$F$ by adjoining all relevant roots of unity: let$n = n_1 n_2 \cdots n_k$, adjoin a primitive$n$th root of unity$\zeta_n$ to$F$. Adjoining roots of unity does not destroy the solvability of the Galois group (solvability is inherited by quotients and subgroups, so if the Galois group of$F(\zeta_n)/F$ is abelian and the Galois group of$E \cdot F(\zeta_n)/F(\zeta_n)$ is solvable, then the Galois group of$E \cdot F(\zeta_n)/F$ is solvable as an extension of two solvable groups). With roots of unity present, each step$F_i \subseteq F_{i+1}$ is a cyclic Galois extension by Kummer theory. The Galois group of$F_{i+1}/F_i$ is cyclic. The Galois group of the full radical tower over$F(\zeta_n)$ is therefore an iterated extension of cyclic groups, hence solvable. The Galois group$G = \mathrm{Gal}(E/F)$ is a quotient of a subgroup of this solvable group, and both subgroups and quotients of solvable groups are solvable.

For the backward direction, if $G$ is solvable, one works down the derived series. The derived series$G = G^{(0)} \supset G^{(1)} \supset \cdots \supset G^{(m)} = \{e\}$ corresponds, via the Galois correspondence, to a tower of intermediate fields
$$F = E^{G^{(0)}} \subseteq E^{G^{(m-1)}} \subseteq \cdots \subseteq E^{G^{(1)}} \subseteq E^{G^{(0)}} = E.$$
Wait — the Galois correspondence reverses inclusion, so the derived series corresponds to $F = E^G \subseteq E^{G^{(m-1)}} \subseteq \cdots \subseteq E^{G^{(1)}} \subseteq E$. Each consecutive extension$E^{G^{(k+1)}}/E^{G^{(k)}}$ has Galois group$G^{(k)}/G^{(k+1)}$ (since$G^{(k+1)} \trianglelefteq G^{(k)}$ by definition of the derived series), and these quotients are abelian. After adjoining the appropriate roots of unity to the base field at each step, the key lemma of Kummer theory converts each abelian-group extension into a tower of cyclic Kummer extensions: if$K$ contains a primitive$n$th root of unity and$L/K$ is a cyclic Galois extension of degree$n$, then$L = K(\alpha)$ with$\alpha^n \in K$. This produces the desired radical tower containing the splitting field$E$.

\begin{proposition}[Kummer Theory, Key Lemma]
Let $K$ be a field containing a primitive$n$th root of unity$\zeta_n$ (with$n$ invertible in$K$). Then cyclic Galois extensions of$K$ of degree$n$ are exactly the extensions of the form$K(\sqrt[n]{a})$ for$a \in K^{\times}$. More precisely,$L/K$ is cyclic of degree$n$ if and only if$L = K(\alpha)$ with$\alpha^n \in K$.
\end{proposition}

The role of roots of unity in the proof deserves emphasis. Without roots of unity already present in the base field, a radical extension $K(\sqrt[n]{a})/K$ may fail to be Galois (the other roots$\zeta_n^j \sqrt[n]{a}$ may not all lie in$K(\sqrt[n]{a})$). Adjoining roots of unity is necessary to ensure that each step of the radical tower gives a cyclic (and therefore abelian) Galois extension. The price is that the radical tower may be larger than the splitting field, but this is fine — solvability by radicals only requires the splitting field to be *contained* in some radical extension.

\begin{remark}
The theorem as stated requires characteristic zero. In characteristic $p > 0$, the Frobenius endomorphism introduces complications: an extension$K(\alpha)/K$ with$\alpha^p \in K$ may be purely inseparable, and purely inseparable extensions are invisible to Galois theory. For$p$-power extensions in characteristic$p$, one uses instead *Artin–Schreier* extensions: the degree-$p$ extensions of a characteristic-$p$ field containing no$p$th root of unity are$K(\alpha)$ where$\alpha^p - \alpha = a \in K$ (these arise from the Artin–Schreier polynomial$x^p - x - a$). With Artin–Schreier theory filling in the gaps, an appropriate analog of Galois's theorem holds in characteristic$p$ as well.
\end{remark}

**Example 1.** The cubic $f = x^3 - 2$ over$\mathbb{Q}$:$\mathrm{Gal}(f/\mathbb{Q}) \cong S_3$, solvable (derived series$S_3 \supset A_3 \supset \{e\}$). Explicit radical tower: adjoin the square root$\sqrt{-3}$ (so that$\omega = \frac{-1 + \sqrt{-3}}{2} \in \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})$), then adjoin$\sqrt[3]{2}$. The full tower is$\mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\omega) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\omega, \sqrt[3]{2})$. Each step is a root extraction:$\mathbb{Q}(\omega) = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})$ (a square root), and$\mathbb{Q}(\omega, \sqrt[3]{2})/\mathbb{Q}(\omega)$ adjoins a cube root (a cyclic Kummer extension of degree$3$).

**Example 2.** The quartic $f = x^4 - 2$ over$\mathbb{Q}$:$\mathrm{Gal}(f/\mathbb{Q}) \cong D_4$, solvable (derived series$D_4 \supset \{e, r^2\} \supset \{e\}$). Radical tower:$\mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(i) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(i, \sqrt{2}) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(i, \sqrt[4]{2})$. The intermediate field$\mathbb{Q}(i, \sqrt{2})$ corresponds to the subgroup$\{e, r^2\}$ of$D_4$ (the center), and the top step adjoins the fourth root$\sqrt[4]{2}$.

**Example 3.** Every polynomial of degree $\leq 4$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ is solvable by radicals. The Galois group is a transitive subgroup of$S_4$. All five transitive subgroups of$S_4$ — namely$S_4$,$A_4$,$D_4$,$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$, and$V_4 = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ — are solvable. The general quartic formula (Ferrari's method, refining Cardano's cubic formula) provides an explicit radical expression in each case.

**Example 4.** The polynomial $f = x^5 - 5x + 12$ over$\mathbb{Q}$: this polynomial is irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$ (no rational roots; one checks$f(\pm 1), f(\pm 2), f(\pm 3), f(\pm 4), f(\pm 6), f(\pm 12)$ are all nonzero). Its discriminant and reduction modulo small primes can be used to show the Galois group is$S_5$. Since$S_5$ is not solvable (as$A_5$ is simple and non-abelian),$f$ is not solvable by radicals — its roots cannot be expressed using nested radicals applied to the coefficients.

**Example 5.** The fifth cyclotomic polynomial $\Phi_5(x) = x^4 + x^3 + x^2 + x + 1$ over$\mathbb{Q}$:$\mathrm{Gal}(\Phi_5/\mathbb{Q}) \cong (\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z})^* \cong \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$, which is cyclic, hence solvable. Explicit radical expression:$\zeta_5 = e^{2\pi i/5}$. One has$\cos(2\pi/5) = \frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{4}$ and$\sin(2\pi/5) = \frac{\sqrt{10+2\sqrt{5}}}{4}$, giving$\zeta_5 = \frac{(\sqrt{5}-1) + i\sqrt{10+2\sqrt{5}}}{4}$. The radical tower is$\mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5}) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5}, \sqrt{10+2\sqrt{5}}) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_5)$, of length 3 with steps of degrees$2$,$2$,$2$.
