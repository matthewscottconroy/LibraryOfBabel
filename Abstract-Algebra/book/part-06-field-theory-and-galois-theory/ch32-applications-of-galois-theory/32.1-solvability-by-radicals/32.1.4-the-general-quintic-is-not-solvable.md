# 32.1.4 The General Quintic Is Not Solvable: $\mathrm{Gal} = S_5$

The most celebrated application of Galois's theorem is the impossibility of solving the general quintic by radicals. The "general quintic" means the polynomial $f = x^5 + a_4 x^4 + a_3 x^3 + a_2 x^2 + a_1 x + a_0$ where$a_0, \ldots, a_4$ are independent transcendental quantities — formal parameters over$\mathbb{Q}$, so that$f$ lives over the field$F = \mathbb{Q}(a_0, \ldots, a_4)$ of rational functions in five variables. In this setting, the Galois group is as large as it can possibly be:$\mathrm{Gal}(f/F) \cong S_5$, the full symmetric group on five elements. Since$S_5$ is not solvable, Galois's theorem immediately gives the conclusion. But one must also understand why specific quintics fail and why others succeed.

\begin{theorem}
Let $F = \mathbb{Q}(a_0, a_1, a_2, a_3, a_4)$ where$a_0, \ldots, a_4$ are algebraically independent over$\mathbb{Q}$, and let$f = x^5 + a_4 x^4 + a_3 x^3 + a_2 x^2 + a_1 x + a_0 \in F[x]$. Then$\mathrm{Gal}(f/F) \cong S_5$.
\end{theorem}

The key to the theorem is the symmetric function perspective. If $\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_5$ are the five roots of$f$ in the splitting field$E$, then by Vieta's formulas the coefficients$a_i$ are precisely the elementary symmetric polynomials$e_i(\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_5)$: for instance,$a_4 = -(\alpha_1 + \cdots + \alpha_5)$ and$a_0 = (-1)^5 \alpha_1 \cdots \alpha_5$. This means that$F = \mathbb{Q}(\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_5)^{S_5}$, the fixed field of the symmetric group action on the rational functions in five variables. The splitting field$E = \mathbb{Q}(\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_5)$ is purely transcendental over$\mathbb{Q}$,$S_5$ acts faithfully on$E$ fixing$F$, and$[E:F] = |S_5| = 120$, so the Galois group is the full$S_5$.

\begin{proposition}
$S_5$ is not solvable. The derived series stabilizes at$A_5$:
$$S_5 \supset A_5 \supset A_5 \supset A_5 \supset \cdots$$
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The commutator subgroup $[S_5, S_5] = A_5$: this is because$S_5/A_5 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is the largest abelian quotient of$S_5$ (the sign map is the only homomorphism from$S_5$ to an abelian group with trivial kernel on transpositions). Now$[A_5, A_5] = A_5$: the group$A_5$ is simple (its only normal subgroups are$\{e\}$ and$A_5$ itself), and since$A_5$ is non-abelian,$[A_5, A_5] \neq \{e\}$; since the commutator subgroup is normal, it must equal$A_5$. The derived series therefore never reaches$\{e\}$, so$S_5$ is not solvable.$\square$
\end{proof}

The simplicity of $A_5$ is the heart of the obstruction. To see why$A_5$ is simple: any normal subgroup$N \trianglelefteq A_5$ must be a union of conjugacy classes of$A_5$. The conjugacy classes of$A_5$ have sizes$1, 15, 20, 12, 12$ (the identity; products of two disjoint transpositions; 3-cycles; and two classes of 5-cycles). Since$|N|$ must divide$|A_5| = 60$ and must include the class of size 1, one checks all unions of conjugacy classes whose total size divides 60: only$1$ and$60$ work. This combinatorial argument establishes simplicity without any appeal to the theory of simple groups.

By Galois's theorem, the general quintic has no radical formula. The impossibility is total: there exists no expression $F(a_0, \ldots, a_4)$ involving finitely many additions, subtractions, multiplications, divisions, and$n$th root extractions that gives a root of$x^5 + a_4 x^4 + a_3 x^3 + a_2 x^2 + a_1 x + a_0$ for all choices of coefficients. This contrasts with degrees$\leq 4$: the symmetric groups$S_2, S_3, S_4$ are all solvable, so the quadratic, cubic (Cardano), and quartic (Ferrari) formulas exist.

\begin{remark}
The impossibility of the general quintic does not preclude solvability of specific quintics. A specific irreducible quintic $f \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ is solvable by radicals if and only if$\mathrm{Gal}(f/\mathbb{Q})$ is a solvable transitive subgroup of$S_5$. The solvable transitive subgroups of$S_5$ are:$\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$ (cyclic, order 5),$D_5$ (dihedral, order 10), and the Frobenius group$F_{20} \cong \mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z} \rtimes \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ (order 20). The unsolvable ones are$A_5$ and$S_5$. Most quintics (in a density sense) have Galois group$S_5$, but infinitely many specific families have solvable Galois groups.
\end{remark}

**Example 1.** The polynomial $f = x^5 - 2$ over$\mathbb{Q}$: the splitting field is$E = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[5]{2}, \zeta_5)$ with$[E:\mathbb{Q}] = 20$. The Galois group is the Frobenius group$F_{20} \cong \mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z} \rtimes \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$, generated by$\sigma: \sqrt[5]{2} \mapsto \zeta_5 \sqrt[5]{2}$ (order 5) and$\tau: \zeta_5 \mapsto \zeta_5^2$ (order 4). This group is solvable: derived series$F_{20} \supset \mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z} \supset \{e\}$, with quotients$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ and$\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$, both abelian. Consistently,$x^5-2$ is solvable by radicals.

**Example 2.** The polynomial $f = x^5 - 5x + 12$ over$\mathbb{Q}$: this is irreducible (no rational roots by the rational root theorem). Using Dedekind's theorem, one reduces modulo small primes to find the cycle types of Frobenius elements. Mod 3:$f \equiv x^5 - 5x + 12 \equiv x^5 + x \equiv x(x^4+1) \pmod 3$. In$\mathbb{F}_3$,$x^4 + 1$: checking$0^4+1=1$,$1^4+1=2$,$2^4+1=17\equiv 2$, no roots; further,$x^4+1$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{F}_3$ (if it factored as two quadratics,$x^4+1 = (x^2+ax+b)(x^2-ax+b^{-1})$ over$\mathbb{F}_3$, checking coefficients shows no solution). So mod 3,$f$ factors as$(1)(4)$, giving a Frobenius element of cycle type$(1)(4)$, i.e., a 4-cycle. Since 4-cycles are even-odd... actually a 4-cycle is an odd permutation (a 4-cycle is a product of 3 transpositions). So the Galois group contains an odd permutation, hence$\mathrm{Gal}(f) \not\subseteq A_5$. Mod 7:$x^5-5x+12 \equiv x^5+2x+5 \pmod 7$; since$2^5+2\cdot 2+5 = 32+4+5 = 41 \equiv 6$,$x=2$ is not a root;$1^5+2+5=8\equiv 1$,$3^5+6+5 = 243+11=254\equiv 254-36\cdot7=2$, no; checking all residues mod 7 shows$f$ is irreducible mod 7, giving a Frobenius element of order 5, i.e., a 5-cycle. A transitive subgroup of$S_5$ containing a 4-cycle and a 5-cycle is all of$S_5$ (by a theorem on primitive groups). Therefore$\mathrm{Gal}(f/\mathbb{Q}) \cong S_5$, and$f$ is not solvable by radicals.

**Example 3.** The polynomial $f = x^5 - 4x + 2$ over$\mathbb{Q}$: by Eisenstein at$p=2$,$f$ is irreducible. Mod 3:$x^5-4x+2 \equiv x^5+2x+2 \pmod 3$; checking$0^5+0+2=2$,$1^5+2+2=5\equiv 2$,$2^5+4+2=38\equiv 2$ (no roots), and the polynomial is irreducible mod 3 (no degree-2 factors since neither$x^2+1$,$x^2+x+2$,$x^2+2x+2$ over$\mathbb{F}_3$ divides it), giving a 5-cycle. Mod 2:$f \equiv x^5 + 1 = (x+1)(x^4+x^3+x^2+x+1) \pmod 2$; since$x^4+x^3+x^2+x+1 = \Phi_5$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{F}_2$, the Frobenius at 2 has type$(1)(4)$, a 4-cycle. Again a transitive subgroup of$S_5$ with a 5-cycle and a 4-cycle is$S_5$. So$\mathrm{Gal}(f/\mathbb{Q}) \cong S_5$, and$f$ is not solvable by radicals.

**Example 4.** Degree-5 polynomials with Galois group $\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$: these are the solvable quintics with cyclic Galois group. An example is$f = \Phi_{11}^{-}(x)$, a "period polynomial" of the 11th cyclotomic field. More explicitly, the polynomial$f = x^5 + x^4 - 4x^3 - 3x^2 + 3x + 1$ is the minimal polynomial of$\zeta_{11} + \zeta_{11}^{-1}$, which generates the unique degree-5 subfield of$\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{11})$ (since$\phi(11) = 10$ and the unique subgroup of order$2$ in$(\mathbb{Z}/11\mathbb{Z})^* \cong \mathbb{Z}/10\mathbb{Z}$ gives a degree-5 fixed field). The Galois group of this polynomial is$\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$ (cyclic, hence solvable).

**Example 5.** For $n \geq 5$, the general degree-$n$ polynomial over$\mathbb{Q}(a_0, \ldots, a_{n-1})$ has Galois group$S_n$. Since$A_n$ is simple for$n \geq 5$ (proven by the same conjugacy-class argument as for$A_5$),$S_n$ is not solvable for any$n \geq 5$. This shows that the impossibility of solving by radicals is not a peculiarity of degree 5 — it persists for all degrees$\geq 5$, with the obstruction coming from the simplicity of the alternating group.
