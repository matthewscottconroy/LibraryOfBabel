# 32.1.5 Abel–Ruffini: A Historical Perspective

The impossibility of solving the general quintic by radicals was not discovered by Galois. Niels Henrik Abel published a proof in 1824, and Paolo Ruffini had made several serious but incomplete attempts in the years before. What distinguishes these contributions, and how they relate to the Galois theory we now have, deserves careful attention — both for historical accuracy and because the different proof strategies illuminate different aspects of the problem.

Ruffini, working in the early 1800s, recognized that the problem was connected to permutations of the roots and made several attempts to show that no permutation group with the right properties could exist. His approach was to suppose that a radical solution existed and to analyze the form it would take, then to argue (using nascent group-theoretic ideas) that no such form was compatible with all five roots. His arguments contained gaps — in particular, the concept of a group and the notion of the "resolvent" were not sufficiently developed to carry the argument to completion — but the core insight was present: the symmetry group of the equation imposes constraints that a radical formula cannot satisfy. Contemporary mathematicians, including Lagrange, had already observed that the methods successful for cubics and quartics (reducing to resolvent equations of lower degree) seemed to fail systematically at degree 5; Ruffini's contribution was to identify the group-theoretic reason why.

Abel's 1824 paper, "Mémoire sur les équations algébriques, où l'on démontre l'impossibilité de la résolution de l'équation générale du cinquième degré," was the first complete and accepted proof. Abel's argument proceeded by careful case analysis of what any hypothetical radical expression for a root of the general quintic would have to look like. Any radical expression is built up from the coefficients by a finite sequence of field operations and root extractions; Abel analyzed the structure of such expressions and showed that their values under permutation of the roots were too constrained to account for all five roots simultaneously. The argument was combinatorial and algebraic, and it was recognized as rigorous by Cauchy and others. Abel's proof did not, however, explain which specific quintics (if any) were solvable — it applied only to the "general" (parametric) case.

Galois's work, done in the years around 1830 and published posthumously after his death in a duel at age 20, superseded and generalized Abel's result in a profound way. Where Abel had proved a single impossibility theorem for the general quintic, Galois developed a complete theory that could, in principle, determine whether any specific polynomial of any degree is solvable by radicals. His key innovation was to associate to each polynomial not just a number but a group — now called the Galois group — and to identify solvability by radicals with a purely group-theoretic property: the solvability of that group. The Abel–Ruffini theorem becomes a corollary: the Galois group of the general quintic is $S_5$, and$S_5$ is not solvable, so the general quintic is not solvable by radicals.

The full import of Galois's approach extends beyond the quintic. The Galois group of a polynomial encodes all the algebraic relations among its roots, and the subgroup structure of the Galois group — via the fundamental theorem of Galois theory — determines the lattice of intermediate fields and hence all possible intermediate algebraic constructions. This means that Galois theory does not merely settle the quintic problem but provides a complete algorithm (in principle) for any polynomial: compute the Galois group, check whether it is solvable, and if so, read off the radical tower from the derived series. No analytic arguments about the form of solutions are needed; the group alone carries the information.

\begin{remark}
The methods Abel and Galois used were radically different in spirit, though they reached the same conclusion in the degree-5 case. Abel's proof is closer to what we might call "direct" — it works with the explicit algebraic form of radical expressions and shows a contradiction. Galois's proof is "structural" — it abstracts away from the explicit form and works with the group of symmetries. The structural approach is vastly more powerful: it generalizes immediately to all degrees, all base fields, and provides positive results (identifying which polynomials are solvable) as well as negative ones. The development of abstract algebra as a discipline — the systematic study of groups, rings, fields, and their homomorphisms — grew directly out of Galois's approach and the need to make it fully rigorous.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
Galois's original manuscripts were not published until 1846, fourteen years after his death at age 20 in 1832. The proof was reconstructed and clarified by Liouville, who recognized the depth of the work. The notion of a "group" in the modern sense — a set with a binary operation satisfying specific axioms — was formalized later, largely by Cayley and Klein. What Galois called a "group" was a specific group of permutations of the roots, and the abstraction to an abstract group (independent of any particular representation) came over the following decades. The full proof of Galois's main theorem in the rigorous form presented in §32.1.3 uses tools (normal subgroups, quotient groups, Kummer theory) that were developed and clarified by Jordan, Dedekind, Artin, and others in the period 1870–1940.
\end{remark}

**Example 1.** The quadratic formula $x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$ gives the roots of the general quadratic directly. The Galois group of$x^2 + bx + c$ over$\mathbb{Q}(b,c)$ is$S_2 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, which is abelian and hence solvable. The single square root step corresponds to the unique quotient in the derived series$S_2 \supset \{e\}$.

**Example 2.** For the cubic $x^3 + px + q$ over$\mathbb{Q}(p,q)$, the Galois group is$S_3$. The derived series$S_3 \supset A_3 \supset \{e\}$ gives the structure of the Cardano formula: the first step (going from$S_3$ to$A_3$) corresponds to adjoining$\sqrt{\Delta}$, and the second step (going from$A_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ to$\{e\}$) corresponds to adjoining a cube root. The Cardano formula is precisely the reconstruction of this derived series:$\mathbb{Q}(p,q) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(p,q,\sqrt{\Delta}) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(p,q,\sqrt{\Delta},\sqrt[3]{\Delta'})$ for appropriate expressions$\Delta, \Delta'$.

**Example 3.** For the quartic, the Galois group of the general quartic $x^4 + ax^3 + bx^2 + cx + d$ over$\mathbb{Q}(a,b,c,d)$ is$S_4$. The derived series$S_4 \supset A_4 \supset V_4 \supset \{e\}$ has length 3, corresponding to the three-level structure of the Ferrari quartic formula: one square root (discriminant), one cube root (resolvent cubic), and one more square root.

**Example 4.** The polynomial $x^5 - x - 1$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ is irreducible (Eisenstein does not apply directly, but one can check it has no rational roots and is irreducible over$\mathbb{Z}$ by other methods). Its Galois group over$\mathbb{Q}$ is$S_5$ (verifiable via Dedekind's theorem by examining reductions mod small primes: mod 2,$x^5+x+1 = (x^2+x+1)(x^3+x^2+1)$, giving cycle type$(2)(3)$, an odd permutation; mod 3, one finds an irreducible factor of degree 5 or a pattern yielding a 5-cycle). Since$\mathrm{Gal}(x^5-x-1/\mathbb{Q}) \cong S_5$, this polynomial is not solvable by radicals.

**Example 5.** A specific solvable quintic: $x^5 - 5x^4 + 5x^3 + 5x^2 - 5x - 1$. This factors as related to$\Phi_{11}$ or to a solvable subgroup. More concretely, the polynomial$x^5 + x^4 - 4x^3 - 3x^2 + 3x + 1 = \Phi_{11}(x)\cdot(\text{lower degree})$... a cleaner example: the minimal polynomial of$2\cos(2\pi/11)$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ has degree 5 (since$[\mathbb{Q}(\cos(2\pi/11)):\mathbb{Q}] = \frac{\phi(11)}{2} = 5$) and the Galois group is cyclic of order 5 (a quotient of$(\mathbb{Z}/11\mathbb{Z})^* \cong \mathbb{Z}/10\mathbb{Z}$). A cyclic group of order 5 is abelian and hence solvable, so this degree-5 polynomial is solvable by radicals, consistent with the fact that$\cos(2\pi/11)$ can be expressed using nested radicals (though the expression is complicated).
