# 32.1 Solvability by Radicals

For centuries, the central problem of algebra was to find explicit formulas for the roots of a polynomial in terms of its coefficients, using only arithmetic operations and the extraction of roots. The quadratic formula had been known since antiquity; Cardano's formula for cubics and Ferrari's reduction of quartics to cubics were major triumphs of the sixteenth century. The pattern suggested that formulas should exist in all degrees, and attempts to find one for the quintic occupied mathematicians through the eighteenth century. The impasse was resolved by Abel (who proved impossibility for the general degree-5 case) and then by Galois, who gave a complete group-theoretic criterion.

A field extension $K/F$ is a radical extension if $K = F(\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_k)$ where each $\alpha_i^{n_i} \in F(\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_{i-1})$ for some positive integer $n_i$ — each generator is obtained from the previous field by extracting a root. A polynomial $f \in F[x]$ is solvable by radicals if its splitting field is contained in a radical extension of $F$. Galois's theorem identifies the precise algebraic obstruction: $f$ is solvable by radicals if and only if its Galois group is a solvable group. A group is solvable if it has a subnormal series with abelian quotients; equivalently, if its derived series $G \supset G' \supset G'' \supset \cdots$ eventually reaches the trivial group.

For polynomials of degree at most 4, the Galois group is always solvable (the relevant groups are subgroups of $S_4$, and all subgroups of $S_4$ are solvable), confirming that radical formulas exist and explaining why Cardano and Ferrari's formulas work. For the general polynomial of degree 5 over $\mathbb{Q}$, the Galois group is $S_5$, and $S_5$ is not solvable: its derived series reaches $A_5$, which is simple and non-abelian, and stops there. This non-solvability of $S_5$ — traced back to the simplicity of $A_5$, which is itself a group-theoretic fact proved by purely combinatorial means — is the ultimate explanation for why no quintic formula exists. The connection between a structural property of a finite group and the impossibility of an analytic construction is Galois theory at its most profound.
