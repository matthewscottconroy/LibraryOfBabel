# 32.2.1 Ruler-and-Compass Constructibility: Degree-$2^k$ Extensions

The ancient Greek problems of geometric construction — doubling the cube, trisecting the angle, squaring the circle — occupied mathematicians for over two thousand years before field theory resolved all three simultaneously. The key is to translate the geometric operations of ruler and compass into algebraic ones, at which point the impossibility proofs become immediate. What seems like geometry becomes a question about field extensions, and the power-of-2 constraint on degrees provides a definitive answer.

A ruler-and-compass construction begins with two points (say $0$ and$1$ on the number line) and proceeds by the two basic operations: drawing the line through any two previously constructed points, and drawing the circle centered at one constructed point with radius equal to the distance between two constructed points. A real number$\alpha$ is *constructible* if the point$(\alpha, 0)$ can be reached from the initial points$\{(0,0),(1,0)\}$ in finitely many such steps. Since the intersection of two lines gives a rational operation (solving a linear system), the intersection of a line and a circle gives a quadratic operation (solving a linear-quadratic system, which involves at most a square root), and the intersection of two circles similarly reduces to a line-circle intersection via subtraction, the only "new" algebraic operation available at each step is extracting a square root of a quantity already constructed.

More precisely, at each step one is working over a field $F_i$ already constructed; the new step adds the square root of some element already in$F_i$, giving$F_{i+1} = F_i(\sqrt{c})$ for some$c \in F_i$, which is an extension of degree 1 (if$c$ is already a square in$F_i$) or 2. Starting from$F_0 = \mathbb{Q}$, after$k$ steps one reaches a field$F_k$ of degree$[F_k:\mathbb{Q}] = 2^j$ for some$j \leq k$.

\begin{theorem}[Algebraic Characterization of Constructibility]
A real number $\alpha$ is constructible by ruler and compass from$0$ and$1$ if and only if there exists a tower of fields
$$\mathbb{Q} = F_0 \subseteq F_1 \subseteq \cdots \subseteq F_k$$
with $\alpha \in F_k$ and each$[F_{i+1}:F_i] \leq 2$. Equivalently,$\alpha$ is constructible if and only if$\alpha$ lies in some field of degree$2^k$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ for some$k \geq 0$.
\end{theorem}

A necessary condition for constructibility follows immediately from the Tower Law.

\begin{corollary}
If $\alpha$ is constructible, then$[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}]$ is a power of$2$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
If $\alpha \in F_k$ as above, then$[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}]$ divides$[F_k:\mathbb{Q}] = 2^j$ by the Tower Law. So$[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}]$ is a power of$2$.
\end{proof}

This corollary is the main tool for proving impossibility. To show that a given real number $\alpha$ is not constructible, it suffices to exhibit an odd prime$p$ that divides$[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}]$, since then$[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}]$ cannot be a power of 2. The corollary is a necessary condition, not sufficient: there exist numbers of degree$2^k$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ that are not constructible (for instance,$\sqrt[4]{3}$ has degree 4 over$\mathbb{Q}$, and while the degree is$4 = 2^2$, it lies in the tower$\mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3}) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{3})$, so it is in fact constructible — this example works; for a non-constructible degree-4 example, one needs a more refined argument).

\begin{remark}
The full characterization requires not just that $[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}]$ is a power of 2, but that$\alpha$ lies in a tower of quadratic extensions. These are different conditions: for instance, whether$\alpha$ lies in a degree-$2^k$ extension that is itself obtained by successive quadratic steps (as opposed to a single step of degree$2^k$). For the classical impossibility results, the necessary condition (degree a power of 2) suffices, since the numbers in question have degree divisible by an odd prime.
\end{remark}

The translation from geometry to algebra is the key step. Each ruler-and-compass operation corresponds to an algebraic operation in $\mathbb{C}$ (or$\mathbb{R}$):
- Drawing a line through two points with coordinates in $F$ amounts to working within$F$ itself (linear combinations).
- Finding the intersection of two lines with $F$-coordinates gives a new point with$F$-coordinates (solving a linear system).
- Finding the intersection of a line and a circle (or two circles) with $F$-coefficients requires solving a quadratic equation over$F$, which introduces at most$\sqrt{D}$ for some$D \in F$.

The upward closure of this process from $\mathbb{Q}$ is exactly the iterated quadratic tower, and the constructible real numbers are precisely$\bigcup_k \{F_k : [F_k:\mathbb{Q}] = 2^k,\ F_k \text{ obtained by successive square root adjoins}\}$.

**Example 1.** The number $\sqrt{2}$ is constructible:$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}):\mathbb{Q}] = 2$, a power of 2. Explicitly, one constructs$\sqrt{2}$ as the length of the diagonal of a unit square.

**Example 2.** The number $\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}$ is constructible: it lies in$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2},\sqrt{3})$, which has the tower$\mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2},\sqrt{3})$ with each step of degree 2, giving total degree 4 over$\mathbb{Q}$.

**Example 3.** The number $\sqrt[3]{2}$ is not constructible:$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}):\mathbb{Q}] = 3$, which is not a power of 2 (it is divisible by the odd prime 3). This settles the doubling-the-cube problem (see §32.2.2).

**Example 4.** The number $\cos(2\pi/9)$ satisfies$8x^3 - 6x - 1 = 0$ (from$\cos(3 \cdot 2\pi/9) = \cos(2\pi/3) = -1/2$), which is irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$, giving$[\mathbb{Q}(\cos(2\pi/9)):\mathbb{Q}] = 3$. Not a power of 2, hence$\cos(2\pi/9)$ is not constructible, meaning the regular 9-gon cannot be constructed by ruler and compass.

**Example 5.** The number $\cos(2\pi/17)$: since$[\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{17}):\mathbb{Q}] = \phi(17) = 16 = 2^4$, and$\cos(2\pi/17) = (\zeta_{17} + \zeta_{17}^{-1})/2$, which lies in the real subfield of$\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{17})$ of degree$8 = 2^3$ over$\mathbb{Q}$, it is constructible. Gauss's 1796 discovery that$\cos(2\pi/17)$ is constructible — and hence that the regular 17-gon is constructible — follows from the fact that$\phi(17) = 16 = 2^4$. See §32.2.5 for the explicit radical expression.
