# 32.2.2 Doubling the Cube: Degree-3 Extension, Not Constructible

The problem of *doubling the cube*, known in antiquity as the Delian problem (after a legend attributing it to the oracle at Delos), asks: given a cube of side length $1$ and hence volume$1$, construct by ruler and compass a cube of volume$2$. Since the side length of the desired cube is$\sqrt[3]{2}$, the problem reduces to constructing the real number$\sqrt[3]{2}$ from the points$0$ and$1$ using ruler and compass. Once the algebraic criterion for constructibility is in hand, the proof of impossibility requires only a single computation of a field degree.

\begin{theorem}
The cube cannot be doubled by ruler-and-compass construction. Equivalently, $\sqrt[3]{2}$ is not constructible.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The minimal polynomial of $\sqrt[3]{2}$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ is$x^3 - 2$, which is irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$ by Eisenstein's criterion with$p = 2$. Therefore$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}):\mathbb{Q}] = 3$. By the corollary of §32.2.1, any constructible number$\alpha$ must satisfy$[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}] = 2^k$ for some$k \geq 0$. Since$3$ is not a power of$2$,$\sqrt[3]{2}$ is not constructible.$\square$
\end{proof}

The proof's brevity is striking. The two-thousand-year problem yields immediately once one knows: (1) the algebraic criterion for constructibility (degree must be a power of 2), and (2) the irreducibility of $x^3 - 2$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ (giving degree 3). No intricate geometric argument is needed; the impossibility is a direct consequence of the prime 3 not being a power of 2.

It is instructive to understand why no clever geometric trick can overcome this obstruction. The algebraic argument shows that any sequence of ruler-and-compass steps, starting from $0$ and$1$, can only produce real numbers lying in successive quadratic extensions of$\mathbb{Q}$. After$k$ steps, one is inside a field$F_k$ with$[F_k:\mathbb{Q}] = 2^j$ for some$j \leq k$. For$\sqrt[3]{2}$ to appear in$F_k$, the Tower Law would require$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}):\mathbb{Q}] = 3$ to divide$[F_k:\mathbb{Q}] = 2^j$. But$3 \nmid 2^j$ for any$j$. The obstruction is not about the complexity of the construction but about the arithmetic of field degrees.

\begin{remark}
The Delian problem was stated as: given an altar in the shape of a cube, double its volume to appease the god. The legend says the oracle specified this construction to the Athenians as a condition for ending a plague. Mathematicians worked on it for centuries without success. Some attempted approximate constructions, and others found mechanical (non-ruler-and-compass) solutions: Menaechmus, using intersections of conic sections, gave a solution around 350 BCE. The proof that an exact ruler-and-compass construction is impossible had to wait for the algebraic framework, which was not available until the 19th century. The moral is that Galois theory resolves in a few lines what centuries of geometric intuition could not.
\end{remark}

The impossibility proof generalizes immediately: no cube root of any positive rational $q$ that is not itself a perfect cube can be constructed. If$q = a/b$ in lowest terms and$q$ is not a perfect cube, then the minimal polynomial of$q^{1/3}$ over$\mathbb{Q}$ has degree$3$ (irreducibility follows from the rational root theorem applied to$x^3 - a/b$, or equivalently to$bx^3 - a$ over$\mathbb{Z}$), and the same degree argument applies. Similarly, no$n$th root of a rational number — for$n$ having an odd prime factor — can be constructible unless the root is rational.

**Example 1.** $\sqrt[3]{2}$: minimal polynomial$x^3 - 2$ (Eisenstein,$p=2$), degree 3. Not constructible. The cube of side 1 cannot be doubled by ruler and compass.

**Example 2.** $\sqrt[3]{7}$: minimal polynomial$x^3 - 7$ (Eisenstein,$p=7$), degree 3. Not constructible. More generally, for any prime$p$,$\sqrt[3]{p}$ has$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{p}):\mathbb{Q}] = 3$ and hence is not constructible.

**Example 3.** $\sqrt[3]{8} = 2 \in \mathbb{Q}$: minimal polynomial$x - 2$, degree 1. Constructible (rational). This is consistent: doubling the volume of a cube of side$2$ to get a cube of side$\sqrt[3]{16} = 2\sqrt[3]{2}$ is equally impossible.

**Example 4.** $\sqrt[3]{4}$: minimal polynomial$x^3 - 4$ (irreducible by Eisenstein at$p=2$), degree 3. Not constructible. Note$\sqrt[3]{4} = (\sqrt[3]{2})^2 \in \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$, confirming$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{4}):\mathbb{Q}] = 3$. Same obstruction as$\sqrt[3]{2}$.

**Example 5.** Compare with $\sqrt[4]{2}$: minimal polynomial$x^4 - 2$ (Eisenstein,$p=2$), degree$4 = 2^2$. The necessary condition for constructibility is satisfied, and indeed$\sqrt[4]{2}$ is constructible via the tower$\mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2})$ with each step of degree 2. The cube-root obstruction is specific to degree 3; fourth roots of 2 are constructible because$4 = 2^2$ is a power of 2.
