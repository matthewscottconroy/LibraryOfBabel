# 32.2.3 Trisecting the Angle: Similar Obstruction

The problem of *trisecting an arbitrary angle* asks whether, given any angle, one can construct by ruler and compass the angle one-third its measure. The problem is not to trisect some particular angle — a right angle of $90°$ can easily be trisected to$30°$, since$30°$ is constructible — but to trisect every angle. A single non-trisectable angle suffices to prove the impossibility in general. The angle$60°$ provides the cleanest example: if$60°$ cannot be trisected, then angle trisection is impossible in general, since the$60°$ angle is itself constructible (it appears in an equilateral triangle).

\begin{theorem}
The $60°$ angle cannot be trisected by ruler and compass. Consequently, not every angle can be trisected.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Trisecting a $60°$ angle means constructing a$20°$ angle, which is equivalent to constructing$\cos(20°)$. Applying the triple-angle identity$\cos(3\theta) = 4\cos^3(\theta) - 3\cos(\theta)$ with$\theta = 20°$ gives:
$$\cos(60°) = 4\cos^3(20°) - 3\cos(20°), \qquad \text{i.e.,} \quad \frac{1}{2} = 4\cos^3(20°) - 3\cos(20°).$$
Setting $\alpha = \cos(20°)$ and multiplying by 2:$8\alpha^3 - 6\alpha - 1 = 0$. So$\alpha$ is a root of$g(x) = 8x^3 - 6x - 1$.

To show $g$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$: by the rational root theorem, the only possible rational roots are$\pm 1, \pm 1/2, \pm 1/4, \pm 1/8$. Checking:$g(1) = 8-6-1=1$,$g(-1) = -8+6-1=-3$,$g(1/2) = 8/8 - 3 - 1 = 1 - 4 = -3$,$g(-1/2) = -1+3-1=1$,$g(1/4) = 8/64 - 6/4 - 1 = 1/8 - 3/2 - 1 < 0$...$g(1/4) = 8(1/64) - 6(1/4) - 1 = 1/8 - 3/2 - 1 = 1/8 - 20/8 = -19/8 \neq 0$. None are roots, so$g$ has no rational root and, being a cubic, is irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}$. Therefore$[\mathbb{Q}(\cos 20°):\mathbb{Q}] = 3$, which is not a power of 2, so$\cos(20°)$ is not constructible by §32.2.1, and the$20°$ angle cannot be constructed.$\square$
\end{proof}

The structure of the proof is identical to that for doubling the cube: a triple-angle formula reduces trisection to finding a root of a cubic polynomial, the cubic is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$, the resulting degree-3 extension is not a power of 2, and the algebraic criterion rules out constructibility.

The general principle is this: trisecting an angle $\theta$ requires constructing$\cos(\theta/3)$. From$\cos(3\cdot\theta/3) = \cos(\theta)$, the number$c = \cos(\theta/3)$ satisfies$4c^3 - 3c = \cos\theta$, i.e.,$8c^3 - 6c - 2\cos\theta = 0$ (after clearing fractions if$\cos\theta$ is rational). When this cubic is irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}(\cos\theta)$, the degree is 3, not a power of 2, and trisection is impossible. If$\cos\theta$ is rational and the cubic is irreducible, the degree is 3. Trisection of$\theta$ is possible only if this cubic factors over$\mathbb{Q}(\cos\theta)$, which is quite special.

\begin{remark}
Not all angles are non-trisectable. An angle $\theta$ is trisectable (by ruler and compass) if and only if$\cos(\theta/3)$ is constructible. Since$\cos(90°) = 0$, trisecting$90°$ requires constructing$\cos(30°) = \sqrt{3}/2$, which is in$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3})$, a degree-2 extension. Similarly,$120°$ trisects to$40°$:$\cos(40°)$ satisfies$8x^3 - 6x + 1 = 0$, which has the rational root$x = 1/2$ (check:$8/8 - 3 + 1 = 0$, yes!). So$\cos(40°)$ is a root of$(x - 1/2)(8x^2 + 4x - 2)$ over$\mathbb{Q}$, and the quadratic factor$8x^2 + 4x - 2$ gives$\cos(40°) = \frac{-4 \pm \sqrt{16 + 64}}{16} = \frac{-4 \pm 4\sqrt{5}}{16} = \frac{-1 \pm \sqrt{5}}{4}$. Taking the positive root,$\cos(40°) \in \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$, degree 2 over$\mathbb{Q}$, so$40°$ is constructible and$120°$ is trisectable.
\end{remark}

**Example 1.** The angle $\theta = 60°$. Trisection requires$\cos(20°)$; minimal polynomial$8x^3 - 6x - 1$, irreducible, degree 3. Not trisectable.

**Example 2.** The angle $\theta = 90°$. Trisection requires$\cos(30°) = \sqrt{3}/2 \in \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3})$, degree 2 over$\mathbb{Q}$. This is a power of 2, so$90°$ is trisectable: one constructs$\cos(30°)$ and hence$30°$ directly, since$\cos(30°) = \sqrt{3}/2$ and$\sqrt{3}$ is constructible.

**Example 3.** The angle $\theta = 45°$. Trisection requires$\cos(15°) = \cos(45° - 30°) = \cos 45°\cos 30° + \sin 45° \sin 30° = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2} = \frac{\sqrt{6}+\sqrt{2}}{4}$. This lies in$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2},\sqrt{3})$, degree 4 over$\mathbb{Q}$. Degree 4 is$2^2$, a power of 2, so$45°$ is trisectable (and indeed$15°$ is constructible).

**Example 4.** The angle $\theta = 40°$. Trisection requires$\cos(40°/3)$. Since$3 \times 40° = 120°$ and$\cos(120°) = -1/2$, we need a root of$8x^3 - 6x + 1 = 0$. Checking$x = 1/2$:$8/8 - 3 + 1 = 0$. So$x = 1/2 = \cos(60°)$ is a root, meaning the cubic factors as$(2x - 1)(4x^2 + 2x - 1)$. The root we want,$\cos(40°/3) = \cos(40°/3)$... wait: trisecting$40°$ gives$40°/3 \approx 13.33°$. We apply$4c^3 - 3c = \cos(40°) = (−1+\sqrt5)/4$ (from the computation in the remark). The cubic$8c^3 - 6c - 2\cos(40°)$ over$\mathbb{Q}(\cos(40°)) = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$ may or may not be irreducible; the question is subtle and depends on the specific value.

**Example 5.** The general angle $\theta$ whose cosine is transcendental over$\mathbb{Q}$: then$\cos\theta \notin \overline{\mathbb{Q}}$, and any tower of extensions from$\mathbb{Q}(\cos\theta)$ stays within the algebraic closure of$\mathbb{Q}(\cos\theta)$ over$\mathbb{Q}(\cos\theta)$. The cubic$8x^3 - 6x - 2\cos\theta$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{Q}(\cos\theta)$ when$\cos\theta$ is transcendental (since the cubic has no roots that could be expressed algebraically in terms of$\cos\theta$ without increasing transcendence degree), and trisection fails for degree-3 reasons.
