# 32.2.4 Squaring the Circle: Transcendence of $\pi$

The problem of *squaring the circle* asks whether one can construct, by ruler and compass, a square whose area equals that of a given circle of radius $1$. Such a square would have side length$\sqrt{\pi}$, so the problem reduces to constructing$\sqrt{\pi}$ from$0$ and$1$ using the two basic ruler-and-compass operations. The impossibility of this construction is qualitatively different from the impossibilities of doubling the cube or trisecting the angle, and its proof requires a fundamentally deeper tool.

For doubling the cube and trisecting the angle, the obstruction was arithmetic: the relevant numbers were algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}$ but had minimal polynomial degrees divisible by odd primes, preventing them from lying in any tower of quadratic extensions. The numbers$\sqrt[3]{2}$ and$\cos(20°)$ are genuine algebraic numbers — they satisfy polynomial equations with rational coefficients — they simply have the wrong degree. The obstruction for$\sqrt{\pi}$ is of a completely different nature:$\pi$ is not algebraic at all.

\begin{theorem}[Lindemann, 1882]
The number $\pi$ is transcendental over$\mathbb{Q}$. In particular,$\sqrt{\pi}$ is transcendental over$\mathbb{Q}$, and the circle cannot be squared by ruler and compass.
\end{theorem}

The transcendence of $\pi$ was proved by Ferdinand von Lindemann in 1882, building on Hermite's 1873 proof of the transcendence of$e$. Lindemann's theorem (the Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem in its full generality) states: if$\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n$ are distinct algebraic numbers, then$e^{\alpha_1}, \ldots, e^{\alpha_n}$ are linearly independent over$\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ (the algebraic closure of$\mathbb{Q}$). In particular, if$\alpha$ is a non-zero algebraic number, then$e^\alpha$ is transcendental. Taking$\alpha = i\pi$: by Euler's identity$e^{i\pi} = -1$, and$-1$ is algebraic (it satisfies$x + 1 = 0$). If$i\pi$ were algebraic, then by Lindemann's theorem$e^{i\pi}$ would be transcendental — contradiction. So$i\pi$ is transcendental, and since$i$ is algebraic,$\pi$ itself must be transcendental (if$\pi$ were algebraic,$i\pi$ would be algebraic as a product of algebraic numbers).

The impossibility of squaring the circle follows immediately:

\begin{corollary}
The number $\sqrt{\pi}$ is transcendental over$\mathbb{Q}$, and therefore not constructible by ruler and compass.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
If $\sqrt{\pi}$ were algebraic over$\mathbb{Q}$, then$\pi = (\sqrt{\pi})^2$ would be algebraic (being the square of an algebraic number). But$\pi$ is transcendental. So$\sqrt{\pi}$ is transcendental. A transcendental number cannot lie in any finite-degree extension of$\mathbb{Q}$ (every finite extension is algebraic), so$\sqrt{\pi}$ cannot lie in any tower of quadratic extensions starting from$\mathbb{Q}$. Therefore$\sqrt{\pi}$ is not constructible.$\square$
\end{proof}

The distinction between the three classical impossibilities is worth emphasizing. Doubling the cube and trisecting the angle are impossible for algebraic reasons: the desired numbers are algebraic but have degree 3 over $\mathbb{Q}$, and$3$ is not a power of$2$. Squaring the circle is impossible for transcendental reasons: the desired number$\sqrt{\pi}$ is not algebraic at all. This is a logically stronger impossibility — the obstruction is not about the structure of field extensions but about$\pi$ not satisfying any polynomial equation over$\mathbb{Q}$. The proof of transcendence requires analytic tools (properties of the exponential function), not just algebra.

\begin{remark}
The transcendence of $\pi$ resolved one of the most ancient open problems in mathematics. Archimedes and other Greek mathematicians gave increasingly accurate approximations to$\pi$ (Archimedes showed$3\frac{10}{71} < \pi < 3\frac{1}{7}$), and Indian and Chinese mathematicians computed many decimal places, but no algebraic expression for$\pi$ involving only rationals and radicals was found because none exists. The work of Hermite and Lindemann completed the picture:$e$ and$\pi$ are both transcendental, and the circle cannot be squared. The full Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem also establishes that$e$ is transcendental and that values like$e^{\sqrt{2}}$,$\sin(1)$,$\cos(1)$,$\ln(2)$ are all transcendental.
\end{remark}

**Example 1.** The number $e$: Hermite proved in 1873 that$e$ is transcendental over$\mathbb{Q}$. Therefore$e$ is not constructible (not algebraic over$\mathbb{Q}$, hence certainly not in a finite extension of$\mathbb{Q}$). This contrasts with$e \approx 2.718$, which can be approximated by constructible numbers but is not itself constructible.

**Example 2.** The number $\ln 2$: since$e^{\ln 2} = 2$ and$2$ is algebraic and nonzero, and$\ln 2$ is a nonzero real number (so$e^{\ln 2} = 2$), if$\ln 2$ were algebraic then$e^{\ln 2} = 2$ would be transcendental by Lindemann's theorem — contradiction. Therefore$\ln 2$ is transcendental and not constructible.

**Example 3.** The number $\pi^2$: since$\pi$ is transcendental and$\pi^2 = (\pi)^2$ is a polynomial in$\pi$ over$\mathbb{Q}$,$\pi^2$ is also transcendental (if$\pi^2$ satisfied a polynomial equation$a_n (\pi^2)^n + \cdots + a_0 = 0$, this would give a polynomial in$\pi$ of degree$2n$, making$\pi$ algebraic). So$\pi^2$ is transcendental and not constructible.

**Example 4.** The number $e + \pi$: it is not known whether$e + \pi$ is transcendental (though it is known that at least one of$e + \pi$ and$e\pi$ is transcendental, since$e$ and$\pi$ cannot both be algebraic numbers satisfying$x^2 - (e+\pi)x + e\pi = 0$ — but neither$e+\pi$ nor$e\pi$ has been individually proved transcendental). This illustrates that transcendence questions quickly become difficult even for natural combinations of$e$ and$\pi$.

**Example 5.** The number $\sqrt{\pi}$ versus$\pi$ itself: a geometric question specifically requires$\sqrt{\pi}$, not$\pi$, because the circle of radius$1$ has area$\pi$ and one needs a square of side$\sqrt{\pi}$ to have the same area. The transcendence argument applies to both: if$\sqrt{\pi}$ were algebraic,$\pi = (\sqrt{\pi})^2$ would be algebraic. Conversely, since$\pi$ is transcendental,$\sqrt{\pi}$ is transcendental. The squaring-the-circle problem fails at both$\pi$ and$\sqrt{\pi}$.
