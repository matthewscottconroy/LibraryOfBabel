# 32.2.5 Constructible Regular Polygons: Gauss and the 17-gon

Constructing a regular $n$-gon by ruler and compass is equivalent to constructing the complex number$\zeta_n = e^{2\pi i/n}$, a primitive$n$th root of unity. Since$\zeta_n$ lies in the cyclotomic field$\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$, the constructibility question becomes: does$\zeta_n$ lie in a tower of quadratic extensions over$\mathbb{Q}$? Since$\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)/\mathbb{Q}) \cong (\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^*$ is abelian, the necessary and sufficient condition is simply that$[\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n):\mathbb{Q}] = \phi(n)$ is a power of$2$.

\begin{theorem}[Gauss–Wantzel]
The regular $n$-gon is constructible by ruler and compass if and only if$n = 2^k p_1 p_2 \cdots p_r$, where$k \geq 0$ and$p_1, \ldots, p_r$ are distinct Fermat primes (primes of the form$2^{2^m} + 1$).
\end{theorem}

The necessity direction (Wantzel, 1837) uses the algebraic criterion: if the $n$-gon is constructible, then$\phi(n)$ must be a power of$2$. Writing$n = 2^a p_1^{e_1} \cdots p_r^{e_r}$, the formula$\phi(n) = 2^{\max(a-1,0)} \prod_{i} p_i^{e_i-1}(p_i-1)$ shows that$\phi(n)$ is a power of$2$ only if each$e_i = 1$ (so no repeated odd prime factor) and each$p_i - 1$ is a power of$2$ (so each odd prime factor is a Fermat prime). The sufficiency direction (Gauss, 1796) shows that when$\phi(n) = 2^k$, the Galois group$(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^*$ is a$2$-group, and every$2$-group has a chain of subgroups of index$2$ reaching the trivial group; by the Galois correspondence, this gives a chain of quadratic extensions of$\mathbb{Q}$ with compositum equal to$\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$, witnessing constructibility.

The known Fermat primes are $F_0 = 3$,$F_1 = 5$,$F_2 = 17$,$F_3 = 257$,$F_4 = 65537$ (corresponding to$m = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4$ in the formula$2^{2^m} + 1$). Whether there are any others is an open problem in number theory. Fermat numbers$F_m = 2^{2^m} + 1$ for$m \geq 5$ are known to be composite:$F_5 = 4294967297 = 641 \times 6700417$ (Euler, 1732).

Gauss's 1796 discovery that the regular $17$-gon is constructible was the first new constructibility result in over two millennia (since the ancient Greeks). Gauss worked out the explicit radical expression for$\cos(2\pi/17)$ by computing the subfield structure of$\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{17})$. Since$(\mathbb{Z}/17\mathbb{Z})^* \cong \mathbb{Z}/16\mathbb{Z}$ is cyclic of order$16 = 2^4$, there is a unique chain of subgroups
$$\{0\} \subset \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Z}/16\mathbb{Z},$$
corresponding (via Galois correspondence) to a chain of quadratic extensions:
$$\mathbb{Q} \subset K_1 \subset K_2 \subset K_3 \subset \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{17}),$$
where each $[K_{i+1}:K_i] = 2$. Gauss used the Gaussian *periods* (partial sums of roots of unity over cosets of subgroups) to identify these intermediate fields. The result is:
$$16\cos\frac{2\pi}{17} = -1 + \sqrt{17} + \sqrt{34 - 2\sqrt{17}} + 2\sqrt{17 + 3\sqrt{17} - \sqrt{34 - 2\sqrt{17}} - 2\sqrt{34 + 2\sqrt{17}}},$$
an expression involving three levels of nested square roots, consistent with the tower of length 4.

\begin{remark}
The constructibility of the regular $257$-gon ($\phi(257) = 256 = 2^8$) and$65537$-gon ($\phi(65537) = 65536 = 2^{16}$) follows from the same Galois-theoretic argument, though the explicit radical expressions become increasingly unwieldy. Johann Gustav Hermes reportedly spent 10 years constructing the explicit radical expression for$\cos(2\pi/65537)$, filling thousands of pages of manuscript (preserved at Göttingen). The practical usefulness of these constructions is essentially zero — the interest is entirely mathematical. The Gauss–Wantzel theorem provides a complete and final answer to the classical question of which regular polygons are constructible.
\end{remark}

**Example 1.** The regular $3$-gon (equilateral triangle):$\phi(3) = 2 = 2^1$. Constructible. Indeed, the equilateral triangle is one of the most elementary constructions: draw two circles of radius 1 centered at the endpoints of a segment of length 1, and the intersection gives the third vertex.

**Example 2.** The regular $5$-gon (pentagon):$\phi(5) = 4 = 2^2$. Constructible. Explicitly,$\cos(2\pi/5) = \frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{4}$, which lies in$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$, degree 2 over$\mathbb{Q}$. The tower is$\mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5}) \subset \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_5)$, each step of degree 2.

**Example 3.** The regular $7$-gon (heptagon):$\phi(7) = 6$, which has the odd factor$3$. Since$7$ is not a Fermat prime ($7 = 2^2 + 3$ is not of the form$2^{2^m}+1$), the$7$-gon is not constructible. The minimal polynomial of$2\cos(2\pi/7)$ is$x^3 + x^2 - 2x - 1$, of degree$3$, confirming the degree obstruction.

**Example 4.** The regular $17$-gon:$\phi(17) = 16 = 2^4$. Constructible. Gauss's explicit formula for$\cos(2\pi/17)$ (given above) involves four levels of nested square roots. The first level gives$\sqrt{17}$; the second gives$\sqrt{34 \pm 2\sqrt{17}}$; and so on. This is a remarkable formula and was the centerpiece of Gauss's Disquisitiones Arithmeticae (1801).

**Example 5.** The regular $15$-gon:$n = 15 = 3 \times 5$,$\phi(15) = \phi(3)\phi(5) = 2 \times 4 = 8 = 2^3$. Since$3$ and$5$ are both Fermat primes, the$15$-gon is constructible. Alternatively,$\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{15}) = \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_3) \cdot \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_5)$, both of whose degree-2 and degree-4 extensions are quadratic towers, and their compositum has degree$\phi(15) = 8$ over$\mathbb{Q}$. The regular$15$-gon can also be constructed directly from the regular pentagon and equilateral triangle by the classical construction of$72° - 60° = 12°$-angle subtraction, then using the$24°$-angle... more directly, the vertices of a regular$15$-gon can be obtained by combining vertices of a regular triangle and pentagon inscribed in the same circle.
