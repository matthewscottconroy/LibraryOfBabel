# 32.2 Classical Geometric Constructions

The ancient Greeks posed three problems that, despite the efforts of the best mathematicians over two millennia, resisted all attempts at solution by ruler and compass: doubling the cube (constructing a cube with twice the volume of a given cube), trisecting an arbitrary angle, and squaring the circle (constructing a square with the same area as a given circle). In each case, the impossibility was not for want of trying, but because the problems are genuinely impossible. Galois theory, via the theory of field extensions, provides the proof.

The algebraic translation is this: a real number $\alpha$ is constructible by ruler and compass from a set of given lengths if and only if $\alpha$ lies in some tower $\mathbb{Q} = F_0 \subset F_1 \subset \cdots \subset F_n$ where each $[F_{i+1} : F_i] = 2$. Equivalently, $[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha) : \mathbb{Q}]$ must be a power of 2. Every ruler-and-compass step either stays within the current field (intersecting two lines, each determined by previously constructed points) or adjoins a square root (intersecting a circle with a line or another circle), so each step at most doubles the degree. If the target requires a degree-3 extension over $\mathbb{Q}$, no tower of degree-2 steps can reach it.

Doubling the cube requires constructing $\sqrt[3]{2}$, whose minimal polynomial $x^3 - 2$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ (by Eisenstein), giving $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}):\mathbb{Q}] = 3$, not a power of 2. Trisecting $60°$ requires constructing $\cos(20°)$, which satisfies the irreducible polynomial $8x^3 - 6x - 1$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, also of degree 3. Squaring the circle requires constructing $\sqrt{\pi}$, but $\pi$ is transcendental (Lindemann's theorem, 1882), so $\sqrt{\pi}$ does not even lie in an algebraic extension of $\mathbb{Q}$. On the positive side, a regular $n$-gon is constructible if and only if Euler's totient $\phi(n)$ is a power of 2, which holds precisely when $n = 2^k p_1 \cdots p_r$ with $p_i$ distinct Fermat primes. Gauss proved the regular 17-gon constructible at age 18 — a discovery that reportedly convinced him to pursue mathematics rather than classics.
