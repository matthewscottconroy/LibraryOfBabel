# 32.3.1 Existence and Uniqueness of $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$

Among the applications of Galois theory, the classification of finite fields is among the most complete and elegant. One might expect the theory of finite fields to be complicated, given the variety of possible sizes and the variety of ways a finite field might be structured. The remarkable fact is that the structure is entirely determined by a single number: there is exactly one finite field of each prime power order, and no finite fields of any other order.

\begin{theorem}[Existence and Uniqueness of Finite Fields]
For every prime $p$ and every positive integer$n$, there exists a field$\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ with exactly$p^n$ elements, and it is unique up to isomorphism. Conversely, any finite field has order$p^n$ for some prime$p$ and positive integer$n$.
\end{theorem}

The first step is to show that any finite field has prime power order. Let $F$ be a finite field. Its characteristic is a prime$p$ (since if the characteristic were composite,$F$ would contain zero divisors, contradicting the field axioms). The prime subfield$\mathbb{F}_p \subseteq F$ gives$F$ the structure of a vector space over$\mathbb{F}_p$; if this vector space has dimension$n$, then$|F| = p^n$.

The existence of $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ is established by constructing it as a splitting field. Consider the polynomial$x^{p^n} - x \in \mathbb{F}_p[x]$. Its formal derivative is$p \cdot x^{p^n-1} - 1 = -1$ (since$p = 0$ in$\mathbb{F}_p$), so$\gcd(x^{p^n} - x, (x^{p^n}-x)') = \gcd(x^{p^n}-x, -1) = 1$. The polynomial is therefore separable — it has$p^n$ distinct roots in any splitting field. Let$E$ be the splitting field of$x^{p^n} - x$ over$\mathbb{F}_p$. The set of roots$S = \{\alpha \in E : \alpha^{p^n} = \alpha\}$ is closed under the field operations: if$\alpha^{p^n} = \alpha$ and$\beta^{p^n} = \beta$, then$(\alpha + \beta)^{p^n} = \alpha^{p^n} + \beta^{p^n} = \alpha + \beta$ by the Frobenius (freshman's dream) in characteristic$p$, and$(\alpha\beta)^{p^n} = \alpha^{p^n}\beta^{p^n} = \alpha\beta$. Also$(-\alpha)^{p^n} = (-1)^{p^n}\alpha^{p^n} = -\alpha$ (since$(-1)^{p^n} = -1$ in characteristic$p$), and if$\alpha \neq 0$ then$(\alpha^{-1})^{p^n} = (\alpha^{p^n})^{-1} = \alpha^{-1}$. Therefore$S$ is a subfield of$E$ with$|S| = p^n$, so$S = \mathbb{F}_{p^n}$.

Uniqueness follows by a symmetric argument: any field $F$ with$|F| = p^n$ is a degree-$n$ extension of$\mathbb{F}_p$ (a vector space over$\mathbb{F}_p$ of dimension$n$). The multiplicative group$F^* = F \setminus \{0\}$ has order$p^n - 1$, so every non-zero element satisfies$\alpha^{p^n - 1} = 1$, giving$\alpha^{p^n} = \alpha$. The zero element also satisfies$0^{p^n} = 0$. Therefore every element of$F$ is a root of$x^{p^n} - x$, which has at most$p^n$ roots. Since$|F| = p^n$ and all elements are roots,$F$ is a splitting field of$x^{p^n} - x$ over$\mathbb{F}_p$. As splitting fields are unique up to isomorphism, any two fields of order$p^n$ are isomorphic.

\begin{remark}
The key ingredient in both existence and uniqueness is the same: that the set of roots of $x^{p^n} - x$ forms a field, which follows from the Frobenius endomorphism$\phi: x \mapsto x^p$ being a field homomorphism in characteristic$p$. This is a special feature of characteristic$p$ with no analog over$\mathbb{Q}$ or other characteristic-zero fields. Over$\mathbb{Q}$, the polynomial$x^n - x$ does not have the property that its roots form a subfield, because$(a + b)^n \neq a^n + b^n$ in general.
\end{remark}

**Example 1.** $\mathbb{F}_4 = \mathbb{F}_{2^2}$: constructed as the splitting field of$x^4 - x = x(x-1)(x^2+x+1)$ over$\mathbb{F}_2$. Since$x^2 + x + 1$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{F}_2$ (no roots:$0^2+0+1 = 1 \neq 0$ and$1^2+1+1 = 1 \neq 0$), adjoining a root$\alpha$ with$\alpha^2 + \alpha + 1 = 0$ gives$\mathbb{F}_4 = \{0, 1, \alpha, \alpha+1\}$. One can verify:$(\alpha+1)^2 = \alpha^2 + 1 = (\alpha+1)+1-1 = \alpha$... more precisely$\alpha^2 = \alpha + 1$,$(\alpha+1)^2 = \alpha^2 + 1 = \alpha + 1 + 1 = \alpha$, and all four elements satisfy$x^4 = x$.

**Example 2.** $\mathbb{F}_8 = \mathbb{F}_{2^3}$: constructed as the splitting field of$x^8 - x$ over$\mathbb{F}_2$. One finds an irreducible cubic over$\mathbb{F}_2$, e.g.,$x^3 + x + 1$ (no roots:$0^3+0+1=1$,$1^3+1+1=1$). Adjoining a root$\beta$ with$\beta^3 = \beta + 1$ gives$\mathbb{F}_8 = \{0, 1, \beta, \beta^2, \beta+1, \beta^2+1, \beta^2+\beta, \beta^2+\beta+1\}$, all satisfying$x^8 = x$.

**Example 3.** $\mathbb{F}_{9} = \mathbb{F}_{3^2}$: constructed as the splitting field of$x^9 - x$ over$\mathbb{F}_3$. An irreducible quadratic over$\mathbb{F}_3$ is$x^2 + 1$ (no roots:$0^2+1=1$,$1^2+1=2$,$2^2+1=5\equiv 2$). Adjoining$i$ with$i^2 = -1 = 2$ gives$\mathbb{F}_9 = \{0, 1, 2, i, 2i, 1+i, 1+2i, 2+i, 2+2i\}$, all satisfying$x^9 = x$.

**Example 4.** There is no field of order $6$: since$6 = 2 \times 3$ is not a prime power, no field of order$6$ can exist. Proof: the characteristic of any field is prime, so the prime subfield is either$\mathbb{F}_2$ or$\mathbb{F}_3$. If the characteristic is$2$, then$|F|$ is a power of$2$, but$6$ is not a power of$2$. If the characteristic is$3$, then$|F|$ is a power of$3$, but$6$ is not a power of$3$. Contradiction.

**Example 5.** $\mathbb{F}_{p^1} = \mathbb{F}_p$: for$n = 1$,$\mathbb{F}_p$ is the prime field$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ with$p$ elements. Every element satisfies$x^p = x$ (Fermat's little theorem:$a^p \equiv a \pmod p$ for all$a$). So$\mathbb{F}_p$ is the splitting field of$x^p - x = x(x-1)(x-2)\cdots(x-(p-1))$ over itself, consistent with the general construction.
