# 32.3 Finite Fields

Finite fields occupy a unique place in algebra: their structure is completely determined by a single invariant, the number of elements, and this number must be a prime power. For each prime $p$ and positive integer $n$, there exists a unique field of cardinality $p^n$, denoted $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ or $GF(p^n)$. It is the splitting field of the polynomial $x^{p^n} - x$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$: the roots of this polynomial are exactly the $p^n$ elements of the field, and the polynomial separates cleanly into distinct linear factors (its derivative is $-1$, coprime to itself, confirming no repeated roots). Existence follows from the splitting field construction; uniqueness follows from the fact that any two splitting fields of the same polynomial over the same base field are isomorphic.

The Galois group of $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$ is cyclic of order $n$, generated by the Frobenius automorphism $\phi: x \mapsto x^p$. That $\phi$ is an automorphism — not merely an endomorphism — is a special feature of finite fields, and it reflects the surjectivity of the Frobenius map on finite fields (every element is a $p$-th power, since the map $x \mapsto x^p$ is injective on a finite set and hence bijective). The fact that $\phi$ has order exactly $n$ in $\text{Gal}(\mathbb{F}_{p^n}/\mathbb{F}_p)$ follows from the tower of fixed fields: $\phi^k$ fixes exactly $\mathbb{F}_{p^k}$, and $\phi^n = \text{id}$.

The subfield lattice of $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ is completely determined by the Galois correspondence. Since $\text{Gal}(\mathbb{F}_{p^n}/\mathbb{F}_p) \cong \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is cyclic, its subgroups are in bijection with divisors of $n$: for each $d \mid n$, there is a unique subgroup of order $n/d$ (generated by $\phi^d$), and the corresponding fixed field is $\mathbb{F}_{p^d}$. Consequently $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ contains a subfield isomorphic to $\mathbb{F}_{p^d}$ if and only if $d \mid n$, and no other subfields. The lattice of subfields of $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ is anti-isomorphic to the divisibility lattice of $n$ — a clean, complete, and easily visualized classification. This is perhaps the most elegant illustration of the Galois correspondence: a purely arithmetic combinatorial structure (divisors of an integer) perfectly mirrors the algebraic structure (subfields of a finite field), with no complications or exceptional cases whatsoever.
