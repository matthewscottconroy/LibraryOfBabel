# Chapter 32 — Applications of Galois Theory

Abstract theories earn their names by solving concrete problems, and Galois theory has no shortage of triumphs. This chapter collects three of the deepest: the resolution of a question about polynomial equations that had occupied mathematicians for three centuries, the algebraic explanation of why certain classical geometric constructions are impossible, and the complete structure theory of finite fields. Each application demonstrates the same underlying principle — that properties of a field extension are determined by properties of its Galois group, so questions about fields reduce to questions about groups, where combinatorial and structural methods apply.

The first and most celebrated application concerns solvability by radicals. A polynomial is solvable by radicals over $F$ if its roots can be expressed using the field operations together with the extraction of $n$-th roots, iterated any finite number of times. Formulas of this kind exist for polynomials of degree 2, 3, and 4 — the quadratic formula, Cardano's formulas for cubics, and Ferrari's reduction for quartics. The question of a general degree-5 formula had been open since the sixteenth century. Galois gave the definitive answer: a polynomial is solvable by radicals if and only if its Galois group is a solvable group in the group-theoretic sense — one that has a composition series with abelian factors. For the general quintic, the Galois group is the full symmetric group $S_5$, which contains the simple non-abelian group $A_5$ as a composition factor. Because $A_5$ cannot be further resolved into abelian pieces, the general quintic is not solvable by radicals, and no analogue of the quadratic formula can exist in degree five or higher.

The second application transforms the ancient problems of Greek geometry into algebra. A length is constructible by ruler and compass if and only if it belongs to a field extension of $\mathbb{Q}$ whose degree is a power of 2 — because every step of a ruler-and-compass construction either stays in the current field or passes to a degree-2 extension. Doubling the cube requires constructing $\sqrt[3]{2}$, which satisfies an irreducible cubic over $\mathbb{Q}$, making $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}):\mathbb{Q}] = 3$, which is not a power of 2. Trisecting a general angle fails for the same reason: $\cos(20°)$ is a root of $8x^3 - 6x - 1$, an irreducible cubic. Squaring the circle requires constructing $\sqrt{\pi}$, but $\pi$ is transcendental (Lindemann, 1882), hence not algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}$ at all. The positive result in this direction — that the regular 17-gon is constructible — is due to Gauss: $\text{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{17})/\mathbb{Q}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/16\mathbb{Z}$, a cyclic group of order $2^4$, and a group of 2-power order is always solvable through a tower of index-2 subgroups, each yielding a constructible degree-2 step.

The third application concerns finite fields. For each prime $p$ and positive integer $n$, there exists up to isomorphism exactly one field of order $p^n$, denoted $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$. It is the splitting field of $x^{p^n} - x$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$, and it is a Galois extension of $\mathbb{F}_p$ with Galois group $\text{Gal}(\mathbb{F}_{p^n}/\mathbb{F}_p) \cong \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, generated by the Frobenius automorphism $\phi: x \mapsto x^p$. The subfield structure of $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ is completely determined by the Galois correspondence: $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ contains a unique copy of $\mathbb{F}_{p^d}$ for each divisor $d$ of $n$, and no others. The Galois correspondence for cyclic groups thus perfectly mirrors the divisibility lattice of $n$ — a complete and beautiful classification that would be impossible to deduce without the machinery developed in Chapters 29 through 31.
