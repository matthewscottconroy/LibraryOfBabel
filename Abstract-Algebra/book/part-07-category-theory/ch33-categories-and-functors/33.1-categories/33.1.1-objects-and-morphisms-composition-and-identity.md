# 33.1.1 Objects and Morphisms; Composition and Identity

One of the central insights driving twentieth-century mathematics was the recognition that mathematical structures are best understood not in isolation but through the maps between them. Groups are illuminated by homomorphisms, topological spaces by continuous maps, vector spaces by linear transformations. The properties of an object — whether it is free, projective, or simple — are ultimately expressions of how maps into or out of it behave relative to maps into or out of other objects. Category theory takes this observation seriously and axiomatizes it: the fundamental data of a mathematical context is not just what the objects are, but what the maps between them are and how those maps compose.

The formalism begins with a deliberately spare definition. A *category* $\mathcal{C}$ consists of four pieces of data: a collection$\mathrm{ob}(\mathcal{C})$ of *objects*; for each ordered pair of objects$A, B \in \mathrm{ob}(\mathcal{C})$, a set$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A, B)$ of *morphisms* from$A$ to$B$ (also written$f \colon A \to B$); for each triple of objects$A, B, C$, a *composition map*$\circ \colon \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(B, C) \times \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A, B) \to \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A, C)$; and for each object$A$, a distinguished *identity morphism*$\mathrm{id}_{A} \in \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A, A)$. Composition is written$g \circ f$ for "first$f$, then$g$," matching the convention for composing functions. The hom-sets$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A, B)$ are required to be pairwise disjoint: a morphism carries with it the information of its domain and codomain, so the same function might appear as different morphisms if its domain and codomain are not specified.

The identity morphisms and composition must satisfy two axioms. Associativity requires $(h \circ g) \circ f = h \circ (g \circ f)$ whenever all composites are defined, and the unit laws require$f \circ \mathrm{id}_{A} = f = \mathrm{id}_{B} \circ f$ for any$f \colon A \to B$. Associativity ensures that any composite$A \to B \to C \to D$ has an unambiguous meaning regardless of parenthesization; the unit laws ensure that identity morphisms behave as neutral elements. Together, these axioms capture precisely the minimum structure needed for diagram-chasing arguments: one can compose morphisms freely, reparenthesize at will, and always draw well-defined commutative diagrams.

The elegance of the definition is partly in what it omits. There is no assumption that morphisms are functions, that objects have elements, or that composition is commutative. The axioms are purely abstract, and they are satisfied by an enormous range of mathematical contexts. This generality is not a dilution of content but an amplification: any theorem proved from the category axioms alone applies simultaneously to every category, regardless of the nature of its objects and morphisms.

\begin{definition}
A \emph{category} $\mathcal{C}$ consists of:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A collection $\mathrm{ob}(\mathcal{C})$ of \emph{objects}.
  \item For each pair $A, B \in \mathrm{ob}(\mathcal{C})$, a set$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A, B)$ of \emph{morphisms} from$A$ to$B$.
  \item For each triple $A, B, C \in \mathrm{ob}(\mathcal{C})$, a composition map$\circ \colon \mathrm{Hom}(B, C) \times \mathrm{Hom}(A, B) \to \mathrm{Hom}(A, C)$,$(g, f) \mapsto g \circ f$.
  \item For each $A \in \mathrm{ob}(\mathcal{C})$, an identity morphism$\mathrm{id}_{A} \in \mathrm{Hom}(A, A)$.
\end{enumerate}
These data satisfy: (i) $(h \circ g) \circ f = h \circ (g \circ f)$ for all composable triples; (ii)$\mathrm{id}_{B} \circ f = f = f \circ \mathrm{id}_{A}$ for all$f \colon A \to B$.
\end{definition}

**Example 1.** In $\mathbf{Set}$, the objects are sets and the morphisms from$A$ to$B$ are all functions$f \colon A \to B$. Composition is ordinary function composition, and$\mathrm{id}_{A}$ is the identity function on$A$. Associativity and the unit laws are the familiar properties of function composition. This is the simplest and most foundational example; indeed, much of category theory is an effort to abstract the key features of$\mathbf{Set}$.

**Example 2.** In $\mathbf{Grp}$, the objects are groups and the morphisms are group homomorphisms. Composition is the composite of homomorphisms. That the composite of two homomorphisms is again a homomorphism follows from the definition: if$f \colon G \to H$ and$g \colon H \to K$ satisfy$f(ab) = f(a)f(b)$ and$g(xy) = g(x)g(y)$, then$(g \circ f)(ab) = g(f(a)f(b)) = g(f(a))g(f(b))$. The identity morphism at$G$ is the identity homomorphism$\mathrm{id}_{G}(x) = x$.

**Example 3.** Let $R$ be a ring. In$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$, the objects are left$R$-modules and the morphisms are$R$-linear maps. Composition is composition of functions, and the identity at each module is the identity map. The compatibility of composition with the$R$-module structure is again a direct verification. This category is the natural home for linear algebra over$R$ and for homological algebra.

**Example 4.** Any monoid $(M, \cdot, e)$ forms a category$\mathbf{B}M$ with a single object$*$ and morphism set$\mathrm{Hom}(*, *) = M$, where composition is the monoid multiplication and the identity is the unit$e$. The category axioms are precisely the monoid axioms: associativity of composition is associativity of multiplication, and the unit laws are the monoid unit laws. Monoids are thus exactly the one-object categories, and functors out of$\mathbf{B}M$ are exactly$M$-actions on objects in the target category.

**Example 5.** Let $n \geq 0$ be a natural number and form the category$\mathbf{n}$ whose objects are$\{0, 1, \ldots, n-1\}$ and with a unique morphism from$i$ to$j$ whenever$i \leq j$ (and no morphism otherwise). This is the poset$\{0 < 1 < \cdots < n-1\}$ viewed as a category. The category$\mathbf{2}$ has two objects and a single non-identity morphism$0 \to 1$; it is the walking arrow, used to parameterize morphisms in diagrams.

The five examples above span from the mundane to the structural: sets with functions, groups with homomorphisms, modules with linear maps, a monoid abstracted as a one-object category, and a poset abstracted as a category with at most one morphism between any two objects. All satisfy the same axioms. This uniformity is the promise of the categorical framework.
