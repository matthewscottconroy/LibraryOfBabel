# 33.1.2 The Category Axioms: Associativity and Unit Laws

Having set up the raw data of a category — objects, hom-sets, composition, and identity morphisms — the question becomes: what minimal conditions guarantee that reasoning in this context is coherent? The answer is two axioms, and it is worth pausing to appreciate how much work they do and why no fewer axioms would suffice.

The first axiom is *associativity*: $(h \circ g) \circ f = h \circ (g \circ f)$ whenever all composites are defined. This ensures that any sequence of composable morphisms$A \to B \to C \to D \to \cdots$ has an unambiguous meaning regardless of parenthesization. Without associativity, writing$h \circ g \circ f$ without parentheses would be ambiguous, and drawing a commutative diagram would require specifying not just which maps appear but in which order they are composed. With it, one writes$h \circ g \circ f$ without ambiguity, and "diagram chasing" — following arrows around a commutative diagram — becomes a coherent activity. The second axiom is the pair of *unit laws*:$\mathrm{id}_{B} \circ f = f$ and$f \circ \mathrm{id}_{A} = f$ for any$f \colon A \to B$. These say that identity morphisms are genuine neutral elements for composition, acting trivially on either side. They ensure that every hom-set$\mathrm{Hom}(A, A)$ is non-empty (it contains at least$\mathrm{id}_{A}$) and that one can freely insert and remove identity morphisms in a diagram without changing its meaning.

These two axioms are the categorical analogues of the group axioms of associativity and identity (without inverses), and the parallel is instructive. In a monoid, one abstracts the notion of "composition of operations" and retains only what is needed to compose and to have a neutral element. In a category, one abstracts the notion of "structure-preserving map" and retains only what is needed to compose maps coherently with a distinguished identity. The absence of an inverse axiom reflects the key difference between a monoid and a group, and similarly, morphisms in a category need not be invertible — that is the additional condition for an isomorphism (Section 33.2.3).

The payoff is universality. Any result proved from the category axioms alone holds simultaneously in every category: for sets, groups, modules, topological spaces, and beyond. The category axioms support an elaborate theory of diagrams, limits, functors, and adjoints while imposing almost no restrictions on what the objects and morphisms are. This is not because the axioms are vacuous — they are tight enough to prove nontrivial results — but because they capture the essence of composition at its most fundamental.

\begin{remark}
Associativity has a subtle consequence: a composite $f_{n} \circ \cdots \circ f_{2} \circ f_{1}$ of$n$ composable morphisms is well-defined without parentheses, since any parenthesization yields the same result. This is proved by induction and relies on associativity at each step. In a non-associative setting (such as certain loop algebras), this fails, and the order of parenthesization genuinely matters.
\end{remark}

**Example 1.** In $\mathbf{Set}$, associativity is the familiar fact$(h \circ g) \circ f = h \circ (g \circ f)$ for functions: for any$a \in A$,$((h \circ g) \circ f)(a) = (h \circ g)(f(a)) = h(g(f(a)))$ and$(h \circ (g \circ f))(a) = h((g \circ f)(a)) = h(g(f(a)))$. These agree. The unit laws say$(\mathrm{id}_{B} \circ f)(a) = \mathrm{id}_{B}(f(a)) = f(a)$ and$(f \circ \mathrm{id}_{A})(a) = f(\mathrm{id}_{A}(a)) = f(a)$. Both are trivially verified.

**Example 2.** In the poset category associated to a partial order $(P, \leq)$, there is at most one morphism from any object to another, so all equations between composable morphisms hold vacuously. Associativity reduces to: if$a \leq b$,$b \leq c$,$c \leq d$, then transitivity applied in either order gives$a \leq d$ — the same result. The unit law at$a$ corresponds to reflexivity:$a \leq a$ gives the unique identity morphism at$a$.

**Example 3.** Let $G$ be a group, viewed as a one-object category$\mathbf{B}G$. The associativity axiom is exactly the associativity of the group operation:$(h \cdot g) \cdot f = h \cdot (g \cdot f)$ in$G$. The unit law corresponds to the group identity:$e \cdot g = g = g \cdot e$ for all$g \in G$. So the two category axioms, applied to the one-object case, recover two of the three group axioms (associativity and identity). The third group axiom — existence of inverses — has no categorical analogue in general; it is a special property that makes every morphism an isomorphism.

**Example 4.** The *path category* of a directed graph $\Gamma$ illustrates why the axioms are not vacuous in a combinatorial setting. The objects of the path category are the vertices of$\Gamma$; the morphisms from vertex$u$ to vertex$v$ are all directed paths from$u$ to$v$ (including the trivial empty path at each vertex). Composition of paths is concatenation. Associativity holds: the path$(p \cdot q) \cdot r$ traverses the same edges in the same order as$p \cdot (q \cdot r)$. The identity at each vertex is the empty path. This example shows that the category axioms are genuine constraints even in a combinatorial setting and not merely a convenient repackaging.

**Example 5.** In a ring $R$, consider the category$\mathbf{B}R$ with one object$*$ and morphism set$\mathrm{Hom}(*, *) = R$, with "composition" given by multiplication$r \circ s = rs$. Then the associativity axiom is the associativity of ring multiplication, and the unit axiom is the condition that the multiplicative identity$1 \in R$ satisfies$1 \cdot r = r = r \cdot 1$. This is the same construction as for monoids: a ring (ignoring the addition) is a monoid under multiplication, hence a one-object category. Adding back the additive structure on hom-sets will be the subject of Chapter 37 on abelian categories and additive enrichment.

The strength of the axiom system lies in its minimality: associativity and the unit laws are exactly the conditions needed to make sense of diagrams and composites, nothing more. Every theorem in category theory derives ultimately from these two axioms, together with the specific axioms of whatever additional structure (limits, adjoints, enrichment) is imposed.
