# 33.1.4 Examples: $\mathbf{Set}$,$\mathbf{Grp}$,$\mathbf{Ring}$,$R$-$\mathbf{Mod}$,$\mathbf{Top}$, Posets, Groups

The formal definition of a category is only as illuminating as its examples, and here category theory reveals its scope. The most familiar examples are the categories that organize classical mathematics: $\mathbf{Set}$ (sets and functions),$\mathbf{Grp}$ (groups and group homomorphisms),$\mathbf{Ring}$ (unital rings and ring homomorphisms),$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ (left$R$-modules and$R$-linear maps for a fixed ring$R$),$\mathbf{Top}$ (topological spaces and continuous maps), and$\mathbf{Vect}_{F}$ (vector spaces over a field$F$ and linear maps). In each case, the morphisms are the structure-preserving maps, composition is ordinary function composition, and the identity morphisms are the identity functions. The category axioms are satisfied because function composition is associative and identity functions are units for it.

What is more surprising — and more instructive — is the zoo of categories that look nothing like "sets with structure." Any poset $(P, \leq)$ becomes a category: the objects are the elements of$P$, and$\mathrm{Hom}(a, b)$ contains exactly one element (say, a formal symbol$(a \leq b)$) if$a \leq b$, and is empty otherwise. Composition corresponds to transitivity ($a \leq b$ and$b \leq c$ implies$a \leq c$), and the identity morphism at$a$ corresponds to reflexivity ($a \leq a$). The category axioms are: associativity holds trivially (there is at most one morphism between any two objects, so there is nothing to check), and the unit law is reflexivity. Functors between poset-categories are order-preserving maps, and natural transformations correspond to pointwise inequalities ($\eta \colon F \Rightarrow G$ is a natural transformation if$F(a) \leq G(a)$ for all$a$, since naturality is automatic when there is at most one morphism between any two objects). This example shows that category theory subsumes order theory entirely.

Even more striking is the observation that any group $G$ is a category$\mathbf{B}G$ with a single object$*$ and morphism set$\mathrm{Hom}(*, *) = G$, where composition of morphisms is the group multiplication. The identity morphism is the identity element$e \in G$, and associativity of composition is associativity of the group operation. The group axioms say precisely that this data satisfies the category axioms with the added feature that every morphism is an isomorphism (each element$g$ has an inverse$g^{-1}$). Studying functors out of$\mathbf{B}G$ into the category of vector spaces recovers exactly the theory of linear representations of$G$ — this is one of the deep connections between category theory and representation theory.

\begin{example}
The category $\mathbf{Ab}$ of abelian groups and group homomorphisms is a full subcategory of$\mathbf{Grp}$: its objects are those groups satisfying$ab = ba$ for all$a, b$, and its morphisms are all group homomorphisms between them. The inclusion functor$\mathbf{Ab} \hookrightarrow \mathbf{Grp}$ is fully faithful. This subcategory is the natural home for algebraic topology (since fundamental groups of simply connected spaces are trivial and higher homotopy groups are abelian) and for homological algebra.
\end{example}

**Example 1.** In $\mathbf{Set}$, the object$\{1, 2, 3\}$ has$3! = 6$ automorphisms (permutations), forming the symmetric group$S_{3}$. The hom-set$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Set}}(\{1\}, \{a, b, c\})$ has three elements, corresponding to the three functions picking one element of$\{a, b, c\}$. The hom-set$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Set}}(\emptyset, A)$ has exactly one element for every set$A$ (the unique empty function), confirming that$\emptyset$ is the initial object of$\mathbf{Set}$.

**Example 2.** In $\mathbf{Grp}$, the hom-set$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Grp}}(\mathbb{Z}, G)$ is in natural bijection with$|G|$ (the underlying set of$G$): a homomorphism$f \colon \mathbb{Z} \to G$ is determined by$f(1) \in G$, and every element of$G$ arises as$f(1)$ for a unique such$f$. This makes$\mathbb{Z}$ the free group on one generator, and the functor$\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}, -)$ is naturally isomorphic to the forgetful functor$U \colon \mathbf{Grp} \to \mathbf{Set}$.

**Example 3.** The category $\mathbf{Top}$ has all topological spaces as objects and all continuous maps as morphisms. A key feature distinguishing$\mathbf{Top}$ from$\mathbf{Set}$ is that two topological spaces can be homeomorphic (isomorphic in$\mathbf{Top}$) without being equal as sets. For example, the unit interval$[0, 1]$ and the unit circle$S^{1}$ are very different topological spaces, but a loop in$[0, 1]$ is not the same as a loop in$S^{1}$: the fundamental group$\pi_{1}(S^{1}) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ while$\pi_{1}([0,1]) = 0$, reflecting a genuinely categorical difference.

**Example 4.** A *preorder* is a category where each hom-set has at most one element. Any equivalence relation $\sim$ on a set$S$ defines a preorder (hence a category): objects are elements of$S$, and there is a unique morphism from$a$ to$b$ iff$a \sim b$. The category axioms translate to:$a \sim a$ (reflexivity, giving the identity) and if$a \sim b$ and$b \sim c$ then$a \sim c$ (transitivity, giving composition). This includes posets (where$a \sim b$ and$b \sim a$ implies$a = b$) and equivalence relations (which are symmetric preorders).

**Example 5.** The category $\mathbf{Mat}_{F}$ of matrices over a field$F$ has natural numbers$\{0, 1, 2, \ldots\}$ as objects and an$m \times n$ matrix as a morphism from$n$ to$m$. Composition is matrix multiplication: a morphism$A \colon n \to m$ and$B \colon p \to n$ compose to the product$AB \colon p \to m$. The identity at$n$ is the$n \times n$ identity matrix. This category is equivalent (in the categorical sense) to$\mathbf{Vect}_{F}$ restricted to finite-dimensional vector spaces, since any such space has a basis and is isomorphic to$F^{n}$ for some$n$. This equivalence is not an isomorphism — the objects are different — but the categories are indistinguishable for categorical purposes.

The diversity of examples reflects the universality of the categorical framework. One and the same set of axioms captures continuous functions between spaces, order relations between elements, group multiplication, linear algebra over a field, and much more. What unites them all is the structure of composition and identity, and it is this shared structure that category theory exploits.
