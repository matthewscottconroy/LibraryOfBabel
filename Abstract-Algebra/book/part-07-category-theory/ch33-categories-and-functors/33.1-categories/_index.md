# 33.1 Categories

What is a mathematical structure, stripped of all content that is not strictly necessary? This is the question that the definition of a category answers. A category $\mathcal{C}$ consists of a collection of *objects* and, for each ordered pair of objects $(A, B)$, a set $\text{Hom}(A, B)$ of *morphisms* from $A$ to $B$. Morphisms compose: given $f \in \text{Hom}(A, B)$ and $g \in \text{Hom}(B, C)$, there is a composite $g \circ f \in \text{Hom}(A, C)$. Every object $A$ carries a distinguished identity morphism $\text{id}_A \in \text{Hom}(A, A)$. The axioms demand only that composition be associative and that identity morphisms act as two-sided units. Nothing more is required — and this austerity is the source of the definition's power.

The definition is deliberately silent on what objects *are*. In $\mathbf{Set}$ they are sets; in $\mathbf{Grp}$ they are groups; in $\mathbf{Top}$ they are topological spaces; in a poset viewed as a category, they are elements of the poset and there is exactly one morphism $a \to b$ whenever $a \leq b$. A single group $G$ yields a category $\mathbf{B}G$ with one object and morphism set $G$, where composition is the group operation — and from this perspective, a functor out of $\mathbf{B}G$ is exactly a group action. The definition is broad enough to contain all of these and yet structured enough to produce a genuine theory.

The distinction between small and large categories is a technical but necessary point. A category is *small* if its objects form a set (rather than a proper class). The category $\mathbf{Set}$ of all sets is large — its objects do not form a set under standard foundations — but many naturally occurring categories are small, and the distinction matters for the construction of functor categories and for the statement of the adjoint functor theorem. Dealing carefully with size is part of working fluently with categories, and this section introduces the standard conventions used throughout.

The most important early examples — $\mathbf{Set}$, $\mathbf{Grp}$, $\mathbf{Ring}$, $\mathbf{Mod}_R$, $\mathbf{Top}$, posets, and the degenerate one-object categories corresponding to monoids — should be understood not as a list to memorize but as a family of instances that simultaneously justify the definition and train categorical intuition. The opposite category $\mathcal{C}^{\text{op}}$, obtained by formally reversing all arrows, is immediately available: every theorem about categories has a dual obtained by applying it to the opposite category, and this *duality principle* doubles the content of every result proved.
