# 33.2.2 Epimorphisms (Generalized Surjections)

By duality, if monomorphisms generalize injectivity by left-cancellation, then epimorphisms generalize surjectivity by right-cancellation. A morphism $f \colon A \to B$ is an *epimorphism* (or is *epic*) if for any object$C$ and any two morphisms$g, h \colon B \to C$, the equation$g \circ f = h \circ f$ implies$g = h$. In$\mathbf{Set}$, this coincides with surjectivity. If$f$ is surjective and$g \circ f = h \circ f$, then for any$b \in B$ there exists$a$ with$f(a) = b$, so$g(b) = g(f(a)) = h(f(a)) = h(b)$; since$b$ was arbitrary,$g = h$. Conversely, if$f$ is not surjective, pick$b \in B \setminus \mathrm{im}(f)$, and let$g, h \colon B \to \{0, 1\}$ with$g = \chi_{\{b\}}$ (the characteristic function of$\{b\}$) and$h = 0$; then$g \circ f = 0 = h \circ f$ but$g(b) = 1 \neq 0 = h(b)$, so$g \neq h$.

The surprise arrives in other categories. In $\mathbf{Ring}$, the inclusion$\iota \colon \mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Q}$ is an epimorphism despite being far from surjective. The key: if$g, h \colon \mathbb{Q} \to S$ are ring homomorphisms that agree on$\mathbb{Z}$ (meaning$g \circ \iota = h \circ \iota$), then for any rational$p/q$ with$q \neq 0$, one has$g(1) = h(1) = 1_{S}$, so$g(q) = h(q)$ is invertible in$S$ (since both$g$ and$h$ are ring homomorphisms and$q \in \mathbb{Z}$), and
$$g\!\left(\frac{p}{q}\right) = g(p) \cdot g(q)^{-1} = h(p) \cdot h(q)^{-1} = h\!\left(\frac{p}{q}\right).$$
Therefore $g = h$ on all of$\mathbb{Q}$. The morphism$\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Q}$ is "right-dense" in the categorical sense: any two maps out of$\mathbb{Q}$ that agree on$\mathbb{Z}$ are forced to agree everywhere.

This divergence between "surjective" and "epic" is not a pathology — it is a feature that category theory makes visible. The inclusion of any dominant open subset $U \hookrightarrow X$ of an irreducible algebraic variety is an epimorphism in the category of varieties. In the category of commutative rings, ring localizations$R \to S^{-1}R$ are epimorphisms: a ring map out of$S^{-1}R$ is determined by its values on$R$ (since the images of elements of$S$ must be invertible, forcing the values on$S^{-1}R$). In the category of Hausdorff topological spaces, the inclusion$\iota \colon D \hookrightarrow X$ of a dense subspace is always an epimorphism: if$g, h \colon X \to Y$ are continuous maps to a Hausdorff space that agree on the dense subset$D$, then by the density of$D$ and the Hausdorff property,$g = h$ on all of$X$.

\begin{definition}
A morphism $f \colon A \to B$ in a category$\mathcal{C}$ is an \emph{epimorphism} (or is \emph{epic}) if it is right-cancellable: for any object$C$ and any morphisms$g, h \colon B \to C$, the equation$g \circ f = h \circ f$ implies$g = h$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
In any category: (1) identity morphisms are epimorphisms; (2) composites of epimorphisms are epimorphisms; (3) if $g \circ f$ is an epimorphism, then$g$ is an epimorphism.
\end{proposition}

The proof is exactly dual to the proof for monomorphisms.

**Example 1.** In $\mathbf{Ab}$, the quotient map$\pi \colon \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is an epimorphism. If$g, h \colon \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \to A$ satisfy$g \circ \pi = h \circ \pi$, then$g(\bar{k}) = g(\pi(k)) = h(\pi(k)) = h(\bar{k})$ for all$k \in \mathbb{Z}$; since every element of$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is$\bar{k}$ for some$k$, we get$g = h$. More generally, every surjective group homomorphism is an epimorphism in$\mathbf{Grp}$ (by the same argument), and conversely, every epimorphism in$\mathbf{Grp}$ is surjective — this is a non-obvious result that distinguishes$\mathbf{Grp}$ from$\mathbf{Ring}$.

**Example 2.** In $\mathbf{Ring}$, the inclusion$\iota \colon \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Q}$ is a non-surjective epimorphism, as shown above. More generally, for any multiplicative set$S \subseteq R$, the localization map$R \to S^{-1}R$ is an epimorphism in$\mathbf{Ring}$: any ring map$f \colon S^{-1}R \to T$ is determined by$f|_{R}$ via$f(r/s) = f(r) \cdot f(s)^{-1}$ (and$f(s)$ must be invertible in$T$). The same argument as for$\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Q}$ applies.

**Example 3.** In the category $\mathbf{HausTop}$ of Hausdorff topological spaces, the inclusion$\iota \colon \mathbb{Q} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is an epimorphism. If$g, h \colon \mathbb{R} \to Y$ are continuous maps to a Hausdorff space$Y$ that agree on$\mathbb{Q}$, then for any$x \in \mathbb{R}$, pick rationals$q_{n} \to x$; continuity gives$g(x) = \lim g(q_{n}) = \lim h(q_{n}) = h(x)$. So$g = h$.

**Example 4.** In $R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$, the epimorphisms are precisely the surjective module homomorphisms. The proof: if$f \colon M \to N$ is an epimorphism in$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$, consider the two maps$g, h \colon N \to N/\mathrm{im}(f)$ where$g = \pi$ (the quotient map) and$h = 0$. Then$g \circ f = 0 = h \circ f$ (since$f$ lands in$\mathrm{im}(f)$, which maps to$0$ under$\pi$). Epic$f$ gives$g = h$, so$\pi = 0$, meaning$N/\mathrm{im}(f) = 0$, i.e.,$\mathrm{im}(f) = N$ and$f$ is surjective.

**Example 5.** In the category of commutative rings, the ring map $\phi \colon k[x] \to k[x, x^{-1}]$ (inverting$x$) is an epimorphism. Any two ring maps out of$k[x, x^{-1}]$ that agree on$k[x]$ must agree on$x^{-1}$ as well (since$\phi(x)$ is invertible and determines$\phi(x)^{-1}$). This is a special case of the localization epimorphism$R \to S^{-1}R$ with$S = \{1, x, x^{2}, \ldots\}$.

The epimorphism condition captures the idea that $f$ "generates" or "determines"$B$ from the perspective of morphisms out of$B$, which is a weaker and more flexible notion than surjectivity. In sheaf theory and localizations, the morphisms that ought to be "surjective" for categorical purposes are exactly the epimorphisms, not the pointwise surjections. Recognizing this distinction is essential for working with categories where the morphisms carry nontrivial algebraic or topological constraints.
