# 33.2.3 Isomorphisms; Automorphism Groups

The notion of two mathematical structures being "the same" is one of the most fundamental in all of mathematics. In set theory, sameness means equality; but in a category, structures are not identical sets and the correct notion of sameness must be relative to the morphisms available. Category theory formalizes this as the notion of an *isomorphism*: two objects are isomorphic if there is a morphism between them that is invertible with respect to composition. This definition is purely categorical — it makes no reference to elements, underlying sets, or any specific construction.

\begin{definition}
A morphism $f \colon A \to B$ is an \emph{isomorphism} if there exists a morphism$g \colon B \to A$ satisfying$g \circ f = \mathrm{id}_{A}$ and$f \circ g = \mathrm{id}_{B}$. The morphism$g$ is called the \emph{inverse} of$f$ and is denoted$f^{-1}$. Objects$A$ and$B$ are \emph{isomorphic}, written$A \cong B$, if an isomorphism between them exists.
\end{definition}

The inverse, when it exists, is unique: if $g$ and$g'$ are both inverses of$f$, then
$$g = g \circ \mathrm{id}_{B} = g \circ (f \circ g') = (g \circ f) \circ g' = \mathrm{id}_{A} \circ g' = g'.$$
Isomorphism is an equivalence relation on $\mathrm{ob}(\mathcal{C})$: reflexivity uses$\mathrm{id}_{A}$, symmetry exchanges$f$ and$f^{-1}$, and transitivity composes two isomorphisms. Every isomorphism is both a monomorphism and an epimorphism (as one checks from the definitions using$f^{-1}$ as the canceller), though the converse fails in general (Section 33.2.4).

In the standard algebraic and topological categories, the categorical notion recovers the expected ones. Isomorphisms in $\mathbf{Grp}$ are bijective homomorphisms with homomorphic inverse — that is, group isomorphisms in the classical sense. Isomorphisms in$\mathbf{Top}$ are bijective continuous maps with continuous inverse — homeomorphisms. Isomorphisms in$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ are bijective$R$-linear maps — module isomorphisms. The categorical definition makes clear why the inverse must be a morphism of the same type: the "sameness" of two objects is always relative to the category's morphisms.

\begin{remark}
The fact that in $\mathbf{Top}$ the inverse of a continuous bijection need not be continuous — the classic example is$f \colon [0, 1) \to S^{1}$ defined by$f(t) = e^{2\pi i t}$, which is a continuous bijection but not a homeomorphism — illustrates why the categorical definition is the right one: a bijection that is not a homeomorphism is not an isomorphism in$\mathbf{Top}$.
\end{remark}

The *automorphism group* $\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}}(A)$ of an object$A$ in a category$\mathcal{C}$ is the collection of all isomorphisms$A \to A$, equipped with composition as the group operation. That this is a group follows from: the identity$\mathrm{id}_{A}$ is an automorphism (the neutral element), inverses are categorical inverses, and the composite of two isomorphisms is an isomorphism. The automorphism group is exactly the group of "symmetries" of the object$A$ as seen from within the category$\mathcal{C}$.

**Example 1.** In $\mathbf{Set}$,$\mathrm{Aut}(\{1, 2, \ldots, n\}) = S_{n}$, the symmetric group on$n$ letters, whose elements are the bijections of the set to itself. Every symmetric group arises in this way. More generally, for any set$X$,$\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathbf{Set}}(X)$ is the group of all permutations of$X$.

**Example 2.** In $\mathbf{Vect}_{F}$, for a finite-dimensional space$V$ with$\dim V = n$,$\mathrm{Aut}(V) \cong GL_{n}(F)$, the general linear group of invertible$n \times n$ matrices over$F$. This depends on the choice of basis for the identification$V \cong F^{n}$, but the isomorphism type$GL_{n}(F)$ is independent of this choice. More intrinsically,$\mathrm{Aut}(V)$ is the group of invertible linear endomorphisms of$V$, and the general linear groups arise as automorphism groups of vector spaces.

**Example 3.** In $\mathbf{Grp}$,$\mathrm{Aut}(G)$ for a group$G$ is the classical automorphism group: the group of all group automorphisms$G \to G$ under composition. The inner automorphisms$\mathrm{Inn}(G) = \{x \mapsto gxg^{-1} : g \in G\}$ form a normal subgroup of$\mathrm{Aut}(G)$, and the outer automorphism group$\mathrm{Out}(G) = \mathrm{Aut}(G)/\mathrm{Inn}(G)$ measures symmetries not coming from conjugation. The group-theoretic study of automorphisms is thus an instance of the categorical study of automorphism groups.

**Example 4.** In the poset category of a partially ordered set $(P, \leq)$, the only isomorphisms are the identity morphisms: if$f \colon a \to b$ and$g \colon b \to a$ are morphisms (meaning$a \leq b$ and$b \leq a$), then by antisymmetry$a = b$, so$f = g = \mathrm{id}_{a}$. Thus$\mathrm{Aut}(a) = \{e\}$ is the trivial group for every element$a$ of a poset. This reflects the fact that a partially ordered set has no nontrivial symmetries that are visible from the categorical structure.

**Example 5.** In a category $\mathbf{B}G$ with one object$*$ corresponding to a group$G$, every morphism is an isomorphism (each$g \in G$ has inverse$g^{-1}$). The automorphism group$\mathrm{Aut}(*) = G$ is the group itself. This illustrates the general categorical principle: a category in which every morphism is an isomorphism is called a *groupoid*, and a one-object groupoid is precisely a group.

The automorphism group connects category theory to representation theory: to study an object $A$ by its symmetries means to study functors from$\mathrm{Aut}(A)$ (viewed as a group, hence as a one-object category) to categories of interest. The general linear groups, symmetric groups, and other classical groups of symmetries all arise as automorphism groups of objects in appropriate categories — a perspective that unifies Galois theory, representation theory, and topology under the categorical framework.
