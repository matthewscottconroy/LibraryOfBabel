# 33.2.4 Examples Distinguishing Categorical from Set-Theoretic Notions

A natural expectation — reinforced by experience in $\mathbf{Set}$ — is that a morphism which is both monic and epic must be an isomorphism. After all, a function that is both injective and surjective is a bijection, and in$\mathbf{Set}$ that bijection is automatically an isomorphism (its set-theoretic inverse is again a function, hence a morphism). The surprise of categorical thinking is that this implication fails outside$\mathbf{Set}$, and the failure is both instructive and algebraically significant.

The cleanest example is the inclusion $\iota \colon \mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Q}$ in$\mathbf{Ring}$. This morphism is monic: it is an injective ring homomorphism, and injective maps are always left-cancellable (if$\iota \circ g = \iota \circ h$ for ring maps$g, h$ into$\mathbb{Z}$, then$g(x) = \iota(g(x)) = \iota(h(x)) = h(x)$ since$\iota$ is injective). It is also epic, as established in Section 33.2.2: any two ring homomorphisms out of$\mathbb{Q}$ that agree on$\mathbb{Z}$ must agree everywhere. Yet it is obviously not an isomorphism, since no ring homomorphism$\mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{Z}$ can serve as an inverse — any ring homomorphism$\mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{Z}$ would send$1/2$ to an element$n \in \mathbb{Z}$ with$2n = 1$, which is impossible. So$\iota$ is a bimorphism (both monic and epic) that is not an isomorphism.

The resolution to this apparent puzzle lies in recognizing what the categorical notions of monomorphism and epimorphism actually test. A monomorphism tests "left determinacy": once you know what $f$ does to maps into its domain, it separates those maps. An epimorphism tests "right determinacy":$f$ determines maps out of its codomain. In$\mathbf{Set}$, the morphisms are functions, and these two notions perfectly capture injectivity and surjectivity, which together suffice for invertibility. In richer categories, a map can be "right-determining" without being surjective, because maps out of$B$ are more constrained by the algebraic structure of$B$ than arbitrary functions of the underlying set.

The categorical notions also diverge from one another more subtly. In $\mathbf{Ab}$, every epimorphism is surjective (a result specific to this category) and every monomorphism is injective; moreover, every bimorphism in$\mathbf{Ab}$ is an isomorphism. In$\mathbf{Grp}$, the same holds: epimorphisms are surjective, monomorphisms are injective, and bimorphisms are isomorphisms. But in$\mathbf{Ring}$, epimorphisms need not be surjective (as$\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Q}$ shows), and so bimorphisms need not be isomorphisms.

**Example 1.** The inclusion $\iota \colon \mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Q}$ in$\mathbf{Ring}$ is a bimorphism that is not an isomorphism, as described above. This example is archetypal and should be committed to memory: it shows that "bijective ring map" is not the right notion of "same ring" in the categorical sense (since$\iota$ is injective but not surjective, yet still epic).

**Example 2.** In the category $\mathbf{HausTop}$ of Hausdorff topological spaces, the inclusion$\iota \colon \mathbb{Q} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is a bimorphism. It is monic (injective continuous map) and epic (inclusion of a dense subspace in a Hausdorff space; see Section 33.2.2). It is not an isomorphism: there is no continuous map$r \colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{Q}$ with$r \circ \iota = \mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{Q}}$, since$\mathbb{R}$ is connected and$\mathbb{Q}$ is not.

**Example 3.** In the category of commutative rings, any localization $R \to S^{-1}R$ at a multiplicative set$S$ is an epimorphism and a ring map (hence monic if$R \hookrightarrow S^{-1}R$ is injective, which happens when$S$ has no zero-divisors). For example,$\mathbb{Z}[x] \to \mathbb{Z}[x, x^{-1}]$ (invert$x$) is a monic epimorphism that is not an isomorphism:$\mathbb{Z}[x, x^{-1}]$ contains$x^{-1}$, which is not in$\mathbb{Z}[x]$.

**Example 4.** In the category of topological abelian groups (which is additive but not abelian in the categorical sense), the inclusion $\iota \colon \mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}$ (with the subspace topology) is a monic and epic morphism that is not an isomorphism. This example illustrates why the *isomorphism condition* — that every monic-and-epic must be an isomorphism — is one of the key axioms of abelian categories (Section 37.2.1). Categories that fail this condition are genuinely outside the abelian world.

**Example 5.** In $R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ for any ring$R$, every bimorphism (monic-and-epic) is an isomorphism. This is because monomorphisms are injective, epimorphisms are surjective (see Section 33.2.2, Example 4 for the proof that epimorphisms in$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ are surjective), and a bijective module homomorphism is automatically an isomorphism (its set-theoretic inverse is$R$-linear). This confirms that$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ is an abelian category (where bimorphisms are isomorphisms), in contrast to$\mathbf{Ring}$ (where they need not be).

Recognizing these distinctions — between being bijective on underlying sets and being an isomorphism categorically, between surjective and epic, between injective and monic — is essential for working fluently in abstract categories. The categorical notions are not weakened versions of the set-theoretic ones; they are the appropriate generalizations that respect the morphism structure of the category and that support the full machinery of universal properties, limits, and adjoints.
