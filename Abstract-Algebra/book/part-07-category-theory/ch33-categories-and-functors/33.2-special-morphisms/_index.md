# 33.2 Special Morphisms

Once a category is in hand, the first task is to classify morphisms by their structural behavior. In set theory, functions are classified as injective, surjective, or bijective based on their behavior on elements. Category theory provides purely arrow-theoretic replacements for these notions — definitions that make no reference to elements and that therefore apply in any category, even when objects have no elements in any meaningful sense.

A morphism $f: A \to B$ is a *monomorphism* if it is left-cancellable: whenever $f \circ g = f \circ h$, it follows that $g = h$. This captures injectivity without mentioning elements. In $\mathbf{Set}$ and $\mathbf{Grp}$ and $\mathbf{Mod}_R$, monomorphisms are exactly injective maps — but the categorical definition works equally well in the category of schemes, sheaves, or any other structure. Dually, $f$ is an *epimorphism* if it is right-cancellable: $g \circ f = h \circ f$ implies $g = h$. Surjective maps are always epimorphisms, but the converse can fail in striking ways: the inclusion $\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Q}$ is an epimorphism in $\mathbf{Ring}$ even though it is far from surjective, because any two ring homomorphisms out of $\mathbb{Q}$ that agree on $\mathbb{Z}$ must agree everywhere.

An *isomorphism* is a morphism $f: A \to B$ for which there exists a two-sided inverse $f^{-1}: B \to A$ satisfying $f^{-1} \circ f = \text{id}_A$ and $f \circ f^{-1} = \text{id}_B$. Every isomorphism is both a monomorphism and an epimorphism, but the converse is false in general — the $\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Q}$ example is again a counterexample. A morphism that is both a monomorphism and an epimorphism but not an isomorphism is called a *bimorphism that is not an isomorphism*, and its existence in a category signals a lack of certain completeness properties. In $\mathbf{Set}$, $\mathbf{Ab}$, and $\mathbf{Mod}_R$, every bimorphism is indeed an isomorphism, reflecting the well-behaved nature of these categories.

The *automorphism group* $\text{Aut}(A)$ of an object $A$ — the set of isomorphisms from $A$ to itself — is a group under composition. This is a categorical fact that holds in any category, and it connects the categorical framework back to the group theory developed in earlier chapters. The automorphism groups of objects in concrete categories recover familiar structures: $\text{Aut}(A)$ in $\mathbf{Set}$ for a finite set $A$ of size $n$ is $S_n$; in $\mathbf{Vect}_k$ for an $n$-dimensional space it is $\text{GL}_n(k)$; in $\mathbf{Grp}$ it is the automorphism group of a group in the classical sense.
