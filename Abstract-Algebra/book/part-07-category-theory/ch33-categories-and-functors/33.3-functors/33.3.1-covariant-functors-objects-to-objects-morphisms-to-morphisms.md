# 33.3.1 Covariant Functors: Objects to Objects, Morphisms to Morphisms

Having defined categories as the contexts in which mathematical structures live, the next question is: what should the "maps between contexts" be? A group homomorphism preserves the group operations; a continuous map preserves open sets; a ring homomorphism preserves addition and multiplication. In each case, the key is that the map sends structure to structure compatibly. A functor is the analogue for categories: it sends objects to objects and morphisms to morphisms in a way that respects composition and identity, translating one categorical context into another.

The intuition is that a functor is to categories what a homomorphism is to groups — a structure-preserving map — but now the "structure" being preserved is the entire system of objects, morphisms, and composition. When one has a functor $F \colon \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$, one can "push" any diagram in$\mathcal{C}$ forward to a diagram in$\mathcal{D}$, and commutativity of the original diagram is preserved. This makes functors the mechanism by which one transfers information between mathematical contexts: from topology to algebra, from one algebraic structure to another, from a category to its opposite.

\begin{definition}
A \emph{covariant functor} $F \colon \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ consists of:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item An assignment $A \mapsto F(A)$ of objects of$\mathcal{D}$ to objects of$\mathcal{C}$.
  \item For each pair of objects $A, B \in \mathcal{C}$, a function$F \colon \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A, B) \to \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{D}}(F(A), F(B))$.
\end{enumerate}
These must satisfy: (i) $F(g \circ f) = F(g) \circ F(f)$ for all composable pairs; (ii)$F(\mathrm{id}_{A}) = \mathrm{id}_{F(A)}$ for all$A \in \mathcal{C}$.
\end{definition}

The name "covariant" distinguishes this from contravariant functors (Section 33.3.2): a covariant functor preserves the direction of arrows. Condition (i) says that $F$ respects composition — applying$F$ to a composite is the same as composing the images. Condition (ii) says that$F$ sends identity morphisms to identity morphisms. Together, these ensure that$F$ translates any commutative diagram in$\mathcal{C}$ to a commutative diagram in$\mathcal{D}$: if$h \circ g \circ f = k$ in$\mathcal{C}$, then$F(h) \circ F(g) \circ F(f) = F(k)$ in$\mathcal{D}$.

The ubiquitous example in topology is the fundamental group functor $\pi_{1} \colon \mathbf{Top}_{*} \to \mathbf{Grp}$ from pointed topological spaces to groups. A pointed continuous map$f \colon (X, x_{0}) \to (Y, y_{0})$ induces a group homomorphism$f_{*} \colon \pi_{1}(X, x_{0}) \to \pi_{1}(Y, y_{0})$ by sending a loop$\gamma$ based at$x_{0}$ to the loop$f \circ \gamma$ based at$y_{0}$. The functoriality conditions say that the identity map on$(X, x_{0})$ induces the identity homomorphism on$\pi_{1}(X, x_{0})$, and that the induced map of a composite is the composite of induced maps:$(g \circ f)_{*} = g_{*} \circ f_{*}$. Both are straightforward to verify from the definition of the induced map, and the entire classical theory of induced homomorphisms in algebraic topology is the statement that$\pi_{1}$ is a functor.

**Example 1.** The forgetful functor $U \colon \mathbf{Grp} \to \mathbf{Set}$ sends each group$G$ to its underlying set$U(G)$ and each group homomorphism$f \colon G \to H$ to the same function$f$ viewed as a map of sets. The functoriality conditions are trivial:$U(g \circ f) = g \circ f = U(g) \circ U(f)$ and$U(\mathrm{id}_{G}) = \mathrm{id}_{U(G)}$. This is a covariant functor that "forgets" the group structure. Analogous forgetful functors exist for rings, modules, and topological spaces.

**Example 2.** For a fixed ring $R$ and a fixed right$R$-module$N$, the tensor product defines a covariant functor$- \otimes_{R} N \colon R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod} \to \mathbf{Ab}$. An$R$-module$M$ maps to$M \otimes_{R} N$, and an$R$-linear map$f \colon M \to M'$ maps to$f \otimes \mathrm{id}_{N} \colon M \otimes_{R} N \to M' \otimes_{R} N$. The functoriality conditions follow from the universal property of the tensor product.

**Example 3.** The abelianization functor $\mathrm{Ab} \colon \mathbf{Grp} \to \mathbf{Ab}$ sends a group$G$ to its abelianization$G^{\mathrm{ab}} = G/[G, G]$ and a homomorphism$f \colon G \to H$ to the induced map$f^{\mathrm{ab}} \colon G^{\mathrm{ab}} \to H^{\mathrm{ab}}$ (well-defined because$f([G,G]) \subseteq [H, H]$). One verifies$\mathrm{Ab}(g \circ f) = \mathrm{Ab}(g) \circ \mathrm{Ab}(f)$ and$\mathrm{Ab}(\mathrm{id}_{G}) = \mathrm{id}_{G^{\mathrm{ab}}}$ directly. The abelianization functor is left adjoint to the inclusion$\mathbf{Ab} \hookrightarrow \mathbf{Grp}$ (Chapter 35).

**Example 4.** The free module functor $F \colon \mathbf{Set} \to R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ sends a set$S$ to the free$R$-module$R^{(S)} = \bigoplus_{s \in S} R$ with basis$S$, and sends a function$f \colon S \to T$ to the$R$-linear map$F(f) \colon R^{(S)} \to R^{(T)}$ extending$f$ (sending the basis element$e_{s}$ to$e_{f(s)}$). Functoriality follows because$F(g \circ f)$ and$F(g) \circ F(f)$ agree on the basis$S$ and linear maps are determined by their values on a basis.

**Example 5.** The covariant hom-functor: for a fixed object $A$ in a locally small category$\mathcal{C}$, the assignment$B \mapsto \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A, B)$ defines a covariant functor$\mathrm{Hom}(A, -) \colon \mathcal{C} \to \mathbf{Set}$. A morphism$f \colon B \to C$ maps to the post-composition function$f_{*} \colon \mathrm{Hom}(A, B) \to \mathrm{Hom}(A, C)$ defined by$f_{*}(g) = f \circ g$. Functoriality:$(g \circ f)_{*}(h) = g \circ f \circ h = g_{*}(f_{*}(h))$ and$(\mathrm{id}_{B})_{*}(h) = h$. These hom-functors are the backbone of the Yoneda theory developed in Chapter 34.

Every time mathematics passes from a structure to an associated structure through a natural assignment — from spaces to groups, from rings to modules, from groups to their abelianizations — it is defining a functor. Recognizing which constructions are functorial is a key step in organizing mathematical knowledge and making it transportable from one context to another.
