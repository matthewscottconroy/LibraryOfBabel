# 33.3.2 Contravariant Functors: Arrow Reversal

Not all natural constructions preserve the direction of maps. When one passes from a vector space $V$ to its dual space$V^{*} = \mathrm{Hom}_{F}(V, F)$, a linear map$f \colon V \to W$ induces a map in the opposite direction: the pullback$f^{*} \colon W^{*} \to V^{*}$, sending a functional$\phi \in W^{*}$ to$\phi \circ f \in V^{*}$. If$f$ goes from$V$ to$W$, then$f^{*}$ goes from$W^{*}$ to$V^{*}$ — the direction is reversed. This reversal is the paradigm for a *contravariant functor*.

The formal definition is obtained from that of a covariant functor by reversing the direction of the morphism assignment. A *contravariant functor* $F \colon \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ assigns to each object$A \in \mathcal{C}$ an object$F(A) \in \mathcal{D}$, and to each morphism$f \colon A \to B$ in$\mathcal{C}$ a morphism$F(f) \colon F(B) \to F(A)$ in$\mathcal{D}$ — note the reversal of domain and codomain. The functoriality conditions become:$F(g \circ f) = F(f) \circ F(g)$ (composition reverses order) and$F(\mathrm{id}_{A}) = \mathrm{id}_{F(A)}$. The simplest and most useful way to think about a contravariant functor$\mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ is as a covariant functor$\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}} \to \mathcal{D}$: passing to the opposite category formally reverses all arrows, converting contravariance into covariance. This equivalence is not just notational — it means every theorem about covariant functors applies to contravariant functors when rephrased using opposite categories.

\begin{definition}
A \emph{contravariant functor} $F \colon \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ is equivalently a covariant functor$F \colon \mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}} \to \mathcal{D}$. Explicitly,$F$ assigns objects to objects and sends$f \colon A \to B$ in$\mathcal{C}$ to$F(f) \colon F(B) \to F(A)$ in$\mathcal{D}$, with$F(g \circ f) = F(f) \circ F(g)$ and$F(\mathrm{id}_{A}) = \mathrm{id}_{F(A)}$.
\end{definition}

The paradigm example in algebra is the contravariant hom-functor. For a fixed module $M$ in$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$, the assignment$N \mapsto \mathrm{Hom}_{R}(N, M)$ defines a contravariant functor$\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(-, M) \colon R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod} \to \mathbf{Ab}$. An$R$-linear map$f \colon N' \to N$ induces a map in the opposite direction by precomposition:$f^{*} \colon \mathrm{Hom}_{R}(N, M) \to \mathrm{Hom}_{R}(N', M)$,$f^{*}(\phi) = \phi \circ f$. The contravariance is exactly the source of the left-exactness of$\mathrm{Hom}$ and the genesis of Ext groups in homological algebra. The contravariant functor$\mathrm{Hom}(-, M)$ converts injections into surjections (partially), and its failure to convert arbitrary surjections into injections is measured by$\mathrm{Ext}^{1}_{R}(-, M)$.

**Example 1.** The dual vector space functor $(-)^{*} \colon \mathbf{Vect}_{F} \to \mathbf{Vect}_{F}$ sending$V \mapsto V^{*} = \mathrm{Hom}_{F}(V, F)$ is a contravariant functor: a linear map$f \colon V \to W$ induces$f^{*} \colon W^{*} \to V^{*}$ by$f^{*}(\phi) = \phi \circ f$. The contravariance:$(g \circ f)^{*}(\phi) = \phi \circ (g \circ f) = (\phi \circ g) \circ f = f^{*}(g^{*}(\phi))$, so$(g \circ f)^{*} = f^{*} \circ g^{*}$. This reversal is the reason that the matrix of the dual map is the transpose of the original matrix.

**Example 2.** In algebraic geometry, the "regular functions" contravariant functor $\mathcal{O} \colon \mathbf{AlgVar}^{\mathrm{op}} \to \mathbf{CRing}$ sends an algebraic variety$X$ to its coordinate ring$\mathcal{O}(X) = k[X]$, and a morphism$f \colon X \to Y$ to the pullback ring map$f^{*} \colon k[Y] \to k[X]$,$f^{*}(\phi) = \phi \circ f$. This contravariant functor is the basis of the duality between geometry (varieties) and algebra (rings) that underlies algebraic geometry.

**Example 3.** The power set functor $\mathcal{P} \colon \mathbf{Set}^{\mathrm{op}} \to \mathbf{Set}$ sends a set$S$ to$\mathcal{P}(S)$ and a function$f \colon S \to T$ to the preimage map$f^{-1} \colon \mathcal{P}(T) \to \mathcal{P}(S)$,$f^{-1}(B) = \{a \in S : f(a) \in B\}$. This is contravariant:$(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$, reversing the order. This contravariant power set functor is used in the theory of Boolean algebras and in the semantics of predicate logic.

**Example 4.** Singular cohomology gives a contravariant functor $H^{n}(-; G) \colon \mathbf{Top}^{\mathrm{op}} \to \mathbf{Ab}$ for each$n \geq 0$ and abelian group$G$. A continuous map$f \colon X \to Y$ induces a map$f^{*} \colon H^{n}(Y; G) \to H^{n}(X; G)$ (the contravariant induced map) by pulling back cocycles. This contravariance is the reason that cohomology "goes the wrong way" compared to homology, and it is the source of its richer algebraic structure (cup products, etc.).

**Example 5.** For any ring $R$ and fixed right$R$-module$M$, the functor$\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(-, M) \colon R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}^{\mathrm{op}} \to \mathbf{Ab}$ is contravariant. Applied to the short exact sequence$0 \to A \xrightarrow{f} B \xrightarrow{g} C \to 0$, it yields the left-exact sequence$0 \to \mathrm{Hom}(C, M) \xrightarrow{g^{*}} \mathrm{Hom}(B, M) \xrightarrow{f^{*}} \mathrm{Hom}(A, M)$. The failure of the last map$f^{*}$ to be surjective in general is measured by$\mathrm{Ext}^{1}_{R}(C, M)$. The contravariant nature of$\mathrm{Hom}(-, M)$ is fundamental to this story.

Contravariant functors appear throughout mathematics wherever a construction "transposes" maps: cohomology theories, sheaves of functions, the algebraic geometry functor sending a variety to its ring of regular functions, and the operator algebra functor sending a $C^{*}$-algebra to its state space are all contravariant in nature. The reinterpretation as a covariant functor on the opposite category allows all theorems about covariant functors to apply, with arrows reversed.
