# 33.3.3 Full, Faithful, and Essentially Surjective Functors

Not all functors are equally faithful translators between categories. Some miss morphisms, some conflate distinct morphisms, and some fail to reach every isomorphism class of objects in the target. These different modes of failure — and success — are captured by three properties: faithfulness, fullness, and essential surjectivity. Together, their combination gives the correct categorical notion of "sameness" for categories: an equivalence.

A functor $F \colon \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ is *faithful* if, for every pair of objects$A, B \in \mathcal{C}$, the map$F_{A,B} \colon \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A, B) \to \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{D}}(F(A), F(B))$ is injective. Faithfulness means$F$ does not conflate distinct morphisms: different maps$A \to B$ in$\mathcal{C}$ yield different maps$F(A) \to F(B)$ in$\mathcal{D}$. A functor is *full* if each such map$F_{A,B}$ is surjective: every morphism$F(A) \to F(B)$ in$\mathcal{D}$ arises as$F(f)$ for some$f \colon A \to B$ in$\mathcal{C}$. A *fully faithful* functor is both full and faithful, meaning$F_{A,B}$ is a bijection for all$A, B$: the morphism sets are in exact correspondence. A functor is *essentially surjective* if every object$D \in \mathcal{D}$ is isomorphic to$F(C)$ for some$C \in \mathcal{C}$.

\begin{definition}
A functor $F \colon \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ is an \emph{equivalence of categories} if it is full, faithful, and essentially surjective. Two categories$\mathcal{C}$ and$\mathcal{D}$ are \emph{equivalent}, written$\mathcal{C} \simeq \mathcal{D}$, if an equivalence between them exists.
\end{definition}

Equivalence is the correct notion of "sameness" for categories. Two equivalent categories are indistinguishable for all categorical purposes — any categorical statement (existence of limits, properties of functors, adjunctions) that holds in one holds in the other. Equivalence is strictly weaker than isomorphism of categories, which would require a functor $F$ with a strict inverse$G$ (meaning$G \circ F = \mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{C}}$ and$F \circ G = \mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{D}}$ on the nose). In practice, isomorphism of categories is too strong a notion — it would require$F(G(D)) = D$ exactly rather than merely$F(G(D)) \cong D$ — and equivalence is what arises naturally.

The formal criterion for equivalence via a single functor (full, faithful, essentially surjective) is equivalent to the existence of a quasi-inverse: a functor $G \colon \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{C}$ with natural isomorphisms$G \circ F \cong \mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{C}}$ and$F \circ G \cong \mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{D}}$. The equivalence between these formulations uses the axiom of choice (to construct$G$ from essential surjectivity) and is a fundamental theorem in category theory.

**Example 1.** The forgetful functor $U \colon \mathbf{Grp} \to \mathbf{Set}$ is faithful but not full. It is faithful: two distinct homomorphisms$f, g \colon G \to H$ are distinct as functions, so$U(f) \neq U(g)$. It is not full: not every function$U(G) \to U(H)$ is a group homomorphism. It is not essentially surjective (trivially, since every set has a group structure and$U$ hits all sets up to bijection — so actually$U$ is essentially surjective, but not full). The faithful, non-full nature of$U$ reflects that$\mathbf{Grp}$ has more morphisms (requiring homomorphism conditions) than$\mathbf{Set}$.

**Example 2.** The inclusion functor $\iota \colon \mathbf{Ab} \hookrightarrow \mathbf{Grp}$ is full and faithful. It is faithful: distinct homomorphisms between abelian groups remain distinct in$\mathbf{Grp}$. It is full: a group homomorphism between two abelian groups is automatically a morphism in$\mathbf{Ab}$ (the commutativity condition is a property of the groups, not the morphism). But$\iota$ is not essentially surjective: non-abelian groups (like$S_{3}$) are not isomorphic to any abelian group. So$\iota$ is a fully faithful non-equivalence.

**Example 3.** The functor $F \colon \mathbf{Set} \to \mathbf{Set}$ sending every set to the two-element set$\{0, 1\}$ and every function to the identity on$\{0, 1\}$ is neither faithful nor full (distinct sets map to the same object). This is an essentially surjective functor that fails both faithfulness and fullness, illustrating that essential surjectivity alone is far from sufficient for equivalence.

**Example 4.** The category $\mathbf{FDVect}_{F}$ of finite-dimensional$F$-vector spaces is equivalent to the category$\mathbf{Mat}_{F}$ of matrices over$F$ (whose objects are natural numbers and morphisms$n \to m$ are$m \times n$ matrices, with composition given by matrix multiplication). The equivalence functor sends each$n$-dimensional space$V$ to the natural number$n$ (after choosing a basis). This is essentially surjective (every natural number$n$ arises as the dimension of$F^{n}$), and the bijection on morphisms is the correspondence between linear maps and matrices via bases. This is the prototype for equivalences of categories arising from a "skeleton" construction.

**Example 5.** Morita equivalence: for two rings $R$ and$S$, the module categories$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ and$S\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ are equivalent if and only if$R$ and$S$ are Morita equivalent (which happens, for example, when$S \cong M_{n}(R)$ for some$n$). The equivalence functor is given by tensoring with a suitable bimodule. This is one of the most important applications of category equivalence in algebra: it shows that$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ and$M_{n}(R)\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ have exactly the same categorical structure, even though$R$ and$M_{n}(R)$ are very different rings.

The three properties — faithfulness, fullness, essential surjectivity — give a fine-grained vocabulary for measuring how well a functor translates between categories. A fully faithful functor embeds $\mathcal{C}$ into$\mathcal{D}$ without losing morphism information; adding essential surjectivity makes it an equivalence. In practice, many of the most important functors in mathematics are equivalences or fully faithful embeddings, and identifying them as such is a key step in understanding the relationship between different mathematical structures.
