# 33.3.4 Examples: Forgetful, Free, Hom, Power Set, Fundamental Group

Functors are not exotic constructions invented for categorical purposes — they are the organizing principle behind constructions that appear throughout mathematics. The most important examples deserve careful discussion, both because they appear constantly in practice and because they illustrate the full range of what a functor can do.

*Forgetful functors* are the simplest: they strip away structure, retaining only part of what is present. The functor $U \colon \mathbf{Grp} \to \mathbf{Set}$ sends a group$G$ to its underlying set$|G|$ and a group homomorphism to the underlying function. These functors are always faithful — distinct homomorphisms are distinct as functions — but rarely full, since not every function between underlying sets respects the algebraic structure. Forgetful functors will acquire deeper significance in Chapter 35, where they appear systematically as right adjoints to free functors. The same pattern applies to forgetful functors$\mathbf{Ring} \to \mathbf{Ab}$ (forget multiplicative structure),$\mathbf{Top} \to \mathbf{Set}$ (forget topology), and$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod} \to \mathbf{Ab}$ (forget the$R$-action).

*Free functors* go in the opposite direction, freely generating the most general possible structure. The free group functor $F \colon \mathbf{Set} \to \mathbf{Grp}$ sends a set$S$ to the free group$F(S)$ on$S$ as generators — the group of all reduced words in the symbols$s, s^{-1}$ for$s \in S$. A function$f \colon S \to T$ extends uniquely to a homomorphism$F(f) \colon F(S) \to F(T)$ sending each generator$s$ to$f(s)$. Free functors are left adjoint to their corresponding forgetful functors (the free-forgetful adjunction, discussed in detail in Section 35.1.4), and the universal property of free objects is the content of this adjunction.

The *hom-functors* form a bifunctor $\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(-, -) \colon \mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}} \times \mathcal{C} \to \mathbf{Set}$. For a fixed object$A$, the covariant functor$\mathrm{Hom}(A, -) \colon \mathcal{C} \to \mathbf{Set}$ sends$B$ to$\mathrm{Hom}(A, B)$ and$f \colon B \to C$ to post-composition$f_{*}$; the contravariant functor$\mathrm{Hom}(-, A) \colon \mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}} \to \mathbf{Set}$ sends$B$ to$\mathrm{Hom}(B, A)$ and$f \colon B \to C$ to pre-composition$f^{*}$. These hom-functors are the backbone of the Yoneda theory: a general functor$F \colon \mathcal{C} \to \mathbf{Set}$ is representable if it is naturally isomorphic to$\mathrm{Hom}(A, -)$ for some object$A$, and the Yoneda lemma characterizes natural transformations from$\mathrm{Hom}(A, -)$ to any$F$ in terms of elements of$F(A)$.

**Example 1.** The free abelian group functor $F \colon \mathbf{Set} \to \mathbf{Ab}$ sends a set$S$ to$\mathbb{Z}^{(S)} = \bigoplus_{s \in S} \mathbb{Z}$, the free abelian group with basis$S$. A function$f \colon S \to T$ extends uniquely to a homomorphism$F(f) \colon \mathbb{Z}^{(S)} \to \mathbb{Z}^{(T)}$ by$F(f)(e_{s}) = e_{f(s)}$, where$\{e_{s}\}$ is the standard basis. The universal property says: every function from$S$ into an abelian group$A$ extends uniquely to a homomorphism from$\mathbb{Z}^{(S)}$ to$A$. This is the content of the adjunction$F \dashv U$ where$U \colon \mathbf{Ab} \to \mathbf{Set}$ is the forgetful functor.

**Example 2.** The power set functor $\mathcal{P} \colon \mathbf{Set} \to \mathbf{Set}$ (covariant version) sends a set$S$ to its power set$\mathcal{P}(S)$ and a function$f \colon S \to T$ to the direct image map$f_{*} \colon \mathcal{P}(S) \to \mathcal{P}(T)$,$f_{*}(A) = f(A) = \{f(a) : a \in A\}$. Functoriality:$(g \circ f)_{*}(A) = (g \circ f)(A) = g(f(A)) = g_{*}(f_{*}(A))$. The contravariant version sends$f$ to the preimage$f^{-1} \colon \mathcal{P}(T) \to \mathcal{P}(S)$. Both versions arise in sheaf theory and in the study of Boolean algebras.

**Example 3.** The fundamental group functor $\pi_{1} \colon \mathbf{Top}_{*} \to \mathbf{Grp}$, as discussed in Section 33.3.1, assigns to each pointed space$(X, x_{0})$ the group of homotopy classes of loops based at$x_{0}$ and sends pointed maps to induced homomorphisms. A key consequence of functoriality: if$f \colon (X, x_{0}) \to (Y, y_{0})$ is a homotopy equivalence (an isomorphism in$\mathbf{Top}_{*}$, up to based homotopy), then$f_{*} \colon \pi_{1}(X, x_{0}) \to \pi_{1}(Y, y_{0})$ is a group isomorphism. This is because functors send isomorphisms to isomorphisms.

**Example 4.** The singular homology functors $H_{n} \colon \mathbf{Top} \to \mathbf{Ab}$ for$n \geq 0$ assign to each topological space$X$ its$n$-th singular homology group$H_{n}(X; \mathbb{Z})$ and to each continuous map$f \colon X \to Y$ the induced homomorphism$f_{*} \colon H_{n}(X) \to H_{n}(Y)$. These are covariant functors. Functoriality is a fundamental theorem:$(g \circ f)_{*} = g_{*} \circ f_{*}$ and$(\mathrm{id}_{X})_{*} = \mathrm{id}_{H_{n}(X)}$. This is how one proves, for instance, that a retract of a space has homology groups that are direct summands.

**Example 5.** The Galois correspondence in field theory can be expressed as a contravariant functor from intermediate fields (of a Galois extension $K/F$) to subgroups of the Galois group$\mathrm{Gal}(K/F)$. The functor sends an intermediate field$E$ to$\mathrm{Gal}(K/E)$ and an inclusion of fields$E \subseteq E'$ to the inclusion of subgroups$\mathrm{Gal}(K/E') \subseteq \mathrm{Gal}(K/E)$ (in the opposite direction). The Galois correspondence is an equivalence of categories (for finite Galois extensions) between the opposite of the poset of intermediate fields and the poset of subgroups of the Galois group.

What all these examples share is the pattern of a natural, choice-free assignment of structure to structure and of maps to maps. The functorial structure is not an optional addition — it is the mathematically essential content of the construction, capturing how morphisms (the maps between structures) transform under the functor. Functoriality is what distinguishes a natural mathematical construction from an arbitrary assignment.
