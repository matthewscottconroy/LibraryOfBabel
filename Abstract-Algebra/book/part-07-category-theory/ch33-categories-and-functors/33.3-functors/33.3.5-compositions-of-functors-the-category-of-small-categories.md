# 33.3.5 Compositions of Functors; the Category of Small Categories

Just as morphisms in a category compose, functors compose: if $F \colon \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ and$G \colon \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{E}$, then the composite$G \circ F \colon \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{E}$ is the functor defined by$(G \circ F)(A) = G(F(A))$ on objects and$(G \circ F)(f) = G(F(f))$ on morphisms. One verifies immediately that$G \circ F$ is a functor:$(G \circ F)(g \circ f) = G(F(g \circ f)) = G(F(g) \circ F(f)) = G(F(g)) \circ G(F(f)) = (G \circ F)(g) \circ (G \circ F)(f)$, and$(G \circ F)(\mathrm{id}_{A}) = G(F(\mathrm{id}_{A})) = G(\mathrm{id}_{F(A)}) = \mathrm{id}_{G(F(A))} = \mathrm{id}_{(G \circ F)(A)}$. There is also an identity functor$\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{C}} \colon \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{C}$ for each category$\mathcal{C}$, sending each object and morphism to itself.

The key observation is that functors and categories form a category in their own right. The *category of small categories*, denoted $\mathbf{Cat}$, has small categories as objects and functors between them as morphisms. Composition of morphisms in$\mathbf{Cat}$ is composition of functors, the identity morphisms are the identity functors, and the associativity and unit laws follow from those of function composition. This "meta-category" is a natural home for category-theoretic arguments that treat categories themselves as mathematical objects to be compared and related.

The formation of composite functors respects the properties of fullness, faithfulness, and essential surjectivity. Composites of faithful functors are faithful; composites of full functors are full; composites of equivalences are equivalences. These facts follow directly from the definitions: if $F_{A,B}$ and$G_{F(A), F(B)}$ are injective (faithful), then$(G \circ F)_{A,B} = G_{F(A),F(B)} \circ F_{A,B}$ is injective (composite of injections); similarly for surjectivity. This makes the subcategory of$\mathbf{Cat}$ consisting of equivalences closed under composition — the equivalences form the "isomorphisms" of$\mathbf{Cat}$ in the appropriate sense.

\begin{remark}
$\mathbf{Cat}$ itself is a large category (its objects form a proper class) but locally small: functors between two small categories form a legitimate set, since a functor is determined by its values on objects and morphisms, both of which are sets when the source category is small. The size issue (Section 33.1.3) reappears here: the category of all (not just small) categories is even larger, and one must be careful about which universe one is working in.
\end{remark}

Beyond vertical composition, there is also *horizontal composition* of natural transformations, which adds a second dimension to $\mathbf{Cat}$ and makes it the prototype of a *2-category*. A 2-category has objects (here: categories), morphisms between objects (here: functors), and morphisms between morphisms (here: natural transformations). The two types of composition for natural transformations — vertical (composing natural transformations between the same pair of functors) and horizontal (composing natural transformations associated to composable functors) — satisfy the *interchange law*, and together they give$\mathbf{Cat}$ a rich 2-categorical structure.

**Example 1.** The composite $\pi_{1} \circ \mathrm{Top}_{*} \xrightarrow{U} \mathbf{Top}_{*} \xrightarrow{\pi_{1}} \mathbf{Grp}$ is the fundamental group functor itself (where$U$ is the inclusion of pointed spaces into$\mathbf{Top}_{*}$). More interesting: the composite of the abelianization functor$\mathrm{Ab} \colon \mathbf{Grp} \to \mathbf{Ab}$ with$\pi_{1} \colon \mathbf{Top}_{*} \to \mathbf{Grp}$ gives the first homology functor$H_{1}(-; \mathbb{Z}) \colon \mathbf{Top}_{*} \to \mathbf{Ab}$:$H_{1}(X; \mathbb{Z}) \cong \pi_{1}(X, x_{0})^{\mathrm{ab}}$ for path-connected$X$.

**Example 2.** The composite of the free group functor $F \colon \mathbf{Set} \to \mathbf{Grp}$ with the abelianization functor$\mathrm{Ab} \colon \mathbf{Grp} \to \mathbf{Ab}$ is the free abelian group functor$\mathbb{Z}^{(-)} \colon \mathbf{Set} \to \mathbf{Ab}$, which sends a set$S$ to$\bigoplus_{s \in S} \mathbb{Z}$. This shows that composing two left adjoints (both$F$ and$\mathrm{Ab}$ are left adjoints) gives another left adjoint — a special case of the general principle that composites of left (respectively right) adjoints are left (respectively right) adjoints.

**Example 3.** In $\mathbf{Cat}$, the identity functor$\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{C}}$ is the identity morphism at$\mathcal{C}$. The unit law$G \circ \mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{C}} = G$ and$\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{D}} \circ F = F$ for functors$F \colon \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ and$G \colon \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{E}$ is verified immediately:$(G \circ \mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{C}})(A) = G(\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{C}}(A)) = G(A)$ and similarly for morphisms. These are the category axioms of$\mathbf{Cat}$, confirming that$\mathbf{Cat}$ is indeed a category.

**Example 4.** The functor $\mathbf{Grp} \to \mathbf{Set} \to \mathbf{Grp}$ (first forget, then apply the free group functor) sends a group$G$ to the free group$F(|G|)$ on the underlying set of$G$. There is a canonical epimorphism$F(|G|) \to G$ (the "evaluation" map, sending each generator$g$ to$g \in G$), which is the counit of the free-forgetful adjunction. This composite functor is far from the identity on$\mathbf{Grp}$ — it replaces every group by a (typically much larger) free group — and illustrates how composites of functors can produce constructions quite different from the identity.

**Example 5.** For any two functors $F \colon \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ and$G \colon \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{E}$, if$F$ and$G$ are both equivalences of categories, then$G \circ F$ is an equivalence. Proof: faithful because the composite of injections is injective, full because the composite of surjections is surjective (using fullness of$G$ and the fact that$F$ is essentially surjective to hit every object in$\mathcal{D}$ up to isomorphism), and essentially surjective because$G$ is essentially surjective. This shows that equivalences of categories are the isomorphisms in$\mathbf{Cat}$ in the appropriate (non-strict) sense.

The category $\mathbf{Cat}$ has additional structure beyond that of an ordinary category: between any two functors$F, G \colon \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$, there are natural transformations (the subject of Chapter 34), which themselves compose. This extra layer of composition makes$\mathbf{Cat}$ the prototype of a 2-category, and it is the first step in an ascending hierarchy of increasingly refined notions of mathematical structure — from categories to 2-categories to$(\infty, 1)$-categories — that appears in modern algebra, topology, and mathematical physics.
