# 33.3 Functors

A functor is to categories what a homomorphism is to groups: a structure-preserving map between the two levels of categorical structure — objects and morphisms. Precisely, a *covariant functor* $F: \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ assigns to each object $A \in \mathcal{C}$ an object $F(A) \in \mathcal{D}$, and to each morphism $f: A \to B$ in $\mathcal{C}$ a morphism $F(f): F(A) \to F(B)$ in $\mathcal{D}$, subject to $F(\text{id}_A) = \text{id}_{F(A)}$ and $F(g \circ f) = F(g) \circ F(f)$. These two conditions say that functors preserve identity and composition — they carry the categorical skeleton of one world faithfully into another.

The most immediately natural examples are the *forgetful functors*, which drop structure. The functor $U: \mathbf{Grp} \to \mathbf{Set}$ sends each group to its underlying set and each homomorphism to the corresponding function, "forgetting" that the set was a group. Similarly, $\mathbf{Ring} \to \mathbf{Ab}$ forgets multiplication, and $\mathbf{Ab} \to \mathbf{Grp}$ forgets commutativity. These are not trivial maps — they have adjoints (the free group, free ring, and abelianization functors) and their interaction with those adjoints is one of the richest sources of structure in algebra. The hom-functor $\text{Hom}(A, -): \mathcal{C} \to \mathbf{Set}$ is another fundamental example: it sends each object $B$ to the set of morphisms from $A$ to $B$, and sends each morphism $f: B \to C$ to the function $f_* = f \circ -$ given by post-composition with $f$.

A *contravariant functor* $F: \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ reverses arrows: it sends $f: A \to B$ to $F(f): F(B) \to F(A)$, and satisfies $F(g \circ f) = F(f) \circ F(g)$. The contravariant hom-functor $\text{Hom}(-, B): \mathcal{C}^{\text{op}} \to \mathbf{Set}$ and the dual vector space functor $V \mapsto V^* = \text{Hom}(V, k)$ are the canonical examples. Cohomology theories — which assign abelian groups to spaces contravariantly — are among the most important contravariant functors in mathematics.

Functors are further classified by how faithfully they represent their source category. A functor is *faithful* if it is injective on hom-sets, *full* if it is surjective on hom-sets, and *essentially surjective* if every object in the target is isomorphic to some object in the image. A functor that is full, faithful, and essentially surjective is an *equivalence of categories* — the correct notion of isomorphism at the categorical level. Functors themselves are objects of a category: the category $\mathbf{Cat}$ of small categories has small categories as objects and functors as morphisms. This self-referential structure — categories of categories, functors between functors — is one of the characteristic features of category theory and prepares the ground for the next chapter's central concept, natural transformations.
