# 34.1.1 Definition: Commuting Squares for Every Morphism

Having defined functors as structure-preserving maps between categories, the next layer of categorical structure asks: what is a "map between functors"? If $F$ and$G$ both assign mathematical objects to mathematical objects in a coherent way, when should we regard them as related? The answer, due to Eilenberg and Mac Lane, is the concept of a natural transformation, and it captures something that mathematicians had long recognized informally: the distinction between "canonical" and "non-canonical" maps.

\begin{definition}
Let $F, G: \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ be functors between categories$\mathcal{C}$ and$\mathcal{D}$. A *natural transformation*$\eta: F \Rightarrow G$ is a family of morphisms$\{\eta_A: F(A) \to G(A)\}_{A \in \mathcal{C}}$ in$\mathcal{D}$, one for each object$A$ of$\mathcal{C}$, satisfying the *naturality condition*: for every morphism$f: A \to B$ in$\mathcal{C}$, the diagram
$$\begin{array}{ccc} F(A) & \xrightarrow{F(f)} & F(B) \\ {\scriptstyle \eta_A} \downarrow & & \downarrow {\scriptstyle \eta_B} \\ G(A) & \xrightarrow{G(f)} & G(B) \end{array}$$
commutes: $\eta_B \circ F(f) = G(f) \circ \eta_A$. The morphisms$\eta_A$ are called the *components* of$\eta$.
\end{definition}

The naturality condition is not merely a technical requirement — it says that the components $\eta_A$ are truly "uniform" across all objects of$\mathcal{C}$, in the sense that they interlock with every morphism in a coherent way. One could imagine assigning a morphism$\eta_A: F(A) \to G(A)$ for each object without any compatibility condition, but such an assignment would be arbitrary and mathematically unilluminating. The naturality square forces the components to behave consistently as$A$ varies: moving from$A$ to$B$ via$f$ commutes with applying the transformation. This is exactly the condition that makes natural transformations the correct morphisms in functor categories and that gives them their expressive power in the Yoneda lemma.

**Example 1.** Let $F = \mathrm{id}_{\mathbf{Set}}$ be the identity functor and$G: \mathbf{Set} \to \mathbf{Set}$ the power-set functor sending$X \mapsto \mathcal{P}(X)$ and$f \mapsto f_*$ (direct image). Define$\eta_X: X \to \mathcal{P}(X)$ by$\eta_X(x) = \{x\}$, the singleton assignment. For any function$f: X \to Y$, the naturality square requires$\eta_Y \circ f = f_* \circ \eta_X$. Both sides send$x \in X$ to$\{f(x)\} \in \mathcal{P}(Y)$, so the square commutes. The family$\{\eta_X\}$ is a natural transformation$\mathrm{id}_{\mathbf{Set}} \Rightarrow \mathcal{P}$: it assigns to each set a canonical injection into its power set.

**Example 2.** In $\mathbf{Ab}$, let$F = \mathrm{id}_{\mathbf{Ab}}$ and$G: A \mapsto A$ the functor sending each abelian group$A$ to itself but viewing it through multiplication by$n$ on morphisms — actually, let$G(A) = A/nA$ (the quotient by$n$-multiples) and define$\eta_A: A \to A/nA$ as the canonical quotient map. For any group homomorphism$f: A \to B$, the naturality square$\eta_B \circ f = \bar{f} \circ \eta_A$ (where$\bar{f}: A/nA \to B/nB$ is the induced map) commutes by the definition of induced maps on quotients. This natural transformation witnesses that taking$n$-torsion quotients is functorial and natural.

**Example 3.** In $\mathbf{Vect}_k$, consider the two functors$F = \mathrm{id}$ and$G = (-)^{**}$ (double dual). For each finite-dimensional$k$-vector space$V$, define$\eta_V: V \to V^{**}$ by$\eta_V(v)(\phi) = \phi(v)$. The naturality of this family — the fact that$\eta_W \circ f = f^{**} \circ \eta_V$ for every linear map$f: V \to W$ — is exactly the assertion that evaluation is natural, not dependent on any basis choice. This is the paradigmatic natural isomorphism (when restricted to finite-dimensional spaces), to be revisited in Section 34.1.2.

\begin{remark}
One must not confuse a natural transformation $\eta: F \Rightarrow G$ with a morphism in the target category$\mathcal{D}$. A natural transformation is a structured family of morphisms: it lives at a higher categorical level than individual arrows. This is the foundation of the "2-categorical" structure of **Cat**, in which categories are objects, functors are 1-morphisms, and natural transformations are 2-morphisms.
\end{remark}

The key insight is what naturality rules out. The naturality square forces the components to behave consistently as $A$ varies: moving from$A$ to$B$ via$f$ commutes with applying the transformation. Any assignment of maps$\eta_A: F(A) \to G(A)$ that fails the naturality check for even one morphism$f$ is not a natural transformation — it might be useful locally, but it does not assemble into a coherent global operation. This is the precise formalization of the informal mathematical principle that a construction is "independent of choices" or "basis-free."

**Example 4.** Consider functors $F, G: \mathbf{Grp} \to \mathbf{Set}$ where$F$ is the forgetful functor (underlying set) and$G(H) = H \times H$ (underlying set of the direct square). Define$\eta_H: H \to H \times H$ by$\eta_H(h) = (h, h)$ (the diagonal map). For any group homomorphism$\phi: H \to K$, the naturality square says$\eta_K \circ \phi = (\phi \times \phi) \circ \eta_H$, which reads:$(\phi(h), \phi(h)) = (\phi(h), \phi(h))$. This holds trivially, so$\{\eta_H\}$ is a natural transformation. The diagonal is a natural transformation because it is defined uniformly — no group-specific structure enters the definition.

**Example 5.** Consider $F = \mathrm{id}_{\mathbf{Ring}}$ and$G: R \mapsto R$ the functor sending each commutative ring to itself but with morphisms acting by$\phi \mapsto \phi$ (i.e.,$G = \mathrm{id}$ also). Now let$\eta_R: R \to R$ be the Frobenius endomorphism$r \mapsto r^p$ in characteristic$p$. For any ring homomorphism$\phi: R \to S$ between$\mathbb{F}_p$-algebras, naturality requires$\eta_S \circ \phi = \phi \circ \eta_R$, i.e.,$\phi(r)^p = \phi(r^p)$. Since$\phi$ is a ring homomorphism,$\phi(r^p) = \phi(r)^p$, and naturality holds. The Frobenius is a natural endomorphism of the identity functor on the category of commutative$\mathbb{F}_p$-algebras.

The entire notion of a "canonical" or "basis-free" isomorphism in algebra turns out to be captured by natural isomorphisms, as explored in the next section. The naturality condition is the precise mathematical content behind phrases like "the map does not depend on choices" — when a construction is natural, the naturality squares commute for every morphism in the relevant category, leaving no room for arbitrary choices to enter. This conceptual clarity is one of the great contributions of category theory to mathematics.
