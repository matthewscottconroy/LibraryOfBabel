# 34.1.2 Natural Isomorphisms; Canonical vs. Non-Canonical Maps

\begin{definition}
A natural transformation $\eta: F \Rightarrow G$ is a *natural isomorphism* if each of its components$\eta_A: F(A) \to G(A)$ is an isomorphism in$\mathcal{D}$. When this holds, one writes$F \cong G$ and says the two functors are *naturally isomorphic*. The inverse natural transformation$\eta^{-1}: G \Rightarrow F$ is defined by$(\eta^{-1})_A = (\eta_A)^{-1}$, and it satisfies the naturality condition because one obtains the naturality square for$\eta^{-1}$ by inverting both sides of the naturality squares for$\eta$.
\end{definition}

The conceptual content of natural isomorphisms is the formalization of what mathematicians mean when they say an isomorphism is *canonical* or *natural* — that it does not depend on any arbitrary choice. For decades, algebraists spoke of isomorphisms that were "natural" or "independent of coordinates" without having a precise definition. Eilenberg and Mac Lane's 1945 paper introduced natural transformations specifically to make this distinction rigorous.

**Example 1.** The double dual in $\mathbf{Vect}_k$. For finite-dimensional vector spaces over a field$k$, there is a natural isomorphism$\iota: \mathrm{id}_{\mathbf{Vect}_k} \Rightarrow (-)^{**}$ whose component at$V$ sends$v \in V$ to the evaluation functional$\mathrm{ev}_v: \phi \mapsto \phi(v)$ in$V^{**}$. For any linear map$f: V \to W$, the naturality square asserts$\iota_W \circ f = f^{**} \circ \iota_V$: both sides send$v$ to the functional$\phi \mapsto \phi(f(v))$ in$W^{**}$. No basis appears in the definition of$\iota_V$: the map is defined purely in terms of evaluation, and it is an isomorphism for finite-dimensional$V$ because$\dim V = \dim V^{**}$ and injectivity is evident.

**Example 2.** The isomorphism $V \cong V^*$ in the presence of an inner product. For any finite-dimensional inner product space$(V, \langle -, - \rangle)$ over$\mathbb{R}$, the Riesz representation theorem gives an isomorphism$\Phi_V: V \to V^*$ by$\Phi_V(v) = \langle v, - \rangle$. This is an isomorphism for each specific$(V, \langle -, - \rangle)$, but it is *not* natural in$V$: for a linear map$f: V \to W$, naturality would require$\Phi_W \circ f = f^* \circ \Phi_V$, but$f^*: W^* \to V^*$ goes in the opposite direction. More precisely, there is no natural isomorphism between the identity functor and the dual functor$(-)^*: \mathbf{Vect}_k^{\mathrm{op}} \to \mathbf{Vect}_k$ — the functors do not even have the same variance. The Riesz isomorphism requires a choice of inner product, changes when the inner product changes, and hence is not natural.

\begin{proposition}
There is no natural isomorphism between the identity functor $\mathrm{id}_{\mathbf{Vect}_k}$ and the dual functor$(-)^*$ on the category of finite-dimensional$k$-vector spaces.
\end{proposition}

*Proof sketch.* Any natural isomorphism $\eta: \mathrm{id} \Rightarrow (-)^*$ would have components$\eta_V: V \to V^*$ natural in$V$. Consider the one-dimensional case$V = k$:$\eta_k: k \to k^* \cong k$ is multiplication by some scalar$\lambda \in k^\times$. For$V = k \oplus k$, naturality with respect to the automorphism swapping the two summands would force$\eta$ to treat both summands identically, ultimately forcing$\lambda = 0$ in the isomorphism component, a contradiction.$\square$

**Example 3.** Abelianization quotient in $\mathbf{Grp}$. For each group$G$, let$q_G: G \to G^{\mathrm{ab}} = G/[G,G]$ be the canonical quotient. For any group homomorphism$f: G \to H$, since$f([G,G]) \subseteq [H,H]$, there is an induced homomorphism$f^{\mathrm{ab}}: G^{\mathrm{ab}} \to H^{\mathrm{ab}}$, and$q_H \circ f = f^{\mathrm{ab}} \circ q_G$. The family$\{q_G\}$ is a natural transformation$q: \mathrm{id}_{\mathbf{Grp}} \Rightarrow \mathrm{Ab}$, where$\mathrm{Ab}: \mathbf{Grp} \to \mathbf{Grp}$ sends each group to its abelianization. This is not a natural isomorphism (since$q_G$ is rarely an isomorphism), but it is natural.

**Example 4.** Naturality in $\mathbf{Ring}$: the unit map. For each commutative ring$R$, the map$\eta_R: \mathbb{Z} \to R$ sending$n \mapsto n \cdot 1_R$ is the unique ring homomorphism from$\mathbb{Z}$ to$R$. The family$\{\eta_R\}$ is a natural transformation from the constant functor$\Delta_{\mathbb{Z}}: \mathbf{CRing} \to \mathbf{CRing}$ to the identity functor, expressing the fact that$\mathbb{Z}$ is the initial object in$\mathbf{CRing}$.

\begin{remark}
Two categories $\mathcal{C}$ and$\mathcal{D}$ are *equivalent* if there exist functors$F: \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ and$G: \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{C}$ together with natural isomorphisms$GF \cong \mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{C}}$ and$FG \cong \mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{D}}$. This is the correct notion of "sameness" for categories — weaker than isomorphism (which requires$GF = \mathrm{id}$ and$FG = \mathrm{id}$ on the nose) but sufficient for virtually all categorical purposes. Skeletal categories and their originals are always equivalent in this sense.
\end{remark}

**Example 5.** The natural isomorphism in $\mathbf{Ab}$ between$\mathrm{Hom}(A \oplus B, C)$ and$\mathrm{Hom}(A, C) \oplus \mathrm{Hom}(B, C)$. For abelian groups$A$,$B$,$C$, restriction to each summand gives an isomorphism$\mathrm{Hom}(A \oplus B, C) \cong \mathrm{Hom}(A, C) \times \mathrm{Hom}(B, C)$. This isomorphism is natural in$C$ (in the variable$C$): for any homomorphism$\phi: C \to D$, the square involving post-composition with$\phi$ commutes. It is also natural in$A$ and$B$ (contravariantly). The naturality is precisely what allows one to say that the isomorphism is "the right one" — it is the one built entirely from the universal property of the direct sum, without any choice of splitting or basis.

The formalization of the canonical/non-canonical distinction was one of the motivations for Eilenberg and Mac Lane's introduction of natural transformations in 1945. In this sense, natural transformations provide the precise language for a distinction that was previously only expressible as mathematical folklore, and natural isomorphisms are the correct categorical analog of "canonical isomorphism."
