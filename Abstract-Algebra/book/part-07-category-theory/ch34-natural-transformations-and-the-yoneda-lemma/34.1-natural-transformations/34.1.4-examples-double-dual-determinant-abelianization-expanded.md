# 34.1.4 Examples: Double Dual, Determinant, Abelianisation — Why Naturality Matters

The concept of a natural transformation exists to answer a question every mathematician eventually confronts: when two constructions on objects produce results that are "the same" across all objects simultaneously, in a way that is compatible with all the morphisms between those objects, what precise condition captures this? The three classical examples in this section — the double dual of a vector space, the determinant of a linear map, and the abelianisation of a group — illuminate three distinct faces of naturality and, collectively, explain why the definition is exactly right.

## The Double Dual: The Archetypal Natural Transformation

Let $\mathbf{Vect}_k$ be the category of finite-dimensional $k$-vector spaces and linear maps. Define two functors $\mathbf{Vect}_k \to \mathbf{Vect}_k$:
- The identity functor $\mathrm{Id}$: sends $V$ to $V$ and $f: V \to W$ to $f$.
- The double dual functor $(-)^{**}$: sends $V$ to $V^{**} = (V^*)^*$ and $f: V \to W$ to $f^{**}: V^{**} \to W^{**}$ defined by $f^{**}(\phi) = \phi \circ f^*$, where $f^*(g) = g \circ f$ for $g: W \to k$.

**The canonical map.** For each $V$, define $\eta_V: V \to V^{**}$ by $\eta_V(v)(\phi) = \phi(v)$ for $\phi \in V^*$. In words: evaluation at $v$ gives a functional on $V^*$.

**Claim:** $\eta_V$ is an isomorphism (since $\dim V = \dim V^{**}$ in finite dimensions and $\eta_V$ is injective). Moreover, $\eta = \{\eta_V\}_V$ is a *natural* isomorphism $\mathrm{Id} \Rightarrow (-)^{**}$.

**Verification of naturality.** For any $f: V \to W$ we must check:
$$\eta_W \circ f = f^{**} \circ \eta_V.$$
Take $v \in V$. Then:
- Left side: $\eta_W(f(v))$ is evaluation at $f(v)$, so for $\psi \in W^*$: $\eta_W(f(v))(\psi) = \psi(f(v))$.
- Right side: $f^{**}(\eta_V(v))$; since $f^{**}(\phi) = \phi \circ f^*$ and $f^*(\psi) = \psi \circ f$, we get $f^{**}(\eta_V(v))(\psi) = \eta_V(v)(f^*(\psi)) = \eta_V(v)(\psi \circ f) = (\psi \circ f)(v) = \psi(f(v))$.

Both sides agree. $\square$

**Contrast with the single dual.** There is also an isomorphism $V \cong V^*$ for each finite-dimensional $V$ — but it requires choosing a basis. No natural isomorphism $\mathrm{Id} \Rightarrow (-)^*$ can exist: a natural transformation $\alpha: \mathrm{Id} \Rightarrow (-)^*$ would require, for every $f: V \to V$ (say an endomorphism), the commutativity $\alpha_V \circ f = f^* \circ \alpha_V$. Taking $f$ to be multiplication by a scalar $\lambda$, we get $\lambda \alpha_V = \lambda^{-1} \alpha_V$ (since $f^*$ is multiplication by $\lambda$ on the dual in a transposed sense — in fact $f^* = \lambda \cdot \mathrm{id}_{V^*}$ as well, so this fails only when $\lambda \neq \lambda^{-1}$). More precisely, one can show by a Yoneda-type argument that no natural isomorphism $\mathrm{Id} \to (-)^*$ can exist by considering maps between spaces of different dimensions. The non-canonicity of the isomorphism $V \cong V^*$ is not just a practical limitation; it is a theorem.

## The Determinant: A Natural Transformation of Functors to Monoids

Let $GL_n: \mathbf{CRing} \to \mathbf{Grp}$ be the functor sending a commutative ring $R$ to the group $GL_n(R)$ of invertible $n \times n$ matrices, and let $(-)^\times: \mathbf{CRing} \to \mathbf{Grp}$ send $R$ to its group of units $R^\times$. For any ring map $\phi: R \to S$, the induced map $GL_n(R) \to GL_n(S)$ applies $\phi$ entrywise, and $(-)^\times$ applies $\phi$ directly.

The determinant $\det_R: GL_n(R) \to R^\times$ is a group homomorphism (since $\det(AB) = \det A \cdot \det B$ and invertible matrices have units as determinants) defined for every $R$.

**Naturality.** For every ring map $\phi: R \to S$ and every $A \in GL_n(R)$:
$$\phi(\det_R(A)) = \det_S(\phi(A)).$$
This holds because the determinant formula involves only ring operations (sums and products of matrix entries), and $\phi$ preserves those. The square
$$\begin{array}{ccc} GL_n(R) & \xrightarrow{\det_R} & R^\times \\ \downarrow^{GL_n(\phi)} & & \downarrow^{\phi^\times} \\ GL_n(S) & \xrightarrow{\det_S} & S^\times \end{array}$$
commutes, verifying that $\det: GL_n \Rightarrow (-)^\times$ is a natural transformation of functors $\mathbf{CRing} \to \mathbf{Grp}$.

**Significance.** The naturality of the determinant explains why "the determinant" is a single coherent concept rather than a collection of unrelated formulas for different rings. It is a theorem not about matrices over a fixed ring, but about the interaction between matrix groups and unit groups *across all rings simultaneously*.

## Abelianisation: A Natural Transformation with a Universal Property

The abelianisation functor $G \mapsto G^{\mathrm{ab}} = G/[G,G]$ is a functor $\mathbf{Grp} \to \mathbf{Ab}$ from the category of all groups to the full subcategory of abelian groups. There is a natural transformation $\pi: \mathrm{Id}_{\mathbf{Grp}} \Rightarrow \iota \circ (-)^{\mathrm{ab}}$ (where $\iota: \mathbf{Ab} \hookrightarrow \mathbf{Grp}$ is the inclusion) with components $\pi_G: G \twoheadrightarrow G^{\mathrm{ab}}$ being the canonical quotient maps.

**Naturality.** For any group homomorphism $f: G \to H$: since $f([G,G]) \subseteq [H,H]$ (homomorphisms preserve commutators: $f([g_1,g_2]) = [f(g_1), f(g_2)]$), there is an induced map $f^{\mathrm{ab}}: G^{\mathrm{ab}} \to H^{\mathrm{ab}}$ such that $\pi_H \circ f = f^{\mathrm{ab}} \circ \pi_G$. This is exactly the naturality condition.

**Universal property.** The abelianisation has a universal property that the naturality encodes: for any abelian group $A$ and any homomorphism $\phi: G \to A$, there is a unique $\bar\phi: G^{\mathrm{ab}} \to A$ with $\phi = \bar\phi \circ \pi_G$. Rephrasing this: $\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Ab}}(G^{\mathrm{ab}}, A) \cong \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Grp}}(G, A)$ naturally in both $G$ and $A$. This bijection is a natural isomorphism of functors in $G$ and $A$, which is the statement that $(-)^{\mathrm{ab}}: \mathbf{Grp} \to \mathbf{Ab}$ is left adjoint to $\iota: \mathbf{Ab} \hookrightarrow \mathbf{Grp}$.

**Example.** For $G = S_3$: $[S_3, S_3] = A_3$, so $S_3^{\mathrm{ab}} = S_3/A_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. For any homomorphism $f: S_3 \to A$ with $A$ abelian, $f$ must factor through $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$: either $f$ is trivial or $f$ is the sign homomorphism. This is the universal property at work.

## What "Natural" Rules Out: Two Non-Examples

**Non-example 1: Choosing a basis.** There is no natural isomorphism between $V$ and $\bigoplus_{i=1}^n k$ (abstract $k^n$) because choosing an isomorphism requires choosing a basis. The isomorphism type is canonical; the isomorphism itself is not.

**Non-example 2: Splitting a short exact sequence.** Given a short exact sequence $0 \to A \xrightarrow{f} B \xrightarrow{g} C \to 0$ of abelian groups, there is no *natural* choice of splitting $s: C \to B$ with $g \circ s = \mathrm{id}_C$. Splittings exist when $C$ is free (or projective), but the choice of splitting depends on $C$ in a non-functorial way.

## The Definition Revisited

These examples jointly justify the definition of natural transformation. A natural transformation $\alpha: F \Rightarrow G$ between functors $F, G: \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ consists of:
1. For each object $A \in \mathcal{C}$, a morphism $\alpha_A: F(A) \to G(A)$ in $\mathcal{D}$ (the *component* at $A$).
2. For each morphism $f: A \to B$ in $\mathcal{C}$, the *naturality square* commutes: $G(f) \circ \alpha_A = \alpha_B \circ F(f)$.

The double dual example shows that condition (2) is genuinely restrictive — it fails for the single dual. The determinant example shows that (2) is a theorem, not a definition, when the maps are given by an explicit formula. The abelianisation example shows that (2) encodes a universal property and connects to adjunction.
