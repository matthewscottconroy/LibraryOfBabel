# 34.1.4 Examples: Double Dual, Determinant, Abelianization

Three central examples illustrate the scope of natural transformations across different areas of mathematics, and together they reinforce why naturality is not an optional refinement but an essential feature of mathematical constructions.

**Example 1. The double dual natural isomorphism.** This is the example that motivated the entire theory. For finite-dimensional vector spaces over a field $k$, there is a natural isomorphism$\iota: \mathrm{id}_{\mathbf{Vect}_k} \Rightarrow (-)^{**}$ whose component at$V$ sends a vector$v \in V$ to the evaluation functional$\mathrm{ev}_v \in V^{**}$ defined by$\mathrm{ev}_v(\phi) = \phi(v)$. Let us check naturality carefully: for any linear map$f: V \to W$, the naturality square requires$\iota_W \circ f = f^{**} \circ \iota_V$. For$v \in V$ and$\psi \in W^*$, the left side gives$\iota_W(f(v))(\psi) = \psi(f(v))$, and the right side gives$f^{**}(\iota_V(v))(\psi) = \iota_V(v)(f^*(\psi)) = \iota_V(v)(\psi \circ f) = (\psi \circ f)(v) = \psi(f(v))$. Both sides agree, confirming naturality. Each$\iota_V$ is injective (send$v \mapsto \mathrm{ev}_v$ is injective by direct verification) and, for finite-dimensional$V$, is an isomorphism by dimension count. This natural isomorphism witnesses that the process of taking the double dual is not an arbitrary operation but a canonical one.

**Example 2. The determinant as a natural transformation.** For each commutative ring $R$, the determinant defines a group homomorphism$\det_R: GL_n(R) \to R^\times$ from the general linear group (invertible$n \times n$ matrices with entries in$R$) to the multiplicative group of units. This is natural in$R$: for any ring homomorphism$\phi: R \to S$, denote by$\phi_*: GL_n(R) \to GL_n(S)$ the map applying$\phi$ entry-wise, and by$\phi^\times: R^\times \to S^\times$ the restriction of$\phi$ to units. The naturality square$\det_S \circ \phi_* = \phi^\times \circ \det_R$ holds because the determinant formula is a polynomial in the matrix entries, so applying$\phi$ to each entry before or after computing the determinant gives the same answer. Viewing$GL_n$ and$(-)^\times$ as functors$\mathbf{CRing} \to \mathbf{Grp}$, the determinant is a natural transformation$\det: GL_n \Rightarrow (-)^\times$ between these functors.

To make the naturality concrete: if $R = \mathbb{Z}$ and$S = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$, and$\phi$ is the reduction mod$p$, then$\det_S(\bar{A}) = \overline{\det_R(A)}$ — the determinant of the reduced matrix equals the reduction of the determinant. This holds because the determinant formula involves only ring operations.

**Example 3. Abelianization.** For any group $G$, the commutator subgroup$[G, G]$ is normal in$G$, and the quotient$G^{\mathrm{ab}} = G/[G,G]$ is the abelianization. The quotient map$q_G: G \to G^{\mathrm{ab}}$ is natural: for any group homomorphism$f: G \to H$, one has$q_H \circ f = f^{\mathrm{ab}} \circ q_G$ where$f^{\mathrm{ab}}: G^{\mathrm{ab}} \to H^{\mathrm{ab}}$ is induced by the observation that$f([G,G]) \subseteq [H,H]$. Thus the family$\{q_G\}_{G \in \mathbf{Grp}}$ defines a natural transformation from the identity functor$\mathrm{id}_{\mathbf{Grp}}$ to the abelianization functor$\mathrm{Ab}: \mathbf{Grp} \to \mathbf{Ab} \hookrightarrow \mathbf{Grp}$.

As a specific illustration: let $f: S_3 \to S_4$ be the standard inclusion of symmetric groups. The abelianization$S_3^{\mathrm{ab}} = S_3/A_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ and$S_4^{\mathrm{ab}} = S_4/A_4 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. The induced map$f^{\mathrm{ab}}: \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is the identity, and the naturality square commutes: the sign homomorphism for$S_3$ composed with$f$ equals the sign homomorphism for$S_4$.

**Example 4. The inclusion of $\mathbf{Ab}$ into$\mathbf{Grp}$ and its adjoint.** The abelianization functor$\mathrm{Ab}: \mathbf{Grp} \to \mathbf{Ab}$ and the inclusion$\iota: \mathbf{Ab} \hookrightarrow \mathbf{Grp}$ form an adjoint pair$\mathrm{Ab} \dashv \iota$ (cf. Section 35.1.4). The natural transformation$q: \mathrm{id}_{\mathbf{Grp}} \Rightarrow \iota \circ \mathrm{Ab}$ defined by the quotient maps is the unit of this adjunction. The naturality of the unit is what makes the adjunction a genuine adjunction — it ensures that the bijection$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Ab}}(G^{\mathrm{ab}}, A) \cong \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Grp}}(G, A)$ is natural in both$G$ and$A$.

**Example 5. Cohomology as a natural transformation.** Fix a coefficient group $A$. For topological spaces, the Universal Coefficient Theorem gives, for each space$X$ and each$n$, an isomorphism$H^n(X; A) \cong \mathrm{Hom}(H_n(X), A) \oplus \mathrm{Ext}^1(\mathrm{H}_{n-1}(X), A)$. This isomorphism is natural in$X$: for any continuous map$f: X \to Y$, the maps induced on homology and cohomology are compatible with the isomorphism. Naturality here is not just elegant — it is essential for the theorem to be applicable: without naturality, one could not use the isomorphism to compare different spaces via maps between them.

The ubiquity of these examples — spanning linear algebra, ring theory, group theory, and topology — reflects the universality of the naturality condition. What unites them is that in each case, the transformation is defined by a uniform formula that works simultaneously for all objects, with no choices or parameters that vary between objects. This uniformity is precisely what naturality captures formally, and it is why natural transformations are the right morphisms in functor categories.
