# 34.1 Natural Transformations

The concept of a natural transformation captures what mathematicians mean when they say a construction is "canonical" or "doesn't depend on any choices." Two functors $F, G: \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ can be related by a natural transformation $\alpha: F \Rightarrow G$, which is a collection of morphisms $\{\alpha_A: F(A) \to G(A)\}_{A \in \mathcal{C}}$ satisfying a single coherence condition: for every morphism $f: A \to B$ in $\mathcal{C}$, the diagram with vertices $F(A)$, $F(B)$, $G(A)$, $G(B)$ and arrows $F(f)$, $G(f)$, $\alpha_A$, $\alpha_B$ commutes. This naturality square is the entire definition, but it has enormous consequences.

The prototype is the double-dual embedding. For a vector space $V$ over a field $k$, define $\eta_V: V \to V^{**}$ by $\eta_V(v)(\phi) = \phi(v)$. This is natural: for any linear map $f: V \to W$, the maps $\eta_W \circ f$ and $f^{**} \circ \eta_V$ are equal — and this equality is not a computation but a consequence of naturality. By contrast, the isomorphism $V \cong V^*$ for finite-dimensional spaces requires choosing a basis, and the resulting map changes when the basis changes. The difference between these two situations — one natural, one not — is exactly what the definition captures. Eilenberg and Mac Lane invented the language of categories and functors specifically in order to state the definition of natural transformation.

Natural transformations compose: if $\alpha: F \Rightarrow G$ and $\beta: G \Rightarrow H$, the composite $\beta \circ \alpha: F \Rightarrow H$ is defined componentwise by $(\beta \circ \alpha)_A = \beta_A \circ \alpha_A$, and naturality is preserved. This makes natural transformations the morphisms in a category whose objects are functors — the *functor category* $[\mathcal{C}, \mathcal{D}]$ or $\mathcal{D}^{\mathcal{C}}$. A *natural isomorphism* is a natural transformation in which every component is an isomorphism, or equivalently, an isomorphism in the functor category. When we write $F \cong G$ for functors, we mean a natural isomorphism between them.

The examples that appear throughout this section — the abelianization map $G \twoheadrightarrow G/[G, G]$ as a natural transformation from the identity on $\mathbf{Grp}$ to the composite of abelianization with inclusion, the determinant as a natural transformation between functor categories on rings, the unit map of an adjunction — all illustrate the same phenomenon: canonically defined families of morphisms satisfying a coherence condition. Learning to recognize and produce natural transformations is one of the core skills of categorical thinking.
