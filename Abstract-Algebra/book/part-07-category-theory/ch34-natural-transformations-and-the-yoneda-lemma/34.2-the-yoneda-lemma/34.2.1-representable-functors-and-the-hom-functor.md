# 34.2.1 Representable Functors and the Hom-Functor $\mathrm{h}^A$

Among all functors from a category $\mathcal{C}$ to$\mathbf{Set}$, the most fundamental are those that arise directly from the hom-sets of$\mathcal{C}$ itself. These *representable functors* encode what it means to "look at$\mathcal{C}$ from the perspective of a fixed object," and they are the central objects of study in the Yoneda theory.

\begin{definition}
Let $\mathcal{C}$ be a locally small category and$A \in \mathcal{C}$ an object. The *covariant representable functor*$\mathrm{h}^A: \mathcal{C} \to \mathbf{Set}$ is defined by:
- On objects: $\mathrm{h}^A(B) = \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A, B)$,
- On morphisms: for $f: B \to C$, the map$\mathrm{h}^A(f) = f_*: \mathrm{Hom}(A, B) \to \mathrm{Hom}(A, C)$ is post-composition:$f_*(g) = f \circ g$.
\par
Dually, the *contravariant representable functor* $\mathrm{h}_A: \mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}} \to \mathbf{Set}$ is defined by$\mathrm{h}_A(B) = \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(B, A)$, with$\mathrm{h}_A(f) = f^*: \mathrm{Hom}(C, A) \to \mathrm{Hom}(B, A)$ being pre-composition:$f^*(g) = g \circ f$.
\end{definition}

Both $\mathrm{h}^A$ and$\mathrm{h}_A$ are indeed functors: composition respects post- and pre-composition in the required way, and identity morphisms act as identities. The local smallness hypothesis ensures that$\mathrm{h}^A(B) = \mathrm{Hom}(A, B)$ is a genuine set for each$B$.

\begin{definition}
A functor $F: \mathcal{C} \to \mathbf{Set}$ is *representable* if$F \cong \mathrm{h}^A$ for some object$A \in \mathcal{C}$, i.e., if$F$ is naturally isomorphic to some covariant hom-functor. The object$A$ is called a *representing object* for$F$. Similarly, a functor$F: \mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}} \to \mathbf{Set}$ is representable if$F \cong \mathrm{h}_A$ for some$A$.
\end{definition}

**Example 1.** In $\mathbf{Set}$, the functor$F(B) = B$ (the identity functor) is representable:$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Set}}(\{*\}, B) \cong B$ naturally, where$\{*\}$ is any one-element set. A morphism$\{*\} \to B$ is exactly a choice of element, so the hom-set is in natural bijection with$B$ itself. The representing object is any singleton set.

**Example 2.** In $\mathbf{Grp}$, fix a group$G$ and consider the functor$F(H) = U(H)$ (underlying set, i.e., the forgetful functor). Is this representable? We need an object$A \in \mathbf{Grp}$ with$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Grp}}(A, H) \cong U(H)$ naturally. The free group$\mathbb{Z} = F(\{1\})$ on one generator works: a group homomorphism$\mathbb{Z} \to H$ is determined by where the generator$1$ maps, so$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Grp}}(\mathbb{Z}, H) \cong U(H)$ naturally. Thus the underlying set functor on$\mathbf{Grp}$ is represented by$\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 3.** In $\mathbf{Ring}$, the functor$F(R) = U(R)$ (underlying set) is represented by the polynomial ring$\mathbb{Z}[x]$: a ring homomorphism$\mathbb{Z}[x] \to R$ is determined by the image of$x$, which can be any element of$R$. So$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Ring}}(\mathbb{Z}[x], R) \cong U(R)$ naturally. More generally,$\mathbb{Z}[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ represents the functor$R \mapsto U(R)^n$ (the functor of$n$-tuples of elements).

The key insight is that representability expresses a universal property. Saying $F$ is represented by$A$ means there is a distinguished element$u \in F(A)$ (the *universal element*, corresponding to$\mathrm{id}_A$ under the natural bijection$F(A) \cong \mathrm{Nat}(\mathrm{h}^A, F)$, as the Yoneda lemma will show) such that every element$x \in F(B)$ arises uniquely as$F(f)(u)$ for some morphism$f: A \to B$.

**Example 4.** In $\mathbf{Ab}$, consider the functor$F(B) = B \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{Z}[i] / (i^2 + 1)$ sending each abelian group to a Gaussian integer tensor product. This functor is representable: it is naturally isomorphic to$\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}[i], B)$ by... actually,$\mathbb{Z}[i]$ represents the functor$B \mapsto \{b \in B : 2b = 0, \text{...}\}$ — for a cleaner example: the functor$B \mapsto B \times B$ on$\mathbf{Ab}$ is represented by$\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}$: any homomorphism$\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z} \to B$ is determined by the images of the two generators, giving a natural bijection$\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}, B) \cong B \times B$.

**Example 5.** The bilinear maps functor. Fix abelian groups $M$ and$N$. The functor$F(P) = \{\text{bilinear maps } M \times N \to P\}$ on$\mathbf{Ab}$ is representable; its representing object is the tensor product$M \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} N$. A bilinear map$M \times N \to P$ corresponds uniquely to a group homomorphism$M \otimes N \to P$. The universal element is the bilinear map$(m, n) \mapsto m \otimes n$, and every bilinear map factors uniquely through it. This is the universal property of the tensor product, rephrased as representability.

\begin{remark}
The universal property of every fundamental algebraic construction — tensor products, free objects, products, coproducts, limits, colimits — is a statement of representability. The Yoneda lemma, developed in the following sections, provides the precise theorem that makes this observation rigorous and powerful: representable functors are completely determined by their representing object, and any two objects representing the same functor are canonically isomorphic.
\end{remark}
