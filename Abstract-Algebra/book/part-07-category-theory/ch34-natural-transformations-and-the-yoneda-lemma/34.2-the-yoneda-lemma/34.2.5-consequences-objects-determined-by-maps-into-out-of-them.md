# 34.2.5 Consequences: Objects Determined by Maps into/out of Them

The philosophical content of the Yoneda Lemma is a principle that resonates throughout modern mathematics: an object is completely determined by its relationships to all other objects.

\begin{corollary}
Two objects $A$ and$B$ in a locally small category$\mathcal{C}$ are isomorphic if and only if$\mathrm{h}^A \cong \mathrm{h}^B$ as functors — that is, if and only if$\mathrm{Hom}(A, -)$ and$\mathrm{Hom}(B, -)$ are naturally isomorphic.
\end{corollary}

*Proof.* If $A \cong B$ via$f: A \to B$, then$\mathbf{y}(f): \mathrm{h}_A \Rightarrow \mathrm{h}_B$ is a natural isomorphism by full faithfulness of$\mathbf{y}$. Conversely, if$\mathrm{h}_A \cong \mathrm{h}_B$, then the isomorphism in$[\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}, \mathbf{Set}]$ lies in the image of$\mathbf{y}$ (by fullness), so it comes from an isomorphism$A \cong B$ in$\mathcal{C}$.$\square$

There is no "internal" information about $A$ that is not captured by the external relationships expressed through its hom-sets. An object is entirely characterized by what maps into it (or out of it).

**Example 1. Universal properties as representability.** The tensor product $M \otimes_R N$ of$R$-modules is the representing object for the functor$P \mapsto \{R\text{-bilinear maps } M \times N \to P\}$. When one says this, one is asserting that$\mathrm{Hom}_R(M \otimes N, P) \cong \mathrm{Bil}(M \times N, P)$ naturally in$P$. The Yoneda Lemma guarantees that if two$R$-modules$T$ and$T'$ both satisfy this universal property (i.e., both represent the same functor), then$T \cong T'$ via a canonical isomorphism — the unique isomorphism compatible with the universal elements. This is the categorical explanation for why any two constructions of the tensor product are canonically isomorphic.

**Example 2. Free group.** The free group $F(S)$ on a set$S$ represents the functor$G \mapsto \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Set}}(S, U(G))$ on$\mathbf{Grp}$: a group homomorphism$F(S) \to G$ is the same as a function$S \to U(G)$. The Yoneda Lemma implies that any two free groups on$S$ — however constructed — are canonically isomorphic via the unique isomorphism that is the identity on generators.

**Example 3. Products via representability.** The product $A \times B$ in$\mathcal{C}$ represents the functor$C \mapsto \mathrm{Hom}(C, A) \times \mathrm{Hom}(C, B)$: to give a morphism$C \to A \times B$ is the same as giving morphisms$C \to A$ and$C \to B$. The Yoneda Lemma says this determines$A \times B$ up to unique isomorphism, which is why the product can be constructed in many ways (as Cartesian products of sets, as direct products of groups, etc.) but the result is always the same up to canonical isomorphism.

**Example 4. Consequences for definitions.** Rather than defining objects by their "internal structure" — elements, generators, relations — one may define them by their universal property, i.e., by specifying which functor they represent. The polynomial ring $k[x]$ can be defined as the free commutative$k$-algebra on one generator, i.e., the representing object for the functor$R \mapsto U(R)$ on commutative$k$-algebras. This definition specifies$k[x]$ by what it does (its universal property) rather than by what it is made of (polynomials), and it ensures that any two constructions satisfying the same universal property are canonically isomorphic.

**Example 5. The covariant case.** Dually, the contravariant Yoneda lemma gives $\mathrm{Nat}(\mathrm{h}_A, F) \cong F(A)$ for contravariant$F: \mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}} \to \mathbf{Set}$. In$\mathbf{Top}$, the representable functor$\mathrm{Hom}(-, X)$ detects the topology of$X$: natural transformations$\mathrm{Hom}(-, X) \Rightarrow \mathrm{Hom}(-, Y)$ correspond to continuous maps$X \to Y$. Two spaces with naturally isomorphic hom-functors are homeomorphic. This is a powerful principle in algebraic topology, where spaces are often studied through their function spaces.

\begin{theorem}[Uniqueness of Representing Objects]
If $A$ and$A'$ are both representing objects for the same functor$F: \mathcal{C} \to \mathbf{Set}$, then there is a unique isomorphism$A \xrightarrow{\sim} A'$ in$\mathcal{C}$ compatible with the universal elements.
\end{theorem}

*Proof.* By the Yoneda Lemma, natural isomorphisms $\mathrm{h}^A \cong F \cong \mathrm{h}^{A'}$ compose to give$\mathrm{h}^A \cong \mathrm{h}^{A'}$. By the full faithfulness of the Yoneda embedding, this corresponds to a unique isomorphism$A \cong A'$.$\square$

The consequence for mathematical practice is far-reaching. Instead of constructing a specific object (the tensor product, the free group, the direct limit) and then proving it has a universal property, one may *define* the object by its universal property and then prove existence separately. The uniqueness is always automatic from the Yoneda Lemma, and the categorical properties of the object (how it behaves under functors, what limits and colimits it participates in) follow from the universal property without any knowledge of the specific construction. This is why the tensor product, the free group, the direct sum, and the polynomial ring can all be defined without specifying a particular construction — the universal property determines the answer up to isomorphism, and any specific construction is merely a proof of existence.
