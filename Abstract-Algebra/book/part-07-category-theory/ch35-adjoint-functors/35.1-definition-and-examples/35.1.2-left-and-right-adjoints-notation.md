# 35.1.2 Left and Right Adjoints; Notation $F \dashv G$

The notation $F \dashv G$ for an adjunction carries directional information that reflects a deep asymmetry. In the hom-set bijection$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{D}}(FA, B) \cong \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A, GB)$, the left adjoint$F: \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ appears as the functor applied to the source, while the right adjoint$G: \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{C}$ appears applied to the target.

The intuition behind the left-right asymmetry is the free-forgetful paradigm. The left adjoint $F$ takes a simple object in$\mathcal{C}$ and "builds" or "freely generates" a more structured object in$\mathcal{D}$. Specifying a map out of the built object$FA$ is highly constrained (it must respect all the built-in structure), and the adjunction says this is equivalent to specifying a much freer map out of the original simple object$A$ into the "underlying" version$GB$ of the target. The right adjoint$G$ "forgets" structure, and the adjunction measures the asymmetry between free constructions and forgotten ones.

**Example 1.** Free modules. For a commutative ring $R$, the free module functor$R^{(-)} = R \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{Z}[-]: \mathbf{Set} \to R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ is left adjoint to the forgetful functor$U: R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod} \to \mathbf{Set}$. A module map from the free module$R^S = \bigoplus_{s \in S} R$ to an$R$-module$M$ is determined freely by specifying the image of each basis element$e_s$, which can be any element of$M$:$\mathrm{Hom}_R(R^S, M) \cong M^S = \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Set}}(S, U(M))$. Here the left adjoint builds the most general module with generators indexed by$S$, and the right adjoint forgets the module structure.

**Example 2.** Polynomial algebras. The polynomial ring functor $k[-]: \mathbf{Set} \to k\text{-}\mathbf{Alg}$ (sending a set$S$ to$k[x_s : s \in S]$) is left adjoint to the forgetful functor$U: k\text{-}\mathbf{Alg} \to \mathbf{Set}$. An algebra map from$k[S]$ to an algebra$A$ is freely determined by the images of the generators. The left adjoint builds the free commutative$k$-algebra, and the right adjoint forgets everything except the underlying set.

**Example 3.** Inclusion of abelian groups into groups. The inclusion $\iota: \mathbf{Ab} \hookrightarrow \mathbf{Grp}$ is right adjoint to abelianization$\mathrm{Ab}: \mathbf{Grp} \to \mathbf{Ab}$. Here the right adjoint "forgets" the commutativity constraint (a commutative group is viewed merely as a group), while the left adjoint "forces" commutativity (by quotienting out commutators). The adjunction bijection$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Ab}}(G^{\mathrm{ab}}, A) \cong \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Grp}}(G, A)$ (for$A \in \mathbf{Ab}$) reflects this: mapping from the abelianization (most efficient quotient) to an abelian group is the same as mapping from the original group into an abelian group.

**Example 4.** Discrete and indiscrete topology. For the category $\mathbf{Top}$ of topological spaces and the forgetful functor$U: \mathbf{Top} \to \mathbf{Set}$, there are two distinct adjoints. The *discrete* topology functor$D: \mathbf{Set} \to \mathbf{Top}$ (giving every set the discrete topology where all subsets are open) is left adjoint to$U$: a continuous map from a discrete space$D(S)$ to any topological space$X$ is just a function (no continuity constraint), so$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Top}}(D(S), X) \cong \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Set}}(S, U(X))$. The *indiscrete* topology functor$I: \mathbf{Set} \to \mathbf{Top}$ (only$\emptyset$ and the whole set are open) is right adjoint to$U$: a continuous map from any space to an indiscrete space$I(S)$ is just a function, so$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Top}}(X, I(S)) \cong \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Set}}(U(X), S)$. The left adjoint$D$ builds the "most open" (most free) topology, while the right adjoint$I$ builds the "least open" (most forgetful) topology.

This left-builds, right-forgets pattern appears throughout algebra and geometry. Left adjoints tend to be "more aggressive" — they create new structure, and constructions built by left adjoints preserve colimits (Section 35.2.2). Right adjoints tend to be "more conservative" — they pass structure through without fundamentally altering it, and they preserve limits.

**Example 5.** The global sections and constant sheaf adjunction. For a topological space $X$, let$\mathbf{Sh}(X)$ denote the category of sheaves of abelian groups on$X$, and$\mathbf{Ab}$ the category of abelian groups. The constant sheaf functor$\underline{(-)}: \mathbf{Ab} \to \mathbf{Sh}(X)$ (sending$A$ to the constant sheaf$\underline{A}$) is left adjoint to the global sections functor$\Gamma(X, -): \mathbf{Sh}(X) \to \mathbf{Ab}$. The adjunction$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Sh}(X)}(\underline{A}, \mathcal{F}) \cong \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Ab}}(A, \Gamma(X, \mathcal{F}))$ reflects the universal property of constant sheaves.

\begin{remark}
The notation $F \dashv G$ has become standard, and the visual mnemonic is the underlying hom-set bijection: the left adjoint$F$ sits on the left, acting on sources ($FA$), while the right adjoint$G$ sits on the right, acting on targets ($GB$). When a functor has both a left adjoint and a right adjoint, the three functors form an *adjoint triple*, and such triples are especially common in geometry (e.g., the direct image, lower shriek, and upper star functors in the context of morphisms of topological spaces or schemes).
\end{remark}
