# 35.1 Definition and Examples

The adjunction between two functors is a precisely calibrated reciprocity: maps out of a "free" or "generated" object on the left correspond naturally to maps into a "forgetful" or "restricted" object on the right. Formally, functors $F: \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ and $G: \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{C}$ form an adjoint pair $F \dashv G$ when there is a natural bijection $\phi_{A,B}: \text{Hom}_{\mathcal{D}}(F(A), B) \xrightarrow{\sim} \text{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A, G(B))$ for all $A \in \mathcal{C}$ and $B \in \mathcal{D}$. The word *natural* here carries the precise categorical meaning from the previous chapter: the bijection must commute with all morphisms in both variables, making $\phi$ a natural isomorphism of bifunctors $\mathcal{C}^{\text{op}} \times \mathcal{D} \to \mathbf{Set}$.

The canonical example is the free group — forgetful adjunction: a group homomorphism $F(S) \to G$ from a free group on a set $S$ to any group $G$ is the same data as a function $S \to U(G)$ from the set $S$ into the underlying set of $G$. In both directions, the correspondence is trivial to describe: a homomorphism is determined by where generators go, and any function on generators extends uniquely to a homomorphism. The naturality is immediate. The same pattern — free object on the left, forgetful functor on the right — appears for free modules $F(S) = R^{\oplus S}$ over a ring $R$, for free algebras, for polynomial rings, and for the free commutative ring $\mathbb{Z}[x_s : s \in S]$.

An equivalent formulation uses the *unit* and *counit* of the adjunction. The unit is the natural transformation $\eta: \text{Id}_{\mathcal{C}} \Rightarrow G \circ F$ with components $\eta_A: A \to G(F(A))$; in the free-forgetful case, $\eta_S: S \to U(F(S))$ is the canonical inclusion of generators. The counit is $\varepsilon: F \circ G \Rightarrow \text{Id}_{\mathcal{D}}$ with components $\varepsilon_B: F(G(B)) \to B$; in the free-forgetful case, $\varepsilon_G: F(U(G)) \to G$ is the evaluation homomorphism that sends each generator $g \in U(G)$ to $g \in G$. The hom-set bijection can be recovered entirely from $\eta$ and $\varepsilon$ via the formulas $\phi(f) = G(f) \circ \eta_A$ and $\phi^{-1}(g) = \varepsilon_B \circ F(g)$, and the triangle identities $(\varepsilon F)(F\eta) = \text{id}_F$ and $(G\varepsilon)(\eta G) = \text{id}_G$ capture the compatibility between unit and counit.

The tensor-hom adjunction $(- \otimes_R N) \dashv \text{Hom}_R(N, -)$ over a commutative ring $R$ is the algebraic adjunction of most immediate importance. The natural bijection $\text{Hom}_R(M \otimes_R N, P) \cong \text{Hom}_R(M, \text{Hom}_R(N, P))$ identifies bilinear maps with linear maps into a hom-module, and it is responsible for the fact that tensor products distribute over direct sums, that flat modules are exactly those for which $- \otimes N$ preserves exact sequences, and that the derived functors $\text{Tor}$ and $\text{Ext}$ arise as the non-exactness of the two sides. Recognizing the tensor-hom adjunction at work is one of the most valuable skills in homological algebra.
