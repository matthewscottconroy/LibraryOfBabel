# 35.2.1 Adjoints Are Unique up to Natural Isomorphism

One of the most reassuring properties of adjoint functors is that they are unique, in the appropriate sense. If a functor has a right adjoint, that right adjoint is determined up to natural isomorphism; and if a functor has a left adjoint, that left adjoint is likewise determined up to natural isomorphism.

\begin{theorem}
If $F \dashv G$ and$F \dashv G'$, then$G \cong G'$ as functors. If$F \dashv G$ and$F' \dashv G$, then$F \cong F'$ as functors.
\end{theorem}

*Proof.* Suppose $F \dashv G$ and$F \dashv G'$. Then for any$A \in \mathcal{C}$ and$B \in \mathcal{D}$, there are natural bijections$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A, GB) \cong \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{D}}(FA, B) \cong \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A, G'B)$. Composing these, one obtains a natural bijection$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A, GB) \cong \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A, G'B)$ that is natural in both$A$ and$B$. By the Yoneda Lemma applied to the variable$A$, this natural bijection (of representable functors of$A$) forces a natural isomorphism$G \cong G'$. The uniqueness of left adjoints is proved dually.$\square$

The key insight is that this uniqueness is not merely "up to isomorphism" in some vague sense — it is uniqueness up to *canonical natural isomorphism*, meaning the isomorphism $G \cong G'$ is itself canonically determined by the data of the two adjunctions.

**Example 1.** The free group on a set $S$ is unique up to canonical isomorphism. Any two free groups$F(S)$ and$F'(S)$ satisfying the universal property (i.e., both left adjoint to the forgetful functor evaluated at$S$) are naturally isomorphic: there is a unique group isomorphism$F(S) \cong F'(S)$ that sends each generator of$F(S)$ to the corresponding generator of$F'(S)$. This is why we speak of "the" free group on$S$ rather than "a" free group, even though it can be constructed in multiple ways (reduced words, path groupoids, etc.).

**Example 2.** The tensor product $M \otimes_R N$ is unique up to canonical isomorphism. Any two$R$-modules$T$ and$T'$ equipped with universal bilinear maps from$M \times N$ (i.e., both representing the bilinear maps functor) are canonically isomorphic. The isomorphism is the unique one compatible with the universal bilinear maps, and it is the content of the uniqueness of left adjoints applied to the tensor-hom adjunction.

**Example 3.** Products in any category are unique up to canonical isomorphism. If $(P, \pi_A, \pi_B)$ and$(P', \pi_A', \pi_B')$ are both products of$A$ and$B$ in a category$\mathcal{C}$, then there is a unique isomorphism$P \cong P'$ compatible with the projections. In$\mathbf{Set}$, both the Cartesian product$A \times B = \{(a,b)\}$ and any bijectively equivalent set serve as products; the canonical isomorphism is the unique function making the projection triangles commute.

**Example 4.** Colimits are unique up to canonical isomorphism. This is the dual statement: if $\varinjlim D$ and$\varinjlim' D$ are both colimits of the same diagram$D$, then there is a unique isomorphism between them compatible with the cocone structure maps. Applied to the specific case of coproducts: in$\mathbf{Ab}$, any two direct sums of abelian groups$A \oplus B$ and$A \oplus' B$ (however constructed) are canonically isomorphic.

**Example 5.** The algebraic closure of a field. The algebraic closure $\bar{F}$ of a field$F$ can be characterized as the "free algebraically closed field extension" of$F$: it is the left adjoint to the inclusion of algebraically closed fields into all fields (over$F$). By uniqueness of left adjoints, any two algebraic closures of$F$ are isomorphic (though not canonically so — the non-uniqueness of the isomorphism reflects that there may be non-trivial field automorphisms). This is the categorical version of the classical uniqueness theorem for algebraic closures.

\begin{remark}
The canonical nature of the isomorphism $G \cong G'$ is important in practice. When one defines the tensor product via generators and relations, or via multilinear maps, or via the bar construction, the results are canonically isomorphic: the isomorphism between any two constructions is the unique one compatible with the universal property. This is why definitions by universal properties are so powerful: they produce objects that are not merely "equal up to choices" but canonically identified, with no ambiguity in how different constructions are compared.
\end{remark}

The uniqueness theorem also has a useful converse flavor: if one wants to show that two functors are naturally isomorphic, one strategy is to show that both are adjoints to the same functor. For example, showing that $\mathrm{Hom}_R(M \otimes N, P) \cong \mathrm{Hom}_R(M, \mathrm{Hom}(N, P))$ can be approached by showing that$- \otimes N$ and$\mathrm{Hom}(N, -)$ are mutually adjoint, from which the isomorphism follows abstractly without any explicit computation.
