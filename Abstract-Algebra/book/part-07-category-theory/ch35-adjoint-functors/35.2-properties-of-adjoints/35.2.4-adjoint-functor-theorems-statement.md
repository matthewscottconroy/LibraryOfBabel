# 35.2.4 Adjoint Functor Theorems (Statement)

The examples of the previous sections all involve adjunctions that were constructed explicitly — one writes down the free group or tensor product and verifies the universal property directly. But in many situations, one knows that a functor should have an adjoint (perhaps because it preserves the right kind of limits) without having a direct construction. The adjoint functor theorems provide conditions under which an adjoint is guaranteed to exist, without requiring an explicit construction.

The key motivating observation is the necessary condition from RAPL: if $G: \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{C}$ has a left adjoint, then$G$ must preserve all limits. The adjoint functor theorems give conditions under which this necessary condition is also sufficient.

\begin{theorem}[General Adjoint Functor Theorem, Freyd]
A functor $G: \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{C}$ has a left adjoint if and only if:
1. $G$ preserves all small limits that exist in$\mathcal{D}$,
2. for each object $A \in \mathcal{C}$, the *solution set condition* holds: there exists a (small) set$\{D_i\}_{i \in I}$ of objects in$\mathcal{D}$ and morphisms$f_i: A \to G(D_i)$ such that every morphism$A \to G(D)$ in$\mathcal{C}$ factors as$A \xrightarrow{f_i} G(D_i) \xrightarrow{G(g)} G(D)$ for some$i \in I$ and some$g: D_i \to D$ in$\mathcal{D}$.
\end{theorem}

Condition 1 is the necessary condition from RAPL. Condition 2 — the solution set condition — is a smallness hypothesis: it says that the "initial approximations" to a universal element can be indexed by a set, not a proper class. Without it, a limit-preserving functor might still fail to have a left adjoint due to size issues.

\begin{theorem}[Special Adjoint Functor Theorem (SAFT)]
Let $\mathcal{D}$ be a locally small, complete category (has all small limits) that is *well-powered* (each object has only a set of subobjects) and has a *cogenerating set* (a set$\mathcal{S}$ of objects such that for any two distinct morphisms$f \neq g: A \to B$ in$\mathcal{D}$, there exists$S \in \mathcal{S}$ and$h: B \to S$ with$hf \neq hg$). Then any limit-preserving functor$G: \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{C}$ has a left adjoint.
\end{theorem}

Under the SAFT hypotheses, the solution set condition is automatic: the cogenerating set provides a small collection of "test objects" that detect all morphisms, and well-poweredness bounds the size of subobjects. One need only verify that $G$ preserves limits.

**Example 1.** Existence of free groups, revisited. Let $\mathcal{D} = \mathbf{Grp}$ and$G = U: \mathbf{Grp} \to \mathbf{Set}$. The forgetful functor preserves all limits (products of groups, kernels, etc. are computed on underlying sets). For each set$S$, the solution set for$A = S$ consists of groups of cardinality at most$|\mathbb{N} \cup S|$ with functions$S \to U(G_i)$ — a set because groups of bounded cardinality form a set up to isomorphism. The GAFT guarantees that$U$ has a left adjoint, i.e., free groups exist. This is an existence proof, not a construction.

**Example 2.** Sheafification. Let $\mathcal{D} = \mathbf{PSh}(X, \mathbf{Ab})$ (presheaves of abelian groups) and$G = \iota: \mathbf{Sh}(X, \mathbf{Ab}) \hookrightarrow \mathbf{PSh}(X, \mathbf{Ab})$ (the inclusion). The inclusion preserves limits (limits of sheaves are computed as limits of presheaves, which are sheaves). The SAFT (or a direct argument) guarantees the existence of a left adjoint to the inclusion — the sheafification functor$F \mapsto F^+$ — even before constructing it explicitly.

**Example 3.** Completions of metric spaces. The category of complete metric spaces and uniformly continuous maps is a reflective subcategory of the category of metric spaces. The SAFT applies (complete metric spaces form a complete, well-powered category with a cogenerating set), guaranteeing that the inclusion has a left adjoint — the completion functor $X \mapsto \bar{X}$.

**Example 4.** SAFT for module categories. Let $R$ be a Noetherian ring and$\mathcal{D} = R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$. This category is locally small, complete, well-powered, and has a cogenerating set$\{E(R/\mathfrak{p}) : \mathfrak{p} \in \mathrm{Spec}(R)\}$ consisting of injective hulls of residue fields (one for each prime). Any limit-preserving functor$G: R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod} \to \mathcal{C}$ has a left adjoint by SAFT.

**Example 5.** A functor preserving limits but lacking a left adjoint. The inclusion $\mathbf{HTop} \hookrightarrow \mathbf{Top}$ of the full subcategory on connected spaces preserves limits but is not a reflective subcategory (the connected-components functor is not a left adjoint to this inclusion in general). The solution set condition can fail for large spaces — this illustrates why SAFT's cogenerating-set hypothesis is necessary, not merely sufficient.

\begin{remark}
The adjoint functor theorems are not merely existence results — they transform the problem of constructing adjoints into the much more tractable problem of verifying limit-preservation. In practice, the SAFT is especially useful in "nice" categories (locally presentable categories, Grothendieck abelian categories) where all the smallness hypotheses are automatic, and every limit-preserving functor with a solution set automatically has a left adjoint. These categories — the natural homes of algebra and geometry — were partly designed with the SAFT in mind.
\end{remark}
