# 35.2 Properties of Adjoints

Once an adjunction is identified, it carries a rich cargo of structural consequences. The first and simplest is uniqueness: adjoints are unique up to natural isomorphism. If $F \dashv G$ and $F \dashv G'$, then $G \cong G'$ by a natural isomorphism; similarly, two left adjoints to the same functor are naturally isomorphic. This means that if a functor has an adjoint, the adjoint is essentially unique — it can be characterized by its universal property, and any two objects satisfying that property are canonically isomorphic. This is one of the most practically useful facts in category theory, because it licenses us to construct adjoints by universal properties without worrying about the choice of construction.

The preservation theorem is the deepest structural property: right adjoints preserve limits, and left adjoints preserve colimits. More precisely, if $G: \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{C}$ is a right adjoint and $D: \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{D}$ is a diagram with limit $L$, then $G(L)$ is the limit of $G \circ D$ in $\mathcal{C}$. Dually, left adjoints preserve colimits. The proof is a clean application of the Yoneda lemma and the hom-set bijection, and it immediately explains a long list of classical facts: the forgetful functor from groups to sets preserves products (Cartesian products of groups are direct products); the forgetful functor from rings to abelian groups preserves products; tensor products distribute over direct sums; abelianization preserves coproducts (the free product of groups maps to the direct sum of their abelianizations). All of these are consequences of a single theorem.

A *reflective subcategory* is a full subcategory $\mathcal{D} \hookrightarrow \mathcal{C}$ for which the inclusion functor has a left adjoint — the *reflector* or *localization* functor. The abelian groups form a reflective subcategory of groups, with abelianization as the reflector. The category of sheaves sits as a reflective subcategory inside the category of presheaves, with sheafification as the reflector. The category of complete metric spaces sits inside metric spaces with completion as the reflector. In each case, the reflector sends an object to its "best approximation" in the subcategory, and the unit of the adjunction is the canonical map to this approximation.

The *adjoint functor theorem* addresses the converse question: when does a functor have an adjoint? Freyd's general adjoint functor theorem states that a functor $G: \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{C}$ between complete categories has a left adjoint if and only if $G$ preserves all limits and satisfies a *solution set condition* — a smallness condition guaranteeing that the category of potential left adjoints is well-behaved. The special adjoint functor theorem applies when $\mathcal{D}$ is a complete, well-powered, locally small category with a cogenerating set: then preservation of limits alone is sufficient. These theorems are powerful existence results that guarantee the existence of free algebras, localizations, and completions without explicit construction, and they connect the theory of adjoint functors to the theory of limits and colimits developed in the next chapter.
