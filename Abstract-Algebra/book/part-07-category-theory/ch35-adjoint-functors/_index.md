# Chapter 35 — Adjoint Functors

If category theory is the mathematics of mathematics, then adjoint functors are the central theorem of that mathematics. Daniel Kan introduced adjunctions in 1958 while studying simplicial sets, and Mac Lane later declared that "adjoint functors arise everywhere." Lawvere went further: in his 1969 essay "Adjointness in Foundations," he argued that the adjunction is not merely a useful pattern but the unifying concept of all mathematics, the formal expression of the complementary relationship between construction and constraint that runs through every mathematical field. Whether one accepts this sweeping claim or not, the ubiquity of adjoint pairs in algebra, topology, logic, and geometry is undeniable.

Two functors $F: \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ and $G: \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{C}$ form an *adjoint pair*, written $F \dashv G$, if there is a natural bijection $\text{Hom}_{\mathcal{D}}(F(A), B) \cong \text{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A, G(B))$ for all objects $A \in \mathcal{C}$ and $B \in \mathcal{D}$. Here $F$ is called the *left adjoint* and $G$ the *right adjoint*. The naturality condition — that this bijection is natural in both $A$ and $B$ — is what elevates the definition above a mere coincidence of cardinality. The bijection says that maps out of $F(A)$ in $\mathcal{D}$ correspond exactly to maps into $G(B)$ in $\mathcal{C}$, and the naturality says this correspondence is coherent with all morphisms in both categories.

The free group functor $F: \mathbf{Set} \to \mathbf{Grp}$ is left adjoint to the forgetful functor $U: \mathbf{Grp} \to \mathbf{Set}$, and the adjunction bijection $\text{Hom}_{\mathbf{Grp}}(F(S), G) \cong \text{Hom}_{\mathbf{Set}}(S, U(G))$ encodes the universal property of free groups: a homomorphism from a free group is determined by where the generators go. The tensor-hom adjunction $(- \otimes_R N) \dashv \text{Hom}_R(N, -)$ over a ring $R$ is the engine of module theory, converting between bilinear maps and linear maps and generating most of the structure of homological algebra. The abelianization functor $\mathbf{Grp} \to \mathbf{Ab}$ is left adjoint to the inclusion $\mathbf{Ab} \hookrightarrow \mathbf{Grp}$. Free algebras of all kinds — free modules, free rings, polynomial algebras — arise as left adjoints to forgetful functors, and this gives a uniform explanation for their universal properties.

The adjunction can be encoded in two equivalent ways beyond the hom-set bijection. The *unit* $\eta: \text{Id}_{\mathcal{C}} \Rightarrow G \circ F$ is a natural transformation whose components $\eta_A: A \to G(F(A))$ give the "canonical map into the free object"; the *counit* $\varepsilon: F \circ G \Rightarrow \text{Id}_{\mathcal{D}}$ with components $\varepsilon_B: F(G(B)) \to B$ gives the "evaluation map." These satisfy the *triangle identities* $(\varepsilon F) \circ (F \eta) = \text{id}_F$ and $(G \varepsilon) \circ (\eta G) = \text{id}_G$, which encode the fact that the unit and counit are inverse to each other in the appropriate sense.

The deepest structural property of adjoint functors is the preservation theorem: right adjoints preserve limits and left adjoints preserve colimits. This single fact explains why the forgetful functor from groups to sets preserves products (it is a right adjoint), why the tensor product distributes over direct sums (the tensor functor is a left adjoint and direct sums are colimits), and why free constructions of all kinds preserve coproducts. The *adjoint functor theorem* gives conditions under which a functor with appropriate continuity properties must have an adjoint, connecting the existence of adjoints to the completeness properties of the categories involved. Together these results make the theory of adjoint functors a powerful tool that unifies dozens of separate theorems into a single categorical principle.
