# 36.1.1 Diagrams as Functors $D: \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$

When working in a category, one constantly encounters configurations of objects and morphisms arranged in a specific shape: a pair of maps with a common domain, a commutative square, a chain of composable morphisms. Rather than treating each such configuration as a separate ad hoc notion, category theory provides a single unifying language for all of them.

\begin{definition}
A *diagram of shape $\mathcal{J}$* in a category$\mathcal{C}$ is a functor$D: \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$, where$\mathcal{J}$ is a small category called the *index category* or *shape category*. The objects and morphisms of$\mathcal{J}$ encode the "template" of the diagram, while$D$ fills in actual objects and morphisms of$\mathcal{C}$.
\end{definition}

The index category $\mathcal{J}$ acts as a template. Different choices of$\mathcal{J}$ give rise to different types of diagrams, and the universal constructions (limits and colimits) arising from these diagrams encompass virtually all the fundamental constructions in algebra.

**Example 1.** The parallel pair diagram. Let $\mathcal{J}$ be the category with two objects$\{0, 1\}$ and two non-identity morphisms$f, g: 0 \to 1$ (and all identity morphisms). A functor$D: \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$ assigns objects$D(0) = A$ and$D(1) = B$ and two morphisms$D(f), D(g): A \to B$ — a pair of parallel morphisms in$\mathcal{C}$. The limit of such a diagram is the equalizer of$D(f)$ and$D(g)$ (Section 36.2.1), and the colimit is the coequalizer.

**Example 2.** The cospan diagram. Let $\mathcal{J}$ be the category$\bullet \to \bullet \leftarrow \bullet$ (two objects pointing to a third, from left and right). A functor$D: \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$ assigns a cospan$A \xrightarrow{f} C \xleftarrow{g} B$ — two morphisms with a common codomain. The limit of this diagram is the pullback (fibered product)$A \times_C B$ (Section 36.2.2).

**Example 3.** The span diagram. The opposite shape $\bullet \leftarrow \bullet \rightarrow \bullet$ (two morphisms from a common source) gives a span$A \xleftarrow{f} C \xrightarrow{g} B$. The colimit of a span diagram is the pushout (amalgamated coproduct) in$\mathcal{C}$ (Section 36.3.2).

**Example 4.** Discrete diagrams. If $\mathcal{J}$ is a discrete category (objects only, no non-identity morphisms), a functor$D: \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$ is simply a collection of objects$\{D(j)\}_{j \in \mathcal{J}}$ indexed by the objects of$\mathcal{J}$, with no compatibility conditions. The limit of a discrete diagram is the product$\prod_j D(j)$, and the colimit is the coproduct$\coprod_j D(j)$.

**Example 5.** Directed systems. Let $\mathcal{J}$ be the category associated to a directed poset$(I, \leq)$ (one morphism$i \to j$ whenever$i \leq j$, all composed naturally). A functor$D: \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$ is exactly a *directed system*: a collection of objects$\{D(i)\}_{i \in I}$ and morphisms$\phi_{ij}: D(i) \to D(j)$ for$i \leq j$, satisfying$\phi_{ii} = \mathrm{id}$ and$\phi_{jk} \circ \phi_{ij} = \phi_{ik}$. The colimit of such a diagram is the direct (inductive) limit$\varinjlim D$ (Section 36.3.3).

The key insight is that every commutative diagram one draws in algebra or topology is a functor from the underlying graph-category of the diagram. When one says a square commutes, one is asserting that two morphisms in $\mathcal{J}$ that are equal (by composition) map to equal composites in$\mathcal{C}$ under$D$ — which is precisely the functoriality condition. This encoding transforms the informal notion of a commutative diagram into a precise mathematical object.

\begin{remark}
Not every functor $D: \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$ corresponds to a "commutative diagram" in the informal sense — a diagram commutes when two parallel composites are equal, and this corresponds to the specific case where$\mathcal{J}$ has two objects$A, B$ with two parallel paths of composable morphisms from$A$ to$B$, and$D$ sends the corresponding composites to the same morphism in$\mathcal{C}$. The general categorical definition is more flexible: it allows arbitrary index categories, including ones with loops (endomorphisms) and multiple morphisms between the same pair of objects.
\end{remark}

The formalism of diagrams as functors makes it possible to speak of limits and colimits for arbitrary shapes simultaneously, rather than treating each shape separately. The product, equalizer, pullback, direct limit, free product, and many other constructions are all special cases of the single notion of a limit (or colimit) of a functor, as the following sections will develop.
