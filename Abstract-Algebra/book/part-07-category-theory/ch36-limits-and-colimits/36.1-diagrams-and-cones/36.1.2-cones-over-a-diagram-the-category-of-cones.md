# 36.1.2 Cones over a Diagram; the Category of Cones

Given a diagram $D: \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$, it is natural to ask: what does it mean for a single object$N$ in$\mathcal{C}$ to "map into" the entire diagram compatibly? This question leads to the notion of a cone, which formalizes the idea of a compatible family of maps into a diagram.

\begin{definition}
A *cone* over $D: \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$ with *apex*$N \in \mathcal{C}$ is a family of morphisms$\{\psi_j: N \to D(j)\}_{j \in \mathcal{J}}$, one for each object$j \in \mathcal{J}$, such that for every morphism$f: j \to k$ in$\mathcal{J}$, the triangle
$$N \xrightarrow{\psi_j} D(j) \xrightarrow{D(f)} D(k)$$
commutes with $N \xrightarrow{\psi_k} D(k)$, i.e.,$D(f) \circ \psi_j = \psi_k$.
\par
Equivalently, a cone with apex $N$ over$D$ is a natural transformation$\psi: \Delta N \Rightarrow D$, where$\Delta N: \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$ is the constant functor sending every object of$\mathcal{J}$ to$N$ and every morphism to$\mathrm{id}_N$.
\end{definition}

The compatibility condition $D(f) \circ \psi_j = \psi_k$ for all$f: j \to k$ is the heart of the definition. Without it, a "cone" would just be an arbitrary family of maps from$N$ to the objects in the diagram, with no coherence. The condition says that the legs of the cone (the maps$\psi_j$) interlock with the internal structure of the diagram: traversing from$N$ to$D(j)$ and then along$D(f)$ gives the same result as going directly to$D(k)$. Visually,$N$ sits "above" the diagram and sends a "leg" down to each object, with all the resulting triangles commuting.

**Example 1.** Cone over a discrete diagram. If $\mathcal{J} = \{0, 1\}$ (discrete, no non-identity morphisms), a cone over$D: \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$ with apex$N$ is just a pair of morphisms$\psi_0: N \to D(0)$ and$\psi_1: N \to D(1)$ with no additional compatibility condition. A cone with apex$N$ is thus any pair of morphisms from$N$ to the two objects. The "best" such cone (the limit) will be the product$D(0) \times D(1)$.

**Example 2.** Cone over a cospan. For $D: (\bullet \to \bullet \leftarrow \bullet) \to \mathcal{C}$ given by$A \xrightarrow{f} C \xleftarrow{g} B$, a cone with apex$N$ consists of morphisms$h_A: N \to A$,$h_C: N \to C$, and$h_B: N \to B$ such that$f \circ h_A = h_C$ and$g \circ h_B = h_C$. The map to$C$ is forced by the maps to$A$ and$B$:$h_C = f \circ h_A = g \circ h_B$. Such a cone is thus determined by a compatible pair$(h_A, h_B)$ with$f \circ h_A = g \circ h_B$. The limit cone is the pullback.

**Example 3.** Cone in $\mathbf{Set}$ over a directed system. For an inverse system$\{A_i, \phi_{ij}\}_{i \in I}$ (a functor from a directed poset), a cone with apex$N$ consists of functions$\psi_i: N \to A_i$ for all$i$, such that$\phi_{ij} \circ \psi_j = \psi_i$ for$i \leq j$. A compatible sequence in the inverse limit is precisely such a cone with$N = \{*\}$ (a one-element set).

\begin{definition}
The *category of cones* $\mathrm{Cone}(D)$ has:
- **Objects**: cones $(N, \{\psi_j\})$ over$D$,
- **Morphisms**: a morphism from $(N, \psi)$ to$(N', \phi)$ is a morphism$h: N \to N'$ in$\mathcal{C}$ such that$\phi_j \circ h = \psi_j$ for all$j \in \mathcal{J}$ (a map of apices compatible with all legs).
\end{definition}

**Example 4.** Morphisms of cones for a product. A morphism from a cone $(N, f, g)$ (with$f: N \to A$ and$g: N \to B$) to a cone$(N', f', g')$ (with$f': N' \to A$ and$g': N' \to B$) is a morphism$h: N \to N'$ with$f' \circ h = f$ and$g' \circ h = g$. The category of cones over a discrete two-object diagram is the category of compatible pairs of maps into$A$ and$B$, with morphisms that are maps between sources compatible with both legs.

**Example 5.** Cones in $\mathbf{Grp}$ over a parallel pair. For$f, g: G \to H$ in$\mathbf{Grp}$, a cone with apex$K$ over the parallel pair diagram consists of group homomorphisms$\phi_0: K \to G$ and$\phi_1: K \to H$ with$f \circ \phi_0 = \phi_1 = g \circ \phi_0$ (both paths to$H$ agree). This forces$f \circ \phi_0 = g \circ \phi_0$, i.e.,$\phi_0$ lands in the equalizer$\{x \in G : f(x) = g(x)\}$.

The category of cones is the natural domain for the universal property of limits: a limit is a *terminal* object in $\mathrm{Cone}(D)$ — a cone through which every other cone factors uniquely. This is the definition developed in Section 36.1.3.
