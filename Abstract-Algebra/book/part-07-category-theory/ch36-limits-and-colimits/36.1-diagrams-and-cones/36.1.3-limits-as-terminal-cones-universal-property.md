# 36.1.3 Limits as Terminal Cones; Universal Property

Among all the cones over a diagram $D: \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$, one might ask whether there is a "best" or "most efficient" one — a cone through which every other cone factors uniquely. This is precisely the definition of a limit.

\begin{definition}
A *limit* of a diagram $D: \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$ is a terminal object in the category$\mathrm{Cone}(D)$ of cones over$D$. Explicitly, a limit is a cone$(\varprojlim D, \{\pi_j\}_{j \in \mathcal{J}})$ such that for any other cone$(N, \{\psi_j\}_{j \in \mathcal{J}})$ over$D$, there exists a unique morphism$u: N \to \varprojlim D$ in$\mathcal{C}$ satisfying$\pi_j \circ u = \psi_j$ for all$j \in \mathcal{J}$.
\par
The object $\varprojlim D$ is called the *limit* (or *projective limit*, or *inverse limit*) of the diagram, and the morphisms$\pi_j: \varprojlim D \to D(j)$ are called the *projection maps* or *structure maps*.
\end{definition}

The universal property is what makes limits useful. To give a map into $\varprojlim D$ is exactly the same as giving a compatible family of maps into all the$D(j)$: every cone factors through the limit uniquely. This universality implies that limits, when they exist, are unique up to unique isomorphism.

\begin{proposition}
Limits are unique up to unique isomorphism: if $(\varprojlim D, \pi_j)$ and$(\varprojlim' D, \pi'_j)$ are both limits of$D$, then there exists a unique isomorphism$\varprojlim D \xrightarrow{\sim} \varprojlim' D$ in$\mathcal{C}$ compatible with all projection maps.
\end{proposition}

*Proof.* By terminality of $\varprojlim D$ in$\mathrm{Cone}(D)$, there is a unique morphism$u: \varprojlim' D \to \varprojlim D$ with$\pi_j \circ u = \pi'_j$, and a unique morphism$v: \varprojlim D \to \varprojlim' D$ with$\pi'_j \circ v = \pi_j$. Then$u \circ v: \varprojlim D \to \varprojlim D$ satisfies$\pi_j \circ (u \circ v) = \pi_j$, and by uniqueness$u \circ v = \mathrm{id}$. Similarly$v \circ u = \mathrm{id}$.$\square$

**Example 1.** The limit of the empty diagram $D: \emptyset \to \mathcal{C}$ (no objects, no morphisms). A cone over the empty diagram has no conditions at all — every object is trivially a cone. The terminal cone is a *terminal object*$T \in \mathcal{C}$: an object admitting a unique morphism from every other object. In$\mathbf{Set}$, any one-element set$\{*\}$ is a limit of the empty diagram. In$\mathbf{Grp}$, the trivial group$\{e\}$ is terminal. In$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$, the zero module$0$ is terminal.

**Example 2.** The limit of a discrete two-object diagram $D: \{0, 1\} \to \mathcal{C}$ with$D(0) = A$ and$D(1) = B$. A cone with apex$N$ is a pair$(f: N \to A, g: N \to B)$ with no further conditions. The limit is the *product*$A \times B$ with projections$\pi_A: A \times B \to A$ and$\pi_B: A \times B \to B$, such that any pair$(f, g)$ factors uniquely through$A \times B$ via the unique morphism$(f, g): N \to A \times B$ with$\pi_A \circ (f, g) = f$ and$\pi_B \circ (f, g) = g$. In$\mathbf{Grp}$:$G \times H$ with component-wise structure.

**Example 3.** The limit of a parallel pair $f, g: A \to B$ in$\mathbf{Ab}$. A cone$(N, h_A, h_B)$ over this diagram with apex$N$ satisfies$f \circ h_A = h_B$ and$g \circ h_A = h_B$, forcing$f \circ h_A = g \circ h_A$. The limit is the *equalizer*$\ker(f - g) = \{a \in A : f(a) = g(a)\}$ with the inclusion map. Any$N \to A$ with$f \circ h = g \circ h$ factors uniquely through$\ker(f-g)$.

**Example 4.** Limit in $\mathbf{Ring}$. The limit of the diagram$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \leftarrow \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ (reduction maps) is the pullback:$\{(a, b) \in \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} : a \equiv b \pmod{2}\}$, which can be computed as$\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ (by the Chinese Remainder perspective, accounting for the pullback structure). This illustrates that limits of rings include fibered products.

The connection to the Yoneda lemma gives a deeper perspective. The universal property of the limit says that the functor $\mathrm{Hom}(-, \varprojlim D)$ is naturally isomorphic to the functor$N \mapsto \mathrm{Nat}(\Delta N, D)$ (the set of cones from$N$ to$D$). The Yoneda lemma guarantees that$\varprojlim D$ is determined up to isomorphism by this representability, and — as Section 36.4.2 shows — this representability is exactly why right adjoints preserve limits.

**Example 5.** Limits in $\mathbf{Top}$. The product$X \times Y$ with the product topology is the limit of the discrete two-object diagram; the limit topology on any product is characterized by the universal property: a continuous map$Z \to X \times Y$ corresponds to a pair of continuous maps$Z \to X$ and$Z \to Y$. More generally, all small limits exist in$\mathbf{Top}$, and they are computed on the underlying sets with the appropriate universal topology.

\begin{remark}
A category $\mathcal{C}$ is called *complete* if every small diagram in$\mathcal{C}$ has a limit, and *finitely complete* (or *left exact*) if every finite diagram has a limit. The categories$\mathbf{Set}$,$\mathbf{Grp}$,$\mathbf{Ab}$,$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$,$\mathbf{Ring}$, and$\mathbf{Top}$ are all complete. Section 36.2.2 shows that completeness is equivalent to having all products and equalizers.
\end{remark}
