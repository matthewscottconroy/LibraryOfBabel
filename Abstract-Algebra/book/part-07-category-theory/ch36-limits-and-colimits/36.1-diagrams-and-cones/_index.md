# 36.1 Diagrams and Cones

The definition of a limit begins with a *diagram* — but a diagram in the categorical sense is more structured than a picture of objects and arrows. Formally, a diagram of shape $\mathcal{J}$ in a category $\mathcal{C}$ is a functor $D: \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$ from a small category $\mathcal{J}$ (the *index category*) to $\mathcal{C}$. The objects of $\mathcal{J}$ index the "vertices" of the diagram, the morphisms of $\mathcal{J}$ index the "arrows," and the functor $D$ assigns actual objects and morphisms in $\mathcal{C}$ to these shapes. This formalization allows the theory of limits to apply to diagrams of any shape — discrete, linear, parallel, zigzag, or any other — without requiring separate definitions for each shape.

A *cone* over a diagram $D: \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$ with apex $C$ consists of a family of morphisms $\{c_j: C \to D(j)\}_{j \in \mathcal{J}}$ such that for every morphism $u: i \to j$ in $\mathcal{J}$, the triangle $D(u) \circ c_i = c_j$ commutes. The commutativity condition is the key: it says that all the "legs" of the cone are compatible with the structure of the diagram. Without it, we would have only a family of maps, not a coherent geometric object. The category of cones over $D$ has cones as objects and cone morphisms (compatible families of morphisms between apices) as morphisms.

The *limit* of $D$, written $\lim D$ or $\varprojlim D$, is the terminal object in the category of cones: a cone $(\lim D, \{\lambda_j\})$ such that for every other cone $(C, \{c_j\})$, there is a unique morphism $u: C \to \lim D$ satisfying $\lambda_j \circ u = c_j$ for all $j$. This unique morphism is often called the *mediating morphism*. Terminal objects in a category are unique up to unique isomorphism (as one verifies by applying the universal property twice), so the limit — if it exists — is determined up to unique isomorphism by the diagram.

The power of this definition lies in its uniformity. By varying the shape of the index category $\mathcal{J}$, one recovers all the familiar constructions: a discrete two-object $\mathcal{J}$ gives binary products, a two-object category with two parallel arrows gives equalizers, a cospan $\bullet \to \bullet \leftarrow \bullet$ gives pullbacks, and the empty category gives terminal objects. In every case, the verification that a specific object satisfies the universal property is the verification that it is a terminal cone over the appropriate diagram. The single framework encompasses all of these, and theorems proved about limits in general apply to all special cases simultaneously.
