# 36.2.1 Terminal Objects; Products; Equalizers

The abstract definition of a limit as a terminal cone becomes much more concrete when one specifies the shape of the index category $\mathcal{J}$. Three fundamental shapes give rise to terminal objects, products, and equalizers — constructions that appear throughout mathematics, often without the language of limits being explicitly invoked.

**Terminal objects** arise from the empty diagram, where $\mathcal{J} = \emptyset$. A cone over the empty diagram with apex$N$ has no conditions at all, so every object is a cone; the terminal cone is an object$T$ admitting a unique morphism from every other object.

\begin{definition}
A *terminal object* in $\mathcal{C}$ is an object$T$ such that for every$A \in \mathcal{C}$, there exists a unique morphism$A \to T$.
\end{definition}

**Example 1.** Terminal objects in standard categories:
- In $\mathbf{Set}$: any one-element set$\{*\}$ is terminal; a function to$\{*\}$ is uniquely determined.
- In $\mathbf{Grp}$: the trivial group$\{e\}$ is terminal; the only group homomorphism to it sends everything to$e$.
- In $R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$: the zero module$0$ is terminal (and initial — a "zero object").
- In $\mathbf{Top}$: any one-point space$\{*\}$ is terminal.
- In a poset viewed as a category: a terminal object is a maximum element (an element $\top$ with$x \leq \top$ for all$x$).

**Products** arise from a discrete diagram — an index category with objects indexed by a set $I$ and no non-identity morphisms.

\begin{definition}
A *product* of objects $\{A_i\}_{i \in I}$ in$\mathcal{C}$ is an object$\prod_{i \in I} A_i$ equipped with projection morphisms$\pi_j: \prod_i A_i \to A_j$ for each$j \in I$, satisfying the universal property: for any object$N$ and family of morphisms$\{f_i: N \to A_i\}_{i \in I}$, there exists a unique morphism$(f_i)_i: N \to \prod_i A_i$ with$\pi_j \circ (f_i)_i = f_j$ for all$j$.
\end{definition}

**Example 2.** Products in various categories:
- In $\mathbf{Set}$:$A \times B = \{(a,b) : a \in A, b \in B\}$ with the coordinate projections.
- In $\mathbf{Grp}$:$G \times H$ with component-wise multiplication.
- In $\mathbf{Ab}$:$A \oplus B = A \times B$ (product equals direct sum for finite families).
- In $\mathbf{Ring}$:$R \times S$ with component-wise operations.
- In $\mathbf{Top}$:$X \times Y$ with the product (Tychonoff) topology.

For infinite families, products in $\mathbf{Ab}$ are the full direct product$\prod_i A_i$ (all sequences), while the coproduct (direct sum) consists only of finitely-supported sequences. Products can be very large.

**Equalizers** arise from the parallel pair diagram $\mathcal{J} = \{0 \rightrightarrows 1\}$: a category with two objects and two parallel non-identity morphisms.

\begin{definition}
Given morphisms $f, g: A \to B$ in$\mathcal{C}$, the *equalizer*$\mathrm{Eq}(f, g)$ is an object$E$ equipped with a morphism$e: E \to A$ such that$f \circ e = g \circ e$, and with the universal property: for any$N$ and$h: N \to A$ with$f \circ h = g \circ h$, there exists a unique morphism$u: N \to E$ with$e \circ u = h$.
\end{definition}

**Example 3.** Equalizers in various categories:
- In $\mathbf{Set}$:$\mathrm{Eq}(f, g) = \{a \in A : f(a) = g(a)\}$ with the inclusion map.
- In $\mathbf{Grp}$ (or$\mathbf{Ab}$):$\mathrm{Eq}(f, g) = \ker(f - g) = \{a \in A : f(a) = g(a)\}$, the subgroup of$A$ on which$f$ and$g$ agree.
- In $R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$:$\mathrm{Eq}(f, g) = \ker(f - g)$, the kernel of the difference map.
- In $\mathbf{Top}$:$\mathrm{Eq}(f, g) = \{x \in X : f(x) = g(x)\}$ with the subspace topology; this is closed when$Y$ is Hausdorff and$f, g: X \to Y$ are continuous.

**Example 4.** Equalizer as fiber. The equalizer of $f: A \to B$ and the constant map$c_b: A \to B$ (sending everything to$b \in B$) is$f^{-1}(b) = \{a \in A : f(a) = b\}$, the fiber of$f$ over$b$. This shows that fibers, kernels, and more general preimages are all examples of equalizers.

**Example 5.** Kernels as equalizers. In any category with a zero object $0$, the *kernel* of a morphism$f: A \to B$ is the equalizer of$f$ and the zero morphism$0_{AB}: A \to 0 \to B$. In$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$, this recovers the usual kernel$\ker(f) = \{a \in A : f(a) = 0\}$.

\begin{theorem}
A category $\mathcal{C}$ that has all small products and all equalizers has all small limits. More precisely, the limit of any small diagram$D: \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$ can be constructed as the equalizer of two maps between products.
\end{theorem}

*Proof sketch.* Given $D: \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$, form the product$P = \prod_{j \in \mathcal{J}} D(j)$ (over all objects of$\mathcal{J}$) and$Q = \prod_{f \in \mathrm{Mor}(\mathcal{J})} D(\mathrm{cod}(f))$ (over all morphisms of$\mathcal{J}$). Define two maps$s, t: P \to Q$ by$\pi_f \circ s = \pi_{\mathrm{cod}(f)}$ and$\pi_f \circ t = D(f) \circ \pi_{\mathrm{dom}(f)}$. The equalizer of$s$ and$t$ is the limit$\varprojlim D$.$\square$

This theorem means that products and equalizers are the "atomic" building blocks: to verify that a category has all small limits, it suffices to check these two special cases.
