# 36.2.2 Pullbacks (Fibered Products); General Limits from Products and Equalizers

The *pullback* is the limit of the cospan diagram $A \xrightarrow{f} C \xleftarrow{g} B$, a shape that encodes two morphisms with a common codomain.

\begin{definition}
Given morphisms $f: A \to C$ and$g: B \to C$ in$\mathcal{C}$, the *pullback* (or *fibered product*)$A \times_C B$ is an object equipped with morphisms$p_A: A \times_C B \to A$ and$p_B: A \times_C B \to B$ such that$f \circ p_A = g \circ p_B$, and with the universal property: for any object$N$ with morphisms$h_A: N \to A$ and$h_B: N \to B$ satisfying$f \circ h_A = g \circ h_B$, there exists a unique morphism$u: N \to A \times_C B$ with$p_A \circ u = h_A$ and$p_B \circ u = h_B$.
\end{definition}

**Example 1.** Pullback in $\mathbf{Set}$. The pullback is the fiber product$A \times_C B = \{(a, b) \in A \times B : f(a) = g(b)\}$ with the natural projections to$A$ and$B$. For instance, if$f: A \to C$ and$g: B \to C$ with$A = \{1, 2, 3\}$,$B = \{x, y\}$,$C = \{p, q\}$,$f(1) = f(2) = p$,$f(3) = q$,$g(x) = p$,$g(y) = q$: then$A \times_C B = \{(1,x), (2,x), (3,y)\}$.

**Example 2.** Pullback as preimage. If $f: A \to C$ is any map and$g: B \hookrightarrow C$ is the inclusion of a subset$B \subseteq C$, then$A \times_C B = f^{-1}(B) = \{a \in A : f(a) \in B\}$. The pullback "restricts" the domain of$f$ to the preimage of$B$. In topology: if$f: X \to Y$ is continuous and$Z \hookrightarrow Y$ is a subspace, then$X \times_Y Z = f^{-1}(Z)$ with the subspace topology.

**Example 3.** Pullback in $\mathbf{Grp}$. For group homomorphisms$f: G \to K$ and$h: H \to K$, the pullback is the *subdirect product*$G \times_K H = \{(g, h) \in G \times H : f(g) = h(h)\}$ with component-wise multiplication. This is a subgroup of$G \times H$. For example, the pullback of$f, g: S_3 \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ (both being the sign homomorphism) is$\{(\sigma, \tau) \in S_3 \times S_3 : \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) = \mathrm{sgn}(\tau)\} = (A_3 \times A_3) \cup (S_3 \setminus A_3) \times (S_3 \setminus A_3)$.

**Example 4.** Pullback in $R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$. For module maps$f: M \to P$ and$g: N \to P$, the pullback is$M \times_P N = \{(m, n) \in M \times N : f(m) = g(n)\}$ with the inherited module structure. This appears naturally in the construction of fibered direct sums and in the Mayer–Vietoris sequence.

**Example 5.** Pullback in $\mathbf{Ring}$. For ring maps$f: R \to T$ and$g: S \to T$, the pullback is$R \times_T S = \{(r, s) \in R \times S : f(r) = g(s)\}$ with component-wise ring operations. This construction appears in the "glueing" of rings along a common quotient.

The key theorem connecting pullbacks to the general theory:

\begin{theorem}
Every small limit can be constructed from products and equalizers. Specifically, if $\mathcal{C}$ has all small products and all equalizers, then$\mathcal{C}$ has all small limits.
\end{theorem}

*Explicit construction.* Given a small diagram $D: \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$, form the two products:
$$P = \prod_{j \in \mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{J})} D(j) \qquad \text{and} \qquad Q = \prod_{(f: j \to k) \in \mathrm{Mor}(\mathcal{J})} D(k).$$
Define two maps $s, t: P \to Q$ by:
- The $f$-component of$s$ is$\pi_k: P \to D(k)$ (project to the codomain object),
- The $f$-component of$t$ is$D(f) \circ \pi_j: P \to D(j) \to D(k)$ (project to domain, then apply the diagram map).

Then $\varprojlim D = \mathrm{Eq}(s, t)$: an element of$\mathrm{Eq}(s,t)$ is a family$\{x_j \in D(j)\}$ with$x_k = D(f)(x_j)$ for all morphisms$f: j \to k$ in$\mathcal{J}$, which is exactly a compatible cone from the terminal object.$\square$

As a special case: the pullback $A \times_C B$ can be constructed as the equalizer of$f \circ \pi_A, g \circ \pi_B: A \times B \to C$, i.e., as the subobject$\{(a, b) \in A \times B : f(a) = g(b)\}$ of the product. This shows pullbacks are special cases of limits from products and equalizers.

\begin{remark}
This theorem provides the standard proof that $\mathbf{Set}$,$\mathbf{Grp}$,$\mathbf{Ab}$,$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$,$\mathbf{Ring}$, and$\mathbf{Top}$ are all complete: one verifies that these categories have all products and all equalizers (both straightforward), and the theorem gives all small limits for free. The theorem also shows that checking "completeness" reduces to checking two specific types of limits.
\end{remark}
