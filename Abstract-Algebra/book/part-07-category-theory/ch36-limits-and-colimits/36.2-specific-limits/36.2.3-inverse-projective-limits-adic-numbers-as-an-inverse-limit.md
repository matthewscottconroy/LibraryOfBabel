# 36.2.3 Inverse (Projective) Limits; $p$-adic Numbers as an Inverse Limit

Many of the most important constructions in algebra and topology are built by assembling an infinite sequence of approximations into a single coherent object. Completions of metric spaces, profinite groups, and formal power series rings all share the same categorical blueprint.

\begin{definition}
An *inverse system* (or *projective system*) in a category $\mathcal{C}$ consists of objects$\{A_i\}_{i \in I}$ indexed by a directed poset$(I, \leq)$, together with morphisms$\phi_{ij}: A_j \to A_i$ for each$i \leq j$ (note the direction: maps go from "larger" to "smaller" indices), satisfying$\phi_{ii} = \mathrm{id}_{A_i}$ and$\phi_{ij} \circ \phi_{jk} = \phi_{ik}$ for all$i \leq j \leq k$.
\par
The *inverse limit* (or *projective limit*) $\varprojlim_i A_i$ is the limit of this diagram (viewed as a functor from the opposite of the directed poset$I$ to$\mathcal{C}$).
\end{definition}

In $\mathbf{Set}$ or$\mathbf{Ab}$, the inverse limit can be described concretely:$\varprojlim_i A_i = \{(a_i)_{i \in I} \in \prod_i A_i : \phi_{ij}(a_j) = a_i \text{ for all } i \leq j\}$, the subobject of the product consisting of *compatible sequences*.

**Example 1.** The $p$-adic integers$\mathbb{Z}_p$. Define the inverse system$\cdots \to \mathbb{Z}/p^3\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/p^2\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ where each map is reduction modulo the smaller power of$p$. The inverse limit is:
$$\mathbb{Z}_p = \varprojlim_n \mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z} = \{(a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots) : a_n \in \mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z},\, a_{n-1} \equiv a_n \pmod{p^{n-1}}\}.$$
An element of $\mathbb{Z}_p$ is a sequence of compatible residues — "an integer known modulo all powers of$p$ simultaneously." The element$5 \in \mathbb{Z}$ corresponds to the sequence$(5 \bmod p, 5 \bmod p^2, 5 \bmod p^3, \ldots)$. The element$-1 \in \mathbb{Z}_p$ corresponds to$(p-1, p^2-1, p^3-1, \ldots)$. Elements of$\mathbb{Z}_p$ can be written in base$p$ as formal series$a_0 + a_1 p + a_2 p^2 + \cdots$ with$a_i \in \{0, 1, \ldots, p-1\}$. The integers$\mathbb{Z}$ embed diagonally into$\mathbb{Z}_p$, with$n \mapsto (n \bmod p^k)_k$.

**Example 2.** The profinite completion of $\mathbb{Z}$. The inverse system$\{\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\}$ indexed by positive integers$n$ (ordered by divisibility, with transition maps$\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ for$n \mid m$) has inverse limit$\hat{\mathbb{Z}} = \varprojlim_n \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, the *profinite completion* of$\mathbb{Z}$. By the Chinese Remainder Theorem,$\hat{\mathbb{Z}} \cong \prod_p \mathbb{Z}_p$ (product over all primes$p$). This ring encodes all the finite residues of an integer simultaneously and appears in number theory as the ring through which the absolute Galois group of$\mathbb{Q}$ acts.

**Example 3.** Formal power series. The inverse system $k[x]/(x^n)$ (truncated polynomial algebras, with maps$k[x]/(x^{n+1}) \to k[x]/(x^n)$ reducing modulo$x^n$) has inverse limit$k[[x]] = \varprojlim_n k[x]/(x^n)$, the ring of formal power series over$k$. An element of$k[[x]]$ is a compatible sequence$(f_n \in k[x]/(x^n))_n$ of truncated polynomials, i.e., a formal series$\sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n x^n$ with no convergence requirements.

**Example 4.** Profinite groups. A *profinite group* is an inverse limit of finite groups (in the category of topological groups with the discrete topology on the finite groups and the limit topology on the inverse limit). The absolute Galois group $\mathrm{Gal}(\bar{F}/F)$ of a field$F$ is the inverse limit$\varprojlim_{E/F \text{ finite Galois}} \mathrm{Gal}(E/F)$. Its topology (the Krull topology) is the inverse limit topology, making it a profinite group. The inverse limit captures the coherence of all finite Galois groups simultaneously.

**Example 5.** Completion of a local ring. For a local ring $(R, \mathfrak{m})$ with maximal ideal$\mathfrak{m}$, the$\mathfrak{m}$-adic completion is$\hat{R} = \varprojlim_n R/\mathfrak{m}^n$. For$R = \mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$ (the localization of$\mathbb{Z}$ at$p$),$\hat{R} = \mathbb{Z}_p$. For$R = k[x]_{(x)}$ (polynomial ring localized at the ideal$(x)$),$\hat{R} = k[[x]]$. The completion preserves many algebraic properties (e.g., Noetherianness) and is fundamental in algebraic geometry (local-to-global arguments, deformation theory).

\begin{remark}
Inverse limits are computed "term-wise" in $\mathbf{Set}$ and$\mathbf{Ab}$, but their exactness properties are more subtle. In$\mathbf{Ab}$, the inverse limit functor$\varprojlim$ is not exact in general: it is left exact (preserves exact sequences on the left) but may fail to be right exact. The obstruction to right exactness is measured by the derived functor$\varprojlim^1$ (the "lim-one" functor), which appears in the Milnor exact sequence in algebraic topology and in derived algebraic geometry.
\end{remark}

**Example 6.** Inverse limits of rings and modules. For a Noetherian local ring $(R, \mathfrak{m})$, the$\mathfrak{m}$-adic completion$\hat{R} = \varprojlim_n R/\mathfrak{m}^n$ is an inverse limit of quotient rings. The system maps$R/\mathfrak{m}^{n+1} \to R/\mathfrak{m}^n$ are the canonical quotient maps, and elements of$\hat{R}$ are compatible sequences of residues modulo all powers of$\mathfrak{m}$. By the Artin–Rees lemma, the$\mathfrak{m}$-adic completion of a finitely generated module$M$ is$\hat{M} = \hat{R} \otimes_R M$ (when$R$ is Noetherian), and the natural map$M \to \hat{M}$ is an injection when$M$ is torsion-free. The$\mathfrak{m}$-adic completion is the fundamental localization tool in commutative algebra and algebraic geometry, used in the study of formal neighborhoods and in Hensel's lemma.

The Mittag–Leffler condition provides a sufficient criterion for the exactness of inverse limits: if in an inverse system $\{A_n, \phi_{n+1,n}\}$ the images$\phi_{n+1,n}(A_{n+1})$ stabilize (i.e., for each$n$, the decreasing chain of images$\phi_{n+k,n}(A_{n+k}) \subseteq A_n$ eventually stabilizes), then the derived functor$\varprojlim^1 A_n = 0$, and the inverse limit is exact. This condition is satisfied in the$p$-adic case ($\mathbb{Z}_p = \varprojlim \mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$), confirming that inverse limits of finite groups satisfying the Mittag–Leffler condition are well-behaved.
