# 36.3.4 Filtered and Sifted Colimits

Not all colimits behave equally well with respect to other categorical operations. Certain special classes of colimits enjoy stronger commutativity properties that make them indispensable in algebra and the theory of algebraic structures.

\begin{definition}
An index category $\mathcal{J}$ is *filtered* if it satisfies two conditions:
1. For any two objects $j, k \in \mathcal{J}$, there exists an object$l \in \mathcal{J}$ with morphisms$j \to l$ and$k \to l$ (any two objects have a common "future"),
2. For any two parallel morphisms $f, g: j \to k$, there exists a morphism$h: k \to l$ with$h \circ f = h \circ g$ (any two parallel morphisms are eventually equalized).
\par
A *filtered colimit* (or *filtered direct limit*) is a colimit over a filtered category.
\end{definition}

Every directed poset is filtered (condition 1 is directedness; condition 2 is trivially satisfied since posets have at most one morphism between any pair of objects). So direct limits are special cases of filtered colimits, but filtered colimits are more general.

\begin{theorem}[Filtered colimits commute with finite limits]
In $\mathbf{Set}$ (and in$\mathbf{Ab}$,$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$, and more generally in any locally finitely presentable category), filtered colimits commute with finite limits: if$D: \mathcal{I} \times \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$ is a functor with$\mathcal{I}$ finite and$\mathcal{J}$ filtered, then
$$\varinjlim_{\mathcal{J}} \varprojlim_{\mathcal{I}} D(-,-) \cong \varprojlim_{\mathcal{I}} \varinjlim_{\mathcal{J}} D(-,-).$$
\end{theorem}

**Example 1.** In $\mathbf{Set}$, filtered colimits commute with finite products. Let$\{A_i\}$ and$\{B_i\}$ be directed systems of sets. Then$\varinjlim (A_i \times B_i) \cong (\varinjlim A_i) \times (\varinjlim B_i)$: an element of the direct limit of products is a pair$(a, b)$ where$a$ comes from some$A_i$ and$b$ from some$B_j$, and since the system is directed, both$a$ and$b$ eventually live in a common$A_k \times B_k$.

**Example 2.** In $R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$, filtered colimits preserve exactness. If$0 \to A_i \to B_i \to C_i \to 0$ is an exact sequence for each$i$, with compatible maps between the sequences, then$0 \to \varinjlim A_i \to \varinjlim B_i \to \varinjlim C_i \to 0$ is exact. This is a key property: the direct limit of short exact sequences is exact. It fails for general (non-filtered) colimits: the coproduct (infinite direct sum) of short exact sequences is exact, but the colimit of a non-filtered diagram of exact sequences need not be.

**Example 3.** The commutativity of filtered colimits with kernels. In $R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$,$\varinjlim \ker(f_i) \cong \ker(\varinjlim f_i)$: the direct limit of kernels is the kernel of the direct limit map. This reflects the filtered commutativity theorem applied to equalizers (kernels in$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ are equalizers with the zero map). It fails for non-directed (non-filtered) colimits in general.

\begin{definition}
A category $\mathcal{J}$ is *sifted* if the diagonal functor$\mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{J} \times \mathcal{J}$ is final (every object of$\mathcal{J} \times \mathcal{J}$ can be "reached" from an object in the image of the diagonal). Equivalently,$\mathcal{J}$ is sifted if and only if colimits over$\mathcal{J}$ commute with finite products in$\mathbf{Set}$.
\end{definition}

Sifted colimits include:
- *Filtered colimits*: every filtered category is sifted.
- *Reflexive coequalizers*: the coequalizer of a pair $(f, g: A \to B)$ where there exists a common section$s: B \to A$ (i.e.,$f \circ s = g \circ s = \mathrm{id}_B$) is a sifted colimit.

**Example 4.** Sifted colimits in Lawvere theories. A Lawvere theory is a small category $\mathbb{T}$ with finite products. The category of models (product-preserving functors$\mathbb{T} \to \mathbf{Set}$) is closed under sifted colimits in$[\mathbb{T}, \mathbf{Set}]$: the sifted colimit of models is again a model. This is not true for general colimits: the coproduct of two group homomorphisms (as a set map) need not be a group homomorphism in the obvious sense. Sifted colimits are exactly the colimits that preserve the "algebraic" character of models.

**Example 5.** Finitely presentable objects. An object $A$ in a category$\mathcal{C}$ is *finitely presentable* (or *compact*) if$\mathrm{Hom}(A, -)$ preserves filtered colimits:$\mathrm{Hom}(A, \varinjlim D) \cong \varinjlim \mathrm{Hom}(A, D(-))$ for every filtered diagram$D$. In$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$, the finitely presentable modules are exactly the finitely presented modules (admitting a finite presentation with finitely many generators and relations). In$\mathbf{Grp}$, finitely presentable groups are finitely presented groups. A category$\mathcal{C}$ is *locally finitely presentable* if it is cocomplete and has a set of finitely presentable objects that generate all objects as filtered colimits. Such categories include$\mathbf{Set}$,$\mathbf{Ab}$,$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$,$\mathbf{Grp}$, and$\mathbf{Ring}$, and they are the natural setting for model theory and universal algebra from a categorical perspective.

\begin{remark}
The distinction between filtered and sifted colimits is important for the theory of algebraic structures: filtered colimits commute with all finite limits, while sifted colimits commute only with finite products. In the setting of $\infty$-categories and homotopical algebra, analogous notions of filtered and sifted colimits play a central role, and the locally finitely presentable condition becomes the setting for "compactly generated" higher categories. Both notions will reappear in the context of derived functors and model categories.
\end{remark}
