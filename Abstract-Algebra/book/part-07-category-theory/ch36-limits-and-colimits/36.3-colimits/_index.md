# 36.3 Colimits

Colimits are the dual of limits: they arise by reversing all arrows. A *cocone* under a diagram $D: \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$ with nadir $C$ consists of morphisms $\{c_j: D(j) \to C\}_{j \in \mathcal{J}}$ compatible with the diagram — for every morphism $u: i \to j$ in $\mathcal{J}$, we need $c_j \circ D(u) = c_i$. The colimit $\text{colim}\, D$ is the *initial cocone*: a cocone through which every other cocone factors uniquely. Everything about limits and colimits is formally dual, and every theorem about limits has a dual theorem about colimits — but the geometric and algebraic flavor of each is quite different, and colimits are in many ways more flexible and more immediately useful in algebra.

*Coproducts* are colimits over a discrete diagram. In $\mathbf{Set}$ the coproduct is the disjoint union; in $\mathbf{Ab}$ and $\mathbf{Mod}_R$ it is the direct sum $\bigoplus A_i$ (which, for finite collections, coincides with the direct product — a special feature of additive categories that fails for infinite collections); in $\mathbf{Grp}$ it is the free product $A * B$. Coproducts in $\mathbf{Grp}$ are much more complicated than in $\mathbf{Ab}$, reflecting the fact that non-abelian groups have a much richer combinatorial structure.

*Coequalizers* are colimits over a parallel pair $\bullet \rightrightarrows \bullet$. The coequalizer of $f, g: A \rightrightarrows B$ is the quotient of $B$ by the equivalence relation generated by $f(a) \sim g(a)$ for all $a \in A$. In $\mathbf{Ab}$, coequalizers are quotients by subgroups; cokernels are the special case $g = 0$. *Pushouts* are colimits over the span $\bullet \leftarrow \bullet \rightarrow \bullet$: given $f: A \to B$ and $g: A \to C$, the pushout $B \sqcup_A C$ is the "gluing" of $B$ and $C$ along the image of $A$. In $\mathbf{Grp}$ the pushout is the amalgamated free product; in topology it is the attaching construction; in commutative algebra the pushout of ring homomorphisms is the tensor product $B \otimes_A C$.

*Filtered colimits* (also called *direct limits* or *inductive limits*) are colimits over filtered index categories — those in which any two objects have a common upper bound. Filtered colimits commute with finite limits in $\mathbf{Set}$ and $\mathbf{Ab}$, a crucially useful fact. The algebraic closure of a field $k$ can be constructed as a filtered colimit: one takes the colimit of the directed system of all finite algebraic extensions of $k$ ordered by inclusion. The rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$ are the filtered colimit of the directed system $\mathbb{Z} \to \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z} \to \frac{1}{6}\mathbb{Z} \to \cdots$ of inclusions of cyclic groups. Filtered colimits are the colimits that preserve most of the algebraic structure of the diagram, and they play a central role in the theory of flat modules and in model theory.
