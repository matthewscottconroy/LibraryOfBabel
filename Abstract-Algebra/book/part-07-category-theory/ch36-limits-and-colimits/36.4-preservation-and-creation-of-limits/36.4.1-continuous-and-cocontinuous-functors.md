# 36.4.1 Continuous and Cocontinuous Functors

Once limits and colimits are defined in individual categories, a natural question arises about how functors interact with them. The behavior of a functor with respect to limits and colimits encodes deep information about its nature and is one of the primary tools for understanding functors in practice.

\begin{definition}
A functor $F: \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ *preserves a limit* of$D: \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$ if, whenever$(\varprojlim D, \{\pi_j\})$ is a limit cone in$\mathcal{C}$, the cone$(F(\varprojlim D), \{F(\pi_j)\})$ is a limit cone for$F \circ D$ in$\mathcal{D}$. A functor is *continuous* (or *limit-preserving*) if it preserves all small limits. A functor is *cocontinuous* (or *colimit-preserving*) if it preserves all small colimits.
\end{definition}

The terminology "continuous" is motivated by an analogy with topology: a continuous function between spaces preserves convergence (limits of sequences, or more generally of nets), and a continuous functor preserves the limits of diagrams. This analogy is more than superficial: in the right framework (using the topology on the morphism category), categorical continuity is a generalization of topological continuity.

**Example 1.** The forgetful functor $U: \mathbf{Grp} \to \mathbf{Set}$ is continuous. It preserves all limits: products of groups are products of underlying sets, kernels of group homomorphisms are kernels of the underlying functions, and so on. This is not a coincidence:$U$ is right adjoint to the free group functor, and right adjoints are always continuous (RAPL, Section 35.2.2).

$U$ is not cocontinuous: the coproduct in$\mathbf{Grp}$ is the free product$G * H$, and$U(G * H)$ is the underlying set of the free product, which is much larger than$U(G) \sqcup U(H)$ (the disjoint union). Specifically,$U(G * H) \neq U(G) \sqcup U(H)$ in general (there are elements of$G * H$ that are not in either$G$ or$H$, such as alternating products$g_1 h_1 g_2 h_2 \cdots$).

**Example 2.** The hom functor $\mathrm{Hom}_R(M, -): R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod} \to \mathbf{Ab}$ is continuous but not cocontinuous. It preserves all limits (as a right adjoint to$- \otimes_R M$). It fails to preserve colimits: for instance,$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is not zero, but$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Q}) = 0$ and$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, and the exact sequence$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z} \to 0$ applied to$\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}, -)$ shows non-preservation of cokernels.

**Example 3.** The tensor product $- \otimes_R N: R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod} \to R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ is cocontinuous but not continuous. As a left adjoint, it preserves all colimits. It fails to preserve limits: for a product$\prod_i M_i$, one has$(\prod_i M_i) \otimes N \not\cong \prod_i (M_i \otimes N)$ in general for infinite products. Specifically,$(\prod_{n \geq 1} \mathbb{Z}) \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{Q} \ncong \prod_{n \geq 1} \mathbb{Q}$ — the left side is a countable product of$\mathbb{Q}$'s modulo some torsion relations, while the right side is an uncountable product.

It is important to distinguish preservation from *creation* of limits.

\begin{definition}
A functor $F: \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ *creates limits* of$D: \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$ if: whenever$F \circ D$ has a limit$(\ell, \{\mu_j\})$ in$\mathcal{D}$, there exists a unique cone$(L, \{\lambda_j\})$ in$\mathcal{C}$ over$D$ such that$F(L) = \ell$ and$F(\lambda_j) = \mu_j$, and moreover$(L, \lambda_j)$ is a limit of$D$ in$\mathcal{C}$.
\end{definition}

**Example 4.** The forgetful functor $U: R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod} \to \mathbf{Ab}$ creates limits. Limits of$R$-modules are computed on the underlying abelian groups: given a diagram$D$ of$R$-modules, the limit of$U \circ D$ in$\mathbf{Ab}$ carries a unique$R$-module structure making it the limit of$D$ in$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$. The$R$-module structure on the limit is inherited component-wise from the diagram.

**Example 5.** The forgetful functor $U: \mathbf{Grp} \to \mathbf{Set}$ creates limits of diagrams in$\mathbf{Grp}$: the limit of a diagram of groups, computed as a subset of the product of the underlying sets (with the equalizer structure), carries a unique group structure making it the limit in$\mathbf{Grp}$. This is the categorical explanation for why limits of groups, rings, and modules are computed "term-wise" on the underlying algebraic operations.

\begin{remark}
A functor $F$ creates limits if and only if both (1)$F$ preserves limits, and (2)$F$ *reflects* limits: if$(L, \lambda_j)$ is a cone in$\mathcal{C}$ such that$(F(L), F(\lambda_j))$ is a limit in$\mathcal{D}$, then$(L, \lambda_j)$ is already a limit in$\mathcal{C}$. Together, preservation and reflection of limits amount to creating limits. The standard forgetful functors in algebra create limits precisely because algebraic structures are determined by their underlying sets with the appropriate operations, which are preserved and reflected by the forgetful functor.
\end{remark}
