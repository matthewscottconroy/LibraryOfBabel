# Chapter 36 — Limits and Colimits

One of the most striking features of category theory is the way it unifies disparate constructions under a single definition. The Cartesian product of sets, the direct product of groups, the product of topological spaces, the fibered product (pullback) of rings, the inverse limit of a tower of groups — these are all products or limits in their respective categories. Their duals — disjoint unions, free products, pushouts, direct limits — are coproducts or colimits. Before category theory, each of these had a separate definition and required separate verification of its properties. Category theory collapses them all into two dual concepts, and proves general theorems that apply to every instance at once.

A *diagram* in a category $\mathcal{C}$ is a functor $D: \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$ from a small *index category* $\mathcal{J}$. The shape of $\mathcal{J}$ determines the shape of the diagram: a discrete two-object category gives a pair of objects (for binary products), a category $\bullet \rightrightarrows \bullet$ gives a pair of parallel morphisms (for equalizers), and a span $\bullet \leftarrow \bullet \rightarrow \bullet$ gives a diagram for pullbacks. A *cone* over $D$ is an object $C$ together with a compatible family of morphisms $C \to D(j)$ for each object $j \in \mathcal{J}$, where compatible means that the cone morphisms commute with all the arrows in the diagram. The *limit* of $D$, when it exists, is the *terminal cone*: a cone $\lim D$ with the property that every other cone factors through it by a unique morphism. The universal property is built into the definition, and uniqueness up to unique isomorphism follows immediately.

The dual construction — a *colimit* — is obtained by reversing all arrows. A *cocone* under $D$ consists of an object $C$ together with morphisms $D(j) \to C$ compatible with the diagram, and the colimit is the *initial cocone*. In $\mathbf{Set}$, limits are computed as subsets of products (satisfying compatibility conditions) and colimits as quotients of coproducts (with elements identified by the compatibility relations). In $\mathbf{Ab}$ and $\mathbf{Mod}_R$, both limits and colimits exist and can be computed explicitly: the limit of a diagram of $R$-modules is a submodule of the product, and the colimit is a quotient of the coproduct.

The classical constructions of algebra find their place in this framework. The $p$-adic integers $\mathbb{Z}_p = \varprojlim \mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$ are the limit of an inverse (projective) system — a tower of finite rings connected by reduction maps — and the limit captures all compatible sequences of approximations. The algebraic closure of a field is a filtered colimit of algebraic extensions. The amalgamated free product of groups is a pushout. The group ring and the tensor product of algebras arise as colimits of suitable diagrams. Each of these familiar constructions becomes transparent once it is recognized as an instance of the universal limit or colimit construction.

Perhaps the most powerful result of this chapter is the interaction between limits, colimits, and adjoints: right adjoints preserve limits and left adjoints preserve colimits (the theorem proved abstractly in Chapter 35 now becomes a tool for computation). Representable functors $\text{Hom}(A, -)$ are right adjoints to the constant functor on $A$ and therefore preserve all limits. This single fact explains why $\text{Hom}(A, \prod B_i) \cong \prod \text{Hom}(A, B_i)$, why $\text{Hom}(A, -)$ converts equalizers to equalizers, and why the injective modules in $\mathbf{Mod}_R$ are exactly those for which $\text{Hom}_R(-, E)$ is exact. The theory of limits and colimits is not a collection of isolated definitions but a unified framework with deep connections to every other part of category theory.
