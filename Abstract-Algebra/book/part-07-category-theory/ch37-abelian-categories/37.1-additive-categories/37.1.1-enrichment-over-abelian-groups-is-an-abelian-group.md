# 37.1.1 Enrichment over Abelian Groups: $\mathrm{Hom}(A,B)$ Is an Abelian Group

The categories that appear in algebra — modules over a ring, abelian groups, chain complexes — share a feature not present in $\mathbf{Set}$ or$\mathbf{Grp}$: one can add morphisms. If$f, g: M \to N$ are two$R$-module homomorphisms, their pointwise sum$(f + g)(m) = f(m) + g(m)$ is again a homomorphism, and this addition makes$\mathrm{Hom}_R(M, N)$ into an abelian group. The axiom system of *additive categories* captures exactly this structure.

\begin{definition}
An *additive category* is a category $\mathcal{A}$ satisfying:
1. Each hom-set $\mathrm{Hom}(A, B)$ carries the structure of an abelian group (written additively), and composition is *bilinear*:$h \circ (f + g) = h \circ f + h \circ g$ and$(f + g) \circ h = f \circ h + g \circ h$ for all composable morphisms.
2. $\mathcal{A}$ has a *zero object*$0$: an object that is simultaneously initial and terminal.
3. $\mathcal{A}$ has all finite biproducts (Section 37.1.2).
\end{definition}

Conditions 1 and 2 are sometimes taken as the sole definition, and condition 3 is then derived as a consequence. More precisely, in the presence of a zero object and bilinear composition, the existence of finite biproducts is equivalent to the enrichment over abelian groups — a non-trivial theorem that shows the additive axioms are tightly interlocked.

**Example 1.** The category $R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ of left modules over a ring$R$. For any two$R$-modules$M$ and$N$,$\mathrm{Hom}_R(M, N)$ is an abelian group under pointwise addition:$(f + g)(m) = f(m) + g(m)$. The zero element is the zero homomorphism$0(m) = 0$ for all$m$. Composition distributes over addition:$(h \circ (f + g))(m) = h(f(m) + g(m)) = h(f(m)) + h(g(m)) = (h \circ f)(m) + (h \circ g)(m)$. The zero object is the zero module$\{0\}$.

**Example 2.** The category $\mathbf{Ab}$ of abelian groups is a special case: take$R = \mathbb{Z}$ in Example 1. The hom-group$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(A, B) = \mathrm{Hom}(A, B)$ is the abelian group of all group homomorphisms from$A$ to$B$, with pointwise addition.

**Example 3.** The category $\mathrm{Ch}(R)$ of chain complexes of$R$-modules. A morphism between chain complexes$f: C_\bullet \to D_\bullet$ is a collection of module maps$f_n: C_n \to D_n$ commuting with the differentials. The sum$(f + g)_n = f_n + g_n$ is again a chain map (by bilinearity of the module hom-groups), so$\mathrm{Hom}(C_\bullet, D_\bullet)$ is an abelian group. The zero object is the zero complex$0_\bullet$ (all zero modules).

**Example 4.** The non-example: $\mathbf{Set}$. In$\mathbf{Set}$,$\mathrm{Hom}(X, Y)$ is just a set of functions; there is generally no natural way to add two functions$f, g: X \to Y$ (unless$Y$ happens to carry a group structure).$\mathbf{Set}$ has no zero object: the empty set is initial but not terminal (no function from a non-empty set to$\emptyset$), and one-element sets are terminal but not initial.

**Example 5.** The non-example: $\mathbf{Grp}$. In$\mathbf{Grp}$,$\mathrm{Hom}(G, H)$ is a set of group homomorphisms with no natural group structure (the pointwise product of two homomorphisms is generally not a homomorphism, since$G$ may be non-abelian). The trivial group is both initial and terminal (so$\mathbf{Grp}$ has a zero object), but the enrichment over abelian groups fails.$\mathbf{Grp}$ is not additive.

The enrichment over abelian groups is more than a convenience; it is a structural prerequisite for all of homological algebra. Without the ability to add morphisms, one cannot form kernels in the categorical sense (as equalizers with the zero morphism), cannot define exactness, and cannot speak of derived functors.

\begin{remark}
The condition that composition be bilinear is crucial and not automatic from the existence of abelian group structures on hom-sets. One could, in principle, put an abelian group structure on each $\mathrm{Hom}(A,B)$ that is not compatible with composition. The bilinearity axiom ensures that the abelian group structure on hom-sets is compatible with the categorical structure, making the enrichment meaningful.
\end{remark}

The enrichment over abelian groups also interacts with the biproduct structure: the canonical isomorphism $\mathrm{Hom}(A \oplus B, C) \cong \mathrm{Hom}(A, C) \oplus \mathrm{Hom}(B, C)$ (a consequence of the universal property of biproducts) is an isomorphism of abelian groups, and similarly for$\mathrm{Hom}(C, A \oplus B) \cong \mathrm{Hom}(C, A) \oplus \mathrm{Hom}(C, B)$. These isomorphisms are natural in$C$, and they are the key structural facts underlying the projectivity of free modules and the injectivity of divisible modules.
