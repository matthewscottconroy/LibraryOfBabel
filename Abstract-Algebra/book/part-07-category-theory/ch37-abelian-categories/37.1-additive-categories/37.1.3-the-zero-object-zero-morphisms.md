# 37.1.3 The Zero Object; Zero Morphisms

In an additive category, the zero object $0$ plays a role analogous to both the trivial module and the zero element: it is the object from which every structure starts and into which everything can be sent.

\begin{definition}
A *zero object* in a category $\mathcal{C}$ is an object$0$ that is simultaneously initial (there is a unique morphism$0 \to A$ for every$A$) and terminal (there is a unique morphism$A \to 0$ for every$A$).
\end{definition}

The existence of a zero object is one of the defining conditions of an additive category, and its presence distinguishes additive categories from categories like $\mathbf{Set}$ (which has a terminal but no initial = terminal) or$\mathbf{Ring}$ (which has an initial but the initial and terminal objects are different:$\mathbb{Z}$ is initial and the zero ring$\{0\}$ with$1 = 0$ is terminal).

**Example 1.** Zero objects in standard additive categories:
- In $R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$: the zero module$\{0\}$ is both initial (unique module map$0 \to M$ sends$0 \mapsto 0_M$) and terminal (unique module map$M \to 0$ sends everything to$0$).
- In $\mathbf{Ab}$: the trivial group$\{0\}$.
- In $\mathrm{Ch}(R)$: the zero complex$\cdots \to 0 \to 0 \to 0 \to \cdots$.

\begin{definition}
For any pair of objects $A, B$ in an additive category with zero object$0$, the *zero morphism*$0_{AB}: A \to B$ is the unique composite$A \to 0 \to B$, factoring through the zero object.
\end{definition}

The zero morphism is well-defined because the maps $A \to 0$ and$0 \to B$ are unique (by the terminal and initial properties of$0$). In the abelian group structure on$\mathrm{Hom}(A, B)$, the zero morphism$0_{AB}$ is the additive identity element.

\begin{proposition}
In an additive category, the following hold:
1. $f \circ 0 = 0$ for any composable$f$.
2. $0 \circ g = 0$ for any composable$g$.
3. $f + (-f) = 0$ where$-f$ is the additive inverse in$\mathrm{Hom}(A, B)$.
\end{proposition}

*Proof.* For (1): $f \circ 0_{CA} = f \circ (A \to 0 \to A)$... actually let us be precise. If$0_{AB}: A \to B$ denotes the zero morphism and$g: B \to C$, then$g \circ 0_{AB}: A \to C$ factors as$A \to 0 \to B \xrightarrow{g} C$. But the composite$0 \to B \xrightarrow{g} C$ factors through the zero object as$0 \to C$ (the unique map), so$g \circ 0_{AB} = 0_{AC}$. Similarly,$0_{AB} \circ f = 0$ for any$f: D \to A$.$\square$

**Example 2.** In $\mathbf{Ab}$, the zero morphism$0_{AB}: A \to B$ sends every element of$A$ to$0 \in B$. The bilinearity$h \circ (f + g) = h \circ f + h \circ g$ means that morphisms distribute over addition as expected.

**Example 3.** Kernel and cokernel via zero morphisms. The *kernel* of $f: A \to B$ in an additive category is the equalizer of$f$ and$0_{AB}$: the universal object$K$ with morphism$k: K \to A$ such that$f \circ k = 0$ and any$g: X \to A$ with$f \circ g = 0$ factors uniquely through$K$. The *cokernel* is the coequalizer of$f$ and$0$: the universal$Q$ with$q: B \to Q$ such that$q \circ f = 0$.

In $R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$:$\ker(f) = \{m \in M : f(m) = 0\}$ is the usual kernel submodule, and$\mathrm{coker}(f) = N / \mathrm{im}(f)$ is the usual cokernel. These categorical kernels and cokernels coincide with the classical algebraic ones.

**Example 4.** Zero morphism in $\mathrm{Ch}(R)$. A chain map$f: C_\bullet \to D_\bullet$ is a zero morphism (as a morphism in$\mathrm{Ch}(R)$) if and only if each component$f_n: C_n \to D_n$ is the zero$R$-module map. The zero complex$0_\bullet$ receives and sends unique chain maps from/to every complex, confirming it is a zero object.

**Example 5.** The failure in $\mathbf{Grp}$. In$\mathbf{Grp}$, the trivial group$\{e\}$ is both initial and terminal — so$\mathbf{Grp}$ has a zero object! However,$\mathbf{Grp}$ is not additive because the hom-sets$\mathrm{Hom}(G, H)$ do not carry a natural abelian group structure (for non-abelian$G$). The zero morphism$0_{GH}: G \to H$ (the trivial homomorphism sending everything to$e_H$) exists, but one cannot "add" two group homomorphisms in a natural way.

\begin{remark}
Zero morphisms make it possible to speak of kernels and cokernels in purely categorical terms, without reference to elements. This is the key step enabling the definition of exact sequences in abstract additive (and abelian) categories: an exact sequence $A \xrightarrow{f} B \xrightarrow{g} C$ requires$g \circ f = 0$ (which uses the zero morphism) and$\ker(g) = \mathrm{im}(f)$. Without zero morphisms, neither the condition$g \circ f = 0$ nor the definition of$\ker$ and$\mathrm{im}$ as categorical constructions would make sense.
\end{remark}
