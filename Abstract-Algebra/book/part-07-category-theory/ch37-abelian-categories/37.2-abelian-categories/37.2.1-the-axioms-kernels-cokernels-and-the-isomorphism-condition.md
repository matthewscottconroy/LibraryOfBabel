# 37.2.1 The Axioms: Kernels, Cokernels, and the Isomorphism Condition

An additive category provides the scaffolding — hom-groups, biproducts, zero morphisms — but it does not yet guarantee that the first isomorphism theorem holds, that every morphism decomposes into an epimorphism followed by a monomorphism, or that "short exact sequences" make sense. These properties require additional axioms, and the resulting structure is an *abelian category*.

\begin{definition}
An *abelian category* is an additive category $\mathcal{A}$ satisfying:
1. Every morphism $f: A \to B$ has a kernel: an object$\ker(f)$ with monomorphism$k: \ker(f) \to A$ such that$f \circ k = 0$ and with the universal property that any$g: X \to A$ with$f \circ g = 0$ factors uniquely through$k$.
2. Every morphism $f: A \to B$ has a cokernel: an object$\mathrm{coker}(f)$ with epimorphism$c: B \to \mathrm{coker}(f)$ such that$c \circ f = 0$ and with the universal property that any$h: B \to Y$ with$h \circ f = 0$ factors uniquely through$c$.
3. Every monomorphism is the kernel of its own cokernel.
4. Every epimorphism is the cokernel of its own kernel.
\end{definition}

Conditions 3 and 4, together called the *isomorphism condition* or the *AB condition*, are what force the first isomorphism theorem to hold. They say that monomorphisms and epimorphisms in the category are precisely the "injective" and "surjective" morphisms in the categorical sense, with no gap between the algebraic and the categorical notions.

**Example 1.** In $R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$: kernels are submodules (the usual kernel$\ker f = \{m : f(m) = 0\}$), cokernels are quotient modules ($\mathrm{coker} f = N/\mathrm{im}(f)$). Monomorphisms in$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ are injective maps (this can be verified: a monic map is injective as an$R$-linear map), so every monic is the kernel of its cokernel: if$f: M \hookrightarrow N$ is injective,$\mathrm{coker}(f) = N/f(M)$, and$\ker(\text{quot}) = f(M) \cong M$. Similarly, epimorphisms are surjective, and every epi is the cokernel of its kernel. All four axioms are verified.

**Example 2.** The category $\mathbf{Ab}$ of abelian groups is abelian. This is the special case$R = \mathbb{Z}$ of Example 1.

**Example 3.** The non-example: Hausdorff topological abelian groups. Let $\mathcal{A}$ be the category of Hausdorff topological abelian groups with continuous homomorphisms. Kernels exist (usual kernels with the subspace topology) and cokernels exist (quotient by the closure of the image, with the quotient topology). However, the isomorphism condition can fail. Consider the inclusion$\iota: \mathbb{Q}_{\mathrm{disc}} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}$ (rationals with discrete topology into reals with standard topology). This$\iota$ is a monomorphism (it is injective), and its cokernel in$\mathcal{A}$ is$\mathbb{R}/\overline{\mathbb{Q}} = \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{R} = 0$ (since$\mathbb{Q}$ is dense in$\mathbb{R}$). But$\iota \neq \ker(0)$ because$\ker(0) = \mathbb{R}$ (with its standard topology), not$\mathbb{Q}$ with the discrete topology. So Condition 3 fails:$\iota$ is a monomorphism but not the kernel of its cokernel.

\begin{proposition}
In an abelian category, a morphism is both a monomorphism and an epimorphism if and only if it is an isomorphism.
\end{proposition}

*Proof.* Suppose $f: A \to B$ is both monic and epic. Since$f$ is epic, it is the cokernel of its kernel$k: \ker(f) \to A$ (by Condition 4). Since$f$ is also monic,$\ker(f) = 0$ (the zero object). So$f$ is the cokernel of$0 \to A$, which is the identity on$A$ (up to isomorphism). Hence$f$ is an isomorphism.$\square$

This proposition fails in many non-abelian additive categories. For example, the inclusion $\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Q}$ is a monomorphism and an epimorphism in the category of torsion-free abelian groups, but not an isomorphism.

**Example 4.** The category $\mathrm{Sh}(X, \mathbf{Ab})$ of sheaves of abelian groups. Kernels are computed stalk-wise (the kernel sheaf). Cokernels require sheafification of the naive presheaf cokernel. The isomorphism conditions hold, making$\mathrm{Sh}(X, \mathbf{Ab})$ an abelian category. This is the natural setting for sheaf cohomology.

**Example 5.** The category of finitely generated $k[x]$-modules (for a field$k$). This is a full subcategory of$k[x]\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$, which is abelian. Kernels and cokernels of maps between finitely generated modules are finitely generated (since$k[x]$ is Noetherian), so the category is closed under the operations and inherits the abelian structure.

\begin{remark}
The axioms of an abelian category distill exactly what is needed for homological algebra to function: the ability to define exact sequences, to decompose morphisms canonically (via the image-coimage factorization of Section 37.2.2), and to perform diagram-chasing arguments (via the Freyd–Mitchell embedding of Section 37.2.4) with full confidence. The four axioms listed above are concise, and their logical consequences — the snake lemma, the five lemma, the horseshoe lemma, and the entire machinery of derived functors — are all deduced from these axioms alone.
\end{remark}
