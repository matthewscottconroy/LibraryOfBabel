# 37.2.3 Examples: $\mathbf{Ab}$,$R$-$\mathbf{Mod}$, Sheaves of Abelian Groups

The definition of an abelian category would be of limited value if there were few examples. In practice, abelian categories are everywhere in algebra and geometry, and three fundamental examples — abelian groups, modules over a ring, and sheaves of abelian groups — collectively cover nearly all the ground in which homological algebra is practiced.

**Example 1. $\mathbf{Ab}$, the category of abelian groups.** This is the simplest and most fundamental example of an abelian category. Kernels are the usual subgroup kernels$\ker(f) = \{a \in A : f(a) = 0\}$, and cokernels are quotients$\mathrm{coker}(f) = B/\mathrm{im}(f)$. Monomorphisms in$\mathbf{Ab}$ are exactly the injective group homomorphisms (if$f$ is monic and$f(a) = 0$, take the map$\mathbb{Z} \to A$ sending$1 \mapsto a$; monicity forces$a = 0$). Epimorphisms are exactly the surjective homomorphisms (one verifies that epimorphisms in$\mathbf{Ab}$ coincide with surjections — this uses the fact that$\mathbf{Ab}$ is abelian). Every monic is the kernel of its cokernel: if$f: A \hookrightarrow B$ is injective,$\mathrm{coker}(f) = B/f(A)$, and$\ker(B \to B/f(A)) = f(A) \cong A$. Similarly, every epi is the cokernel of its kernel. In fact,$\mathbf{Ab}$ is the category from which the name "abelian category" is derived.

**Example 2. $R$-$\mathbf{Mod}$, left modules over a ring$R$.** This is the primary example in homological algebra. For any ring$R$ (not necessarily commutative), the category$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ is abelian:
- Kernels: $\ker(f) = \{m \in M : f(m) = 0\}$ with the inclusion map, a submodule.
- Cokernels: $\mathrm{coker}(f) = N/\mathrm{im}(f) = N/f(M)$ with the quotient map.
- Monomorphisms = injections, epimorphisms = surjections (in $R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$).
- Axiom 3: an injection $f: M \hookrightarrow N$ is the kernel of the quotient$N \to N/f(M)$.$\checkmark$
- Axiom 4: a surjection $f: M \twoheadrightarrow N$ is the cokernel of$\ker(f) \hookrightarrow M$.$\checkmark$

Special cases include: $\mathbb{Z}\text{-}\mathbf{Mod} = \mathbf{Ab}$,$k\text{-}\mathbf{Mod} = \mathbf{Vect}_k$ (vector spaces over a field$k$), and$k[G]\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ (representations of a group$G$). All are abelian categories.

**Example 3. $\mathbf{Vect}_k$, vector spaces over a field$k$.** As a special case of$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ with$R = k$, this is abelian. Additionally,$\mathbf{Vect}_k$ has the special property that every short exact sequence splits:$0 \to V' \to V \to V'' \to 0$ always gives$V \cong V' \oplus V''$, because every vector space has a basis and every subspace has a complement. This makes$\mathrm{Ext}^1_k(V'', V') = 0$ for all$V', V''$, and is the reason that the cohomology of a field is trivial.

**Example 4. $\mathrm{Sh}(X, \mathbf{Ab})$, sheaves of abelian groups on a topological space$X$.** The verification that this is abelian requires more care, but the result holds:
- Kernels: the kernel of a sheaf map $f: \mathcal{F} \to \mathcal{G}$ is the sheaf$\ker(f)$ with sections$\ker(f)(U) = \ker(f_U: \mathcal{F}(U) \to \mathcal{G}(U))$. Since the kernel of a presheaf map of sheaves is automatically a sheaf, no sheafification is needed.
- Cokernels: the cokernel requires sheafification. The naive presheaf cokernel $U \mapsto \mathcal{G}(U)/f(\mathcal{F}(U))$ is not a sheaf in general; one must sheafify to obtain the categorical cokernel$\mathrm{coker}(f)$.
- The axioms then hold, making $\mathrm{Sh}(X, \mathbf{Ab})$ abelian.

Sheaf cohomology $H^n(X, \mathcal{F})$ is defined using the derived functors of the global sections functor$\Gamma(X, -)$, and its existence and properties (long exact sequences, functoriality) depend entirely on$\mathrm{Sh}(X, \mathbf{Ab})$ being an abelian category.

**Example 5. Non-examples and their failures.**

- $\mathbf{Top}$: there is no natural notion of cokernel for continuous maps (the "cokernel" of a map$f: X \to Y$ would have to be a quotient of$Y$ in the topological sense, but quotients in$\mathbf{Top}$ do not satisfy the abelian category axioms).$\mathbf{Top}$ is not even additive.

- $\mathbf{Grp}$: the trivial group is a zero object, and kernels exist (usual subgroup kernels). Cokernels also exist (quotient by the normal closure). However, monomorphisms in$\mathbf{Grp}$ are injective group homomorphisms, but epimorphisms are NOT the same as surjective homomorphisms: the inclusion$\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Q}$ is an epimorphism in$\mathbf{Grp}$ (since if$f, g: \mathbb{Q} \to H$ agree on$\mathbb{Z}$, they agree on$\mathbb{Q}$) but is not surjective. This violates the isomorphism condition.

- Category of finitely generated abelian groups: this is an abelian category (as a full subcategory of $\mathbf{Ab}$ that is closed under kernels and cokernels, since subgroups and quotients of finitely generated abelian groups are finitely generated). It has enough projectives (free abelian groups of finite rank) but not enough injectives (finitely generated abelian groups are never divisible, except$\{0\}$).

The contrast between examples and non-examples clarifies why the commutativity of the group law (or more precisely, the enrichment over abelian groups) is not merely a convenience but a structural necessity for the category to be abelian.
