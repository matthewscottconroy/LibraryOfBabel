# 37.2.4 The Freyd–Mitchell Embedding Theorem

Despite the generality of the abelian category axioms, there is a remarkable theorem that brings every small abelian category back within reach of the most elementary proof techniques.

\begin{theorem}[Freyd–Mitchell Embedding Theorem]
Every small abelian category $\mathcal{A}$ admits a full, faithful, and exact functor$\Phi: \mathcal{A} \hookrightarrow R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ into the category of left modules over some ring$R$. The functor$\Phi$ is exact: it preserves and reflects short exact sequences.
\end{theorem}

The practical import of the theorem is this: to prove a diagram-chasing result in an arbitrary abelian category, it suffices to prove it for modules over a ring — one then invokes the embedding to transfer the result. Statements such as the snake lemma, the five lemma, and the horseshoe lemma involve only finite diagrams and finitely many objects; any such diagram, lying in a small full subcategory of $\mathcal{A}$, can be embedded into$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ for some$R$. There, one can chase elements — "let$x \in A$ with$f(x) = 0$..." — the kind of argument that is natural for modules but appears to be unavailable in an abstract abelian category. The embedding theorem grants a license to use such arguments freely.

**Example 1.** The five lemma. Given a commutative diagram with exact rows and four outer vertical isomorphisms in an abelian category $\mathcal{A}$, one wants to conclude that the middle vertical map is an isomorphism. The proof by diagram chasing involves choosing elements and tracking them around the diagram — a style of argument that presupposes working with actual elements. By Freyd–Mitchell, the finite diagram lives in a small full abelian subcategory of$\mathcal{A}$, which embeds into$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$. The five lemma then follows from the module version, and since$\Phi$ reflects isomorphisms (as a faithful exact functor), the result transfers back to$\mathcal{A}$.

**Example 2.** The snake lemma in sheaves. In $\mathrm{Sh}(X, \mathbf{Ab})$, one wants to show that a morphism of short exact sequences of sheaves gives a long exact sequence of kernels and cokernels with a connecting morphism. The argument requires chasing local sections, which is most naturally done by working in$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ (via the embedding) rather than with the sheaf axioms directly.

**Example 3.** Cohomological calculations. In an abstract Grothendieck abelian category (an abelian category with a generator, satisfying the AB5 axiom), one often wants to compute derived functors. The Freyd–Mitchell theorem allows one to verify that the categorical definitions match the standard calculations done in $R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$, providing a consistency check between the abstract and concrete approaches.

**Example 4.** Functoriality of exact sequences. A natural transformation between two short exact sequences (a map of short exact sequences) in an abelian category $\mathcal{A}$ induces a map on the associated long exact sequences (if they arise from a cohomological functor). The proof that this induced map is well-defined and natural uses diagram chasing, which is justified by embedding into$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$.

The surprise in the theorem is the generality of "some ring $R$": the ring may be non-commutative, possibly large, and bears no obvious relationship to the original category$\mathcal{A}$. The proof proceeds via the Yoneda embedding: for a small abelian category$\mathcal{A}$, one constructs the functor category$[\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{op}}, \mathbf{Ab}]$ (which is a Grothendieck abelian category), and then finds a suitable exact left adjoint from this category to$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ for an appropriate ring$R$ (the endomorphism ring of a projective generator). The full embedding of$\mathcal{A}$ into$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ is the composite.

\begin{remark}
The theorem is not constructive in a useful sense. Knowing that an embedding into $R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ exists for some abstract$R$ does not help compute specific homological invariants. One cannot use it to say "$\mathrm{Ext}^1_{\mathcal{A}}(A, B) = \mathrm{Ext}^1_R(\Phi A, \Phi B)$" — the embedding need not preserve Ext groups unless$\mathcal{A}$ is already a subcategory of$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ in a compatible way. The theorem is a powerful theoretical tool that justifies element-chasing arguments, but it does not replace direct calculation in specific categories.
\end{remark}

**Example 5.** The smallness hypothesis is essential. The theorem requires $\mathcal{A}$ to be *small* (its objects form a set). For large abelian categories like$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ itself, the theorem does not apply directly. Instead, one works with small full subcategories: any finite diagram in$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ lives in a small full abelian subcategory, and one may apply Freyd–Mitchell to that subcategory. In practice, this means: to prove a diagram lemma in any abelian category, draw the finite diagram, apply Freyd–Mitchell to the small subcategory generated by the diagram and all its kernels and cokernels, embed into$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$, chase elements, and transfer the result back.
