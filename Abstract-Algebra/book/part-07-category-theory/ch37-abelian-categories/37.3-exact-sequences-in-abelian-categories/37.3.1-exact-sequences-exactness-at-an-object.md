# 37.3.1 Exact Sequences; Exactness at an Object

With kernels and images defined in an abelian category, one can ask a precise question about any pair of composable morphisms: does the image of the first equal the kernel of the second?

\begin{definition}
A sequence of morphisms $A \xrightarrow{f} B \xrightarrow{g} C$ in an abelian category is *exact at$B$* if$\mathrm{im}(f) = \ker(g)$ — that is, if the image of$f$ (as a subobject of$B$) coincides with the kernel of$g$. A longer sequence$A_0 \xrightarrow{f_0} A_1 \xrightarrow{f_1} A_2 \xrightarrow{f_2} \cdots$ is *exact* if it is exact at every interior term$A_i$ (i.e., exact at every$A_i$ that has both an incoming and an outgoing morphism).
\end{definition}

The condition $\mathrm{im}(f) = \ker(g)$ is equivalent to two separate inclusions: (1)$\mathrm{im}(f) \subseteq \ker(g)$, which is equivalent to$g \circ f = 0$ (the composite is zero), and (2)$\ker(g) \subseteq \mathrm{im}(f)$, which says every element annihilated by$g$ comes from$A$. The first condition is easy to check and purely formal; the second is the substantive content.

**Example 1.** Exactness in $\mathbf{Ab}$. The sequence$\mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{2} \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\pi} \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{0} 0$ is exact:
- Exactness at the first $\mathbb{Z}$:$\mathrm{im}(0 \to \mathbb{Z}) = 0 = \ker(2)$ (since$2$ is injective). Wait, let us start from the correct terms. Consider$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{2} \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\pi} \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to 0$:
  - Exact at $\mathbb{Z}$ (first):$\mathrm{im}(0 \to \mathbb{Z}) = \{0\} = \ker(2) = \{0\}$.$\checkmark$ (multiplication by 2 is injective.)
  - Exact at $\mathbb{Z}$ (second):$\mathrm{im}(2\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Z}) = 2\mathbb{Z} = \ker(\pi)$ (since$\pi(n) = 0$ iff$n$ is even).$\checkmark$
  - Exact at $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$:$\mathrm{im}(\pi) = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} = \ker(0)$.$\checkmark$ ($\pi$ is surjective.)

**Example 2.** Exactness at a term expresses properties of individual maps. For a single morphism $f: A \to B$:
- $0 \to A \xrightarrow{f} B$ is exact at$A$ iff$\ker(f) = 0$ iff$f$ is a monomorphism.
- $A \xrightarrow{f} B \to 0$ is exact at$B$ iff$\mathrm{im}(f) = B$ iff$f$ is an epimorphism.
- Thus, $0 \to A \xrightarrow{f} B \to 0$ is exact iff$f$ is both monic and epic iff$f$ is an isomorphism (in an abelian category).

**Example 3.** Exactness in $R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$. The sequence$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\cdot 6} \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\pi} \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \to 0$ is exact:
- Exact at $\mathbb{Z}$: multiplication by 6 is injective, so$\ker(\cdot 6) = 0 = \mathrm{im}(0 \to \mathbb{Z})$.$\checkmark$
- Exact at $\mathbb{Z}$:$\ker(\pi) = 6\mathbb{Z} = \mathrm{im}(\cdot 6)$.$\checkmark$
- Exact at $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$:$\pi$ is surjective.$\checkmark$

**Example 4.** A non-exact sequence. In $\mathbf{Ab}$, consider$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{f} \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{g} \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ where$f(n) = 2n$ (multiplication by 2) and$g(n) = n \bmod 2$. Check:$\mathrm{im}(f) = \{0, 2\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ and$\ker(g) = \{0, 2\} = \mathrm{im}(f)$. This IS exact at the middle$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$. But the sequence$0 \to \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{f} \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ is NOT exact at the domain$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ since$f$ is not injective ($f(2) = 0$).

**Example 5.** Long exact sequences. A longer sequence $A_0 \to A_1 \to A_2 \to \cdots \to A_n$ is exact everywhere iff it is exact at each$A_i$ for$1 \leq i \leq n-1$. The long exact sequence in homology:$\cdots \to H_n(X) \to H_n(X, A) \to H_{n-1}(A) \to H_{n-1}(X) \to \cdots$ (arising from the short exact sequence of chain complexes$0 \to C_\bullet(A) \to C_\bullet(X) \to C_\bullet(X, A) \to 0$) is exact everywhere. This is the prototype for all long exact sequences in homological algebra.

\begin{remark}
Exact sequences encode and measure the "failure" of morphisms to be bijective. The condition $\mathrm{im}(f) = \ker(g)$ at each term records a cascade of partial information: where one map fails to be surjective, the next map compensates by being less injective. This interplay is the central language of homological algebra, and the ability to form and manipulate exact sequences in an abstract abelian category — not just in$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ — is one of the main payoffs of the abstract framework.
\end{remark}

The notion of exactness is entirely intrinsic to the abelian category structure and does not require any reference to elements. In $R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$, the definition coincides with the element-level definition because monomorphisms and epimorphisms coincide with injections and surjections; in a general abelian category, the definition is the same but the proof that it has the expected properties uses the abelian axioms rather than elements directly. This is the value of the abstract framework: results proved from the axioms — the five lemma, the snake lemma, the horseshoe lemma — hold in every abelian category simultaneously, including sheaf categories, functor categories, and the abelian categories arising in representation theory.

A sequence $A_0 \to A_1 \to \cdots \to A_n$ is often called a *complex* if each consecutive composite is zero (i.e.,$f_{i+1} \circ f_i = 0$ for all$i$), and it is exact iff it is a complex and the homology at each interior term vanishes. The homology of a complex,$H_i = \ker(A_i \to A_{i+1})/\mathrm{im}(A_{i-1} \to A_i)$, measures the failure of exactness and is the primary object of study in homological algebra.
