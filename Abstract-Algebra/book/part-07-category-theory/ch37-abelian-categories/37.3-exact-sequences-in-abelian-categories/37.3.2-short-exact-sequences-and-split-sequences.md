# 37.3.2 Short Exact Sequences and Split Sequences

The most fundamental exact sequences are the short ones, which encode how one object is built from two others.

\begin{definition}
A *short exact sequence* in an abelian category is an exact sequence of the form $0 \to A \xrightarrow{f} B \xrightarrow{g} C \to 0$, i.e.,$f$ is a monomorphism (exactness at$A$),$\mathrm{im}(f) = \ker(g)$ (exactness at$B$), and$g$ is an epimorphism (exactness at$C$).
\end{definition}

The three conditions assert: $A$ embeds into$B$ as a subobject via$f$, and$C$ is the "quotient"$B/A$ in the categorical sense. In$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$, this means$f(A) \cong A$ is a submodule of$B$, and$g$ identifies$C$ with$B/f(A)$.

**Example 1.** The archetypal short exact sequence: for any subgroup $H \leq G$ in$\mathbf{Ab}$, the sequence$0 \to H \hookrightarrow G \to G/H \to 0$ is short exact. Here$f$ is the inclusion,$g$ is the quotient map, and exactness at$G$ says$\ker(g) = H = \mathrm{im}(f)$.

**Example 2.** In $R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$:$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\cdot n} \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \to 0$ for any$n \geq 1$. For$n = 4$:$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\cdot 4} \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \to 0$. This captures the fact that$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ is the quotient of$\mathbb{Z}$ by the subgroup$4\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 3.** Non-isomorphic extensions: $0 \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to 0$ (where the first map sends$1 \mapsto 2$ and the second reduces mod 2) is a non-split short exact sequence. The middle term$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ is not isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, showing that the extension is non-trivial.

\begin{definition}
A short exact sequence $0 \to A \xrightarrow{f} B \xrightarrow{g} C \to 0$ *splits* if any of the following equivalent conditions hold:
1. There exists a *section* $s: C \to B$ with$g \circ s = \mathrm{id}_C$.
2. There exists a *retraction* $r: B \to A$ with$r \circ f = \mathrm{id}_A$.
3. $B \cong A \oplus C$ via an isomorphism compatible with$f$ and$g$.
\end{definition}

**Example 4.** The sequence $0 \to A \to A \oplus C \to C \to 0$ (with$f = \iota_A$ and$g = \pi_C$) always splits, with section$s = \iota_C: C \to A \oplus C$ and retraction$r = \pi_A: A \oplus C \to A$. This is the "trivial" extension.

**Example 5.** The sequence $0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\cdot 2} \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to 0$ does not split. A section$s: \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ would require an element$s(1) \in \mathbb{Z}$ with$2 \cdot s(1) = 0$ in$\mathbb{Z}$, forcing$s(1) = 0$, but then$g(s(1)) = g(0) = 0 \neq 1 = \mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}}(1)$. So no section exists and the sequence is non-split.

\begin{proposition}
A short exact sequence $0 \to A \to B \to C \to 0$ in$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ splits if$C$ is projective or$A$ is injective.
\end{proposition}

*Proof sketch.* If $C$ is projective, the lifting property of$C$ against the epimorphism$B \to C$ provides a section. If$A$ is injective, the extension property of$A$ against the monomorphism$A \to B$ provides a retraction.$\square$

The non-split extensions of $C$ by$A$ (up to the appropriate equivalence) are classified by the group$\mathrm{Ext}^1_R(C, A)$: short exact sequences$0 \to A \to B \to C \to 0$ up to isomorphism compatible with$A$ and$C$ form a set in bijection with$\mathrm{Ext}^1_R(C, A)$. The trivial extension (direct sum) corresponds to$0 \in \mathrm{Ext}^1_R(C, A)$, and the sequence splits iff the corresponding extension class is zero.

\begin{remark}
Short exact sequences can be thought of as measuring how $B$ is "built from"$A$ and$C$. If$B$ were simply$A \oplus C$, the sequence would split and$B$ would be the most straightforward combination. Non-split sequences capture the non-trivial ways of fitting$A$ inside$B$ with quotient$C$. The entire theory of extension groups$\mathrm{Ext}^1_R(C, A)$, and more generally of derived functors$\mathrm{Ext}^n_R(C, A)$, is built around this idea: these groups measure the "thickness" of the layers of modules that can be stacked between$A$ and$C$.
\end{remark}

**Example 6.** Non-split extensions in nature. In $\mathbf{Ab}$, the short exact sequence$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\cdot 2} \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to 0$ is non-split (no section$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ exists, since$\mathbb{Z}$ is torsion-free). The extension class lies in$\mathrm{Ext}^1_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, and this non-trivial class corresponds to the non-trivial extension. In contrast,$0 \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z} \to 0$ is split, since$\gcd(2, 3) = 1$ means there are no non-trivial extensions between$\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ and$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ (as$\mathrm{Ext}^1(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}) = 0$).

The Baer sum gives $\mathrm{Ext}^1_R(C, A)$ the structure of an abelian group: two extension classes can be added via the Baer sum construction (pulling back and pushing forward), and the zero element is the split extension. This group structure on the set of extension classes is one of the starting points of homological algebra, and it motivates the definition of the higher Ext groups$\mathrm{Ext}^n_R(C, A)$ for$n \geq 2$, which classify longer iterated extensions.
