# 37.3 Exact Sequences in Abelian Categories

Exact sequences are the central objects of homological algebra, and the abelian category framework is precisely designed to make them well-defined and well-behaved. A sequence of morphisms $\cdots \to A_{n+1} \xrightarrow{f_{n+1}} A_n \xrightarrow{f_n} A_{n-1} \to \cdots$ in an abelian category is *exact at $A_n$* if $\text{im}(f_{n+1}) = \ker(f_n)$ — that is, the image of the incoming morphism equals the kernel of the outgoing morphism, where both image and kernel are understood in the categorical sense as subobjects of $A_n$. The sequence is *exact* if it is exact at every object. A *short exact sequence* $0 \to A \xrightarrow{f} B \xrightarrow{g} C \to 0$ is exact and additionally expresses $A$ as a "sub-object" of $B$ and $C$ as the "quotient" $B/A$.

Functors between abelian categories are classified by their behavior on exact sequences. A functor is *left exact* if it preserves kernels — equivalently, if it sends a short exact sequence $0 \to A \to B \to C \to 0$ to an exact sequence $0 \to F(A) \to F(B) \to F(C)$ (without the final $0$). A functor is *right exact* if it preserves cokernels, sending the short exact sequence to $F(A) \to F(B) \to F(C) \to 0$ (without the initial $0$). A functor is *exact* if it is both left and right exact, preserving short exact sequences entirely. In $R\text{-Mod}$, the functor $\text{Hom}_R(M, -)$ is left exact but generally not right exact (its failure to be right exact defines $\text{Ext}^1_R(M, -)$), while $M \otimes_R -$ is right exact but generally not left exact (its failure defines $\text{Tor}_1^R(M, -)$).

The *snake lemma* is the most important diagram lemma in homological algebra, and its proof works in any abelian category. Given a commutative diagram with exact rows $0 \to A' \xrightarrow{f} A \xrightarrow{g} A'' \to 0$ and vertical maps $\alpha: A' \to B'$, $\beta: A \to B$, $\gamma: A'' \to B''$ fitting into exact rows $0 \to B' \xrightarrow{f'} B \xrightarrow{g'} B'' \to 0$, there is a natural exact sequence $\ker \alpha \to \ker \beta \to \ker \gamma \xrightarrow{\delta} \text{coker}\, \alpha \to \text{coker}\, \beta \to \text{coker}\, \gamma$. The connecting homomorphism $\delta$ is the "snake": to compute $\delta(a'')$, one lifts $a''$ to an element of $A$, maps it to $B$ via $\beta$, and then maps backward. The snake lemma generates long exact sequences in homology and is the computational engine behind most of the classical homological algebra in the chapters to follow.

The *five lemma* and *nine lemma* are consequences of the snake lemma and are equally indispensable. The five lemma states conditions under which the middle map in a morphism of short exact sequences is an isomorphism; the nine lemma (or $3 \times 3$ lemma) addresses a $3 \times 3$ commutative diagram. Both hold in any abelian category, and both are proved by element-chasing arguments that are justified by the Freyd–Mitchell embedding theorem when working abstractly. The long exact sequence in homology — associating to a short exact sequence of chain complexes a long exact sequence of homology groups — is a derived consequence of the snake lemma applied repeatedly, and it is the foundational computation of homological algebra.
