# Chapter 37 — Abelian Categories

The machinery of homological algebra — exact sequences, long exact sequences in homology, derived functors, spectral sequences — was developed in the 1940s and 1950s primarily in the context of modules over rings and sheaves over topological spaces. The proofs in these two settings were structurally identical, the arguments proceeding by element-chasing in both cases, yet the two settings seemed superficially unrelated. Grothendieck's 1957 Tôhoku paper, "Sur quelques points d'algèbre homologique," asked the decisive question: what are the minimal axioms on a category that guarantee all of homological algebra can be carried out within it? The answer was the definition of an *abelian category*, and the answer transformed both algebra and geometry.

An abelian category is a category satisfying four cumulative layers of axioms. It must first be *preadditive* (enriched over abelian groups: each hom-set is an abelian group and composition is bilinear), then *additive* (with finite biproducts — objects that are simultaneously products and coproducts), then *preabelian* (with kernels and cokernels for every morphism), and finally *abelian* (the canonical morphism from the coimage to the image of every morphism is an isomorphism). This last axiom — that coimage equals image — is the categorical form of the first isomorphism theorem, and it is precisely what is needed for the standard diagram lemmas to hold. The prototype is $R\text{-Mod}$: the category of left modules over a ring $R$, where all these properties hold and can be verified concretely. The category of abelian groups $\mathbf{Ab} = \mathbb{Z}\text{-Mod}$ is the simplest non-trivial case.

The importance of the axioms is that they are satisfied not only by module categories but by any category of sheaves of abelian groups on a topological space (or, more generally, on a site), by functor categories $[\mathcal{C}, \mathbf{Ab}]$ for any small category $\mathcal{C}$, and by many other categories arising in representation theory, algebraic geometry, and mathematical physics. The abelian category framework thus provides a single language for homological algebra across all these settings. A proof of the snake lemma in an abelian category is a proof that works simultaneously for modules, for sheaves, for representations of a quiver, and for any other abelian category — with no modification required.

A central technical tool is the *Freyd–Mitchell embedding theorem*, proved by Peter Freyd and Barry Mitchell in the early 1960s: every small abelian category embeds fully and exactly into the module category $R\text{-Mod}$ for some ring $R$, by a functor that preserves and reflects exactness. "Fully and exactly" means that the embedding preserves and reflects all exact sequences. The theorem justifies *element-chasing* arguments in abstract abelian categories: even though objects in an abelian category may have no elements in any literal sense, any diagram argument that could be made by element-chasing in $R\text{-Mod}$ is valid in any abelian category. This is not merely a convenience — it is what makes abstract homological algebra computationally tractable.

The exact sequences, short exact sequences, the five lemma, the nine lemma, and above all the snake lemma — all holding in any abelian category — are the tools that make homological algebra a coherent subject rather than a collection of ad hoc computations. The connecting homomorphism in the snake lemma, the long exact sequence in homology, and the derived functor construction all have their natural home in an abelian category. This chapter both completes the categorical framework of Part VII and opens the door to the homological algebra developed in the chapters ahead.
