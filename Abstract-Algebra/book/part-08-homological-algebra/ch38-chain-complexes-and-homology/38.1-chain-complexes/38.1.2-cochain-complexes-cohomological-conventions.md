# 38.1.2 Cochain Complexes; Cohomological Conventions

While chain complexes have their differentials decreasing the degree, many natural constructions in algebra and geometry produce complexes where the maps go in the opposite direction. This directionality shift is not merely cosmetic: it reflects the distinction between "mapping out of" a space (giving cochain complexes) versus "mapping into" a space (giving chain complexes), and the two orientations arise in fundamentally different geometric and algebraic contexts.

\begin{definition}
A \emph{cochain complex} $(C^\bullet, d)$ consists of$R$-modules$C^n$ for$n \in \mathbb{Z}$ and$R$-linear maps$d^n: C^n \to C^{n+1}$ satisfying$d^{n+1} \circ d^n = 0$ for all$n$. The maps$d^n$ are called \emph{coboundary maps} or \emph{differentials}, and the \emph{cohomology} in degree$n$ is$H^n(C^\bullet) = \ker(d^n) / \mathrm{im}(d^{n-1})$.
\end{definition}

The key difference is notational and directional: superscripts instead of subscripts, upward differentials instead of downward ones. The condition $d^2 = 0$ is unchanged. Elements of$\ker(d^n)$ are \emph{cocycles} in degree$n$; elements of$\mathrm{im}(d^{n-1})$ are \emph{coboundaries}; cohomology measures the cocycles that are not coboundaries. Everything from chain complex theory has an exact parallel in the cochain setting, with arrows reversed.

Cochain complexes arise naturally wherever one is mapping into a coefficient module rather than mapping out of a geometric space. In group cohomology, the cochain complex $C^n(G, A)$ consists of functions from$G^n$ to a coefficient module$A$, with coboundary$d^n$ enforcing a compatibility condition. The cohomology groups$H^n(G, A)$ classify extensions, crossed homomorphisms, and other group-theoretic data. In sheaf cohomology, the \v{C}ech complex of a covering computes the cohomology of a sheaf via alternating cochains assigned to intersections of open sets. In algebraic de Rham theory,$\Omega^n(M)$ in degree$n$ carries the exterior derivative, and the de Rham cohomology$H^n_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)$ records global topological features of the manifold.

The distinction between chain and cochain complexes is purely notational when considered abstractly: a cochain complex in degrees $\{0, 1, 2, \ldots\}$ is the same thing as a chain complex with the indexing reversed, via the substitution$C_{-n} = C^n$. In practice, one uses whichever convention matches the geometric direction of the application. Chain complexes (subscripts, decreasing) are standard for resolutions, singular homology, and derived functors applied from the left. Cochain complexes (superscripts, increasing) are standard for cohomology theories, sheaves, and right derived functors. This text follows both conventions according to context and will specify the active convention when clarity requires it.

A cochain complex in degrees $0, 1, 2, \ldots$ concentrated in non-negative degrees is called a \emph{cochain complex of non-negative type}. If such a complex has$C^n = 0$ for$n < 0$ and$C^0 \neq 0$, it is often written without the zero terms:$C^0 \xrightarrow{d^0} C^1 \xrightarrow{d^1} C^2 \to \cdots$. The augmentation$C^{-1} \to C^0$ is sometimes written separately when the complex is a resolution. These conventions become automatic with practice and should not cause confusion once the reader has internalized the bidirectionality of the formalism.

\begin{example}
The de Rham complex of $\mathbb{R}^2$ is the cochain complex
$$0 \to \Omega^0(\mathbb{R}^2) \xrightarrow{d} \Omega^1(\mathbb{R}^2) \xrightarrow{d} \Omega^2(\mathbb{R}^2) \to 0,$$
where $\Omega^0 = C^\infty(\mathbb{R}^2)$,$\Omega^1 = \{f\,dx + g\,dy\}$, and$\Omega^2 = \{h\,dx \wedge dy\}$. The first differential is$d(f) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}dx + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}dy$; the second is$d(f\,dx + g\,dy) = \left(\frac{\partial g}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right)dx \wedge dy$. Since$\mathbb{R}^2$ is contractible, the Poincar\'e lemma gives$H^0 = \mathbb{R}$ and$H^1 = H^2 = 0$: every closed form is exact.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
The group cochain complex $C^\bullet(G, M)$ for a group$G$ acting on an abelian group$M$ has$C^n(G, M) = \mathrm{Map}(G^n, M)$ with coboundary
$$(d^n f)(g_1, \ldots, g_{n+1}) = g_1 \cdot f(g_2, \ldots, g_{n+1}) + \sum_{i=1}^n (-1)^i f(g_1, \ldots, g_i g_{i+1}, \ldots, g_{n+1}) + (-1)^{n+1} f(g_1, \ldots, g_n).$$
One verifies $d^{n+1} \circ d^n = 0$ directly. For$n=1$, a cocycle$f: G \to M$ satisfies$f(gh) = f(g) + g \cdot f(h)$ — the \emph{crossed homomorphism} condition. For$n=2$, a cocycle$c: G \times G \to M$ satisfies the associativity condition that classifies group extensions.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
The \v{C}ech cochain complex for an open cover $\mathcal{U} = \{U_0, U_1, U_2\}$ of a space$X$ with a sheaf$\mathcal{F}$ has
$$C^0(\mathcal{U}, \mathcal{F}) = \prod_i \mathcal{F}(U_i), \quad C^1(\mathcal{U}, \mathcal{F}) = \prod_{i < j} \mathcal{F}(U_i \cap U_j), \quad C^2(\mathcal{U}, \mathcal{F}) = \mathcal{F}(U_0 \cap U_1 \cap U_2).$$
The coboundary $d^0: C^0 \to C^1$ sends$(s_i)$ to$(s_j|_{U_i \cap U_j} - s_i|_{U_i \cap U_j})$. The condition$H^0(\mathcal{U}, \mathcal{F}) = \ker(d^0)$ recovers global sections: a$0$-cocycle is a compatible family of local sections, hence a global section by the sheaf axiom.
\end{example}

The parallel between the examples reinforces the central point: the cochain complex is not a new construction but the same construction with the arrows reversed. The cohomology groups measure the same phenomenon — cycles that are not boundaries — but in a dual setting where the maps go from smaller to larger. Learning to move between chain and cochain conventions fluidly is one of the key skills in homological algebra, and the conversion $C_n \leftrightarrow C^{-n}$ makes this passage completely formal.
