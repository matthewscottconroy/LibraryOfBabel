# 38.1.3 Morphisms of Chain Complexes

Having defined the objects of our new algebraic setting, the next step is to determine what the correct maps between them should be. The guiding principle in any category is that morphisms should preserve all of the structure that defines the objects. For groups, homomorphisms preserve the group operation. For rings, ring homomorphisms preserve both addition and multiplication. For chain complexes, the structure beyond the underlying modules is entirely encoded in the differentials, so a map of chain complexes must be compatible with those differentials.

\begin{definition}
A \emph{chain map} $f: C_\bullet \to D_\bullet$ is a collection of$R$-linear maps$f_n: C_n \to D_n$, one for each$n \in \mathbb{Z}$, satisfying the commutativity condition
$$d_n^D \circ f_n = f_{n-1} \circ d_n^C$$
for all $n$. Equivalently, every square in the ladder diagram
$$\cdots \to C_{n+1} \xrightarrow{d^C} C_n \xrightarrow{d^C} C_{n-1} \to \cdots$$
$$\cdots \to D_{n+1} \xrightarrow{d^D} D_n \xrightarrow{d^D} D_{n-1} \to \cdots$$
(connected by the vertical maps $f_n$) must commute.
\end{definition}

The commutativity condition says that $f$ "respects the boundary relation": applying the boundary map and then$f$ gives the same result as applying$f$ and then the boundary map. This is the direct analogue of requiring group homomorphisms to commute with the group operation: the differential is the operation, and the chain map must respect it in every degree simultaneously.

The first and most important consequence is that chain maps carry cycles to cycles and boundaries to boundaries, and therefore induce well-defined maps on homology. If $z \in Z_n(C) = \ker(d_n^C)$, then$d_n^D(f_n(z)) = f_{n-1}(d_n^C(z)) = f_{n-1}(0) = 0$, so$f_n(z) \in Z_n(D)$. If$z = d_{n+1}^C(c)$ is a boundary, then$f_n(z) = f_n(d_{n+1}^C(c)) = d_{n+1}^D(f_{n+1}(c))$ is a boundary in$D_n$. The induced map on homology$f_*: H_n(C) \to H_n(D)$, defined by$f_*([z]) = [f_n(z)]$, is therefore well-defined.

The composition of two chain maps is again a chain map: if $f: C_\bullet \to D_\bullet$ and$g: D_\bullet \to E_\bullet$ are chain maps, then$(g \circ f)_n = g_n \circ f_n$ is easily seen to commute with all differentials. The identity chain map$\mathrm{id}: C_\bullet \to C_\bullet$ is defined by$(\mathrm{id})_n = \mathrm{id}_{C_n}$. This makes chain complexes and chain maps into a category$\mathrm{Ch}(R\text{-Mod})$, with all the categorical machinery immediately available.

It is worth emphasizing that a chain map does not require any condition on the individual $f_n$ beyond$R$-linearity and the commutativity with differentials. In particular,$f_n$ need not be injective or surjective in any degree. A chain map can carry a non-zero cycle to the zero class, map a non-zero complex to the zero complex, or do other things that would be forbidden for module isomorphisms. The only invariant that a chain map is forced to respect is the homology.

\begin{example}
Let $f: \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ be the reduction map$f(x) = x \bmod 2$. The module$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ has the free resolution$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 4} \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \to 0$ and$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ has$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 2} \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to 0$. A chain map lifting$f$ must provide a$\mathbb{Z}$-linear map$\tilde{f}: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ making the augmentation square commute. Since$f(x \bmod 4) = x \bmod 2$, we can take$\tilde{f}_0 = \mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{Z}}$ at degree$0$ and$\tilde{f}_1 = \mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{Z}}$ at degree$1$. Check:$\tilde{f}_0 \circ (\times 4) = \times 4$ and$(\times 2) \circ \tilde{f}_1 = \times 2$. These are not equal, so$\tilde{f}_0 = \mathrm{id}$ does not work. Instead take$\tilde{f}_1 = \times 2$: then$\tilde{f}_0 \circ (\times 4) = \times 4$ and$(\times 2) \circ (\times 2) = \times 4$. This works.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
The inclusion and projection in a short exact sequence $0 \to A_\bullet \xrightarrow{i} B_\bullet \xrightarrow{p} C_\bullet \to 0$ of chain complexes are chain maps. Exactness at each module in degree$n$ is a chain-complex condition: we require$0 \to A_n \xrightarrow{i_n} B_n \xrightarrow{p_n} C_n \to 0$ exact, and we require that$i$ and$p$ commute with differentials. These two requirements together constitute the definition of a short exact sequence in$\mathrm{Ch}(R\text{-Mod})$, to be discussed in Section 38.1.4.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Consider two chain maps $f, g: C_\bullet \to D_\bullet$ that are equal on homology but not as chain maps. For instance, let$C_\bullet = D_\bullet = 0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{0} \mathbb{Z} \to 0$ (with zero differential). Then$H_0 = H_1 = \mathbb{Z}$. The map$f_0 = f_1 = \mathrm{id}$ and the map$g_0 = \mathrm{id}, g_1 = -\mathrm{id}$ are both chain maps (the zero differential commutes with anything). They induce the same map$\mathrm{id}: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ on$H_0$ but differ on$H_1$ (one is$\mathrm{id}$, the other is$-\mathrm{id}$). This shows that the map$f_*$ on homology depends non-trivially on the chain map, not just on what it does to individual modules.
\end{example}

The functorial passage from chain maps to maps on homology is the central mechanism of the whole theory. Once one knows that $H_n$ is a functor from$\mathrm{Ch}(R\text{-Mod})$ to$R\text{-Mod}$, every algebraic construction on chain complexes — taking mapping cones, forming direct sums, passing to subcomplexes — automatically yields corresponding constructions on all homology groups. The chain map is the vehicle by which geometric and algebraic information passes into the homological world, and the commutativity condition is the passport it must carry.
