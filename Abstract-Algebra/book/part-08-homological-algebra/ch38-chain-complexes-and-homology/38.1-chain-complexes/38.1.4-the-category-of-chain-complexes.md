# 38.1.4 The Category of Chain Complexes $\mathrm{Ch}(R\text{-Mod})$

Once chain complexes and chain maps are in hand, it is natural to organize them into a category and ask what categorical properties this category has. The answer is gratifyingly complete: chain complexes of $R$-modules and chain maps form a category$\mathrm{Ch}(R\text{-Mod})$ that is itself an abelian category. This is not merely a formal observation — it means that all of the powerful machinery of abelian categories (kernels, cokernels, exact sequences, the snake lemma, the five lemma) applies to complexes just as it applies to modules. The entire theory of chain complexes can therefore be developed using the language of exact sequences, and the long exact sequence theorem of Section 38.2.3 is a result about abelian categories, not merely about modules.

The abelian structure on $\mathrm{Ch}(R\text{-Mod})$ is computed degreewise. The \emph{kernel} of a chain map$f: C_\bullet \to D_\bullet$ is the complex$(\ker f_n)_{n \in \mathbb{Z}}$ with differentials inherited from$C_\bullet$: since$d^C$ maps$\ker f_n$ into$\ker f_{n-1}$ (because$f_{n-1}(d^C(x)) = d^D(f_n(x)) = d^D(0) = 0$ for$x \in \ker f_n$), the restriction of$d^C_n$ to$\ker f_n$ indeed lands in$\ker f_{n-1}$ and makes$\ker(f)$ into a subcomplex of$C_\bullet$. Similarly, the \emph{cokernel} of$f$ is$(\mathrm{coker}\, f_n)_{n \in \mathbb{Z}}$ with the induced differentials from$D_\bullet$.

\begin{proposition}
A sequence $A_\bullet \xrightarrow{f} B_\bullet \xrightarrow{g} C_\bullet$ of chain complexes is exact in$\mathrm{Ch}(R\text{-Mod})$ if and only if the sequence$A_n \xrightarrow{f_n} B_n \xrightarrow{g_n} C_n$ is exact in$R\text{-Mod}$ for every$n \in \mathbb{Z}$.
\end{proposition}

This "degreewise" principle makes it straightforward to import exactness arguments from module theory into arguments about complexes. To check whether a sequence of complexes is exact, one checks degree by degree. To apply the snake lemma to a diagram of complexes, one applies it degree by degree and then assembles the results. This reduction to module theory is what makes $\mathrm{Ch}(R\text{-Mod})$ tractable in practice.

The homology functor $H_n: \mathrm{Ch}(R\text{-Mod}) \to R\text{-Mod}$ extracts the$n$th homology module from a chain complex. It is an additive functor between abelian categories. However,$H_n$ is not exact: a short exact sequence of chain complexes does not in general produce a short exact sequence of homology modules. Instead, as we will see in Section 38.2.3, one obtains a long exact sequence. This failure of exactness, and the connecting homomorphism that repairs it, is the fundamental theorem that drives all of homological algebra.

The abelian category structure of $\mathrm{Ch}(R\text{-Mod})$ also produces some tools not available for modules. The \emph{mapping cone} of a chain map$f: C_\bullet \to D_\bullet$ is the complex$\mathrm{Cone}(f)_n = D_n \oplus C_{n-1}$ with differential
$$d_{\mathrm{Cone}}(d, c) = (d^D(d) + f(c), -d^C(c)).$$
The cone fits into a short exact sequence of complexes $0 \to D_\bullet \to \mathrm{Cone}(f) \to C_\bullet[-1] \to 0$, and applying homology to this sequence yields the long exact sequence associated to$f$. The mapping cone is the algebraic analogue of the topological mapping cone, and it shows that the long exact sequence is a purely categorical phenomenon.

\begin{example}
The zero complex $0 = (\cdots \to 0 \to 0 \to \cdots)$ is both a zero object and an initial and terminal object in$\mathrm{Ch}(R\text{-Mod})$. The direct sum$C_\bullet \oplus D_\bullet$ is defined degreewise:$(C \oplus D)_n = C_n \oplus D_n$ with differential$d^C \oplus d^D$. The direct sum of the trivial resolution$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 2} \mathbb{Z} \to 0$ and itself is the complex$0 \to \mathbb{Z}^2 \xrightarrow{\mathrm{diag}(\times 2, \times 2)} \mathbb{Z}^2 \to 0$, which has$H_0 \cong (\mathbb{Z}/2)^2$ and$H_n = 0$ for$n \neq 0$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
A \emph{subcomplex} $A_\bullet \subseteq C_\bullet$ is a chain complex with$A_n \subseteq C_n$ for all$n$ and$d^C(A_n) \subseteq A_{n-1}$, i.e., the differential restricts to a differential on$A_\bullet$. The quotient complex$(C/A)_n = C_n/A_n$ inherits a differential from$C_\bullet$, and the natural sequence$0 \to A_\bullet \to C_\bullet \to (C/A)_\bullet \to 0$ is exact in$\mathrm{Ch}(R\text{-Mod})$. This is the setting of the long exact sequence theorem: every short exact sequence of complexes arises (at least locally) as a subcomplex and its quotient.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
The tensor product of two chain complexes $C_\bullet$ and$D_\bullet$ is the complex$(C \otimes D)_n = \bigoplus_{p+q=n} C_p \otimes_R D_q$ with total differential$d(c \otimes d) = d^C(c) \otimes d + (-1)^p c \otimes d^D(d)$ for$c \in C_p$. The sign$(-1)^p$ is forced by the requirement$d^2 = 0$: it enforces a "Koszul sign rule" that is ubiquitous in homological algebra. This tensor product makes$\mathrm{Ch}(R\text{-Mod})$ into a symmetric monoidal category, and it is the algebraic counterpart of the product of topological spaces.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
The \emph{shift} or \emph{suspension} $C_\bullet[k]$ of a chain complex$C_\bullet$ by an integer$k$ is defined by$C_\bullet[k]_n = C_{n+k}$ with differential$(-1)^k d_{n+k}$. Shifting by$1$ corresponds to "shifting the grading down by one": the module previously in degree$n+1$ moves to degree$n$. The long exact sequence of the mapping cone uses the shift$C_\bullet[-1]$: the connecting homomorphism$H_n(C) \to H_{n-1}(A)$ in a long exact sequence is, in this language, a degree$-1$ map on homology, reflecting the fact that the connecting homomorphism crosses one degree.
\end{example}

The abelian category structure of $\mathrm{Ch}(R\text{-Mod})$ underpins everything that follows. When we prove general theorems about derived functors, spectral sequences, and resolutions, we are working in an abelian category, and the results apply equally to chain complexes of$R$-modules, of sheaves, of representations, or of any other abelian category one cares to work in. This level of generality is not an abstraction for its own sake: it is what makes the homological machinery portable across all of algebra and geometry.
