# 38.1 Chain Complexes

The abstract notion of a chain complex distills the essential structure that makes homology computable. Before Noether's reformulation in the 1920s, Poincaré's Betti numbers were defined ad hoc in terms of combinatorial data attached to triangulated spaces; it was Noether who recognized that the boundary operations on simplices form a sequence of group homomorphisms with a single governing relation, and that this algebraic skeleton is the real subject of study. The result is a definition of breathtaking generality: a chain complex works over any ring $R$, with modules replacing abelian groups, and the purely algebraic condition $\partial \circ \partial = 0$ replaces any geometric intuition about boundaries.

Formally, a chain complex $(C_\bullet, \partial_\bullet)$ over a ring $R$ is a sequence $\cdots \to C_{n+1} \xrightarrow{\partial_{n+1}} C_n \xrightarrow{\partial_n} C_{n-1} \to \cdots$ of $R$-modules and $R$-module homomorphisms satisfying $\partial_n \circ \partial_{n+1} = 0$ for every integer $n$. The integer $n$ is called the degree or homological degree of the module $C_n$. The maps $\partial_n$ are the differentials or boundary operators. The defining condition says the image of each differential is a submodule of the kernel of the next, which is precisely what makes the quotient $\ker \partial_n / \operatorname{im} \partial_{n+1}$ well-defined. A cochain complex reverses the indexing: the differentials $d^n: C^n \to C^{n+1}$ raise degree, and cohomological conventions place the $n$th term in the superscript. Both conventions arise naturally — simplicial and singular homology are naturally chain complexes, while de Rham cohomology and sheaf cohomology are naturally cochain complexes.

Morphisms between chain complexes are chain maps: a chain map $f: C_\bullet \to D_\bullet$ is a collection of $R$-module homomorphisms $f_n: C_n \to D_n$ satisfying $\partial_n^D \circ f_n = f_{n-1} \circ \partial_n^C$ for all $n$. This commutativity condition encodes the idea that $f$ "respects the differential structure," and it is precisely what is needed to ensure that $f$ descends to a well-defined map on homology. With chain maps as morphisms, chain complexes over $R$ form a category $\mathbf{Ch}(R)$, which is an abelian category: it has kernels, cokernels, and the isomorphism theorems hold. The abelian structure of $\mathbf{Ch}(R)$ is the foundation on which short exact sequences of complexes — and the long exact sequences they generate — are built.
