# 38.2.1 Cycles, Boundaries, and Homology: $H_n = \ker d_n / \mathrm{im}\, d_{n+1}$

Given a chain complex, the question one immediately wants to ask is: where does the chain complex fail to be exact? An exact complex is one where $\ker d_n = \mathrm{im}\, d_{n+1}$ at every degree, meaning no information is "trapped" in the kernel that was not already produced by the previous boundary map. The homology modules measure precisely the extent to which this condition fails, degree by degree. Before defining these modules, it helps to build intuition for what the cycles and boundaries represent in concrete terms.

Think of the chain modules $C_n$ as "things of dimension$n$": in topology,$0$-chains are formal sums of points,$1$-chains are formal sums of paths,$2$-chains are formal sums of surfaces, and so on. The differential$d_n$ computes the "boundary" of an$n$-dimensional object, giving a formal sum of$(n-1)$-dimensional objects. A cycle in degree$n$ is an$n$-dimensional object with no boundary — a closed loop, a closed surface, or more generally any element that maps to zero under$d_n$. A boundary in degree$n$ is an$n$-dimensional object that is itself the boundary of something one dimension higher. The homology in degree$n$ asks: which closed things are not already boundaries? These are the "holes" at dimension$n$.

\begin{definition}
Let $C_\bullet$ be a chain complex. The \emph{$n$-cycles} are$Z_n = Z_n(C_\bullet) = \ker(d_n: C_n \to C_{n-1})$. The \emph{$n$-boundaries} are$B_n = B_n(C_\bullet) = \mathrm{im}(d_{n+1}: C_{n+1} \to C_n)$. The condition$d^2 = 0$ guarantees$B_n \subseteq Z_n$, and the \emph{$n$th homology module} is
$$H_n(C_\bullet) = Z_n / B_n = \ker(d_n) / \mathrm{im}(d_{n+1}).$$
\end{definition}

The containment $B_n \subseteq Z_n$ — every boundary is a cycle — is the direct translation of$d_n \circ d_{n+1} = 0$: an element$d_{n+1}(c)$ in the image of$d_{n+1}$ satisfies$d_n(d_{n+1}(c)) = 0$, hence lies in the kernel of$d_n$. The converse inclusion$Z_n \subseteq B_n$ would say the complex is exact at degree$n$. Homology therefore measures the failure of exactness:$H_n = 0$ if and only if the complex is exact at$C_n$.

Elements of $H_n$ are equivalence classes of cycles, where two cycles$z$ and$z'$ are declared equivalent if$z - z' \in B_n$ is a boundary. A class$[z] \in H_n$ is nontrivial precisely when the cycle$z$ is "not the boundary of anything" — when$z$ represents a genuine hole that the chain complex cannot fill. This language carries over directly from topology: a nontrivial class in$H_1$ of a simplicial complex corresponds to a loop that cannot be contracted, and a nontrivial class in$H_2$ corresponds to a closed surface that does not bound a solid region.

In algebra, the same language applies. A nontrivial class in the homology of a projective resolution of a module $M$ records a failure of projectivity. A nontrivial class in group cohomology records an extension or a cohomological obstruction. A nontrivial class in the Koszul complex records a failure of a sequence to be regular. The definition is the same in every case: cycles that are not boundaries represent something that "should be zero but isn't."

\begin{example}
For the complex $0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times n} \mathbb{Z} \to 0$ (with$C_1 = C_0 = \mathbb{Z}$ and$d_1 = \times n$):$Z_1 = \ker(\times n) = 0$,$B_1 = 0$ (nothing maps into$C_1$), so$H_1 = 0$. And$Z_0 = \mathbb{Z}$ (everything:$d_0 = 0$ maps to the zero complex),$B_0 = \mathrm{im}(\times n) = n\mathbb{Z}$, so$H_0 = \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. This is the standard free resolution of$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For the complex $0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{0} \mathbb{Z} \to 0$ with zero differential:$Z_1 = \mathbb{Z}$,$B_1 = 0$, so$H_1 = \mathbb{Z}$. And$Z_0 = \mathbb{Z}$,$B_0 = 0$, so$H_0 = \mathbb{Z}$. A complex with zero differential has homology equal to its chain modules. This is consistent with the algebraic topology of a discrete space: no boundaries to speak of, and every cycle is genuine.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Consider the Koszul complex over $\mathbb{Z}$:
$$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\binom{m}{n}} \mathbb{Z}^2 \xrightarrow{(n\ {-m})} \mathbb{Z} \to 0.$$
Here $d_2(k) = (mk, nk)$ and$d_1(a, b) = na - mb$. Check:$d_1 \circ d_2(k) = n(mk) - m(nk) = mnk - mnk = 0$. The cycle group in degree$1$ is$Z_1 = \ker(d_1) = \{(a,b): na = mb\}$. The image$B_1 = \mathrm{im}(d_2) = \{(mk, nk): k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$. When$\gcd(m,n) = 1$, one can show$Z_1 = B_1$, so$H_1 = 0$ — the complex is exact. When$\gcd(m,n) = d > 1$, one finds$H_1 \cong \mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}$, recording the failure of$m, n$ to be coprime.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $C_\bullet$ be a chain complex of free abelian groups concentrated in degrees$0$ through$2$:
$$0 \to \mathbb{Z}^2 \xrightarrow{d_2} \mathbb{Z}^3 \xrightarrow{d_1} \mathbb{Z} \to 0,$$
with $d_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ and$d_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. One checks$d_1 \circ d_2 = 0$. Directly:$H_0 = \mathbb{Z}/\mathrm{im}(d_1) = \mathbb{Z}/\mathbb{Z} = 0$ (since$d_1(e_1) = 1$ is a generator);$H_1 = \ker(d_1)/\mathrm{im}(d_2) = \{(a,b,c): a+b+c=0\}/\mathrm{im}(d_2)$, which one computes as$0$ by checking the Smith normal form; and$H_2 = \ker(d_2) = 0$ (since the columns of$d_2$ are linearly independent). This complex is exact; it arises as the augmented simplicial chain complex of a triangle with its interior.
\end{example}

The homology groups $H_n(C_\bullet)$ depend functorially on$C_\bullet$: a chain map$f: C_\bullet \to D_\bullet$ induces maps$f_*: H_n(C) \to H_n(D)$, as shown in Section 38.2.2. This functoriality is what allows homological invariants to be compared and related, and it is the engine that drives the rest of the theory.
