# 38.2.2 Induced Maps on Homology

A chain map $f: C_\bullet \to D_\bullet$ carries every module$C_n$ into$D_n$ in a differential-compatible way, so one expects it to carry cycles to cycles and boundaries to boundaries. The verification of this expectation is straightforward but worth making explicit, because it establishes the functoriality that underlies all of homological algebra. Without this functoriality, homology would be an invariant assigned to each complex in isolation, unable to detect the relationships between complexes. With it, homology becomes a machine for translating algebraic maps into information about structure.

\begin{proposition}
A chain map $f: C_\bullet \to D_\bullet$ induces well-defined$R$-linear maps$f_* = H_n(f): H_n(C) \to H_n(D)$ for all$n$, defined by$f_*([z]) = [f_n(z)]$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
We need to check two things: that $f_n$ carries cycles to cycles, and that it carries boundaries to boundaries (so the map descends to the quotient).

If $z \in Z_n(C) = \ker(d_n^C)$, so$d_n^C(z) = 0$, then the commutativity of the chain map gives$d_n^D(f_n(z)) = f_{n-1}(d_n^C(z)) = f_{n-1}(0) = 0$. Thus$f_n(z) \in Z_n(D)$.

If $z = d_{n+1}^C(c)$ is a boundary in$C_n$, then$f_n(z) = f_n(d_{n+1}^C(c)) = d_{n+1}^D(f_{n+1}(c))$, which is a boundary in$D_n$.

Therefore $f_n$ maps$Z_n(C)$ into$Z_n(D)$ and$B_n(C)$ into$B_n(D)$, and the induced map$f_*([z]) = [f_n(z)]$ is well-defined on the quotient$H_n(C) = Z_n(C)/B_n(C)$.
\end{proof}

The functoriality of homology follows immediately: $H_n(\mathrm{id}_{C_\bullet}) = \mathrm{id}_{H_n(C)}$ (because$(\mathrm{id})_n = \mathrm{id}_{C_n}$ induces the identity on quotients) and$H_n(g \circ f) = H_n(g) \circ H_n(f)$ (because$(g \circ f)_n = g_n \circ f_n$ at the chain level). This says that$H_n$ is a functor$\mathrm{Ch}(R\text{-Mod}) \to R\text{-Mod}$ for every$n$.

The practical importance of this functoriality is enormous. Whenever one has a chain map $f: C_\bullet \to D_\bullet$, one gets induced maps on all homology groups simultaneously and for free. Any algebraic construction that produces chain maps — taking subcomplexes, forming tensor products, composing resolutions — automatically yields corresponding maps on homology. This is what allows one to study a complicated algebraic situation by finding a good chain complex model and reading off homological information.

\begin{example}
Consider the chain map $f: (0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 2} \mathbb{Z} \to 0) \to (0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 4} \mathbb{Z} \to 0)$ given by$f_0 = \mathrm{id}$ and$f_1 = \times 2$. Check commutativity:$(\times 4) \circ f_1 = \times 8$ and$f_0 \circ (\times 2) = \times 2$. These are not equal, so this is not a chain map. Instead take$f_1 = \mathrm{id}$: then$(\times 4) \circ \mathrm{id} = \times 4$ and$f_0 \circ (\times 2) = \times 2$. Still not equal. The correct chain map from the resolution of$\mathbb{Z}/2$ to the resolution of$\mathbb{Z}/4$ requires$f_0: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ and$f_1: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ with$f_0 \circ (\times 2) = (\times 4) \circ f_1$. Taking$f_0 = \times 2$ and$f_1 = \mathrm{id}$ works:$(\times 2)(\times 2) = \times 4 = (\times 4)(\mathrm{id})$. This chain map induces$f_*: H_0 = \mathbb{Z}/2 \to H_0 = \mathbb{Z}/4$ given by$f_*([1]) = [2]$, corresponding to the injection$\mathbb{Z}/2 \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Z}/4$ sending$\bar{1} \mapsto \bar{2}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
The reduction map $\pi: \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ sending$\bar{x} \mapsto \bar{x}$ can be lifted to a chain map between their free resolutions. Taking the resolution$P_\bullet: 0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 4} \mathbb{Z} \to 0$ of$\mathbb{Z}/4$ and$Q_\bullet: 0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 2} \mathbb{Z} \to 0$ of$\mathbb{Z}/2$, a chain map lift is$\tilde{\pi}_0 = \mathrm{id}$ and$\tilde{\pi}_1 = \times 2$: check$(\times 2) \circ (\times 2) = \times 4 = \mathrm{id} \circ (\times 4)$. The induced map on$H_0$ is$\pi_*: \mathbb{Z}/4 \to \mathbb{Z}/2$, which is the reduction map$\bar{x} \mapsto \bar{x}$, as expected.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
The zero chain map $0: C_\bullet \to D_\bullet$ (sending everything to zero) induces the zero map on all homology groups. This is immediate from the definition:$0_*([z]) = [0_n(z)] = [0] = 0$. The identity chain map induces the identity on homology. Chain maps thus "extend" the ordinary module-map notions to the complexes setting in a fully compatible way.
\end{example}

The key insight is that the two conditions — $f_n$ maps cycles to cycles and$f_n$ maps boundaries to boundaries — are both necessary for the induced map to be well-defined, and both follow from the single commutativity condition$d^D f = f d^C$. This economy is characteristic of the homological approach: one condition implies two consequences, and those two consequences are exactly what is needed. Chain homotopy (Section 38.3.1) will refine this picture further: two chain maps that differ by a chain homotopy induce the same map on homology, showing that the map on homology is an even coarser invariant of the chain map than the chain map itself.
