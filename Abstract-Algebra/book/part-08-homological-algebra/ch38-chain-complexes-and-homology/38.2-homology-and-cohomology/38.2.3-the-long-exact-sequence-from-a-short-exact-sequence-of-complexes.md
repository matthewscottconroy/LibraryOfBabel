# 38.2.3 The Long Exact Sequence from a Short Exact Sequence of Complexes

The most important result in homological algebra — and the one that gives the subject most of its computational reach — concerns what happens to homology when chain complexes are assembled into a short exact sequence. If the homology functor were exact, the story would be simple: a short exact sequence of complexes would produce a short exact sequence of homology modules, and nothing new would arise. The surprise is that exactness breaks, but it breaks in a completely controlled and useful way: the short exact sequence is "unrolled" into an infinite long exact sequence, with a connecting homomorphism threading the degrees together.

\begin{definition}
A \emph{short exact sequence of chain complexes} is a sequence
$$0 \to A_\bullet \xrightarrow{i} B_\bullet \xrightarrow{p} C_\bullet \to 0$$
of chain complexes and chain maps such that $0 \to A_n \xrightarrow{i_n} B_n \xrightarrow{p_n} C_n \to 0$ is a short exact sequence of$R$-modules for every$n \in \mathbb{Z}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Long Exact Sequence in Homology]
A short exact sequence of chain complexes $0 \to A_\bullet \xrightarrow{i} B_\bullet \xrightarrow{p} C_\bullet \to 0$ gives rise to a long exact sequence in homology:
$$\cdots \to H_{n+1}(C) \xrightarrow{\partial} H_n(A) \xrightarrow{i_*} H_n(B) \xrightarrow{p_*} H_n(C) \xrightarrow{\partial} H_{n-1}(A) \to \cdots$$
where the \emph{connecting homomorphism} $\partial: H_n(C) \to H_{n-1}(A)$ is constructed explicitly in Section 38.2.4.
\end{theorem}

The maps $i_*$ and$p_*$ are induced by$i$ and$p$ in the usual functorial way (Section 38.2.2); the connecting homomorphism$\partial$ is the new ingredient. The sequence is exact at every term, meaning:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\ker(i_*) = \mathrm{im}(\partial)$ at$H_n(A)$,
\item $\ker(p_*) = \mathrm{im}(i_*)$ at$H_n(B)$,
\item $\ker(\partial) = \mathrm{im}(p_*)$ at$H_n(C)$.
\end{itemize}

To see why the short exact sequence cannot produce an exact sequence in homology directly: the functor $H_n$ is neither left-exact nor right-exact. Given$0 \to A \to B \to C \to 0$ at the level of modules, the induced sequence$H_n(A) \to H_n(B) \to H_n(C)$ is neither automatically exact at the middle nor automatically injective or surjective at the ends. The connecting homomorphism$\partial$ supplies the "missing" part of the exact sequence, sliding down one degree to do so.

To understand why this matters: essentially every long exact sequence that appears in algebra and topology arises from a short exact sequence of complexes by this construction. The long exact sequence of a pair $(X, A)$ in singular homology arises from$0 \to C_\bullet(A) \to C_\bullet(X) \to C_\bullet(X, A) \to 0$. The long exact sequence relating group cohomology for a normal subgroup arises from a short exact sequence of cochain complexes. The Mayer–Vietoris sequence in topology arises from a short exact sequence built from the cover. The long exact sequence in algebraic$K$-theory arises from a localization sequence. All are instances of this one theorem.

\begin{example}
The short exact sequence $0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 2} \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/2 \to 0$ of$\mathbb{Z}$-modules can be viewed as a short exact sequence of chain complexes concentrated in degree$0$. The long exact sequence is then just$\cdots \to 0 \to H_0(\mathbb{Z}) \to H_0(\mathbb{Z}) \to H_0(\mathbb{Z}/2) \to 0 \to \cdots$, which reads$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 2} \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/2 \to 0$. No new information: the connecting homomorphism is zero because all the source complexes are in degree$0$ only.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Consider the short exact sequence of complexes:
$$0 \to (0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 2} \mathbb{Z} \to 0) \xrightarrow{i} (0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 4} \mathbb{Z} \to 0) \xrightarrow{p} (0 \to \mathbb{Z}/2 \xrightarrow{0} \mathbb{Z}/2 \to 0) \to 0,$$
where $i_0 = i_1 = \mathrm{id}$ and... (this requires some care). More cleanly: the short exact sequence$0 \to \mathbb{Z}/2 \xrightarrow{\times 2} \mathbb{Z}/4 \xrightarrow{\pi} \mathbb{Z}/2 \to 0$ of modules, viewed as complexes in degree$0$, gives a trivial long exact sequence. To see a non-trivial connecting homomorphism, one needs complexes in multiple degrees.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $X$ be the circle$S^1$ and$A = \{N, S\}$ be the two-point subspace (north and south poles). The short exact sequence$0 \to C_\bullet(A) \to C_\bullet(S^1) \to C_\bullet(S^1, A) \to 0$ gives the long exact sequence
$$0 \to H_1(A) \to H_1(S^1) \to H_1(S^1, A) \xrightarrow{\partial} H_0(A) \to H_0(S^1) \to H_0(S^1, A) \to 0.$$
Since $A$ is two points:$H_0(A) = \mathbb{Z}^2$,$H_n(A) = 0$ for$n > 0$. Since$S^1$ is connected with one loop:$H_0(S^1) = \mathbb{Z}$,$H_1(S^1) = \mathbb{Z}$,$H_n(S^1) = 0$ for$n > 1$. The relative homology$H_1(S^1, A) \cong \mathbb{Z}^2$ (two arcs), and the connecting homomorphism$\partial: \mathbb{Z}^2 \to \mathbb{Z}^2$ maps each arc to the difference of its boundary points. The long exact sequence assembles these pieces into a coherent picture of the topology of the pair.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
The Mayer–Vietoris sequence for a space $X = U \cup V$ with$U \cap V$ connected comes from the short exact sequence of chain complexes
$$0 \to C_\bullet(U \cap V) \xrightarrow{(i_U, -i_V)} C_\bullet(U) \oplus C_\bullet(V) \xrightarrow{j_U + j_V} C_\bullet(X) \to 0$$
(where the last map requires a mild argument using barycentric subdivision to ensure surjectivity). The long exact sequence gives the Mayer–Vietoris sequence
$$\cdots \to H_n(U \cap V) \to H_n(U) \oplus H_n(V) \to H_n(X) \xrightarrow{\partial} H_{n-1}(U \cap V) \to \cdots$$
which is the primary tool for computing homology by decomposing a space into simpler pieces.
\end{example}

The statement that $H_n$ converts short exact sequences of complexes into long exact sequences of modules is sometimes called the fundamental theorem of homological algebra, and its consequences are inexhaustible. Once one knows this theorem, the strategy for computing homology groups is clear: find a short exact sequence of complexes with two terms whose homology is known, and use the long exact sequence to determine the third. The connecting homomorphism$\partial$ is the bridge between degrees, and its explicit construction in the next section is essential for making this strategy work in practice.
