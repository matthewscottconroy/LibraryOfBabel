# 38.2 Homology and Cohomology

The homology groups of a chain complex are its primary invariants, and their definition is immediately transparent once the setup is in place. Given a chain complex $C_\bullet$, the $n$th homology module is $H_n(C_\bullet) = \ker(\partial_n) / \operatorname{im}(\partial_{n+1})$. Elements of $\ker \partial_n$ are called $n$-cycles and elements of $\operatorname{im} \partial_{n+1}$ are called $n$-boundaries. A cycle is a "closed" element — its boundary is zero — and a boundary is one that "came from somewhere." Homology asks: which closed things are non-trivially closed, not merely the boundary of something in the next degree? The answer is a module, and its structure encodes the obstructions, the holes, the genuine topological or algebraic features that the complex is measuring.

A chain map $f: C_\bullet \to D_\bullet$ sends cycles to cycles and boundaries to boundaries, so it induces well-defined homomorphisms $f_*: H_n(C_\bullet) \to H_n(D_\bullet)$ for every $n$. This functoriality — the assignment $C_\bullet \mapsto H_n(C_\bullet)$ is a functor from $\mathbf{Ch}(R)$ to $R$-modules — is not just a formal nicety; it is what makes homology computable and comparable. When a short exact sequence $0 \to A_\bullet \to B_\bullet \to C_\bullet \to 0$ of chain complexes is given, the snake lemma produces a long exact sequence in homology: $\cdots \to H_n(A_\bullet) \to H_n(B_\bullet) \to H_n(C_\bullet) \xrightarrow{\delta} H_{n-1}(A_\bullet) \to \cdots$. The connecting homomorphism $\delta$ is the algebraic heart of the construction, built by a diagram-chasing argument that has no content beyond the definitions, but whose consequences are far-reaching. The Mayer–Vietoris sequence, the long exact sequence of a pair in singular homology, and the long exact sequences of derived functors all arise from this template.

Cohomology is the dual theory, and the two are related but genuinely different in structure. For a cochain complex $C^\bullet$, the cohomology $H^n(C^\bullet) = \ker(d^n) / \operatorname{im}(d^{n-1})$ is defined identically with the indexing reversed. The Universal Coefficient Theorem relates $H^n(C^\bullet; R)$ to $\operatorname{Hom}(H_n(C_\bullet), R)$ with a correction term involving $\operatorname{Ext}^1$, showing that cohomology is in general richer than the naive dual of homology. The cup product on cohomology — defined by a bilinear pairing on cochain level that passes to cohomology — gives $H^*(C^\bullet)$ the structure of a graded ring, encoding multiplicative information invisible to homology. This extra ring structure, together with Poincaré duality in the manifold setting, makes cohomology the preferred language for much of modern algebraic topology and algebraic geometry.
