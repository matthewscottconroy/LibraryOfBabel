# 38.3.1 Chain Homotopies Between Maps of Complexes

Two chain maps $f, g: C_\bullet \to D_\bullet$ can induce different maps on some homology groups, or they can induce the same map on all homology groups without being equal as chain maps. The question of when two chain maps are "the same for homological purposes" has a clean answer: they are chain homotopic. Chain homotopy is the algebraic counterpart of the topological notion of a homotopy between continuous maps, and the analogy is deeper than it might first appear.

In topology, a homotopy between two continuous maps $f, g: X \to Y$ is a continuous map$H: X \times [0,1] \to Y$ with$H(-,0) = f$ and$H(-,1) = g$. The interval$[0,1]$ provides a "one-dimensional witness" to the deformation from$f$ to$g$. In the algebraic setting, the interval is replaced by maps that go up one degree — they provide one-dimensional "witnesses" at each degree that track how$f$ and$g$ differ. The precise condition is designed so that these witnesses exactly account for the difference$f - g$ in terms of boundaries.

\begin{definition}
Two chain maps $f, g: C_\bullet \to D_\bullet$ are \emph{chain homotopic} (written$f \simeq g$) if there exist$R$-linear maps$h_n: C_n \to D_{n+1}$ for all$n$, satisfying
$$f_n - g_n = d^D_{n+1} \circ h_n + h_{n-1} \circ d^C_n$$
for all $n$. The collection$\{h_n\}_{n \in \mathbb{Z}}$ is called a \emph{chain homotopy} from$g$ to$f$.
\end{definition}

The formula $f - g = dh + hd$ deserves careful reading. On the left is the difference of two chain maps, which is itself a chain map. On the right,$dh$ means "apply$h$ to get something in degree$n+1$, then take the boundary to get back to degree$n$"; while$hd$ means "take the boundary of the input to get something in degree$n-1$, then apply$h$ to get back to degree$n$." The sum$dh + hd$ therefore captures all the ways that the "one-higher" map$h$ can interact with the boundary operator.

Note the degree: $h_n: C_n \to D_{n+1}$ goes up by one. This is the key structural feature. If$h$ went to the same degree or lower, the formula$dh + hd$ would have the wrong degrees for any comparison with$f - g$.

Chain homotopy is an equivalence relation. Reflexivity: $f \simeq f$ via the zero homotopy$h = 0$ (then$dh + hd = 0 = f - f$). Symmetry: if$h$ is a chain homotopy from$g$ to$f$, then$-h$ is a chain homotopy from$f$ to$g$. Transitivity: if$h$ is a homotopy from$g$ to$f$ and$h'$ is a homotopy from$g'$ to$g$, then$h + h'$ is a homotopy from$g'$ to$f$ (since$d(h+h') + (h+h')d = (dh + hd) + (dh' + h'd) = (f-g) + (g-g') = f - g'$). The equivalence classes of chain maps under chain homotopy are the morphisms in the homotopy category$K(R\text{-Mod})$ (Section 38.3.4).

Chain homotopy is also compatible with composition: if $f \simeq g$ via$h: C \to D$ and$\phi: D \to E$ is any chain map, then$\phi \circ f \simeq \phi \circ g$ via$\phi \circ h$. And if$\psi: A \to C$ is any chain map, then$f \circ \psi \simeq g \circ \psi$ via$h \circ \psi$. This compatibility ensures that chain homotopy equivalence descends to the quotient and gives a well-defined homotopy category.

\begin{example}
Consider $C_\bullet = D_\bullet = (0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times n} \mathbb{Z} \to 0)$ with$C_1 = C_0 = \mathbb{Z}$. Any chain map$f: C \to D$ is given by integers$f_0, f_1$ with$f_0 \cdot n = n \cdot f_1$, i.e.,$f_0 = f_1$. A chain homotopy$h: C \to D$ has$h_0: C_0 \to D_1 = \mathbb{Z}$ and$h_1: C_1 \to D_2 = 0$ (the latter is forced to be zero). The homotopy formula at degree$0$ reads$f_0 - g_0 = d^D_1 h_0 + h_{-1} d^C_0$; since$d^C_0 = 0$ and$d^D_1 = \times n$, this is$f_0 - g_0 = n \cdot h_0(1)$. So$f$ and$g$ are chain homotopic if and only if$f_0 - g_0 \in n\mathbb{Z}$, i.e.,$f_0 \equiv g_0 \pmod{n}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
The identity map $\mathrm{id}: C_\bullet \to C_\bullet$ and the zero map$0: C_\bullet \to C_\bullet$ are chain homotopic if and only if$\mathrm{id} = dh + hd$ for some$h$. For a chain complex with trivial differential (all$d_n = 0$), the condition reduces to$\mathrm{id} = 0$, which is impossible unless$C_\bullet = 0$. So on a complex with non-zero modules and trivial differential, the identity and zero maps are not chain homotopic, and their induced maps on homology (the identity and zero map on$H_n = C_n$) are indeed different.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
The projection $\pi: C_\bullet \to H_*(C)$ (to the complex with zero differential whose modules are the homology groups) is not itself a chain map in general. However, if$C_\bullet$ is a complex of free modules with trivial differential (so$C_\bullet \cong H_*(C)$ as graded modules), then any two maps$f, g: C_\bullet \to C_\bullet$ that agree on homology are chain homotopic. This is the easy case of the comparison theorem, and it illustrates that chain homotopy is the right notion for "same up to homological information."
\end{example}

The formula $f - g = dh + hd$ has a transparent geometric meaning in topology, where it says that the difference between the two chain maps is accounted for by a "prism" operator$h$ that simultaneously maps forward and records the homotopy. The algebraic formula captures this geometry exactly:$h$ provides an explicit boundary witnessing the equality of$[f(z)]$ and$[g(z)]$ for every cycle$z$, as the next section demonstrates.
