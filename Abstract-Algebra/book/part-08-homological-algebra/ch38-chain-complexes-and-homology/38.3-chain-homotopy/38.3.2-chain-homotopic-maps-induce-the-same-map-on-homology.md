# 38.3.2 Chain Homotopic Maps Induce the Same Map on Homology

The fundamental result about chain homotopies is that chain homotopic maps induce equal maps on homology. This is the algebraic analogue of the topological fact that homotopic maps induce the same map on singular homology, and the proof in the algebraic setting is essentially the same computation. More strikingly, the proof is a single line: evaluating the homotopy formula on a cycle and observing that a term vanishes.

\begin{theorem}
If $f, g: C_\bullet \to D_\bullet$ are chain homotopic via$h = \{h_n\}$, then$H_n(f) = H_n(g)$ as maps$H_n(C) \to H_n(D)$ for all$n$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $z \in Z_n(C)$ be a cycle, so$d^C_n(z) = 0$. The homotopy formula gives
$$f_n(z) - g_n(z) = d^D_{n+1}(h_n(z)) + h_{n-1}(d^C_n(z)) = d^D_{n+1}(h_n(z)) + h_{n-1}(0) = d^D_{n+1}(h_n(z)).$$
This is a boundary in $D_n$. Therefore$[f_n(z)] = [g_n(z)]$ in$H_n(D)$, which means$H_n(f)([z]) = H_n(g)([z])$ for every cycle$z$, so$H_n(f) = H_n(g)$.
\end{proof}

The key insight is that the second term in the homotopy formula, $h_{n-1} \circ d^C_n$, vanishes on cycles. This is the algebraic content of the claim that$h$ is a witness: when restricted to cycles, the formula$f - g = dh + hd$ says$f(z) - g(z) = dh(z)$, exhibiting$f(z) - g(z)$ explicitly as a boundary. Chain homotopy is thus exactly calibrated to kill the difference$f - g$ on homology.

The converse is false: two chain maps can induce the same map on all homology groups without being chain homotopic. This failure is measured by the \emph{homotopy category} $K(R\text{-Mod})$ (Section 38.3.4) and the \emph{derived category}$D(R\text{-Mod})$: in the homotopy category, chain homotopic maps become equal; in the derived category, even more maps become equal (quasi-isomorphisms are inverted). The chain homotopy notion is strictly stronger than "same on homology" and strictly weaker than "equal as chain maps."

\begin{example}
The two maps $f, g: P_\bullet \to Q_\bullet$ arising as different lifts of the same module map$\phi: M \to N$ via the comparison theorem (Section 39.1.3) are chain homotopic. The chain homotopy is constructed inductively using the projectivity of the$P_n$'s: at each step, the difference$f_n - g_n$ maps cycles to boundaries, and the projective lifting property provides the map$h_n$ that witnesses this. This is the prototype for all applications of chain homotopy in the theory of resolutions.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Over $\mathbb{Z}$, consider the two chain maps$f, g: (0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{0} \mathbb{Z} \to 0) \to (0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{0} \mathbb{Z} \to 0)$ given by$f_0 = f_1 = \mathrm{id}$ and$g_0 = g_1 = -\mathrm{id}$. The homotopy formula requires$f_0 - g_0 = 2 \cdot \mathrm{id} = dh + hd = d_1 h_0 + h_{-1} d_0$. Since$d = 0$, this requires$2 \cdot \mathrm{id} = 0$, which is impossible over$\mathbb{Z}$. So$f$ and$g$ are not chain homotopic, and they induce different maps ($+\mathrm{id}$ versus$-\mathrm{id}$) on homology$H_0 = H_1 = \mathbb{Z}$. Chain homotopy is genuinely stronger than just "same on homology."
\end{example}

\begin{example}
The identity map $\mathrm{id}: C_\bullet \to C_\bullet$ is always chain homotopic to itself via the zero homotopy$h = 0$. More interestingly, if$C_\bullet$ is a \emph{contractible} complex — one chain homotopy equivalent to the zero complex — then$\mathrm{id}_{C}$ is chain homotopic to$0$. The chain homotopy$h$ witnessing this is called a \emph{chain contraction} of$C$. A chain contraction is a collection of maps$s_n: C_n \to C_{n+1}$ with$ds + sd = \mathrm{id}$. Acyclic complexes (those with$H_n = 0$ for all$n$) are not necessarily contractible, but over a field or for complexes of free modules, every acyclic complex is contractible.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
The augmented bar resolution $B_\bullet \to \mathbb{Z}$ of the trivial$\mathbb{Z}[G]$-module$\mathbb{Z}$ (Section 40.4.4) is contractible as a complex of abelian groups: there is an explicit chain contraction$s: \mathbb{Z} \to B_0$ (send$1$ to the unit group element) and$s_n: B_n \to B_{n+1}$ (insert a unit element at the front of each bar). This contractibility is what implies$H_n(G, \mathbb{Z}) = H_n(B_\bullet \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}[G]} \mathbb{Z})$ is what we want; the chain contraction ensures the bar complex is the "correct" model for group cohomology.
\end{example}

A complementary perspective: chain homotopy equivalences (maps with a chain homotopy inverse, Section 38.3.3) are the isomorphisms in the homotopy category. They are strictly stronger than quasi-isomorphisms. Understanding the hierarchy — isomorphism of complexes, chain homotopy equivalence, quasi-isomorphism — is essential for working with derived categories and for understanding which invariants can be computed from which types of equivalence.
