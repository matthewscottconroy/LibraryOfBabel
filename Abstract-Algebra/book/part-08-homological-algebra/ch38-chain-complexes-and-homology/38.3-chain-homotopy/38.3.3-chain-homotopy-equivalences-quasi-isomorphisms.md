# 38.3.3 Chain Homotopy Equivalences; Quasi-Isomorphisms

Two chain complexes can be related by maps in several ways, ranging from the strongest to the weakest notion of sameness. Understanding the hierarchy of these notions — isomorphism, chain homotopy equivalence, quasi-isomorphism — is essential for working in homological algebra, because different constructions produce different levels of the hierarchy and require different tools to exploit. The derived category, which is the correct setting for homological algebra in full generality, is precisely the category obtained by equating the weakest of these three notions (quasi-isomorphism).

\begin{definition}
A chain map $f: C_\bullet \to D_\bullet$ is a \emph{quasi-isomorphism} if it induces isomorphisms$H_n(f): H_n(C) \xrightarrow{\sim} H_n(D)$ for all$n$.
\end{definition}

A quasi-isomorphism is an isomorphism "on homology" even though it need not be an isomorphism of chain complexes. The chain complexes $C$ and$D$ may look very different — different ranks, different differentials, different lengths — while having identical homology. For example, any chain complex$C_\bullet$ is quasi-isomorphic to the complex$H_*(C)$ with zero differential (its homology viewed as a complex), by a map that sends each cycle to its class. But this map is almost never a chain isomorphism.

\begin{definition}
A \emph{chain homotopy equivalence} is a chain map $f: C_\bullet \to D_\bullet$ for which there exists a chain map$g: D_\bullet \to C_\bullet$ such that$g \circ f \simeq \mathrm{id}_{C}$ and$f \circ g \simeq \mathrm{id}_{D}$.
\end{definition}

Every chain homotopy equivalence is a quasi-isomorphism: if $g \circ f \simeq \mathrm{id}_{C}$, then$H_n(g) \circ H_n(f) = H_n(g \circ f) = H_n(\mathrm{id}_C) = \mathrm{id}_{H_n(C)}$ (using Section 38.3.2 and the functoriality of$H_n$). Similarly$H_n(f) \circ H_n(g) = \mathrm{id}$, so$H_n(f)$ is an isomorphism. The converse fails.

The simplest example where the converse fails: let $C_\bullet$ be any acyclic complex (one with$H_n = 0$ for all$n$) that is not contractible. The zero complex$0$ has$H_n(0) = 0$, so the chain map$0 \to C_\bullet$ is a quasi-isomorphism. But it is not a chain homotopy equivalence, because a chain homotopy inverse would require a chain map$C_\bullet \to 0$ such that the composite is chain homotopic to$\mathrm{id}_0$ (automatic) and the other composite is chain homotopic to$\mathrm{id}_{C}$, i.e.,$\mathrm{id}_{C} \simeq 0$, meaning$C$ is contractible.

\begin{proposition}
Over a field, every quasi-isomorphism of bounded-below complexes of vector spaces is a chain homotopy equivalence.
\end{proposition}

Over $\mathbb{Z}$ or more general rings, this fails: the inclusion$0 \to C_\bullet$ where$C_\bullet$ is acyclic but not contractible is a quasi-isomorphism that is not a chain homotopy equivalence.

The correct framework for handling quasi-isomorphisms is the derived category $D(R\text{-Mod})$ (introduced formally in Section 38.3.4): in this category, quasi-isomorphisms become isomorphisms by definition, and two complexes are isomorphic in$D$ if and only if they are connected by a zig-zag of quasi-isomorphisms. This is the natural home of derived functors and of all computations in homological algebra that are "independent of the choice of resolution."

\begin{example}
The two projective resolutions of $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$:
\begin{align*}
P_\bullet &= (0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 2} \mathbb{Z} \to 0) \\
Q_\bullet &= (0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 4} \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 2} \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 2} \mathbb{Z} \to 0)
\end{align*}
both have $H_0 = \mathbb{Z}/2$ and$H_n = 0$ for$n > 0$. The comparison theorem (Section 39.1.3) provides a chain map$P_\bullet \to Q_\bullet$ that is a quasi-isomorphism (both sides have the same homology). Both complexes are chain homotopy equivalent to the one-term complex$\mathbb{Z}/2$ (viewed as a complex in degree zero). This example shows that the notion of "two projective resolutions of the same module" is precisely "two complexes that are quasi-isomorphic to the same module."
\end{example}

\begin{example}
The inclusion $\iota: \mathbb{Z}/2 \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Z}/4$ gives a short exact sequence$0 \to \mathbb{Z}/2 \to \mathbb{Z}/4 \to \mathbb{Z}/2 \to 0$. The complex$(\mathbb{Z}/4, 0)$ (in degree$0$) is quasi-isomorphic to$(\mathbb{Z}/4, 0)$, of course. But$\mathbb{Z}/4$ and$\mathbb{Z}/2 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2$ have the same associated graded with respect to the filtration$\mathbb{Z}/4 \supset 2\mathbb{Z}/4 \supset 0$, yet are not isomorphic as modules. This illustrates the extension problem that arises when recovering homology from associated-graded data.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For a projective module $P$, the chain complex$0 \to P \to P \to 0$ (with differential$\mathrm{id}: P \to P$) is acyclic. It is also contractible: the chain homotopy$h_0: P \to P$ (with$h_0 = \mathrm{id}$,$h_n = 0$ for$n \neq 0$) satisfies$dh + hd = \mathrm{id}_{P \to P}$. So for projective modules, acyclicity implies contractibility. This fails for general modules: the complex$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 2} \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/2 \to 0$ is acyclic (it is a free resolution), but it is not contractible as a$\mathbb{Z}$-module complex (a contraction would require a section$\mathbb{Z}/2 \to \mathbb{Z}$, which does not exist).
\end{example}

The three levels of sameness — isomorphism, chain homotopy equivalence, quasi-isomorphism — reflect three progressively weaker notions of "the same complex." The homological information in a chain complex is fully captured by the quasi-isomorphism class; the chain homotopy equivalence class carries slightly more; and the isomorphism class carries the most. Derived functor theory is exactly the enterprise of computing invariants that depend only on the quasi-isomorphism class, and the comparisons in Sections 39.1.3 and 40.1.3 are what make this possible.
