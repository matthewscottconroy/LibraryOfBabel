# 38.3.4 The Homotopy Category $K(R\text{-Mod})$

The category $\mathrm{Ch}(R\text{-Mod})$ of chain complexes has too many morphisms for some purposes: two chain maps that are chain homotopic carry identical homological information, yet they are distinct morphisms. To work modulo this redundancy, one passes to the \emph{homotopy category}$K(R\text{-Mod})$, which has the same objects as$\mathrm{Ch}(R\text{-Mod})$ but whose morphisms are chain homotopy classes of chain maps.

\begin{definition}
The \emph{homotopy category} $K(R\text{-Mod})$ has:
\begin{itemize}
\item Objects: chain complexes of $R$-modules.
\item Morphisms: $\mathrm{Hom}_{K}(C, D) = \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathrm{Ch}}(C, D) / \simeq$, the set of chain homotopy classes.
\item Composition: induced from $\mathrm{Ch}(R\text{-Mod})$, which is well-defined on homotopy classes because chain homotopy is compatible with composition.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

Composition is well-defined because if $f \simeq f'$ and$g \simeq g'$, then$g \circ f \simeq g \circ f' \simeq g' \circ f'$ (using the compatibility of chain homotopy with pre- and post-composition). The functor$H_n: \mathrm{Ch}(R\text{-Mod}) \to R\text{-Mod}$ factors through$K(R\text{-Mod})$: since chain homotopic maps induce the same map on homology,$H_n$ is unambiguously defined on the morphism classes of$K$.

The homotopy category is not abelian — it lacks kernels and cokernels in the usual sense — but it has a \emph{triangulated structure}. This structure replaces the role of exact sequences:

\begin{definition}
The \emph{mapping cone} of a chain map $f: C_\bullet \to D_\bullet$ is the complex$\mathrm{Cone}(f)$ with$\mathrm{Cone}(f)_n = D_n \oplus C_{n-1}$ and differential
$$d_{\mathrm{Cone}}(d, c) = (d^D(d) + f_{n-1}(c),\ -d^C_{n-1}(c)).$$
\end{definition}

The mapping cone fits into a natural short exact sequence of complexes $0 \to D_\bullet \to \mathrm{Cone}(f) \to C_\bullet[-1] \to 0$, and applying$H_n$ gives the long exact sequence associated to$f$:
$$\cdots \to H_n(C) \xrightarrow{f_*} H_n(D) \to H_n(\mathrm{Cone}(f)) \to H_{n-1}(C) \to \cdots$$
A \emph{distinguished triangle} in $K(R\text{-Mod})$ is a triangle$C \xrightarrow{f} D \to \mathrm{Cone}(f) \to C[-1]$ (and any triangle isomorphic to such a one in$K$). The triangulated structure consists of all distinguished triangles together with the shift functor$[1]$, and the axioms of a triangulated category are verified for$K(R\text{-Mod})$.

The derived category $D(R\text{-Mod})$ is then obtained from$K(R\text{-Mod})$ by a formal procedure of inverting quasi-isomorphisms.

\begin{definition}
The \emph{derived category} $D(R\text{-Mod})$ is the localization of$K(R\text{-Mod})$ at the class of all quasi-isomorphisms.
\end{definition}

In $D(R\text{-Mod})$, a quasi-isomorphism$f: C \to D$ becomes an isomorphism. Two chain complexes are isomorphic in$D$ if and only if they are connected by a zig-zag$C \xleftarrow{\sim} E_1 \xrightarrow{\sim} E_2 \xleftarrow{\sim} \cdots \xrightarrow{\sim} D$ of quasi-isomorphisms. In particular, any module$M$ (in degree$0$) is isomorphic in$D$ to any of its projective or injective resolutions: the augmentation$P_\bullet \to M$ is a quasi-isomorphism, so$P_\bullet \cong M$ in$D$.

\begin{example}
The two free resolutions of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$,
$$P_\bullet = (0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times n} \mathbb{Z} \to 0) \quad \text{and} \quad Q_\bullet = (0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times n^2} \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 1} \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times n} \mathbb{Z} \to 0),$$
are both quasi-isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ (in degree$0$). In$D(\mathbb{Z}\text{-Mod})$, all three are isomorphic. Any functor defined on$D(\mathbb{Z}\text{-Mod})$ will give the same result on all three, which is the content of the well-definedness of derived functors.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
The homotopy category $K^b(R\text{-Mod})$ (bounded complexes) is useful in representation theory: for a finite-dimensional algebra$A$ over a field, the bounded derived category$D^b(A\text{-mod})$ of finitely generated$A$-modules is the natural home for Morita theory, tilting theory, and the study of equivalences between module categories. Two algebras$A$ and$B$ are \emph{derived equivalent} if$D^b(A\text{-mod}) \cong D^b(B\text{-mod})$ as triangulated categories; this is a weaker notion than Morita equivalence but captures many of the same invariants.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
In $K(R\text{-Mod})$, the complex$C_\bullet = (0 \to R \xrightarrow{\mathrm{id}} R \to 0)$ (with$R$ in degrees$1$ and$0$ and$d_1 = \mathrm{id}$) is isomorphic to the zero complex in$K$. To see this, the chain contraction$s_0: R \to R$ defined by$s_0 = \mathrm{id}: C_0 \to C_1$ satisfies$ds + sd = \mathrm{id}: C_0 \to C_0$ and$ds + sd = \mathrm{id}: C_1 \to C_1$. So$\mathrm{id}_C \simeq 0$ in$K$, making$C$ contractible and isomorphic to$0$ in$K$. This reflects the fact that$C$ is acyclic: its homology is$0 = H_*(0)$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
The derived category is the natural home for many classical theorems. The universal coefficient theorem for cohomology states:
$$0 \to \mathrm{Ext}^1_{\mathbb{Z}}(H_{n-1}(X), G) \to H^n(X; G) \to \mathrm{Hom}(H_n(X), G) \to 0.$$
This splits (unnaturally); both sides can be computed from $C_\bullet(X)$ using the derived functor$R\mathrm{Hom}(-, G)$ applied in$D(\mathbb{Z}\text{-Mod})$. The natural framework for stating and proving the universal coefficient theorem is precisely the derived category, where the formula becomes$H^n(X; G) = H^n(R\mathrm{Hom}(C_\bullet(X), G))$.
\end{example}

The derived category is one of the central objects of modern algebra, geometry, and representation theory. It is the natural domain for derived functors ($\mathrm{Ext}$,$\mathrm{Tor}$,$R\Gamma$,$L\otimes$), for the formulation of Grothendieck duality, and for the study of equivalences between categories of geometric and algebraic origin. Every concept introduced in Chapter 38 — chain complexes, chain maps, homotopy, quasi-isomorphism — is precisely what is needed to construct this category and understand its morphisms.
