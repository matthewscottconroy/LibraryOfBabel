# 38.3 Chain Homotopy

Isomorphism of chain complexes — having an inverse chain map on the nose — is far too strong a notion of equivalence for most purposes. The right notion is chain homotopy equivalence, and understanding it properly is what unlocks the power of the homological machinery. Two chain maps $f, g: C_\bullet \to D_\bullet$ are chain homotopic if there exist $R$-module maps $h_n: C_n \to D_{n+1}$ such that $f_n - g_n = \partial_{n+1}^D \circ h_n + h_{n-1} \circ \partial_n^C$ for every $n$. The collection $\{h_n\}$ is called a chain homotopy from $f$ to $g$. The key theorem is immediate from the definition: chain homotopic maps induce the same map on every homology group. The proof is a one-line computation — if $\partial x = 0$, then $f(x) - g(x) = \partial h(x) + h(\partial x) = \partial(h(x))$, which is a boundary — but the consequence is fundamental: homology is invariant under chain homotopy, not just under chain isomorphism.

A chain map $f: C_\bullet \to D_\bullet$ is a chain homotopy equivalence if there exists a chain map $g: D_\bullet \to C_\bullet$ such that $g \circ f$ is chain homotopic to the identity on $C_\bullet$ and $f \circ g$ is chain homotopic to the identity on $D_\bullet$. Two complexes related by such a map are called chain homotopy equivalent, and they have isomorphic homology groups in every degree. The relationship between chain homotopy equivalence and quasi-isomorphism — a chain map inducing isomorphisms on all homology groups — is subtle. Every chain homotopy equivalence is a quasi-isomorphism, but the converse fails in general: a quasi-isomorphism need not have an inverse up to chain homotopy. This gap is precisely what the derived category is designed to close, by formally inverting quasi-isomorphisms, but the study of that construction belongs to a more advanced chapter.

The homotopy category $\mathbf{K}(R)$ is the category of chain complexes over $R$ with morphisms being chain homotopy classes of chain maps. This category is no longer abelian — the failure of the abelian structure in $\mathbf{K}(R)$ is exactly the kind of defect that the derived category repairs — but it is triangulated, and triangulated categories carry their own rich structure. For immediate purposes, the practical payoff of chain homotopy is the Comparison Theorem: any two projective resolutions of the same module are chain homotopy equivalent, which means the homology of a complex constructed from a projective resolution is independent of the resolution chosen. This independence is the cornerstone of the theory of derived functors developed in Chapter 40.
