# Chapter 38 — Chain Complexes and Homology

The central insight of homological algebra is that topology can be encoded in algebra. A topological space, or more generally a module, can be probed by constructing a sequence of abelian groups connected by maps that square to zero — a chain complex — and then measuring the degree to which that squaring-to-zero condition is not "used up." The homology groups that result are invariants: they do not change under deformations, and they detect holes, obstructions, and structural features that no single map or module could see on its own. This chapter builds the formalism from the ground up.

A chain complex $C_\bullet$ is a sequence $\cdots \to C_{n+1} \xrightarrow{\partial_{n+1}} C_n \xrightarrow{\partial_n} C_{n-1} \to \cdots$ of $R$-modules and homomorphisms satisfying $\partial_n \circ \partial_{n+1} = 0$ for all $n$. That single relation — "the boundary of a boundary is zero" — is both elementary and profound. It means that the image of $\partial_{n+1}$ is contained in the kernel of $\partial_n$, so one can form the quotient $H_n(C_\bullet) = \ker \partial_n / \operatorname{im} \partial_{n+1}$. Elements of the kernel are called cycles and elements of the image are called boundaries; homology measures the cycles that are not boundaries, the "independent loops" at each degree. In topological examples, these correspond precisely to the genuine $n$-dimensional holes in a space — and Poincaré's original Betti numbers are exactly the ranks of these groups.

The morphisms between chain complexes — chain maps — are the natural structure-preserving maps: they are collections of module homomorphisms that commute with the boundary operators. A chain map $f: C_\bullet \to D_\bullet$ induces, functorially, maps $f_*: H_n(C_\bullet) \to H_n(D_\bullet)$ on every homology group. This functoriality is indispensable: it means that homology is not just a collection of groups attached to a single complex but a genuine functor from the category of chain complexes to the category of graded modules. Short exact sequences of chain complexes yield, via the snake lemma, long exact sequences in homology — one of the most powerful tools in the subject.

Chain homotopy provides the correct notion of equivalence. Two chain maps $f$ and $g$ from $C_\bullet$ to $D_\bullet$ are chain homotopic if there exist maps $h_n: C_n \to D_{n+1}$ satisfying $f_n - g_n = \partial_{n+1}^D \circ h_n + h_{n-1} \circ \partial_n^C$. Chain homotopic maps induce identical maps on homology, and a chain homotopy equivalence — a chain map with a homotopy inverse — induces isomorphisms on all homology groups, even though it may not be an isomorphism of complexes term by term. This is the algebraic shadow of the topological fact that homotopy equivalent spaces have the same homology groups. The homotopy category of chain complexes, obtained by identifying chain homotopic maps, is the first step toward the derived category, which will appear much later in the story.

The cochain variant, where the differential raises degree — $C^{n-1} \xrightarrow{d^{n-1}} C^n \xrightarrow{d^n} C^{n+1}$ — produces cohomology groups $H^n = \ker d^n / \operatorname{im} d^{n-1}$. Cohomology carries additional structure: the cup product makes $H^*(C^\bullet)$ into a graded ring, a feature that homology does not automatically possess. The interplay between homology and cohomology, unified by the Universal Coefficient Theorem, is one of the recurring themes of the chapters ahead.
