# 39.1.1 Definition: Exact Complex of Projective Modules Mapping to $M$

The concept of a projective resolution arises from a natural question: how can one study an arbitrary module $M$ using modules with better properties? Free modules are the simplest possible$R$-modules — they have a basis, every element is uniquely expressible as a finite linear combination, and they satisfy the universal mapping property that makes all diagrams easy. Projective modules share the key lifting property of free modules while being somewhat more general. The strategy of replacing$M$ by a long exact sequence of projective modules — a projective resolution — allows homological invariants of$M$ to be computed even when$M$ itself is complicated or lacks an obvious structure.

The key property of projective modules that drives everything is the lifting property: if $P$ is projective,$N \twoheadrightarrow M$ is surjective, and$f: P \to M$ is any map, then$f$ lifts to a map$\tilde{f}: P \to N$ with$\pi \circ \tilde{f} = f$. This property is exactly what is needed to build chain maps between resolutions inductively (the Comparison Theorem, Section 39.1.3) and to ensure that derived functor computations are independent of the choice of resolution.

\begin{definition}
A \emph{projective resolution} of an $R$-module$M$ is an exact complex
$$\cdots \to P_2 \xrightarrow{d_2} P_1 \xrightarrow{d_1} P_0 \xrightarrow{\varepsilon} M \to 0$$
in which each $P_n$ is a projective$R$-module. The map$\varepsilon: P_0 \to M$ is the \emph{augmentation map}. Exactness means:$\varepsilon$ is surjective,$\ker(\varepsilon) = \mathrm{im}(d_1)$, and$\ker(d_n) = \mathrm{im}(d_{n+1})$ for all$n \geq 1$.
\end{definition}

The phrase "builds $M$ from projective pieces" captures the spirit of the construction. One first approximates$M$ from above by a projective$P_0$, then builds a projective approximation to the error term$\ker(\varepsilon)$, then a projective approximation to the new error, and so on. The resolution is typically written without the module$M$, as a complex$P_\bullet$ together with an augmentation; the module$M$ is recovered as$H_0(P_\bullet^{\mathrm{aug}}) = P_0 / \mathrm{im}(d_1)$.

It is important to understand what "exact" means at each position. At $P_0$:$\ker(\varepsilon) = \mathrm{im}(d_1)$, meaning every element of$P_0$ that maps to zero in$M$ is in the image of$d_1$. At$P_n$ for$n \geq 1$:$\ker(d_n) = \mathrm{im}(d_{n+1})$, meaning the only elements of$P_n$ that map to zero under$d_n$ are exactly those coming from$P_{n+1}$. Taken together, these conditions say that the augmented complex$P_\bullet \to M \to 0$ is exact everywhere. The module$M$ never actually appears as one of the$P_n$; it appears only at the end as the cokernel of$d_1$.

\begin{example}
The simplest projective resolution is for $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ over$\mathbb{Z}$. The standard free resolution is
$$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times n} \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\pi} \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \to 0,$$
where $\pi$ is the quotient map and the differential is multiplication by$n$. Exactness:$\pi$ is surjective;$\ker(\pi) = n\mathbb{Z} = \mathrm{im}(\times n)$; and$\ker(\times n) = 0 = \mathrm{im}(0)$ (nothing maps into the copy of$\mathbb{Z}$ in degree$1$). This is a projective (in fact free) resolution of length$1$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
The $\mathbb{Z}$-module$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ has the resolution
$$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 6} \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \to 0.$$
More interestingly, $\mathbb{Z}/2 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2$ has the resolution
$$0 \to \mathbb{Z}^2 \xrightarrow{\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}} \mathbb{Z}^2 \to \mathbb{Z}/2 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2 \to 0,$$
illustrating that a direct sum of modules can be resolved by a direct sum of individual resolutions.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Over the polynomial ring $k[x]$ for a field$k$, the module$k = k[x]/(x)$ (with$x$ acting as zero) has the resolution
$$0 \to k[x] \xrightarrow{\times x} k[x] \to k \to 0.$$
This has the same form as the $\mathbb{Z}/n$ resolution with$n = x$, reflecting the general principle that for a PID, every cyclic module has a free resolution of length$1$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For a non-PID example, the residue field $k = k[x,y]/(x,y)$ over the polynomial ring$k[x,y]$ has the Koszul resolution
$$0 \to k[x,y] \xrightarrow{\binom{-y}{x}} k[x,y]^2 \xrightarrow{(x\ y)} k[x,y] \to k \to 0.$$
This is a free resolution of length $2$, reflecting the fact that$k[x,y]$ has global dimension$2$ (Hilbert's Syzygy Theorem). Applying$\mathrm{Hom}_{k[x,y]}(-, k)$ to this resolution gives all$\mathrm{Ext}^*_{k[x,y]}(k, k)$, which turns out to be an exterior algebra.
\end{example}

The length of the resolution, if it terminates, is a measure of how "far from projective" the module $M$ is. A module$M$ is projective if and only if it has a resolution of length$0$ (it resolves itself). As the global dimension of the ring$R$ increases, modules can require resolutions of greater length, and the computation of homological invariants becomes correspondingly more elaborate. The fundamental insight is that the choice of projective resolution does not matter — any two resolutions give chain-homotopy-equivalent complexes after applying any additive functor — and this is what makes projective resolutions into a reliable computational tool.
