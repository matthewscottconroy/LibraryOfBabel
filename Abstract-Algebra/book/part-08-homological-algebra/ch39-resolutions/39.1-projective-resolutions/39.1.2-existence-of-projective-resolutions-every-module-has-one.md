# 39.1.2 Existence of Projective Resolutions: Every Module Has One

Before one can use projective resolutions as a computational tool, one must know they exist. The existence theorem is reassuringly universal: every $R$-module$M$ has a projective resolution, regardless of the ring$R$ or the structure of$M$. The proof is constructive, proceeding inductively by a greedy algorithm that resolves one "layer" of$M$ at a time.

The construction begins by choosing a surjection $\varepsilon: P_0 \twoheadrightarrow M$ where$P_0$ is a free (hence projective)$R$-module. Since every module is a quotient of a free module — take the free module on its underlying set, or on any generating set — this first step is always possible. Set$M_1 = \ker \varepsilon \subseteq P_0$. Now apply the same argument to$M_1$: choose a surjection$d_1: P_1 \twoheadrightarrow M_1$ with$P_1$ free. Set$M_2 = \ker(P_1 \to M_1)$ and continue. At each stage one has a free module$P_n$ surjecting onto the kernel$M_n$ of the previous map; the kernel$M_{n+1} = \ker(P_n \to M_n)$ is the raw material for the next stage. The resulting complex$\cdots \to P_2 \to P_1 \to P_0 \to M \to 0$ is exact at every term by construction: exactness at$P_n$ means$\ker(P_n \to P_{n-1}) = \mathrm{im}(P_{n+1} \to P_n)$, which holds because$d_n: P_n \to M_n$ is surjective with kernel$M_{n+1}$ and the next map$P_{n+1} \to P_n$ has image exactly$M_{n+1}$.

The resolution produced by this procedure is in fact a *free* resolution, since each $P_n$ is chosen to be free. Free resolutions are projective resolutions in which the stronger condition (freeness) replaces projectivity; they always exist and are often the resolutions one computes in practice. The resolution may be infinite: the process generates a new kernel$M_{n+1}$ at each step, and there is no guarantee that$M_n = 0$ for large$n$. Whether the resolution can be taken to be finite is a delicate question about the ring$R$ and the module$M$, addressed by the notion of projective dimension in Section 39.1.4.

One important refinement: if $M$ is finitely generated over a Noetherian ring$R$, one can always arrange that each$P_n$ is finitely generated. The key observation is that if$M$ is finitely generated and$R$ is Noetherian, then any submodule of a finitely generated free$R$-module is again finitely generated (since$R$ is Noetherian, submodules of finitely generated modules are finitely generated). Consequently, each syzygy module$M_{n+1} = \ker(P_n \to M_n)$ is finitely generated, and one can take$P_{n+1}$ to be the free module on a finite generating set of$M_{n+1}$. The construction therefore remains within the category of finitely generated modules at every step.

It is also worth understanding what freedom one has in the choice of resolution. The choice of surjection $P_0 \twoheadrightarrow M$ is not unique: one can choose a minimal generating set for$M$ or a larger one. Different choices yield different resolutions, all of which are projective resolutions of$M$. The Comparison Theorem (Section 39.1.3) will show that any two projective resolutions of$M$ are connected by a chain homotopy equivalence, so the choice does not affect any homological invariant derived from the resolution. In practice, one tries to make the resolution as small as possible — using minimal generating sets at each step, or exploiting special structure of the ring — in order to simplify computations.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Let $R = \mathbb{Z}$ and$M = \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$. The module$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ is generated by$1$, so take$P_0 = \mathbb{Z}$ with surjection$\varepsilon: n \mapsto \bar{n}$. The kernel is$M_1 = 6\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}$. Since$M_1$ is free, take$P_1 = \mathbb{Z}$ with surjection$d_1: 1 \mapsto 6$. The kernel of$d_1$ is$0$, so the resolution terminates:
$$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 6} \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\pi} \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \to 0.$$
This is a length-1 free resolution, reflecting the fact that $\mathbb{Z}$ has global dimension$1$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Let $R = \mathbb{Z}$ and$M = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$. Since$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ by the Chinese Remainder Theorem, the same length-1 resolution applies. Alternatively, working directly: take$P_0 = \mathbb{Z}^2$ surjecting onto$M$ by$(a, b) \mapsto (\bar{a}, \bar{b})$ with kernel generated by$(2, 0)$ and$(0, 3)$. Thus$M_1 = 2\mathbb{Z} \oplus 3\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}^2$ is free, giving a length-1 resolution
$$0 \to \mathbb{Z}^2 \xrightarrow{\begin{pmatrix}2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3\end{pmatrix}} \mathbb{Z}^2 \to M \to 0.$$
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Let $R = \mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^2)$ (the dual numbers) and$M = R/(x) \cong \mathbb{Z}$. Take$P_0 = R$ surjecting onto$M$ via$f(x) \mapsto f(0)$. The kernel is$(x) = xR$. Now$(x) \cong R/(x^2/x) = R/(x) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ as abelian groups, but as an$R$-module:$xR \cong R/\mathrm{Ann}(x) = R/(x)$ since$x \cdot x = x^2 = 0$ in$R$. Thus the kernel of$P_0 \to M$ is again isomorphic to$M$ as an$R$-module, giving a surjection$P_1 = R \to xR$, with kernel$xR$ again. The resolution is infinite:
$$\cdots \to R \xrightarrow{\times x} R \xrightarrow{\times x} R \to M \to 0.$$
This illustrates that the greedy construction need not terminate; $M = \mathbb{Z}$ has infinite projective dimension over$R = \mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^2)$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** Let $R = k[x, y]$ (polynomial ring over a field$k$) and$M = k = R/(x, y)$ (the residue field). To construct a free resolution, take$P_0 = R$ with augmentation$f \mapsto f(0, 0)$; the kernel is the ideal$(x, y)$. The ideal$(x, y)$ requires two generators, so take$P_1 = R^2$ with map$(f, g) \mapsto fx + gy$; the kernel of this map is generated by the single syzygy$(y, -x)$ (one checks$yx - xy = 0$). Thus$P_2 = R$ with map$1 \mapsto (y, -x)$, and the kernel is$0$. The resulting length-2 Koszul resolution is:
$$0 \to R \xrightarrow{\begin{pmatrix} y \\ -x \end{pmatrix}} R^2 \xrightarrow{\begin{pmatrix} x & y \end{pmatrix}} R \to k \to 0.$$
The fact that the resolution terminates at length 2 reflects Hilbert's Syzygy Theorem: $k[x, y]$ has global dimension 2.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
The existence proof constructs resolutions in the category of $R$-modules using only the universal property of free modules. It works without any assumption on the ring — commutative or not, Noetherian or not — which is why every module has a projective resolution. The same argument (dualizing) shows every module has an injective resolution (Section 39.2.2), once one knows that injective hulls exist; but injective hulls require Zorn's Lemma in an essential way, whereas the projective case only needs the axiom of choice to choose a surjection$P_0 \twoheadrightarrow M$.
\end{remark}
