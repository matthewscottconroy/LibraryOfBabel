# 39.1.4 Projective Dimension: The Length of the Shortest Projective Resolution

Not every module requires an infinitely long projective resolution. When a module $M$ has a finite projective resolution, the minimum length of such a resolution is a numerical invariant that encodes how far$M$ is from being projective itself. This invariant, the projective dimension, connects the homological structure of individual modules to global properties of the ring.

The *projective dimension* $\mathrm{pd}(M)$ of an$R$-module$M$ is the smallest$n \geq 0$ such that$M$ admits a projective resolution of the form$0 \to P_n \to \cdots \to P_1 \to P_0 \to M \to 0$, with$P_k = 0$ for$k > n$. If no such finite resolution exists, one sets$\mathrm{pd}(M) = \infty$. A module has$\mathrm{pd}(M) = 0$ if and only if$M$ itself is projective, since then$0 \to M \to M \to 0$ is a length-zero resolution. More generally,$\mathrm{pd}(M) \leq n$ if and only if$\mathrm{Ext}^{n+1}_R(M, N) = 0$ for all$R$-modules$N$; this characterization, which will be established once derived functors are in hand, shows that projective dimension measures the "reach" of$M$ in the Ext groups.

There is a useful reformulation in terms of syzygies. If $0 \to K \to P_{n-1} \to \cdots \to P_0 \to M \to 0$ is a partial free resolution of length$n$, then$\mathrm{pd}(M) \leq n$ if and only if the$n$th syzygy module$K = \Omega^n(M)$ is projective. Thus checking whether a module has projective dimension$\leq n$ reduces to checking projectivity of the$n$th syzygy — a condition that can sometimes be verified by more elementary means.

The *global dimension* of $R$ is the supremum$\mathrm{gl.dim}(R) = \sup_M \mathrm{pd}(M)$ over all$R$-modules$M$. Global dimension is a measure of how complicated the module category of$R$ is. A field has global dimension$0$: every module over a field is free, hence projective. The ring$\mathbb{Z}$ has global dimension$1$: every submodule of a free abelian group is free (since$\mathbb{Z}$ is a PID and submodules of free modules over a PID are free), so every$\mathbb{Z}$-module has a free resolution of length at most$1$. The polynomial ring$F[x]$ likewise has global dimension$1$ for any field$F$, by the same argument. Polynomial rings in more variables have finite global dimension controlled by the number of variables, as Hilbert's Syzygy Theorem (Section 39.3.3) makes precise.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** $\mathrm{pd}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}) = 1$ for$n \geq 2$. The resolution$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times n} \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \to 0$ shows$\mathrm{pd} \leq 1$. Since$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is not projective (it is not free and cannot be a direct summand of a free$\mathbb{Z}$-module, as any direct summand of a free$\mathbb{Z}$-module is torsion-free),$\mathrm{pd} \geq 1$. Thus$\mathrm{pd}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}) = 1$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Over $R = k[x,y]$, the residue field$k = R/(x,y)$ has projective dimension$2$. The Koszul resolution
$$0 \to R \xrightarrow{\begin{pmatrix} y \\ -x \end{pmatrix}} R^2 \xrightarrow{\begin{pmatrix} x & y \end{pmatrix}} R \to k \to 0$$
is a length-2 free resolution. Since $\mathrm{Ext}^2_{k[x,y]}(k, k) \cong k \neq 0$ (computable from this resolution), we have$\mathrm{pd}(k) = 2$. This is consistent with Hilbert's Syzygy Theorem:$\mathrm{gl.dim}(k[x,y]) = 2$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Over $R = \mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^2)$, consider$M = \mathbb{Z}$ with trivial$x$-action ($x \cdot m = 0$). As computed in Example 3 of Section 39.1.2, the resolution is
$$\cdots \to R \xrightarrow{\times x} R \xrightarrow{\times x} R \to \mathbb{Z} \to 0,$$
which is infinite. The $n$th syzygy module is always$xR \cong \mathbb{Z}$, never projective over$R$ (since$xR$ has the same form as$M$). Thus$\mathrm{pd}_R(\mathbb{Z}) = \infty$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** A direct sum $M = A \oplus B$ satisfies$\mathrm{pd}(A \oplus B) = \max(\mathrm{pd}(A), \mathrm{pd}(B))$. To see this, take projective resolutions$P_\bullet \to A$ and$Q_\bullet \to B$; their direct sum$P_\bullet \oplus Q_\bullet$ is a projective resolution of$A \oplus B$ of length$\max(\mathrm{pd}(A), \mathrm{pd}(B))$. For example,$\mathrm{pd}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}) = \max(1, 1) = 1$.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}
For any short exact sequence $0 \to A \to B \to C \to 0$ of$R$-modules, the following hold:
\begin{align*}
\mathrm{pd}(C) &\leq \max(\mathrm{pd}(A), \mathrm{pd}(B)) + 1, \\
\mathrm{pd}(B) &\leq \max(\mathrm{pd}(A), \mathrm{pd}(C)), \\
\mathrm{pd}(A) &\leq \max(\mathrm{pd}(B), \mathrm{pd}(C) - 1).
\end{align*}
These inequalities — and the Horseshoe Lemma which makes them precise — allow one to bound projective dimensions inductively from a short exact sequence.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}
The connection between projective dimension and Ext groups is not merely a coincidence: it is the defining feature of Ext as a derived functor. Specifically, $\mathrm{pd}(M) = \sup\{n : \mathrm{Ext}^n_R(M, N) \neq 0 \text{ for some } N\}$. The global dimension$\mathrm{gl.dim}(R)$ can therefore be computed either from projective dimensions or from the vanishing of Ext groups, and these two viewpoints are exactly dual: the former is a property of the left variable of$\mathrm{Ext}$, the latter of the right. The equality$\mathrm{gl.dim}(R) = \sup_M \mathrm{pd}(M) = \sup_M \mathrm{id}(M)$ (where$\mathrm{id}$ is injective dimension, Section 39.2.3) is a deep theorem that will follow from the balancing of Ext.
\end{remark}
