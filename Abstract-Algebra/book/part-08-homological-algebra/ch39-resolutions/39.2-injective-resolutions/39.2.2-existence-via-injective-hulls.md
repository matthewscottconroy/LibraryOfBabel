# 39.2.2 Existence via Injective Hulls

The existence of injective resolutions is guaranteed by a theorem in the same spirit as the existence of projective resolutions, but with an important difference: while free modules are abundant and easily constructed, injective modules are harder to get one's hands on. Their existence rests on the Baer criterion and an application of Zorn's Lemma, which together produce the *injective hull* of any module.

The Baer criterion is the fundamental tool. It states that an $R$-module$I$ is injective if and only if every$R$-linear map from an ideal$\mathfrak{a} \leq R$ to$I$ extends to a map from all of$R$ to$I$. In other words, it suffices to check extension just for ideals, not for all submodules of all free modules. This dramatic reduction in the verification requirement makes it feasible to construct injective modules: one can verify injectivity by checking a condition on the ring structure alone.

The *injective hull* $E(M)$ of an$R$-module$M$ is an injective module containing$M$ as an essential submodule — that is, every nonzero submodule of$E(M)$ has nonzero intersection with$M$. The essentiality condition means$M$ is "large" inside$E(M)$: there is no room to enlarge$E(M)$ further while keeping$M$ essential. The injective hull exists and is unique up to isomorphism; its construction proceeds by Zorn's Lemma: one considers the poset of all essential extensions of$M$ (submodules of a large enough injective module that contain$M$ as an essential submodule), partially ordered by inclusion, shows that every chain has an upper bound, and takes the maximal element. The Baer criterion then verifies that this maximal essential extension is itself injective.

With injective hulls in hand, the construction of an injective resolution follows the same greedy inductive pattern as for projective resolutions. Set $I^0 = E(M)$ and let$\eta: M \to I^0$ be the canonical embedding. Let$M^1 = I^0 / M$ (the first cokernel) and set$I^1 = E(M^1)$; the quotient map$I^0 \to M^1$ composed with the embedding$M^1 \to I^1$ gives$d^0: I^0 \to I^1$. Continue: at each step, take the cokernel$M^{n+1} = I^n / \ker(d^n)$ of the previous map and embed it into its injective hull$I^{n+1} = E(M^{n+1})$. The resulting complex$0 \to M \to I^0 \to I^1 \to I^2 \to \cdots$ is exact at every term by construction. The injective dimension$\mathrm{id}(M)$ is the length of the shortest injective resolution of$M$, in direct analogy with projective dimension.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Over $R = \mathbb{Z}$, the injective hull of$\mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$ is the Prüfer group$\mathbb{Z}(p^\infty) = \varinjlim \mathbb{Z}/p^k\mathbb{Z}$. To see this:$\mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$ embeds into$\mathbb{Z}(p^\infty)$ via$\bar{1} \mapsto 1/p^n \pmod{\mathbb{Z}}$, and$\mathbb{Z}(p^\infty)$ is divisible hence injective. The embedding is essential because every nonzero element of$\mathbb{Z}(p^\infty)$ of the form$a/p^k$ (with$\gcd(a, p) = 1$) generates a cyclic subgroup$\cong \mathbb{Z}/p^k\mathbb{Z}$, which intersects$\mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$ nontrivially for$k \leq n$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Over $R = \mathbb{Z}$, the injective hull of$\mathbb{Z}$ is$\mathbb{Q}$. The integers embed essentially into$\mathbb{Q}$: every nonzero subgroup of$\mathbb{Q}$ contains a nonzero rational$a/b$, and$ba/b = a \neq 0$ lies in$\mathbb{Z}$. Since$\mathbb{Q}$ is a divisible abelian group, it is injective. The injective resolution is
$$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z} \to 0.$$
Here $\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z} \cong \bigoplus_p \mathbb{Z}(p^\infty)$ is the direct sum of all Prüfer groups, which is again injective (direct sums of injective modules over any ring are injective, by the Baer criterion applied termwise). Thus$\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}) = 1$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Over $R = k[x]/(x^2)$, the residue field$k = R/(x)$ has injective hull$E(k) = R$ itself (the ring is a local Frobenius algebra, so the injective hull of the residue field is the free module of rank 1). The injective resolution of$k$ is then$0 \to k \to R \xrightarrow{x} R \xrightarrow{x} R \to \cdots$ (infinite and periodic), analogous to the projective resolution being infinite in Example 3 of Section 39.1.2.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** For a finite-dimensional $k$-algebra$A$ that is not self-injective, the injective hull of a simple module can be computed via the socle filtration. For instance, with$A = k[x]/(x^3)$, the simple module$k = A/(x)$ has injective hull$A$ itself (again by the Frobenius property), and the injective resolution is periodic of period 2.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
The role of Zorn's Lemma in constructing injective hulls is unavoidable: the statement "every module has an injective resolution" is actually equivalent (over ZF set theory) to the axiom of choice. This contrasts with the projective case, where one only needs the weaker axiom that every set has a choice function (also equivalent to the axiom of choice, but the projective construction is more direct). The key distinction is that free modules are explicit and computable, while injective modules are large objects whose construction fundamentally uses set-theoretic maximality.
\end{remark}
