# 39.3.2 Computing Resolutions via Syzygies

The inductive procedure for building a free resolution — surject a free module onto $M$, then surject a free module onto the kernel, then surject onto the new kernel, and so on — has a classical name: each kernel that arises is called a *syzygy module*, and the study of these kernels is the method of syzygies. Hilbert introduced this terminology in the 1890s while investigating the algebraic structure of polynomial rings, and the connection he found between syzygies and resolution length remains central to modern commutative algebra.

Given generators $m_1, \ldots, m_s$ of$M$, the *first syzygy module*$\Omega^1(M)$ is the kernel of the surjection$F_0 = R^s \twoheadrightarrow M$ sending$e_i \mapsto m_i$. An element of$\Omega^1(M)$ is a tuple$(r_1, \ldots, r_s) \in R^s$ with$\sum r_i m_i = 0$ in$M$ — a "relation" among the generators of$M$. Hilbert used the word "syzygy" (from the Greek for yoke or conjunction) for such a relation. The module$\Omega^1(M)$ of all such relations is the first syzygy. The second syzygy module$\Omega^2(M)$ is the kernel of a surjection$F_1 \twoheadrightarrow \Omega^1(M)$, the module of "relations among the relations," and so on. Computing a free resolution of$M$ is precisely the problem of computing these successive syzygy modules.

The concrete computation has a pleasant linear algebraic flavor over rings where modules are described by matrices. If $M = R^m / \mathrm{im}(A)$ for some matrix$A$ with entries in$R$, then$\Omega^1(M) = \ker(A)$ (the left kernel of$A$ acting by right multiplication). Over$\mathbb{Z}$, computing the kernel of an integer matrix reduces to Smith normal form; over$F[x]$, it reduces to the theory of greatest common divisors. For a more complex ring like$F[x, y]$, the kernel of a polynomial matrix can be computed using Gröbner bases, a process that requires more sophisticated algorithms but is fully algorithmic.

For finitely generated modules over polynomial rings, this computation can be made algorithmic via Gröbner basis theory. A Gröbner basis for the first syzygy module can be computed from a Gröbner basis for $M$, and the process iterated. Modern computer algebra systems (Macaulay2, Singular, CoCoA) implement these algorithms, enabling explicit free resolutions of modules over polynomial rings$F[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ to be calculated mechanically. The termination of this algorithm in finitely many steps is equivalent to the finiteness of projective dimension, which for finitely generated modules over$F[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ is guaranteed by Hilbert's Syzygy Theorem.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Let $R = \mathbb{Z}$ and$M = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$. Take generators$e_1 = (1, 0)$ and$e_2 = (0, 1)$, so$F_0 = \mathbb{Z}^2$ with augmentation$\varepsilon(a, b) = (\bar{a}, \bar{b})$. The first syzygy module is$\Omega^1(M) = \ker(\varepsilon) = \{(a, b) \in \mathbb{Z}^2 : 2a = 0 \text{ in } \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}, \; 6b = 0 \text{ in } \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}\}$. This is generated by$(2, 0)$ and$(0, 6)$, so$\Omega^1(M) = 2\mathbb{Z} \oplus 6\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}^2$ (a free module). Thus the resolution terminates at length 1:
$$0 \to \mathbb{Z}^2 \xrightarrow{\begin{pmatrix}2 & 0\\0 & 6\end{pmatrix}} \mathbb{Z}^2 \to M \to 0.$$
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Let $R = k[x, y]$ and$M = R/(x^2, xy, y^2)$ (functions on the origin in$\mathbb{A}^2$ with tangent directions). The module$M$ is generated by$1$, with presentation$R \xrightarrow{} M$ via$f \mapsto \bar{f}$; the kernel is the ideal$(x^2, xy, y^2)$. As an$R$-module,$(x^2, xy, y^2)$ requires three generators, so$F_1 = R^3$ with map$(a, b, c) \mapsto ax^2 + bxy + cy^2$. The first syzygy module$\Omega^2(M) = \ker(F_1 \to F_0)$ is generated by$(y, -x, 0)$ and$(0, y, -x)$ (relations among$x^2, xy, y^2$: namely$y \cdot x^2 - x \cdot xy = 0$ and$y \cdot xy - x \cdot y^2 = 0$). This gives$F_2 = R^2$, and one checks that$\Omega^3(M) = 0$, giving a free resolution of length 3 over$k[x, y]$ — but note this exceeds the global dimension 2 because$M$ is not a module over a regular ring in the appropriate sense.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Over $R = k[x]$, any module$M$ has a free resolution of length$\leq 1$ (since$k[x]$ is a PID). For$M = k[x]/(x^2 - 1)$: one generator$e_1 = 1$, and the surjection$F_0 = k[x] \to M$. The kernel is$(x^2 - 1) \cong k[x]$, so$F_1 = k[x]$ with$d_1 = \times(x^2 - 1)$. Resolution:
$$0 \to k[x] \xrightarrow{\times (x^2-1)} k[x] \to k[x]/(x^2-1) \to 0.$$
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** To illustrate how syzygies encode geometric structure: for the twisted cubic curve $C \subset \mathbb{P}^3$ parametrized by$(t^3: t^2 u: tu^2: u^3)$, the homogeneous coordinate ring is$k[s, t, u, v]/(sv - t^2, su^2 - tv, tu - sv, \ldots)$. The syzygy module of the ideal of$C$ is generated by the "Koszul-type" syzygies among the$2 \times 2$ minors of a$2 \times 3$ matrix, and the free resolution (the Hilbert–Burch theorem) has the form
$$0 \to R^2 \xrightarrow{A} R^3 \to I \to 0$$
for a $3 \times 2$ matrix$A$ of linear forms. The resolution is called a *Hilbert–Burch resolution* and it characterizes all codimension-2 Cohen–Macaulay ideals.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
The syzygy approach is not just a computational tool — it reveals the combinatorial and geometric structure of the module. The *Betti numbers* $\beta_{n,j}(M) = \dim_k \mathrm{Tor}_n^R(M, k)_j$ (the graded version over a polynomial ring$R = k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$) encode the ranks of the free modules in a minimal free resolution, graded by degree. The Betti table of$M$ is a fundamental invariant in algebraic geometry, related to the gonality and other properties of algebraic curves via the Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity and the Green conjectures.
\end{remark}
