# 39.3.3 Hilbert's Syzygy Theorem: $F[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ Has Global Dimension$n$

Hilbert's Syzygy Theorem is one of the landmark results of nineteenth-century algebra, and it reads as a theorem about homological algebra avant la lettre. Published in 1890 as part of Hilbert's revolutionary proof of the finite basis theorem, it asserts that the "chains of syzygies" over a polynomial ring must terminate — a statement that today we recognize as a theorem about global dimension. Understanding it in modern terms illuminates both the power of the homological framework and the depth of Hilbert's original insight.

\begin{theorem}[Hilbert's Syzygy Theorem]
Let $F$ be a field and$R = F[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$. Then$\mathrm{gl.dim}(R) = n$: every finitely generated$R$-module$M$ has a free resolution of length at most$n$, and there exist modules (such as the residue field$F = R/(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$) whose shortest free resolution has length exactly$n$.
\end{theorem}

The proof proceeds by induction on $n$: for$n = 1$, the polynomial ring$F[x]$ is a PID, and every submodule of a free$F[x]$-module is free, giving global dimension$1$. For the inductive step, one uses the fact that$F[x_1, \ldots, x_n] = F[x_1, \ldots, x_{n-1}][x_n]$ and appeals to a key lemma: if$R$ has global dimension$d$ and$x$ is a non-zerodivisor in$R$, then$R[x]$ (or equivalently$R$ with one additional variable) has global dimension$\leq d + 1$. The$n$th syzygy module of any module over$R$ is free (not merely projective), which is what enables the induction.

The sharpness of the bound $n$ is demonstrated by the Koszul complex. The Koszul complex$K_\bullet(x_1, \ldots, x_n; R)$ is a complex of free$R$-modules:
$$0 \to R \to R^n \to \binom{n}{2} R \to \cdots \to R^n \to R \to F \to 0$$
(with ranks given by binomial coefficients $\binom{n}{k}$) whose differentials are the natural exterior algebra maps. This complex is a free resolution of$F = R/(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ of length exactly$n$. The fact that$\mathrm{Ext}^n_R(F, F) \cong F \neq 0$ (computable from the Koszul complex) proves that the global dimension is exactly$n$, not less.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** $n = 1$:$F[x]$, global dimension 1. The Koszul complex is$0 \to F[x] \xrightarrow{\times x} F[x] \to F \to 0$, a length-1 resolution.$\mathrm{Ext}^1_{F[x]}(F, F) \cong F$ confirms the sharp bound.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** $n = 2$:$F[x, y]$, global dimension 2. The Koszul complex is
$$0 \to F[x,y] \xrightarrow{\begin{pmatrix} y \\ -x \end{pmatrix}} F[x,y]^2 \xrightarrow{\begin{pmatrix} x & y \end{pmatrix}} F[x,y] \to F \to 0.$$
Applying $\mathrm{Hom}_{F[x,y]}(-, F)$ gives$0 \to F \xrightarrow{0} F^2 \xrightarrow{0} F \to 0$, so$\mathrm{Ext}^0 = F$,$\mathrm{Ext}^1 = F^2$,$\mathrm{Ext}^2 = F$. The non-vanishing$\mathrm{Ext}^2 = F \neq 0$ confirms$\mathrm{gl.dim}(F[x,y]) \geq 2$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** $n = 3$:$F[x, y, z]$, global dimension 3. The Koszul complex has ranks$1, 3, 3, 1$ (i.e.,$\binom{3}{0}, \binom{3}{1}, \binom{3}{2}, \binom{3}{3}$). The differential$d_1: R^3 \to R$ is$(x, y, z)$, the differential$d_2: R^3 \to R^3$ is the matrix of$2 \times 2$ minors of the row vector$(x, y, z)$:
$$d_2 = \begin{pmatrix} y & z & 0 \\ -x & 0 & z \\ 0 & -x & -y \end{pmatrix},$$
and $d_3: R \to R^3$ sends$1 \mapsto (z, -y, x)^T$. Applying$\mathrm{Hom}(-, F)$ shows$\mathrm{Ext}^3 = F \neq 0$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** For a non-residue-field module: $M = F[x,y]/(x, y^2)$ over$R = F[x,y]$. A free resolution is:
$$0 \to R \xrightarrow{\begin{pmatrix} y^2 \\ -x \end{pmatrix}} R^2 \xrightarrow{\begin{pmatrix} x & y^2 \end{pmatrix}} R \to M \to 0$$
(one checks exactness: the kernel of $(x, y^2)$ is generated by$(y^2, -x)$ since$R$ is a domain, and the kernel of$\begin{pmatrix} y^2 \\ -x \end{pmatrix}$ is 0 since$f y^2 = 0$ and$fx = 0$ in$R$ implies$f = 0$). This length-2 resolution confirms$\mathrm{pd}_R(M) \leq 2$, consistent with$\mathrm{gl.dim}(F[x,y]) = 2$.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
Hilbert's Syzygy Theorem has a far-reaching generalization: a local Noetherian ring $R$ has finite global dimension if and only if it is *regular*, in which case$\mathrm{gl.dim}(R) = \dim(R)$. This is the Auslander–Buchsbaum–Serre theorem. The polynomial ring$F[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ is regular of Krull dimension$n$, which gives$\mathrm{gl.dim}(F[x_1, \ldots, x_n]) = n$ as a special case. The theorem thus places Hilbert's classical result in the broader context of regular rings and the relationship between commutative algebra and algebraic geometry: the smooth points of an algebraic variety correspond (locally) to regular local rings, which are precisely the rings with finite global dimension.
\end{remark}
