# Chapter 39 — Resolutions

Most modules arising in algebra and geometry are complicated, and there is rarely a direct route to understanding them. The strategy of resolutions is to replace a complicated module by a chain complex of simpler — "nicer" — modules that together capture all the same algebraic information. The niceness condition can take several forms: projective modules, injective modules, or free modules, each offering different technical advantages. The resulting resolution is an exact complex that maps onto (or out of) the given module, and the algebraic data encoded in this scaffolding turns out to be exactly what is needed to define and compute derived functors.

A projective resolution of a module $M$ is an exact sequence $\cdots \to P_2 \to P_1 \to P_0 \to M \to 0$ in which every $P_n$ is projective. Such resolutions always exist: since every module is a quotient of a free module, one builds the resolution inductively by taking free covers and iterating. The comparison theorem ensures that any two projective resolutions of $M$ are chain homotopy equivalent, so that any invariant constructed from a resolution — such as the derived functors Ext and Tor — is independent of the choice. The projective dimension $\operatorname{pd}(M)$ is the length of the shortest projective resolution, measuring in a precise sense how far $M$ is from being projective itself. A module of projective dimension zero is already projective.

Injective resolutions dualize the picture: one constructs an exact sequence $0 \to M \to I^0 \to I^1 \to I^2 \to \cdots$ with every $I^n$ injective. The existence of injective resolutions is less obvious than that of projective ones, and depends on the existence of injective hulls — a theorem due to Baer and Eckmann–Schopf. The injective dimension of $M$ measures the length of the shortest injective resolution. The global dimension of a ring $R$, defined as the supremum of projective dimensions of all $R$-modules (or equivalently of all injective dimensions), is one of the most important numerical invariants of $R$ itself.

Free resolutions form a special case of projective resolutions but deserve separate attention because of their combinatorial concreteness and their connection to commutative algebra. Hilbert's syzygy theorem — one of the first homological theorems ever proved, predating the general framework by sixty years — states that every finitely generated module over the polynomial ring $k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ has a free resolution of length at most $n$. The proof proceeds by analyzing the module of syzygies — the relations among generators — and showing that the syzygy sequence terminates. This theorem identifies the global dimension of $k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ as exactly $n$, making the ring's homological complexity mirror the geometric dimension of affine $n$-space. The Koszul complex, a canonical free resolution of the residue field $k$ over $k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$, makes this connection explicit and serves as the prototype for many more sophisticated constructions in commutative algebra and algebraic geometry.
