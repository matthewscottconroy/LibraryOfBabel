# 40.1.3 Independence of Resolution; Well-Definedness

The construction of derived functors would be vacuous if the resulting groups depended on which resolution was chosen. A module typically admits many different projective or injective resolutions — different free resolutions of $\mathbb{Z}/m$ as a$\mathbb{Z}$-module, for instance — and for$R^n F(M)$ and$L_n F(M)$ to be invariants of$M$, one must show that any two resolutions yield the same cohomology. This is the content of the Comparison Theorem, which is both conceptually crucial and a non-trivial piece of homological algebra in its own right.

The proof proceeds in two steps. Given two injective resolutions $I^\bullet$ and$J^\bullet$ of$M$, the Comparison Theorem first constructs a chain map$\phi: I^\bullet \to J^\bullet$ lifting the identity on$M$. The key property of injective modules is exploited here: since each$J^n$ is injective, one can extend partial maps inductively up the resolution. Concretely, at degree$0$: the composition$M \hookrightarrow I^0 \to I^0/M$ and the injection$M \hookrightarrow J^0$ allow one to extend$\mathrm{id}_M: M \to M$ through the injection$M \hookrightarrow J^0$ (by injectivity of$J^0$) to get$\phi_0: I^0 \to J^0$. At degree$1$:$\phi_0$ induces a map$I^0/M \to J^0/M$, and one extends through$J^0/M \hookrightarrow J^1$ using injectivity of$J^1$, and so on.

Next, one shows that any two such lifting chain maps are chain homotopic. If $\phi, \psi: I^\bullet \to J^\bullet$ both lift$\mathrm{id}_M$, then their difference$\phi - \psi$ lifts the zero map$0: M \to M$. One constructs a chain homotopy$h: I^\bullet \to J^{\bullet - 1}$ with$\phi - \psi = d^J h + h d^I$ by the same inductive argument, now using the injectivity of the$J^n$'s to lift the null-homotopy step by step. After applying$F$, one obtains$F(\phi) - F(\psi) = d^{F(J)} F(h) + F(h) d^{F(I)}$, which is a chain homotopy in$F(I^\bullet) \to F(J^\bullet)$. Chain homotopy equivalent complexes have the same cohomology (since$d \circ (dh + hd) = (dh + hd) \circ d$ and both sides give zero on cohomology), so$H^n(F(I^\bullet)) \cong H^n(F(J^\bullet))$.

The conceptual upshot is that the derived functors are canonical: they are genuine functors $\mathcal{A} \to \mathcal{B}$, not just constructions that depend on arbitrary choices. The chain homotopy category plays a central role — two chain maps that are chain homotopic become equal in the homotopy category, and derived functors are precisely the functors that can be defined as honest functors on that homotopy category. This perspective points ahead toward the derived category, where one inverts quasi-isomorphisms and derived functors acquire their most natural formulation.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** Two injective resolutions of $\mathbb{Z}$: the standard$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z} \to 0$, and a "redundant" one$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Q} \oplus \mathbb{Q}' \to \mathbb{Q}' \oplus \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z} \to 0$ (where$\mathbb{Q}' \cong \mathbb{Q}$ is a redundant injective summand inserted in the middle and then cancelled). A chain map from the redundant resolution to the standard one maps$\mathbb{Q} \oplus \mathbb{Q}'$ to$\mathbb{Q}$ by projecting onto the first factor; the chain homotopy witnesses the "cancellation" of the redundant$\mathbb{Q}'$. After applying$\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}/n, -)$, both resolutions give the same$\mathrm{Ext}^1_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}/n, \mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/n$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Two projective resolutions of $k = F[x]/(x)$ over$R = F[x]$: the standard$0 \to F[x] \xrightarrow{\times x} F[x] \to k \to 0$, and a "doubled" one using$F[x]^2$ at each level. The Comparison Theorem provides a chain map between them; it is unique up to homotopy. After applying$k \otimes_{F[x]} -$, one obtains$k \otimes F[x] = k$ at each level in the standard resolution and$k^2$ at each level in the doubled one. The two chain complexes are chain homotopy equivalent (with the homotopy given by the extra summands of$k$ cancelling), and their homologies agree:$\mathrm{Tor}_0 = k$,$\mathrm{Tor}_1 = k$, and higher$\mathrm{Tor}$ vanishes.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Functoriality: given a map $f: M \to N$, the induced map$R^n F(f): R^n F(M) \to R^n F(N)$ is defined as follows. Choose injective resolutions$I^\bullet \to M$ and$J^\bullet \to N$; the Comparison Theorem provides a chain map$\tilde{f}: I^\bullet \to J^\bullet$ over$f$. Apply$F$ to get$F(\tilde{f}): F(I^\bullet) \to F(J^\bullet)$, which induces a map on cohomology. The independence from the choice of$\tilde{f}$ (up to chain homotopy) guarantees the map$R^n F(f)$ is well-defined. Functoriality ($R^n F(g \circ f) = R^n F(g) \circ R^n F(f)$) then follows from the uniqueness-up-to-homotopy statement.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
The deepest formulation of well-definedness goes through the *derived category* $D(\mathcal{A})$, where one inverts all quasi-isomorphisms (chain maps that induce isomorphisms on all homology groups). In the derived category, any two resolutions of$M$ are isomorphic (as they are both quasi-isomorphic to$M$ concentrated in degree 0), so derived functors become genuine functors on$D(\mathcal{A})$. This formulation makes it manifest that derived functors depend only on the quasi-isomorphism class of the input, not on the choice of resolution. The derived category perspective also reveals that the long exact sequence of derived functors is an expression of an exact triangle in$D(\mathcal{A})$ — the distinguished triangles of a triangulated category.
\end{remark}
