# 40.3.1 $\mathrm{Tor}_n^R(M,N)$: Left Derived Functor of$M \otimes_R -$

The tensor product $M \otimes_R -$ is the canonical right-exact functor in module theory: given$0 \to A \to B \to C \to 0$, one obtains an exact sequence$M \otimes A \to M \otimes B \to M \otimes C \to 0$, but the first map need not be injective. The Tor groups are the left derived functors of$M \otimes_R -$, measuring this failure of injectivity on the left. Their name — from the French "torsion" — reflects the original observation that$\mathrm{Tor}_1^{\mathbb{Z}}(M, N)$ captures torsion in module products, a point made concrete in the explicit computations of Section 40.3.4.

The construction is the standard left-derived-functor recipe: choose a projective resolution
$$\cdots \to P_2 \to P_1 \to P_0 \to N \to 0,$$
apply $M \otimes_R -$ to obtain the chain complex$M \otimes_R P_\bullet$ (with$N$ removed), and define
$$\mathrm{Tor}_n^R(M, N) = H_n(M \otimes_R P_\bullet).$$
The zeroth group is $\mathrm{Tor}_0^R(M, N) = H_0(M \otimes P_\bullet) = M \otimes_R N$, recovering the original functor. The higher groups$\mathrm{Tor}_n^R(M, N)$ for$n \geq 1$ are the homological obstructions: they vanish precisely when$M \otimes_R -$ is exact enough at the relevant level.

A short exact sequence $0 \to A \to B \to C \to 0$ in the second variable gives a long exact sequence
$$\cdots \to \mathrm{Tor}_1^R(M, A) \to \mathrm{Tor}_1^R(M, B) \to \mathrm{Tor}_1^R(M, C) \to M \otimes A \to M \otimes B \to M \otimes C \to 0,$$
and an analogous sequence holds in the first variable. This long exact sequence is the principal tool for Tor computations: it allows induction, passing from known groups on simpler modules to the target module via a short exact sequence.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** $\mathrm{Tor}_n^{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}, N) = 0$ for$n \geq 1$. Since$\mathbb{Z}$ is free, the projective resolution is$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z} \to 0$. Tensoring with$N$ gives$0 \to N \to N \to 0$, so$\mathrm{Tor}_0 = N$ and$\mathrm{Tor}_n = 0$ for$n \geq 1$. This is the statement that$\mathbb{Z}$ is flat (which it is, being free).
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** $\mathrm{Tor}_n^{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Q}, N)$. Since$\mathbb{Q}$ is flat over$\mathbb{Z}$ (as a localization of$\mathbb{Z}$, hence flat),$\mathrm{Tor}_n^{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Q}, N) = 0$ for$n \geq 1$. Alternatively: use the projective resolution of$\mathbb{Q}$ as a direct limit of$\mathbb{Z}$-modules. Actually$\mathbb{Q}$ has no finite projective resolution, but it is flat, which is why Tor vanishes. For$\mathrm{Tor}_0$:$\mathbb{Q} \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} N \cong N/\{$torsion elements$\} \otimes \mathbb{Q}$ — this is the rationalization of$N$, killing all torsion.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Use the long exact Tor sequence to compute $\mathrm{Tor}_1^{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}/n, M)$ from$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times n} \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/n \to 0$. The long exact sequence in the first variable (fixing$M$ in the second) gives:
$$\mathrm{Tor}_1^{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}, M) \to \mathrm{Tor}_1^{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}, M) \to \mathrm{Tor}_1^{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}/n, M) \to \mathbb{Z} \otimes M \xrightarrow{\times n} \mathbb{Z} \otimes M \to \mathbb{Z}/n \otimes M \to 0.$$
Since $\mathrm{Tor}_1^{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}, M) = 0$ (as$\mathbb{Z}$ is free), this becomes$0 \to \mathrm{Tor}_1^{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}/n, M) \to M \xrightarrow{\times n} M \to M/nM \to 0$. Thus$\mathrm{Tor}_1^{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}/n, M) \cong \ker(\times n: M \to M) = M[n]$, the$n$-torsion subgroup of$M$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** The name "torsion" is justified: $\mathrm{Tor}_1^{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}/m, \mathbb{Z}/n) \cong \mathbb{Z}/\gcd(m,n)$ (Section 40.3.4), and$\mathrm{Tor}_1^{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}/m, M) \cong M[m]$ (the$m$-torsion of$M$). In particular, if$M$ is torsion-free (e.g.,$M = \mathbb{Z}$ or$M = \mathbb{Q}$), then$\mathrm{Tor}_1^{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}/m, M) = 0$ — and in fact$\mathrm{Tor}_n^{\mathbb{Z}}(A, M) = 0$ for all$n \geq 1$ when$M$ is torsion-free, since torsion-free$\mathbb{Z}$-modules are flat.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
Tor also has a long exact sequence in the first variable. From $0 \to M' \to M \to M'' \to 0$, one gets:
$$\cdots \to \mathrm{Tor}_1(M', N) \to \mathrm{Tor}_1(M, N) \to \mathrm{Tor}_1(M'', N) \to M' \otimes N \to M \otimes N \to M'' \otimes N \to 0.$$
This long exact sequence, together with the symmetry $\mathrm{Tor}_n^R(M, N) \cong \mathrm{Tor}_n^R(N, M)$ (Section 40.3.2), makes Tor into a fully bifunctorial invariant that can be computed from either variable. The two long exact sequences (in first and second variable) are the primary tools for Tor calculations via induction on short exact sequences.
\end{remark}
