# 40.3.2 Symmetry: Tor Is Symmetric in Both Arguments

The definition of $\mathrm{Tor}_n^R(M, N)$ via a projective resolution of$N$ treats the two arguments asymmetrically, but the resulting groups are symmetric:$\mathrm{Tor}_n^R(M, N) \cong \mathrm{Tor}_n^R(N, M)$ for all$n$. This is a non-trivial theorem, since the two constructions — resolving$N$ and tensoring with$M$ versus resolving$M$ and tensoring with$N$ — seem a priori unrelated. The symmetry is most naturally understood through double complexes.

The argument proceeds by choosing projective resolutions $P_\bullet \to M$ and$Q_\bullet \to N$ simultaneously and forming the double complex$P_p \otimes_R Q_q$ with horizontal differential$d^h = d_p \otimes \mathrm{id}$ and vertical differential$d^v = (-1)^p \mathrm{id} \otimes d_q$ (the sign$(-1)^p$ is needed to make the double complex anticommute, so that the total differential squares to zero). The total complex$\mathrm{Tot}(P \otimes Q)_n = \bigoplus_{p+q=n} P_p \otimes_R Q_q$ has total differential$d = d^h + d^v$.

The total complex can then be filtered in two ways: first by rows (fixing $q$ and varying$p$) or first by columns (fixing$p$ and varying$q$). The horizontal-first spectral sequence computes the homology of the total complex as$\mathrm{Tor}_n^R(M, N)$ (computed by resolving$N$ with$Q_\bullet$ and tensoring with$M$ — taking vertical homology first gives$H_q^v(P_p \otimes Q_\bullet) = P_p \otimes H_q(Q_\bullet)$; since$Q_\bullet$ resolves$N$, this is$P_p \otimes N$ at$q = 0$ and$0$ at$q > 0$; then taking horizontal homology gives$H_p(P_\bullet \otimes N) = \mathrm{Tor}_p^R(M, N)$). The vertical-first spectral sequence similarly gives$\mathrm{Tor}_n^R(N, M)$. Since both spectral sequences converge to the same limit — the homology of$\mathrm{Tot}(P \otimes Q)$ — the two computations must agree:$\mathrm{Tor}_n^R(M, N) \cong H_n(\mathrm{Tot}(P \otimes Q)) \cong \mathrm{Tor}_n^R(N, M)$.

The symmetry of Tor has a direct practical consequence: it does not matter which argument one resolves when computing Tor. One always chooses whichever resolution is more tractable. This is especially useful when one module has a simpler free resolution than the other.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** $\mathrm{Tor}_1^{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}/6, \mathbb{Z}/4)$. One can resolve$\mathbb{Z}/6$:$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 6} \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/6 \to 0$, tensor with$\mathbb{Z}/4$, and get$0 \to \mathbb{Z}/4 \xrightarrow{\times 6} \mathbb{Z}/4 \to 0$. Then$\mathrm{Tor}_1 = \ker(\times 6 = \times 2: \mathbb{Z}/4 \to \mathbb{Z}/4) = \{0, 2\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2 = \mathbb{Z}/\gcd(6,4)$. Alternatively, resolve$\mathbb{Z}/4$:$0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 4} \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/4 \to 0$, tensor with$\mathbb{Z}/6$, and get$0 \to \mathbb{Z}/6 \xrightarrow{\times 4} \mathbb{Z}/6 \to 0$. Then$\mathrm{Tor}_1 = \ker(\times 4 = \times 4: \mathbb{Z}/6 \to \mathbb{Z}/6)$. We need$4x \equiv 0 \pmod 6$, i.e.,$2x \equiv 0 \pmod 3$, i.e.,$x \equiv 0 \pmod 3$. So kernel$= \{0, 3\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2$. Both give$\mathbb{Z}/2$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** $\mathrm{Tor}_1^{k[x]}(k[x]/(f), k[x]/(g))$ where$f, g \in k[x]$. Use the resolution of$k[x]/(f)$:$0 \to k[x] \xrightarrow{\times f} k[x] \to k[x]/(f) \to 0$. Tensoring with$k[x]/(g)$ gives$0 \to k[x]/(g) \xrightarrow{\times f} k[x]/(g) \to 0$. The kernel of multiplication by$f$ on$k[x]/(g)$ is the set of$\bar{h}$ with$fh \equiv 0 \pmod g$, i.e.,$g | fh$. So the kernel is$\overline{(g/\gcd(f,g))} \cdot k[x]/(g) \cong k[x]/(\gcd(f,g))$, giving$\mathrm{Tor}_1 \cong k[x]/(\gcd(f,g))$ as a$k[x]$-module. By symmetry, the same result holds for$\mathrm{Tor}_1^{k[x]}(k[x]/(g), k[x]/(f))$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** The double complex proof in miniature: take $M = N = \mathbb{Z}/2$ over$R = \mathbb{Z}$, with both resolutions$P_\bullet = Q_\bullet: 0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 2} \mathbb{Z} \to 0$. The double complex has$P_p \otimes Q_q = \mathbb{Z}$ for$p, q \in \{0, 1\}$ and$0$ otherwise. The total complex$\mathrm{Tot}_{n}$ for$n = 0, 1, 2$ is$\mathbb{Z}$,$\mathbb{Z}^2$,$\mathbb{Z}$ respectively. Computing the homology of$\mathrm{Tot}$ gives$\mathbb{Z}/2$ in degrees 0, 1, 2; but actually for computing$\mathrm{Tor}_n(\mathbb{Z}/2, \mathbb{Z}/2)$ we get$\mathbb{Z}/2$ in degree 1, consistently with both approaches.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** An application of symmetry: computing $\mathrm{Tor}_n^R(M, R/I)$ for an ideal$I$. From the short exact sequence$0 \to I \to R \to R/I \to 0$, the long exact Tor sequence gives$\mathrm{Tor}_1^R(M, R/I) \cong \ker(M \otimes I \to M \otimes R) = \ker(M \otimes I \to M)$. By symmetry,$\mathrm{Tor}_1^R(M, R/I) \cong \mathrm{Tor}_1^R(R/I, M) \cong \ker(I \otimes M \to M)$. This recovers the classical formula:$\mathrm{Tor}_1^R(R/I, M) \cong \ker(I \otimes M \to M)$, with elements of the form$\sum a_i \otimes m_i$ with$\sum a_i m_i = 0$ in$M$.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
Over a commutative ring $R$, the isomorphism$\mathrm{Tor}_n^R(M, N) \cong \mathrm{Tor}_n^R(N, M)$ is natural in both variables. Over a non-commutative ring$R$, the statement requires care:$\mathrm{Tor}_n^R(M, N)$ requires$M$ to be a right$R$-module and$N$ a left$R$-module, so the symmetry statement must account for the natural identification$M \otimes_R N \cong N \otimes_{R^{\mathrm{op}}} M$ (where$M$ becomes a left$R^{\mathrm{op}}$-module and$N$ a right$R^{\mathrm{op}}$-module). The double complex proof goes through in this generality with appropriate modifications.
\end{remark}
