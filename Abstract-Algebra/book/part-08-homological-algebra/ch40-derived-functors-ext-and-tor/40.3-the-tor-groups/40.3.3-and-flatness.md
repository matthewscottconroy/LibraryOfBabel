# 40.3.3 $\mathrm{Tor}_0 = \otimes$;$\mathrm{Tor}_1$ and Flatness

The basic facts about $\mathrm{Tor}_0$ and flatness complete the analogy with the Ext–projectivity correspondence. The zeroth Tor group is simply the tensor product:$\mathrm{Tor}_0^R(M, N) = M \otimes_R N$, which follows immediately from the definition (taking homology in degree zero of the complex$M \otimes P_\bullet$ recovers$M \otimes N$, since the sequence$M \otimes P_1 \to M \otimes P_0 \to M \otimes N \to 0$ is exact by right-exactness of$M \otimes -$). For higher degrees, the central characterization is: a module$N$ is flat if and only if$\mathrm{Tor}_1^R(M, N) = 0$ for all$M$, if and only if$\mathrm{Tor}_n^R(M, N) = 0$ for all$M$ and all$n \geq 1$.

To see why this is the right characterization, recall that $N$ is flat if and only if$- \otimes_R N$ is an exact functor. If$N$ is flat, then for any projective resolution$P_\bullet \to M$, the complex$P_\bullet \otimes N$ is still exact in positive degrees (since each map$d_n: P_n \to P_{n-1}$ is injective on the kernel of$P_{n-1} \to P_{n-2}$, and tensoring with a flat module preserves injectivity for flat modules — this uses the characterization of flatness as$\mathrm{Tor}_1 = 0$). Conversely, if$\mathrm{Tor}_1^R(M, N) = 0$ for all$M$, then for any short exact sequence$0 \to A \to B \to M \to 0$ the long exact sequence in Tor gives$0 \to A \otimes N \to B \otimes N \to M \otimes N \to 0$, which is the flatness condition.

This connects Tor to the fundamental hierarchy of modules: free implies projective implies flat, and flat is strictly weaker than projective in general. The vanishing of $\mathrm{Tor}_1^R(M, N)$ for all$M$ is thus a coarser condition than the vanishing of$\mathrm{Ext}^1_R(N, -)$ for all targets (which characterizes projectivity). The flat dimension$\mathrm{fd}(N)$ is defined as the smallest$k$ such that$\mathrm{Tor}_n^R(M, N) = 0$ for all$M$ and$n > k$, in analogy with projective dimension. Over Noetherian rings, finitely generated flat modules are projective (by a theorem of Serre), but over non-Noetherian rings the distinction matters.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** $\mathbb{Z}$ is flat over$\mathbb{Z}$ (free$\Rightarrow$ flat), so$\mathrm{Tor}_n^{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}, N) = 0$ for all$n \geq 1$. More generally,$\mathbb{Q}$ is flat over$\mathbb{Z}$ (as a localization), so$\mathrm{Tor}_n^{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Q}, N) = 0$ for all$n \geq 1$. But$\mathbb{Q}$ is not projective over$\mathbb{Z}$ (it is not free and not a direct summand of a free module, since it is divisible while free modules are not). Thus$\mathrm{Tor}$ vanishes but$\mathrm{Ext}^1_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{Z}) \neq 0$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is not flat over$\mathbb{Z}$ for$n \geq 2$. Indeed,$\mathrm{Tor}_1^{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}/n, \mathbb{Z}/n) \cong \mathbb{Z}/n \neq 0$. Alternatively: the injection$\mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times n} \mathbb{Z}$ remains injective, but after tensoring with$\mathbb{Z}/n$ we get$\mathbb{Z}/n \xrightarrow{\times n} \mathbb{Z}/n$, which is the zero map — hence not injective (unless$n = 1$). This directly shows$\mathbb{Z}/n$ fails the flatness criterion.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** Flat resolutions. Every module has a flat resolution (not just a projective resolution): use the same greedy construction, but take flat modules at each step instead of projective modules. Since projective $\Rightarrow$ flat, any projective resolution is also a flat resolution. But flat resolutions can sometimes be shorter: for instance, over certain non-Noetherian rings, some modules have flat resolutions of finite length but infinite projective dimension. The flat dimension$\mathrm{fd}(M) \leq \mathrm{pd}(M)$ always, with equality over Noetherian rings for finitely generated modules.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** The connection to tensor products over fields: if $k$ is a field and$M, N$ are$k$-modules (i.e.,$k$-vector spaces), then every module is flat (in fact projective), so$\mathrm{Tor}_n^k(M, N) = 0$ for all$n \geq 1$. The tensor product$M \otimes_k N$ is exact in all three variables. This is the reason why homological computations over fields are trivial:$\mathrm{gl.dim}(k) = 0$. The presence of torsion (as in$\mathbb{Z}$-modules) or of non-trivial ideals (as in$k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$-modules) is what makes Tor interesting.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}
Let $0 \to M' \to M \to M'' \to 0$ be a short exact sequence of$R$-modules. If two of the three modules are flat, then so is the third. More precisely:
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $M$ and$M''$ are flat, then$M'$ is flat.
\item If $M'$ and$M''$ are flat, then$M$ is flat.
\end{enumerate}
This follows from the long exact Tor sequence: if $\mathrm{Tor}_1(-, M) = 0$ and$\mathrm{Tor}_1(-, M'') = 0$, the sequence forces$\mathrm{Tor}_1(-, M') = 0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}
The Tor-flatness correspondence and the Ext-projectivity correspondence are formally parallel, but there is an asymmetry in practice: while projective modules are "easy" to construct (any module is a quotient of a free module), flat modules are in some sense "harder" to detect, since they are defined by a condition on all possible tensor products. The Baer criterion for injectivity and the lifting property for projectivity have flat analogues (Lazard's theorem: a module is flat if and only if it is a filtered colimit of finitely generated free modules), but this characterization is less directly useful for computations.
\end{remark}
