# 40.4.1 $G$-Modules and the Group Ring$\mathbb{Z}[G]$

Group cohomology is a specialization of the general Ext machinery to the situation where the ring is a group ring. To set it up properly, one needs to translate the representation-theoretic language of group actions into the module-theoretic language of homological algebra. The key translation is provided by the group ring.

A *$G$-module* is an abelian group$M$ equipped with an action of the group$G$ by group automorphisms: one has a map$G \times M \to M$ written$(g, m) \mapsto g \cdot m$, satisfying$(gh) \cdot m = g \cdot (h \cdot m)$,$e \cdot m = m$, and$g \cdot (m + m') = g \cdot m + g \cdot m'$. The group ring$\mathbb{Z}[G]$ is the free$\mathbb{Z}$-module on the elements of$G$, with ring multiplication extending the group law:$(\sum a_g g)(\sum b_h h) = \sum_{g,h} a_g b_h (gh)$. A$G$-module is then the same thing as a left$\mathbb{Z}[G]$-module: the$G$-action extends by linearity to a$\mathbb{Z}[G]$-action. This translation is functorial in both directions, making$G\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ and$\mathbb{Z}[G]\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$ equivalent categories.

The functor that extracts the "fixed" part of a $G$-module is the invariants functor$M \mapsto M^G = \{m \in M : g \cdot m = m \text{ for all } g \in G\}$. This is a left-exact functor from$G$-modules to abelian groups: a$G$-equivariant injection$A \hookrightarrow B$ induces an injection$A^G \hookrightarrow B^G$, but a$G$-equivariant surjection$B \twoheadrightarrow C$ need not induce a surjection on invariants. Group cohomology is the right derived functor of the invariants functor. Since$M^G \cong \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}[G]}(\mathbb{Z}, M)$ — where$\mathbb{Z}$ carries the trivial$G$-action$g \cdot n = n$ — this fits perfectly into the Ext framework:$H^n(G, M) = \mathrm{Ext}^n_{\mathbb{Z}[G]}(\mathbb{Z}, M)$.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** The trivial $G$-module$\mathbb{Z}$:$G$ acts on$\mathbb{Z}$ by the identity ($g \cdot n = n$ for all$g, n$). The corresponding$\mathbb{Z}[G]$-module structure: any$(\sum a_g g) \cdot n = (\sum a_g) n = |G|_{\text{sum}} \cdot n$. The invariants:$\mathbb{Z}^G = \mathbb{Z}$ (all elements are fixed). Group cohomology$H^n(G, \mathbb{Z})$ with trivial coefficients is one of the fundamental invariants of$G$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** Let $G = \mathbb{Z}/2 = \{1, g\}$ act on$M = \mathbb{Z}$ by$g \cdot n = -n$ (the sign representation). Then$M^G = \{n \in \mathbb{Z} : -n = n\} = 0$. The corresponding$\mathbb{Z}[G]$-module:$\mathbb{Z}[G] = \mathbb{Z}[g]/(g^2 - 1)$, and$g$ acts as$-1$ on$M = \mathbb{Z}$. Computing$H^1(G, M)$: a crossed homomorphism$f: G \to M$ satisfies$f(gh) = f(g) + g \cdot f(h)$, so$f(1) = 0$ and$f(g) + g \cdot f(g) = f(g) - f(g) = 0$ for$f(g^2) = f(1) = 0$. Wait:$f(g \cdot g) = f(g) + g \cdot f(g) = f(g) + (-f(g)) = 0$ ✓. So any value$f(g) \in \mathbb{Z}$ gives a valid crossed homomorphism. Principal ones:$f(g) = g \cdot m - m = -m - m = -2m$. So$H^1 = \mathbb{Z} / 2\mathbb{Z} = \mathbb{Z}/2$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** The norm element: in $\mathbb{Z}[G]$, the *norm element*$N = \sum_{g \in G} g$ plays a central role. For a finite group$G$ of order$n$,$N \cdot m = \sum_{g} g \cdot m$ for any$G$-module$M$. The image of$N$ in$M$ is the *trace* of$m$, and$\mathrm{im}(N: M \to M) \subseteq M^G$ (since$h \cdot Nm = \sum_g h g m = \sum_g g m = Nm$ for any$h \in G$). The map$N: M \to M^G$ is the *norm map*, and it appears in the Tate cohomology of finite groups.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** Galois modules: if $L/K$ is a finite Galois extension with Galois group$G = \mathrm{Gal}(L/K)$, then$L^\times$ is a$G$-module via the natural action of$G$ on the multiplicative group of$L$. More generally, any algebraic object naturally acted on by$G$ — rings of integers, abelian varieties, roots of unity$\mu_n$ — becomes a$G$-module, and its group cohomology$H^n(G, M)$ carries arithmetic information. For example,$H^1(G, L^\times) = 0$ by Hilbert's Theorem 90, and$H^2(G, L^\times)$ is related to the Brauer group$\mathrm{Br}(K)$.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
The group ring $\mathbb{Z}[G]$ is in general non-commutative (unless$G$ is abelian). This means the general machinery of non-commutative homological algebra is needed; however, since we are computing$\mathrm{Ext}_{\mathbb{Z}[G]}(\mathbb{Z}, M)$ with$\mathbb{Z}$ as the first variable (which has the trivial module structure, making it a bimodule), the computation reduces to the "one-sided" theory and all the tools of Section 40.2 apply verbatim. The group ring is also self-augmented via the augmentation map$\varepsilon: \mathbb{Z}[G] \to \mathbb{Z}$ sending$\sum a_g g \mapsto \sum a_g$, and the augmentation ideal$I_G = \ker(\varepsilon)$ is generated by$\{g - 1 : g \in G\}$. The module$\mathbb{Z} = \mathbb{Z}[G]/I_G$, and its projective resolution via the bar complex (Section 40.4.4) is built from the augmentation ideal.
\end{remark}
