# 40.4.4 The Bar Complex; Explicit Cocycles

The bar resolution provides an explicit and canonical free $\mathbb{Z}[G]$-resolution of the trivial module$\mathbb{Z}$, making group cohomology fully computable in terms of explicit functions on$G$. While the abstract derived functor definition ensures the existence of group cohomology, the bar complex is what allows one to write down cocycles, verify the cocycle condition, and work concretely with$H^n(G, M)$.

In degree $n$, the bar resolution is the free$\mathbb{Z}[G]$-module$B_n = \mathbb{Z}[G] \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{Z}[G^n]$, which has a free$\mathbb{Z}[G]$-basis consisting of symbols$[g_1 | g_2 | \cdots | g_n]$ for$(g_1, \ldots, g_n) \in G^n$. The boundary map$\partial_n: B_n \to B_{n-1}$ is the alternating sum over face maps:
$$\partial_n [g_1 | \cdots | g_n] = g_1 [g_2 | \cdots | g_n] + \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} (-1)^i [g_1 | \cdots | g_i g_{i+1} | \cdots | g_n] + (-1)^n [g_1 | \cdots | g_{n-1}].$$
This is the standard simplicial differential, and one verifies $\partial_{n-1} \circ \partial_n = 0$ by checking that adjacent face maps satisfy the simplicial identity. The augmentation map$\varepsilon: B_0 = \mathbb{Z}[G] \to \mathbb{Z}$ sends each group element to$1$, giving the full resolution$\cdots \to B_2 \to B_1 \to B_0 \xrightarrow{\varepsilon} \mathbb{Z} \to 0$.

Applying $\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}[G]}(B_\bullet, M)$ to this resolution gives the *bar cochain complex*. A$\mathbb{Z}[G]$-module map$B_n \to M$ is determined by its values on the basis elements$[g_1 | \cdots | g_n]$, and these values can be arbitrary elements of$M$ (with no equivariance condition needed, since the basis generates$B_n$ freely as a$\mathbb{Z}[G]$-module). The result is a function$c: G^n \to M$. The induced coboundary map becomes the standard formula for the group cohomology differential:
$$(dc)(g_1, \ldots, g_{n+1}) = g_1 \cdot c(g_2, \ldots, g_{n+1}) + \sum_{i=1}^{n} (-1)^i c(g_1, \ldots, g_i g_{i+1}, \ldots, g_{n+1}) + (-1)^{n+1} c(g_1, \ldots, g_n).$$
A function $c: G^n \to M$ satisfying$dc = 0$ is an$n$-cocycle; those of the form$c = db$ for some$(n-1)$-cochain$b$ are$n$-coboundaries; and$H^n(G, M) = Z^n/B^n$ is the cohomology.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** 1-cocycles. In degree $n = 1$, the coboundary formula gives$(dc)(g_1, g_2) = g_1 \cdot c(g_2) - c(g_1 g_2) + c(g_1)$. A 1-cocycle satisfies$dc = 0$, i.e.,$c(g_1 g_2) = c(g_1) + g_1 \cdot c(g_2)$ — the crossed homomorphism condition. A 1-coboundary is$c = db$ for$b: G^0 \to M$ (a constant function$b = m \in M$), giving$(db)(g) = g \cdot m - m$. So 1-coboundaries are the principal derivations, and$H^1(G, M) = Z^1/B^1$ is the group of crossed homomorphisms modulo principal ones — recovering the classical description.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** 2-cocycles and group extensions. A 2-cocycle is a function $c: G \times G \to M$ satisfying$(dc)(g, h, k) = 0$, i.e.,
$$g \cdot c(h, k) - c(gh, k) + c(g, hk) - c(g, h) = 0.$$
This is the classical cocycle condition for group extensions. A 2-coboundary is $c(g, h) = (db)(g, h) = g \cdot b(h) - b(gh) + b(g)$ for some$b: G \to M$. The class$[c] \in H^2(G, M)$ corresponds to the group extension$1 \to M \to E \to G \to 1$ where$E = M \times G$ with multiplication$(m, g)(m', h) = (m + g \cdot m' + c(g, h), gh)$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** $H^1(\mathbb{Z}, M)$ for any$G = \mathbb{Z}$-module$M$. With$G = \mathbb{Z} = \langle t \rangle$, a 1-cocycle$f: \mathbb{Z} \to M$ satisfies$f(m + n) = f(m) + t^m f(n)$. Setting$a = f(1)$, one computes$f(n) = (1 + t + t^2 + \cdots + t^{n-1}) \cdot a$ for$n \geq 1$. So$f$ is determined by$f(1) \in M$. A 1-coboundary:$f(n) = t^n \cdot m - m$ for some$m \in M$. The coboundary of constant function$m$ gives$f(n) = t^n m - m$. For$n = 1$:$f(1) = (t - 1)m$. So$H^1(\mathbb{Z}, M) = M / (t - 1)M = M_{G}$, the coinvariants.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** $H^2(\mathbb{Z}/2, \mathbb{Z})$ with non-trivial action$g \cdot n = -n$. A 2-cocycle$c: (\mathbb{Z}/2)^2 \to \mathbb{Z}$ satisfies$g \cdot c(h, k) - c(gh, k) + c(g, hk) - c(g, h) = 0$. With$G = \{1, g\}$, there are four values$c(1,1), c(1,g), c(g,1), c(g,g)$. The normalization$c(1, g) = c(g, 1) = 0$ (from cocycle conditions with the identity) and the condition$g \cdot c(g, g) - c(1, g) + c(g, g \cdot g) - c(g, g) = 0$ gives$-c(g,g) - 0 + c(g, 1) - c(g,g) = 0$, so$-2c(g,g) = 0$. Thus$c(g, g) \in \mathbb{Z}$ is constrained only by$2c(g,g) \equiv 0$, meaning$c(g,g)$ is arbitrary over$\mathbb{Z}$ but the coboundary$c = db$ forces$c(g,g) = g \cdot b(g) - b(1) = -b(g) - b(1)$. Modding out coboundaries,$H^2(\mathbb{Z}/2, \mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2$, reflecting the two extensions$\mathbb{Z} \rtimes \mathbb{Z}/2$ (split, the infinite dihedral group with sign action) and the non-split extension.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
The bar complex is infinite (in general), but for finite groups $G$ of order$n$, the cohomology$H^k(G, M)$ is annihilated by$|G|$ for$k \geq 1$:$|G| \cdot H^k(G, M) = 0$. This follows from the norm map argument: the composite$M^G \to M \to M_G \to M^G$ (restriction, then inclusion, then norm) is multiplication by$|G|$, and using transfer/corestriction maps one shows$|G|$ kills the cohomology. In particular, for finite$G$ and$M$ a$\mathbb{Q}$-vector space (so$|G|$ is invertible in$M$), all higher cohomology vanishes:$H^k(G, M) = 0$ for$k \geq 1$ — a key simplification that underlies Maschke's theorem in representation theory.
\end{remark}
