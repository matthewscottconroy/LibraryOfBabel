# Chapter 40 — Derived Functors: Ext and Tor

The functor $\operatorname{Hom}_R(M, -)$ is left exact: a short exact sequence $0 \to A \to B \to C \to 0$ yields the exact sequence $0 \to \operatorname{Hom}(M, A) \to \operatorname{Hom}(M, B) \to \operatorname{Hom}(M, C)$, but the last map need not be surjective. The tensor product functor $M \otimes_R -$ is right exact: it preserves $B \to C \to 0$ but may fail to preserve injections $A \hookrightarrow B$. These failures are not pathologies to be avoided — they are the subject of study. Derived functors are the systematic way to measure exactly how much exactness fails, degree by degree, and the result is new functors that carry information invisible to the original ones.

The construction begins with resolutions. To form the right derived functors $R^n F$ of a left-exact functor $F$, take an injective resolution $0 \to M \to I^\bullet$ of the input module, apply $F$ termwise to get a complex $F(I^\bullet)$ (no longer necessarily exact), and define $R^n F(M) = H^n(F(I^\bullet))$. The Comparison Theorem ensures that any two injective resolutions produce isomorphic cohomology, so $R^n F(M)$ is well-defined. Dually, left derived functors $L_n F$ of a right-exact functor $F$ are defined using projective resolutions: take $P_\bullet \to M$, apply $F$ to get $F(P_\bullet)$, and set $L_n F(M) = H_n(F(P_\bullet))$. In both cases, a short exact sequence of input modules yields a long exact sequence connecting all derived functors — the engine of most computations in homological algebra.

The derived functors of $\operatorname{Hom}$ and $\otimes$ have names: $\operatorname{Ext}^n_R(M, N) = R^n \operatorname{Hom}_R(M, -)(N)$ and $\operatorname{Tor}_n^R(M, N) = L_n(M \otimes_R -)(N)$. These are the central objects of this chapter. Both can be computed from either argument: one can resolve $M$ projectively and apply $\operatorname{Hom}(-, N)$, or resolve $N$ injectively and apply $\operatorname{Hom}(M, -)$, and the answer is the same; similarly for Tor. This symmetry is the content of the "balance" theorems and reflects a deep bilateral structure. Explicit computations — $\operatorname{Ext}^1_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}/m, \mathbb{Z}/n) \cong \mathbb{Z}/\gcd(m,n)$, and the same for Tor — show that these groups carry genuine arithmetic information about the modules.

Beyond computation, Ext has a geometric interpretation: $\operatorname{Ext}^1_R(M, N)$ classifies short exact sequences $0 \to N \to E \to M \to 0$ up to isomorphism of extensions, with the Baer sum making this set into a group. This interpretation, due to Baer, predates the general theory of derived functors and provided one of the original motivations for the subject. Vanishing theorems are equally important: $\operatorname{Ext}^n_R(M, N) = 0$ for all $N$ and $n \geq 1$ characterizes projective modules; analogously, $\operatorname{Tor}_n^R(M, N) = 0$ for all $N$ and $n \geq 1$ characterizes flat modules. Group cohomology, defined as $H^n(G, A) = \operatorname{Ext}^n_{\mathbb{Z}[G]}(\mathbb{Z}, A)$, shows how derived functors unify algebraic topology and group theory: $H^2(G, A)$ classifies group extensions with abelian kernel, and the bar complex gives explicit cocycles.
