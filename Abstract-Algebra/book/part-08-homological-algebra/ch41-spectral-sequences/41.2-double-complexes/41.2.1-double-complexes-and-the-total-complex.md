# 41.2.1 Double Complexes and the Total Complex

Double complexes are the most natural algebraic source of spectral sequences, and they arise throughout homological algebra whenever one simultaneously resolves two objects or composes two derived functor computations. The idea is to work in two directions at once — horizontally and vertically — and to combine the two differentials into a single total differential on the "diagonal" complex.

A *double complex* $C^{\bullet,\bullet}$ consists of modules$C^{p,q}$ for$p, q \in \mathbb{Z}$, together with horizontal differentials$d^h: C^{p,q} \to C^{p+1,q}$ and vertical differentials$d^v: C^{p,q} \to C^{p,q+1}$, all satisfying$(d^h)^2 = 0$,$(d^v)^2 = 0$, and the commutativity (or anti-commutativity) condition$d^h d^v \pm d^v d^h = 0$. The sign convention matters: in the anticommuting convention$d^h d^v + d^v d^h = 0$, the total complex has total differential$d = d^h + d^v$ satisfying$d^2 = (d^h)^2 + d^h d^v + d^v d^h + (d^v)^2 = 0$ without any additional signs. In the commuting convention, one introduces a sign$(-1)^p$ on$d^v$ in the column$C^{p,\bullet}$ to achieve the same effect.

The *total complex* $\mathrm{Tot}(C)^n = \bigoplus_{p+q=n} C^{p,q}$ assembles all modules of total degree$n$ into a single module, with total differential$d = d^h + d^v$ (with appropriate signs). The goal of the whole apparatus is to compute$H_n(\mathrm{Tot}(C))$: the homology of this single complex. The total complex is often more tractable than the original double complex because it has only one differential, but computing its homology directly is difficult. The two spectral sequences of the double complex (Section 41.2.2) provide two systematic approaches to this computation, each exploiting one of the two directions of the bigrading.

\begin{example}
**Example 1.** The double complex arising from a projective resolution. Let $P_\bullet \to M$ be a projective resolution and$0 \to N \to I^\bullet$ an injective resolution. The double complex$\mathrm{Hom}_R(P_p, I^q)$ with horizontal differential$d^h = \mathrm{Hom}(d_p, I^q)$ and vertical differential$d^v = \mathrm{Hom}(P_p, d^q)$ is the standard double complex for proving that$\mathrm{Ext}$ is balanced. The total complex$\mathrm{Tot}(\mathrm{Hom}(P_\bullet, I^\bullet))$ has cohomology$\mathrm{Ext}^n_R(M, N)$ (to be computed from either spectral sequence).
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 2.** The double complex from two resolutions of $M$ and$N$ for Tor. Let$P_\bullet \to M$ and$Q_\bullet \to N$ be projective resolutions. The double complex$P_p \otimes_R Q_q$ with horizontal differential$d^h = d_p \otimes \mathrm{id}$ and vertical$d^v = (-1)^p \mathrm{id} \otimes d_q$ is the double complex proving the symmetry of Tor. The total complex$\mathrm{Tot}(P \otimes Q)$ has homology$\mathrm{Tor}_*^R(M, N)$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 3.** The double complex for computing $H^*(G, M)$ via the bar resolution. The bar resolution$B_\bullet \to \mathbb{Z}$ is a free$\mathbb{Z}[G]$-resolution. Applying$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}[G]}(B_\bullet, -)$ and combining with an injective resolution of$M$ gives a double complex whose total complex computes$\mathrm{Ext}^*_{\mathbb{Z}[G]}(\mathbb{Z}, M) = H^*(G, M)$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Example 4.** The Koszul complex as a double complex. The Koszul complex $K_\bullet(x_1, x_2; R)$ for a sequence$(x_1, x_2)$ in a ring$R$ can be expressed as the total complex of the double complex:
$$\begin{array}{ccc}
0 & \to & R \\
\downarrow{x_1} & & \downarrow{x_1} \\
R & \to & R \\
\end{array}$$
(where the horizontal maps are multiplication by $x_2$ and the vertical maps are multiplication by$x_1$, with appropriate signs). The total complex is$0 \to R \to R^2 \to R \to 0$ with the Koszul differentials. The two spectral sequences of this double complex both compute the homology of the Koszul complex$H_*(K(x_1, x_2; R))$, which measures the interaction between the two elements$x_1$ and$x_2$.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
The convention for the total complex also includes a choice of whether to use the *direct sum* ($\bigoplus$) or the *direct product* ($\prod$) in assembling the modules of total degree$n$. For a first-quadrant double complex (where$C^{p,q} = 0$ unless$p, q \geq 0$), the two conventions agree (since the sum is finite in each degree). For general double complexes (with entries in all four quadrants), the two conventions give different total complexes with different homological properties. The direct sum is usually preferred in algebra; the direct product appears in completions and in the theory of formal power series.
\end{remark}
