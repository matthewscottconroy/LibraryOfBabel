# Chapter 41 — Spectral Sequences

Among the tools of homological algebra, spectral sequences occupy a singular position: they are simultaneously the most technically demanding and the most computationally powerful. A spectral sequence is not a single algebraic object but a machine — a structured sequence of approximations, each derived from the last by taking homology, that converges to a target that would be inaccessible by direct computation. Jean Leray invented spectral sequences while imprisoned during World War II, seeking to compute the cohomology of fiber bundles from the cohomology of the base and fiber. That such a computation is possible at all, given how entangled the total space's topology is with those of its pieces, is remarkable; that it can be carried out algorithmically via a sequence of bigraded differential modules is one of the deepest insights in twentieth-century mathematics.

The formal structure is as follows. A spectral sequence (in homological grading) is a collection of bigraded modules $\{E_r^{p,q}\}$ for $r \geq 1$ (or $r \geq 0$, or $r \geq 2$, depending on convention), equipped with differentials $d_r: E_r^{p,q} \to E_r^{p-r, q+r-1}$ satisfying $d_r^2 = 0$, together with isomorphisms $E_{r+1}^{p,q} \cong H(E_r^{p,q}, d_r)$. Each page $E_r$ is the homology of the previous page. The sequence is said to converge to a graded module $H_*$ if for each total degree $n = p + q$ the module $E_\infty^{p,q}$ is the associated graded of a filtration on $H_n$. The word "converge" is appropriate: the pages stabilize (in the sense that each $E_r^{p,q}$ stabilizes for large $r$, under finiteness conditions), and the limiting page $E_\infty$ determines the target up to extension problems.

The most natural source of spectral sequences is a filtered complex or a double complex. Given a double complex $C^{p,q}$ with two commuting differentials — a horizontal $\partial'$ and a vertical $\partial''$ — one can filter the total complex by either row or column, producing two spectral sequences that both converge to the same cohomology of the total complex. This observation is the key to proving the balance theorems for Ext and Tor (which arise from the double complex $\operatorname{Hom}(P_p, I^q)$) and to deriving the Grothendieck spectral sequence for the composition of derived functors. The collapse of a spectral sequence — when all differentials on some page $E_r$ and beyond are zero — is the ideal outcome: it means the $E_r$ page is already $E_\infty$, and the computation terminates.

The Lyndon–Hochschild–Serre (LHS) spectral sequence is the central algebraic example: for a group extension $1 \to N \to G \to Q \to 1$ and a $G$-module $A$, it gives $E_2^{p,q} = H^p(Q, H^q(N, A)) \Rightarrow H^{p+q}(G, A)$. The $E_2$ page is cohomology of $Q$ with coefficients in the cohomology of $N$, and the spectral sequence records how these piece together into the cohomology of the whole group $G$. The five-term exact sequence extracted from the low-degree terms of the LHS spectral sequence — relating $H^1(G, A)$ to $H^1(Q, A^N)$ and $H^1(N, A)^Q$ with a connecting map through $H^2(Q, A^N)$ — is among the most useful exact sequences in group theory. Galois cohomology, the arithmetic of number fields, and the theory of central simple algebras all flow through this machine.
