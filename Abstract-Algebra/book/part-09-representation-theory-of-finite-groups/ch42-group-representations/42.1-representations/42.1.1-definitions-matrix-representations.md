# 42.1.1 Definitions: $\rho: G \to GL(V)$; Matrix Representations

Imagine you want to study a group $G$ — say the symmetry group of a molecule, or the Galois group of a polynomial, or an abstract finite group given by generators and relations. The group elements are abstract objects satisfying certain axioms, and while the axioms constrain the group's structure, they leave its "shape" mysterious. Representation theory offers a way to make this shape visible: embed$G$ inside a concrete group of linear transformations, where you can draw pictures, compute eigenvalues, and bring all of linear algebra to bear. The central question is deceptively simple: in how many ways can$G$ act faithfully, or nearly faithfully, on a vector space?

Formally, a *representation* of a group $G$ over a field$k$ is a group homomorphism$\rho: G \to GL(V)$, where$V$ is a finite-dimensional$k$-vector space and$GL(V)$ denotes the group of invertible$k$-linear automorphisms of$V$. The homomorphism condition requires$\rho(gh) = \rho(g) \circ \rho(h)$ for all$g, h \in G$, and$\rho(e) = \mathrm{id}_V$, and$\rho(g^{-1}) = \rho(g)^{-1}$. The *dimension* (or *degree*) of the representation is$n = \dim_k V$. When we speak of "a representation of$G$," we often denote it by the pair$(V, \rho)$ or simply by the vector space$V$ when the map$\rho$ is understood from context.

\begin{definition}
A \emph{representation} of a group $G$ over a field$k$ is a group homomorphism$\rho: G \to GL(V)$ for some finite-dimensional$k$-vector space$V$. The \emph{degree} of$\rho$ is$\dim_k V$.
\end{definition}

A *matrix representation* arises from a representation by choosing an ordered basis $\mathcal{B} = \{v_1, \ldots, v_n\}$ of$V$: with respect to$\mathcal{B}$, each linear map$\rho(g)$ is expressed as an invertible$n \times n$ matrix with entries in$k$, yielding a group homomorphism$\rho: G \to GL_n(k)$. The two formulations — abstract linear maps versus concrete matrices — are equivalent once a basis is chosen. A change of basis replaces the matrix$\rho(g)$ by$P \rho(g) P^{-1}$ for a fixed invertible matrix$P$ (the change-of-basis matrix), which is precisely the notion of equivalence we will formalize in §42.1.3. For conceptual arguments, the basis-free formulation is cleaner; for explicit computations and character theory, the matrix formulation is indispensable. Both perspectives will be used freely throughout.

The key insight motivating everything that follows is that a representation makes the abstract symmetry group $G$ visible as concrete geometric transformations of a linear space. A single group can have many non-isomorphic representations of different dimensions, and the full collection of these representations encodes deep structural information about$G$ itself. The program of representation theory is to classify all representations of a given group up to isomorphism, identify the indecomposable building blocks, and use this classification to extract group-theoretic information — a program that, for finite groups over$\mathbb{C}$, reaches a remarkably complete answer via the character theory developed in Chapters 43–45.

It is important to emphasize that the map $\rho$ need not be injective. When$\rho$ is injective,$G$ embeds into$GL(V)$ and we say the representation is *faithful*; the group structure is fully captured. When$\rho$ is not injective, the kernel$\ker \rho = \{g \in G : \rho(g) = \mathrm{id}_V\}$ is a normal subgroup of$G$, and the representation factors as$G \twoheadrightarrow G/\ker\rho \hookrightarrow GL(V)$: it is really a faithful representation of the quotient. Every group has at least one representation, the *trivial representation*$\rho(g) = \mathrm{id}_V$ for all$g$, which has as large a kernel as possible. The richness of the theory comes from understanding the landscape between this degenerate extreme and faithful representations.

**Example 1.** The cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} = \langle r \mid r^n = e \rangle$ admits a two-dimensional real representation by rotation matrices:
$$\rho(r^k) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(2\pi k/n) & -\sin(2\pi k/n) \\ \sin(2\pi k/n) & \cos(2\pi k/n) \end{pmatrix}.$$
This is a group homomorphism because rotating by $2\pi k/n$ and then by$2\pi \ell/n$ equals rotating by$2\pi(k+\ell)/n$. The representation is faithful: different powers of$r$ give different rotation angles, hence different matrices.

**Example 2.** The symmetric group $S_3 = \{e, (12), (13), (23), (123), (132)\}$ has a faithful two-dimensional complex representation. Identify the three cube roots of unity with the vertices of a triangle: let$\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$. Then$S_3$ acts on$\mathbb{C}$ by permuting$\{1, \omega, \omega^2\}$, and restricting to the two-dimensional real subspace spanned by$\{1, \omega, \omega^2\}$ in$\mathbb{C}$ (which is a 2-dimensional space over$\mathbb{R}$ since these three points are collinear in the sense that$1 + \omega + \omega^2 = 0$), one gets a faithful 2-dimensional real representation. Concretely, in the basis$\{e_1 - e_3, e_2 - e_3\}$ for the standard representation (to be defined in §42.2.2):
\begin{align*}
\rho((12)) &= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
\rho((123)) = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{align*}
One verifies: $\rho((12))^2 = I$,$\rho((123))^3 = I$, and$\rho((12))\rho((123))\rho((12))^{-1} = \rho((132))$ as required.

**Example 3.** The dihedral group $D_n = \langle r, s \mid r^n = s^2 = e,\, srs^{-1} = r^{-1} \rangle$ has a faithful two-dimensional real representation:
$$\rho(r^k) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(2\pi k/n) & -\sin(2\pi k/n) \\ \sin(2\pi k/n) & \cos(2\pi k/n) \end{pmatrix}, \quad \rho(r^k s) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(2\pi k/n) & \sin(2\pi k/n) \\ \sin(2\pi k/n) & -\cos(2\pi k/n) \end{pmatrix}.$$
The rotation matrices represent the rotational symmetries, and the reflection matrices (determinant $-1$) represent the reflections. The relations$r^n = e$,$s^2 = e$, and$srs^{-1} = r^{-1}$ can be verified directly.

**Example 4.** The quaternion group $Q_8 = \{\pm 1, \pm i, \pm j, \pm k\}$ has a faithful two-dimensional complex representation using the Pauli matrices. Setting
$$\rho(i) = \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{-1} & 0 \\ 0 & -\sqrt{-1} \end{pmatrix}, \quad \rho(j) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \rho(k) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \sqrt{-1} \\ \sqrt{-1} & 0 \end{pmatrix},$$
one checks $\rho(i)^2 = \rho(j)^2 = \rho(k)^2 = \rho(ijk) = -I$, confirming this is a representation of$Q_8$. Note that$Q_8$ and$D_4$ both have order 8 and both have faithful 2-dimensional complex representations — yet these groups are not isomorphic, as their representation theories (Chapter 44) will reveal.

The choice of field $k$ matters. Over$\mathbb{R}$, the cyclic group$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ has no faithful 1-dimensional representation (since$-1$ is the only real number of multiplicative order 2, and there is no real number of order 4), but it has the faithful 2-dimensional rotation representation above. Over$\mathbb{C}$, however,$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ has the faithful 1-dimensional representation$\rho(k) = i^k$ (where$i = \sqrt{-1}$). The field$\mathbb{C}$ is the "natural" field for representation theory because its algebraic closure allows every matrix to have eigenvalues, enabling the detailed spectral analysis that character theory exploits. Unless otherwise specified, we work over$k = \mathbb{C}$ from §43.1 onward.
