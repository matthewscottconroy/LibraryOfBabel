# 42.1.2 Representations as $k[G]$-Modules

One of the most elegant moves in algebra is to translate a problem about group actions into a problem about ring modules. The payoff is immediate: any theorem proved for modules over arbitrary rings applies at once to representations of groups. The key observation, which we now develop carefully, is that a representation of $G$ is *exactly the same data* as a module over the group algebra$k[G]$.

The *group algebra* $k[G]$ is defined as follows. As a$k$-vector space, it has a formal basis indexed by the elements of$G$:$k[G] = \bigoplus_{g \in G} k \cdot g$. An element of$k[G]$ is a formal$k$-linear combination$\sum_{g \in G} a_g g$ with$a_g \in k$, almost all zero. The ring multiplication extends the group law$k$-bilinearly:
$$\left(\sum_{g \in G} a_g g\right)\!\left(\sum_{h \in G} b_h h\right) = \sum_{g, h \in G} a_g b_h (gh).$$
One checks that this multiplication is associative (since the group law is), and the identity element of $G$ serves as the multiplicative identity of$k[G]$. When$G$ is finite,$k[G]$ is a finite-dimensional$k$-algebra of dimension$|G|$.

\begin{definition}
The \emph{group algebra} $k[G]$ is the$k$-algebra with underlying$k$-vector space$\bigoplus_{g \in G} k \cdot g$ and multiplication extending the group law$k$-bilinearly.
\end{definition}

The equivalence between representations and modules now runs as follows. Given a representation $\rho: G \to GL(V)$, define a left$k[G]$-module structure on$V$ by
$$\left(\sum_{g \in G} a_g g\right) \cdot v = \sum_{g \in G} a_g \rho(g)(v).$$
This extension is forced by $k$-linearity and the module axioms, and the module conditions reduce exactly to the conditions that$\rho$ is a group homomorphism:$(gh) \cdot v = g \cdot (h \cdot v)$ follows from$\rho(gh) = \rho(g)\rho(h)$, and$e \cdot v = v$ follows from$\rho(e) = \mathrm{id}_V$. Conversely, given a left$k[G]$-module$V$, define$\rho(g)(v) = g \cdot v$; the module axioms guarantee$\rho$ is a group homomorphism$G \to GL(V)$. These two constructions are inverse to each other, giving a bijection:
$$\{\text{representations of } G \text{ over } k\} \longleftrightarrow \{\text{left } k[G]\text{-modules}\}.$$

The key insight is that this correspondence is an equivalence of *categories*, not merely of objects. Subrepresentations become $k[G]$-submodules,$G$-equivariant maps become$k[G]$-module homomorphisms, irreducible representations become simple modules, and direct sums of representations become direct sums of modules. This means that all the machinery developed in the abstract study of modules — Schur's lemma, the Jordan–Hölder theorem, semisimplicity, the structure theory of Artinian rings — is immediately available and applicable to representation theory. We do not need to re-prove these theorems; we inherit them wholesale.

This is not merely notational convenience. The group algebra $k[G]$ is a concrete, finitely-presented$k$-algebra, and its ring-theoretic properties directly control the behavior of all representations of$G$. When$k[G]$ is semisimple (which, by Maschke's theorem, occurs precisely when$\mathrm{char}(k) \nmid |G|$), every representation decomposes into irreducibles. When$k[G]$ is isomorphic to a product of matrix algebras (by the Artin–Wedderburn theorem), the irreducible representations can be explicitly described. The group algebra is the algebraic "brain" of the theory.

**Example 1.** Let $G = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} = \{1, r\}$ and$k = \mathbb{R}$. The group algebra is$\mathbb{R}[\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}] = \mathbb{R} \cdot 1 \oplus \mathbb{R} \cdot r$, a two-dimensional algebra. The element$r$ satisfies$r^2 = 1$ in$G$, so in$k[G]$ we have$r^2 = 1$. The algebra$\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2 - 1) \cong \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}$ (via$x \mapsto (1,-1)$), showing$\mathbb{R}[\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}] \cong \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}$. The two simple modules correspond to the two 1-dimensional representations: the trivial representation (sending$r \mapsto 1$) and the sign representation (sending$r \mapsto -1$).

**Example 2.** Let $G = \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z} = \{1, r, r^2\}$ and$k = \mathbb{C}$. Then$\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}] \cong \mathbb{C}[x]/(x^3 - 1) \cong \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C}$ (since$x^3 - 1 = (x-1)(x-\omega)(x-\omega^2)$ splits completely over$\mathbb{C}$, where$\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$). The three simple modules are the three one-dimensional representations sending$r \mapsto 1, \omega, \omega^2$ respectively — consistent with the general fact (§42.2.4) that every irreducible of an abelian group over$\mathbb{C}$ is one-dimensional.

**Example 3.** Let $G = S_3$ and$k = \mathbb{C}$. Since$|S_3| = 6$ and$\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{C}) = 0$, Maschke's theorem guarantees$\mathbb{C}[S_3]$ is semisimple. By the Artin–Wedderburn theorem (§43.2),$\mathbb{C}[S_3] \cong \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C} \times M_2(\mathbb{C})$, corresponding to the three irreducible representations of$S_3$: the trivial (dimension 1), the sign (dimension 1), and the standard (dimension 2). The group algebra is 6-dimensional as a vector space, and$1^2 + 1^2 + 2^2 = 6$ checks out.

**Example 4.** The cyclic group $G = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ over a field$k$ of characteristic$p$ gives$k[\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}] \cong k[x]/(x^p - 1) = k[x]/(x-1)^p$ (since$x^p - 1 = (x-1)^p$ in characteristic$p$). This algebra has a unique maximal ideal (generated by$x-1$) and is local, not semisimple. The module$k[x]/(x-1)^p$ is indecomposable but not simple, illustrating precisely how Maschke's theorem fails when$\mathrm{char}(k) \mid |G|$: the representation$g \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ of$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ over$\mathbb{F}_p$ has an invariant subspace spanned by$e_1$ but no equivariant complement.

Throughout this chapter, we will shuttle freely between the language of representations and the language of $k[G]$-modules, using whichever perspective makes the argument most transparent. Schur's lemma will be proved once, in the module language, and its consequences for representation theory will be immediate. The Artin–Wedderburn theorem will be stated for rings and applied to$k[G]$. The module-theoretic perspective is not a luxury — it is the language in which the theory becomes fully organized.
