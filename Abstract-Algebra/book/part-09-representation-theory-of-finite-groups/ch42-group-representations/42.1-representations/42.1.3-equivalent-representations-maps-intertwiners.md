# 42.1.3 Equivalent Representations; $G$-Maps (Intertwiners)

In mathematics, the right notion of "sameness" between two objects is just as important as the objects themselves. For vector spaces, the right notion is linear isomorphism. For groups, it is group isomorphism. For representations, we need a notion that respects both the linear structure of the vector space and the group action simultaneously. The result is the concept of an *intertwiner*, or $G$-map.

Two representations $(V, \rho)$ and$(W, \sigma)$ of$G$ over$k$ are *equivalent* (or *isomorphic*) if there exists a$k$-linear isomorphism$\phi: V \xrightarrow{\sim} W$ that intertwines the two actions: for all$g \in G$ and$v \in V$,
$$\phi(\rho(g)(v)) = \sigma(g)(\phi(v)),$$
or in more compact notation, $\phi \circ \rho(g) = \sigma(g) \circ \phi$ for all$g \in G$. Such a map$\phi$ is called a *$G$-map*, a *$G$-module homomorphism*, or an *intertwining operator*. More generally, a$G$-map from$(V, \rho)$ to$(W, \sigma)$ is any$k$-linear map (not necessarily an isomorphism) satisfying this intertwining condition. In the language of$k[G]$-modules,$G$-maps are precisely$k[G]$-module homomorphisms.

\begin{definition}
A \emph{$G$-map} (or \emph{intertwiner}) from$(V,\rho)$ to$(W,\sigma)$ is a$k$-linear map$\phi: V \to W$ satisfying$\phi \circ \rho(g) = \sigma(g) \circ \phi$ for all$g \in G$. Two representations are \emph{equivalent} if there exists a$G$-map that is also a$k$-linear isomorphism.
\end{definition}

The condition for equivalence can be given a vivid matrix interpretation: if one chooses bases for $V$ and$W$, the intertwiner$\phi$ becomes an invertible matrix$P$, and the equivalence condition reads$P \rho(g) = \sigma(g) P$ for all$g \in G$, or equivalently$\sigma(g) = P \rho(g) P^{-1}$. Two matrix representations are therefore equivalent precisely when they differ by a *simultaneous* change of basis — the same matrix$P$ conjugates every group element at once. This is much stronger than asking that individual matrices$\rho(g)$ and$\sigma(g)$ be conjugate for each$g$ separately.

The set of all $G$-maps from$V$ to$W$ is denoted$\mathrm{Hom}_G(V, W)$ (or$\mathrm{Hom}_{k[G]}(V, W)$). It forms a$k$-vector space, and its dimension is a fundamental invariant of the pair$(V, W)$. Schur's lemma (§42.3) will show that when$V$ and$W$ are irreducible representations over an algebraically closed field,$\dim \mathrm{Hom}_G(V, W) = \delta_{V \cong W}$ — it is 1 if$V \cong W$ and 0 otherwise. This orthogonality is the engine behind all character theory.

The goal of representation theory can now be stated precisely: classify all representations of $G$ up to equivalence. For a given group$G$ and field$k$, the isomorphism classes of representations form a set, and the program is to list all irreducible ones and describe how any representation decomposes into these irreducibles. This is completely accomplished for finite groups over$\mathbb{C}$ by the character theory of Chapter 44.

**Example 1.** Consider $G = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} = \{1, r\}$ with two representations$\rho, \sigma: G \to GL_2(\mathbb{R})$:
$$\rho(r) = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \sigma(r) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
These are equivalent via $P = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$: indeed$P\rho(r) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \sigma(r)P$. Both representations decompose as the trivial representation direct-summed with the sign representation, just in different orderings.

**Example 2.** The representations $\rho, \sigma: \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z} \to GL_2(\mathbb{C})$ defined by$\rho(r) = \mathrm{diag}(\omega, \omega^2)$ and$\sigma(r) = \mathrm{diag}(\omega^2, \omega)$ (where$\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$) are equivalent via the permutation matrix$P = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$: both decompose as the direct sum of the two nontrivial 1-dimensional characters of$\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 3.** The two 2-dimensional complex representations of $S_3$ defined by the natural permutation action on$\{e_1, e_2, e_3\}$ restricted to the hyperplane$\{a_1 e_1 + a_2 e_2 + a_3 e_3 : a_1 + a_2 + a_3 = 0\}$ and by the rotation/reflection action on$\mathbb{R}^2$ (from Example 2 of §42.1.1) are equivalent: they are both realizations of the unique (up to isomorphism) 2-dimensional irreducible of$S_3$. The intertwiner between them can be written down explicitly using the eigenvectors of the$(123)$-matrix in each basis.

**Example 4.** Consider the group $G = \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ and the two one-dimensional complex representations$\rho_1(r^k) = i^k$ and$\rho_2(r^k) = (-i)^k$ (where$i = \sqrt{-1}$). These are *not* equivalent: a$G$-map$\phi: (\mathbb{C}, \rho_1) \to (\mathbb{C}, \rho_2)$ would be a scalar$\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$ satisfying$\lambda \cdot i^k = (-i)^k \cdot \lambda$ for all$k$, which requires$i^k = (-i)^k$ for all$k$. Taking$k=1$ gives$i = -i$, a contradiction. The four 1-dimensional representations of$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ (sending the generator to$1, i, -1, -i$ respectively) are mutually non-equivalent.

One important consequence of the intertwiner perspective is that it allows us to decompose representations into "isotypic components." If $V \cong V_1^{m_1} \oplus \cdots \oplus V_r^{m_r}$ is a direct sum of irreducibles, then by Schur's lemma the summands from different isomorphism classes cannot "interact" via any$G$-map:$\mathrm{Hom}_G(V_i, V_j) = 0$ for$i \neq j$. Each isotypic component$V_i^{m_i}$ is canonical (it depends only on$V$, not on the choice of decomposition), even though the decomposition within each isotypic component into individual copies of$V_i$ is not canonical when$m_i > 1$. This is a direct analogue of the primary decomposition of modules over principal ideal domains.
