# 42.1.4 Subrepresentations; Quotient Representations

Just as a subgroup of $G$ inherits the group structure, a subspace of a representation can inherit the representation structure — but only under an additional condition: the subspace must be stable under the group action. Without stability, the subspace does not carry its own representation of$G$.

\begin{definition}
A \emph{subrepresentation} of $(V, \rho)$ is a$k$-subspace$W \subseteq V$ that is \emph{$G$-stable}:$\rho(g)(W) \subseteq W$ for all$g \in G$. When$W$ is$G$-stable, the restriction$\rho|_W: G \to GL(W)$ defined by$(\rho|_W)(g) = \rho(g)|_W$ is itself a representation of$G$, called a \emph{subrepresentation} of$V$.
\end{definition}

In $k[G]$-module language, a subrepresentation is simply a$k[G]$-submodule of$V$. The$G$-stability condition says that the action of every group element, when restricted to$W$, stays within$W$. Note that since$\rho(g)$ is invertible,$\rho(g)(W) \subseteq W$ automatically implies$\rho(g)(W) = W$ (applying the condition to$g^{-1}$ gives$W \subseteq \rho(g)(W)$). The group acts on$W$ by isomorphisms, not merely injections.

The *quotient representation* is equally natural. If $W \subseteq V$ is a subrepresentation, the quotient vector space$V/W$ carries a well-defined$G$-action by$g \cdot (v + W) = \rho(g)(v) + W$. The$G$-stability of$W$ ensures this is independent of the coset representative: if$v' = v + w$ with$w \in W$, then$\rho(g)(v') = \rho(g)(v) + \rho(g)(w)$, and since$\rho(g)(w) \in W$, we have$\rho(g)(v') + W = \rho(g)(v) + W$. This gives a well-defined representation$G \to GL(V/W)$, and there is a short exact sequence of$G$-maps:
$$0 \to W \to V \to V/W \to 0.$$
The natural projection $\pi: V \to V/W$ is a$G$-map because$\pi(\rho(g)(v)) = \rho(g)(v) + W = g \cdot \pi(v)$.

The central question — whether this sequence splits in the category of representations — is the question of whether $V \cong W \oplus (V/W)$ as representations. This is not automatic; it requires finding a$G$-stable complement to$W$ in$V$, i.e., a subrepresentation$W' \subseteq V$ with$V = W \oplus W'$ as vector spaces. Maschke's theorem (§43.1) answers this affirmatively when$\mathrm{char}(k) \nmid |G|$.

\begin{definition}
A representation $V$ is \emph{irreducible} (or \emph{simple}) if$V \neq 0$ and the only$G$-stable subspaces of$V$ are$0$ and$V$ itself. Irreducible representations are the atomic building blocks; every semisimple representation decomposes as their direct sum.
\end{definition}

A representation is *irreducible* if it has no proper nonzero subrepresentations. Irreducible representations are the atomic building blocks: every "nice" representation (in the semisimple case) is a direct sum of irreducibles, and the classification problem reduces to enumerating and understanding these indecomposable pieces. The subrepresentation structure of a representation is the direct analogue of the composition series of a module: by the Jordan–Hölder theorem, any two composition series of a finite-dimensional representation have the same multiset of irreducible factors (the *composition factors*), though possibly in different orders. This multiset is an invariant of the representation.

It is also important to note a subtlety: a representation can be *indecomposable* (not isomorphic to a direct sum of two nonzero subrepresentations) without being irreducible. Indecomposable but reducible representations arise in the modular setting (characteristic $p \mid |G|$) and are a major focus of modular representation theory. In the semisimple setting (Maschke's theorem), this subtlety disappears: every indecomposable representation is irreducible.

**Example 1.** In the 2-dimensional permutation representation of $S_3$ on$\mathbb{C}^3$ restricted to... consider the full permutation representation$V = \mathbb{C}^3$ of$S_3$ with basis$\{e_1, e_2, e_3\}$ and$\rho(\sigma)(e_i) = e_{\sigma(i)}$. The subspace$W = \mathrm{span}\{e_1 + e_2 + e_3\}$ is$G$-stable (the sum is fixed by all permutations) and gives a subrepresentation isomorphic to the trivial representation. The hyperplane$W' = \{a_1 e_1 + a_2 e_2 + a_3 e_3 : a_1 + a_2 + a_3 = 0\}$ is also$G$-stable (since$\sum a_{\sigma(i)} = \sum a_i = 0$), giving the standard representation subrepresentation. We have$V = W \oplus W'$.

**Example 2.** The representation of $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ over$\mathbb{F}_p$ via$g \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ (where$g$ is the generator) has$W = \mathrm{span}\{e_1\}$ as the unique proper nonzero subrepresentation (since$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} e_1 = e_1$). The quotient$V/W$ is the trivial representation. However, there is no$G$-stable complement to$W$ in$V$ — the sequence$0 \to W \to V \to V/W \to 0$ does not split. The representation is indecomposable but not irreducible.

**Example 3.** The regular representation of $S_3$ over$\mathbb{C}$ (dimension 6) has many subrepresentations. The subspace spanned by$\sum_{g \in S_3} g$ is a 1-dimensional trivial subrepresentation. The augmentation ideal (spanned by$\{g - e : g \in S_3\}$, dimension 5) is another subrepresentation. By Maschke's theorem and Artin–Wedderburn, the full decomposition is$\mathbb{C}[S_3] \cong V_{\mathrm{triv}} \oplus V_{\mathrm{sgn}} \oplus V_{\mathrm{std}}^{\oplus 2}$ as left modules (each irreducible appearing with multiplicity equal to its dimension).

**Example 4.** For $G = D_4 = \langle r, s \mid r^4 = s^2 = e,\, srs = r^{-1} \rangle$ acting on$\mathbb{R}^2$ by rotations and reflections, the two coordinate axes$\mathrm{span}\{e_1\}$ and$\mathrm{span}\{e_2\}$ are *not* subrepresentations: the rotation$\rho(r)(e_1) = e_2 \notin \mathrm{span}\{e_1\}$. However, the 2-dimensional representation is irreducible over$\mathbb{R}$ (no proper nonzero$G$-stable subspace exists). Over$\mathbb{C}$, it decomposes into two 1-dimensional representations.

The relationship between subrepresentations and quotient representations underlies the Jordan–Hölder theorem, and the composition factors — the irreducible quotients appearing in any composition series — are the fundamental invariants. Even in the non-semisimple case, where representations may not split, the composition factors are well-defined and provide the best available coarse classification. The study of how they can be "assembled" into extensions of one another is the content of cohomological methods (Ext groups, projective covers) that become central in modular representation theory.
