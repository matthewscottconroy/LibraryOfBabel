# 42.1.5 Direct Sums, Tensor Products, and Dual Representations

Representations of a fixed group $G$ can be combined in several natural ways, each endowing the collection of representations with additional algebraic structure. Understanding these operations is essential both for constructing new representations from known ones and for appreciating the ring structure of the representation ring$R(G)$ that emerges in §44.4.4.

**Direct sums.** The *direct sum* $(V \oplus W, \rho \oplus \sigma)$ is defined by letting$G$ act on each summand independently:$(\rho \oplus \sigma)(g)(v, w) = (\rho(g)(v), \sigma(g)(w))$. In matrix terms (choosing bases that respect the direct sum decomposition), each$(\rho \oplus \sigma)(g)$ is block-diagonal with blocks$\rho(g)$ and$\sigma(g)$. Direct sums answer the question of how to combine two representations while keeping them "non-interacting." The dimension is additive:$\dim(V \oplus W) = \dim V + \dim W$, and the character is additive:$\chi_{V \oplus W}(g) = \chi_V(g) + \chi_W(g)$.

**Tensor products.** The *tensor product* $(V \otimes_k W, \rho \otimes \sigma)$ is more subtle:$(\rho \otimes \sigma)(g)(v \otimes w) = \rho(g)(v) \otimes \sigma(g)(w)$, extended by linearity. This action is well-defined because$G$ acts diagonally on simple tensors, and it gives a representation of dimension$(\dim V)(\dim W)$. The character of the tensor product is *multiplicative*: if$\rho(g)$ has eigenvalues$\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_m$ and$\sigma(g)$ has eigenvalues$\mu_1, \ldots, \mu_n$, then$(\rho \otimes \sigma)(g)$ has eigenvalues$\{\lambda_i \mu_j\}_{i,j}$, so$\chi_{V \otimes W}(g) = \chi_V(g) \chi_W(g)$.

**Dual representations.** The *dual representation* $(V^*, \rho^*)$ — also called the *contragredient* — is defined on the dual space$V^* = \mathrm{Hom}_k(V, k)$ by:$(\rho^*(g)(f))(v) = f(\rho(g^{-1})(v))$ for$f \in V^*$,$v \in V$. The inversion$g^{-1}$ is necessary to ensure$\rho^*$ is a group homomorphism rather than an anti-homomorphism:
$$(\rho^*(gh))(f)(v) = f(\rho((gh)^{-1})(v)) = f(\rho(h^{-1})\rho(g^{-1})(v)) = (\rho^*(g)(\rho^*(h)(f)))(v).$$
The character of the dual is $\chi_{V^*}(g) = \overline{\chi_V(g)}$ over$\mathbb{C}$, since the eigenvalues of$\rho(g)^{-T}$ are the reciprocals of those of$\rho(g)$, and these reciprocals are complex conjugates because the eigenvalues of$\rho(g)$ are roots of unity.

These operations interact in important ways. The natural isomorphism $\mathrm{Hom}_k(V, W) \cong V^* \otimes_k W$ as vector spaces lifts to an isomorphism of representations, where$G$ acts on$\mathrm{Hom}_k(V, W)$ by conjugation:$(g \cdot f)(v) = \rho_W(g)(f(\rho_V(g^{-1})(v)))$. Under this isomorphism, the$G$-fixed subspace$\mathrm{Hom}_k(V, W)^G$ consists precisely of the$G$-maps from$V$ to$W$: those$f$ satisfying$f \circ \rho_V(g) = \rho_W(g) \circ f$ for all$g$. This connects the tensor-hom adjunction to Frobenius reciprocity in Chapter 45.

The surprise here is that these operations give the set of isomorphism classes of finite-dimensional representations of $G$ the structure of a commutative ring, known as the *representation ring*$R(G)$: addition corresponds to direct sum, multiplication to tensor product, and the unit is the trivial representation. The *character* of a representation — the function$\chi_V(g) = \mathrm{tr}(\rho(g))$ — is a ring homomorphism from$R(G)$ into the ring of class functions on$G$ (with pointwise operations). This connection, developed in Chapter 44, is the cornerstone of the character theory of finite groups.

**Example 1.** Let $G = S_3$ with irreducibles: the trivial$\mathbf{1}$, the sign$\epsilon$, and the 2-dimensional standard$V$. Compute the tensor product$\epsilon \otimes V$: for$g \in S_3$,$\chi_{\epsilon \otimes V}(g) = \chi_\epsilon(g) \chi_V(g) = \mathrm{sgn}(g) \chi_V(g)$. Since$\chi_V(e) = 2$,$\chi_V((12)) = 0$,$\chi_V((123)) = -1$, multiplying by the sign gives$\chi_{\epsilon \otimes V}(e) = 2$,$\chi_{\epsilon \otimes V}((12)) = 0$,$\chi_{\epsilon \otimes V}((123)) = -1$. This is the same as$\chi_V$! So$\epsilon \otimes V \cong V$: the standard representation of$S_3$ is isomorphic to its twist by the sign. (This reflects the fact that$V \otimes \epsilon \cong V$ whenever the determinant character of$V$ is trivial.)

**Example 2.** For $G = \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ over$\mathbb{C}$ with characters$\chi_0, \chi_1, \chi_2$ sending the generator$r$ to$1, \omega, \omega^2$ respectively, the tensor product is:$\chi_i \otimes \chi_j = \chi_{i+j \mod 3}$. This is because$(\chi_i \otimes \chi_j)(r) = \omega^i \cdot \omega^j = \omega^{i+j}$. The representation ring$R(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}[\omega]$ is the ring of Eisenstein integers.

**Example 3.** For $G = S_3$, compute$V \otimes V$ where$V$ is the 2-dimensional standard representation. We have$\chi_{V \otimes V}(e) = 4$,$\chi_{V \otimes V}((12)) = 0^2 = 0$,$\chi_{V \otimes V}((123)) = (-1)^2 = 1$. Decomposing using the inner product:$\langle \chi_{V \otimes V}, \mathbf{1} \rangle = \frac{1}{6}(4 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot 0 + 2 \cdot 1) = 1$;$\langle \chi_{V \otimes V}, \epsilon \rangle = \frac{1}{6}(4 - 0 + 2) = 1$;$\langle \chi_{V \otimes V}, \chi_V \rangle = \frac{1}{6}(8 + 0 - 2) = 1$. Hence$V \otimes V \cong \mathbf{1} \oplus \epsilon \oplus V$. This decomposition can be verified by noting$\mathrm{Sym}^2(V) \cong \mathbf{1} \oplus V$ and$\wedge^2(V) \cong \epsilon$ (a 1-dimensional space since$\dim V = 2$).

**Example 4.** For any representation $V$ over$\mathbb{C}$, the natural map$V^* \otimes V \to \mathrm{End}(V) \cong \mathrm{Hom}_k(V, V)$ defined by$f \otimes v \mapsto (w \mapsto f(w) v)$ is a$G$-isomorphism. The trivial representation appears in$V^* \otimes V$ with multiplicity$\dim \mathrm{Hom}_G(V, V)$. When$V$ is irreducible, Schur's lemma gives$\dim \mathrm{Hom}_G(V, V) = 1$, so the trivial representation appears exactly once in$V^* \otimes V$. This is consistent with:$\langle \chi_{V^*} \chi_V, \mathbf{1} \rangle = \langle \chi_V, \chi_V \rangle = 1$ by orthonormality.

**Example 5.** For $G = Q_8 = \{\pm 1, \pm i, \pm j, \pm k\}$, the unique 2-dimensional irreducible representation$V$ satisfies$V \cong V^*$ (the representation is self-dual, because the character of$Q_8$ is real-valued). Checking:$\chi_V(g^{-1}) = \overline{\chi_V(g)}$, and for$Q_8$ all character values are real integers ($\chi_V(1) = 2$,$\chi_V(-1) = -2$,$\chi_V(\pm i) = \chi_V(\pm j) = \chi_V(\pm k) = 0$), so$\chi_{V^*} = \chi_V$ and$V^* \cong V$. This self-duality has consequences for the symmetry and antisymmetry of the tensor product$V \otimes V$.
