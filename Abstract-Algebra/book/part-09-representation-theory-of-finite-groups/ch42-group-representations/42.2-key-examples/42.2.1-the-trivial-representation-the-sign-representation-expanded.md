# 42.2.1 The Trivial, Sign, Permutation, and Standard Representations

Before developing the general theory, it is essential to have a gallery of concrete representations to think with. The examples in this section are not merely illustrations — they are the building blocks from which the entire subject is constructed. Understanding them deeply, including their subrepresentations and decompositions, is the prerequisite to everything in the chapters that follow.

## Setup

Throughout, $G$ is a finite group, $k$ is a field (most often $k = \mathbb{C}$), and $V$ is a finite-dimensional $k$-vector space. A representation $(\rho, V)$ of $G$ consists of a group homomorphism $\rho: G \to GL(V)$.

## The Trivial Representation

**Definition.** The *trivial representation* $\mathbf{1}$ of $G$ is the one-dimensional representation on $k$ where every group element acts as the identity: $\rho(g) = \mathrm{id}_k = 1$ for all $g \in G$.

In terms of the group algebra, $\mathbf{1}$ corresponds to the $k[G]$-module $k$ where every $g$ acts as multiplication by 1, or equivalently, where the augmentation ideal $I_G = \ker(\varepsilon: k[G] \to k)$ acts as zero.

**As a module.** $\mathbf{1} \cong k[G]/I_G$ as a $k[G]$-module, where $I_G$ is the augmentation ideal generated by $\{g - 1 : g \in G\}$.

**Example: $G = S_3$.** The trivial representation $\mathbf{1}$ is the one-dimensional space $\mathbb{C}$ where every permutation acts as 1. The vector $(1, 1, 1) \in \mathbb{C}^3$ spans the trivial subrepresentation of the permutation representation (below).

**Intertwining operators.** By Schur's lemma, $\mathrm{Hom}_G(\mathbf{1}, \mathbf{1}) \cong k$ (scalars), and $\mathrm{Hom}_G(\mathbf{1}, W) = W^G$ (the fixed-point subspace of $W$).

## The Sign Representation of $S_n$

**Definition.** The *sign representation* (or *alternating representation*) $\mathrm{sgn}$ of the symmetric group $S_n$ is the one-dimensional representation on $k$ where the permutation $\sigma$ acts by multiplication by the sign $\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) \in \{+1, -1\}$:
$$\rho(\sigma) = \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) \in k^\times.$$

This is well-defined because $\mathrm{sgn}: S_n \to \{+1, -1\}$ is a group homomorphism (the sign of a product equals the product of signs).

**Irreducibility.** $\mathrm{sgn}$ is irreducible since it is one-dimensional and $\rho$ is non-trivial (for $n \geq 2$, transpositions have sign $-1$).

**Distinction from trivial.** For $n \geq 2$, $\mathrm{sgn} \ncong \mathbf{1}$ because $\rho$ is not the trivial map. They become isomorphic only when restricted to $A_n$ (the alternating group), where the sign is always $+1$.

**Example: $G = S_3$.** The sign representation sends:
$$e \mapsto 1, \quad (12) \mapsto -1, \quad (13) \mapsto -1, \quad (23) \mapsto -1,$$
$$\quad (123) \mapsto 1, \quad (132) \mapsto 1.$$
The vector subspace spanned by $(1, -1, 0)$ or similar antisymmetric combinations in $\mathbb{C}^3$ relates to this representation via the standard decomposition below.

**Tensor product identity.** $\mathrm{sgn} \otimes \mathrm{sgn} \cong \mathbf{1}$ (since $(-1)(-1) = 1$), and $\mathrm{sgn} \otimes V \cong V \otimes \mathrm{sgn}$ twists any representation by the sign. This operation maps irreducible representations to irreducible representations, "twisting" them by the sign character. For $S_3$, the two-dimensional standard representation is isomorphic to its own twist: $\mathrm{std} \otimes \mathrm{sgn} \cong \mathrm{std}$ — because $\mathrm{std}$ contains both even and odd permutation behavior.

## The Permutation Representation

**Definition.** Let $G$ act on a finite set $X = \{x_1, \ldots, x_n\}$ (for instance, $G = S_n$ acting on $\{1, \ldots, n\}$). The *permutation representation* $k[X]$ is the $k$-vector space with basis $\{e_{x_1}, \ldots, e_{x_n}\}$ where $g$ acts by permuting basis vectors: $\rho(g)(e_{x_i}) = e_{g \cdot x_i}$.

As a $k[G]$-module, $k[X] \cong k[G/G_x]$ where $G_x$ is the stabiliser of any $x \in X$ (assuming $G$ acts transitively). In the case $X = G$ with $G$ acting by left multiplication, we obtain the *regular representation* $k[G]$ itself.

**Decomposition.** The permutation representation always contains the trivial representation as a subspace: the vector $\sum_{x \in X} e_x$ is fixed by all $g \in G$ (since $g$ permutes the basis vectors). The complement of this trivial subrepresentation is in general reducible.

**Example: $G = S_3$ acting on $X = \{1, 2, 3\}$.** The permutation representation $\mathbb{C}^3$ with basis $\{e_1, e_2, e_3\}$ has:
- Trivial subrepresentation: $\mathrm{span}\{e_1 + e_2 + e_3\}$.
- Complement: $\{(a,b,c) : a+b+c = 0\} = \mathrm{span}\{e_1 - e_2, e_2 - e_3\}$.

The complement is the standard representation (see below). Thus:
$$\mathbb{C}^3 \cong \mathbf{1} \oplus \mathrm{std}$$
as representations of $S_3$.

## The Standard Representation of $S_n$

**Definition.** The *standard representation* $\mathrm{std}$ of $S_n$ is the $(n-1)$-dimensional subrepresentation of $k^n$ consisting of vectors that sum to zero:
$$\mathrm{std} = \left\{(a_1, \ldots, a_n) \in k^n : \sum_{i=1}^n a_i = 0\right\}.$$
The symmetric group $S_n$ acts by permuting coordinates: $\sigma \cdot (a_1, \ldots, a_n) = (a_{\sigma^{-1}(1)}, \ldots, a_{\sigma^{-1}(n)})$.

**Irreducibility.** $\mathrm{std}$ is irreducible for $n \geq 2$. *Proof sketch:* Suppose $W \subseteq \mathrm{std}$ is a nonzero $G$-stable subspace. Let $v = \sum_i a_i e_i \in W$ be nonzero; WLOG $a_1 \neq a_2$. Applying the transposition $(12)$ and subtracting, $v - (12)v = (a_1 - a_2)(e_1 - e_2) \in W$. By applying all transpositions, we find $e_i - e_j \in W$ for all $i \neq j$. These span $\mathrm{std}$, so $W = \mathrm{std}$.

**Example: $G = S_3$, $n = 3$, $k = \mathbb{C}$.** $\mathrm{std}$ is 2-dimensional, with basis $f_1 = e_1 - e_2$ and $f_2 = e_2 - e_3$. The matrices of $(12)$ and $(123)$ in this basis are:
$$\rho((12)) = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \rho((123)) = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
One can verify these are $2 \times 2$ matrices satisfying the relations of $S_3$ and generating an irreducible representation.

**For $n = 4$:** $\mathrm{std}$ of $S_4$ is a 3-dimensional irreducible representation. Since $|S_4| = 24$, the character table has 5 conjugacy classes and 5 irreducible representations. We have found $\mathbf{1}$, $\mathrm{sgn}$, $\mathrm{std}$ (dim 3), and $\mathrm{sgn} \otimes \mathrm{std}$ (dim 3). The fifth representation has dimension $d_5$ with $1^2 + 1^2 + 3^2 + 3^2 + d_5^2 = 24$, giving $d_5 = 2$.

## The Relationship Among These Representations

Over $\mathbb{C}$, every representation decomposes as a direct sum of irreducibles. For $S_3$, there are exactly three irreducible representations: $\mathbf{1}$, $\mathrm{sgn}$, and $\mathrm{std}$. Their dimensions satisfy $1^2 + 1^2 + 2^2 = 6 = |S_3|$ (this is the theorem that the sum of squares of dimensions of irreducibles equals the group order, which will be proved in Chapter 44).

The regular representation $\mathbb{C}[S_3]$ (6-dimensional) decomposes as:
$$\mathbb{C}[S_3] \cong \mathbf{1}^{\oplus 1} \oplus \mathrm{sgn}^{\oplus 1} \oplus \mathrm{std}^{\oplus 2},$$
where each irreducible $V_i$ appears with multiplicity $\dim V_i$ (a general theorem). This can be verified: $1 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 2 = 6 = |S_3|$.
