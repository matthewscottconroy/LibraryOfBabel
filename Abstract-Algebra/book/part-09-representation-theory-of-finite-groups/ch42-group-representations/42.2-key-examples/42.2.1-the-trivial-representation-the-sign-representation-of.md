# 42.2.1 The Trivial Representation; the Sign Representation of $S_n$

Before developing the full general theory, it is instructive to work through the simplest and most ubiquitous examples. These low-dimensional representations reappear constantly throughout the subject, both as building blocks in decompositions and as test cases for general theorems.

**The trivial representation.** Every group $G$ admits the *trivial representation*: take$V = k$ (the field itself, a 1-dimensional vector space) and define$\rho(g) = \mathrm{id}_k$ for every$g \in G$. Every group element acts as the identity scalar map, so the kernel is all of$G$ — the representation captures no information about$G$ whatsoever. One might wonder why such a degenerate object deserves special attention.

The answer is threefold. First, the trivial representation is the multiplicative identity in the representation ring $R(G)$:$V \otimes k \cong V$ for any representation$V$. Second, the multiplicity of the trivial representation in any given representation$V$ has a clean formula: by the averaging principle, it equals$\dim V^G$, the dimension of the$G$-fixed subspace$V^G = \{v \in V : \rho(g)(v) = v \text{ for all } g \in G\}$. This fixed subspace controls "invariant theory." Third, Frobenius reciprocity (§45.2.1) relates the multiplicity of the trivial representation in induced representations to restriction, providing a key computational tool.

**The sign representation of $S_n$.** The symmetric group$S_n$ has a natural homomorphism$\mathrm{sgn}: S_n \to \{+1, -1\}$, the *signature* (or *sign*) map, defined by$\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) = (-1)^{N(\sigma)}$ where$N(\sigma)$ is the number of inversions (or equivalently, the parity of the number of transpositions in any decomposition of$\sigma$). The map$\mathrm{sgn}$ is a group homomorphism$S_n \to (\{+1,-1\}, \times) \hookrightarrow k^*$, giving a 1-dimensional representation$\epsilon: S_n \to GL_1(k) \cong k^*$ with$\epsilon(\sigma) = \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)$. This is the *sign representation* (also called the *alternating representation*).

The sign representation is nontrivial for $n \geq 2$ and different from the trivial representation: transpositions map to$-1$, not$+1$. Since it is 1-dimensional, it is automatically irreducible. The kernel is the alternating group$A_n = \ker(\mathrm{sgn}) \trianglelefteq S_n$.

The interaction between the trivial and sign representations with other representations of $S_n$ is pervasive. Tensoring any representation$V$ of$S_n$ with$\epsilon$ produces a new representation$\epsilon \otimes V$ in which even permutations act as before and odd permutations act with a sign change. This "twist by sign" operation is an involution on the representation ring of$S_n$, and it interchanges representations parametrized by a Young diagram$\lambda$ with those parametrized by the *transpose* (or conjugate) diagram$\lambda'$.

**Example 1.** In $S_2 = \{e, (12)\}$, the two 1-dimensional representations over$\mathbb{R}$ are exactly the trivial representation ($e \mapsto 1$,$(12) \mapsto 1$) and the sign representation ($e \mapsto 1$,$(12) \mapsto -1$). Every representation of$S_2$ decomposes as a direct sum of copies of these two. For example, the 2-dimensional permutation representation (on$\mathrm{span}\{e_1, e_2\}$ with$(12)(e_1) = e_2$) decomposes as the trivial representation (spanned by$e_1 + e_2$) plus the sign representation (spanned by$e_1 - e_2$, since$(12)(e_1 - e_2) = e_2 - e_1 = -(e_1 - e_2)$).

**Example 2.** In $S_3$, the sign representation$\epsilon$ satisfies:$\epsilon(e) = 1$,$\epsilon((12)) = \epsilon((13)) = \epsilon((23)) = -1$,$\epsilon((123)) = \epsilon((132)) = 1$. The twist of the standard representation$V$ by the sign gives a representation$\epsilon \otimes V$ whose character agrees with$\chi_V$ on the identity and 3-cycles, and disagrees on transpositions by sign. Computing:$\chi_{\epsilon \otimes V}(e) = 2$,$\chi_{\epsilon \otimes V}((12)) = (-1)(0) = 0$,$\chi_{\epsilon \otimes V}((123)) = (1)(-1) = -1$. This is the same as$\chi_V$, so$\epsilon \otimes V \cong V$: the standard representation of$S_3$ is self-conjugate (invariant under the sign twist).

**Example 3.** In $S_4$ (order 24), the sign representation and the trivial representation are both 1-dimensional. The full list of irreducibles includes a 1-dimensional trivial, a 1-dimensional sign, a 2-dimensional "mixed" representation, and two 3-dimensional representations$V_+$ and$V_-$ with$V_- \cong \epsilon \otimes V_+$. The dimension formula checks:$1 + 1 + 4 + 9 + 9 = 24$.

**Example 4.** For the alternating group $A_4$ (order 12), the sign homomorphism of$S_4$ restricted to$A_4$ is the trivial homomorphism (since$A_4 \subseteq \ker(\mathrm{sgn})$). Therefore$A_4$ has no sign representation. Its 1-dimensional representations are the three characters$A_4 \to \{1, \omega, \omega^2\} \subset \mathbb{C}^*$ that factor through$A_4/V_4 \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$, where$V_4 = \{e, (12)(34), (13)(24), (14)(23)\}$ is the Klein four-group. So the "role" of the sign representation in$A_4$ is replaced by the cube-root-of-unity characters.

Both the trivial and sign representations play foundational roles in the theory. They are the unique 1-dimensional representations of $S_n$ (for$n \geq 3$, since$[S_n, S_n] = A_n$ has index 2), and every 1-dimensional representation of any group$G$ must factor through$G/[G,G]$, the abelianization. The study of 1-dimensional representations is thus the study of the character group of$G/[G,G]$, a finite abelian group. For$S_n$,$G/[G,G] \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, giving exactly two 1-dimensional representations: trivial and sign.
