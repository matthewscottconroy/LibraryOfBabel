# 42.2.3 The Regular Representation $k[G]$

Among all representations of $G$, one stands out as the most fundamental: the *regular representation*, in which$G$ acts on itself by left multiplication. This single representation, though it is far from irreducible, contains all irreducible representations as subrepresentations — it is the universal home of representation theory.

**Construction.** Take $V = k[G]$, the group algebra viewed as a$k$-vector space with basis$\{g : g \in G\}$. Define the left regular representation by$\rho(g)(h) = gh$ for basis elements$h \in G$, extended linearly to all of$k[G]$. This is indeed a representation:$\rho(g)\rho(h)(x) = \rho(g)(hx) = g(hx) = (gh)x = \rho(gh)(x)$. The dimension is$|G|$, one basis vector per group element.

\begin{definition}
The \emph{regular representation} of $G$ over$k$ is the left$k[G]$-module$k[G]$, where$G$ acts by left multiplication.
\end{definition}

**The character of the regular representation.** The matrix of $\rho(g)$ in the basis$G$ is the permutation matrix of the bijection$h \mapsto gh$ on$G$. The diagonal entry corresponding to basis element$h$ is 1 if$gh = h$ (i.e.,$g = e$) and 0 otherwise. Therefore:
$$\chi_{\mathrm{reg}}(g) = \begin{cases} |G| & \text{if } g = e, \\ 0 & \text{if } g \neq e. \end{cases}$$
The regular representation has a massive character value at the identity (reflecting its large dimension) and vanishes everywhere else (reflecting the fact that no non-identity element has any fixed point in the left multiplication action).

**Containing all irreducibles.** The key result about the regular representation is that it contains every irreducible representation of $G$ as a subrepresentation, and each irreducible$V_i$ appears with multiplicity exactly$n_i = \dim V_i$. The proof follows from the Artin–Wedderburn decomposition (§43.2): under the isomorphism$k[G] \cong \prod_i M_{n_i}(k)$, the left$k[G]$-module$k[G]$ decomposes as$\bigoplus_i M_{n_i}(k)$, and each$M_{n_i}(k)$ as a module over itself decomposes as$n_i$ copies of the column space$k^{n_i}$. This gives$k[G] \cong \bigoplus_i V_i^{\oplus n_i}$.

One can verify this directly using the character formula. Using the multiplicity formula $m_i = \langle \chi_{\mathrm{reg}}, \chi_{V_i} \rangle$:
$$m_i = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} \chi_{\mathrm{reg}}(g) \overline{\chi_{V_i}(g)} = \frac{1}{|G|} \cdot |G| \cdot \overline{\chi_{V_i}(e)} = n_i.$$
The sum collapses to a single term ($g = e$) because$\chi_{\mathrm{reg}}(g) = 0$ for$g \neq e$, and$\chi_{V_i}(e) = n_i$. This calculation is a beautiful direct verification that each irreducible appears with multiplicity$n_i$.

**Example 1.** For $G = S_3$ over$\mathbb{C}$, the regular representation has dimension 6. Using the decomposition formula: the trivial representation (dimension 1) appears with multiplicity 1, the sign representation (dimension 1) appears with multiplicity 1, and the standard representation (dimension 2) appears with multiplicity 2. Check:$1 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 2 = 6 = |S_3|$. In matrix form, the 6-dimensional regular representation splits as$\mathbb{C}[S_3] \cong V_{\mathrm{triv}} \oplus V_{\mathrm{sgn}} \oplus V_{\mathrm{std}} \oplus V_{\mathrm{std}}$.

**Example 2.** For $G = \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} = \{1, r, r^2, r^3\}$ over$\mathbb{C}$, the regular representation has dimension 4. The four irreducibles are 1-dimensional characters$\chi_k(r^j) = i^{jk}$ for$k = 0,1,2,3$ (where$i = \sqrt{-1}$). Each appears with multiplicity 1. The Artin–Wedderburn decomposition is$\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}] \cong \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C}$ (four 1-dimensional matrix algebras), corresponding to the four 1-dimensional irreducibles. The decomposition can be made explicit via idempotents:$e_k = \frac{1}{4}\sum_{j=0}^{3} i^{-jk} r^j$ are the four minimal central idempotents.

**Example 3.** For $G = Q_8$ over$\mathbb{C}$, the regular representation has dimension 8. There are five irreducibles (since$Q_8$ has five conjugacy classes): four of dimension 1 (the four characters factoring through$Q_8/Q_8' \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$) and one of dimension 2. The decomposition is$\mathbb{C}[Q_8] \cong \mathbb{C}^{\oplus 4} \oplus V_2^{\oplus 2}$ as representations (since the 2-dimensional irreducible appears with multiplicity 2). Check:$1^2 + 1^2 + 1^2 + 1^2 + 2^2 = 8 = |Q_8|$.

**Example 4.** For $G = A_4$ over$\mathbb{C}$, the regular representation has dimension 12. The irreducibles are three 1-dimensional characters (values in$\{1, \omega, \omega^2\}$, each appearing with multiplicity 1) and one 3-dimensional standard representation (appearing with multiplicity 3). Check:$1 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot 3 = 12 = |A_4|$. The fact that$V_{\mathrm{std}}$ appears with multiplicity 3 reflects that a 3-dimensional irreducible must contribute$3^2 = 9$ dimensions' worth to the group algebra: 3 copies of a 3-dimensional space.

The regular representation is in this sense a universal object: if you can decompose it, you have essentially solved the entire classification problem for representations of $G$. In Chapter 43, we will see that the decomposition of$k[G]$ as a$k[G]$-module corresponds precisely to the Artin–Wedderburn isomorphism$k[G] \cong \prod_i M_{n_i}(k)$, where each matrix block records not just the existence but the full endomorphism structure of the corresponding irreducible. The regular representation is the canonical home of all representations of$G$.
