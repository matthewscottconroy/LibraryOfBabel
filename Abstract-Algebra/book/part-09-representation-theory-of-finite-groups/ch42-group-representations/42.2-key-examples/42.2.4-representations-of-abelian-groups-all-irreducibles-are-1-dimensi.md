# 42.2.4 Representations of Abelian Groups: All Irreducibles Are 1-Dimensional

Abelian groups enjoy a dramatically simplified representation theory over algebraically closed fields of appropriate characteristic: every irreducible representation is 1-dimensional. This result, simple to state and clean to prove, draws a sharp boundary between abelian and nonabelian groups in their representation-theoretic behavior.

**Statement and proof.** Suppose $G$ is a finite abelian group,$k$ is algebraically closed, and$\mathrm{char}(k) \nmid |G|$. Let$(V, \rho)$ be an irreducible representation of$G$ over$k$. Since$G$ is abelian, the operators$\rho(g)$ for different$g$ all commute:$\rho(g)\rho(h) = \rho(gh) = \rho(hg) = \rho(h)\rho(g)$. Because$k$ is algebraically closed and$V$ is finite-dimensional, each$\rho(g)$ has at least one eigenvalue$\lambda_g \in k$. The corresponding eigenspace$E_g = \ker(\rho(g) - \lambda_g \mathrm{id}_V)$ is nonempty, and by commutativity it is$G$-stable: if$\rho(g)v = \lambda_g v$, then$\rho(g)(\rho(h)(v)) = \rho(h)(\rho(g)(v)) = \lambda_g \rho(h)(v)$, so$\rho(h)(v) \in E_g$ for all$h \in G$. Since$V$ is irreducible and$E_g$ is a nonzero$G$-stable subspace, we must have$E_g = V$, meaning$\rho(g) = \lambda_g \mathrm{id}_V$ for every$g$. But then every 1-dimensional subspace of$V$ is$G$-stable, and since$V$ is irreducible,$\dim V = 1$.

\begin{theorem}
Let $G$ be a finite abelian group,$k$ algebraically closed with$\mathrm{char}(k) \nmid |G|$. Then every irreducible representation of$G$ over$k$ is 1-dimensional.
\end{theorem}

**The character group.** Since every irreducible is 1-dimensional, an irreducible representation of $G$ is exactly a group homomorphism$\chi: G \to k^*$. Since$g^{|G|} = e$ in$G$, we need$\chi(g)^{|G|} = 1$: every value is a$|G|$-th root of unity. Over$\mathbb{C}$, the roots of unity in$\mathbb{C}^*$ form the group$\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$ (embedded in$\mathbb{C}^*$ via$a \mapsto e^{2\pi i a}$). The collection of all homomorphisms$G \to \mathbb{C}^*$ forms a group under pointwise multiplication$(\chi_1 \chi_2)(g) = \chi_1(g)\chi_2(g)$, called the *Pontryagin dual* or *character group* of$G$, denoted$\hat{G}$.

\begin{proposition}
For a finite abelian group $G$,$\hat{G} \cong G$ (non-canonically). In particular,$|\hat{G}| = |G|$, consistent with the count$\sum_i n_i^2 = |G|$ with all$n_i = 1$.
\end{proposition}

The isomorphism $\hat{G} \cong G$ is not natural (it depends on choices of generators), but it exists for all finite abelian groups. The proof uses the structure theorem$G \cong \mathbb{Z}/n_1\mathbb{Z} \times \cdots \times \mathbb{Z}/n_k\mathbb{Z}$ and the fact that$\widehat{\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}} \cong \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ (via the$n$th roots of unity). This non-canonical isomorphism is the starting point for Fourier analysis on finite abelian groups.

**Contrast with the nonabelian case.** For a nonabelian group, not all irreducibles can be 1-dimensional. The dimension formula $\sum_i n_i^2 = |G|$ combined with the fact that the number of 1-dimensional irreducibles equals$|G/[G,G]|$ (the size of the abelianization) forces higher-dimensional irreducibles to exist whenever$|G/[G,G]| < |G|$, i.e., whenever$[G,G] \neq \{e\}$. The 1-dimensional representations of a group$G$ (abelian or not) are exactly the homomorphisms$G \to k^*$, which factor through the abelianization$G/[G,G]$.

**Example 1.** $G = \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} = \langle r \mid r^6 = e \rangle$ over$\mathbb{C}$. The six 1-dimensional irreducibles are$\chi_k: r^j \mapsto e^{2\pi i jk/6}$ for$k = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5$. We have$\chi_0 =$ trivial,$\chi_3: r \mapsto -1$ (order 2),$\chi_2: r \mapsto e^{2\pi i/3}$ and$\chi_4 = \bar{\chi}_2$ (order 3),$\chi_1: r \mapsto e^{2\pi i/6}$ (order 6, faithful),$\chi_5 = \bar{\chi}_1$. The dimension count:$6 \cdot 1^2 = 6 = |G|$. The group algebra$\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}] \cong \mathbb{C}^6$.

**Example 2.** $G = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ (the Klein four-group) over$\mathbb{C}$. The four 1-dimensional irreducibles correspond to the four elements of$\hat{G}$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|cccc}
 & $e$ &$a$ &$b$ &$ab$ \\
\hline
$\chi_1$ &$1$ &$1$ &$1$ &$1$ \\
$\chi_2$ &$1$ &$1$ &$-1$ &$-1$ \\
$\chi_3$ &$1$ &$-1$ &$1$ &$-1$ \\
$\chi_4$ &$1$ &$-1$ &$-1$ &$1$ \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
These four characters form the complete character table of $V_4 = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. Note all entries are$\pm 1$ (consistent with the eigenvalues of order-2 elements).

**Example 3.** $G = \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ over$\mathbb{C}$. The$n$ irreducibles are$\chi_k: r^j \mapsto \zeta_n^{jk}$ for$k = 0, \ldots, n-1$, where$\zeta_n = e^{2\pi i/n}$. The Fourier transform on$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is the map$f \mapsto \hat{f}$ with$\hat{f}(k) = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} f(j) \zeta_n^{-jk}$; this is precisely the expansion of a class function in the basis of irreducible characters.

**Example 4.** $G = (\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^2$ for a prime$p$. The$p^2$ irreducibles are all 1-dimensional, indexed by pairs$(a, b) \in (\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^2$:$\chi_{(a,b)}(x, y) = \zeta_p^{ax + by}$. This 2-dimensional Fourier analysis underlies the representation theory of$\mathbb{F}_{p^2}^+$ and appears in the character theory of the Heisenberg group over$\mathbb{F}_p$.

The 1-dimensionality of all irreducibles over algebraically closed fields reflects the maximally commutative structure of abelian groups. In contrast, for $G = S_3$ (the simplest nonabelian group), the abelianization is$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, giving 2 one-dimensional irreducibles, but the dimension formula forces a third irreducible of dimension 2:$1^2 + 1^2 + 2^2 = 6 = |S_3|$. Higher-dimensional irreducibles are the genuinely noncommutative phenomena that make the general theory rich.
