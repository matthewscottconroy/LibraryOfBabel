# 42.2 Key Examples

Definitions acquire meaning only through examples, and representation theory is no exception. This section builds a library of representations that will serve as test cases, intuition pumps, and recurring protagonists throughout the rest of the text. Each example is chosen not merely to illustrate the definition but to highlight a feature of the general theory: some are irreducible, some decompose, some are canonical constructions associated to every group.

The simplest representations are the one-dimensional ones. The trivial representation sends every $g \in G$ to the identity scalar $1 \in k^\times$; it exists for every group and serves as the additive identity under direct sum (contributing no interesting structure). For $S_n$, there is a second one-dimensional representation: the sign representation $\epsilon$, defined by $\epsilon(\sigma) = \text{sgn}(\sigma) \in \{+1, -1\}$. These two are the only one-dimensional representations of $S_n$ for $n \geq 2$, a fact that is itself a theorem (since one-dimensional representations are homomorphisms $G \to k^\times$, and the abelianization of $S_n$ for $n \geq 3$ is $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$).

The permutation representation and the closely related standard representation of $S_n$ are the central examples of the section. The permutation representation realizes $S_n$ on $\mathbb{C}^n$ by permuting the standard basis vectors: $\rho(\sigma)(e_i) = e_{\sigma(i)}$. This $n$-dimensional representation is not irreducible: the line $\text{span}(e_1 + \cdots + e_n)$ is always a trivial subrepresentation, and its orthogonal complement $V_0 = \{(a_1, \ldots, a_n) : \sum a_i = 0\}$ is an $(n-1)$-dimensional invariant subspace called the standard representation. For $S_3$, the standard representation is irreducible (it is 2-dimensional, and one can check it has no 1-dimensional subrepresentation), and it, together with the trivial and sign representations, provides a complete list of irreducibles for $S_3$.

The regular representation stands apart from all others in its universality. The group $G$ acts on the vector space $k[G]$ by left multiplication: $g \cdot e_h = e_{gh}$. This representation has dimension $|G|$, making it large, but its importance lies in the fact that every irreducible representation of $G$ appears as a subrepresentation of the regular representation. For abelian groups, the story of one-dimensional representations is particularly clean: since every group element $\rho(g)$ commutes with every other, Schur's lemma (established in Section 42.3) forces every irreducible to be one-dimensional, and the irreducibles of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ are exactly the $n$ characters $\rho_k([m]) = e^{2\pi i km/n}$.
