# 42.3.1 $G$-Maps between Irreducibles Are 0 or Isomorphisms

Among all the results in the basic theory of representations, Schur's lemma is arguably the most impactful relative to the length of its proof. It is a one-paragraph observation whose consequences cascade through the entire theory: the orthogonality of characters, the block structure of the endomorphism algebra, the count of irreducibles, and the structure of the Artin–Wedderburn decomposition all rest on Schur's lemma.

The intuition is this: irreducible representations have "no room" for a partial map. A $G$-map$\phi: V \to W$ between irreducibles is constrained to be either zero or an isomorphism because its kernel and image are both$G$-stable subspaces, and irreducibility leaves only trivial choices for such subspaces.

\begin{lemma}[Schur's Lemma, basic form]
Let $V$ and$W$ be irreducible representations of$G$ over any field$k$, and let$\phi: V \to W$ be a$G$-map. Then either$\phi = 0$ or$\phi$ is an isomorphism.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The kernel $\ker \phi$ is a$G$-stable subspace of$V$: if$v \in \ker \phi$, then for any$g \in G$,$\phi(\rho(g)(v)) = \sigma(g)(\phi(v)) = \sigma(g)(0) = 0$, so$\rho(g)(v) \in \ker \phi$. Since$V$ is irreducible,$\ker \phi$ is either$0$ or$V$. Similarly,$\mathrm{im}\, \phi$ is a$G$-stable subspace of$W$; since$W$ is irreducible,$\mathrm{im}\, \phi$ is either$0$ or$W$. If$\ker \phi = V$ then$\phi = 0$; otherwise$\ker \phi = 0$ and$\mathrm{im}\, \phi = W$, making$\phi$ bijective, hence an isomorphism.
\end{proof}

The elegant consequence is that $\mathrm{Hom}_G(V, W) = 0$ whenever$V \not\cong W$ are non-isomorphic irreducibles. This *orthogonality* of non-isomorphic irreducibles is the representation-theoretic version of the fact that non-isomorphic simple modules have no nonzero maps between them. It underpins the entire structure of character theory: the inner product formula$\langle \chi_V, \chi_W \rangle = \dim_{\mathbb{C}} \mathrm{Hom}_G(V, W)$ equals$0$ for non-isomorphic irreducibles and$1$ for identical ones (over$\mathbb{C}$, by the strong form in §42.3.2).

This connects to a broader principle: the category of representations of $G$ is a semisimple abelian category (under the hypotheses of Maschke's theorem), and in any such category the simple objects are mutually "orthogonal" in the sense captured by Schur's lemma. The lemma is essentially a theorem about simple modules over any ring, not just group algebras — it holds whenever$V$ is a simple$R$-module and$W$ is any$R$-module.

**Example 1.** Let $G = \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ over$\mathbb{C}$, with irreducibles$\chi_0, \chi_1, \chi_2$ (values$1, \omega, \omega^2$ on the generator$r$). A$G$-map$\phi: \chi_1 \to \chi_2$ is a$\mathbb{C}$-linear map$\mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}$, hence multiplication by some scalar$\lambda$. The equivariance condition requires$\lambda \chi_1(r) = \chi_2(r) \lambda$, i.e.,$\lambda \omega = \omega^2 \lambda$, i.e.,$\omega = \omega^2$. This is false, so$\lambda = 0$: the only$G$-map from$\chi_1$ to$\chi_2$ is zero. Schur's lemma is illustrated directly.

**Example 2.** Let $G = S_3$ and let$\phi: V_{\mathrm{std}} \to V_{\mathrm{std}}$ be a$G$-map, where$V_{\mathrm{std}}$ is the 2-dimensional standard representation. By the basic form of Schur's lemma,$\phi$ is either zero or an isomorphism. In fact, by the strong form (§42.3.2), since$\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed,$\phi = \lambda \mathrm{Id}$ for some$\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$. The 2-dimensional standard representation of$S_3$ has no nontrivial self-maps other than scalars. This means, in particular, that the decomposition of any representation as a direct sum of copies of$V_{\mathrm{std}}$ is "rigid" within the isotypic component.

**Example 3.** Let $G = \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ over$\mathbb{R}$, and consider the 2-dimensional irreducible rotation representation$V$ (sending the generator$r$ to the rotation matrix by$90°$). A$G$-map$\phi: V \to V$ must commute with the rotation:$\phi \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \phi$. Matrices that commute with$\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ are$\begin{pmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a \end{pmatrix}$ for$a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ — the real$2 \times 2$ matrices isomorphic to$\mathbb{C}$. So$\mathrm{End}_G(V) \cong \mathbb{C} \neq \mathbb{R}$. This shows that over a non-algebraically closed field,$\mathrm{End}_G(V)$ need not equal$k$; it can be a larger division algebra. The representation$V$ is irreducible over$\mathbb{R}$ but becomes reducible over$\mathbb{C}$ (into two 1-dimensional complex representations).

**Example 4.** Let $G = Q_8$ over$\mathbb{H}$ (the real quaternions). The 2-dimensional complex representation$V$ of$Q_8$ (via the Pauli matrices) has$\mathrm{End}_{Q_8}(V)_{\mathbb{R}} \cong \mathbb{H}$: the quaternion algebra appears as the endomorphism algebra of the unique 2-dimensional irreducible when working over$\mathbb{R}$. Over$\mathbb{C}$, however,$\mathrm{End}_{Q_8}(V)_{\mathbb{C}} \cong \mathbb{C}$ (Schur's lemma applies, since$\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed). This is why$Q_8$ has an "extra" flavor that$D_4$ does not: the Schur index of the 2-dimensional irreducible of$Q_8$ over$\mathbb{R}$ is 2 (related to the fact that the underlying division algebra is$\mathbb{H}$, of degree 2 over its center$\mathbb{R}$).

**Example 5.** Verify that there is no nonzero $G$-map from the trivial representation$\mathbf{1}$ of$S_3$ to the sign representation$\epsilon$. A$G$-map$\phi: k \to k$ is multiplication by$\lambda \in k$. Equivariance requires$\lambda \cdot 1 = \mathrm{sgn}(g) \cdot \lambda$ for all$g \in S_3$. Taking$g = (12)$ gives$\lambda = -\lambda$, hence$\lambda = 0$ (in characteristic$\neq 2$). So$\mathrm{Hom}_G(\mathbf{1}, \epsilon) = 0$, consistent with$\mathbf{1} \not\cong \epsilon$.

Schur's lemma is, in a precise sense, the only theorem you need to begin the proof of every major theorem in character theory. The proofs of the orthogonality relations, the Artin–Wedderburn decomposition, and Frobenius reciprocity all use Schur's lemma as their essential input. It is the irreducibility-to-scalar-maps bridge that makes linear algebra "remember" irreducibility, and it is what ensures that the character table of a finite group is a well-defined, square, invertible matrix.
