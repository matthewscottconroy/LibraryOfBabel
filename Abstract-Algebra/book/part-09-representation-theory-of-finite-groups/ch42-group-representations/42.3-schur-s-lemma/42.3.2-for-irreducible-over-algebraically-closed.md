# 42.3.2 $\mathrm{Hom}_G(V,V) = k$ for Irreducible$V$ over Algebraically Closed$k$

The basic form of Schur's lemma — that maps between non-isomorphic irreducibles are zero — already has significant consequences. But over an algebraically closed field, the lemma sharpens in a crucial way: every self-map of an irreducible is a scalar. This "strong form" is what makes character theory over $\mathbb{C}$ so precise, and it is the statement that must be checked carefully when working over other fields.

\begin{lemma}[Schur's Lemma, strong form]
Let $V$ be an irreducible representation of$G$ over an algebraically closed field$k$. Then every$G$-map$\phi: V \to V$ is a scalar multiple of the identity:$\phi = \lambda \cdot \mathrm{id}_V$ for some$\lambda \in k$. Equivalently,$\mathrm{End}_G(V) \cong k$ as$k$-algebras.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since $k$ is algebraically closed and$V$ is finite-dimensional, every linear map has at least one eigenvalue. Let$\lambda \in k$ be an eigenvalue of$\phi$. Then$\phi - \lambda \mathrm{id}_V$ is a$G$-map (a$k$-linear combination of$G$-maps) with a nonempty kernel — the$\lambda$-eigenspace of$\phi$. By the basic form of Schur's lemma, this$G$-map must be either zero or an isomorphism. Since its kernel is nonzero, it cannot be an isomorphism. Therefore$\phi - \lambda \mathrm{id}_V = 0$, i.e.,$\phi = \lambda \mathrm{id}_V$.
\end{proof}

The algebraic closure is essential: over $\mathbb{R}$, for instance, the irreducible 2-dimensional representation of$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ (by rotations) has$\mathrm{End}_G(V) \cong \mathbb{C}$ (real$2 \times 2$ matrices commuting with the rotation matrix), which is larger than$\mathbb{R}$. Over$\mathbb{C}$, the same representation becomes reducible (splitting into two 1-dimensional complex representations), and the endomorphism algebra of each 1-dimensional piece is$\mathbb{C}$.

The strong form implies that the endomorphism algebra $\mathrm{End}_G(V) = \mathrm{Hom}_G(V, V)$ is a$k$-algebra that is also a division algebra (every nonzero element is an isomorphism, by the basic form) and finite-dimensional over$k$. Over an algebraically closed field, the only finite-dimensional division algebra over$k$ is$k$ itself, so$\mathrm{End}_G(V) \cong k$.

**Absolute irreducibility.** A representation $V$ over$k$ is called *absolutely irreducible* if it remains irreducible over every field extension$k \subseteq k'$, equivalently if$\mathrm{End}_G(V) \cong k$. The strong form of Schur's lemma says: over an algebraically closed field, every irreducible is absolutely irreducible. Over non-algebraically-closed fields, an irreducible may fail to be absolutely irreducible — it might "split" into multiple pieces over the algebraic closure.

**Example 1.** Let $G = \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ and$V$ the 2-dimensional real representation$\rho(r^k) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(k\pi/2) & -\sin(k\pi/2) \\ \sin(k\pi/2) & \cos(k\pi/2) \end{pmatrix}$. Over$\mathbb{R}$,$V$ is irreducible (no 1-dimensional real subspace is preserved by the rotation). The$G$-maps$\phi: V \to V$ are matrices that commute with$\rho(r) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Solving, they are$\begin{pmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a \end{pmatrix}$, i.e.,$a + bi$ acting on$\mathbb{R}^2 \cong \mathbb{C}$. So$\mathrm{End}_G(V) \cong \mathbb{C} \neq \mathbb{R}$. Over$\mathbb{C}$,$V \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{C} \cong \mathbb{C}^2$ splits into the 1-dimensional representations$r \mapsto i$ and$r \mapsto -i$.

**Example 2.** Let $G = S_3$ over$\mathbb{C}$, and$V$ the 2-dimensional standard representation. Every$G$-map$\phi: V \to V$ is a$2 \times 2$ complex matrix commuting with both$\rho((12)) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and$\rho((123)) = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. The strong form of Schur's lemma guarantees$\phi = \lambda I$ for some$\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$. One can verify this directly: a matrix commuting with both a non-scalar symmetric matrix and a non-scalar non-symmetric matrix must be scalar.

**Example 3.** For $G = Q_8$ over$\mathbb{R}$, the 2-dimensional complex representation (Pauli matrices) can be viewed as a 4-dimensional real representation by writing$\mathbb{C}^2 \cong \mathbb{R}^4$. The endomorphism algebra of this 4-dimensional real representation is$\mathrm{End}_{Q_8}(V)_{\mathbb{R}} \cong \mathbb{H}$ (the real quaternion algebra), which has dimension 4 over$\mathbb{R}$. Over$\mathbb{C}$, this 4-dimensional real representation splits into two copies of the 2-dimensional complex irreducible, and$\mathrm{End}_{Q_8}(V)_{\mathbb{C}} \cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$, consistent with two copies of a single irreducible.

**Example 4.** For $G = A_4$ over$\mathbb{Q}$, the three 1-dimensional characters (which take values$1, \omega, \omega^2$ for$\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$) are defined over$\mathbb{Q}(\omega)$, not over$\mathbb{Q}$ itself. Over$\mathbb{Q}$, they appear combined into a 2-dimensional$\mathbb{Q}$-irreducible$V$ with$\mathrm{End}_{A_4}(V) \cong \mathbb{Q}(\omega) = \mathbb{Q}[x]/(x^2 + x + 1)$ (a degree-2 extension of$\mathbb{Q}$). Over$\mathbb{C}$, this 2-dimensional rational representation splits into two 1-dimensional complex irreducibles. The Schur index (the degree of$\mathrm{End}_G(V)$ over its center) in this case is 1, but the field of definition is$\mathbb{Q}(\omega)$, not$\mathbb{Q}$.

The strong form of Schur's lemma is the foundation for the orthogonality of characters. Knowing that $\mathrm{End}_G(V) \cong k$ means that the only freedom in a self-map of an irreducible is a global scalar — the representation is "absolutely irreducible" and cannot be further decomposed even over larger fields. This rigidity is what makes the character theory over$\mathbb{C}$ so complete: each irreducible is uniquely pinned down by its character, and the inner products of characters compute exactly the multiplicities of irreducibles in any representation. The entire machine of orthogonality relations rests on the simple fact that$\mathrm{End}_G(V) = \mathbb{C}$.
