# 42.3.3 Consequences for Structure of Representations

Schur's lemma has immediate and far-reaching structural consequences that form the backbone of the rest of the theory. The first direct consequence — that $\mathrm{Hom}_G(V, W) = 0$ for non-isomorphic irreducibles — implies that in any direct sum of irreducibles, there are no nonzero$G$-maps between summands from different isomorphism classes. Taken together, the two forms of Schur's lemma completely determine the structure of the endomorphism algebra of any semisimple representation.

**The endomorphism algebra of a direct sum of irreducibles.** Suppose $V \cong V_1^{m_1} \oplus V_2^{m_2} \oplus \cdots \oplus V_r^{m_r}$, where$V_i^{m_i}$ denotes$m_i$ copies of the irreducible$V_i$ and the$V_i$ are mutually non-isomorphic, over an algebraically closed field$k$. A$G$-map$\phi: V \to V$ must map each isotypic component$V_i^{m_i}$ into itself (since$\mathrm{Hom}_G(V_i, V_j) = 0$ for$i \neq j$). Within the$i$-th block,$\phi$ acts on$m_i$ copies of$V_i$, and by the strong form of Schur's lemma, each individual$G$-map$V_i \to V_i$ is a scalar. Therefore:

\begin{proposition}
Over an algebraically closed field $k$, if$V \cong V_1^{m_1} \oplus \cdots \oplus V_r^{m_r}$ with the$V_i$ mutually non-isomorphic irreducibles, then
$$\mathrm{End}_G(V) \cong M_{m_1}(k) \times M_{m_2}(k) \times \cdots \times M_{m_r}(k).$$
Any $G$-map$\phi: V \to V$ is block-diagonal, with the$i$-th block being an arbitrary$m_i \times m_i$ matrix of scalars acting on the$m_i$ copies of$V_i$.
\end{proposition}

The block structure means that $G$-maps respect the isotypic decomposition: the isotypic component$V_i^{m_i}$ is a canonical subrepresentation (independent of any choices in the decomposition), and a$G$-map may mix the$m_i$ copies of$V_i$ within this component but cannot send anything from one isotypic component to another.

**Canonical decomposition into isotypic components.** This block structure implies that the decomposition of $V$ into isotypic components$V = \bigoplus_i V_i^{m_i}$ is *canonical*: it does not depend on any choices. The isotypic component of$V$ corresponding to the irreducible$V_i$ is the image of the projection$\pi_i: V \to V$ given by the idempotent in the center of$\mathrm{End}_G(V)$ corresponding to the$i$-th factor of$M_{m_1}(k) \times \cdots \times M_{m_r}(k)$. However, the further decomposition of$V_i^{m_i}$ into individual copies of$V_i$ is *not* canonical when$m_i > 1$: there are many ways to choose$m_i$ copies of$V_i$ inside$V_i^{m_i}$, and they are permuted by the$GL_{m_i}(k)$ automorphisms of the$i$-th block.

**Connection to Artin–Wedderburn.** The endomorphism algebra calculation is precisely the algebraic shadow of the Artin–Wedderburn decomposition. The endomorphism algebra $\mathrm{End}_{k[G]}(k[G]) \cong k[G]^{\mathrm{op}}$ (right multiplication by a fixed element is a left-module endomorphism) decomposes as a product of matrix algebras indexed by the irreducibles of$G$, and this is the key to the Artin–Wedderburn theorem for$k[G]$ (§43.2).

**Example 1.** Let $G = S_3$ and$V = \mathbb{C}[S_3]$ (the regular representation). The decomposition is$V \cong V_{\mathrm{triv}} \oplus V_{\mathrm{sgn}} \oplus V_{\mathrm{std}} \oplus V_{\mathrm{std}}$, with$m_1 = m_2 = 1$ and$m_3 = 2$. By the proposition:
$$\mathrm{End}_{S_3}(\mathbb{C}[S_3]) \cong M_1(\mathbb{C}) \times M_1(\mathbb{C}) \times M_2(\mathbb{C}) \cong \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C} \times M_2(\mathbb{C}).$$
Since $\mathrm{End}_{k[G]}(k[G]) \cong k[G]^{\mathrm{op}}$ and$\mathbb{C}[S_3]$ is commutative up to the matrix algebra structure... more precisely,$\mathbb{C}[S_3] \cong \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C} \times M_2(\mathbb{C})$ by Artin–Wedderburn, consistent with the above.

**Example 2.** Let $G = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ (the Klein four-group) with$V = \chi_1 \oplus \chi_1 \oplus \chi_2$ (two copies of$\chi_1$ and one copy of$\chi_2$, where$\chi_1, \chi_2$ are distinct 1-dimensional irreducibles). The endomorphism algebra is$\mathrm{End}_G(V) \cong M_2(\mathbb{C}) \times M_1(\mathbb{C})$. A$G$-map$\phi: V \to V$ acts by a$2 \times 2$ matrix on the$\chi_1$-isotypic component and by a scalar on the$\chi_2$-isotypic component.

**Example 3.** Consider $V = V_{\mathrm{std}}^{\oplus 2}$ for$G = S_3$ (two copies of the 2-dimensional standard representation). Then$\mathrm{End}_{S_3}(V) \cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$. A basis for$V$ might be$(e_1, e_2, f_1, f_2)$ where$(e_1, e_2)$ and$(f_1, f_2)$ span the two copies of$V_{\mathrm{std}}$. An endomorphism is given by a matrix$\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ of scalars mapping$(e_1, e_2) \mapsto (a e_1 + a e_2, c f_1 + c f_2)$... more precisely, the$2 \times 2$ matrix$\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ acts by$e_i \mapsto a e_i + c f_i$ and$f_i \mapsto b e_i + d f_i$. Any such matrix is a$G$-endomorphism.

**Example 4.** For $G = A_4$ and$V = V_1 \oplus V_2 \oplus V_3 \oplus V_4^{\oplus 2}$ (adding an extra copy of the 3-dimensional irreducible$V_4$), the endomorphism algebra is$\mathrm{End}_{A_4}(V) \cong \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C} \times M_2(\mathbb{C})$. The three 1-dimensional irreducibles each appear with multiplicity 1, contributing$\mathbb{C}$ blocks; the 3-dimensional irreducible appears with multiplicity 2, contributing an$M_2(\mathbb{C})$ block.

The irreducible representations are not merely building blocks; they generate a complete, orthogonal system of "directions" in representation space, and Schur's lemma is the precise statement that these directions do not interact. The endomorphism algebra calculation shows concretely how the decomposition into isotypic components "diagonalizes" the algebra of self-maps: instead of an arbitrary matrix algebra of size $\dim V$, one gets a product of smaller matrix algebras of sizes$m_1, \ldots, m_r$. This reduction in complexity is the core of the power of semisimple representation theory.
