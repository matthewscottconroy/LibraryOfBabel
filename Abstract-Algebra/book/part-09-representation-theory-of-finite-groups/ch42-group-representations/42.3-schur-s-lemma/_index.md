# 42.3 Schur's Lemma

Every intertwining map between two irreducible representations is either zero or an isomorphism. This is Schur's lemma, and despite occupying only two lines of proof — the kernel and image of an intertwiner are both subrepresentations, so by irreducibility each is trivial or the whole space — its consequences pervade the entire theory. The lemma was formulated by Issai Schur in 1905 and immediately became one of the foundational tools of representation theory, a fact that has not changed in the century since.

The real power of Schur's lemma emerges over an algebraically closed field, where a second conclusion holds: any $G$-equivariant endomorphism of an irreducible representation $V$ is a scalar multiple of the identity. The proof is a one-line eigenvalue argument: over an algebraically closed field $k$, every linear operator on a finite-dimensional space has an eigenvalue $\lambda$, and $\phi - \lambda \, \text{id}$ is also an intertwiner. If $\phi$ is not a scalar, then $\phi - \lambda\, \text{id}$ is a nonzero non-isomorphism between two irreducibles, contradicting the first part. Hence $\phi = \lambda\,\text{id}$. In module-theoretic terms, this says that $\text{End}_{k[G]}(V) \cong k$ when $V$ is irreducible and $k$ is algebraically closed.

The consequences of this second form are numerous and striking. If $G$ is abelian and $k$ is algebraically closed, then for any irreducible representation $V$, every operator $\rho(g)$ is itself a scalar (it is a $G$-equivariant endomorphism, since $G$ is abelian). But then every one-dimensional subspace is $G$-stable, and irreducibility forces $\dim V = 1$. This gives a complete structural theorem: over $\mathbb{C}$, every irreducible representation of a finite abelian group is one-dimensional, and there are exactly $|G|$ of them. More generally, Schur's lemma constrains the algebra $\text{Hom}_G(V, W)$ for any pair of irreducibles $V, W$: this space is zero if $V \not\cong W$ and one-dimensional (isomorphic to $k$) if $V \cong W$. This fact underlies the orthogonality relations of the next chapter and the semisimplicity structure of the group algebra explored in Chapter 43.
