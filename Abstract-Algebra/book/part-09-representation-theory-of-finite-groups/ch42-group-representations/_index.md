# Chapter 42 — Group Representations

Abstract groups, as we have studied them, capture symmetry in its purest form: a set of transformations closed under composition, inversion, and containing an identity. Yet this abstraction can be a barrier. To understand a group concretely — to compute with it, to see its action, to connect it to other branches of mathematics — it helps enormously to realize the group as a collection of matrices. Representation theory is precisely this realization. A representation of a group $G$ is a homomorphism $\rho: G \to GL(V)$ from $G$ into the group of invertible linear transformations of a vector space $V$. Every element of $G$ becomes an invertible matrix; every group-theoretic relation becomes a linear-algebraic identity. The abstract becomes visible.

There is a second, equivalent point of view that will prove equally powerful. A representation of $G$ on $V$ is the same data as making $V$ into a module over the group algebra $k[G]$, the algebra whose basis elements are the group elements and whose multiplication extends the group law. This equivalence — representation as homomorphism, representation as module — threads through the entire chapter and unifies the language of linear algebra with the language of ring theory developed earlier. The group algebra $k[G]$ will serve as the central algebraic object whose module theory is exactly the representation theory of $G$.

The most fundamental question one can ask about a representation is whether it breaks apart. A subrepresentation is a $G$-stable subspace $W \subseteq V$: one where $\rho(g)(W) \subseteq W$ for every $g \in G$. If $V$ has no proper nonzero subrepresentation, it is called irreducible, and these are the atomic building blocks from which all representations are assembled. Understanding a representation means understanding how it decomposes into irreducibles, and much of this chapter is devoted to constructing enough examples to make this decomposition vivid. The trivial representation, the sign representation of $S_n$, the permutation representation, the standard representation of $S_3$, and the regular representation of $G$ on $k[G]$ itself all make appearances, each illuminating a different feature of the general theory.

The chapter culminates in Schur's lemma, a result of deceptive simplicity and vast consequence. It asserts that any $G$-equivariant linear map between irreducible representations is either zero or an isomorphism, and that over an algebraically closed field such as $\mathbb{C}$, any equivariant endomorphism of an irreducible representation is a scalar multiple of the identity. The proof is a two-line exercise in eigenvalue theory, yet the consequences are far-reaching: irreducible representations of abelian groups over $\mathbb{C}$ must be one-dimensional, the algebra of endomorphisms of an irreducible representation is a division algebra, and the decomposition of a representation into irreducibles carries with it a canonical multiplicity structure. Schur's lemma is the silent engine behind every calculation in the chapters that follow.
