# 43.1.1 Statement: $\mathrm{char}(k) \nmid |G| \Rightarrow$ Complete Reducibility

The central structural result of classical representation theory is Maschke's theorem, which answers the question: when can every representation be decomposed into irreducible pieces? The answer is controlled by a single arithmetic condition, elegant in its simplicity and decisive in its consequences.

\begin{theorem}[Maschke's Theorem]
Let $G$ be a finite group and$k$ a field with$\mathrm{char}(k) \nmid |G|$ (i.e., the characteristic of$k$ does not divide$|G|$, which is automatic when$\mathrm{char}(k) = 0$). Then every representation of$G$ over$k$ is \emph{completely reducible}: it decomposes as a direct sum of irreducible subrepresentations.
\end{theorem}

In module language, the condition is equivalent to saying that $k[G]$ is a \emph{semisimple ring} — every$k[G]$-module is a direct sum of simple modules. The most common setting is$k = \mathbb{C}$ (or any field of characteristic zero), where the condition$\mathrm{char}(k) \nmid |G|$ is automatically satisfied. More generally, any field$k$ of characteristic$p$ works as long as$p \nmid |G|$.

\begin{example}
Consider representations of $S_3$ over$\mathbb{F}_5$. Since$|S_3| = 6$ and$5 \nmid 6$, Maschke's theorem applies and every representation of$S_3$ over$\mathbb{F}_5$ is completely reducible. In contrast, representations of$S_3$ over$\mathbb{F}_2$ or$\mathbb{F}_3$ are \emph{not} guaranteed to be completely reducible, since$2 \mid 6$ and$3 \mid 6$.
\end{example}

The characteristic condition is the precise threshold. The theorem gives a complete, clean answer in the "nonmodular" case: no representation is indecomposable without being irreducible, no extension problems arise, and the structure of any representation is fully determined by the multiplicity of each irreducible as a direct summand. This is a remarkable and somewhat surprising result — the group symmetry is so constraining that it forces every subrepresentation to have a complementary subrepresentation.

In a general module over a general ring, submodules need not split. Consider the $\mathbb{Z}$-module$\mathbb{Z}$: the submodule$2\mathbb{Z}$ has no complement, since any complement would have to be a copy of$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, which is not a submodule of$\mathbb{Z}$. The category of modules over a ring can be arbitrarily complicated, with indecomposable modules of any finite length. Maschke's theorem identifies the precise arithmetic condition that makes the representation category of$G$ maximally well-behaved: semisimplicity is the condition that collapses all this complexity.

\begin{proposition}
A representation $V$ of$G$ over$\mathbb{C}$ is completely reducible if and only if every$G$-stable subspace$W \subseteq V$ has a$G$-stable complement$W'$ with$V = W \oplus W'$.
\end{proposition}

The proof, given in §43.1.2, is constructive and reveals the mechanism: averaging over the group elements produces the required splitting. The idea is to take any linear complement (which exists by ordinary linear algebra) and "symmetrize" it to produce a $G$-equivariant one. This averaging process requires dividing by$|G|$, which is why the theorem fails when$\mathrm{char}(k) \mid |G|$ — the averaging procedure simply cannot be carried out.

\begin{example}
Let $G = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} = \{0, 1\}$ act on$V = \mathbb{C}^2$ by$\rho(1) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. The subspace$W = \mathrm{span}\{e_1\}$ is$G$-stable (since$\rho(1)(e_1) = e_1$), and we seek a$G$-stable complement. The averaging procedure starts with the projection$p: V \to W$ sending$e_1 \mapsto e_1$,$e_2 \mapsto 0$, and averages:$\bar{p} = \frac{1}{2}[\mathrm{Id} \circ p \circ \mathrm{Id} + \rho(1) \circ p \circ \rho(1)^{-1}]$. Computing$\rho(1)^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$ and carrying out the computation yields$\bar{p} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}$. The kernel$\ker\bar{p} = \mathrm{span}\{e_1 - 2e_2\}$ is the$G$-stable complement, and one checks directly that$\rho(1)(e_1 - 2e_2) = e_1 + e_2 - 2e_2 = e_1 - e_2$... wait, let us verify:$V = \mathbb{C}^2$ decomposes as$W \oplus \ker\bar{p}$, and the complement is$G$-stable precisely because$\bar{p}$ is$G$-equivariant.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
Maschke's theorem extends to compact topological groups if one replaces the sum $\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g \in G}$ by integration against the normalized Haar measure. For example, every continuous finite-dimensional representation of a compact Lie group (such as$U(n)$ or$SO(3)$) is completely reducible. The key is that the Haar measure provides the averaging mechanism. For non-compact groups like$\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})$ or infinite discrete groups, no such averaging exists, and indecomposable non-irreducible representations are common.
\end{remark}

The significance of complete reducibility for the structure of the representation theory cannot be overstated. Once one knows every representation is a direct sum of irreducibles, the entire classification problem reduces to: (a) classifying all irreducible representations, and (b) determining which direct sums occur. Problem (b) is answered by the multiplicity formula using characters (Chapter 44). Problem (a) is answered, in structural terms, by the Artin–Wedderburn theorem (§43.2), which shows that the irreducibles correspond exactly to the simple summands of the group algebra $k[G]$ and that their number equals the number of conjugacy classes of$G$.
