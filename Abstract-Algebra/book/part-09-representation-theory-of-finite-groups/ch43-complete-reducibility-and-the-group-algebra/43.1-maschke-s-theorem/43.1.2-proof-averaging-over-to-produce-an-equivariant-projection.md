# 43.1.2 Proof: Averaging over $G$ to Produce an Equivariant Projection

The proof of Maschke's theorem has a single elegant idea at its core: \emph{averaging over the group}. Once understood, the proof is both completely transparent and strikingly powerful, serving as a template for many other constructions in representation theory.

\begin{theorem}[Maschke, proof]
Let $G$ be a finite group and$k$ a field with$\mathrm{char}(k) \nmid |G|$. Let$(V, \rho)$ be a finite-dimensional representation of$G$ over$k$, and let$W \subseteq V$ be a$G$-stable subspace. Then there exists a$G$-stable subspace$W' \subseteq V$ such that$V = W \oplus W'$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
It suffices to find a $G$-equivariant projection$\bar{p}: V \to W$ — a$k$-linear map satisfying$\bar{p}^2 = \bar{p}$,$\mathrm{im}\,\bar{p} = W$, and$\bar{p}(\rho(g)v) = \rho(g)\bar{p}(v)$ for all$g \in G$,$v \in V$. Given such a map,$\ker\bar{p}$ is a$G$-stable subspace (since$\bar{p}$ is$G$-equivariant), and standard linear algebra gives$V = \mathrm{im}\,\bar{p} \oplus \ker\bar{p} = W \oplus \ker\bar{p}$.

Since $W \subseteq V$ is a subspace of a vector space, there exists a$k$-linear projection$p: V \to W$ (not necessarily$G$-equivariant). Now define the \emph{averaged projection}:
$$\bar{p} = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} \rho(g) \circ p \circ \rho(g)^{-1}.$$
Division by $|G|$ is valid in$k$ precisely because$\mathrm{char}(k) \nmid |G|$, so$|G| \neq 0$ in$k$.

We verify three properties:

\textbf{(i) $G$-equivariance.} For any$h \in G$ and$v \in V$:
\begin{align*}
\rho(h) \circ \bar{p}(v) &= \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} \rho(h)\rho(g) p(\rho(g)^{-1} v) \\
&= \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} \rho(hg) p(\rho(g)^{-1} v).
\end{align*}
Substituting $g' = hg$ (so$g = h^{-1}g'$ and$\rho(g)^{-1} = \rho(h^{-1}g')^{-1} = \rho(g'^{-1}h)$):
$$= \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g' \in G} \rho(g') p(\rho(g'^{-1}h) v) = \bar{p}(\rho(h)v).$$
Thus $\bar{p} \circ \rho(h) = \rho(h) \circ \bar{p}$ for all$h$.

\textbf{(ii) Image contained in $W$.} For each$g \in G$, since$p$ maps into$W$ and$W$ is$G$-stable,$\rho(g)(p(\rho(g)^{-1}v)) \in \rho(g)(W) \subseteq W$. Hence$\bar{p}(v) \in W$ for all$v$.

\textbf{(iii) $\bar{p}$ fixes$W$ pointwise.} For$w \in W$, since$W$ is$G$-stable,$\rho(g)^{-1}(w) \in W$, so$p(\rho(g)^{-1}(w)) = \rho(g)^{-1}(w)$ (as$p$ is a projection onto$W$ that fixes$W$). Therefore:
$$\bar{p}(w) = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} \rho(g)(\rho(g)^{-1}(w)) = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} w = w.$$

Properties (ii) and (iii) give $\bar{p}^2 = \bar{p}$ (since$\bar{p}$ fixes every element of its own image). The kernel$W' = \ker\bar{p}$ is$G$-stable by (i), and$V = W \oplus W'$ by standard projection theory. Applying this argument inductively on dimension gives a complete decomposition into irreducibles.
\end{proof}

The averaging trick admits several illuminating perspectives. First, the operator $T \mapsto \frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g \in G} \rho(g) T \rho(g)^{-1}$ is the \emph{Reynolds operator} — it projects any linear map onto the space of$G$-equivariant maps. This operator appears throughout the theory (for example, in computing$\mathrm{Hom}_G(V,W)$ and in the proof of character orthogonality) and is the finite-group analogue of integration against the Haar measure for compact groups.

\begin{example}
Let $G = S_3$ act on$V = \mathbb{C}^3$ by permuting coordinates:$\rho(\sigma)(e_i) = e_{\sigma(i)}$. The subspace$W = \{(x,y,z) : x+y+z=0\}$ is$G$-stable (the standard representation). Take$p: V \to W$ to be the projection along$\mathrm{span}\{(1,1,1)\}$:$p(x,y,z) = (x - \tfrac{x+y+z}{3}, y - \tfrac{x+y+z}{3}, z - \tfrac{x+y+z}{3})$. Since$p$ already maps$G$-equivariantly (the averaging leaves it unchanged because the permutation action is symmetric), the complement is$W' = \mathrm{span}\{(1,1,1)\}$, giving$V = W \oplus W'$ as$S_3$-representations.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
The inductive step in the proof uses finite-dimensionality: at each step one finds a $G$-stable complement to a proper subrepresentation, and the dimension strictly decreases, so the process terminates. The theorem can fail for infinite-dimensional representations even over$\mathbb{C}$: the group$\mathbb{Z}$ acting on$\mathbb{C}^2$ by$\rho(n) = \begin{pmatrix}1 & n \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$ is not semisimple. The averaging argument fails because$\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_g$ becomes an infinite series that diverges.
\end{remark}

The proof also makes clear why Maschke fails in characteristic $p \mid |G|$. It is not merely that a different proof might work; the averaging formula literally produces the zero map when$|G| = 0$ in$k$, and the conclusion is false — indecomposable non-irreducible representations exist, as shown in §43.1.3.
