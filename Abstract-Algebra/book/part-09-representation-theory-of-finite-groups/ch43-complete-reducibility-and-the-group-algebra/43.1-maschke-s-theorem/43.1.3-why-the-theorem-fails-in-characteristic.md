# 43.1.3 Why the Theorem Fails in Characteristic $p \mid |G|$

When $\mathrm{char}(k) = p$ and$p \mid |G|$, the averaging argument breaks down immediately: the denominator$|G|$ is zero in$k$, so the averaged projection$\bar{p} = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} \rho(g) \circ p \circ \rho(g)^{-1}$ cannot even be written down. This failure of the proof is not a gap to be patched — Maschke's theorem genuinely fails in this case, and the obstruction is intrinsic.

The canonical counterexample uses the augmentation ideal. In the regular representation $k[G]$, the \emph{augmentation map}$\varepsilon: k[G] \to k$ is the$k$-algebra homomorphism that sums coefficients:$\varepsilon\!\left(\sum_g a_g g\right) = \sum_g a_g$. The \emph{augmentation ideal}$I = \ker \varepsilon$ is the subspace of elements whose coefficients sum to zero; it is a two-sided ideal of$k[G]$ and in particular a$k[G]$-submodule (subrepresentation) of the regular representation.

\begin{proposition}
When $\mathrm{char}(k) = p$ and$p \mid |G|$, the augmentation ideal$I \subseteq k[G]$ has no$G$-stable complement in$k[G]$ as a$k[G]$-module.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If $k[G] = I \oplus J$ as$k[G]$-modules, then$J \cong k[G]/I \cong k$ (the trivial representation), so$J = k[G] \cdot e_0$ for some idempotent$e_0 \in J$ with$e_0^2 = e_0$. The only$k[G]$-submodules of$k[G]$ isomorphic to the trivial module are generated by central idempotents fixed by all group elements. The natural candidate is$e_0 = \frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g \in G} g$, but this element requires dividing by$|G| = 0$ in$k$, so it does not exist. Moreover, the element$N = \sum_{g \in G} g$ satisfies$\varepsilon(N) = |G| = 0$ in characteristic$p$, so$N \in I$, meaning$N$ cannot generate the complement$J$. A more careful argument shows no such idempotent exists: any central idempotent$e_0 \in Z(k[G])$ with$J = k[G]e_0$ would need$g \cdot e_0 = e_0$ for all$g$, hence$e_0 \in Z(k[G])$, and one checks that the only such idempotents are$0$ and$1$, neither of which generates a complement to$I$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
Take $G = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} = \langle \sigma \rangle$ and$k = \mathbb{F}_p$. The group algebra is$k[G] \cong \mathbb{F}_p[x]/(x^p - 1) = \mathbb{F}_p[x]/(x-1)^p$ (using the Frobenius and the fact that$x^p - 1 = (x-1)^p$ in characteristic$p$). The augmentation ideal is$I = (x-1)/(x-1)^p$, generated by$(x-1)$ in$k[G]$. This is the unique maximal ideal. The regular representation$k[G]$ has a filtration$0 \subset I^{p-1} \subset I^{p-2} \subset \cdots \subset I \subset k[G]$, where each quotient$I^j/I^{j+1}$ is the trivial 1-dimensional module. This is a uniserial module with$p$ composition factors, all isomorphic to$k$, but the whole module is indecomposable — it cannot be written as a direct sum of two non-zero submodules. The only indecomposable$k[G]$-modules are the modules$k[G]/(x-1)^j$ for$1 \leq j \leq p$, and none of them (except the trivial module itself) is semisimple.
\end{example}

The study of representations in this \emph{modular} setting — when $\mathrm{char}(k) \mid |G|$ — is called \emph{modular representation theory}, and it is substantially harder than the semisimple theory. Several features of the semisimple case fail dramatically:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Representations can be indecomposable without being irreducible. The number of indecomposable modules can be infinite (it is infinite for $G = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ when$p \geq 3$).
\item Projective modules and injective modules are not the same as semisimple modules. The projective indecomposable modules (PIMs) are the direct summands of $k[G]$ and carry a richer structure.
\item Characters (ordinary traces) no longer classify representations. One must use \emph{Brauer characters} — lifts of modular eigenvalues to characteristic zero — to recover some classification power.
\item The group algebra $k[G]$ has a nontrivial Jacobson radical$J(k[G])$, which is equal to the augmentation ideal when$G$ is a$p$-group. The structure of$k[G]/J(k[G])$ (the "modular irreducibles") is only part of the story.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{example}
For $G = S_3$ over$\mathbb{F}_3$:$|S_3| = 6 = 2 \cdot 3$, so$3 \mid |G|$. The group algebra$\mathbb{F}_3[S_3]$ has three irreducible modules (trivial, sign, and a 2-dimensional standard representation), but unlike the characteristic-zero case, not every$\mathbb{F}_3[S_3]$-module is semisimple. The module structure is controlled by blocks: there is a block of defect 1 (containing the 3-modular representations) and a block of defect 0. Understanding the Cartan matrix — which records the composition multiplicities — requires substantially more work than the$\mathbb{C}$ case.
\end{example}

The tools developed for the semisimple case (characters, Artin–Wedderburn) must be replaced or supplemented by new invariants: Brauer characters, blocks, defect groups, and the theory of $p$-modular systems. This rich and technically demanding subject is treated in Chapter 51. The present chapter restricts attention to the semisimple (nonmodular) case, which is both more tractable and, for many purposes, the most important.
