# 43.1.4 Reformulation: $k[G]$ Is a Semisimple Ring

Maschke's theorem has a clean algebraic reformulation that connects it directly to the abstract ring theory developed earlier in the text. Understanding the statement in terms of semisimplicity reveals the structural mechanism and prepares the way for the Artin–Wedderburn theorem.

\begin{definition}
A ring $R$ is \emph{semisimple} (in the sense of Jacobson) if its Jacobson radical is zero:$J(R) = 0$. Equivalently (for Artinian rings):$R$ is semisimple if and only if every left$R$-module is semisimple (a direct sum of simple modules), if and only if every left$R$-module is projective, if and only if every short exact sequence of left$R$-modules splits.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}
Let $G$ be a finite group and$k$ a field. Then$k[G]$ is a semisimple ring if and only if$\mathrm{char}(k) \nmid |G|$.
\end{theorem}

Maschke's theorem is exactly the forward direction: if $\mathrm{char}(k) \nmid |G|$, then$k[G]$ is semisimple. The key insight is that the representation-theoretic content of Maschke's theorem is entirely captured by this ring-theoretic property. Every statement about representations of$G$ translates into a statement about$k[G]$-modules, and the semisimplicity of$k[G]$ is the single condition that makes the module theory as simple as possible.

In particular, semisimplicity implies that every short exact sequence of $k[G]$-modules splits — which is exactly Maschke's theorem rephrased: every subrepresentation has a$G$-stable complement. The equivalence of the various characterizations of semisimplicity (via the radical, via projectivity, via semisimplicity of all modules) is part of the general theory of Artinian rings and applies to$k[G]$ once one notes that$k[G]$ is finite-dimensional as a$k$-vector space, hence Artinian.

\begin{example}
The ring $\mathbb{C}[S_3]$ is semisimple, since$\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{C}) = 0$ and$|S_3| = 6$. Its Jacobson radical is zero:$J(\mathbb{C}[S_3]) = 0$. By Artin–Wedderburn (§43.2.3),$\mathbb{C}[S_3] \cong \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C} \times M_2(\mathbb{C})$, a product of three matrix algebras. Every$\mathbb{C}[S_3]$-module is therefore a direct sum of at most three types of simple modules: the trivial module, the sign module, and the standard 2-dimensional module.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
The ring $\mathbb{F}_3[S_3]$ is not semisimple. Its Jacobson radical$J(\mathbb{F}_3[S_3])$ is nontrivial — it contains the augmentation ideal (intersected with the 3-Sylow part) — and not every module is projective. For instance, the trivial module$\mathbb{F}_3$ is not projective as a$\mathbb{F}_3[S_3]$-module. The structure of$\mathbb{F}_3[S_3]$ requires the theory of blocks and defect groups to describe.
\end{example}

The equivalence "semisimple $\Leftrightarrow$ every module is projective" is especially instructive in the group algebra context. A projective$k[G]$-module is one that appears as a direct summand of a free$k[G]$-module (a direct sum of copies of$k[G]$ itself). In the semisimple case, every$k[G]$-module — every representation — is projective, meaning every representation is a direct summand of some free module (some direct sum of copies of the regular representation). This confirms the picture given by the decomposition of the regular representation in §43.3: the regular representation contains all irreducibles and serves as the "universal" home.

Over an algebraically closed field $k$ of characteristic zero, the semisimplicity of$k[G]$ immediately invites the application of the Artin–Wedderburn theorem: since$k[G]$ is semisimple Artinian and$k = \bar{k}$, there is an isomorphism of rings
$$k[G] \cong \prod_{i=1}^r M_{n_i}(k),$$
one matrix factor for each isomorphism class of irreducible representations. This decomposition, worked out in §43.2, is the structural heart of the theory and encodes the dimensions of all irreducibles, the number of irreducibles, and the complete representation ring of $G$ in a single compact formula.

\begin{remark}
The Jacobson radical $J(k[G])$ is related to the \emph{augmentation ideal} when$\mathrm{char}(k) = p \mid |G|$. More precisely, if$G$ is a$p$-group and$\mathrm{char}(k) = p$, then$k[G]$ is a local ring with unique maximal (left) ideal equal to the augmentation ideal$I$, and$J(k[G]) = I$. The nilpotency index of$J(k[G])$ equals the nilpotency class of$G$ in a precise sense. For non-$p$-groups, the radical is the intersection of the augmentation ideals of the$p$-Sylow subgroups (in a suitable sense), and the block theory decomposes$k[G]$ into pieces corresponding to$p$-blocks.
\end{remark}

The semisimplicity of $k[G]$ also has a cohomological interpretation. The Hochschild cohomology$HH^n(k[G], M) = 0$ for all$n \geq 1$ and all$k[G]$-bimodules$M$ when$k[G]$ is semisimple. This is because semisimplicity forces the global dimension of$k[G]$ to be zero (all projective dimensions are zero). In particular, the group cohomology$H^n(G, M) = 0$ for$n \geq 1$ and any$G$-module$M$ when$\mathrm{char}(k) \nmid |G|$ — a result sometimes called Maschke's theorem in cohomological form. This vanishing of group cohomology is the representation-theoretic reason why many "extension problems" in group theory have unique solutions when the characteristic is coprime to the group order.

\begin{example}
The Maschke condition $\mathrm{char}(k) \nmid |G|$ is equivalent to$k[G]$ being semisimple, which is equivalent to the triviality of$\mathrm{Ext}^1_{k[G]}(M, N) = 0$ for all modules$M, N$. This last condition says every extension$0 \to N \to E \to M \to 0$ of$k[G]$-modules splits. In group cohomology language,$H^1(G, M) = 0$ for all$G$-modules$M$ when$\mathrm{char}(k) \nmid |G|$, meaning there are no non-split extensions of$G$-modules.
\end{example}
