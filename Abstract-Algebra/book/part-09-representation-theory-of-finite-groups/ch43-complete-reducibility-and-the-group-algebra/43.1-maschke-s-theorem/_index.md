# 43.1 Maschke's Theorem

The key structural question in representation theory is whether every representation decomposes as a direct sum of irreducibles. The answer, in the most important setting, is yes: if $G$ is a finite group and $k$ is a field whose characteristic does not divide $|G|$, then every representation of $G$ over $k$ is completely reducible. This is Maschke's theorem, proved by Heinrich Maschke in 1898, and it is the theorem that makes the classification of representations tractable.

The proof operates by the averaging trick. Suppose $W \subseteq V$ is a $G$-stable subspace. One can always find some linear projection $\pi: V \to W$ — a map satisfying $\pi^2 = \pi$ and $\text{im}\,\pi = W$ — but there is no reason for $\pi$ to respect the $G$-action. The fix is to average: define $\bar\pi = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} \rho(g) \circ \pi \circ \rho(g)^{-1}$. A direct computation shows that $\bar\pi$ is still a projection onto $W$, and crucially, it satisfies $\bar\pi \circ \rho(h) = \rho(h) \circ \bar\pi$ for every $h \in G$ — it is $G$-equivariant. The kernel of a $G$-equivariant projection is a $G$-stable subspace, and $\ker \bar\pi$ is the desired $G$-stable complement to $W$. So $V = W \oplus \ker \bar\pi$ as a direct sum of representations. The only arithmetic operation required is division by $|G|$, which is why the hypothesis $\text{char}(k) \nmid |G|$ is essential.

The failure of Maschke's theorem in characteristic $p$ dividing $|G|$ is not a technical nuisance but a genuine mathematical phenomenon. Over $\mathbb{F}_p$ with $G = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$, the upper-triangular unipotent representation sending the generator to the matrix with diagonal entries 1 and upper-right entry 1 has an invariant subspace $\text{span}(e_1)$ with no invariant complement: the averaging argument breaks down because $\frac{1}{|G|} = \frac{1}{p} = 0$ in $\mathbb{F}_p$. This failure is the entry point into modular representation theory, the study of representations in characteristic $p$, which carries its own rich and substantially more complex structure. Maschke's theorem, in its failure as much as its success, draws the boundary between these two worlds.

A reformulation of Maschke's theorem in ring-theoretic language is especially illuminating: the theorem is equivalent to the statement that the group algebra $k[G]$ is a semisimple ring whenever $\text{char}(k) \nmid |G|$. A ring is semisimple if every module over it is completely reducible, which is exactly what Maschke asserts for $k[G]$-modules. This algebraic reformulation opens the door to the Artin–Wedderburn theorem, which identifies the precise structure of every semisimple ring.
