# 43.2.1 Semisimple Rings: Every Module Is Projective

To appreciate the Artin–Wedderburn theorem, it helps to first understand the class of rings it classifies. The definition of semisimplicity admits several equivalent formulations, each revealing a different facet of the concept.

\begin{definition}
A left $R$-module$M$ is \emph{simple} (or \emph{irreducible}) if it has no submodules other than$0$ and$M$ itself. It is \emph{semisimple} if it is a direct sum of simple submodules. A ring$R$ is \emph{semisimple} if it is semisimple as a left module over itself.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Equivalent characterizations of semisimplicity]
For an Artinian ring $R$, the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $R$ is semisimple (i.e.,$R$ is semisimple as a left$R$-module).
\item Every left $R$-module is semisimple.
\item Every left $R$-module is projective.
\item Every short exact sequence of left $R$-modules splits.
\item The Jacobson radical $J(R) = 0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

The equivalence $(3) \Leftrightarrow (4)$ is formal: a module$M$ is projective if and only if every surjection onto$M$ splits, i.e., every short exact sequence$0 \to A \to B \to M \to 0$ splits. If all modules are projective, all short exact sequences split, which is$(4)$. Conversely, if all short exact sequences split, then any module$M$ is a direct summand of a free module (embed$M$ into a free module$F$ and split the sequence$0 \to \ker q \to F \xrightarrow{q} M \to 0$), so$M$ is projective.

The theory of semisimple rings is particularly clean because such rings are completely determined by their simple modules. Two semisimple rings with the same set of simple modules (up to isomorphism) are isomorphic as rings, and every simple module appears as a direct summand of the ring itself viewed as a module over itself. This self-duality — the ring contains all the information about its representation theory — is one of the hallmarks of semisimplicity.

\begin{example}
The ring $\mathbb{C}$ is semisimple (it is a field, hence a simple module over itself). The ring$\mathbb{Z}$ is not semisimple:$J(\mathbb{Z}) = 0$ but$\mathbb{Z}$ is not Artinian, and the module$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is not projective. The ring$M_n(\mathbb{C})$ is semisimple: its only left ideal is$\{0\}$ or all of$M_n(\mathbb{C})$ times a column matrix, and as a left module over itself it decomposes as$n$ copies of the column module$\mathbb{C}^n$. The ring$\mathbb{C}[x]/(x^2)$ is not semisimple: the ideal$(x)/(x^2)$ is a non-zero submodule with no complement.
\end{example}

Semisimple rings include all fields, all finite products of matrix algebras over division rings, and — crucially for our purposes — the group algebras $k[G]$ when$\mathrm{char}(k) \nmid |G|$. They are never infinite-dimensional over their center (in the Artinian case), and their Jacobson radical is zero, meaning there are no "nilpotent" obstructions to semisimplicity.

The Artinian condition (descending chain condition on left ideals) is the correct finiteness hypothesis. Every finite-dimensional algebra over a field is Artinian, so $k[G]$ with$|G| < \infty$ is automatically in this class. In fact,$k[G]$ is both Artinian and Noetherian, since it is finite-dimensional.

\begin{proposition}
If $R$ is a semisimple ring, then$R \cong S_1^{m_1} \oplus \cdots \oplus S_r^{m_r}$ as left$R$-modules, where$S_1, \ldots, S_r$ are the distinct simple left$R$-modules (up to isomorphism) and$m_i \geq 1$. The isomorphism type of$R$ as a ring is completely determined by the multiset$\{(S_i, m_i)\}$.
\end{proposition}

This proposition is the core of the Artin–Wedderburn theorem before its explicit form is stated. The key calculation is $\mathrm{End}_R(R) \cong R^{\mathrm{op}}$ (right multiplication is a left$R$-module endomorphism), and$\mathrm{End}_R(S_i^{m_i}) \cong M_{m_i}(D_i)$ where$D_i = \mathrm{End}_R(S_i)^{\mathrm{op}}$. Identifying$R^{\mathrm{op}}$ with a product of matrix algebras gives the explicit Artin–Wedderburn decomposition.

\begin{remark}
The condition "every module is projective" is very strong. For comparison: over $\mathbb{Z}$, projective modules are the same as free modules (since$\mathbb{Z}$ is a PID), and certainly not every$\mathbb{Z}$-module is free ($\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is not). The semisimple condition forces all the "homological complexity" to vanish: the projective dimension of every module is$0$, the global dimension of$R$ is$0$, and$\mathrm{Ext}^n_R(M, N) = 0$ for all$n \geq 1$ and all$R$-modules$M, N$. This is the algebraic meaning of the statement that representation theory over$\mathbb{C}$ is "exact" — no extension problems arise.
\end{remark}

The complete structure of all semisimple Artinian rings is given by the Artin–Wedderburn theorem in §43.2.3.
