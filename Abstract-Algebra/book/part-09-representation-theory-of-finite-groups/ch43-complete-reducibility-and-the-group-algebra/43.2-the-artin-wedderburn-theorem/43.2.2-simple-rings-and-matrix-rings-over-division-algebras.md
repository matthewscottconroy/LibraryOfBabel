# 43.2.2 Simple Rings and Matrix Rings over Division Algebras

Before classifying semisimple rings, one must classify their building blocks: \emph{simple} rings. Understanding what "simple" means at the ring level, and seeing why matrix rings are the canonical examples, prepares the ground for the full Artin–Wedderburn theorem.

\begin{definition}
A ring $R$ is \emph{simple} if its only two-sided ideals are$\{0\}$ and$R$ itself, and$R^2 \neq 0$.
\end{definition}

The condition $R^2 \neq 0$ excludes the zero ring; every nontrivial simple ring is automatically nonzero. Simple rings are the ring-theoretic analogues of simple groups, and just as simple groups are the building blocks of all finite groups (via composition series), simple rings are the building blocks of semisimple rings.

\begin{example}
Matrix rings $M_n(k)$ over a field$k$ are the canonical examples of simple rings. To see why: let$I \subseteq M_n(k)$ be a nonzero two-sided ideal and let$A \in I$ be a nonzero matrix. There exist$i,j$ with$A_{ij} \neq 0$. Multiplying on the left by the elementary matrix$E_{ki}$ (which has a 1 in position$(k,i)$ and zeros elsewhere) and on the right by$E_{j\ell}$ gives$A_{ij} E_{k\ell} \in I$. Since$A_{ij} \neq 0$ in$k$, the scalar$A_{ij}^{-1}$ exists, and$E_{k\ell} \in I$. Varying$k, \ell$, all elementary matrices lie in$I$, which generates$M_n(k)$. Thus$I = M_n(k)$.
\end{example}

More generally, $M_n(D)$ is simple for any division ring$D$. The same argument applies with$A_{ij}^{-1}$ replaced by the multiplicative inverse in$D$.

\begin{theorem}[Wedderburn's Theorem on Simple Artinian Rings]
Every simple Artinian ring is isomorphic to $M_n(D)$ for some positive integer$n$ and some division ring$D$. The pair$(n, D)$ is unique up to isomorphism of$D$.
\end{theorem}

The proof strategy is as follows. Since $R$ is simple Artinian, it has a minimal left ideal$L$ (which exists because descending chains of left ideals stabilize). As a left$R$-module,$L$ is simple (a minimal left ideal is a simple submodule). Since$R$ is simple, the annihilator of$L$ as a two-sided ideal is either$R$ or$0$; since$L \neq 0$ and$RL \neq 0$ (as$L$ is a left ideal), the annihilator is$0$. One then shows$R \cong L^n$ as left$R$-modules for some$n$, and identifies$\mathrm{End}_R(L) \cong D$ (a division ring by Schur's lemma, since$L$ is simple). The isomorphism$R \cong M_n(D)$ follows from taking endomorphism algebras of both sides and using$\mathrm{End}_R(R) \cong R^{\mathrm{op}}$.

The division ring $D$ arises as$\mathrm{End}_R(S)^{\mathrm{op}}$, where$S$ is the unique simple left$R$-module. This echoes the Schur's lemma calculation from §42.3: endomorphisms of a simple module form a division ring. In the case$R = M_n(k)$, the simple module is the column module$k^n$, and$\mathrm{End}_{M_n(k)}(k^n) \cong k$ (by Schur's lemma and the fact that$k$ is algebraically closed).

\begin{example}
For $R = M_2(\mathbb{R})$: the simple left module is$\mathbb{R}^2$, and$\mathrm{End}_{M_2(\mathbb{R})}(\mathbb{R}^2) \cong \mathbb{R}$. For$R = M_1(\mathbb{H}) \cong \mathbb{H}$ (the quaternion algebra): the simple left module is$\mathbb{H}$ itself, and$\mathrm{End}_{\mathbb{H}}(\mathbb{H}) \cong \mathbb{H}^{\mathrm{op}} \cong \mathbb{H}$ (since$\mathbb{H}$ is isomorphic to its opposite via the map$q \mapsto \bar{q}$). So$\mathbb{H}$ is a simple ring with$n=1$ and$D = \mathbb{H}$.
\end{example}

Over an algebraically closed field $k$, the classification simplifies dramatically: every finite-dimensional division algebra over$k$ is$k$ itself. This follows because any element of a finite-dimensional division algebra$D$ over$k$ generates a field extension$k \subseteq k[\alpha] \subseteq D$, and since$k$ is algebraically closed,$\alpha \in k$ already. Hence$D = k$, and every simple Artinian$k$-algebra is isomorphic to$M_n(k)$ for some$n$.

\begin{corollary}
If $k$ is algebraically closed, every simple Artinian$k$-algebra is isomorphic to$M_n(k)$ for some$n \geq 1$.
\end{corollary}

This is the version of the theorem that applies to $k[G]$ when$k = \mathbb{C}$: the simple components of$\mathbb{C}[G]$ are all matrix algebras over$\mathbb{C}$, with no division algebra complications. The representation-theoretic meaning is that every irreducible$\mathbb{C}[G]$-module is a column module$\mathbb{C}^{n_i}$ for some$n_i$, and there are no "quaternionic" or other non-matrix-type irreducibles.

\begin{remark}
Over $\mathbb{R}$ (not algebraically closed), there are three types of simple components:$M_n(\mathbb{R})$,$M_n(\mathbb{C})$, and$M_n(\mathbb{H})$. A representation of$G$ over$\mathbb{R}$ can be of "real type," "complex type," or "quaternionic type" depending on which division algebra appears in the corresponding simple component. The Frobenius–Schur indicator detects these types from the character values.
\end{remark}
