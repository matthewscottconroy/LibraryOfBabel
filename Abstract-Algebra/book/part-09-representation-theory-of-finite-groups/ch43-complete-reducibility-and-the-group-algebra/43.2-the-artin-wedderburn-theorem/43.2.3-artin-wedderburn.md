# 43.2.3 Artin–Wedderburn: $R \cong \prod M_{n_i}(D_i)$

The Artin–Wedderburn Theorem is the capstone of the structural theory of semisimple rings. Its statement is clean, its proof is illuminating, and its applications to group algebras are immediate and decisive.

\begin{theorem}[Artin–Wedderburn]
Every semisimple Artinian ring $R$ is isomorphic, as a ring, to a finite product
$$R \cong M_{n_1}(D_1) \times M_{n_2}(D_2) \times \cdots \times M_{n_r}(D_r),$$
where each $D_i$ is a division ring. The decomposition is unique up to reordering of the factors and isomorphism of the division rings. The number of factors$r$ equals the number of isomorphism classes of simple left$R$-modules, and the$i$th simple module is isomorphic to the column module$D_i^{n_i}$ as a left$M_{n_i}(D_i)$-module.
\end{theorem}

The proof strategy is to decompose $R$ as a left module over itself. Since$R$ is semisimple,$R \cong S_1^{m_1} \oplus \cdots \oplus S_r^{m_r}$ as left modules, where$S_1, \ldots, S_r$ are the distinct simple left$R$-modules. Taking endomorphism algebras of both sides:
$$R^{\mathrm{op}} \cong \mathrm{End}_R(R) \cong \mathrm{End}_R\!\left(\bigoplus_i S_i^{m_i}\right) \cong \prod_i \mathrm{End}_R(S_i^{m_i}) \cong \prod_i M_{m_i}(D_i^{\mathrm{op}}),$$
where $D_i = \mathrm{End}_R(S_i)$ is a division ring by Schur's lemma, and we use$\mathrm{End}_R(S_i^{m_i}) \cong M_{m_i}(\mathrm{End}_R(S_i))$. Taking opposites:$R \cong \prod_i M_{m_i}(D_i)$ (since$M_n(D)^{\mathrm{op}} \cong M_n(D^{\mathrm{op}})$ and we can relabel$D_i^{\mathrm{op}}$ as$D_i$). Setting$n_i = m_i$ completes the identification.

\begin{example}
For $R = \mathbb{C}[S_3]$: there are three simple modules (trivial, sign, standard of dimension 2), and the theorem gives$\mathbb{C}[S_3] \cong \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C} \times M_2(\mathbb{C})$. As a dimension check:$\dim_{\mathbb{C}} \mathbb{C}[S_3] = 6 = 1 + 1 + 4$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For $R = \mathbb{C}[\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}]$: since$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is abelian and$\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed, all irreducible representations are 1-dimensional (by Schur's lemma: any endomorphism of an irreducible$\mathbb{C}[G]$-module is scalar, so$G$ must act by scalars on any irreducible, which means the representation is 1-dimensional when$G$ is abelian). There are$n$ conjugacy classes ($\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is abelian), hence$n$ irreducibles, each 1-dimensional. The Artin–Wedderburn decomposition is:
$$\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}] \cong \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C} \times \cdots \times \mathbb{C} \quad (n \text{ copies}).$$
Concretely, this is the decomposition of $\mathbb{C}[x]/(x^n - 1) \cong \prod_{k=0}^{n-1} \mathbb{C}[x]/(x - \zeta^k)$ by the Chinese Remainder Theorem, where$\zeta = e^{2\pi i/n}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For $R = \mathbb{C}[Q_8]$ where$Q_8 = \{\pm 1, \pm i, \pm j, \pm k\}$ is the quaternion group:$|Q_8| = 8$ and$Q_8$ has 5 conjugacy classes ($\{1\}$,$\{-1\}$,$\{\pm i\}$,$\{\pm j\}$,$\{\pm k\}$), hence 5 irreducible representations. The dimension formula$\sum n_i^2 = 8$ with 5 terms forces$n_i = (1,1,1,1,2)$ (since$1+1+1+1+4 = 8$). The decomposition is:
$$\mathbb{C}[Q_8] \cong \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C} \times M_2(\mathbb{C}).$$
The four 1-dimensional representations come from the quotient $Q_8/\{\pm 1\} \cong (\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^2$, and the unique 2-dimensional irreducible is the "standard" representation coming from$\mathbb{H} \cong \mathbb{C}^2$ (the quaternions viewed as$2 \times 2$ complex matrices).
\end{example}

The practical power of this theorem is that it replaces an abstract semisimple ring with a concrete, completely classified object. Every invariant of $R$ — its simple modules, their multiplicities, its center, its$K$-theory — can be read off from the product decomposition:

- **Simple modules**: There is exactly one isomorphism class of simple module for each factor $M_{n_i}(D_i)$, namely the column module$D_i^{n_i}$.
- **Center**: $Z(R) \cong \prod_i Z(M_{n_i}(D_i)) \cong \prod_i Z(D_i)$.
- **Primitive idempotents**: The central idempotents $e_i$ (the identity element of the$i$th factor) give a decomposition$1 = e_1 + \cdots + e_r$ into orthogonal central idempotents, corresponding to the block decomposition of$R$.

For representation theory, this means that understanding the group algebra $k[G]$ reduces entirely to identifying the matrix algebra factors, each corresponding to a distinct irreducible representation. The theorem transforms the classification problem for representations of$G$ into a purely combinatorial question: how many irreducibles are there, and what are their dimensions?

The uniqueness clause of the theorem is equally important. Because $R \cong \prod_i M_{n_i}(D_i)$ is unique up to reordering, the multiset$\{(n_i, D_i)\}$ is a complete invariant of$R$ as a ring. For group algebras over$\mathbb{C}$, all$D_i = \mathbb{C}$, and the complete invariant is the multiset$\{n_1, \ldots, n_r\}$ of irreducible dimensions — a finite sequence of positive integers whose squares sum to$|G|$.

\begin{example}
For $G = A_4$:$|A_4| = 12$, four conjugacy classes. Artin–Wedderburn gives$\mathbb{C}[A_4] \cong \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C} \times M_3(\mathbb{C})$, with dimension check$1+1+1+9 = 12$ \checkmark. The three 1-dimensional factors come from the quotient$A_4/V_4 \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$, whose three 1-dimensional representations inflate to$A_4$. The$M_3(\mathbb{C})$ factor corresponds to the unique 3-dimensional irreducible, which arises from the natural action of$A_4$ on$\mathbb{C}^3$ (permuting coordinates and restricting to even permutations, then subtracting the trivial invariant line).
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
Over a non-algebraically-closed field such as $\mathbb{R}$ or$\mathbb{Q}$, the Artin–Wedderburn theorem still applies, but the division algebras$D_i$ need not be the ground field. For real representations of$G$, the possible$D_i$ are$\mathbb{R}$,$\mathbb{C}$, and$\mathbb{H}$, corresponding to the three types of real representations detected by the Frobenius–Schur indicator. For representations over$\mathbb{Q}$, the Schur index can be any positive integer, and the division algebras$D_i$ are central simple algebras over$\mathbb{Q}$ (quaternion algebras and their generalizations). This richness over non-algebraically-closed fields is part of what makes rational representation theory substantially more complex than complex representation theory.
\end{remark}
