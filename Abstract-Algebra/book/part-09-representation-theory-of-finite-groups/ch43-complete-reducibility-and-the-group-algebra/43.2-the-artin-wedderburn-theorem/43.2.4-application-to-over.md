# 43.2.4 Application to $k[G]$ over$\mathbb{C}$:$\mathbb{C}[G] \cong \prod M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})$

The Artin–Wedderburn theorem, applied to the group algebra over $\mathbb{C}$, yields the central structural result of classical representation theory. The combination of Maschke's theorem (semisimplicity), the algebraic closedness of$\mathbb{C}$ (forcing all division algebras to be$\mathbb{C}$ itself), and Artin–Wedderburn gives a single clean decomposition that encodes the entire representation theory of$G$.

\begin{theorem}
Let $G$ be a finite group and$V_1, \ldots, V_r$ the irreducible representations of$G$ over$\mathbb{C}$ (one from each isomorphism class), with$n_i = \dim_{\mathbb{C}} V_i$. Then there is an isomorphism of$\mathbb{C}$-algebras:
$$\mathbb{C}[G] \cong M_{n_1}(\mathbb{C}) \times M_{n_2}(\mathbb{C}) \times \cdots \times M_{n_r}(\mathbb{C}).$$
\end{theorem}

The correspondence is explicit: the $i$th irreducible representation$V_i$ corresponds to the natural left module of$M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})$, namely$\mathbb{C}^{n_i}$ with$M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})$ acting by matrix-vector multiplication. A representation of$G$ is a left$\mathbb{C}[G]$-module, and via the isomorphism it is the same as a left module over the product of matrix algebras. Such a module decomposes as$\bigoplus_i M_i$ where each$M_i$ is a left$M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})$-module, and every left$M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})$-module is a direct sum of copies of$\mathbb{C}^{n_i}$.

This isomorphism carries several immediate and important consequences.

\textbf{The dimension formula.} As $\mathbb{C}$-vector spaces,$\dim \mathbb{C}[G] = |G|$ on the left and$\sum_i n_i^2$ on the right (since$\dim M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C}) = n_i^2$). This gives:
$$\sum_{i=1}^r n_i^2 = |G|.$$

\textbf{The count of irreducibles.} The center of a product of matrix algebras is the product of the centers:
$$Z(\mathbb{C}[G]) \cong \prod_i Z(M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})) \cong \mathbb{C}^r.$$
The center $Z(\mathbb{C}[G])$ has a natural basis indexed by conjugacy classes of$G$ (the class sums$\hat{C} = \sum_{g \in C} g$), so$\dim Z(\mathbb{C}[G])$ equals the number of conjugacy classes. Thus$r =$ number of conjugacy classes, a result elaborated in §43.3.3.

\begin{example}
For $G = S_3$:$|S_3| = 6$, three conjugacy classes, so$r = 3$ and$n_1^2 + n_2^2 + n_3^2 = 6$. The only solution in positive integers is$(n_1, n_2, n_3) = (1,1,2)$. Thus:
$$\mathbb{C}[S_3] \cong \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C} \times M_2(\mathbb{C}).$$
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For $G = S_4$:$|S_4| = 24$, five conjugacy classes (identity, transpositions, 3-cycles, double transpositions, 4-cycles), so$r = 5$ and$\sum n_i^2 = 24$. The unique solution with all$n_i \geq 1$ is$(1,1,2,3,3)$ (since$1+1+4+9+9 = 24$). Thus:
$$\mathbb{C}[S_4] \cong \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C} \times M_2(\mathbb{C}) \times M_3(\mathbb{C}) \times M_3(\mathbb{C}).$$
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For $G = \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$:$n$ conjugacy classes,$n$ irreducibles all of dimension 1. The decomposition is$\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}] \cong \mathbb{C}^n$. Concretely, this is the discrete Fourier transform: the isomorphism sends the generator$\sigma$ to$(\zeta^0, \zeta^1, \zeta^2, \ldots, \zeta^{n-1})$ where$\zeta = e^{2\pi i/n}$.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
The isomorphism $\mathbb{C}[G] \cong \prod_i M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})$ is not canonical — it depends on a choice of isomorphism, which in turn depends on choices of bases for the irreducible representations. However, the \emph{factors} (as rings) and their \emph{multiplicities} are canonical invariants of$G$. The projection maps$\mathbb{C}[G] \to M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})$ are the irreducible representations themselves, and these are canonical up to isomorphism.
\end{remark}

The isomorphism also has a Fourier-analytic interpretation: the map $a \mapsto (\rho_1(a), \rho_2(a), \ldots, \rho_r(a))$ — sending an element$a \in \mathbb{C}[G]$ to its matrix in each irreducible representation — is the non-abelian generalization of the discrete Fourier transform. For$G$ abelian, this reduces to the usual DFT on finite abelian groups. The Artin–Wedderburn isomorphism is thus the algebraic backbone of non-abelian Fourier analysis on finite groups.

The isomorphism $\mathbb{C}[G] \cong \prod_i M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})$ also gives a decomposition of the identity element into orthogonal central idempotents. For each irreducible$V_i$, the corresponding central idempotent$e_i \in Z(\mathbb{C}[G])$ satisfies:
$$e_i = \frac{n_i}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} \chi_i(g^{-1}) g = \frac{n_i}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} \overline{\chi_i(g)} g.$$
These elements satisfy $e_i e_j = \delta_{ij} e_i$,$\sum_i e_i = 1$, and$e_i \mathbb{C}[G] \cong M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})$ (the$i$th block). The formula for$e_i$ is essentially the "projection onto the$V_i$-isotypic component" expressed in the group algebra. It is the algebraic incarnation of the character orthogonality relations: the coefficient$n_i \overline{\chi_i(g)}/|G|$ is the Fourier coefficient of the "delta function at$g$" in the$\chi_i$-direction.

\begin{example}
For $G = S_3$ and$\chi_3$ the standard character:$e_3 = \frac{2}{6}\sum_{g \in S_3} \overline{\chi_3(g)} g = \frac{1}{3}[2e + 0 \cdot (12) + 0 \cdot (13) + 0 \cdot (23) + (-1)(123) + (-1)(132)]$. Thus$e_3 = \frac{1}{3}(2e - (123) - (132))$. One can verify$e_3^2 = e_3$ and$e_3 \cdot \mathbb{C}[S_3] \cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$ (a 4-dimensional piece of$\mathbb{C}[S_3]$).
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
The Artin–Wedderburn isomorphism for $\mathbb{C}[G]$ can be viewed as a non-commutative analogue of the Chinese Remainder Theorem. For a cyclic group$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, the group algebra$\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}] \cong \mathbb{C}[x]/(x^n - 1) \cong \prod_{k=0}^{n-1} \mathbb{C}$ is exactly the CRT decomposition of$\mathbb{C}[x]/(x^n-1)$ into fields$\mathbb{C}[x]/(x - \zeta^k)$. For non-abelian$G$, the matrix factors$M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})$ replace the fields$\mathbb{C}$ — the non-commutativity of the group introduces non-commutative simple rings as components.
\end{remark}
