# 43.2 The Artin–Wedderburn Theorem

A semisimple ring has a complete and explicit structure theorem: it is isomorphic to a finite direct product of matrix rings over division algebras. This is the Artin–Wedderburn theorem, one of the cornerstones of noncommutative algebra. Wedderburn proved the finite-dimensional case over a field in 1908, and Artin extended it to the general (Artinian) setting in the 1920s. In the context of group algebras, the theorem tells us precisely what $k[G]$ looks like as a ring, and since the category of $k[G]$-modules is the same as the category of representations of $G$, the theorem simultaneously describes all representations.

The starting point is the notion of a simple ring: a ring with no two-sided ideals other than $0$ and itself. The theorem asserts that every simple Artinian ring is isomorphic to a matrix ring $M_n(D)$ over a division algebra $D$. The modules over $M_n(D)$ are completely classified: up to isomorphism, there is exactly one simple module, namely $D^n$ viewed as a column space. A semisimple ring, being a finite product of simple rings, therefore has exactly as many isomorphism classes of simple modules as it has simple factors. For the group algebra $k[G]$ over an algebraically closed field $k$ of characteristic not dividing $|G|$, the division algebras that appear must all equal $k$ itself (by Schur's lemma: the endomorphism algebra of each irreducible is a division algebra over $k$, and the only finite-dimensional division algebra over an algebraically closed field is $k$). So the Artin–Wedderburn decomposition takes the clean form $k[G] \cong M_{n_1}(k) \times M_{n_2}(k) \times \cdots \times M_{n_r}(k)$, where $r$ is the number of irreducible representations and $n_i = \dim V_i$.

The implications are striking. Comparing dimensions gives $n_1^2 + n_2^2 + \cdots + n_r^2 = |G|$, the celebrated dimension formula. The center of each matrix ring $M_n(k)$ is one-dimensional (spanned by the identity matrix), so the center of $k[G]$ has dimension $r$ over $k$; but the center of $k[G]$ also has a basis consisting of the class sums $C_i = \sum_{g \in C} g$ for each conjugacy class $C$ of $G$, which gives $r = $ (number of conjugacy classes). The abstract ring-theoretic machinery has revealed a deep connection: the number of irreducible representations equals the number of conjugacy classes. This is the algebraic duality between group elements and irreducible representations that the character table will make fully explicit.
