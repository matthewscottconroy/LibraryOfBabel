# 43.3.1 Each Irreducible Appears in $k[G]$ with Multiplicity$= \dim V_i$

The decomposition of the regular representation is one of the most striking results in the theory: it reveals that the regular representation $k[G]$ does not merely contain all irreducibles, but contains each one with a very specific multiplicity that depends on the irreducible itself. Concretely, in the decomposition of$k[G]$ as a left$k[G]$-module, the irreducible representation$V_i$ appears exactly$n_i = \dim V_i$ times.

\begin{theorem}
Let $G$ be a finite group and$k$ an algebraically closed field with$\mathrm{char}(k) \nmid |G|$. Let$V_1, \ldots, V_r$ be the irreducible representations of$G$ over$k$. Then as left$k[G]$-modules:
$$k[G] \cong \bigoplus_{i=1}^r V_i^{\oplus n_i}, \quad \text{where } n_i = \dim_k V_i.$$
\end{theorem}

The proof is a direct reading of the Artin–Wedderburn isomorphism $k[G] \cong \prod_i M_{n_i}(k)$. As a left module over itself, the matrix algebra$M_n(k)$ decomposes as a direct sum of its column modules:$M_n(k) \cong (k^n)^{\oplus n}$, with$n$ copies of the irreducible column module$k^n$. This decomposition comes from writing$M_n(k)$ as the direct sum of its$n$ columns$C_1, \ldots, C_n$, where$C_j = \{A \in M_n(k) : A_{ij} = 0 \text{ for } i \neq j\}$ — but more naturally,$M_n(k)$ as a left module over itself is the direct sum of$n$ copies of the simple column module.

Under the Artin–Wedderburn decomposition, the $i$th matrix factor$M_{n_i}(k)$ contributes exactly$n_i$ copies of the simple module$V_i$ and zero copies of any$V_j$ with$j \neq i$ (since$M_{n_i}(k)$-modules and$M_{n_j}(k)$-modules do not interact as$k[G]$-modules when$i \neq j$). Summing over all factors gives the stated decomposition.

\begin{example}
For $G = S_3$ over$\mathbb{C}$: let$V_1 =$ trivial (dim 1),$V_2 =$ sign (dim 1),$V_3 =$ standard (dim 2). The decomposition gives:
$$\mathbb{C}[S_3] \cong V_1^{\oplus 1} \oplus V_2^{\oplus 1} \oplus V_3^{\oplus 2}.$$
Dimension check: $1 + 1 + 4 = 6 = |S_3|$. Each representation appears once for each dimension.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For $G = \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ over$\mathbb{C}$: all$n$ irreducibles$\chi_k$ are 1-dimensional ($k = 0, 1, \ldots, n-1$), where$\chi_k(\sigma^j) = \zeta^{jk}$ and$\zeta = e^{2\pi i/n}$. Each appears exactly once (multiplicity = dimension = 1):
$$\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}] \cong \bigoplus_{k=0}^{n-1} V_k.$$
This is the spectral decomposition: the regular representation decomposes into $n$ one-dimensional "frequency components," which is exactly what the discrete Fourier transform computes.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For $G = Q_8$ over$\mathbb{C}$: dimensions are$(1,1,1,1,2)$, so the regular representation decomposes as:
$$\mathbb{C}[Q_8] \cong V_1 \oplus V_2 \oplus V_3 \oplus V_4 \oplus V_5^{\oplus 2},$$
where $V_5$ is the unique 2-dimensional irreducible. Dimension check:$1+1+1+1+4 = 8 = |Q_8|$.
\end{example}

To see why this matters: the decomposition is the complete answer to the question of which representations occur "naturally" in $G$'s self-action. Every irreducible$V_i$ is present — the regular representation is a universal home for all representations — and the multiplicity$n_i$ is not arbitrary but is forced by the dimension. In particular, a 1-dimensional irreducible appears exactly once, while a 2-dimensional irreducible appears twice.

\begin{proposition}
The multiplicity of $V_i$ in the regular representation$k[G]$ equals$n_i$, the dimension of$V_i$.
\end{proposition}

This can be recovered from the character-theoretic formula for multiplicities (Chapter 44): the multiplicity of $V_i$ in a representation$V$ is$\langle \chi_V, \chi_i \rangle = \frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g \in G} \chi_V(g)\overline{\chi_i(g)}$. For the regular representation,$\chi_{\mathrm{reg}}(e) = |G|$ and$\chi_{\mathrm{reg}}(g) = 0$ for$g \neq e$. Therefore:
$$m_i = \frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g \in G} \chi_{\mathrm{reg}}(g)\overline{\chi_i(g)} = \frac{1}{|G|} \cdot |G| \cdot \overline{\chi_i(e)} = \overline{n_i} = n_i.$$

This calculation is the complete proof using characters, and it confirms the multiplicity-equals-dimension result with a simple computation. The regular representation is thus the "universal" representation, encoding the full representation theory of $G$ in a single object: knowing the decomposition of$k[G]$ is equivalent to knowing all irreducible representations together with their dimensions.

\begin{remark}
The character of the regular representation $\chi_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is particularly simple:$\chi_{\mathrm{reg}}(e) = |G|$ and$\chi_{\mathrm{reg}}(g) = 0$ for all$g \neq e$. This can be seen directly from the permutation action:$g$ acts on the basis$\{h : h \in G\}$ of$k[G]$ by left multiplication, and$g \cdot h = gh$. For the trace, we need$gh = h$, which happens only when$g = e$. So$\chi_{\mathrm{reg}}(g) = |\{h \in G : gh = h\}| = |\{e\}| = 1$ if$g = e$, and... wait, more carefully:$\mathrm{tr}(\rho_{\mathrm{reg}}(g))$ counts the basis vectors$h$ with coefficient of$h$ in$g \cdot h = gh$ equal to 1, i.e., counts$h$ with$gh = h$, i.e.,$g = e$. When$g \neq e$, no$h$ satisfies$gh = h$ (since$g \neq e$ in a group), so$\chi_{\mathrm{reg}}(g) = 0$.
\end{remark}

The decomposition of the regular representation also implies that every irreducible representation $V_i$ is a direct summand of$k[G]$, hence projective. This is one way to see that all representations are projective in the semisimple case: they are direct summands of direct sums of copies of$k[G]$, and direct summands of projective modules are projective.

\begin{example}
For $G = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} = \{e, g\}$ over$\mathbb{C}$: the regular representation$\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}]$ has basis$\{e, g\}$. The two irreducibles are the trivial ($V_+$:$g \mapsto 1$) and sign ($V_-$:$g \mapsto -1$), both 1-dimensional. The decomposition$\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}] \cong V_+ \oplus V_-$ is realized by the change of basis:$v_+ = e + g$ (fixed by$g$:$g \cdot v_+ = g + g^2 = g + e = v_+$) and$v_- = e - g$ ($g \cdot v_- = g - e = -v_-$). This is the DFT of$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.
\end{example}
