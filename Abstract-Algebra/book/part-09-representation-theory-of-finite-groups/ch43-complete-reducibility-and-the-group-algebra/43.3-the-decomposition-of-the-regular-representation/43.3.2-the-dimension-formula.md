# 43.3.2 The Dimension Formula $\sum n_i^2 = |G|$

From the decomposition of the regular representation — each irreducible $V_i$ appears with multiplicity$n_i = \dim V_i$ — a dimension count gives an elegant and fundamental identity. This formula is a cornerstone of the theory: it is simultaneously a consistency check, a strong constraint on the representation ring, and a theorem about the group algebra.

\begin{theorem}[Dimension Formula]
Let $G$ be a finite group and$V_1, \ldots, V_r$ the irreducible representations of$G$ over an algebraically closed field$k$ with$\mathrm{char}(k) \nmid |G|$. Then:
$$\sum_{i=1}^r n_i^2 = |G|, \quad \text{where } n_i = \dim_k V_i.$$
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The regular representation has $\dim k[G] = |G|$ (one basis vector per group element). The decomposition$k[G] \cong \bigoplus_{i=1}^r V_i^{\oplus n_i}$ gives total dimension$\sum_i n_i \cdot \dim V_i = \sum_i n_i^2$. Equating:$\sum_i n_i^2 = |G|$.
\end{proof}

The practical power of this constraint is immediate. It is a strong restriction on the possible dimensions of irreducible representations: the squares of all the $n_i$ must sum to exactly$|G|$. For groups of small order, this — combined with the constraint$r =$ number of conjugacy classes — severely limits the possible dimension vectors.

\begin{example}
For $S_3$,$|S_3| = 6$ and$r = 3$ (three conjugacy classes). The only way to write$6 = n_1^2 + n_2^2 + n_3^2$ with$n_i \geq 1$ and three terms is$1 + 1 + 4 = 6$. Thus$(n_1, n_2, n_3) = (1, 1, 2)$: two 1-dimensional irreducibles (trivial and sign) and one 2-dimensional irreducible (standard).
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For $S_4$,$|S_4| = 24$ and$r = 5$ (five conjugacy classes). We need$n_1^2 + \cdots + n_5^2 = 24$ with all$n_i \geq 1$. Since$n_1 = 1$ always (trivial representation), we need$1 + n_2^2 + n_3^2 + n_4^2 + n_5^2 = 24$. By inspection (or systematic enumeration):$1 + 1 + 4 + 9 + 9 = 24$, so$(n_1, \ldots, n_5) = (1, 1, 2, 3, 3)$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For $A_4$,$|A_4| = 12$ and$r = 4$ (four conjugacy classes). We need$n_1^2 + n_2^2 + n_3^2 + n_4^2 = 12$. The solutions with$n_i \geq 1$ and four terms:$1 + 1 + 1 + 9 = 12$, giving$(1,1,1,3)$. (One checks that$1+1+4+4=10 \neq 12$, and$1+1+4+\cdots$ doesn't work.) So$A_4$ has three 1-dimensional and one 3-dimensional irreducibles.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For $D_4$ (dihedral group of order 8),$|D_4| = 8$ and$r = 5$. We need$\sum n_i^2 = 8$ with five terms:$1+1+1+1+4 = 8$, giving$(1,1,1,1,2)$. Same count for$Q_8$ ($|Q_8|=8$,$r=5$): same dimension sequence but different character tables (the two groups are non-isomorphic but have the same dimension vector).
\end{example}

The dimension formula also has a more structural reading via the Artin–Wedderburn isomorphism: since $\mathbb{C}[G] \cong \prod_i M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})$ as$\mathbb{C}$-algebras, comparing dimensions gives$|G| = \sum_i \dim M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C}) = \sum_i n_i^2$. In this form, the formula is a tautology given Artin–Wedderburn, but it condenses remarkable structural information.

\begin{remark}
The dimension formula has a character-theoretic avatar: it is equivalent to the statement that the regular character satisfies $\langle \chi_{\mathrm{reg}}, \chi_{\mathrm{reg}} \rangle = |G|$ (which follows from$\chi_{\mathrm{reg}}(e) = |G|$,$\chi_{\mathrm{reg}}(g) = 0$ for$g \neq e$). Alternatively, substituting$g = e$ in the second orthogonality relation$\sum_i |\chi_i(g)|^2 = |C_G(g)|$ and noting$|C_G(e)| = |G|$:$\sum_i |\chi_i(e)|^2 = \sum_i n_i^2 = |G|$.
\end{remark}

Combined with the conjugacy class count in §43.3.3, these two constraints — $\sum n_i^2 = |G|$ and$r =$ number of conjugacy classes — are often sufficient to determine the dimensions of all irreducibles of a given group, without constructing any explicit representation.

The dimension formula also constrains higher-order quantities. For example, the number $k(G)$ of conjugacy classes and the group order$|G|$ are related by the identity$|G| = \sum_{i=1}^{k(G)} n_i^2$. The ratio$|G|/k(G)$ is the average value of$n_i^2$, which equals the average squared dimension of an irreducible representation (weighted by count, not by multiplicity). For abelian groups, all$n_i = 1$ so$|G|/k(G) = 1$. For non-abelian groups, some$n_i > 1$ forces$|G|/k(G) > 1$, which provides a quantitative measure of "how non-abelian" a group is.

\begin{example}
For $G = D_4$:$|G| = 8$,$k(G) = 5$, dimensions$(1,1,1,1,2)$. Average$n_i^2 = 8/5 = 1.6$. The group is "mildly non-abelian" — most irreducibles are 1-dimensional but there is one 2-dimensional one.
\par
For $G = S_5$:$|G| = 120$,$k(G) = 7$ (conjugacy classes by cycle type:$[1^5], [2,1^3], [2^2,1], [3,1^2], [3,2], [4,1], [5]$). The dimensions satisfy$\sum n_i^2 = 120$ with 7 terms and$n_1 = n_2 = 1$ (trivial and sign). The dimension vector is$(1,1,4,4,5,5,6)$ — the irreducibles of$S_5$ have dimensions$1,1,4,4,5,5,6$. Average$n_i^2 = 120/7 \approx 17.1$.
\end{example}
\par
\begin{remark}
The dimension formula $\sum n_i^2 = |G|$ can be viewed as a special case of the Plancherel formula for the group$G$: the$L^2$ norm of the delta function$\delta_e$ (value 1 at$e$, 0 elsewhere) equals$1$, and its Fourier expansion gives$\sum_i n_i^2/|G|^2 \cdot |G| = \sum_i n_i^2/|G| = 1$. Thus$\sum_i n_i^2 = |G|$ is the Plancherel identity for the function$\delta_e$.
\end{remark}
