# 43.3 The Decomposition of the Regular Representation

The regular representation of $G$ — the action of $G$ on the group algebra $k[G]$ by left multiplication — is in one sense the largest and most redundant representation one can write down. It has dimension $|G|$, containing every irreducible subrepresentation many times over. Yet this redundancy is precisely the point. The regular representation is in another sense the most canonical and informative: every irreducible representation of $G$ appears inside it, and the multiplicity with which it appears reveals a beautiful numerical structure.

By Maschke's theorem and the Artin–Wedderburn decomposition, $k[G] \cong \bigoplus_i V_i^{\oplus m_i}$ as representations, where the sum runs over all irreducible representations $V_i$ and $m_i$ is the multiplicity of $V_i$. To determine $m_i$, one uses a standard trick: compute $\text{Hom}_{k[G]}(k[G], V_i)$. On one hand, by the Yoneda-style argument (maps from a free module to $M$ are determined by the image of the generator), this space is isomorphic to $V_i$ as a $k$-vector space, so its dimension is $\dim V_i$. On the other hand, using the decomposition $k[G] \cong \bigoplus_j V_j^{\oplus m_j}$ and Schur's lemma, $\text{Hom}_{k[G]}(k[G], V_i) \cong m_i \cdot \text{Hom}_{k[G]}(V_i, V_i) \cong m_i \cdot k$, which has dimension $m_i$. Therefore $m_i = \dim V_i$.

This single equation — each irreducible $V_i$ appears in the regular representation with multiplicity equal to its own dimension — has far-reaching consequences. Summing dimensions over all irreducibles gives $\sum_i (\dim V_i)^2 = \dim k[G] = |G|$. For $S_3$ with irreducibles of dimensions 1, 1, 2, this reads $1 + 1 + 4 = 6$; for $S_4$ with irreducibles of dimensions 1, 1, 2, 3, 3, it reads $1 + 1 + 4 + 9 + 9 = 24$. The formula is a theorem, but it feels like magic: the dimensions of the irreducible representations are not independent — they are constrained to be the side-lengths of squares that tile the group order. This is the numerical shadow of the Artin–Wedderburn theorem, and it is the first of many numerical miracles in the representation theory of finite groups.
