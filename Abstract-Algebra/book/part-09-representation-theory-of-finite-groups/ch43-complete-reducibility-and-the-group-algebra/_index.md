# Chapter 43 — Complete Reducibility and the Group Algebra

The question at the heart of representation theory is not merely whether a representation decomposes, but whether it must. Over a field of characteristic zero — or more precisely, over any field whose characteristic does not divide $|G|$ — the answer is yes, always and without exception. This is Maschke's theorem, and it transforms the problem of classifying representations from an open-ended question into a finite one: since every representation decomposes completely into irreducibles, classifying all representations of $G$ reduces to classifying the finitely many irreducibles and recording which ones appear and with what multiplicity. The proof is a marvel of the averaging trick: given any $G$-stable subspace $W \subseteq V$, take any linear projection $\pi: V \to W$ and symmetrize it to $\bar{\pi} = \frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g \in G} g \pi g^{-1}$. The result is automatically $G$-equivariant, still a projection onto $W$, and its kernel is the desired $G$-stable complement. Division by $|G|$ is what makes this work, which is precisely why the theorem fails when $\text{char}(k)$ divides $|G|$.

With complete reducibility established, the group algebra $k[G]$ takes center stage. As a $k[G]$-module over itself, $k[G]$ is the regular representation; Maschke's theorem guarantees it decomposes into irreducibles. But the full structure of $k[G]$ as a ring is captured by the Artin–Wedderburn theorem, one of the great theorems of ring theory. A semisimple ring — one in which every module is completely reducible — is isomorphic to a direct product of matrix rings over division algebras. Over an algebraically closed field, those division algebras must all be the field itself, so $k[G] \cong M_{n_1}(k) \times M_{n_2}(k) \times \cdots \times M_{n_r}(k)$ where $r$ is the number of isomorphism classes of irreducible representations and $n_i = \dim V_i$. The Artin–Wedderburn theorem does not merely classify $k[G]$: it classifies all its modules at once, since a module over a product of matrix rings decomposes according to the factors.

The decomposition of the regular representation is where these structural theorems yield their most elegant numerical output. Since $k[G]$ decomposes as $\bigoplus_i V_i^{\oplus \dim V_i}$, each irreducible representation $V_i$ appears with multiplicity equal to its own dimension. Comparing dimensions immediately gives the celebrated formula $\sum_i (\dim V_i)^2 = |G|$. For $S_3$ this reads $1^2 + 1^2 + 2^2 = 6$, a fact that seems like numerology but is a theorem. A second consequence is that the number of irreducible representations of $G$ equals the dimension of the center $Z(k[G])$, which in turn equals the number of conjugacy classes of $G$. This connection — between representation theory and the conjugacy structure of the group — previews the deep duality encoded in the character table and motivates everything that follows in Chapter 44.
