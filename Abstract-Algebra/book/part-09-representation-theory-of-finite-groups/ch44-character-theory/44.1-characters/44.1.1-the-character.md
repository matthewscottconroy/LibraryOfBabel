# 44.1.1 The Character $\chi_V(g) = \mathrm{tr}(\rho(g))$

A representation $(V, \rho)$ of a group$G$ carries a great deal of linear-algebraic data: for each$g \in G$, there is a matrix$\rho(g) \in GL(V)$, and these matrices multiply together according to the group law. Working directly with these matrices is often cumbersome, both because they depend on a choice of basis and because keeping track of all entries simultaneously is unwieldy. Character theory offers a remarkable compression: rather than recording the full matrix$\rho(g)$, one records only its trace.

\begin{definition}
Let $(V, \rho)$ be a finite-dimensional representation of$G$ over a field$k$. The \emph{character} of$V$ is the function$\chi_V: G \to k$ defined by$\chi_V(g) = \mathrm{tr}(\rho(g))$.
\end{definition}

The key insight is that the trace is a basis-independent quantity. If one replaces the basis of $V$ by another, the matrix$\rho(g)$ is replaced by a conjugate$P\rho(g)P^{-1}$, and conjugate matrices have the same trace:$\mathrm{tr}(P\rho(g)P^{-1}) = \mathrm{tr}(\rho(g))$ (using cyclicity of trace:$\mathrm{tr}(ABC) = \mathrm{tr}(CAB)$). This means$\chi_V(g)$ is an intrinsic invariant of the representation, not an artifact of coordinate choices.

\begin{example}
The trivial representation $V = k$ with$\rho(g) = 1$ for all$g$ has character$\chi_{\mathrm{triv}}(g) = 1$ for all$g$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For $G = S_3$ acting on$V = \mathbb{C}^3$ by permuting coordinates,$\rho(\sigma)$ is the permutation matrix of$\sigma$. The trace of a permutation matrix equals the number of fixed points:
\begin{align*}
\chi_V(e) &= 3, \\
\chi_V((12)) &= 1 \quad (\text{since } (12) \text{ fixes only } 3), \\
\chi_V((123)) &= 0 \quad (\text{since } (123) \text{ fixes no element}).
\end{align*}
This is the character of the 3-dimensional permutation representation.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For the sign representation $V = \mathbb{C}$ with$\rho(\sigma) = \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)$, the character is$\chi_{\mathrm{sgn}}(\sigma) = \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) \in \{+1, -1\}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For the standard representation of $S_3$ (the 2-dimensional irreducible arising as the complement to the trivial 1-dimensional in the permutation representation), one can compute directly. Writing matrices in the basis$\{e_1 - e_2, e_1 - e_3\}$:
$$\rho((12)) = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \rho((123)) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Then $\chi_{\mathrm{std}}(e) = 2$,$\chi_{\mathrm{std}}((12)) = -1 + 1 = 0$,$\chi_{\mathrm{std}}((123)) = 0 + (-1) = -1$.
\end{example}

To see why this compression loses no essential information over $\mathbb{C}$: the key theorem is that characters are \emph{complete invariants} for representations over$\mathbb{C}$ (proved in §44.2.5). Two representations are isomorphic if and only if they have the same character. At first glance it seems implausible that a single scalar function could determine an entire family of matrices up to simultaneous conjugacy. The mechanism is the inner product on class functions (§44.2.1), which allows one to recover all multiplicities of irreducible constituents from the character alone.

\begin{remark}
The character $\chi_V$ carries strictly less information than the full representation$\rho$ over non-algebraically closed fields or in positive characteristic. Over$\mathbb{R}$, representations with the same complex character may still differ as real representations (they may be of "real type" vs "quaternionic type"). In characteristic$p$, the trace is not always a complete invariant. The completeness theorem is specific to characteristic-zero algebraically closed fields.
\end{remark}

Characters are the "shadows" of representations in the sense that they are simpler scalar functions, yet they are informative enough to classify representations completely over $\mathbb{C}$. This is the foundational miracle of character theory: a drastic compression of information (from an$n \times n$ matrix for each$g$ to a single number per$g$) loses nothing essential about the representation-theoretic structure.

To appreciate the compression involved, consider a representation of $S_3$ of dimension 2. The "raw data" of the representation consists of two$2 \times 2$ invertible matrices (one for each generator, say$(12)$ and$(123)$), subject to the relations of$S_3$. In general, this is a 4-dimensional space of choices per generator. The character compresses all this to just three numbers:$\chi(e) = 2$,$\chi((12)) \in \mathbb{C}$,$\chi((123)) \in \mathbb{C}$. The orthogonality theory then shows these three numbers (plus the constraint that they are sums of roots of unity and have$\langle \chi, \chi \rangle = 1$) are sufficient to identify the representation uniquely.

\begin{example}
For $G = D_4$ and the 2-dimensional irreducible$\chi_5$: the character values are$\chi_5(e) = 2$,$\chi_5(r^2) = -2$,$\chi_5(r) = 0$,$\chi_5(s) = 0$,$\chi_5(rs) = 0$. This single function on the 5 conjugacy classes of$D_4$ determines the representation completely. Any two 2-dimensional representations of$D_4$ with these character values are isomorphic to each other. In contrast, there are many non-isomorphic 2-dimensional real Lie group representations with similar numerical data.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
The character $\chi_V$ takes values in the ring$\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{|G|}]$ of algebraic integers in the cyclotomic field$\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{|G|})$. For symmetric groups$S_n$, all character values happen to be ordinary integers (because all representations of$S_n$ can be defined over$\mathbb{Q}$, in fact over$\mathbb{Z}$). For groups with complex characters (like$A_4$ or$\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$), the character values involve cube roots of unity or other algebraic numbers. The field generated by all character values of$G$ is called the \emph{character field} and is related to the splitting field of$G$ over$\mathbb{Q}$.
\end{remark}
