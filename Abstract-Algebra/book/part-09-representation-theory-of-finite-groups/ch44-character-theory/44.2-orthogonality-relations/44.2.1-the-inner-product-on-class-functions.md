# 44.2.1 The Inner Product on Class Functions

Since characters are class functions, the natural arena for studying them is the vector space $\mathrm{Cl}(G, \mathbb{C})$ of all complex-valued class functions on$G$. This space carries a canonical Hermitian inner product that respects the group structure and serves as the main computational tool of character theory.

\begin{definition}
The \emph{inner product} on $\mathrm{Cl}(G, \mathbb{C})$ is defined by
$$\langle f_1, f_2 \rangle = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{h \in G} f_1(h)\, \overline{f_2(h)}.$$
\end{definition}

This is a Hermitian inner product: it is linear in the first argument, conjugate-linear in the second, and $\langle f, f \rangle > 0$ for$f \neq 0$. The factor of$1/|G|$ is a normalization that makes the arithmetic work out cleanly in subsequent theorems — specifically, the irreducible characters will be orthonormal, not merely orthogonal.

The definition makes $\mathrm{Cl}(G, \mathbb{C})$ into a finite-dimensional inner product space, and the dimension of this space is the number of conjugacy classes of$G$ — since the indicator functions$\delta_{[g]}$ (value 1 on the class of$g$, zero elsewhere) form an orthogonal basis (though not orthonormal:$\langle \delta_{[g]}, \delta_{[g]} \rangle = |[g]|/|G| = 1/|C_G(g)|$).

\begin{example}
For $G = S_3$ with conjugacy classes$C_1 = \{e\}$,$C_2 = \{(12),(13),(23)\}$,$C_3 = \{(123),(132)\}$: take$f_1 = \chi_{\mathrm{triv}}$ (all values 1) and$f_2 = \chi_{\mathrm{sgn}}$ (values$+1, -1, +1$ on$C_1, C_2, C_3$). Then:
$$\langle f_1, f_2 \rangle = \frac{1}{6}[1 \cdot \overline{1} + 1 \cdot \overline{(-1)} + 1 \cdot \overline{(-1)} + 1 \cdot \overline{(-1)} + 1 \cdot \overline{1} + 1 \cdot \overline{1}] = \frac{1}{6}[1 - 1 - 1 - 1 + 1 + 1] = 0.$$
The trivial and sign characters are orthogonal.
\end{example}

The key insight is that this inner product is exactly the right tool for decomposing characters. By Maschke's theorem, every representation over $\mathbb{C}$ decomposes into irreducibles, and the character of the whole representation is a sum of irreducible characters with non-negative integer multiplicities. The inner product provides the mechanism to extract each irreducible piece.

A useful reformulation makes the connection to representation theory explicit. For two representations $V$ and$W$ of$G$:
$$\langle \chi_V, \chi_W \rangle = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} \chi_V(g)\,\overline{\chi_W(g)} = \dim_{\mathbb{C}} \mathrm{Hom}_G(W, V).$$
The last equality follows from the averaging (Reynolds) argument: $\dim \mathrm{Hom}_G(W,V) = \mathrm{tr}(\pi_{\mathrm{eq}})$ where$\pi_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{1}{|G|}\sum_g \rho_V(g) \otimes \rho_W(g^{-1})^T$ is the equivariant projector on$\mathrm{Hom}(W,V)$, and computing its trace gives$\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_g \mathrm{tr}(\rho_V(g))\overline{\mathrm{tr}(\rho_W(g))} = \langle \chi_V, \chi_W \rangle$.

\begin{remark}
The inner product can also be written using conjugacy class representatives. If $C_1, \ldots, C_r$ are the conjugacy classes with representatives$g_1, \ldots, g_r$ and sizes$|C_j|$, then:
$$\langle f_1, f_2 \rangle = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{j=1}^r |C_j|\, f_1(g_j)\,\overline{f_2(g_j)} = \sum_{j=1}^r \frac{f_1(g_j)\,\overline{f_2(g_j)}}{|C_G(g_j)|},$$
where $|C_G(g)| = |G|/|[g]|$ is the centralizer size. This form is convenient for character table computations.
\end{remark}

The definition also connects to the representation-theoretic operation of \emph{averaging}, or \emph{Reynolds operator}: the map $T \mapsto \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{h \in G} \rho(h) T \rho(h)^{-1}$ projects any linear map onto the space of$G$-equivariant maps. The inner product arises naturally when one applies this averaging idea to compute$\mathrm{Hom}_G(V, W)$, and this is precisely the route through which the orthogonality relations are proved in §44.2.2.

\begin{example}
For $G = S_4$ and the two 3-dimensional characters$\chi_4 = (3,1,0,-1,-1)$ and$\chi_5 = (3,-1,0,1,-1)$ (using columns$e, (12), (123), (1234), (12)(34)$ with class sizes$1,6,8,6,3$):
\begin{align*}
\langle \chi_4, \chi_5 \rangle &= \frac{1}{24}\left[1 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 + 6 \cdot 1 \cdot (-1) + 8 \cdot 0 \cdot 0 + 6 \cdot (-1)(1) + 3 \cdot (-1)(-1)\right] \\
&= \frac{1}{24}[9 - 6 + 0 - 6 + 3] = \frac{0}{24} = 0. \checkmark
\end{align*}
The two 3-dimensional irreducibles of $S_4$ are orthogonal, as the first orthogonality theorem predicts.
\end{example}

The inner product makes the set of all class functions into a Hilbert space of dimension $r$ (the number of conjugacy classes). This is the finite-dimensional analogue of the$L^2$ space in harmonic analysis. The irreducible characters form an orthonormal basis, playing the same role as the characters$e^{in\theta}$ in the$L^2$ theory of the circle group$U(1)$. The inner product thus gives the entire theory a Fourier-analytic flavor: decomposing a representation is computing Fourier coefficients, and the orthogonality relations are the Parseval identity in this finite setting.

\begin{remark}
The inner product $\langle f_1, f_2 \rangle = \frac{1}{|G|}\sum_g f_1(g)\overline{f_2(g)}$ can be generalized: for any positive measure$\mu$ on$G$ with$\mu(G) = 1$, one gets an inner product on class functions. The specific choice of uniform measure (each element has weight$1/|G|$) is forced by the requirement that all$G$-translates of any class function have the same inner product with themselves — i.e., the inner product is$G$-invariant. This uniqueness of the normalized Haar measure on a finite group (uniform measure) is the finite analogue of the uniqueness of Haar measure on compact groups.
\end{remark}
