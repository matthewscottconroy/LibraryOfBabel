# 44.2.2 First Orthogonality: Irreducible Characters Are Orthonormal

The first orthogonality relation is the central theorem of character theory. It asserts that the irreducible characters form an orthonormal set — and, given the dimension count from §43.3.3, actually an orthonormal \emph{basis} — for the space of class functions.

\begin{theorem}[First Orthogonality]
Let $\chi_1, \ldots, \chi_r$ be the irreducible characters of$G$ over$\mathbb{C}$. Then:
$$\langle \chi_i, \chi_j \rangle = \delta_{ij}.$$
That is, distinct irreducible characters are orthogonal, and each irreducible character has norm $1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The key ingredient is Schur's lemma. For any $\mathbb{C}$-linear map$T: V_i \to V_j$, define the averaged map$\bar{T} = \frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g \in G} \rho_j(g) \circ T \circ \rho_i(g)^{-1}$. Then$\bar{T}$ is a$G$-equivariant map$V_i \to V_j$. By Schur's lemma: (a) if$i \neq j$,$\bar{T} = 0$; (b) if$i = j$,$\bar{T} = \lambda \cdot \mathrm{Id}$ for some scalar$\lambda$.

Taking $T = E_{ab}$ (the matrix unit with 1 in position$(a,b)$, zero elsewhere) and computing traces in cases (a) and (b) yields the \emph{Schur orthogonality relations on matrix entries}:
$$\frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} (\rho_i(g))_{ab}\,\overline{(\rho_j(g))_{cd}} = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{n_i} \delta_{ac} \delta_{bd} & \text{if } i = j, \\ 0 & \text{if } i \neq j.\end{cases}$$

Summing over $a = b$ and$c = d$ (i.e., taking traces on both sides) gives:
$$\frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} \chi_i(g)\,\overline{\chi_j(g)} = \frac{1}{n_i} \cdot n_i \cdot \delta_{ij} = \delta_{ij}.$$
\end{proof}

The consequences of orthonormality are far-reaching. First, the irreducible characters are linearly independent in $\mathrm{Cl}(G, \mathbb{C})$. Second — and here the dimension count is crucial — the number of irreducible representations of$G$ over$\mathbb{C}$ equals the number of conjugacy classes of$G$, which is exactly$\dim \mathrm{Cl}(G, \mathbb{C})$. This means the irreducible characters do not just form an orthonormal set; they form an orthonormal \emph{basis} for the entire space of class functions.

\begin{corollary}
Every class function $f \in \mathrm{Cl}(G, \mathbb{C})$ can be uniquely written as$f = \sum_i \langle f, \chi_i \rangle \chi_i$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{example}
For $G = S_3$ with$\chi_1$ = trivial,$\chi_2$ = sign,$\chi_3$ = standard: compute$\langle \chi_1, \chi_1 \rangle = \frac{1}{6}(1 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 1) = 1$. For$\langle \chi_3, \chi_3 \rangle$: using$\chi_3(e) = 2$,$\chi_3((12)) = 0$,$\chi_3((123)) = -1$:
$$\langle \chi_3, \chi_3 \rangle = \frac{1}{6}(1 \cdot 4 + 3 \cdot 0 + 2 \cdot 1) = \frac{6}{6} = 1. \checkmark$$
For $\langle \chi_1, \chi_3 \rangle = \frac{1}{6}(1 \cdot 2 + 3 \cdot 0 + 2 \cdot (-1)) = \frac{0}{6} = 0. \checkmark$
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For $G = D_4 = \langle r, s \mid r^4 = s^2 = 1, \, srs^{-1} = r^{-1}\rangle$: the five conjugacy classes are$\{1\}$,$\{r^2\}$,$\{r, r^3\}$,$\{s, r^2s\}$,$\{rs, r^3s\}$ with sizes$1, 1, 2, 2, 2$. The five irreducible characters have dimensions$(1,1,1,1,2)$. A character of norm 1 satisfying the dimension formula can be found by:
$$\langle \chi, \chi \rangle = \frac{1}{8}(n_1^2 + n_2^2 + 2n_3^2 + 2n_4^2 + 2n_5^2) = 1,$$
where $n_j = \chi(g_j)$ for class representative$g_j$.
\end{example}

The surprise here is the equality between the number of irreducibles and the number of conjugacy classes. These two quantities come from completely different structural considerations — one from the representation theory via Artin–Wedderburn, the other from the combinatorics of the group — yet they must always agree. Character orthogonality is the natural pathway to understanding it: the irreducible characters are an orthonormal basis for $\mathrm{Cl}(G, \mathbb{C})$, and the dimension of this space counts conjugacy classes. From the computational standpoint, first orthogonality is what makes the character table a square matrix.

\begin{remark}
The finer Schur orthogonality on matrix entries $\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_g (\rho_i(g))_{ab}\overline{(\rho_j(g))_{cd}} = \frac{1}{n_i}\delta_{ij}\delta_{ac}\delta_{bd}$ says that the$n_i^2$ matrix-entry functions of$V_i$ and the$n_j^2$ matrix-entry functions of$V_j$ are orthogonal subsets of$L^2(G)$. Together, these$\sum_i n_i^2 = |G|$ functions form an orthogonal basis for all$L^2(G)$ (functions$G \to \mathbb{C}$). This is the \emph{Peter–Weyl theorem} in its finite-group form.
\end{remark}
