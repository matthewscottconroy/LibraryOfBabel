# 44.2.4 Decomposing a Representation: $m_i = \langle \chi_V, \chi_{V_i} \rangle$

By Maschke's theorem, any representation $V$ of a finite group over$\mathbb{C}$ decomposes as a direct sum of irreducibles:$V \cong \bigoplus_i m_i V_i$, where$m_i \geq 0$ are the multiplicities. The first orthogonality relation immediately yields the multiplicity formula, which is the main computational tool in representation theory.

\begin{theorem}[Multiplicity Formula]
Let $V \cong \bigoplus_{i=1}^r m_i V_i$ be the decomposition of a representation$V$ into irreducibles. Then:
$$m_i = \langle \chi_V, \chi_{V_i} \rangle = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} \chi_V(g)\,\overline{\chi_{V_i}(g)}.$$
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Taking characters of both sides and using additivity: $\chi_V = \sum_i m_i \chi_{V_i}$. Taking the inner product with$\chi_{V_j}$ and using first orthogonality:
$$\langle \chi_V, \chi_{V_j} \rangle = \sum_i m_i \langle \chi_{V_i}, \chi_{V_j} \rangle = \sum_i m_i \delta_{ij} = m_j.$$
\end{proof}

The practical procedure is then: given a representation $V$ (often presented concretely, for example as a permutation representation or a tensor product), compute$\chi_V(g)$ for each conjugacy class representative$g$, then compute the inner products with each irreducible character$\chi_{V_i}$. The result is a non-negative integer$m_i$, telling how many times$V_i$ appears as a summand of$V$.

\begin{example}
Decompose the permutation representation of $S_3$ on$\{1,2,3\}$. The character is$\chi_{\mathrm{perm}}(g) = |\mathrm{Fix}(g)|$:$\chi_{\mathrm{perm}}(e) = 3$,$\chi_{\mathrm{perm}}((12)) = 1$,$\chi_{\mathrm{perm}}((123)) = 0$. Using$|C_1| = 1$,$|C_2| = 3$,$|C_3| = 2$:
\begin{align*}
m_1 &= \frac{1}{6}(1 \cdot 3 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 0 \cdot 1) = \frac{6}{6} = 1, \\
m_2 &= \frac{1}{6}(1 \cdot 3 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot 1 \cdot (-1) + 2 \cdot 0 \cdot 1) = \frac{0}{6} = 0, \\
m_3 &= \frac{1}{6}(1 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 + 3 \cdot 1 \cdot 0 + 2 \cdot 0 \cdot (-1)) = \frac{6}{6} = 1.
\end{align*}
So $\chi_{\mathrm{perm}} = \chi_1 + \chi_3$: the permutation representation decomposes as trivial$\oplus$ standard.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Decompose the representation of $D_4$ on$\mathbb{C}^4$ by acting on the vertices of a square (labeled$1,2,3,4$ in cyclic order). The element$r$ (rotation by$90°$) has no fixed vertices, so$\chi(r) = 0$. The element$r^2$ has no fixed vertices,$\chi(r^2) = 0$. The identity:$\chi(e) = 4$. The reflection$s$ (through vertical axis) fixes 0 vertices if no vertex lies on the axis, or 2 if the axis passes through vertices: for the labeling with$s$ fixing vertices 1 and 3,$\chi(s) = 2$. The reflection$rs$ (through a diagonal) fixes 0 vertices:$\chi(rs) = 0$. Using the$D_4$ character table with class sizes$(1,1,2,2,2)$:
$$m_i = \frac{1}{8}[1 \cdot \chi(e) n_1(e) + 1 \cdot \chi(r^2) n_1(r^2) + 2 \cdot \chi(r) n_1(r) + 2 \cdot \chi(s) n_1(s) + 2 \cdot \chi(rs) n_1(rs)].$$
\end{example}

As a consistency check, once all multiplicities $m_i$ are known, the dimension formula$\dim V = \sum_i m_i \dim V_i = \sum_i m_i \chi_{V_i}(e)$ must hold.

\begin{proposition}[Irreducibility criterion]
A representation $V$ is irreducible if and only if$\langle \chi_V, \chi_V \rangle = 1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\langle \chi_V, \chi_V \rangle = \langle \sum_i m_i \chi_i, \sum_j m_j \chi_j \rangle = \sum_i m_i^2$. This equals 1 if and only if exactly one$m_i = 1$ and all others are 0, i.e.,$V$ is irreducible.
\end{proof}

This irreducibility criterion is especially convenient: it requires only a single inner product computation rather than a search for invariant subspaces.

\begin{example}
For the standard representation of $S_3$:$\langle \chi_3, \chi_3 \rangle = \frac{1}{6}(4 + 0 + 2) = 1$. Irreducible. \checkmark
\par
For the permutation representation of $S_3$:$\langle \chi_{\mathrm{perm}}, \chi_{\mathrm{perm}} \rangle = \frac{1}{6}(9 + 3 + 0) = 2 \neq 1$. Reducible, confirming the decomposition$\chi_{\mathrm{perm}} = \chi_1 + \chi_3$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Decompose the tensor product $\chi_4 \otimes \chi_4$ for the standard 3-dimensional representation of$S_4$. The character values of$\chi_4 \otimes \chi_4$ are$\chi_4(g)^2$:
\par
$(\chi_4)^2(e) = 9$,$(\chi_4)^2((12)) = 1$,$(\chi_4)^2((123)) = 0$,$(\chi_4)^2((1234)) = 1$,$(\chi_4)^2((12)(34)) = 1$.
\par
Compute multiplicities using class sizes $(1,6,8,6,3)$:
\begin{align*}
m_1 &= \frac{1}{24}(9 + 6 + 0 + 6 + 3) = \frac{24}{24} = 1, \\
m_2 &= \frac{1}{24}(9 - 6 + 0 - 6 + 3) = \frac{0}{24} = 0, \\
m_3 &= \frac{1}{24}(18 + 0 + 0 + 0 + 6) = \frac{24}{24} = 1, \\
m_4 &= \frac{1}{24}(27 + 6 + 0 - 6 - 3) = \frac{24}{24} = 1, \\
m_5 &= \frac{1}{24}(27 - 6 + 0 + 6 - 3) = \frac{24}{24} = 1.
\end{align*}
So $\chi_4 \otimes \chi_4 \cong \chi_1 \oplus \chi_3 \oplus \chi_4 \oplus \chi_5$ (dimension check:$9 = 1+2+3+3$ \checkmark).
\end{example}

The multiplicity formula is the workhorse of practical character theory: any time one has a concrete representation (from a physical or combinatorial problem) and wants to identify its irreducible components, the formula provides the answer in $r$ inner product computations. This efficiency — reducing a potentially infinite search for invariant subspaces to$r$ dot products — is the computational payoff of the entire theory.
