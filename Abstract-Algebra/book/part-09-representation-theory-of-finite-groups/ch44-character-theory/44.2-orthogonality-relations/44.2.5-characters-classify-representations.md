# 44.2.5 Characters Classify Representations: $\chi_V = \chi_W \Leftrightarrow V \cong W$

Two representations $V$ and$W$ of$G$ over$\mathbb{C}$ are isomorphic if and only if they have the same character:$\chi_V = \chi_W$. This is the classification theorem of character theory, and it is arguably the most important single result in the subject.

\begin{theorem}[Characters Classify Representations]
Let $V$ and$W$ be finite-dimensional representations of$G$ over$\mathbb{C}$. Then$V \cong W$ if and only if$\chi_V = \chi_W$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$(\Rightarrow)$ Isomorphic representations are related by a change of basis, and the trace is invariant under change of basis. Hence isomorphic representations have equal characters.

$(\Leftarrow)$ Suppose$\chi_V = \chi_W$. For each irreducible$V_i$, the multiplicity of$V_i$ in$V$ is$m_i(V) = \langle \chi_V, \chi_{V_i} \rangle$, and similarly$m_i(W) = \langle \chi_W, \chi_{V_i} \rangle$. Since$\chi_V = \chi_W$, we have$m_i(V) = m_i(W)$ for all$i$. Since$V \cong \bigoplus_i m_i(V) V_i$ and$W \cong \bigoplus_i m_i(W) V_i$, we conclude$V \cong W$.
\end{proof}

The significance of this theorem cannot be overstated. It says that the character is a \emph{complete invariant} for representations: all the information about how a representation decomposes into irreducibles — and hence all information about the representation up to isomorphism — is encoded in the single function $\chi_V: G \to \mathbb{C}$. One never needs to keep track of the individual matrices$\rho(g)$ or the specific invariant subspaces; the character suffices.

\begin{example}
The permutation representation of $S_3$ on$\{1,2,3\}$ and the direct sum of the trivial and standard representations are not obviously isomorphic as abstract representations. The character theorem makes this immediate: both have character with values$\chi(e) = 3$,$\chi((12)) = 1$,$\chi((123)) = 0$, so they are isomorphic.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For $G = D_4$ and$G' = Q_8$: both groups have the same order 8, the same number of conjugacy classes 5, and the same dimension vector$(1,1,1,1,2)$ for their irreducible representations. However, their character tables are genuinely different. The unique 2-dimensional irreducible character$\chi_5$ of$D_4$ satisfies$\chi_5(r^2) = -2$ (since$r^2$ acts as$-\mathrm{Id}$ on the standard 2-dimensional representation), while the analogous character of$Q_8$ satisfies$\chi_5(-1) = -2$. But the character values on the other conjugacy classes differ, so the two groups cannot be "confused" by their character tables, even though they have the same abstract dimension data.
\end{example}

The classification theorem has a practical implication: to determine whether two concrete representations (given by explicit matrices) are isomorphic, it suffices to compute their characters and check equality. Since characters are much simpler objects than the full matrices, this reduces an isomorphism problem to a finite computation.

\begin{example}
Consider the two 2-dimensional representations of $\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$:$\rho_1(\sigma) = \begin{pmatrix}\omega & 0 \\ 0 & \omega^2\end{pmatrix}$ and$\rho_2(\sigma) = \begin{pmatrix}\omega^2 & 0 \\ 0 & \omega\end{pmatrix}$ where$\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$. The characters are$\chi_{\rho_1}(\sigma^k) = \omega^k + \omega^{2k}$ and$\chi_{\rho_2}(\sigma^k) = \omega^{2k} + \omega^k$. These are equal, confirming$\rho_1 \cong \rho_2$. Indeed, both decompose as$V_1 \oplus V_2$ where$V_1$ and$V_2$ are the 1-dimensional characters$\chi_1$ and$\chi_2$. The two representations are isomorphic (related by swapping the two summands).
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
The classification theorem fails in the modular setting. Over a field of characteristic $p \mid |G|$, there exist non-isomorphic representations with the same Brauer character (the modular analogue of the ordinary character). One must use the full Brauer character table, together with decomposition numbers relating ordinary and modular representations, to classify modular representations.
\end{remark}

This is what makes character theory such a powerful organizing principle: it replaces the study of linear maps with the study of scalar functions, and these functions carry just enough information to distinguish any two inequivalent representations.

The classification theorem has a practical implication: to determine whether two concrete representations (given by explicit matrices) are isomorphic, it suffices to compute their characters and check equality. Since characters are much simpler objects than the full matrices, this reduces an isomorphism problem to a finite computation.

\begin{example}
Consider two 6-dimensional representations of $S_3$:$V = \mathrm{Ind}^{S_3}_H \mathbf{1}$ (regular representation of$H = \langle (12) \rangle$, induced to$S_3$) and$W = V_1^{\oplus 3} \oplus V_3^{\oplus ?}$... let us instead consider the two representations$V = V_3^{\oplus 3}$ (three copies of the standard) and$W = V_1 \oplus V_2 \oplus V_3^{\oplus 2}$ of$S_3$. Both have dimension 6. Their characters:$\chi_V = 3\chi_3 = (6, 0, -3)$ and$\chi_W = \chi_1 + \chi_2 + 2\chi_3 = (1+1+4, 1-1+0, 1+1-2) = (6, 0, 0)$. Since$\chi_V \neq \chi_W$, the two representations are not isomorphic — even though they have the same dimension.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
The converse direction: if $\chi_V = \chi_W$, then$V \cong W$. Take$G = Q_8$ and consider all representations of dimension 2 over$\mathbb{C}$. The unique irreducible 2-dimensional representation$V_5$ has character$\chi_5 = (2,-2,0,0,0)$. Any other 2-dimensional representation$W$ with$\chi_W = (2,-2,0,0,0)$ must be isomorphic to$V_5$. For example, the representation$\rho: Q_8 \to GL_2(\mathbb{C})$ given by the$2 \times 2$ complex matrix realization of the quaternions ($i \mapsto \begin{pmatrix}i&0\\0&-i\end{pmatrix}$,$j \mapsto \begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$) has trace$2$ at$1$, trace$-2$ at$-1$, and trace$0$ at$\pm i, \pm j, \pm k$. So this explicit quaternionic representation is isomorphic to$V_5$, as the character confirms.
\end{example}
