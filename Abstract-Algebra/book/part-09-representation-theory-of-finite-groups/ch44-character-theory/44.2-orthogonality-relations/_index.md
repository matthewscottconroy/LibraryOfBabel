# 44.2 Orthogonality Relations

The space $\text{Cl}(G)$ of class functions on $G$ admits a natural inner product: $\langle f_1, f_2 \rangle = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} f_1(g) \overline{f_2(g)}$. With respect to this inner product, the irreducible characters of $G$ form an orthonormal set — and in fact an orthonormal basis for $\text{Cl}(G)$. These are the Schur–Frobenius orthogonality relations, the technical heart of classical character theory, and their proof is a computation using the averaging technique that appeared in Maschke's theorem and Schur's lemma.

The proof of the first orthogonality relation begins by averaging the matrix coefficients of two representations. Define the matrix $T_{ij} = \sum_{g \in G} \rho(g^{-1})_{ki} \sigma(g)_{jl}$ and observe that it is an intertwiner from $\sigma$ to $\rho$ (by a direct computation involving the group sum). If $\rho \not\cong \sigma$, Schur's lemma forces $T_{ij} = 0$; if $\rho = \sigma$ is irreducible, Schur's lemma forces $T_{ij} = \lambda \delta_{kl}$ for some scalar, and tracing determines $\lambda = \frac{|G|}{\dim \rho}$. Taking traces in the representation indices yields the orthogonality of characters: $\langle \chi_\rho, \chi_\sigma \rangle = \delta_{\rho, \sigma}$ when both are irreducible. This is the first orthogonality relation.

The second orthogonality relation is a column version of the first: $\sum_i \chi_i(g) \overline{\chi_i(h)} = |C_G(g)| \delta_{[g],[h]}$, where the sum runs over all irreducible characters and $C_G(g)$ is the centralizer of $g$. It follows from the first relation by regarding the character table as a matrix and using the fact that it is square (established via Artin–Wedderburn). Together, the two orthogonality relations say that the character table, when its columns are normalized appropriately, is a unitary matrix. The practical import of the first relation is also immediate: the multiplicity of an irreducible $V_i$ in any representation $W$ is exactly $\langle \chi_W, \chi_i \rangle$, and two representations are isomorphic if and only if their characters are equal. Character theory thus reduces the isomorphism problem for representations — a priori a linear-algebraic problem involving bases and matrices — to a numerical computation.
