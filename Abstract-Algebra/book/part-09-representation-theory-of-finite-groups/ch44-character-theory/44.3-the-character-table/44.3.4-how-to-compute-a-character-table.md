# 44.3.4 How to Compute a Character Table

Computing a character table from scratch involves combining several methods in a systematic pipeline. No single method suffices for all groups; the art is knowing which tools to deploy and in what order. We describe the general procedure and illustrate with the computation of the character table of $S_4$.

**Step 1: Identify conjugacy classes.** This is a pure group-theoretic computation. For $S_n$, conjugacy classes correspond to cycle types, so this step is combinatorial. For other groups, one uses the definition:$g \sim h$ iff$h = xgx^{-1}$ for some$x \in G$. The count$r$ (number of classes) determines the size of the character table.

**Step 2: Find initial characters.** Several characters are available without effort:
- The trivial character $\chi_1 \equiv 1$.
- The sign character when $G$ has an abelian quotient (e.g.,$S_n \to \{+1,-1\}$ gives the sign for any$S_n$).
- More generally, all 1-dimensional characters come from group homomorphisms $G \to \mathbb{C}^*$, i.e., from the abelianization$G^{\mathrm{ab}} = G/[G,G]$.
- Permutation characters: if $G$ acts on a set$X$, then$\chi_{\mathrm{perm}}(g) = |\mathrm{Fix}(g)|$ is immediate.

**Step 3: Decompose known characters.** Using $m_i = \langle \chi, \chi_i \rangle$, decompose known characters into irreducibles. New irreducibles discovered here are added to the table.

**Step 4: Generate new characters by tensor products.** The product $\chi_i \cdot \chi_j$ (pointwise) is the character of$V_i \otimes V_j$. Decompose it; the new irreducibles may not have appeared before.

**Step 5: Use induction.** Characters induced from subgroups (Chapter 45) produce further characters. For $S_n$, induction from Young subgroups is the main source of all irreducibles.

**Step 6: Apply constraints to fill gaps.** Once most rows are known, use row/column orthogonality, integrality, and the dimension formula to pin down remaining entries.

**Worked Example: $S_4$.**

$|S_4| = 24$, conjugacy classes by cycle type:

$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{Class} & \text{Representative} & \text{Size} \\
\hline
C_1 & e & 1 \\
C_2 & (12) & 6 \\
C_3 & (123) & 8 \\
C_4 & (1234) & 6 \\
C_5 & (12)(34) & 3
\end{array}
$$

Five classes, $r = 5$. Dimension formula:$\sum n_i^2 = 24$ with 5 terms and$n_i \geq 1$. Since$S_4^{\mathrm{ab}} = S_4/A_4 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, there are exactly two 1-dimensional characters:$\chi_1 \equiv 1$ and$\chi_2 = \mathrm{sgn}$. Remaining:$n_3^2 + n_4^2 + n_5^2 = 24 - 1 - 1 = 22$ with all$n_j \geq 2$. Solutions:$4 + 9 + 9 = 22$, giving$(n_3, n_4, n_5) = (2,3,3)$.

The 2-dimensional character $\chi_3$ comes from the quotient$S_4 \to S_4/V_4 \cong S_3$ composed with the standard 2-dimensional irreducible of$S_3$. One computes: elements of$C_5 = \{(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)\}$ lie in$V_4$ and map to the identity in$S_3$, so$\chi_3|_{C_5} = 2$. Elements of$C_3$ (3-cycles) map to 3-cycles in$S_3$ with character$-1$, so$\chi_3((123)) = -1$. Transpositions map to transpositions with character$0$. 4-cycles$(1234)$ map to...$(1234)$ in$S_4/V_4$: the image of$(1234)$ in$S_3$ has order 2 (it's a transposition), so character 0. Thus$\chi_3 = (2, 0, -1, 0, 2)$.

For $\chi_4$ and$\chi_5$ (both dimension 3): the permutation representation on$\{1,2,3,4\}$ has character$(4, 2, 1, 0, 0)$. Subtracting the trivial:$\chi_{\mathrm{std}} = (3, 1, 0, -1, -1)$. Check:$\langle \chi_{\mathrm{std}}, \chi_{\mathrm{std}} \rangle = \frac{1}{24}(9 + 6 + 0 + 6 + 3) = \frac{24}{24} = 1$. So$\chi_4 = (3, 1, 0, -1, -1)$. Then$\chi_5 = \chi_{\mathrm{sgn}} \cdot \chi_4 = (3, -1, 0, 1, -1)$.

The complete character table of $S_4$:

$$
\begin{array}{c|ccccc}
S_4 & e & (12) & (123) & (1234) & (12)(34) \\
|C_j|: & 1 & 6 & 8 & 6 & 3 \\
\hline
\chi_1 & 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\
\chi_2 & 1 & -1 & 1 & -1 & 1 \\
\chi_3 & 2 & 0 & -1 & 0 & 2 \\
\chi_4 & 3 & 1 & 0 & -1 & -1 \\
\chi_5 & 3 & -1 & 0 & 1 & -1
\end{array}
$$

Verification: $\sum_i n_i^2 = 1+1+4+9+9 = 24 = |S_4|$ \checkmark. Row norms:$\frac{1}{24}(1 \cdot 4 + 6 \cdot 0 + 8 \cdot 1 + 6 \cdot 0 + 3 \cdot 4) = \frac{24}{24} = 1$ for$\chi_3$ \checkmark. Column norms:$\sum_i |\chi_i((12))|^2 = 1+1+0+1+1 = 4 = |C_{S_4}((12))| = 4$ \checkmark.

\begin{remark}
Once the table is almost complete, the orthogonality relations take over as constraints. The row-orthonormality and column-orthonormality conditions, combined with integrality, typically pin down the remaining entries uniquely. For large groups, computer algebra systems such as GAP or Magma implement character table algorithms that handle groups of order in the millions. The key insight throughout is that the character table is a highly overdetermined structure: there are far more constraints than free entries.
\end{remark}
