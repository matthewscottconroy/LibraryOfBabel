# 44.4.1 Burnside's Theorem: Groups of Order $p^a q^b$ Are Solvable

Burnside's theorem, proved in 1904, states that if $|G| = p^a q^b$ for primes$p$ and$q$, then$G$ is solvable. At the time, no purely group-theoretic proof was known — the theorem was inaccessible to the combinatorial and structural methods available. The proof runs through character theory, and its route is one of the most celebrated applications of the subject.

\begin{theorem}[Burnside, 1904]
If $G$ is a finite group with$|G| = p^a q^b$ for primes$p$ and$q$ and non-negative integers$a, b$, then$G$ is solvable.
\end{theorem}

The proof reduces to two key lemmas.

\begin{lemma}
If $G$ has a conjugacy class of size$p^k$ with$k \geq 1$ and$p$ a prime, then$G$ is not simple (unless$G$ is the trivial group).
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}
If $\chi$ is an irreducible character of$G$,$g \in G$, and$\gcd(n, |[g]|) = 1$ where$n = \chi(e)$, then either$\chi(g) = 0$ or$|\chi(g)| = n$ (i.e.,$g$ acts as a scalar in the representation$V$).
\end{lemma}

The mechanism connecting conjugacy class sizes to character values is the following. For an irreducible character $\chi_i$ of degree$n_i$ and a conjugacy class$C$ of size$|C|$, the quantity$\omega_i(C) = |C|\chi_i(g_C)/n_i$ (where$g_C \in C$) is an algebraic integer. This follows because$\omega_i(C)$ is the eigenvalue of the class sum$\hat{C} = \sum_{g \in C} g$ acting on$V_i$ by Schur's lemma (since$\hat{C} \in Z(\mathbb{C}[G])$, it acts as a scalar on the irreducible$V_i$).

\begin{proof}[Proof of Burnside's Theorem, sketch]
We proceed by strong induction on $|G| = p^a q^b$. The base cases (when$a = 0$ or$b = 0$, i.e.,$G$ is a$p$-group or$q$-group) are handled by the fact that$p$-groups are solvable (they have nontrivial centers, enabling an inductive series).

For the inductive step, we show $G$ has a proper normal subgroup. By Sylow theory,$G$ has a Sylow$p$-subgroup$P$ of order$p^a$. The center$Z(P)$ is nontrivial (since$P$ is a$p$-group). Choose a non-identity element$z \in Z(P)$; its conjugacy class$[z]$ satisfies$|[z]| = |G|/|C_G(z)|$. Since$P \subseteq C_G(z)$, we have$|C_G(z)| \geq p^a$, so$|[z]|$ divides$q^b$, i.e.,$|[z]| = q^c$ for some$0 \leq c \leq b$.

If $c = 0$, then$z \in Z(G)$, and$\langle z \rangle$ is a proper normal subgroup (since$z \neq e$ and$G$ is not a$p$-group for$b > 0$).

If $c \geq 1$: apply the second lemma with$|C| = q^c$ and each irreducible character$\chi_i$ of degree$n_i$. For each irreducible$\chi_i$ with$\gcd(n_i, q) = 1$, either$\chi_i(z) = 0$ or$|chi_i(z)| = n_i$ (implying$z$ acts as a scalar, hence$z \in Z(V_i)$, which combined with irreducibility forces$z \in Z(G)$ or$\rho_i(z) = \zeta I$ for some root of unity$\zeta$).

The key calculation: $\sum_i n_i \chi_i(z) = |G| \cdot \delta_{z,e} = 0$ (the last zero coming from the fact that$z \neq e$ and this sum is the character of the regular representation at$z$). Separating the trivial term:$1 + \sum_{i \geq 2} n_i \chi_i(z) = 0$. The irreducibles with$q \mid n_i$ contribute terms divisible by$q$; those with$\gcd(n_i, q) = 1$ and$\chi_i(z) \neq 0$ contribute$n_i \chi_i(z)/q^c = \omega_i$ (algebraic integers) scaled by$q^c/n_i$... the argument continues by showing a contradiction to$|Z(G)| > 1$ unless a proper normal subgroup exists. Once a proper normal subgroup$N \trianglelefteq G$ is found, both$N$ and$G/N$ have order of the form$p^{a'} q^{b'}$ with strictly smaller exponents, so by induction both are solvable, hence$G$ is solvable.
\end{proof}

The historical importance of Burnside's theorem extends beyond its content. It demonstrated that character theory — a tool that might have seemed like pure linear algebra or harmonic analysis — could resolve structural questions in group theory that had no evident group-theoretic proof. The purely group-theoretic proof was eventually found by Goldschmidt and Matsuyama in the 1970s, but it is significantly more involved than the character-theoretic argument.

\begin{remark}
Burnside's theorem immediately implies that all groups of order less than 60 are solvable (since $60 = 2^2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5$ has three prime factors, so$60 = p^a q^b$ does not hold, but all smaller orders do have at most two prime factors:$56 = 2^3 \cdot 7$,$48 = 2^4 \cdot 3$, etc.). The smallest non-solvable group is$A_5$ of order 60, which cannot be handled by Burnside's theorem (and indeed requires the full Feit–Thompson theorem for the general case of groups of odd order).
\end{remark}
\par
Burnside's theorem remains the canonical example of the unreasonable effectiveness of characters in finite group theory.
