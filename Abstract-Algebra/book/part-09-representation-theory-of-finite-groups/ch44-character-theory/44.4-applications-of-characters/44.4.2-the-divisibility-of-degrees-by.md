# 44.4.2 The Divisibility of Degrees by $|G : Z(G)|$

The dimensions of irreducible representations are not arbitrary positive integers: they are tightly constrained by the group's structure. The basic divisibility theorem says each degree divides the group order, and a sharper version refines this by accounting for the center.

\begin{theorem}[Divisibility of degrees]
Let $G$ be a finite group and$V_i$ an irreducible representation of$G$ over$\mathbb{C}$ with$\dim V_i = n_i$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $n_i \mid |G|$.
\item $n_i \mid [G : Z(G)] = |G|/|Z(G)|$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

The intuition for the second statement: central elements $z \in Z(G)$ act as scalars in any irreducible representation (by Schur's lemma, since$\rho(z): V_i \to V_i$ is a$G$-equivariant map, hence a scalar multiple of the identity). The center thus contributes nothing to the "size" of an irreducible representation — all the complexity comes from$G/Z(G)$. This is why the index$[G:Z(G)]$ is the natural bound on$n_i^2$, not just$|G|$.

\begin{proof}[Proof of $n_i \mid |G|$]
Consider the class sums $\hat{C}_j = \sum_{g \in C_j} g \in Z(\mathbb{C}[G])$. Since$V_i$ is irreducible, Schur's lemma implies$\rho_i(\hat{C}_j) = \omega_{ij} \mathrm{Id}_{V_i}$ for some scalar$\omega_{ij}$. Taking traces:$|C_j| \chi_i(g_j) = n_i \omega_{ij}$, so$\omega_{ij} = |C_j|\chi_i(g_j)/n_i$.

The key claim is that $\omega_{ij}$ is an algebraic integer. The class sums$\hat{C}_j$ span the center$Z(\mathbb{C}[G])$ and satisfy integral multiplication rules:$\hat{C}_j \hat{C}_k = \sum_l a_{jk}^l \hat{C}_l$ where$a_{jk}^l \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ are non-negative integers (structure constants of the center). Therefore$\omega_{ij}$ is an eigenvalue of an integer matrix (the matrix of left multiplication by$\hat{C}_j$ in the class sum basis), hence an algebraic integer.

Now $\frac{|G|}{n_i} = \frac{1}{n_i} \sum_j |C_j| \cdot \frac{|C_j||\chi_i(g_j)|^2}{1} \cdot \frac{1}{|C_j|}$... more cleanly: from$\sum_j |C_j|\chi_i(g_j)\overline{\chi_i(g_j)} = |G|$ (row norm), dividing by$n_i$ and using$\omega_{ij} = |C_j|\chi_i(g_j)/n_i$:
$$\frac{|G|}{n_i} = \sum_j \overline{\omega_{ij}} \cdot \overline{\chi_i(g_j)} \cdot \frac{n_i}{|C_j|} \cdot \frac{|C_j|}{n_i}.$$
A cleaner route: $\frac{|G|}{n_i^2} = \langle \chi_i, \chi_i \rangle \cdot \frac{|G|}{n_i} \cdot \frac{1}{n_i}$... The standard argument shows$|G|/n_i = \sum_j |C_j| |\chi_i(g_j)|^2/n_i = \sum_j \overline{\omega_{ij}} \cdot \overline{\chi_i(g_j)}$, which is a sum of products of algebraic integers (since character values are algebraic integers and$\overline{\omega_{ij}}$ is an algebraic integer). Since$|G|/n_i$ is also rational, it is an ordinary integer.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
For $G = S_4$: the irreducible degrees are$1,1,2,3,3$. We check:$1 \mid 24$,$2 \mid 24$,$3 \mid 24$ — all hold. The center$Z(S_4) = \{e\}$, so$[G:Z(G)] = 24$, giving no extra constraint here. For$G = S_3$: degrees$1,1,2$.$Z(S_3) = \{e\}$,$[G:Z(G)] = 6$, and$2 \mid 6$ \checkmark.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For $G = D_4$: degrees$(1,1,1,1,2)$. Center$Z(D_4) = \{e, r^2\}$, order 2. So$[G:Z(G)] = 4$. Each$n_i \in \{1,2\}$ divides 4 \checkmark.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For $G = Q_8$: same degrees as$D_4$. Center$Z(Q_8) = \{1,-1\}$, order 2.$[G:Z(G)] = 4$. The 2-dimensional irreducible has degree 2 which divides 4 \checkmark. The bound is tight:$n_5 = 2 = \sqrt{[G:Z(G)]} = \sqrt{4}$.
\end{example}

The refined divisibility $n_i \mid [G:Z(G)]$ is handled similarly, factoring out the central contribution. The extreme case where equality holds — i.e., where$n_i^2 = [G:Z(G)]$ — occurs for$Q_8$ and$D_4$, and more generally for groups of "central type" where the entire representation theory is controlled by the quotient$G/Z(G)$.

\begin{remark}
An even sharper result holds: $n_i$ divides$[G:A]$ for every abelian normal subgroup$A \trianglelefteq G$. This follows from Clifford theory (the theory of how irreducible representations restrict to normal subgroups). The most refined form of divisibility, due to Ito and others, states that$n_i$ divides$|G:A|$ for any abelian subgroup$A$ that is normal in some specific sense. These refinements are useful in the classification of groups with specific representation-theoretic properties.
\end{remark}
