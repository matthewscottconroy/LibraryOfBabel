# 44.4.4 The Character Ring (Representation Ring) $R(G)$

The set of isomorphism classes of representations of $G$ carries a natural ring structure: direct sum provides addition, and tensor product provides multiplication. Making this algebraically precise leads to the \emph{representation ring}$R(G)$, an important invariant that connects representation theory to algebraic$K$-theory and equivariant topology.

\begin{definition}
The \emph{representation ring} $R(G)$ is the free abelian group with one generator$[V_i]$ for each irreducible representation$V_i$ of$G$ over$\mathbb{C}$, equipped with the multiplication$[V] \cdot [W] = [V \otimes W]$ (extended linearly). The unit element is the class$[\mathbb{C}]$ of the trivial representation.
\end{definition}

Formally, $R(G)$ is constructed as the Grothendieck group of the monoid of isomorphism classes under direct sum: one introduces formal differences$[V] - [W]$ (called \emph{virtual representations}), allowing subtraction. The resulting group is free abelian with basis$\{[V_1], \ldots, [V_r]\}$, and the tensor product makes it a commutative ring.

\begin{example}
For $G = S_3$:$R(S_3)$ is a free abelian group of rank 3 with generators$[\chi_1], [\chi_2], [\chi_3]$. The multiplication table (tensor products computed from character values):
\begin{align*}
[\chi_1] \cdot [\chi_j] &= [\chi_j] \quad (\text{trivial is the unit}), \\
[\chi_2]^2 &= [\chi_1] \quad (\text{sign}^2 = \text{trivial}), \\
[\chi_2] \cdot [\chi_3] &= [\chi_3] \quad (\text{sgn} \otimes \text{std} \cong \text{std}), \\
[\chi_3]^2 &= [\chi_1] + [\chi_2] + [\chi_3] \quad (\text{std} \otimes \text{std decomposes into all three irreducibles}).
\end{align*}
These are read from the character table: $\chi_3^2(e) = 4$,$\chi_3^2((12)) = 0$,$\chi_3^2((123)) = 1$, and decomposing gives multiplicities$m_1 = m_2 = m_3 = 1$.
\end{example}

The structure constants of $R(G)$ are the non-negative integers$c_{ij}^k$ defined by$[V_i] \otimes [V_j] = \sum_k c_{ij}^k [V_k]$ (the \emph{Clebsch–Gordan coefficients}). These are computable from the character table:
$$c_{ij}^k = \langle \chi_i \cdot \chi_j, \chi_k \rangle = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} \chi_i(g)\chi_j(g)\overline{\chi_k(g)}.$$

\begin{example}
For $G = \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$: the three irreducibles are$V_0, V_1, V_2$ (with$V_k(\sigma^j) = \zeta^{jk}$). Since$\chi_{V_a} \cdot \chi_{V_b} = \chi_{V_{a+b \bmod 3}}$, the representation ring satisfies$[V_1]^3 = [V_0]$ (the unit), and$R(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}[t]/(t^3 - 1)$ as rings, where$t = [V_1]$.
\end{example}

The character map $[V] \mapsto \chi_V$ gives an injective ring homomorphism$R(G) \hookrightarrow \mathrm{Cl}(G, \mathbb{C})$, where class functions form a ring under pointwise operations. Injectivity is precisely the classification theorem: two representations with the same character are isomorphic. The image is the subring of class functions spanned by the irreducible characters, and after tensoring with$\mathbb{C}$, this becomes all of$\mathrm{Cl}(G, \mathbb{C})$. Thus:
$$R(G) \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{C} \cong \mathrm{Cl}(G, \mathbb{C}) \cong \mathbb{C}^r.$$

\begin{remark}
For a compact Lie group $G$, the representation ring$R(G)$ is defined analogously (using finite-dimensional continuous complex representations) and is the same as the equivariant$K$-theory group$K_G^0(\mathrm{pt})$. The Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence relates$R(G)$ to the topological$K$-theory of the classifying space$BG$. For$G = U(1)$,$R(G) \cong \mathbb{Z}[t, t^{-1}]$ (Laurent polynomials), reflecting the fact that every representation of$U(1)$ is a direct sum of characters$t^n$ (weight$n$ representations). The representation ring connects smoothly to equivariant algebraic topology, where it classifies equivariant vector bundles.
\end{remark}

The representation ring is also the natural setting for \emph{Adams operations} $\psi^k: R(G) \to R(G)$, defined on characters by$\psi^k(\chi)(g) = \chi(g^k)$. These operations satisfy$\psi^k \circ \psi^l = \psi^{kl}$ and are ring homomorphisms (unlike exterior powers, which are not). For example,$\psi^2(\chi_3)$ for the standard representation of$S_3$:$\psi^2(\chi_3)(g) = \chi_3(g^2)$. On$S_3$:$\psi^2(\chi_3)(e) = \chi_3(e) = 2$,$\psi^2(\chi_3)((12)) = \chi_3((12)^2) = \chi_3(e) = 2$,$\psi^2(\chi_3)((123)) = \chi_3((123)^2) = \chi_3((132)) = -1$. So$\psi^2(\chi_3) = (2, 2, -1) = \chi_1 + \chi_2$ (trivial + sign). Adams operations are important in the proof of the Adams conjecture in topology and in the study of$J$-homomorphisms.

\begin{example}
The representation ring of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$:$R(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}[t]/(t^n - 1)$ where$t = [V_1]$ (the standard 1-dimensional character$\sigma \mapsto \zeta_n$). The ring structure is:
$$[V_j] = t^j, \quad [V_j] \cdot [V_k] = [V_{j+k \bmod n}] = t^{j+k}.$$
The Adams operations act as $\psi^m(t^j) = t^{mj}$. The augmentation map$\varepsilon: R(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}) \to \mathbb{Z}$ sending$[V] \mapsto \dim V$ is the ring homomorphism$t \mapsto 1$, i.e., evaluation at$t = 1$.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
The Burnside ring $A(G)$ is a related but different object: it is the Grothendieck ring of finite$G$-sets (under disjoint union as addition and Cartesian product as multiplication). There is a natural ring homomorphism$A(G) \to R(G)$ sending a$G$-set$X$ to the permutation representation$\mathbb{C}[X]$. This map is far from an isomorphism in general — many non-isomorphic$G$-sets can give isomorphic permutation representations — but it connects the combinatorial and representation-theoretic invariants of$G$.
\end{remark}
