# 44.4 Applications of Characters

The machinery of characters was built to classify representations, but it does something more remarkable: it proves theorems about groups that have no obvious connection to linear algebra. This section collects the most striking applications of character theory to the structure of finite groups, culminating in Burnside's $p^a q^b$ theorem — a result whose only known proof for decades was the one using characters.

Burnside's theorem, proved in 1904, asserts that every group of order $p^a q^b$ (with $p, q$ prime) is solvable. The proof proceeds through a chain of character-theoretic lemmas. First, one shows that every irreducible character degree divides $|G|$. Second, and more delicately, if $C$ is a conjugacy class whose size $|C|$ is a prime power $p^a > 1$, then for every irreducible character $\chi_i$, either $\chi_i(g) = 0$ for $g \in C$, or $p \mid \chi_i(e)$. A careful application of the column orthogonality relation then shows that the existence of such a class implies the existence of a proper normal subgroup. The $p^a q^b$ theorem follows by induction: a group of order $p^a q^b$ with $a, b > 0$ must have a conjugacy class of prime-power size (by Sylow theory applied to its $p$-subgroups or $q$-subgroups), hence a proper normal subgroup, and induction gives solvability. The argument is entirely character-theoretic, and no purely group-theoretic proof was found until Thompson and Goldschmidt's work much later.

Characters also detect normal subgroups directly. The kernel of a character $\chi_i$ is the set $\ker \chi_i = \{g \in G : \chi_i(g) = \chi_i(e)\}$; this is a normal subgroup of $G$ (since characters are class functions and equal to their degree exactly when the corresponding matrix is the identity). Every normal subgroup of $G$ arises as an intersection of kernels of irreducible characters. More precisely, $N \trianglelefteq G$ if and only if $N = \bigcap_i \ker \chi_i$ for some subset of irreducibles — the intersection of all irreducibles whose kernels contain $N$. This gives a complete lattice-theoretic picture: the normal subgroups of $G$ are encoded in the combinatorics of the character table. The representation ring $R(G)$, built from formal integer linear combinations of characters with the product given by tensor product, is a further algebraic structure that captures the interplay between addition (direct sum) and multiplication (tensor product) of representations; its properties and connection to $K$-theory make it the bridge from the representation theory of finite groups to more modern algebraic topology.
