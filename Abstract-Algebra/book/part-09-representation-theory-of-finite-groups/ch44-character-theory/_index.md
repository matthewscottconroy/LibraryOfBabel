# Chapter 44 — Character Theory

A representation $\rho: G \to GL(V)$ carries an enormous amount of data: for each group element $g$, an entire invertible matrix $\rho(g)$. Much of that data is redundant. Two representations are isomorphic — they describe the same abstract action — if and only if their matrices are related by a global change of basis. The character $\chi_V: G \to k$ defined by $\chi_V(g) = \text{tr}(\rho(g))$ strips away the basis-dependence and retains only the trace. That this single number per group element suffices to determine the representation up to isomorphism is one of the most remarkable theorems in algebra. Characters are the spectral decomposition of finite group theory.

The key structural fact is that characters are class functions: $\chi_V(hgh^{-1}) = \chi_V(g)$ for all $h, g \in G$, since the trace is invariant under conjugation. This means characters live in the space of class functions on $G$, a vector space of dimension equal to the number of conjugacy classes. The orthogonality relations, due to Frobenius and Schur, equip this space with a canonical inner product $\langle \chi, \psi \rangle = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} \chi(g) \overline{\psi(g)}$, and they assert that the irreducible characters form an orthonormal basis for this space. Two consequences follow immediately: first, a representation $V$ is irreducible if and only if $\langle \chi_V, \chi_V \rangle = 1$; second, the multiplicity of an irreducible $V_i$ in an arbitrary representation $W$ is precisely $\langle \chi_W, \chi_{V_i} \rangle$. Decomposing a representation becomes a computation.

The character table of a group $G$ organizes all this information into a square matrix: rows indexed by irreducible representations, columns by conjugacy classes, entries the values of the irreducible characters. That the table is square — the number of irreducibles equals the number of conjugacy classes, as established in Chapter 43 — is itself a theorem. The row and column orthogonality relations impose strong constraints on the entries, often forcing the table to be unique. For groups like $S_3$, $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$, $D_4$, and $Q_8$, the character table can be determined almost entirely from these constraints together with the dimension formula $\sum_i (\dim V_i)^2 = |G|$.

The power of character theory extends well beyond classification. Burnside's $p^a q^b$ theorem — that every group of order $p^a q^b$ for primes $p, q$ is solvable — was proved in 1904 using characters, decades before any character-free proof was found. The argument passes through a delicate divisibility property: the degree of any irreducible representation divides $|G|$, and a group element lying in a conjugacy class of prime-power size forces the existence of a nontrivial normal subgroup. Characters can also detect normal subgroups directly: $N$ is a normal subgroup of $G$ if and only if $N$ is an intersection of kernels of irreducible characters, making the lattice of normal subgroups visible in the character table. These applications reveal that character theory is not merely a classification tool but a bridge between the linear algebra of representations and the group-theoretic structure of $G$ itself.
