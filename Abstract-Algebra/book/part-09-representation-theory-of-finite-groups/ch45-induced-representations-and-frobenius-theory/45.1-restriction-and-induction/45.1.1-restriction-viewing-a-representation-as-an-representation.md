# 45.1.1 Restriction $\mathrm{Res}^G_H V$: Viewing a$G$-Representation as an$H$-Representation

Given a representation $(V, \rho)$ of a group$G$ and a subgroup$H \leq G$, the simplest operation one can perform is to forget part of the structure: restrict attention to the action of$H$ alone. This operation is trivially defined but surprisingly useful, both as a tool for studying large-group representations via their subgroup behavior and as one half of the induction-restriction adjunction.

\begin{definition}
The \emph{restricted representation} $\mathrm{Res}^G_H V$ (also written$V|_H$) is the vector space$V$ equipped with the$H$-action$h \cdot v = \rho(h)(v)$ for$h \in H$.
\end{definition}

Nothing changes about the vector space or the matrices representing elements of $H$; one merely stops asking what$\rho(g)$ looks like for$g \notin H$. In the language of modules, a$k[G]$-module$V$ becomes a$k[H]$-module by restriction along the ring homomorphism$k[H] \hookrightarrow k[G]$ induced by the group inclusion$H \hookrightarrow G$. Restriction is functorial: any$G$-equivariant map$\phi: V \to W$ is automatically$H$-equivariant, giving a functor$\mathrm{Res}^G_H: \mathrm{Rep}(G) \to \mathrm{Rep}(H)$.

\begin{example}
Let $G = S_3$ and$H = \langle (12) \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. The standard representation$V_3$ of$S_3$ (2-dimensional) restricts to$H$ as a 2-dimensional$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$-representation. The matrix of$(12)$ in$V_3$ has trace$\chi_3((12)) = 0$ and eigenvalues$\pm 1$. So$\mathrm{Res}^G_H V_3 \cong \mathbb{C}_{+1} \oplus \mathbb{C}_{-1}$, the direct sum of the trivial and sign representations of$H$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let $G = S_4$,$H = S_3 \leq S_4$ (fixing element 4). The standard 3-dimensional irreducible$\chi_4$ of$S_4$ has$\chi_4|_{S_3}$: evaluating on elements of$S_3$ (which have the same cycle type in$S_4$ and$S_3$ for permutations of$\{1,2,3\}$):$\chi_4(e) = 3$,$\chi_4((12)) = 1$,$\chi_4((123)) = 0$. This is$\chi_1 + \chi_3$ for$S_3$ (trivial + standard), so$\mathrm{Res}^{S_4}_{S_3} \chi_4 = \chi_1^{S_3} + \chi_3^{S_3}$.
\end{example}

The key point about restriction is that it does not preserve irreducibility. An irreducible $G$-representation may well become reducible when restricted to a subgroup$H$: the subgroup may not have enough group elements to prevent invariant subspaces from appearing.

\begin{definition}
The \emph{branching rule} for the pair $H \leq G$ describes the decomposition$\mathrm{Res}^G_H V_i = \bigoplus_j b_{ij} W_j$, where$V_i$ is an irreducible of$G$ and$W_j$ are the irreducibles of$H$. The non-negative integers$b_{ij}$ are the \emph{branching multiplicities}.
\end{definition}

Branching rules are a central theme in representation theory, important both in the abstract setting and in physics, where symmetry breaking corresponds precisely to restricting a representation from a large symmetry group to a subgroup. For the symmetric group chain $S_n \supset S_{n-1} \supset \cdots \supset S_1$, the branching rule is the "Young's rule":$\mathrm{Res}^{S_n}_{S_{n-1}} V_\lambda = \bigoplus_\mu V_\mu$, where$\mu$ ranges over partitions obtained by removing one box from the Young diagram of$\lambda$.

\begin{example}
For $G = D_4$,$H = \langle r^2 \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$: the 2-dimensional irreducible$\chi_5$ of$D_4$ restricts to$H$ with character$\chi_5(1) = 2$,$\chi_5(r^2) = -2$, so$\mathrm{Res}^{D_4}_H \chi_5$ has character$(2,-2)$ on$H$. Since$H$ has two irreducibles (trivial and sign, with characters$(1,1)$ and$(1,-1)$), and$2 = -1 \cdot 1 +$ doesn't work with trivial, but$(2,-2) = 0 \cdot (1,1) + 2 \cdot (1,-1)$, so$\mathrm{Res}^{D_4}_H \chi_5 \cong \mathrm{sign}^{\oplus 2}$.
\end{example}

The character of the restricted representation is simply the original character restricted to $H$:$\chi_{\mathrm{Res}^G_H V}(h) = \chi_V(h)$ for all$h \in H$. This triviality at the character level is what makes restriction easy to compute: one evaluates characters of$G$ on elements of$H$ and then decomposes using the$H$-character table.

\begin{example}
For $G = S_4$ and$H = V_4 = \{e, (12)(34), (13)(24), (14)(23)\}$ (the Klein 4-group):$V_4$ is a normal subgroup of$S_4$. The 3-dimensional irreducible$\chi_4$ of$S_4$ restricts to$V_4$. From the character table,$\chi_4(e) = 3$ and$\chi_4((12)(34)) = -1$. The irreducible characters of$V_4 \cong (\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^2$ are four 1-dimensional characters$\psi_{ij}$ ($i, j \in \{0,1\}$) with$\psi_{ij}((12)(34)) = (-1)^i$,$\psi_{ij}((13)(24)) = (-1)^j$,$\psi_{ij}((14)(23)) = (-1)^{i+j}$. The multiplicity of$\psi_{ij}$ in$\mathrm{Res}^{S_4}_{V_4} \chi_4$ is$\frac{1}{4}(3 + (-1)^i(-1) + (-1)^j(-1) + (-1)^{i+j}(-1))$. For$\psi_{00}$ (trivial):$\frac{1}{4}(3-1-1-1) = 0$. For$\psi_{10}$:$\frac{1}{4}(3+1-1+1) = 1$. By symmetry, all non-trivial characters appear once except the trivial one. So$\mathrm{Res}^{S_4}_{V_4} \chi_4 = \psi_{10} + \psi_{01} + \psi_{11}$ (the three non-trivial characters of$V_4$), dimension$1+1+1 = 3$ \checkmark.
\end{example}

Restriction is also functorial with respect to further restrictions: if $K \leq H \leq G$, then$\mathrm{Res}^H_K \circ \mathrm{Res}^G_H = \mathrm{Res}^G_K$. This transitivity is trivial from the definition but important for the Mackey formula (§45.3.1), where chains of restrictions and re-inductions appear.
