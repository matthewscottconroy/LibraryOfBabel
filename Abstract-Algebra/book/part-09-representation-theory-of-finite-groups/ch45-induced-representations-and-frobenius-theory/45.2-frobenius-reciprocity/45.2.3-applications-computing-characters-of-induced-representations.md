# 45.2.3 Applications: Computing Characters of Induced Representations

The practical workflow enabled by Frobenius reciprocity is central to many computations in representation theory. We describe the general procedure and work through several detailed examples.

**General procedure.** To decompose $\mathrm{Ind}^G_H W = \bigoplus_i m_i V_i$ into$G$-irreducibles, use:
$$m_i = \langle \chi_{\mathrm{Ind} W}, \chi_i \rangle_G = \langle \chi_W, \chi_i|_H \rangle_H.$$
This replaces an induction computation (which would require computing $\chi_{\mathrm{Ind} W}$ on all conjugacy classes of$G$ via the character formula) with a restriction computation (evaluating$\chi_i$ on elements of$H$ and computing an inner product over the smaller group$H$).

\begin{example}
**Building the character table of $A_4$ via induction.**$|A_4| = 12$,$r = 4$, dimensions$(1,1,1,3)$. The subgroup$H = \langle (123) \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ has three 1-dimensional irreducibles:$\psi_k((123)^j) = \omega^{jk}$ for$k = 0,1,2$ and$\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$.
\par
Compute $\mathrm{Ind}^{A_4}_H \psi_0$ (trivial): dimension$[A_4:H] \cdot 1 = 4$. By Frobenius reciprocity,$\langle \chi_i^{A_4}, \psi_0 \rangle_H = \frac{1}{3}\sum_{h \in H} \chi_i(h)$. For the trivial character$\chi_1$:$\frac{1}{3}(1+1+1) = 1$. For the 3-dim character$\chi_4$ (with$\chi_4((123)) = 0$ by computation):$\frac{1}{3}(3+0+0) = 1$. For$\chi_2, \chi_3$ (with values$1, \omega, \omega^2$ or$1, \omega^2, \omega$):$\frac{1}{3}(1 + \omega^k + \omega^{2k}) = 0$ for$k = 1,2$. So$\mathrm{Ind}^{A_4}_H \psi_0 = \chi_1 + \chi_4$, dimension$1+3=4$ \checkmark.
\par
Compute $\mathrm{Ind}^{A_4}_H \psi_1$:$\langle \chi_1, \psi_1 \rangle_H = \frac{1}{3}(1 + 1 \cdot \omega + 1 \cdot \omega^2) = 0$.$\langle \chi_4, \psi_1 \rangle_H = \frac{1}{3}(3 + 0 + 0) = 1$.$\langle \chi_2, \psi_1 \rangle_H = \frac{1}{3}(1 + \omega \cdot \omega + \omega^2 \cdot \omega^2) = \frac{1}{3}(1 + \omega^2 + \omega^4) = \frac{1}{3}(1 + \omega^2 + \omega) = 0$... actually need exact values. Let$\chi_2|_H = (1, \omega, \omega^2)$ (values on$e, (123), (132)$). Then$\langle \chi_2|_H, \psi_1 \rangle_H = \frac{1}{3}(1 \cdot 1 + \omega \cdot \overline{\omega} + \omega^2 \cdot \overline{\omega^2}) = \frac{1}{3}(1+1+1) = 1$. So$\mathrm{Ind}^{A_4}_H \psi_1 = \chi_2 + \chi_4$, dim$1+3=4$ \checkmark.
\par
Similarly $\mathrm{Ind}^{A_4}_H \psi_2 = \chi_3 + \chi_4$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
**Classifying irreducibles of $S_n$ inductively.** For$S_n$ with the subgroup$S_{n-1} \leq S_n$ (fixing element$n$), Frobenius reciprocity gives the \emph{branching rule}: the multiplicity of$V_\lambda^{S_n}$ in$\mathrm{Ind}^{S_n}_{S_{n-1}} V_\mu^{S_{n-1}}$ equals the multiplicity of$V_\mu^{S_{n-1}}$ in$\mathrm{Res}^{S_n}_{S_{n-1}} V_\lambda^{S_n}$. Young's rule states the latter equals 1 if$\mu$ is obtained from$\lambda$ by removing one box, and 0 otherwise. So:
$$\mathrm{Ind}^{S_n}_{S_{n-1}} V_\mu^{S_{n-1}} = \bigoplus_{\lambda: \mu \subset \lambda} V_\lambda^{S_n},$$
the sum over partitions $\lambda$ of$n$ obtained by adding one box to$\mu$.
\par
This gives a complete recursive description of all irreducible representations of $S_n$: start with$S_1$ (trivial only) and inductively build up by induction, using Frobenius reciprocity to compute decompositions.
\end{example}

Frobenius reciprocity thus transforms the inductive step in many classification problems into a combinatorial rule, replacing representation-theoretic computation with combinatorics of partitions (for $S_n$), root systems (for Lie groups), or double cosets (for general groups). The general lesson is that induced representations are often easier to understand via their restrictions to subgroups, and Frobenius reciprocity makes this perspective rigorous and computable.

\begin{example}
**Characters of $D_4$ via induction from$C_4$.** Let$G = D_4 = \langle r, s \rangle$ and$H = \langle r \rangle \cong C_4$. The index$[G:H] = 2$. The four characters of$C_4$ are$\chi_k(r^j) = i^{jk}$ for$k = 0,1,2,3$.
\par
For $k = 0$ (trivial):$\mathrm{Ind}^{D_4}_{C_4} \chi_0$ has dimension 2. By Frobenius reciprocity, multiplicity of$\chi_m^{D_4}$ in$\mathrm{Ind} \chi_0$ equals multiplicity of$\chi_0 = \mathbf{1}$ in$\mathrm{Res}^{D_4}_{C_4} \chi_m^{D_4}$. The four 1-dim characters of$D_4$ all have$\chi_m(r) = \pm 1$: trivial ($\chi_1$) and$\chi_3$ (with$\chi_3(r) = -1$) restrict to give$\mathbf{1}$ and the sign, so$\langle \mathbf{1}, \mathrm{Res} \chi_1 \rangle_{C_4} = 1$ and$\langle \mathbf{1}, \mathrm{Res} \chi_3 \rangle_{C_4} = 0$ (since$\chi_3|_{C_4} = \chi_2^{C_4}$ not trivial). More carefully:$\mathrm{Ind}^{D_4}_{C_4} \chi_0 = \chi_1^{D_4} + \chi_2^{D_4}$ (the two 1-dim characters that are trivial on$r$).
\par
For $k = 1$:$\mathrm{Ind}^{D_4}_{C_4} \chi_1$ has dimension 2. The 2-dim irreducible$\chi_5$ of$D_4$ restricts to$C_4$ with$\chi_5(r) = 0$,$\chi_5(r^2) = -2$. Decomposing over$C_4$:$0 = i^1 + i^{-1}$, so$\chi_5|_{C_4} = \chi_1^{C_4} + \chi_3^{C_4}$ (since$i + (-i) = 0$). By Frobenius reciprocity,$\langle \chi_1^{C_4}, \mathrm{Res} \chi_5 \rangle_{C_4} = 1$. So$\mathrm{Ind} \chi_1 = \chi_5^{D_4}$, the unique 2-dimensional irreducible!
\end{example}

The example above shows how induction from cyclic subgroups can produce all higher-dimensional irreducibles efficiently. For $D_4$ specifically, all five irreducibles can be obtained either as inflations from the abelianization or as inductions from$C_4$.
