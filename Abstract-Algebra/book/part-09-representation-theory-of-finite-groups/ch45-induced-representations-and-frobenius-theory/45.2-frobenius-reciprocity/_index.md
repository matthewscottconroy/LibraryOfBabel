# 45.2 Frobenius Reciprocity

Frobenius reciprocity is the statement that induction and restriction are adjoint functors: for a subgroup $H \leq G$, a representation $\sigma$ of $H$, and a representation $\rho$ of $G$, there is a natural isomorphism $\text{Hom}_G(\text{Ind}_H^G \sigma, \rho) \cong \text{Hom}_H(\sigma, \text{Res}_H^G \rho)$. In terms of inner products on characters, this becomes $\langle \chi^G, \chi_\rho \rangle_G = \langle \chi_\sigma, \text{Res}\,\chi_\rho \rangle_H$, where the two inner products are computed on $G$ and $H$ respectively. Frobenius proved this in 1898, and it remains one of the most useful computational tools in representation theory.

The proof can be given cleanly in the language of modules. The induced representation is $\text{Ind}_H^G \sigma = k[G] \otimes_{k[H]} W$, and there is a tensor-hom adjunction: $\text{Hom}_{k[G]}(k[G] \otimes_{k[H]} W, V) \cong \text{Hom}_{k[H]}(W, \text{Hom}_{k[G]}(k[G], V)) \cong \text{Hom}_{k[H]}(W, V)$, where the last identification uses the fact that $\text{Hom}_{k[G]}(k[G], V) \cong V$ as $k[H]$-modules (maps from $k[G]$ are determined by the image of $1$). This chain of natural isomorphisms is exactly Frobenius reciprocity. The adjunction perspective makes clear why reciprocity holds: induction is a left adjoint, defined by a universal property, and adjunction isomorphisms between Hom spaces are exactly what universal properties produce.

The practical power of Frobenius reciprocity lies in converting hard computations on $G$ into easy ones on $H$. To decompose $\text{Ind}_H^G \sigma$ into irreducibles of $G$, one computes the multiplicity of each irreducible $\rho_i$ as $\langle \text{Ind}_H^G \sigma, \rho_i \rangle_G = \langle \sigma, \text{Res}_H^G \rho_i \rangle_H$. The right-hand side is an inner product on the smaller group $H$, often much simpler to evaluate. Conversely, to decompose the restriction $\text{Res}_H^G \rho$ of an irreducible of $G$ to $H$, one induces each irreducible of $H$ and uses the adjunction to read off the multiplicities. In the study of symmetric groups, this strategy is systematized into the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule and the branching rules, which describe precisely how irreducibles of $S_n$ restrict to $S_{n-1}$ and how they induce to $S_{n+1}$.
