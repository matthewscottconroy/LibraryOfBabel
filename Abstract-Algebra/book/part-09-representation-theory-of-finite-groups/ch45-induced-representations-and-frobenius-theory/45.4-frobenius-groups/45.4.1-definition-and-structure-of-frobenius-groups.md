# 45.4.1 Definition and Structure of Frobenius Groups

A group $G$ is called a \emph{Frobenius group} if it acts faithfully and transitively on a set$X$ in such a way that every non-identity element of$G$ fixes at most one point of$X$. This "almost fixed-point-free" condition imposes a very rigid structure on$G$ and leads to one of the most elegant theorems in finite group theory.

\begin{definition}
A group $G$ is a \emph{Frobenius group} if it has a faithful transitive action on a set$X$ such that every non-identity element fixes at most one point. The \emph{Frobenius complement}$H$ is the stabilizer of any point$x_0 \in X$.
\end{definition}

The purely group-theoretic formulation: $G$ is a Frobenius group with complement$H$ if$H \neq 1$,$H \neq G$, and$H \cap gHg^{-1} = \{e\}$ for all$g \notin H$. The conjugates of$H$ in$G$ intersect each other trivially, meaning they are "spread out" within$G$ without any overlap.

\begin{proposition}
This intersection condition has immediate consequences. The $|G:H|$ conjugates of$H$ are pairwise distinct subgroups of$G$, and they cover$|G:H| \cdot (|H| - 1) = |G| - |G:H|$ non-identity elements. The remaining$|G:H|$ elements (those fixing no point of$X$) together with the identity form a subset of size$|G:H| = |X|$.
\end{proposition}

The great theorem of Frobenius (§45.4.2) is that this set is actually a normal subgroup. Assuming this, the structure theorem follows:

\begin{theorem}[Structure of Frobenius Groups]
Let $G$ be a Frobenius group with complement$H$ and Frobenius kernel$K$ (the set of fixed-point-free elements together with the identity). Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $K \trianglelefteq G$ (normality is Frobenius's theorem, proved in §45.4.2).
\item $G = K \rtimes H$ (a semidirect product with$K \cap H = \{e\}$ and$G = KH$).
\item $|K| = |G:H| = |X|$.
\item $H$ acts on$K$ by conjugation with no nontrivial fixed points: if$hkh^{-1} = k$ for$h \in H$ and$k \in K$, then$k = e$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof of (4)]
Suppose $h \in H$ and$k \in K$ satisfy$hkh^{-1} = k$, i.e.,$[h,k] = e$. Then$k \in C_G(h)$. But$h \in H$ and$k \in K$ with$k \neq e$ would imply$k \in H \cap kHk^{-1}$ (since$h = khk^{-1}$ implies$h \in kHk^{-1}$), contradicting the defining intersection condition. So$k = e$.
\end{proof}

The fixed-point-free action of $H$ on$K$ is the key structural feature. It severely constrains both the structure of$K$ (Thompson's theorem:$K$ is nilpotent) and the possible groups$H$ that can appear as Frobenius complements (Zassenhaus:$H$ must have at most one involution if$|H|$ is even; the possible structures of$H$ are classified by a theorem of Zassenhaus).

\begin{example}
The dihedral group $D_n$ for odd$n$: acts on the vertices of a regular$n$-gon. Rotations (non-identity) fix no vertex; reflections fix exactly one vertex. Frobenius complement$H \cong C_2$, kernel$K \cong C_n$. The action is:$s r s^{-1} = r^{-1}$ (for the reflection$s$ and rotation$r$), which has no fixed points on$C_n \setminus \{e\}$ since$r^{-1} = r$ only if$r^2 = e$, but$r$ has order$n$ which is odd, so$r \neq e$ implies$r^{-1} \neq r$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
The group $AGL(1,p) = \mathbb{F}_p \rtimes \mathbb{F}_p^*$ (maps$x \mapsto ax+b$ on$\mathbb{F}_p$): the translation subgroup$K = \{x \mapsto x+b\} \cong \mathbb{F}_p$ acts without fixed points (a non-trivial translation fixes no point). The stabilizer of$0$ is$H = \{x \mapsto ax\} \cong C_{p-1}$. The action of$H$ on$K$: conjugating the translation$t_b: x \mapsto x+b$ by$m_a: x \mapsto ax$ gives$(m_a t_b m_a^{-1})(x) = a(a^{-1}x + b) = x + ab = t_{ab}(x)$. So$m_a$ acts on$K \cong \mathbb{F}_p$ by multiplication by$a$. This action is fixed-point-free:$ab = b$ with$a \neq 1$ forces$b = 0$.
\end{example}
