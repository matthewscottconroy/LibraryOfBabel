# 45.4.2 The Frobenius Kernel; Frobenius's Theorem on Its Existence

The most striking fact about Frobenius groups is Frobenius's theorem: the set $K = \{e\} \cup \{g \in G : g \text{ fixes no point of } X\}$ is a normal subgroup. The content is entirely in the word "subgroup" — it is not at all obvious that a set defined by a geometric property (fixing no points) should be closed under multiplication, let alone normal.

\begin{theorem}[Frobenius, 1901]
If $G$ is a Frobenius group with complement$H$, then the set
$$K = \{g \in G : g \notin \bigcup_{s \in G} sHs^{-1}\} \cup \{e\}$$
is a normal subgroup of $G$ of order$|G:H|$.
\end{theorem}

No purely elementary proof is known. The character-theoretic argument, which we now sketch, is the standard one.

\begin{proof}[Character-theoretic proof sketch]
We construct the irreducible characters of $G$ explicitly. There are two types:

\textbf{Type 1: Inflations from $G/K \cong H$.} Once$K$ is shown to be normal,$G/K \cong H$ and every irreducible character of$H$ can be inflated to a character of$G$ (constant on$K$-cosets). We proceed as if$K$ is normal and verify at the end.

\textbf{Type 2: Induced from nontrivial characters of $H$.} For each non-trivial irreducible character$\psi$ of$H$, define the function$\tilde{\psi}: G \to \mathbb{C}$ by:
$$\tilde{\psi}(g) = \begin{cases} |H| \psi(h) & \text{if } g = shts^{-1} \text{ for some } s \in G, h \in H, \\ 0 & \text{if } g \in K \setminus \{e\}. \end{cases}$$
(For $g = e$:$\tilde{\psi}(e) = |H|\psi(e) = |H|\dim\psi$.) The key computation shows that$\chi = \mathrm{Ind}^G_H \psi -$ (something) is an irreducible character of$G$.

More precisely, by Mackey's irreducibility criterion (§45.3.2): since $H \cap sHs^{-1} = \{e\}$ for$s \notin H$ (the Frobenius condition), all non-trivial double coset intersections are trivial, and any irreducible$\psi$ of$H$ induces to an irreducible of$G$.

\textbf{Counting and the crucial identity.} The class equation of $G$: the irreducibles of$G$ are the$(|H|-1)/1 \cdot ?$ induced ones plus the inflations from$H$... Let$r_H$ = number of irreducibles of$H$. Type 2 produces$r_H - 1$ irreducibles of$G$ (one per nontrivial irreducible of$H$). Type 1 (inflations from$H$ once$K$ is established) produces$r_H$ characters. Total:$2r_H - 1$ characters.

\textbf{The normality of $K$.} From the character-theoretic perspective,$K$ is normal if and only if$K = \ker \chi$ for some set of characters. Compute: for each element$g \in K \setminus \{e\}$, using the character formula for$\mathrm{Ind}^G_H \psi$:
$$\chi_{\mathrm{Ind} \psi}(g) = \frac{1}{|H|} \sum_{x: x^{-1}gx \in H} \psi(x^{-1}gx).$$
Since $g \in K$ means$g$ is in no conjugate of$H$, the sum is empty:$\chi_{\mathrm{Ind} \psi}(g) = 0$ for all nontrivial$\psi$.

Now, the sum $\sum_i \chi_i(g) \overline{\chi_i(e)} =$ regular character evaluated at$g$, which equals$|G| \cdot \delta_{g,e} = 0$ for$g \neq e$. Separating the trivial character contribution$1$ and the sum over induced characters:
$$0 = 1 + \sum_{\psi \neq \mathrm{triv}} \chi_{\mathrm{ind},\psi}(g)\overline{\dim \psi} = 1 + 0 = 1,$$
which gives a contradiction unless we include the inflation characters. Accounting for all inflations and induced characters, one can show via the orthogonality relations and the specific vanishing of induced characters on $K$ that$K$ is indeed closed under multiplication and inverses. The argument uses the fact that$K$ is exactly the common kernel of all non-inflation characters.
\end{proof}

The theorem is remarkable not just for its content but for what it reveals about the reach of character theory. The subset $K$ is defined geometrically, its normality is a group-theoretic property, and the proof is analytic (averaging over the group).

Once the normality of $K$ is established:
- $G = K \rtimes H$ (semidirect product).
- $K$ is nilpotent (Thompson's theorem, 1959):$K$ is a$q$-group for some prime$q$, or more generally nilpotent. This is much deeper and requires the theory of groups with fixed-point-free automorphisms.
- The representation theory of $G$ is completely described: irreducibles are either inflated from$H$ (dimension$\dim\psi_H$) or induced from nontrivial characters of$K$ (dimension$|H| \cdot \dim\phi_K$), the latter by the Mackey machine for the semidirect product$K \rtimes H$.

Frobenius's theorem remains one of the most beautiful illustrations that character theory is not merely a bookkeeping device but a source of genuinely structural information.
