# 45.4 Frobenius Groups

A Frobenius group is a transitive permutation group $G$ on a set $X$ in which every non-identity element fixes at most one point, yet no non-identity element fixes all points. Equivalently, fixing a point $x_0 \in X$ and letting $H = \text{Stab}_G(x_0)$ be the stabilizer (the Frobenius complement), the condition is that $H \cap H^g = \{e\}$ for every $g \notin H$. The set $N = \{e\} \cup (G \setminus \bigcup_{g \in G} gHg^{-1})$ of elements fixing no point — together with the identity — is a candidate for a normal subgroup. Frobenius's theorem, proved in 1901, asserts that $N$ is indeed a subgroup, automatically normal in $G$, called the Frobenius kernel.

The remarkable fact is that Frobenius's theorem has no known proof that avoids character theory. The argument uses the induced characters from $H$ in an essential way. Each non-trivial irreducible character $\sigma$ of $H$ induces to an irreducible character of $G$, and the orthogonality relations on $G$, combined with the rigid intersection property $H \cap H^g = \{e\}$, force the induced characters to account for all non-trivial irreducibles of $G$ except those that factor through $G/H$. A counting argument with the regular character then shows that the putative kernel $N$ has the right size and supports a well-defined class function that must be the character of a representation whose kernel is exactly $N$. The group structure of $N$ — in particular its closure under multiplication — drops out as a consequence.

The dihedral groups $D_n$ for odd $n$ are the simplest examples of Frobenius groups: $G = D_n$ acts on the $n$ vertices of a regular $n$-gon, the stabilizer of any vertex is $H \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, and the Frobenius kernel is the rotation subgroup $N \cong \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. The affine groups $\text{AGL}(1, q) = \mathbb{F}_q \rtimes \mathbb{F}_q^\times$ for prime powers $q$ provide a family of Frobenius groups where the kernel is the translation subgroup $\mathbb{F}_q$ and the complement is the multiplicative group $\mathbb{F}_q^\times$. In all cases, the Frobenius kernel is a normal subgroup whose existence is guaranteed by the character-theoretic argument, and the structure $G = N \rtimes H$ holds with $N$ acting on $H$ by conjugation. The Frobenius groups stand as one of the most elegant illustrations of a general principle: that the linear algebra of representations can force purely combinatorial and group-theoretic conclusions that lie beyond the reach of elementary methods.
