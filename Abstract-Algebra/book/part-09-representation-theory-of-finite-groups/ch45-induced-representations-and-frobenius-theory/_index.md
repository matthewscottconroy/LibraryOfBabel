# Chapter 45 — Induced Representations and Frobenius Theory

The representation theory developed in Chapters 42–44 answers the question of what the representations of a given group look like, but it leaves open an equally important question: where do representations come from? In practice, groups arise with natural subgroups, and the representations of a large group $G$ are often best understood by first building representations from a subgroup $H$ and then extending them upward. This process of extension is induction. Given a representation $(\sigma, W)$ of $H$, the induced representation $\text{Ind}_H^G \sigma$ is a representation of $G$ of dimension $[G:H] \cdot \dim W$, built by distributing $W$ over the cosets of $H$ in $G$ and letting $G$ act by permuting them according to left multiplication. Induction turns the representation theory of subgroups into raw material for the representation theory of the ambient group.

The fundamental theorem governing induction is Frobenius reciprocity, which asserts an adjunction: $\langle \text{Ind}_H^G \sigma, \rho \rangle_G = \langle \sigma, \text{Res}_H^G \rho \rangle_H$ for any representation $\rho$ of $G$. In categorical language, induction is the left adjoint of restriction. In practical language, it means that to determine how many times an irreducible representation $\rho$ of $G$ appears inside the induced representation $\text{Ind}_H^G \sigma$, it suffices to compute the simpler inner product on $H$ — how many times $\sigma$ appears in the restriction of $\rho$ to $H$. This adjunction is not merely a computational convenience; it reflects a deep structural symmetry between upward and downward passage between groups, and it is the engine behind the construction of character tables of large groups from character tables of their subgroups.

When one applies induction and then restriction in sequence, the result is governed by Mackey's theorem. Given subgroups $H, K \leq G$, the restriction to $K$ of a representation induced from $H$ decomposes as a sum of induced representations, one for each double coset $HgK$ in $G$. Mackey's formula makes precise how the interaction between two subgroups controls the behavior of representations, and his irreducibility criterion — giving necessary and sufficient conditions for an induced representation to be irreducible — follows as a corollary. These tools allow a systematic analysis of the representation theory of groups built from subgroup data, including the classical families of permutation groups and their relatives.

The chapter closes with Frobenius groups, a class of groups where the induced representation perspective yields a stunning structural theorem. A group $G$ is a Frobenius group if it acts transitively on a set such that every non-identity element fixes at most one point; equivalently, there is a subgroup $H$ (the Frobenius complement) such that $H \cap H^g = \{e\}$ for all $g \notin H$. The set of elements that fix no point, together with the identity, turns out to form a normal subgroup $N$ — the Frobenius kernel. Frobenius's original proof of this fact in 1901 used character theory in an essential way: the characters of $G$ induced from those of $H$ must satisfy an overdetermined system of orthogonality relations, and the only resolution is that $N$ is a genuine subgroup. To this day no character-free proof of Frobenius's theorem is known, making it one of the most striking demonstrations that the linear algebra of representations can reveal purely combinatorial facts about group structure.
