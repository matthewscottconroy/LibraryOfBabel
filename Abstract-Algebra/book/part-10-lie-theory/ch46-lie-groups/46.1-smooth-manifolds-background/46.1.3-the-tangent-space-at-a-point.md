# 46.1.3 The Tangent Space at a Point

At every point of a smooth manifold there is an $n$-dimensional real vector space that captures the idea of "directions in which one can move infinitesimally." This is the *tangent space*$T_p M$ at$p \in M$. Unlike the manifold itself, which may be curved or topologically complicated, the tangent space is always a genuine vector space, and this is what makes it so analytically useful: one can add tangent vectors, scale them, and apply the full machinery of linear algebra to study the local geometry of$M$.

There are two standard and equivalent definitions of $T_p M$, each illuminating a different aspect of the concept. The first definition is *kinematic*: a tangent vector at$p$ is an equivalence class of smooth curves passing through$p$. Specifically, consider all smooth curves$\gamma: (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon) \to M$ with$\gamma(0) = p$; two such curves$\gamma_1$ and$\gamma_2$ are equivalent if, in any (equivalently, every) coordinate chart$\phi$ near$p$, the derivatives$\frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}(\phi \circ \gamma_1)(t)$ and$\frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}(\phi \circ \gamma_2)(t)$ are equal as vectors in$\mathbb{R}^n$. The tangent space$T_p M$ is the set of such equivalence classes. Intuitively, a tangent vector is the velocity of a particle at$p$, and two particles are "going the same direction" if their first-order behavior in coordinates agrees.

The second definition is *algebraic*: a tangent vector at $p$ is a *derivation at$p$*, meaning a linear map$v: C^\infty(M) \to \mathbb{R}$ satisfying the Leibniz rule$v(fg) = v(f)g(p) + f(p)v(g)$ for all$f, g \in C^\infty(M)$. The derivation perspective says: a tangent vector is the same as the notion of "directional derivative at$p$." In coordinates, the derivations at$p$ are exactly the partial derivatives$\partial/\partial x^i\big|_p$, which confirms the$n$-dimensionality of$T_p M$.

\begin{definition}
Let $M$ be a smooth$n$-manifold and$p \in M$. The \emph{tangent space}$T_p M$ is the$n$-dimensional real vector space of all derivations at$p$. A smooth map$F: M \to N$ induces a linear map$dF_p: T_p M \to T_{F(p)} N$ called the \emph{differential} of$F$ at$p$, defined by$(dF_p(v))(f) = v(f \circ F)$ for all$v \in T_p M$ and$f \in C^\infty(N)$.
\end{definition}

In coordinates, if $\phi = (x^1, \ldots, x^n)$ is a chart near$p$ and$\psi = (y^1, \ldots, y^m)$ is a chart near$F(p)$, then$dF_p$ is represented by the Jacobian matrix$\left(\frac{\partial(\psi \circ F \circ \phi^{-1})^j}{\partial x^i}\right)$. The assignment$F \mapsto dF_p$ is functorial:$d(G \circ F)_p = dG_{F(p)} \circ dF_p$ (the chain rule). This functoriality will be crucial when we differentiate Lie group homomorphisms to obtain Lie algebra maps.

**Example 1.** For $M = \mathbb{R}^n$ and$p = 0$, the tangent space$T_0 \mathbb{R}^n$ is canonically identified with$\mathbb{R}^n$ itself: the derivation$v = \sum a_i \partial/\partial x^i\big|_0$ corresponds to the vector$(a_1, \ldots, a_n)$. More generally, for any open subset$U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and any point$p \in U$, we have$T_p U \cong \mathbb{R}^n$.

**Example 2.** For the matrix space $M_n(\mathbb{R}) \cong \mathbb{R}^{n^2}$ and the point$I \in GL_n(\mathbb{R})$, the tangent space$T_I GL_n(\mathbb{R})$ is again all of$M_n(\mathbb{R})$, since$GL_n$ is open in$M_n(\mathbb{R})$. This is the first instance of the general principle: the tangent space at the identity of a matrix Lie group is the corresponding matrix Lie algebra.

**Example 3.** For $G = SL_n(\mathbb{R}) = \{A \in M_n(\mathbb{R}) : \det A = 1\}$ and$p = I$, the tangent space$T_I SL_n(\mathbb{R})$ is the kernel of$d(\det)_I: T_I GL_n \to T_1 \mathbb{R}$. Computing:$d(\det)_I(X) = \frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0} \det(I + tX) = \mathrm{tr}(X)$. Therefore$T_I SL_n(\mathbb{R}) = \{X \in M_n(\mathbb{R}) : \mathrm{tr}(X) = 0\}$, the space of trace-zero matrices. This is the Lie algebra$\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbb{R})$.

**Example 4.** For $G = SO(n) = \{A \in M_n(\mathbb{R}) : A^T A = I, \, \det A = 1\}$ and$p = I$, one differentiates the constraint$A^T A = I$. Setting$A(t) = I + tX + O(t^2)$ and differentiating$A(t)^T A(t) = I$ at$t = 0$ gives$X^T + X = 0$, i.e.,$X$ is skew-symmetric. Therefore$T_I SO(n) = \{X \in M_n(\mathbb{R}) : X^T = -X\}$, the Lie algebra$\mathfrak{so}(n)$. Its dimension is$n(n-1)/2$, matching$\dim SO(n)$.

The collection of tangent spaces $\{T_p M\}_{p \in M}$ assembles into the *tangent bundle*$TM = \bigsqcup_{p \in M} T_p M$, which is itself a smooth manifold of dimension$2n$. A smooth map$F: M \to N$ induces a smooth bundle map$dF: TM \to TN$. The tangent bundle perspective is essential for defining vector fields (smooth sections of$TM$), which in turn provide the rigorous foundation for the Lie bracket on a Lie algebra via left-invariant vector fields (Section 47.1.2).
