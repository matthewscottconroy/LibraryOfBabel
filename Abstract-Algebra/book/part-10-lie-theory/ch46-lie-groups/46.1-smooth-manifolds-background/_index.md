# 46.1 Smooth Manifolds (Background)

Before a Lie group can be defined, one needs the language of smooth manifolds — the geometric framework that allows us to speak of "a group that is also a space on which calculus makes sense." A smooth manifold of dimension $n$ is a topological space that locally resembles $\mathbb{R}^n$, equipped with an atlas of coordinate charts whose transition functions are infinitely differentiable. The circle $S^1$, the sphere $S^2$, and the torus $T^2$ are manifolds; an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is trivially a manifold with a single chart. Most of the manifolds in this chapter arise as smooth submanifolds of $\mathbb{R}^{n^2}$ defined by polynomial equations — for instance, $SL_n(\mathbb{R}) = \{A \in M_n(\mathbb{R}) : \det A = 1\}$ — and the smooth structure in those cases is inherited from the ambient Euclidean space.

The crucial construction is the tangent space $T_p M$ at a point $p \in M$. Abstractly it is the vector space of derivations of smooth functions at $p$, or equivalently the space of velocity vectors of smooth curves through $p$; in coordinates it is simply $\mathbb{R}^n$. Smooth maps between manifolds induce linear maps on tangent spaces — the differential or pushforward $df_p: T_p M \to T_{f(p)} N$ — and this functoriality is the bridge between the global, nonlinear geometry of manifolds and the local, linear world of linear algebra. The inverse function theorem holds for smooth maps, and it provides the main tool for verifying that a level set of a smooth function is a smooth submanifold: if $f: M \to N$ is smooth and $q \in N$ is a regular value (meaning $df_p$ is surjective at every $p \in f^{-1}(q)$), then $f^{-1}(q)$ is a smooth submanifold of codimension $\dim N$.

This section covers exactly as much differential geometry as Lie theory requires. The reader need not be an expert in manifold theory; the key facts are: smooth maps, the tangent space, the differential of a map, and the regular value theorem. These tools suffice to define Lie groups rigorously, to verify that the classical groups are smooth submanifolds of $GL_n$, and to make sense of the exponential map in Chapter 47. For readers with a strong background in linear algebra (Part 2) but limited exposure to differential geometry, the matrix Lie group perspective — working entirely inside $M_n(\mathbb{R})$ or $M_n(\mathbb{C})$ — provides a concrete alternative that avoids much of the abstract manifold machinery while losing none of the essential ideas.
