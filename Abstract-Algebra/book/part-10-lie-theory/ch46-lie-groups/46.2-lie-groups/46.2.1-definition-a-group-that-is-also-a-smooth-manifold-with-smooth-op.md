# 46.2.1 Definition: A Group That Is Also a Smooth Manifold, with Smooth Operations

Mathematics is full of objects that carry two independent structures — a topology and an algebra, say — and the richest theory emerges when those two structures are fully compatible. A Lie group is precisely such an object: it is simultaneously a smooth manifold and a group, with the requirement that the group operations be smooth. This compatibility condition is what transforms an abstract group into something amenable to calculus, and the resulting interplay between algebra and geometry is the source of Lie theory's extraordinary power.

\begin{definition}
A \emph{Lie group} is a smooth manifold $G$ equipped with a group structure such that the multiplication map$\mu: G \times G \to G$,$(g, h) \mapsto gh$, and the inversion map$\iota: G \to G$,$g \mapsto g^{-1}$, are both smooth. Here$G \times G$ carries the product smooth manifold structure. The identity element is denoted$e \in G$.
\end{definition}

One can in fact replace the two conditions by a single one: $G$ is a Lie group if and only if the map$G \times G \to G$,$(g,h) \mapsto gh^{-1}$, is smooth. This formulation economizes the hypotheses but obscures the intuition. The separate smoothness of multiplication and inversion is conceptually clearer.

Lie groups are the mathematical objects that encode *continuous symmetry*. Finite groups capture discrete symmetries — the symmetry group of a regular polygon or a crystal lattice — while Lie groups capture the symmetries of continuous phenomena: rotations of Euclidean space, phase transformations in quantum mechanics, gauge transformations in a quantum field theory. The symmetry group of any physical system with a continuous parameter (an angle, a phase, a coupling constant) is a Lie group, and the smooth structure allows one to differentiate with respect to that parameter, connecting the global symmetry to a linear structure that governs it infinitesimally.

The dimension of a Lie group, as a smooth manifold, records how many independent continuous symmetry parameters it possesses. The smoothness requirement is the bridge: it ensures one can differentiate at the identity, obtaining a vector space $T_e G$ that carries all the "first-order" information about how$G$ operates. This vector space, equipped with the Lie bracket to be constructed in Chapter 47, is the Lie algebra$\mathfrak{g}$ of$G$, and it is a linearized approximation to$G$ that encodes the local structure of$G$ completely.

**Example 1.** The real numbers $(\mathbb{R}, +)$ form a Lie group of dimension 1. The smooth manifold structure is the standard one on$\mathbb{R}$, the group operation is addition, and$\mu(x,y) = x + y$ and$\iota(x) = -x$ are both smooth (in fact, polynomial). Similarly,$(\mathbb{R}^\times, \cdot)$, the nonzero reals under multiplication, is a Lie group of dimension 1 (it is an open submanifold of$\mathbb{R}$).

**Example 2.** The circle group $S^1 = \{z \in \mathbb{C} : |z| = 1\}$ under complex multiplication is a compact Lie group of dimension 1. It can also be written as$U(1)$ or$\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$. The multiplication map$(e^{i\theta}, e^{i\phi}) \mapsto e^{i(\theta+\phi)}$ is smooth in the standard manifold structure on$S^1$, and$z \mapsto z^{-1} = \bar{z}$ is smooth. The Lie algebra of$S^1$ is$i\mathbb{R}$, the imaginary axis in$\mathbb{C}$, or equivalently$\mathbb{R}$ with the trivial bracket (a 1-dimensional abelian Lie algebra).

**Example 3.** The general linear group $GL_n(\mathbb{R})$ of invertible$n \times n$ real matrices is a Lie group of dimension$n^2$. As noted in Section 46.2.2, it is an open subset of$M_n(\mathbb{R}) \cong \mathbb{R}^{n^2}$, so it is naturally a smooth manifold. Matrix multiplication is smooth because it is polynomial in matrix entries, and matrix inversion is smooth (by Cramer's rule: each entry of$A^{-1}$ is a rational function of the entries of$A$ with denominator$\det(A) \neq 0$). This is the archetypal Lie group and the starting point for the classification of matrix Lie groups.

**Example 4.** The torus $T^n = (S^1)^n = (\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^n$ is a compact abelian Lie group of dimension$n$. It is the direct product of$n$ copies of the circle group, and it inherits a smooth manifold structure as a product. The tori are especially important in representation theory: every compact connected Lie group contains a *maximal torus*, which plays the role of the Cartan subalgebra in the structure theory.

The tangent space $T_e G$ at the identity element$e$ is the central object of study. Because the group operations are smooth, one can differentiate them at the identity to get linear maps between tangent spaces. The multiplication map in particular, differentiated at$e$, shows that$T_e G$ carries a rich algebraic structure — the Lie bracket — coming from the second-order behavior of the group multiplication. This is worked out in Chapter 47, which constructs the Lie algebra$\mathfrak{g} = T_e G$ and establishes it as the fundamental invariant of$G$.
