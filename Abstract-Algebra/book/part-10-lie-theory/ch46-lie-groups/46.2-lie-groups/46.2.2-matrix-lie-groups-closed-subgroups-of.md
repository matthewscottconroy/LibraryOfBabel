# 46.2.2 Matrix Lie Groups: Closed Subgroups of $GL_n$

The most important Lie groups in practice are not abstract smooth manifolds with group structures, but rather groups of invertible matrices. A *matrix Lie group* is a closed subgroup of $GL_n(\mathbb{R})$ or$GL_n(\mathbb{C})$. The general linear group itself is a Lie group: it is an open subset of the space of all$n \times n$ real matrices$M_n(\mathbb{R}) \cong \mathbb{R}^{n^2}$ (open because the determinant is a continuous function and$GL_n = \det^{-1}(\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\})$), and matrix multiplication and inversion are smooth operations (they are polynomial and rational functions of the matrix entries, respectively).

The key theorem is that every closed subgroup of a Lie group is itself a Lie group. Applied to $GL_n$, this says that any closed subgroup of$GL_n(\mathbb{R})$ — meaning any subgroup that is also a closed subset in the topology of$M_n(\mathbb{R})$ — inherits a smooth manifold structure from the ambient space and therefore becomes a Lie group in its own right. The "closed" hypothesis is essential: a subgroup that is merely algebraically defined but not topologically closed may fail to be a smooth submanifold. For instance, a dense wind on the torus (an irrational-slope line on$\mathbb{R}^2/\mathbb{Z}^2$) is a subgroup but not a manifold.

The practical significance of this closed subgroup theorem is immense: rather than verifying the manifold axioms directly, one simply exhibits a group as a closed subgroup of $GL_n$. The "closedness" is automatic for groups defined by algebraic equations. If$G = \{A \in GL_n : p(A) = 0\}$ for a continuous function$p$, then$G$ is closed as the preimage of a closed set under a continuous map.

**Example 1.** The special linear group $SL_n(\mathbb{R}) = \{A \in M_n(\mathbb{R}) : \det A = 1\}$ is a closed subgroup of$GL_n(\mathbb{R})$. Closedness follows since$\det: M_n(\mathbb{R}) \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous and$\{1\}$ is closed in$\mathbb{R}$. By the closed subgroup theorem,$SL_n(\mathbb{R})$ is a Lie group. Its dimension is$n^2 - 1$, as expected from the single scalar constraint$\det A = 1$.

**Example 2.** The orthogonal group $O(n) = \{A \in M_n(\mathbb{R}) : A^T A = I\}$ is a closed subgroup of$GL_n(\mathbb{R})$. The map$A \mapsto A^T A - I$ is continuous from$M_n(\mathbb{R})$ to$M_n(\mathbb{R})$, and$O(n)$ is the preimage of$\{0\}$, hence closed. The dimension of$O(n)$ is$n(n-1)/2$, since the condition$A^T A = I$ imposes$n(n+1)/2$ scalar constraints (one for each entry of the symmetric matrix$A^T A - I$) on the$n^2$-dimensional space$M_n(\mathbb{R})$, giving$n^2 - n(n+1)/2 = n(n-1)/2$.

**Example 3.** The unitary group $U(n) = \{A \in M_n(\mathbb{C}) : A^* A = I\}$ is a closed subgroup of$GL_n(\mathbb{C})$. Here$A^* = \bar{A}^T$ is the conjugate transpose. As a real Lie group (forgetting the complex structure),$U(n)$ has dimension$n^2$. Its Lie algebra is$\mathfrak{u}(n) = \{X \in M_n(\mathbb{C}) : X^* + X = 0\}$, the skew-Hermitian matrices.

**Example 4.** The symplectic group $Sp(2n, \mathbb{R}) = \{A \in M_{2n}(\mathbb{R}) : A^T J A = J\}$, where$J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & I_n \\ -I_n & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ is the standard symplectic matrix, is a closed subgroup of$GL_{2n}(\mathbb{R})$ of dimension$n(2n+1)$. It is the symmetry group of the standard symplectic form on$\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ and governs Hamiltonian mechanics.

The closed subgroup theorem is also useful negatively: it tells us when a subset fails to be a submanifold. The dense subgroup $\{(e^{it}, e^{i\alpha t}) : t \in \mathbb{R}\} \subseteq T^2$ (for$\alpha$ irrational) is a genuine subgroup and a smooth injective immersion of$\mathbb{R}$ into$T^2$, but it is dense and hence not closed, and it fails to be an embedded submanifold. Its closure is all of$T^2$, and the image is not a Lie subgroup in the sense of having a smooth manifold structure compatible with the subspace topology.

Matrix Lie groups cover essentially all examples that appear in algebra and physics, and they have the added virtue that the Lie bracket on the associated Lie algebra has an explicit formula as the matrix commutator $[X,Y] = XY - YX$. This makes concrete computation possible, and from now on we work primarily with matrix Lie groups.
