# 46.2.3 The Classical Groups: $GL_n$,$SL_n$,$O(n)$,$SO(n)$,$U(n)$,$SU(n)$,$Sp(2n)$

Each classical Lie group arises as the automorphism group of a fundamental geometric structure — a vector space, a volume form, a Euclidean metric, a Hermitian inner product, or a symplectic form. Understanding the groups in this light reveals why they appear so ubiquitously in mathematics and physics, and explains the pattern of their dimensions and Lie algebras.

The organizing principle is: a classical group consists of all invertible linear transformations preserving a specified structure on $\mathbb{F}^n$ (for$\mathbb{F} = \mathbb{R}$ or$\mathbb{C}$). The structure determines the group; computing the derivative of the preservation condition gives the Lie algebra.

**The general and special linear groups.** The group $GL_n(\mathbb{R})$ preserves no additional structure beyond the vector space itself; it is the group of all invertible linear transformations of$\mathbb{R}^n$ and has dimension$n^2$. The subgroup$SL_n(\mathbb{R}) \leq GL_n(\mathbb{R})$ preserves the standard volume form$dx_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dx_n$ (the determinant-1 condition), and has dimension$n^2 - 1$. Over$\mathbb{C}$:$GL_n(\mathbb{C})$ has real dimension$2n^2$, and$SL_n(\mathbb{C})$ has real dimension$2(n^2-1)$.

**The orthogonal and special orthogonal groups.** The orthogonal group $O(n) = \{A \in GL_n(\mathbb{R}) : A^T A = I\}$ consists of the isometries of the standard Euclidean inner product$\langle x, y \rangle = x^T y$ on$\mathbb{R}^n$. Condition:$\langle Ax, Ay \rangle = \langle x, y \rangle$ for all$x, y$, which is$A^T A = I$. Dimension:$n(n-1)/2$. The group$O(n)$ has two connected components, distinguished by$\det A = \pm 1$; the identity component is$SO(n) = O(n) \cap SL_n(\mathbb{R})$, the orientation-preserving isometries.

**Example 1.** $SO(2) = \left\{\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{pmatrix} : \theta \in \mathbb{R}\right\} \cong S^1$. This is the group of rotations of the plane. As a Lie group it is isomorphic to$U(1)$ via$e^{i\theta} \leftrightarrow \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$. Its Lie algebra is$\mathfrak{so}(2) = \mathrm{span}\left\{\begin{pmatrix}0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}\right\}$, which is one-dimensional abelian.

**Example 2.** $SO(3)$, the rotation group in three dimensions, has dimension 3. It is not simply connected:$\pi_1(SO(3)) = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. Its universal cover is$SU(2) \cong S^3$, and the covering map$SU(2) \to SO(3)$ is the source of the spinor representations in quantum mechanics. The Lie algebra$\mathfrak{so}(3)$ is spanned by the three infinitesimal rotation matrices$L_x, L_y, L_z$ satisfying$[L_x, L_y] = L_z$ (and cyclic permutations), which are the angular momentum commutation relations.

**The unitary and special unitary groups.** Over $\mathbb{C}$: the unitary group$U(n) = \{A \in GL_n(\mathbb{C}) : A^* A = I\}$ preserves the standard Hermitian form$\langle x, y \rangle = \bar{x}^T y$ on$\mathbb{C}^n$. As a real Lie group,$\dim_{\mathbb{R}} U(n) = n^2$. The special unitary group$SU(n) = U(n) \cap SL_n(\mathbb{C})$ has$\dim_{\mathbb{R}} SU(n) = n^2 - 1$.$SU(2)$ is diffeomorphic to the 3-sphere$S^3$ and is the simplest compact non-abelian Lie group.

**Example 3.** $SU(2) = \left\{\begin{pmatrix}a & -\bar{b} \\ b & \bar{a}\end{pmatrix} : a, b \in \mathbb{C},\, |a|^2 + |b|^2 = 1\right\}$. This is diffeomorphic to$S^3 \subset \mathbb{R}^4 \cong \mathbb{C}^2$. The Lie algebra is$\mathfrak{su}(2) = \mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{iI, \sigma_x, \sigma_y, \sigma_z\} \cap \ker(\mathrm{tr})$, where$\sigma_x, \sigma_y, \sigma_z$ are the Pauli matrices. After complexification,$\mathfrak{su}(2) \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{C} \cong \mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$, the prototype for all of higher weight theory.

**The symplectic group.** The symplectic group $Sp(2n, \mathbb{R}) = \{A \in GL_{2n}(\mathbb{R}) : A^T J A = J\}$ preserves the standard symplectic form$\omega(x,y) = x^T J y$ on$\mathbb{R}^{2n}$, where$J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & I_n \\ -I_n & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. The symplectic form is non-degenerate and skew-symmetric. Dimension:$n(2n+1)$.

**Example 4.** $Sp(2, \mathbb{R}) = SL_2(\mathbb{R})$. In 2 dimensions, the symplectic form is just the area form, and its automorphisms are the area-preserving linear maps, which are precisely the determinant-1 matrices. More generally,$Sp(2n, \mathbb{R}) \subset SL_{2n}(\mathbb{R})$ (the symplectic condition forces determinant 1 automatically).

The connections across this list appear in the Dynkin diagram classification of Chapter 49: $\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbb{C})$ corresponds to type$A_{n-1}$; the odd orthogonal algebras$\mathfrak{so}(2n+1)$ to type$B_n$; the symplectic algebras$\mathfrak{sp}(2n)$ to type$C_n$; the even orthogonal algebras$\mathfrak{so}(2n)$ to type$D_n$. The classical groups are not merely a convenient list: they are the complete answer to the classification problem for simple Lie algebras, with five exceptional types$G_2, F_4, E_6, E_7, E_8$ rounding out the picture.
