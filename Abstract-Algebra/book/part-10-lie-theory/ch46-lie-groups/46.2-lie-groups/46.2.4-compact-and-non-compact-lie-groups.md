# 46.2.4 Compact and Non-Compact Lie Groups

Compactness is one of the most important properties a Lie group can possess, and its presence or absence fundamentally shapes the representation theory. A Lie group is *compact* if its underlying manifold is compact — intuitively, it has finite volume and no directions that "escape to infinity." This single topological property has deep algebraic consequences.

The compact Lie groups among the classical series are $O(n)$,$SO(n)$,$U(n)$,$SU(n)$, and the compact symplectic group$Sp(n)$ (also written$USp(2n)$). The compactness follows from the norm-preserving conditions: if$A^T A = I$, then each column of$A$ is a unit vector, so the entries of$A$ are bounded. Being a closed subset of a bounded set, these groups are compact. The non-compact groups include$GL_n(\mathbb{R})$,$SL_n(\mathbb{R})$,$SL_n(\mathbb{C})$, and the real symplectic group$Sp(2n, \mathbb{R})$, all of which allow matrices whose entries grow without bound.

A compact Lie group $G$ possesses a unique bi-invariant Haar measure$dg$ of total mass 1 — a translation-invariant probability measure on$G$. This measure allows one to *average* over the group: for any continuous function$f: G \to V$ into a vector space, one can form the average$\int_G f(g)\, dg \in V$. Averaging is a fundamental tool in representation theory, because it allows one to symmetrize any object and make it$G$-equivariant. In particular:

\begin{theorem}[Weyl's Unitarian Trick]
Every finite-dimensional representation of a compact Lie group is completely reducible: it decomposes as a direct sum of irreducible subrepresentations. Moreover, every representation of a compact Lie group is equivalent (via a change of basis) to a unitary representation.
\end{theorem}

The proof is the averaging argument: given an inner product on the representation space $V$, average it over$G$ to produce a$G$-invariant inner product. With respect to this inner product, every$G$-stable subspace$W \subseteq V$ has an invariant orthogonal complement$W^\perp$, and the representation splits as$V = W \oplus W^\perp$. This parallels Maschke's theorem for finite groups, and the finite/compact analogy runs throughout representation theory.

For non-compact groups, this averaging argument fails, and the representation theory is qualitatively different. Over $\mathbb{R}$, any finite-dimensional unitary representation of a non-compact semisimple group must be trivial (or factor through a compact quotient). The interesting representations are infinite-dimensional.

**Example 1.** The non-compact group $SL_2(\mathbb{R})$ has no non-trivial finite-dimensional unitary representations. Its unitary dual — the collection of unitary irreducible representations — is infinite-dimensional and was classified by Bargmann in 1947: it consists of the principal series, the discrete series, and the complementary series. The discrete series representations are$L^2$-realizable and appear in the theory of automorphic forms; the modular forms of weight$k$ arise from the discrete series representation of weight$k$ for$SL_2(\mathbb{R})$.

**Example 2.** The group $SU(2)$ is compact, and its representation theory mirrors that of$\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ in being completely reducible and having a complete list of irreducibles. The irreducibles are$V_0, V_1, V_2, \ldots$ of dimensions$1, 2, 3, \ldots$, where$V_n$ is the symmetric power$\mathrm{Sym}^n(\mathbb{C}^2)$. These are the "spin-$n/2$" representations of quantum mechanics.

**Example 3.** Every compact connected Lie group $G$ contains a maximal torus$T \leq G$, and the exponential map$\exp: \mathfrak{g} \to G$ is surjective (every group element is an exponential). Conjugates of$T$ cover all of$G$:$G = \bigcup_{g \in G} gTg^{-1}$. The structure of the maximal torus and the roots of$\mathfrak{g}$ relative to the Lie algebra$\mathfrak{t}$ of$T$ organizes the full representation theory.

**Example 4.** The compact group $SU(n)$ and the non-compact group$SL_n(\mathbb{C})$ have the same Lie algebra$\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbb{C})$ (after complexification). This is the phenomenon of a real form:$SU(n)$ is a compact real form of$SL_n(\mathbb{C})$, meaning$\mathfrak{su}(n) \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{C} \cong \mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbb{C})$. The finite-dimensional complex representation theory of$\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbb{C})$ governs representations of both$SU(n)$ (on compact groups, these are the unitary reps) and$SL_n(\mathbb{C})$ (as algebraic group representations).

The interplay between the compact group $K$ and its complexification$G$ is the core of the Cartan-Weyl theory. For$K = SU(n)$ and$G = SL_n(\mathbb{C})$, the finite-dimensional irreducible representations of$G$ are in bijection with those of$K$, and both are classified by dominant integral weights. The compact picture provides the geometric intuition (Weyl's integration formula, the Peter-Weyl theorem), while the complex algebraic picture provides the combinatorial machinery (root systems, Verma modules, the Weyl character formula).
