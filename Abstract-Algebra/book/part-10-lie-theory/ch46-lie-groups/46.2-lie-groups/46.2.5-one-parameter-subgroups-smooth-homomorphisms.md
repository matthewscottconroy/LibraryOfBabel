# 46.2.5 One-Parameter Subgroups: Smooth Homomorphisms $\mathbb{R} \to G$

The simplest way to probe the structure of a Lie group is to look at the smooth curves through the identity that are also subgroup homomorphisms. A *one-parameter subgroup* of a Lie group $G$ is a smooth group homomorphism$\gamma: (\mathbb{R}, +) \to G$. The defining property$\gamma(s + t) = \gamma(s)\gamma(t)$ with$\gamma(0) = e$ means that$\gamma$ traces out a continuous "flow" through the group that respects the group law — it is simultaneously a curve and a group homomorphism.

The name "one-parameter subgroup" is slightly misleading: $\gamma$ is a homomorphism from$\mathbb{R}$, not a subgroup, and its image may not even be a closed subgroup of$G$. However, the term is standard, and the key point is the compatibility of the smooth and algebraic structures along the map$\gamma$.

For matrix Lie groups, one-parameter subgroups take a particularly concrete form. If $\gamma: \mathbb{R} \to G \leq GL_n$ is a smooth homomorphism with$\gamma(0) = I$, then differentiating the relation$\gamma(s + t) = \gamma(s)\gamma(t)$ with respect to$t$ at$t = 0$ gives
$$\gamma'(s) = \gamma(s) \cdot \gamma'(0).$$
This is a first-order linear ODE for $\gamma$. Setting$X = \gamma'(0) \in T_I G = \mathfrak{g}$, the unique solution satisfying$\gamma(0) = I$ is$\gamma(t) = e^{tX}$ — the matrix exponential applied to$tX$. Every one-parameter subgroup of a matrix Lie group is therefore an exponential curve$t \mapsto e^{tX}$ through the identity.

The key insight is that the tangent vector $X = \gamma'(0)$ completely determines$\gamma$: distinct tangent vectors at$e$ give distinct one-parameter subgroups, and every element of$T_e G = \mathfrak{g}$ arises as the velocity of some one-parameter subgroup. The collection of all one-parameter subgroups, parameterized by their initial velocities, is therefore in natural bijection with$\mathfrak{g}$. This is the most direct way to see that the Lie algebra encodes all the "infinitesimal directions" of the group.

**Example 1.** For $G = GL_n(\mathbb{R})$ and$X = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ (a nilpotent matrix), the one-parameter subgroup is
$$\gamma(t) = e^{tX} = I + tX = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & t \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
(Higher powers of $X$ vanish.) This is the group of upper triangular matrices with 1s on the diagonal and a free off-diagonal entry.

**Example 2.** For $G = SO(2)$ and$X = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \in \mathfrak{so}(2)$, the one-parameter subgroup is
$$e^{tX} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos t & -\sin t \\ \sin t & \cos t \end{pmatrix}.$$
This is the family of rotations by angle $t$. As$t$ varies over$\mathbb{R}$, this traces out all of$SO(2) \cong S^1$, so the exponential map is surjective onto the compact group$SO(2)$.

**Example 3.** For $G = SL_2(\mathbb{R})$ and$X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \in \mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{R})$, the one-parameter subgroup is
$$e^{tX} = \begin{pmatrix} e^t & 0 \\ 0 & e^{-t} \end{pmatrix}.$$
As $t \to \pm\infty$, this goes to infinity in$SL_2(\mathbb{R})$, reflecting the non-compactness of the group.

**Example 4.** For $G = SU(2)$ and$X = \begin{pmatrix} i & 0 \\ 0 & -i \end{pmatrix} \in \mathfrak{su}(2)$, the one-parameter subgroup is
$$e^{tX} = \begin{pmatrix} e^{it} & 0 \\ 0 & e^{-it} \end{pmatrix}.$$
As $t$ ranges over$[0, 2\pi)$, this traces the maximal torus of$SU(2)$, a copy of$S^1 \leq S^3$.

The one-parameter subgroup perspective foreshadows the role of the exponential map: $\exp: \mathfrak{g} \to G$,$X \mapsto e^X$, converts infinitesimal data (Lie algebra elements) into global group elements by following the one-parameter subgroup for time 1. The Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula (Section 47.3.3) then expresses the product$e^X e^Y$ in terms of$X$,$Y$, and their nested commutators, making precise the sense in which the Lie algebra structure controls the group multiplication near the identity.
