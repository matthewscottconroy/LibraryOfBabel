# 46.2 Lie Groups

A Lie group is a group $G$ that is simultaneously a smooth manifold, with the requirement that the two structures are compatible: the group multiplication $\mu: G \times G \to G$ and the inversion map $\iota: G \to G$ are both smooth. This compatibility condition is surprisingly powerful. Because left-multiplication $L_g: G \to G$ by any fixed element $g$ is a diffeomorphism, the manifold looks the same near every point; all the topology and geometry of $G$ is encoded in a single neighborhood of the identity. This homogeneity is what makes Lie groups so tractable: they are the manifolds with the most symmetry possible.

The primary examples throughout this book are the matrix Lie groups, which are closed subgroups of $GL_n(\mathbb{R})$ or $GL_n(\mathbb{C})$. A theorem of Cartan (the "closed subgroup theorem") says that every closed subgroup of a Lie group is itself a Lie group — so checking that a subset is a closed subgroup suffices to establish that it is a Lie group. This gives the classical groups their Lie group structure essentially for free: $SL_n$ is the preimage of $\{1\}$ under the continuous map $\det$, hence closed; $O_n$ is the preimage of $\{I\}$ under $A \mapsto A^T A$; and so on. The compact groups $SO_n$, $U_n$, $SU_n$, and $Sp_n$ are closed and bounded in $M_n$ and hence compact as manifolds, a fact with profound consequences for their representation theory.

One-parameter subgroups — smooth homomorphisms $\gamma: \mathbb{R} \to G$ — play a central role. They are the "straight lines through the identity" in the Lie group, and every such line is determined by its velocity vector $\gamma'(0) \in T_e G$. For matrix Lie groups, the one-parameter subgroup with initial velocity $X$ is exactly $t \mapsto e^{tX}$, the matrix exponential. The Lie algebra of $G$ is precisely the set of all such velocity vectors, and the one-parameter subgroups are the bridges between the Lie algebra and the Lie group. This section also introduces the compact versus non-compact distinction, which governs global behavior: compact Lie groups have finite-dimensional irreducibles that are all unitary, while non-compact groups such as $SL_2(\mathbb{R})$ have more intricate infinite-dimensional representation theory.
