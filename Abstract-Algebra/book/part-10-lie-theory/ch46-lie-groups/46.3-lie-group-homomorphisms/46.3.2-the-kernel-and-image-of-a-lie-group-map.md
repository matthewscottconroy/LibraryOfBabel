# 46.3.2 The Kernel and Image of a Lie Group Map

The algebraic isomorphism theorems have smooth analogues for Lie groups, and they work essentially as expected — with the additional content that the relevant subgroups inherit smooth manifold structures.

The kernel of a Lie group homomorphism $\phi: G \to H$ is$\ker\phi = \{g \in G : \phi(g) = e_H\}$. This is a closed normal subgroup of$G$: closed because it is the preimage of the closed set$\{e_H\}$ under the continuous map$\phi$, and normal because$g(\ker\phi)g^{-1} \subseteq \ker\phi$ follows from$\phi$ being a homomorphism. Being a closed subgroup of a Lie group, the closed subgroup theorem guarantees that$\ker\phi$ is itself a Lie group.

The image $\mathrm{im}\,\phi = \phi(G)$ is a Lie subgroup of$H$: it is a subgroup (as$\phi$ is a homomorphism) and carries a smooth manifold structure making$\phi: G \to \mathrm{im}\,\phi$ smooth. However,$\mathrm{im}\,\phi$ need not be a closed submanifold of$H$ — it may be only an *immersed* submanifold, meaning the inclusion map$\mathrm{im}\,\phi \hookrightarrow H$ is a smooth injective immersion but not an embedding. The topology of$\mathrm{im}\,\phi$ as a Lie subgroup (induced from$G$) may differ from the subspace topology inherited from$H$.

\begin{theorem}[First Isomorphism Theorem for Lie Groups]
Let $\phi: G \to H$ be a Lie group homomorphism. Then the quotient$G/\ker\phi$ carries a natural Lie group structure, and the induced map$\bar{\phi}: G/\ker\phi \xrightarrow{\sim} \mathrm{im}\,\phi$ is an isomorphism of Lie groups.
\end{theorem}

**Example 1.** The map $\det: GL_n(\mathbb{R}) \to \mathbb{R}^\times$ has kernel$\ker\det = SL_n(\mathbb{R})$ and image$\mathrm{im}\,\det = \mathbb{R}^\times$. The first isomorphism theorem gives$GL_n(\mathbb{R})/SL_n(\mathbb{R}) \cong \mathbb{R}^\times$ as Lie groups.

**Example 2.** The map $\phi: \mathbb{R} \to U(1)$,$t \mapsto e^{2\pi i t}$, is a Lie group homomorphism with$\ker\phi = \mathbb{Z}$ (a discrete normal subgroup) and image$U(1)$. The first isomorphism theorem gives$\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z} \cong U(1) = S^1$ as Lie groups. This is the simplest example of constructing a compact group as a quotient of a non-compact one by a discrete subgroup.

**Example 3.** The covering map $\pi: SU(2) \to SO(3)$ is a Lie group homomorphism (to be discussed in detail in 46.3.4) with$\ker\pi = \{I, -I\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, a discrete normal subgroup. The image is all of$SO(3)$, and the theorem gives$SU(2)/\{\pm I\} \cong SO(3)$.

**Example 4.** The adjoint representation $\mathrm{Ad}: G \to GL(\mathfrak{g})$ has kernel$\ker\mathrm{Ad} = Z(G)$, the center of$G$ (the elements that commute with everything). For$G = SL_2(\mathbb{C})$, the center is$Z(G) = \{\pm I\}$, and$\mathrm{Ad}: SL_2(\mathbb{C}) \to GL(\mathfrak{sl}_2)$ descends to an injection$PSL_2(\mathbb{C}) = SL_2(\mathbb{C})/\{\pm I\} \hookrightarrow GL(\mathfrak{sl}_2)$.

The structural results parallel the group-theoretic isomorphism theorems perfectly and confirm that the smooth and algebraic structures on a Lie group are tightly interlocked. The Lie algebra version is equally clean: if $\phi: G \to H$ is a Lie group homomorphism with differential$d\phi_e: \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{h}$, then$\ker(d\phi_e) = \mathrm{Lie}(\ker\phi)$ (the Lie algebra of the kernel is the kernel of the differential). This means the Lie algebra functor$\mathrm{Lie}$ is exact in a suitable sense: it converts the kernel-image-cokernel sequence of groups into the corresponding sequence of Lie algebras.
