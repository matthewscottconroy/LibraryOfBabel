# 46.3.3 Discrete Subgroups; Quotient Lie Groups

Not every subgroup of a Lie group is a smooth submanifold of the same dimension, and the extreme case of this phenomenon is the discrete subgroup. A *discrete subgroup* $\Gamma \leq G$ is a closed subgroup with the property that its points are isolated: every element$\gamma \in \Gamma$ has a neighborhood in$G$ containing no other element of$\Gamma$. Equivalently,$\Gamma$ has the discrete topology as a subspace of$G$. Such a subgroup looks algebraically like an abstract group with no continuous structure, embedded as a scatter of isolated points in the ambient Lie group.

Discrete subgroups arise naturally in many contexts. The integers $\mathbb{Z} \leq \mathbb{R}$ are a discrete normal subgroup of$(\mathbb{R},+)$. The Gaussian integers$\mathbb{Z}[i] \leq \mathbb{C}$ form a discrete subgroup of$(\mathbb{C},+)$. In a matrix group,$SL_n(\mathbb{Z}) \leq SL_n(\mathbb{R})$ is a discrete subgroup (the integer-entry matrices form a discrete set in the space of real matrices). These arithmetic discrete subgroups are central objects in number theory.

When $\Gamma$ is a discrete normal subgroup of$G$, the quotient$G/\Gamma$ inherits both a smooth manifold structure and a group structure from$G$, making it a Lie group. The projection$\pi: G \to G/\Gamma$ is a smooth Lie group homomorphism, and it is moreover a *covering map* since$\Gamma$ acts on$G$ by left multiplication, discretely and properly. The kernel of$\pi$ is exactly$\Gamma$, and$\pi$ is a local diffeomorphism.

**Example 1.** $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z} \cong S^1$. The integers$\mathbb{Z}$ sit as a discrete normal subgroup of$(\mathbb{R}, +)$, and the quotient$\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$ is the circle group$S^1 \cong U(1)$. The covering map$\pi: \mathbb{R} \to S^1$,$t \mapsto e^{2\pi i t}$, has fiber$\mathbb{Z}$. More generally,$\mathbb{R}^n/\mathbb{Z}^n \cong T^n$, the$n$-torus.

**Example 2.** $SU(2)/\{\pm I\} \cong SO(3)$. The subgroup$\{\pm I\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is a discrete normal subgroup of$SU(2)$. The quotient$SU(2)/\{\pm I\}$ is diffeomorphic to$SO(3)$ via the adjoint representation:$SU(2)$ acts on$\mathfrak{su}(2) \cong \mathbb{R}^3$ by conjugation, and the kernel of this action is exactly$\{\pm I\}$.

**Example 3.** $SL_2(\mathbb{R})/SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$. Here$SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$ is discrete but not normal, so the quotient is a homogeneous space rather than a group. The space$SL_2(\mathbb{R})/SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$ is diffeomorphic (as a smooth manifold) to the unit tangent bundle of the modular curve$\mathbb{H}/SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$, where$\mathbb{H}$ is the upper half-plane. The modular curve is a non-compact hyperbolic Riemann surface of finite area, and its geometric properties underlie the theory of modular forms.

**Example 4.** The lattice $\Gamma = \mathbb{Z} \cdot \omega_1 + \mathbb{Z} \cdot \omega_2 \leq \mathbb{C}$ (for$\omega_1, \omega_2$ linearly independent over$\mathbb{R}$) is a discrete subgroup of$(\mathbb{C}, +)$, and the quotient$\mathbb{C}/\Gamma$ is a complex torus (an elliptic curve). As a Lie group,$\mathbb{C}/\Gamma \cong T^2$ (the real 2-torus), but the complex structure depends on the ratio$\tau = \omega_2/\omega_1 \in \mathbb{H}$.

The deeper interplay between Lie groups and their discrete subgroups — particularly arithmetic subgroups like $SL_n(\mathbb{Z}) \leq SL_n(\mathbb{R})$ — is the subject of the Langlands program and the theory of automorphic forms. Lattices in semisimple Lie groups (discrete cocompact or finite-covolume subgroups) have been classified by Margulis's superrigidity theorem, one of the landmark results of twentieth-century mathematics. These examples hint at the extraordinary depth that emerges from the interaction between the continuous Lie group structure and its discrete subgroups.
