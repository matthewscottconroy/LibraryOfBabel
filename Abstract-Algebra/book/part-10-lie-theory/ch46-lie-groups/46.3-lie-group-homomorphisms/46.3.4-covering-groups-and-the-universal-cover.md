# 46.3.4 Covering Groups and the Universal Cover

The theory of covering spaces from algebraic topology has a clean Lie-theoretic counterpart: every connected Lie group $G$ has a universal covering group$\tilde{G}$, which is both the universal cover in the topological sense and a Lie group, with the covering map$\pi: \tilde{G} \to G$ being a Lie group homomorphism.

Concretely, $\tilde{G}$ is the unique simply connected Lie group that covers$G$. The covering map$\pi: \tilde{G} \to G$ is a Lie group homomorphism whose kernel is the fundamental group$\pi_1(G)$, regarded as a discrete normal subgroup of$\tilde{G}$. This makes$G \cong \tilde{G}/\pi_1(G)$ as Lie groups. The fundamental group$\pi_1(G)$ sits in the center of$\tilde{G}$ — this is a general fact about connected Lie groups and follows from the fact that the kernel of a covering map is always central in the source for a covering of connected spaces.

The crucial consequence for Lie theory is that $\tilde{G}$ and$G$ have the same Lie algebra. Since$\pi$ is a local diffeomorphism, it is an isomorphism on tangent spaces:$d\pi_e: T_e \tilde{G} \cong T_e G$. Thus$\mathrm{Lie}(\tilde{G}) = \mathfrak{g} = \mathrm{Lie}(G)$. Two Lie groups with the same Lie algebra are locally isomorphic but may differ globally through their topology. The universal cover is the "most fundamental" representative of a given Lie algebra, and all other connected Lie groups with that algebra are quotients of$\tilde{G}$ by discrete central subgroups of$\pi_1(\tilde{G})$.

**Example 1.** The covering $\pi: \mathbb{R} \to S^1$,$t \mapsto e^{2\pi it}$, exhibits$\mathbb{R}$ as the universal cover of$S^1$. The fundamental group$\pi_1(S^1) = \mathbb{Z}$, and indeed$S^1 \cong \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$. Both$\mathbb{R}$ and$S^1$ have the same Lie algebra$\mathbb{R}$ (the abelian 1-dimensional Lie algebra).

**Example 2.** The covering $\pi: SU(2) \to SO(3)$. The group$SU(2)$ is diffeomorphic to the 3-sphere$S^3$, which is simply connected ($\pi_1(S^3) = 0$). So$SU(2)$ is the universal cover of$SO(3)$. The covering map is 2-to-1 because$\pi_1(SO(3)) = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, and the kernel is$\ker\pi = \{\pm I\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. 

The map $\pi$ is constructed explicitly via the adjoint action:$SU(2)$ acts on$\mathfrak{su}(2) \cong \mathbb{R}^3$ by conjugation, preserving the Killing form (an inner product on$\mathbb{R}^3$). This gives a homomorphism$SU(2) \to SO(3)$, which one verifies is surjective with kernel$\{\pm I\}$.

Physically, this 2-to-1 cover is the source of spinors in quantum mechanics: the rotation group of physical space is $SO(3)$, but the relevant symmetry group for quantum spin is$SU(2)$. A rotation by$2\pi$ in$SO(3)$ is the identity, but in$SU(2)$ it is$-I \neq I$. Representations of$SU(2)$ that do not factor through$SO(3)$ (the "projective" or "spinor" representations) are the half-integer spin representations$V_{1/2}, V_{3/2}, \ldots$.

**Example 3.** The spin group $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is defined as the universal cover of$SO(n)$ for$n \geq 3$. For$n \geq 3$,$\pi_1(SO(n)) = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, so the cover is 2-to-1. The spin groups are the universal covers relevant to spinor representations in higher dimensions:
\begin{align*}
\mathrm{Spin}(3) &\cong SU(2) \cong S^3, \\
\mathrm{Spin}(4) &\cong SU(2) \times SU(2), \\
\mathrm{Spin}(5) &\cong Sp(4), \\
\mathrm{Spin}(6) &\cong SU(4).
\end{align*}
These low-dimensional isomorphisms are the "accidental isomorphisms" reflecting the Dynkin diagram identifications $B_1 = A_1$,$D_2 = A_1 \times A_1$,$B_2 = C_2$,$D_3 = A_3$.

**Example 4.** For $G = SL_2(\mathbb{R})$, the fundamental group$\pi_1(SL_2(\mathbb{R})) = \mathbb{Z}$. The universal cover$\widetilde{SL_2(\mathbb{R})}$ is a non-algebraic Lie group (it has no faithful finite-dimensional representation), which explains certain subtleties in the representation theory of$SL_2(\mathbb{R})$.

Covering theory for Lie groups bridges topology (the fundamental group), algebra (the Lie algebra, which is the same for all covering groups), and physics (spin and spinors). The classification of all connected Lie groups with a given Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ is equivalent to the classification of discrete central subgroups of$\tilde{G}$, which is in turn governed by the fundamental group$\pi_1(G)$ and the structure of the center$Z(\tilde{G})$.
