# Chapter 46 — Lie Groups

Symmetry is one of the organizing principles of mathematics and physics, and the theory of Lie groups is its most powerful expression. When Sophus Lie set out in the 1870s to do for differential equations what Galois had done for polynomial equations — to classify them by their symmetry groups — he discovered that the relevant groups were not finite combinatorial objects but smooth families of transformations parameterized by continuous variables. A rotation of the plane depends on a single real number, the angle; an element of $SL_n(\mathbb{R})$ depends on $n^2 - 1$ real parameters. These are not merely groups; they are smooth manifolds, and their group operations are smooth maps. This double structure — simultaneously algebraic and geometric — is what defines a Lie group, and it turns out to be extraordinarily constrained and rich.

The simplest examples are already instructive. The circle $S^1 \cong SO(2)$ is a one-dimensional compact Lie group under rotation; the real line $\mathbb{R}$ is a one-dimensional non-compact Lie group under addition. The general linear group $GL_n(\mathbb{R})$ is an open subset of the $n^2$-dimensional space of $n \times n$ matrices, making it an $n^2$-dimensional manifold, and matrix multiplication and inversion are manifestly smooth. From $GL_n$ one obtains a menagerie of classical groups — $SL_n$, $O_n$, $SO_n$, $U_n$, $SU_n$, $Sp_{2n}$ — by imposing algebraic constraints such as determinant one or orthogonality. Each constraint cuts out a smooth submanifold, and the resulting closed subgroups of $GL_n$ are themselves Lie groups. These matrix Lie groups are the concrete objects at the heart of the theory, and most phenomena can be understood by studying them.

The geometric structure of a Lie group has deep consequences. Because left-multiplication by a fixed element $g$ is a diffeomorphism, the local structure near any point looks the same as the local structure near the identity. This homogeneity means that a Lie group is determined, in a precise sense, by its infinitesimal data at the identity — its Lie algebra. The tangent space $T_e G$ at the identity carries a natural bracket operation, making it a Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$, and the exponential map $\exp: \mathfrak{g} \to G$ connects the two structures, at least locally. This passage from global to infinitesimal will be the central theme of Chapter 47.

Lie group homomorphisms are smooth group homomorphisms — maps that respect both structures simultaneously. The kernel of such a map is a closed normal subgroup, and the image is a Lie subgroup; the first isomorphism theorem holds in this smooth setting. Among the most important phenomena here is the relationship between a Lie group and its covering spaces: the universal cover $\tilde{G}$ of a connected Lie group $G$ is again a Lie group, and $G \cong \tilde{G} / \Gamma$ for a discrete central subgroup $\Gamma$. The canonical example is the double cover $SU(2) \to SO(3)$ with kernel $\{\pm I\}$, which underlies the theory of spinors in quantum mechanics: a spin-$\frac{1}{2}$ particle requires a full $720°$ rotation to return to its initial state, a physical consequence of the non-trivial topology of $SO(3)$.

Section 46.3 develops Lie group homomorphisms, quotients, and covering groups, establishing the categorical framework that will be reflected at the level of Lie algebras in Chapter 47.
