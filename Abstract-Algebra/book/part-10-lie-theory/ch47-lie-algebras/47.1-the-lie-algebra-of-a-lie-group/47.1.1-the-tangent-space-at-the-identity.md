# 47.1.1 $\mathfrak{g} = T_e G$: The Tangent Space at the Identity

Of all the points in a Lie group, the identity element $e$ is geometrically and algebraically distinguished. The tangent space at$e$, denoted$\mathfrak{g} = T_e G$, is the *Lie algebra* of$G$ — a vector space that captures the infinitesimal structure of the group and encodes an enormous amount of information about$G$ itself.

The intuition is this. The group $G$ is a smooth manifold, so near any point it looks like Euclidean space. But near the identity, the group structure gives this Euclidean neighborhood extra richness: one can not only move in directions (tangent vectors) but multiply and take inverses of nearby elements. Differentiating the multiplication map at the identity extracts from this rich structure a bilinear operation — the Lie bracket — on the tangent space$T_e G$. The resulting algebraic object$(\mathfrak{g}, [\cdot, \cdot])$ is the Lie algebra.

As a vector space over $\mathbb{R}$,$\mathfrak{g}$ has dimension equal to$\dim G$. For matrix groups, there is a concrete description:$\mathfrak{g}$ consists of all matrices$X \in M_n$ such that$e^{tX} \in G$ for all$t \in \mathbb{R}$. This is because the one-parameter subgroup through$e$ with initial velocity$X$ is$t \mapsto e^{tX}$, and if this curve must lie in$G$, then$X$ is a tangent vector to$G$ at the identity in the most literal sense.

\begin{definition}
The \emph{Lie algebra} of a Lie group $G$ is the vector space$\mathfrak{g} = T_e G$ equipped with the Lie bracket$[\cdot, \cdot]: \mathfrak{g} \times \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g}$ defined via left-invariant vector fields (Section 47.1.2). For matrix groups, the bracket is the matrix commutator:$[X, Y] = XY - YX$.
\end{definition}

The key insight is that $\mathfrak{g}$ carries not just a vector space structure but an additional algebraic operation — the Lie bracket — that reflects how the group multiplication in$G$ interacts with the manifold structure at the infinitesimal level. The tangent space alone is "just" a vector space; the Lie bracket transforms$\mathfrak{g}$ into an object rich enough to encode the local structure of$G$ completely.

**Example 1.** For $G = GL_n(\mathbb{R})$, the Lie algebra is$\mathfrak{gl}_n(\mathbb{R}) = M_n(\mathbb{R})$, all$n \times n$ real matrices. Since$GL_n$ is open in$M_n(\mathbb{R}) \cong \mathbb{R}^{n^2}$, its tangent space at any point is all of$M_n(\mathbb{R})$. The bracket is$[X,Y] = XY - YX$.

**Example 2.** For $G = SL_2(\mathbb{C})$, the Lie algebra is$\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C}) = \{X \in M_2(\mathbb{C}) : \mathrm{tr}(X) = 0\}$. A standard basis is
$$e = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad f = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad h = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix},$$
with brackets $[h,e] = 2e$,$[h,f] = -2f$,$[e,f] = h$. These are the most important relations in all of Lie theory, and they recur throughout the representation theory of every semisimple Lie algebra.

**Example 3.** For $G = SO(3)$, the Lie algebra is$\mathfrak{so}(3) = \{X \in M_3(\mathbb{R}) : X^T = -X\}$, skew-symmetric$3 \times 3$ matrices. A natural basis is
$$L_x = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad L_y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad L_z = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix},$$
satisfying $[L_x, L_y] = L_z$,$[L_y, L_z] = L_x$,$[L_z, L_x] = L_y$. These are the angular momentum commutation relations of quantum mechanics.

**Example 4.** For $G = U(n)$, the Lie algebra is$\mathfrak{u}(n) = \{X \in M_n(\mathbb{C}) : X + X^* = 0\}$, skew-Hermitian matrices. Note: the Lie algebra is a real vector space even though we work with complex matrices. The special case$\mathfrak{su}(2)$ has basis$\{e_1, e_2, e_3\}$ where$e_j = \frac{i}{2}\sigma_j$ and$\sigma_j$ are the Pauli matrices, with$[e_j, e_k] = \epsilon_{jkl} e_l$.

For simply connected groups, the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ determines$G$ completely. Two Lie groups with isomorphic Lie algebras are locally isomorphic, and if they are both simply connected, they are globally isomorphic. This is the theorem that makes the Lie algebra the fundamental invariant in the classification theory: classifying Lie algebras is equivalent (for simply connected groups) to classifying Lie groups.
