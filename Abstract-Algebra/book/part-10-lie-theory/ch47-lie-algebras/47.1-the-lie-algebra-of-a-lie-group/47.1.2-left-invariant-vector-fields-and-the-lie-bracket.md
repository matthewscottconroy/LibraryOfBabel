# 47.1.2 Left-Invariant Vector Fields and the Lie Bracket

To give $\mathfrak{g} = T_e G$ its Lie algebra structure, one must define a bracket operation on tangent vectors at the identity. The natural route goes through vector fields on all of$G$: one extends each tangent vector at$e$ to a vector field on the entire group, uses the Lie bracket of vector fields (a classical construction in differential geometry), and then reads off the resulting structure at the identity.

A *vector field* on $G$ is a smooth assignment$X: G \to TG$,$g \mapsto X_g \in T_g G$, of a tangent vector to each point. Such a vector field is *left-invariant* if it is compatible with left translations: writing$L_g: G \to G$ for the map$h \mapsto gh$, the condition is$(dL_g)_h(X_h) = X_{gh}$ for all$g, h \in G$. A left-invariant vector field is one that "looks the same from every group element": translating the field by the group element$g$ reproduces the original field.

The crucial observation is that left-invariant vector fields on $G$ are in bijection with$T_e G$: given any tangent vector$v \in T_e G$, there is a unique left-invariant vector field$X^v$ with$X^v_e = v$, defined by$X^v_g = (dL_g)_e(v)$. Conversely, any left-invariant vector field is determined by its value at$e$. This bijection allows one to identify$\mathfrak{g}$ with the space of left-invariant vector fields on$G$.

\begin{definition}
The \emph{Lie bracket} $[X, Y]$ of two vector fields is the commutator of their actions as derivations on smooth functions:
$$[X, Y](f) = X(Y(f)) - Y(X(f))$$
for $f \in C^\infty(G)$. This is again a vector field (the commutator of two derivations is a derivation). If$X$ and$Y$ are left-invariant, then$[X, Y]$ is left-invariant as well. The Lie bracket on$\mathfrak{g}$ is defined by transferring this construction to$T_e G$:$[v, w] = [X^v, X^w]_e$.
\end{definition}

For matrix groups, this abstract construction yields the concrete formula $[X, Y] = XY - YX$: the matrix commutator. Let us verify this directly. If$X, Y \in \mathfrak{gl}_n$ and$f: GL_n \to \mathbb{R}$ is smooth, then the left-invariant vector field$X^X$ acts at$A \in GL_n$ by$X^X_A(f) = \frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0} f(A e^{tX})$. One computes$[X^X, X^Y]_I(f) = X^X_I(X^Y \cdot f) - X^Y_I(X^X \cdot f) = \frac{\partial^2}{\partial s\,\partial t}\big|_{s=t=0} f(e^{tX} e^{sY}) - (X \leftrightarrow Y)$. Expanding via the BCH formula to second order, the mixed$st$-term picks out$[X,Y] = XY - YX$.

**Example 1.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$ with basis$\{e, f, h\}$ as in Example 2 of the previous section, verify the brackets directly as matrix commutators:
\begin{align*}
[h, e] &= \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\0&0\end{pmatrix} = 2e.
\end{align*}
Similarly $[h, f] = -2f$ and$[e, f] = h$. This$\mathfrak{sl}_2$-triple is the model for all root$\mathfrak{sl}_2$-subalgebras in semisimple Lie algebras.

**Example 2.** For $\mathfrak{gl}_n$, the matrix units$E_{ij}$ (with a 1 in position$(i,j)$ and zeros elsewhere) satisfy$[E_{ij}, E_{kl}] = \delta_{jk} E_{il} - \delta_{li} E_{kj}$. This is the fundamental relation for general linear Lie algebras. Restricting to trace-zero combinations gives the structure constants of$\mathfrak{sl}_n$.

**Example 3.** For $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ with basis$\{L_x, L_y, L_z\}$ of skew-symmetric matrices, compute:
$$[L_x, L_y] = L_xL_y - L_yL_x = \begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\0&0&-1\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\0&0&0\\-1&0&0\end{pmatrix} - (\text{transpose}) = L_z.$$
The cyclic symmetry $[L_x,L_y]=L_z$,$[L_y,L_z]=L_x$,$[L_z,L_x]=L_y$ reflects the orientation of$\mathbb{R}^3$.

The Lie bracket is the algebraic encoding of the non-commutativity of the group: it measures the extent to which $g$ and$h$ fail to commute, at the infinitesimal level. If the Lie bracket is identically zero, then$G$ is locally abelian near the identity, meaning$gh \approx hg$ for$g, h$ close to$e$ (to first order). Non-vanishing brackets encode genuine non-commutativity, and the structure of the bracket determines all the representation-theoretic properties of$\mathfrak{g}$.
