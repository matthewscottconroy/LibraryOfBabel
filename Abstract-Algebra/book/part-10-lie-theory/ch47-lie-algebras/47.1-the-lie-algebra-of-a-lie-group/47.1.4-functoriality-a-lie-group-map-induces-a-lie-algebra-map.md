# 47.1.4 Functoriality: A Lie Group Map Induces a Lie Algebra Map

The construction of the Lie algebra is not just an operation on objects — it is functorial, meaning that maps between Lie groups give rise to maps between their Lie algebras in a consistent and natural way. This functoriality is the algebraic backbone of Lie theory, and it is what makes the passage to Lie algebras a genuine tool for solving problems about Lie groups.

Given a Lie group homomorphism $\phi: G \to H$, the differential at the identity$d\phi_e: T_e G \to T_e H$ is a linear map from$\mathfrak{g}$ to$\mathfrak{h}$. The crucial property is that$d\phi_e$ preserves the Lie bracket:$d\phi_e([X, Y]) = [d\phi_e(X), d\phi_e(Y)]$. This is not immediate from the definition of the differential alone; it follows because the bracket on$\mathfrak{g}$ encodes the group multiplication on$G$ (via left-invariant vector fields), and$\phi$ is a group homomorphism. Concretely, since$\phi$ intertwines multiplication —$\phi(gh) = \phi(g)\phi(h)$ — it intertwines conjugation —$\phi(ghg^{-1}) = \phi(g)\phi(h)\phi(g)^{-1}$ — and differentiating this intertwining property twice gives the bracket-preservation of$d\phi_e$. Thus$d\phi_e$ is a Lie algebra homomorphism, denoted$\mathrm{Lie}(\phi): \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{h}$.

The assignment $G \mapsto \mathfrak{g}$ and$\phi \mapsto d\phi_e$ is a functor from the category of Lie groups to the category of Lie algebras. This functor satisfies the usual composition rule: if$\psi: H \to K$ is another Lie group homomorphism, then$d(\psi \circ \phi)_e = d\psi_e \circ d\phi_e$. The identity map goes to the identity map, and the functor respects all categorical structure. This means every question about maps between Lie groups can be transformed into a question about maps between Lie algebras — a linear-algebraic question in place of a differential-geometric one.

The depth of this functoriality is revealed by Lie's third theorem, which asserts that the functor is an equivalence of categories between the full subcategory of simply connected Lie groups and the category of finite-dimensional real Lie algebras. Every Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ integrates to a unique simply connected Lie group$\widetilde{G}$, and every Lie algebra homomorphism$\phi: \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{h}$ lifts to a unique Lie group homomorphism$\Phi: \widetilde{G} \to H$ from the simply connected cover. The differentiation functor is thus a complete and reversible linearization of Lie group theory in the simply connected case, and the deviation from this in the non-simply-connected case is measured precisely by the fundamental group$\pi_1(G)$.

For non-simply-connected groups, one cannot always lift a Lie algebra homomorphism to a Lie group homomorphism, but one can always lift it to a homomorphism from the universal cover. The kernel of the covering map $p: \widetilde{G} \to G$ is a discrete normal subgroup isomorphic to$\pi_1(G)$, and whether a Lie algebra homomorphism$\phi: \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{h}$ integrates to a map$G \to H$ (rather than just$\widetilde{G} \to H$) depends on whether$\phi$ is compatible with this kernel — a topological, not just algebraic, condition. The purely algebraic category of Lie algebras thus "forgets" the global topology, keeping only the local structure near the identity.

**Example 1.** The determinant map $\det: GL_n(\mathbb{R}) \to \mathbb{R}^\times$ is a Lie group homomorphism. Its differential at the identity is$d(\det)_I: M_n(\mathbb{R}) \to \mathbb{R}$, and a direct computation gives$d(\det)_I(X) = \mathrm{tr}(X)$. This is the Lie algebra homomorphism$\mathrm{tr}: \mathfrak{gl}_n \to \mathbb{R}$ (where$\mathbb{R}$ is abelian, so the bracket is zero). The kernel of$\mathrm{tr}$ is$\mathfrak{sl}_n$, which corresponds — by the subalgebra/subgroup dictionary — to the kernel of$\det$, namely$SL_n(\mathbb{R})$. Functoriality makes this correspondence transparent.

**Example 2.** The inclusion $\iota: SU(2) \hookrightarrow SL_2(\mathbb{C})$ is a Lie group homomorphism. Its differential$d\iota_e: \mathfrak{su}(2) \to \mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$ is the inclusion of real Lie algebras$\mathfrak{su}(2) \hookrightarrow \mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$. The complex Lie algebra$\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$ is the complexification of$\mathfrak{su}(2)$:$\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C}) \cong \mathfrak{su}(2) \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{C}$. This illustrates that the functor interacts cleanly with the operation of complexification.

**Example 3.** The adjoint representation $\mathrm{Ad}: G \to GL(\mathfrak{g})$ is a Lie group homomorphism. Its differential is$\mathrm{ad}: \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g})$, the adjoint representation of the Lie algebra, given by$\mathrm{ad}(X)(Y) = [X,Y]$. This is the functoriality applied to the specific map$g \mapsto (Y \mapsto gYg^{-1})$, and it shows that the Lie algebra of$GL(\mathfrak{g})$ (which is$\mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g})$) receives a canonical map from$\mathfrak{g}$. The adjoint representation is the prototype of the general functoriality, and its properties — which are studied in Section 47.4 — follow directly from the general theory.

**Example 4.** Consider the exponential map $\exp: \mathbb{R} \to SO(2)$,$t \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} \cos t & -\sin t \\ \sin t & \cos t \end{pmatrix}$. This is a Lie group homomorphism from$(\mathbb{R}, +)$ to$SO(2)$. Its differential at$0$ sends$1 \in \mathbb{R}$ to$\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \in \mathfrak{so}(2)$, which is a Lie algebra homomorphism$\mathbb{R} \to \mathfrak{so}(2)$ (necessarily an isomorphism, since both have dimension 1). This is the one-parameter subgroup perspective: every element$X \in \mathfrak{g}$ determines a Lie group homomorphism$\mathbb{R} \to G$ via$t \mapsto \exp(tX)$, and functoriality recovers$X$ as the image of$1 \in \mathbb{R}$ under the differential.

The functoriality of the Lie algebra construction unifies many seemingly disparate constructions. Whenever one has a natural operation on Lie groups — taking products, subgroups, quotients, extensions — the corresponding operation on Lie algebras is obtained by applying the functor. This is one of the central organizing principles of the subject.
