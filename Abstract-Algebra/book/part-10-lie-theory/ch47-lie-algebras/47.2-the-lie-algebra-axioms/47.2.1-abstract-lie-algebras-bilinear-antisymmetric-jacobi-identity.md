# 47.2.1 Abstract Lie Algebras: Bilinear, Antisymmetric, Jacobi Identity

Having seen how the Lie algebra arises from a Lie group, one can abstract the structure and study Lie algebras in their own right, over any field, without any reference to a group. This abstraction is one of the key moves in mathematics: once we identify the essential algebraic properties of the bracket operation, we can study them purely algebraically and apply the results back to groups, physics, and geometry alike.

An *abstract Lie algebra* over a field $k$ is a$k$-vector space$\mathfrak{g}$ equipped with a bilinear map$[\cdot, \cdot]: \mathfrak{g} \times \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g}$ called the *Lie bracket*, subject to two axioms. The first axiom is *antisymmetry*:$[X, X] = 0$ for all$X \in \mathfrak{g}$. By bilinearity, this is equivalent to$[X, Y] = -[Y, X]$ for all$X, Y$. In the matrix context, this corresponds to the fact that$[X, Y] = XY - YX = -(YX - XY) = -[Y, X]$. The second and more substantive axiom is the *Jacobi identity*:$[X, [Y, Z]] + [Y, [Z, X]] + [Z, [X, Y]] = 0$ for all$X, Y, Z \in \mathfrak{g}$. This identity has no analogue in ring theory (where associativity takes its place) and is the defining constraint that separates Lie algebras from all other bilinear structures.

To see why the Jacobi identity matters, rewrite it as $[X, [Y, Z]] = [[X, Y], Z] + [Y, [X, Z]]$. This says exactly that$\mathrm{ad}(X) = [X, -]: \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g}$ is a *derivation* of the bracket — it obeys the Leibniz rule for the bracket, treating the bracket as a "product." In other words,$X$ acts on$\mathfrak{g}$ via$\mathrm{ad}(X)$, and this action is compatible with the bracket structure. The Jacobi identity is therefore the abstract axiom that makes the adjoint action consistent, and it is the condition that ensures$\mathrm{ad}: \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g})$ is itself a Lie algebra homomorphism — a theme developed thoroughly in Section 47.4.

One can verify the Jacobi identity directly for the matrix commutator $[X, Y] = XY - YX$: expanding$[X, [Y, Z]] + [Y, [Z, X]] + [Z, [X, Y]]$ yields a sum of twelve terms$\pm$ products of three matrices, which cancel pairwise. The remarkable fact is that this cancellation requires no assumption on the matrices other than the existence of associative multiplication — thus any associative algebra becomes a Lie algebra via the commutator bracket. The converse fails: not every Lie algebra is the commutator algebra of an associative one, though Ado's theorem guarantees that every finite-dimensional Lie algebra embeds in some$\mathfrak{gl}_n$.

The dimension-1 case is trivial: a 1-dimensional Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g} = k$ must have$[X, X] = 0$ for the unique (up to scalar) element$X$, so the bracket is identically zero. All 1-dimensional Lie algebras are abelian and isomorphic. The first interesting case is dimension 2 over$\mathbb{C}$: up to isomorphism there are exactly two — the abelian algebra and the non-abelian algebra$\mathfrak{b}_1$ with basis$\{h, e\}$ and$[h, e] = e$ (the "ax+b algebra," the Lie algebra of the affine group of the line). The classification explodes in dimension 3 and above, but the structure is organized by the theory of solvable and semisimple Lie algebras developed in Chapter 48.

**Example 1.** The simplest nontrivial simple Lie algebra is $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C}) = \mathrm{span}\{e, f, h\}$ with brackets$[h, e] = 2e$,$[h, f] = -2f$,$[e, f] = h$. One verifies antisymmetry immediately. The Jacobi identity amounts to checking three independent cyclic triples. For example:$[h, [e, f]] + [e, [f, h]] + [f, [h, e]] = [h, h] + [e, 2f] + [f, 2e] = 0 + 2h - 2h = 0$. The factor of 2 in$[h, e] = 2e$ (rather than$[h, e] = e$) is a normalization convention tied to the theory of roots.

**Example 2.** The algebra $\mathfrak{so}(3) = \mathrm{span}\{L_x, L_y, L_z\}$ with brackets$[L_x, L_y] = L_z$,$[L_y, L_z] = L_x$,$[L_z, L_x] = L_y$ is the Lie algebra of the rotation group$SO(3)$. This bracket is exactly the cross product of vectors in$\mathbb{R}^3$, and the Jacobi identity for the cross product is a classical identity from vector calculus. The algebra$\mathfrak{so}(3)$ is isomorphic to$\mathfrak{su}(2)$ via an explicit change of basis, and also isomorphic to$\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{R})$ after passing to complex coefficients — these identifications are at the heart of the spin representation.

**Example 3.** The Heisenberg algebra $\mathfrak{h} = \mathrm{span}\{p, q, z\}$ with$[p, q] = z$,$[p, z] = [q, z] = 0$ is an example of a Lie algebra that is nilpotent (and solvable) but not simple. It satisfies all the axioms but brackets into its center: the Jacobi identity is trivially satisfied since all triple brackets vanish. Its role in quantum mechanics — where$p$ and$q$ are position and momentum operators — makes it the most physically important small Lie algebra.

**Example 4.** Let $A$ be any associative$k$-algebra (e.g., the matrix algebra$M_n(k)$, a group algebra, or the universal enveloping algebra of a Lie algebra). Define$[a, b] = ab - ba$ for$a, b \in A$. Antisymmetry and bilinearity are immediate. The Jacobi identity follows by expanding and canceling: this is the standard proof that any associative algebra is a Lie algebra under the commutator. The resulting Lie algebra is denoted$A^-$ or$A_{\mathrm{Lie}}$.

The axioms of a Lie algebra are designed to capture the essential properties of the infinitesimal symmetry of smooth groups, but they also arise naturally in the theory of derivations, in Poisson algebras, in the structure theory of Jordan algebras, and throughout mathematical physics. The two axioms — antisymmetry and the Jacobi identity — together ensure that the adjoint action $X \mapsto [X,-]$ is an "action by symmetries" in a precise sense, and this is the algebraic core of the entire subject.
