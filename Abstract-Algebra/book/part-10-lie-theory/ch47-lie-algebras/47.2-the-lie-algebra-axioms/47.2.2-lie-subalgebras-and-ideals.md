# 47.2.2 Lie Subalgebras and Ideals

Just as group theory is organized around subgroups and normal subgroups, and ring theory around subrings and ideals, Lie algebra theory is organized around subalgebras and ideals — with the parallel being remarkably precise. Understanding these substructures is the first step toward the structural classification of Lie algebras.

A *Lie subalgebra* of $\mathfrak{g}$ is a subspace$\mathfrak{h} \subseteq \mathfrak{g}$ that is closed under the bracket:$[\mathfrak{h}, \mathfrak{h}] \subseteq \mathfrak{h}$. A subalgebra is itself a Lie algebra in its own right under the restricted bracket. An *ideal* is a subspace$\mathfrak{a} \subseteq \mathfrak{g}$ satisfying the stronger condition$[\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{a}] \subseteq \mathfrak{a}$ — not just brackets of$\mathfrak{a}$ with itself, but brackets of any element of$\mathfrak{g}$ with any element of$\mathfrak{a}$. Since the bracket is antisymmetric, this is equivalent to$[\mathfrak{a}, \mathfrak{g}] \subseteq \mathfrak{a}$ as well, so there is no distinction between left and right ideals in Lie algebras (unlike in ring theory, where one-sided ideals can differ). An ideal is always a subalgebra, but not conversely.

The significance of ideals becomes clear when one forms quotients. If $\mathfrak{a}$ is an ideal in$\mathfrak{g}$, the quotient vector space$\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{a}$ inherits a well-defined Lie bracket from$\mathfrak{g}$: one sets$[X + \mathfrak{a}, Y + \mathfrak{a}] = [X, Y] + \mathfrak{a}$, and this is independent of the choice of representatives precisely because$\mathfrak{a}$ is an ideal. The isomorphism theorems for Lie algebras hold in precise analogy with those for groups: if$\phi: \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{h}$ is a Lie algebra homomorphism then$\ker \phi$ is an ideal in$\mathfrak{g}$, and$\mathfrak{g}/\ker\phi \cong \mathrm{im}\,\phi$.

The correspondence between the algebra and the group is: a connected Lie subgroup $H \leq G$ corresponds to a Lie subalgebra$\mathfrak{h} \leq \mathfrak{g}$, and$H$ is a normal subgroup of$G$ if and only if$\mathfrak{h}$ is an ideal in$\mathfrak{g}$. The exponential map converts subalgebra structure to subgroup structure, and the ideal condition precisely captures normality at the infinitesimal level. This is one of the main instances of the "Lie dictionary" developed in Section 47.3.4.

Every Lie algebra has two trivial ideals: the zero ideal $\{0\}$ and$\mathfrak{g}$ itself. A Lie algebra is *simple* if it is non-abelian and has no proper nonzero ideals. The *center*$Z(\mathfrak{g}) = \{Z \in \mathfrak{g} : [Z, X] = 0 \text{ for all } X\}$ is always an ideal (indeed,$[X, Z(\mathfrak{g})] \subseteq Z(\mathfrak{g})$ since brackets with central elements are zero). The *derived subalgebra*$[\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}]$ is the span of all brackets$[X, Y]$; it is always an ideal since$[Z, [X,Y]] = [[Z,X], Y] + [X, [Z,Y]]$ by the Jacobi identity. These two canonical ideals — the center and the derived subalgebra — are the starting points for the structural analysis in Chapter 48.

**Example 1.** In $\mathfrak{gl}_n$, the space$\mathfrak{sl}_n$ of trace-zero matrices is an ideal: if$\mathrm{tr}(A) = 0$ and$B$ is arbitrary, then$\mathrm{tr}([B, A]) = \mathrm{tr}(BA) - \mathrm{tr}(AB) = 0$ since trace is invariant under cyclic permutations. The quotient$\mathfrak{gl}_n / \mathfrak{sl}_n \cong k$ is the 1-dimensional abelian Lie algebra corresponding to the trace.

**Example 2.** In $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C}) = \mathrm{span}\{e, f, h\}$, the span$\mathrm{span}\{e\}$ is a subalgebra (since$[e, e] = 0$) but not an ideal:$[h, e] = 2e \in \mathrm{span}\{e\}$, but$[f, e] = -h \notin \mathrm{span}\{e\}$. The algebra$\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$ has no proper nonzero ideals and is simple. Its center is zero: a central element$ae + bf + ch$ must satisfy$[h, ae + bf + ch] = 2ae - 2bf = 0$, giving$a = b = 0$, and then$[e, ch] = -2ce = 0$, giving$c = 0$.

**Example 3.** In the Borel subalgebra $\mathfrak{b} = \mathrm{span}\{h, e\}$ of$\mathfrak{sl}_2$ (upper triangular traceless matrices), the nilradical$\mathfrak{n} = \mathrm{span}\{e\}$ is an ideal:$[h, e] = 2e \in \mathfrak{n}$ and$[e, e] = 0 \in \mathfrak{n}$. The quotient$\mathfrak{b}/\mathfrak{n} \cong \mathrm{span}\{h\}$ is 1-dimensional and abelian. This gives a concrete example of the solvable structure studied in Chapter 48:$\mathfrak{b}$ is solvable because$[\mathfrak{b}, \mathfrak{b}] = \mathfrak{n}$ is abelian.

**Example 4.** For $\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbb{C})$ with$n \geq 3$, one can check that$\mathfrak{g}$ is simple. Any nonzero ideal$\mathfrak{a}$ must contain some root space$\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}$ (a scalar multiple of some matrix unit$E_{ij}$ with$i \neq j$). Bracketing with other root vectors then forces$\mathfrak{a}$ to contain the entire algebra. The key step uses that the root system$A_{n-1}$ is *connected* in the sense that any root can be obtained from any other by a chain of brackets, which forces any nonzero ideal to be all of$\mathfrak{g}$.

The notion of a simple Lie algebra — having no proper nonzero ideals — is the analogue of a simple group, and the classification of simple Lie algebras (via Dynkin diagrams, as developed in Chapter 49) is one of the supreme achievements of mathematics. The subalgebra/ideal distinction, parallel to the subgroup/normal-subgroup distinction in group theory, provides the organizational framework for all the structural theorems that follow.
