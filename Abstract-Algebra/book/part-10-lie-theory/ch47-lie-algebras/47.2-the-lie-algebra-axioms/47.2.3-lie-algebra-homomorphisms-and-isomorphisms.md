# 47.2.3 Lie Algebra Homomorphisms and Isomorphisms

A *Lie algebra homomorphism* $\phi: \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{h}$ is a linear map that preserves the Lie bracket:$\phi([X, Y]) = [\phi(X), \phi(Y)]$ for all$X, Y \in \mathfrak{g}$. The two conditions — linearity and bracket-preservation — together make$\phi$ a morphism in the category of Lie algebras. The bracket-preservation condition is the substantive one: it says that$\phi$ intertwines the "infinitesimal symmetry structures" of$\mathfrak{g}$ and$\mathfrak{h}$.

The kernel of a Lie algebra homomorphism, $\ker \phi = \{X \in \mathfrak{g} : \phi(X) = 0\}$, is an ideal in$\mathfrak{g}$: if$X \in \ker\phi$ and$Y \in \mathfrak{g}$, then$\phi([Y, X]) = [\phi(Y), \phi(X)] = [\phi(Y), 0] = 0$, so$[Y, X] \in \ker\phi$. The image$\mathrm{im}\,\phi$ is a subalgebra of$\mathfrak{h}$. The first isomorphism theorem holds:$\mathfrak{g}/\ker\phi \cong \mathrm{im}\,\phi$ as Lie algebras.

A Lie algebra homomorphism is an *isomorphism* if it is a bijective linear map; since both $\phi$ and$\phi^{-1}$ are linear, and the bracket-preservation of$\phi^{-1}$ follows from that of$\phi$ by invertibility, one need only check bijectivity. This parallels the situation for group homomorphisms, where a bijective homomorphism is automatically an isomorphism. Two Lie algebras that are isomorphic — that is, related by a bijective bracket-preserving linear map — are structurally identical as Lie algebras, even if they arise from different groups or in different contexts. The classification of Lie algebras up to isomorphism, achieved via root systems and Dynkin diagrams for the semisimple case, is one of the great theorems of the subject.

A *representation* of $\mathfrak{g}$ is a Lie algebra homomorphism$\rho: \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{gl}(V)$ for some vector space$V$. This is the same as giving$V$ the structure of a$\mathfrak{g}$-module, and the two terminologies are used interchangeably. The adjoint representation$\mathrm{ad}: \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g})$, defined by$\mathrm{ad}(X)(Y) = [X, Y]$, is the canonical example and shows that every Lie algebra acts on itself. The kernel of$\mathrm{ad}$ is the center$Z(\mathfrak{g})$, so$\mathrm{ad}$ is faithful (an injective homomorphism) if and only if$\mathfrak{g}$ is center-free. In particular, semisimple Lie algebras have trivial center, so the adjoint representation is a faithful finite-dimensional representation — this provides an explicit embedding of$\mathfrak{g}$ into a matrix algebra, confirming that every center-free Lie algebra is a matrix Lie algebra.

**Example 1.** There is a Lie algebra isomorphism $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C}) \cong \mathfrak{so}(3, \mathbb{C})$. The algebra$\mathfrak{so}(3, \mathbb{C})$ has basis$\{L_1, L_2, L_3\}$ with$[L_i, L_j] = \epsilon_{ijk} L_k$ (Levi-Civita symbol). The map$e \mapsto \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(L_1 + iL_2)$,$f \mapsto \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(L_1 - iL_2)$,$h \mapsto -iL_3$ is a Lie algebra isomorphism. Verifying bracket-preservation confirms the isomorphism. This is the algebraic reason why spin-$1/2$ particles in quantum mechanics are related to$SO(3)$.

**Example 2.** The map $\phi: \mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C}) \to \mathfrak{gl}_2(\mathbb{C})$ defined by$e \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$,$f \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$,$h \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ is the standard 2-dimensional representation. It is a Lie algebra homomorphism: one checks$\phi([h, e]) = \phi(2e) = 2\phi(e)$ and$[\phi(h), \phi(e)] = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = 2\phi(e)$. This is injective, confirming that$\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$ is isomorphic to its image, the subalgebra of$2 \times 2$ trace-zero matrices.

**Example 3.** The adjoint representation of $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$ is$\mathrm{ad}: \mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C}) \to \mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})) \cong \mathfrak{gl}_3(\mathbb{C})$. Computing$\mathrm{ad}(h)$:$\mathrm{ad}(h)(e) = [h,e] = 2e$,$\mathrm{ad}(h)(h) = [h,h] = 0$,$\mathrm{ad}(h)(f) = [h,f] = -2f$. In the basis$\{e, h, f\}$, the matrix of$\mathrm{ad}(h)$ is$\mathrm{diag}(2, 0, -2)$. Similarly$\mathrm{ad}(e)$ and$\mathrm{ad}(f)$ are nilpotent matrices. The adjoint representation is a 3-dimensional representation and it is faithful since$Z(\mathfrak{sl}_2) = 0$.

**Example 4.** The trace map $\mathrm{tr}: \mathfrak{gl}_n \to k$ (where$k$ is the ground field viewed as an abelian Lie algebra) is a Lie algebra homomorphism:$\mathrm{tr}([X, Y]) = \mathrm{tr}(XY - YX) = 0$ for all$X, Y$. Its kernel is$\mathfrak{sl}_n$, confirming that$\mathfrak{sl}_n$ is an ideal and that$\mathfrak{gl}_n / \mathfrak{sl}_n \cong k$ as Lie algebras.

The category of Lie algebras over a field $k$ has many pleasant properties inherited from the category of vector spaces: direct products (the direct sum$\mathfrak{g} \oplus \mathfrak{h}$ with componentwise bracket), quotients, kernels, and images all behave as expected. The classification of Lie algebras up to isomorphism is the central structural problem, and it is completely solved in the semisimple case by the Cartan–Killing classification of Chapter 49.
