# 47.2.4 The Lie Algebras of the Classical Groups

Each classical Lie group has a classical Lie algebra, obtained by differentiating the defining matrix condition at the identity. The resulting algebras are concrete and computable, and their structure will ultimately be organized by the Dynkin diagram classification. Working out the classical Lie algebras explicitly is both essential preparation for the classification and an excellent exercise in applying the general theory.

The rule for finding the Lie algebra of a matrix Lie group $G$ is: differentiate the defining condition. If$G = \{A \in GL_n : F(A) = 0\}$ for some smooth condition$F$, then$\mathfrak{g} = \{X \in M_n : \frac{d}{dt}F(e^{tX})\big|_{t=0} = 0\}$. For groups defined by quadratic conditions like$A^T A = I$, this differentiation rule gives linear conditions on$X$.

The full list of classical Lie algebras is as follows:

$\mathfrak{gl}_n(\mathbb{R}) = M_n(\mathbb{R})$, all$n \times n$ real matrices with bracket$[X, Y] = XY - YX$. This is the Lie algebra of$GL_n(\mathbb{R})$.

$\mathfrak{sl}_n = \{X \in M_n : \mathrm{tr}(X) = 0\}$, the trace-zero matrices. This is an ideal in$\mathfrak{gl}_n$ of dimension$n^2 - 1$. The condition comes from differentiating$\det(e^{tX}) = e^{t \cdot \mathrm{tr}(X)} = 1$, giving$\mathrm{tr}(X) = 0$.

$\mathfrak{so}(n) = \{X \in M_n(\mathbb{R}) : X + X^T = 0\}$, the skew-symmetric matrices, of dimension$n(n-1)/2$. The condition comes from differentiating$A^T A = I$:$X^T + X = 0$.

$\mathfrak{u}(n) = \{X \in M_n(\mathbb{C}) : X + X^* = 0\}$, the skew-Hermitian matrices, of real dimension$n^2$. The condition from$A^* A = I$ gives$X^* + X = 0$.

$\mathfrak{su}(n) = \mathfrak{u}(n) \cap \mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbb{C})$, the trace-zero skew-Hermitian matrices, of real dimension$n^2 - 1$.

$\mathfrak{sp}(2n) = \{X \in M_{2n} : X^T J + JX = 0\}$, where$J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & I_n \\ -I_n & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ is the standard symplectic matrix, of dimension$n(2n+1)$.

The connections across this list are illuminating. The complexification $\mathfrak{so}(n) \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{C}$ and$\mathfrak{su}(n) \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{C}$ give the complex semisimple Lie algebras$\mathfrak{so}(n, \mathbb{C})$ and$\mathfrak{sl}(n, \mathbb{C})$, which are then classified by the Dynkin diagrams:$\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbb{C})$ corresponds to type$A_{n-1}$; the odd orthogonal algebras to type$B$; the symplectic algebras to type$C$; the even orthogonal algebras to type$D$. This classification — which will be developed in Chapter 49 — shows that the classical groups are not just a convenient list but the complete answer to the question of which simple Lie algebras exist, with five exceptional types$G_2, F_4, E_6, E_7, E_8$ rounding out the picture.

**Example 1.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$: the trace-zero$2 \times 2$ complex matrices have basis$e = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$,$f = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$,$h = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ with$[h, e] = 2e$,$[h, f] = -2f$,$[e, f] = h$. This is 3-dimensional, simple, and is the type$A_1$ algebra.

**Example 2.** For $\mathfrak{so}(3)$: the skew-symmetric real$3 \times 3$ matrices have basis
$$L_x = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix},\quad L_y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix},\quad L_z = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$
with $[L_x, L_y] = L_z$,$[L_y, L_z] = L_x$,$[L_z, L_x] = L_y$. This is isomorphic to$\mathfrak{su}(2)$ and to$\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{R})$, all of type$B_1 = A_1$.

**Example 3.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_3(\mathbb{C})$ (type$A_2$): this is 8-dimensional. The Cartan subalgebra consists of diagonal traceless matrices$\mathrm{diag}(a, b, c)$ with$a + b + c = 0$, which is 2-dimensional. The six root spaces are spanned by the off-diagonal matrix units$E_{ij}$ for$i \neq j$. This algebra has the rich root system of type$A_2$, with six roots arranged as the vertices of a hexagon.

**Example 4.** For $\mathfrak{su}(2)$: the skew-Hermitian trace-zero$2 \times 2$ matrices have basis$e_1 = \begin{pmatrix} i & 0 \\ 0 & -i \end{pmatrix}$,$e_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$,$e_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ with$[e_1, e_2] = 2e_3$,$[e_2, e_3] = 2e_1$,$[e_3, e_1] = 2e_2$. This is isomorphic to$\mathfrak{so}(3)$ over$\mathbb{R}$ (real dimension 3), and its complexification is$\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$.

The classical Lie algebras provide the concrete working material for the abstract classification. Their structures — roots, Cartan subalgebras, Weyl groups — can all be computed directly, and these computations are the testing ground for the general theory. The fact that the classical series $A_n, B_n, C_n, D_n$ together with the five exceptional types exhaust all possibilities is the content of the Cartan–Killing classification.
