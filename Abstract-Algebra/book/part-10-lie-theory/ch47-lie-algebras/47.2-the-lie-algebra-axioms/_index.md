# 47.2 The Lie Algebra Axioms

Having derived Lie algebras from Lie groups, it is natural to abstract away the geometric origin and study them on purely algebraic grounds. An abstract Lie algebra over a field $k$ is a $k$-vector space $\mathfrak{g}$ equipped with a bilinear map $[\cdot, \cdot]: \mathfrak{g} \times \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g}$ satisfying antisymmetry ($[x,x] = 0$ for all $x$, which implies $[x,y] = -[y,x]$) and the Jacobi identity $[x,[y,z]] + [y,[z,x]] + [z,[x,y]] = 0$. This abstract formulation generalizes the matrix commutator and the Lie bracket of vector fields, and it opens the door to algebraic techniques: subalgebras, ideals, quotients, and homomorphisms are all defined by analogy with ring theory, with the bracket replacing multiplication.

A Lie subalgebra is a subspace closed under the bracket; a Lie ideal $\mathfrak{i} \subset \mathfrak{g}$ satisfies the stronger condition $[\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{i}] \subset \mathfrak{i}$. Ideals play the role that normal subgroups play in group theory: they are exactly the kernels of Lie algebra homomorphisms, and they allow the formation of quotient algebras $\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{i}$. The derived algebra $[\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}]$ is always an ideal, and its successive iterates form the derived series. The center $Z(\mathfrak{g}) = \{x : [x,y] = 0 \text{ for all } y\}$ is also an ideal. Abelian Lie algebras (where all brackets vanish) are the "most commutative" extreme; simple Lie algebras — with no proper ideals and $[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}] = \mathfrak{g}$ — are the "most non-commutative."

The Lie algebras of the classical matrix groups are the central examples. One computes $\mathfrak{gl}_n = M_n(k)$ with $[A,B] = AB - BA$; $\mathfrak{sl}_n = \{A : \text{tr}(A) = 0\}$; $\mathfrak{so}_n = \{A : A + A^T = 0\}$; $\mathfrak{u}_n = \{A : A + A^\dagger = 0\}$; and $\mathfrak{sp}_{2n} = \{A : A^T J + JA = 0\}$ where $J$ is the standard symplectic matrix. Each computation involves differentiating the defining equation of the group at the identity: for example, differentiating $\det(e^{tA}) = e^{t\,\text{tr}(A)} = 1$ at $t=0$ gives $\text{tr}(A) = 0$ as the condition for $A \in \mathfrak{sl}_n$. These Lie algebras, together with the five exceptional ones discovered by Killing and Cartan, are all the simple Lie algebras over $\mathbb{C}$.
