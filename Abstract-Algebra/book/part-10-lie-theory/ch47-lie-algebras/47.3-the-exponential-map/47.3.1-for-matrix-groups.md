# 47.3.1 $\exp: \mathfrak{g} \to G$ for Matrix Groups:$\exp(X) = e^X = \sum X^n/n!$

The exponential map is the bridge between the Lie algebra and the Lie group — it converts infinitesimal data (elements of $\mathfrak{g}$) into global data (elements of$G$). For matrix Lie groups, this map is simply the matrix exponential, defined by the power series$\exp(X) = e^X = \sum_{n=0}^\infty X^n/n!$.

The series converges absolutely for any $X \in M_n$ (since$\|X^n/n!\| \leq \|X\|^n/n!$ and$\sum \|X\|^n/n! = e^{\|X\|} < \infty$), so$e^X$ is a well-defined matrix for every$X$. Several foundational properties flow directly from the definition. First,$e^0 = I$, so the exponential sends the origin of$\mathfrak{g}$ to the identity of$G$. Second, differentiating the curve$t \mapsto e^{tX}$ at$t = 0$ gives velocity$X$, confirming that$\frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0} e^{tX} = X$; this is consistent with$X$ being a tangent vector to$G$ at$I$. Third,$\det(e^X) = e^{\mathrm{tr}(X)}$, a formula that follows from the Jordan normal form of$X$ and explains why the trace-zero condition characterizes$\mathfrak{sl}_n$:$e^X \in SL_n$ iff$\det(e^X) = 1$ iff$\mathrm{tr}(X) = 0$.

The exponential also satisfies $e^X e^{-X} = I$, confirming that$e^X$ is always invertible and therefore lies in$GL_n$. For matrices that commute ($XY = YX$), one has$e^{X+Y} = e^X e^Y$, but this identity fails in general — the failure is measured by the commutator$[X, Y]$, and quantifying this failure precisely is the content of the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula (Section 47.3.3). The intuition is that commutativity in the algebra corresponds to commutativity in the group, and the bracket measures both deviations simultaneously.

For the group $SO(n)$: if$X \in \mathfrak{so}(n)$ (so$X^T = -X$), then$(e^X)^T = e^{X^T} = e^{-X} = (e^X)^{-1}$, confirming$e^X \in O(n)$. Since$\det(e^X) = e^{\mathrm{tr}(X)} = e^0 = 1$ (skew-symmetric matrices have trace zero), we get$e^X \in SO(n)$. Similarly, for$\mathfrak{u}(n)$: if$X^* = -X$, then$(e^X)^* = e^{X^*} = e^{-X} = (e^X)^{-1}$, so$e^X \in U(n)$.

**Example 1.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$ with$X = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & t \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ (a nilpotent matrix,$X^2 = 0$):$e^X = I + X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & t \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. This is a unipotent upper triangular matrix in$SL_2$, corresponding to a one-parameter subgroup of unipotent elements. The exponential terminates after finitely many terms for nilpotent matrices.

**Example 2.** For $\mathfrak{so}(2) = \{t\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} : t \in \mathbb{R}\}$: letting$J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, one computes$J^2 = -I$,$J^3 = -J$,$J^4 = I$, and so on. The series gives$e^{tJ} = \cos(t) I + \sin(t) J = \begin{pmatrix} \cos t & -\sin t \\ \sin t & \cos t \end{pmatrix} \in SO(2)$. The Lie algebra$\mathfrak{so}(2)$ thus exponentiates to the group of planar rotations, with the Lie algebra element$J$ generating a full$2\pi$-periodic orbit.

**Example 3.** For $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ with$X = i\theta \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$:$X^2 = -\theta^2 I$, so$e^X = \cos(\theta) I + i\sin(\theta)\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} e^{i\theta} & 0 \\ 0 & e^{-i\theta} \end{pmatrix}$. This is a diagonal unitary matrix of determinant 1.

**Example 4.** For a semisimple element $H = \mathrm{diag}(h_1, \ldots, h_n)$ in$\mathfrak{gl}_n$:$e^H = \mathrm{diag}(e^{h_1}, \ldots, e^{h_n})$. The exponential simply exponentiates the diagonal entries. This illustrates the general principle: the exponential map is easy to compute explicitly for semisimple elements (those that are diagonalizable), and the general case reduces to the semisimple and nilpotent parts via the Jordan decomposition and the formula$e^{S+N} = e^S e^N$ (valid when$SN = NS$, which holds for the Jordan decomposition).

The matrix exponential is not just a computational device: it is the canonical way to convert the infinitesimal, linear data of the Lie algebra into the global, multiplicative data of the Lie group. Understanding the exponential map is the key to understanding the relationship between the group and its Lie algebra.
