# 47.3.2 $\exp$ Is a Local Diffeomorphism; Surjectivity for Compact$G$

The exponential map is not merely a map from $\mathfrak{g}$ to$G$; near the origin it is an invertible smooth map — a local diffeomorphism. The derivative of$\exp$ at$0 \in \mathfrak{g}$ is the identity map (since$\frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0} e^{tX} = X$ for every$X$), so the inverse function theorem guarantees that$\exp$ is a diffeomorphism from some open neighborhood of$0$ in$\mathfrak{g}$ onto an open neighborhood of$e$ in$G$. This means that the exponential map gives a genuine coordinate chart near the identity — every element sufficiently close to$e$ is uniquely a matrix exponential, and the logarithm$\log = \exp^{-1}$ is well defined near the identity.

The local diffeomorphism property has a striking consequence: it shows that the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ determines$G$ completely in a neighborhood of the identity. Two Lie groups with the same Lie algebra are locally isomorphic — they agree near the identity, though they may differ globally. This local equivalence is the foundation of the dictionary between Lie groups and Lie algebras: at the local (infinitesimal) level, the two structures are interchangeable. The failure of global equivalence is measured by topology, specifically by the fundamental group$\pi_1(G)$.

The matrix logarithm $\log: U \to \mathfrak{g}$ is defined on a neighborhood$U$ of$I$ in$GL_n$ by the power series$\log(I + A) = A - \frac{A^2}{2} + \frac{A^3}{3} - \cdots$, which converges for$\|A\| < 1$. For$g$ near the identity,$g = I + A$ with$A$ small, this gives$\log(g) \in \mathfrak{g}$. The composite$\log \circ \exp = \mathrm{id}$ near$0$, confirming the local inverse relationship.

Globally, however, the behavior of $\exp$ depends on the group. For compact connected Lie groups, the exponential map is surjective: every element$g \in G$ can be written as$g = e^X$ for some$X \in \mathfrak{g}$. The proof uses the fact that on a compact group, every element lies in some maximal torus, and on a torus the exponential is surjective. Concretely, every unitary matrix$U \in U(n)$ can be diagonalized (by unitary change of basis) to$\mathrm{diag}(e^{i\theta_1}, \ldots, e^{i\theta_n})$, which is the exponential of$\mathrm{diag}(i\theta_1, \ldots, i\theta_n) \in \mathfrak{u}(n)$.

Surjectivity fails strikingly for non-compact groups. In $SL_2(\mathbb{R})$, the matrix$g = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ is not in the image of the exponential — it has no real logarithm in$\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{R})$. One way to see this: if$g = e^X$ with$X \in \mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{R})$, then$g$ must be connected to the identity by a smooth path in$SL_2(\mathbb{R})$ via$t \mapsto e^{tX}$. But$g$ has eigenvalues$-1$ (a repeated eigenvalue with nontrivial Jordan form), and tracing through the path$e^{tX}$ shows that the eigenvalues along the path are$e^{\pm t\lambda}$ for some$\lambda$; for these to reach$-1$ simultaneously one would need$\lambda$ to be a purely imaginary number, which contradicts$X \in \mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{R})$.

**Example 1.** $G = SU(2)$ is compact. Every$A \in SU(2)$ has the form$\begin{pmatrix} a & -\bar{b} \\ b & \bar{a} \end{pmatrix}$ with$|a|^2 + |b|^2 = 1$. Writing$a = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta \cos\phi$ and diagonalizing, one can always write$A = e^X$ for some$X \in \mathfrak{su}(2)$. The exponential map from$\mathfrak{su}(2) \cong \mathbb{R}^3$ to$SU(2) \cong S^3$ is not injective (elements differing by$4\pi$ give the same group element), but it is surjective.

**Example 2.** $G = GL_n(\mathbb{R})$ is non-compact and the exponential is not surjective. For example,$e^X = \mathrm{diag}(-1, -1)$ requires the eigenvalues of$X$ to be$i\pi$ (up to$2\pi i$ multiples), which are not real. Since$X$ must be a real matrix, a real matrix exponential always has positive determinant (as$\det(e^X) = e^{\mathrm{tr}(X)} > 0$), so matrices in the component of$GL_n(\mathbb{R})$ with negative determinant cannot be exponentials of real matrices.

**Example 3.** For a compact connected simple Lie group such as $SO(3)$, the exponential map is surjective. Every rotation in$\mathbb{R}^3$ can be written as$e^X$ for some skew-symmetric matrix$X = \theta\hat{n}$ (axis-angle representation), where$\hat{n}$ is the unit axis vector and$\theta$ is the rotation angle. Rodriguez's formula gives$e^X = I + \sin\theta \hat{n} + (1 - \cos\theta)\hat{n}^2$ (writing$\hat{n}$ as a skew-symmetric matrix).

The interplay between compactness and the global surjectivity of $\exp$ is one of the central themes connecting topology and algebra in Lie theory. Compact groups have the nicest representation theory (all representations are completely reducible, the Haar measure allows averaging, and the exponential is surjective), while non-compact groups present greater complexity but also greater richness.
