# 47.3.3 The Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff Formula

When $X$ and$Y$ are commuting matrices,$e^X e^Y = e^{X+Y}$. But in general,$e^X e^Y$ and$e^{X+Y}$ are different: the product in the group does not reduce to addition in the Lie algebra. The Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff (BCH) formula quantifies exactly this discrepancy by expressing$e^X e^Y$ as$e^Z$ for an explicitly computed$Z$ in terms of$X$,$Y$, and their repeated brackets.

For matrices $X, Y$ with$\|X\| + \|Y\|$ sufficiently small that all series converge, one has$e^X e^Y = e^Z$ where
$$Z = X + Y + \frac{1}{2}[X,Y] + \frac{1}{12}\bigl([X,[X,Y]] - [Y,[X,Y]]\bigr) + \cdots$$
with all higher terms consisting of nested Lie brackets of $X$ and$Y$. The remarkable fact is that$Z$ lies entirely in the Lie algebra$\mathfrak{g}$ (since it is built from$X$ and$Y$ using only the bracket, and$\mathfrak{g}$ is closed under brackets), and it is expressed purely in terms of the Lie algebra structure — no reference to the group multiplication is needed beyond what the bracket encodes.

The proof proceeds by differentiating the identity $e^{Z(t)} = e^{tX} e^{tY}$ with respect to$t$ and extracting the coefficient of each power of$t$, using the formula$\frac{d}{dt} e^{Z(t)} = e^{Z(t)} \cdot \frac{1 - e^{-\mathrm{ad}(Z)}}{\mathrm{ad}(Z)} \dot{Z}(t)$. The resulting differential equation for$Z(t)$ can be solved order by order in$t$, and each order contributes a term built entirely from iterated brackets of$X$ and$Y$. The general coefficient is given by the Dynkin formula, involving Bernoulli numbers and nested commutators.

The key insight of BCH is that the local group structure of $G$ near the identity is completely and explicitly determined by the Lie algebra$\mathfrak{g}$. To multiply nearby group elements, one converts them to Lie algebra elements via$\log$, applies the BCH formula to compute the Lie algebra element corresponding to the product, and maps back via$\exp$. Since the BCH formula involves only the bracket, two Lie groups with isomorphic Lie algebras are locally isomorphic as groups — they multiply elements in a neighborhood of the identity in exactly the same way. This is the precise sense in which "the Lie algebra determines the Lie group locally," and it is what makes the passage from global group theory to linear algebra both exact and computable.

**Example 1.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$ with$X = te$ and$Y = sf$ (small$t, s \in \mathbb{C}$):$[X, Y] = ts[e, f] = tsh$,$[X, [X, Y]] = t^2s[e, h] = -2t^2se$,$[Y, [X, Y]] = ts^2[f, h] = 2ts^2f$. The BCH formula to third order gives$Z = te + sf + \frac{1}{2}tsh + \frac{1}{12}(-2t^2se - 2ts^2f) + \cdots$, which lies in$\mathfrak{sl}_2$ as expected.

**Example 2.** In any Lie algebra where $[X, Y]$ commutes with both$X$ and$Y$ (i.e.,$\mathrm{ad}(X)^2(Y) = \mathrm{ad}(Y)^2(X) = 0$), the BCH formula terminates:$Z = X + Y + \frac{1}{2}[X, Y]$. This is the Heisenberg case: in the Heisenberg algebra$\mathfrak{h}$ with$[X, Y] = Z$ central, we get$e^X e^Y = e^{X + Y + \frac{1}{2}[X,Y]}$ exactly. This is the Heisenberg group multiplication law.

**Example 3.** The BCH formula has a striking application to computing the Lie bracket from the group. For any Lie group $G$ and any$X, Y \in \mathfrak{g}$:
$$[X, Y] = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{1}{t^2} \log\bigl(e^{tX} e^{tY} e^{-tX} e^{-tY}\bigr).$$
This shows that the bracket is the "leading non-commutativity" of the group at second order in $t$. For$SL_2(\mathbb{R})$ with$X = h$ and$Y = e$:$e^{tX} e^{tY} e^{-tX} e^{-tY} = I + t^2[X, Y] + O(t^3) = I + t^2 \cdot 2e + O(t^3)$, consistent with$[h, e] = 2e$.

**Example 4.** For the classical Lie algebras, the BCH formula shows that the Campbell–Hausdorff product $Z = X \star Y$ defines a local group operation on a neighborhood of$0$ in$\mathfrak{g}$. For$\mathfrak{gl}_n$, this local group operation matches matrix multiplication near the identity:$e^X \cdot e^Y = e^{X \star Y}$ for small$X, Y$. The local group structure is entirely encoded in$\mathfrak{g}$ and its bracket, confirming the fundamental principle that the Lie algebra is the infinitesimal description of the Lie group.

The BCH formula is both a theoretical tool (proving that all simply connected Lie groups with isomorphic Lie algebras are isomorphic) and a practical one (computing explicitly how group elements near the identity multiply). Its existence also implies that any finite-dimensional Lie algebra over $\mathbb{R}$ or$\mathbb{C}$ is the Lie algebra of some Lie group, which is one direction of Lie's third theorem.
