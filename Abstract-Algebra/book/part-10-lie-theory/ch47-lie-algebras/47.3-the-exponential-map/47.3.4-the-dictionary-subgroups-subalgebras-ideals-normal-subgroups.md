# 47.3.4 The Dictionary: Subgroups $\leftrightarrow$ Subalgebras, Ideals$\leftrightarrow$ Normal Subgroups

One of the most useful theorems in Lie theory is the precise correspondence, mediated by the exponential map, between the subgroup structure of $G$ and the subalgebra structure of$\mathfrak{g}$. This "dictionary" reduces questions about Lie groups — which can be geometrically and topologically complex — to questions about vector subspaces satisfying a single algebraic closure condition.

For a connected Lie group $G$ with Lie algebra$\mathfrak{g}$, the correspondence is as follows: connected Lie subgroups of$G$ correspond bijectively to Lie subalgebras of$\mathfrak{g}$, and connected normal Lie subgroups of$G$ correspond bijectively to ideals of$\mathfrak{g}$. The dictionary is implemented by the exponential map in both directions: given a Lie subgroup$H \leq G$, its Lie algebra is$\mathfrak{h} = T_e H \subseteq \mathfrak{g}$; conversely, given a Lie subalgebra$\mathfrak{h} \subseteq \mathfrak{g}$, the corresponding connected Lie subgroup is generated by$\{\exp(X) : X \in \mathfrak{h}\}$.

The deeper statement is Lie's theorem (the Lie correspondence): for a simply connected Lie group $G$, the assignment$H \mapsto \mathrm{Lie}(H)$ is a bijection from connected closed subgroups of$G$ to Lie subalgebras of$\mathfrak{g}$, and this bijection is order-reversing in neither direction. In the simply connected case, simply connected Lie groups are in one-to-one correspondence with Lie algebras, and the correspondence respects all the structural notions: quotients correspond to quotient algebras, the intersection of subgroups corresponds to the intersection of subalgebras, and the commutator subgroup$[G, G]$ corresponds to the derived algebra$[\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}]$.

The ideal condition for subgroups deserves special explanation. The normality condition $gHg^{-1} = H$ for all$g \in G$ is equivalent, infinitesimally, to$\mathrm{Ad}(g)(\mathfrak{h}) \subseteq \mathfrak{h}$ for all$g \in G$, which upon differentiating in$g$ at the identity gives$[\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{h}] \subseteq \mathfrak{h}$ — exactly the ideal condition. Thus normality of a connected subgroup is precisely the ideal condition on the Lie algebra, and the quotient group$G/H$ has Lie algebra$\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}$.

**Example 1.** In $G = GL_n(\mathbb{R})$, the subgroup$SL_n(\mathbb{R}) = \ker(\det)$ is a normal subgroup. The corresponding Lie algebra ideal is$\mathfrak{sl}_n = \ker(\mathrm{tr}) \trianglelefteq \mathfrak{gl}_n$. The quotient$GL_n/SL_n \cong \mathbb{R}^{\times}$ has Lie algebra$\mathfrak{gl}_n/\mathfrak{sl}_n \cong \mathbb{R}$. The dictionary works perfectly at every level.

**Example 2.** In $G = SL_2(\mathbb{C})$, the upper triangular subgroup$B = \left\{\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ 0 & a^{-1} \end{pmatrix}\right\}$ has Lie algebra$\mathfrak{b} = \mathrm{span}\{h, e\}$ (the Borel subalgebra). This is a subalgebra but not an ideal in$\mathfrak{sl}_2$:$[f, h] = 2f \notin \mathfrak{b}$. Correspondingly,$B$ is not normal in$SL_2$.

**Example 3.** In $G = SO(3)$, the only nontrivial normal subgroup is$\{I, -I\} \cap SO(3) = \{I\}$ — since$SO(3)$ is simple as a Lie group ($SO(3) \cong SU(2)/\{\pm I\}$). Correspondingly,$\mathfrak{so}(3)$ has no proper nonzero ideals. The dictionary predicts this: simple Lie algebra$\leftrightarrow$ no proper connected normal subgroups.

**Example 4.** The center $Z(G)$ of a connected Lie group$G$ corresponds to the center$Z(\mathfrak{g})$ of its Lie algebra, in the sense that$\mathrm{Lie}(Z(G)) = Z(\mathfrak{g})$. For$G = SU(2)$, the center is$\{\pm I\}$, a discrete subgroup with Lie algebra$\{0\} = Z(\mathfrak{su}(2))$ (since$\mathfrak{su}(2) \cong \mathfrak{sl}_2$ has trivial center). The discreteness of the center is an example of how the dictionary handles the passage from$G$ to$G/Z(G) \cong SO(3)$: both groups have the same Lie algebra$\mathfrak{so}(3) \cong \mathfrak{su}(2)$, but different fundamental groups ($\pi_1(SU(2)) = 0$,$\pi_1(SO(3)) = \mathbb{Z}/2$).

For non-simply-connected groups, the correspondence still holds for the Lie algebra structures, but one must account for the covering ambiguity. The dictionary is the central organizational principle of Lie theory, and it is what makes it possible to classify Lie groups by classifying Lie algebras: once the algebra is understood, the group is determined up to a finite covering ambiguity measured by $\pi_1$.
