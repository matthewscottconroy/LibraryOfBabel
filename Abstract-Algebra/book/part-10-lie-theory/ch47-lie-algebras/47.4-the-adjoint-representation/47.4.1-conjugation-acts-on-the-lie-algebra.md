# 47.4.1 $\mathrm{Ad}: G \to GL(\mathfrak{g})$: Conjugation Acts on the Lie Algebra

Every group acts on itself by conjugation: $g$ sends$h$ to$ghg^{-1}$. For a Lie group, this conjugation action at the group level differentiates to give an action of$G$ on its Lie algebra$\mathfrak{g}$. This is the *adjoint representation*$\mathrm{Ad}: G \to GL(\mathfrak{g})$, and it is the most natural representation a Lie group possesses — it requires no external choice and arises directly from the group structure.

Formally, for each $g \in G$, the conjugation map$\mathrm{conj}_g: G \to G$,$h \mapsto ghg^{-1}$, is a smooth group automorphism fixing$e$. Its differential at the identity,$d(\mathrm{conj}_g)_e: T_e G \to T_e G$, is therefore a linear automorphism of$\mathfrak{g}$. This defines$\mathrm{Ad}(g) \in GL(\mathfrak{g})$, and the assignment$g \mapsto \mathrm{Ad}(g)$ is a group homomorphism (and in fact a smooth map), hence a representation of$G$ on$\mathfrak{g}$.

For matrix Lie groups, the adjoint action takes the explicit form $\mathrm{Ad}(g)(X) = gXg^{-1}$: the group$G$ acts on its Lie algebra$\mathfrak{g} \subseteq M_n$ simply by matrix conjugation. One can verify this is well defined: if$X \in \mathfrak{g}$ (meaning$e^{tX} \in G$ for all$t$) and$g \in G$, then$e^{t\,gXg^{-1}} = g e^{tX} g^{-1} \in G$, confirming that$gXg^{-1} \in \mathfrak{g}$.

The adjoint representation thus expresses how the Lie group acts on its own infinitesimal structure, measuring how the shape of the "infinitesimal neighborhood" of the identity is twisted by the global group elements. Its kernel consists of those $g$ for which$gXg^{-1} = X$ for all$X \in \mathfrak{g}$; for connected groups, this is precisely the center$Z(G)$.

**Example 1.** For $G = GL_n(\mathbb{C})$ and$g \in G$,$X \in \mathfrak{gl}_n$:$\mathrm{Ad}(g)(X) = gXg^{-1}$. This is the usual similarity transformation on matrices. Two matrices are in the same$\mathrm{Ad}(G)$-orbit iff they have the same Jordan form (over$\mathbb{C}$). The invariants of this action (functions$f$ with$f(gXg^{-1}) = f(X)$) are exactly the conjugation-invariant functions — the symmetric polynomials in the eigenvalues of$X$.

**Example 2.** For $G = SU(2)$ and$g \in SU(2)$,$X \in \mathfrak{su}(2) \cong \mathbb{R}^3$:$\mathrm{Ad}(g)$ is a rotation of$\mathbb{R}^3$. The map$\mathrm{Ad}: SU(2) \to GL(\mathfrak{su}(2)) \cong GL_3(\mathbb{R})$ has image in$SO(3)$ and kernel$\{\pm I\}$, giving the classical double cover$SU(2) \to SO(3)$. This is one of the most important examples: the adjoint representation geometrically exhibits the spin-1 (vector) representation of the rotation group.

**Example 3.** For $G = SO(n)$: every$g \in SO(n)$ acts on$\mathfrak{so}(n)$ (skew-symmetric matrices) by$X \mapsto gXg^T = gXg^{-1}$. This is again a similarity transformation that preserves the skew-symmetry: if$X^T = -X$, then$(gXg^{-1})^T = (g^{-1})^T X^T g^T = gX^T g^{-1} = -gXg^{-1}$. The adjoint representation of$SO(n)$ on$\mathfrak{so}(n)$ is an irreducible representation for$n \geq 3$, corresponding to the "antisymmetric tensor" representation.

**Example 4.** Computing $\mathrm{Ad}$ for$\mathfrak{sl}_2$: take$g = e^{tH} = \begin{pmatrix} e^t & 0 \\ 0 & e^{-t} \end{pmatrix} \in SL_2$ and$X = e = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Then$\mathrm{Ad}(g)(X) = gXg^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} e^t & 0 \\ 0 & e^{-t} \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} e^{-t} & 0 \\ 0 & e^{t} \end{pmatrix} = e^{2t} e$. So diagonal elements act on the positive root vector by scaling — precisely the eigenvalue relation$[H, e] = 2e$ at the Lie algebra level. This shows that$\mathrm{Ad}$ and$\mathrm{ad}$ are related by the exponential:$\mathrm{Ad}(e^{tH}) = e^{t \cdot \mathrm{ad}(H)}$.

The relationship $\mathrm{Ad}(e^X) = e^{\mathrm{ad}(X)}$ (valid for matrix Lie groups) is the formal statement that differentiating$\mathrm{Ad}$ at the identity gives$\mathrm{ad}$, and it is one of the most used formulas in concrete calculations with Lie groups and algebras.
