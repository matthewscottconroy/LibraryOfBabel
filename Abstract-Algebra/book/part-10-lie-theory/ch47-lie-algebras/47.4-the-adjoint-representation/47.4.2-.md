# 47.4.2 $\mathrm{ad}: \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g})$:$\mathrm{ad}(X)(Y) = [X,Y]$

Having defined the adjoint representation $\mathrm{Ad}: G \to GL(\mathfrak{g})$ of the Lie group, one can differentiate it at the identity to obtain a representation of the Lie algebra on itself. The resulting map$\mathrm{ad}: \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g})$ is the *adjoint representation of the Lie algebra*, defined by$\mathrm{ad}(X)(Y) = [X, Y]$.

To see how this emerges, differentiate the family of maps $\mathrm{Ad}(e^{tX})(Y) = e^{tX} Y e^{-tX}$ with respect to$t$ at$t = 0$. On the left, one gets$d(\mathrm{Ad})_e(X)(Y)$, which is by definition$\mathrm{ad}(X)(Y)$. On the right, the product rule gives$XY - YX = [X, Y]$. So$\mathrm{ad}(X)$ is the map$Y \mapsto [X, Y]$, confirming that the adjoint action of the Lie algebra on itself is precisely the bracket.

This identification has concrete importance. Rather than thinking of the Lie bracket $[X, Y]$ abstractly as a bilinear operation, one can think of$\mathrm{ad}(X)$ as a specific linear endomorphism of$\mathfrak{g}$ — an element of$\mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g})$. This allows one to apply linear algebra directly to the bracket structure. The eigenvalues of$\mathrm{ad}(H)$ for a semisimple element$H$ (i.e., one with diagonalizable$\mathrm{ad}(H)$) are the roots of the root space decomposition. The adjoint representation$\mathrm{ad}$ is also the canonical embedding of$\mathfrak{g}$ into its own endomorphism algebra, and for semisimple Lie algebras it is faithful — meaning$\mathfrak{g}$ embeds into$\mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g})$ via$\mathrm{ad}$ with trivial kernel. This is a consequence of Ado's theorem in the semisimple case.

The formula $\mathrm{Ad}(e^X) = e^{\mathrm{ad}(X)}$ encodes the relationship between the group and algebra levels: the group element$e^X$ acts on$\mathfrak{g}$ by the linear map$e^{\mathrm{ad}(X)} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{(\mathrm{ad}(X))^n}{n!}$. For example,$\mathrm{Ad}(e^X)(Y) = Y + [X, Y] + \frac{1}{2}[X,[X,Y]] + \cdots$, which is the beginning of the expansion of$e^X Y e^{-X}$ by iterated commutators — a formula used constantly in physics (as the "Hadamard lemma") and in representation theory.

**Example 1.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C}) = \mathrm{span}\{e, f, h\}$:$\mathrm{ad}(h)(e) = [h,e] = 2e$,$\mathrm{ad}(h)(f) = [h,f] = -2f$,$\mathrm{ad}(h)(h) = [h,h] = 0$. In the basis$\{e, h, f\}$, the matrix of$\mathrm{ad}(h)$ is$\mathrm{diag}(2, 0, -2)$. The eigenvalues$2, 0, -2$ are the weights of the adjoint representation — in the language of root theory,$2$ and$-2$ are the roots of$\mathfrak{sl}_2$, and$0$ is the weight of the Cartan element$h$ itself.

**Example 2.** $\mathrm{ad}(e)(e) = [e,e] = 0$,$\mathrm{ad}(e)(f) = [e,f] = h$,$\mathrm{ad}(e)(h) = [e,h] = -2e$. The matrix of$\mathrm{ad}(e)$ in the basis$\{e,h,f\}$ is$\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. This is a nilpotent matrix:$(\mathrm{ad}(e))^3 = 0$. The nilpotency of$\mathrm{ad}(e)$ reflects the fact that$e$ is a nilpotent element of$\mathfrak{sl}_2$ (as a matrix:$e^2 = 0$), and in semisimple Lie algebras, nilpotency of the element and nilpotency of the adjoint action are closely related (though the connection is subtle in general).

**Example 3.** For $\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbb{C})$ and the diagonal element$H = \mathrm{diag}(h_1, \ldots, h_n)$ in the Cartan subalgebra:$\mathrm{ad}(H)(E_{ij}) = [H, E_{ij}] = (h_i - h_j) E_{ij}$. So each matrix unit$E_{ij}$ is an eigenvector of$\mathrm{ad}(H)$ with eigenvalue$h_i - h_j$. The eigenvalue functional$\alpha_{ij}(H) = h_i - h_j$ (for$i \neq j$) is a root, and the matrix unit$E_{ij}$ spans the corresponding root space. This explicit computation shows that the root space decomposition of$\mathfrak{sl}_n$ is obtained by simultaneously diagonalizing all operators$\mathrm{ad}(H)$ for$H$ in the Cartan subalgebra.

**Example 4.** The map $\mathrm{ad}: \mathfrak{sl}_2 \to \mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{sl}_2) \cong \mathfrak{gl}_3$ is a 3-dimensional representation. One can check it is injective: if$\mathrm{ad}(X) = 0$ then$[X, Y] = 0$ for all$Y$, so$X \in Z(\mathfrak{sl}_2) = 0$. The image is the subalgebra of$\mathfrak{gl}_3$ spanned by$\mathrm{ad}(e), \mathrm{ad}(f), \mathrm{ad}(h)$, isomorphic to$\mathfrak{sl}_2$. The adjoint representation of a simple Lie algebra is thus always a faithful irreducible representation — it is the "spin-1" representation in the case of$\mathfrak{sl}_2$.

The adjoint representation is both a fundamental example and a fundamental tool. Understanding $\mathrm{ad}$ for$\mathfrak{sl}_2$ is essentially all of$\mathfrak{sl}_2$ representation theory, and the same ideas — applied via$\mathfrak{sl}_2$-triples — control the representation theory of any semisimple Lie algebra.
