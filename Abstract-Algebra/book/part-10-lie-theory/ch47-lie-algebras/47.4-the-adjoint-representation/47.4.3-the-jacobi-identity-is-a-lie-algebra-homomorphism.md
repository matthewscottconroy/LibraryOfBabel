# 47.4.3 The Jacobi Identity = $\mathrm{ad}$ Is a Lie Algebra Homomorphism

The Jacobi identity, which appears in the axioms of a Lie algebra as a somewhat mysterious "twisted associativity," has a transparent interpretation in terms of the adjoint representation: it is precisely the condition that $\mathrm{ad}: \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g})$ is a Lie algebra homomorphism.

To see this, unpack what it means for $\mathrm{ad}$ to be a Lie algebra homomorphism. A Lie algebra homomorphism must satisfy$\mathrm{ad}([X, Y]) = [\mathrm{ad}(X), \mathrm{ad}(Y)]$ in$\mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g})$, where the bracket in$\mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g})$ is the commutator of linear maps. Evaluating both sides on an element$Z \in \mathfrak{g}$: the left side gives$\mathrm{ad}([X,Y])(Z) = [[X,Y], Z]$; the right side gives$(\mathrm{ad}(X)\mathrm{ad}(Y) - \mathrm{ad}(Y)\mathrm{ad}(X))(Z) = [X,[Y,Z]] - [Y,[X,Z]]$. Setting these equal yields$[[X,Y],Z] = [X,[Y,Z]] - [Y,[X,Z]]$, which, after rearranging, is exactly the Jacobi identity$[X,[Y,Z]] + [Y,[Z,X]] + [Z,[X,Y]] = 0$.

There is a second, equivalent way to phrase this. The Jacobi identity $[X,[Y,Z]] = [[X,Y],Z] + [Y,[X,Z]]$ says that$\mathrm{ad}(X)$ is a *derivation* of the Lie bracket: it acts on$[Y, Z]$ via the Leibniz rule, differentiating each factor in turn. An operator satisfying the Leibniz rule for a product is always called a derivation, and the space of derivations of any algebra forms a Lie algebra under commutator. The Jacobi identity therefore says that the entire Lie algebra$\mathfrak{g}$ acts on itself by derivations via the adjoint action — the bracket "derives" itself. This self-referential structure is the algebraic heart of why Lie algebras are so well-behaved.

The derivation perspective gives a third interpretation. A derivation of $\mathfrak{g}$ is a linear map$D: \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g}$ satisfying$D([X, Y]) = [D(X), Y] + [X, D(Y)]$ (the Leibniz rule). The set$\mathrm{Der}(\mathfrak{g})$ of all derivations forms a Lie subalgebra of$\mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g})$. The Jacobi identity says exactly that$\mathrm{ad}(X) \in \mathrm{Der}(\mathfrak{g})$ for every$X \in \mathfrak{g}$. The derivations in the image of$\mathrm{ad}$ are called *inner derivations*, and the quotient$\mathrm{Der}(\mathfrak{g})/\mathrm{Inn}(\mathfrak{g})$ is the space of *outer derivations*, which is isomorphic to the first cohomology group$H^1(\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g})$. For semisimple Lie algebras, all derivations are inner — a consequence of the vanishing of$H^1(\mathfrak{g}, M)$ for any semisimple-module$M$ (Weyl's theorem).

**Example 1.** Verify that $\mathrm{ad}(h)$ is a derivation of$\mathfrak{sl}_2$: we need$\mathrm{ad}(h)([e, f]) = [\mathrm{ad}(h)(e), f] + [e, \mathrm{ad}(h)(f)]$. Left side:$\mathrm{ad}(h)(h) = [h,h] = 0$. Right side:$[2e, f] + [e, -2f] = 2h - 2h = 0$. Confirmed.

**Example 2.** Verify that $\mathrm{ad}(e)$ is a derivation:$\mathrm{ad}(e)([h, f]) = [\mathrm{ad}(e)(h), f] + [h, \mathrm{ad}(e)(f)]$. Left:$\mathrm{ad}(e)(-2f) = -2[e,f] = -2h$. Right:$[-2e, f] + [h, h] = -2h + 0 = -2h$. Confirmed. The Jacobi identity for$e, h, f$ in the order$(e, h, f)$ is equivalent to$\mathrm{ad}(e)$ being a derivation.

**Example 3.** For any Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$, the map$\mathrm{ad}: \mathfrak{g} \to \mathrm{Der}(\mathfrak{g})$ is a Lie algebra homomorphism. The kernel is$Z(\mathfrak{g})$ (the center), and the image is the Lie subalgebra$\mathrm{Inn}(\mathfrak{g})$ of inner derivations. For$\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$: the center is zero, so$\mathrm{ad}$ is an isomorphism$\mathfrak{sl}_2 \cong \mathrm{Inn}(\mathfrak{sl}_2) = \mathrm{Der}(\mathfrak{sl}_2)$ (since$\mathfrak{sl}_2$ is simple, all its derivations are inner).

**Example 4.** The Heisenberg algebra $\mathfrak{h} = \mathrm{span}\{p, q, z\}$ with$[p,q] = z$,$[p,z] = [q,z] = 0$ has$\mathrm{ad}(z) = 0$ (since$z$ is central). The outer derivation$D$ with$D(p) = q$,$D(q) = D(z) = 0$ is not inner (no element of$\mathfrak{h}$ has the same adjoint action). So$H^1(\mathfrak{h}, \mathfrak{h}) \neq 0$, confirming that the Heisenberg algebra is not semisimple (it is nilpotent).

The interpretation of the Jacobi identity as the homomorphism property of $\mathrm{ad}$ reveals it not as a mysterious axiom but as a consistency condition: the Lie algebra acts on itself in a way compatible with its own structure. This is the infinitesimal shadow of the group acting on itself by conjugation, and it is the algebraic formulation of the self-consistency of symmetry.
