# 47.4 The Adjoint Representation

Every Lie algebra carries a canonical representation on itself: the adjoint representation. It arises from the group-theoretic operation of conjugation. For a Lie group $G$ and a fixed $g \in G$, the conjugation map $C_g: G \to G$ defined by $C_g(h) = ghg^{-1}$ is an automorphism of $G$ fixing the identity. Its differential at the identity is a linear automorphism $\text{Ad}(g): \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g}$, and the resulting map $\text{Ad}: G \to GL(\mathfrak{g})$ is a Lie group homomorphism called the adjoint representation of $G$. Differentiating once more — differentiating $\text{Ad}$ at the identity of $G$ — gives the adjoint representation of the Lie algebra: $\text{ad}: \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g})$, $\text{ad}_x(y) = [x,y]$.

The Jacobi identity, from this perspective, has a beautiful reinterpretation: it says precisely that $\text{ad}$ is a Lie algebra homomorphism, i.e., $\text{ad}_{[x,y]} = [\text{ad}_x, \text{ad}_y]$ as linear maps on $\mathfrak{g}$. Thus the Jacobi identity is not merely an abstract axiom but the statement that the adjoint action respects the bracket structure. The kernel of $\text{ad}$ is the center $Z(\mathfrak{g})$; if $Z(\mathfrak{g}) = 0$ (as for all simple Lie algebras), then $\mathfrak{g}$ embeds into $\mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g})$ via $\text{ad}$, giving a concrete matrix realization.

From the adjoint representation one constructs the Killing form: $B(x,y) = \text{tr}(\text{ad}_x \circ \text{ad}_y)$. This is a symmetric bilinear form on $\mathfrak{g}$ that is $\text{Ad}$-invariant — meaning $B(\text{Ad}(g)x, \text{Ad}(g)y) = B(x,y)$ for all $g \in G$ — and it captures deep structural information. Cartan's criterion states that $\mathfrak{g}$ is semisimple if and only if $B$ is non-degenerate, and $\mathfrak{g}$ is solvable if and only if $B$ vanishes identically on the derived algebra $[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]$. For $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$ with basis $\{e,f,h\}$, one computes $B(h,h) = 8$, $B(e,f) = B(f,e) = 4$, and all other pairings zero, giving a non-degenerate form consistent with semisimplicity. The Killing form will be indispensable in Chapter 48, where it is used to classify algebras by their structural type, and in Chapter 49, where it provides the inner product on the dual of the Cartan subalgebra used to define root systems.
