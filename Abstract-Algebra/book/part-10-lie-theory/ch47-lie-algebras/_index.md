# Chapter 47 — Lie Algebras

The central difficulty in studying Lie groups directly is that smooth manifolds are global objects — their topology can be complicated, their group operations are not linear, and there is no obvious way to classify them by the methods of algebra. Lie's great insight was that this global complexity is almost entirely captured by infinitesimal data: the structure of the Lie group near the identity. The tangent space $T_e G$ at the identity of a Lie group $G$ is a vector space, and the non-commutativity of the group operation is encoded, to first order, in a bilinear bracket $[\cdot, \cdot]: \mathfrak{g} \times \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g}$ called the Lie bracket. The resulting algebraic structure — a vector space with an antisymmetric bilinear bracket satisfying the Jacobi identity $[x,[y,z]] + [y,[z,x]] + [z,[x,y]] = 0$ — is a Lie algebra, and it is both tractable and, for simply connected groups, completely determines the Lie group.

For matrix Lie groups, the Lie algebra has a beautifully concrete description. The Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g} \subset \mathfrak{gl}_n$ of a matrix Lie group $G \subset GL_n$ consists of all matrices $X$ such that $e^{tX} \in G$ for all $t \in \mathbb{R}$; equivalently, it is the set of velocity vectors of smooth curves through the identity in $G$. The bracket is simply the matrix commutator $[X, Y] = XY - YX$. Computing the Lie algebras of the classical groups is then a straightforward exercise in differentiation: $\mathfrak{sl}_n = \{X : \text{tr}(X) = 0\}$, $\mathfrak{so}_n = \{X : X^T + X = 0\}$, $\mathfrak{u}_n = \{X : X^\dagger + X = 0\}$, and so forth. In each case, the Lie algebra is a linear subspace of $\mathfrak{gl}_n$ closed under the commutator, and its dimension equals the dimension of the group as a manifold.

The exponential map $\exp: \mathfrak{g} \to G$, defined for matrix groups by the convergent power series $\exp(X) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty X^k / k!$, is a local diffeomorphism near the origin. It carries the flat linear structure of $\mathfrak{g}$ into the curved group $G$, and it is the key tool for translating between the two worlds. The Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula $\exp(X)\exp(Y) = \exp\!\left(X + Y + \frac{1}{2}[X,Y] + \cdots\right)$ makes precise how the non-commutativity of $G$ is encoded in the bracket of $\mathfrak{g}$: the group product, near the identity, is determined entirely by the Lie bracket structure. This formula has profound consequences — it implies, for instance, that the Lie group structure of a simply connected Lie group is completely determined by its Lie algebra.

The adjoint representation is the canonical way a Lie algebra acts on itself. Conjugation $g \mapsto hgh^{-1}$ is an automorphism of $G$, and differentiating it at the identity gives the adjoint action $\text{Ad}: G \to GL(\mathfrak{g})$; differentiating once more gives $\text{ad}: \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g})$ with $\text{ad}_x(y) = [x,y]$. The Jacobi identity, seen from this perspective, says precisely that $\text{ad}$ is a Lie algebra homomorphism. From the adjoint representation one constructs the Killing form $B(x,y) = \text{tr}(\text{ad}_x \circ \text{ad}_y)$, a symmetric bilinear form whose non-degeneracy will be the key criterion for semisimplicity in Chapter 48. The Lie algebra $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$, with its basis $\{e, f, h\}$ satisfying $[h,e] = 2e$, $[h,f] = -2f$, $[e,f] = h$, is the prototype that governs all of representation theory; the reader will encounter it again and again throughout Chapters 49 and 50.
