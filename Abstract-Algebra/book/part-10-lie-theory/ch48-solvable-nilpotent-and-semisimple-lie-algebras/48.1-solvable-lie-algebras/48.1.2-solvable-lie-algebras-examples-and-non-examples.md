# 48.1.2 Solvable Lie Algebras; Examples and Non-Examples

A Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ is *solvable* if its derived series eventually reaches zero:$\mathfrak{g}^{(n)} = 0$ for some$n$. Intuitively, a solvable Lie algebra is one that can be "unwound" by repeatedly passing to commutator subalgebras, arriving at commutativity in finitely many steps. The class of solvable Lie algebras is closed under subalgebras, quotients, and extensions: if$\mathfrak{a}$ is a solvable ideal of$\mathfrak{g}$ and$\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{a}$ is solvable, then$\mathfrak{g}$ is solvable. This extension property makes solvability behave much like solvability for groups.

The principal examples make the definition concrete. Every abelian Lie algebra satisfies $[\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}] = 0$, so$\mathfrak{g}^{(1)} = 0$ immediately — these are solvable of derived length 1. The Borel subalgebra$\mathfrak{b} = \{\text{upper triangular matrices in } \mathfrak{gl}_n\}$ provides the canonical non-abelian example. Its first derived subalgebra$[\mathfrak{b}, \mathfrak{b}]$ consists of strictly upper triangular matrices (zero diagonal), which form a nilpotent algebra; from there the derived series reaches zero in finitely many further steps. This shows$\mathfrak{b}$ is solvable, and it foreshadows Lie's theorem: solvable algebras are, in a precise sense, the Lie algebras that look like upper triangular matrices.

The surprise is how few algebras fail to be solvable once one moves beyond simple ones. The Lie algebra $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$ — spanned by$e, f, h$ with$[h,e] = 2e$,$[h,f] = -2f$,$[e,f] = h$ — is the most basic non-example: one computes$[\mathfrak{sl}_2, \mathfrak{sl}_2] = \mathfrak{sl}_2$ itself, so the derived series is constant and never reaches zero. More generally, any semisimple Lie algebra satisfies$[\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}] = \mathfrak{g}$ and is therefore as far from solvable as possible. The Levi decomposition (Section 48.3.4) makes precise the sense in which solvable algebras and semisimple algebras are the complementary building blocks of all finite-dimensional Lie algebras.

Solvability also has a clean Lie group interpretation. A connected Lie group $G$ has a solvable Lie algebra if and only if its identity component is a *solvable group* in the classical sense — one whose derived series$G \supset [G,G] \supset [[G,G],[G,G]] \supset \cdots$ eventually reaches$\{e\}$. The prototype is the group of invertible upper triangular matrices$B_n$ (the Borel subgroup), which is solvable and whose Lie algebra is precisely$\mathfrak{b}_n$.

**Example 1.** The 2-dimensional non-abelian Lie algebra $\mathfrak{b} = \mathrm{span}\{h, e\}$ with$[h, e] = e$: this is the "ax+b algebra" (Lie algebra of the group of affine transformations$x \mapsto ax + b$). It is solvable:$\mathfrak{b}^{(1)} = \mathrm{span}\{e\}$,$\mathfrak{b}^{(2)} = [\mathrm{span}\{e\}, \mathrm{span}\{e\}] = 0$.

**Example 2.** The Borel subalgebra $\mathfrak{b}_3$ of$\mathfrak{sl}_3$: upper triangular traceless$3 \times 3$ matrices. A basis is$\{h_1, h_2, e_{12}, e_{13}, e_{23}\}$ where$h_1 = \mathrm{diag}(1,-1,0)$,$h_2 = \mathrm{diag}(0,1,-1)$, and$e_{ij}$ are matrix units. Compute:$[\mathfrak{b}_3, \mathfrak{b}_3] = \mathrm{span}\{e_{12}, e_{13}, e_{23}\}$ (the strictly upper triangular matrices). Then$[[\mathfrak{b}_3, \mathfrak{b}_3], [\mathfrak{b}_3, \mathfrak{b}_3]] = \mathrm{span}\{[e_{12}, e_{23}]\} = \mathrm{span}\{e_{13}\}$. Finally$[\mathrm{span}\{e_{13}\}, \mathrm{span}\{e_{13}\}] = 0$. The derived series has length 3.

**Example 3.** $\mathfrak{sl}_3(\mathbb{C})$ is not solvable. One can check$[\mathfrak{sl}_3, \mathfrak{sl}_3] = \mathfrak{sl}_3$ directly: the trace-zero matrices are spanned by the$h_i$ and$e_{ij}$, all of which are brackets of other elements. For instance,$e_{12} = [e_{12}, h_1/2]$... more cleanly,$[e_{12}, e_{23}] = e_{13}$,$[e_{12}, e_{21}] = h_1$, etc. Every basis element is a bracket, so$[\mathfrak{sl}_3, \mathfrak{sl}_3] = \mathfrak{sl}_3$.

**Example 4.** A direct sum of solvable algebras is solvable. A direct sum of a solvable and a semisimple algebra is neither solvable nor semisimple in general. For example, $\mathfrak{gl}_2 \cong \mathfrak{sl}_2 \oplus k$ (center): the center$k$ is abelian (solvable), but$\mathfrak{gl}_2$ is not semisimple (its Killing form is degenerate — the center is in the radical of$B$), and it is not solvable since$[\mathfrak{gl}_2, \mathfrak{gl}_2] = \mathfrak{sl}_2$ which is not solvable.

The classification of solvable Lie algebras is far more complex than the classification of semisimple ones, and no analogue of the Cartan–Killing classification exists in full generality for solvable algebras. However, Lie's theorem and its corollary (Section 48.1.3–48.1.4) provide a complete structural description of how solvable algebras act on complex vector spaces.
