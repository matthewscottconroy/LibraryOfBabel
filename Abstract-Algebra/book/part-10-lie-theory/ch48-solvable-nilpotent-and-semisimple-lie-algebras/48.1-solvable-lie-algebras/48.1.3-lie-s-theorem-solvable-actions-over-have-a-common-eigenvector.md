# 48.1.3 Lie's Theorem: Solvable Actions over $\mathbb{C}$ Have a Common Eigenvector

The key structural insight about solvable Lie algebras is that their representations over $\mathbb{C}$ are, in a strong sense, as simple as possible. Recall that for a single linear operator$T$ on a finite-dimensional complex vector space, one can always find an eigenvector. Lie's theorem extends this to the entire algebra simultaneously: if$\mathfrak{g}$ is solvable and$V$ is a nonzero finite-dimensional$\mathfrak{g}$-module, then there exists a vector$v \in V$ that is a common eigenvector for every operator in the representation, meaning$X \cdot v = \lambda(X) v$ for all$X \in \mathfrak{g}$, where$\lambda : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathbb{C}$ is a linear functional. This is the Lie algebra analogue of the classical fact that any family of commuting diagonalizable operators over$\mathbb{C}$ shares a common eigenvector.

The proof proceeds by induction on $\dim \mathfrak{g}$. The solvability hypothesis guarantees the existence of a codimension-1 ideal$\mathfrak{a} \subseteq \mathfrak{g}$ containing$[\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}]$: since$\mathfrak{g}^{(1)} \subsetneq \mathfrak{g}$ (otherwise$\mathfrak{g}$ would fail the first step of solvability), one can find such an$\mathfrak{a}$ by choosing any hyperplane between$\mathfrak{g}^{(1)}$ and$\mathfrak{g}$. By induction,$\mathfrak{a}$ has a common eigenvector with eigenvalue$\mu : \mathfrak{a} \to \mathbb{C}$; one then considers the subspace$W \subseteq V$ of all vectors transforming by$\mu$ under$\mathfrak{a}$. The crucial calculation shows that$W$ is preserved by the full action of$\mathfrak{g}$ — this uses the fact that for$X \in \mathfrak{g}$ and$Y \in \mathfrak{a}$, the bracket$[X, Y] \in \mathfrak{a}$ forces$\mu([X, Y]) = 0$ (by a trace argument), which in turn shows$X$ maps$W$ into itself. Applying the base case to the one-dimensional extension gives the common eigenvector in$W \subseteq V$.

The trace argument is worth spelling out because it is the technical heart of the proof. For $X \in \mathfrak{g}$ and$Y \in \mathfrak{a}$,$w \in W$: one computes that$X \cdot (Y \cdot w) - Y \cdot (X \cdot w) = [X,Y] \cdot w = \mu([X,Y]) w$. Taking the trace of both sides over a suitable$\mu([X,Y])$-stable subspace shows that$n \cdot \mu([X,Y]) = 0$, where$n = \dim W$. Over$\mathbb{C}$ (or any field of characteristic zero), this forces$\mu([X,Y]) = 0$.

The algebraically closed hypothesis and characteristic-zero assumption are both necessary. Over $\mathbb{R}$, a one-dimensional solvable algebra$\mathbb{R}$ can act on$\mathbb{R}^2$ by rotations ($t \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} \cos t & -\sin t \\ \sin t & \cos t \end{pmatrix}$) with no real eigenvector. Over fields of characteristic$p > 0$, the trace argument breaks down because$n \cdot \mu([X,Y]) = 0$ does not imply$\mu([X,Y]) = 0$ when$p | n$. The theorem is thus specifically a theorem about complex (or more generally, algebraically closed, characteristic zero) Lie theory.

**Example 1.** Let $\mathfrak{b} = \mathrm{span}\{h, e\}$ with$[h, e] = e$ act on$V = \mathbb{C}^2$ by$\rho(h) = \begin{pmatrix} a & 0 \\ 0 & b \end{pmatrix}$ and$\rho(e) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & c \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ (the standard 2-dimensional representation). The vector$v_1 = (1, 0)$ satisfies$\rho(h) v_1 = a v_1$ and$\rho(e) v_1 = 0$. So$v_1$ is a common eigenvector with$\lambda(h) = a$,$\lambda(e) = 0$, confirming Lie's theorem.

**Example 2.** The Borel subalgebra $\mathfrak{b}_n$ acting on$\mathbb{C}^n$ by matrix multiplication: the standard basis vector$e_1 = (1, 0, \ldots, 0)$ is a common eigenvector since every upper triangular matrix leaves$e_1$ as an eigenvector with eigenvalue equal to the$(1,1)$ entry. Lie's theorem predicts this, and the entire basis is produced by the iteration in Lie's corollary (Section 48.1.4).

**Example 3.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$, which is not solvable: there is no common eigenvector in the standard 2-dimensional representation. The operator$h$ has eigenvectors$e_1$ (eigenvalue$+1$) and$e_2$ (eigenvalue$-1$), but neither is an eigenvector for$e$ or$f$. This failure is exactly predicted: Lie's theorem requires solvability.

**Example 4.** Let $\mathfrak{g}$ be the 3-dimensional Heisenberg algebra acting on$\mathbb{C}^2$ by a 2-dimensional representation. The Heisenberg algebra is nilpotent (hence solvable), so Lie's theorem guarantees a common eigenvector. The central element$z$ acts by a scalar$\lambda(z)$, and if$\lambda(z) = 0$, one can find a common eigenvector by more elementary means. If$\lambda(z) \neq 0$, the Stone–von Neumann theorem tells us that no finite-dimensional irreducible representation exists, so the 2-dimensional representation must be reducible and a common eigenvector exists in the reducible module.

The content of Lie's theorem makes solvable Lie algebras (over $\mathbb{C}$) tractable: their representations always have the simplest possible structure at the eigenvector level. The theorem is the starting point for the theory of Borel subalgebras, parabolic subalgebras, and the general study of how solvable subalgebras of semisimple algebras interact with the full representation theory.
