# 48.1.4 Corollary: Solvable Algebras Are Upper-Triangularizable

Lie's theorem produces a single common eigenvector, but a simple iteration gives much more. Having found a common eigenvector $v_1$ for a solvable$\mathfrak{g}$ acting on$V$, one passes to the quotient$V / \langle v_1 \rangle$: the action of$\mathfrak{g}$ descends to this quotient (since$\langle v_1 \rangle$ is stable), and$V / \langle v_1 \rangle$ is again a$\mathfrak{g}$-module. Applying Lie's theorem again yields a second eigenvector$v_2$ in the quotient, which lifts to a vector in$V$ that, together with$v_1$, spans a two-dimensional stable subspace. Continuing in this way produces a complete flag$0 = V_0 \subset V_1 \subset V_2 \subset \cdots \subset V_n = V$ of$\mathfrak{g}$-stable subspaces with$\dim V_k = k$. With respect to a basis adapted to this flag, every element of$\mathfrak{g}$ acts by an upper triangular matrix.

The conclusion is stark: the image of any finite-dimensional representation of a solvable Lie algebra over $\mathbb{C}$ can be simultaneously upper-triangularized. Equivalently, there exists a basis of$V$ in which every operator$\rho(X)$ is upper triangular. This is the Lie algebra version of the Lie–Kolchin theorem (or the Borel fixed-point theorem in algebraic group language): solvable groups and algebras over algebraically closed fields of characteristic zero stabilize a complete flag in any representation.

The structure of upper triangular matrices is transparent: the diagonal entries of $\rho(X)$ are the eigenvalues$\lambda(X)$ produced by Lie's theorem, and the strictly upper-triangular part captures the non-diagonalizable behavior. In particular, the operators$\rho([X, Y]) = [\rho(X), \rho(Y)]$ are strictly upper triangular (since the diagonal of a commutator of upper triangular matrices is zero), which shows that$[\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}]$ acts by nilpotent matrices in any representation. This nilpotency of the derived subalgebra is the bridge toward Cartan's criterion for solvability in terms of the Killing form, and toward the nilpotent theory of Chapter 48.2.

The contrapositive is also useful: if a Lie algebra acting on $V$ cannot be simultaneously upper-triangularized, it must contain a non-solvable subalgebra — in particular, a subalgebra isomorphic to$\mathfrak{sl}_2$. This is the algebraic mechanism behind many non-existence theorems in representation theory.

**Example 1.** The Borel algebra $\mathfrak{b}_n$ acting on$\mathbb{C}^n$ by matrix multiplication: the standard basis$e_1, e_2, \ldots, e_n$ is already adapted to the flag$\mathrm{span}\{e_1\} \subset \mathrm{span}\{e_1, e_2\} \subset \cdots \subset \mathbb{C}^n$, and every upper triangular matrix is upper triangular in this basis. This is the tautological example.

**Example 2.** The 2-dimensional non-abelian algebra $\mathfrak{b} = \mathrm{span}\{h, e\}$ with$[h,e] = e$ in any faithful representation: we can find a common eigenvector$v_1$ with$h \cdot v_1 = \lambda v_1$ and$e \cdot v_1 = 0$ (since$e$ acts nilpotently on eigenvectors of$h$ with "wrong" eigenvalue). Then in the basis$\{v_1, v_2\}$ adapted to the flag,$\rho(h) = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda & * \\ 0 & \mu \end{pmatrix}$ and$\rho(e) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & * \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.

**Example 3.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$ acting on$V = \mathbb{C}^2$ by the standard representation: one cannot simultaneously upper-triangularize$e = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$,$f = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$,$h = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$. The matrices$e$ and$f$ have no common eigenvector:$e$ preserves$\mathrm{span}\{e_1\}$ while$f$ does not. This is the representation-theoretic confirmation that$\mathfrak{sl}_2$ is not solvable.

**Example 4.** Cartan's criterion: for a solvable $\mathfrak{g}$, the Killing form satisfies$B(X, Y) = 0$ for$X \in [\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}]$ and all$Y \in \mathfrak{g}$. Proof: in the upper triangular basis,$\rho(X)$ is strictly upper triangular for$X \in [\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}]$ (as a commutator of upper triangular matrices), and$\rho(Y)$ is upper triangular. The product$\rho(X)\rho(Y)$ of a strictly upper triangular and upper triangular matrix has zero trace. So$B(X,Y) = \mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{ad}(X)\mathrm{ad}(Y)) = 0$, making$B$ degenerate on$[\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}] \times \mathfrak{g}$, and hence on all of$\mathfrak{g}$ if$[\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}] \neq 0$. The solvable algebra's Killing form is degenerate, confirming (one direction of) Cartan's criterion.

The upper-triangularization theorem is the definitive structural result about solvable Lie algebras in characteristic zero, and it explains why solvable algebras are "easier" than semisimple ones: their entire representation theory is controlled by the eigenvalue functional $\lambda: \mathfrak{g} \to \mathbb{C}$, with the complication confined to the off-diagonal (nilpotent) part.
