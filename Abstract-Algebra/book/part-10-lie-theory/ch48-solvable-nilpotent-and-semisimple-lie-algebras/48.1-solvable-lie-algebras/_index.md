# 48.1 Solvable Lie Algebras

Solvable Lie algebras are defined by the behavior of the derived series. Beginning with $\mathfrak{g}^{(0)} = \mathfrak{g}$ and setting $\mathfrak{g}^{(k+1)} = [\mathfrak{g}^{(k)}, \mathfrak{g}^{(k)}]$, a Lie algebra is solvable when this series eventually reaches zero: $\mathfrak{g}^{(n)} = 0$ for some $n$. This mirrors the definition of a solvable group in terms of the derived series, and the analogy runs deep: the Lie algebra of a solvable Lie group is solvable, and conversely. Abelian Lie algebras are trivially solvable (since $[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}] = 0$), and extensions of solvable algebras by solvable algebras are solvable. The prototype is the Borel subalgebra $\mathfrak{b}_n$ of upper triangular $n \times n$ matrices: its derived algebra $[\mathfrak{b}_n, \mathfrak{b}_n]$ consists of strictly upper triangular matrices, and iterating gives a chain that terminates.

The fundamental theorem about solvable Lie algebras over $\mathbb{C}$ is Lie's theorem: if $\mathfrak{g}$ is solvable and $\rho: \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{gl}(V)$ is any finite-dimensional representation over $\mathbb{C}$, then $V$ has a common eigenvector for all operators $\rho(x)$, $x \in \mathfrak{g}$. Equivalently, there is a basis of $V$ with respect to which all matrices $\rho(x)$ are upper triangular. This is the precise sense in which solvable algebras are "tame": all their representations can be simultaneously triangularized. The proof proceeds by induction on $\dim \mathfrak{g}$, using the fact that $\mathfrak{g}$ always contains a codimension-one ideal (since $\mathfrak{g}/[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]$ is abelian and hence has codimension-one subspaces).

Lie's theorem has an important corollary: a solvable Lie algebra over $\mathbb{C}$ is isomorphic to a subalgebra of $\mathfrak{b}_n$ for some $n$. This gives a concrete upper triangular normal form and makes the structure of solvable algebras, while not fully classified in general, reasonably explicit in the representation-theoretic sense. Notably, Lie's theorem fails over fields that are not algebraically closed, or in characteristic $p > 0$ — the condition that the field be $\mathbb{C}$ (or at least algebraically closed of characteristic zero) is essential. This algebraic sensitivity is one reason why the theory over $\mathbb{C}$ is cleaner than the real theory, and it motivates working over $\mathbb{C}$ throughout most of the structural theory.
