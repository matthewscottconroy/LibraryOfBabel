# 48.2.1 The Lower Central Series; Nilpotent Lie Algebras

Alongside the derived series, Lie algebras possess a second canonical descending filtration. The *lower central series* is defined by $\mathfrak{g}^0 = \mathfrak{g}$,$\mathfrak{g}^1 = [\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}]$, and inductively$\mathfrak{g}^{n+1} = [\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}^n]$. The critical difference from the derived series is in how successive terms are produced: instead of bracketing each term with itself, one always brackets with the full algebra$\mathfrak{g}$. This makes the lower central series descend more rapidly than the derived series — one can verify that$\mathfrak{g}^{(n)} \subseteq \mathfrak{g}^n$ for all$n$ — so nilpotency (defined below) is a strictly stronger condition than solvability.

A Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ is *nilpotent* if$\mathfrak{g}^n = 0$ for some$n$; the smallest such$n$ is the *nilpotency class* of$\mathfrak{g}$. Every nilpotent Lie algebra is solvable, but not conversely: the Borel algebra of upper triangular matrices is solvable but not nilpotent. The canonical nilpotent examples are the strictly upper triangular matrices$\mathfrak{n}_n$ (with zeros on and below the diagonal), which satisfy$\mathfrak{n}^n = 0$, and the Heisenberg algebra$\mathfrak{h}$ of nilpotency class 2 (discussed in Section 48.2.3). The algebra$\mathfrak{sl}_2$ is not nilpotent: one computes that$\mathfrak{sl}_2^1 = [\mathfrak{sl}_2, \mathfrak{sl}_2] = \mathfrak{sl}_2$, so the lower central series is constant.

The intuitive content of nilpotency is that the algebra acts on itself by "eventually zero" operators: elements of $\mathfrak{g}$ act by nilpotent derivations via the adjoint representation. This is the perspective that Engel's theorem makes precise. Each term$\mathfrak{g}^n$ is an ideal of$\mathfrak{g}$, and the quotients$\mathfrak{g}^n / \mathfrak{g}^{n+1}$ are abelian and central in the quotient$\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{g}^{n+1}$. Nilpotent Lie algebras are in many ways the simplest non-abelian Lie algebras, and they arise naturally as the "unipotent radical" components in the structure theory of reductive groups.

The center $Z(\mathfrak{g})$ plays a key role: if$\mathfrak{g}$ is nilpotent of class$n$, then$\mathfrak{g}^{n-1} \subseteq Z(\mathfrak{g})$ (since$[\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}^{n-1}] = \mathfrak{g}^n = 0$). Thus nilpotent algebras always have nontrivial center. Conversely, one can often prove nilpotency by exhibiting a central extension structure: if$Z(\mathfrak{g}) \neq 0$ and$\mathfrak{g}/Z(\mathfrak{g})$ is nilpotent, then$\mathfrak{g}$ is nilpotent.

**Example 1.** The strictly upper triangular algebra $\mathfrak{n}_3$ of$3 \times 3$ matrices (zero on and below the diagonal) has basis$\{e_{12}, e_{13}, e_{23}\}$ with$[e_{12}, e_{23}] = e_{13}$ and all other brackets zero. The lower central series:$\mathfrak{n}_3^1 = [\mathfrak{n}_3, \mathfrak{n}_3] = \mathrm{span}\{e_{13}\}$,$\mathfrak{n}_3^2 = [\mathfrak{n}_3, \mathrm{span}\{e_{13}\}] = 0$. Nilpotent of class 2.

**Example 2.** The lower central series of $\mathfrak{b}_2 = \mathrm{span}\{h, e\}$ (Borel subalgebra of$\mathfrak{sl}_2$):$\mathfrak{b}_2^1 = [\mathfrak{b}_2, \mathfrak{b}_2] = \mathrm{span}\{e\}$,$\mathfrak{b}_2^2 = [\mathfrak{b}_2, \mathrm{span}\{e\}] = \mathrm{span}\{[h,e]\} = \mathrm{span}\{e\}$. The lower central series stabilizes at$\mathrm{span}\{e\} \neq 0$, so$\mathfrak{b}_2$ is solvable but *not* nilpotent.

**Example 3.** For $\mathfrak{n}_n$ (strictly upper triangular$n \times n$ matrices):$\mathfrak{n}_n^k$ consists of matrices nonzero only in position$(i,j)$ with$j - i > k$. The series reaches zero after$n-1$ steps:$\mathfrak{n}_n^{n-1} = 0$. The nilpotency class is$n-1$. In particular$\mathfrak{n}_2 = \mathrm{span}\{e_{12}\}$ is abelian (class 1),$\mathfrak{n}_3$ has class 2,$\mathfrak{n}_4$ has class 3, etc.

**Example 4.** The derived series and lower central series can differ: for $\mathfrak{n}_4$ (class 3), the derived series is$\mathfrak{n}_4^{(0)} = \mathfrak{n}_4$,$\mathfrak{n}_4^{(1)} = [\mathfrak{n}_4, \mathfrak{n}_4] =$ span of$\{e_{13}, e_{14}, e_{24}\}$ (one step above diagonal),$\mathfrak{n}_4^{(2)} = [[\mathfrak{n}_4, \mathfrak{n}_4], [\mathfrak{n}_4, \mathfrak{n}_4]] = \mathrm{span}\{e_{14}\}$ (two steps above),$\mathfrak{n}_4^{(3)} = 0$. The lower central series$\mathfrak{n}_4^1 =$ span of$e_{13}, e_{14}, e_{24}$,$\mathfrak{n}_4^2 =$ span of$e_{14}$,$\mathfrak{n}_4^3 = 0$. In this case they agree; in general the derived series descends at least as fast as the lower central series.

Nilpotent Lie algebras occupy a middle position in the hierarchy: abelian $\subset$ nilpotent$\subset$ solvable$\subset$ all Lie algebras. The classification of nilpotent Lie algebras in low dimensions is known (up to dimension 7 or so), but no analogue of the semisimple classification exists, reflecting the fundamentally different and more complex nature of the nilpotent case.
