# 48.2.2 Engel's Theorem: $\mathrm{ad}(X)$ Nilpotent for All$X$ $\Leftrightarrow$ $\mathfrak{g}$ Nilpotent

The definition of nilpotency is stated in terms of the lower central series, a filtration that depends on the bracket structure of $\mathfrak{g}$ as a whole. Engel's theorem repackages this global condition as a pointwise condition on individual elements. The theorem states that a finite-dimensional Lie algebra$\mathfrak{g}$ is nilpotent if and only if for every$X \in \mathfrak{g}$, the adjoint operator$\mathrm{ad}(X) : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g}$ defined by$\mathrm{ad}(X)(Y) = [X, Y]$ is a nilpotent linear map (i.e.,$(\mathrm{ad}(X))^n = 0$ for some$n$ depending on$X$). This result holds over any field, with no characteristic assumption.

The proof of the non-trivial direction — that pointwise nilpotency of $\mathrm{ad}$ implies$\mathfrak{g}$ nilpotent — is a beautiful induction. If every$\mathrm{ad}(X)$ is nilpotent, then$\mathfrak{g}$ acts on itself via nilpotent operators. By a general lemma (itself proved by induction on the dimension of the representation space), a Lie algebra of nilpotent operators on a vector space has a nonzero vector annihilated by the entire algebra; applied to$\mathrm{ad}: \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g})$, this yields a nonzero element$Y \in \mathfrak{g}$ with$[X, Y] = 0$ for all$X$, i.e., a nonzero element of the center$Z(\mathfrak{g})$. One then passes to$\mathfrak{g}/Z(\mathfrak{g})$, where the hypothesis persists, and inducting on dimension establishes that$Z(\mathfrak{g}^n) \neq 0$ for all$n$, which forces the lower central series to reach zero.

The easy direction — nilpotency of $\mathfrak{g}$ implies nilpotency of each$\mathrm{ad}(X)$ — follows directly from the definition. If$\mathfrak{g}^n = 0$, then for any$X$:$(\mathrm{ad}(X))^n(Y) = [X, [X, \ldots [X, Y] \ldots ]] \in \mathfrak{g}^n = 0$ for all$Y$. So$(\mathrm{ad}(X))^n = 0$.

The power of Engel's theorem lies in its intrinsic character: it characterizes nilpotency without reference to any particular filtration or representation, directly in terms of the adjoint action. This makes it easy to apply in practice. For example, to check whether a given subalgebra $\mathfrak{n} \subseteq \mathfrak{gl}_n$ is nilpotent, one need only verify that each matrix$X \in \mathfrak{n}$ is nilpotent as a linear map — the theorem then guarantees that the Lie algebra itself is nilpotent, which by the analogue of the corollary to Lie's theorem means$\mathfrak{n}$ can be simultaneously strictly upper-triangularized.

There is a stronger form of Engel's theorem for representations: if $\rho: \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{gl}(V)$ is a representation in which every$\rho(X)$ is nilpotent, then$V$ has a complete flag of$\mathfrak{g}$-stable subspaces, and in a suitable basis every$\rho(X)$ is strictly upper triangular. This is the direct analogue of Lie's theorem for nilpotent (rather than solvable) algebras, but with the conclusion that every operator is strictly upper triangular (rather than merely upper triangular). The algebraic difference between solvable and nilpotent is exactly this: solvable$\rightsquigarrow$ upper triangular, nilpotent$\rightsquigarrow$ strictly upper triangular.

**Example 1.** The strictly upper triangular algebra $\mathfrak{n}_n$: for any$X \in \mathfrak{n}_n$,$X^n = 0$ (products of$n$ strictly upper triangular matrices are zero). So every element is nilpotent, and Engel's theorem predicts (and confirms) that$\mathfrak{n}_n$ is nilpotent. The adjoint action:$\mathrm{ad}(X)(Y) = XY - YX$ for strictly upper triangular$X, Y$, and one can check$(\mathrm{ad}(X))^k = 0$ for$k \geq n-1$.

**Example 2.** Borel subalgebra $\mathfrak{b}_n$ (upper triangular matrices): the diagonal element$h = \mathrm{diag}(1, 0, \ldots, 0, -1, \ldots)$ satisfies$\mathrm{ad}(h)(E_{ij}) = (h_i - h_j) E_{ij}$, which is not nilpotent (has nonzero eigenvalues). So not all adjoint operators are nilpotent, and Engel's theorem correctly predicts that$\mathfrak{b}_n$ is not nilpotent (it is solvable but not nilpotent).

**Example 3.** Verify Engel's theorem for $\mathfrak{n}_3 = \mathrm{span}\{e_{12}, e_{13}, e_{23}\}$:$\mathrm{ad}(e_{12})(e_{23}) = [e_{12}, e_{23}] = e_{13}$,$\mathrm{ad}(e_{12})(e_{13}) = 0$,$\mathrm{ad}(e_{12})(e_{12}) = 0$. So$(\mathrm{ad}(e_{12}))^2 = 0$. Similarly for the other basis elements. Every adjoint operator is nilpotent, confirming nilpotency of$\mathfrak{n}_3$.

**Example 4.** Engel's theorem in representation theory: consider $\mathfrak{g} = \mathrm{span}\{N_1, N_2\} \subseteq \mathfrak{gl}_3$ where$N_1 = E_{12}$,$N_2 = E_{23}$ (elementary nilpotent matrices). Both are nilpotent, and$[N_1, N_2] = E_{13}$, which is also nilpotent. The Lie algebra spanned by$N_1, N_2$ is$\mathfrak{n}_3$, which Engel's theorem confirms is nilpotent. In the standard basis, every element acts strictly upper triangularly.

Engel's theorem is one of the two fundamental structure theorems for Lie algebras (the other being Lie's theorem), and together they characterize the two extreme cases: solvable $\leftrightarrow$ upper triangularizable, nilpotent$\leftrightarrow$ strictly upper triangularizable.
