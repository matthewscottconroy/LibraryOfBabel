# 48.2.3 Nilpotent $\Rightarrow$ Solvable; the Heisenberg Algebra

That every nilpotent Lie algebra is solvable follows directly from the definitions. If $\mathfrak{g}^n = 0$ under the lower central series, then since$\mathfrak{g}^{(k)} \subseteq \mathfrak{g}^k$ for all$k$ (the derived series is contained in the lower central series term by term), one gets$\mathfrak{g}^{(n)} = 0$ as well. The inclusion$\mathfrak{g}^{(k)} \subseteq \mathfrak{g}^k$ holds because at each step the derived series brackets a smaller set with itself, while the lower central series brackets the full$\mathfrak{g}$ with the current term — the latter is at least as aggressive a descent. The converse fails: the Borel algebra of upper triangular matrices is solvable but not nilpotent, since the commutator of two diagonal matrices is zero yet the lower central series stabilizes at the nonzero nilradical.

The *Heisenberg algebra* $\mathfrak{h}$ is the archetypal small nilpotent Lie algebra, and it appears throughout mathematics and physics. It is 3-dimensional, spanned by elements$\{p, q, z\}$, with bracket relations$[p, q] = z$ and$[p, z] = [q, z] = 0$. The center is$Z(\mathfrak{h}) = \mathrm{span}(z)$, and the derived subalgebra is$[\mathfrak{h}, \mathfrak{h}] = \mathrm{span}(z) = Z(\mathfrak{h})$. The lower central series is therefore$\mathfrak{h} \supset \mathrm{span}(z) \supset 0$, terminating after two steps, so$\mathfrak{h}$ is nilpotent of class 2. Since the derived subalgebra is already zero in the second derived term,$\mathfrak{h}$ is also solvable — consistent with the general theorem.

The Heisenberg algebra has a canonical matrix realization as strictly upper triangular $3 \times 3$ matrices:
$$p \leftrightarrow \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad q \leftrightarrow \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad z \leftrightarrow \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
One computes $[p, q] = pq - qp = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = z$. This confirms the bracket relations and shows that$\mathfrak{h} \cong \mathfrak{n}_3$.

The physical significance of the Heisenberg algebra is that it encodes the canonical commutation relations of quantum mechanics. The elements $p$ and$q$ correspond to the position and momentum operators$\hat{q}$ and$\hat{p}$, and the central element$z$ corresponds to$i\hbar$ times the identity operator, encoding the relation$[\hat{q}, \hat{p}] = i\hbar$. The Heisenberg algebra is the infinitesimal form of the Heisenberg group, and its irreducible unitary representations are classified by the Stone–von Neumann theorem: for each nonzero value of$\hbar$, there is essentially a unique irreducible unitary representation (the Schrödinger representation on$L^2(\mathbb{R})$). This connection between the simplest non-abelian nilpotent Lie algebra and the foundations of quantum mechanics is one of the most striking examples of abstract algebra in natural science.

**Example 1.** The Heisenberg algebra $\mathfrak{h}$ has exactly one non-trivial subalgebra structure up to isomorphism: its ideal lattice is$0 \subset \mathrm{span}(z) \subset \mathfrak{h}$. Every 1-dimensional subalgebra is abelian (since$[X, X] = 0$); the 2-dimensional subalgebras are$\mathrm{span}(p, z)$ and$\mathrm{span}(q, z)$ and$\mathrm{span}(p+\lambda q, z)$ (all abelian), or any span of$p$ and$q$ without$z$ (which gives$[p, q] = z \notin \mathrm{span}(p, q)$, not a subalgebra). So the only 2-dimensional subalgebras are abelian.

**Example 2.** The Heisenberg group $H_n$ for higher dimensions: spanned by$\{p_1, \ldots, p_n, q_1, \ldots, q_n, z\}$ with$[p_i, q_j] = \delta_{ij} z$ and all other brackets zero. This is nilpotent of class 2 and its center is$\mathrm{span}(z)$. The Stone–von Neumann theorem classifies its irreducible unitary representations by the nonzero values of the central character$\chi(z) = i\hbar$ (one irrep for each$\hbar \neq 0$, plus the trivial representations with$\hbar = 0$).

**Example 3.** The gap between solvable and nilpotent: $\mathfrak{b}_2 = \mathrm{span}\{h, e\}$ with$[h, e] = e$ is solvable ($\mathfrak{b}_2^{(1)} = \mathrm{span}(e)$,$\mathfrak{b}_2^{(2)} = 0$) but not nilpotent ($\mathfrak{b}_2^1 = \mathrm{span}(e)$,$\mathfrak{b}_2^2 = [\mathfrak{b}_2, \mathrm{span}(e)] = \mathrm{span}\{[h,e]\} = \mathrm{span}(e) \neq 0$, the series is constant). The element$\mathrm{ad}(h)$ is not nilpotent (it has eigenvalue 1 on$e$), confirming Engel's theorem.

**Example 4.** The nilpotent algebra $\mathfrak{n}_4$ (4-dimensional, strictly upper triangular$3 \times 3$ matrices extended): class 3. It is strictly between nilpotent class 2 (Heisenberg) and class$n-1$ ($\mathfrak{n}_n$). The Heisenberg algebra is the unique (up to isomorphism) 3-dimensional non-abelian nilpotent Lie algebra; in dimension 4 and beyond, the classification becomes more complex.

The Heisenberg algebra thus plays the role of the "minimal example" of nilpotency beyond abelian, just as $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ plays the role of the minimal simple Lie algebra. Both appear constantly as building blocks in more complex structures, and mastery of their properties is essential for the rest of the theory.
