# 48.3.1 Simple and Semisimple Lie Algebras

Having understood what makes a Lie algebra as "structured" as possible (solvable, nilpotent), we turn to algebras that are in some sense maximally non-abelian. A Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ is *simple* if$\dim \mathfrak{g} > 1$,$\mathfrak{g}$ is non-abelian, and$\mathfrak{g}$ has no proper nonzero ideals. The non-abelian condition excludes the one-dimensional abelian algebra$k$ (which has no proper ideals vacuously), ensuring the definition captures genuine complexity. Simple Lie algebras are the irreducible atoms of Lie theory: they cannot be further decomposed by passing to ideals, just as simple groups cannot be decomposed by passing to normal subgroups.

A Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ is *semisimple* if it is a direct sum of simple ideals:$\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{g}_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathfrak{g}_r$ with each$\mathfrak{g}_i$ simple. This decomposition is canonical — the simple summands are precisely the minimal ideals of$\mathfrak{g}$, and they are uniquely determined. The prototype is$\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbb{C})$ (traceless$n \times n$ matrices), which is simple for$n \geq 2$. The classical semisimple algebras —$\mathfrak{so}_n$,$\mathfrak{sp}_{2n}$, products of these — all admit such direct-sum decompositions into simple pieces.

Semisimple Lie algebras are the non-abelian counterpart of fields: they carry no "quotient structure" that would allow them to be simplified, and they are entirely determined by their intrinsic symmetry. Crucially, a Lie algebra is semisimple if and only if its radical (the largest solvable ideal) is zero — so semisimplicity and solvability are precisely complementary. The classification of all simple Lie algebras over $\mathbb{C}$, carried out in Chapter 49 via root systems and Dynkin diagrams, is one of the landmark achievements of nineteenth- and twentieth-century mathematics, revealing that only a finite number of "shapes" of simple Lie algebra exist.

The characterization of semisimplicity in terms of the derived subalgebra is also clean: a Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ is semisimple if and only if$[\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}] = \mathfrak{g}$ (equivalently,$\mathfrak{g}$ has no nonzero abelian ideals). This follows because any solvable ideal produces a nonzero abelian ideal (the last nonzero term of the derived series), and conversely any abelian ideal is solvable.

**Example 1.** $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$ is simple. Any nonzero ideal$\mathfrak{a}$ must contain some nonzero element$ae + bf + ch$. If$a \neq 0$: bracketing with$f$ gives$[f, ae + bf + ch] = ah - 2bf \in \mathfrak{a}$; bracketing again with$e$ (if$h \in \mathfrak{a}$) gives$2e \in \mathfrak{a}$, then$[e, f] = h$,$[f, h] = -2f \in \mathfrak{a}$, so$\mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{sl}_2$. Similar arguments handle the other cases. Thus$\mathfrak{sl}_2$ has no proper nonzero ideals.

**Example 2.** $\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbb{C})$ for$n \geq 2$ is simple (corresponding to type$A_{n-1}$). The proof uses the fact that any nonzero ideal must contain a root space$\mathfrak{g}_\alpha = \mathrm{span}\{E_{ij}\}$, and then the root system$A_{n-1}$ is connected (any root can be reached from any other by a chain of brackets), forcing the ideal to be all of$\mathfrak{sl}_n$.

**Example 3.** $\mathfrak{gl}_2(\mathbb{C}) = \mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C}) \oplus \mathbb{C} \cdot I$ (where$\mathbb{C} \cdot I$ is the center). This is not semisimple: the center$\mathbb{C} \cdot I$ is a 1-dimensional abelian ideal, which is solvable. The radical of$\mathfrak{gl}_2$ is$\mathbb{C} \cdot I$, and the semisimple quotient is$\mathfrak{gl}_2 / \mathbb{C} \cdot I \cong \mathfrak{sl}_2$.

**Example 4.** $\mathfrak{so}(4, \mathbb{C}) \cong \mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C}) \oplus \mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$ (type$D_2 = A_1 \oplus A_1$). This is semisimple but not simple: it splits as a direct sum of two copies of$\mathfrak{sl}_2$. Each copy is a simple ideal, and their direct sum has no other ideals. The corresponding Lie group$SO(4)$ locally looks like$SU(2) \times SU(2)$, and the famous formula$SO(4) \cong (SU(2) \times SU(2)) / \{\pm(I,I)\}$ is the group-level version of this splitting.

The classification of simple Lie algebras — $A_n, B_n, C_n, D_n$ and five exceptionals — is the pinnacle of the structure theory, and the rest of Part X (Chapters 49 and 50) is devoted to understanding it and its representation-theoretic consequences.
