# 48.3.3 Weyl's Theorem: Every Representation of a Semisimple Algebra Is Completely Reducible

One of the most important properties of semisimple Lie algebras is that their representation theory is as clean as possible. *Weyl's Complete Reducibility Theorem* states that every finite-dimensional representation of a semisimple Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ over$\mathbb{C}$ is completely reducible: it decomposes as a direct sum of irreducible subrepresentations. This is the Lie algebra analogue of Maschke's theorem for finite group representations, but it requires a different proof — there is no finite group averaging to appeal to, and the semisimple Lie algebra is typically infinite in a suitable sense.

The proof proceeds via the Casimir element. For a semisimple Lie algebra with non-degenerate Killing form $B$, one chooses dual bases$\{X_i\}$ and$\{X^i\}$ with$B(X_i, X^j) = \delta_{ij}$ and forms the Casimir element$C = \sum_i X_i X^i$ in the universal enveloping algebra$\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})$. The element$C$ lies in the center of$\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})$: it commutes with every element of$\mathfrak{g}$. By Schur's lemma, in any irreducible representation$C$ acts by a scalar$c_V \cdot \mathrm{id}_V$, and crucially,$c_V \neq 0$ for any nontrivial irreducible. This scalar separates non-isomorphic irreducibles and allows one to construct, for any stable subspace$W \subseteq V$, an equivariant projection$V \to W$ using the action of$C$.

The key argument is as follows. Given a $\mathfrak{g}$-module$V$ and a$\mathfrak{g}$-stable subspace$W \subseteq V$, one wants to find a$\mathfrak{g}$-equivariant complement. The Casimir element acts on$V/W$ with some eigenvalue$c_{V/W}$, and acts on$W$ with a possibly different eigenvalue$c_W$. If$c_{V/W} \neq c_W$, the two eigenspaces split$V$ equivariantly. In the "difficult" case where$V/W$ is trivial (one-dimensional with$\mathfrak{g}$ acting by zero), one uses a direct cohomological argument: the obstruction to splitting lies in$H^1(\mathfrak{g}, \mathrm{Hom}(V/W, W))$, which vanishes for semisimple$\mathfrak{g}$ by a direct computation using the Casimir.

The consequences of complete reducibility permeate all of representation theory. It means that to classify all representations of $\mathfrak{g}$, it suffices to classify the irreducibles — and irreducibles are classified by their highest weights (Chapter 50). It also means that every representation is "rigid" in a cohomological sense: the first cohomology group$H^1(\mathfrak{g}, M)$ vanishes for all finite-dimensional$\mathfrak{g}$-modules$M$, which in turn implies that every extension of two semisimple-algebra representations splits. By contrast, solvable Lie algebras can have non-split extensions, which is one reason their representation theory is more intricate.

**Example 1.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$: the 4-dimensional representation$V_1 \otimes V_1 = V_2 \oplus V_0$ (Clebsch–Gordan decomposition). This direct sum decomposition is guaranteed by Weyl's theorem: any 4-dimensional representation of$\mathfrak{sl}_2$ containing a 3-dimensional irreducible$V_2$ and a 1-dimensional trivial$V_0$ must split as their direct sum, with no extension between them.

**Example 2.** By Weyl's theorem, the symmetric power $\mathrm{Sym}^n(\mathbb{C}^2)$ for$\mathfrak{sl}_2$ acting on$\mathbb{C}^2$ is irreducible. This is$V_n$, the$(n+1)$-dimensional representation. More generally, any tensor power of the standard representation of$\mathfrak{sl}_n$ decomposes as a direct sum of irreducibles, and this decomposition is governed by the Schur–Weyl duality (the action of$\mathfrak{sl}_n$ and$S_k$ on$(\mathbb{C}^n)^{\otimes k}$ commute and together produce the full endomorphism algebra).

**Example 3.** For the Heisenberg algebra $\mathfrak{h}$ (which is nilpotent, hence not semisimple): the 2-dimensional representation$\rho(p) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$,$\rho(q) = \rho(z) = 0$ has a 1-dimensional stable subspace$\mathrm{span}\{e_1\}$ but no equivariant complement: any complement$\mathrm{span}\{e_2 + \lambda e_1\}$ must satisfy$\rho(p)(e_2 + \lambda e_1) = e_1 \in \mathrm{span}\{e_2 + \lambda e_1\}$, which fails. So Weyl's theorem fails for the Heisenberg algebra, confirming that complete reducibility is special to semisimple algebras.

**Example 4.** Whitehead's lemmas (proved en route to Weyl's theorem): $H^1(\mathfrak{g}, M) = 0$ and$H^2(\mathfrak{g}, M) = 0$ for all finite-dimensional modules$M$ over a semisimple$\mathfrak{g}$. The first vanishing says there are no nontrivial derivations from$\mathfrak{g}$ to$M$ that are not inner; the second says there are no nontrivial extensions of$\mathfrak{g}$ by$M$ as Lie algebras. Together they say that semisimple Lie algebras are "rigid" — they have no deformations.

Weyl's theorem is the foundation on which all of highest weight theory rests. It tells us that the representation ring of $\mathfrak{g}$ is a free abelian group on the classes of irreducibles, and that computing with representations reduces entirely to computing with irreducibles.
