# 48.3.4 The Radical; Levi Decomposition: $\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{s} \ltimes \mathrm{rad}(\mathfrak{g})$

Not every Lie algebra is semisimple or solvable, and the question of how to decompose a general finite-dimensional Lie algebra into manageable pieces is answered by the Levi decomposition. The *radical* $\mathrm{rad}(\mathfrak{g})$ of a Lie algebra$\mathfrak{g}$ is the largest solvable ideal: it exists because the sum of two solvable ideals is solvable (this follows from the extension property of solvability), so one can take the sum of all solvable ideals to obtain a unique maximal one. The radical measures how far$\mathfrak{g}$ is from being semisimple:$\mathfrak{g}$ is semisimple iff$\mathrm{rad}(\mathfrak{g}) = 0$, and the quotient$\mathfrak{g}/\mathrm{rad}(\mathfrak{g})$ is always semisimple.

*Levi's theorem* states that this quotient is not merely an abstract semisimple algebra — it can be lifted back into $\mathfrak{g}$. More precisely, there exists a semisimple subalgebra$\mathfrak{s} \subseteq \mathfrak{g}$, called a *Levi factor*, such that$\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{s} \oplus \mathrm{rad}(\mathfrak{g})$ as vector spaces, with the bracket structure being that of a semidirect product:$\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{s} \ltimes \mathrm{rad}(\mathfrak{g})$. The algebra$\mathfrak{s}$ acts on$\mathrm{rad}(\mathfrak{g})$ via the adjoint representation, and all the data of$\mathfrak{g}$ is encoded in this action together with the internal structure of$\mathfrak{s}$ and$\mathrm{rad}(\mathfrak{g})$.

The key insight is that this decomposition reduces the general theory to two specialized problems: the classification of semisimple Lie algebras (accomplished in Chapter 49) and the study of solvable Lie algebras together with semisimple actions on them. The Levi factor is unique up to conjugation by an element of the form $\exp(\mathrm{ad}(X))$ with$X \in \mathrm{rad}(\mathfrak{g})$, so the decomposition is canonical in a strong sense.

The Levi decomposition has a group-level analogue: every connected Lie group $G$ has a decomposition$G = S \ltimes \mathrm{Rad}(G)$, where$S$ is a semisimple subgroup and$\mathrm{Rad}(G)$ is the solvable radical — the Lie algebra statement is the infinitesimal shadow of this structural fact. For instance, the Borel subgroup$B$ of$SL_n$ has radical equal to the unipotent radical$N$ (strictly upper triangular matrices), and the Levi factor is the diagonal torus$T$:$B = T \ltimes N$.

**Example 1.** $\mathfrak{gl}_n = \mathfrak{sl}_n \ltimes k$ (where$k = \mathbb{C} \cdot I$ is the center). The radical is the center$k$ (1-dimensional abelian), and the Levi factor is$\mathfrak{sl}_n$. The semidirect product is actually a direct product since the center acts trivially on everything.

**Example 2.** The Poincaré algebra $\mathfrak{p}$ in physics (Lie algebra of the Poincaré group$\mathbb{R}^{1,3} \rtimes SO(1,3)$): the radical is the translation algebra$\mathbb{R}^{1,3}$ (a 4-dimensional abelian ideal), and the Levi factor is$\mathfrak{so}(1,3) \cong \mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$ (as real Lie algebras). The Levi decomposition exhibits the Poincaré algebra as a semidirect product of the Lorentz algebra with the translation algebra.

**Example 3.** For the Borel subalgebra $\mathfrak{b}$ of$\mathfrak{sl}_3$ (upper triangular traceless matrices):$\mathrm{rad}(\mathfrak{b}) = \mathfrak{n}$ (strictly upper triangular matrices, the nilpotent radical). The Levi factor is the diagonal traceless matrices$\mathfrak{h} = \mathrm{span}\{h_1, h_2\}$ (abelian, semisimple). The Borel algebra is$\mathfrak{h} \ltimes \mathfrak{n}$ with$\mathfrak{h}$ acting on$\mathfrak{n}$ via the adjoint representation.

**Example 4.** A general non-example: the algebra $\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{sl}_2 \ltimes \mathfrak{n}$ where$\mathfrak{sl}_2$ acts on the 2-dimensional nilpotent ideal$\mathfrak{n} = \mathrm{span}\{v, w\}$ via the standard representation ($h \cdot v = v$,$h \cdot w = -w$,$e \cdot w = v$,$f \cdot v = w$, etc.). This is a 5-dimensional non-solvable non-semisimple Lie algebra. Its radical is$\mathfrak{n}$, its Levi factor is$\mathfrak{sl}_2$, and the Levi decomposition gives$\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{sl}_2 \ltimes \mathfrak{n}$.

The Levi decomposition is the definitive structure theorem for arbitrary finite-dimensional Lie algebras. It says that every Lie algebra is an extension of a semisimple algebra by a solvable one, and this extension always splits (unlike in group theory, where extensions need not split). Combined with the classification of semisimple algebras (Chapter 49), this organizes the entire landscape of Lie algebras.
