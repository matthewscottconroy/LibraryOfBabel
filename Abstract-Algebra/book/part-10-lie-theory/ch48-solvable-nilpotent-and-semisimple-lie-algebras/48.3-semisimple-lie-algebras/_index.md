# 48.3 Semisimple Lie Algebras

Semisimple Lie algebras occupy the top of the structural hierarchy: they are the Lie algebras with no nonzero solvable ideals. This condition is equivalent to requiring that the radical $\text{rad}(\mathfrak{g})$ — the unique maximal solvable ideal, shown to exist and be unique — is zero. A slightly stronger notion is simplicity: a Lie algebra is simple if it is non-abelian and has no proper ideals at all. Every semisimple Lie algebra is a direct sum of simple ideals, unique up to order: $\mathfrak{g} \cong \mathfrak{g}_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathfrak{g}_k$. The classification of semisimple Lie algebras thus reduces to the classification of simple ones, which is accomplished in Chapter 49 via root systems.

Cartan's criterion, proved using the Killing form $B(x,y) = \text{tr}(\text{ad}_x \circ \text{ad}_y)$, gives a practical test for semisimplicity: $\mathfrak{g}$ is semisimple if and only if $B$ is non-degenerate. The proof is a beautiful application of the Killing form's properties — in particular its $\text{ad}$-invariance — combined with the fact that the radical is precisely the orthogonal complement of $[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]$ with respect to $B$. Cartan also proved a criterion for solvability: $\mathfrak{g}$ is solvable if and only if $B(x,y) = 0$ for all $x \in \mathfrak{g}$ and $y \in [\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]$. Thus the Killing form detects both extremes of the structural hierarchy.

The representation theory of semisimple Lie algebras is vastly cleaner than that of general Lie algebras, thanks to Weyl's theorem on complete reducibility: every finite-dimensional representation of a semisimple Lie algebra over $\mathbb{C}$ is completely reducible, i.e., decomposes as a direct sum of irreducible subrepresentations. The standard proof uses a non-degenerate invariant bilinear form (or the Casimir element of the universal enveloping algebra) to construct complements to invariant subspaces. This theorem is the representation-theoretic counterpart of Maschke's theorem for finite groups (Part 3), and it is the foundation for the entire structure of highest weight theory in Chapter 50: one can study each irreducible component independently, and the classification of all irreducibles is a well-posed and completely solved problem.
