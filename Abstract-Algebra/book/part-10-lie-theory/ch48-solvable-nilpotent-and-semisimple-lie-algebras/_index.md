# Chapter 48 — Solvable, Nilpotent, and Semisimple Lie Algebras

Not every Lie algebra is simple, and understanding the full landscape requires a structural theory analogous to the way group theory distinguishes between solvable and simple groups. The parallel is more than an analogy: the derived series $\mathfrak{g}^{(0)} = \mathfrak{g}$, $\mathfrak{g}^{(1)} = [\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}]$, $\mathfrak{g}^{(2)} = [\mathfrak{g}^{(1)}, \mathfrak{g}^{(1)}]$, $\ldots$ for Lie algebras mirrors the derived series for groups, and a Lie algebra is solvable when this series terminates at zero. The Lie algebra $\mathfrak{b}_n$ of upper triangular $n \times n$ matrices is the archetypal solvable Lie algebra, and Lie's theorem — the Lie-algebraic counterpart of the theorem on solvable groups — tells us that every representation of a solvable Lie algebra over $\mathbb{C}$ has a common eigenvector, and hence a simultaneous triangularization. Solvable Lie algebras are in this sense the "structurally simple" ones: they have no complexity that cannot be peeled away layer by layer.

Nilpotent Lie algebras are a stricter subclass, defined by the vanishing of the lower central series $\mathfrak{g}^0 = \mathfrak{g}$, $\mathfrak{g}^1 = [\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}^0]$, $\mathfrak{g}^2 = [\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}^1]$, $\ldots$. Every nilpotent Lie algebra is solvable, but the converse fails. Engel's theorem gives an intrinsic characterization: $\mathfrak{g}$ is nilpotent if and only if every adjoint operator $\text{ad}_x$ is a nilpotent linear map. The Heisenberg algebra — the $3$-dimensional algebra with basis $\{p, q, z\}$ satisfying $[p, q] = z$ and $[z, p] = [z, q] = 0$ — is the fundamental example, arising naturally in quantum mechanics from the canonical commutation relation $[P, Q] = -i\hbar I$.

At the opposite extreme from solvable algebras are the semisimple ones. A Lie algebra is semisimple when it has no nonzero solvable ideals, or equivalently (by Cartan's criterion) when its Killing form $B(x,y) = \text{tr}(\text{ad}_x \circ \text{ad}_y)$ is non-degenerate. This is a remarkable fact: a single bilinear form, computable from the bracket alone, detects the most fundamental structural property of a Lie algebra. A semisimple Lie algebra decomposes as a direct sum of simple ideals, each of which will be classified in Chapter 49. Weyl's theorem on complete reducibility is the representation-theoretic counterpart: every finite-dimensional representation of a semisimple Lie algebra over $\mathbb{C}$ is completely reducible, decomposing as a direct sum of irreducibles. This fails for solvable algebras and makes semisimple representation theory tractable.

The Levi decomposition theorem unifies the whole picture. Every finite-dimensional Lie algebra over a field of characteristic zero decomposes as a semidirect product $\mathfrak{g} = \text{rad}(\mathfrak{g}) \rtimes \mathfrak{s}$, where $\text{rad}(\mathfrak{g})$ is the unique maximal solvable ideal (the radical) and $\mathfrak{s}$ is a semisimple Levi factor, unique up to conjugacy. The Poincaré Lie algebra of spacetime symmetries is a vivid example: its radical is the four-dimensional translation algebra $\mathbb{R}^4$, and its Levi factor is the semisimple Lorentz algebra $\mathfrak{so}(3,1)$. The classification of arbitrary Lie algebras thus reduces to: classify the solvable algebras (hard, not yet complete in general) and classify the semisimple ones (the celebrated and complete result of Chapter 49).
