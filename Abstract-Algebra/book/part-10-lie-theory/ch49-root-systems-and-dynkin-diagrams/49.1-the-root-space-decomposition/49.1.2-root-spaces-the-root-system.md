# 49.1.2 Root Spaces $\mathfrak{g}_\alpha$; the Root System$\Phi \subset \mathfrak{h}^*$

Once a Cartan subalgebra $\mathfrak{h}$ is chosen, the commuting family of diagonalizable operators$\{\mathrm{ad}(H) : H \in \mathfrak{h}\}$ acts on$\mathfrak{g}$, and since these operators commute and are all diagonalizable over$\mathbb{C}$, they can be simultaneously diagonalized. The simultaneous eigenspaces are the *root spaces*. For each linear functional$\alpha \in \mathfrak{h}^*$, define$\mathfrak{g}_\alpha = \{X \in \mathfrak{g} : [H, X] = \alpha(H) X \text{ for all } H \in \mathfrak{h}\}$. The eigenvalue$\alpha(H)$ depends linearly on$H$, making$\alpha$ an element of the dual space$\mathfrak{h}^*$. The zero-eigenspace for all$H$ simultaneously is exactly$\mathfrak{h}$ itself (since$\mathfrak{h}$ is its own centralizer in a semisimple algebra), and the nonzero eigenspaces yield the *roots*: the set$\Phi = \{\alpha \in \mathfrak{h}^* \setminus \{0\} : \mathfrak{g}_\alpha \neq 0\}$.

The root space decomposition is $\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{h} \oplus \bigoplus_{\alpha \in \Phi} \mathfrak{g}_\alpha$. This is a direct sum, and it is the fundamental decomposition of$\mathfrak{g}$ that underlies all subsequent structure theory. The key non-trivial theorem is that each root space$\mathfrak{g}_\alpha$ is one-dimensional. This follows from the$\mathfrak{sl}_2$-theory: one shows that$\mathfrak{h} \oplus \mathfrak{g}_\alpha \oplus \mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}$ is an$\mathfrak{sl}_2$-subalgebra (or a direct sum of such), and the representation theory of$\mathfrak{sl}_2$ forces the eigenspaces to be one-dimensional.

A key structural property of root spaces: if $\alpha, \beta \in \Phi$ with$\alpha + \beta \neq 0$ and$\mathfrak{g}_\alpha, \mathfrak{g}_\beta$ are nonzero, then$[\mathfrak{g}_\alpha, \mathfrak{g}_\beta] \subseteq \mathfrak{g}_{\alpha + \beta}$. This is immediate from the definition: if$[H, X] = \alpha(H) X$ and$[H, Y] = \beta(H) Y$, then$[H, [X, Y]] = [[H, X], Y] + [X, [H, Y]] = (\alpha(H) + \beta(H))[X, Y]$. Thus root vectors "add" their roots, making the root space decomposition a grading of$\mathfrak{g}$ by the additive group generated by$\Phi$ inside$\mathfrak{h}^*$.

In the example $\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbb{C})$, the roots are the functionals$\alpha_{ij}(H) = h_i - h_j$ for$i \neq j$ (where$H = \mathrm{diag}(h_1, \ldots, h_n)$), and the root space$\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha_{ij}}$ is spanned by the matrix unit$E_{ij}$. There are$n^2 - n$ roots in total, and together with the$(n-1)$-dimensional Cartan subalgebra they account for all$n^2 - 1 = \dim \mathfrak{sl}_n$ dimensions. The set$\Phi$ of roots, viewed as a finite subset of$\mathfrak{h}^* \cong \mathbb{R}^\ell$ (via the Killing form), carries a rich geometric structure that is studied abstractly as a root system.

**Example 1.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_2$:$\mathfrak{h} = \mathrm{span}\{h\}$,$\Phi = \{+2\omega, -2\omega\}$ where$\omega \in \mathfrak{h}^*$ is defined by$\omega(h) = 1$. Conventionally one writes$\alpha = 2\omega$ as the positive root. The root spaces are$\mathfrak{g}_{+\alpha} = \mathrm{span}\{e\}$ and$\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha} = \mathrm{span}\{f\}$. The root system$A_1 = \{\pm\alpha\}$.

**Example 2.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_3$: rank 2,$\mathfrak{h} = \mathrm{span}\{H_1, H_2\}$. The 6 roots are$\pm\alpha_1$,$\pm\alpha_2$,$\pm(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2)$ where$\alpha_1(H_1) = 2$,$\alpha_1(H_2) = -1$,$\alpha_2(H_1) = -1$,$\alpha_2(H_2) = 2$ (simple roots), and$\alpha_1 + \alpha_2$ is the highest root. Root spaces:$\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha_1} = \mathrm{span}\{E_{12}\}$,$\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha_2} = \mathrm{span}\{E_{23}\}$,$\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha_1 + \alpha_2} = \mathrm{span}\{E_{13}\}$, and their negatives. Dimension check:$2 + 6 = 8 = \dim \mathfrak{sl}_3$. Root system:$A_2$ (hexagon).

**Example 3.** For $\mathfrak{so}(5, \mathbb{C})$ (type$B_2$): rank 2 with 8 roots. The simple roots are$\alpha_1$ (long) and$\alpha_2$ (short), with Cartan matrix$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$. The 8 roots include 2 long ($\pm\alpha_1 + \pm\alpha_2$ combinations) and 2 short ones, arranged in a square (not regular hexagon like$A_2$).

**Example 4.** The root space decomposition respects the Killing form: $B(\mathfrak{g}_\alpha, \mathfrak{g}_\beta) = 0$ unless$\alpha + \beta = 0$. This means the Killing form pairs$\mathfrak{g}_\alpha$ with$\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}$ non-degenerately, and since each root space is 1-dimensional, for each$\alpha \in \Phi$ the restriction$B|_{\mathfrak{g}_\alpha \times \mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}}$ is a non-degenerate pairing. This pairing is what allows normalization of the generators$e_\alpha, f_\alpha$ in the$\mathfrak{sl}_2$-triples.

The root space decomposition is the fundamental tool of semisimple Lie theory. All of the classification (Chapter 49) and representation theory (Chapter 50) flows from understanding how the root system $\Phi \subset \mathfrak{h}^*$ organizes the structure of$\mathfrak{g}$.
