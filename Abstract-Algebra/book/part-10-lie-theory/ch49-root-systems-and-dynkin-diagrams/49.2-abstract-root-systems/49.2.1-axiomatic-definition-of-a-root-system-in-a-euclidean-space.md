# 49.2.1 Axiomatic Definition of a Root System in a Euclidean Space

Having extracted four key properties of the root system associated to a semisimple Lie algebra, one defines an *abstract root system* as a finite set $\Phi \subset V \setminus \{0\}$ in a Euclidean space$(V, \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle)$ satisfying exactly those properties: (i)$\Phi$ spans$V$; (ii) for every$\alpha \in \Phi$, the only scalar multiples of$\alpha$ in$\Phi$ are$\pm\alpha$; (iii) for all$\alpha, \beta \in \Phi$, the Cartan integer$\langle \beta, \alpha^\vee \rangle = 2\langle \beta, \alpha \rangle / \langle \alpha, \alpha \rangle$ is an integer; (iv) for all$\alpha \in \Phi$, the reflection$s_\alpha(\beta) = \beta - \langle \beta, \alpha^\vee \rangle \alpha$ sends$\Phi$ to itself. The rank of the root system is$\dim V$. A root system is *irreducible* if it cannot be written as the orthogonal union of two nonempty proper subsets, each stable under all reflections.

The passage to abstract root systems is a major conceptual move. It shows that the geometry controlling the structure of simple Lie algebras is not intrinsically about Lie algebras at all — it is about finite reflection groups acting on Euclidean spaces. The classification problem for simple Lie algebras is thereby reduced to a problem in Euclidean geometry: classify all finite sets of vectors closed under a family of reflections and satisfying the integrality condition. This reduction is analogous to how the classification of finite Coxeter groups (groups generated by reflections) was reduced to a combinatorial problem.

Two root systems are isomorphic if there is an orthogonal linear isomorphism of their ambient Euclidean spaces sending one root system to the other. The condition (ii) — that roots come only in pairs $\pm\alpha$ — rules out root systems where, say,$2\alpha$ and$3\alpha$ could both be roots, keeping the geometry clean. Conditions (iii) and (iv) together impose the powerful integrality and reflection-stability that ultimately force the classification to be finite: there are only a small number of irreducible root systems up to isomorphism, corresponding exactly to the classical and exceptional simple Lie algebras.

The conditions also constrain the possible lengths of roots in a given root system. In any irreducible root system, roots can have at most two distinct lengths (long and short roots), and if roots of different lengths exist, the ratio of lengths is $\sqrt{2}$ (types$B_n$,$C_n$) or$\sqrt{3}$ (type$G_2$). The type$A_n$ root systems have all roots of the same length, as do$D_n$,$E_6$,$E_7$,$E_8$, and$F_4$ has two root lengths with ratio$\sqrt{2}$.

**Example 1.** The root system $A_1$: take$V = \mathbb{R}^1$ with the standard inner product, and$\Phi = \{1, -1\}$. Check: spans$\mathbb{R}^1$ ✓, only$\pm 1$ are scalar multiples ✓,$\langle 1, 1^\vee \rangle = 2 \in \mathbb{Z}$ ✓,$s_1(1) = 1 - 2 = -1 \in \Phi$ ✓. This is the root system of$\mathfrak{sl}_2$.

**Example 2.** The root system $A_2$: take$V = \mathbb{R}^2$,$\Phi = \{\pm e_1, \pm e_2, \pm(e_1 - e_2)\}$ (six roots, vertices of a regular hexagon after appropriate embedding). More concretely, place the roots at$\pm(1,0), \pm(0,1), \pm(1,-1)$ (scaled appropriately). All roots have the same length. The Cartan integers between adjacent roots (at$120°$) are$-1$. This is the root system of$\mathfrak{sl}_3$.

**Example 3.** The root system $B_2$: in$V = \mathbb{R}^2$, take$\Phi = \{\pm e_1, \pm e_2, \pm e_1 \pm e_2\}$ (8 roots: 4 short at$\pm e_i$, 4 long at$\pm e_1 \pm e_2$). Length of$e_i$ is 1, length of$e_1 \pm e_2$ is$\sqrt{2}$. Check integrality:$\langle e_2, e_1^\vee \rangle = 2\langle e_2, e_1 \rangle / |e_1|^2 = 0 \in \mathbb{Z}$ (orthogonal).$\langle e_2, (e_1+e_2)^\vee \rangle = 2\langle e_2, e_1+e_2 \rangle / |e_1+e_2|^2 = 2 \cdot 1/2 = 1 \in \mathbb{Z}$. Reflections work. This is the root system of$\mathfrak{sp}(4) \cong \mathfrak{so}(5)$.

**Example 4.** The root system $G_2$: in$V = \mathbb{R}^2$, take the 12 roots consisting of 6 short roots (vertices of a regular hexagon of radius 1) and 6 long roots (vertices of a regular hexagon of radius$\sqrt{3}$). The Cartan integers between a long root and an adjacent short root are$-3$ (long acts on short with Cartan integer$-1$, short acts on long with$-3$). This is the maximum allowed by$4\cos^2\theta \leq 3$ and corresponds to$\theta = 150°$. The root system of the exceptional Lie algebra$G_2$ is the only rank-2 root system with a triple bond.

The abstraction to root systems separates the Euclidean geometry from the algebraic structure, and the subsequent classification of root systems (Section 49.3) is a purely geometric theorem, independent of Lie algebra theory.
