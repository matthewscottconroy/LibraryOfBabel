# 49.2.2 Positive Roots and Simple Roots; the Weyl Chamber

A root system $\Phi$ comes in opposite pairs: for each root$\alpha$, the negative$-\alpha$ is also a root. To work effectively with$\Phi$, one needs a way to choose one root from each pair and to organize the roots into a manageable generating set. A *base* (or *simple system*)$\Delta \subset \Phi$ is a subset with two properties:$\Delta$ is a vector-space basis of$V$, and every root in$\Phi$ can be written as an integer linear combination of$\Delta$ in which all coefficients are non-negative (a *positive root*) or all coefficients are non-positive (a *negative root*). The elements of$\Delta$ are the *simple roots*$\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_\ell$, and they generate$\Phi$ combinatorially.

Bases exist and can be constructed geometrically. Choose a vector $t \in V$ not perpendicular to any root, and declare a root$\alpha$ positive if$\langle \alpha, t \rangle > 0$. The positive roots$\Phi^+$ are those with$\langle \cdot, t \rangle > 0$, and the simple roots are the positive roots that cannot be written as the sum of two positive roots. The resulting$\Delta$ is always a base, and every base arises this way for some generic vector$t$. Different choices of$t$ in the same connected component of$V \setminus \bigcup_\alpha H_\alpha$ (where$H_\alpha$ is the hyperplane perpendicular to$\alpha$) give the same base; different components give different bases.

The connected components of $V \setminus \bigcup_{\alpha \in \Phi} H_\alpha$ are the *Weyl chambers*. There are exactly$|W|$ Weyl chambers (one for each element of the Weyl group$W$), and they are permuted simply transitively by$W$. The *fundamental Weyl chamber* is the open cone$\{v \in V : \langle v, \alpha_i \rangle > 0 \text{ for all } i\}$ determined by a chosen base. The Weyl chambers provide a geometric partition of the ambient space that encodes the symmetry of the root system; the closure of any chamber is a fundamental domain for the action of$W$ on$V$.

The decomposition $\Phi = \Phi^+ \sqcup \Phi^-$ is fundamental for representation theory: positive roots are those by which the "raising operators"$e_\alpha$ raise weights, negative roots correspond to "lowering operators"$f_\alpha$. The Weyl vector$\rho = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha \in \Phi^+} \alpha$ (half-sum of positive roots) plays a distinguished role in the Weyl character formula.

**Example 1.** For $A_1 = \{\pm\alpha\}$: the two Weyl chambers are the positive and negative halves of the real line. Choosing$t > 0$ gives$\Phi^+ = \{\alpha\}$,$\Delta = \{\alpha\}$. The fundamental Weyl chamber is$\{v : v > 0\}$.

**Example 2.** For $A_2$ (hexagonal root system, 6 roots): choosing$t = (1, \epsilon)$ for small$\epsilon > 0$ gives$\Phi^+ = \{\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_1 + \alpha_2\}$ (the three roots in the upper half-plane), with simple roots$\Delta = \{\alpha_1, \alpha_2\}$. The fundamental Weyl chamber is the cone$\{v : \langle v, \alpha_i \rangle > 0, i=1,2\}$, one of 6 chambers (since$|W| = |S_3| = 6$).

**Example 3.** For $B_2$ (8 roots): the positive roots for the standard choice are$\{\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_1 + \alpha_2, \alpha_1 + 2\alpha_2\}$ (4 positive roots, consistent with$|\Phi^+| = 4 = 8/2$). The simple roots are$\alpha_1$ (long) and$\alpha_2$ (short). The 4 Weyl chambers (since$|W| = 8$ for$B_2 = C_2$) are the 8 sectors... actually$|W(B_2)| = 8$ and there are 8 Weyl chambers. The fundamental chamber is$\{v : \langle v, \alpha_1 \rangle > 0, \langle v, \alpha_2 \rangle > 0\}$.

**Example 4.** The Weyl vector for $A_2$:$\rho = \frac{1}{2}(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 + (\alpha_1 + \alpha_2)) = \alpha_1 + \alpha_2$. One can verify$\langle \rho, \alpha_i^\vee \rangle = 1$ for each simple root, so$\rho = \omega_1 + \omega_2$ (sum of fundamental weights), which is always true in general.

The choice of positive roots is an organizing choice, not an intrinsic one — all choices are related by the Weyl group. But once made, it allows the algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ to be decomposed as$\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{n}^- \oplus \mathfrak{h} \oplus \mathfrak{n}^+$ (triangular decomposition), where$\mathfrak{n}^\pm = \bigoplus_{\alpha \in \Phi^\pm} \mathfrak{g}_\alpha$, which is the foundation for the construction of Verma modules in Chapter 50.
