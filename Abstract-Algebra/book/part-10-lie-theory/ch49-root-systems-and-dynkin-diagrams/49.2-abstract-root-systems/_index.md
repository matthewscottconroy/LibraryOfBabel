# 49.2 Abstract Root Systems

Having extracted the root system from a semisimple Lie algebra, one can study root systems as purely combinatorial-geometric objects in their own right. An abstract root system is a finite set $\Phi$ of nonzero vectors in a real Euclidean space $E$ satisfying: (1) $\Phi$ spans $E$; (2) for each $\alpha \in \Phi$, the only scalar multiples of $\alpha$ in $\Phi$ are $\pm \alpha$; (3) for each $\alpha \in \Phi$, the reflection $s_\alpha: E \to E$ across the hyperplane orthogonal to $\alpha$ preserves $\Phi$; and (4) for all $\alpha, \beta \in \Phi$, the Cartan integer $\langle \beta, \alpha^\vee \rangle = \frac{2\langle \beta, \alpha \rangle}{\langle \alpha, \alpha \rangle}$ is an integer. These four axioms are a distillation of the properties verified in Section 49.1, and they encode a remarkable amount of rigidity: a root system is determined by very constrained combinatorial data.

The key structural insight is the choice of positive roots and simple roots. A hyperplane in $E$ that avoids all roots divides $\Phi$ into positive roots $\Phi^+$ and negative roots $\Phi^- = -\Phi^+$. Among the positive roots, the simple roots $\Delta = \{\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_r\}$ are those that cannot be written as a sum of two positive roots; they form a basis for $E$, and every positive root is a non-negative integer combination of the simple roots. The Cartan matrix $A = (A_{ij})$ with $A_{ij} = \langle \alpha_i, \alpha_j^\vee \rangle$ encodes all pairwise relationships between the simple roots, and it determines the root system up to isomorphism.

The Weyl group $W$ of a root system is the group of orthogonal transformations of $E$ generated by the reflections $s_{\alpha_i}$ across the hyperplanes orthogonal to the simple roots. It is a finite group that acts faithfully on the root system: $W \subset \text{Aut}(\Phi)$. For type $A_{n-1}$, the Weyl group is the symmetric group $S_n$ permuting the coordinates; for type $B_n$, it is the hyperoctahedral group of signed permutations. The Weyl chambers — the connected components of $E \setminus \bigcup_{\alpha \in \Phi} \alpha^\perp$ — are permuted simply transitively by $W$, so $|W|$ equals the number of chambers. Each chamber is a simplicial cone, and the "dominant Weyl chamber" $\{v : \langle v, \alpha_i \rangle > 0 \text{ for all } i\}$ will serve as the positive cone for highest weight theory in Chapter 50.
