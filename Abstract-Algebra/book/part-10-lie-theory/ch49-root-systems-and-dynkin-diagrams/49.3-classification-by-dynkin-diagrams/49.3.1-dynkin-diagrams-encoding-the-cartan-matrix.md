# 49.3.1 Dynkin Diagrams: Encoding the Cartan Matrix

The Cartan matrix is the essential data of a root system, but as a matrix of integers it is unwieldy for a first-pass classification. The *Dynkin diagram* is a graphical encoding that makes the structure immediately visible. The recipe is as follows: draw one node for each simple root $\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_\ell$; connect nodes$i$ and$j$ with$A_{ij} A_{ji} \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$ edges (so 0 edges means the roots are orthogonal, 1 edge means the roots make a$120°$ angle, 2 edges correspond to a$135°$ angle, and 3 edges to a$150°$ angle); when$A_{ij} \neq A_{ji}$ (i.e., when the two roots have different lengths), add an arrow on the edge pointing toward the shorter root. The Dynkin diagram is a labeled graph that completely encodes the Cartan matrix and hence the entire root system.

The simplicity of this encoding is striking. The number of edges between two nodes measures the "severity" of their interaction: orthogonal simple roots (zero edges) contribute independently, while roots connected by a triple edge (three bonds) are locked in a tight relationship that gives rise to the exceptional algebra $G_2$. The arrow direction distinguishes$B_n$ from$C_n$, which would otherwise look the same: both have a single double edge at one end of a chain, but$B_n$ has the arrow pointing toward the shorter root at the end (the short simple root), while$C_n$ has it reversed.

The key theorem is that two root systems (or equivalently, two simple Lie algebras over $\mathbb{C}$) are isomorphic if and only if their Dynkin diagrams are isomorphic as labeled graphs. This reduces the classification of simple Lie algebras to a purely combinatorial question about which labeled graphs can arise as Dynkin diagrams. The constraints (the Cartan integers must satisfy$A_{ij} A_{ji} \leq 3$, the graph must be connected for a simple algebra, and the matrix must be positive definite in a suitable sense) are strong enough to limit the possibilities to a finite and completely explicit list.

The positive-definiteness condition is worth explaining. A Cartan matrix must come from a root system where the inner product is positive definite on $V$. This translates to requiring that the symmetrized Cartan matrix (the matrix$D A D^{-1}$ where$D$ is a diagonal matrix of root lengths) is positive definite. For connected Dynkin diagrams, the positive-definiteness condition is equivalent to: the diagram must be a tree (no cycles), and it must be one of the specific types in the classification list. Cycle-containing diagrams give indefinite Cartan matrices corresponding to affine or hyperbolic Kac–Moody algebras.

**Example 1.** The $A_n$ Dynkin diagram:$n$ nodes in a chain$\circ - \circ - \cdots - \circ$ with single edges (since all roots have equal length and adjacent simple roots make$120°$ angles, giving one edge, no arrow). The Cartan matrix has$2$'s on the diagonal,$-1$'s on the off-diagonals adjacent to the diagonal, and$0$'s elsewhere — a tridiagonal matrix.

**Example 2.** The $B_n$ Dynkin diagram for$n \geq 2$: a chain of$n$ nodes with a double edge at the right end:$\circ - \circ - \cdots - \circ \Rightarrow \circ$ (arrow pointing right, toward the short root at the right end). The last two nodes have Cartan integers$A_{n-1,n} = -1$ and$A_{n,n-1} = -2$, giving a double edge with arrow pointing from$n-1$ (long) to$n$ (short).

**Example 3.** The $C_n$ Dynkin diagram for$n \geq 3$: also a chain with a double edge at the right end, but with the arrow reversed:$\circ - \circ - \cdots - \circ \Leftarrow \circ$. The Cartan integers at the double edge are$A_{n-1,n} = -2$,$A_{n,n-1} = -1$: the last node is long (arrow points left, toward the second-to-last short node). Compare with$B_n$ where the arrow points right.

**Example 4.** The $G_2$ Dynkin diagram: two nodes with a triple edge and an arrow:$\circ \Rrightarrow \circ$ (three edges, arrow pointing from the long root node toward the short root node). Cartan matrix:$A_{12} = -1$,$A_{21} = -3$, product$3$, angle$150°$. The$G_2$ diagram is the only connected Dynkin diagram with a triple edge; no rank-2 diagram with a quadruple edge exists (that would require$A_{12} A_{21} = 4$, but$4\cos^2\theta \leq 3$ for non-equal roots at obtuse angles strictly less than$180°$).

The Dynkin diagram encodes a remarkable amount of information in a tiny picture: the rank (number of nodes), the relative root lengths (arrow direction), the angles between simple roots (number of edges), and — via the classification theorem — the entire structure of the simple Lie algebra.
