# 49.3.2 The Classification: $A_n$,$B_n$,$C_n$,$D_n$,$E_6$,$E_7$,$E_8$,$F_4$,$G_2$

The classification of irreducible root systems — equivalently, of simple Lie algebras over $\mathbb{C}$ — is one of the great theorems of mathematics. The answer consists of four infinite families of *classical types* and five *exceptional types*, totaling exactly nine classes. That there are finitely many in each rank, and that the classification terminates, is itself a non-trivial theorem.

The classical types are: $A_n$ for$n \geq 1$, whose Dynkin diagram is a simple chain of$n$ nodes with single edges ($\circ - \circ - \cdots - \circ$), corresponding to the algebra$\mathfrak{sl}_{n+1}(\mathbb{C})$ of traceless$(n+1) \times (n+1)$ matrices;$B_n$ for$n \geq 2$, a chain with a double edge and inward arrow at the right end, corresponding to$\mathfrak{so}(2n+1)$;$C_n$ for$n \geq 3$, a chain with a double edge and outward arrow at the right end, corresponding to$\mathfrak{sp}(2n)$; and$D_n$ for$n \geq 4$, a chain with a fork (two nodes attached at the right end), corresponding to$\mathfrak{so}(2n)$. The lower bounds on$n$ avoid overlaps:$A_1 = B_1 = C_1$,$B_2 = C_2$,$A_3 = D_3$, and$D_2 = A_1 \oplus A_1$ (reducible), so the families are defined with ranges that make them genuinely distinct.

The exceptional types are $E_6$,$E_7$,$E_8$ (with$E$-shaped diagrams — a long chain with one branch point),$F_4$ (a chain of four nodes with a double edge in the middle), and$G_2$ (two nodes connected by a triple edge). These five exceptions do not fit into any infinite family; they arise from special geometric configurations that happen to satisfy the integrality and positive-definiteness constraints. There are exactly these types and no others: the classification proof shows that any connected Dynkin diagram satisfying the required conditions must be one of these nine, a result proved by exhaustive analysis of admissible subgraphs.

The key numerical data for each type are: $\dim \mathfrak{g} = \ell + 2|\Phi^+|$ (rank plus twice the number of positive roots),$|\Phi| = 2|\Phi^+|$, and$|W|$ (the Weyl group order). For the classical series:$A_n$ has$\dim = n(n+2)$,$|\Phi| = n(n+1)$,$|W| = (n+1)!$;$B_n$ has$\dim = n(2n+1)$,$|\Phi| = 2n^2$,$|W| = 2^n n!$;$C_n$ has$\dim = n(2n+1)$,$|\Phi| = 2n^2$,$|W| = 2^n n!$ (same as$B_n$ in dimension and Weyl group order);$D_n$ has$\dim = n(2n-1)$,$|\Phi| = 2n(n-1)$,$|W| = 2^{n-1} n!$.

**Example 1.** $A_1 \cong B_1 \cong C_1$: the single simple Lie algebra of rank 1 is$\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$, which corresponds to$SU(2)$ (or$SO(3)$,$Sp(2)$). Dimension 3, root system$\{+\alpha, -\alpha\}$, Weyl group$\mathbb{Z}/2$.

**Example 2.** $B_2 = C_2$: the rank-2 simple algebra$\mathfrak{so}(5) \cong \mathfrak{sp}(4)$. Dimension$2(2 \cdot 2 + 1) = 10$, root system with 8 roots (4 positive), Weyl group$(\mathbb{Z}/2)^2 \rtimes S_2$ of order 8.

**Example 3.** $A_3 = D_3$: the rank-3 simple algebra.$A_3 \cong \mathfrak{sl}_4$,$D_3 \cong \mathfrak{so}(6)$, and these are isomorphic. Dimension$A_3$:$3 \cdot 5 = 15 = D_3$:$3(2 \cdot 3 - 1) = 15$. Weyl group:$S_4$ (for$A_3$)$\cong$ the Weyl group of$D_3$, both of order 24.

**Example 4.** $E_8$: the largest exceptional algebra. Rank 8, dimension 248, root system with 240 roots (120 positive), Weyl group of order$696729600 = 2^{14} \cdot 3^5 \cdot 5^2 \cdot 7$. The$E_8$ root system is the densest sphere packing lattice in$\mathbb{R}^8$ (proven optimal by Viazovska, 2016). The$E$-series arise from starting with$A_5$ or$D_4$ and extending by one node — only$E_6, E_7, E_8$ satisfy positive definiteness (if you try to add a node to$E_8$ you get$\hat{E}_8$, the affine Kac–Moody diagram, which is only positive semidefinite).

The classification theorem is proved by showing that any connected positive-definite Dynkin diagram is one of these nine types. The proof uses the fact that any subdiagram of a valid diagram is also valid, and then exhaustively rules out all other configurations by showing they lead to non-positive-definite Cartan matrices.
