# 49.3 Classification by Dynkin Diagrams

A Dynkin diagram is a graph that encodes the Cartan matrix of a root system in a visually transparent way. It has one node for each simple root $\alpha_i$. Two nodes $i$ and $j$ are connected by $|A_{ij} A_{ji}|$ edges (where $A_{ij} = \langle \alpha_i, \alpha_j^\vee \rangle$), which can be $0$, $1$, $2$, or $3$. When the roots have unequal lengths (when $A_{ij} \neq A_{ji}$), an arrow is drawn on the bond pointing from the longer root to the shorter root — the arrow says "this root is shorter." The key fact is that the Cartan integer product $A_{ij} A_{ji} = 4\cos^2(\theta_{ij})$ where $\theta_{ij}$ is the angle between $\alpha_i$ and $\alpha_j$, and the constraint $A_{ij} A_{ji} \in \{0,1,2,3\}$ (which follows from the root system axioms) limits the possible configurations severely.

The classification theorem states that the connected Dynkin diagrams correspond exactly to: the four infinite families $A_n$ ($n \geq 1$), $B_n$ ($n \geq 2$), $C_n$ ($n \geq 3$), $D_n$ ($n \geq 4$), and the five exceptional diagrams $G_2$, $F_4$, $E_6$, $E_7$, $E_8$. The $A_n$ series is a chain of $n$ nodes connected by single bonds; $B_n$ and $C_n$ are chains with a double bond at one end, pointing in opposite directions; $D_n$ has a fork at one end. The exceptional diagrams have no infinite family analog — they arise from special geometric coincidences that can only occur for specific ranks. The proof that no other diagrams are admissible amounts to showing that the Cartan matrix must be positive definite, and the positive-definite integer Gram matrices form a finite list.

The significance of this classification can scarcely be overstated. It is the complete list of all simple Lie algebras over $\mathbb{C}$: the $A_n$ series corresponds to $\mathfrak{sl}_{n+1}$, $B_n$ to $\mathfrak{so}_{2n+1}$, $C_n$ to $\mathfrak{sp}_{2n}$, and $D_n$ to $\mathfrak{so}_{2n}$, while the exceptional algebras $G_2$, $F_4$, $E_6$, $E_7$, $E_8$ are genuine novelties. The dimensions of the exceptional algebras — 14, 52, 78, 133, 248 — have become significant numbers in mathematics and physics. The ADE pattern ($A_n$, $D_n$, $E_6$, $E_7$, $E_8$, the simply-laced diagrams) reappears in the classification of finite subgroups of $SU(2)$ via the McKay correspondence, in the classification of simple singularities in algebraic geometry, and in string theory — a remarkable and still not fully understood web of connections that suggests the Dynkin diagrams encode something fundamental about the structure of symmetric spaces at the deepest level.
