# 49.4.3 Reconstruction: Every Dynkin Diagram Gives a Unique Simple Lie Algebra

Serre's theorem, combined with the classification of Dynkin diagrams, closes the loop: the classification of simple Lie algebras over $\mathbb{C}$ is complete. The argument has two directions. On one side, every simple Lie algebra$\mathfrak{g}$ determines a root system, which determines a Dynkin diagram; on the other side, every connected Dynkin diagram in the classification list determines (via the Cartan matrix and Serre's relations) a Lie algebra. The theorem that these two constructions are inverse to each other — and that the Lie algebra produced by Serre's relations from a valid Dynkin diagram is indeed simple — completes the classification.

The simplicity of the Lie algebra constructed from a Dynkin diagram follows from the structure of Serre's relations: one shows that the algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ presented by these generators and relations has no proper nonzero ideals, essentially because any ideal must be a union of root spaces, and the root string structure forces such an ideal to be all of$\mathfrak{g}$ or$\{0\}$. The uniqueness — that the algebra is determined up to isomorphism by the Dynkin diagram — follows from the fact that the Cartan matrix uniquely determines the root system (up to isometry), which in turn determines the Lie algebra via the root space decomposition.

The significance of this reconstruction theorem is difficult to overstate. It reduces the entire classification of simple Lie algebras to a finite combinatorial problem — the enumeration of connected Dynkin diagrams satisfying certain positivity conditions — and then provides an explicit presentation of each algebra in terms of generators and relations. The classification is therefore not merely an existence result but a constructive one: given any Dynkin diagram from the list $A_n, B_n, C_n, D_n, E_6, E_7, E_8, F_4, G_2$, the corresponding simple Lie algebra can be written down explicitly as a quotient of a free Lie algebra by Serre's relations.

This explicit description underlies all of the subsequent theory: the classification of representations by highest weights, the Weyl character formula, and the rich interplay between Lie algebras, algebraic groups, and modular forms. The reconstruction theorem also explains why the "integrable" highest weight representations of a Kac–Moody algebra (the affine generalization) are the correct objects to study: they are the analogues of the finite-dimensional representations of simple Lie algebras, constructed from the same Serre-type presentation but with a generalized (affine) Cartan matrix.

**Example 1.** From the $A_2$ Dynkin diagram to$\mathfrak{sl}_3$: Start with generators$\{e_1, f_1, h_1, e_2, f_2, h_2\}$ and Cartan matrix$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$. The presentation gives:$[h_i, h_j] = 0$,$[h_1, e_1] = 2e_1$,$[h_1, e_2] = -e_2$,$[e_1, f_1] = h_1$,$[e_1, f_2] = 0$,$[e_1, [e_1, e_2]] = 0$,$[e_2, [e_2, e_1]] = 0$, plus$f$-versions. The resulting algebra has basis$\{e_1, e_2, [e_1, e_2], f_1, f_2, [f_1, f_2], h_1, h_2\}$ (8-dimensional), and one identifies$e_1 = E_{12}$,$e_2 = E_{23}$,$[e_1, e_2] = E_{13}$, etc. This is$\mathfrak{sl}_3$.

**Example 2.** From the $G_2$ Dynkin diagram to the exceptional$\mathfrak{g}_2$: the 14 generators$\{e_1, e_2, f_1, f_2, h_1, h_2\}$ plus the root vectors for the 6 positive roots generate the 14-dimensional algebra. Serre's relations include$(\mathrm{ad}\, e_1)^2 e_2 = 0$ and$(\mathrm{ad}\, e_2)^4 e_1 = 0$. The non-trivial part of the theorem is that these relations force the algebra to be 14-dimensional (and not larger or smaller), simple, and unique up to isomorphism.

**Example 3.** The isomorphism $A_3 \cong D_3$: both Dynkin diagrams produce the same Lie algebra$\mathfrak{sl}_4 \cong \mathfrak{so}(6)$. The$A_3$ Cartan matrix gives$\mathfrak{sl}_4$ directly; the$D_3$ Cartan matrix (which has the same shape as$A_3$ by the coincidence that the fork of$D_3$ is the same as the chain of$A_3$) gives$\mathfrak{so}(6)$. The reconstruction theorem says both produce the same algebra, and the explicit isomorphism$\mathfrak{sl}_4 \cong \mathfrak{so}(6)$ is realized by the spin representation: the 4-dimensional spinor representation of$\mathfrak{so}(6)$ is the standard representation of$\mathfrak{sl}_4$.

**Example 4.** The reconstruction theorem and Chevalley groups: for each Dynkin diagram, the Chevalley basis (Section 49.4.1) gives an integral form of the Lie algebra, which can then be reduced modulo any prime $p$. The reconstruction theorem over$\mathbb{C}$ ensures that the resulting Lie algebra over$\mathbb{F}_p$ (for large enough$p$) is still simple, and the corresponding group (the Chevalley group) is a finite simple group. The entire classification of finite simple groups (excepting the sporadic groups) is built on this foundation.

The classification theorem — Dynkin diagrams classify simple Lie algebras — is thus not merely a beautiful piece of pure mathematics but the foundation of a vast enterprise connecting algebra, geometry, number theory, and physics.
