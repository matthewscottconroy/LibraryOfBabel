# Chapter 49 — Root Systems and Dynkin Diagrams

One of the most astonishing results in all of mathematics is the complete classification of simple Lie algebras over $\mathbb{C}$. The result is a short finite list — the classical families $A_n$, $B_n$, $C_n$, $D_n$ and the five exceptional algebras $G_2$, $F_4$, $E_6$, $E_7$, $E_8$ — and it is achieved not by grinding through cases but by reducing the problem to a combinatorial question about configurations of vectors in a Euclidean space. This reduction is the theory of root systems and Dynkin diagrams, and it stands as a paradigm of how abstract algebra can be controlled by beautiful geometric data.

The path from a semisimple Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ to its root system begins with the choice of a Cartan subalgebra $\mathfrak{h} \subset \mathfrak{g}$: a maximal abelian subalgebra consisting entirely of ad-semisimple elements. Because the elements of $\mathfrak{h}$ simultaneously diagonalize the adjoint action, the Lie algebra decomposes as $\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{h} \oplus \bigoplus_{\alpha \in \Phi} \mathfrak{g}_\alpha$, where each root space $\mathfrak{g}_\alpha = \{x \in \mathfrak{g} : [h, x] = \alpha(h) x \text{ for all } h \in \mathfrak{h}\}$ is associated to a linear functional $\alpha \in \mathfrak{h}^*$ called a root. The set $\Phi$ of roots is finite, spans $\mathfrak{h}^*$, and satisfies a rigid set of axioms: it is symmetric ($-\alpha \in \Phi$ whenever $\alpha \in \Phi$), it is invariant under the reflections $s_\alpha(\beta) = \beta - \langle \beta, \alpha^\vee \rangle \alpha$ where $\alpha^\vee = 2\alpha/\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle$ is the coroot, and the Cartan integers $\langle \beta, \alpha^\vee \rangle$ are always integers. These axioms are the definition of an abstract root system.

From the root system one extracts still more combinatorial structure. Choosing a hyperplane in $\mathfrak{h}^*_{\mathbb{R}}$ that avoids all roots divides them into positive and negative, and among the positive roots a minimal set of simple roots $\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_r$ (where $r = \dim \mathfrak{h}$ is the rank) is selected; every positive root is a non-negative integer combination of the simple roots. The Cartan matrix $A_{ij} = \langle \alpha_i, \alpha_j^\vee \rangle$ encodes the pairwise geometry of the simple roots, and from the Cartan matrix one reads off the Dynkin diagram: a graph with $r$ nodes where the $i$-th and $j$-th nodes are connected by $A_{ij} A_{ji} \in \{0,1,2,3\}$ edges, with an arrow when the roots have different lengths. The classification theorem states that connected Dynkin diagrams correspond exactly to simple Lie algebras, and the admissible connected diagrams are precisely the $A_n$, $B_n$, $C_n$, $D_n$ series and the five exceptional cases. The proof that no other diagrams are possible involves a beautiful analysis with integer quadratic forms.

The Weyl group $W$, generated by the simple reflections $s_{\alpha_i}$, acts on the root system and on the weight lattice. It is a finite group that encodes the symmetry of the root system: for type $A_n$ it is the symmetric group $S_{n+1}$, for $B_n$ and $C_n$ it is the hyperoctahedral group $(\mathbb{Z}/2)^n \rtimes S_n$, and so on. The Weyl chambers are the connected components of $\mathfrak{h}^*_{\mathbb{R}}$ with the root hyperplanes removed, and $W$ acts simply transitively on them. Understanding the Weyl group is indispensable for representation theory: the Weyl character formula in Chapter 50 is a sum over the Weyl group. The Serre relations, finally, give a presentation of any simple Lie algebra by generators and relations read directly from the Dynkin diagram, confirming that the Dynkin diagram determines the Lie algebra uniquely and completely.
