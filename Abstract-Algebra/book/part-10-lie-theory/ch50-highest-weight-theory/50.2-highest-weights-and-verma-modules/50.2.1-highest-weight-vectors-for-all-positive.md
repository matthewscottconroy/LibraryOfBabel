# 50.2.1 Highest Weight Vectors: $e_\alpha \cdot v_\lambda = 0$ for All Positive$\alpha$

Having understood that weights form a finite subset of the weight lattice and that root vectors shift weights, one immediately faces a key question: where does the weight space decomposition "start"? In a finite-dimensional representation, the set of weights is bounded, and since positive root vectors shift weights upward, there must exist weights that cannot be shifted further. The vectors sitting in such maximal weight spaces are the highest weight vectors, and they are the starting point from which any finite-dimensional irreducible representation can be reconstructed.

A vector $v \in V_\lambda$ is a *highest weight vector* of weight$\lambda$ if$e_\alpha \cdot v = 0$ for all positive roots$\alpha$. Since$e_\alpha$ shifts the weight of a vector by$+\alpha$, the condition says that$v$ lies in the "ceiling" of the weight diagram: no positive root vector can raise$v$ to a higher weight space. In any finite-dimensional representation, such vectors exist: since the weight set is finite and the partial order on weights is well-founded (there is a maximum in the dominant direction), one can always find a maximal weight$\lambda$, and any nonzero vector in$V_\lambda$ is then automatically annihilated by all$e_\alpha$.

The weight $\lambda$ of a highest weight vector is called the *highest weight* of the (sub)representation generated by$v$. The subrepresentation$\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g}) \cdot v$ generated by$v$ is a highest weight module — it is spanned by all vectors obtained from$v$ by applying lowering operators$f_\alpha$. The crucial point, which we develop in the next subsections, is that the isomorphism type of this subrepresentation is completely determined by$\lambda$ alone: there is, up to isomorphism, exactly one irreducible module with highest weight$\lambda$.

It suffices to check the condition $e_\alpha \cdot v = 0$ only for the simple positive roots$\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_\ell$: if$e_i \cdot v = 0$ for all simple roots and$v \in V_\lambda$, then$e_\alpha \cdot v = 0$ for all positive roots$\alpha$ (since every positive root vector can be written as a repeated bracket of simple root vectors, and by the weight argument, applying$e_\alpha$ to$v$ would land in$V_{\lambda + \alpha}$ which is empty for$\lambda$ maximal). This is an important practical simplification.

**Example 1.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ and$V_n$: the vector$v_0$ is the highest weight vector, with$e \cdot v_0 = 0$ and$h \cdot v_0 = n v_0$. All other weight spaces$V_{n-2}, V_{n-4}, \ldots, V_{-n}$ have vectors$f^k v_0$ that are not highest weight vectors (since$e \cdot f^k v_0 = k(n-k+1) f^{k-1} v_0 \neq 0$ for$k \geq 1$).

**Example 2.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_3$ and the standard representation$\mathbb{C}^3$: the highest weight vector is$e_1$ (the first standard basis vector, with weight$\epsilon_1$). It satisfies$E_{12} \cdot e_1 = 0$ (the image of$e_1$ under$E_{12}$ would land in weight$\epsilon_1 + (\epsilon_1 - \epsilon_2) = 2\epsilon_1 - \epsilon_2$, which is not a weight of$\mathbb{C}^3$). Similarly$E_{13} \cdot e_1 = 0$,$E_{23} \cdot e_1 = 0$. The highest weight is$\epsilon_1 = \omega_1$ (the first fundamental weight of$\mathfrak{sl}_3$).

**Example 3.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_3$ and the adjoint representation: the highest weight vector is$E_{13}$ (root vector for the highest root$\alpha_1 + \alpha_2$). It satisfies$e_{\alpha_1} \cdot E_{13} = [E_{12}, E_{13}] = 0$ and$e_{\alpha_2} \cdot E_{13} = [E_{23}, E_{13}] = 0$. The highest weight is$\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 = \omega_1 + \omega_2$ in terms of fundamental weights.

**Example 4.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_3$ and$L(2\omega_1)$ (the symmetric square of the standard representation): the highest weight vector is$e_1 \otimes e_1$ (or$e_1^2$ in$\mathrm{Sym}^2(\mathbb{C}^3)$). Its weight is$2\epsilon_1 = 2\omega_1$. Applying$e_{\alpha_1} = E_{12}$ or$e_{\alpha_2} = E_{23}$ gives zero, confirming it is the highest weight vector.

The concept of a highest weight vector reduces the classification of irreducible representations to the classification of their "starting points" — the dominant integral weights. Once the highest weight is fixed, the entire representation is determined by applying lowering operators, which is the content of the Verma module theory in Section 50.2.
