# 50.2.4 $M(\lambda)$ Has a Unique Irreducible Quotient$L(\lambda)$

The Verma module $M(\lambda)$ is in general far from irreducible — it can contain many proper submodules, each generated by vectors that happen to be annihilated by all positive root vectors. But despite this complexity, Verma modules have a remarkable property: there is a unique minimal quotient, and that quotient is irreducible. This is the content of the present subsection, and it is the key step that connects the construction of Verma modules to the classification of irreducible representations.

The key structural fact is that $M(\lambda)$ has a unique maximal proper submodule$J(\lambda)$. To see this, one observes that any submodule$N \subsetneq M(\lambda)$ cannot contain the highest weight vector$v_\lambda$ (because if it did,$N$ would contain all of$\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g}) \cdot v_\lambda = M(\lambda)$). Since$v_\lambda$ is the unique weight vector of weight$\lambda$ up to scalars, every proper submodule is contained in the direct sum of the weight spaces$M(\lambda)_\mu$ for$\mu < \lambda$. The sum of all proper submodules is therefore still a proper submodule (it still misses$v_\lambda$), and this sum is$J(\lambda)$ — the unique maximal proper submodule.

The quotient $L(\lambda) = M(\lambda)/J(\lambda)$ is the unique irreducible$\mathfrak{g}$-module with highest weight$\lambda$. It is irreducible by construction (any proper submodule of$L(\lambda)$ would lift to a proper submodule of$M(\lambda)$ strictly containing$J(\lambda)$, contradicting maximality). Conversely, any irreducible module$V$ with a highest weight vector of weight$\lambda$ is a quotient of$M(\lambda)$ by universality, and since$V$ is irreducible the kernel of the surjection is maximal, hence equal to$J(\lambda)$, giving$V \cong L(\lambda)$. This identifies the irreducible representations with highest weight: they are in bijection with$\mathfrak{h}^*$.

The remaining question — which $\lambda$ give finite-dimensional$L(\lambda)$ — is answered by the dominant integrality condition. For non-dominant or non-integral$\lambda$, the module$L(\lambda)$ exists but is infinite-dimensional, and it lacks the "nice" structure that makes representation theory tractable. For dominant integral$\lambda$,$L(\lambda)$ is finite-dimensional and is the protagonist of Chapter 50.

**Example 1.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ and$\lambda = 2$:$J(2)$ is generated by$f^3 v_2$ (weight$2 - 6 = -4$). The quotient$L(2) = M(2)/J(2)$ has basis$\{v_2, f v_2, f^2 v_2\}$ with weights$2, 0, -2$. This is$V_2$, the 3-dimensional irreducible.

**Example 2.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ and$\lambda = 1/2$ (non-integral):$M(1/2)$ is irreducible, so$J(1/2) = 0$ and$L(1/2) = M(1/2)$. This infinite-dimensional irreducible is part of the representation theory of$\mathfrak{sl}_2$ but has no classical analogue in the finite-dimensional story.

**Example 3.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_3$ and$\lambda = \omega_1 + \omega_2$ (the highest weight of the adjoint representation):$L(\omega_1 + \omega_2) = \mathfrak{sl}_3$ (the adjoint representation, dimension 8). The Verma module$M(\omega_1 + \omega_2)$ is infinite-dimensional, and$J(\omega_1 + \omega_2)$ is a large submodule generated by several singular vectors. Taking the quotient gives exactly the 8-dimensional adjoint representation.

**Example 4.** The structure of $J(\lambda)$ for dominant integral$\lambda$: it is generated by the "singular vectors"$f_i^{\langle \lambda, \alpha_i^\vee \rangle + 1} v_\lambda$ for each simple root$\alpha_i$. These are the maximal submodules of$M(\lambda)$ that must be killed to obtain the finite-dimensional$L(\lambda)$. For$\mathfrak{sl}_2$ and$\lambda = n$:$J(n)$ is generated by$f^{n+1} v_n$, consistent with the above.

The existence and uniqueness of $L(\lambda)$ for every$\lambda \in \mathfrak{h}^*$ provides the complete indexing set for irreducible representations. The finite-dimensional irreducibles — our primary objects of interest — are exactly the$L(\lambda)$ for$\lambda \in P^+$ (dominant integral weights), as established in Section 50.3.
