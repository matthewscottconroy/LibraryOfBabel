# 50.3.1 Dominant Integral Weights: $\langle \lambda, \alpha_i^\vee \rangle \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$

The question of which highest weights $\lambda$ give rise to finite-dimensional irreducible representations$L(\lambda)$ has a clean and complete answer: precisely those$\lambda$ that are dominant integral. Understanding why this condition is both necessary and sufficient requires combining the$\mathfrak{sl}_2$-analysis with the structure of the Verma module, and the answer has a satisfying geometric meaning in terms of the Weyl chamber.

A weight $\lambda \in P$ is *dominant integral* if$\langle \lambda, \alpha_i^\vee \rangle \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ for all simple roots$\alpha_i$. The integrality is already guaranteed by membership in the weight lattice$P$, so the real content of the condition is the non-negativity: one requires that$\lambda$ lies in the closed dominant Weyl chamber, that is, on the correct side of each simple-root hyperplane. The set of dominant integral weights is$P^+ = \{\lambda = \sum m_i \omega_i : m_i \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\}$, the non-negative integer combinations of fundamental weights.

To see why this matters, consider what happens along a single $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-triple for the simple root$\alpha_i$. The restriction of$L(\lambda)$ to this subalgebra must be a direct sum of finite-dimensional$\mathfrak{sl}_2$-representations. The highest weight in this restriction is$\langle \lambda, \alpha_i^\vee \rangle$, which must therefore be a non-negative integer. If$\langle \lambda, \alpha_i^\vee \rangle$ were negative, the$\mathfrak{sl}_2$-subalgebra would force the existence of an infinite-dimensional component, preventing$L(\lambda)$ from being finite-dimensional. Conversely, when$\langle \lambda, \alpha_i^\vee \rangle \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ for all$i$, the$\mathfrak{sl}_2$-triples can be used to control all lowering operators simultaneously and show that$M(\lambda)$ has a finite-dimensional quotient. The dominant integral weights are precisely the lattice points in the closed dominant chamber, and they parametrize all finite-dimensional irreducibles.

The necessity of the condition is clear: if $L(\lambda)$ is finite-dimensional, then for each simple root$\alpha_i$ the restriction to the$\mathfrak{sl}_2$-triple gives a finite-dimensional$\mathfrak{sl}_2$-representation with highest weight$\langle \lambda, \alpha_i^\vee \rangle$, which must be a non-negative integer. The sufficiency — that the condition is also sufficient — is the deeper and more difficult direction, which is the content of Section 50.3.4.

**Example 1.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_2$: the condition is$\langle \lambda, h^\vee \rangle = \lambda(h) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$, i.e.,$\lambda = n\omega$ for$n \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$. The dominant integral weights are$\{0, \omega, 2\omega, 3\omega, \ldots\}$, corresponding to$V_0, V_1, V_2, V_3, \ldots$ with dimensions$1, 2, 3, 4, \ldots$.

**Example 2.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_3$: the dominant integral weights are$\lambda = m\omega_1 + n\omega_2$ with$m, n \geq 0$. The Dynkin labels$(m, n)$ are the values$\langle \lambda, \alpha_1^\vee \rangle = m$ and$\langle \lambda, \alpha_2^\vee \rangle = n$. For example:$(1,0)$ gives the standard representation$\mathbb{C}^3$ (dimension 3);$(0,1)$ gives the dual$(\mathbb{C}^3)^*$ (dimension 3);$(1,1)$ gives the adjoint representation (dimension 8);$(2,0)$ gives$\mathrm{Sym}^2(\mathbb{C}^3)$ (dimension 6).

**Example 3.** The weight $(3, 0)$ for$\mathfrak{sl}_3$: dominant integral, Dynkin labels$(3,0)$. The Weyl dimension formula gives$\dim L(3,0) = \frac{1}{2}(3+1)(0+1)(3+0+2) = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 4 \cdot 1 \cdot 5 = 10$. This is the space of degree-3 symmetric tensors on$\mathbb{C}^3$, i.e.,$\mathrm{Sym}^3(\mathbb{C}^3)$.

**Example 4.** A non-dominant weight for $\mathfrak{sl}_3$:$\lambda = \omega_1 - \omega_2$. The Dynkin labels are$(1, -1)$. Since$\langle \lambda, \alpha_2^\vee \rangle = -1 < 0$, this weight is not dominant integral. The corresponding$L(\lambda)$ is infinite-dimensional. The restriction to the$\mathfrak{sl}_2$-triple for$\alpha_2$ would have to contain a representation with highest weight$-1$, which is impossible in the finite-dimensional theory (but corresponds to an infinite-dimensional "lowest weight" module going in the other direction).

The dominant integral weights form the positive cone $P^+ \cong \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^\ell$ inside the weight lattice, and they parametrize the finite-dimensional irreducibles in perfect bijection. This is the classification theorem, developed in detail in Sections 50.3.3 and 50.3.4.
