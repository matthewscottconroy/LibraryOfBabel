# 50.3.2 Fundamental Weights $\omega_i$; the Positive Cone$P^+$

The dominant integral weights $P^+$ form a free commutative monoid, and its generators — the fundamental weights — play a role analogous to that of a basis for a vector space. Understanding the fundamental weights explicitly is essential both for computations and for understanding the geometry of the weight lattice.

The *fundamental weights* $\omega_1, \ldots, \omega_\ell$ are defined by the duality condition$\langle \omega_i, \alpha_j^\vee \rangle = \delta_{ij}$: the$i$-th fundamental weight pairs to$1$ with the$i$-th simple coroot and to$0$ with all others. This makes the$\omega_i$ precisely the dual basis to the simple coroots in$\mathfrak{h}^*$. Every dominant integral weight$\lambda$ has a unique expression$\lambda = m_1 \omega_1 + \cdots + m_\ell \omega_\ell$ with$m_i = \langle \lambda, \alpha_i^\vee \rangle \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$; the integers$m_i$ are called the *Dynkin labels* of$\lambda$.

The fundamental representations $L(\omega_i)$ are the building blocks from which more complex representations are constructed (for instance, via tensor products). For$\mathfrak{sl}_2$, there is only one fundamental weight$\omega_1$, and$L(\omega_1)$ is the standard two-dimensional representation. For$\mathfrak{sl}_{n+1}$, the fundamental weights correspond to the exterior powers of the standard representation:$L(\omega_i) \cong \bigwedge^i \mathbb{C}^{n+1}$. This connects back to familiar constructions from linear algebra and illustrates why the fundamental weights are the natural generators.

The positive cone $P^+$ is thus the set of weights whose Dynkin labels are all non-negative, a discrete analogue of the closed positive orthant in$\mathbb{R}^\ell$. The fundamental weights$\omega_1, \ldots, \omega_\ell$ are the "corners" of the cone, corresponding to the extreme rays. The Weyl vector$\rho = \omega_1 + \cdots + \omega_\ell$ (sum of all fundamental weights) lies at a distinguished interior point of the cone and plays a fundamental role in the character and dimension formulas.

**Example 1.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_2$:$\omega = \omega_1$ with$\langle \omega, h^\vee \rangle = 1$. As a functional on$\mathfrak{h} = \mathrm{span}\{h\}$:$\omega(h) = 1$. In terms of the root:$\alpha = 2\omega$, so$\omega = \alpha/2$. The fundamental weight is "half the root." The Weyl vector:$\rho = \omega$,$\rho(h) = 1$.

**Example 2.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_3$:$\omega_1$ and$\omega_2$ satisfy$\langle \omega_i, \alpha_j^\vee \rangle = \delta_{ij}$. In the coordinates$\epsilon_1, \epsilon_2, \epsilon_3$ on$\mathbb{R}^3$ restricted to$\sum h_i = 0$:$\omega_1 = \frac{2}{3}\epsilon_1 - \frac{1}{3}\epsilon_2 - \frac{1}{3}\epsilon_3 = \epsilon_1 - \frac{1}{3}(\epsilon_1 + \epsilon_2 + \epsilon_3)$. Simpler:$\omega_1 = \alpha_1/3 + 2\alpha_2/3$... let us just state$L(\omega_1) = \mathbb{C}^3$,$L(\omega_2) = (\mathbb{C}^3)^* = \bigwedge^2 \mathbb{C}^3$ (both 3-dimensional), and$L(\omega_1 + \omega_2) = \mathfrak{sl}_3$ (the adjoint, 8-dimensional).

**Example 3.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_4$ ($A_3$): the three fundamental representations are$L(\omega_1) = \mathbb{C}^4$ (standard, dimension 4),$L(\omega_2) = \bigwedge^2 \mathbb{C}^4$ (dimension 6),$L(\omega_3) = \bigwedge^3 \mathbb{C}^4$ (dimension 4, isomorphic to the dual of the standard). The Weyl vector$\rho = \omega_1 + \omega_2 + \omega_3$ has Dynkin labels$(1,1,1)$.

**Example 4.** For $\mathfrak{sp}(4)$ ($C_2$): two fundamental weights$\omega_1$ (giving the standard 4-dimensional representation) and$\omega_2$ (giving a 5-dimensional representation, the "adjoint-like" irreducible). The Weyl vector$\rho = \omega_1 + \omega_2$, and$\langle \rho, \alpha_1^\vee \rangle = 1$,$\langle \rho, \alpha_2^\vee \rangle = 1$. All dominant weights are$m\omega_1 + n\omega_2$ with$m, n \geq 0$, and the Weyl dimension formula gives their dimensions.

The fundamental weights and fundamental representations are the atoms of representation theory: every finite-dimensional irreducible is a unique (dominant) combination of the fundamental weights, and every representation decomposes into irreducibles, each of which is a highest weight module. This is the complete picture of finite-dimensional representations of $\mathfrak{g}$.
