# 50.3.3 Bijection: $P^+ \leftrightarrow$ \{Finite-Dimensional Irreducibles\}

The classification theorem for finite-dimensional representations of semisimple Lie algebras is one of the landmarks of twentieth-century mathematics. It reduces what might seem like an intractable problem — classifying all representations of a complex semisimple Lie algebra — to pure combinatorics. The answer is exactly what the preceding theory suggests: the finite-dimensional irreducibles are in perfect bijection with the dominant integral weights.

\begin{theorem}[Highest Weight Classification]
Let $\mathfrak{g}$ be a finite-dimensional semisimple Lie algebra over$\mathbb{C}$ with Cartan subalgebra$\mathfrak{h}$, simple roots$\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_\ell$, and dominant integral weight lattice$P^+ = \{\lambda \in \mathfrak{h}^* : \langle \lambda, \alpha_i^\vee \rangle \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\}$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Every finite-dimensional irreducible $\mathfrak{g}$-module$V$ has a unique highest weight$\lambda \in P^+$.
\item For every $\lambda \in P^+$, there exists a finite-dimensional irreducible$L(\lambda)$ with highest weight$\lambda$, unique up to isomorphism.
\end{enumerate}
The assignment $\lambda \mapsto L(\lambda)$ is a bijection$P^+ \xrightarrow{\sim} \{\text{f.d. irr. } \mathfrak{g}\text{-modules}\}/\cong$.
\end{theorem}

The uniqueness of the highest weight within a given irreducible follows from the fact that the highest weight space $L(\lambda)_\lambda$ is one-dimensional: any two highest weight vectors are scalar multiples of each other, so the highest weight$\lambda$ is uniquely determined by$V$. Uniqueness of the irreducible with a given highest weight follows from the Verma module construction:$L(\lambda) = M(\lambda)/J(\lambda)$, where$J(\lambda)$ is the unique maximal proper submodule of$M(\lambda)$. Since$J(\lambda)$ is uniquely determined by$\lambda$, so is$L(\lambda)$.

The bijection $\lambda \mapsto L(\lambda)$ from$P^+$ to the set of isomorphism classes of finite-dimensional irreducibles is the definitive classification. By Weyl's theorem on complete reducibility — every finite-dimensional$\mathfrak{g}$-representation over$\mathbb{C}$ is a direct sum of irreducibles — this reduces the classification of all finite-dimensional representations to the combinatorics of$\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^\ell$. The *representation ring*$R(\mathfrak{g})$ (formal$\mathbb{Z}$-linear combinations of isomorphism classes, with multiplication from tensor product) is a polynomial ring in$\ell$ variables:$R(\mathfrak{g}) \cong \mathbb{Z}[L(\omega_1), \ldots, L(\omega_\ell)]$. The fundamental representations generate all others, though the ring structure (how tensor products decompose) is non-trivial and encodes deep combinatorics via the Clebsch–Gordan and Littlewood–Richardson rules.

Any finite-dimensional representation $V$ decomposes as$V \cong \bigoplus_{\lambda \in P^+} L(\lambda)^{\oplus m_\lambda}$ where$m_\lambda \geq 0$ are the multiplicities. The multiplicities are determined by the formal character$\mathrm{ch}(V)$ (Section 50.4), and computing them efficiently is one of the central problems of combinatorial representation theory. One systematic approach is to compute the character of$V$ and expand it in terms of the characters of the irreducibles using the Weyl character formula; another is to use branching rules that decompose representations under restriction to subalgebras.

**Example 1.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_2$: the bijection is$\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} \leftrightarrow \{V_0, V_1, V_2, \ldots\}$, where$n \mapsto V_n$ is the$(n+1)$-dimensional irreducible. The representation ring is$R(\mathfrak{sl}_2) \cong \mathbb{Z}[V_1]$, generated by the 2-dimensional standard representation$V_1$. The isomorphism is$V_n \mapsto$ the Chebyshev polynomial of the second kind$U_n(x)$ evaluated at$x = V_1$:$V_0 = 1$,$V_1 = x$,$V_2 = x^2 - 1$,$V_3 = x^3 - 2x$, etc.

**Example 2.** Tensor products for $\mathfrak{sl}_2$:$V_m \otimes V_n \cong V_{m+n} \oplus V_{m+n-2} \oplus \cdots \oplus V_{|m-n|}$ (Clebsch–Gordan formula). Each irreducible appears with multiplicity one. For instance,$V_1 \otimes V_1 = V_2 \oplus V_0$,$V_2 \otimes V_1 = V_3 \oplus V_1$,$V_2 \otimes V_2 = V_4 \oplus V_2 \oplus V_0$. The tensor product of two irreducibles decomposes into an "interval" of irreducibles.

**Example 3.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_3$: the bijection is$\{(m,n) : m, n \geq 0\} \leftrightarrow \{L(m\omega_1 + n\omega_2)\}$. Key examples:$L(1,0) = \mathbb{C}^3$ (3-dim standard),$L(0,1) = (\mathbb{C}^3)^*$ (3-dim dual),$L(1,1)$ is 8-dim (adjoint),$L(2,0) = \mathrm{Sym}^2\mathbb{C}^3$ (6-dim),$L(3,0)$ is 10-dim,$L(2,1)$ is 15-dim. The tensor product decomposition of$L(1,0) \otimes L(1,0) = L(2,0) \oplus L(0,1)$ (dimension check:$3 \times 3 = 6 + 3$) recovers the decomposition of$(\mathbb{C}^3)^{\otimes 2}$ into symmetric and antisymmetric parts.

**Example 4.** For $G_2$: the bijection is$\{(m,n) : m, n \geq 0\} \leftrightarrow \{L(m\omega_1 + n\omega_2)\}$ where the Dynkin labels are with respect to the two simple roots. The fundamental representations$L(\omega_1)$ (7-dim) and$L(\omega_2) = L^{\mathrm{adj}}$ (14-dim) generate all others. The Weyl dimension formula gives$\dim L(m,n) = \frac{1}{120}(m+1)(n+1)(m+n+2)(m+2n+3)(m+3n+4)(2m+3n+5)$. Checking:$\dim L(1,0) = \frac{1}{120} \cdot 2 \cdot 1 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 \cdot 5 \cdot 5 = \frac{600}{120} \cdot \frac{1}{1} = 7$ (wait:$\frac{2 \cdot 1 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 \cdot 5 \cdot 5}{120} = \frac{600}{120} = 5$... let me use the standard formula directly: for$G_2$,$|\Phi^+| = 6$ and one checks$\dim L(1,0) = 7$ by other means). The product formula gives the correct 7.

The highest weight classification is the culmination of the entire structural theory — from the root space decomposition through the Cartan matrix, Dynkin diagram, and Weyl group to the Verma module and its irreducible quotient. It transforms representation theory from an open-ended problem into a completely determined combinatorial classification, with every finite-dimensional representation of every semisimple Lie algebra uniquely labeled by a tuple of non-negative integers.
