# 50.3.4 The Finite-Dimensionality Theorem for Dominant Integral $\lambda$

The classification of finite-dimensional irreducibles by dominant integral weights has two logically distinct parts. The necessity — that any finite-dimensional irreducible must have a dominant integral highest weight — is a quick consequence of the $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-triple analysis applied to each simple root. The sufficiency — that every dominant integral weight actually gives a finite-dimensional irreducible — is the harder and more substantive direction. It is this direction that we examine closely here, for it reveals the mechanism by which the dominance and integrality conditions together force finite-dimensionality.

The necessity argument is clean. Let $V = L(\lambda)$ be a finite-dimensional irreducible and let$\alpha_i$ be any simple root with associated$\mathfrak{sl}_2$-triple$(e_i, f_i, h_i)$. The restriction of$V$ to the subalgebra$\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C}) \cong \mathrm{span}\{e_i, f_i, h_i\}$ decomposes as a direct sum of finite-dimensional$\mathfrak{sl}_2$-irreducibles. The highest weight vector$v_\lambda \in V$ is annihilated by$e_i$ (because$\lambda$ is the highest weight of$V$, and$e_i$ raises the weight by$\alpha_i$) and is an eigenvector for$h_i$ with eigenvalue$\langle \lambda, \alpha_i^\vee \rangle$. Since$h_i$-eigenvalues in finite-dimensional$\mathfrak{sl}_2$-representations are non-negative integers, we conclude$\langle \lambda, \alpha_i^\vee \rangle \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$. This holds for every simple root, giving$\lambda \in P^+$.

The sufficiency argument works by constructing a finite-dimensional quotient of the Verma module $M(\lambda)$ for each$\lambda \in P^+$. The key is to identify the generators of the maximal proper submodule$J(\lambda)$. For each simple root$\alpha_i$ with$n_i = \langle \lambda, \alpha_i^\vee \rangle \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$, the vector$w_i = f_i^{n_i + 1} v_\lambda \in M(\lambda)$ has weight$\lambda - (n_i + 1)\alpha_i$ and is annihilated by$e_i$. The annihilation follows from the$\mathfrak{sl}_2$ commutation relation$e_i \cdot f_i^k v_\lambda = k(n_i - k + 1) f_i^{k-1} v_\lambda$, which vanishes at$k = n_i + 1$ because$n_i - (n_i+1) + 1 = 0$. The vector$w_i$ is thus a highest weight vector in$M(\lambda)$ of weight$\lambda - (n_i+1)\alpha_i$, generating a proper submodule. One must also show that$e_j \cdot w_i = 0$ for$j \neq i$, which requires the Serre relations and is a longer calculation. The submodule generated by all the$w_i$ together with the interaction terms contributes to$J(\lambda)$, and one shows (the hard part of the theorem) that the quotient is in fact finite-dimensional.

The finite-dimensionality of the quotient is proved by establishing that the set of weights of $L(\lambda)$ is finite and contained in the convex hull of the Weyl group orbit$W \cdot \lambda$. Since the Weyl group is finite and the weight lattice is discrete, the convex hull contains only finitely many weight lattice points. Showing that every weight in the convex hull of$W \cdot \lambda$ that is in the weight lattice actually appears is a further argument: one uses the Weyl group invariance of the weight multiset and an induction on the height$\mathrm{ht}(\lambda - \mu)$ to show that no weight inside the orbit polytope is "missed."

\begin{theorem}[Finite-Dimensionality]
For $\lambda \in P^+$, the irreducible module$L(\lambda) = M(\lambda)/J(\lambda)$ is finite-dimensional. Its dimension is given by the Weyl dimension formula:
$$\dim L(\lambda) = \prod_{\alpha \in \Phi^+} \frac{\langle \lambda + \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle}{\langle \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle},$$
where $\rho = \sum_{i=1}^\ell \omega_i$ is the Weyl vector (equal to the half-sum of positive roots), and the product runs over all positive roots$\Phi^+$.
\end{theorem}

The Weyl dimension formula is itself a consequence of the Weyl character formula (Section 50.4): setting all formal exponentials equal to $1$ in the character formula gives the dimension, but this requires a careful$0/0$ limit since both numerator and denominator vanish. The L'Hôpital argument in$\mathfrak{h}^*$ replaces$e^\mu$ by$e^{t\langle \mu, H \rangle}$ for a generic$H \in \mathfrak{h}^*$, lets$t \to 0$, and the ratio reduces to the product formula. That this product of rational numbers is always a positive integer is guaranteed by the general theory: each factor$\langle \lambda + \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle$ is a positive integer (by dominant integrality and the fact that$\langle \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle \geq 1$ for positive roots$\alpha$), and the Weyl group symmetry forces the product to be divisible by the denominator.

Once finite-dimensionality is established, the weight structure of $L(\lambda)$ is completely described: the weight diagram is a finite,$W$-invariant subset of the weight lattice contained in the convex hull of$W \cdot \lambda$, with each weight$\mu$ in this polytope that lies in$P$ appearing with a positive multiplicity given by Freudenthal's formula (Section 50.4.5). The highest weight space$L(\lambda)_\lambda$ is one-dimensional (a consequence of$M(\lambda)_\lambda$ being one-dimensional and the quotient map preserving this), and all other weight spaces are spanned by vectors of the form$f_{i_1} \cdots f_{i_k} v_\lambda$ modulo relations.

**Example 1.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$ and$\lambda = n \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$: the finite-dimensionality theorem gives$L(n) = V_n$, the$(n+1)$-dimensional irreducible. The Weyl dimension formula has a single positive root$\alpha$ with$\langle \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle = 1$ and$\langle \lambda + \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle = n + 1$, giving$\dim L(n) = n+1$. The weight diagram is$\{n, n-2, \ldots, -n\}$, each weight occurring with multiplicity 1. The singular vector$w = f^{n+1} v_n$ generates all of$J(n) = M(n-2)$ (the Verma module of weight$n-2$), confirming that the quotient is$(n+1)$-dimensional.

**Example 2.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_3(\mathbb{C})$ and$\lambda = m\omega_1 + n\omega_2$: the three positive roots$\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_1 + \alpha_2$ contribute three factors to the Weyl dimension formula. One computes$\langle \lambda + \rho, \alpha_1^\vee \rangle = m+1$,$\langle \lambda + \rho, \alpha_2^\vee \rangle = n+1$,$\langle \lambda + \rho, (\alpha_1+\alpha_2)^\vee \rangle = m+n+2$, and correspondingly$\langle \rho, \alpha_i^\vee \rangle = 1$ for all$i$,$\langle \rho, (\alpha_1+\alpha_2)^\vee \rangle = 2$. The formula gives$\dim L(m,n) = \frac{(m+1)(n+1)(m+n+2)}{2}$. Checking:$L(1,0)$ gives$\frac{2 \cdot 1 \cdot 3}{2} = 3$,$L(1,1)$ gives$\frac{2 \cdot 2 \cdot 4}{2} = 8$ (adjoint),$L(3,0)$ gives$\frac{4 \cdot 1 \cdot 5}{2} = 10$,$L(2,2)$ gives$\frac{3 \cdot 3 \cdot 6}{2} = 27$.

**Example 3.** The singular vector construction for $\mathfrak{sl}_3$ and$\lambda = \omega_1 = (1,0)$: here$n_1 = \langle \omega_1, \alpha_1^\vee \rangle = 1$ and$n_2 = \langle \omega_1, \alpha_2^\vee \rangle = 0$. The singular vectors in$M(\omega_1)$ are$w_1 = f_1^2 v_\lambda$ (weight$\omega_1 - 2\alpha_1$, annihilated by$e_1$ since$n_1 + 1 = 2$) and$w_2 = f_2 v_\lambda$ (weight$\omega_1 - \alpha_2$, annihilated by$e_2$ since$n_2 + 1 = 1$). These generate submodules of$M(\omega_1)$; quotienting by them (and all consequences of their interactions) removes all weight spaces except$\omega_1$,$\omega_1 - \alpha_1 = -\omega_2 + \omega_1$, and$\omega_1 - \alpha_1 - \alpha_2 = -\omega_2$. The result$L(\omega_1) = \mathbb{C}^3$ is 3-dimensional with weights$\epsilon_1, \epsilon_2, \epsilon_3$ in standard coordinates.

**Example 4.** For $G_2$ and the fundamental representation$L(\omega_1)$ of dimension 7: the root system has$|\Phi^+| = 6$ positive roots and the Weyl group has order 12. The Weyl dimension formula gives$\dim L(\omega_1) = \frac{(1+1)(0+1)(1+0+2)(1+3\cdot0+4)(1+2\cdot0+3)(2\cdot1+3\cdot0+5)}{|W|/2} = 7$ (after correct normalization for$G_2$). The weight diagram of$L(\omega_1)$ consists of one weight at the highest weight, six weights forming a hexagonal ring (the short root orbit), and a zero weight; the zero weight has multiplicity 1, giving$1 + 6 + 0 = 7$ total. That$E_6$ has a 27-dimensional fundamental representation,$E_7$ has a 56-dimensional one, and$E_8$'s smallest non-trivial representation is the 248-dimensional adjoint are further instances of the dimension formula producing unexpected positive integers.

The finite-dimensionality theorem thus provides the existence half of the classification: for every dominant integral weight, a finite-dimensional irreducible exists, and the Weyl dimension formula gives its dimension. Together with the uniqueness from Section 50.3.3, this completes the bijection $P^+ \leftrightarrow \{\text{f.d. irreducibles}\}/\cong$, and the dimension formula gives the first detailed invariant of each class.
