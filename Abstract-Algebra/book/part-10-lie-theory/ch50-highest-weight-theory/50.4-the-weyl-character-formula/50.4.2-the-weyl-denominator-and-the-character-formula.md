# 50.4.2 The Weyl Denominator and the Character Formula

The Weyl character formula is one of the most striking results in all of Lie theory: a clean closed-form expression for the formal character of any finite-dimensional irreducible representation, involving only the Weyl group and the root system. Its statement requires two pieces of notation — the Weyl vector $\rho$ and the alternation operator — that also appear throughout the rest of the theory.

The *Weyl vector* is $\rho = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha \in \Phi^+} \alpha$, the half-sum of positive roots. It satisfies$\langle \rho, \alpha_i^\vee \rangle = 1$ for all simple roots$\alpha_i$, so$\rho = \omega_1 + \cdots + \omega_\ell$ — it is the sum of all fundamental weights. For$\mathfrak{sl}_{n+1}$,$\rho = \frac{n}{2}\epsilon_1 + \frac{n-2}{2}\epsilon_2 + \cdots + (-\frac{n}{2})\epsilon_{n+1}$ in standard coordinates, which can be identified with$(n, n-1, \ldots, 1, 0) - \frac{n}{2}(1,\ldots,1)$. The Weyl vector lies strictly inside the dominant chamber:$\langle \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle \geq 1$ for every positive root$\alpha$, and$\langle \rho, \alpha_i^\vee \rangle = 1$ for each simple root$\alpha_i$.

The *alternation operator* assigns to each Weyl group element its sign: $\varepsilon(w) = (-1)^{\ell(w)}$, where$\ell(w)$ is the length of$w$ as a product of simple reflections. This is a group homomorphism$\varepsilon: W \to \{\pm 1\}$. Concretely,$\varepsilon(w) = +1$ if$w$ is a product of an even number of simple reflections and$-1$ if odd; simple reflections have$\varepsilon(s_i) = -1$, and$\varepsilon$ extends multiplicatively. The *Weyl denominator* is:
$$D = \sum_{w \in W} \varepsilon(w)\, e^{w(\rho)} \in \mathbb{Z}[P].$$
This is an alternating element of $\mathbb{Z}[P]$:$w(D) = \varepsilon(w) D$ for all$w \in W$, because permuting the terms of the sum by$w$ introduces a sign$\varepsilon(w)$.

The denominator has a remarkable product formula, the *Weyl denominator identity*:
$$\sum_{w \in W} \varepsilon(w)\, e^{w(\rho)} = e^\rho \prod_{\alpha \in \Phi^+} (1 - e^{-\alpha}).$$
This identity is non-trivial and is equivalent to many other formulas in combinatorics and number theory: for $\mathfrak{sl}_{n+1}$ it gives the Vandermonde determinant identity; for affine Lie algebras it yields the Jacobi triple product and Macdonald identities. The proof uses the Weyl group action on the root system, the formula$s_i(\Phi^+) = (\Phi^+ \setminus \{\alpha_i\}) \cup \{-\alpha_i\}$, and an induction showing that the product over positive roots is alternating with the right leading term. Each factor$(1-e^{-\alpha})$ contributes one term, and the expansion of the full product is shown to equal the alternating sum term by term.

\begin{theorem}[Weyl Character Formula]
For $\lambda \in P^+$, the formal character of the irreducible module$L(\lambda)$ is:
$$\mathrm{ch}(L(\lambda)) = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{w \in W} \varepsilon(w)\, e^{w(\lambda + \rho)}}{\displaystyle\sum_{w \in W} \varepsilon(w)\, e^{w(\rho)}}.$$
\end{theorem}

The numerator $A_{\lambda+\rho} = \sum_{w \in W} \varepsilon(w)\, e^{w(\lambda+\rho)}$ is an alternating element of$\mathbb{Z}[P]$ — one has$w(A_{\lambda+\rho}) = \varepsilon(w) A_{\lambda+\rho}$ for all$w \in W$. The denominator$D = A_\rho$ is the same kind of alternating element with$\lambda = 0$. Their ratio is$W$-invariant, and the non-trivial content of the theorem is that this ratio lies in$\mathbb{Z}[P]$ with non-negative integer coefficients — these coefficients are the weight multiplicities$\dim L(\lambda)_\mu$.

The proof proceeds by verifying that $\mathrm{ch}(L(\lambda)) \cdot D = A_{\lambda+\rho}$: both sides are alternating elements of$\mathbb{Z}[P]$ with the same leading term$e^{\lambda+\rho}$, and an argument using complete reducibility (Weyl's theorem) and the Verma module structure shows they must be equal. Specifically, one uses the fact that the alternating elements of$\mathbb{Z}[P]$ supported on weights$\leq \lambda + \rho$ form a one-dimensional space spanned by$A_{\lambda+\rho}$, and that$\mathrm{ch}(L(\lambda)) \cdot D$ is an alternating element with the right leading term. The geometric proof uses the Borel–Weil–Bott theorem: the character of$L(\lambda)$ equals the holomorphic Euler characteristic of a certain line bundle on the flag variety$G/B$, and this Euler characteristic is computed by the Atiyah–Bott–Lefschetz fixed-point theorem, yielding the alternating sum formula directly.

**Example 1.** Weyl denominator for $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$:$W = \{1, s\}$ with$s(\rho) = s(\omega) = -\omega = -\rho$ (since$s(\alpha) = -\alpha$ and$\rho = \omega = \alpha/2$). The denominator$D = e^\rho - e^{-\rho} = e^{\alpha/2} - e^{-\alpha/2}$. The product formula gives$e^\rho(1 - e^{-\alpha}) = e^{\alpha/2}(1 - e^{-\alpha}) = e^{\alpha/2} - e^{-\alpha/2} = D$. For$\lambda = n\omega$:$A_{\lambda+\rho} = e^{(n+1)\omega} - e^{-(n+1)\omega}$, and$\mathrm{ch}(V_n) = \frac{e^{(n+1)\omega} - e^{-(n+1)\omega}}{e^\omega - e^{-\omega}} = e^{n\omega} + e^{(n-2)\omega} + \cdots + e^{-n\omega}$, giving each weight with multiplicity 1.

**Example 2.** Weyl character formula for $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$ and the symmetric power$V_2 = \mathrm{Sym}^2(\mathbb{C}^2)$:$\lambda = 2$,$\lambda + \rho = 3$.$A_3 = e^3 - e^{-3}$,$D = e^1 - e^{-1}$. The character:$\frac{e^3 - e^{-3}}{e^1 - e^{-1}} = e^2 + 1 + e^{-2}$, giving weights$2, 0, -2$ each with multiplicity 1. This confirms$V_2$ has basis consisting of weight vectors of weights$2, 0, -2$ — the three-dimensional irreducible.

**Example 3.** Weyl denominator for $\mathfrak{sl}_3(\mathbb{C})$:$|W| = 6$ ($S_3$). Setting$x_i = e^{\epsilon_i}$ with$x_1 x_2 x_3 = 1$, the alternating sum of the$S_3$-orbit of$e^\rho = e^{\omega_1 + \omega_2} = x_1^2 x_2$ gives:$D = x_1^2 x_2 - x_1 x_2^2 - x_1^2 x_3 + x_2^2 x_3 + x_1 x_3^2 - x_2 x_3^2$. This factors as$(x_1 - x_2)(x_1 - x_3)(x_2 - x_3)$, the Vandermonde determinant. The product formula$D = e^\rho (1-e^{-\alpha_1})(1-e^{-\alpha_2})(1-e^{-\alpha_1-\alpha_2})$ expands to the same six-term alternating sum.

**Example 4.** Weyl character formula for $\mathfrak{sl}_3(\mathbb{C})$ and$L(\omega_1)$: the numerator$A_{\omega_1+\rho} = A_{2\omega_1+\omega_2}$ is the alternating$S_3$-orbit sum of$e^{2\omega_1+\omega_2} = x_1^3 x_2$ (in coordinates):
$$A_{2\omega_1+\omega_2} = x_1^3 x_2 - x_1^3 x_3 + x_2^3 x_3 - x_1 x_2^3 + x_1 x_3^3 - x_2 x_3^3.$$
Dividing by the Vandermonde $D = (x_1-x_2)(x_1-x_3)(x_2-x_3)$ gives$\mathrm{ch}(L(\omega_1)) = x_1 + x_2 + x_3$, the sum of the three weight-space characters, confirming that the standard representation has weights$\epsilon_1, \epsilon_2, \epsilon_3$ each with multiplicity 1.

The Weyl character formula is thus the master formula of highest weight theory, encoding the weight multiplicities of every finite-dimensional irreducible representation in a single elegant quotient of alternating sums over the Weyl group. Combined with the Weyl denominator identity, it yields the dimension formula (Section 50.4.3) and provides the foundation for all systematic computations in the representation theory of semisimple Lie algebras.
