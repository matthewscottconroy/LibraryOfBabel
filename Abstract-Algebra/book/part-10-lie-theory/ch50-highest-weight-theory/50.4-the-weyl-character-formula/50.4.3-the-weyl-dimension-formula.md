# 50.4.3 The Weyl Dimension Formula

The Weyl character formula computes the full formal character, encoding every weight multiplicity. But often one needs only the total dimension of $L(\lambda)$. Setting all formal exponentials equal to$1$ in the formal character gives the dimension, but this requires a careful limiting argument since the Weyl denominator vanishes at$e^\lambda = 1$ for all$\lambda$. Carrying out this argument yields a remarkably elegant result.

Setting $e^\mu = 1$ for all$\mu \in P$ in the character formula requires computing a$0/0$ limit. The standard approach replaces$e^\mu$ by$e^{t\langle \mu, \xi \rangle}$ for a generic element$\xi \in \mathfrak{h}^*$ and a formal variable$t \to 0$. The numerator$A_{\lambda+\rho}$ and denominator$D = A_\rho$ both vanish to first order as$t \to 0$ (since the alternating sums cancel), but their ratio remains well-defined. After cancellation using L'Hopital's rule (or the Taylor expansion), one obtains the *Weyl dimension formula*.

\begin{theorem}[Weyl Dimension Formula]
For $\lambda \in P^+$ and$\mathfrak{g}$ a semisimple Lie algebra with positive root system$\Phi^+$ and Weyl vector$\rho$:
$$\dim L(\lambda) = \prod_{\alpha \in \Phi^+} \frac{\langle \lambda + \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle}{\langle \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle}.$$
\end{theorem}

The denominator $\langle \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle$ is always a positive integer: since$\rho = \sum_i \omega_i$ and each$\langle \omega_i, \alpha^\vee \rangle \geq 0$ with equality possible only for specific simple roots, one has$\langle \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle \geq 1$ for all$\alpha \in \Phi^+$. In fact,$\langle \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle$ equals the "height" of$\alpha^\vee$ in a certain sense: for a simple root$\alpha_i$,$\langle \rho, \alpha_i^\vee \rangle = 1$; for a higher root,$\langle \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle = \mathrm{ht}(\alpha) = \sum_i n_i$ where$\alpha = \sum_i n_i \alpha_i$. (More precisely,$\langle \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle$ for$\alpha = \sum n_i \alpha_i$ is$\sum_i n_i$, the total height of$\alpha$.)

The numerator $\langle \lambda + \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle = \langle \lambda, \alpha^\vee \rangle + \langle \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle$ is a positive integer for all$\alpha \in \Phi^+$ when$\lambda \in P^+$: since$\langle \lambda, \alpha^\vee \rangle \geq 0$ (for$\alpha$ a positive root expressible as a non-negative combination of simple roots) and$\langle \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle \geq 1$. The formula thus gives a ratio of products of positive integers. That this ratio is always a positive integer — the dimension of a representation — is the remarkable content of the theorem; it is not obvious from the formula alone, but follows from the existence of$L(\lambda)$.

For $\mathfrak{sl}_{n+1}$ with$\lambda = \sum_{i=1}^n m_i \omega_i$ and positive roots$\alpha_{ij} = \epsilon_i - \epsilon_j$ for$1 \leq i < j \leq n+1$, we have$\langle \lambda + \rho, \alpha_{ij}^\vee \rangle = (m_i + m_{i+1} + \cdots + m_{j-1}) + (j - i)$ and$\langle \rho, \alpha_{ij}^\vee \rangle = j - i$. The formula becomes a ratio of products of consecutive differences, which equals the hook-length formula for the corresponding Young diagram. This connects the abstract Weyl formula to the combinatorial theory of symmetric functions. The Weyl dimension formula is also a polynomial in the Dynkin labels$(m_1, \ldots, m_\ell)$ of total degree$|\Phi^+|$ (the number of positive roots), since each factor$\langle \lambda + \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle$ is a linear polynomial in the$m_i$. This polynomial structure makes it easy to tabulate the dimensions of all irreducibles ordered by complexity of the highest weight.

**Example 1.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$ with$\lambda = n\omega$:$\Phi^+ = \{\alpha\}$,$\langle \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle = 1$,$\langle \lambda + \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle = n + 1$. So$\dim V_n = (n+1)/1 = n+1$. This matches the direct construction, and the formula is linear in$n$ (degree 1 =$|\Phi^+|$). The formula immediately gives all irreducible dimensions without needing to construct any of the representations:$1, 2, 3, 4, 5, \ldots$ for$n = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, \ldots$.

**Example 2.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_3(\mathbb{C})$ with$\lambda = m\omega_1 + n\omega_2$: the three positive roots give factors$(m+1)/1$,$(n+1)/1$,$(m+n+2)/2$. So$\dim L(m,n) = \frac{(m+1)(n+1)(m+n+2)}{2}$. Verify:$\dim L(1,1) = \frac{2 \cdot 2 \cdot 4}{2} = 8$ (adjoint).$\dim L(3,0) = \frac{4 \cdot 1 \cdot 5}{2} = 10$.$\dim L(2,2) = \frac{3 \cdot 3 \cdot 6}{2} = 27$.$\dim L(4,0) = \frac{5 \cdot 1 \cdot 6}{2} = 15$. The formula is a degree-3 polynomial in$(m,n)$, as$|\Phi^+| = 3$.

**Example 3.** For $\mathfrak{sp}(4)$ (type$C_2$) with$\lambda = m\omega_1 + n\omega_2$: the four positive roots are$\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_1 + \alpha_2, 2\alpha_1 + \alpha_2$. Computing the inner products with$\rho$:$\langle \rho, \alpha_1^\vee \rangle = 1$,$\langle \rho, \alpha_2^\vee \rangle = 1$,$\langle \rho, (\alpha_1+\alpha_2)^\vee \rangle = 2$,$\langle \rho, (2\alpha_1+\alpha_2)^\vee \rangle = 3$. The numerators are$m+1, n+1, m+n+2, m+2n+3$. The dimension formula gives$\dim L(m,n) = \frac{(m+1)(n+1)(m+n+2)(m+2n+3)}{1 \cdot 1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3} = \frac{(m+1)(n+1)(m+n+2)(m+2n+3)}{6}$. For$(m,n) = (1,0)$:$\frac{2 \cdot 1 \cdot 3 \cdot 4}{6} = 4$ (standard), and for$(0,1)$:$\frac{1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5}{6} = 5$ (the second fundamental representation). For$(1,1)$:$\frac{2 \cdot 2 \cdot 4 \cdot 6}{6} = 16$.

**Example 4.** For $G_2$ with$\lambda = m\omega_1 + n\omega_2$ (6 positive roots): the dimension formula gives$\dim L(m,n) = \frac{(m+1)(n+1)(m+n+2)(m+2n+3)(m+3n+4)(2m+3n+5)}{120}$. For$\lambda = \omega_1 = (1,0)$:$\dim = \frac{2 \cdot 1 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 \cdot 5 \cdot 7}{120} = \frac{840}{120} = 7$. For$\lambda = \omega_2 = (0,1)$:$\dim = \frac{1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot 8}{120} = \frac{1680}{120} = 14$. For$(1,1)$:$\frac{2 \cdot 2 \cdot 4 \cdot 6 \cdot 8 \cdot 10}{120} = \frac{7680}{120} = 64$. These match the known smallest representations of$G_2$ and are the distinctive fingerprint of this exceptional algebra.

The Weyl dimension formula is practical and powerful: it computes dimensions of arbitrary irreducible representations from the Dynkin labels alone, with no need to construct the representation explicitly or compute any weight multiplicities. It is also a polynomial in the Dynkin labels $(m_1, \ldots, m_\ell)$, a product of$|\Phi^+|$ linear forms, which makes it easy to tabulate representations by dimension for any given Lie algebra.
