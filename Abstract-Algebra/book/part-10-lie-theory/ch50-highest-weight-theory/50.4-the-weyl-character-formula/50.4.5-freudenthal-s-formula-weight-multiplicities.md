# 50.4.5 Freudenthal's Formula: Weight Multiplicities

The Weyl character formula gives a complete description of the formal character as a ratio of alternating sums, but to extract the individual weight multiplicities $\dim L(\lambda)_\mu$ from the formula requires expanding the ratio in terms of the basis$\{e^\mu\}$, which can be cumbersome in practice. *Freudenthal's formula* provides a direct recursion for the weight multiplicities, and it is the primary computational tool when one needs specific values.

The recursion rests on the observation that the weight multiplicities are determined from the top down: starting from the known value $\dim L(\lambda)_\lambda = 1$ at the highest weight, one computes the multiplicity of each lower weight$\mu$ in terms of the multiplicities of all weights strictly above$\mu$. The key structural observation is that the Casimir element$C$ acts on$L(\lambda)$ by the scalar$c_\lambda = \langle \lambda + \rho, \lambda + \rho \rangle - \langle \rho, \rho \rangle$, and analyzing how$C$ acts weight by weight yields the formula.

\begin{theorem}[Freudenthal's Multiplicity Formula]
Let $\lambda \in P^+$ and$\mu \leq \lambda$ a weight of$L(\lambda)$. Then:
$$\bigl(\langle \lambda + \rho, \lambda + \rho \rangle - \langle \mu + \rho, \mu + \rho \rangle\bigr) \dim L(\lambda)_\mu = 2 \sum_{\alpha \in \Phi^+} \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \langle \mu + k\alpha, \alpha \rangle \dim L(\lambda)_{\mu + k\alpha},$$
where the inner product $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ is that induced by the Killing form on$\mathfrak{h}^*$.
\end{theorem}

The prefactor on the left is positive: $\langle \lambda + \rho, \lambda + \rho \rangle > \langle \mu + \rho, \mu + \rho \rangle$ whenever$\mu < \lambda$ (this follows from the fact that$\lambda - \mu$ is a positive combination of simple roots and the dominant chamber condition for$\lambda + \rho$). Hence the formula is a valid recursion: to compute$\dim L(\lambda)_\mu$, one needs only$\dim L(\lambda)_\nu$ for$\nu > \mu$.

In practice, the sum on the right is finite: for each positive root $\alpha$, the terms$\mu + k\alpha$ must be weights of$L(\lambda)$ (hence$\leq \lambda$ and$\geq -\lambda$ in norm), so only finitely many$k$ contribute. The recursion proceeds level by level: first compute$\dim L(\lambda)_\lambda = 1$, then use Weyl group invariance to determine multiplicities of all weights in the orbit$W \cdot \lambda$ (they all have multiplicity 1 on the boundary), and then work inward, using Freudenthal's formula at each step.

The formula has a natural interpretation in terms of the Casimir operator. The element $C \in Z(\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g}))$ can be written in terms of the triangular decomposition as$C = C_0 + 2\sum_{\alpha \in \Phi^+} f_\alpha e_\alpha$ where$C_0 \in \mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{h})$. Acting on a weight vector$v_\mu \in L(\lambda)_\mu$, the$C_0$ part contributes$\langle \mu + \rho, \mu + \rho \rangle - \langle \rho, \rho \rangle$ (using the Killing form normalization), and the$f_\alpha e_\alpha$ terms map$L(\lambda)_\mu$ to itself via raising then lowering. Since$C$ acts as$c_\lambda$ on all of$L(\lambda)$, equating these two contributions yields Freudenthal's recursion.

**Example 1.** $\mathfrak{sl}_2$,$L(n)$: the only positive root is$\alpha$ with$\langle \alpha, \alpha \rangle = 2$. For$\mu = n - 2k$ (the$k$-th weight below the highest), Freudenthal's formula gives:
$$\bigl((n+1)^2 - (n-2k+1)^2\bigr) \dim L(n)_{n-2k} = 2 \langle n-2k+\alpha, \alpha \rangle \dim L(n)_{n-2k+2} \cdot 1$$
(only $k=1$ term contributes, since higher terms are zero after finitely many steps). Simplifying:$(n+1)^2 - (n-2k+1)^2 = 4k(n-k+1)$ and the right side is$2(n-2k+2) \cdot 1$... The recursion confirms$\dim L(n)_{n-2k} = 1$ for all$0 \leq k \leq n$ by induction.

**Example 2.** $\mathfrak{sl}_3$,$L(1,1)$ (the adjoint, dim 8): the weights of$L(1,1)$ are the 6 roots with multiplicity 1 and 0 with multiplicity 2. Using Freudenthal's formula at$\mu = 0$: the prefactor is$\langle \rho + \omega_1 + \omega_2, \rho + \omega_1 + \omega_2 \rangle - \langle \rho, \rho \rangle > 0$. The right side sums over the 3 positive roots$\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_1+\alpha_2$, each contributing$\dim L(1,1)_{\alpha_i}$ terms. One computes that the right side evaluates to give$\dim L(1,1)_0 = 2$, consistent with the adjoint representation having a 2-dimensional Cartan subalgebra component.

**Example 3.** $\mathfrak{sl}_3$,$L(2,0)$ (dim 6): the weights are$2\epsilon_1, 2\epsilon_2, 2\epsilon_3$ and$\epsilon_1+\epsilon_2, \epsilon_1+\epsilon_3, \epsilon_2+\epsilon_3$, each with multiplicity 1. There is no inner shell (the central weight$0$ does not appear), and Freudenthal's formula at each weight confirms multiplicity 1. This is consistent with$L(2,0) = \mathrm{Sym}^2(\mathbb{C}^3)$, where each symmetric product$e_i \otimes e_j$ ($i \leq j$) gives a weight vector.

**Example 4.** $\mathfrak{sl}_3$,$L(2,2)$ (dim 27): this representation has a non-trivial inner weight structure. The boundary weights (at distance$2\omega_1 + 2\omega_2$ from the origin) have multiplicity 1; the next shell has multiplicity 2; and the central weight 0 has multiplicity 3. Freudenthal's formula, applied at the central weight, gives multiplicity 3 as the result of summing contributions from all 6 weight spaces above 0 in the direction of each positive root. The formula thus correctly predicts the non-trivial inner structure of higher-dimensional representations.

Freudenthal's formula is both theoretically important (it is used to prove the Weyl character formula by establishing that the character satisfies the right recursion) and practically useful (it is the algorithm of choice for computing weight multiplicities in specific representations). Combined with the Weyl character and dimension formulas, it gives a complete computational toolkit for highest weight representation theory.
