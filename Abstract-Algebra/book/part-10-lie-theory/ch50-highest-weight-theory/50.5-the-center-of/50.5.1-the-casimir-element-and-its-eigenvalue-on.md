# 50.5.1 The Casimir Element and Its Eigenvalue on $L(\lambda)$

Schur's lemma tells us that any element of $\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})$ that commutes with all of$\mathfrak{g}$ must act as a scalar on any irreducible representation. The question is: are there interesting central elements in$\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})$, and what information do their eigenvalues carry? The simplest and most important answer is given by the Casimir element, a canonical quadratic element of$\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})$ that plays the role of the Laplace–Beltrami operator on the corresponding Lie group.

\begin{definition}
Let $\{x_i\}$ be a basis of$\mathfrak{g}$ and$\{x^i\}$ the dual basis with respect to the Killing form$\kappa$, so that$\kappa(x_i, x^j) = \delta_{ij}$. The *Casimir element* is
$$C = \sum_i x_i x^i \in \mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g}).$$
\end{definition}

The element $C$ is well-defined (independent of the choice of basis) because any change of basis preserving the Killing form leaves$\sum x_i x^i$ unchanged, and the Killing form is non-degenerate for semisimple$\mathfrak{g}$. One verifies that$C$ is central: for any$y \in \mathfrak{g}$,
$$[y, C] = [y, \sum_i x_i x^i] = \sum_i ([y, x_i] x^i + x_i [y, x^i]) = 0$$
by the invariance of the Killing form (which implies that the structure constants satisfy a symmetry that causes cancellation). By Schur's lemma, $C$ acts as a scalar$c_\lambda \cdot \mathrm{Id}$ on any irreducible$\mathfrak{g}$-module$L(\lambda)$.

To compute $c_\lambda$, one applies$C$ to the highest weight vector$v_\lambda \in L(\lambda)$. Splitting$C$ into its triangular components using the Chevalley basis$\{e_\alpha, f_\alpha, h_i\}$:
$$C = \sum_{i=1}^\ell h_i h^i + \sum_{\alpha \in \Phi^+} (e_\alpha f_\alpha + f_\alpha e_\alpha)$$
where $h_i, h^i$ are dual bases of$\mathfrak{h}$. Since$e_\alpha \cdot v_\lambda = 0$ (highest weight vector), the term$f_\alpha e_\alpha \cdot v_\lambda = f_\alpha \cdot 0 = 0$, and$e_\alpha f_\alpha \cdot v_\lambda = [e_\alpha, f_\alpha] \cdot v_\lambda + f_\alpha e_\alpha \cdot v_\lambda = h_\alpha \cdot v_\lambda = \langle \lambda, \alpha^\vee \rangle v_\lambda$. The$\mathfrak{h}$-part contributes$\langle \lambda, \lambda \rangle v_\lambda$ (using the identification of$\mathfrak{h}$ with$\mathfrak{h}^*$ via the Killing form). Combining:
$$c_\lambda = \langle \lambda, \lambda \rangle + 2\sum_{\alpha \in \Phi^+} \langle \lambda, \alpha \rangle / \langle \alpha, \alpha \rangle \cdot \langle \alpha, \alpha \rangle / 2 = \langle \lambda, \lambda + 2\rho \rangle,$$
where $\rho = \sum_{\alpha \in \Phi^+} \alpha/2$ is the Weyl vector (more precisely,$c_\lambda = \langle \lambda, \lambda \rangle + 2\langle \lambda, \rho \rangle = \langle \lambda, \lambda + 2\rho \rangle$).

\begin{theorem}
The Casimir element $C \in Z(\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g}))$ acts on the irreducible module$L(\lambda)$ ($\lambda \in P^+$) by the scalar
$$c_\lambda = \langle \lambda, \lambda + 2\rho \rangle,$$
where $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ is the inner product on$\mathfrak{h}^*$ induced by the Killing form.
\end{theorem}

The eigenvalue formula $c_\lambda = \langle \lambda + \rho, \lambda + \rho \rangle - \langle \rho, \rho \rangle$ is an equivalent form (expanding$\langle \lambda + \rho, \lambda + \rho \rangle = \langle \lambda, \lambda \rangle + 2\langle \lambda, \rho \rangle + \langle \rho, \rho \rangle$). This version shows that$c_\lambda$ depends only on the shifted weight$\lambda + \rho$, a pattern that recurs throughout highest weight theory (notably in the Harish-Chandra isomorphism). The Casimir eigenvalue is strictly positive for$\lambda \neq 0$: since$\lambda \in P^+$ implies$\langle \lambda, \rho \rangle \geq 0$, one has$c_\lambda = \langle \lambda, \lambda + 2\rho \rangle \geq 0$ with equality only at$\lambda = 0$.

**Example 1.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ with the Killing form$\kappa(x,y) = 4\mathrm{tr}(xy)$ (normalized so that$\kappa(h,h) = 8$,$\kappa(e,f) = 4$): the Casimir element in the Chevalley basis is$C = \frac{1}{8}h^2 + \frac{1}{4}(ef + fe) = \frac{1}{4}(h^2/2 + ef + fe)$. Acting on the highest weight vector$v_n$ of$V_n$:$h \cdot v_n = n v_n$,$e \cdot v_n = 0$,$f e \cdot v_n = 0$,$ef \cdot v_n = h \cdot v_n + fe \cdot v_n = n v_n$. So$C \cdot v_n = \frac{1}{4}(n^2/2 + n) \cdot v_n$... Using the formula:$c_n = \langle n\omega, n\omega + 2\omega \rangle = n(n+2)\langle \omega, \omega \rangle$, giving$c_n = n(n+2)/2$ in the standard normalization. This eigenvalue distinguishes all non-isomorphic$\mathfrak{sl}_2$-irreducibles with different values of$n(n+2)$.

**Example 2.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_3$ and$\lambda = \omega_1 = (1,0)$:$\langle \omega_1, \omega_1 + 2\rho \rangle = \langle \omega_1, \omega_1 + 2\omega_1 + 2\omega_2 \rangle = 3\langle \omega_1, \omega_1 \rangle + 2\langle \omega_1, \omega_2 \rangle$. Using the standard inner product for$\mathfrak{sl}_3$:$\langle \omega_1, \omega_1 \rangle = 2/3$,$\langle \omega_1, \omega_2 \rangle = 1/3$. So$c_{\omega_1} = 3 \cdot 2/3 + 2 \cdot 1/3 = 2 + 2/3 = 8/3$. For$\lambda = \omega_1 + \omega_2$ (adjoint):$c_{\omega_1 + \omega_2} = \langle \omega_1 + \omega_2, \omega_1 + \omega_2 + 2\rho \rangle = \langle \omega_1 + \omega_2, 3\omega_1 + 3\omega_2 \rangle = 3(2/3 + 2 \cdot 1/3 + 2/3) = 3 \cdot 4/3 = 4$.

**Example 3.** The Casimir eigenvalue in Weyl's proof of complete reducibility: to show that every short exact sequence $0 \to L(\mu) \to V \to L(\lambda) \to 0$ splits, one uses the Casimir element. If$c_\mu \neq c_\lambda$, then$C$ acts by different scalars on the two pieces, forcing the sequence to split: the two eigenspaces for$C$ acting on$V$ give the direct sum decomposition. If$c_\mu = c_\lambda$ (which can happen, e.g.,$c_0 = 0$), a separate argument using$\mathfrak{sl}_2$-subalgebras is needed. The key case is$\lambda = 0$ (trivial representation), for which$c_0 = 0$, handled separately using the structure of the Killing form.

**Example 4.** Casimir eigenvalue and harmonic analysis: on the compact real form $G$ of the complex Lie group corresponding to$\mathfrak{g}$, the Casimir element corresponds to the Laplace–Beltrami operator$\Delta$ on the group manifold (under the Peter–Weyl decomposition$L^2(G) = \bigoplus_{\lambda \in P^+} L(\lambda) \otimes L(\lambda)^*$). The eigenvalue of$\Delta$ on the$L(\lambda)$-isotypic component is$-c_\lambda$. For$G = SU(2)$:$\Delta$ on$S^3 = SU(2)$ has eigenvalues$-n(n+2)$ on the$n$-th spherical harmonic, matching$-c_n = -n(n+2)/2$ up to normalization. This is the bridge between Lie algebra theory and the spectral theory of compact symmetric spaces.

The Casimir element is the most basic element of the center $Z(\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g}))$, and the eigenvalue$c_\lambda = \langle \lambda, \lambda + 2\rho \rangle$ is the most fundamental scalar attached to a highest weight module. The structure of the full center$Z(\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g}))$ — which contains many more elements — is described by the Harish-Chandra isomorphism (Section 50.5.2).
