# 50.5.2 The Harish-Chandra Isomorphism $Z(\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})) \cong \mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{h}^*]^W$

The Casimir element is just the lowest-degree generator of the center $Z(\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g}))$, which is in general a large and rich commutative ring. Understanding the full center amounts to understanding all the "conserved quantities" that commute with the$\mathfrak{g}$-action, and the Harish-Chandra isomorphism gives a complete and explicit description of this center.

The statement is: there is an algebra isomorphism $Z(\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})) \cong \mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{h}^*]^W$, where$\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{h}^*]^W$ is the ring of polynomial functions on$\mathfrak{h}^*$ that are invariant under the Weyl group. This isomorphism — the *Harish-Chandra isomorphism* — sends the center of the enveloping algebra to the invariant polynomials on the weight space, with the Weyl group acting as the symmetry group. For$\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{sl}_{n+1}$, the invariant polynomials are generated by the elementary symmetric polynomials in the natural coordinates on$\mathfrak{h}^*$, and the corresponding generators of$Z(\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g}))$ are higher Casimir elements built from symmetric tensors.

The isomorphism is constructed via the *Harish-Chandra map* $\gamma: \mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g}) \to \mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{h})$, defined as follows. The PBW theorem gives a vector space decomposition
$$\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g}) = \mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{h}) \oplus \bigl(\mathfrak{n}^- \mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g}) + \mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})\mathfrak{n}^+\bigr),$$
and $\gamma$ is the projection onto the first factor$\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{h})$. Restricted to$Z(\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g}))$, this is an algebra homomorphism (the center commutes with both$\mathfrak{n}^+$ and$\mathfrak{n}^-$, which allows the projection to respect the algebra structure). The image lands in$\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{h}) \cong \mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{h}^*]$ (polynomial functions on$\mathfrak{h}^*$, identifying$h \in \mathfrak{h}$ with the evaluation functional on$\mathfrak{h}^*$).

However, the naive map $\gamma$ does not quite land in the$W$-invariant polynomials — it lands in a shifted version. The correct statement uses the *twisted* Harish-Chandra map$\tilde{\gamma} = \gamma \circ \tau_\rho$, where$\tau_\rho$ is the automorphism of$\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{h})$ induced by the shift$\lambda \mapsto \lambda - \rho$ on$\mathfrak{h}^*$. With this twist:

\begin{theorem}[Harish-Chandra]
The twisted Harish-Chandra map $\tilde{\gamma}: Z(\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})) \to \mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{h}^*]^W$ is an algebra isomorphism. In particular,$Z(\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g}))$ is a polynomial algebra in$\ell = \mathrm{rank}(\mathfrak{g})$ generators of degrees$d_1, \ldots, d_\ell$, where the$d_i$ are the degrees of the basic polynomial invariants of the Weyl group.
\end{theorem}

The proof that $\tilde{\gamma}$ is well-defined (lands in$W$-invariants) uses the fact that for any$z \in Z(\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g}))$ and any weight$\lambda$, the eigenvalue of$z$ on$L(\lambda)$ equals$\tilde{\gamma}(z)(\lambda)$ — this follows from computing how$z$ acts on the highest weight vector and tracing through the PBW decomposition. Since$L(\lambda) \cong L(w\lambda)$ is false in general but the Casimir eigenvalue satisfies$c_\lambda = c_{w\lambda}$ (by the$W$-invariance of the inner product), the image must be$W$-invariant.

The *central character* of $L(\lambda)$ is the algebra homomorphism$\chi_\lambda: Z(\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})) \to \mathbb{C}$ sending each$z \in Z(\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g}))$ to its eigenvalue on$L(\lambda)$. By the Harish-Chandra isomorphism,$\chi_\lambda = \chi_\mu$ if and only if$\lambda + \rho$ and$\mu + \rho$ lie in the same$W$-orbit. This is the *linkage principle*: two irreducibles$L(\lambda)$ and$L(\mu)$ have the same central character if and only if$\lambda$ and$\mu$ are$W$-linked (i.e.,$\mu + \rho = w(\lambda + \rho)$ for some$w \in W$). This plays a crucial role in the representation theory of the BGG category$\mathcal{O}$ and in the classification of highest weight modules.

For $\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{sl}_{n+1}$, the degrees are$d_i = i+1$ for$i = 1, \ldots, n$, so the center$Z(\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n+1}))$ is a polynomial algebra in$n$ generators of degrees$2, 3, \ldots, n+1$. The degree-2 generator is the Casimir element$C$. The higher generators are the "higher Casimirs" built from the trace-form: the$k$-th Casimir is$C_k = \sum_{i_1, \ldots, i_k} x_{i_1 i_2} x_{i_2 i_3} \cdots x_{i_k i_1}$ (a cyclic trace), and these$\ell$ elements generate the center.

**Example 1.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ (rank 1):$Z(\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{sl}_2)) \cong \mathbb{C}[C]$, generated by the single Casimir element$C = h^2/4 + ef + fe/2$ (in various normalizations). The Weyl group$W = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ acts on$\mathfrak{h}^* \cong \mathbb{C}$ by$\lambda \mapsto -\lambda$, and$\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{h}^*]^W = \mathbb{C}[\lambda^2]$. Under the Harish-Chandra isomorphism,$C \mapsto (\lambda + 1/2)^2 - 1/4 = \lambda^2 + \lambda$ (after the$\rho$-shift,$\rho = 1/2$), which is the$W$-invariant polynomial$(\lambda+\rho)^2 - \rho^2 = \lambda(\lambda + 2\rho)$.

**Example 2.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_3$ (rank 2):$Z(\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{sl}_3)) \cong \mathbb{C}[C_2, C_3]$, generated by the Casimir$C_2$ (degree 2) and a cubic element$C_3$ (degree 3). The Weyl group$W = S_3$ acts on$\mathbb{C}^2 \cong \mathfrak{h}^*$, and$\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{h}^*]^{S_3}$ is generated by the symmetric polynomials of degrees 2 and 3 in$\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3$ (with$\sum \lambda_i = 0$): the power sums$p_2 = \sum \lambda_i^2$ and$p_3 = \sum \lambda_i^3$. Under$\tilde{\gamma}$:$C_2 \mapsto p_2 + (\text{lower terms involving } \rho)$ and$C_3 \mapsto p_3 + (\text{lower terms})$.

**Example 3.** The linkage principle for $\mathfrak{sl}_2$:$\chi_\lambda = \chi_\mu$ iff$(\lambda + \rho)^2 = (\mu + \rho)^2$, i.e.,$\lambda + 1 = \pm(\mu + 1)$. So either$\lambda = \mu$ or$\lambda + \mu + 2 = 0$, i.e.,$\mu = -\lambda - 2$. For$\lambda = n \geq 0$: the linked weight is$\mu = -n - 2 < 0$. The two modules$L(n)$ (finite-dimensional, dimension$n+1$) and$M(-n-2)$ (infinite-dimensional Verma) share the same central character — they are "linked" in the BGG category$\mathcal{O}$, and indeed$M(n)$ contains$M(-n-2)$ as a submodule.

**Example 4.** Harish-Chandra isomorphism and the Langlands program: the central character $\chi_\lambda: Z(\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})) \to \mathbb{C}$ is the infinitesimal character of the representation. In the context of automorphic forms and the Langlands correspondence, the infinitesimal character is part of the data specifying an automorphic representation. The Harish-Chandra isomorphism identifies the infinitesimal character with a$W$-orbit in$\mathfrak{h}^*$, and the relationship between infinitesimal characters and L-functions is one of the central themes of the Langlands program. The Harish-Chandra isomorphism is thus not merely a result about Lie algebras but a bridge between abstract algebra and deep arithmetic questions.

The Harish-Chandra isomorphism $Z(\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})) \cong \mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{h}^*]^W$ is the definitive description of the center of the enveloping algebra: it identifies the center as a polynomial algebra of rank$\ell$, parametrizes all central characters by$W$-orbits in$\mathfrak{h}^*$, and provides the algebraic foundation for the theory of$\mathcal{D}$-modules on flag varieties, the BGG category$\mathcal{O}$, and the geometric Langlands program.
