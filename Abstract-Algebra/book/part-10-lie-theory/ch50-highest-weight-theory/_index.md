# Chapter 50 — Highest Weight Theory

The classification of simple Lie algebras by Dynkin diagrams, achieved in Chapter 49, raises an immediate next question: given a simple Lie algebra, what are all its finite-dimensional representations? The answer, developed by Cartan and given its definitive form by Weyl, is one of the most elegant results in representation theory. Every finite-dimensional irreducible representation is determined up to isomorphism by a single vector in the weight lattice — its highest weight — and the irreducibles are in bijection with the dominant integral weights, which form a free commutative monoid generated by the fundamental weights $\omega_1, \ldots, \omega_r$. The theory is as clean as the classification itself: an infinite family of irreducibles, each labeled by a combinatorial datum that can be read from the Dynkin diagram.

The proof begins with the observation that any finite-dimensional representation $V$ of $\mathfrak{g}$ decomposes into weight spaces under the action of the Cartan subalgebra $\mathfrak{h}$: $V = \bigoplus_{\lambda \in \mathfrak{h}^*} V_\lambda$ where $V_\lambda = \{v \in V : h \cdot v = \lambda(h) v \text{ for all } h \in \mathfrak{h}\}$. The positive root vectors $e_\alpha$ raise weights (they map $V_\lambda$ to $V_{\lambda+\alpha}$) and negative root vectors $f_\alpha$ lower them. In any finite-dimensional module, weight-raising cannot continue indefinitely, so there must exist a highest weight $\lambda$ — a weight $v_\lambda$ with $e_\alpha \cdot v_\lambda = 0$ for all positive roots $\alpha$. The module is then generated by applying lowering operators to $v_\lambda$, and the study of $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ subalgebras associated to each root shows that $\lambda$ must be dominant and integral: $\langle \lambda, \alpha_i^\vee \rangle \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ for each simple root $\alpha_i$.

To construct irreducibles directly, one builds the Verma module $M(\lambda)$: the universal $\mathfrak{g}$-module generated by a highest weight vector of weight $\lambda$, constructed via the universal enveloping algebra $\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})$ and the Poincaré–Birkhoff–Witt theorem. The Verma module $M(\lambda)$ is in general infinite-dimensional; it has a unique maximal proper submodule, and the quotient $L(\lambda) = M(\lambda) / (\text{max. submod.})$ is the unique irreducible with highest weight $\lambda$. When $\lambda$ is dominant integral, $L(\lambda)$ is finite-dimensional, and this finite-dimensionality theorem is one of the deep results of the chapter.

The Weyl character formula is the crown of the theory. It expresses the character $\text{ch}(L(\lambda)) = \sum_\mu (\dim L(\lambda)_\mu) e^\mu$ (a formal sum in the group ring $\mathbb{Z}[P]$ of the weight lattice $P$) as a ratio: $\text{ch}(L(\lambda)) = \frac{\sum_{w \in W} (-1)^{\ell(w)} e^{w(\lambda + \rho) - \rho}}{\prod_{\alpha > 0}(1 - e^{-\alpha})}$, where $\rho = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha > 0} \alpha$ is the Weyl vector. Specializing this to the trivial representation gives the Weyl denominator identity; differentiating gives the Weyl dimension formula $\dim L(\lambda) = \prod_{\alpha > 0} \frac{\langle \lambda + \rho, \alpha \rangle}{\langle \rho, \alpha \rangle}$, which computes the dimension of any irreducible from the inner product data of the root system. For $\mathfrak{sl}_2$, this gives $\dim L(n) = n+1$; for $\mathfrak{sl}_3$, $\dim L(m\omega_1 + n\omega_2) = \frac{(m+1)(n+1)(m+n+2)}{2}$. The Harish-Chandra isomorphism, treated in Section 50.5, identifies the center $Z(\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g}))$ with the Weyl-invariant polynomial ring $\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{h}^*]^W$ via a ring isomorphism, connecting the algebraic structure of the enveloping algebra to the geometry of the weight lattice.
