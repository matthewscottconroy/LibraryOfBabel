# 51.1.1 Maschke Fails in Characteristic $p \mid |G|$; Non-Split Extensions

Maschke's theorem (Part IX, Chapter 42) is the cornerstone of classical representation theory: every representation of a finite group $G$ over a field$k$ is completely reducible, provided$|G|$ is invertible in$k$. The proof is elegant and elementary — one averages any$k$-linear complement to a$G$-stable subspace over all group elements to produce a$G$-equivariant complement, and this averaging requires dividing by$|G|$. When$\mathrm{char}(k) = p$ and$p \mid |G|$, the denominator vanishes and the argument fails. But the failure is not merely a gap in the proof: the theorem itself becomes false, and the entire character of the representation theory changes.

The simplest example is instructive. Take $G = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ and$k = \mathbb{F}_p$. The group algebra$k[G] \cong k[x]/(x^p - 1)$. In characteristic$p$,$x^p - 1 = (x-1)^p$, so$k[G] \cong k[x]/(x-1)^p$. This is a local ring with maximal ideal$(x-1)$ and a unique simple module (the one-dimensional trivial representation$k$). The regular representation$k[G]$ — which is free of rank 1 as a$k[G]$-module — has a filtration with$p$ composition factors, all isomorphic to$k$, but this filtration does not split: the extension class$\mathrm{Ext}^1_{k[G]}(k, k) \cong k$ is nonzero at every level, and the successive quotients of$k[G]$ are genuinely non-split extensions of$k$ by$k$. There is no way to write$k[G]$ as a direct sum of copies of the trivial module, even though that is the only simple module available.

What does a non-split extension look like concretely? For $G = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ with generator$g$ and$k = \mathbb{F}_p$, consider the two-dimensional module$M = k^2$ on which$g$ acts by the Jordan block$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. The subspace spanned by$e_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ is stable under$g$ (since$g \cdot e_1 = e_1$) and gives a copy of the trivial representation inside$M$. The quotient$M/\langle e_1 \rangle$ is also trivial. But there is no$G$-stable complement to$\langle e_1 \rangle$: a complement would have to be spanned by a vector of the form$e_2 + \lambda e_1$, and$g \cdot (e_2 + \lambda e_1) = e_1 + e_2 + \lambda e_1 = (e_2 + \lambda e_1) + (1)\cdot e_1$, which lies in the complement only if$e_1 = 0$, a contradiction. This two-dimensional module is indecomposable but not simple, and it witnesses the failure of complete reducibility in characteristic$p$.

This collapse of semisimplicity has far-reaching consequences. Non-split extensions between simple modules become central objects of study: the groups $\mathrm{Ext}^n_{k[G]}(S, T)$ capture the "hidden complexity" of the module category. The indecomposable modules — those that cannot be decomposed as a direct sum of two proper submodules — proliferate wildly; in characteristic$p$, even cyclic$p$-groups can have infinitely many indecomposable representations. For$G = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$, the indecomposable modules are$M_d = k[x]/(x-1)^d$ for$1 \leq d \leq p$, and these are precisely the modules$k[G]/(g-1)^d \cdot k[G]$, each with a single Jordan block of size$d$. For$G = \mathbb{Z}/p^2\mathbb{Z}$, there are still only finitely many indecomposable modules over$k$, but for$G = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ with$p > 2$, the number of indecomposable modules is already infinite (this is a module category of "infinite representation type"), and the problem of classifying all indecomposable$k[G]$-modules is wildly undecidable.

The homological algebra of non-split extensions is governed by the Ext groups $\mathrm{Ext}^n_{k[G]}(S, T)$. For a$p$-group$G$ and any simple module$S$ (which must be the trivial module, since$k[G]$ is local), these Ext groups are non-zero in all positive degrees. More precisely,$\mathrm{Ext}^n_{k[\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}]}(k, k) \cong k$ for all$n \geq 0$, which means the trivial module has infinite projective dimension. This is in stark contrast with the characteristic-0 situation, where$\mathrm{Ext}^n_{k[G]}(V, W) = 0$ for all$n \geq 1$ and all$V, W$ by the complete reducibility of the regular representation. The failure of projective dimension to be finite is the homological signature of the breakdown of semisimplicity.

The correct organizational principles for this richer world are the Jacobson radical, projective modules, blocks, and Brauer characters, all of which are developed in the remaining sections of this chapter. The fundamental insight that drives the theory is this: while simple modules no longer generate all modules via direct sums, projective indecomposable modules do generate all modules as quotients (any module has a projective cover), and the structure of these projective modules — their radical filtrations, their decomposition matrices, their relationship to ordinary characters via Brauer theory — encodes the modular representation theory completely.

\begin{example}
For $G = S_3$ (the symmetric group on 3 letters) and$k = \mathbb{F}_3$, we have$|G| = 6 = 2 \cdot 3$, so$p = 3 \mid |G|$. The ordinary representation theory over$\mathbb{C}$ has three irreducible characters: the trivial character$\chi_1$, the sign character$\chi_2$, and the two-dimensional standard representation$\chi_3$. In characteristic 3, the number of simple$k[G]$-modules equals the number of$3$-regular conjugacy classes (those of order coprime to 3). The conjugacy classes of$S_3$ are:$\{e\}$,$\{(12),(13),(23)\}$,$\{(123),(132)\}$, with orders 1, 2, 3. The$3$-regular classes are those of elements with order not divisible by 3, i.e.,$\{e\}$ and$\{(12),(13),(23)\}$. So there are 2 simple modules over$\mathbb{F}_3$. One checks that these are the trivial module$k$ and the sign module$k_{\text{sgn}}$, each one-dimensional, and the two-dimensional standard representation over$\mathbb{F}_3$ is a non-split extension of$k$ by$k_{\text{sgn}}$ (or vice versa). The failure of the two-dimensional representation to remain simple in characteristic 3 is precisely the failure of Maschke's theorem.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Consider $G = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ (the Klein four-group) and$k = \mathbb{F}_2$. Here$|G| = 4 = 2^2$, so$p = 2 \mid |G|$. The group algebra is$k[G] \cong k[x,y]/(x^2, y^2)$ (since$g^2 = e$ for all$g \in G$, so$(g-1)^2 = g^2 - 1 = 0$ in characteristic 2, i.e.,$g = 1 + (g-1)$ with$(g-1)^2 = 0$). This is a local ring with maximal ideal$\mathfrak{m} = (x,y)$ satisfying$\mathfrak{m}^3 = 0$ but$\mathfrak{m}^2 \neq 0$. There is a unique simple module$k$ (the trivial representation), and the indecomposable modules over$k[x,y]/(x^2,y^2)$ form an infinite family (this is a ring of infinite representation type). The failure of Maschke's theorem here is total: the regular representation$k[G]$ is a 4-dimensional indecomposable module, not a direct sum of copies of the trivial$k$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For $G = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ and$k = \mathbb{F}_p$, the extensions$\mathrm{Ext}^1_{k[G]}(k,k)$ are classified by elements of$\mathrm{Hom}(G, k^+) \cong k$ (here$k^+$ denotes$k$ with its additive group structure). The non-trivial extension corresponding to the element$1 \in k$ is exactly the Jordan block module$M_2$ with$g$ acting by$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. The zero element of$\mathrm{Ext}^1$ corresponds to the split extension$k \oplus k$. This illustrates the general principle:$\mathrm{Ext}^1_{k[G]}(S,T)$ parametrizes isomorphism classes of short exact sequences$0 \to T \to M \to S \to 0$, with the zero element being the split extension$S \oplus T$.
\end{example}
