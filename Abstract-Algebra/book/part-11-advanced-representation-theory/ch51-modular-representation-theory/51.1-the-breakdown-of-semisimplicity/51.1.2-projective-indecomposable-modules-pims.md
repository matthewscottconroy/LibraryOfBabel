# 51.1.2 Projective Indecomposable Modules (PIMs)

In the classical semisimple setting, simple modules are the atoms from which everything else is built — every module decomposes as a direct sum of simples, and understanding the theory amounts to cataloguing the simples. In the modular setting, this pleasant picture collapses: simples no longer generate all modules via direct sums, and one needs a richer family of building blocks. The correct replacement is the family of *projective indecomposable modules*, or PIMs.

A module $P$ over$k[G]$ is *projective* if the functor$\mathrm{Hom}_{k[G]}(P, -)$ is exact — that is, if$P$ lifts maps through surjections. Equivalently, every surjection$M \twoheadrightarrow P$ splits, making$P$ a direct summand of$M$. Over a finite-dimensional algebra, this condition has a concrete characterization: projective modules are precisely the direct summands of free modules. In particular,$k[G]$ itself is projective, being free of rank one as a$k[G]$-module. Since$k[G]$ is a free module of rank$|G|$ as a$k$-vector space, any direct summand of$k[G]$ (as a left module) is projective, and by the Krull–Schmidt theorem,$k[G]$ decomposes into a finite direct sum of indecomposable projective modules.

The PIMs are the indecomposable projective $k[G]$-modules, and there is exactly one PIM$P(S)$ for each isomorphism class of simple$k[G]$-module$S$. The construction is canonical: for each primitive idempotent$e \in k[G]$ (an idempotent not expressible as a sum of two nonzero orthogonal idempotents), the module$P = k[G]e$ is a PIM, and every PIM arises this way. The simple module associated to$P(S)$ is its *head*, meaning$P(S)/\mathrm{rad}(P(S)) \cong S$, where$\mathrm{rad}(P(S)) = J(k[G]) \cdot P(S)$ is the radical of$P(S)$. This head condition is the modular analogue of a module being "generated by a highest-weight vector."

The structure of a PIM is far richer than the simple module at its head. The module $P(S)$ is characterized by two properties: its head is$S$, and its *socle*$\mathrm{soc}(P(S))$ — the largest semisimple submodule — is also isomorphic to$S$. This symmetry between the top and the bottom of a PIM is one of the structural surprises of modular representation theory; it comes from the fact that$k[G]$ is a *symmetric algebra* (the algebra is isomorphic to its dual as a bimodule), and it means that$P(S)$ is also *injective* as a$k[G]$-module. In other words, over the group algebra$k[G]$, projective modules and injective modules coincide — a property called *self-injectivity* or the *Nakayama property*, which holds for group algebras but not for general finite-dimensional algebras.

Every projective $k[G]$-module decomposes as a direct sum of PIMs, so the PIMs generate the projective part of the module category. The decomposition of the regular representation$k[G]$ into PIMs mirrors the block decomposition of the group algebra. In this sense, PIMs play in the modular world the role that simples played in the semisimple world — they are the correct indecomposable generators, carrying both projective and structural information that simples alone no longer provide.

One important numerical relationship: if $P(S_j)$ has composition multiplicity$[P(S_j) : S_i] = c_{ij}$ (the$(i,j)$ entry of the Cartan matrix$C$), then the regular representation has$[k[G] : S_i] = \dim_{k} S_i \cdot (\text{number of times } S_i \text{ appears in composition series of } k[G])$. The Cartan matrix encodes how many times each simple appears in each PIM, and as we will see in \S51.3, it satisfies the remarkable relation$C = D^T D$ where$D$ is the decomposition matrix. This is one of the deepest structural facts about modular representation theory.

\begin{example}
For $G = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ and$k = \mathbb{F}_p$, the group algebra$k[G] \cong k[x]/(x-1)^p$ is a local ring with a unique simple module$k$ (the trivial representation). The unique PIM is$k[G]$ itself — the free rank-1 module — which has length$p$ with all composition factors isomorphic to$k$. Its head is$k[G]/\mathrm{rad}(k[G]) \cong k$ and its socle is the submodule$(x-1)^{p-1} k[G] \cong k$. The Cartan matrix is the$1 \times 1$ matrix$(p)$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For $G = S_3$ (the symmetric group) and$k = \mathbb{F}_3$, there are two simple modules$S_1 = k$ (trivial) and$S_2 = k_{\text{sgn}}$ (sign), as noted in the previous section. The two PIMs$P(S_1)$ and$P(S_2)$ each have dimension$3$ over$k$ (since$|G| = 6$ and the PIMs together cover$k[G]$ of dimension 6, with the decomposition$k[G] \cong P(S_1)^{\oplus 1} \oplus P(S_2)^{\oplus 1}$... actually$\dim P(S_i) = 3$ since$6 = 3 + 3$). The PIM$P(S_1)$ has head$S_1$, a middle composition factor, and socle$S_1$; a direct computation shows the middle factor is$S_2$, giving the radical layer structure$S_1 | S_2 | S_1$ (top to bottom: head, middle, socle). Similarly$P(S_2)$ has structure$S_2 | S_1 | S_2$. The Cartan matrix is$C = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Over a field $k$ of characteristic$p$, if$S$ is a simple$k[G]$-module with$\dim_k S = d$, and if the full$p$-part of$|G|$ divides$d$ (so that$|G|_p \mid d$), then$S$ is itself projective. In this case$S = P(S)$ is its own PIM, and the PIM structure is as simple as possible: it has length 1, with head and socle both equal to$S$. Such modules exist when the block containing$S$ has defect 0 (see \S51.2.2). For example, the Steinberg module$\mathrm{St} = L((p-1)\rho)$ for a semisimple algebraic group (see \S51.4.3) is a projective simple module, and for finite groups of Lie type, the Steinberg representation restricted to$G(\mathbb{F}_q)$ gives an example of a projective simple module over$\mathbb{F}_p$.
\end{example}
