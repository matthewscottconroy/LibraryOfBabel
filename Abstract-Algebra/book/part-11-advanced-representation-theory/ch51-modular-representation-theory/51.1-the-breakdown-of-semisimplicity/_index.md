# 51.1 The Breakdown of Semisimplicity

The starting point of modular representation theory is a failure: Maschke's theorem, the cornerstone of the classical theory, no longer holds. The theorem states that if $|G|$ is invertible in the ground field $k$, then every $k[G]$-module is completely reducible. Its proof is almost embarrassingly simple — any $G$-stable complement to a submodule can be produced by averaging over the group, and that averaging requires dividing by $|G|$. When $\mathrm{char}(k) = p$ and $p \mid |G|$, division by $|G|$ is impossible, and the averaging trick breaks down. But the failure is not merely the absence of a proof; it is a genuine structural collapse. Non-split short exact sequences of $k[G]$-modules exist in abundance, and the group ring $k[G]$ is no longer semisimple as a ring.

The simplest non-trivial case makes the breakdown vivid. Let $G = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ and $k = \mathbb{F}_p$. The group algebra is $k[G] \cong k[x]/(x^p - 1)$. In characteristic $p$, the polynomial $x^p - 1 = (x-1)^p$, so $k[G] \cong k[x]/(x-1)^p$. This is a local ring with maximal ideal generated by $(x-1)$ — it has a unique simple module (the trivial representation $k$) but admits $p$ distinct indecomposable modules, one of each $k$-dimension from $1$ to $p$. The module of dimension $j$ has a unique filtration by trivial submodules, but none of these filtrations split. This single example encapsulates the new phenomena: non-splitting extensions, non-isomorphic indecomposable modules, and a module category far more complex than a semisimple one.

Understanding non-split extensions requires the machinery of the projective indecomposable modules, or PIMs. In the semisimple setting, every module is projective; in the modular setting, the projective modules are the "best-behaved" indecomposable modules, and every module admits a projective cover. The PIM $P(S)$ corresponding to a simple module $S$ is the projective cover of $S$; it maps onto $S$ with kernel equal to the radical $\mathrm{rad}(P(S))$. The structure of $P(S)$ — in particular its Loewy series, the filtration $P(S) \supset \mathrm{rad}(P(S)) \supset \mathrm{rad}^2(P(S)) \supset \cdots$ — encodes the extension structure among simple modules in the same block. The Jacobson radical of $k[G]$, the intersection of all maximal left ideals, measures precisely how far $k[G]$ is from being semisimple; when $p \mid |G|$, this radical is nontrivial, and its powers form a descending filtration whose successive quotients are semisimple but whose extensions are nontrivial.

The section establishes the vocabulary and basic results that make modular representation theory tractable: the definition of projective indecomposable modules and their relationship to simple modules via heads and socles; the structure of the Jacobson radical and its successive powers; and the first examples of non-split extensions computed via $\mathrm{Ext}^1_{k[G]}$. These tools lay the groundwork for the block theory and Brauer character theory developed in subsequent sections, which provide global organization for what would otherwise be an unmanageable collection of indecomposable modules.
