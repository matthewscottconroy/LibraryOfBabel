# 51.2.2 Defect Groups; Blocks of Defect 0

The blocks of $k[G]$ are not all equally complicated. Some blocks contain only a single simple module and are as semisimple as possible; others contain many simples bound together by extensions of great depth. The correct measure of this complexity is the *defect group* of a block, a$p$-subgroup of$G$ that precisely captures the "distance from projectivity" of the modules in that block.

To each block $B$ of$k[G]$ one associates a conjugacy class of$p$-subgroups of$G$, the *defect groups* of$B$. The definition is best understood through Higman's criterion: a$k[G]$-module$M$ is said to be *relatively$D$-projective* if the identity map on$M$ factors through the map$M \downarrow^G_D \uparrow^G_D \to M$ given by the counit of the induction-restriction adjunction. In other words,$M$ is a direct summand of the induced module$M \downarrow^G_D \uparrow^G_D$, obtained by restricting to$D$ and then inducing back to$G$. The *defect group* of a block$B$ is the minimal$p$-subgroup$D \leq G$ such that every$B$-module is relatively$D$-projective. This$D$ is well-defined up to conjugacy in$G$, and its order$|D| = p^d$ defines the *defect*$d$ of the block.

Alternatively, the defect group of a block $B$ containing a primitive central idempotent$e$ can be read off from the Brauer homomorphism. The key algebraic fact is that$e$ belongs to the$k$-span of the set$\{g \in G : g \in C_G(P) \text{ for some block } B' \text{ of } k[N_G(D)] \text{ with Brauer correspondent } B\}$, and the defect group$D$ is the Sylow$p$-subgroup of the centralizer of the support. Concretely: if$|G|_p = p^a$ is the full$p$-power in$|G|$, the defect group$D$ of the block$B$ is a$p$-subgroup satisfying$|D| = p^d$ where$d$ is the *defect* defined by the congruence$|G| = p^d \cdot m$ with$p \nmid m$ and$|D| = p^d$ for the specific block.

A *block of defect 0* has trivial defect group $D = \{1\}$. The concrete characterization is: a block$B$ has defect 0 if and only if it contains exactly one simple module$S$, that module is itself projective (so$S = P(S)$ is both simple and projective), and$\dim_k S$ is divisible by the full$p$-power$|G|_p$. In this case,$B \cong M_d(k)$ (a matrix algebra over$k$) and the representation theory inside$B$ is entirely trivial — there is one simple module, it is projective, and every$B$-module is a direct sum of copies of$S$. Blocks of defect 0 are the modular analogue of simple summands in the Artin–Wedderburn decomposition of a semisimple algebra. Finding them amounts to identifying the simple modules of$k[G]$ that are projective, which is equivalent (by the dimension criterion) to finding irreducible representations in characteristic 0 whose dimension is divisible by$|G|_p$.

For the *principal block* $B_0$ — the unique block containing the trivial module — the defect group is a Sylow$p$-subgroup$P \leq G$. This is the most complex block in general, as it carries the most ordinary characters and the most elaborate pattern of extensions between simples. The defect group of the principal block being a Sylow$p$-subgroup follows from the fact that the trivial module$k$ is$P$-projective but not properly$P'$-projective for any proper subgroup$P' \subsetneq P$. The principal block tends to have the largest defect, the most simples, and the most interesting modular representation theory; it is the primary focus of the deep structural conjectures (Broué, Donovan) in the field.

The *Donovan conjecture* asserts that for any finite $p$-group$D$, there are only finitely many Morita equivalence classes of blocks of group algebras over$k$ with defect group$D$. This is one of the central open problems of modular representation theory: it would mean the representation theory of a block is controlled, up to Morita equivalence, by its defect group alone. While the conjecture is open in full generality, it is known for several families of defect groups including cyclic groups (Dade), Klein four-groups, and other specific cases.

\begin{example}
For $G = A_5$ (alternating group) and$k = \mathbb{F}_5$, the Sylow 5-subgroup is$\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$. The principal block$B_0$ has defect group$D \cong \mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$ (defect$d = 1$). All other blocks must have defect groups that are proper subgroups of$\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$, so they have defect 0. Checking dimensions: the 5-dimensional ordinary irreducible representation of$A_5$ has dimension divisible by 5, so its reduction modulo 5 should be projective. Indeed, one can verify that this gives a block of defect 0 containing a single projective simple module of dimension 5.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For $G = GL_2(\mathbb{F}_p)$ and$k = \mathbb{F}_p$, the order is$|G| = (p^2-1)(p^2-p) = p(p-1)^2(p+1)$. The Sylow$p$-subgroup is the group of upper unitriangular matrices, isomorphic to$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$. The principal block has defect group$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ (defect 1). The blocks of defect 0 correspond to irreducible representations whose dimension is divisible by$p$; for$GL_2(\mathbb{F}_p)$, these include the Steinberg representation of dimension$p$. One can verify that the Steinberg module is projective and simple, hence constitutes a block of defect 0 by itself.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For $G = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ and$k = \mathbb{F}_p$, there is only one block (as computed above), and it has defect group$D = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ (defect$d = 1$, since$|G| = p$ and$|G|_p = p$, so$|D| = p^1 = p$). The single PIM has Loewy length$p$, consistent with having a nontrivial defect group. There are no blocks of defect 0 in this case, since the only module is the trivial one, which is not projective (as$\mathrm{Ext}^1(k,k) \neq 0$). This example shows that for$p$-groups, every block has defect$= \log_p |G|$ and there are no defect-0 blocks.
\end{example}
