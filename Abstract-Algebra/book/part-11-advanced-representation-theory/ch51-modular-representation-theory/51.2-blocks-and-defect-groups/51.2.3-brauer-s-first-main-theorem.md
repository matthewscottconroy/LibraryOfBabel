# 51.2.3 Brauer's First Main Theorem

One of the central themes of modular representation theory is the relationship between the representation theory of $G$ and that of its subgroups — particularly its$p$-subgroups and their normalizers. Brauer's First Main Theorem makes this relationship precise at the level of blocks, establishing a canonical bijection that has guided the subject for over seventy years.

*Brauer's First Main Theorem* states: for any $p$-subgroup$D \leq G$, there is a canonical bijection between the set of blocks of$k[G]$ with defect group$D$ and the set of blocks of$k[N_G(D)]$ with defect group$D$. The map, called the *Brauer correspondence*, takes a block$B$ of$k[G]$ with defect group$D$ to a block$b$ of$k[N_G(D)]$ with the same defect group$D$; the correspondence is implemented at the level of primitive central idempotents via the Brauer homomorphism$\mathrm{Br}_D: Z(k[G]) \to Z(k[C_G(D)])$.

The Brauer homomorphism $\mathrm{Br}_D$ is defined on class sums as follows. For a conjugacy class$\mathcal{C}$ of$G$, decompose$\hat{\mathcal{C}} = \sum_{g \in \mathcal{C}} g$ by whether the centralizer$C_G(g)$ contains$D$:$\mathrm{Br}_D(\hat{\mathcal{C}}) = \sum_{g \in \mathcal{C}, D \leq C_G(g)} g$ if any such$g$ exist, and$\mathrm{Br}_D(\hat{\mathcal{C}}) = 0$ otherwise. This map sends the center of$k[G]$ to the center of$k[C_G(D)]$, and after restricting to$Z(k[N_G(D)])$ (via the inclusion$C_G(D) \leq N_G(D)$), it implements the bijection of the theorem. The key point is that primitive central idempotents in$Z(k[G])$ supported on$D$-centralized elements correspond bijectively to primitive central idempotents in$Z(k[N_G(D)])$.

The depth of the theorem becomes clearer when one considers why $N_G(D)$ and not$C_G(D)$ appears. The group$C_G(D)$ is the centralizer of$D$ — the elements that commute with all of$D$ — while$N_G(D)$ is the normalizer, a larger group containing$C_G(D)$ as a normal subgroup. The Brauer homomorphism lands in the center of$k[C_G(D)]$, but the block of$k[N_G(D)]$ containing the image is the correct Brauer correspondent. The passage from$C_G(D)$ to$N_G(D)$ is necessary to keep the defect groups consistent: blocks of$k[N_G(D)]$ can have defect group$D$ (a Sylow$p$-subgroup of$N_G(D)$ in many cases), while blocks of$k[C_G(D)]$ would have larger defect groups in general.

This is remarkable because $N_G(D)$ is typically a much smaller group than$G$, and the blocks of$N_G(D)$ with a given defect group may be significantly simpler to analyze. The theorem says that to count or index the blocks of$G$ with defect group$D$, one only needs to look at the normalizer. For$D$ a Sylow$p$-subgroup$P$ (the case of the principal block), Brauer's theorem says the principal block of$G$ corresponds to the principal block of$N_G(P)$ — a result with significant consequences for the structure of both blocks.

The deeper question — whether not just the number but the entire representation theory of the two sides is equivalent — is the subject of the *Broué conjecture*: when $D$ is abelian, the block$B$ of$k[G]$ and its Brauer correspondent$b$ of$k[N_G(D)]$ should be *derived equivalent* (their derived categories of modules should be equivalent as triangulated categories). This conjecture, still open in general despite extensive evidence, has been one of the driving forces in modular representation theory over the past four decades, connecting the field to homological algebra, algebraic$K$-theory, and the geometry of classifying spaces. The conjecture implies in particular that$B$ and$b$ have the same number of simple modules, the same Cartan matrix, and the same decomposition numbers — all structural invariants that can be verified without proving the full derived equivalence.

\begin{example}
For $G = S_4$ (symmetric group on 4 letters) and$p = 2$, the Sylow 2-subgroup is$D_8$ (dihedral group of order 8). The principal block$B_0$ has defect group$P \cong D_8$. Brauer's theorem then says that the blocks of$k[G]$ with defect group$D_8$ biject with the blocks of$k[N_{S_4}(D_8)]$ with defect group$D_8$. One computes$N_{S_4}(D_8) = D_8$ itself (as$D_8$ is already its own normalizer in$S_4$ up to conjugacy for the specific Sylow 2-subgroup). The corresponding block of$k[D_8]$ is the principal block of$k[D_8]$, and the Broué conjecture predicts that the principal block of$k[S_4]$ and the principal block of$k[D_8]$ are derived equivalent. This has been verified in this case.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For $G = A_5$ and$p = 5$, the Sylow 5-subgroup$P \cong \mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$ is abelian. The normalizer$N_{A_5}(P)$ is the Frobenius group of order 20 (denoted$F_{20}$, the group of affine transformations$x \mapsto ax + b$ of$\mathbb{F}_5$ with$a \neq 0$). Brauer's theorem says the blocks of$k[A_5]$ with defect group$P$ biject with the blocks of$k[F_{20}]$ with defect group$P$. The principal block of$k[A_5]$ corresponds to a specific block of$k[F_{20}]$. Since$P$ is abelian (cyclic), Broué's conjecture predicts that these two blocks are derived equivalent. This case (cyclic defect groups) was proved in full generality by Rickard and, independently, by Rouquier, as part of the general theory of blocks with cyclic defect groups.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For $G = \mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb{F}_p)$ and$p$ an odd prime, the Sylow$p$-subgroup$P \cong \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ is cyclic. The normalizer$N_G(P)$ is a dihedral group of order$p(p-1)/2$ if$4 \mid p-1$, and otherwise of order$p(p-1)$. By Brauer's theorem, the blocks of$k[G]$ with defect$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ biject with blocks of$k[N_G(P)]$ with the same defect group. For$\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb{F}_p)$, the principal block has defect group$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ (cyclic of order$p$), and Brauer's First Main Theorem combined with the theory of blocks with cyclic defect groups (Dade's theorem) completely determines the decomposition numbers and Cartan matrix of the principal block from the structure of the local subgroup$N_G(P)$.
\end{example}
