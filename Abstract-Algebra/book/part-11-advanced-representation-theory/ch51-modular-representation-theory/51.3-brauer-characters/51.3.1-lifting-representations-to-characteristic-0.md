# 51.3.1 Lifting Representations to Characteristic 0

One of the most powerful tools in modular representation theory is the idea of comparing characteristic $p$ representations with characteristic 0 representations — the two settings illuminate each other. The mechanism that makes this comparison precise is the lifting theory: using the ring of integers of a$p$-adic field as an intermediary between characteristic 0 and characteristic$p$.

The setup is as follows. Let $K$ be a complete discretely valued field with$\mathrm{char}(K) = 0$ and residue characteristic$p$, for instance$K = \mathbb{Q}_p$ or a finite unramified extension. Let$\mathcal{O}$ be the ring of integers of$K$, with maximal ideal$(\pi)$ and residue field$k = \mathcal{O}/(\pi) \cong \mathbb{F}_{p^f}$. The ring$\mathcal{O}$ sits as a bridge: one can study$\mathcal{O}[G]$-lattices (finitely generated$\mathcal{O}[G]$-modules that are free over$\mathcal{O}$) and then pass to characteristic 0 by tensoring with$K$ or to characteristic$p$ by reducing modulo$(\pi)$. The choice of$K$ is flexible; for the purposes of representation theory, we will usually take$K$ to be large enough that it contains all$|G|$-th roots of unity (so that$K[G]$ is split semisimple, i.e., all irreducible$K[G]$-representations are absolutely irreducible over$K$).

Given a $K[G]$-module$V$ (a characteristic 0 representation), one can always find an$\mathcal{O}[G]$-stable lattice$\Lambda \subset V$ (since$G$ is finite, one can average any$\mathcal{O}$-lattice over$G$: replace$\Lambda$ by$\sum_{g \in G} g \cdot \Lambda$, which is still a lattice since$G$ is finite). The reduction$\bar{\Lambda} = \Lambda \otimes_{\mathcal{O}} k$ is then a$k[G]$-module, giving a *reduction modulo$p$* map from characteristic-0 representations to characteristic-$p$ representations. The reduction is not unique (different lattices give different reductions), but the composition factors of$\bar{\Lambda}$ are independent of the choice of lattice — this is the content of the Brauer–Nesbitt theorem. Going the other direction, *lifting*: not every$k[G]$-module is the reduction of an$\mathcal{O}[G]$-module, but every projective$k[G]$-module does lift, by the Deformation Lemma (projective modules over residue fields lift to projective modules over complete local rings). This asymmetry — projective modules lift freely while arbitrary modules may not — is the key to the Brauer character theory.

The reason projective modules lift so cleanly is the following: a $k[G]$-module$P$ is projective if and only if it is a direct summand of$k[G]^n$ for some$n$. Since$\mathcal{O}[G]$ is a free$\mathcal{O}$-module and the reduction$\mathcal{O}[G] \otimes_{\mathcal{O}} k \cong k[G]$ is surjective, the idempotents that split off$P$ from$k[G]^n$ can be lifted (by Nakayama's lemma over the complete local ring$\mathcal{O}$) to idempotents in$\mathcal{O}[G]^n$, giving an$\mathcal{O}[G]$-module$\tilde{P}$ reducing to$P$. In contrast, a non-projective module$M$ may have non-trivial$\mathrm{Ext}^1(M, M)$ obstructions to deforming.

The non-uniqueness of reductions is itself informative. If $\Lambda$ and$\Lambda'$ are two different$\mathcal{O}[G]$-stable lattices in the same$K[G]$-module$V$, their reductions$\bar{\Lambda}$ and$\bar{\Lambda}'$ may be non-isomorphic as$k[G]$-modules. However, they have the same composition factors (the same multiset of simple$k[G]$-modules) by the Brauer–Nesbitt theorem. A standard technique for producing different lattices is to replace$\Lambda$ by$g\Lambda$ for some$g \in K[G]$; by taking the$\mathcal{O}[G]$-span of all these, one can access a range of reductions. The set of all possible reductions of a given characteristic-0 module is controlled by the theory of "modular reductions" and is related to the geometry of the Bruhat–Tits building for$GL_n(K)$.

\begin{example}
For $G = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ and the non-trivial character$\chi: G \to K^*$ sending the generator$g$ to a primitive$p$-th root of unity$\zeta_p \in K^*$, the one-dimensional$K[G]$-module$K_\chi$ has$\mathcal{O}[G]$-stable lattice$\mathcal{O}_\chi = \mathcal{O}$ (with$g$ acting by$\zeta_p$). The reduction$\mathcal{O}_\chi \otimes_{\mathcal{O}} k$ has$g$ acting by$\bar\zeta_p = 1$ (since$\zeta_p \equiv 1 \pmod{(\pi)}$ in characteristic$p$), giving the trivial$k[G]$-module. So every non-trivial character of$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ reduces to the trivial representation modulo$p$. This is why the modular representation theory of a$p$-group in characteristic$p$ is so much simpler than the characteristic-0 theory: all ordinary characters reduce to the unique simple module.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For $G = S_3$ and$p = 3$, take$K = \mathbb{Q}_3(\sqrt{-3})$ (which contains a primitive cube root of unity$\omega = (-1 + \sqrt{-3})/2$). The three ordinary irreducible$K[G]$-modules are$K$ (trivial, dimension 1),$K_{\text{sgn}}$ (sign, dimension 1), and$V$ (standard, dimension 2). For the standard 2-dimensional representation$V$, take the$\mathcal{O}[G]$-lattice$\Lambda = \mathcal{O}^2$ with the standard matrix action. Modulo 3 (since$\omega \equiv 1 \pmod{(\pi)}$ where$(\pi)$ is the maximal ideal of$\mathcal{O}$), the action of the generators of$S_3$ on$\bar\Lambda = \mathbb{F}_3^2$ has a fixed subspace (the trivial summand in characteristic 3) that does not split off. The reduction$\bar\Lambda$ is indecomposable of length 2 with composition factors$S_1$ (trivial) and$S_2$ (sign). This shows that the decomposition matrix entry$d_{V, S_1} = d_{V, S_2} = 1$ (the standard representation reduces to a module with both simples appearing once).
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For the regular representation $K[G]$, the natural$\mathcal{O}[G]$-lattice is$\mathcal{O}[G]$ itself. Its reduction modulo$p$ is$k[G]$, the full group algebra. The characteristic-0 decomposition of$K[G]$ into simple modules is$K[G] \cong \bigoplus_i V_i^{\oplus \dim V_i}$ (each irreducible$V_i$ appearing with multiplicity$\dim V_i$, by the Artin–Wedderburn theorem). The reduction of each$V_i^{\oplus \dim V_i}$ gives$\bar{V}_i^{\oplus \dim V_i}$, and the composition factors of$k[G]$ are determined by the decomposition matrix:$[k[G] : S_j] = \sum_i (\dim V_i) \cdot d_{ij}$. This is consistent with$[P(S_j) : S_j] = c_{jj}$ and the relation$C = D^T D$.
\end{example}
