# 51.4.3 Steinberg's Tensor Product Theorem for Algebraic Groups

The classification of irreducible representations of semisimple Lie algebras over $\mathbb{C}$ by dominant integral highest weights — the theorem of the highest weight — carries over to algebraic groups over algebraically closed fields of any characteristic. But in characteristic$p$, an extraordinary new feature appears: the *Frobenius endomorphism* shatters every irreducible representation into a tensor product of smaller pieces, each living in a "Frobenius twist" of a restricted module. This is Steinberg's tensor product theorem, one of the cornerstones of the modular theory, which reduces an infinite classification problem to a finite one.

Let $G$ be a connected semisimple algebraic group over$\overline{\mathbb{F}}_p$, and let$P^+ = \{\lambda \in X^*(T) : \langle \lambda, \alpha^\vee \rangle \geq 0 \text{ for all simple } \alpha\}$ denote the dominant integral weights. For each$\lambda \in P^+$, there is an irreducible rational$G$-representation$L(\lambda)$ with highest weight$\lambda$, and these exhaust all irreducibles up to isomorphism.

\begin{definition}
The *Frobenius endomorphism* $\phi: G \to G$ is the algebraic group endomorphism that raises each matrix entry to the$p$-th power. For any rational$G$-representation$M$, the *Frobenius twist*$M^{[1]}$ is the representation on the same underlying vector space but with the action twisted through$\phi$: the element$g \in G$ acts on$M^{[1]}$ by the same formula as$\phi(g)$ acts on$M$. Iterating,$M^{[r]}$ denotes the$r$-fold Frobenius twist.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
$L(\lambda)^{[1]} \cong L(p\lambda)$ for every dominant integral weight$\lambda$.
\end{proposition}

This follows because the highest weight of $L(\lambda)^{[1]}$ is the character$\lambda \circ \phi$, and on the torus$T$ the Frobenius acts by raising characters to the$p$-th power, so$(\lambda \circ \phi)(t) = \lambda(\phi(t)) = \lambda(t^p) = p\lambda(t)$. The highest weight of$M^{[1]}$ is therefore$p$ times the highest weight of$M$.

\begin{definition}
A dominant weight $\lambda \in P^+$ is *restricted* if$\langle \lambda, \alpha^\vee \rangle \leq p - 1$ for all simple roots$\alpha$. The set of restricted dominant weights is the *restricted region*$X_1 = \{\lambda \in P^+ : 0 \leq \langle \lambda, \alpha^\vee \rangle \leq p-1 \text{ for all simple } \alpha\}$.
\end{definition}

Since there are finitely many simple roots, the restricted region is a finite set (a $p$-dilate of the fundamental domain for$P^+$), with$|X_1| = p^r$ where$r$ is the rank of$G$.

\begin{theorem}[Steinberg's Tensor Product Theorem, 1963]
Every dominant integral weight $\lambda \in P^+$ has a unique base-$p$ expansion
$$\lambda = \lambda_0 + p\lambda_1 + p^2\lambda_2 + \cdots + p^r\lambda_r$$
with each $\lambda_i \in X_1$ restricted. The corresponding irreducible module satisfies
$$L(\lambda) \cong L(\lambda_0) \otimes L(\lambda_1)^{[1]} \otimes L(\lambda_2)^{[2]} \otimes \cdots \otimes L(\lambda_r)^{[r]}.$$
\end{theorem}

The proof uses the Frobenius morphism in an essential way. One shows that the tensor product on the right is irreducible with highest weight $\lambda$ by a weight argument: the highest weight vector is the tensor product of highest weight vectors, and one checks irreducibility using the structure of the Frobenius twist. The key input is that tensoring a restricted module$L(\lambda_0)$ with a Frobenius-twisted module$L(\mu)^{[1]}$ introduces no new$\mathfrak{g}$-module structure beyond what is already present, because the Lie algebra$\mathfrak{g}$ acts on$L(\mu)^{[1]}$ through the Frobenius map and hence acts "at a higher scale."

**Example 1.** Let $G = SL_2$ over$\overline{\mathbb{F}}_5$, so$p = 5$ and rank 1. The restricted weights are$\lambda_0 \in \{0, 1, 2, 3, 4\}$ (corresponding to the$1$-,$2$-,$3$-,$4$-, and$5$-dimensional representations). For$\lambda = 7 = 2 + 5 \cdot 1$, we get$\lambda_0 = 2$,$\lambda_1 = 1$. Steinberg's theorem says$L(7) \cong L(2) \otimes L(1)^{[1]}$. The representation$L(2)$ is the symmetric square$\mathrm{Sym}^2(k^2)$, of dimension$3$. The twist$L(1)^{[1]}$ has dimension$2$ but the$SL_2$ action is via the Frobenius: a matrix$\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ acts as$\begin{pmatrix} a^5 & b^5 \\ c^5 & d^5 \end{pmatrix}$. The tensor product$L(2) \otimes L(1)^{[1]}$ has dimension$6$ and highest weight$2 + 5 = 7$, as expected.

**Example 2.** For $G = SL_2$ over$\overline{\mathbb{F}}_2$ ($p = 2$), consider$\lambda = 5 = 1 + 2 \cdot 0 + 4 \cdot 1$. The base-$2$ expansion is$5 = 1 + 0 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 4$, so$\lambda_0 = 1$,$\lambda_1 = 0$,$\lambda_2 = 1$. Steinberg gives$L(5) \cong L(1) \otimes L(0)^{[1]} \otimes L(1)^{[2]} \cong L(1) \otimes k \otimes L(1)^{[2]} \cong L(1) \otimes L(1)^{[2]}$. Here$L(1)$ is the standard$2$-dimensional representation, and$L(1)^{[2]}$ is the standard representation twisted by$\phi^2$ (squaring matrix entries twice). The tensor product has dimension$4$ and highest weight$1 + 4 = 5$.

**Example 3.** The *Steinberg module* $\mathrm{St} = L((p-1)\rho)$, where$\rho = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha > 0} \alpha$ is the Weyl vector, is the unique restricted irreducible module that is also projective as a$G$-module (and as a$u(\mathfrak{g})$-module). For$G = SL_2$ and$p = 5$, the Steinberg module is$L(4) = \mathrm{Sym}^4(k^2)$, of dimension$5$. Its character is given by the Weyl character formula without corrections (unlike other restricted modules, which may require Jantzen's sum formula to compute). The Steinberg module is absolutely pivotal: it appears as a direct summand in every projective module and satisfies$\mathrm{St} \otimes L \cong \mathrm{St}^{\oplus d}$ for appropriate$d$.

\begin{corollary}
The classification of irreducible rational $G$-representations reduces to the classification of irreducible modules$L(\lambda)$ for$\lambda \in X_1$ — a finite set. All other irreducibles are obtained from these by Frobenius twisting and tensoring.
\end{corollary}

This reduction is one of the most powerful structural results in modular representation theory. It converts an a priori infinite classification into a finite computation — but in practice, computing the restricted irreducibles $L(\lambda)$ for$\lambda \in X_1$ is already extremely difficult. For$G = SL_2$, the restricted irreducibles are$L(0), L(1), \ldots, L(p-1)$, and their dimensions$1, 2, \ldots, p$ are known. For higher-rank groups, determining$\dim L(\lambda)$ for$\lambda \in X_1$ is an open problem in general (the Lusztig conjecture, proved for large$p$ and related to quantum groups at roots of unity, gives a conjectural formula via the$p$-Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomials).

\begin{theorem}[Character formula for the Steinberg module]
$$\mathrm{ch}(\mathrm{St}) = \prod_{\alpha > 0} \frac{e^{(p-1)\rho} - e^{(p-1)\rho - p\alpha}}{1 - e^{-\alpha}} = \prod_{\alpha > 0} (e^{(p-1)\alpha/2} + e^{(p-3)\alpha/2} + \cdots + e^{-(p-1)\alpha/2}),$$
a product of $p$-analogues of the Weyl denominator.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}
The Steinberg tensor product theorem has a parallel in the theory of quantum groups (Chapter 53): at a root of unity $q = e^{2\pi i/\ell}$, the representation theory of$U_q(\mathfrak{g})$ exhibits an analogous decomposition via the quantum Frobenius map, and the "Frobenius twists" in the quantum setting correspond to representations pulled back through the quantum Frobenius. The Lusztig conjecture for algebraic groups is equivalent, via this analogy, to a corresponding conjecture about quantum groups at roots of unity — a connection that has been the key to proving the conjecture in the large$p$ case (Kazhdan–Lusztig, Kashiwara–Tanisaki, Andersen–Jantzen–Soergel).
\end{remark}

The tensor product theorem is thus not merely a classification theorem — it reveals that the Frobenius endomorphism organizes the entire modular representation theory of $G$ into a hierarchical structure, with the restricted representations at the base and all others built from them by a canonical inductive procedure.
