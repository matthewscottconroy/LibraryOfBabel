# Chapter 51 — Modular Representation Theory

The classical theory of group representations rests on a single foundational pillar: Maschke's theorem, which guarantees that every representation of a finite group $G$ over a field of characteristic zero — or, more generally, over any field whose characteristic does not divide $|G|$ — decomposes as a direct sum of irreducible subrepresentations. This semisimplicity is what makes the ordinary theory so clean: characters determine representations, the group ring $k[G]$ splits as a product of matrix algebras, and every module question reduces to questions about simple modules. But when the characteristic of $k$ divides the order of $G$, the entire edifice collapses. Maschke's theorem fails, and with it goes the promise of complete reducibility. What replaces it is a richer, more intricate theory — modular representation theory — in which the indecomposable but non-irreducible modules become the true protagonists.

The failure of semisimplicity in characteristic $p$ when $p \mid |G|$ is not merely a technical inconvenience; it signals that the group ring $k[G]$ carries genuine non-semisimple structure that encodes deep arithmetic information about $G$. The simplest example is already instructive: when $G = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ and $k = \mathbb{F}_p$, the group ring $k[G] \cong \mathbb{F}_p[x]/(x^p)$ is a local ring with a unique simple module but $p$ distinct indecomposable modules, one for each dimension from $1$ to $p$. The module of dimension $k$ has a unique composition series with $k$ trivial composition factors, but it does not split. This failure to split — the existence of non-split extensions — is the hallmark of the modular setting. Studying these extensions, and organizing them into the right categorical structures, is the work of the chapter.

The organizing principle that emerges from Richard Brauer's foundational work is the block decomposition: the group ring $k[G]$ decomposes as a product of indecomposable two-sided ideals $B_1 \times \cdots \times B_r$, called blocks, and every $k[G]$-module lies entirely within one block. Each block $B_i$ is itself a non-semisimple local-to-global object, characterized by a $p$-subgroup $D_i \leq G$ called its defect group, which measures the block's distance from semisimplicity. A block of defect zero contains a single irreducible representation, which is itself projective — here the modular theory behaves as well as the ordinary theory. Blocks of higher defect contain multiple irreducible and indecomposable modules, and the combinatorial structure of these blocks — their Brauer trees, Cartan matrices, and decomposition numbers — remains a subject of active research.

Brauer characters provide the essential bridge between the modular theory and the familiar world of ordinary characters. The key insight is that while representations in characteristic $p$ cannot be directly compared to complex representations, any module over $\mathbb{F}_p$ (or more precisely, over a $p$-adic lift) can be analyzed by restricting to the $p$-regular elements of $G$ — those whose order is coprime to $p$ — and reading off a class function valued in a characteristic-zero ring. These Brauer characters satisfy orthogonality relations analogous to those of ordinary characters, and the transition matrix between ordinary characters and Brauer characters — the decomposition matrix — encodes how ordinary irreducible representations break apart when reduced modulo $p$. The Cartan matrix, recording the composition multiplicities of projective indecomposable modules, is related to the decomposition matrix by the equation $C = D^T D$, a relation that has no analogue in the ordinary theory.

The chapter closes with modular representations of Lie algebras in characteristic $p$. Here a new phenomenon appears: the restricted Lie algebra structure, governed by the $[p]$-power map $x \mapsto x^{[p]}$, plays the role that the full enveloping algebra plays in characteristic zero. Steinberg's tensor product theorem, a deep result with no classical counterpart, shows that every irreducible representation of a simply connected algebraic group over $\mathbb{F}_p$ factors as a tensor product of Frobenius twists of "restricted" representations — those on which the $[p]$-power map acts as prescribed. This theorem reveals a fractal self-similarity in the modular representation theory of algebraic groups that is one of the most beautiful structural results in the subject.
