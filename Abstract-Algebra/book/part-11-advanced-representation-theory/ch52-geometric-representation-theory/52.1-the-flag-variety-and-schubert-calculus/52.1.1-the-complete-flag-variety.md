# 52.1.1 The Complete Flag Variety $G/B$

One of the deepest insights in modern representation theory is that the representation theory of a Lie group $G$ is fundamentally geometric — it is controlled by the geometry of spaces on which$G$ acts. The simplest and most important such space is the *complete flag variety*$G/B$, the quotient of a semisimple algebraic group$G$ by a Borel subgroup$B$. For$G = GL_n(\mathbb{C})$ and$B$ the subgroup of upper triangular matrices,$G/B$ is exactly the variety parameterizing complete flags$0 = V_0 \subset V_1 \subset \cdots \subset V_n = \mathbb{C}^n$ with$\dim V_i = i$. The appearance of flags here is not accidental: a Borel subgroup is precisely the stabilizer of a complete flag in the standard representation, so the quotient$G/B$ naturally parameterizes all such flags via the$G$-action.

\begin{definition}
Let $G$ be a connected semisimple algebraic group over$\mathbb{C}$, and let$B \subset G$ be a Borel subgroup (a maximal connected solvable subgroup). The *complete flag variety* of$G$ is the quotient$\mathcal{F}\ell = G/B$, regarded as a projective algebraic variety with the quotient topology and the natural$G$-equivariant structure sheaf.
\end{definition}

The flag variety $G/B$ is a smooth projective algebraic variety of complex dimension$|\Phi^+|$ — the number of positive roots of$G$, which also equals$\dim \mathfrak{n}^+$, the dimension of the nilpotent radical of the Borel. It carries a transitive action of$G$ by left multiplication. This action is the source of its representation-theoretic power: the group$G$ acts on the cohomology of$G/B$, and the resulting representations are intimately linked to the irreducible representations of$G$ and its Lie algebra$\mathfrak{g}$.

**Example 1.** For $G = GL_2(\mathbb{C})$, the flag variety is$G/B = \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C})$, the Riemann sphere. A complete flag in$\mathbb{C}^2$ is just the choice of a one-dimensional subspace (a line through the origin), so$G/B$ parametrizes lines in$\mathbb{C}^2$, which is projective space$\mathbb{P}^1$. The$GL_2$-action by change of basis is the standard action on$\mathbb{P}^1$ by Möbius transformations. In this case,$|\Phi^+| = 1$ and$G/B$ has complex dimension 1.

**Example 2.** For $G = GL_3(\mathbb{C})$, a complete flag is a chain$0 \subset V_1 \subset V_2 \subset \mathbb{C}^3$ with$\dim V_1 = 1$,$\dim V_2 = 2$. The flag variety is a complex manifold of dimension$|\Phi^+| = 3$ (the three positive roots$e_1 - e_2$,$e_2 - e_3$,$e_1 - e_3$ of$\mathfrak{gl}_3$). It can be described as the incidence variety$\{(\ell, H) : \ell \subset H\} \subset \mathbb{P}^2 \times (\mathbb{P}^2)^*$ — pairs consisting of a line$\ell$ in$\mathbb{P}^2$ and a hyperplane$H$ (a line in the dual) containing$\ell$. The Euler characteristic is$|W| = |S_3| = 6$, reflecting the six Schubert cells (Bruhat cells) into which$G/B$ decomposes.

The key connection between the flag variety and representation theory is encoded in the Borel–Weil theorem. For each weight $\lambda$ of the torus$T \subset B$, there is a natural$G$-equivariant line bundle$\mathcal{L}_\lambda$ on$G/B$, constructed as the associated bundle
$$\mathcal{L}_\lambda = G \times^B k_\lambda$$
where $k_\lambda$ is the one-dimensional$B$-module of weight$\lambda$ (the torus acts by$\lambda$ and the unipotent radical acts trivially).

\begin{theorem}[Borel–Weil]
If $\lambda$ is a dominant integral weight, then$H^0(G/B, \mathcal{L}_\lambda) \cong L(\lambda)^*$, the dual of the irreducible$G$-representation of highest weight$\lambda$. For$\lambda$ not dominant,$H^0(G/B, \mathcal{L}_\lambda) = 0$.
\end{theorem}

**Example 3.** For $G = SL_2(\mathbb{C})$, the flag variety is$G/B = \mathbb{P}^1$. The line bundle$\mathcal{L}_\lambda = \mathcal{O}(n)$ for$\lambda = n\omega$ ($n \geq 0$,$\omega$ the fundamental weight). The global sections are$H^0(\mathbb{P}^1, \mathcal{O}(n)) = \mathrm{Sym}^n(\mathbb{C}^2)^*$, of dimension$n+1$ — the dual of the$(n+1)$-dimensional irreducible of$SL_2$, as predicted. For$n < 0$ (the line bundle$\mathcal{O}(n)$ has negative degree), there are no global sections, and indeed$H^0 = 0$.

The higher cohomology groups $H^i(G/B, \mathcal{L}_\lambda)$ for$i > 0$ are handled by the Borel–Weil–Bott theorem.

\begin{theorem}[Borel–Weil–Bott]
For $\lambda \in \mathfrak{h}^*$ integral:
\begin{itemize}
\item If $\lambda + \rho$ is not in the interior of any Weyl chamber (i.e.,$\langle \lambda + \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle = 0$ for some root$\alpha$), then$H^i(G/B, \mathcal{L}_\lambda) = 0$ for all$i$.
\item If $\lambda + \rho$ is in the closure of a Weyl chamber, let$w \in W$ be the unique Weyl group element such that$w(\lambda + \rho) - \rho$ is dominant. Then$H^i(G/B, \mathcal{L}_\lambda) = 0$ for$i \neq \ell(w)$, and$H^{\ell(w)}(G/B, \mathcal{L}_\lambda) \cong L(w(\lambda+\rho) - \rho)$.
\end{itemize}
\end{theorem}

**Example 4.** For $G = SL_2$,$\lambda = -3\omega$ (anti-dominant). Then$\lambda + \rho = (-3 + 1)\omega = -2\omega$, and the unique$w \in W$ with$w(-2\omega) > 0$ is the non-trivial element$w_0$ with$\ell(w_0) = 1$. Bott's theorem gives$H^1(\mathbb{P}^1, \mathcal{O}(-3)) \cong L(w_0(-2\omega) - \rho) = L(2\omega - \omega) = L(\omega)$, the 2-dimensional standard representation. This can be verified directly:$H^1(\mathbb{P}^1, \mathcal{O}(-n)) \cong \mathbb{C}^{n-1}$ for$n \geq 2$ by Serre duality.

The Borel–Weil–Bott theorem is the first and clearest indication that the geometry of $G/B$ encodes the full representation theory of$G$: every irreducible representation arises as a cohomology group of a line bundle on the flag variety. This is both a computational tool and a philosophical statement: representations are not abstract algebraic objects but geometric objects — sections of bundles on a single canonical space.

\begin{remark}
In characteristic $p > 0$, the Borel–Weil theorem continues to hold, but the Bott theorem fails: higher cohomology groups of line bundles on$G/B$ are no longer always irreducible or always zero. The failure of Bott's theorem is the geometric reflection of the failure of Weyl's character formula in the modular setting, and computing these higher cohomology groups is one of the central unsolved problems in the modular representation theory of algebraic groups (related to the Lusztig conjecture and Andersen's work on Jantzen filtrations).
\end{remark}
