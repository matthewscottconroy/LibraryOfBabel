# 52.1.2 Schubert Cells and the Bruhat Decomposition

To study the geometry of $G/B$ systematically, one needs a decomposition into manageable pieces. The natural decomposition comes from the action of the Borel subgroup$B$ itself: since$B$ acts on$G/B$ by left multiplication, the orbits of this action partition the flag variety into locally closed subvarieties. These orbits are the *Schubert cells*, and their combinatorial indexing by the Weyl group$W$ is one of the central organizing principles of the theory.

For each $w \in W$, the Schubert cell is$C_w = BwB/B$, an orbit of$B$ in$G/B$. The key geometric fact is that$C_w \cong \mathbb{A}^{\ell(w)}$ — it is an affine space of dimension equal to the length$\ell(w)$ of$w$ in the Weyl group. The decomposition$G/B = \bigsqcup_{w \in W} C_w$ is the *Bruhat decomposition*, and it shows that$G/B$ carries a natural CW-complex structure where the cells have only even real dimension. This forces the cohomology$H^*(G/B; \mathbb{Z})$ to be torsion-free and concentrated in even degrees — a remarkable topological consequence of a purely algebraic decomposition.

\begin{theorem}[Bruhat Decomposition]
Let $G$ be a semisimple algebraic group with Borel subgroup$B$ and Weyl group$W = N_G(T)/T$. Then
$$G = \bigsqcup_{w \in W} BwB$$
as a disjoint union of double cosets, and $G/B = \bigsqcup_{w \in W} BwB/B$ as a disjoint union of locally closed subsets. Each Bruhat cell$C_w = BwB/B \cong \mathbb{A}^{\ell(w)}$ is isomorphic to affine space of dimension$\ell(w)$.
\end{theorem}

The proof of the affine-space isomorphism uses the root system. Write $B = TU^+$ where$U^+ = \prod_{\alpha > 0} U_\alpha$ is the unipotent radical. For$w \in W$, let$\Phi^+(w) = \{\alpha \in \Phi^+ : w^{-1}\alpha < 0\}$ be the set of positive roots sent negative by$w^{-1}$; then$|\Phi^+(w)| = \ell(w)$. One shows that the multiplication map$\prod_{\alpha \in \Phi^+(w)} U_\alpha \to C_w$ is an isomorphism of varieties, where the product is taken in any fixed order. Since each$U_\alpha \cong \mathbb{A}^1$, this gives$C_w \cong \mathbb{A}^{\ell(w)}$.

**Example 1.** For $G = GL_3(\mathbb{C})$ and$W = S_3$, the six Weyl group elements give cells$C_w$ of dimensions$0, 1, 1, 2, 2, 3$. Explicitly:
- $w = e$ (identity):$C_e = eB/B$, a single point (dimension 0).
- $w = s_1 = (12)$:$C_{s_1} \cong \mathbb{A}^1$ (dimension 1).
- $w = s_2 = (23)$:$C_{s_2} \cong \mathbb{A}^1$ (dimension 1).
- $w = s_1 s_2 = (132)$:$C_{s_1 s_2} \cong \mathbb{A}^2$ (dimension 2).
- $w = s_2 s_1 = (123)$:$C_{s_2 s_1} \cong \mathbb{A}^2$ (dimension 2).
- $w = w_0 = (13)$:$C_{w_0} \cong \mathbb{A}^3$ (dimension 3, the dense open cell).

The closure $\overline{C_w}$ of a Schubert cell is a *Schubert variety*$X_w$, a projective subvariety of$G/B$ of complex dimension$\ell(w)$. The Schubert varieties are the geometric heart of the theory: they encode the combinatorics of the Weyl group via the *Bruhat order*$\leq$ on$W$, since$X_w = \bigsqcup_{v \leq w} C_v$. This means that$v \leq w$ in the Bruhat order if and only if$X_v \subset X_w$ — a beautiful interaction between the partial order on$W$ and the geometry of$G/B$.

\begin{definition}
The *Bruhat order* on $W$ is defined by$v \leq w$ if and only if$v$ can be obtained from$w$ by removing simple reflections from a reduced expression for$w$. Equivalently,$v \leq w$ iff$X_v \subset X_w$ iff$C_v \subset \overline{C_w}$.
\end{definition}

**Example 2.** In $S_3$ with simple reflections$s_1 = (12)$,$s_2 = (23)$, the Bruhat order has$e < s_1, e < s_2 < s_1 s_2, s_2 s_1 < w_0$. Concretely,$X_{s_1}$ is a$\mathbb{P}^1$ inside the flag variety;$X_{s_2}$ is another$\mathbb{P}^1$;$X_{s_1 s_2}$ is a surface containing both$X_{s_1}$ and$X_{s_2}$; and$X_{w_0} = G/B$ is the entire flag variety.

Schubert varieties are generally singular. The question of when $X_w$ is smooth has a beautiful combinatorial answer in type$A$.

\begin{theorem}[Pattern Avoidance Criterion, Lakshmibai–Sandhya 1990]
For $G = GL_n$ and$w \in S_n$, the Schubert variety$X_w$ is smooth if and only if$w$ avoids the permutation patterns$3412$ and$4231$ — meaning there are no indices$i < j < k < l$ with$w(i), w(j), w(k), w(l)$ in the relative order of$3,4,1,2$ or$4,2,3,1$ respectively.
\end{theorem}

**Example 3.** In $S_4$, the permutation$w = 2143$ avoids$3412$ and$4231$, so$X_w$ is smooth. The permutation$w = 3412$ itself does not avoid$3412$ (it contains the pattern trivially), so$X_{3412}$ is singular.

The nature of the singularities of Schubert varieties is deep and has two related descriptions. Analytically, the singular locus of $X_w$ is$\bigcup_{v \lessdot_s w} X_v$ where the union runs over those$v$ covered by$w$ in the Bruhat order for which the corresponding Schubert variety is singular along$X_v$. Algebraically, the singularity is measured by the *Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomial*$P_{v,w}(q)$: the variety$X_w$ is smooth along$X_v$ if and only if$P_{v,w}(q) = 1$.

\begin{remark}
The Bruhat decomposition is the starting point for computing the cohomology of $G/B$ (Section 52.1.3), the representation-theoretic content of the flag variety (via the Beilinson–Bernstein theorem, Section 52.2), and the theory of perverse sheaves and Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomials (Section 52.3). Each level of the theory builds on the combinatorial skeleton provided by the Bruhat decomposition and the Bruhat order on$W$.
\end{remark}

**Example 4.** The affine Grassmannian $\mathrm{Gr}_G = G(\mathbb{C}((t)))/G(\mathbb{C}[[t]])$ admits an analogous Bruhat decomposition indexed by the coweight lattice (the affine Weyl group orbits). The corresponding Schubert varieties in$\mathrm{Gr}_G$ are the arenas of the geometric Satake equivalence (Section 54.4.3). The finite-dimensional Schubert varieties of$G/B$ thus have infinite-dimensional analogues that play an equally central role in the geometric Langlands program.
