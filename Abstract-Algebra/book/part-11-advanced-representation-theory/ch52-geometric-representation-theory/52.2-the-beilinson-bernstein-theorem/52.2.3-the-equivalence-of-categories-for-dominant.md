# 52.2.3 The Equivalence of Categories (for Dominant $\lambda$)

The central result of this section is the *Beilinson–Bernstein localization theorem*, proved in 1981, which gives a complete answer to when the localization and global sections functors are mutually inverse equivalences. The theorem is one of the cornerstones of geometric representation theory: it transforms the algebraic study of $\mathfrak{g}$-modules into the geometric study of$\mathcal{D}$-modules on the flag variety, where the full machinery of algebraic geometry becomes available.

\begin{definition}
A weight $\lambda \in \mathfrak{h}^*$ is *dominant* if$\langle \lambda + \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle \notin \{0, -1, -2, \ldots\}$ for all positive roots$\alpha$, equivalently$\langle \lambda + \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle > 0$ for all$\alpha > 0$ when$\lambda$ is integral. It is *antidominant* if$\langle \lambda + \rho, \alpha^\vee \rangle \notin \{1, 2, 3, \ldots\}$ for all positive roots$\alpha$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Beilinson–Bernstein Localization, 1981]
If $\lambda \in \mathfrak{h}^*$ is dominant, then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The global sections functor $\Gamma: \mathcal{D}_\lambda\text{-mod} \to M(\mathfrak{g}, \lambda)$ is exact.
\item The unit map $M \xrightarrow{\sim} \Gamma(\Delta_\lambda(M))$ is an isomorphism for every$M \in M(\mathfrak{g}, \lambda)$.
\item The counit map $\Delta_\lambda(\Gamma(\mathcal{N})) \xrightarrow{\sim} \mathcal{N}$ is an isomorphism for every$\mathcal{N} \in \mathcal{D}_\lambda\text{-mod}$.
\end{enumerate}
In particular, $\Delta_\lambda$ and$\Gamma$ are mutually inverse equivalences of abelian categories:
$$M(\mathfrak{g}, \lambda) \simeq \mathcal{D}_\lambda\text{-mod}.$$
\end{theorem}

The dominance hypothesis on $\lambda$ is essential: it ensures that$H^i(G/B, \mathcal{D}_\lambda \otimes_{\mathcal{D}_{G/B}} \mathcal{M}) = 0$ for$i > 0$ and every$\mathcal{D}_\lambda$-module$\mathcal{M}$, which is what makes$\Gamma$ exact. Geometrically, dominance ensures that the line bundle$\mathcal{L}_\lambda$ is "positive enough" that the relevant higher cohomology vanishes.

**Example 1.** For $G = SL_2$ and$\lambda = n\omega$ with$n \geq 0$ (dominant integral), the theorem says that$M(\mathfrak{sl}_2, n\omega) \simeq \mathcal{D}_{n\omega}\text{-mod}$ on$\mathbb{P}^1$. The category$M(\mathfrak{sl}_2, n\omega)$ consists of finitely generated$\mathfrak{sl}_2$-modules on which the Casimir element$C = H^2/4 + EF + FE$ acts by the scalar$n(n+2)/4$ (the value of the Casimir on the highest weight representation$L(n\omega)$). This is a category with simple objects$L(k\omega)$ for$k \equiv n \pmod{2}$ (only finitely many, since the central character is fixed). The corresponding$\mathcal{D}_{n\omega}$-modules on$\mathbb{P}^1$ are the holonomic$\mathcal{D}$-modules: ordinary sheaves (since$\mathbb{P}^1$ is smooth of dimension 1, all coherent$\mathcal{D}$-modules are holonomic).

**Example 2.** For $\lambda = -\rho$ (antidominant, not dominant), the theorem fails:$\Gamma$ is not exact, and the localization functor is not an equivalence. In this case, the module$M(-\rho)$ (the Verma module of weight$-\rho$) is in the category but$H^1(G/B, \Delta_{-\rho}(M(-\rho))) \neq 0$. The correct statement at$\lambda = -\rho$ involves derived categories: the derived localization is still an equivalence of derived categories, though not of abelian categories.

**Example 3.** For $G = SL_3$ and$\lambda = \omega_1 + \omega_2 = 2\rho/...$ (the sum of fundamental weights, which is dominant integral), the equivalence identifies the abelian category of$\mathfrak{sl}_3$-modules with central character$\chi_\lambda$ with the abelian category of coherent$\mathcal{D}_\lambda$-modules on the flag variety$GL_3/B$. The Verma modules$M(w \cdot \lambda)$ for$w \in S_3$ localize to the standard$\mathcal{D}$-modules$j_{w,!}(\mathcal{O}_{C_w})$, the direct images of the structure sheaf on each Schubert cell with extension by zero. The simple modules$L(w \cdot \lambda)$ localize to the intersection cohomology$\mathcal{D}$-modules$j_{w,!*}(\mathcal{O}_{C_w})$ — the intermediate extensions, which are the simple perverse sheaves.

The power of this theorem lies in what it enables. Many questions about $\mathfrak{g}$-modules translate, via the equivalence, into geometric questions about$\mathcal{D}$-modules on$G/B$.

\begin{corollary}
Under the Beilinson–Bernstein equivalence, for dominant $\lambda$:
\begin{itemize}
\item Verma modules $M(w \cdot \lambda)$ correspond to standard$\mathcal{D}$-modules$j_{w,!}(\mathcal{O}_{C_w}[{\ell(w)}])$.
\item Simple modules $L(w \cdot \lambda)$ correspond to intersection cohomology$\mathcal{D}$-modules$\mathrm{IC}_w = j_{w,!*}(\mathcal{O}_{C_w}[\ell(w)])$.
\item Projective covers $P(w \cdot \lambda)$ in category$\mathcal{O}$ correspond to certain *tilting*$\mathcal{D}$-modules.
\item The composition multiplicity $[M(w \cdot \lambda) : L(y \cdot \lambda)]$ equals the multiplicity of$\mathrm{IC}_y$ in the standard$\mathcal{D}$-module attached to$X_w$, which by the decomposition theorem equals$P_{y,w}(1)$.
\end{itemize}
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}
For non-dominant $\lambda$, the localization theorem continues to hold at the derived category level. If$\lambda$ is integral but not dominant, one can still define derived localization$R\Gamma: D^b(\mathcal{D}_\lambda\text{-mod}) \to D^b(M(\mathfrak{g},\lambda))$ and$L\Delta_\lambda: D^b(M(\mathfrak{g},\lambda)) \to D^b(\mathcal{D}_\lambda\text{-mod})$, and these are equivalences of triangulated categories. The non-exactness of$\Gamma$ in the non-dominant case corresponds to the non-vanishing of derived pushforwards, which compute the higher cohomology of$\mathcal{D}$-modules and provide additional representation-theoretic information.
\end{remark}

The Beilinson–Bernstein theorem opened a new era in representation theory: it made the purely algebraic problems of Verma module theory accessible to the full toolkit of algebraic geometry, including sheaf theory, perverse sheaves, the decomposition theorem, and eventually $\ell$-adic methods and the Weil conjectures. Its impact on the proof of the Kazhdan–Lusztig conjecture (the same year, 1981) was immediate and decisive.
