# 52.2.4 Standard and Costandard $\mathcal{D}$-Modules$\leftrightarrow$ Verma Modules

The Beilinson–Bernstein equivalence is not just an abstract isomorphism of categories — it identifies specific and important classes of modules on both sides. Understanding which algebraic objects correspond to which geometric ones is what gives the theorem its computational teeth. The dictionary between $\mathfrak{g}$-modules and$\mathcal{D}$-modules on$G/B$ is organized by the Bruhat stratification of$G/B$ into Schubert cells$C_w$.

\begin{definition}
For each $w \in W$, let$j_w: C_w \hookrightarrow G/B$ denote the locally closed inclusion of the Schubert cell. The *standard$\mathcal{D}_\lambda$-module* (or *Verma sheaf*)$\Delta_w = j_{w,!}(\mathcal{O}_{C_w}[\ell(w)])$ is the$!$-extension of the structure sheaf (with shift), and the *costandard$\mathcal{D}_\lambda$-module* (or *dual Verma sheaf*)$\nabla_w = j_{w,*}(\mathcal{O}_{C_w}[\ell(w)])$ is the$*$-extension.
\end{definition}

These two constructions differ at the singular boundary of the Schubert variety: $\Delta_w$ extends by zero outside$C_w$ (using$j_!$, "extension with compact support"), while$\nabla_w$ extends by the constant sheaf (using$j_*$, "direct image"). When$X_w$ is smooth (e.g., when$w = e$ or$w = w_0$), these two extensions agree, but in general they differ precisely at the singular locus of$\overline{C_w}$.

\begin{theorem}[BB Dictionary]
Under the Beilinson–Bernstein equivalence $M(\mathfrak{g}, \lambda) \simeq \mathcal{D}_\lambda\text{-mod}$, for dominant integral$\lambda$:
\begin{itemize}
\item The Verma module $M(w \cdot \lambda)$ corresponds to the standard$\mathcal{D}$-module$\Delta_w$.
\item The dual Verma module $M(w \cdot \lambda)^\vee$ corresponds to the costandard$\mathcal{D}$-module$\nabla_w$.
\item The simple module $L(w \cdot \lambda)$ corresponds to the intersection cohomology$\mathcal{D}$-module$\mathrm{IC}_w = j_{w,!*}(\mathcal{O}_{C_w}[\ell(w)])$ (the intermediate extension).
\end{itemize}
\end{theorem}

**Example 1.** For $G = SL_2$,$G/B = \mathbb{P}^1$,$\lambda = n\omega$ dominant ($n \geq 1$), the Weyl group$W = \{e, s\}$:
- $w = e$:$C_e = \{pt\}$, the fixed point.$\Delta_e = \delta_{pt}$, the delta-function$\mathcal{D}$-module at the point. This corresponds to the Verma module$M(e \cdot n\omega) = M(n\omega)$ of highest weight$n\omega$, which is the "largest" Verma module in the block.
- $w = s$:$C_s = \mathbb{A}^1 \subset \mathbb{P}^1$, the complement of the fixed point.$\Delta_s = j_{!}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{A}^1}[1])$, the$!$-extension of the structure sheaf of$\mathbb{A}^1$ to$\mathbb{P}^1$. This is not a line bundle (it has a "hole" at infinity). It corresponds to$M(s \cdot n\omega) = M(-n\omega - 2)$.

**Example 2.** The intermediate extension $\mathrm{IC}_w = j_{w,!*}$, also called the intersection cohomology$\mathcal{D}$-module, is characterized uniquely as the simple$\mathcal{D}$-module whose restriction to$C_w$ is$\mathcal{O}_{C_w}[\ell(w)]$ and which has no submodule or quotient supported on the boundary$\overline{C_w} \setminus C_w$. For smooth Schubert varieties$X_w$, the intermediate extension is the same as$\Delta_w = \nabla_w$ (all three coincide). For singular$X_w$, the IC sheaf has non-trivial "correction terms" at the singular locus.

For $G = SL_3$, consider$w = w_0 = (13) \in S_3$ (the longest element,$\ell(w_0) = 3$). The Schubert variety$X_{w_0} = G/B$ is smooth (it is the whole flag variety), so$\mathrm{IC}_{w_0}$ is just the constant$\mathcal{D}$-module$\mathcal{O}_{G/B}[3]$. This corresponds, under the BB dictionary, to the simple finite-dimensional representation$L(w_0 \cdot \lambda)$.

The key insight is that the multiplicity $[M(w \cdot \lambda) : L(y \cdot \lambda)]$ — the number of times the simple module$L(y \cdot \lambda)$ appears as a composition factor of the Verma module$M(w \cdot \lambda)$ — is encoded geometrically.

\begin{theorem}[Kazhdan–Lusztig, BB dictionary]
$$[M(w \cdot \lambda) : L(y \cdot \lambda)] = \text{multiplicity of } \mathrm{IC}_y \text{ in a composition series of } \Delta_w = P_{y,w}(1),$$
where $P_{y,w}(q)$ is the Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomial.
\end{theorem}

**Example 3.** For $G = SL_3$, consider the Verma module$M(w_0 \cdot \lambda)$ (for appropriate$\lambda$). Its composition factors include$L(w \cdot \lambda)$ for all$w \leq w_0 = w_0$ in the Bruhat order — all of them. The multiplicities are$[M(w_0 \cdot \lambda) : L(y \cdot \lambda)] = P_{y,w_0}(1)$ for each$y \in W$. For$y = e$ (identity),$P_{e,w_0}(q) = 1$ (since$X_{w_0} = G/B$ is smooth); for other$y$, one needs to compute the KL polynomials.

\begin{remark}
The passage from algebra to geometry in this dictionary is not merely a translation — it changes what is obvious and what requires proof. On the algebraic side, the existence of a non-split extension $0 \to L(y \cdot \lambda) \to M \to M(w \cdot \lambda) \to 0$ (a composition factor) is a theorem about infinite-dimensional modules that is very hard to establish directly. On the geometric side, the same fact becomes the assertion that the IC sheaf$\mathrm{IC}_y$ occurs in the stalks of$\Delta_w$ near the stratum$C_y$ — which follows from the geometry of the Schubert variety via the decomposition theorem, a result proved using the Weil conjectures. The geometry thus provides access to algebraic facts that had seemed hopelessly hard to prove directly.
\end{remark}

**Example 4.** For $G = SL_4$, consider the permutation$w = 4321$ (the longest element, reversing all indices) and$y = 3412$ (a singular permutation). The Schubert variety$X_{3412}$ is singular, with the singularity first appearing at the boundary stratum$C_{1324}$. The KL polynomial$P_{1324,3412}(q) = 1 + q$ (non-trivial, degree 1). This means the stalk of$\mathrm{IC}_{3412}$ at a point of$C_{1324}$ is 2-dimensional:$H^{-1}(\mathrm{IC}_{3412}|_{pt \in C_{1324}}) \cong \mathbb{C}$ and$H^0 = \mathbb{C}$. On the algebraic side, this encodes the Verma module filtration multiplicities:$[M(w_{3412} \cdot \lambda) : L(w_{1324} \cdot \lambda)] = P_{1324,3412}(1) = 2$.
