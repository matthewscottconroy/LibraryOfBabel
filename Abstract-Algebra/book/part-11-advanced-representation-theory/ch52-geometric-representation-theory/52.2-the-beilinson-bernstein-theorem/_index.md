# 52.2 The Beilinson–Bernstein Theorem

The Beilinson–Bernstein theorem, published in 1981, is one of the most influential results in twentieth-century representation theory. In a single stroke, it transformed the study of representations of semisimple Lie algebras from a purely algebraic enterprise into a geometric one, by establishing an equivalence of categories between $U(\mathfrak{g})$-modules with a fixed central character and $\mathcal{D}$-modules on the flag variety $G/B$. The theorem's statement is concise: for a dominant weight $\lambda$, the global sections functor $\Gamma: \mathcal{D}_\lambda\text{-mod}(G/B) \to U(\mathfrak{g})_\chi\text{-mod}$ is an equivalence of categories, with quasi-inverse given by localization $\Delta$. Here $\mathcal{D}_\lambda$ is the sheaf of $\lambda$-twisted differential operators on $G/B$ — a sheaf of algebras that specializes to the usual sheaf of differential operators when $\lambda = 0$ — and $U(\mathfrak{g})_\chi$ denotes $U(\mathfrak{g})$ modulo the ideal generated by the central character $\chi = \chi_\lambda$.

To understand the power of this equivalence, one must understand what $\mathcal{D}$-modules are and why they appear naturally in representation theory. A $\mathcal{D}$-module on a smooth variety $X$ is a sheaf $\mathcal{M}$ of modules over the sheaf $\mathcal{D}_X$ of algebraic differential operators. The algebra $\mathcal{D}_X$ is generated by $\mathcal{O}_X$ and the tangent sheaf $\mathcal{T}_X$, subject to the relations $[f, g] = 0$ for functions and $[\xi, f] = \xi(f)$ for vector fields acting as derivations. When $X = G/B$ and $G$ acts on $X$, the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ acts on $X$ by vector fields, giving a Lie algebra map $\mathfrak{g} \to \mathcal{T}(G/B) \to \mathcal{D}(G/B)$. This map lifts to a ring homomorphism $U(\mathfrak{g}) \to \mathcal{D}(G/B)$, and the Harish-Chandra isomorphism identifies the kernel with the ideal generated by a central character. The global sections of a $\mathcal{D}_{G/B}$-module are therefore automatically a $U(\mathfrak{g})$-module with a prescribed central character.

The key geometric fact underlying the Beilinson–Bernstein theorem is the vanishing of higher cohomology: for a dominant $\lambda$, the higher sheaf cohomology $H^k(G/B, \mathcal{M})$ vanishes for all $k > 0$ and all $\mathcal{D}_\lambda$-modules $\mathcal{M}$. This vanishing theorem — proved by Beilinson and Bernstein using the geometry of the flag variety — is what makes the global sections functor $\Gamma$ exact and hence capable of being an equivalence. Under this equivalence, the geometric dictionary is explicit: the Verma module $M(\lambda)$ corresponds to the $\mathcal{D}$-module $j_! \mathcal{O}_{C_e}$ associated with the open Schubert cell $C_e \cong \mathbb{A}^N$ (the "big cell"), and the simple module $L(\lambda)$ corresponds to the intermediate extension $j_{!*} \mathcal{O}_{C_e}$ — the intersection cohomology sheaf on the closure, which is the unique perverse subsheaf of $Rj_* \mathcal{O}_{C_e}$ with no subobject or quotient supported on the boundary.

The section develops the theory of $\mathcal{D}$-modules on algebraic varieties in the generality needed for the theorem, proves the Beilinson–Bernstein equivalence, and works out the geometric dictionary for the standard modules (Verma modules, their duals, and simple quotients). This dictionary is not just aesthetically satisfying — it is computationally powerful. Questions about extensions between modules, about composition multiplicities in Verma modules, and about projective objects in Category $\mathcal{O}$ all become questions about the geometry of Schubert varieties and the topology of their intersection cohomology sheaves. The theorem thereby reduces the algebraic complexity of representation theory to geometric complexity, which can be attacked by the full force of algebraic geometry and topology.
