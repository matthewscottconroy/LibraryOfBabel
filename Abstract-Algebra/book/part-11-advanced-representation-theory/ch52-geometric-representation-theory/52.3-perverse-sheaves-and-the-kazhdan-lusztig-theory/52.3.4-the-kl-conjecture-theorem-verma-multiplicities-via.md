# 52.3.4 The KL Conjecture (Theorem): Verma Multiplicities via $P_{x,w}$

In the same 1979 paper where they introduced the polynomials bearing their names, Kazhdan and Lusztig made a startling conjecture connecting these combinatorial objects to the composition series of Verma modules. The problem of computing composition multiplicities $[M(w \cdot \lambda) : L(y \cdot \lambda)]$ — the number of times the simple module$L(y \cdot \lambda)$ appears as a composition factor of the Verma module$M(w \cdot \lambda)$ — had been open since the BGG theory was developed in the 1970s. What Kazhdan and Lusztig proposed was that the answer is simply the KL polynomial evaluated at 1.

\begin{theorem}[Kazhdan–Lusztig Conjecture = Beilinson–Bernstein + Brylinski–Kashiwara, 1981]
For $y \leq w$ in the Bruhat order and$\lambda$ dominant integral,
$$[M(w \cdot \lambda) : L(y \cdot \lambda)] = P_{y,w}(1).$$
Here $w \cdot \lambda = w(\lambda + \rho) - \rho$ denotes the dot action.
\end{theorem}

**Example 1.** For $G = SL_2$,$W = \{e, s\}$, and dominant integral$\lambda = n\omega$ ($n \geq 0$):
- $[M(n\omega) : L(n\omega)] = P_{e,e}(1) = 1$. (Every Verma contains its highest weight simple with multiplicity 1.)
- $[M(n\omega) : L(s \cdot n\omega)] = [M(n\omega) : L((-n-2)\omega)]$: but$s \cdot n\omega = s(n\omega + \omega) - \omega = (-n-2)\omega$, which is not dominant for$n \geq 0$. In the block for$\chi_\lambda$, the Verma modules are$M(n\omega)$ and$M(s \cdot n\omega) = M((-n-2)\omega)$. The composition multiplicities:$[M(n\omega) : L(n\omega)] = P_{e,e}(1) = 1$ and$[M(n\omega) : L(s \cdot n\omega)] = P_{e,s}(1) = 1$ (for$n \geq 0$, the Verma$M(n\omega)$ has composition series$L(n\omega)$ over$L((-n-2)\omega)$).

This matches the classical calculation: $M(n\omega)$ is the Verma module of highest weight$n$, and for$n \geq 0$ it has two composition factors: the finite-dimensional$L(n\omega)$ at the top and the infinite-dimensional$L((-n-2)\omega) = L(-(n+2)\omega)$ at the bottom.

**Example 2.** For $G = SL_4$, the first non-trivial KL polynomial is$P_{e,(3412)}(q) = 1 + q$. The KL theorem says$[M((3412) \cdot \lambda) : L(e \cdot \lambda)] = P_{e,(3412)}(1) = 1 + 1 = 2$. This means the simple module$L(\lambda)$ (the one with the highest dominant weight in the block) occurs with multiplicity 2 in the Verma module$M((3412) \cdot \lambda)$. This was genuinely surprising before the KL theorem: the naive expectation from the BGG theory was that multiplicities would typically be 0 or 1, but the geometry of Schubert singularities forces multiplicities$> 1$.

**Example 3.** More systematically, for $G = SL_n$ and dominant$\lambda$, all KL polynomials in the principal block can be computed from the geometry of$\mathrm{Gr}(k,n)$ (partial flag varieties). For$G = SL_3$, all KL polynomials are 1 (since all Schubert varieties are smooth), so all composition multiplicities are 0 or 1. The first group where non-trivial multiplicities appear is$G = SL_4$.

The proof of the KL theorem proceeds via the Beilinson–Bernstein equivalence and the decomposition theorem.

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
\textbf{Step 1.} By the Beilinson–Bernstein equivalence (for dominant $\lambda$), the Verma module$M(w \cdot \lambda)$ corresponds to the standard$\mathcal{D}$-module$\Delta_w = j_{w,!}(\mathcal{O}_{C_w}[\ell(w)])$, and the simple module$L(y \cdot \lambda)$ corresponds to the IC sheaf$\mathrm{IC}_y = j_{y,!*}(\mathcal{O}_{C_y}[\ell(y)])$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Let $\pi: \tilde{X}_w \to X_w$ be the Bott–Samelson resolution, a smooth variety with a proper birational map to the Schubert variety$X_w$. By the decomposition theorem of BBD,$R\pi_*(\mathbb{Q}_{\tilde{X}_w}[\ell(w)])$ decomposes as a direct sum of shifted IC sheaves:
$$R\pi_*(\mathbb{Q}_{\tilde{X}_w}[\ell(w)]) \cong \bigoplus_{y \leq w} \mathrm{IC}_y^{\oplus m_{y,w}}$$
for some non-negative integers $m_{y,w}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} A careful analysis shows $m_{y,w} = P_{y,w}(1)$: the decomposition multiplicities are the KL polynomials evaluated at 1.

\textbf{Step 4.} Taking global sections and using $\Gamma(\mathrm{IC}_y) = L(y \cdot \lambda)$ (the BB dictionary), one reads off$[M(w \cdot \lambda) : L(y \cdot \lambda)] = m_{y,w} = P_{y,w}(1)$. \qed
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The decomposition theorem used in Step 2 is itself a deep theorem — it was proved using the Weil conjectures (in particular, Deligne's proof of the Riemann hypothesis for varieties over finite fields) and the theory of mixed Hodge modules. The strategy is to work over a finite field (replacing $\mathbb{C}$ by$\overline{\mathbb{F}}_p$ and singular cohomology by$\ell$-adic cohomology), where the Frobenius action provides the "weight" filtration needed to split the direct image. The positivity of KL polynomial coefficients then follows from the fact that Frobenius eigenvalues on$\ell$-adic cohomology are Weil numbers (algebraic integers whose absolute values are controlled by their "weight"). This is a striking example of how arithmetic geometry (the Weil conjectures) resolves questions in complex representation theory.
\end{remark}

The KL theorem raised, rather than closed, a host of further questions. The analogous statement for representations in characteristic $p$ — the Lusztig conjecture — predicts that the analogous multiplicities are controlled by the KL polynomials for the affine Weyl group (computed at a$p$-th root of unity). This was proved for large$p$ by Kazhdan–Lusztig and Kashiwara–Tanisaki, but is still not fully established for small$p$. The KL theorem also has a categorical refinement: the numbers$P_{y,w}(1)$ are not just multiplicities, but dimensions of graded Ext groups in a Koszul dual category — the starting point of Soergel's approach to the KL conjecture via bimodules.
