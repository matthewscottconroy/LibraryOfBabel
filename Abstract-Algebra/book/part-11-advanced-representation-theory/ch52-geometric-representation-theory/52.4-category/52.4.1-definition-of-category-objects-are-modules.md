# 52.4.1 Definition of Category $\mathcal{O}$; Objects Are$\mathfrak{g}$-Modules

Representation theory of a semisimple Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ over$\mathbb{C}$ involves many different classes of modules, ranging from the finite-dimensional irreducibles to wild infinite-dimensional beasts. To do homological algebra — to talk about projective resolutions, Ext groups, and derived functors — one needs to work inside a category with good finiteness properties. *Category$\mathcal{O}$*, introduced by Bernstein, Gelfand, and Gelfand in 1976, is the right framework: it is large enough to contain all the interesting infinite-dimensional modules arising from Borel subalgebras (like Verma modules), but small enough to be tractable via homological algebra.

\begin{definition}
Let $\mathfrak{g}$ be a semisimple Lie algebra over$\mathbb{C}$ with triangular decomposition$\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{n}^- \oplus \mathfrak{h} \oplus \mathfrak{n}^+$. *Category$\mathcal{O}$* consists of all$\mathfrak{g}$-modules$M$ satisfying:
\begin{itemize}
\item[(a)] $M$ is finitely generated as a$U(\mathfrak{g})$-module.
\item[(b)] $M$ is a weight module:$M = \bigoplus_{\lambda \in \mathfrak{h}^*} M_\lambda$ with each weight space$M_\lambda = \{v \in M : h \cdot v = \lambda(h)v \text{ for all } h \in \mathfrak{h}\}$ finite-dimensional.
\item[(c)] $M$ is locally$\mathfrak{n}^+$-finite: for every$v \in M$, the subspace$U(\mathfrak{n}^+) \cdot v$ is finite-dimensional.
\end{itemize}
Morphisms in $\mathcal{O}$ are all$\mathfrak{g}$-module homomorphisms.
\end{definition}

Condition (c) is the crucial constraint distinguishing $\mathcal{O}$ from all weight modules. Without it, one could take infinite direct sums$\bigoplus_n L(n\omega)$ and lose all control over homological structure.

**Example 1.** Every finite-dimensional $\mathfrak{g}$-module is in$\mathcal{O}$: it is finitely generated (by a highest weight vector), has finite-dimensional weight spaces (trivially), and is locally$\mathfrak{n}^+$-finite (since$\mathfrak{n}^+$ acts nilpotently on finite-dimensional modules). Thus$L(\lambda)$ for any dominant integral$\lambda$ is in$\mathcal{O}$.

**Example 2.** Every Verma module $M(\lambda) = U(\mathfrak{g}) \otimes_{U(\mathfrak{b})} k_\lambda$ is in$\mathcal{O}$. Condition (a):$M(\lambda)$ is generated by the highest weight vector$1 \otimes 1$. Condition (b): by PBW,$M(\lambda)$ has weight spaces$M(\lambda)_\mu$ for$\mu \leq \lambda$ (in the dominance order), each finite-dimensional. Condition (c):$(U(\mathfrak{n}^+)) \cdot v$ is finite-dimensional for every$v \in M(\lambda)$ because$\mathfrak{n}^+$ acts by raising weights, and$M(\lambda)$ has no weights above$\lambda$, so$\mathfrak{n}^+$ acts nilpotently on the finite-dimensional space$\bigoplus_{\mu \geq \nu} M(\lambda)_\mu$ for each$\nu$.

**Example 3.** The module $M = U(\mathfrak{sl}_2) / U(\mathfrak{sl}_2) \cdot e$ (where$e \in \mathfrak{n}^+$ is the raising operator) is the Verma module$M(0)$ of weight 0 for$\mathfrak{sl}_2$. It has a basis$\{f^n \otimes 1 : n \geq 0\}$ with$f$ acting as a shift,$h$ acting by$h \cdot f^n = -2n \cdot f^n$, and$e \cdot f^n = n(1-n) f^{n-1}$. The weight spaces are$M(0)_{-2n} = \mathbb{C} f^n$, each one-dimensional. The condition (c) is satisfied:$e \cdot f^n = n(1-n)f^{n-1}$, so$U(\mathfrak{n}^+) \cdot f^n = \mathrm{span}\{1, e \cdot f^n, e^2 \cdot f^n, \ldots\} = \mathrm{span}\{1, \ldots, f^{n-1}\}$, finite-dimensional.

\begin{theorem}[Bernstein–Gelfand–Gelfand, 1976]
Category $\mathcal{O}$ is an abelian Noetherian category. It has enough projectives, and every module in$\mathcal{O}$ has finite projective dimension. The category decomposes as a direct sum of *blocks*:
$$\mathcal{O} = \bigoplus_{\chi} \mathcal{O}_\chi$$
indexed by central characters $\chi: Z(\mathfrak{g}) \to \mathbb{C}$.
\end{theorem}

**Example 4.** For $\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{sl}_2$, the blocks of$\mathcal{O}$ are indexed by values of the Casimir$C = H^2/4 + EF + FE$ (the central element generating$Z(\mathfrak{sl}_2)$). The Casimir acts on$M(\lambda)$ by$\lambda(\lambda + 2)/4 \cdot \mathrm{id}$. Two Verma modules$M(\lambda)$ and$M(\mu)$ lie in the same block iff$\lambda(\lambda+2) = \mu(\mu+2)$, i.e.,$\mu = \lambda$ or$\mu = -\lambda - 2$ (the two solutions of$x(x+2) = c$). So each block contains exactly two Verma modules$M(\lambda)$ and$M(-\lambda-2)$, with two simple modules$L(\lambda)$ and$L(-\lambda-2)$ (for non-integral or negative integral$\lambda$) or one finite-dimensional simple$L(\lambda)$ and one infinite-dimensional simple$L(-\lambda-2)$ (for$\lambda \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$).

\begin{remark}
The Noetherian property of $\mathcal{O}$ — the fact that every ascending chain of submodules stabilizes — is equivalent to the finite-generation assumption (a). Without this, even$\mathcal{O}$-type modules can be pathological. The Noetherian property is also what guarantees the existence of projective covers and makes homological algebra work smoothly. Category$\mathcal{O}$ was specifically designed to have this property while remaining large enough to contain the interesting modules of Lie theory.
\par
The structure of each block of $\mathcal{O}$ is that of a *highest-weight category* in the sense of Cline–Parshall–Scott: it has a finite set of simple objects$\{L(w \cdot \lambda)\}_{w \in W}$ (for any fixed$\lambda$ in the block), standard objects (Verma modules), and projective covers, satisfying axioms that make the structural theory uniform across all such categories. Highest-weight categories are the natural categorical framework in which the Kazhdan–Lusztig theory lives.
\end{remark}
