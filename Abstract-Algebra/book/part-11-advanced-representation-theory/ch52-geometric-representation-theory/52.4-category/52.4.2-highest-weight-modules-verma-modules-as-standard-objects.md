# 52.4.2 Highest Weight Modules; Verma Modules as Standard Objects

The organizing principle of Category $\mathcal{O}$ is the notion of a *highest weight module*. A nonzero vector$v \in M$ is a highest weight vector of weight$\lambda$ if$\mathfrak{h} \cdot v = \lambda(h) v$ for all$h \in \mathfrak{h}$ (it lies in the$\lambda$ weight space) and$\mathfrak{n}^+ \cdot v = 0$ (it is annihilated by all positive root vectors). A module is a highest weight module if it is generated by a single highest weight vector. The key insight is that every module in Category$\mathcal{O}$ is a quotient of a direct sum of highest weight modules, so the highest weight modules are the "generators" of the category.

\begin{definition}
The *Verma module* of weight $\lambda \in \mathfrak{h}^*$ is
$$M(\lambda) = U(\mathfrak{g}) \otimes_{U(\mathfrak{b})} k_\lambda,$$
where $k_\lambda$ is the one-dimensional$\mathfrak{b} = \mathfrak{h} \oplus \mathfrak{n}^+$-module with$\mathfrak{h}$ acting by$\lambda$ and$\mathfrak{n}^+$ acting by 0.
\end{definition}

The universal property of $M(\lambda)$ is: for any$\mathfrak{g}$-module$N$ and any highest weight vector$v \in N$ of weight$\lambda$, there is a unique$\mathfrak{g}$-module map$M(\lambda) \to N$ sending$1 \otimes 1 \mapsto v$. In particular, every highest weight module of weight$\lambda$ is a quotient of$M(\lambda)$.

**Example 1.** For $\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{sl}_2$ with basis$\{e, h, f\}$, the Verma module$M(\lambda)$ for$\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$ has$\mathbb{C}$-basis$\{f^n \otimes 1 : n \geq 0\}$ (by PBW, since$M(\lambda) \cong U(\mathfrak{n}^-) \otimes_{\mathbb{C}} k_\lambda$ as a vector space). The action is:$h \cdot f^n \otimes 1 = (\lambda - 2n) f^n \otimes 1$,$f \cdot f^n \otimes 1 = f^{n+1} \otimes 1$,$e \cdot f^n \otimes 1 = n(\lambda - n + 1) f^{n-1} \otimes 1$. The weights of$M(\lambda)$ are$\{\lambda - 2n : n \geq 0\}$, an infinite set of weights descending from$\lambda$.

**Example 2.** The Verma module $M(\lambda)$ is simple (i.e.,$M(\lambda) = L(\lambda)$) if and only if$\lambda + \rho$ is not a non-negative integer times a simple root... more precisely, for$\mathfrak{sl}_2$:$M(\lambda)$ is simple iff$\lambda \notin \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$. For$\lambda = n \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$, the Verma module$M(n)$ has a unique maximal submodule (generated by$f^{n+1} \otimes 1$), and the quotient$M(n)/\langle f^{n+1} \rangle = L(n)$ is the$(n+1)$-dimensional simple module.

Category $\mathcal{O}$ decomposes as a direct sum of blocks$\mathcal{O} = \bigoplus_\chi \mathcal{O}_\chi$, indexed by central characters$\chi: Z(\mathfrak{g}) \to \mathbb{C}$.

\begin{theorem}
Two Verma modules $M(\lambda)$ and$M(\mu)$ lie in the same block if and only if$\mu \in W \cdot \lambda$ (in the dot-action$w \cdot \lambda = w(\lambda+\rho)-\rho$). Each block$\mathcal{O}_\chi$ is a *highest-weight category*: it has finitely many simples$\{L(w \cdot \lambda)\}_{w \in W}$, standard objects$\{M(w \cdot \lambda)\}$, costandard objects$\{M(w \cdot \lambda)^\vee\}$, and projective covers$\{P(w \cdot \lambda)\}$.
\end{theorem}

**Example 3.** For $\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{sl}_3$ and$\lambda = 0$ (the trivial weight), the dot-action orbit is$W \cdot 0 = \{w \cdot 0 : w \in S_3\} = \{-\rho, -s_1 \cdot \rho, -s_2 \cdot \rho, \ldots\} = \{0, -\alpha_1, -\alpha_2, -\alpha_1 - \alpha_2, -2\alpha_1 - \alpha_2, -\alpha_1 - 2\alpha_2\}$ (six elements, one for each$w \in S_3$). The corresponding block has six Verma modules, six simple modules, and the composition multiplicities are governed by the KL polynomials for$S_3$ (all equal to 1 in this case).

The projective covers $P(\lambda)$ in Category$\mathcal{O}$ are more elaborate objects.

\begin{proposition}
For each $\lambda \in W \cdot \mu$ (a dominant weight), the projective cover$P(w \cdot \mu)$ of$L(w \cdot \mu)$ in$\mathcal{O}_\mu$ has a *Verma filtration*: a filtration$0 = P_0 \subset P_1 \subset \cdots \subset P_k = P(w \cdot \mu)$ with each successive quotient$P_i/P_{i-1} \cong M(v \cdot \mu)$ for some$v \in W$. The multiplicity$(P(w \cdot \mu) : M(v \cdot \mu))$ equals$[M(v \cdot \mu) : L(w \cdot \mu)] = P_{w,v}(1)$ by BGG reciprocity.
\end{proposition}

**Example 4.** For $\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{sl}_2$ and dominant integral$\lambda = n$ ($n \geq 0$), the projective cover$P(n)$ of$L(n)$ in$\mathcal{O}_n$ is:
- If $n \geq 0$:$P(n) = M(n)$ (the Verma module itself is projective for the highest weight in the block — this is a special feature of the top element of the block).
- $P(-n-2)$ (the projective cover of the infinite-dimensional simple$L(-n-2)$) has a Verma filtration$0 \subset M(-n-2) \subset P(-n-2)$ with$P(-n-2)/M(-n-2) \cong M(n)$. So$P(-n-2)$ has two layers:$M(-n-2)$ at the bottom and$M(n)$ at the top.

This "BGG reciprocity" formula $(P(v \cdot \mu) : M(w \cdot \mu)) = [M(w \cdot \mu) : L(v \cdot \mu)]$ is one of the fundamental structural results of Category$\mathcal{O}$: it says that the Verma filtration multiplicities of projectives equal the composition multiplicities of Vermas — a remarkable symmetry that is far from obvious and that, together with the KL theorem, determines the entire structure of each block.

\begin{remark}
Category $\mathcal{O}$ has a Koszul grading: each block is equivalent (as an abelian category) to the category of graded modules over a Koszul algebra$A$. The graded version of Category$\mathcal{O}$ — denoted$\mathcal{O}^{\text{gr}}$ — has the additional structure that the Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomials$P_{y,w}(q)$ record the graded composition multiplicities:$[M(w \cdot \lambda) : L(y \cdot \lambda)]_q = P_{y,w}(q)$ in the Grothendieck group of$\mathcal{O}^{\text{gr}}$. This Koszul structure was established by Beilinson–Ginzburg–Soergel and is the deepest structural result about Category$\mathcal{O}$.
\end{remark}
