# 52.4.3 The BGG Resolution of $L(\lambda)$

A fundamental question in the homological algebra of Category $\mathcal{O}$ is: what is the projective resolution of a simple module$L(\lambda)$? For dominant integral$\lambda$, the simple module$L(\lambda)$ is also finite-dimensional (by the theorem of the highest weight), and Bernstein, Gelfand, and Gelfand constructed a beautiful and explicit resolution of$L(\lambda)$ by Verma modules. This *BGG resolution* is one of the central tools of the subject, giving explicit access to Ext groups and providing a bridge between the Weyl character formula and homological algebra.

\begin{theorem}[Bernstein–Gelfand–Gelfand, 1975]
For $\lambda$ dominant integral, there is an exact sequence
$$0 \to M(w_0 \cdot \lambda) \to \cdots \to \bigoplus_{\ell(w)=k} M(w \cdot \lambda) \to \cdots \to M(\lambda) \to L(\lambda) \to 0,$$
where the sum at position $k$ runs over all$w \in W$ with$\ell(w) = k$, the differentials are$\mathfrak{g}$-module maps between Verma modules, and the sequence has length$|\Phi^+| = \ell(w_0)$.
\end{theorem}

The maps in the resolution are the canonical Verma module homomorphisms: for $y \leq w$ in the Bruhat order with$\ell(w) = \ell(y) + 1$ (so$w$ covers$y$), there is a unique (up to scalar) nonzero map$M(w \cdot \lambda) \to M(y \cdot \lambda)$. The BGG resolution uses a specific choice of signs for these maps to make the composition zero — the differentials are alternating sums of these covering maps.

**Example 1.** For $\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{sl}_2$ and$\lambda = n\omega$ ($n \geq 0$),$W = \{e, s\}$,$w_0 = s$. The BGG resolution is:
$$0 \to M(s \cdot n\omega) \to M(n\omega) \to L(n\omega) \to 0.$$
Here $s \cdot n\omega = s(n\omega + \omega) - \omega = (-n-2)\omega$. The map$M(-n-2) \to M(n)$ sends the highest weight vector of$M(-n-2)$ to$f^{n+1} \otimes 1 \in M(n)$ (the unique, up to scalar, nonzero map). The cokernel is$M(n)/\mathrm{Im}(M(-n-2)) = M(n)/\langle f^{n+1} \rangle = L(n)$, the$(n+1)$-dimensional simple. Length of resolution =$|\Phi^+| = 1$ for$\mathfrak{sl}_2$.

**Example 2.** For $\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{sl}_3$ and$\lambda = \omega_1 + \omega_2$ (the sum of fundamental weights),$W = S_3$ with$|W| = 6$ elements. The BGG resolution has length$|\Phi^+| = 3$:
$$0 \to M(w_0 \cdot \lambda) \to M(s_1 w_0 \cdot \lambda) \oplus M(s_2 w_0 \cdot \lambda) \to M(s_1 \cdot \lambda) \oplus M(s_2 \cdot \lambda) \oplus M(s_1 s_2 s_1 \cdot \lambda?) \to \ldots$$
More explicitly, the six Verma modules are arranged at positions $k = 0, 1, 2, 3$ with$1, 2, 2, 1$ Vermas (since$|S_3| = 6 = 1+2+2+1$). The resolution has the shape of the Weyl group poset under the Bruhat order.

The BGG resolution provides an algebraic proof of the Weyl character formula.

\begin{theorem}[Weyl character formula from BGG]
In the Grothendieck group $K(\mathcal{O})$, the BGG resolution gives
$$[L(\lambda)] = \sum_{w \in W} (-1)^{\ell(w)} [M(w \cdot \lambda)].$$
Since $\mathrm{ch}(M(\mu)) = e^\mu \prod_{\alpha > 0}(1 - e^{-\alpha})^{-1}$, this yields
$$\mathrm{ch}(L(\lambda)) = \frac{\sum_{w \in W} (-1)^{\ell(w)} e^{w(\lambda+\rho)}}{\sum_{w \in W} (-1)^{\ell(w)} e^{w\rho}} = \frac{\sum_{w \in W} (-1)^{\ell(w)} e^{w(\lambda+\rho)}}{\prod_{\alpha > 0}(e^{\alpha/2} - e^{-\alpha/2})},$$
the Weyl character formula.
\end{theorem}

**Example 3.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ and$\lambda = n\omega$: the BGG gives$[L(n)] = [M(n)] - [M(-n-2)]$, so$\mathrm{ch}(L(n)) = e^n/(1-e^{-2}) - e^{-n-2}/(1-e^{-2}) = (e^n - e^{-n-2})/(1-e^{-2}) = e^{n/2}(e^{n/2} - e^{-n/2-2}) \cdot \frac{1}{e^{1/2} \cdot (e^{1/2} - e^{-1/2})}$. After simplification:$\mathrm{ch}(L(n)) = (e^{(n+1)/2} - e^{-(n+1)/2})/(e^{1/2} - e^{-1/2}) = [n+1]_{e^{1/2}}$, the$q$-integer analogue, which for$q = e^{1/2}$ gives$e^{n/2} + e^{n/2 - 1} + \cdots + e^{-n/2}$ — the correct character of the$(n+1)$-dimensional representation.

The BGG resolution also computes Ext groups in Category $\mathcal{O}$.

\begin{corollary}
For any $N \in \mathcal{O}_\lambda$ (in the same block),
$$\mathrm{Ext}^k_{\mathcal{O}}(L(\lambda), N) \cong H^k(\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{O}}(\text{BGG complex}, N)).$$
In particular, $\mathrm{Ext}^k_{\mathcal{O}}(L(\lambda), M(w \cdot \lambda)) = \begin{cases} \mathbb{C} & \text{if } k = \ell(w) \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$
\end{corollary}

**Example 4.** For $\mathfrak{sl}_2$,$\mathrm{Ext}^0_{\mathcal{O}}(L(n), M(n)) = \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{O}}(L(n), M(n))$: since$L(n)$ is the unique simple quotient of$M(n)$ and$M(n)$ has a quotient map$M(n) \to L(n)$, the only homomorphism$L(n) \to M(n)$ must land in the socle... actually$L(n)$ has a non-zero map to$M(n)$ only if$L(n)$ embeds in$M(n)$. For$n \geq 0$, the socle of$M(n)$ is$L(-n-2)$, so$\mathrm{Hom}(L(n), M(n)) = 0$. And$\mathrm{Ext}^1_{\mathcal{O}}(L(n), M(n)) \cong \mathbb{C}$ (the unique non-split extension$0 \to M(n) \to P(-n-2) \to L(n) \to 0$ corresponds to this Ext class). This confirms$k = \ell(s) = 1$ for the non-trivial element.

\begin{remark}
The BGG resolution has a geometric interpretation under the Beilinson–Bernstein equivalence: it corresponds to the Čech resolution of the IC sheaf $\mathrm{IC}_{w_0}$ (the constant sheaf on$G/B$) by standard$\mathcal{D}$-modules indexed by Schubert cells. This is the sheaf-theoretic incarnation of the resolution, and it connects the BGG complex directly to the combinatorics of the Bruhat decomposition. The differentials in the BGG resolution are the maps between standard$\mathcal{D}$-modules coming from the boundary inclusions of Schubert cells, and the exactness of the resolution is equivalent to the acyclicity of the Čech complex — a topological fact about the flag variety.
\end{remark}
