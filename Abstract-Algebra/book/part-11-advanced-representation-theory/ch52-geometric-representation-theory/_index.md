# Chapter 52 — Geometric Representation Theory

One of the most profound discoveries of twentieth-century mathematics is that representation theory — the study of how algebraic objects act on vector spaces — is secretly a branch of algebraic geometry. This is not merely a matter of using geometric language for algebraic objects; it is a precise equivalence of categories, where modules over an algebra are literally the same thing as sheaves on a geometric space. Geometric representation theory is the systematic development and exploitation of this equivalence, and its central achievement is the Beilinson–Bernstein localization theorem of 1981, which establishes an equivalence between the category of modules over the universal enveloping algebra $U(\mathfrak{g})$ (with a fixed central character) and the category of $\mathcal{D}$-modules on the flag variety $G/B$. Representation theory becomes geometry, and geometric intuition — intersections, cohomology, sheaves — becomes a tool for solving algebraic problems.

The flag variety $G/B$ is the natural home for the geometry of a reductive group $G$. It parametrizes the complete flags $0 \subset V_1 \subset V_2 \subset \cdots \subset V_n = V$ in the defining representation, and it is simultaneously a projective algebraic variety, a homogeneous $G$-space, and a union of affine cells — the Schubert cells — indexed by elements of the Weyl group $W$. The Bruhat decomposition $G = \bigsqcup_{w \in W} BwB$ descends to a cell decomposition $G/B = \bigsqcup_{w \in W} C_w$ where the Schubert cell $C_w$ has dimension $\ell(w)$ (the length of $w$). The closures $\overline{C_w}$ are the Schubert varieties, and their intersection theory — Schubert calculus — encodes the structure constants of the cohomology ring $H^*(G/B, \mathbb{Z})$ and, through it, the combinatorics of the Weyl group. The Chevalley formula and its generalizations express the cup product of Schubert classes in terms of the Bruhat order, making the cohomology ring of the flag variety a combinatorial object of remarkable precision.

The route from geometry to representation theory passes through $\mathcal{D}$-modules. A $\mathcal{D}$-module on $G/B$ is a sheaf of modules over the sheaf of differential operators on $G/B$; equivalently, it is a sheaf carrying flat connection. The space of global sections of a $\mathcal{D}$-module on $G/B$ is a module over the ring of global differential operators, which the Beilinson–Bernstein theorem identifies with a quotient of $U(\mathfrak{g})$ by a central character ideal. The theorem asserts that for dominant regular weights $\lambda$, this global sections functor is an equivalence of categories. The geometric side of this equivalence makes the Verma modules and their simple quotients transparent: the Verma module $M(\lambda)$ corresponds to the $\mathcal{D}$-module associated with the structure sheaf on a Schubert cell, and the simple module $L(\lambda)$ corresponds to the intermediate extension — the intersection cohomology sheaf — of that cell closure.

Perverse sheaves are the objects that make the Kazhdan–Lusztig theory work. The Kazhdan–Lusztig conjecture, formulated in 1979 and proved in 1981 by Brylinski–Kashiwara and independently by Beilinson–Bernstein, states that the multiplicity $[M(w \cdot \lambda) : L(y \cdot \lambda)]$ of a simple module in a Verma module equals the value $P_{y,w}(1)$ of the Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomial $P_{y,w}$ at $q = 1$. These polynomials, defined by a combinatorial formula in the Hecke algebra, turn out to count the dimensions of the stalks of intersection cohomology sheaves on Schubert varieties. The purely algebraic question — what is the multiplicity of $L$ in $M$? — is answered by a topological computation — what is the intersection cohomology of a singular variety? This is one of the most striking examples in mathematics of an algebraic question being solved by topology, and it initiated a revolution in representation theory that continues today through the theory of Category $\mathcal{O}$, Soergel bimodules, and the categorification program.
