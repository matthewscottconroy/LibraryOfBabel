# 53.1.1 Bialgebras: Algebra and Coalgebra Structures

A recurring theme in representation theory is that the category of representations of a group or Lie algebra has more structure than a mere abelian category: it has tensor products, and the tensor product of two representations is again a representation. To understand this additional structure algebraically, one needs to know how the algebra acts on tensor products — and this is precisely what a *coalgebra structure* encodes. A *bialgebra* is an algebraic object that combines an algebra structure (governing how elements multiply) with a coalgebra structure (governing how elements act on tensor products), in a compatible way.

\begin{definition}
A *bialgebra* over a field $k$ is a$k$-vector space$H$ equipped with:
\begin{itemize}
\item An associative algebra structure: multiplication $m: H \otimes H \to H$ and unit$\eta: k \to H$.
\item A coassociative coalgebra structure: comultiplication $\Delta: H \to H \otimes H$ and counit$\varepsilon: H \to k$.
\end{itemize}
such that $\Delta$ and$\varepsilon$ are algebra homomorphisms (equivalently,$m$ and$\eta$ are coalgebra maps).
\end{definition}

The comultiplication $\Delta$ is the key ingredient: if$H$ acts on modules$V$ and$W$, then$H$ acts on$V \otimes W$ by$h \cdot (v \otimes w) = \sum_{(h)} h_{(1)} \cdot v \otimes h_{(2)} \cdot w$, where$\Delta(h) = \sum_{(h)} h_{(1)} \otimes h_{(2)}$ in Sweedler notation. Coassociativity$(\Delta \otimes 1) \circ \Delta = (1 \otimes \Delta) \circ \Delta$ ensures this action is associative on triple tensor products.

**Example 1.** The group algebra $k[G]$ of a group$G$ is a bialgebra with:
$$\Delta(g) = g \otimes g, \quad \varepsilon(g) = 1, \quad m(g \otimes h) = gh, \quad \eta(1) = e.$$
The "diagonal" comultiplication records that $g$ acts on a tensor product$V \otimes W$ by acting simultaneously on both factors:$g \cdot (v \otimes w) = g_{(1)} \cdot v \otimes g_{(2)} \cdot w = gv \otimes gw$. This is precisely the definition of the tensor product of group representations.

**Example 2.** The universal enveloping algebra $U(\mathfrak{g})$ of a Lie algebra$\mathfrak{g}$ is a bialgebra with:
$$\Delta(x) = x \otimes 1 + 1 \otimes x, \quad \varepsilon(x) = 0 \quad \text{for } x \in \mathfrak{g}.$$
The "Leibniz" comultiplication records that Lie algebra elements act as derivations on tensor products: $x \cdot (v \otimes w) = x_{(1)} \cdot v \otimes x_{(2)} \cdot w = (x \cdot v) \otimes w + v \otimes (x \cdot w)$, which is the standard Leibniz rule for the tensor product action of a Lie algebra.

**Example 3.** Let us verify the compatibility condition for $U(\mathfrak{sl}_2)$. The compatibility says$\Delta \circ m = (m \otimes m) \circ (1 \otimes \tau \otimes 1) \circ (\Delta \otimes \Delta)$, i.e.,$\Delta$ is an algebra map. Check:$\Delta([e,f]) = \Delta(h)$, i.e.,$\Delta(ef - fe) = \Delta(e)\Delta(f) - \Delta(f)\Delta(e)$. We have:
\begin{align*}
\Delta(ef - fe) &= \Delta(h) = h \otimes 1 + 1 \otimes h, \\
\Delta(e)\Delta(f) - \Delta(f)\Delta(e) &= (e \otimes 1 + 1 \otimes e)(f \otimes 1 + 1 \otimes f) - (f \otimes 1 + 1 \otimes f)(e \otimes 1 + 1 \otimes e) \\
&= (ef - fe) \otimes 1 + 1 \otimes (ef - fe) = h \otimes 1 + 1 \otimes h. \checkmark
\end{align*}

Both the group algebra and universal enveloping algebra are *cocommutative*: $\tau \circ \Delta = \Delta$ where$\tau: H \otimes H \to H \otimes H$ is the tensor flip. Cocommutativity reflects the fact that tensor products of classical representations are symmetric:$V \otimes W \cong W \otimes V$ as representations.

\begin{definition}
A bialgebra $H$ is *cocommutative* if$\tau \circ \Delta = \Delta$, i.e.,$\sum_{(h)} h_{(1)} \otimes h_{(2)} = \sum_{(h)} h_{(2)} \otimes h_{(1)}$ for all$h \in H$.
\end{definition}

**Example 4.** The *polynomial algebra* $k[x]$ with$\Delta(x) = x \otimes 1 + 1 \otimes x$ (an "additive group" comultiplication) is a cocommutative bialgebra: it is the universal enveloping algebra$U(\mathfrak{g}_a)$ of the one-dimensional abelian Lie algebra$\mathfrak{g}_a = k$ (the additive group), so it fits into Example 2 above.

Quantum groups break cocommutativity. In $U_q(\mathfrak{g})$, the comultiplication is$\Delta(E) = E \otimes K + 1 \otimes E$,$\Delta(F) = F \otimes 1 + K^{-1} \otimes F$, which is *not* cocommutative:$\tau(\Delta(E)) = K \otimes E + E \otimes 1 \neq E \otimes K + 1 \otimes E = \Delta(E)$. This asymmetry is what makes the tensor category of quantum group representations *braided* rather than symmetric — a deformation of the classical symmetric structure that introduces genuinely new phenomena.

\begin{proposition}
In any bialgebra, the counit $\varepsilon: H \to k$ satisfies$(\varepsilon \otimes 1) \circ \Delta = 1 = (1 \otimes \varepsilon) \circ \Delta$ (the counit axioms). Equivalently, for any$H$-module$M$, the trivial module$k$ (with$h \cdot 1 = \varepsilon(h)$) satisfies$k \otimes M \cong M \cong M \otimes k$ as$H$-modules.
\end{proposition}

This proposition says that the trivial module is the unit object for the tensor product — a necessary condition for the tensor category to be monoidal. The counit axioms are the bialgebra axioms that guarantee this unit property.

\begin{remark}
The notion of a bialgebra can be dualised: the *dual* of a finite-dimensional bialgebra is again a bialgebra, with multiplication and comultiplication interchanged. For a finite group $G$, the dual of the group algebra$k[G]$ is the function algebra$\mathrm{Fun}(G, k)$, which is commutative (since$G$ is a set) but in general not cocommutative. This duality between "group algebras" and "function algebras on groups" is the origin of the general theory of Hopf algebras and of the algebraic groups perspective.
\end{remark}
