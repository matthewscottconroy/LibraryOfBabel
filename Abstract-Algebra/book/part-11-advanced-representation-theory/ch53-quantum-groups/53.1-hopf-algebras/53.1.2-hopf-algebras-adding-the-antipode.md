# 53.1.2 Hopf Algebras: Adding the Antipode

A bialgebra knows how to act on tensor products, but it does not yet know how to form duals of representations. To dualize a representation — to turn $V$ into its dual$V^* = \mathrm{Hom}_k(V, k)$ — one needs a way to "invert" the action of the algebra, analogously to how a group element$g$ acts on dual representations via$(g \cdot f)(v) = f(g^{-1} v)$. The algebraic structure that captures this inverse is the *antipode*, and a bialgebra equipped with a compatible antipode is a *Hopf algebra*.

\begin{definition}
A *Hopf algebra* is a bialgebra $(H, m, \eta, \Delta, \varepsilon)$ together with a$k$-linear map$S: H \to H$, the *antipode*, satisfying
$$\sum_{(h)} S(h_{(1)}) h_{(2)} = \varepsilon(h) \cdot 1 = \sum_{(h)} h_{(1)} S(h_{(2)})$$
for all $h \in H$. In diagram form:$m \circ (S \otimes \mathrm{id}) \circ \Delta = \eta \circ \varepsilon = m \circ (\mathrm{id} \otimes S) \circ \Delta$.
\end{definition}

The antipode satisfies: $S$ is an anti-algebra anti-coalgebra map, meaning$S(hk) = S(k)S(h)$ and$\Delta(S(h)) = \sum_{(h)} S(h_{(2)}) \otimes S(h_{(1)})$ for all$h, k \in H$. When$H$ is commutative or cocommutative,$S^2 = \mathrm{id}$.

**Example 1.** For the group algebra $k[G]$, the antipode is$S(g) = g^{-1}$. Verify:$\sum_{(g)} S(g_{(1)}) g_{(2)} = S(g) g = g^{-1} g = e = \varepsilon(g) \cdot e = 1$.$\checkmark$

**Example 2.** For the universal enveloping algebra $U(\mathfrak{g})$, the antipode is$S(x) = -x$ for$x \in \mathfrak{g}$, extended as an anti-algebra map. Verify on a product$xy$ for$x, y \in \mathfrak{g}$:$S(xy) = S(y)S(x) = (-y)(-x) = yx$. The antipode axiom:$\sum_{(x)} S(x_{(1)}) x_{(2)} = S(x \otimes 1 + 1 \otimes x)$... let us check on$x \in \mathfrak{g}$:$\Delta(x) = x \otimes 1 + 1 \otimes x$, so$\sum S(x_{(1)})x_{(2)} = S(x) \cdot 1 + S(1) \cdot x = (-x)(1) + (1)(x) = -x + x = 0 = \varepsilon(x) \cdot 1$.$\checkmark$

**Example 3.** For $U_q(\mathfrak{sl}_2)$, the antipode is defined on generators by:
$$S(E) = -EK^{-1}, \quad S(F) = -KF, \quad S(K) = K^{-1}.$$
Verify the antipode axiom on $E$:$\Delta(E) = E \otimes K + 1 \otimes E$, so$\sum S(E_{(1)}) E_{(2)} = S(E) \cdot K + S(1) \cdot E = (-EK^{-1})K + 1 \cdot E = -E + E = 0 = \varepsilon(E) \cdot 1$.$\checkmark$. Note:$S^2(E) = S(-EK^{-1}) = -S(K^{-1})S(E) = -(K)(-EK^{-1}) = KE K^{-1} = q^{-2}E \neq E$, showing$S^2 \neq \mathrm{id}$ for quantum groups (a genuinely non-cocommutative phenomenon).

With the antipode in place, the category $\mathrm{Rep}(H)$ of finite-dimensional$H$-modules becomes a *rigid* monoidal category: given a module$V$, the dual$V^* = \mathrm{Hom}_k(V, k)$ is an$H$-module via$(h \cdot f)(v) = f(S(h) \cdot v)$, and one can form evaluation and coevaluation maps satisfying the standard rigidity axioms.

\begin{proposition}
For a Hopf algebra $H$ and a finite-dimensional$H$-module$V$, the dual$V^*$ with the action$(h \cdot f)(v) = f(S(h)v)$ is an$H$-module. The evaluation map$\mathrm{ev}: V^* \otimes V \to k$,$f \otimes v \mapsto f(v)$, and the coevaluation$\mathrm{coev}: k \to V \otimes V^*$,$1 \mapsto \sum_i e_i \otimes e^i$ (for a basis$\{e_i\}$ and dual basis$\{e^i\}$), are$H$-module maps. This makes$\mathrm{Rep}(H)$ a rigid monoidal category.
\end{proposition}

**Example 4.** For $H = k[G]$ and$V$ a$G$-representation,$V^*$ with$(g \cdot f)(v) = f(g^{-1} v)$ is the standard contragredient representation. The evaluation map$f \otimes v \mapsto f(v)$ is$G$-equivariant:$g \cdot \mathrm{ev}(f \otimes v) = \mathrm{ev}(gf \otimes gv) = (gf)(gv) = f(g^{-1} \cdot gv) = f(v)$.$\checkmark$ This confirms that the standard "dual representation" construction for groups is precisely the rigidity structure of$\mathrm{Rep}(k[G])$ as a monoidal category.

\begin{theorem}[Milnor–Moore]
Over a field $k$ of characteristic 0, every cocommutative Hopf algebra$H$ is isomorphic (as a Hopf algebra) to$U(\mathfrak{g})$ for the Lie algebra$\mathfrak{g}$ of primitive elements$\mathfrak{g} = \{h \in H : \Delta(h) = h \otimes 1 + 1 \otimes h\}$.
\end{theorem}

This theorem says: all cocommutative Hopf algebras come from Lie algebras. Quantum groups violate cocommutativity precisely to escape this classification, producing new Hopf algebras not of the form $U(\mathfrak{g})$.

\begin{remark}
The rigidity of $\mathrm{Rep}(H)$ is essential for topological applications: braided rigid monoidal categories are exactly what one needs to construct knot and link invariants, since the braiding gives the crossing and the rigidity (the antipode) gives the cup-cap moves. For quantum groups$U_q(\mathfrak{g})$, the antipode$S$ satisfies$S^2 = q^{2\rho}(\cdot)q^{-2\rho}$ (conjugation by the element$q^{2\rho}$ acting on weight spaces), where$\rho$ is the Weyl vector. This twisting of$S^2$ is related to the "ribbon element" in the quantum group, which is what enables the Reshetikhin–Turaev construction of framed knot invariants.
\end{remark}
