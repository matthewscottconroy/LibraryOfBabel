# 53.1.3 Group Algebras and Universal Enveloping Algebras as Hopf Algebras

The two main families of Hopf algebras in classical representation theory — group algebras $k[G]$ and universal enveloping algebras$U(\mathfrak{g})$ — are both *cocommutative*: their comultiplications satisfy$\tau \circ \Delta = \Delta$, where$\tau: H \otimes H \to H \otimes H$ is the tensor flip. Cocommutativity encodes the fact that the tensor product of representations is symmetric:$V \otimes W \cong W \otimes V$ as representations. This symmetry is so deeply familiar from classical representation theory that it can seem inevitable. The key insight of quantum group theory is that it is not inevitable — it is a special feature of classical algebra that can and should be deformed.

\begin{theorem}[Milnor–Moore]
Over a field $k$ of characteristic 0, every cocommutative Hopf algebra$H$ that is connected (i.e.,$H = k$ in degree 0 when graded) is isomorphic as a Hopf algebra to the universal enveloping algebra$U(\mathfrak{g})$, where$\mathfrak{g} = P(H) = \{h \in H : \Delta(h) = h \otimes 1 + 1 \otimes h\}$ is the Lie algebra of primitive elements.
\end{theorem}

**Example 1.** For the group algebra $k[G]$ of a finite group$G$ in characteristic 0, the Milnor–Moore theorem does not apply directly (group algebras are not connected in the graded sense). However, the key distinction is clear: the group algebra is *pointed* (all simple comodules are 1-dimensional, corresponding to group-like elements$g$ with$\Delta(g) = g \otimes g$), not *primitively generated*. Group-like elements and primitive elements are dual to each other under the pairing$(k[G])^* = \mathrm{Fun}(G, k)$: group-like elements in$k[G]$ correspond to characters of$G$, and characters of$G$ are characters of$\mathrm{Fun}(G,k)$.

**Example 2.** For $H = U(\mathfrak{sl}_2)$, the primitive elements are exactly$\mathfrak{sl}_2$ itself (spanned by$e, f, h$). One checks:$\Delta(e) = e \otimes 1 + 1 \otimes e$, so$e$ is primitive. Any product$ef$ has$\Delta(ef) = \Delta(e)\Delta(f) = (e \otimes 1 + 1 \otimes e)(f \otimes 1 + 1 \otimes f) = ef \otimes 1 + e \otimes f + f \otimes e + 1 \otimes ef$, which is not primitive (it has an$e \otimes f$ term).

The Milnor–Moore theorem says $U(\mathfrak{g})$ is characterized among cocommutative Hopf algebras by being generated by its primitive elements. The non-commutative but cocommutative case — like group algebras in characteristic$p$, where elements$g - 1$ for$g$ in the$p$-Sylow are nearly primitive — shows the theorem's hypotheses are necessary.

\begin{proposition}
There is a duality between Hopf algebras: if $H$ is a finite-dimensional Hopf algebra, then$H^* = \mathrm{Hom}_k(H, k)$ is again a Hopf algebra, with algebra and coalgebra structures of$H^*$ given by dualizing the coalgebra and algebra structures of$H$ respectively.
\end{proposition}

**Example 3.** For $H = k[G]$ (a finite group),$H^* = \mathrm{Fun}(G, k)$ is the function algebra on$G$. The algebra structure of$H^*$: pointwise multiplication$(fg)(h) = f(h)g(h)$. The coalgebra structure:$\Delta^*(f)(g \otimes h) = f(gh)$, so$\Delta^*(f) = \sum_{g \in G} f_g \otimes \delta_g$ where$f_g = f(g)$ — this is the comultiplication of the function algebra. This is commutative (since$\mathrm{Fun}(G,k)$ is commutative) but in general not cocommutative (unless$G$ is abelian). The function algebra$\mathrm{Fun}(G, k)$ represents the group$G$ as a group scheme, and this algebraic-geometric perspective is the foundation of the theory of algebraic groups.

For an algebraic group $G$ over$k$, the algebra of regular functions$\mathcal{O}(G)$ is a commutative Hopf algebra, with comultiplication$\Delta^*: \mathcal{O}(G) \to \mathcal{O}(G) \otimes \mathcal{O}(G)$ induced by the group multiplication. The cocommutative Hopf algebra$U(\mathfrak{g})$ is dual to$\mathcal{O}(G)$ via the formal group law, providing a deep connection between Lie theory and algebraic group theory.

Quantum groups $U_q(\mathfrak{g})$ are deformations of$U(\mathfrak{g})$: at$q = 1$,$U_q(\mathfrak{g}) \cong U(\mathfrak{g})$ (after the appropriate specialization), but for$q \neq 1$ they are neither commutative nor cocommutative.

\begin{definition}
A Hopf algebra $H$ is *almost cocommutative* if there exists an invertible element$R \in H \otimes H$, the *universal$R$-matrix*, such that$\tau \circ \Delta(h) = R \cdot \Delta(h) \cdot R^{-1}$ for all$h \in H$. An almost cocommutative Hopf algebra with$R$ satisfying the hexagon axioms$((\Delta \otimes \mathrm{id})(R) = R_{13} R_{23}$ and$(\mathrm{id} \otimes \Delta)(R) = R_{13} R_{12})$ is called *quasitriangular*.
\end{definition}

**Example 4.** For $U_q(\mathfrak{sl}_2)$, the universal$R$-matrix is:
$$R = q^{H \otimes H/2} \sum_{n \geq 0} \frac{(1-q^{-2})^n}{[n]_q!} q^{n(n-1)/2} E^n \otimes F^n,$$
an infinite sum (well-defined as an element of the completed tensor product). On the two-dimensional representation $V_1$,$R$ acts as the explicit$4 \times 4$ matrix:
$$R_{V_1, V_1} = \begin{pmatrix} q & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & q - q^{-1} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & q \end{pmatrix}$$
(in the basis $v_1 \otimes v_1, v_1 \otimes v_{-1}, v_{-1} \otimes v_1, v_{-1} \otimes v_{-1}$ where$v_{\pm 1}$ are the weight vectors). As$q \to 1$, this approaches the identity (the tensor flip, restoring cocommutativity).

\begin{remark}
The almost-cocommutativity of $U_q(\mathfrak{g})$ makes the category$\mathrm{Rep}(U_q(\mathfrak{g}))$ braided rather than symmetric, and the braiding is what connects quantum groups to knot theory and topological quantum field theory. The universal$R$-matrix satisfies the Yang–Baxter equation$R_{12} R_{13} R_{23} = R_{23} R_{13} R_{12}$ in$H^{\otimes 3}$ — a consequence of the hexagon axioms — and this ensures the braid group acts consistently on tensor powers$V^{\otimes n}$.
\end{remark}
