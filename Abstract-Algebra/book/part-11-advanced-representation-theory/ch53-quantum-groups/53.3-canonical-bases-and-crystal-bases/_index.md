# 53.3 Canonical Bases and Crystal Bases

The most striking feature of quantum groups — and perhaps the deepest result in their theory — is the existence of canonical bases. In the classical theory of $U(\mathfrak{g})$, all choices of basis for a representation are essentially equivalent; there is no canonical one. But in the quantum deformation $U_q(\mathfrak{g})$, working over the ring $\mathbb{Z}[q, q^{-1}]$ rather than the field $\mathbb{Q}(q)$, Lusztig discovered in 1990 that every simple module $V(\lambda)$ admits a unique $\mathbb{Z}[q, q^{-1}]$-basis with two remarkable properties: it is preserved by the bar involution ($q \mapsto q^{-1}$, extended by certain antipodal symmetries), and it is integral (each basis element lies in the $\mathbb{Z}[q, q^{-1}]$-lattice generated by the divided powers). These two conditions uniquely pin down the basis, and it turns out to have non-negative integer structure constants: the matrix elements of $U_q(\mathfrak{g})$ in the canonical basis lie in $\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}[q, q^{-1}]$. This positivity is trivial for $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ but nontrivial in general; Lusztig proved it using the identification of canonical basis elements with the classes of simple perverse sheaves on a certain algebraic variety (the cyclic quiver variety or flag variety), where positivity follows from the Weil conjectures.

Crystal bases, introduced independently by Kashiwara in 1990, provide a complementary perspective via the $q \to 0$ limit. As $q \to 0$, the action of the divided powers $E_i^{(n)}$ and $F_i^{(n)}$ on a representation degenerates from a linear map on a vector space to a map on a set — the crystal. Formally, a crystal is a set $B$ equipped with maps $\tilde{e}_i, \tilde{f}_i: B \to B \cup \{0\}$ (the crystal operators, corresponding to adding or removing a box in representation-theoretic terms), a weight map $\mathrm{wt}: B \to \Lambda$ into the weight lattice, and integers $\varepsilon_i(b) = \max\{n : \tilde{e}_i^n(b) \neq 0\}$ and $\varphi_i(b) = \max\{n : \tilde{f}_i^n(b) \neq 0\}$ satisfying $\varphi_i(b) - \varepsilon_i(b) = \langle \mathrm{wt}(b), \alpha_i^\vee \rangle$. The crystal of the simple module $V(\lambda)$ is a finite colored directed graph, with vertices corresponding to basis elements (or weight vectors), edges $b \xrightarrow{i} b'$ whenever $\tilde{f}_i(b) = b'$, and no algebra structure — just combinatorics. The highest weight vertex is the unique source, and the weight of each vertex is recovered from the path to it.

The tensor product of crystals is defined combinatorially: on $B(\lambda) \otimes B(\mu)$, the crystal operators act by the tensor product rule, which requires a comparison of the $\varepsilon$ and $\varphi$ values at the two tensor factors. The Kashiwara–Nakashima theorem establishes that the crystal basis of $V(\lambda) \otimes V(\mu)$ decomposes as a disjoint union $\bigsqcup_\nu B(\nu)$ over all $\nu$ appearing in the Clebsch–Gordan decomposition, and the multiplicities are the Littlewood–Richardson coefficients. For $\mathfrak{sl}_n$, crystals are realized concretely by semistandard Young tableaux with entries in $\{1, \ldots, n\}$: the crystal of $V(\lambda)$ is the set of semistandard tableaux of shape $\lambda$, with crystal operators given by Kashiwara's reading algorithm. This combinatorial model makes the crystal basis completely explicit and computable, and it is deeply connected to the Robinson–Schensted–Knuth correspondence and the combinatorics of symmetric functions.

The relationship between crystal bases and canonical bases is subtle but profound. They are not the same basis: the canonical basis lives over $\mathbb{Z}[q, q^{-1}]$ and retains $q$-dependence, while the crystal basis lives in the $q \to 0$ limit. However, they are compatible: the canonical basis elements, when reduced modulo $q$, give precisely the crystal basis. This means that the positivity of canonical basis structure constants can be predicted from the crystal combinatorics, and conversely, the crystal structure can be used to construct the canonical basis inductively. The interplay between these two perspectives — algebraic ($\mathbb{Z}[q,q^{-1}]$-integrality) and combinatorial ($q \to 0$ crystal) — is one of the most beautiful features of quantum group theory.
