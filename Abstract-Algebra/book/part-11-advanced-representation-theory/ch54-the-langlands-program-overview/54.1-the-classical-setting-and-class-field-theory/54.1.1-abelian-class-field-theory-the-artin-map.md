# 54.1.1 Abelian Class Field Theory; the Artin Map

One of the central achievements of 19th and early 20th century number theory was to understand all abelian extensions of a number field $K$ — that is, all Galois extensions$L/K$ with abelian Galois group — in terms of data intrinsic to$K$ itself. The answer, which goes by the name *abelian class field theory*, is both precise and surprising: the abelian extensions of$K$ are completely controlled by the multiplicative structure of the idèle class group$C_K = \mathbb{A}_K^*/K^*$, where$\mathbb{A}_K$ is the adèle ring of$K$. The translation between extensions and subgroups of$C_K$ is provided by the *Artin map*.

\begin{definition}
The *adèle ring* of a number field $K$ is the restricted product$\mathbb{A}_K = \prod'_v K_v$, where the product runs over all places$v$ of$K$ (both archimedean and non-archimedean), and "restricted" means that all but finitely many components lie in the ring of integers$\mathcal{O}_v$. The *idèle class group* is$C_K = \mathbb{A}_K^*/K^*$, where$K^*$ embeds diagonally.
\end{definition}

**Example 1.** For $K = \mathbb{Q}$, the adèle ring is$\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}} = \mathbb{R} \times \hat{\mathbb{Z}} \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{Q} = \mathbb{R} \times \prod'_p \mathbb{Q}_p$, and the idèle class group is$C_{\mathbb{Q}} = \mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}}^* / \mathbb{Q}^*$. By the strong approximation theorem,$C_{\mathbb{Q}} \cong \mathbb{R}_{>0} \times \hat{\mathbb{Z}}^*$, where$\hat{\mathbb{Z}}^* = \varprojlim (\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^*$ is the profinite completion of$\mathbb{Z}^*$.

\begin{theorem}[Artin Reciprocity]
There exists a continuous surjective homomorphism, the *Artin map* or *reciprocity map*,
$$\phi_K: C_K \to \mathrm{Gal}(K^{ab}/K)$$
(with dense image in the profinite topology), where $K^{ab}$ is the maximal abelian extension of$K$. For every finite abelian extension$L/K$, the Artin map induces a canonical isomorphism
$$C_K / N_{L/K}(C_L) \xrightarrow{\sim} \mathrm{Gal}(L/K),$$
where $N_{L/K}: C_L \to C_K$ is the norm map.
\end{theorem}

**Example 2.** For $K = \mathbb{Q}$ and$L = \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$ (the$n$-th cyclotomic field), the Artin map sends the idèle$(\ldots, 1, p, 1, \ldots)$ (with$p$ in the$p$-th component) to$\mathrm{Frob}_p \in \mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)/\mathbb{Q}) \cong (\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^*$, the Frobenius element acting by$\zeta_n \mapsto \zeta_n^p$. The norm subgroup is$N_{\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)/\mathbb{Q}}(C_{\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)}) = \{[x] \in C_{\mathbb{Q}} : x_p \in N_{\mathbb{Q}_p(\zeta_n)/\mathbb{Q}_p}(\mathbb{Q}_p(\zeta_n)^*) \text{ for all } p\}$.

**Example 3.** The Artin map encodes the quadratic reciprocity law. For $K = \mathbb{Q}$ and$L = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{p^*})$ (where$p^* = (-1)^{(p-1)/2} p$ for an odd prime$p$), the Artin map at a prime$q \neq p$ sends$q$ to$\left(\frac{p^*}{q}\right) = \left(\frac{p}{q}\right)(-1)^{(p-1)(q-1)/4}$ (the Legendre symbol). The quadratic reciprocity law$\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)\left(\frac{q}{p}\right) = (-1)^{\frac{p-1}{2}\frac{q-1}{2}}$ follows from the fact that the Artin map is well-defined: the product formula$\prod_v \phi_{K_v}(x) = 1$ for the local Artin maps, applied to$x = p \in \mathbb{Q}^* \subset \mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}}^*$, gives the reciprocity law.

The key insight of class field theory — and the seed of the Langlands program — is that the Artin map can be viewed as a correspondence between one-dimensional Galois representations and automorphic data.

\begin{proposition}
A character $\chi: \mathrm{Gal}(K^{ab}/K) \to \mathbb{C}^*$ (a 1-dimensional Galois representation) corresponds, via the Artin map, to a Hecke character$\psi: C_K \to \mathbb{C}^*$ (an automorphic representation of$GL_1(\mathbb{A}_K)$). The Artin$L$-function$L(s, \chi) = \prod_v L(s, \chi_v)$ equals the Hecke$L$-function$L(s, \psi) = \prod_v L(s, \psi_v)$.
\end{proposition}

**Example 4.** For $K = \mathbb{Q}$ and$\chi: \mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)/\mathbb{Q}) \to \mathbb{C}^*$ a Dirichlet character (a homomorphism$(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^* \to \mathbb{C}^*$), the corresponding Hecke character is$\psi = \chi \circ \phi_{\mathbb{Q}}$. The Artin$L$-function is$L(s, \chi) = \sum_{m \geq 1} \chi(m) m^{-s}$ (the Dirichlet$L$-function), and it equals the Hecke$L$-function by construction. For example,$\chi = \chi_0$ (the trivial character) gives$L(s, \chi_0) = \zeta(s)$ (the Riemann zeta function).

\begin{remark}
The Artin reciprocity law was proved in stages: for $K = \mathbb{Q}$ it follows from Gauss's quadratic reciprocity (for quadratic extensions) and Eisenstein's higher reciprocity laws. The general case — for arbitrary number fields and arbitrary abelian extensions — was proved by Artin in 1927 using his general reciprocity law, which he derived from the special case of cyclotomic fields (proving a conjecture of Hilbert). The proof reduces the general case to the case of cyclotomic extensions via the transfer map in group theory. Emil Artin's proof is one of the masterpieces of early 20th century mathematics, and it set the stage for the entire subsequent development of the Langlands program.
\end{remark}
