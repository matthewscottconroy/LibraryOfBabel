# Chapter 54 — The Langlands Program (Overview)

In 1967, Robert Langlands wrote a letter to André Weil containing a series of conjectures so ambitious, so precise, and so far-reaching that they have shaped the agenda of number theory, representation theory, and algebraic geometry ever since. The Langlands program is not a single conjecture but a vast web of predicted correspondences, each asserting that objects from three apparently unrelated mathematical worlds — Galois representations in number theory, automorphic forms in harmonic analysis, and geometric objects on moduli spaces in algebraic geometry — are secretly the same thing, written in different languages. The program is simultaneously a unification of classical mathematics (class field theory, modular forms, $L$-functions) and a blueprint for discoveries still unmade. Its partial realizations have already produced two of the most spectacular theorems of the twentieth century: the proof of the modularity of elliptic curves by Wiles and Taylor (1995), which implies Fermat's Last Theorem, and the proof of the local Langlands correspondence for $GL_n$ by Harris–Taylor and Henniart (2001). Yet the global number-field Langlands correspondence — for $GL_n$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, say — remains wide open and constitutes what may be the deepest unsolved problem in contemporary mathematics.

The simplest case of the Langlands program is classical: class field theory, which provides a complete description of all abelian Galois extensions of a number field $F$. For $F = \mathbb{Q}$, the Kronecker–Weber theorem says every abelian extension is contained in a cyclotomic field $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$, and the Artin map $(\cdot, \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)/\mathbb{Q}): \mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}}^\times / \mathbb{Q}^\times \to \mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}^{ab}/\mathbb{Q})$ provides a canonical isomorphism between the idèle class group $\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}}^\times / \mathbb{Q}^\times$ and the abelian Galois group $\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}^{ab}/\mathbb{Q})$. This is the $GL_1$ Langlands correspondence: 1-dimensional Galois representations (characters of the absolute Galois group $\mathrm{Gal}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q})$) correspond to Hecke Grössencharacters (characters of the idèle class group). The Langlands program for $GL_n$ predicts that this correspondence extends, in a precise sense, from dimension 1 to dimension $n$: $n$-dimensional Galois representations correspond to automorphic representations of $GL_n(\mathbb{A}_F)$.

The statement for $GL_2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ is the modularity theorem. An automorphic representation of $GL_2(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})$ that is algebraic and cuspidal of weight 2 corresponds to a newform $f = \sum a_n q^n$ of weight 2 and some level $N$; its $L$-function $L(f, s) = \sum a_n n^{-s}$ has an Euler product $\prod_p (1 - a_p p^{-s} + p^{1-2s})^{-1}$ (for primes $p \nmid N$) that matches the $L$-function of a 2-dimensional Galois representation $\rho_f: \mathrm{Gal}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q}) \to GL_2(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_\ell)$ with the property that $\mathrm{tr}(\rho_f(\mathrm{Frob}_p)) = a_p$ for almost all primes $p$. Wiles and Taylor proved that every semistable elliptic curve $E/\mathbb{Q}$ is modular — its Galois representation $\rho_{E,\ell}$ arises from a modular form in this way — by showing that the Galois deformation ring governing the possible lifts of $\rho_{E,\ell}$ modulo $\ell$ is isomorphic to the Hecke ring of a space of modular forms. This "R = T" theorem, proved using techniques from Galois cohomology, Iwasawa theory, and the theory of automorphic forms, remains the deepest known case of the Langlands program over number fields.

The geometric Langlands program replaces the number field by the function field of an algebraic curve $C$ over a field $k$. In this setting, Galois representations become local systems on $C$ — locally constant sheaves of $\hat{G}$-torsors on $C$, where $\hat{G}$ is the Langlands dual group — and automorphic forms become $\mathcal{D}$-modules on the moduli stack $\mathrm{Bun}_G(C)$ of $G$-bundles on $C$. The geometric Langlands correspondence, proved by Frenkel–Ben-Zvi–Nadler–Gaitsgory in various cases and recently completed in remarkable generality by Gaitsgory–collaborators, is an equivalence of derived categories: between the derived category of $\mathcal{D}$-modules on $\mathrm{Bun}_G(C)$ and a certain derived category of local systems on $C$ with structure group $\hat{G}$. The geometric setting turns the correspondence from a web of conjectures into a precise mathematical theorem, and the techniques developed — ind-coherent sheaves, $\infty$-categories, factorization algebras — represent some of the most sophisticated tools in contemporary mathematics.
