## 8.1 Dual Spaces

### 8.1.1 The Dual Space

For a vector space $V$ over $F$, the **dual space** is:
$$V^* = \mathcal{L}(V, F) = \{f: V \to F \mid f \text{ linear}\}$$

Elements of $V^*$ are **linear functionals** (or **covectors**).

$\dim V^* = \dim V$ when $V$ is finite-dimensional.

### 8.1.2 Dual Basis

Given a basis $\mathcal{B} = (\mathbf{e}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{e}_n)$ of $V$, the **dual basis** $\mathcal{B}^* = (e^1, \ldots, e^n)$ of $V^*$ is defined by:
$$e^i(\mathbf{e}_j) = \delta^i_j = \begin{cases} 1 & i = j \\ 0 & i \neq j \end{cases}$$

This is the Kronecker delta. Every $f \in V^*$ expands as $f = \sum_i f(\mathbf{e}_i) e^i$.

### 8.1.3 Double Duality

The **double dual** $V^{**} = (V^*)^*$. There is a canonical (basis-independent) isomorphism:
$$\iota: V \xrightarrow{\sim} V^{**}, \quad \iota(\mathbf{v})(f) = f(\mathbf{v})$$

For finite-dimensional $V$, $\iota$ is an isomorphism, so $V \cong V^{**}$ canonically. This is the algebraic analogue of "thinking of a point as the collection of all functions that evaluate at it."

### 8.1.4 Transpose and Dual Maps

For $T: V \to W$, the **dual map** $T^*: W^* \to V^*$ (or **transpose**) is:
$$(T^* f)(\mathbf{v}) = f(T(\mathbf{v}))$$

In matrix terms: $[T^*] = [T]^T$ (ordinary matrix transpose, with respect to dual bases).

## 8.2 Tensor Products

### 8.2.1 The Universal Property

The **tensor product** $V \otimes W$ of two $F$-vector spaces is a vector space equipped with a bilinear map $\otimes: V \times W \to V \otimes W$ such that:

**Universal property:** For every vector space $U$ and every bilinear map $B: V \times W \to U$, there exists a unique linear map $\tilde{B}: V \otimes W \to U$ with $\tilde{B}(\mathbf{v} \otimes \mathbf{w}) = B(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w})$.

In short: bilinear maps out of $V \times W$ correspond to linear maps out of $V \otimes W$.

### 8.2.2 Existence and Basis

$V \otimes W$ exists and is unique up to unique isomorphism (by the universal property).

If $\mathcal{B} = (\mathbf{e}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{e}_m)$ and $\mathcal{C} = (\mathbf{f}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{f}_n)$ are bases, then:
$$\{\mathbf{e}_i \otimes \mathbf{f}_j \mid 1 \leq i \leq m, 1 \leq j \leq n\}$$
is a basis of $V \otimes W$. So $\dim(V \otimes W) = (\dim V)(\dim W)$.

**Pure tensors** $\mathbf{v} \otimes \mathbf{w}$ span $V \otimes W$ but not every element is pure.

### 8.2.3 Key Properties

- $(V \otimes W) \otimes X \cong V \otimes (W \otimes X)$ (associativity)
- $V \otimes W \cong W \otimes V$ (commutativity, via $\mathbf{v} \otimes \mathbf{w} \mapsto \mathbf{w} \otimes \mathbf{v}$)
- $F \otimes V \cong V$ (unit)
- $V^* \otimes W \cong \mathcal{L}(V, W)$ (linear maps as tensors)
- $(V \oplus U) \otimes W \cong (V \otimes W) \oplus (U \otimes W)$ (distributivity)

### 8.2.4 Tensors in Coordinates

An element of $V \otimes V^*$ (a.k.a. a **$(1,1)$-tensor**) can be written in a basis as:
$$T = \sum_{i,j} T^i{}_j \, \mathbf{e}_i \otimes e^j$$

The **Einstein summation convention:** Repeated upper+lower indices imply summation. E.g., $T^i{}_j v^j$ means $\sum_j T^i{}_j v^j$.

**Contraction:** Contracting index $i$ with index $j$ in $T^{ij}{}_{kl}$ gives $\sum_i T^{ii}{}_{kl}$.

**Trace** = contraction of a $(1,1)$-tensor $T^i{}_j \mapsto \sum_i T^i{}_i$.

## 8.3 Exterior Algebra

### 8.3.1 Alternating Tensors

The $k$-th **exterior power** $\bigwedge^k V$ is defined as the quotient of $V^{\otimes k}$ by the subspace generated by:
$$\mathbf{v}_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathbf{v}_k \quad \text{whenever } \mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{v}_j \text{ for some } i \neq j$$

The image of $\mathbf{v}_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathbf{v}_k$ is the **wedge product** $\mathbf{v}_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \mathbf{v}_k$.

**Key property:** $\mathbf{v}_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \mathbf{v}_k = 0$ if any $\mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{v}_j$, and transposing two factors negates the product.

### 8.3.2 Basis and Dimension

$$\dim \bigwedge^k V = \binom{n}{k} \quad \text{where } n = \dim V$$

A basis of $\bigwedge^k V$: $\{\mathbf{e}_{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge \mathbf{e}_{i_k} \mid i_1 < i_2 < \cdots < i_k\}$.

Special cases:
- $\bigwedge^0 V = F$
- $\bigwedge^1 V = V$
- $\bigwedge^n V \cong F$ (one-dimensional — the **determinant line**)
- $\bigwedge^k V = 0$ for $k > n$

### 8.3.3 The Determinant as Exterior Product

A linear map $T: V \to V$ induces $\bigwedge^n T: \bigwedge^n V \to \bigwedge^n V$. Since $\bigwedge^n V \cong F$ (one-dimensional), this map is multiplication by a scalar:

$$(\bigwedge^n T)(\omega) = \det(T) \cdot \omega$$

This is the coordinate-free definition of the determinant.

### 8.3.4 The Exterior Algebra

The **exterior algebra** (or **Grassmann algebra**):
$$\bigwedge V = \bigoplus_{k=0}^n \bigwedge^k V$$

with multiplication given by $\wedge$. This is a graded, skew-commutative algebra:
$$\alpha \wedge \beta = (-1)^{|\alpha||\beta|} \beta \wedge \alpha$$

**Connections:** Differential forms on a manifold are sections of $\bigwedge T^*M$; exterior differentiation $d$ makes this a cochain complex.

## 8.4 Symmetric Algebra

### 8.4.1 Symmetric Tensors

The $k$-th **symmetric power** $\mathrm{Sym}^k(V)$ is $V^{\otimes k}$ modulo the relation that makes tensors commutative:

$$\mathbf{v}_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathbf{v}_k \sim \mathbf{v}_{\sigma(1)} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathbf{v}_{\sigma(k)} \quad \text{for all } \sigma \in S_k$$

Write the image as $\mathbf{v}_1 \cdots \mathbf{v}_k$ (commutative product).

$$\dim \mathrm{Sym}^k(V) = \binom{n+k-1}{k}$$

### 8.4.2 The Symmetric Algebra

$$\mathrm{Sym}(V) = \bigoplus_{k=0}^\infty \mathrm{Sym}^k(V)$$

This is a commutative graded algebra. If $\mathbf{e}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{e}_n$ is a basis of $V$, then:
$$\mathrm{Sym}(V) \cong F[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$$

The symmetric algebra is the polynomial ring in the basis elements. This identification is canonical (up to the choice of basis).

---

## 8.5 The Full Tensor Algebra

The **tensor algebra**:
$$T(V) = \bigoplus_{k=0}^\infty V^{\otimes k}$$

is the free associative algebra on $V$. Both $\bigwedge V$ and $\mathrm{Sym}(V)$ are quotients of $T(V)$.

**Universal enveloping algebra** $\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})$ (see Chapter 22) is another quotient of $T(\mathfrak{g})$ — this is where representation theory of Lie algebras lives.

## Key Theorems

| Theorem | Statement |
|---------|-----------|
| Universal property of $\otimes$ | Bilinear maps $V \times W \to U$ $\leftrightarrow$ linear maps $V \otimes W \to U$ |
| $V^* \otimes W \cong \mathcal{L}(V,W)$ | Linear maps are tensors |
| $\dim \bigwedge^k V = \binom{n}{k}$ | Exterior power dimensions |
| Det as $\bigwedge^n$ | $\det(T) = $ scalar by which $T$ acts on $\bigwedge^n V$ |
| $\mathrm{Sym}(V) \cong F[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ | Symmetric algebra = polynomial ring |

## Milestone Exercises

1. Let $V = \mathbb{R}^2$ with basis $\mathbf{e}_1, \mathbf{e}_2$. Find a basis for $V \otimes V$, and express $(2\mathbf{e}_1 + \mathbf{e}_2) \otimes (3\mathbf{e}_1 - \mathbf{e}_2)$ in this basis. Is this element a pure tensor?

2. Prove: $\mathbf{u} \wedge \mathbf{v} = -\mathbf{v} \wedge \mathbf{u}$ using the definition of $\bigwedge^2 V$.

3. Show that $\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k$ are linearly independent iff $\mathbf{v}_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \mathbf{v}_k \neq 0$ in $\bigwedge^k V$.

4. Let $T: V \to V$ be a linear map on an $n$-dimensional space. Prove $\bigwedge^n T = \det(T) \cdot \mathrm{id}$.

5. Identify $\mathrm{Sym}^2(\mathbb{R}^2)$ explicitly. What is the relation to quadratic forms?

6. Compute the dual map $T^*: (\mathbb{R}^3)^* \to (\mathbb{R}^2)^*$ for $T(x,y,z) = (x+y, y+z)$.

7. Show $V^* \otimes W \cong \mathcal{L}(V,W)$ by constructing an explicit isomorphism.
