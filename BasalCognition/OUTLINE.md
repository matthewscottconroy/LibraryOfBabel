## INTRODUCTION: The Reach of Mind

*A single Physarum polycephalum — a plasmodial slime mold with no neurons, no synapses, no brain — solves a maze. It optimizes a rail network. It anticipates rhythmic disturbances. What are we to make of this? This is not metaphor. This is not poetry dressed in science. This is data, and it demands an explanation.*

The introduction frames the central tension of the book: the traditional view that cognition is the exclusive product of nervous systems has cracked. Evidence accumulates from every corner of biology that the fundamental capacities we associate with mind — sensing, remembering, deciding, learning, anticipating, communicating — appear in organisms that predate neurons by billions of years. The question is no longer whether these capacities exist in "lower" organisms. The question is what kind of thing cognition really is.

# UNIT I: FIRST PRINCIPLES — What Is Intelligence? What Is Life?

> *Before we can ask where intelligence comes from, we must ask what it is. Before we can ask how life thinks, we must ask what life does. These are not simple questions. They are the oldest questions, and biology has been quietly answering them for four billion years.*

This unit strips the inquiry down to its foundations. It does not assume a definition of intelligence — it constructs one from the ground up, from the logic of living systems. By the end of this unit, the reader will hold a working definition of cognition that is broad enough to be biologically honest and precise enough to be scientifically useful.

# UNIT II: THE INTELLIGENT CELL — Cognition at Life's Fundamental Scale

> *Every cell that has ever lived has faced the same basic problems: find food, avoid poison, reproduce, cooperate, compete, and survive a world that is relentlessly trying to kill it. The solutions evolution has found are breathtakingly sophisticated. The cell is not a passive chemical bag. It is an agent.*

This unit makes the case that the eukaryotic cell — and indeed even the prokaryotic cell — possesses the core architecture of cognition. It can sense, integrate information, make decisions, learn, remember, and act. Understanding cellular cognition is not merely a curiosity; it is the foundation upon which all animal, plant, and fungal intelligence is built.

---

# EPILOGUE: The Strangeness of Life

*We began with a slime mold solving a maze. We end with something much larger: the recognition that the boundaries we drew between life and mind, between intelligence and mere chemistry, between cognitive agents and non-cognitive matter, were not discoveries about nature. They were assumptions about ourselves.*

*Basal cognition is not a minor amendment to our picture of life. It is a re-centering. The neuron did not invent intelligence. It specialized it. The brain did not create mind. It concentrated it. And the great, slow, patient problem-solving that has been underway in every living thing since the Archean — that is the original mind, the ground of which we are a late and perhaps temporary elaboration.*

*The field is young. The questions are old. The work begins.*

## Appendix C: Glossary of Key Terms

**Active inference** — The FEP framework's account of action: acting to minimize prediction error / surprise.
**Allostasis** — Predictive physiological regulation; maintaining stability by anticipating change.
**Autopoiesis** — Self-production; the defining organizational property of living systems (Maturana & Varela).
**Basal cognition** — Cognitive-like capacities (sensing, integrating, acting, learning, anticipating) in organisms lacking nervous systems or in the non-neural components of nervous organisms.
**Bioelectricity** — Electrical potentials and currents generated by ion gradients across cell membranes, serving as an ancient information-processing medium.
**Cognitive light cone** — Levin's term for the spatial and temporal scale over which an agent's competencies are integrated.
**Enactivism** — The view that cognition is constituted by the dynamic coupling of organism and environment, not internal representation alone.
**Free energy principle** — Friston's unifying principle: all self-organizing biological systems act to minimize variational free energy (a proxy for surprise).
**Habituation** — A decrease in response to a repeated, inconsequential stimulus; the simplest form of non-associative learning.
**Homeostasis** — The maintenance of internal variables within tolerable ranges via feedback control.
**Minimal cognition** — The least complex system that satisfies criteria for genuine cognitive agency.
**Morphogenetic field** — A region of developing tissue in which cells respond to positional information to adopt specific fates.
**Quorum sensing** — Bacterial detection of population density via accumulation of signaling molecules, enabling coordinated collective behavior.
**Stigmergy** — Indirect coordination of behavior through environment modification.
**Xenobot** — A synthetic living organism built from frog stem cells, designed computationally and exhibiting novel behaviors.
