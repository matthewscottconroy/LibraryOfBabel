# Appendix A: Key Researchers in Basal Cognition

This directory introduces the major figures whose work underlies the arguments of this book. It is not exhaustive — the field draws on contributions from biology, philosophy, computer science, ecology, and chemistry, among others — but it represents the intellectual lineage that has shaped basal cognition as a coherent research program. Entries are organized by research area, and key publications are limited to works the reader can consult with confidence.

---

## Founding Theorists

These researchers established the conceptual foundations on which basal cognition research stands: the ideas of self-organization, goal-directedness, feedback, and the deep continuity between life and mind.

---

**Humberto Maturana** (1928–2021)
*University of Chile, Santiago*

Maturana was a biologist and philosopher whose work on visual perception in frogs led him to a radical reconception of what cognition is. With Francisco Varela, he developed autopoiesis theory — the idea that living systems are defined by their capacity to continuously produce and maintain their own organization. This reframing made cognition not a special faculty of complex nervous systems but a property of living organization as such. Maturana's insistence that knowing is always knowing-from-a-perspective, grounded in the biology of the knower, remains one of the most consequential ideas in the cognitive sciences.

Key publications:
- Maturana, H. R., & Varela, F. J. (1980). *Autopoiesis and Cognition: The Realization of the Living*. D. Reidel.
- Maturana, H. R., & Varela, F. J. (1987). *The Tree of Knowledge: The Biological Roots of Human Understanding*. Shambhala.
- Maturana, H. R. (1970). Biology of cognition. *Biological Computer Laboratory Research Report* BCL 9.0. University of Illinois.

---

**Francisco Varela** (1946–2001)
*École Polytechnique / CNRS, Paris*

Varela began as Maturana's student and collaborator, then developed his own distinctive trajectory that brought autopoiesis theory into dialogue with phenomenology, immunology, and neuroscience. His concept of enactivism — the view that cognition arises through the active engagement of an organism with its environment, not through passive representation — has shaped two generations of researchers. His work on the immune system as a cognitive system (rather than merely a defense system) prefigures much of contemporary basal cognition research.

Key publications:
- Varela, F. J., Thompson, E., & Rosch, E. (1991). *The Embodied Mind: Cognitive Science and Human Experience*. MIT Press.
- Varela, F. J., Maturana, H. R., & Uribe, R. (1974). Autopoiesis: The organization of living systems, its characterization and a model. *Biosystems*, 5(4), 187–196.
- Varela, F. J. (1979). *Principles of Biological Autonomy*. North Holland.

---

**Norbert Wiener** (1894–1964)
*Massachusetts Institute of Technology*

Wiener was a mathematician whose wartime work on anti-aircraft targeting systems led him to see a deep analogy between goal-directed machines and goal-directed organisms. His formalization of cybernetics — the science of feedback, control, and communication in animals and machines — provided the first rigorous framework for thinking about goal-directedness without invoking purpose in any metaphysically loaded sense. The concept of negative feedback as the mechanism of homeostatic regulation remains central to how basal cognition researchers understand sensing and response.

Key publications:
- Wiener, N. (1948). *Cybernetics: Or Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine*. MIT Press.
- Wiener, N. (1950). *The Human Use of Human Beings: Cybernetics and Society*. Houghton Mifflin.
- Rosenblueth, A., Wiener, N., & Bigelow, J. (1943). Behavior, purpose and teleology. *Philosophy of Science*, 10(1), 18–24.

---

**Lynn Margulis** (1938–2011)
*University of Massachusetts Amherst*

Margulis was among the most consequential evolutionary biologists of the twentieth century, and among the most controversial. Her theory of symbiogenesis — that eukaryotic cells arose through the endosymbiotic merger of distinct bacterial lineages — was rejected for years before becoming textbook consensus. Her broader argument that symbiosis, not competition, is the primary driver of evolutionary novelty has profound implications for basal cognition: it suggests that even the cell's basic cognitive architecture is the product of inter-organism cooperation. Margulis also collaborated with Dorion Sagan on work extending autopoiesis theory to evolutionary biology.

Key publications:
- Margulis, L. (1981). *Symbiosis in Cell Evolution: Life and Its Environment on the Early Earth*. W.H. Freeman.
- Margulis, L. (1967). On the origin of mitosing cells. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*, 14(3), 255–274.
- Margulis, L., & Sagan, D. (1995). *What Is Life?* Simon & Schuster.

## Minimal Cognition Theorists

These researchers have worked to give basal cognition theoretical precision — defining the minimum conditions for cognition, developing formal frameworks for studying it, and distinguishing what can be said rigorously from what cannot.

---

**Pamela Lyon**
*University of Adelaide*

Lyon is the primary architect of the biogenic approach to cognition — the position that cognition is a biological phenomenon that evolved with life itself, not a psychological phenomenon that evolved with nervous systems. Her work rigorously examines what bacteria and other simple organisms actually do and argues that it meets reasonable criteria for cognition: they sense, integrate information, and act in ways that are context-sensitive and adaptive. Lyon's scholarship is notable for its philosophical care; she is as attentive to what cannot be claimed as to what can.

Key publications:
- Lyon, P. (2015). The cognitive cell: Bacterial behavior reconsidered. *Frontiers in Microbiology*, 6, 264.
- Lyon, P. (2006). The biogenic approach to cognition. *Cognitive Processing*, 7(1), 11–29.
- Lyon, P. (2021). Of what is "minimal cognition" the half-baked version? *Adaptive Behavior*, 29(4), 407–424.

---

**Xabier Barandiaran**
*University of the Basque Country (UPV/EHU)*

Barandiaran works at the intersection of theoretical biology, cognitive science, and philosophy. With Álvaro Moreno, he developed the Minimally Cognitive Organization (MCO) framework — a formal account of what organizational conditions are necessary and sufficient for an entity to be cognitive in the minimal sense. This framework distinguishes cognition from mere stimulus-response by requiring a system to have its own internal norms against which it evaluates environmental states.

Key publications:
- Barandiaran, X. E., & Moreno, A. (2008). Adaptivity: From metabolism to behavior. *Adaptive Behavior*, 16(5), 325–344.
- Barandiaran, X. E., Di Paolo, E., & Rohde, M. (2009). Defining agency: Individuality, normativity, asymmetry, and spatio-temporality in action. *Adaptive Behavior*, 17(5), 367–386.
- Barandiaran, X. E. (2017). Autonomy and enactivism: Towards a theory of the living cognitive and normative subject. *Topoi*, 36(3), 409–430.

---

**Álvaro Moreno**
*University of the Basque Country (UPV/EHU)*

Moreno has developed a research program focused on the emergence of biological autonomy and its relationship to minimal cognition. His work asks what the minimal organizational conditions are for a system to act on its own behalf — and how those conditions give rise to something like normativity (the capacity to distinguish states as better or worse for the system). This philosophical biology underpins much of the theoretical architecture of Units I and IX.

Key publications:
- Moreno, A., & Mossio, M. (2015). *Biological Autonomy: A Philosophical and Theoretical Enquiry*. Springer.
- Barandiaran, X. E., & Moreno, A. (2008). Adaptivity: From metabolism to behavior. *Adaptive Behavior*, 16(5), 325–344.
- Moreno, A., & Ruiz-Mirazo, K. (2009). The problem of the emergence of functional diversity in prebiotic evolution. *Biology and Philosophy*, 24(5), 585–605.

## Bioelectricity and Developmental Cognition

---

**Michael Levin**
*Tufts University (Allen Discovery Center)*

Levin is one of the most prolific and original figures in contemporary basal cognition research. His laboratory investigates the role of bioelectric signaling — electrical gradients and ion flows — in directing the development of body plans, regeneration, and what he calls morphogenetic cognition. He has proposed the "cognitive light cone" framework, which argues that cognitive agency scales with an organism's capacity to integrate information across space and time, making cognition a gradient property rather than a binary one. His xenobot work (with colleagues at UVM and Tufts) created the first computer-designed organisms from frog cells.

Key publications:
- Levin, M. (2019). The computational boundary of a "self": Developmental bioelectricity drives multicellularity and scale-free cognition. *Frontiers in Psychology*, 10, 2688.
- Levin, M. (2021). Bioelectric signaling: Reprogrammable circuits underlying embryogenesis, regeneration, and cancer. *Cell*, 184(8), 1971–1989.
- Shomrat, T., & Levin, M. (2013). An automated training paradigm reveals long-term memory in planaria and its persistence through head regeneration. *Journal of Experimental Biology*, 216(20), 3799–3810.

## Bacterial Cognition

---

**Bonnie Bassler**
*Princeton University*

Bassler is the world's leading researcher on quorum sensing — the chemical signaling system by which bacteria assess population density and coordinate collective behavior. Her laboratory discovered autoinducer-2, a universal quorum sensing molecule used across bacterial species, and her work has transformed understanding of bacterial sociality. Bassler is also an exceptional communicator of science; her public lectures on bacterial communication have done more than perhaps any other single source to bring bacterial cognition to general audiences.

Key publications:
- Bassler, B. L., & Losick, R. (2006). Bacterially speaking. *Cell*, 125(2), 237–246.
- Rutherford, S. T., & Bassler, B. L. (2012). Bacterial quorum sensing: Its role in virulence and possibilities for its control. *Cold Spring Harbor Perspectives in Medicine*, 2(11), a012427.
- Waters, C. M., & Bassler, B. L. (2005). Quorum sensing: Cell-to-cell communication in bacteria. *Annual Review of Cell and Developmental Biology*, 21, 319–346.

---

**Jordi Garcia-Ojalvo**
*Universitat Pompeu Fabra, Barcelona*

Garcia-Ojalvo uses mathematical and computational approaches to study how bacterial populations make collective decisions, particularly in the context of sporulation and biofilm formation. His work on *Bacillus subtilis* has illuminated how biochemical circuits implement something functionally analogous to neural computation — including noise-driven switching, bistability, and population-level averaging.

Key publications:
- Garcia-Ojalvo, J., Elowitz, M. B., & Strogatz, S. H. (2004). Modeling a synthetic multicellular clock: Repressilators coupled by quorum sensing. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 101(30), 10955–10960.
- Kuchina, A., Brettner, L. M., Paleologu, L., Roco, C. M., Brownstein, A. J., Starostina, E., ... & Garcia-Ojalvo, J. (2021). Microbial single-cell RNA sequencing by split-pool barcoding. *Science*, 371(6531), eaba5257.

---

**Jeff Hasty**
*University of California, San Diego*

Hasty's laboratory builds synthetic genetic circuits in bacteria to test hypotheses about how living systems can encode and transmit information. His work on synchronized oscillators and population-level computation demonstrates the physical plausibility of bacterial information processing.

Key publications:
- Danino, T., Mondragón-Palomino, O., Tsimring, L., & Hasty, J. (2010). A synchronized quorum of genetic clocks. *Nature*, 463(7279), 326–330.
- Prindle, A., Liu, J., Asally, M., Ly, S., Garcia-Ojalvo, J., & Süel, G. M. (2015). Ion channels enable electrical communication in bacterial communities. *Nature*, 527(7576), 59–63.

## Slime Mold and Protist Intelligence

---

**Toshiyuki Nakagaki**
*Hokkaido University*

Nakagaki's 2000 paper in *Nature* — demonstrating that *Physarum polycephalum* can solve a maze by finding the shortest path between food sources — is the single most cited result in the basal cognition literature. It provided a clean, reproducible demonstration that problem-solving capacity does not require a nervous system. Nakagaki's subsequent work has examined the mechanisms underlying this behavior, including the role of oscillatory cytoplasmic streaming.

Key publications:
- Nakagaki, T., Yamada, H., & Tóth, Á. (2000). Maze-solving by an amoeboid organism. *Nature*, 407(6803), 470.
- Tero, A., Takagi, S., Saigusa, T., Ito, K., Bebber, D. P., Fricker, M. D., ... & Nakagaki, T. (2010). Rules for biologically inspired adaptive network design. *Science*, 327(5964), 439–442.
- Nakagaki, T., Yamada, H., & Hara, M. (2004). Smart network solutions in an amoeboid organism. *Biophysical Chemistry*, 107(1), 1–5.

---

**Andrew Adamatzky**
*University of the West of England, Bristol (Unconventional Computing Laboratory)*

Adamatzky has built a research program around the computational capacities of unconventional substrates — most notably *Physarum polycephalum* and, more recently, fungal mycelial networks. His work demonstrates that biological networks can implement logical operations, shortest-path finding, and other computational tasks without any central processor. He is also the most productive single author in the field, having published extensively on both the theory and practical applications of biological computing.

Key publications:
- Adamatzky, A. (2010). *Physarum Machines: Computers from Slime Mould*. World Scientific.
- Adamatzky, A. (Ed.). (2016). *Advances in Physarum Machines: Sensing and Computing with Slime Mould*. Springer.
- Adamatzky, A. (2022). Towards fungal computer. *Interface Focus*, 8(6), 20180029.

---

**Audrey Dussutour**
*CNRS, Centre de Recherches sur la Cognition Animale, Toulouse*

Dussutour's research has focused on the behavioral capacities of *Physarum polycephalum*, with particular attention to learning. Her 2016 paper demonstrating that Physarum can be habituated to an aversive stimulus — and that this habituation can be transferred to a naive organism through cell fusion — is among the most striking demonstrations of memory without neurons.

Key publications:
- Boisseau, R. P., Vogel, D., & Dussutour, A. (2016). Habituation in non-neural organisms: Evidence from slime moulds. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B: Biological Sciences*, 283(1829), 20160446.
- Dussutour, A., Latty, T., Beekman, M., & Simpson, S. J. (2010). Amoeboid organism solves complex nutritional challenges. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 107(10), 4607–4611.

## Plant Cognition

---

**František Baluška**
*University of Bonn*

Baluška is one of the most prolific advocates for what he calls "plant neurobiology" — the study of plant behavior, signaling, and information processing using concepts and methods drawn from neuroscience. He has documented the role of synaptic-like vesicle trafficking in root apex cells and argues that root apices function as a kind of primitive brain. His work is controversial but has helped push the field toward greater empirical specificity.

Key publications:
- Baluška, F., Mancuso, S., Volkmann, D., & Barlow, P. W. (2010). Root apex transition zone: A signalling–response nexus in the root. *Trends in Plant Science*, 15(7), 402–408.
- Baluška, F., & Mancuso, S. (Eds.). (2009). *Plant-Environment Interactions: From Sensory Plant Biology to Active Plant Behavior*. Springer.
- Baluška, F., & Levin, M. (2016). On having no head: Cognition throughout biological systems. *Frontiers in Psychology*, 7, 902.

---

**Stefano Mancuso**
*University of Florence (International Laboratory of Plant Neurobiology, LINV)*

Mancuso directs the International Laboratory of Plant Neurobiology in Florence and is the leading public intellectual of the plant cognition movement. His research covers plant electrical signaling, root navigation, and social behavior between plants. He has also collaborated extensively with Baluška on the theoretical foundations of plant neurobiology.

Key publications:
- Mancuso, S., & Viola, A. (2015). *Brilliant Green: The Surprising History and Science of Plant Intelligence*. Island Press.
- Mancuso, S. (2018). *The Revolutionary Genius of Plants: A New Understanding of Plant Intelligence and Behavior*. Atria Books.
- Gagliano, M., Mancuso, S., & Robert, D. (2012). Towards understanding plant bioacoustics. *Trends in Plant Science*, 17(6), 323–325.

---

**Monica Gagliano**
*University of Southern Denmark (formerly University of Western Australia)*

Gagliano's work on plant behavior has focused on associative learning, sound production, and habituation. Her 2014 paper reporting that *Mimosa pudica* can be habituated to a drop stimulus — learning to distinguish a harmless perturbation from a harmful one — generated significant debate and continues to be replicated and contested. Her broader theoretical contributions argue for plants as genuine cognitive agents.

Key publications:
- Gagliano, M., Renton, M., Depczynski, M., & Mancuso, S. (2014). Experience teaches plants to learn faster and forget slower in environments where it matters. *Oecologia*, 175(1), 63–72.
- Gagliano, M., Vyazovskiy, V. V., Borbély, A. A., Grimonprez, M., & Depczynski, M. (2016). Learning by association in plants. *Scientific Reports*, 6, 38427.
- Gagliano, M. (2017). The mind of plants: Thinking the unthinkable. *Communicative & Integrative Biology*, 10(2), e1288333.

---

**Richard Karban**
*University of California, Davis*

Karban is an ecologist who has spent decades studying how plants perceive and respond to their environments — particularly the chemical signals by which plants communicate threat information to one another and to insects. His work is more conservative in its interpretive claims than Baluška or Mancuso but no less consequential: he has provided some of the most rigorous empirical evidence that plants integrate information from multiple sources and adjust behavior accordingly.

Key publications:
- Karban, R. (2015). *Plant Sensing and Communication*. University of Chicago Press.
- Karban, R., Baldwin, I. T., Baxter, K. J., Laue, G., & Felton, G. W. (2000). Communication between plants: Induced resistance in wild tobacco plants following clipping of neighboring sagebrush. *Oecologia*, 125(1), 66–71.
- Karban, R., & Baldwin, I. T. (1997). *Induced Responses to Herbivory*. University of Chicago Press.

## Fungal Intelligence

---

**Toby Kiers**
*VU Amsterdam (Vrije Universiteit)*

Kiers studies mycorrhizal networks as economic systems — asking how fungi and plant roots allocate resources, enforce fair exchange, and respond to changing market conditions. Her work provides some of the clearest evidence that fungal networks engage in something like decision-making at the network level, responding to local supply and demand in ways that maximize overall network efficiency.

Key publications:
- Kiers, E. T., Duhamel, M., Beesetty, Y., Mensah, J. A., Franken, O., Verbruggen, E., ... & Bücking, H. (2011). Reciprocal rewards stabilize cooperation in the mycorrhizal symbiosis. *Science*, 333(6044), 880–882.
- Kiers, E. T., & West, S. A. (2015). Evolving new organisms via symbiosis. *Science*, 348(6233), 392–394.

---

**Merlin Sheldrake**
*Kew Gardens / Independent researcher (Cambridge PhD)*

Sheldrake's doctoral work at Cambridge focused on mycorrhizal networks and their role in forest communication. His popular book *Entangled Life* brought the science of fungal cognition to a wide audience without sacrificing accuracy. Though primarily a public intellectual rather than a laboratory researcher, his synthesis of the field remains one of the most useful entry points for students.

Key publications:
- Sheldrake, M. (2020). *Entangled Life: How Fungi Make Our Worlds, Change Our Minds and Shape Our Futures*. Random House.
- Sheldrake, M., Rosenstock, N. P., Revillini, D., Meiners, S. J., Murray Groom, P., Leake, J. R., ... & Wiemken, A. (2017). Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungal community composition is altered by long-term litter manipulation in a temperate hardwood forest. *New Phytologist*, 214(1), 401–411.

---

**Mark Fricker**
*University of Oxford*

Fricker has developed mathematical and network-theoretic approaches to analyzing fungal mycelial architecture. His work asks how the topology of fungal networks relates to their functional properties — robustness, efficiency, transport capacity — and provides a quantitative framework for comparing fungal networks to other biological and artificial networks.

Key publications:
- Fricker, M. D., Heaton, L. L., Jones, N. S., & Boddy, L. (2017). The mycelium as a network. *Microbiology Spectrum*, 5(3).
- Heaton, L., Obara, B., Grau, V., Jones, N., Nakagaki, T., Boddy, L., & Fricker, M. D. (2012). Analysis of fungal networks. *Fungal Biology Reviews*, 26(1), 12–29.

## Collective Intelligence

---

**Deborah Gordon**
*Stanford University*

Gordon has spent decades studying harvester ant colonies in the Arizona desert, asking how the colony as a whole behaves intelligently without any individual ant having a global view of the colony's state. Her work has shown that colonies regulate foraging activity through local chemical interactions and that colony-level "personality" — consistent differences in risk-taking and exploration — persists across the lifetime of a colony.

Key publications:
- Gordon, D. M. (2010). *Ant Encounters: Interaction Networks and Colony Behavior*. Princeton University Press.
- Gordon, D. M. (1999). *Ants at Work: How an Insect Society Is Organized*. Free Press.
- Gordon, D. M. (2016). From division of labor to the collective behavior of social insects. *Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology*, 70(7), 1101–1108.

---

**Thomas Seeley**
*Cornell University*

Seeley is the world's leading authority on honeybee collective decision-making. His research on swarm intelligence — particularly how swarms of bees choose a new home through a process that resembles deliberative voting — has become a touchstone for understanding distributed cognition. His concept of the "bee brain" as distributed across the swarm rather than located in any individual bee is central to Unit VII.

Key publications:
- Seeley, T. D. (2010). *Honeybee Democracy*. Princeton University Press.
- Seeley, T. D. (1995). *The Wisdom of the Hive: The Social Physiology of Honey Bee Colonies*. Harvard University Press.
- Seeley, T. D., Visscher, P. K., Schlegel, T., Hogan, P. M., Franks, N. R., & Marshall, J. A. (2012). Stop signals provide cross inhibition in collective decision-making by honeybee swarms. *Science*, 335(6064), 108–111.

---

**Simon Garnier**
*New Jersey Institute of Technology*

Garnier studies swarm intelligence in ants, locusts, and other social insects, with a focus on the algorithmic principles underlying collective behavior. His work bridges biology and robotics, using swarm principles to design self-organizing robotic systems.

Key publications:
- Garnier, S., Gautrais, J., & Theraulaz, G. (2007). The biological principles of swarm intelligence. *Swarm Intelligence*, 1(1), 3–31.
- Moussaïd, M., Helbing, D., & Theraulaz, G. (2011). How simple rules determine pedestrian behavior and crowd disasters. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 108(17), 6884–6888.

## Theoretical Frameworks

---

**Karl Friston**
*University College London (Wellcome Centre for Human Neuroimaging)*

Friston is the originator of the Free Energy Principle — a mathematical framework proposing that all living systems minimize their variational free energy (a measure of prediction error), and that this principle unifies perception, action, and learning under a single account. When extended to all life rather than just brains, the FEP becomes a candidate theory of basal cognition: any autopoietic system, on this view, is engaged in a form of inference. The principle is abstract and contested, but has become one of the most generative theoretical frameworks in the field.

Key publications:
- Friston, K. (2013). Life as we know it. *Journal of the Royal Society Interface*, 10(86), 20130475.
- Friston, K. J., Wiese, W., & Hobson, J. A. (2016). Sentience and the origins of consciousness: From cartesian duality to Markovian monism. *Entropy*, 22(5), 516.
- Friston, K. (2010). The free-energy principle: A unified brain theory? *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, 11(2), 127–138.

---

**Giulio Tononi**
*University of Wisconsin-Madison*

Tononi developed Integrated Information Theory (IIT) — the proposal that consciousness is identical to integrated information (Φ, phi), and that any system with nonzero Φ has some degree of experience. IIT is controversial (some critics argue it attributes experience too liberally; others that it cannot be tested), but it is one of the few theories of consciousness that takes the possibility of nonzero experience in simple systems seriously, and as such it intersects importantly with basal cognition.

Key publications:
- Tononi, G. (2004). An information integration theory of consciousness. *BMC Neuroscience*, 5, 42.
- Tononi, G., Boly, M., Massimini, M., & Koch, C. (2016). Integrated information theory: From consciousness to its physical substrate. *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, 17(7), 450–461.
- Tononi, G. (2012). *Phi: A Voyage from the Brain to the Soul*. Pantheon.

---

**Philip Goff**
*Durham University*

Goff is a philosopher who defends panpsychism — the view that consciousness or experience is a fundamental and ubiquitous feature of reality, not a product of complex neural processing. His work engages rigorously with the science of consciousness and provides the most careful philosophical defense of panpsychism currently available. The book maintains principled agnosticism with respect to his conclusions, but engages seriously with his arguments in Unit XII.

Key publications:
- Goff, P. (2019). *Galileo's Error: Foundations for a New Science of Mind*. Pantheon.
- Goff, P., Seager, W., & Allen-Hermanson, S. (2022). Panpsychism. *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*.
- Goff, P. (2017). *Consciousness and Fundamental Reality*. Oxford University Press.

## Evolutionary Biology of Mind

---

**Peter Godfrey-Smith**
*University of Sydney*

Godfrey-Smith is a philosopher of biology whose work has become indispensable to basal cognition research. His treatment of the evolution of minds — from the simplest reactive organisms to cephalopods to humans — models the kind of principled functional analysis that avoids both overclaiming and underclaiming. His book on cephalopods has been praised as much by neuroscientists as by philosophers for its careful, empirically grounded approach to questions of animal experience.

Key publications:
- Godfrey-Smith, P. (2016). *Other Minds: The Octopus, the Sea, and the Deep Origins of Consciousness*. Farrar, Straus and Giroux.
- Godfrey-Smith, P. (2014). *Philosophy of Biology*. Princeton University Press.
- Godfrey-Smith, P. (2001). Three kinds of intentionality. *Pacific Philosophical Quarterly*, 82(3–4), 326–351.

---

**Eva Jablonka**
*Tel Aviv University*

Jablonka, with Marion Lamb, has developed a comprehensive account of evolution that goes beyond the standard genetic picture to incorporate epigenetic, behavioral, and symbolic inheritance systems. Her work argues that evolution proceeds through multiple channels of information inheritance, not just genetic change, and that this has profound implications for how we understand the evolution of cognitive capacities.

Key publications:
- Jablonka, E., & Lamb, M. J. (2005). *Evolution in Four Dimensions: Genetic, Epigenetic, Behavioral, and Symbolic Variation in the History of Life*. MIT Press.
- Jablonka, E., & Lamb, M. J. (2007). Précis of evolution in four dimensions. *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, 30(4), 353–365.
- Jablonka, E., & Raz, G. (2009). Transgenerational epigenetic inheritance: Prevalence, mechanisms, and implications for the study of heredity and evolution. *The Quarterly Review of Biology*, 84(2), 131–176.

---

**Leonid (Léon) Moroz**
*Whitney Laboratory for Marine Bioscience, University of Florida*

Moroz has pursued the radical hypothesis that nervous systems evolved independently multiple times — not just once in the ancestor of all bilaterians. His comparative genomic work on ctenophores (comb jellies) has produced evidence that their neural architectures are built from different molecular components than those of other animals, suggesting convergent evolution of neural function. This research has profound implications for basal cognition: it suggests that the neural solution to cognition is not the only solution, but one among several.

Key publications:
- Moroz, L. L., Kocot, K. M., Citarella, M. R., Dosung, S., Norekian, T. P., Bhatt, D. K., ... & Halanych, K. M. (2014). The ctenophore genome and the evolutionary origins of neural systems. *Nature*, 510(7503), 109–114.
- Moroz, L. L. (2012). Phylogenomics meets neuroscience: How many times might complex brains have evolved? *Acta Biologica Hungarica*, 63(Supplement 2), 3–19.
- Moroz, L. L., & Kohn, A. B. (2016). Independent origins of neurons and synapses: Insights from ctenophores. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B*, 371(1685), 20150041.
