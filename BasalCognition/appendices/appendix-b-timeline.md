# Appendix B: A Timeline of Basal Cognition Research

The history of basal cognition research is not a straight line. It moves in waves — a discovery about bacterial behavior here, a philosophical reframing there, a technological breakthrough that opens an entirely new window on what life can do. What follows is a selective timeline of the milestones that have shaped the field. It is not meant to be exhaustive, but to give the reader a sense of how these ideas have accumulated: slowly at first, then with gathering speed as the tools of molecular biology, imaging technology, and computational modeling have made it possible to see what was always there but invisible.

Entries marked with an asterisk (*) indicate findings that were initially controversial and have since been substantially, though not always completely, replicated or confirmed.

---

| Year | Event |
|------|-------|
| **1865** | Claude Bernard establishes the concept of the *milieu intérieur* — the idea that living organisms actively maintain their internal environment within stable limits. This is the first systematic statement of what will later be called homeostasis, and it places active self-regulation at the center of what it means to be alive. Bernard's framing anticipates by a century the idea that even simple organisms are engaged in ongoing, active management of their internal state. |
| **1866** | Ernst Haeckel coins the term *Ökologie* (ecology) and begins his taxonomic work on protists and other microscopic organisms. His meticulously detailed drawings of radiolarians, though sometimes idealized, introduce the scientific world to the astonishing behavioral and morphological complexity of single-celled life. |
| **1873** | Élie Metchnikoff describes the movement of amoeboid cells toward foreign particles — behavior he interprets as purposive and goal-directed. His later work on phagocytosis (for which he shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1908) makes the immune cell the first clear case in biology where a single cell is recognized as an agent performing a function. |
| **1884** | Wilhelm Pfeffer systematically documents chemotaxis in bacteria — the capacity to move directionally in response to chemical gradients. Pfeffer's careful experiments establish that bacteria are not simply buffeted by their chemical environment but actively navigate it. This is the founding observation of what we now call bacterial sensing. |
| **1906** | Herbert Spencer Jennings publishes *Behavior of the Lower Organisms*, the first systematic, book-length study of behavior in protozoa and other simple organisms. Jennings argues that the trial-and-error behavior of paramecia is genuinely behavioral in the relevant sense, and that drawing a sharp line between this and the behavior of higher animals is philosophically unwarranted. |
| **1932** | Walter Cannon coins the term "homeostasis" in *The Wisdom of the Body*, formalizing Bernard's earlier observations into a general physiological principle. Cannon also identifies the role of the autonomic nervous system in maintaining homeostatic regulation — a framing that, inverted, will later prompt the question of how organisms without nervous systems achieve the same result. |
| **1943** | Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts publish "A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity" in the *Bulletin of Mathematical Biophysics*. Their formal model of the neuron as a threshold logic unit launches the field of neural computation and, ultimately, modern artificial intelligence. The paper also implicitly raises the question that basal cognition will later pose explicitly: if neurons are logical units, are other biological signal-processing systems also logical in some sense? |
| **1948** | Norbert Wiener publishes *Cybernetics: Or Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine* (MIT Press), founding the science of feedback, control, and goal-directedness in biological and mechanical systems. Cybernetics provides the first rigorous, non-vitalist account of purposive behavior — establishing that goal-directedness is a property of organizational structure, not of special biological substance. |
| **1953** | James Watson and Francis Crick describe the double-helical structure of DNA in *Nature*, establishing the molecular basis of heredity. The discovery eventually makes it possible to read the informational content of genomes, compare organisms across the tree of life, and reconstruct the evolutionary history of cognitive capacities. |
| **1959** | Julius Adler begins his systematic, decades-long program of research into bacterial chemotaxis at the University of Wisconsin. Adler's work will produce the definitive account of how *E. coli* senses chemical gradients and modulates flagellar rotation — establishing a molecular mechanism for what Pfeffer had observed phenomenologically in 1884. |
| **1961** | François Jacob and Jacques Monod publish their account of the lac operon in the *Journal of Molecular Biology*, describing the first known molecular mechanism of gene regulation. The lac operon is a biological decision-making system: it reads an environmental signal (the presence of lactose), evaluates it against an internal state (the availability of glucose), and produces a regulated output (the expression of lactose-metabolizing enzymes). Jacob and Monod share the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1965. |
| **1972** | Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela introduce the concept of autopoiesis in *De Máquinas y Seres Vivos* (Universitaria, Santiago), arguing that living systems are defined by their capacity to continuously produce and maintain their own organization. Autopoiesis theory will provide the theoretical backbone for the biogenic approach to cognition: if cognition is the exercise of a living system's own organization, then every living system is, in some sense, cognitive. |
| **1973** | Kenneth Nealson, Terry Platt, and J. Woodland Hastings publish a study of bioluminescence in *Vibrio fischeri* (in the *Journal of Bacteriology*), describing the phenomenon that will later be named quorum sensing: bacteria produce light only when their population density reaches a threshold, as if the bacterial community is collectively monitoring its own size and switching behaviors accordingly. |
| **1980** | Maturana and Varela publish *Autopoiesis and Cognition: The Realization of the Living* (D. Reidel), the full theoretical treatment of their framework. The book argues that cognition is the activity of an autopoietic system — that living and knowing are two aspects of the same organizational process. |
| **1981** | Lynn Margulis publishes *Symbiosis in Cell Evolution: Life and Its Environment on the Early Earth* (W.H. Freeman), the definitive presentation of her theory of symbiogenesis — that the eukaryotic cell arose through the endosymbiotic merger of distinct bacterial lineages. Margulis's account fundamentally changes the picture of evolution: cooperation, not only competition, drives the emergence of biological complexity. |
| **1983** | David Rhoades and Gordon Baldwin independently report evidence that trees can release volatile chemicals that prime neighboring trees against herbivore attack — a finding widely described in the popular press as trees "talking" to one another.* The claims were initially met with considerable skepticism and the mechanisms remain debated, but subsequent decades of research on plant volatile signaling have established that inter-plant chemical communication is a genuine phenomenon. |
| **1986** | John White and colleagues publish the complete wiring diagram of the nervous system of *Caenorhabditis elegans* — 302 neurons and approximately 7,000 synaptic connections — in *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B*. The *C. elegans* connectome remains the only complete nervous system map for any organism and provides a benchmark for understanding minimal neural computation. |
| **1991** | Francisco Varela, Evan Thompson, and Eleanor Rosch publish *The Embodied Mind: Cognitive Science and Human Experience* (MIT Press), a landmark synthesis of cognitive science and phenomenology. The book argues that cognition is not computation on internal representations but the ongoing self-organization of an organism coupled to its environment — enactivism. |
| **1994** | E. Peter Greenberg, Clay Fuqua, and Stephen Winans publish the paper coining the term "quorum sensing" in the *Journal of Bacteriology*, giving a name and a framework to the phenomenon Nealson et al. had described in 1973. The term captures the collective decision-making quality of the behavior: bacteria appear to take a vote on their own population density before changing their behavior. |
| **1997** | Suzanne Simard and colleagues publish a study in *Nature* demonstrating that carbon passes between Douglas fir and paper birch trees through underground mycorrhizal networks — the first direct evidence that mycorrhizal networks function as transport systems between plants, not merely between plants and their fungal partners. The paper launches the concept of the "wood wide web" into public consciousness. |
| **1998** | Andy Clark and David Chalmers publish "The Extended Mind" in *Analysis*, arguing that cognitive processes can extend beyond the boundary of the skin when external resources play the right functional role. The paper becomes one of the most cited in philosophy of mind and provides a theoretical framework for understanding collective and distributed cognition in biological systems. |
| **2000** | Toshiyuki Nakagaki, Hiroyasu Yamada, and Ágota Tóth publish "Maze-solving by an amoeboid organism" in *Nature*, demonstrating that *Physarum polycephalum* can find the shortest path through a maze. The single-page paper becomes the founding demonstration of basal cognition research: a system with no neurons, no brain, and no learning in any familiar sense solves a spatial optimization problem. |
| **2002** | Bonnie Bassler and Richard Losick publish "Bacterially Speaking" in *Cell*, a review article that brings the molecular details of quorum sensing to a wide biological audience and argues explicitly that bacterial populations communicate and make collective decisions. |
| **2006** | Pamela Lyon publishes "The Biogenic Approach to Cognition" in *Cognitive Processing*, arguing for a systematic rethinking of cognition that takes bacteria as the paradigm case rather than the borderline case. The paper provides the clearest early statement of what will become the field's theoretical consensus. |
| **2008** | Xabier Barandiaran and Álvaro Moreno publish "Adaptivity: From Metabolism to Behavior" in *Adaptive Behavior*, presenting the Minimally Cognitive Organization (MCO) framework — a formal account of the minimum organizational conditions for cognition. |
| **2010** | Thomas Seeley publishes *Honeybee Democracy* (Princeton University Press), the definitive account of how honeybee swarms choose a new home through a process of distributed deliberation that functionally resembles voting and debate. |
| **2010** | Atsushi Tero, Ryo Kobayashi, and Toshiyuki Nakagaki (with colleagues) publish "Rules for Biologically Inspired Adaptive Network Design" in *Science*, showing that *Physarum* builds networks that approach the efficiency and robustness of human-engineered railway systems. |
| **2013** | Tomer Shomrat and Michael Levin publish "An Automated Training Paradigm Reveals Long-Term Memory in Planaria and Its Persistence Through Head Regeneration" in the *Journal of Experimental Biology*, demonstrating that flatworms trained before decapitation retain behavioral memory after head regeneration — suggesting that memory can be stored in tissue outside the brain. |
| **2013** | Karl Friston publishes "Life as We Know It" in the *Journal of the Royal Society Interface*, extending the Free Energy Principle to all living systems — arguing that any organism capable of persisting through time must be engaged in a form of active inference. |
| **2014** | Monica Gagliano and colleagues publish "Experience Teaches Plants to Learn Faster and Forget Slower in Environments Where It Matters" in *Oecologia*, reporting that *Mimosa pudica* can be habituated to repeated harmless stimulation — a form of learning in the absence of any neural tissue.* The findings have been partially replicated and continue to be investigated. |
| **2015** | Pamela Lyon publishes "The Cognitive Cell: Bacterial Behavior Reconsidered" in *Frontiers in Microbiology*, the most comprehensive statement of her biogenic approach and its application to bacterial behavior. |
| **2015** | Arthur Prindle, Jintao Liu, Munehiro Asally, San Ly, Jordi Garcia-Ojalvo, and Gürol Süel publish "Ion Channels Enable Electrical Communication in Bacterial Communities" in *Nature*, demonstrating that bacterial biofilms use electrical signaling — propagated potassium waves — to coordinate metabolic activity across the colony. |
| **2016** | Romain Boisseau, David Vogel, and Audrey Dussutour publish "Habituation in Non-Neural Organisms: Evidence from Slime Moulds" in *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*, providing the clearest demonstration of habituation in *Physarum* and reporting that trained behavior can be transferred to naive organisms through cell fusion. |
| **2016** | Laura Weiss and colleagues publish a reconstruction of LUCA (the Last Universal Common Ancestor) physiology in *Nature Microbiology*, suggesting that LUCA was an anaerobic, chemolithoautotrophic organism with sophisticated ion-channel-based bioenergetics — implying that basic bioelectric signaling predates the divergence of bacteria and archaea. |
| **2019** | Michael Levin publishes "The Computational Boundary of a 'Self': Developmental Bioelectricity Drives Multicellularity and Scale-Free Cognition" in *Frontiers in Psychology*, presenting the cognitive light cone framework — the proposal that cognitive agency scales continuously with the spatial and temporal reach of an organism's information integration. |
| **2019** | Lincoln Taiz and colleagues publish a critical assessment of plant neurobiology in *Trends in Plant Science*, arguing that claims about plant intelligence and plant neurobiology overclaim the evidence and risk misleading the field. The paper sparks renewed methodological debate about standards of evidence in basal cognition. |
| **2020** | Sam Kriegman, Douglas Blackiston, Michael Levin, and Josh Bongard publish "A Scalable Pipeline for Designing Reconfigurable Organisms" in *PNAS*, describing xenobots — the first organisms designed from scratch using computational evolution and built from frog embryonic cells. Xenobots move, self-organize, and perform simple tasks without any nervous system. |
| **2021** | Anna Kuchina and colleagues publish "Microbial Single-Cell RNA Sequencing by Split-Pool Barcoding" in *Science*, enabling the transcriptional profiling of individual bacterial cells during the sporulation decision in *Bacillus subtilis* and revealing the molecular basis of single-cell decision variability. |
| **2022** | Andrew Adamatzky publishes "Towards Fungal Computer" in *Interface Focus*, reporting evidence for electrical spike trains in living mycelial networks of several fungal species and arguing that fungal networks may be capable of primitive computation. |
| **2024** | The field continues to consolidate around a small number of core theoretical commitments — the SIA (Sensing-Integrating-Acting) triad, the biogenic approach, the Free Energy Principle — while empirical research accelerates on multiple fronts: bioelectric cognition, mycorrhizal network signaling, plant learning, and the computational capacities of unconventional biological substrates. |

---

*A note on disputed findings:* Several entries in this timeline concern results that were initially controversial and remain subjects of active investigation. Scientific knowledge in this field is genuinely incomplete, and the reader should consult primary literature and recent reviews rather than relying on any single source — including this one.
