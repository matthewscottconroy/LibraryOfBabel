# Appendix C: Glossary of Key Terms

This glossary defines the central terms used throughout the book. Entries are listed alphabetically. Where a term has a technical meaning that differs from its ordinary usage, the technical meaning is given priority. Cross-references within the glossary are indicated by **bold text**. References to specific chapters and units of the book appear in parentheses.

---

**Acylhomoserine lactone (AHL)**
A class of small signaling molecules used by many gram-negative bacteria as **autoinducers** in **quorum sensing** systems. AHLs are synthesized by individual bacterial cells, diffuse across cell membranes, and accumulate in the extracellular environment in proportion to population density. When the extracellular concentration of AHL reaches a threshold, it binds to intracellular receptor proteins and triggers coordinated changes in gene expression across the population. AHLs are not universal — gram-positive bacteria use different signaling molecules — but they are among the best-characterized molecular mechanisms of bacterial collective behavior. *See Unit III.*

**Allelopathy**
The production and release of chemical compounds by one plant that affect the growth, survival, or reproduction of neighboring plants. Allelopathic compounds may inhibit germination, disrupt root development, or alter soil microbial communities in ways that disadvantage competitors. The phenomenon demonstrates that plants are not passive occupants of their chemical environments but active participants in chemical interactions with neighbors — a form of chemical communication that, while typically interpreted in competitive terms, shares mechanisms with more clearly cooperative inter-plant signaling. *See Unit V.*

**Allostasis**
The process of maintaining physiological stability through change — by anticipating demands and proactively adjusting internal parameters before perturbation occurs, rather than simply correcting deviations after they happen (as in **homeostasis** in its classical formulation). The concept was introduced by Peter Sterling and Joseph Eyer (1988) to capture the predictive, anticipatory quality of biological regulation. Allostasis is relevant to basal cognition because it implies that even simple organisms must maintain predictive models of their environment — making biological regulation inherently forward-looking. *See Units I and IX.*

**Anthropomorphism**
The attribution of human characteristics — emotions, intentions, experiences, or cognitive capacities — to non-human entities. In basal cognition research, anthropomorphism refers specifically to the uncritical projection of human-like inner experience onto organisms whose experiential capacities (if any) may be radically different. The field maintains **principled functional agnosticism** precisely to avoid anthropomorphism while still taking seriously the functional capacities of non-neural organisms. Anthropomorphism is distinct from — and should not be confused with — the legitimate attribution of functional properties (sensing, integration, goal-directedness) to simple organisms. *See the Preface and Unit XII.*

**Autopoiesis**
Literally "self-production" (from the Greek *autos*, self, and *poiesis*, production). The term was introduced by Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela to describe the defining organizational property of living systems: the continuous production and maintenance of the system's own components and boundaries by processes within the system itself. An autopoietic system makes itself. On Maturana and Varela's account, cognition — the ability of a system to interact with its environment in ways that maintain its organization — is not a special capacity layered on top of life but is identical with it. Every autopoietic system is cognitive; every cognitive system is autopoietic. *See Units I and IX.*

**Autoinducer**
A chemical signal produced and secreted by a bacterium (or other organism) that accumulates in the environment and is detected by both the producing cell and neighboring cells of the same (or related) species. Autoinducers are the molecular currency of **quorum sensing**: they enable individual cells to assess the density of the surrounding population and coordinate collective behaviors. The term "autoinducer" emphasizes the self-referential character of the signal — the population induces its own behavioral change by accumulating its own secretions. *See Unit III.*

**Basal cognition**
The study of cognitive-like capacities — sensing, integrating information, and acting in adaptive ways — in organisms that lack nervous systems, or in cellular and subcellular systems more generally. "Basal" refers not to evolutionary primitiveness but to the idea that these capacities are foundational: they are the ground floor of a continuum of cognitive complexity that extends from bacteria to humans. The field maintains principled agnosticism about whether these capacities involve phenomenal experience; it is concerned with their functional organization and evolutionary origins. *See the Preface and Unit I.*

**Biogenic approach**
Pamela Lyon's term for the theoretical position that cognition is a biological phenomenon — one that originated with life itself and that should be studied in its full biological diversity, from bacteria to brains. The biogenic approach contrasts with the "brain-centric" or "neurogenic" approach, which treats the vertebrate brain as the paradigm case and asks how far down the phylogenetic tree cognition extends. The biogenic approach asks instead: given what we know about bacterial behavior, what is the minimum that cognition requires? *See Units I and XIII.*

**Bioelectricity**
Electrical phenomena arising from the activity of biological systems — specifically, the voltage differences across cell membranes generated by the differential distribution of ions. All cells, not just neurons, maintain membrane voltage differences through ion pumps and channels. These bioelectric signals play roles in development, regeneration, and wound healing that are only beginning to be understood. Michael Levin's laboratory has shown that disrupting bioelectric gradients in embryos produces predictable morphological changes, suggesting that bioelectricity encodes spatial information about body plans. *See Unit II.*

**Biofilm**
A structured community of bacteria embedded in a self-produced matrix of extracellular polymeric substances, typically attached to a surface. Biofilms are not simply aggregations of individual cells: they exhibit differentiation (different cells play different roles), communication (via **quorum sensing** and **electrical signaling**), and resource allocation. The biofilm is the canonical example of bacterial collective behavior and raises the question of whether cognitive attributions should be made at the level of the individual cell or the community. *See Unit III.*

**Cognitive light cone**
Michael Levin's metaphor for the spatial and temporal reach of a system's information integration, adapted from the light cone of special relativity. A system's cognitive light cone defines the extent of past information it can access and the extent of future states it can influence through its actions. Small organisms with short time horizons have narrow cognitive light cones; organisms that plan and remember have wider ones. Levin proposes that cognitive complexity is best measured by the size of this cone rather than by the presence or absence of a nervous system. *See Units II and XIII.*

**Chemotaxis**
Directed movement by an organism in response to a chemical gradient — toward attractive chemicals (positive chemotaxis) or away from repellents (negative chemotaxis). Bacterial chemotaxis is the best-understood model system for basal cognition: *E. coli* compares its current chemical environment with its recent past (via a molecular memory mechanism involving methylation of receptor proteins) and biases its tumbling frequency to achieve net movement up gradients of attractants. The system implements a temporal comparison that is functionally equivalent to derivative-based gradient following. *See Units II and III.*

**Combination problem**
A challenge for **panpsychism**: if consciousness is a fundamental property of matter, how do the micro-experiences of elementary particles or cells combine to produce the unified macro-experience of a human mind? The combination problem is generally considered the most serious philosophical obstacle for panpsychist views and has not been resolved. It is relevant to basal cognition because some theorists (notably Goff) invoke panpsychism to explain cognitive capacities in simple organisms, while critics argue the combination problem undermines this move. *See Unit XII.*

**Connectome**
A complete map of the neural connections in an organism's nervous system, specifying each neuron and each synapse. The only fully mapped connectome for any organism is that of the nematode *Caenorhabditis elegans*, published by John White and colleagues in 1986. The *C. elegans* connectome consists of 302 neurons and approximately 7,000 synaptic connections and provides a benchmark for understanding minimal neural computation. Connectome research illustrates the difference between having the wiring diagram and understanding the computation: knowing every connection in *C. elegans* has not produced a complete account of its behavior. *See Unit VIII.*

**Eliminativist overcaution**
The error of denying cognitive or experiential properties to organisms solely on the grounds that they lack the specific neural structures associated with those properties in humans or vertebrates. The term is used in this book to identify one of two symmetrical errors in the field (the other being uncritical anthropomorphism). Eliminativist overcaution applies the wrong standard — neural presence — to judge the presence of functional properties that may be realized in different substrates. *See the Preface and Unit I.*

**Embodied cognition**
The view that cognition is not a process occurring solely in the brain (or in any central processor) but is constituted by the organism's body, its sensorimotor interactions with the environment, and its developmental history. Embodied cognition is a broad family of positions in cognitive science; the version most relevant to basal cognition is the enactivist variant associated with Varela, Thompson, and Rosch, which holds that a body capable of autonomous action is sufficient for a minimal form of cognition. *See Units I and X.*

**Enactivism**
The view, developed by Varela, Thompson, and Rosch, that cognition is the ongoing, embodied activity of an organism coupled to its environment — not computation on internal representations. The enactivist slogan is "sense-making": cognition is the process by which a living system actively makes sense of its environment in terms of what matters for its continued existence. Enactivism provides theoretical support for basal cognition research by making autonomous action (not neural complexity) the criterion for cognition. *See Units I and IX.*

**Extended mind**
The thesis, proposed by Andy Clark and David Chalmers (1998), that cognitive processes can extend beyond the boundary of brain and body when external resources play the right functional role — storing, processing, or transforming information in ways that are integrated into behavior. In the context of basal cognition, the extended mind thesis raises the question of where the cognitive system of a slime mold, a plant, or a mycorrhizal network begins and ends: is the soil chemistry part of the plant's cognitive system? *See Unit X.*

**Free Energy Principle (FEP)**
A theoretical framework developed by Karl Friston proposing that biological systems minimize their variational free energy — a mathematical quantity measuring the difference between a system's internal model of the world and the actual sensory states it encounters. On Friston's account, all living systems, from bacteria to brains, are engaged in a continuous process of prediction and prediction-error minimization that constitutes a form of inference. When extended to all life, the FEP becomes a candidate theory of basal cognition: any **autopoietic** system is, by definition, engaged in something like Bayesian inference. The principle is mathematically rigorous but its empirical content at the level of simple organisms is contested. *See Unit IX.*

**Functional vs. phenomenal claim**
A distinction central to the methodological stance of this book. A functional claim attributes a capacity or process to an organism — sensing, integrating information, making decisions — on the basis of its observable behavior and organizational structure, without asserting anything about whether there is "something it is like" to be that organism having that experience. A phenomenal claim asserts that there is such subjective experience. Basal cognition research, as practiced in this book, makes functional claims while maintaining **principled functional agnosticism** about phenomenal ones. *See the Preface and Unit I.*

**Goal-directedness**
The property of behavior that is oriented toward an outcome, such that the system adjusts its actions in response to deviations from a target state. Goal-directedness does not require intention or consciousness: a thermostat is goal-directed in the minimal sense. What distinguishes biological goal-directedness from simple mechanical feedback is that biological systems set their own goals (via their internal organization) rather than having goals imposed from without. The question of what constitutes genuine, as opposed to merely apparent, goal-directedness is one of the central philosophical problems of basal cognition. *See Units I and IX.*

**Hard Problem of Consciousness**
David Chalmers' term for the explanatory gap between physical/functional accounts of the brain and the subjective, first-person character of experience. The "easy problems" of consciousness — explaining attention, memory, reportability — may in principle be explained by cognitive and neuroscience; the hard problem asks why there is any subjective experience at all, why physical processes are accompanied by "something it is like." The hard problem is relevant to basal cognition because it defines the boundary between functional attributions (which this book makes) and phenomenal attributions (which this book declines to make). *See Unit XII.*

**Holobiont**
An organism together with all of the symbiotic microorganisms associated with it — its microbiome, in the broadest sense. The holobiont concept, developed most fully by Lynn Margulis and later by Scott Gilbert and colleagues, challenges the idea that the individual organism is the basic unit of biological analysis. A human being, on this view, is a community of some 37 trillion human cells and roughly equal numbers of microbial cells, all interacting in ways that constitute the individual's physiology and behavior. The holobiont concept extends the boundaries of the cognitive system beyond the skin in a direction complementary to the **extended mind** thesis. *See Unit VI.*

**Homeostasis**
The process by which a living system maintains key internal variables within stable ranges despite external perturbations. Walter Cannon coined the term in 1932. Homeostasis is often taken as the most basic form of biological goal-directedness: the system acts to reduce deviations from a set-point. Modern accounts emphasize that biological regulation is more complex than simple set-point maintenance — see **allostasis** — but homeostasis remains the foundational concept for understanding how organisms actively manage their internal states. *See Units I and II.*

**Horizontal gene transfer**
The transfer of genetic material between organisms other than by vertical inheritance from parent to offspring. Horizontal gene transfer (HGT) is the primary mechanism of genetic exchange in bacteria and archaea and has played a significant role in the evolution of eukaryotes as well. HGT is relevant to basal cognition for two reasons: it is a mechanism of information transfer between organisms that does not require any neural or behavioral channel, and it implies that the boundaries of an organism's genetic "self" are more permeable than the standard picture suggests. *See Units III and VI.*

**Integrated Information Theory (IIT)**
Giulio Tononi's theory that consciousness is identical to integrated information (quantified as Φ, phi) — the amount of information generated by a system above and beyond the information generated by its parts independently. IIT predicts that any system with nonzero Φ has some degree of consciousness, including simple organisms, computer chips, and (controversially) the internet. IIT is contested — critics question both its conceptual foundations and its testability — but it is one of the few theories of consciousness that takes the possibility of non-trivial experience in simple biological systems seriously. *See Units IX and XII.*

**Intrinsic vs. derived intentionality**
The distinction between mental states that are "about" something in virtue of their intrinsic properties (intrinsic intentionality, typically attributed to biological minds) and representations that are "about" something only because they have been assigned that meaning by an external interpreter (derived intentionality, attributed to words, maps, and most artifacts). The distinction matters for basal cognition because critics sometimes argue that bacterial "decision-making" is merely derived — a description we impose from outside — rather than a genuine property of the system. *See Units I and XII.*

**Lac operon**
The genetic regulatory system in *Escherichia coli* that controls the expression of genes for metabolizing lactose. The lac operon reads two signals — the presence of lactose and the absence of glucose — and produces a regulated output (expression of lactose-metabolizing enzymes) only when both conditions are met. It functions as a molecular AND gate: a logical decision-making device implemented in biochemistry. Described by François Jacob and Jacques Monod in 1961, for which they received the Nobel Prize, the lac operon remains the paradigm case of molecular decision-making in basal cognition. *See Unit III.*

**LUCA (Last Universal Common Ancestor)**
The hypothetical ancestral organism from which all currently living organisms descend. LUCA is not the first living organism but the most recent common ancestor of bacteria, archaea, and eukaryotes. Reconstruction of LUCA's physiology using comparative genomics (Weiss et al., 2016, *Nature Microbiology*) suggests that LUCA was an anaerobic, chemolithoautotrophic organism with sophisticated ion-channel machinery — implying that bioelectric signaling, and by extension the most basic forms of basal cognition, are as old as life on Earth. *See Units I and XIII.*

**Markov blanket**
A concept from probability theory, used in the **Free Energy Principle** framework, to define the boundary between a system and its environment. A system's Markov blanket consists of the states that "separate" the system from everything else: states that, if known, render the system's internal states and external states conditionally independent. In Friston's framework, every living system has a Markov blanket that defines its boundaries, and the maintenance of that blanket is equivalent to maintaining the distinction between self and non-self. The Markov blanket concept provides a formal account of biological individuality. *See Unit IX.*

**Methylation (DNA/histone)**
The addition of methyl groups to DNA or to histone proteins around which DNA is coiled. DNA methylation typically represses gene expression; histone methylation can either activate or repress, depending on context. Methylation patterns can persist through cell division and, in some cases, across generations — making methylation a potential mechanism of epigenetic inheritance. In basal cognition, methylation is relevant because it provides a molecular mechanism for storing information about past environmental conditions in ways that influence future gene expression — a form of cellular memory. *See Units II and V.*

**Minimally Cognitive Organization (MCO)**
The theoretical framework developed by Xabier Barandiaran and Álvaro Moreno to specify the minimum organizational conditions for cognition. A system has a minimally cognitive organization, on their account, when it is (1) a self-maintaining system with its own internal norms, (2) capable of evaluating environmental states relative to those norms, and (3) capable of modulating its behavior in response to that evaluation. The MCO framework is designed to exclude mere mechanical feedback (which lacks internal norms) while including bacteria (which have them). *See Units I and IX.*

**Multiple realizability**
The philosophical thesis that the same mental state or cognitive function can be realized by many different physical substrates. Multiple realizability is relevant to basal cognition because it provides a principled basis for attributing cognitive properties to organisms with very different physical implementations than the vertebrate brain. If memory, decision-making, or goal-directedness can be realized in neurons, they may also be realized in biochemical signaling networks, electrical gradients, or mycelial architectures. *See Units I and IX.*

**Mycelium**
The vegetative body of a fungus, consisting of a network of thread-like filaments called hyphae. A single mycelial network can extend over many hectares and live for centuries. The mycelium is the ecologically active part of most fungi: it absorbs nutrients, forms symbiotic associations with plant roots (**mycorrhizal networks**), decomposes organic matter, and may transmit electrical and chemical signals across the network. Adamatzky's work suggests that the mycelium may implement primitive computation through electrical spike trains. *See Unit VI.*

**Niche construction**
The process by which organisms modify their own environment — and thereby modify the selection pressures acting on themselves and their descendants. Classic examples include beaver dams and earthworm soil modification. Niche construction is relevant to basal cognition because it demonstrates that even simple organisms are not merely passive recipients of environmental selection but active participants in shaping the conditions of their own existence — a form of extended agency. *See Units V and X.*

**Panpsychism**
The philosophical view that consciousness or experience is a fundamental and ubiquitous feature of reality — present in some form in all matter, not just in complex biological systems. Panpsychism is not the view that rocks or thermostats have human-like experiences, but that they have some form of proto-experiential properties. Contemporary defenders include Philip Goff, who argues that panpsychism avoids the **Hard Problem of Consciousness** by making experience fundamental rather than emergent. This book maintains agnosticism with respect to panpsychism. *See Unit XII.*

**Phenotypic plasticity**
The capacity of a single genotype to produce different phenotypes — different physical, physiological, or behavioral traits — in response to different environmental conditions. Phenotypic plasticity is a form of adaptive flexibility that does not require genetic change and can be understood as a system-level response to environmental information. In basal cognition, phenotypic plasticity is often the behavioral outcome of sensing and integration processes: the organism reads its environment and adjusts its development, physiology, or behavior accordingly. *See Units II and V.*

**Physarum polycephalum**
A species of "slime mold" (more precisely, a myxomycete plasmodial slime mold) that forms a single giant cell containing many nuclei — a plasmodium — capable of flowing, exploring, and solving spatial optimization problems. *Physarum* is the canonical organism of basal cognition research: Nakagaki et al.'s 2000 *Nature* paper on maze-solving, Dussutour's work on habituation, and Adamatzky's work on computing have all used it as a model system. *Physarum* is neither an animal, nor a plant, nor a fungus, but a distinct lineage with its own remarkable capabilities. *See Unit IV and the Epilogue.*

**Principled functional agnosticism**
The methodological stance that this book maintains with respect to phenomenal consciousness in non-neural organisms. We attribute functional properties — sensing, integrating, acting — to simple organisms on the basis of their organizational and behavioral properties, without claiming that these organisms have, or lack, phenomenal experience. The agnosticism is "principled" rather than merely cautious: it reflects the genuine epistemic situation, in which we have no reliable method for determining whether there is "something it is like" to be a bacterium or a slime mold. *See the Preface and Unit I.*

**Quorum sensing**
The mechanism by which bacteria assess their own population density and coordinate collective behaviors accordingly. Individual bacteria secrete **autoinducer** molecules that accumulate in the extracellular environment. When autoinducer concentration crosses a threshold — indicating that the population has reached a "quorum" — bacteria collectively alter gene expression, triggering behaviors such as biofilm formation, bioluminescence, sporulation, or virulence. Quorum sensing is the paradigm case of bacterial collective decision-making. *See Unit III.*

**Rhizosphere**
The zone of soil directly surrounding and influenced by plant roots — typically a few millimeters wide but ecologically among the most active regions on Earth. The rhizosphere is shaped by root exudates (sugars, amino acids, secondary metabolites) that attract and cultivate specific microbial communities. Plant-microbiome interactions in the rhizosphere influence plant nutrition, disease resistance, and stress responses, and may mediate inter-plant communication. The rhizosphere illustrates how plants actively construct their own microbial environment — a form of **niche construction**. *See Unit V.*

**RNA world**
The hypothesis that early life was based on RNA rather than on the DNA-protein system of modern cells — because RNA can both store information (like DNA) and catalyze reactions (like proteins). The RNA world hypothesis, if correct, implies that the most fundamental forms of biological sensing and self-regulation are very old — present in the earliest forms of life before the modern cell plan evolved. *See Unit I.*

**Scale-free cognition**
Michael Levin's proposal that cognitive capacities do not belong exclusively to one level of biological organization (e.g., the individual organism) but emerge at multiple scales simultaneously — from the subcellular to the superorganismal — and that these different-scale cognitive systems interact. On this view, a multicellular organism is composed of cells that are themselves cognitive systems, organized into tissues and organs that are cognitive systems at a higher scale, which are in turn organized into organisms, and potentially into societies. *See Units II and XIII.*

**SIA triad (Sensing-Integrating-Acting)**
The three-component functional framework used throughout this book to characterize minimal cognition: a cognitive system must (1) *sense* — detect and transduce signals from its environment, (2) *integrate* — combine multiple signals and past states to produce a unified evaluation, and (3) *act* — produce output that affects its environment in ways relevant to its survival. The SIA triad is deliberately minimal and substrate-neutral: it applies to bacteria, plants, slime molds, and vertebrate brains alike, and makes no claims about subjective experience. *See Unit I.*

**Sigma factor**
A protein subunit of bacterial RNA polymerase that enables the polymerase to recognize specific promoter sequences on DNA and initiate transcription of particular genes. Different sigma factors recognize different promoters, allowing bacteria to switch between distinct transcriptional programs in response to environmental conditions. A single bacterial cell may harbor multiple sigma factors, which compete for access to RNA polymerase — making sigma factor dynamics a form of molecular decision-making about which genes to express. *See Unit III.*

**Stigmergy**
Indirect coordination between agents through the modification of a shared environment. The term was introduced by Pierre-Paul Grassé (1959) to describe how termites coordinate construction: each termite responds to the local state of the structure, and the structure itself encodes the information that guides subsequent behavior. Stigmergy is the mechanism underlying much collective intelligence in social insects and slime molds: cognition is distributed across the environment as well as the agents. *See Units IV and VII.*

**Structural coupling**
Maturana and Varela's term for the ongoing co-determination of a living system and its environment through their history of interaction. A structurally coupled system is not simply reactive to environmental inputs; it is shaped by its history of interactions and, through its own activity, shapes its environment in turn. Structural coupling is the enactivist alternative to the stimulus-response model of behavior: the organism and environment co-evolve, and the organism's cognitive structure reflects its history of coupling. *See Units I and IX.*

**Superorganism**
A colony of individual organisms — typically social insects — that functions as a single adaptive unit. The superorganism concept, associated most strongly with William Morton Wheeler (1911) and later with E.O. Wilson and Bert Hölldobler, captures the idea that ant or bee colonies exhibit integration and coordination at the colony level that is analogous to the integration of cells in a multicellular organism. The concept raises the question of where the appropriate unit of cognitive attribution lies: the individual ant, or the colony? *See Unit VII.*

**Two-component system**
The dominant signal transduction architecture in bacteria: a membrane-bound sensor histidine kinase detects an environmental signal and autophosphorylates, then transfers the phosphate group to a cytoplasmic response regulator, which alters gene expression or other cellular behavior. Two-component systems are modular and combinable: bacteria typically have dozens, allowing them to monitor many environmental variables simultaneously and integrate multiple signals. They are the molecular basis of most bacterial sensing and decision-making. *See Units II and III.*

**Umwelt**
Jakob von Uexküll's term (from his 1909 work *Umwelt und Innenwelt der Tiere*) for the subjective, self-centered world of an organism as defined by the sensory and motor capacities of that organism's body. Each organism lives in an Umwelt particular to its own biology: a tick's Umwelt is defined by temperature, butyric acid, and the texture of mammalian skin; a bee's by ultraviolet patterns invisible to humans. The Umwelt concept is a reminder that cognition is always perspective-dependent — the world as it is sensed and acted upon by a particular kind of body — and that asking what a bacterium's Umwelt is like is a legitimate scientific question. *See Units I and IV.*

**Valence (biological)**
The affective quality of a stimulus or internal state — whether it is experienced or registered as positive (attractive, beneficial) or negative (aversive, harmful). In neuroscience, valence is associated with the affective dimension of emotion. In basal cognition, the term is used more carefully: organisms without nervous systems behave *as if* stimuli have positive or negative valence — they approach attractants and withdraw from aversive stimuli — and we use the language of valence to describe this behavioral asymmetry without claiming anything about subjective experience. *See Units I and XII.*

**Xenobot**
A class of programmable, self-organizing biological machines created by computational design and assembled from the cells of *Xenopus laevis* (African clawed frog) embryos. First described by Kriegman, Blackiston, Levin, and Bongard in 2020, xenobots move, organize into groups, and perform simple tasks without any genetic engineering — using only the self-organizing properties of embryonic cells placed in novel configurations designed by evolutionary algorithms. Xenobots raise profound questions about the relationship between cellular cognition and organismal design, and about what it means to be a biological individual. *See Units II and XI.*
