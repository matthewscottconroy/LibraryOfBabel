# Appendix D: Curated Further Reading by Unit

The literature in basal cognition is scattered across many disciplines and many decades. This guide provides a curated entry point for each unit of the book — not an exhaustive bibliography, but a carefully selected set of readings that will help the reader go deeper. Each unit includes accessible entry-point readings suitable for advanced undergraduates, more technical primary sources, and landmark books where they exist.

Citations have been verified for accuracy. Where an author or title is referenced without a full citation in the main text, complete publication information is given here.

---

## Unit I: First Principles — What Is Cognition?

**Accessible entry points**

*Lyon, P. (2006). The biogenic approach to cognition. Cognitive Processing, 7(1), 11–29.*
The clearest single-paper statement of the biogenic approach. Lyon argues that cognition should be understood as a biological phenomenon coextensive with life itself, and that bacteria — not vertebrates — are the appropriate paradigm cases. Written for a cognitive science audience but accessible to readers with biological backgrounds. Essential reading before tackling the more formal literature.

*Chalmers, D. J. (1995). Facing up to the problem of consciousness. Journal of Consciousness Studies, 2(3), 200–219.*
The paper that introduced the "hard problem" of consciousness to the philosophical mainstream. Even for readers skeptical of philosophy, Chalmers' distinction between the "easy problems" (explaining cognitive functions) and the "hard problem" (explaining why there is subjective experience at all) is indispensable for understanding why basal cognition must maintain principled agnosticism. Brief, lucid, and worth reading in full.

*Godfrey-Smith, P. (2001). Three kinds of intentionality. Pacific Philosophical Quarterly, 82(3–4), 326–351.*
A careful philosophical analysis of what it means for a biological system to be "about" or "directed toward" something in its environment. Godfrey-Smith distinguishes genuine intentionality from mere derived (observer-assigned) meaning — a distinction that comes up repeatedly when skeptics challenge basal cognition claims.

**Technical primary sources**

*Barandiaran, X. E., & Moreno, A. (2008). Adaptivity: From metabolism to behavior. Adaptive Behavior, 16(5), 325–344.*
The foundational paper for the Minimally Cognitive Organization framework. Dense but rewarding: Barandiaran and Moreno provide precise formal criteria for distinguishing genuine cognitive organization from mere mechanical responsiveness. A key reference for anyone working on the theory of basal cognition.

*Ruiz-Mirazo, K., & Moreno, A. (2004). Basic autonomy as a fundamental step in the synthesis of life. Artificial Life, 10(3), 235–259.*
Develops the concept of basic autonomy — the minimal organizational self-sufficiency of a living system — as the precondition for any cognitive capacity. More formal than the Barandiaran and Moreno paper but provides important conceptual grounding.

**Landmark book**

*Varela, F. J., Thompson, E., & Rosch, E. (1991). The Embodied Mind: Cognitive Science and Human Experience. MIT Press.*
The founding text of enactivism. Varela, Thompson, and Rosch weave together cognitive science, phenomenology, and Buddhist philosophy of mind into an account of cognition as sense-making by a living body. Parts of the book are technically demanding, but Chapters 1, 8, and 9 are accessible and essential.

## Unit II: The Intelligent Cell — Sensing, Signaling, and Memory

**Accessible entry points**

*Bhaskaran, S., & Kumar, P. (2022). A review on cellular decision making. Frontiers in Physiology, 13, 832638.*
An accessible overview of how individual cells integrate multiple signals and produce coherent responses — covering receptor dynamics, signal transduction cascades, and bistable switches. A good first survey for readers coming from a non-cell-biology background.

*Levin, M. (2021). Bioelectric signaling: Reprogrammable circuits underlying embryogenesis, regeneration, and cancer. Cell, 184(8), 1971–1989.*
Levin's most comprehensive review of bioelectricity as a developmental and cognitive signaling system. Covers the evidence for bioelectric pattern memory, the tools used to measure and manipulate membrane voltage, and the implications for regenerative medicine and synthetic biology. Technically demanding but rewarding.

*Berg, H. C. (2000). Motility of bacteria. Physics Today, 53(1), 24–29.*
A shorter, more accessible version of Berg's famous account of bacterial movement, written for physicists but readable by biologists. Berg describes the mathematics of chemotaxis in a way that makes the sophistication of bacterial navigation vivid.

**Technical primary sources**

*Sourjik, V., & Armitage, J. P. (2010). Spatial organization in bacterial chemotaxis. EMBO Journal, 29(16), 2724–2733.*
A review of the molecular and spatial organization of the bacterial chemotaxis machinery. Essential for understanding how a single cell can perform gradient detection, adaptation, and directed movement using a relatively small number of molecular components.

*Bhaskaran, S., & Kumar, P. (2022). A review on cellular decision making. Frontiers in Physiology, 13, 832638.*
(See above; also serves as a technical source for signal integration mechanisms.)

**Landmark book**

*Berg, H. C. (2003). E. coli in Motion. Springer.*
The definitive account of bacterial movement and chemotaxis, written by the scientist who discovered that bacteria swim by rotating helical flagella. Berg's writing is clear, precise, and elegant. Though published in 2003, it remains the best single introduction to bacterial motility.

## Unit III: Microbial Cognition — Bacteria as Cognitive Agents

**Accessible entry points**

*Bassler, B. L., & Losick, R. (2006). Bacterially speaking. Cell, 125(2), 237–246.*
An accessible review of quorum sensing and bacterial collective behavior, written by two leading researchers for a general biological audience. Bassler and Losick make explicit the parallels between bacterial communication and higher-level social cognition — carefully but persuasively.

*Shapiro, J. A. (1998). Thinking about bacterial populations as multicellular organisms. Annual Review of Microbiology, 52, 81–104.*
Shapiro's provocative argument that bacteria in populations behave as integrated multicellular systems, not as independent individuals. A useful counterpoint to single-cell-centric views of bacterial cognition.

*Henke, J. M., & Bassler, B. L. (2004). Bacterial social engagements. Trends in Cell Biology, 14(11), 648–656.*
A readable survey of the social behaviors — cooperation, competition, cheating — that emerge from quorum sensing. Useful for understanding why bacterial collective behavior is not just interesting chemistry but raises genuine questions about microbial agency.

**Technical primary sources**

*Parsek, M. R., & Greenberg, E. P. (2005). Sociomicrobiology: The connections between quorum sensing and biofilms. Trends in Microbiology, 13(1), 27–33.*
Establishes the link between quorum sensing and biofilm formation, arguing that biofilms are best understood as social communities shaped by cooperative and competitive interactions. A key paper for Unit III's argument about bacterial sociality.

*Prindle, A., Liu, J., Asally, M., Ly, S., Garcia-Ojalvo, J., & Süel, G. M. (2015). Ion channels enable electrical communication in bacterial communities. Nature, 527(7576), 59–63.*
The paper demonstrating electrical signaling in bacterial biofilms via propagated potassium waves. One of the most significant recent discoveries in bacterial cognition research, showing that biofilms use a mechanism analogous to neural signaling for long-range coordination.

**Landmark book**

*Shapiro, J. A. (2011). Evolution: A View from the 21st Century. FT Press.*
Shapiro's comprehensive argument that bacteria are not passive recipients of mutation and selection but active agents of genome change. While the evolutionary claims are controversial, the background material on bacterial natural genetic engineering provides an important framework for understanding bacterial agency.

## Unit IV: Protist Intelligence — Thinking Without Neurons

**Accessible entry points**

*Nakagaki, T., Yamada, H., & Tóth, Á. (2000). Maze-solving by an amoeboid organism. Nature, 407(6803), 470.*
The founding paper of protist intelligence research. A single page in Nature; read it before any secondary literature. The simplicity of the experimental design makes the result all the more striking.

*Boisseau, R. P., Vogel, D., & Dussutour, A. (2016). Habituation in non-neural organisms: Evidence from slime moulds. Proceedings of the Royal Society B, 283(1829), 20160446.*
Reports that Physarum polycephalum can be habituated to repeated aversive stimuli and that this habituation can be transferred to naive organisms through cell fusion. A carefully controlled study that remains one of the strongest demonstrations of memory in a non-neural organism.

*Godfrey-Smith, P. (2001). Complexity and the evolution of nervous systems. Biology and Philosophy, 16(4), 455–476.*
Godfrey-Smith examines the relationship between ecological complexity and the evolution of nervous systems, asking what selection pressures drove the emergence of neural control. A useful theoretical frame for understanding why protists occupy the position they do in the cognitive landscape.

**Technical primary sources**

*Tero, A., Takagi, S., Saigusa, T., Ito, K., Bebber, D. P., Fricker, M. D., ... & Nakagaki, T. (2010). Rules for biologically inspired adaptive network design. Science, 327(5964), 439–442.*
Shows that Physarum builds transport networks that approximate the efficiency and robustness of the Tokyo rail network. The paper provides quantitative measures for comparing biological and human-engineered network design.

*Reid, C. R., Latty, T., Dussutour, A., & Beekman, M. (2012). Slime mold uses an externalized spatial "memory" to navigate in complex environments. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 109(43), 17490–17494.*
Demonstrates that Physarum uses the slime it has already deposited to avoid re-exploring territory — an externalized memory system that allows efficient exploration of complex mazes. A beautiful example of stigmergic cognition.

## Unit V: Plant Cognition — Sensing, Signaling, and Learning in Plants

**Accessible entry points**

*Karban, R. (2015). Plant Sensing and Communication. University of Chicago Press.*
The most rigorously documented book on plant information processing available. Karban is a field ecologist whose evidence standards are high and whose interpretive claims are careful. An essential reference for anyone who wants to understand what plants actually do (as opposed to what enthusiasts claim they do).

*Taiz, L., Alkon, D., Draguhn, A., Murphy, A., Blatt, M., Hawes, C., ... & Robinson, D. G. (2019). Plants neither possess nor require consciousness. Trends in Plant Science, 24(8), 677–687.*
The most carefully argued critical response to plant neurobiology. Taiz and colleagues do not deny that plants have sophisticated signaling capacities; they argue that the language of "neurons," "brains," and "consciousness" applied to plants is misleading. Required reading alongside Trewavas and Mancuso.

*Gagliano, M., Renton, M., Depczynski, M., & Mancuso, S. (2014). Experience teaches plants to learn faster and forget slower in environments where it matters. Oecologia, 175(1), 63–72.*
The original habituation paper for Mimosa pudica. Read alongside Taiz et al. to appreciate the genuine evidentiary dispute about what these results mean.

**Technical primary sources**

*Gilroy, S., Białasek, M., Suzuki, N., Górecka, M., Devireddy, A. R., Karpiński, S., & Mittler, R. (2016). ROS, calcium, and electric signals: Key mediators of rapid systemic signaling in plants. Plant Physiology, 171(3), 1606–1615.*
Reviews the molecular mechanisms of rapid long-distance signaling in plants — reactive oxygen species, calcium waves, and electrical signals. Establishes the plausibility of plant-wide information integration without invoking neural mechanisms.

*Trewavas, A. (2003). Aspects of plant intelligence. Annals of Botany, 92(1), 1–20.*
Trewavas' most systematic statement of his arguments for plant intelligence, including his use of distributed computation frameworks. More technically detailed than his popular book but more accessible than the specialist literature.

**Landmark book**

*Trewavas, A. (2014). Plant Behaviour and Intelligence. Oxford University Press.*
The most comprehensive scientific case for plant cognition, written by a plant physiologist with deep experimental expertise. Trewavas is more cautious than Mancuso and more willing to engage with molecular mechanisms; this book provides the best technical foundation for the claims made in Unit V.

## Unit VI: Fungal Intelligence — Networks, Exchange, and the Wood Wide Web

**Accessible entry points**

*Sheldrake, M. (2020). Entangled Life: How Fungi Make Our Worlds, Change Our Minds and Shape Our Futures. Random House.*
The best popular introduction to fungal biology, written with scientific accuracy and literary skill. Sheldrake is particularly strong on mycorrhizal networks, the chemistry of fungal-plant relationships, and the deep strangeness of fungal life. An ideal starting point for students new to the field.

*Fricker, M. D., Heaton, L. L., Jones, N. S., & Boddy, L. (2017). The mycelium as a network. Microbiology Spectrum, 5(3).*
A rigorous network-theoretic analysis of mycelial architecture. Fricker and colleagues show that fungal networks display properties — robustness, efficiency, adaptive rewiring — characteristic of sophisticated information-processing systems.

*Stamets, P. (2005). Mycelium Running: How Mushrooms Can Help Save the World. Ten Speed Press.*
More popular than scholarly, and some claims require scrutiny, but Stamets provides an accessible overview of fungal ecology and the range of environments in which fungi operate. Best used as a supplement to more rigorous sources.

**Technical primary sources**

*Kiers, E. T., Duhamel, M., Beesetty, Y., Mensah, J. A., Franken, O., Verbruggen, E., ... & Bücking, H. (2011). Reciprocal rewards stabilize cooperation in the mycorrhizal symbiosis. Science, 333(6044), 880–882.*
Demonstrates that mycorrhizal networks preferentially allocate carbon to plant roots that provide more phosphorus — a form of market-based reciprocity that requires the fungus to track resource exchange rates across the network. One of the strongest demonstrations of network-level decision-making in fungi.

*Adamatzky, A. (2022). Towards fungal computer. Interface Focus, 8(6), 20180029.*
Reports electrical spike trains in living mycelial networks of several fungal species and argues that these networks may implement primitive logic operations. Technically dense but the experimental sections are accessible.

**Landmark book**

*(See Sheldrake 2020 above; no single technical monograph yet covers fungal cognition comprehensively.)*

## Unit VII: Collective Intelligence — Swarms, Colonies, and Distributed Minds

**Accessible entry points**

*Seeley, T. D. (2010). Honeybee Democracy. Princeton University Press.*
Seeley's account of how honeybee swarms choose new homes through a process of distributed deliberation is one of the most elegant pieces of behavioral biology written in the past thirty years. The book is simultaneously a work of science and an extended meditation on what distributed cognition can look like.

*Gordon, D. M. (2010). Ant Encounters: Interaction Networks and Colony Behavior. Princeton University Press.*
Gordon's concise account of how harvester ant colonies regulate foraging, reproduction, and response to disturbance through local chemical interactions without any central coordination. The book is short, precise, and full of surprising observations.

*Wilson, E. O., & Hölldobler, B. (1988). Superorganism: The Beauty, Elegance, and Strangeness of Insect Societies. W. W. Norton.*
The conceptual foundation for thinking about insect colonies as cognitive units. Wilson and Hölldobler provide the most comprehensive account of insect social organization available.

**Technical primary sources**

*Seeley, T. D., Visscher, P. K., Schlegel, T., Hogan, P. M., Franks, N. R., & Marshall, J. A. (2012). Stop signals provide cross inhibition in collective decision-making by honeybee swarms. Science, 335(6064), 108–111.*
The paper demonstrating that honeybee scouts use inhibitory "stop signals" to suppress competing nest-site proposals — a mechanism that prevents the swarm from splitting its vote and enables convergence on a single decision. A key paper for understanding how distributed systems avoid gridlock.

*Camazine, S., Deneubourg, J.-L., Franks, N. R., Sneyd, J., Theraulaz, G., & Bonabeau, E. (2001). Self-Organization in Biological Systems. Princeton University Press.*
The technical reference for self-organization in social insects and other biological systems. Dense but comprehensive; covers the mathematics of positive and negative feedback, symmetry breaking, and pattern formation.

## Unit VIII: The Neural Transition — From Distributed to Centralized Cognition

**Accessible entry points**

*Godfrey-Smith, P. (2016). Other Minds: The Octopus, the Sea, and the Deep Origins of Consciousness. Farrar, Straus and Giroux.*
Godfrey-Smith traces the evolution of minds from the simplest animals to cephalopods, using the octopus as a living window onto what a radically different kind of intelligence looks like. The book models the methodology this textbook advocates: careful functional analysis combined with principled agnosticism about experience.

*Moroz, L. L. (2012). Phylogenomics meets neuroscience: How many times might complex brains have evolved? Acta Biologica Hungarica, 63(Supplement 2), 3–19.*
Moroz argues that nervous systems evolved independently multiple times — not once in the ancestor of all bilaterians — and that the molecular machinery of neural signaling is not unique to one lineage. A provocative and empirically grounded challenge to the view that there is one neural solution to cognition.

*Shomrat, T., & Levin, M. (2013). An automated training paradigm reveals long-term memory in planaria and its persistence through head regeneration. Journal of Experimental Biology, 216(20), 3799–3810.*
Reports that planarian flatworms trained before decapitation retain behavioral memory after head regeneration — a remarkable finding suggesting that memory can be stored in body tissue outside the brain. One of the most discussed recent papers in basal cognition.

**Technical primary sources**

*Moroz, L. L., Kocot, K. M., Citarella, M. R., Dosung, S., Norekian, T. P., Bhatt, D. K., ... & Halanych, K. M. (2014). The ctenophore genome and the evolutionary origins of neural systems. Nature, 510(7503), 109–114.*
Genomic evidence that ctenophores (comb jellies) built their nervous systems from different molecular components than other animals — supporting the hypothesis of convergent neural evolution. A landmark in evolutionary neuroscience.

*Hartline, D. K., & Colman, D. R. (2007). Rapid conduction and the evolution of giant axons and myelinated fibers. Current Biology, 17(1), R29–R35.*
Reviews the independent evolution of fast neural conduction in multiple lineages, illustrating how the same functional solution (fast signaling) can be reached by different molecular means.

## Unit IX: Theoretical Frameworks — FEP, IIT, and Autopoiesis

**Accessible entry points**

*Friston, K. (2013). Life as we know it. Journal of the Royal Society Interface, 10(86), 20130475.*
Friston's extension of the Free Energy Principle to all living systems. More accessible than his mathematical papers; the conceptual argument is clear, even if the formal apparatus is formidable.

*Tononi, G. (2012). Phi: A Voyage from the Brain to the Soul. Pantheon.*
Tononi's popular account of Integrated Information Theory, written as a philosophical dialogue. The mathematical details are minimized; what remains is a clear account of the theory's claims and their implications for consciousness in simple systems.

**Technical primary sources**

*Maturana, H. R., & Varela, F. J. (1980). Autopoiesis and Cognition: The Realization of the Living. D. Reidel.*
The foundational text. Part I (Maturana's essay on biology of cognition) and Part II (Maturana and Varela's original autopoiesis paper) are both essential. The prose is dense but the argument repays close reading.

*Tononi, G., Boly, M., Massimini, M., & Koch, C. (2016). Integrated information theory: From consciousness to its physical substrate. Nature Reviews Neuroscience, 17(7), 450–461.*
The most complete technical review of IIT, written for neuroscientists. Covers the mathematical formalism, the empirical predictions, and the philosophical objections.

**Accessible extended reading**

*Levin, M. (2019). The computational boundary of a "self": Developmental bioelectricity drives multicellularity and scale-free cognition. Frontiers in Psychology, 10, 2688.*
Levin's cognitive light cone paper is also a compact theoretical synthesis of how bioelectricity, scale-free cognition, and the FEP might be integrated. A useful bridge between Units II and IX.

## Unit X: Embodied and Extended Minds

**Accessible entry points**

*Clark, A., & Chalmers, D. (1998). The extended mind. Analysis, 58(1), 7–19.*
The paper that introduced the extended mind thesis. Eight pages, clearly argued, immediately provocative. Read it in a single sitting and then think about what it implies for mycorrhizal networks and ant colonies.

*Chemero, A. (2009). Radical Embodied Cognitive Science. MIT Press.*
A rigorous philosophical treatment of radical embodied cognition — the view that cognition is not computation on internal representations but dynamical coupling between organism and environment. More demanding than Clark's popular work but provides better theoretical foundations.

**Technical primary sources**

*Pfeifer, R., & Bongard, J. (2007). How the Body Shapes the Way We Think: A New View of Intelligence. MIT Press.*
A comprehensive treatment of embodied intelligence from a robotics perspective. Pfeifer and Bongard demonstrate, through computational experiments and physical robots, how much cognitive work can be offloaded to body morphology and environmental coupling.

*Clark, A. (2008). Supersizing the Mind: Embodiment, Action, and Cognitive Extension. Oxford University Press.*
Clark's book-length defense of the extended mind thesis, with responses to critics and extensions to new domains. Chapter 4 on "the extended mind and the natural world" is particularly relevant for basal cognition.

## Unit XI: AI Implications — What Basal Cognition Tells Us About Artificial Intelligence

**Accessible entry points**

*Brooks, R. A. (1991). Intelligence without representation. Artificial Intelligence, 47(1–3), 139–159.*
Brooks' argument that situated, embodied robots can exhibit intelligent behavior without building internal models of the world. One of the most influential papers in robotics and directly relevant to basal cognition: Brooks reached similar conclusions through engineering that Varela reached through biology.

*Adleman, L. M. (1994). Molecular computation of solutions to combinatorial problems. Science, 266(5187), 1021–1024.*
The founding paper of DNA computing. Adleman demonstrates that DNA molecules can perform parallel computation by encoding a combinatorial problem in molecular form and allowing hybridization to find solutions. Essential background for understanding biological computing.

**Technical primary sources**

*Kagan, B. J., Kitchen, A. C., Tran, N. T., Habibollahi, F., Khajehnejad, M., Parker, B. J., ... & Friston, K. J. (2022). In vitro neurons learn and exhibit sentience when embodied in a simulated game-world. Neuron, 110(23), 3952–3969.*
The DishBrain paper: reports that cortical neurons cultured on a microelectrode array learn to play a simplified version of Pong when given electrophysiological feedback. Raises profound questions about the minimal conditions for learning and (the paper controversially suggests) experience.

*Kriegman, S., Blackiston, D., Levin, M., & Bongard, J. (2020). A scalable pipeline for designing reconfigurable organisms. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 117(4), 1853–1859.*
The original xenobot paper. Kriegman et al. describe the computational pipeline for designing novel organisms from frog cells and demonstrate that the resulting organisms move, self-organize, and perform simple tasks. A landmark in the convergence of synthetic biology and cognitive science.

**Landmark book**

*Brooks, R. A. (2002). Flesh and Machines: How Robots Will Change Us. Pantheon.*
Brooks' popular account of his robotics work and its implications for understanding intelligence. More accessible than the technical papers and provides useful context for understanding why the robotics community arrived at embodied cognition from a different direction than the biologists.

## Unit XII: Philosophy and Ethics of Basal Cognition

**Accessible entry points**

*Godfrey-Smith, P. (2016). Other Minds: The Octopus, the Sea, and the Deep Origins of Consciousness. Farrar, Straus and Giroux.*
(See Unit VIII above.) The final chapters, on the nature of experience and the ethics of our treatment of intelligent animals, are the most relevant for Unit XII.

*Goff, P. (2019). Galileo's Error: Foundations for a New Science of Mind. Pantheon.*
The most accessible current defense of panpsychism, written for general readers. Goff's argument that Galileo's mathematization of nature excluded consciousness from the physical world, and that this exclusion must be undone, is presented here in its clearest form. Readers should engage this alongside Frankish (2016) for a critical perspective.

*Hall, M. (2011). Plants as Persons: A Philosophical Botany. SUNY Press.*
A philosophical treatment of the moral status of plants, drawing on both Western philosophy and non-Western traditions. More accessible than most academic ethics and a good starting point for the ethical implications of basal cognition.

**Technical primary sources**

*Frankish, K. (2016). Illusionism as a theory of consciousness. Journal of Consciousness Studies, 23(11–12), 11–39.*
The clearest statement of illusionism — the view that phenomenal consciousness, as ordinarily conceived, does not exist and that the hard problem is a cognitive illusion. Read alongside Chalmers (1995) and Goff (2019) to triangulate the philosophical debate.

*Allen, C., & Trestman, M. (2017). Animal cognition. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy.*
A comprehensive, peer-reviewed overview of philosophical issues in animal cognition — questions of representation, intentionality, learning, and consciousness. An invaluable reference for the philosophical dimensions of Unit XII.

## Unit XIII: Frontiers — Open Questions and Future Directions

**Accessible entry points**

*Lyon, P. (2015). The cognitive cell: Bacterial behavior reconsidered. Frontiers in Microbiology, 6, 264.*
Lyon's most comprehensive statement of the biogenic approach as it applies to bacterial cells specifically. A natural capstone to the theoretical arc of the book.

*Levin, M. (2019). The computational boundary of a "self": Developmental bioelectricity drives multicellularity and scale-free cognition. Frontiers in Psychology, 10, 2688.*
(See Unit IX above.) As a prospectus for future research directions — particularly in bioelectricity and the scaling of cognition — this paper has no peer.

**Technical primary sources**

*Jablonka, E., & Lamb, M. J. (2005). Evolution in Four Dimensions: Genetic, Epigenetic, Behavioral, and Symbolic Variation in the History of Life. MIT Press.*
Jablonka and Lamb argue that evolution occurs through four distinct inheritance systems — genetic, epigenetic, behavioral, and symbolic — and that the cognitive implications of this multi-channel view of inheritance have barely been explored. A rich source of hypotheses for future research.

*Lyon, P. (2021). Of what is "minimal cognition" the half-baked version? Adaptive Behavior, 29(4), 407–424.*
Lyon's most recent and pointed treatment of the conceptual issues in minimal cognition research. Written for specialists but accessible to advanced undergraduates who have read the earlier Lyon papers. The paper identifies the most pressing open questions in the field with characteristic precision.

**Landmark book**

*Jablonka, E., & Lamb, M. J. (2005). Evolution in Four Dimensions: Genetic, Epigenetic, Behavioral, and Symbolic Variation in the History of Life. MIT Press.*
(See above.) The most comprehensive single work for understanding the evolutionary scope of basal cognition — where it came from, how it ramifies, and where it might be going.
