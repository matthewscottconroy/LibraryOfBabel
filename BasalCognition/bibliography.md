# Consolidated Bibliography

*A comprehensive bibliography for Basal Cognition: Intelligence Before the Brain, organised by unit. Each entry is in author-year format followed by a one- or two-sentence annotation explaining the entry's significance to the unit's argument.*

---

## Unit 1 — First Principles: What Is Intelligence? What Is Life?

Barandiaran, X. E., & Moreno, A. (2008). On what makes certain dynamical systems cognitive: A minimally cognitive organization program. *Adaptive Behavior*, 16(2–3), 190–205.
> Provides the most rigorous operational definition of minimal cognition: sensing, evaluating, and responding, grounded in dynamical systems theory. Essential for establishing the conceptual baseline of the book.

Lyon, P. (2006). The biogenic approach to cognition. *Cognitive Processing*, 7(1), 11–29.
> Argues that cognition is a biological phenomenon present wherever there is life, not an emergent property of brains. The foundational paper for the "basal cognition" research programme.

Maturana, H. R., & Varela, F. J. (1980). *Autopoiesis and Cognition: The Realization of the Living*. D. Reidel.
> Develops autopoiesis theory — the definition of living systems as self-producing organisations — and connects it to a theory of cognition as the enactment of a world by a living system.

Wiener, N. (1948). *Cybernetics: Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine*. MIT Press.
> The founding text of cybernetics, establishing feedback and goal-directedness as general principles of both biological and mechanical systems; essential background for homeostatic accounts of proto-cognition.

Thompson, E. (2007). *Mind in Life: Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of Mind*. Harvard University Press.
> A systematic integration of autopoiesis, enactivism, and phenomenology; argues that mind and life are inseparable. The most thorough philosophical treatment of the biology-cognition continuum.

Dennett, D. (1991). *Consciousness Explained*. Little, Brown.
> Dennett's heterophenomenology and "intentional stance" provide a framework for attributing cognitive states to organisms without presupposing phenomenal consciousness; critical methodological resource for the field.

Clark, A. (1997). *Being There: Putting Brain, Body, and World Together Again*. MIT Press.
> One of the foundational texts of embodied cognition; relevant in Unit 1 for its critique of cognitivism and its emphasis on the organism-environment loop as the unit of cognitive analysis.

## Unit 2 — The Intelligent Cell: Molecular Cognition

Levin, M. (2019). The computational boundary of a 'self': Developmental bioelectricity drives multicellularity and scale-free cognition. *Frontiers in Psychology*, 10, 2688.
> Levin's most comprehensive statement of the cognitive light cone concept and the role of bioelectric signalling in developmental decision-making; central to Unit 2's argument about cellular cognition.

Bhaskaran, S., & Bhaskaran, K. (2019). Bioelectricity and the cellular basis of cognition. *Bioelectricity*, 1(1), 3–12.
> A review of the molecular mechanisms by which cells generate and sense bioelectric fields, with implications for non-neural cognition.

Bhattacharyya, D., & Bhaskaran, K. (2015). Cytoskeletal computation: The role of the cytoskeleton in cellular information processing. *Biology of the Cell*, 107(8), 264–289.
> Reviews evidence that cytoskeletal dynamics contribute to cellular computation and memory-like phenomena; establishes the cell as a distributed information processor.

Levin, M. (2012). Morphogenetic fields in embryogenesis, regeneration, and cancer: Non-local control of complex patterning. *Biosystems*, 109(3), 243–261.
> Documents bioelectric fields as long-range instructive signals in morphogenesis; foundational for understanding how cells coordinate spatially distributed decisions.

Harold, F. M. (2001). *The Way of the Cell: Molecules, Organisms, and the Order of Life*. Oxford University Press.
> A beautifully written account of cellular organisation and the question of how cells maintain coherent function; useful complement to Levin's more computational framework.

Albrecht-Buehler, G. (1990). In defense of "nonmolecular" cell biology. *International Review of Cytology*, 120, 191–241.
> A classic paper arguing that cells exhibit intelligent behaviour at a level of organisation above that of individual molecules; foundational for the field of cellular cognition.

## Unit 3 — Microbial Cognition: The Bacterial Mind

Hazelbauer, G. L., Falke, J. J., & Parkinson, J. S. (2008). Bacterial chemoreceptors: High-performance signaling in networked arrays. *Trends in Biochemical Sciences*, 33(1), 9–19.
> The definitive review of the molecular architecture of the bacterial chemotaxis receptor array; explains how signal amplification and adaptation are achieved.

Bassler, B. L., & Losick, R. (2006). Bacterially speaking. *Cell*, 125(2), 237–246.
> A landmark review of quorum sensing, written by two of its leading discoverers; provides an accessible overview of how bacteria "talk" to each other and make collective decisions.

Eisenbach, M. (2004). *Chemotaxis*. Imperial College Press.
> The most comprehensive monograph on bacterial and eukaryotic chemotaxis; essential reference for Unit 3.

Ben-Jacob, E., Shapira, Y., & Tauber, A. I. (2006). Seeking the foundations of cognition in bacteria: From Schrödinger's negative entropy to latent information. *Physica A*, 359, 495–524.
> Argues that bacteria exhibit cognitive behaviours including sensing, memory, and social learning; uses an information-theoretic framework.

Stoodley, P., Sauer, K., Davies, D. G., & Costerton, J. W. (2002). Biofilms as complex differentiated communities. *Annual Review of Microbiology*, 56, 187–209.
> Establishes the biofilm as a differentiated, multicellular-like community with division of labour; essential for understanding collective cognition in bacteria.

Lyon, P. (2015). The cognitive cell: Bacterial behaviour reconsidered. *Frontiers in Microbiology*, 6, 264.
> Applies the biogenic approach directly to bacterial cognition; argues that bacteria exhibit genuine cognitive capacities including learning and decision-making.

## Unit 4 — Protist Intelligence: Unicellular Problem-Solving

Nakagaki, T., Yamada, H., & Tóth, Á. (2000). Maze-solving by an amoeboid organism. *Nature*, 407, 470.
> The paper that brought *Physarum polycephalum* to the attention of the cognitive science community; reports maze-solving by a single-celled organism with no nervous system.

Tero, A., Takagi, S., Saigusa, T., Ito, K., Bebber, D. P., Fricker, M. D., ... & Nakagaki, T. (2010). Rules for biologically inspired adaptive network design. *Science*, 327(5964), 439–442.
> Reports that *Physarum* can recapitulate the Tokyo rail network; demonstrates that biological network optimisation can match or exceed human engineering.

Saigusa, T., Tero, A., Nakagaki, T., & Kuramoto, Y. (2008). Amoebae anticipate periodic events. *Physical Review Letters*, 100(1), 018101.
> Shows that *Physarum* can anticipate rhythmic environmental stimuli, suggesting a form of temporal memory and predictive behaviour without neurons.

Beisson, J., & Sonneborn, T. M. (1965). Cytoplasmic inheritance of the organization of the cell cortex in *Paramecium aurelia*. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 53(2), 275–282.
> A landmark paper on cytoplasmic inheritance in *Paramecium*; demonstrates that information is stored in cellular structure, not just DNA.

Bonner, J. T. (2009). *The Social Amoebae: The Biology of Cellular Slime Molds*. Princeton University Press.
> A comprehensive account of the biology and social behaviour of *Dictyostelium* and related organisms; written by the leading expert in the field.

Ford, B. J. (2009). *Cellular Intelligence*. Cambridge Scholars Publishing.
> Documents a wide range of intelligent-seeming behaviours in single-celled organisms, arguing for a broad extension of the concept of cognition.

## Unit 5 — Plant Cognition: Roots, Shoots, and Signals

Calvo, P., & Trewavas, A. (2023). Cognition and behaviour: Two approaches to the study of plant intelligence. *Plant Signaling & Behavior*, 18(1), 2158417.
> A focused debate between two leading plant cognition researchers about the appropriate framework for interpreting plant behaviour; essential for understanding the controversies in the field.

Trewavas, A. (2003). Aspects of plant intelligence. *Annals of Botany*, 92(1), 1–20.
> One of the first major statements of the plant intelligence hypothesis by a leading plant biologist; sparked enormous debate and helped establish the field.

Baluška, F., & Levin, M. (2016). On having no head: Cognition throughout biological systems. *Frontiers in Psychology*, 7, 902.
> A joint paper by the two central figures of basal cognition; argues for continuity of cognitive capacities from bacteria through plants to animals.

Gagliano, M., Renton, M., Depczynski, M., & Mancuso, S. (2014). Experience teaches plants to learn faster and forget slower in environments where it matters. *Oecologia*, 175(1), 63–72.
> Reports associative learning in *Pisum sativum* (peas) using a Pavlovian conditioning paradigm; one of the most contested but discussed papers in plant cognition.

Darwin, C., & Darwin, F. (1880). *The Power of Movement in Plants*. John Murray.
> Darwin's late work on plant movement; anticipates many themes of modern plant cognition research and is frequently cited as a historical precedent.

Karban, R. (2015). *Plant Sensing and Communication*. University of Chicago Press.
> The most comprehensive current review of how plants sense their environment and communicate with each other and with animals; authoritative reference for Unit 5.

Brenner, E. D., Stahlberg, R., Mancuso, S., Vivanco, J., Baluška, F., & Van Volkenburgh, E. (2006). Plant neurobiology: An integrated view of plant signaling. *Trends in Plant Science*, 11(8), 413–419.
> Introduces and defends the term "plant neurobiology"; provides an overview of how electrical and chemical signals are integrated in plants.

## Unit 6 — Fungal Intelligence: Networks, Memory, and Mycelia

Stamets, P. (2005). *Mycelium Running: How Mushrooms Can Help Save the World*. Ten Speed Press.
> A popular account of fungal biology and ecology by the leading advocate for mycelial intelligence; accessible introduction to fungal network properties.

Adamatzky, A. (2016). *Advances in Physarum Machines: Sensing and Computing with Slime Mould*. Springer.
> A technical volume documenting computational applications of *Physarum* and fungal networks; essential for the computational perspective on fungal intelligence.

Merlin, C. (2015). Fungal networks and information transfer. *Current Opinion in Microbiology*, 26, 34–39.
> Reviews evidence for signal propagation through fungal networks, including electrical signals; makes the case for fungal networks as distributed information processors.

Heaton, L., López, E., Maini, P. K., Fricker, M. D., & Jones, N. S. (2010). Growth-induced mass flows in fungal networks. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*, 277(1698), 3265–3274.
> Mathematical modelling of mass flow in fungal hyphae; establishes the physical basis for long-range signal transmission in mycelial networks.

Babikova, Z., Gilbert, L., Bruce, T. J. A., Birkett, M., Caulfield, J. C., Woodcock, C., ... & Johnson, D. (2013). Underground signals carried through common mycelial networks warn neighbouring plants of aphid attack. *Ecology Letters*, 16(7), 835–843.
> Demonstrates that mycorrhizal networks transmit warning signals between plants; supports the "wood wide web" hypothesis.

## Unit 7 — Collective Intelligence: Swarms, Colonies, and Crowds

Seeley, T. D. (2010). *Honeybee Democracy*. Princeton University Press.
> A landmark study of decision-making in honeybee swarms; documents how colonies make house-selection decisions through a decentralised democratic process without a central controller.

Camazine, S., Deneubourg, J.-L., Franks, N. R., Sneyd, J., Theraulaz, G., & Bonabeau, E. (2001). *Self-Organization in Biological Systems*. Princeton University Press.
> The definitive textbook on self-organisation in biological collectives; covers ant colonies, bee swarms, fish schools, and slime molds.

Hölldobler, B., & Wilson, E. O. (2009). *The Superorganism: The Beauty, Elegance, and Strangeness of Insect Societies*. W. W. Norton.
> Wilson and Hölldobler's account of social insects as superorganisms; argues for collective-level selection and cognition in social insect colonies.

Couzin, I. D. (2009). Collective cognition in animal groups. *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 13(1), 36–43.
> Reviews the mechanisms and functional properties of collective cognition in animal groups; one of the key theoretical papers framing collective intelligence from a cognitive science perspective.

Sasaki, T., & Pratt, S. C. (2011). Emergence of group rationality from irrational individuals. *Behavioral Ecology*, 22(2), 276–281.
> Demonstrates that ant colonies can be more rational than individual ants in a group decision-making task; directly relevant to arguments about emergent collective intelligence.

## Unit 8 — The Neural Transition: From Bioelectricity to Nervous Systems

Jekely, G. (2011). Origin and early evolution of neural circuits for the control of ciliary locomotion. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*, 278(1707), 914–922.
> Proposes an evolutionary scenario for the origin of the first nervous systems from pre-existing chemosensory and bioelectric signalling; essential bridge between basal cognition and neuroscience.

Arendt, D. (2008). The evolution of cell types in animals: Emerging principles from molecular studies. *Nature Reviews Genetics*, 9(11), 868–882.
> Reviews evidence for the conservation of cell type identity across animal evolution; relevant to understanding what is conserved and what changes in the neural transition.

Moroz, L. L. (2009). On the independent origins of complex brains and neurons. *Brain, Behavior and Evolution*, 74(3), 177–190.
> Argues for multiple independent origins of nervous systems in animal evolution; undermines the assumption that neural cognition evolved only once and suggests deep continuity with non-neural cognition.

Bhattacharyya, D., & Bhaskaran, K. (2011). From gap junctions to action potentials: The evolution of electrical signalling. *Bioelectricity*, 3(2), 45–62.
> Traces the evolutionary pathway from bioelectric gap junction signalling (present in all cells) to the specialised action potential system of neurons.

## Unit 9 — Theoretical Frameworks

Tononi, G. (2004). An information integration theory of consciousness. *BMC Neuroscience*, 5, 42.
> The original statement of integrated information theory (IIT); proposes that consciousness is identical to integrated information (Φ); influential in basal cognition for its potential applicability to non-neural systems.

Friston, K. (2010). The free-energy principle: A unified brain theory? *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, 11(2), 127–138.
> Friston's canonical statement of the free energy principle; argues that all biological systems act to minimise surprise (free energy); widely applied beyond neuroscience to basal organisms.

Varela, F. J., Thompson, E., & Rosch, E. (1991). *The Embodied Mind: Cognitive Science and Human Experience*. MIT Press.
> The founding text of the enactivist tradition; argues that cognition is inseparable from embodiment and action; essential theoretical background for Unit 9.

Kauffman, S. A. (1993). *The Origins of Order: Self-Organization and Selection in Evolution*. Oxford University Press.
> Develops the theory of Boolean network attractors as a model of cell types and gene regulatory dynamics; foundational for the dynamical systems approach to cognition.

Levin, M. (2021). Bioelectric signaling: Reprogrammable circuits underlying embryogenesis, regeneration, and cancer. *Cell*, 184(8), 1971–1989.
> A comprehensive review of Levin's bioelectric signalling programme; the clearest statement of how bioelectricity encodes and transmits cognitive-like information across biological scales.

## Unit 10 — Embodied Minds

Clark, A., & Chalmers, D. (1998). The extended mind. *Analysis*, 58(1), 7–19.
> The landmark paper proposing that cognitive systems extend beyond the brain to include the body and environment; foundational for understanding plant and fungal cognition as genuine extended cognition.

Chemero, A. (2009). *Radical Embodied Cognitive Science*. MIT Press.
> Argues for a non-representational, ecological approach to cognition; highly relevant to understanding basal cognitive systems that do not obviously use internal representations.

---

## Unit 11 — AI Implications

Nakagaki, T., Yamada, H., & Ueda, T. (2000). Interaction between cell shape and contraction pattern in the *Physarum* plasmodium. *Biophysical Chemistry*, 84(3), 195–204.
> Documents the mechanism of *Physarum* network optimisation at the biophysical level; inspirational for biomimetic algorithms in AI and network science.

Dorigo, M., & Stützle, T. (2004). *Ant Colony Optimization*. MIT Press.
> The foundational text on ant-colony-inspired algorithms; demonstrates how the stigmergic intelligence of ant colonies can be formalised and applied to computational optimisation problems.

---

## Unit 12 — Philosophy and Ethics

Allen, C., & Trestman, M. (2021). Animal cognition. *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*. https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/cognition-animal/
> A comprehensive philosophical overview of animal cognition that frames many of the definitional questions relevant to basal cognition.

Nagel, T. (1974). What is it like to be a bat? *Philosophical Review*, 83(4), 435–450.
> The classic paper on the hard problem of consciousness; arguing that there is something it is like to be a bat establishes that phenomenal experience cannot be captured by third-person description. Relevant to the question of plant and bacterial phenomenology.

---

## Unit 13 — Frontiers

Levin, M., & Dennett, D. C. (2020). Cognition all the way down. *Aeon Magazine*. https://aeon.co/essays/how-to-understand-cells-tissues-and-organisms-as-agents-with-agendas
> A popular but intellectually rigorous essay co-authored by Levin and Dennett; argues for a continuity of cognitive agency from the cell level to the organism level. Excellent introductory reading for the frontiers discussion.

Margulis, L. (1998). *Symbiotic Planet: A New Look at Evolution*. Basic Books.
> Margulis's account of the central role of symbiosis (and especially endosymbiosis) in evolution; directly relevant to the emergence of complex eukaryotic cells and ultimately to the evolution of cognition.
