# Epilogue: The Strangeness of Life

Return, one more time, to the slime mold.

It is 1999, a laboratory in Nagoya. A researcher named Toshiyuki Nakagaki places a fragment of *Physarum polycephalum* at one entrance of a maze. At the other entrance, and at several dead ends, he places oat flakes — food. The slime mold extends its pseudopods into the labyrinth. It explores. It fills branches that lead nowhere, then withdraws from them. Within hours, it has found the shortest path. The organism has no nervous system, no neurons, no brain. It has never been in a maze before. It will not remember, in any ordinary sense, that it has solved this one. And yet the solution is there, written in the body of the organism itself: a single thick tube tracing the optimal route, everything else retracted, the dead ends abandoned.

When Nakagaki published this result in *Nature* in 2000, many readers experienced it as a curiosity — a charming puzzle, a neat trick of chemistry and physics. What we have tried to show in this book is that it is something much more than that. It is a window onto a fact about life that we have been slow to see clearly: that the capacities we have called cognitive — sensing an environment, integrating information across space and time, choosing a course of action — are not a late addition to the story of life, not a crown placed on the edifice after the walls were built. They are woven into the fabric of living organization from the beginning.

The bacterium moving up a glucose gradient is doing something that the slime mold navigating a maze is also doing, that the mycorrhizal network allocating phosphorus in response to shifting supply is also doing, that the root tip bending toward water through darkness is also doing. Something that you, reading this sentence, are also doing, though with such elaboration — such layers of memory and language and anticipation — that the kinship is easy to miss. These organisms are all sensing their worlds, integrating what they sense, and acting in ways that serve the improbable, ongoing project of their own existence. The differences between them are real and significant, and they matter enormously for how we study these organisms and what we can claim about them. But they are differences of degree, not differences of kind.

This is not a comfortable conclusion. It requires us to revise the cartography of mind that most of us carry without quite knowing we carry it — the one that places cognition firmly inside the skull, that treats the nervous system as the ticket of entry to the cognitive club, that sees awareness and intelligence looking down from the pinnacle of the vertebrate brain at the merely mechanical world below. That map was never a neutral description of nature. It was an assumption, dressed up as a discovery. And the assumption, as the evidence has accumulated, has not held.

We do not know, and this book has not pretended to know, whether a slime mold experiences anything. Whether there is something it is like to be a *Physarum* plasmodium finding its way through a labyrinth. Whether a bacterium, navigating a chemical gradient by means of a molecular memory older than the Cambrian, has any flicker of what we would call sensation. These questions are real, and they are hard, and they are not answered by any of the evidence we have reviewed. The hard problem of consciousness is hard precisely because no amount of functional description — however detailed, however precise — settles it. We have been honest about this throughout, and we remain agnostic at the close.

But here is what we do know. We know that cognition, functionally understood, is ancient and widespread. We know that bacteria make decisions — molecular decisions, logical decisions — using mechanisms that are exquisitely sensitive, context-dependent, and adaptive. We know that cells integrate information across multiple channels simultaneously and produce outputs that could not be predicted from any single input. We know that organisms without nervous systems solve problems, learn from experience, remember the past, and allocate resources across networks in ways that maximize their own persistence. We know that the boundary between organism and environment is more permeable than we thought, that the boundary between individual and community is more provisional than we thought, and that the boundary between cognitive and non-cognitive — wherever exactly we choose to draw it — lies much further down the tree of life than we thought, perhaps all the way down to its roots.

The field of basal cognition is young. Its theoretical frameworks are still competing, its methodological standards are still being negotiated, and some of its most striking empirical claims remain the subject of active replication and debate. This is not a weakness — it is what a young and vital field looks like. The questions it asks are among the oldest in human inquiry. What is intelligence? What is mind? What is life, and what is life doing when it does what it does? We have not answered these questions in this book. We have tried, instead, to equip the reader to ask them better: with a clearer sense of what the evidence shows, what it does not show, and where the genuinely open ground is.

We know where to look for answers. Not only in the elaborate neural architectures of vertebrate brains — not only in the prefrontal cortex and the hippocampus and the cortical columns that have so long monopolized the attention of cognitive science. But also in the biochemical gradients of bacteria navigating a chemical world that dwarfs in complexity anything we have built. In the electrical fields threading through developing embryos, encoding the future shape of a body in the language of voltage. In the mycelial networks threading through the soil of every forest on Earth, managing resources and resolving conflicts and maintaining ancient relationships across distances that make the word "individual" ring hollow. In the root tips pushing through darkness and clay toward water, integrating chemical signals and gravitational cues and the mechanical resistance of the soil into a trajectory that is, whatever else we call it, directed.

The slime mold does not know it solved the maze. Or perhaps it does, in some sense we cannot yet access or articulate. The question remains open. The work begins.

We began with a slime mold solving a maze. We end with something much larger: the recognition that the boundaries we drew between life and mind, between intelligence and mere chemistry, between cognitive agents and non-cognitive matter, were not discoveries about nature. They were assumptions about ourselves.
