# Introduction: The Reach of Mind

## A Maze, a Mold, and a Question

In the year 2000, a paper appeared in the journal *Nature* that stopped many scientists mid-sentence. Its title was modest: "Maze-Solving by an Amoeboid Organism." Its subject was *Physarum polycephalum* — a plasmodial slime mold, a single multinucleate cell that spreads across forest floors digesting dead organic matter. Its result was, to many, astonishing.

Toshiyuki Nakagaki and colleagues placed the slime mold at the entrance to a maze. Food — oat flakes — was placed at the exit. The organism, with no nervous system, no neurons, no synapses, and no brain, explored the maze. Over the course of several hours, it solved it — not by luck, but by a process of active exploration and retraction that found and reinforced the shortest path (Nakagaki, Yamada, & Tóth, 2000).

This alone might have been explained away. Perhaps the mold was simply following a chemical gradient. Perhaps the result was an artifact of the experimental setup. Interesting, certainly. Conclusive, perhaps not.

But then the experiments multiplied. In 2010, a team showed that *Physarum* could recapitulate the Tokyo rail network — placing food sources at the locations of major cities around Tokyo and allowing the mold to grow connecting tubes between them, the result approximated decades of human engineering for efficiency and resilience (Tero et al., 2010). In 2008, another team showed that *Physarum* could be habituated to rhythmic disturbances and would *anticipate* the next disturbance, adjusting its behavior before the stimulus arrived — a finding that suggested something like prediction, or temporal memory (Saigusa et al., 2008).

None of this involves neurons. None of it involves anything we had, up to that point, considered the machinery of mind.

The question this book addresses is what to make of it.

---

## The Traditional View

The standard answer — the one that dominated cognitive science, neuroscience, and philosophy of mind throughout the twentieth century — would be to say: nothing. Or more precisely: the slime mold is doing something, but that something is not cognition. It is chemistry. It is physics. It is the passive consequence of molecular gradients and fluid dynamics, not the active product of information processing. Cognition, on this view, requires neurons. It requires the specific computational architecture of nervous systems — the action potential, the synapse, the neural circuit. Everything else is mere mechanism.

This is a coherent position. But it is increasingly difficult to maintain in the face of the evidence. The difficulty is not just empirical — it is conceptual. Once we ask, carefully and rigorously, what makes neural processing *cognitive* and mere chemistry *not* cognitive, the answer turns out to be surprisingly elusive. The traditional view, it seems, has been sustained less by an argument than by an assumption.

## The Field of Basal Cognition

"Basal cognition" is the term increasingly used for the systematic study of cognitive-like capacities in organisms lacking nervous systems, and in the non-neural components of organisms that do have them. The term "basal" is meant to evoke both *basic* (fundamental, foundational) and *phylogenetically early* (pertaining to early-diverging lineages).

The field is young. Its practitioners include microbiologists, plant scientists, mycologists, developmental biologists, evolutionary theorists, philosophers of mind, and AI researchers. They do not always agree with each other — about definitions, about mechanisms, about theoretical frameworks, about what the evidence shows. This book treats those disagreements as features rather than bugs: they are evidence that the field is alive, productive, and genuinely engaged with hard questions.

The central claim of the field, stated carefully, is this: **the capacities that we associate with cognition — sensing, integrating information, storing information, making decisions on the basis of past experience, acting on predictions about future states — are present in organisms that predate nervous systems, and in biological processes that occur without neural machinery.** This claim is supported by evidence. What it means — philosophically, ethically, and scientifically — is what this book is about.

---

## Why This Matters

There are at least four reasons why basal cognition matters beyond academic interest.

**It matters for biology.** Understanding how non-neural organisms solve the problems of existence — how they navigate, learn, communicate, cooperate — is fundamental to understanding life. We have built an enormous science of neural cognition. We have barely begun to build a science of non-neural cognition, even though the latter is more ancient, more widespread, and quantitatively more prevalent on Earth than the former.

**It matters for medicine.** The cognitive capacities of cells — their ability to sense their context, integrate signals, make decisions about growth and death, and coordinate with neighboring cells — are directly relevant to cancer biology, to regenerative medicine, and to our understanding of development. Levin's work on bioelectricity (Levin, 2012; 2021) suggests that disruptions in the information-processing capacities of cells and tissues underlie a wide range of pathologies.

**It matters for artificial intelligence.** The dominant paradigm in AI has been to build intelligence from the top down — by designing systems, writing rules, and training models on human-generated data. Basal cognition suggests an alternative: build intelligence from the bottom up, the way evolution did, by creating systems that sense, act, and adapt in the world. The slime mold's network optimization algorithms are, in some respects, more robust and efficient than human-engineered equivalents.

**It matters for ethics.** If cognition — and perhaps, on some accounts, experience — is a property of organisms across the spectrum of life, then our ethical frameworks, which have largely been calibrated to the presence of a nervous system, may be inadequate. This is not a comfortable conclusion. But intellectual honesty demands that we take it seriously.

## A Note on Vocabulary

One of the recurring dangers in this field is the misuse of language. When we say that a bacterium "decides" to form a biofilm, or that a slime mold "remembers" a previous experience, we risk two opposite errors: (1) projecting human mentality onto processes that have nothing to do with human experience — anthropomorphism; and (2) using scare quotes so aggressively that we deny the legitimate use of functional language where it is warranted — what we might call eliminativist overcaution.

This book navigates between these errors by using the vocabulary of cognition in what philosophers call a *functional* sense. When we say that a bacterium "decides," we mean that it has a mechanism for evaluating competing signals and selecting among possible behaviors in a way that is sensitive to environmental conditions — not that it experiences deliberation. When we say a slime mold "remembers," we mean that its current behavior is modified by past experience in a way that is functionally equivalent to what we call memory in neural systems — not that it has a hippocampus.

This disciplined functional language is not a hedge. It is the correct language for describing cognitive phenomena at the level of mechanism, before (and often independent of) any question about subjective experience. Most of this book operates at this level. Where questions of experience become relevant — especially in Unit I and Unit XII — the book flags them explicitly and treats them with the care they deserve.

## How to Use This Book

This book can be read in several ways:

- **Linearly**, from Unit I to Unit XIII, as a complete course in basal cognition. This is the recommended approach for students new to the field.
- **Modularly**, with Units I and IX as the philosophical backbone and other units drawn on as needed. This is the recommended approach for researchers who already have a background in one or more of the empirical areas.
- **As a reference**, using the Key Concepts and Key Researchers sections at the end of each chapter and the consolidated appendices at the back.

**A note for instructors:** Each chapter includes a set of exercises divided into three categories: *Reflection and Discussion* (conceptual questions); *Thought Experiments* (original philosophical puzzles designed to test intuitions); and *Laboratory Investigations* (empirical exercises, some of which can be performed with basic equipment). The laboratory exercises are designed to be genuinely illuminating, not merely illustrative.
