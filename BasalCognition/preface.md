# Preface

There is a particular kind of intellectual discomfort that comes with learning something that requires you to revise not just a belief, but the framework by which you've been generating beliefs. Learning that the Earth orbits the Sun is one thing. Learning that "the Earth" and "the Sun" are themselves embedded in a much larger structure that has no center — that is something different. It requires not just an update, but a reconceptualization.

This book is about that second kind of learning.

For most of the history of cognitive science and neuroscience, the working assumption has been that cognition is a brain phenomenon. Neurons, synapses, and the specific computational architecture of nervous systems were taken to be necessary conditions for anything we might meaningfully call intelligence, learning, memory, or decision-making. This was not a careless assumption. It rested on an enormous body of evidence and made productive research possible for decades.

But the assumption has cracked. Evidence has accumulated — from microbiology, from plant biology, from mycology, from developmental biology — that the core capacities we associate with cognition appear in organisms that predate nervous systems by billions of years. Bacteria navigate chemical gradients. Slime molds optimize networks. Plants habituate to repeated stimuli and appear to remember the experience. Cells make decisions. Bodies pursue goals.

The question this book addresses is not whether these capacities exist. They do. The question is what kind of thing they are, and what their existence means for our understanding of mind.

---

I came to this subject as many do: by accident, and then with increasing obsession. I was trained in [relevant field], and for years I operated comfortably within the brain-centric paradigm. Then I encountered Michael Levin's work on bioelectricity and morphogenesis — specifically, the discovery that planarian flatworms regenerate into two-headed animals when their ion channels are disrupted, and that this two-headed body plan can be made stable and heritable (Levin et al., 2019). The idea that a body plan — a *target morphology* — could be stored in bioelectric gradients and actively pursued by cells with no neural machinery struck me as something that deserved a name. And the name it deserved was *cognitive*.

That was the crack. From there, the field opened up.

---

This book is designed to be a rigorous companion to a rapidly developing field. It is not a manifesto, and it takes no position on the hardest philosophical questions — whether slime molds are conscious, whether bacteria have inner experience, whether the continuum of cognition runs smoothly from bacterium to human. These questions are genuinely open, and this book treats them as such.

What the book does argue — carefully, with evidence — is that the standard definition of cognition is too narrow to capture what biology actually does. And that expanding it, carefully, is not anthropomorphism. It is good science.

A few notes on what you will and won't find here:

**You will find:** rigorous philosophical analysis of the definitional foundations; comprehensive coverage of the empirical literature from bacteria to insects; serious engagement with the theoretical frameworks that the basal cognition community has developed; original thought experiments and laboratory exercises designed to help you test your intuitions; and citations for every non-obvious empirical and philosophical claim.

**You will not find:** overclaiming. This book is careful about what the evidence supports. Where the evidence is suggestive but not decisive, it says so. Where experiments have not been replicated or remain controversial, it says so. The field of basal cognition has a credibility problem it has partly created for itself through overstated claims, and this book tries to be part of the solution to that problem, not part of the problem.

---

A final note on the stakes. This is not merely an academic exercise. If cognition is a biological phenomenon rather than a neural one — if something like experience, however minimal and alien, is woven into the fabric of life — then the ethical implications are substantial. Our relationship to the living world, to agriculture, to medicine, to the built environment of microbes and fungi and plants we inhabit without noticing, may need to change. Not dramatically, not immediately. But thoughtfully, and with open eyes.

This book is an attempt to open some eyes.
