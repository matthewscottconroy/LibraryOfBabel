# Unit I: First Principles — What Is Intelligence? What Is Life?

---

> *"The question is not whether plants, bacteria, and cells are 'intelligent' in the way we are. The question is whether our intelligence is a special case of something far more ancient, far more general, and far more strange than we have dared to imagine."*

---

## Unit Introduction

Before we can ask where intelligence comes from, we must ask what it is. Before we can ask how life thinks, we must ask what life does. These may sound like warm-up questions — the kind of preliminary definitional business that textbooks dispatch in a few paragraphs before getting to the real content. They are not. They are among the hardest questions in all of philosophy and science, and the answers we give — or fail to give — determine everything that follows.

The field of basal cognition is sometimes criticized for moving too fast: for asserting that bacteria think, that plants learn, that slime molds remember, without first establishing what thinking, learning, and remembering actually are. This criticism has merit. The field has, at times, reached for dramatic vocabulary before it had earned the right to use it. This unit is an attempt to earn that right.

We will proceed in three chapters, each addressing a foundational problem.

**Chapter 1** takes up the question of intelligence itself. What has been meant by intelligence across the history of thought? What are the strengths and weaknesses of the major definitions? And is there a definition that is biologically honest — one that neither restricts cognition arbitrarily to neural systems, nor dissolves it so broadly that it becomes meaningless? We argue for a *functional* definition of cognition grounded in the sensing-integrating-acting triad, and we defend this definition against the most serious objections.

**Chapter 2** takes up the question of life. If cognition is a biological phenomenon, we need to understand what makes something biological — and specifically, what makes life the kind of thing that would *need* to cognize. We introduce autopoiesis, thermodynamics, and the origins-of-life research as background for understanding why sensing, integrating, and acting are not optional extras for living systems, but logical necessities given what life is.

**Chapter 3** confronts the hardest problem: the relationship between functional cognition and subjective experience. Philosophers call this the "hard problem of consciousness." We cannot responsibly discuss whether bacteria "know" their world without at least acknowledging that this question has a harder shadow: whether there is something it is *like* to be a bacterium. We address this question honestly, without pretending it is solved, and we establish the methodological stance — functional agnosticism about experience, paired with commitment to empirical rigor about mechanism — that governs the rest of the book.

---

## The Central Argument of This Unit

The unit builds toward a single, carefully qualified position:

**Cognition, understood functionally as the capacity to sense, integrate information, and act in goal-directed ways based on that integration, is a biological phenomenon that predates and is not restricted to nervous systems. The evidence for this is strong. The philosophical justification for the definition is defensible. The question of whether such functional cognition is accompanied by subjective experience remains open and is treated separately from the question of mechanism.**

This position is not original to this book. It is the considered position of several leading researchers in the field (Lyon, 2006; Barandiaran & Moreno, 2008; Levin & Baluška, 2016; Calvo et al., 2020), and it is the position this book defends, develops, and applies across the following twelve units.

---

## Who This Unit Is For

This unit is essential for all readers. If you are a biologist impatient to get to the empirical content, we ask you to spend time here anyway: the conceptual scaffolding built in this unit will prevent you from misinterpreting the empirical findings later. If you are a philosopher skeptical of the entire enterprise, this unit is where you should focus your skepticism — and where you will find the most direct engagement with your concerns. If you are a student new to both fields, this unit is designed to bring you to the level you need.
