# Chapter 1: The Problem of Defining Intelligence

---

> *"What does it mean to be intelligent? Ask ten scientists and receive twelve answers. Ask an octopus, and it will show you something your definitions never anticipated."*

---

## Chapter Introduction

We begin with a problem that is easier to feel than to solve. Everyone knows, pre-theoretically, what intelligence is: it is what makes humans distinctive, what makes some people better at abstract reasoning than others, what lets animals adapt to novel situations. We recognize it in ourselves, in colleagues, in pets, in birds that use tools, in apes that learn sign language. We also recognize its absence — in reflexes, in crystal growth, in the behavior of a thermostat.

But the moment we try to make this recognition precise, things become complicated very quickly.

Is a thermostat intelligent? It senses temperature and responds to maintain a set point. That sounds like sensing and goal-directed behavior. What about a search algorithm? It explores a space of possibilities and finds solutions to problems. What about a liver cell that detects toxins and initiates a detoxification program? What about an immune cell that remembers a pathogen it has encountered before and mounts a faster response upon re-exposure? As we move from the thermostat to the liver cell, our intuition that we are in the presence of something cognitive increases. But what changes, exactly? What is the relevant difference?

This chapter surveys the history of attempts to answer that question, examines the assumptions embedded in each attempt, and builds toward a definition that is philosophically defensible and biologically useful. Along the way, we will find that most traditional definitions of intelligence carry hidden assumptions — about substrate, about representation, about consciousness — that need to be made explicit before they can be evaluated.

---

## Chapter Roadmap

- **Section 1.1** traces the history of intelligence definitions, from commonsense psychology to psychometric approaches to computational models to ecological and behavioral definitions.

- **Section 1.2** examines the critical distinctions between intelligence, cognition, and mind — three terms that are often used interchangeably but that pick out importantly different things.

- **Section 1.3** develops the concept of goal-directedness as the key functional criterion for cognition, using cybernetics and systems theory as analytical tools.

- **Section 1.4** proposes a biological definition of cognition — the one this book will use — and defends it against the most important objections.

---

## The Chapter's Central Argument

By the end of this chapter, you should understand and be able to evaluate the following argument:

1. Traditional definitions of intelligence are either too narrow (restricted to human or animal cognition) or too broad (applying to any physical system) to be biologically useful.

2. The most defensible biological definition of cognition is functional: a system is cognitive if it can sense relevant features of its environment, integrate this sensory information (potentially with stored information from past interactions), and act on the result in ways that tend to maintain or restore states the system "values" — that is, states required for its continued organization and functioning.

3. This definition is neutral about substrate: it does not require neurons, synapses, or any particular physical implementation. It is also neutral about consciousness: it does not require that there be "something it is like" to be the system in question.

4. Applied to biology, this definition picks out a real and important class of phenomena — not all physical processes, but also not only neural ones.

This is the definition that will govern the rest of the book. Challenge it. Test it. Refine it. The quality of what follows depends on the quality of the foundation.
