# Further Reading and References — Chapter 1

---

## Annotated Bibliography

**Barandiaran, X.E., & Moreno, A. (2008). On what makes certain dynamical systems cognitive: A minimally cognitive organization (MCO) program. *Adaptive Behavior*, 16(5), 293–309.**

The most technically rigorous account of minimal cognition in the literature. Barandiaran and Moreno work from the concept of biological autonomy outward, identifying the structural conditions that a system must meet to be genuinely (not merely metaphorically) cognitive. Essential reading for anyone who wants to engage with basal cognition at a philosophically serious level. Dense but rewarding.

---

**Lyon, P. (2006). The biogenic approach to cognition. *Cognitive Processing*, 7(1), 11–29.**

Lyon's foundational paper for the biogenic approach. Proposes a reorientation of the question: instead of asking whether non-neural organisms meet the standards of cognitive science, ask what cognitive capacities are logically required by life itself. This methodological shift is one of the most important moves in the field. Clear, well-argued, and widely cited.

---

**Lyon, P. (2015). The cognitive cell: Bacterial behavior reconsidered. *Frontiers in Microbiology*, 6, 264.**

The application of the biogenic approach specifically to bacteria. Lyon works through the chemotaxis system, the SOS response, and other bacterial behaviors, arguing systematically that they satisfy the criteria for minimal cognition. A model of careful interdisciplinary argument: philosophically rigorous and biologically accurate.

---

**Maturana, H.R., & Varela, F.J. (1980). *Autopoiesis and Cognition: The Realization of the Living*. D. Reidel.**

The foundational text for the claim that cognition and life are inseparable. Maturana and Varela argue that any living system, by virtue of being living, is already cognitive in the sense of enacting a domain of interactions with its environment. Dense and technical, but rewarding for those willing to work through it. The second part of the book ("Cognition") is particularly relevant to Chapter 1.

---

**Varela, F.J., Thompson, E., & Rosch, E. (1991). *The Embodied Mind: Cognitive Science and Human Experience*. MIT Press.**

The book that introduced enactivism to cognitive science. Varela, Thompson, and Rosch argue against representationalism and for a view of cognition as the enactment of a world through organism-environment coupling. Accessible by comparison with *Autopoiesis and Cognition*, and particularly strong on the relationship between phenomenology and cognitive science. The opening chapters on the problem with the classical cognitive science program are still among the best critiques of representationalism available.

---

**Thompson, E. (2007). *Mind in Life: Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of Mind*. Harvard University Press.**

Evan Thompson's systematic development of the enactivist program, with particular attention to the relationship between biological life and phenomenal consciousness. Chapters 5–7, on sense-making, autopoiesis, and the living body, are most relevant to this chapter. Thompson is one of the few writers who manages to be both philosophically rigorous and genuinely engaged with the biology.

---

**Godfrey-Smith, P. (1996). *Complexity and the Function of Mind in Nature*. Cambridge University Press.**

Godfrey-Smith's early work on minimal cognition and the evolution of mind. Develops a biologically and philosophically careful account of what minimal cognition requires, and traces its evolution from simple sensing systems to complex neural architectures. Still one of the best available treatments of the question of where cognition begins.

---

**Wiener, N. (1948). *Cybernetics: Or, Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine*. MIT Press.**

The founding text of cybernetics, where Wiener shows that goal-directed behavior can be mechanistically characterized in terms of negative feedback. Parts of the book are now dated, but Chapter 1 ("Newtonian and Bergsonian Time") and the core chapters on feedback and control remain as clarifying as ever. An essential historical text for understanding the mechanistic basis of goal-directedness.

---

**Dennett, D.C. (1991). *Consciousness Explained*. Little, Brown.**

Dennett's comprehensive (and controversial) naturalistic account of the mind. Most relevant to Chapter 1 for its arguments about the intentional stance — the strategy of interpreting a system's behavior by treating it as rational. Dennett argues that we are entitled to adopt the intentional stance toward any system when doing so helps predict and explain its behavior, regardless of substrate. This view has important implications for basal cognition claims.

---

**Clark, A. (1997). *Being There: Putting Brain, Body, and World Together Again*. MIT Press.**

Clark's accessible introduction to embodied and embedded cognition. Argues against the brain-centric picture of cognition and for a view in which cognitive processes extend into the body and environment. Particularly good on the failure of classical AI and the successes of reactive, embodied robotics. A good bridge between traditional cognitive science and the 4E approaches discussed in Unit X.

---

**Gould, S.J. (1981). *The Mismeasure of Man*. W.W. Norton.**

A critical examination of the history of psychometric intelligence testing, with particular attention to the ways in which cultural assumptions have been built into ostensibly objective measures. Relevant to Chapter 1 for its demonstration of how implicit assumptions about what intelligence *is* have shaped what gets measured. A cautionary tale about the dangers of operationalizing a concept before understanding it.

---

**Nagel, T. (1974). What is it like to be a bat? *Philosophical Review*, 83(4), 435–450.**

The paper that launched a thousand philosophy seminars on consciousness. Nagel argues that there is something it is like to be a bat — a subjective quality to bat experience — and that this subjective character is not captured by any objective, functional description. Directly relevant to the distinction between functional cognition and phenomenal experience that Chapter 1 establishes. Required reading before Chapter 3.
