# Key Concepts — Chapter 1: Defining Intelligence

---

**Cognition (functional definition)** — The set of processes by which a system senses features of its environment or internal state, integrates this sensory information (potentially with stored information from past interactions), and acts on the result in ways that tend to maintain or restore the states required for its continued organization. *Why it matters*: This is the working definition used throughout this book. It is substrate-neutral (no requirement for neurons), biologically grounded (tied to the imperatives of living systems), and empirically tractable.

---

**Sensing-Integrating-Acting (SIA) Triad** — The three-component functional architecture that the basal cognition community identifies as the minimal structure of a cognitive process: (1) detection of relevant environmental or internal signals, (2) combination of these signals (possibly with stored information) in a manner that distinguishes among possible courses of action, and (3) production of behavior directed by this combined assessment. *Why it matters*: The SIA triad provides a concrete, testable framework for identifying cognitive processes in any biological system.

---

**Minimally Cognitive Organization (MCO)** — Barandiaran and Moreno's (2008) technical account of the necessary and sufficient conditions for a biological system to exhibit minimal cognition. Requires: (1) autonomous self-organization (autopoiesis), (2) a sensorimotor loop coupling sensors and effectors, and (3) a modulating layer that regulates the sensorimotor loop by integrating multiple information sources. *Why it matters*: The MCO framework is the most technically rigorous account of minimal cognition available and provides a benchmark for empirical claims.

---

**Goal-Directedness** — The property of a system that maintains particular states or achieves particular outcomes despite disturbance, using flexible means and negative feedback control. In the cybernetic tradition (Wiener, 1948), goal-directedness is fully explicable in mechanistic terms without appeal to intention or consciousness. *Why it matters*: Goal-directedness is the mechanistic basis of all purposive-looking biological behavior, and its presence in every living system is the deepest argument for the claim that cognition and life are inseparable.

---

**Homeostasis** — The maintenance of an organism's internal variables (pH, temperature, osmolarity, etc.) within tolerable ranges through negative feedback control, in the face of external disturbance. Coined by Walter Cannon (1932), following Claude Bernard's concept of the *milieu intérieur*. *Why it matters*: Homeostasis is the simplest and most universal expression of cognitive function in living systems. Every living cell performs homeostasis; no non-living system performs it autonomously.

---

**Biogenic Approach** — Pamela Lyon's (2006) methodological program for studying cognition in biological systems, which begins with what living systems are and need (rather than what human cognition is like) and derives the cognitive capacities that living systems must have. *Why it matters*: The biogenic approach provides the epistemological justification for extending the concept of cognition beyond animals and into the full tree of life.

---

**Functional Claim vs. Phenomenological Claim** — A functional claim describes what a system does in terms of its input-output relationships and organizational role ("the bacterium senses aspartate"). A phenomenological claim makes an assertion about subjective experience ("the bacterium feels desire for aspartate"). These are logically independent: functional cognition can in principle occur without phenomenal experience, and the evidence for functional claims is entirely different from the evidence for phenomenological ones. *Why it matters*: Conflating these two kinds of claims is one of the most common errors in the basal cognition literature. Keeping them separate is the foundation of intellectual honesty in this field.

---

**Intrinsic vs. Derived Intentionality** — A state has intrinsic intentionality if it is genuinely "about" something independent of how it is interpreted. A state has derived intentionality if its "aboutness" is assigned by an external interpreter. The thermostat's reference to temperature is derived (assigned by the engineer). Human beliefs have intrinsic intentionality. Where biological organisms fall on this spectrum is contested. *Why it matters*: The answer determines whether biological cognition is "real" cognition or merely a useful fiction — one of the deepest questions in the philosophy of mind with direct implications for basal cognition.

---

**Anthropomorphism** — The error of projecting specifically human (or animal) characteristics onto systems that lack them — using mentalistic language in ways that imply subjective experience or intentional structure that the evidence does not support. *Why it matters*: One of the two poles between which responsible basal cognition research must navigate. Overclaiming cognitive richness in simple organisms is anthropomorphism; the opposite error is eliminativist overcaution.

---

**Eliminativist Overcaution** — The opposite error from anthropomorphism: refusing to use functional cognitive vocabulary where it is warranted, denying cognitive status to systems that demonstrably sense, integrate, and act in ways that maintain their organization, on the grounds that they "merely" do chemistry or physics. *Why it matters*: This error is equally damaging to the field as anthropomorphism. Both errors prevent accurate description of biological phenomena.

---

**Umwelt** — Jakob von Uexküll's (1909) concept of the "self-world" of an organism: the set of features of the environment that the organism can detect and respond to, constituted by the organism's own sensory and motor capacities. Each organism has its own Umwelt, appropriate to its ecological niche. *Why it matters*: The Umwelt concept grounds the ecological approach to intelligence and reminds us that cognition is always relative to a particular organism-environment relationship, not an absolute capacity.

---

**Multiple Realizability** — The philosophical thesis, associated with Hilary Putnam (1967), that the same mental or cognitive state can be implemented in many different physical substrates. A thought can be realized in a human brain, in principle in a silicon computer, and (if functional definitions are correct) in a bacterial signaling network. *Why it matters*: Multiple realizability is the philosophical foundation for substrate-neutral definitions of cognition. If cognition is multiply realizable, then the absence of neurons does not in principle exclude a system from being cognitive.
