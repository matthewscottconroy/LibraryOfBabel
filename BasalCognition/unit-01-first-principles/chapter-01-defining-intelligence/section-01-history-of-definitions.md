# Section 1.1: A Brief and Turbulent History of Intelligence Definitions

---

## Section Introduction

The history of intelligence definitions is not a steady march toward clarity. It is more like a series of ambitious attempts, each capturing something real and missing something important, each generating new tools and new blind spots. Understanding this history is not merely an exercise in intellectual housekeeping. It prepares us to see why a biologically adequate definition of cognition is harder to achieve than it looks — and why the basal cognition community's definitional choices are principled responses to genuine difficulties, not arbitrary expansions of a convenient concept.

---

## 1.1.1 Folk Psychology and the Commonsense View

The commonsense notion of intelligence is prior to any scientific theory of it. In English, the word traces to the Latin *intelligentia* — understanding, discernment — from *inter* (between) and *legere* (to choose, gather). To be intelligent, in the original sense, is to discern: to distinguish what matters from what doesn't, to pick out the signal from the noise.

This folk-psychological notion has several features worth noting. First, it is inherently *contrastive*: intelligence is defined by contrast with its absence, with stupidity, confusion, or mere mechanism. Second, it is inherently *normative*: to call something intelligent is not merely to describe it but to evaluate it — to say it is doing well at some task. Third, and most relevant to our purposes, it is historically assumed to be *restricted to agents with minds* — to beings that in some sense *know* what they are doing.

This last assumption is the one that basal cognition challenges. But before we challenge it, we need to understand why it was made.

The commonsense restriction of intelligence to minded beings reflects a genuine and important intuition: there is a difference between a boulder rolling downhill and a hawk adjusting its flight path to track a rabbit. Both are physical processes. But only one of them seems to involve the hawk *taking into account* information about the rabbit — modeling the rabbit's trajectory, anticipating its turns, adapting behavior accordingly. The intuition is that this "taking into account" is what makes the hawk's behavior cognitive and the boulder's behavior not.

This intuition is correct. Where commonsense psychology goes wrong is in assuming that "taking into account" requires the specific kind of neural machinery that hawks (and humans) happen to use. As we will see, that assumption is not warranted by the intuition itself.

## 1.1.2 Psychometric Intelligence: Binet, Spearman, and the Question of Measurement

The first scientific definition of intelligence was not really a definition at all — it was a measurement strategy. Alfred Binet, working in Paris at the turn of the twentieth century, was commissioned to identify schoolchildren who needed additional educational support. He developed what would become the first intelligence test — a battery of tasks measuring reasoning, memory, and comprehension — not because he had a theory of what intelligence was, but because he needed a practical tool for identifying children who were struggling (Binet & Simon, 1905).

Binet was famously cautious about what his tests measured. He explicitly warned against treating intelligence as a fixed, innate quantity, and against using test scores as anything other than a practical educational diagnostic (Binet, 1909, as cited in Gould, 1981). These cautions were largely ignored by the psychometric tradition that followed him.

Charles Spearman's contribution was the discovery — using newly developed statistical methods — that performance on a wide variety of cognitive tasks is positively correlated. People who do well on vocabulary tests tend to do well on spatial reasoning tests, on arithmetic tests, on memory tests. Spearman argued that this pattern was best explained by positing a single underlying factor — *g*, or general intelligence — that loaded onto all these tasks to varying degrees (Spearman, 1904).

The *g* factor remains one of the most robust findings in all of psychology (Jensen, 1998; Deary, 2001). It is reliably predictive of a wide range of real-world outcomes, from academic achievement to job performance to health outcomes (Gottfredson, 1997). Whatever *g* measures, it measures something real and important.

But what does it measure? And does it measure intelligence, or one particularly human form of it?

The first problem with the *g*-based definition is that it is inherently anthropocentric. Intelligence tests are designed by humans, for humans, testing specifically human cognitive abilities. The finding that these abilities are correlated within the human population tells us something about the structure of human cognition. It does not tell us what intelligence is as a general phenomenon, any more than a test of speed, endurance, and agility designed by and for cheetahs would tell us what athletic ability means for all animals.

The second problem, which will recur throughout this chapter, is that the psychometric definition offers no mechanistic account. It defines intelligence behaviorally — by performance on certain tasks — without specifying what internal processes generate that performance. This is not necessarily a defect for practical purposes, but it leaves open the question of what we would need to look for in organisms too different from us to sit for a paper-and-pencil test.

## 1.1.3 Multiple Intelligences and the Fragmentation Debate

Howard Gardner's theory of multiple intelligences (1983) was a reaction against the implicit imperialism of the *g*-factor model. Gardner argued that there is no single general intelligence, but rather a set of distinct intelligences — linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, musical, bodily-kinesthetic, interpersonal, intrapersonal, naturalist — each with its own neural substrate, developmental trajectory, and pattern of breakdown following brain damage.

The theory was enormously influential in educational circles and remains so today. Its philosophical motivation was also sound: Gardner was right that the standard definition of intelligence was culturally loaded, that different cultures, different environments, and different evolutionary pressures would select for different cognitive capacities. An octopus's ability to solve a color-matching puzzle in milliseconds, or a bee's ability to navigate by polarized light and perform symbolic communication about spatial locations (von Frisch, 1967), deserve a richer vocabulary than "not that intelligent."

But the theory of multiple intelligences has been heavily criticized on empirical grounds. Psychometric data consistently show that Gardner's "intelligences" are themselves positively correlated, suggesting that *g* is doing real work even within his framework (Waterhouse, 2006). And Gardner's criteria for identifying a genuine "intelligence" — a distinctive neural substrate, a characteristic developmental trajectory, evolutionary plausibility, and others — are neither crisply defined nor consistently applied (Sternberg, 1983).

More fundamentally for our purposes, the theory of multiple intelligences, like the *g*-factor theory, is still a theory of *human* intelligence. It extends the concept somewhat by taking seriously abilities that standardized tests ignore. But it still assumes that intelligence is something that evolved brains have — not something that bacteria, plants, or cells might exhibit.

## 1.1.4 Computational Models: Turing, Shannon, and Intelligence as Information Processing

The twentieth century brought a radically different approach to intelligence: the computational one. The key move was to define intelligence not in terms of a specific substrate (brains, neurons) or a specific output (test scores), but in terms of *functional organization* — the relationship between inputs and outputs, the structure of the information processing that mediates them.

Alan Turing's famous 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" introduced the thought experiment that became known as the Turing Test: if a machine can convince a human interlocutor, in a blind text conversation, that it is human, then the machine is intelligent (Turing, 1950). Turing explicitly argued that questions about the internal substrate of intelligence — whether the machine is made of neurons or silicon — are irrelevant. What matters is the input-output behavior.

This move has enormous philosophical implications. It implicitly endorses what philosophers call *functionalism*: the view that mental states are defined by their functional roles, not by their physical implementation. If intelligence is functional organization, then intelligence can in principle run on any physical substrate. Multiple realizability — the possibility that the same mental process can be implemented in many different physical systems — follows immediately (Putnam, 1967).

The Turing Test definition has well-known problems. It is anthropocentric in a different way from the psychometric definition: it takes *mimicry of human conversational behavior* as the criterion for intelligence, which is both too narrow (many intelligent things cannot have conversations) and potentially too broad (a system might pass the Turing Test through tricks that have nothing to do with understanding). John Searle's Chinese Room argument (1980) made the latter objection with characteristic force: a system can process symbols correctly without thereby understanding them, just as a person in a room following rules for manipulating Chinese symbols outputs correct Chinese without understanding Chinese.

The Chinese Room argument remains contested — Dennett's response (1991) remains a vigorous counterpoint — but it points to a genuine gap in the functional definition: the gap between *behaving as if* one understands and *actually understanding*. This gap, as we will see, runs through the entire field of basal cognition.

Claude Shannon's information theory (Shannon & Weaver, 1949) provided a mathematical framework that seemed to offer a way out of this gap. Information, in Shannon's sense, is a measure of uncertainty reduction. A signal carries information if it changes the probability distribution over possible states of the receiver. This definition is substrate-neutral, precise, and mathematically tractable.

Could we define intelligence in terms of information processing — as the capacity to receive, transform, store, and act on information in adaptive ways? This is roughly the definition implicit in most computational approaches to cognition (see Newell & Simon, 1976, for one influential formulation). And it has a key virtue: it is, in principle, applicable to any system, biological or not.

But it also has a key weakness. In Shannon's technical sense, a rock processing seismic waves "processes information." A thermostat "integrates" information about temperature. A digestive enzyme "responds" to information about substrate concentration. Pure information processing is not yet cognition — unless we specify what *kind* of information processing, in response to what *kinds* of signals, in service of what *kinds* of goals, counts. We need additional constraints. We need, in short, a biological theory.

## 1.1.5 Ecological and Behavioral Definitions: What Animals Actually Do

The ecological and behavioral tradition in intelligence research, associated with figures like Jakob von Uexküll, James Gibson, and, more recently, the proponents of animal cognition and behavioral ecology, takes a different starting point: instead of asking what intelligence is in the abstract, it asks what cognitive capacities animals need in order to survive and reproduce in their actual environments.

Von Uexküll's concept of the *Umwelt* — the subjective environment of an organism, the set of features of the world that the organism is equipped to detect and respond to — is particularly important here (von Uexküll, 1909/1985). Each organism, on this view, inhabits a world that is partly constituted by its own sensory and motor capacities. A tick's Umwelt consists almost entirely of warmth, the scent of butyric acid (the fatty acid found in mammalian skin), and tactile contact. A honeybee's Umwelt includes polarized light, ultraviolet patterns on flowers, and chemical signals from her hivemates. These are not lesser or greater versions of the human Umwelt — they are different Umwelten, each fitted to the ecological niche of the organism in question.

The Umwelt concept has two implications that matter for us. First, it suggests that intelligence should be understood *relative to an organism's life conditions*, not relative to human standards. A bacterium detecting a chemical gradient and moving toward nutrients is being as intelligent as it needs to be, for what it needs to do. Second, it suggests that cognition begins wherever an organism has a discriminating relationship with its environment — wherever some features of the world *matter* to the organism, and the organism *responds* to them in ways that reflect that mattering.

James Gibson's ecological psychology (1979) pushed this further with the concept of *affordances*: the action possibilities offered by the environment to a perceiving organism. Gibson argued that perception is not primarily about building internal representations of the world, but about detecting affordances — what the environment offers for action. This view has important implications for how we think about cognition in simple organisms, as we will see in Section 1.2.

The behavioral and ecological tradition gives us something the psychometric and computational traditions lack: an account of cognition that is *inherently relational* — defined in terms of the relationship between organism and environment, not in terms of internal states alone. This relational character turns out to be crucial for understanding basal cognition.

---

## Section Summary

We have reviewed four major traditions of intelligence definition: folk-psychological, psychometric, computational, and ecological-behavioral. Each captures something real, and each has important limitations:

| Tradition | Key Insight | Key Limitation |
|-----------|------------|----------------|
| Folk-psychological | Intelligence involves "taking into account" | Assumes neural implementation |
| Psychometric | Intelligence is measurable and predictive | Anthropocentric; no mechanistic account |
| Computational | Intelligence is substrate-neutral functional organization | Doesn't specify which functional organizations count |
| Ecological-behavioral | Intelligence is relational, environment-relative | Under-specified about mechanism |

No single tradition offers a complete account. The definition this book will use — developed in Section 1.4 — attempts to integrate the best elements of each: the ecological tradition's relational character, the computational tradition's substrate-neutrality, the behavioral tradition's focus on what organisms actually do, and a mechanistic specification of the kind of information processing that distinguishes cognition from mere physical response.
