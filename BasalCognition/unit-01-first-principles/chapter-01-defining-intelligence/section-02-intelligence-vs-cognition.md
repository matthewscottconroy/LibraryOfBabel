# Section 1.2: Intelligence vs. Cognition vs. Mind — Unpacking the Vocabulary

---

## Section Introduction

Three words — intelligence, cognition, mind — float through the literature of basal cognition often as though they were synonyms. They are not. The distinctions between them matter enormously, because the wrong choice can make a claim either trivially true or obviously false. A bacterium "has a mind" is a claim that almost no serious researcher in this field would make without extensive qualification. A bacterium "exhibits minimal cognition" is a claim that many careful researchers are prepared to defend (Lyon, 2006; Barandiaran & Moreno, 2008). Understanding why requires unpacking the vocabulary.

This section develops a working taxonomy of the relevant concepts, identifies where the real disagreements lie, and prepares the ground for the biological definition introduced in Section 1.4.

---

## 1.2.1 Unpacking the Vocabulary: A Working Taxonomy

Let us begin with the broadest term: **mind**.

In philosophy, "mind" typically refers to the set of cognitive and experiential capacities of a being — its ability to think, perceive, remember, feel, and intend. The term carries a strong implication of subjective experience: there is something it is *like* to have a mind, in Thomas Nagel's famous phrase (Nagel, 1974). Minds, in this sense, are not merely information-processing systems; they are loci of experience, points of view, centers of a phenomenal world.

This is not the concept that basal cognition is primarily concerned with. Whether bacteria have minds in this rich sense — whether there is something it is like to be a bacterium — is a question that the field largely brackets, or addresses only at its philosophical periphery. It is a real and important question, and we will examine it carefully in Chapter 3. But it is not the question that drives the empirical research.

The field is primarily concerned with **cognition** — a narrower and more tractable concept. Cognition, in the sense this book will use, refers to the processes by which an organism gathers, stores, transforms, and acts on information about its environment and internal states. This is a functional concept: it is defined by what the processes do, not by what substrate they run on or whether they are accompanied by experience.

**Intelligence**, in the everyday sense, is a further specification: it refers to the *quality* of cognitive performance — specifically, the capacity to solve novel problems, adapt to new environments, and generalize from past experience to new situations. Intelligence, on this view, comes in degrees: more intelligent systems handle a wider range of situations more effectively.

The working taxonomy is therefore:

| Concept | Core Reference | Substrate Requirement | Experience Requirement |
|---------|---------------|----------------------|------------------------|
| Mind | Experiential subjectivity | None specified | Yes (typically) |
| Cognition | Information processing in service of adaptive behavior | None (functional definition) | No |
| Intelligence | Quality of cognitive performance; problem-solving capacity | None (functional definition) | No |

For most of this book, "cognition" is the operative concept. We ask whether organisms exhibit cognitive processes, not whether they have minds or how intelligent they are.

This matters for a specific reason: **claims about cognition are empirically tractable in a way that claims about mind are not.** We can, in principle, measure whether an organism integrates signals, stores information from past interactions, and uses that information to modulate behavior. We cannot, with current tools, measure whether an organism has subjective experience. This is what philosophers call the hard problem of consciousness, and it is the topic of Chapter 3.

## 1.2.2 The Cognition Problem: Representation, Computation, and the Traditional Picture

The traditional cognitive science picture of cognition, developed through the second half of the twentieth century, rests on three interlocking commitments:

1. **Representationalism**: Cognition involves the manipulation of internal representations — symbols or symbol-like structures that stand for features of the world. Thinking is the computation over these representations. The brain is a representational system; its states have semantic content.

2. **Computationalism**: The relevant operations on representations are computational in the sense of being rule-governed, formal, and (in principle) implementable on a Turing machine. Cognition is the running of a program.

3. **Centralism**: These computations happen inside the organism, in the brain. The environment provides inputs and receives outputs; the cognitive work happens inside.

This picture was productive for decades. It generated research programs in cognitive psychology, artificial intelligence, and linguistics that produced real results. But it was always in tension with certain biological facts, and basal cognition research has amplified those tensions to the point of crisis.

The trouble is this: if cognition requires representations — internal states with determinate semantic content — then we need an account of how biological systems come to have such states. What makes a pattern of neural firing a *representation* of a predator, rather than just a physical state caused by a predator? Philosophers call this the "intentionality problem," and it has never been fully resolved in the representationalist framework.

More specifically for our purposes: if cognition requires representations in the traditional sense, then bacteria almost certainly do not cognize. Their molecular states are causally connected to environmental features, but there is no clear sense in which they "stand for" those features in the way that a symbol stands for what it symbolizes. The molecular state of the chemotaxis system may be exquisitely sensitive to aspartate concentrations, but is it a *representation* of aspartate?

Some philosophers would say yes (Millikan, 1984; Dretske, 1988), on the grounds that the function of that molecular state — what it was selected for — is precisely to carry information about aspartate. On this "teleosemantic" view, representation is a biological concept, definable in terms of biological function, and it applies wherever there is a system whose states have been selected for because they reliably indicate features of the environment.

Others would say no, that representation requires something more — perhaps the capacity for misrepresentation (Fodor, 1987), or a kind of normative structure that mere molecular sensitivity lacks.

This debate has not been settled, and we will not settle it here. But we can note that the basal cognition community has largely sidestepped it by adopting a definition of cognition that is less theoretically loaded. Rather than asking whether an organism has representations, the field asks whether an organism exhibits the right kind of *functional organization* — the sensing-integrating-acting structure that is the topic of Section 1.4.

## 1.2.3 Is Cognition Necessarily Representational? Alternatives to the Standard Picture

The standard picture has been challenged from several directions, some of which are particularly relevant to basal cognition.

**Enactivism** (Varela, Thompson, & Rosch, 1991; Thompson, 2007) argues that cognition is not the internal manipulation of representations, but the *enactment* of a world through organism-environment coupling. On this view, cognition is not inside the organism — it is the dynamic relationship between organism and environment. The bacterium moving up a glucose gradient is not representing glucose internally and then computing the right behavior in response; rather, its sensorimotor coupling with the chemical field *is* the cognitive process. There is no need for an intermediate representational step.

This view has significant advantages for basal cognition: it is immediately applicable to organisms that lack any plausible internal representational system, and it grounds cognition in the biology of sensing and acting rather than in the philosophy of meaning. Its disadvantage is that it is contested: critics argue that enactivism dissolves the explanatory role of internal states rather than explaining it (Adams & Aizawa, 2001).

**Dynamical Systems Theory** approaches to cognition (van Gelder, 1995) make a related move: they argue that cognition is a matter of a system's dynamical trajectory through a state space, not its manipulation of symbolic representations. The cognitive process is the unfolding of a coupled dynamical system over time. This view accommodates non-representational cognition naturally and has been applied to bacterial behavior (see Di Paolo, 2005, for a dynamical systems analysis of bacterial chemotaxis as a cognitive process).

**Radical Embodied Cognitive Science** (Chemero, 2009) pushes further, arguing that cognition is constituted by the body-environment system as a whole, and that representations are not merely unnecessary but positively misleading as explanatory tools.

These alternatives do not agree with each other in detail, and they each face serious objections. But collectively they establish a crucial point: the requirement that cognition be representational is a theoretical commitment, not a datum. It can be revised in the light of evidence and argument. And basal cognition research provides strong motivation for revising it.

## 1.2.4 Minimal Cognition: Finding the Floor

The concept of **minimal cognition** was introduced specifically to address the question of how simple a system can be while still being genuinely cognitive — as opposed to being merely a physical process that responds to its environment (Godfrey-Smith, 1996; Lyon, 2006).

The question matters for the same reason that the question "how small can a living thing be?" matters in biology: it helps identify the essential features of the phenomenon. If we understand what minimal cognition looks like, we understand what cognition essentially is, stripped of the elaborations that evolution has added in particular lineages.

Peter Godfrey-Smith (1996) proposed that minimal cognition requires at least:
1. A system that detects features of its environment
2. Represents those features (in some sense)
3. Uses those representations to control behavior
4. In ways that are relevant to the system's adaptive needs

This is a useful starting point, though the representational requirement (2) is contested, as we have seen.

Pamela Lyon (2006) proposes a "biogenic" approach that grounds the concept of cognition in the biology of living systems rather than in the requirements of cognitive science theory. On her view, cognition is "the set of processes by which a living system registers and responds to information relevant to its continued function and organization." This definition is:
- **Biologically grounded**: it connects cognition to life and its imperatives
- **Substrate-neutral**: it does not require neurons or any particular implementation
- **Non-representational**: it does not require that the registering of information involve internal symbols
- **Functionally specifiable**: it gives us criteria we can in principle apply to real biological systems

The question of where to draw the lower boundary of cognition — what qualifies as "minimal cognition" and what falls below it — is one of the central ongoing debates in the field. We return to it in Chapter 31.

---

## Section Summary

The key distinctions are:

- **Mind** implies experience; **cognition** does not require it
- **Cognition** in the traditional sense requires representation; in the biogenic or enactivist sense it does not
- **Minimal cognition** is the concept designed to find the floor — the simplest system that still deserves the label "cognitive"
- The basal cognition community has largely adopted functional, non-representational, biologically-grounded definitions of cognition that are explicitly designed to be applicable across the tree of life

These are not mere terminological choices. They are theoretical commitments that determine what counts as evidence for cognition in a bacterium or a plant, and what interpretations of that evidence are legitimate.
