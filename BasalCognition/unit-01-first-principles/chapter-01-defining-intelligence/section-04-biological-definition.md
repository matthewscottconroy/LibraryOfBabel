# Section 1.4: Toward a Biological Definition of Cognition

---

## Section Introduction

We have surveyed the history of intelligence definitions, disentangled the concepts of mind, cognition, and intelligence, and developed a framework for thinking about goal-directedness as the mechanistic basis of cognitive behavior. We are now ready to propose the definition that will govern the rest of this book.

This section presents the definition, explains the theoretical choices embedded in it, examines the most serious objections to it, and defends those choices. This is the most philosophically concentrated passage in the book, and it is worth reading slowly. The quality of everything that follows depends on the quality of this foundation.

---

## 1.4.1 The Sensing-Integrating-Acting Triad

The definition this book uses for cognition is functional and biological:

> **A system exhibits cognition to the extent that it (1) senses features of its environment or internal state that are relevant to its continued organization and functioning, (2) integrates this sensory information — potentially with stored information from past interactions — in a manner that distinguishes among possible courses of action, and (3) acts on the result in ways that tend to maintain or restore the states required for its continued organization.**

This is the **sensing-integrating-acting** (SIA) triad. Let us unpack each component.

**Sensing** is the transduction of physical or chemical signals into internal states that carry information about those signals. Crucially, not all of a system's causal interactions with the environment count as sensing: the criterion is that the resulting internal state carry *information* relevant to the system's functioning. A rock being compressed by a glacier is not sensing the glacier; an E. coli cell detecting an aspartate gradient is sensing it, because the detection feeds into a downstream system that modulates behavior in an adaptive way.

**Integration** is the most distinctively cognitive component. It refers to the combination of multiple information sources — from different sensors, from stored representations of past states, from internal monitoring of metabolic state — in a way that is not reducible to any single input. Integration is what allows a system to *weigh* competing signals, to compare current states to past states, and to select among available actions based on a synthesized assessment of the situation. This is what separates even simple cognitive systems from simple reflex arcs.

**Acting** is the production of behavior — changes in the system's internal state or relationship with the environment — that are directed by the integrated assessment. Crucially, the actions must be *relevant to the system's continued organization*: they must tend to maintain the states required for the system to continue functioning as the kind of system it is.

This last clause is the biological anchor of the definition. It ensures that we are not applying the label "cognitive" to any system that happens to process information — a calculator, a weather vane, a thermostat — but only to systems whose information processing is in service of their own continued organization. This is what distinguishes living cognition from mere computation.

## 1.4.2 Barandiaran and Moreno's Minimally Cognitive Organization

The most rigorously developed version of the SIA-style definition is Xabier Barandiaran and Alvaro Moreno's account of the **minimally cognitive organization** (Barandiaran & Moreno, 2008). Their paper is essential reading for anyone working in basal cognition, and we will trace its key moves here.

Barandiaran and Moreno begin with the observation that living systems are *adaptively autonomous* agents: they actively constitute and maintain their own organization in the face of an environment that is indifferent to that organization. This adaptive autonomy is what distinguishes organisms from thermostats: the thermostat's "goal" (maintaining temperature) was assigned by an engineer; the bacterium's goals are constitutive of what it means to be that bacterium.

From this starting point, they propose that a system exhibits minimal cognition when it:
1. Is an *autonomous system* — it constitutes and maintains its own organization through its own processes (autopoiesis, in Maturana & Varela's sense)
2. Has a *sensorimotor loop* — a coupling between its sensors and its effectors that mediates its behavior
3. Has a *modulating layer* — an internal structure that regulates the sensorimotor loop, distinguishing among possible behaviors and selecting among them based on integrated information

The third criterion is crucial. A simple reflex arc — stimulus directly causes response — has a sensorimotor loop but no modulating layer. It cannot distinguish contexts, integrate information from multiple sources, or use stored information. A system with a modulating layer can do all of these things.

Applied to bacteria: E. coli's chemotaxis system has exactly this structure. The methyl-accepting chemoreceptors detect chemical gradients (sensing). The CheA-CheW-CheY phosphorylation cascade, including the methylation adaptation mechanism, integrates the current gradient signal with a short-term memory of recent gradient history (integration with modulation). The flagellar motor switch responds to CheY-P concentration to determine run-or-tumble behavior (acting). On Barandiaran and Moreno's analysis, E. coli satisfies the criteria for minimal cognition (Barandiaran & Moreno, 2008).

This is not an arbitrary definitional move. It is a philosophically principled analysis of what makes a biological system cognitive in a sense that is continuous with, rather than discontinuous from, the cognitive systems that neuroscience studies.

## 1.4.3 Lyon's Biogenic Approach: Cognition as a Biological Phenomenon

Pamela Lyon (2006) arrives at a related position by a different route. Her "biogenic approach" begins with the observation that the field of cognitive science has historically been guilty of a kind of disciplinary imperialism: it has tried to define cognition in terms of what human cognition is like — representation, computation, consciousness — and then asked whether other systems meet those standards.

The biogenic approach reverses this: it begins with the biology and asks what cognitive capacities are needed by any living system, given what living systems are. The answer, Lyon argues, is capacities that allow the system to maintain its organization: detecting relevant environmental features, integrating information about internal states and external conditions, and acting to maintain or restore functional states.

This approach has several virtues:
- It grounds cognition in the biology of living systems rather than in the philosophy of mind
- It generates predictions: if a system is alive, it must have some form of cognition; if it cognizes, it must do so in service of its continued organization
- It avoids the anthropocentric bias of traditional definitions while still being discriminating: not everything is cognitive, just everything alive

Lyon (2015) applies this approach specifically to bacterial cognition, arguing that bacterial behavior satisfies the criteria for cognition as defined in the biogenic approach — and that this is not a metaphor or an analogy but a literal claim about the functional organization of bacterial systems.

## 1.4.4 The Problem of Anthropomorphism and How to Navigate It

The most persistent objection to the basal cognition program is that it is guilty of anthropomorphism — of projecting human (or at least animal) cognitive categories onto systems that have no right to them. When we say that bacteria "decide," or plants "remember," we are (on this objection) using language appropriate to minded beings and applying it to systems that are mere mechanism.

This objection must be taken seriously. The history of biology contains cautionary tales of anthropomorphic projection: the nineteenth-century tendency to describe animal behavior in purely intentional terms without mechanistic account, or the popular-science tendency to describe evolution as "trying" to achieve something. These projections are not harmless; they obscure mechanisms, generate confusion, and make claims that outrun the evidence.

But there is an equal and opposite error: **eliminativist overcaution**, which denies the legitimate use of functional language where it is warranted. When Howard Berg and colleagues describe E. coli's chemotaxis as a "decision" to run or tumble, they are not being sloppy or anthropomorphic; they are using functional language that accurately describes a real process — the bacterium's phosphorylation state determining flagellar rotation direction — in a way that places it in the right theoretical category (Sourjik & Berg, 2002). This is precisely what functional description is for.

The key distinction, which this book will observe consistently, is between:

**Functional claims**: "The bacterium senses aspartate." — This means: the bacterium has a mechanism that detects aspartate concentrations and modulates behavior accordingly. This is a claim about mechanism and function. It is empirically testable and does not imply experience.

**Phenomenological claims**: "The bacterium experiences desire for aspartate." — This is a claim about the subjective character of the bacterium's states. It implies that there is something it is like to be the bacterium seeking aspartate. This is an entirely different and much harder claim.

The basal cognition community operates primarily at the level of functional claims. When individual researchers make phenomenological claims — when they suggest that plants are "conscious" or that slime molds "experience" their world — they are going beyond what the evidence and the theoretical framework support, and they should be called out for doing so.

**The methodological stance of this book** is therefore:
1. Functional language about sensing, integrating, deciding, remembering, and learning is appropriate for biological systems wherever the relevant functional organization is present, regardless of substrate
2. Phenomenological language — language about experience, qualia, what it is like to be — is appropriate only where there is some positive reason to think experience is present, and should be used with explicit qualification
3. Where the evidence is ambiguous or the mechanism is unclear, the book says so explicitly

This stance is neither anthropomorphist nor eliminativist. It is what good science requires: language calibrated to what the evidence supports, neither more nor less.

## 1.4.5 Remaining Objections and Responses

**Objection 1: The definition is too broad. Every living cell cognizes; the word loses meaning.**

Response: This is not a reduction to absurdity — it is a substantive claim about the nature of life. The definition is discriminating within the biological domain: it distinguishes organisms that merely respond to perturbation (Level 1 on our scale from Section 1.3.5) from organisms that integrate multiple signals, store information, and modulate behavior flexibly (Levels 3–6). Not all cognition is equally rich. We speak of "degrees of cognition" rather than a binary distinction. This is philosophically coherent and biologically informative.

**Objection 2: Without representation, cognition is not really cognition — it's just chemistry.**

Response: This objection begs the question. It assumes that representation (in the classical computational sense) is necessary for cognition. We have shown in Section 1.2 that this assumption is a theoretical commitment, not a datum, and that there are well-developed alternatives (enactivism, dynamical systems approaches, teleosemantics) that do not require it. The claim that "it's just chemistry" is precisely the kind of eliminativist move that, applied consistently, would also deny cognitive status to neural processes — which are, after all, also chemistry.

**Objection 3: The definition conflates cognition with metabolism. Any metabolic system "senses, integrates, and acts."**

Response: This is an important objection. The response is twofold. First, the biological anchor of the definition — that the actions tend to maintain the system's continued organization — is more specific than metabolism in general. Not every metabolic process is in service of the system's overall organizational integrity; cognitive processes, on this definition, are precisely those that are. Second, even if some metabolic processes blur into cognitive processes at the margins, this is an argument that the boundary between metabolism and cognition is not sharp — which may be correct, and is itself an interesting biological claim.

**Objection 4: The definition is unfalsifiable. How could we ever show that a biological system does NOT cognize?**

Response: A system fails to cognize, on this definition, when it lacks the modulating layer (in Barandiaran and Moreno's sense) — when its responses to environmental signals are purely passive or mechanically determined by a single stimulus-response mapping, without integration, storage, or flexible selection among behaviors. Prions, for example, are biological molecules that change their structure in response to environmental conditions. They do not satisfy the definition: they sense, in a minimal way, but do not integrate or act in the relevant sense. Dead cells fail to satisfy it. Crystals fail to satisfy it. The definition is not vacuous.

## Section Summary

This book defines cognition as the sensing-integrating-acting triad, where the acting must be in service of the system's continued organizational integrity. This definition is:
- **Functional**: defined by what processes do, not what they are made of
- **Biological**: grounded in the imperatives of living systems
- **Non-representational**: does not require classical symbolic representations
- **Discriminating**: distinguishes cognitive from non-cognitive processes within and beyond biology
- **Continuous with neuroscience**: not a different concept from what neuroscientists study, but a more general one

This definition will be applied consistently throughout the book. When we examine bacteria, plants, fungi, and slime molds, the question we will ask is always: does this system sense relevant features of its environment, integrate this information (possibly with stored information), and act on the result in ways that serve its continued organization? When the answer is yes, we will say it cognizes. When the answer is no, or uncertain, we will say so.
