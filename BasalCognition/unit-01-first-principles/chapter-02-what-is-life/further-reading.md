# Further Reading and References — Chapter 2

---

## Annotated Bibliography

**Schrödinger, E. (1944). *What Is Life? The Physical Aspect of the Living Cell*. Cambridge University Press.**

The book that inspired a generation of physicists to turn their attention to biology. Schrödinger asks how living organisms maintain their ordered structure in the face of the second law of thermodynamics, and answers with the concept of negative entropy (negentropy). Still readable and still stimulating. Part I (on the aperiodic solid) anticipated the discovery of the genetic code; Part II (on the negentropy argument) is directly relevant to Chapter 2. Read alongside Nick Lane's *The Vital Question* for a modern update.

---

**Maturana, H.R., & Varela, F.J. (1980). *Autopoiesis and Cognition: The Realization of the Living*. D. Reidel.**

The primary text for autopoiesis theory. The first half of the book develops the theory in abstract terms; the second half applies it to cognition. Dense and requiring effort, but the reward is a genuinely original and rigorous account of the relationship between life and mind. Read Chapter 6 ("Autopoietic Systems and Nervous Systems") alongside Chapter 2 of this book.

---

**Thompson, E. (2007). *Mind in Life: Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of Mind*. Harvard University Press.**

Thompson's comprehensive treatment of the autopoietic tradition and its relationship to cognitive science. Chapters 4–7 ("The Emergence of Life," "Life and Mind," "Sensorimotor Subjectivity," and "Laying Down a Path in Walking") are most relevant to Chapter 2. Thompson extends and refines Maturana and Varela's account, addressing the most serious objections and integrating it with phenomenological philosophy. One of the most intellectually ambitious books in contemporary cognitive science.

---

**Lane, N. (2015). *The Vital Question: Energy, Evolution, and the Origins of Complex Life*. W.W. Norton.**

Lane argues that the key constraint on biological complexity is the energetics of the cell — specifically, the relationship between genome size, cell volume, and energy production. The book proposes that the origin of the eukaryotic cell (and with it, all complex multicellular life) required a specific evolutionary event: the endosymbiosis of an archaeon with a bacterium. Chapters 1–3 are particularly relevant to Section 2.3's argument about why the thermodynamics of life necessitate sensing. Beautifully written and intellectually serious.

---

**Kauffman, S.A. (1993). *The Origins of Order: Self-Organization and Selection in Evolution*. Oxford University Press.**

Kauffman's magnum opus, developing the theory of autocatalytic sets and the broader argument that self-organization is as important as natural selection in generating biological complexity. Chapters 3–5 (on autocatalytic sets and the origin of life) are most relevant to Chapter 2. Technical in places but worth the effort. The key insight — that autocatalytic organization may be a thermodynamic near-inevitability above a threshold of molecular complexity — remains important and contested.

---

**Luisi, P.L. (2006). *The Emergence of Life: From Chemical Origins to Synthetic Biology*. Cambridge University Press.**

A comprehensive treatment of the origin of life from a chemist's perspective, with particular attention to the role of membrane formation and protocell chemistry. Luisi has been one of the leading experimentalists in the field, working on the synthesis of minimal living systems in the laboratory. Chapter 3 (on autopoiesis) and Chapter 7 (on protocells) are most relevant to Section 2.4. Accessible and up-to-date.

---

**Szostak, J.W., Bartel, D.P., & Luisi, P.L. (2001). Synthesizing life. *Nature*, 409(6818), 387–390.**

A landmark review paper by three leading origin-of-life researchers, proposing a roadmap for the synthesis of a minimal living cell from non-living components. Argues that a protocell containing self-replicating RNA chemistry within a self-replicating fatty acid vesicle could emerge through natural processes. Important for understanding what the minimal requirements for life are and how they relate to the SIA definition of cognition.

---

**Deamer, D.W. (2019). *Assembling Life: How Can Life Begin on Earth and Other Habitable Planets?* Oxford University Press.**

An accessible and up-to-date account of the origin of life by one of the leading researchers in the field. Deamer focuses particularly on the role of lipid membranes and terrestrial environments (hot springs, volcanic pools) in concentrating and organizing prebiotic chemistry. Relevant to Section 2.4 for its account of how the first protocells might have acquired primitive sensing capacities through membrane specialization.

---

**Weiss, M.C. et al. (2016). The physiology and habitat of the last universal common ancestor. *Nature Microbiology*, 1(9), 16116.**

A bioinformatic analysis of the genes shared across all domains of life, from which the authors infer LUCA's physiology and habitat. Finds that LUCA likely lived in a hydrothermal vent environment, was anaerobic and thermophilic, and had a sophisticated molecular biology including translation, ATP synthesis, and ion channels. The paper's finding that LUCA had ion channels is directly relevant to Section 2.4's argument about the ancient origins of bioelectric cognition.

---

**Jacob, F., & Monod, J. (1961). Genetic regulatory mechanisms in the synthesis of proteins. *Journal of Molecular Biology*, 3(3), 318–356.**

The Nobel Prize-winning paper in which Jacob and Monod describe the molecular mechanism of the lac operon — the first molecular "decision circuit" to be characterized in biology. Essential background for Section 2.3's account of metabolic decision-making and for Lab 3 in the exercises.

---

**England, J.L. (2013). Statistical physics of self-replication. *Journal of Chemical Physics*, 139(12), 121923.**

England argues, using statistical mechanics, that the emergence of self-replicating structures is a thermodynamic near-inevitability given appropriate conditions — that life is not an improbable accident but a statistical consequence of physics. If correct, this has profound implications for the universality of life (and hence of cognition) in the universe. The paper is technically demanding but England's popular accounts of the argument are accessible.
