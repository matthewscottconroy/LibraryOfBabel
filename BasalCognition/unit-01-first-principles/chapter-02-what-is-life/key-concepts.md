# Key Concepts — Chapter 2: What Is Life?

---

**Autopoiesis** — The property of a system that continuously produces the components that constitute it, through the very processes that those components constitute. Coined by Maturana and Varela (1980) from the Greek *autos* (self) and *poiesis* (production). *Why it matters*: Autopoiesis is the most rigorous theoretical account of what distinguishes living from non-living systems, and it directly entails that living systems are minimally cognitive: they specify their own domain of interaction with the environment.

---

**Organizational Closure** — The property of a system whose component-producing processes form a closed network: each component is produced by processes that are themselves constituted by components of the system. Organizational closure does not mean the system is closed to matter or energy — it is an organizational, not a physical, boundary. *Why it matters*: Organizational closure is what distinguishes cells from crystals, fires, and other "self-organizing" non-living systems. It is the organizational expression of autopoiesis.

---

**Structural Coupling** — The relationship between an autopoietic system and its environment in which each perturbs the other, with the constraint that the system's autopoietic organization must be maintained. The environment does not instruct the cell; it perturbs it, and the cell specifies its own response. *Why it matters*: Structural coupling is the basis of the cell's "knowledge" of its environment. The history of structural coupling between a lineage of organisms and their environment is the history of their cognitive evolution.

---

**Negentropy** — Schrödinger's term for the negative entropy — the ordered, energy-rich material — that living systems feed on to maintain their own organization. A living system takes in low-entropy material and expels high-entropy material, thereby locally maintaining order while contributing to the overall increase in entropy of the universe. *Why it matters*: The negentropy concept explains how organisms can maintain improbable order without violating the second law of thermodynamics, and grounds the thermodynamic argument for the necessity of sensing.

---

**RNA World** — The hypothesis that the earliest self-replicating biological molecules were RNA molecules, which combine information storage (like DNA) and catalytic activity (like proteins) in a single molecule. The RNA world hypothesis is currently the most widely accepted account of the early stages of the origin of life. *Why it matters*: The RNA world marks the beginning of the information-processing systems that give rise to biological cognition. Self-replicating RNA is the earliest stage at which something like selective responsiveness to environmental conditions becomes biologically possible.

---

**Autocatalytic Set** — A collection of molecules in which the formation of each molecule is catalyzed by other molecules in the set, so that the set as a whole catalyzes its own production. Proposed by Kauffman (1993) as a model for the emergence of metabolic organization at the origin of life. *Why it matters*: Autocatalytic sets provide a model for how self-reproducing chemical organization can emerge spontaneously from complex chemistry, without requiring a template-replicating molecule as the starting point.

---

**LUCA (Last Universal Common Ancestor)** — The most recent common ancestor of all currently living organisms on Earth. LUCA is inferred from features shared across all three domains of life (Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya). Evidence suggests LUCA had a sophisticated molecular biology including ribosomes, DNA replication, ion channels, and primitive chemosensory systems. *Why it matters*: The cognitive capacities of LUCA — particularly its ion channels and likely primitive sensing systems — establish that basic cellular cognition is as old as organized life on Earth.

---

**Membrane Potential** — The electrical potential difference across a cell membrane, resulting from the unequal distribution of ions (primarily Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Cl⁻) on either side. In neurons, changes in membrane potential generate action potentials that carry information. But membrane potentials are present in all living cells, not only neurons. *Why it matters*: The universality of membrane potentials across all life establishes that the physical substrate of bioelectric information processing — the membrane potential — is a universal feature of living systems, not a neural specialty.

---

**Protocell** — A lipid vesicle enclosing self-replicating chemistry, considered a likely precursor to modern cells in the origin of life. Protocells have been created in the laboratory (Szostak et al., 2001) and can undergo competition and selection. *Why it matters*: Protocells mark the first emergence of spatially individuated self-reproducing entities — the first entities with an "inside" and an "outside." With this comes the possibility of minimal sensing: the detection of conditions inside and outside the boundary.

---

**Deep Homology of Sensing** — The finding that the molecular architecture of cellular sensing systems (particularly two-component signaling systems in bacteria) is ancient — present across all domains of life and likely dating to LUCA or before. *Why it matters*: Deep homology of sensing establishes that the cognitive capacity to detect environmental signals and modulate behavior accordingly is a universal feature of life, not an evolutionary innovation of complex animals.

---

**Second-Order Autopoiesis** — Maturana and Varela's concept of multicellular organisms as systems whose autopoietic components (cells) are coupled into a higher-order organizationally closed system. The organism is not itself autopoietic in the strict sense, but is composed of autopoietic units coupled into a higher-level organization. *Why it matters*: Second-order autopoiesis provides the theoretical basis for thinking about cognitive hierarchies in multicellular organisms — cells cognize at one level, tissues at another, the organism as a whole at a third.
