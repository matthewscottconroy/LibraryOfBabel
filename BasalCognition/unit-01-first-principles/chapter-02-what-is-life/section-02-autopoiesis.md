# Section 2.2: Autopoiesis — Life as Self-Production

---

## Section Introduction

The theory of autopoiesis, developed by Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela in the 1970s and published in its definitive form in 1980, is one of the most ambitious theoretical accounts of life ever proposed. It is also one of the most consequential for the field of basal cognition, because it does not merely characterize what living systems do — it argues that cognition is what living systems are.

The claim is strong, and the argument for it is technical. This section reconstructs the argument carefully, makes its key moves explicit, and examines both its power and its limitations.

---

## 2.2.1 The Autopoietic System: Boundaries, Processes, and Identity

Maturana and Varela define an autopoietic system as follows:

> "An autopoietic machine is a machine organized (defined as a unity) as a network of processes of production (transformation and destruction) of components that produces the components which: (i) through their interactions and transformations continuously regenerate and realize the network of processes (relations) that produced them; and (ii) constitute it (the machine) as a concrete unity in the space in which they exist by specifying the topological domain of its realization as such a network." (Maturana & Varela, 1980, p. 78)

This definition is dense, but its core is simple: an autopoietic system is a system whose components are produced by the very processes that those components constitute. The system produces itself.

The canonical example is the cell. A cell's lipid membrane is produced by metabolic processes inside the cell. Those metabolic processes depend on enzymes that are themselves produced by processes inside the cell. Those enzyme-producing processes are directed by genetic information that is maintained and replicated by still other processes inside the cell. The cell is, in the precise technical sense, a network of processes that produces the components that constitute those processes.

Three features of autopoiesis deserve special attention:

**Organizational closure** (or "operational closure"): The processes of an autopoietic system form a closed network. The products of the network are the components of the network. This closure is not a physical boundary (the cell is not closed to matter or energy exchange); it is an organizational structure. The organization of the system is self-referential: each component is produced and specified by the system as a whole.

**Structural coupling**: Although organizationally closed, autopoietic systems are not isolated from their environments. They interact continuously with their environments, and these interactions change the structure of both the system and the environment. Maturana and Varela call this "structural coupling" — the system and its environment mutually perturb and accommodate each other, with the constraint that the autopoietic organization must be maintained. If perturbations from the environment are so drastic that the autopoietic organization cannot be maintained, the system disintegrates — it dies.

**Identity through change**: Because the organization of an autopoietic system is self-producing, the system maintains its identity over time even as its components are continuously replaced. This is the biological analog of Theseus's ship: the cell you are now is not the same collection of molecules as the cell you were five years ago, but it is the same cell because the organizational relations between those molecules are continuously regenerated.

## 2.2.2 Autopoiesis and Cognition: The Living System as a Knowing System

The connection between autopoiesis and cognition is not incidental — it is the heart of Maturana and Varela's theoretical program. Their argument runs as follows:

1. **An autopoietic system is operationally closed**: its organization specifies what counts as a perturbation and what counts as an adequate response to that perturbation. The system does not simply react to the environment; it interacts with its environment according to its own internal organization.

2. **This operational closure means that the system specifies its own world**: the features of the environment that the system "sees" — that count as relevant perturbations requiring a response — are determined by the system's own organization, not by the environment itself. The environment does not instruct the system; it perturbs it, and the system determines its own response.

3. **This is the basic form of cognition**: to be an autopoietic system is to specify, from the inside, what counts as a significant feature of the environment and what counts as an adequate response. In Maturana and Varela's terms, "living systems are cognitive systems, and living as a process is a process of cognition" (Maturana & Varela, 1980, p. 13).

This is a remarkable claim. It does not say that all living systems are intelligent, or that all living systems have minds. It says that the most basic form of what we call cognition — the determination of what counts as a relevant environmental feature and what counts as an adequate response — is structurally identical to what living systems do.

The argument can be made more concrete using the example of bacterial chemotaxis. An E. coli cell in a glucose gradient does not simply respond to glucose concentration mechanically, the way an iron filing responds to a magnetic field. The cell's response is mediated by its own internal organization: by the methylation state of its receptors, the phosphorylation state of the signaling cascade, the recent history of gradient changes. The cell's "interpretation" of the gradient — whether to run or tumble — is determined by its internal state as much as by the external gradient. In Maturana and Varela's terms, the cell specifies the significance of the gradient from the inside.

## 2.2.3 Critiques and Extensions of Autopoiesis Theory

The autopoiesis theory has been enormously influential, but it has also attracted serious criticism.

**The application problem**: Autopoiesis is defined for concrete physical systems in which processes produce components in physical space. It is not obvious how to apply the concept to viruses (which are autopoietic only in combination with a host), to social systems (which Maturana and Varela themselves tried to describe as autopoietic, generating considerable controversy), or to multicellular organisms (in which individual cells are autopoietic but the organism as a whole may not satisfy the strict definition). Pier Luigi Luisi (2003) has argued for a stricter application of the concept, while others (including Evan Thompson, 2007) have argued for extensions and modifications.

**The cognition claim is too strong**: Critics argue that Maturana and Varela's identification of life with cognition conflates two things that should be kept separate. Even if every living system interacts with its environment according to its own internal organization, this does not mean that every living system "knows" its environment in any meaningful sense. The distinction between mere physical response (even organizationally mediated response) and genuine cognition may require more than autopoiesis — it may require the integration of information, the storage of past states, the anticipation of future states. Godfrey-Smith (1994) makes this objection explicitly: autopoiesis is necessary but not sufficient for cognition.

**The response** by Maturana and Varela's defenders is that Godfrey-Smith's objection conflates cognition in the broad biological sense (the specification of what counts as a relevant environmental feature) with cognition in the narrow cognitive-science sense (information processing with representation and memory). The biogenic approach (Lyon, 2006) navigates this tension by treating autopoiesis as the ground condition for cognition — the condition that makes any cognitive process possible — while acknowledging that different cognitive processes differ in their complexity and richness.

For the purposes of this book, we take the following position: autopoiesis is the organizational basis of life, and it entails that all living systems engage in the minimal cognitive process of specifying their own domain of interaction with the environment. This is the floor of cognition. Above this floor, cognitive processes vary enormously in their complexity, flexibility, and richness — and the study of this variation is the business of basal cognition research.

## 2.2.4 Second-Order Autopoiesis: Multicellularity and the Extended Self

One of the most interesting extensions of autopoiesis theory concerns multicellular organisms. A multicellular organism consists of many cells, each of which is autopoietic. The organism as a whole is not straightforwardly autopoietic in the original sense — its "components" (cells) are not produced by processes in the same way that a cell's molecular components are.

Maturana and Varela introduce the concept of **second-order autopoiesis** to describe multicellular organisms: systems whose autopoietic components (cells) are themselves coupled into a higher-order organization that is also organizationally closed. On this view, a multicellular organism is a system of systems — a hierarchy of organizational closure.

This concept is particularly important for basal cognition because it suggests that the cognitive architecture of multicellular organisms is also hierarchical: cells cognize at the cellular level, tissues cognize at the tissue level, and organisms cognize at the organismal level. Michael Levin's concept of the "cognitive light cone" (Levin, 2019), which we examine in Chapter 30, is a rigorous development of this idea: cognitive processes exist at every level of biological organization, and the boundaries between levels are not sharp.

---

## Section Summary

Autopoiesis provides the theoretical foundation for the claim that cognition is constitutive of life:
- Living systems produce their own components through their own processes (organizational closure)
- This organizational closure means that living systems specify their own domain of interaction with the environment
- This specification is the basic form of cognition: the determination of what counts as a relevant environmental feature and what counts as an adequate response
- Autopoiesis is the floor of cognition; above the floor, cognitive processes vary in complexity and richness

The critiques of autopoiesis theory are serious and must be acknowledged. The identification of life with cognition is too strong if taken to imply that all living systems are equally cognitive, or that all forms of environmental responsiveness are cognitively equivalent. The modified position — that autopoiesis is a necessary but not sufficient condition for cognition in the full SIA sense — is both philosophically defensible and empirically useful.
