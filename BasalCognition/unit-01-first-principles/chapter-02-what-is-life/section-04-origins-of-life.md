# Section 2.4: The Origins of Life — Cognition's Oldest Ancestors

---

## Section Introduction

If cognition is constitutive of life — if every living system necessarily engages in some form of sensing, integration, and goal-directed action — then the origins of life and the origins of cognition are the same event. The first cell that emerged from prebiotic chemistry was simultaneously the first cognitive system. Understanding how life began is, on this view, understanding how cognition began.

This section traces the origins of life research and examines what the most ancient living systems were likely capable of — what kind of cognitive architecture they had, and what this implies about the evolutionary history of cognition.

---

## 2.4.1 RNA World Hypothesis and the First Self-Replicating Molecules

The most influential current account of the origin of life is the RNA world hypothesis (Gilbert, 1986). The hypothesis proposes that the earliest self-replicating molecules were RNA molecules — nucleic acids that combine the properties of information storage (like DNA) and catalysis (like proteins). In an RNA world, RNA molecules could serve as both genotype and phenotype: they stored information about their own structure and catalyzed their own replication.

The RNA world hypothesis is supported by several lines of evidence. Ribozymes — RNA molecules with catalytic activity — have been discovered in all domains of life and appear to be relics of the RNA world. The ribosome itself — the molecular machine that synthesizes all proteins in all cells — contains an RNA core that performs the peptide bond formation reaction, suggesting that the ribosome is descended from a purely RNA-based ancestor (Cech, 2000). And synthetic ribozymes capable of self-replication have been created in the laboratory, demonstrating the physical plausibility of RNA-based self-reproduction (Lincoln & Joyce, 2009).

What is the "cognitive" status of a self-replicating RNA molecule? On a strict application of the SIA definition: very minimal. A self-replicating RNA molecule detects substrates (senses), but it does not obviously integrate information or select among possible actions. It replicates when conditions permit and does not replicate when they do not — but this is closer to Level 1 on our goal-directedness scale than to genuine minimal cognition.

The RNA world must therefore have been supplemented by additional organizational features to produce systems that qualify as minimally cognitive. The critical additions are likely:
1. **Enclosure within a membrane** — creating the boundary that makes a cell a self-distinguishing unit
2. **Metabolic networks** — chemical processes that generate the energy and material needed for replication
3. **Regulatory mechanisms** — feedback processes that modulate replication in response to environmental conditions

---

## 2.4.2 Lipid Membranes and the Enclosure of Self

One of the most important events in the origin of life was the enclosure of self-replicating chemistry within a lipid membrane (Deamer, 2017). Lipids are amphipathic molecules — they have a water-attracting head and a water-repelling tail — and in aqueous solution they spontaneously organize into bilayer vesicles. These vesicles are self-sealing, stable, and semi-permeable.

The formation of protocells — vesicles enclosing self-replicating chemistry — marks the emergence of the first organizationally individuated entities: systems that are, in a spatial and chemical sense, distinct from their environment. This individuation is the physical basis of autopoiesis: the cell boundary defines the interior as a distinct organizational domain.

With enclosure comes the possibility of genuine sensing in the SIA sense: the membrane can be selectively permeable, allowing certain molecules in and keeping others out. The first protocells that developed membrane channels or pores that were selectively responsive to environmental chemicals had, in a minimal sense, the first membrane-based sensory apparatus.

Jack Szostak and colleagues have shown that simple fatty acid vesicles can undergo Darwinian evolution — they compete for lipid resources, and those with more efficient replication chemistry grow at the expense of others (Szostak, Bartel, & Luisi, 2001). These are not yet autopoietic systems by the strict definition, but they are on the path toward it.

## 2.4.3 The Last Universal Common Ancestor (LUCA) — What Could It Do?

All life on Earth shares a common ancestor: LUCA, the Last Universal Common Ancestor. LUCA is not the first organism — it is the most recent organism from which all current life descends. Its existence is inferred from the features shared by all three domains of life: Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya.

What did LUCA have? Bioinformatic analyses of the genes shared across all domains of life — the "universal gene set" — suggest that LUCA was already a sophisticated organism. It had:
- The translation machinery (ribosomes) to synthesize proteins from RNA templates
- A complete DNA replication system
- An ATP-based energy system
- A lipid membrane
- Ion pumps — specifically, ion channels and transporters for maintaining membrane potential (Weiss et al., 2016)

The presence of ion channels in LUCA is particularly significant for our purposes. Ion channels are the molecular basis of bioelectricity — of the membrane potentials and electrical signaling that we will examine in depth in Chapter 5. LUCA was not neurally active; it had no nervous system, no neurons, no synapses. But it did have the molecular machinery for bioelectric signaling, in the form of ion channels that allowed selective flow of ions across its membrane.

This means that bioelectric signaling — the ancient language of biological information processing — is as old as life itself. It was not invented by animals for neural communication. It was present at the beginning.

---

## 2.4.4 The Deep Homology of Sensing: Chemoreception at Life's Origin

If LUCA had ion channels and membrane potentials, it could, in principle, have had some form of chemoreception — the ability to detect chemical signals in its environment and modulate its behavior accordingly. The evolutionary precursors of the two-component signaling systems used by modern bacteria for chemotaxis are present in all domains of life, suggesting that they evolved before the divergence of the three domains (Galperin, 2004).

This is a striking finding: the molecular architecture of cellular sensing is ancient — older than the divergence of bacteria from archaea, older than the origin of eukaryotes, possibly close to LUCA itself. The ability of cells to detect environmental chemicals and modulate their behavior accordingly is not a late evolutionary invention; it is one of the most ancient biological capacities we know of.

This deep homology of sensing is one of the strongest arguments for the claim that basal cognition is not a peripheral curiosity but a fundamental feature of life. If the molecular architecture of sensing goes back to LUCA, then every lineage of life has been building on the same ancient cognitive foundation for roughly 3.5 to 4 billion years. Human cognition is not a departure from this ancient heritage — it is an elaboration of it.

---

## Section Summary

The origins-of-life perspective confirms and deepens the argument for basal cognition:

1. Life began with self-replicating chemistry — minimal, but already with some form of environmental sensitivity
2. The enclosure of self-replicating chemistry within membranes created the first organizationally individuated cognitive agents
3. LUCA — the ancestor of all life — already had ion channels and likely had molecular signaling systems, meaning that bioelectric cognition is as ancient as organized life itself
4. The deep homology of sensing systems across all domains of life argues that basic cognitive capacities are not evolutionary novelties but fundamental features of living organization

The implication is stark: we are looking for the origins of cognition in the wrong place if we are looking in the vertebrate nervous system, or even in the nervous systems of invertebrates. The origins of cognition are in the origin of life itself.
