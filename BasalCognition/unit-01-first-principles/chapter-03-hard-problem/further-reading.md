# Further Reading and References — Chapter 3

---

## Annotated Bibliography

**Chalmers, D.J. (1996). *The Conscious Mind: In Search of a Fundamental Theory*. Oxford University Press.**

The book that put the hard problem on the mainstream philosophical agenda. Chalmers argues that consciousness cannot be explained by purely functional or physical accounts, develops the conceivability of philosophical zombies as evidence for this, and proposes his own "naturalistic dualism" — a view that acknowledges the irreducibility of consciousness while trying to give it a place in the natural world. Part I (the hard problem and its implications) is essential; Part III (toward a theory of consciousness) is more speculative and contested.

---

**Nagel, T. (1974). What is it like to be a bat? *Philosophical Review*, 83(4), 435–450.**

The paper that established the fundamental problem of other minds in its modern form. Nagel argues that there is a "subjective character" to the bat's experience of echolocation that no objective, third-person account can capture. Directly relevant to basal cognition: replace "bat" with "bacterium" and you have the core methodological problem the field faces. Essential reading.

---

**Dennett, D.C. (1991). *Consciousness Explained*. Little, Brown.**

The most rigorous and forceful attempt to dissolve the hard problem. Dennett argues that the intuitions that drive the hard problem — qualia, the explanatory gap, the Mary thought experiment — are misleading artifacts of our cognitive architecture, not genuine evidence of an irreducible phenomenal realm. Whether or not you are convinced, understanding Dennett's argument is essential for taking the hard problem seriously. Chapters 3–5 are most directly relevant.

---

**Goff, P. (2019). *Galileo's Error: Foundations for a New Science of Mind*. Pantheon.**

The most accessible and philosophically serious defense of panpsychism currently available. Goff argues that Galileo's mathematization of nature — treating the physical world as purely quantitative — excluded consciousness from the scientific world picture by definition, and that the solution is to recognize consciousness as a fundamental feature of reality. Readable, rigorous, and genuinely thought-provoking. An ideal companion to Chalmers' work.

---

**Strawson, G. (2006). Realistic monism: Why physicalism entails panpsychism. *Journal of Consciousness Studies*, 13(10–11), 3–31.**

Strawson's provocative argument that any serious physicalism — any view committed to the reality of consciousness and the completeness of physics — must be a form of panpsychism. The paper generated a famous symposium, and the responses from other philosophers (also published in the same issue) are worth reading alongside the original. Relevant to Section 3.3 of this chapter.

---

**Tononi, G., Boly, M., Massimini, M., & Koch, C. (2016). Integrated information theory: From consciousness to its physical substrate. *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, 17(7), 450–461.**

A clear and relatively accessible account of IIT by its developers. Explains the theory's core commitments (consciousness = integrated information), its axioms and postulates, its predictions for non-neural systems, and the empirical evidence that supports it. Essential for understanding what a mathematical theory of consciousness looks like, and what its implications are for non-neural organisms.

---

**Seth, A.K. (2021). *Being You: A New Science of Consciousness*. Dutton.**

The most accessible current treatment of the neuroscience of consciousness, by a researcher who manages to be both scientifically rigorous and philosophically careful. Seth's "controlled hallucination" model of consciousness — perception as the brain's predictive model of the world — connects naturally to predictive processing accounts of basal cognition. Chapters 1–4 and 11–13 are most relevant to Chapter 3.

---

**Barron, A.B., & Klein, C. (2016). What insects can tell us about the origins of consciousness. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 113(18), 4900–4908.**

An important paper that attempts to identify behavioral and neurological criteria for the presence of consciousness in insects — organisms that are much simpler than vertebrates but still neural. Relevant to Chapter 3 for its approach to the problem: using theory-driven behavioral criteria as indirect evidence for experience. Directly applicable to the Lab 3 exercise.

---

**Jackson, F. (1982). Epiphenomenal qualia. *Philosophical Quarterly*, 32(127), 127–136.**

The paper introducing the Mary thought experiment, which is one of the most powerful illustrations of the hard problem. Short, clear, and enormously influential. Read alongside Dennett's response ("Quining Qualia," 1988) for a sense of the philosophical debate.

---

**Levine, J. (1983). Materialism and qualia: The explanatory gap. *Pacific Philosophical Quarterly*, 64(4), 354–361.**

The paper that introduced the term "explanatory gap" — the conceptual distance between functional descriptions of consciousness and phenomenal experience itself. Levine's argument is more focused than Chalmers' and directly addresses the question of what a functional explanation of consciousness would need to do to be satisfying.

---

**Taiz, L. et al. (2019). Plants neither possess nor require consciousness. *Trends in Plant Science*, 24(8), 677–687.**

A critical response to the plant neurobiology program, arguing that plants do not possess consciousness and that the use of neurological vocabulary is misleading rather than illuminating. Essential reading as the strongest current critique of the plant cognition program. Read alongside Calvo et al. (2020) for the response.
