# Section 3.1: Chalmers' Hard Problem and Why It Matters Here

---

## Section Introduction

David Chalmers introduced the phrase "the hard problem of consciousness" in a 1995 paper and developed the idea at book length in *The Conscious Mind* (1996). The phrase has become one of the most widely used in all of philosophy, and for good reason: it names something real, it names it precisely, and it names something that a surprising number of scientists and philosophers had been in the habit of overlooking.

The hard problem is not a problem about explaining consciousness in general. Most of what we ordinarily mean by "consciousness" — attention, memory, introspection, the ability to report on one's mental states — Chalmers calls the "easy problems." Not easy in the sense of trivial, but easy in the sense that they are, in principle, tractable by the methods of functional science: we can in principle explain why a system attends to some stimuli rather than others, why it stores and retrieves information, why it produces verbal reports about its internal states. These explanations may be extremely complex and may require decades of research, but they are the right kind of explanation for the right kind of problem.

The hard problem is different. It is the problem of explaining why any of this functional machinery is accompanied by experience — why there is *something it is like* to be the system that performs these functions. Why does it feel like anything to see red, to hear a chord, to feel pain? Why isn't all of this functional processing "in the dark" — accomplished by a system that processes information efficiently but feels nothing?

This is Chalmers' formulation. We need to examine it carefully before applying it to basal cognition.

---

## 3.1.1 The Explanatory Gap: Why Neural Correlates Don't Explain Experience

The standard scientific approach to consciousness is to look for neural correlates: patterns of neural activity that are reliably associated with particular conscious experiences. When someone sees red, certain neurons fire. When someone is in pain, certain neural pathways are activated. This is the neuroscience of consciousness, and it has produced important results.

But — and this is Chalmers' central point — identifying the neural correlate of an experience does not explain the experience. Suppose we know, with complete precision, which neurons fire when someone sees red. We still have not explained why those neurons firing *produces the experience of redness* — the particular subjective quality, the "what it's like," the *qualia* — rather than simply producing the functional output (verbal report "I see red," reaching toward a red object, etc.) in the dark.

The philosopher Joseph Levine (1983) introduced the term "explanatory gap" for this problem: even a complete functional and physical account of what happens in the brain when someone sees red leaves a gap between the account and the experience. We can describe the neural mechanics perfectly and still ask: "Yes, but why does *that* feel like *this*?"

This gap is not (or at least, arguably not) a gap in our current knowledge that more data will fill. It appears to be a conceptual gap: the concepts of functional description are in a different space from the concepts of phenomenal description, and no amount of adding detail to the functional account seems to bridge the space.

Chalmers argues that this conceptual gap reflects a genuine ontological gap: phenomenal consciousness is not reducible to functional organization, and a complete description of a system in functional terms leaves out something real — the subjective character of experience.

## 3.1.2 Qualia, Phenomenal Consciousness, and the Mary Thought Experiment

The term **qualia** (singular: quale) refers to the intrinsic, subjective qualities of experience: the redness of red, the painfulness of pain, the taste of coffee. Qualia are what make experiences feel like something from the inside.

Frank Jackson's "Mary" thought experiment (Jackson, 1982) is one of the most effective illustrations of the qualia concept. Mary is a brilliant neuroscientist who has spent her entire life in a black-and-white room, studying color vision through black-and-white textbooks and monitors. She knows everything that can be known about the physics of color, the neurophysiology of color vision, and the functional role of color representations in cognition. She knows, physically and functionally, everything there is to know about the experience of seeing red.

Then Mary leaves the room and sees red for the first time. Does she learn something new?

The intuitive answer is: yes. She learns what red *looks like*. This is a new piece of knowledge — phenomenal knowledge — that was not contained in her complete functional and physical knowledge.

If this intuition is correct, it follows that phenomenal knowledge is not reducible to functional knowledge — that there is something about experience that a purely functional, third-person description leaves out. And this is exactly the hard problem.

(Objectors, notably Daniel Dennett (2007), argue that the intuition is wrong — that Mary learns nothing genuinely new when she sees red, only a new way of representing facts she already knew. This dispute is ongoing and unresolved.)

## 3.1.3 Why the Hard Problem Matters for Basal Cognition

The hard problem matters for basal cognition for a specific and important reason: **it separates two questions that are easily conflated.**

Question 1 (the easy problem applied to non-neural systems): Does this bacterium / slime mold / plant exhibit functional cognition — does it sense, integrate information, and act in ways that serve its continued organization?

Question 2 (the hard problem applied to non-neural systems): Is there something it is like to be this bacterium / slime mold / plant — is its functional processing accompanied by phenomenal experience?

The evidence and arguments relevant to Question 1 are empirical: we look at the bacterium's molecular architecture, its behavioral responses, its information integration. Question 1 is answerable with current scientific methods, and the bulk of this book is devoted to it.

Question 2 is a different matter. The hard problem means that we cannot infer phenomenal experience from functional organization — not for bacteria, not for plants, and (this is the uncomfortable implication) not even for other humans. We infer that other humans have phenomenal experience by analogy with ourselves, backed by the fact that they are biologically similar to us and behave in ways that parallel our own experiential reports. These analogical inferences become less reliable as the systems in question become less similar to us.

The basal cognition community is sometimes accused of sneaking phenomenal claims in through the back door when it asserts that bacteria "sense," "decide," or "remember." This accusation is sometimes warranted — when researchers use phenomenological vocabulary loosely. But it is not warranted when functional vocabulary is used carefully and explicitly distinguished from phenomenological claims.

Conversely, the basal cognition community is sometimes accused by its critics of being blind to the phenomenal dimension — of treating the question of experience as though it were dissolved by the functional account. This accusation is also sometimes warranted.

The responsible position — the one this book takes — is to hold both questions open simultaneously: to pursue Question 1 rigorously and empirically, while acknowledging that Question 2 remains open and treating claims about it with the care they deserve.

## 3.1.4 The Methodological Implications: Third-Person Science and Its Limits

The hard problem poses a methodological problem for science. Science is, fundamentally, a third-person enterprise: it studies systems from the outside, characterizing their properties and behaviors in terms that are in principle accessible to any observer. But phenomenal experience is, by its nature, a first-person phenomenon: it is accessible directly only to the experiencing subject.

This means that the standard scientific approach to experience is always indirect. We cannot directly observe another creature's experience; we can only observe its behavior, its neural activity, and its reports. These are all third-person data, and the inference from third-person data to first-person experience is the famous "problem of other minds."

For organisms that cannot produce verbal reports — bacteria, plants, slime molds — the problem of other minds is especially acute. We have no behavioral analog of the verbal report that we can use to infer the presence of experience. We can observe that the organism's behavior is sensitive to its environment in sophisticated ways. But sophisticated sensitivity, as we argued in Chapter 1, is a matter of functional organization, not necessarily of experience.

Some researchers in basal cognition (notably Paco Calvo and colleagues) have proposed that we can use behavioral criteria — the richness, flexibility, and context-dependence of an organism's responses — as indirect evidence for experience. This is a reasonable proposal, and we will discuss it further in Section 3.4. But it is important to acknowledge what it is: an inference from third-person data to first-person phenomena, mediated by theoretical assumptions about the relationship between function and experience. Such inferences can be made more or less carefully and more or less transparently, but they cannot be escaped.

---

## Section Summary

- The hard problem of consciousness is the problem of explaining why functional cognition is accompanied by phenomenal experience.
- The hard problem is distinct from the "easy problems" of cognition, which are tractable by functional and empirical methods.
- The hard problem matters for basal cognition because it separates two questions: (1) Does this organism exhibit functional cognition? (answerable empirically); (2) Is there something it is like to be this organism? (currently not answerable by standard scientific methods).
- Responsible basal cognition research holds both questions open simultaneously, pursues (1) empirically, and treats claims about (2) with explicit qualification and intellectual humility.
