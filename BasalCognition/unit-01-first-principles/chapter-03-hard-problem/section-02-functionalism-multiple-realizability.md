# Section 3.2: Functionalism, Multiple Realizability, and the Substrate Question

---

## Section Introduction

If the hard problem establishes that functional cognition and phenomenal experience are logically independent, a natural question follows: is there any reason to think that phenomenal experience, like functional cognition, might be multiply realizable — present in systems that are very different from the human brain?

This section examines functionalism — the philosophical theory that mental states are defined by their functional roles — and its implications for the possibility of non-neural consciousness. Functionalism is both the philosophical foundation for the substrate-neutral definitions of cognition used in this book and the source of some of the most contested claims about the distribution of experience across biology.

---

## 3.2.1 Putnam's Functionalism and What It Claims

Hilary Putnam introduced functionalism in the 1960s as a response to the dominant theories of mind: behaviorism (mental states are dispositions to behave) and type identity theory (mental states are identical to specific brain states). Both theories, Putnam argued, were too restrictive.

Behaviorism failed because it could not account for the causal role of mental states in producing behavior — it reduced mental states to behavioral dispositions without explaining why those dispositions existed. Type identity theory failed because it required that mental states in different organisms be identical to the same physical brain states. If pain is identical to C-fiber firing in humans, and an octopus has no C-fibers, then an octopus cannot feel pain — a counterintuitive result (Putnam, 1967).

Putnam's functionalism holds instead that mental states are defined by their **functional roles**: their causal relations to inputs (stimuli), outputs (behaviors), and other mental states. Pain, on this view, is the state that is typically caused by tissue damage, that causes distress and withdrawal behavior, and that motivates the organism to avoid the damaging stimulus. Any state that plays this functional role — regardless of what it is made of — is pain.

This is the principle of **multiple realizability**: the same mental state can be realized in many different physical substrates. Pain in humans is realized in neural tissue. Functionally equivalent pain in a silicon robot (hypothetically) would be realized in electronic circuits. Functionally equivalent pain in an octopus would be realized in a radically different neural architecture.

Multiple realizability is the philosophical foundation for the substrate-neutral definition of cognition used in this book. If cognitive states are defined by their functional roles, then cognitive states can in principle be realized in any substrate that implements the right functional organization.

---

## 3.2.2 Multiple Realizability and Basal Cognition

The extension of multiple realizability to basal cognition is conceptually direct, but philosophically contested. The argument runs:

1. Cognitive states are defined by their functional roles (functionalism)
2. Functional roles can be realized in many different physical substrates (multiple realizability)
3. Bacteria, plants, and slime molds implement functional organizations that parallel those of neural systems in the relevant respects (sensing, integrating, acting in goal-directed ways)
4. Therefore, bacteria, plants, and slime molds may implement cognitive states

The validity of step 3 is an empirical question that the rest of this book addresses. The validity of steps 1–2 is a philosophical question. There is one important challenge worth addressing here.

**The "fine-grained" challenge**: Ned Block (1978) argued that functionalism cannot be correct as stated, because it would attribute mental states to systems that obviously do not have them. If pain is just the state that plays the pain-role — caused by damage, causing distress and avoidance — then the United States economy might count as being in pain during a recession (since it is "damaged," it causes "distress" in many agents, it motivates "avoidance" behaviors). This is Block's "China Brain" / "China Nation" reductio.

The standard response is to specify the functional roles more precisely: the relevant functional organization must be at the right *level* of physical description (neural or sub-neural, not national), and the causal interactions must be of the right *kind* (direct physical causation, not the mediated causation that links economic agents). But specifying the right level and the right kind without simply stipulating that it must be neural is the challenge.

For basal cognition, this challenge is significant. The claim is not that the economy cognizes, but that bacterial signaling networks implement functional organizations that are sufficiently similar (in the relevant respects) to neural signaling networks to count as cognitive. What are the relevant respects? The SIA definition — sensing environmental features, integrating information from multiple sources, acting in goal-directed ways — is an attempt to specify them without reference to substrate. Whether this specification is principled or ad hoc is one of the key methodological questions the field faces.

## 3.2.3 Biological Naturalism vs. Computational Functionalism

John Searle (1980, 1992) has been the most influential critic of computational functionalism as applied to consciousness. His Chinese Room argument (Section 1.1.4) was directed at the claim that consciousness is a matter of running the right program. His broader position — **biological naturalism** — holds that consciousness is caused by specific biological processes in the brain, and that no merely computational or functional equivalent can produce consciousness.

On Searle's view, consciousness is not multiply realizable: it requires specific neurobiological processes, just as digestion requires specific biochemical processes. You can simulate digestion on a computer, but the simulation does not digest anything. Similarly, you can simulate consciousness (in the sense of producing the functional outputs associated with consciousness), but the simulation is not conscious.

Searle's biological naturalism is a serious position, but it faces a serious challenge: **it cannot explain why the specific neurobiological processes it identifies produce consciousness rather than merely producing the functional outputs.** If the objection to functionalism is that it cannot explain why the right functional organization produces consciousness, the objection applies equally to biological naturalism: it cannot explain why the right biological processes produce consciousness. The hard problem is equally hard for biological naturalism and for functionalism.

There is, however, a weaker version of Searle's position that is more defensible: not that consciousness requires neurons specifically, but that consciousness requires *physical processes of a kind that can support the relevant causal power*, and that it is an open empirical question what kinds of physical processes can support this. This position is consistent with the claim that non-neural biological systems might be conscious, while denying that merely formal or computational systems could be.

---

## 3.2.4 What Substrate Constraints Actually Matter

The honest answer to the question "what substrate is required for consciousness?" is that we do not know. We know that certain kinds of neural architecture in humans produce consciousness. We know (or strongly infer) that similar architectures in other mammals produce consciousness. We know very little about what happens in radically different architectures — octopus neural systems, insect neural systems, and least of all non-neural biological systems.

What we can say is this:

1. **The hard problem does not favor neurons**: the hard problem is equally mysterious for neural systems and for non-neural systems. We do not understand why neurons produce consciousness, so we cannot use our understanding of neural consciousness to argue that non-neural systems cannot be conscious.

2. **Multiple realizability is consistent with constraints**: even if consciousness is multiply realizable, it may be realizable only in substrates that have certain physical properties — perhaps substrates that can support a certain kind of temporal dynamics, or a certain kind of causal integration. What those properties are, we do not know.

3. **The question is empirically tractable in principle, even if not currently in practice**: the development of better theories of consciousness (Integrated Information Theory, Global Workspace Theory, higher-order theories) will generate predictions about which physical systems can be conscious. Testing those predictions in non-neural biological systems is one of the most important research programs for the coming decades.

For the purposes of this book: we take a position of principled agnosticism. We do not know whether non-neural organisms are conscious. We have strong evidence that they are cognitively active (in the functional sense). The relationship between these two facts is one of the most interesting open questions in all of biology.

---

## Section Summary

- Functionalism holds that mental states are defined by functional roles, implying multiple realizability
- Multiple realizability is the philosophical foundation for substrate-neutral definitions of cognition
- Searle's biological naturalism challenges this, but faces its own version of the hard problem
- The honest position: we do not know what substrate constraints consciousness requires
- This uncertainty neither supports nor undermines the basal cognition program's empirical claims about functional cognition in non-neural systems
