# Section 3.3: Panpsychism, Panprotopsychism, and the Continuum of Experience

---

## Section Introduction

If the hard problem is real — if there is an explanatory gap between functional organization and phenomenal experience that cannot be bridged by functional description — then we face a choice among theories about the distribution and origins of experience. The mainstream view has been that experience is a late evolutionary product, restricted to complex nervous systems. But the hard problem presses us to ask whether this view is philosophically sustainable.

Panpsychism — the view that experience (or something experience-like) is a fundamental and ubiquitous feature of reality — has had a surprising philosophical renaissance in the past two decades. It is no longer the eccentric position it once seemed. This section examines panpsychism and its variants, evaluates the philosophical arguments for and against it, and assesses its relevance to basal cognition.

A key caveat from the outset: **panpsychism is a metaphysical position, not a scientific one**. It cannot be directly tested by current scientific methods. It is relevant to basal cognition because it bears on the philosophical interpretation of basal cognitive findings — on what significance to attach to the functional cognition documented in non-neural organisms. But it should not be confused with the empirical claims of basal cognition research itself.

---

## 3.3.1 The Contemporary Revival of Panpsychism

Panpsychism has roots in pre-Socratic philosophy, was developed by Leibniz, was a serious position in the philosophies of William James (1890), Alfred North Whitehead (1929), and Bertrand Russell (1927), and has been revived in recent years by philosophers including David Chalmers (2010), Philip Goff (2019), and Galen Strawson (2006).

The contemporary revival is driven not by romanticism about nature but by the hard problem. The argument, stated simply, is this:

1. The hard problem establishes that phenomenal consciousness is not reducible to functional organization — there is something about experience that no functional account captures.

2. If consciousness is a fundamental feature of reality that cannot be reduced to or derived from purely physical properties as normally described, then we face a choice: either consciousness is restricted to complex nervous systems (emergentism) or it is distributed more broadly throughout nature (panpsychism).

3. **Emergentism is deeply problematic**: if consciousness is not reducible to functional organization, then how can it emerge from functional organization? What is the mechanism of emergence? Strong emergence — the appearance of a genuinely novel property from the interaction of components that lack that property — is philosophically controversial precisely because it seems to violate the principle that complex things are made of simpler things. If consciousness is not in neurons, why does a collection of neurons produce it?

4. **Panpsychism avoids this problem** by holding that consciousness is present at every level of reality. Complex consciousness (human consciousness) is composed of simpler forms of consciousness (the consciousness of neurons, of molecules, of fundamental particles). No emergence is required — only combination and organization.

This argument is not a proof. It depends on the premise that strong emergence is implausible (which some philosophers deny), and it faces serious objections of its own. But it has convinced a significant number of serious philosophers that panpsychism deserves a place at the table.

---

## 3.3.2 Varieties of Panpsychism

"Panpsychism" covers a range of positions. The most important distinction is between:

**Micropsychism (constitutive panpsychism)**: The view that the phenomenal experiences of complex systems (like human minds) are composed of simpler phenomenal experiences present in their components (neurons, molecules, particles). The human experience of redness is built up from countless micro-experiences, each minuscule and alien to our own, but phenomenal in character.

**Cosmopsychism (priority monism)**: The view that the fundamental bearer of consciousness is the universe as a whole, and that individual conscious beings are aspects or parts of this cosmic consciousness. Individual experiences are not composed of micro-experiences; rather, the cosmic whole is experiential, and individuals are aspects of it. Cosmopsychism avoids the combination problem (see Section 3.3.3) at the cost of a different kind of implausibility.

**Panprotopsychism (Russellian monism)**: The view, associated with Bertrand Russell (1927) and developed by David Chalmers (2010), that the fundamental properties of the physical world include "proto-phenomenal" properties — properties that are not themselves phenomenal, but that constitute or give rise to phenomenal properties when organized in the right ways. On this view, the fundamental particles don't have experience, but they have properties that are the intrinsic nature of the physical properties we observe, and these intrinsic properties, organized in the right way, give rise to experience. This is a more modest position than full micropsychism and avoids some of its problems.

---

## 3.3.3 The Combination Problem

The most serious objection to panpsychism — especially micropsychism — is the **combination problem**: how do micro-experiences combine to form the unified, rich experience of a human mind?

The problem is not trivial. My experience of seeing a sunset is unified — the red, the orange, the warmth on my skin, the sound of distant traffic are all parts of a single, integrated experience. If this experience is built from countless micro-experiences in my neurons, how do they combine into this unity? Why don't I experience each neuron's micro-experience separately? How do billions of tiny sparks of micro-experience produce one large, coherent, unified experience?

This problem — which is sometimes called the "subject summing problem" or the "generation problem" — is arguably as hard as the hard problem itself. Philip Goff (2019) acknowledges the combination problem and offers a response in terms of a "constitutive grounding" relationship between micro- and macro-experiences. Others argue that the combination problem is so severe that it makes panpsychism worse than the alternatives.

For our purposes, the important point is this: the combination problem means that panpsychism does not obviously predict that every system with any kind of processing is conscious. Even on a panpsychist view, the kind of organization required to combine micro-experiences into a unified macro-experience may be demanding — it may require exactly the kind of integrated, hierarchical, recursive organization that complex neural systems provide. If so, the panpsychist view and the neural-centric view may make similar predictions about which systems are richly conscious, even if they disagree about whether simple systems have proto-phenomenal properties.

---

## 3.3.4 What Panpsychism Would Predict About Non-Neural Organisms

If panpsychism (of the micropsychist variety) is true, then non-neural organisms — bacteria, plants, fungi — would have micro-experiences. These would not be experiences like human experiences; they would be minimal, alien, alien in ways we probably cannot imagine. But they would be phenomenal: there would be something it is like to be them, however minimal and strange.

What would this mean empirically? This is where panpsychism becomes frustrating: it is very difficult to generate empirically testable predictions from panpsychist premises. Because panpsychism posits micro-experiences at the fundamental physical level, and because our physical theories are formulated in terms of structural properties (mass, charge, spin) that are silent about intrinsic phenomenal properties, there is no obvious way to test the panpsychist claim.

Some researchers (notably Calvo & Trewavas, 2020) have suggested that the richness and flexibility of plant responses — the degree to which they appear to involve integration of information across the organism, anticipation of future states, and sensitivity to context — might serve as indirect evidence for some form of experience. But this argument faces the hard problem in reverse: the evidence would be evidence for functional sophistication, not for phenomenal experience.

The honest assessment is this: panpsychism is a serious philosophical position that addresses a real problem (the hard problem) with a coherent (if controversial) answer. It is relevant to basal cognition as a philosophical backdrop — it makes it *less surprising* if non-neural organisms have some form of experience. But it does not provide empirically testable predictions that would allow basal cognition researchers to claim positive evidence for experience in their systems.

---

## 3.3.5 Russellian Monism as a Middle Path

Of the variants discussed, Russellian monism (panprotopsychism) is perhaps the most defensible from the perspective of basal cognition. It avoids the full implausibility of micropsychism (experience in quarks) while maintaining that the fundamental physical world has intrinsic properties that are not purely structural — properties that may ground phenomenal experience when organized in the right ways.

Crucially, Russellian monism does not predict that any particular system is conscious. Whether the intrinsic properties of a physical system give rise to phenomenal experience depends on how those properties are organized — and this is an open empirical question that different theories of consciousness answer differently.

This agnostic quality makes Russellian monism a philosophically honest position for basal cognition researchers: it refuses to claim that non-neural organisms are definitely conscious, but it also refuses to claim they are definitely not — and it grounds both possibilities in a coherent metaphysical framework.

---

## Section Summary

- Panpsychism is motivated by the hard problem: if consciousness cannot emerge from purely functional organization, it may need to be a fundamental feature of reality
- The combination problem is the most serious objection: how do micro-experiences combine into unified macro-experiences?
- Russellian monism (panprotopsychism) is the most defensible variant: physical reality has intrinsic (proto-phenomenal) properties, but experience requires specific kinds of organization
- For basal cognition: panpsychism does not generate testable predictions, but it provides a coherent metaphysical framework within which non-neural experience is conceptually possible
