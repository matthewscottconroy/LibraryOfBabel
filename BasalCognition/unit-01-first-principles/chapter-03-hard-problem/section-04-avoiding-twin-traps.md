# Section 3.4: Avoiding the Twin Traps — Methodological Stance for Basal Cognition

---

## Section Introduction

This section is the practical payoff of the philosophical work done in Sections 3.1–3.3. We have established that the hard problem is real, that functionalism provides a foundation for substrate-neutral cognitive claims but does not resolve the experience question, and that panpsychism is a coherent but not empirically tractable position. Now we turn to what all of this means for how basal cognition research should be conducted and communicated.

The challenge is navigating between two errors that threaten the credibility and integrity of the field: anthropomorphism on one side, and eliminativist overcaution on the other. This section characterizes each error precisely, explains why each is damaging, and proposes a disciplined methodological stance that avoids both.

---

## 3.4.1 The Trap of Anthropomorphism in Basal Cognition Research

Anthropomorphism is the attribution of human (or specifically, human-like) characteristics to non-human systems. In the context of basal cognition, it typically takes two forms:

**Phenomenal anthropomorphism**: Claiming that non-neural organisms have experiences analogous to human experiences — that a plant "feels" pain when damaged, that a bacterium "enjoys" moving toward food, that a slime mold "wants" to find the exit. These claims project not only cognitive status but phenomenal character onto systems that provide no evidence for the latter.

**Cognitive anthropomorphism**: Using cognitive vocabulary in ways that imply a richness or complexity of processing that the evidence does not support — implying, for example, that a bacterium "thinks through" its response to a chemical gradient, or that a plant "deliberates" about which direction to grow. The functional processes involved are real, but the vocabulary implies something more — a kind of consciousness of the process — that goes beyond the evidence.

Both forms of anthropomorphism are dangerous for the same reason: they make claims that cannot be supported by the evidence, and in doing so, they undermine the credibility of the well-supported empirical claims. When a popular science account claims that plants "are conscious" or that slime molds "have goals" in a phenomenological sense, it gives ammunition to critics who can correctly point out that these claims are unsupported — and those critics then unfairly discount the genuine and important empirical findings.

Some specific examples from the literature where anthropomorphic language has been used carelessly:
- Claiming that Monica Gagliano's Mimosa habituation experiments demonstrate "plant consciousness" (they demonstrate behavioral plasticity, which is important on its own terms without the phenomenological claim)
- Describing mycorrhizal networks as engaging in "intentional" resource allocation, when the evidence supports only that resource allocation varies with cost-benefit ratios in ways that parallel market exchange
- Claiming that slime mold "decisions" involve anything analogous to the subjective experience of deliberation

None of these errors invalidates the underlying empirical findings. The findings stand on their own. The anthropomorphic interpretation is an unjustified addition.

---

## 3.4.2 The Trap of Eliminativist Overcaution

The opposite error is eliminativist overcaution: refusing to apply cognitive vocabulary where it is genuinely warranted, on the grounds that non-neural systems are "merely" chemical or physical. This error typically appears as:

**Vocabulary restriction without justification**: Insisting that words like "sense," "decide," "remember," and "learn" can only apply to neural systems, without giving a principled reason why this is so — without, that is, specifying what feature of neural systems makes these words appropriate there and inappropriate elsewhere.

**Dismissing functional equivalents**: Treating the absence of a neural mechanism as grounds for denying cognitive status, even when the functional organization is equivalent. If an E. coli cell achieves short-term memory through receptor methylation while a neuron achieves short-term memory through synaptic potentiation, and the functional roles are equivalent, then refusing to call E. coli's mechanism "memory" requires a principled argument beyond "it doesn't use the same mechanism."

**Confusing substrate with function**: Claiming that because we understand the chemistry of a process (it is "just chemistry"), it is not cognitive — as if understanding the mechanism of a process somehow reclassified it. Neural processes are also "just chemistry." Understanding how a cognitive process is implemented does not entail that it isn't cognitive.

Eliminativist overcaution is as damaging to the field as anthropomorphism, though it is less frequently discussed. It prevents accurate description of biological phenomena, imposes an unjustified neural bias on the concept of cognition, and closes off research programs that would be scientifically productive.

---

## 3.4.3 The Methodological Stance of This Book

Between the two traps, there is a path that is philosophically defensible and empirically productive. We call it **principled functional agnosticism about experience**. Here are its components:

**1. Functional vocabulary is appropriate wherever functional organization is present.**

When we say that a bacterium "senses aspartate," we are making a claim about its molecular architecture and its behavioral response — a claim that is verifiable by standard scientific methods. The word "senses" here is used in its functional sense: the bacterium has a mechanism that detects aspartate concentrations and modulates behavior accordingly. This claim does not imply experience. It is appropriate wherever the relevant functional organization is present, regardless of substrate.

**2. Phenomenal vocabulary is appropriate only with explicit qualification.**

When we say anything that implies experience — that a system "feels," "experiences," "is aware of," or has "something it is like to be" it — we are making a phenomenal claim that goes beyond the functional evidence. Such claims should:
- Be explicitly flagged as phenomenal claims, not functional ones
- Be accompanied by an acknowledgment that the evidence does not directly support them
- Be framed as hypotheses or philosophical positions, not empirical findings
- Be distinguished from the functional claims that the empirical evidence does support

**3. Agnosticism is not denial.**

Saying that we do not know whether a bacterium has phenomenal experience is not the same as saying it doesn't. The honest position is genuine uncertainty. The absence of evidence for experience in a system is not, by itself, evidence for the absence of experience — especially given the hard problem, which tells us that experience may not leave functional traces that we know how to detect.

**4. The question of experience is scientifically tractable, in principle.**

We should resist the temptation to place the question of non-neural experience entirely outside science. The development of better theories of consciousness — theories that specify, in physical terms, which organizational properties are necessary and sufficient for experience — will generate predictions about non-neural systems. Testing those predictions requires:
- Better theories of consciousness with empirical bite
- Better methods for probing the internal states of non-neural organisms
- Better criteria for what counts as behavioral evidence for experience

This is a research program, not a completed one. But it is a legitimate and important program, and basal cognition research contributes to it by providing detailed empirical accounts of what non-neural organisms actually do.

---

## 3.4.4 Practical Guidance: How to Read This Book

The following conventions are used throughout this book:

**Standard functional claims** ("the bacterium senses aspartate," "the slime mold solves the maze," "the plant habituates to repeated stimulation") are used without qualification. These are functional claims about mechanism and behavior, supported by empirical evidence.

**Contested functional claims** are marked as such ("the bacterium appears to anticipate future conditions, though the mechanism of this effect is debated").

**Phenomenal claims** are made explicitly and carefully ("if, as some researchers have proposed, plants have some form of experience, then...") and are distinguished from the functional claims that surround them.

**Evaluative claims** about the significance of non-neural cognition ("this finding challenges the brain-centric view of intelligence") are substantive claims that this book defends — but they are claims about functional organization and its implications for how we think about cognition, not claims about experience.

The reader who keeps these distinctions in mind will find this book both more useful and more intellectually honest than treatments that collapse the distinctions.

---

## 3.4.5 A Final Note: The Stakes of Getting This Right

Why does this methodological precision matter? It matters for three reasons.

**Scientific credibility**: The basal cognition field has been criticized, sometimes justifiably, for overclaiming. Rigorous methodological standards — distinguishing functional from phenomenal claims, acknowledging uncertainty, using vocabulary precisely — are essential to establishing the field's credibility.

**Ethical implications**: If we overclaim (asserting experience where there is no evidence for it), we may make decisions about how to treat non-neural organisms that are based on fiction rather than evidence. If we underclaim (denying experience where there may be some), we may fail to extend moral consideration to organisms that warrant it. The stakes are real, and they require intellectual honesty.

**Scientific progress**: Questions about experience in non-neural organisms are not going to be answered by assertion — by simply choosing to use phenomenological language or refusing to. They will be answered by developing better theories, better methods, and better criteria. The methodological discipline required to keep functional and phenomenal claims separate is precisely the discipline required to make progress on the harder question.

---

## Section Summary

- Anthropomorphism projects human (or neural) characteristics onto non-neural systems without evidence
- Eliminativist overcaution refuses functional cognitive vocabulary where it is warranted
- The principled stance: functional vocabulary wherever functional organization is present; phenomenal vocabulary only with explicit qualification and acknowledgment of uncertainty
- Agnosticism about experience is not denial — it is intellectual honesty about a genuinely hard problem
- The question of experience in non-neural organisms is scientifically tractable in principle, even if current methods are insufficient to answer it
