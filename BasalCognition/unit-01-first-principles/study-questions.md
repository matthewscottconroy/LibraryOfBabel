# Study Questions — Unit I: First Principles

*These questions are designed to push beyond recall and toward genuine intellectual engagement with the foundational problems of basal cognition. They require synthesis across chapters and willingness to defend a position under pressure.*

## Question 1: The Problem of Definition

Lyon (2006) proposes that cognition should be defined as the capacity to sense, integrate information, and act in goal-directed ways based on that integration — a "biogenic" definition that is broad enough to apply to bacteria but precise enough to exclude thermostats.

**(a)** A critic argues that this definition is too broad: under Lyon's definition, a thermostat "senses" temperature, "integrates" it against a set-point, and "acts" by activating the heating system. The thermostat is goal-directed in the relevant functional sense. Is the thermostat cognitively equivalent to a bacterium on this account? If not, what additional criterion distinguishes them?

**(b)** A second critic argues the definition is too narrow: it ties cognition to the triad of sensing, integrating, and acting, but many paradigm cases of cognition (dreaming, imagining absent objects, doing mental arithmetic) involve no immediate action. How should a biogenic account handle cognition in the absence of current action?

**(c)** Construct your own definition of minimal cognition that addresses both objections. Then identify one organism discussed in Unit I that your definition would classify as cognitive, and one it would exclude, and explain why.

## Question 2: Autopoiesis and Cognition

Maturana and Varela (1980) argue that cognition is not a property that living systems *have* but something they *do*: it is the process of living itself, the self-maintenance of a living system's organisation in the face of environmental perturbation. On this view, even a bacterium maintaining homeostasis is "knowing" its environment in the only relevant sense.

**(a)** What are the philosophical commitments required to accept this position? (Consider: what must you believe about the relationship between function and intentionality?)

**(b)** Dennett's "intentional stance" (1991) proposes that we can attribute beliefs and desires to any system whenever doing so is predictively useful, regardless of that system's internal constitution. Does this support or undermine the autopoietic account? Is there a meaningful difference between "genuinely" being cognitive and "being usefully described as" cognitive?

**(c)** Suppose we discover a chemical reaction network in a deep-sea hydrothermal vent that maintains itself in a far-from-equilibrium state by catalysing specific reactions from its environment. It is self-maintaining but not living in the standard biological sense. Is it cognitive on the autopoietic account? What implications does your answer have for the relationship between life and cognition?

## Question 3: The Hard Problem at the Cellular Level

Nagel's (1974) argument that there is "something it is like" to be a bat — that phenomenal experience cannot be captured by third-person description — has typically been applied to animals with complex nervous systems. Unit I's Chapter 3 extends this question to bacteria.

**(a)** Reconstruct the strongest version of the argument that there might be something it is like to be a bacterium. What properties of the bacterium, if any, would ground such phenomenal states? (Consider: the bacterium has preferences, responds to noxious stimuli, and modifies its behaviour based on past experience. Are any of these sufficient?)

**(b)** Reconstruct the strongest version of the argument that there is nothing it is like to be a bacterium — that phenomenal consciousness requires a level of neural integration that bacteria simply lack. Is this argument empirical, conceptual, or both?

**(c)** Unit I proposes "functional agnosticism about experience" as its methodological stance: remaining agnostic about whether bacteria have phenomenal experience while taking their functional capacities seriously. Is this methodologically coherent? What would it take to resolve the agnosticism? Can the agnosticism ever be resolved in principle?

## Question 4: Life, Entropy, and the Need to Cognize

Chapter 2 argues that cognition is not an optional extra for living systems but a logical necessity: any system that maintains itself far from thermodynamic equilibrium in an uncertain environment must sense, integrate, and act. This is an argument from the nature of life to the universality of cognition.

**(a)** Evaluate this argument. Does the thermodynamic argument establish that all living things cognize, or only that all living things must *do something functionally similar to* cognizing? Is the distinction meaningful?

**(b)** Viruses occupy a contested category: they are not alive in the standard metabolic sense (they do not maintain themselves against entropy independently), but they exhibit sophisticated decision-making (the lysogeny/lysis decision in phage lambda is context-sensitive and apparently adaptive). Where do viruses fit in the argument from thermodynamics to cognition?

**(c)** The "hard problem of life" (what distinguishes living from non-living matter) and the "hard problem of consciousness" (what distinguishes cognitive from non-cognitive processing) may be formally analogous. What does the difficulty of defining life tell us about the difficulty of defining cognition? Does the analogy help, or does it just double the difficulty?

## Question 5: History and Scope of the Concept

The concept of cognition has historically been tied to specific anatomical structures (the brain, the nervous system) and specific taxa (vertebrates, mammals, humans). Basal cognition research is, in part, a project of conceptual expansion.

**(a)** Compare two historical turning points in the expansion of cognitive vocabulary: (i) the extension of learning and memory to invertebrates (e.g., *Aplysia* studies by Kandel in the 1960s–1970s), and (ii) the extension of these concepts to bacteria and plants in the 2000s–2020s. What drove each expansion? What resistance was encountered, and why?

**(b)** Is there a risk that expanding the concept of cognition to all living things renders it meaningless — a case of conceptual inflation in which "cognitive" simply becomes a synonym for "alive"? How does the biogenic definition guard against this, if at all?

**(c)** Consider the political and ethical dimensions of conceptual expansion. How does the recognition of cognition in organisms previously considered non-cognitive affect their moral status? Is this a reason to be cautious about the expansion, or a reason to embrace it? Whose interests are served by different positions in this debate?
