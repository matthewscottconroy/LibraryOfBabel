# Key Concepts: Chapter 4

---

**Bistability**
A property of a dynamical system in which two distinct stable steady states exist simultaneously, separated by an unstable intermediate. In cell biology, bistability is generated by positive feedback loops or double-negative (mutual inhibition) feedback loops combined with nonlinear kinetics. A bistable signaling switch can be in a "high" or "low" activity state, and can switch between them when driven past a threshold by a sufficiently large perturbation. *Why it matters*: Bistability is the molecular basis of irreversible cellular commitment — to differentiation, to cell cycle progression, to apoptosis. It is also the basis of hysteresis, meaning the cell's current state reflects its history, not merely its current conditions.

**Bet-Hedging**
An evolutionary strategy in which a lineage sacrifices optimal performance in any single environment in order to reduce variance in fitness across different environmental conditions. At the cellular level, bet-hedging is implemented through stochastic gene expression that generates phenotypic heterogeneity in genetically identical cell populations. *Why it matters*: Bet-hedging explains why cells sometimes "choose randomly" — the randomness is not a bug but an adaptation to an unpredictable world. Persister cells in bacterial populations are a classic example: their existence is costly under normal conditions but survival-ensuring under antibiotic stress.

**Chemotaxis**
Directed movement of a cell in response to a chemical gradient, toward attractants and away from repellents. In bacteria, chemotaxis is accomplished by biasing a random walk (run-tumble behavior) using temporal comparison of receptor occupancy. In eukaryotes, chemotaxis involves spatial polarization of the cell and directed pseudopod extension. *Why it matters*: Chemotaxis is the paradigmatic example of goal-directed cellular behavior — it demonstrates that a cell can "navigate toward" a desired state using information about its environment, without any neural substrate.

**Flagellar Motor**
A rotary nanoscale engine in bacteria, powered by the proton motive force (proton gradient across the cell membrane), that rotates the helical flagellum. The motor's direction of rotation (counterclockwise = run; clockwise = tumble) is regulated by the concentration of phosphorylated CheY (CheY-P), which is itself regulated by chemoreceptor activity. The motor switch is cooperative, ensuring sharp binary switching rather than graded responses. *Why it matters*: The flagellar motor is the actuator through which cellular decision-making (mediated by the chemotaxis signaling network) is expressed as physical behavior in the world. It is the cell's "body" in the most direct sense.

**G-Protein Coupled Receptor (GPCR)**
The largest class of cell surface receptors, characterized by seven transmembrane helices. Upon ligand binding, GPCRs activate heterotrimeric G-proteins by catalyzing GDP-to-GTP exchange on the Galpha subunit, which then activates downstream effectors (adenylyl cyclase, phospholipase C, ion channels). GPCRs mediate responses to light, odorants, hormones, neurotransmitters, and many other signals. *Why it matters*: GPCRs are ancient, evolutionarily conserved, and extraordinarily diverse — they represent one of evolution's most successful solutions to the problem of sensing the external world and translating that sensing into intracellular chemistry.

**Hysteresis**
A property of bistable systems in which the current state depends on prior history, not just current conditions. A system exhibiting hysteresis will switch from low to high state at a different (higher) signal threshold than the threshold at which it switches from high to low state. The result is that two cells in identical current conditions may be in different states if they have different histories. *Why it matters*: Hysteresis is a form of cellular memory — it allows a cell to "remember" a past experience (sufficient signal to flip a switch) in the form of its current molecular configuration. This memory can persist long after the original signal is removed.

**Mechanosensing**
The detection of physical forces — stretch, compression, shear stress, substrate stiffness — by cellular receptor systems. Key mechanisms include mechanosensitive ion channels (MscL, MscS in bacteria; Piezo1, Piezo2 in eukaryotes) and integrin-mediated detection of substrate stiffness through cytoskeletal tension. *Why it matters*: Mechanosensing demonstrates that cellular cognition is not limited to chemical signals — cells sample the physical properties of their environment and adjust behavior accordingly. This is relevant to development (stem cell differentiation guided by substrate stiffness) and to physiology (vascular mechanosensing, touch and proprioception).

**Network Motif**
A recurring pattern of connections in a signaling or gene regulatory network that appears more frequently than expected by chance and confers specific computational properties. Examples include the feedforward loop (implements temporal filtering), the negative feedback loop (implements adaptation and oscillation), and the coherent feedforward loop with AND logic (implements a persistence detector). First systematically analyzed by Alon and colleagues. *Why it matters*: Network motifs suggest that evolution has converged on specific, reusable computational architectures for solving recurring problems in signal processing. Recognizing these motifs allows us to predict the dynamic behavior of a network from its topology — a powerful bridge between structure and function.

**Run-Tumble Algorithm**
The behavioral strategy of swimming bacteria like *E. coli*: alternating between straight "runs" (flagella rotating counterclockwise, forming a bundle) and "tumbles" (brief clockwise rotation of one or more flagella, causing random reorientation). In the absence of gradients, this produces a random walk; in the presence of attractant gradients, tumble probability decreases when moving up-gradient, producing a biased random walk that efficiently navigates toward attractant sources. *Why it matters*: The run-tumble algorithm is a simple, elegant solution to the navigation problem under conditions of uncertainty and limited information. It illustrates a general principle: that adaptive behavior can emerge from the interaction of simple stochastic rules with environmental feedback, without requiring complex centralized computation.

**Signal Transduction**
The process by which an extracellular signal is converted into an intracellular chemical change that triggers a cellular response. Signal transduction typically involves receptor activation, second messenger production (cAMP, calcium, PIP3), and post-translational modification cascades (phosphorylation, ubiquitination) that ultimately alter protein activity, localization, and gene expression. *Why it matters*: Signal transduction is the molecular implementation of perception and response in the cell. Understanding its architecture — its amplification, logical operations, temporal dynamics, and spatial organization — is essential for understanding how cells "make sense of" their environment.
