# Further Reading: Chapter 5

---

**Levin, M. (2021). Bioelectric signaling: reprogrammable circuits underlying embryogenesis, regeneration, and cancer. *Cell*, *184*(8), 1971–1989.**
The most comprehensive recent review of developmental bioelectricity by its leading practitioner. Covers the molecular mechanisms, the experimental evidence for bioelectric control of morphogenesis, and the theoretical framework of anatomical homeostasis. Includes a clear discussion of both established findings and speculative extensions. Essential reading for anyone who wants to understand the current state and future directions of this field.

---

**Levin, M. (2012). Molecular bioelectricity in developmental biology: new tools and recent discoveries. *BioEssays*, *34*(3), 205–217.**
An earlier review that provides excellent coverage of the key experimental tools — voltage-sensitive dyes, optogenetics, pharmacological ion channel manipulation — that have made modern developmental bioelectricity possible. Good historical context on how the field developed from the electrophysiology tradition. More accessible than the 2021 Cell review for readers new to the field.

---

**Kriegman, S., Blackiston, D., Levin, M., & Bongard, J. (2020). A scalable pipeline for designing reconfigurable organisms. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences USA*, *117*(4), 1853–1859.**
The original Xenobot paper. Describes the computational pipeline for designing living machines from frog cells and demonstrates that the designed organisms exhibit the predicted locomotion. Beyond the technical results, worth reading for its implications about the relationship between genome, morphology, and behavior — and for the philosophical questions it raises about what constitutes a "living organism."

---

**Zhao, M., Song, B., Pu, J., Wada, T., Reid, B., Tai, G., & Bhatt, A. (2006). Electrical signals control wound healing through phosphatidylinositol-3-OH kinase-γ and PTEN. *Nature*, *442*(7101), 457–460.**
A landmark paper demonstrating that endogenous electric fields generated by wounds are genuine instructive signals for cell migration, not mere physical side effects. Shows the specific molecular pathway (PI3K/PTEN) through which cells sense and respond to the wound field, connecting bioelectric physics to familiar cell biology signaling vocabulary. A model of mechanistic rigor in a field that sometimes prioritizes correlation over mechanism.

---

**Doyle, D. A., Morais Cabral, J., Pfuetzner, R. A., Kuo, A., Gulbis, J. M., Cohen, S. L., Bhatt, B. T., & MacKinnon, R. (1998). The structure of the potassium channel: molecular basis of K+ conduction and selectivity. *Science*, *280*(5360), 69–77.**
The Nobel Prize-winning structure of the KcsA potassium channel from bacteria. A landmark in structural biology that revealed, at atomic resolution, how ion selectivity is achieved. The bacterial origin of this channel is philosophically important: it establishes that the molecular machinery of bioelectricity is among the most ancient features of life. Technically demanding but the figures are clear enough to understand the key point — the selectivity filter's mechanism — without reading every word.

---

**Coste, B., Mathur, J., Schmidt, M., Earley, T. J., Ranade, S., Petrus, M. J., Dubin, A. E., & Patapoutian, A. (2010). Piezo1 and Piezo2 are essential components of distinct mechanically activated cation channels. *Science*, *330*(6000), 55–60.**
The discovery paper for the Piezo mechanosensitive channels, which won the Nobel Prize in 2021. A model of careful molecular identification — using siRNA screening to identify channels required for mechanosensation, then electrophysiological characterization confirming their mechanical gating. Demonstrates that major classes of bioelectric sensors were still being discovered as recently as 2010, underscoring that our understanding of cellular sensing remains incomplete.

---

**Beane, W. S., Morokuma, J., Adams, D. S., & Levin, M. (2011). A chemical genetics approach reveals H,K-ATPase-mediated membrane voltage is required for planarian head regeneration. *Chemistry & Biology*, *18*(1), 77–89.**
A key demonstration that bioelectric signals control the head-versus-tail decision in planarian regeneration. Uses pharmacological manipulation of membrane potential (not genetics) to alter the regenerative outcome, providing clear causal evidence that bioelectric state — not just correlating gene expression — is causally upstream of morphogenetic decisions. Technically accessible; the experiments are elegantly designed and clearly reported.

---

**Mousavi, S. A. R., Chauvin, A., Pascaud, F., Kellenberger, S., & Farmer, E. E. (2013). Glutamate receptor-like genes mediate leaf-to-leaf wound signalling. *Nature*, *500*(7463), 422–426.**
Demonstrates that plants use electrical signals — mediated by glutamate receptor-like channels — to communicate wound information rapidly from leaf to leaf. Connects plant bioelectricity to a molecular mechanism (glutamate receptor activation) that is also central to neuroscience. A striking example of deep evolutionary conservation of bioelectric signaling logic across kingdoms.

---

**Levin, M. (2019). The computational boundary of a "self": developmental bioelectricity drives multicellularity and scale-free cognition. *Frontiers in Psychology*, *10*, 2688.**
The paper introducing the "cognitive light cone" concept in its most developed form. Levin argues that what distinguishes different levels of cognitive sophistication is the spatiotemporal scale of coherent, goal-directed coordination, and that bioelectric signaling is the mechanism that extends the cognitive light cone of multicellular organisms. More theoretical than most of Levin's work; engages explicitly with philosophy of mind and cognitive science. Worth reading alongside a critical philosophical commentary.

---

**McCaig, C. D., Rajnicek, A. M., Song, B., & Zhao, M. (2005). Controlling cell behavior electrically: current views and future potential. *Physiological Reviews*, *85*(3), 943–978.**
A comprehensive review of the evidence that applied electric fields can control cell behavior, including migration, division, differentiation, and gene expression. Covers the historical evidence, the experimental systems used, the molecular mechanisms proposed, and the potential clinical applications. Useful for understanding how endogenous electric fields discovered in wound healing contexts connect to the broader program of using bioelectricity therapeutically.
