# Section 2: Bioelectric Signaling in Development

Embryonic development faces a fundamental problem: starting from a fertilized egg, a single cell must orchestrate the construction of a complex, three-dimensional organism with hundreds of distinct cell types arranged in precise spatial patterns. The information guiding this process must come from somewhere — and for most of the history of developmental biology, that somewhere was understood to be DNA. Genes encode morphogens; morphogens diffuse across tissues creating concentration gradients; cells read their position in those gradients and adopt appropriate fates.

That picture is not wrong, but it is incomplete. A parallel layer of developmental information appears to be carried by bioelectric patterns — spatial patterns of membrane potential and ion flux that overlay the molecular gradient landscape and interact with it in complex, bidirectional ways. This section examines the evidence for that claim.

---

## Morphogenetic Fields and Bioelectric Gradients

The concept of a "morphogenetic field" predates molecular biology. Early experimental embryologists in the late 19th and early 20th centuries showed that cells in early embryos could be rearranged, transplanted, or surgically removed, and the embryo would often regulate to produce a normal organism anyway. The concept of the morphogenetic field — a region of embryonic tissue with the capacity to produce a specific structure — was invoked to explain this regulatory ability without specifying its molecular basis.

When bioelectricity researchers began measuring membrane potentials across embryonic tissues with voltage-sensitive dyes, they found something remarkable: distinct, stable patterns of membrane potential that correlated with the boundaries of morphogenetic fields and with the future positions of organ systems. Different regions of a developing frog embryo, for example, have measurably different membrane potentials before any morphological differentiation is visible. Regions with more negative membrane potentials (more hyperpolarized) tend to give rise to different structures than more depolarized regions (Levin et al., 2002).

Are these bioelectric patterns cause or consequence? This is the critical question, and the best evidence that they are causal comes from perturbation experiments. When ion channel activity is pharmacologically altered — using drugs that change the membrane potential of specific cell types in a targeted way — predictable changes in organ development result. Artificially depolarizing cells that would normally be hyperpolarized (or vice versa) at specific developmental stages can redirect their fate, alter the size of organs, and even generate ectopic structures in wrong locations (Levin, 2012).

## Positional Information from Bioelectric Gradients

One of the most striking functions of bioelectric patterns in development is the encoding of positional information — information that tells a cell where it is in the body, so it can adopt the appropriate fate.

In the *Xenopus* tadpole system, researchers found that a spatial pattern of membrane potential exists across the face region of the embryo before the face develops. When this bioelectric pattern was disrupted — specifically, when cells in the wrong region were made to adopt the membrane potential typical of eye-forming cells — ectopic eyes formed in those locations. Conversely, preventing the normal bioelectric pattern in the eye-forming region produced malformed or absent eyes (Pai et al., 2012).

This result suggests that the bioelectric state of a cell carries positional information that is read by the cell to determine its fate — analogous to the role of morphogen concentrations, but in a different channel of biological information. Crucially, the relationship between bioelectric state and fate is not simply a one-way read-out of genetic instructions. The bioelectric patterns themselves can be altered by experience (through gap junction remodeling, changes in ion channel expression), and these alterations propagate to downstream gene expression and cell fate decisions.

The mechanism by which membrane potential influences gene expression is not entirely understood, but several pathways have been identified. Membrane depolarization can trigger calcium entry through voltage-gated channels, and calcium is a ubiquitous second messenger that activates transcription factors (notably NFAT family members). Membrane potential also influences the activity of transport proteins that carry signaling molecules (such as serotonin) across the membrane, and the intracellular concentration of these molecules affects gene expression. The bioelectric state thus connects to the molecular biology of development through multiple routes.

## Left-Right Asymmetry: Bioelectricity Sets the Body Axis

One of the most thoroughly characterized roles of bioelectricity in development is the establishment of left-right body asymmetry. In vertebrates, the heart, liver, spleen, and many other organs are asymmetrically positioned — heart on the left, liver on the right (in most individuals). This asymmetry must be specified very early in development, before any organ is forming, and must be consistent across the billions of cells that will eventually make up each organ. How does the left side of the embryo know to be different from the right side?

Part of the answer involves bioelectricity. Work in *Xenopus* by Levin and colleagues showed that the H+/K+-ATPase pump — an ion pump that exchanges protons for potassium ions — is asymmetrically distributed between the left and right sides of the early embryo (Levin et al., 2002). This asymmetric ion pump activity creates an asymmetric membrane potential and, through the resulting electroosmotic movement of small molecules through gap junctions, an asymmetric distribution of signaling molecules (including serotonin). These asymmetric molecular distributions then activate left-specific or right-specific gene expression programs.

The bioelectric asymmetry appears to be upstream of the molecular asymmetries (Nodal, Lefty, Pitx2) that are better known from later stages of left-right development. This means that bioelectric signals are not merely reporting an already-established molecular asymmetry but are actually creating it — converting the molecular chirality of certain proteins (including the cilia motor protein dynein) into a macroscopic, tissue-scale asymmetry that guides organ positioning.

This is a remarkable result, not just for developmental biology but for our conceptual framework. The handedness of protein molecules — a molecular-scale physical property — is being converted into a centimeter-scale positional signal through bioelectricity. The cell is using electrical physics as a translation layer between the molecular and the anatomical.

## Regeneration and Planaria

Perhaps the most dramatic evidence for the role of bioelectricity in encoding anatomical information comes from studies of regeneration — the ability of certain organisms to regrow lost body parts. Planarian flatworms are among the most remarkable regenerators in the animal kingdom: a planarian cut into pieces will regenerate a complete worm from each piece, including the correct number of heads and tails in the correct positions.

Research by Michael Levin's group has shown that the regenerative decision — whether to regrow a head or a tail at a cut surface — is controlled by bioelectric signals (Beane et al., 2011). The membrane potential of the cells at the wound site determines which structure regenerates. Cells at head-generating surfaces are more hyperpolarized than cells at tail-generating surfaces. Experimentally reversing this polarity — depolarizing cells that would normally regenerate a tail, or hyperpolarizing cells that would normally regenerate a head — causes planaria to regenerate two-headed or two-tailed worms.

The control exerted by this bioelectric signal is upstream of the molecular signals (Wnt pathway activity) that are the direct regulators of head versus tail identity. Membrane potential influences Wnt signaling, and Wnt signaling determines structure — the bioelectric layer is "above" the molecular genetic layer in the causal chain. Crucially, once the new head or tail is specified and growing, the regenerated animal will maintain its double-headed or double-tailed phenotype indefinitely through subsequent re-amputations — even though the drug that originally disrupted the bioelectric signal has long since been washed out. The new bioelectric pattern has become self-sustaining, encoded in the ongoing activity of ion channels and maintained by gap junction coupling across the tissue.

This is bioelectric memory — a pattern of activity that encodes structural information and can be stably maintained and transmitted to regenerating tissues. It is qualitatively similar to the activity-dependent memory of synaptic networks in the brain, but encoded not in synaptic weights but in tissue-wide bioelectric patterns maintained by ion channel expression and gap junction coupling.

## Cancer as a Bioelectric Disease

A striking and still-contested body of evidence suggests that cancer — the uncontrolled proliferation of cells that have escaped normal growth regulation — has a bioelectric dimension. Many cancer cells are found to have more depolarized membrane potentials than their normal counterparts. Conversely, experimental depolarization of normal cells can drive them toward tumor-like behavior, while hyperpolarization can suppress tumor growth.

Levin and colleagues have proposed that membrane potential is not merely correlated with malignancy but causally involved in it (Levin, 2021). Normal cells in a tissue maintain a bioelectric community — a tissue-level pattern of membrane potentials mediated by gap junction coupling — that enforces growth restraint through bioelectric signaling. Cells with abnormal membrane potentials disrupt this community state, lose the bioelectric cues that suppress proliferation, and begin to grow autonomously. Cancer, in this framing, is not merely a disease of mutated genes but a disease of disrupted bioelectric community — a failure of the tissue-level bioelectric information system that normally regulates individual cell behavior.

This hypothesis is provocative and not yet fully established. It is consistent with a significant body of correlative and experimental evidence, but the mechanistic pathways remain to be fully worked out. We examine it further in the next section.
