# Section 4: Bioelectricity Beyond Development

The story of bioelectricity in biology is not confined to embryonic development. Bioelectric signals regulate wound healing, coordinate immune responses, govern gut physiology, and — in a striking recent development — appear in synthetic living systems (Xenobots) that have no evolutionary precedent. This section broadens the picture, showing that bioelectricity is not a specialized tool of embryogenesis but a general-purpose information medium used throughout the life of an organism.

---

## Wound Healing: The Electric Signal of Injury

When tissue is injured, the breach in the epithelial barrier creates an immediate bioelectric signal. Normal epithelial tissue maintains a transepithelial potential — a voltage difference between the apical and basal surfaces of the epithelial sheet — by actively pumping ions. A wound interrupts this potential, creating a local "short circuit" that generates endogenous electric fields extending hundreds of micrometers from the wound edge. This wound-induced electric field is detectable within seconds of injury and persists for hours or days as healing proceeds (Reid et al., 2005).

Migrating cells in the wound vicinity respond to this field by a phenomenon called galvanotaxis — directed movement in response to an electric field. Epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and macrophages all show galvanotaxis: they migrate toward the cathode (the wound edge, which is typically the negative pole of the wound field) and thus toward the site of injury, participating in wound closure. The galvanotactic response is mediated by asymmetric redistribution of membrane proteins — including growth factor receptors — driven by the electric field, which concentrates signaling molecules on the cathodal side of the cell and triggers directional motility.

The wound electric field is not merely a stimulus that triggers migration; it appears to be part of the signal that coordinates the complex choreography of wound healing — attracting different cell types in the right sequence, stimulating proliferation in cells that need to divide, and suppressing proliferation once healing is complete. When the wound field is reversed by external electrodes, wound healing is disrupted; when it is augmented, healing accelerates (Zhao et al., 2006). This has led to clinical interest in electric-field-based therapies for chronic wounds, particularly diabetic ulcers.

## Macrophage Cognition: Electric Fields Guide Immune Response

Macrophages — the "big eaters" of the immune system — are extraordinary cellular navigators. They must track down pathogens, damaged cells, and debris in complex tissue environments, often navigating through dense extracellular matrices over millimeter distances. A key directional signal guiding macrophage migration toward sites of infection and injury is the wound electric field.

Macrophages exhibit galvanotaxis with a sensitivity and directional precision comparable to their chemotactic responses. They can orient in electric fields as weak as a few millivolts per millimeter — well within the range of physiological wound fields — and their galvanotactic threshold is shifted by inflammatory signals. In the presence of bacterial products or pro-inflammatory cytokines, macrophages become more sensitive to electric fields, enabling them to track wound signals more effectively during active infection (Zhao et al., 2006).

The mechanisms underlying macrophage galvanotaxis involve asymmetric activation of PI3K signaling and Rho GTPases in response to the field — the same molecular machinery that mediates chemotaxis. This convergence suggests that electric field sensing and chemical gradient sensing use shared downstream signaling machinery, differing primarily in their sensing mechanisms. The electric field is simply another gradient cue that the macrophage integrates with chemical and physical cues in its navigation decision.

What is cognitively interesting about macrophage galvanotaxis is that it represents a form of action at a distance: the wound site signals its location to distant macrophages through a physical field rather than through diffusion of a chemical molecule. The electric field propagates essentially instantaneously over the relevant tissue distances (compared to diffusion times), providing rapid, directional guidance that chemical gradients cannot match. Bioelectricity here functions as a fast, directional communication channel — exactly analogous to the role of nerve impulses in coordinating animal behavior, but operating through tissue physics rather than specialized neural cells.

## The Gut as Bioelectric System

The gastrointestinal tract is increasingly recognized as a site of sophisticated bioelectric regulation. The gut wall contains a layered epithelium maintained by transepithelial ion transport, a dense network of enteric neurons (the enteric nervous system, sometimes called the "second brain"), and a rich population of non-neural cells including enterochromaffin cells, mast cells, and macrophages — all embedded in a tissue that must coordinate complex peristaltic movements, secretion, absorption, and immune surveillance.

Enterochromaffin cells — epithelial cells distributed throughout the gut lining — release serotonin in response to mechanical stimulation (food and fluid moving through the lumen). This serotonin acts as a paracrine signal to nearby enteric neurons, but it also influences the membrane potential of neighboring epithelial cells through serotonin receptors. The result is a wave of bioelectric activity that propagates along the gut wall, coordinating peristaltic contractions.

This gut bioelectric signaling has a spatial organization that is remarkable for epithelial tissue: the wave of depolarization preceding a peristaltic contraction propagates in a defined oral-to-aboral direction, even though gut epithelial cells are not as overtly specialized for electrical signaling as neurons. The directionality arises from asymmetric gap junction coupling and ion channel distribution along the gut axis — a tissue-scale bioelectric organization that coordinates the muscle contractions of peristalsis.

Disruption of gut bioelectricity has been implicated in motility disorders. Patients with certain forms of irritable bowel syndrome have abnormal patterns of gut electrical activity, and some forms of gastroparesis (paralysis of stomach emptying) involve loss of the slow wave electrical rhythm that paces gastric contractions. Understanding gut bioelectricity is thus not merely intellectually interesting but clinically relevant.

## Xenobots: Synthetic Life and Unexpected Bioelectric Behavior

In 2020, a team led by Josh Bongard, Michael Levin, and Sam Kriegman published a remarkable paper in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* describing the creation of Xenobots — the first "living robots," built from living frog cells (Kriegman et al., 2020). Xenobots are small clusters of *Xenopus laevis* cells, ranging from about 0.5 to 1 millimeter in diameter, that self-assemble from dissociated embryonic cells and spontaneously develop motile behavior driven by cilia on their surfaces.

What is philosophically striking about Xenobots is not merely that they move, but that they exhibit behaviors that were not programmed and could not have been predicted from the cellular components alone. Some Xenobot configurations autonomously navigate mazes. Some exhibit collective behavior, forming clusters of their own accord. In a 2021 follow-up study, Xenobots were found to be capable of kinematic self-replication — sweeping loose cells into piles and organizing them into new Xenobots (Kriegman et al., 2021). This self-replication is a form of reproduction not found in any known natural organism of this type, nor anticipated from the genome of *Xenopus laevis*.

The Xenobot work raises profound questions about the relationship between genome, bioelectric state, and behavior. The cells of a Xenobot have the same genome as normal frog cells — they are not genetically modified. What differs is their morphological context: instead of being embedded in the normal bioelectric community of a developing embryo, they are organized into a novel three-dimensional configuration without the normal gap junction connections and bioelectric gradients of the embryo. The behaviors they exhibit emerge from this novel bioelectric and mechanical configuration interacting with cellular sensory and motility machinery that evolved for a different context.

This suggests that behavioral repertoires are not solely encoded in genomes but are partially encoded in — and constrained by — bioelectric and morphological context. Change the context, and you can unlock behavioral modes that are latent in the cells' molecular machinery but not expressed in any normal developmental trajectory. This is a striking demonstration of the cognitive light cone concept: the same cells have different cognitive capacities in different bioelectric communities.

## Synthesis: Bioelectricity as Universal Tissue Language

Taking stock of what this chapter has covered — resting membrane potentials in all cell types, action potentials in plants and protists, bioelectric patterns guiding embryonic development, wound healing coordinated by electric fields, macrophage navigation guided by galvanotaxis, gut peristalsis paced by bioelectric waves, and synthetic cellular systems exhibiting emergent behavior — a pattern emerges.

Bioelectricity is not a specialized tool of neurons, coopted for signaling in a few specialized contexts. It is a universal feature of cellular biology: a consequence of the energetic demands of living (maintaining ion gradients) that has been repeatedly recruited as an information medium at every level of biological organization. From the membrane potential of a single bacterium to the developmental patterning of an embryo to the coordinated immune response of a tissue, bioelectric signals carry information that guides adaptive behavior.

What makes bioelectric information distinctive, compared to molecular information (protein-protein interactions, transcription factor binding, morphogen gradients)? Several features stand out. Bioelectric signals propagate rapidly compared to diffusion — they are not limited by the slow spread of molecules through tissue. They are inherently spatial — they carry information about position and direction, not just identity. And they are integrative — the membrane potential of any cell in a coupled tissue reflects the collective state of the community, not just local conditions.

These properties make bioelectricity particularly suited to the kind of long-range spatial coordination that multicellular organisms require — the kind of coordination that Levin's cognitive light cone concept identifies as the hallmark of organismal-level cognition. Whether or not one accepts the full cognitive framing, the empirical fact of bioelectric coordination across biological scales is not in serious doubt. It is one of the most important and underappreciated aspects of how living systems process information.
