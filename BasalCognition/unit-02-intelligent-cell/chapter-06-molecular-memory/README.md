# Chapter 6: Molecular Memory and Cellular Learning

## Opening Scene

In the summer of 1966, a biologist named H.S. Jennings published an extraordinary observation. He had been studying *Stentor coeruleus* — a single-celled ciliate, shaped like an inverted cone, that attaches to surfaces and waves its cilia to create feeding currents. Jennings touched his Stentor repeatedly with a fine glass thread, expecting the cell to repeatedly contract into its protective ball shape. What he found was more interesting: the cell contracted at first, but after several repetitions, it stopped contracting. It had, apparently, learned that the repeated stimulus was not dangerous. It had habituated.

This observation was largely forgotten for decades — biologists of the mid-20th century had little conceptual framework for single-cell learning, and many doubted Jennings' accounts. Then, in 2019, a team led by Jeremy Gunawardena at Harvard revisited the question. Using modern microscopy and careful quantitative methods, they confirmed and extended Jennings' results: *Stentor* genuinely habituates to mechanical stimulation, and this habituation has the key features of learned behavior — it is acquired through experience, it is reversible, and it is stimulus-specific (Dexter et al., 2019).

*Stentor* has no nervous system. It has no synapses. It has no neurons. And yet it learns.

## The Central Question

If a single cell can learn, what does that tell us about the nature of learning itself? The central question of this chapter is: **what are the minimal physical requirements for memory and learning, and at what level of biological organization do those requirements first appear?**

This is a deeply unsettling question for anyone who equates learning with neural circuitry. If we define learning as an adaptive change in behavior that persists after the experience that caused it, then *Stentor* learns. So does *E. coli*, in a sense — its receptor methylation system stores a short-term "memory" of recent ligand concentrations, allowing it to compare present with past. So does the vertebrate immune system — its T and B cells "remember" past pathogens and respond more rapidly and vigorously to subsequent encounters. And so, in some sense, do the chromatin modifications we call epigenetic marks — molecular memories that can persist across cell divisions, and in some cases across generations.

## A Note on the Contested Terrain

Much of this chapter covers active research frontiers where interpretations are contested. Transgenerational epigenetic inheritance, in particular, is an area where findings from model organisms (particularly plants and *C. elegans*) do not always generalize cleanly to mammals, and where mechanistic explanations remain incomplete. We will be explicit about what is established and what remains debated. Our goal is to think carefully about the implications of these findings without overstating them.
