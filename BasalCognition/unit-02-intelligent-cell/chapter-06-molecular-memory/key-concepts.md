# Key Concepts: Chapter 6

---

**CRISPR-Cas System**
A bacterial and archaeal immune memory system consisting of a genomic array of short "spacer" sequences derived from past viral infections, flanked by repeating palindromic sequences (the CRISPRs), together with Cas nuclease proteins. When a previously encountered phage invades, the spacer is transcribed into a guide RNA that directs Cas proteins to cleave matching phage DNA. New spacers are acquired by Cas1/Cas2 proteins integrating fragments of invader DNA into the CRISPR array, "recording" the infection. *Why it matters*: CRISPR-Cas is the most literal form of biological memory — it stores genetic sequence information about past threats in the genome itself. It demonstrates that single bacterial cells can store, retrieve, and act on molecular information about their individual past experience, without any neural substrate.

**DNA Methylation**
The covalent addition of a methyl group to cytosine residues in DNA, primarily at CpG dinucleotide sequences in vertebrates. Maintained through cell division by the maintenance methyltransferase DNMT1, which copies methyl marks from the template strand to the new strand during replication. Promoter hypermethylation typically silences gene expression by recruiting repressive chromatin remodeling factors. *Why it matters*: DNA methylation is the most durable form of epigenetic memory known — it is faithfully replicated through thousands of cell divisions and can maintain cell type identity throughout an organism's lifetime. It is the molecular basis of the "locked-in" differentiated state of mature cells.

**Epigenetic Memory**
Changes in gene expression that are heritable through cell division but do not involve changes to the DNA sequence. Molecular substrates include DNA methylation, histone modifications, and certain RNA-based mechanisms. Epigenetic memory is established by developmental signals and maintained through the cooperative action of chromatin-modifying enzymes. *Why it matters*: Epigenetic memory is the mechanism by which a cell's history — the developmental signals it and its ancestors have received — is stored and maintained in its current functional state. It explains how genetically identical cells in the same organism can have radically different phenotypes. It also raises the possibility that environmental experiences can leave lasting molecular imprints.

**Habituation**
A decrease in behavioral response following repeated exposure to a stimulus, that is (a) specific to the habituated stimulus, (b) reversible with rest, and (c) subject to dishabituation by a novel strong stimulus. One of the simplest operationally defined forms of learning, found in organisms ranging from invertebrates to mammals — and, as the *Stentor* experiments show, in single cells. *Why it matters*: Habituation is operationally the simplest form of non-neural learning for which we have convincing evidence. Its presence in *Stentor* establishes that learning can occur without a nervous system, placing a lower bound on the structural complexity required for memory.

**Histone Code**
The set of post-translational modifications on histone proteins — primarily on their N-terminal tails — that collectively regulate chromatin structure and gene expression. Key modifications include methylation of H3K4 (associated with active genes), methylation of H3K27 (associated with Polycomb-mediated repression), and acetylation of multiple lysines (associated with open, active chromatin). The "code" analogy refers to the combinatorial nature of these marks and the fact that different combinations are "read" by different regulatory proteins. *Why it matters*: The histone code provides a second layer of chromatin-based memory, above and beyond DNA methylation, that is more dynamic and context-responsive. It is the substrate of "trained immunity," stress memory, and many forms of experience-dependent gene expression change.

**Predictive Switching**
A regulatory behavior in which an organism (or cell) responds to signal A by preparing for signal B, based on the statistical correlation between A and B in the natural environment. In *E. coli*, the presence of lactose triggers pre-induction of maltose metabolism genes, because lactose typically precedes maltose in the bacterial gut environment. The prediction is evolutionarily encoded in the regulatory network architecture. *Why it matters*: Predictive switching demonstrates that single cells can exhibit anticipatory behavior — responding not just to current conditions but to probable future conditions predicted from current information. This is a form of inference at the molecular level, implemented through the architecture of gene regulatory networks shaped by evolutionary experience.

**Trained Immunity**
A form of memory in innate immune cells (particularly macrophages and monocytes) in which prior exposure to a pathogen-associated molecule (such as beta-glucan) leads to enhanced non-specific responses to subsequent pathogen encounters. The trained state is maintained by specific histone methylation and acetylation changes at inflammatory gene promoters and can persist for weeks or months. *Why it matters*: Trained immunity demonstrates that memory-like phenomena are not restricted to adaptive immune cells with specific antigen receptors. It shows that epigenetic changes to chromatin state can function as a form of experiential memory in cells of the innate immune system, generalizing the concept of immunological memory beyond its traditional boundaries.

**Transgenerational Epigenetic Inheritance (TEI)**
The transmission of epigenetic information from parent to offspring through the germline, such that offspring phenotype is influenced by parental epigenetic experience. Well-documented in plants and *C. elegans*; more contested and mechanistically unclear in mammals. Potential mechanisms include incomplete erasure of parental epigenetic marks during gametogenesis, transmission of small RNA molecules in sperm or eggs, and maternal cytoplasmic factors. *Why it matters*: TEI, if real and general, would represent a molecular mechanism for soft inheritance — the transmission of acquired characteristics — and would have significant implications for evolutionary biology. The current evidence suggests it is real but limited (few generations, specific mechanisms, not fully general), warranting careful evaluation rather than either dismissal or uncritical acceptance.
