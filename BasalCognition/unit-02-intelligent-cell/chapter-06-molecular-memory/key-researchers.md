## Jeremy Gunawardena
**Institution**: Harvard Medical School (Department of Systems Biology)
**Key Contribution**: Gunawardena's laboratory takes a mathematically rigorous approach to understanding how biochemical systems process and store information. He is known for applying algebraic topology and network theory to signaling systems. His laboratory's revisitation of the classic *Stentor* habituation experiments (Dexter et al., 2019) provided the first modern, rigorously controlled demonstration of habituation in a single-celled organism, establishing the conceptual and experimental foundation for studying non-neural learning at the cellular level. More broadly, his work on "steady-state" theories of cellular computation provides a mathematical framework for understanding how regulatory networks can implement memory and learning.

**Key Papers and Works**:
- Dexter, J. P., Prabakaran, S., & Gunawardena, J. (2019). A complex hierarchy of avoidance behaviors in a single-cell eukaryote. *Current Biology*, *29*(24), 4323–4329.e2.

## Yitzhak Pilpel
**Institution**: Weizmann Institute of Science, Israel
**Key Contribution**: Pilpel's laboratory studies the evolution of gene regulatory networks, with particular focus on how regulatory network architecture encodes "knowledge" about environmental regularities. The landmark Mitchell et al. (2009) Nature paper from his group demonstrated that *E. coli* regulatory circuits anticipate future environmental states — specifically, that lactose presence triggers preemptive induction of maltose genes, based on the statistical association between these nutrients in the bacterial gut. This work provided the clearest demonstration of evolutionary encoding of predictive behavior in a microbial regulatory network and opened a productive research program on "Bayesian" regulatory networks.

**Key Papers and Works**:
- Mitchell, A., Romano, G. H., Groisman, B., Yona, A., Dekel, E., Kupiec, M., Dahan, O., & Pilpel, Y. (2009). Adaptive prediction of environmental changes by microorganisms. *Nature*, *460*(7252), 220–224.

## Mihai Netea
**Institution**: Radboud University Medical Center, Netherlands
**Key Contribution**: Netea's laboratory discovered and systematically characterized "trained immunity" — the non-specific immunological memory of innate immune cells. His group showed that macrophages and monocytes exposed to pathogens or pathogen components undergo lasting epigenetic changes (histone methylation and acetylation) that enhance their responses to subsequent diverse stimuli, and that these changes can be transmitted to monocyte precursors in the bone marrow. This work fundamentally revised the understanding of immunological memory, showing it is not restricted to adaptive immune cells with antigen-specific receptors.

**Key Papers and Works**:
- Netea, M. G., Quintin, J., & van der Meer, J. W. M. (2011). Trained immunity: a memory for innate host defense. *Cell Host & Microbe*, *9*(5), 355–361.

## Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier
**Institutions**: University of California, Berkeley (Doudna); Max Planck Unit for the Science of Pathogens, Berlin (Charpentier)
**Key Contribution**: Although best known for adapting CRISPR-Cas9 into a gene-editing tool (Nobel Prize in Chemistry, 2020), Doudna and Charpentier's foundational work on CRISPR-Cas mechanisms illuminated the biological function of the system as a bacterial adaptive immune memory. Their mechanistic characterization of how guide RNAs direct Cas proteins to cleave specific DNA sequences, and how spacers are acquired from invading DNA, established the molecular basis of CRISPR as a molecular memory system. Understanding CRISPR as memory — not just as a tool — is the frame this chapter uses.

**Key Papers and Works**:
- Wiedenheft, B., Sternberg, S. H., & Doudna, J. A. (2012). RNA-guided genetic silencing systems in bacteria and archaea. *Nature*, *482*(7385), 331–338.
- Jinek, M., Chylinski, K., Fonfara, I., Hauer, M., Doudna, J. A., & Charpentier, E. (2012). A programmable dual-RNA-guided DNA endonuclease in adaptive bacterial immunity. *Science*, *337*(6096), 816–821.

## Oded Rechavi
**Institution**: Tel Aviv University
**Key Contribution**: Rechavi's laboratory studies transgenerational epigenetic inheritance in *C. elegans*, with particular focus on the transmission of small RNA-based responses across generations. His work demonstrated that exposure to viral RNA triggers a heritable, small-RNA-mediated antiviral response in *C. elegans* that persists for multiple generations. His laboratory has also shown that neuronal activity can generate small RNAs that influence germline gene expression, raising the remarkable possibility that learned behaviors can leave heritable molecular traces. Rechavi is also notable for his clear-headed assessment of when TEI evidence is and is not convincing, making him an important voice for rigor in a field prone to overclaiming.

**Key Papers and Works**:
- Rechavi, O., Minevich, G., & Hobert, O. (2011). Transgenerational inheritance of an acquired small RNA-based antiviral response in *C. elegans*. *Cell*, *147*(6), 1248–1256.
