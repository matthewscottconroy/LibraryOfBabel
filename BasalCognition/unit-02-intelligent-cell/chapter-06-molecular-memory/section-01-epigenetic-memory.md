# Section 1: Epigenetic Memory

The word "epigenetic" has acquired a bewildering range of meanings in popular discourse — from a precise technical term to a vague invocation of environmental influence on gene expression. We will use it precisely: epigenetic refers to changes in gene expression that are heritable through cell division but do not involve changes to the DNA sequence itself. Epigenetic memory is the persistence of such changes over time, maintaining a cell's identity and functional state across divisions that could, in principle, erase it.

Understanding epigenetic memory is essential for understanding cellular identity, development, and disease. But it is also philosophically interesting: epigenetic marks represent a form of molecular memory that is stored at a different level than genetic information, that can be acquired through experience, and that can sometimes — controversially — persist across generations.

## DNA Methylation: Writing in the Genome

The most studied epigenetic modification is DNA methylation — the addition of a methyl group (-CH3) to the 5-position of cytosine residues, primarily at CpG dinucleotide sequences in vertebrates. Methylated cytosine (5-methylcytosine, or 5-mC) is sometimes called the "fifth base" of the genome, because it is maintained with a fidelity comparable to that of the four standard bases during DNA replication.

The enzyme responsible for "reading" existing methylation patterns and "copying" them to the newly synthesized strand during replication is DNMT1, the maintenance methyltransferase. It recognizes hemimethylated DNA (one strand methylated, one unmethylated, as occurs immediately after replication) and methylates the new strand at CpG sites already methylated on the template strand. This copying mechanism makes DNA methylation a heritable epigenetic mark — it is reliably propagated from mother to daughter cells through division, carrying information about the epigenetic state of the original cell into its descendants.

The functional consequence of DNA methylation depends on context. Methylation of CpG-rich promoter regions typically represses gene expression by recruiting methyl-CpG binding proteins and histone deacetylases, which compact chromatin into a transcriptionally silent state. This is how permanently silenced genes — imprinted genes, X-inactivated genes, tissue-specific silenced genes — are kept off indefinitely. Once a promoter is methylated, the gene tends to stay off through all subsequent cell divisions.

Methylation patterns are established during development by de novo methyltransferases (DNMT3A and DNMT3B) that add methyl groups to previously unmethylated sequences. The patterns established by these enzymes reflect the developmental context in which the cell exists and the signals it has received — environmental influences are converted into DNA methylation patterns by pathways that are still being characterized. These patterns are then maintained by DNMT1 through all subsequent divisions, giving the cell a molecular "memory" of the developmental context in which its current identity was specified.

DNA methylation is not merely stable — it can be actively reversed. The TET (ten-eleven translocation) enzymes oxidize 5-mC to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5-hmC) and further oxidized forms, which are then recognized by the base excision repair machinery and replaced with unmethylated cytosine. This active demethylation pathway allows cells to erase methylation marks during reprogramming events — as occurs in primordial germ cells and in the early embryo after fertilization.

## Histone Modification: The Histone Code

DNA in eukaryotic cells is not naked; it is wrapped around histone octamers (two copies each of histones H2A, H2B, H3, and H4) to form nucleosomes, which are further organized into chromatin fibers. The histone proteins — particularly their N-terminal "tails" — are extensively modified by post-translational modifications: methylation, acetylation, phosphorylation, ubiquitination, and more. These modifications collectively constitute the "histone code," a combinatorial system of marks that regulates chromatin accessibility and gene expression (Strahl & Allis, 2000).

Histone modifications are written by "writer" enzymes (histone acetyltransferases, methyltransferases), erased by "erasers" (deacetylases, demethylases), and read by "reader" proteins that recognize specific modifications and recruit other regulatory factors. The combination of marks at any genomic locus constitutes a regulatory state that determines whether the locus is accessible to transcription factors and RNA polymerase, or compacted into silent heterochromatin.

How is histone modification memory maintained through cell division? This is a more complex question than for DNA methylation. Nucleosomes are partially disrupted during DNA replication and must be reassembled on the new chromatin. The question is whether the parental histone marks are faithfully propagated to the reassembled nucleosomes. Evidence suggests that histone modifications can be propagated through a combination of retention of parental histones on both daughter strands and recruitment of modifier enzymes by chromatin-associated proteins that remain bound through replication. The Polycomb and Trithorax complexes — key regulators of developmental gene expression — appear to use this mechanism to maintain gene silencing or activation across cell divisions (Hansen et al., 2008).

The most philosophically interesting aspect of histone modification is its responsiveness to cellular experience. The histone acetylase and deacetylase enzymes are regulated by metabolic state, stress signals, and environmental inputs. The chromatin state of a cell at any time thus reflects the integrated history of the signals it has received. This is molecular memory at the chromatin level: the histone marks record, in a format that persists across cell divisions, something about the conditions in which the cell has lived.

## Transgenerational Epigenetic Inheritance

The most radical claim in epigenetic biology is transgenerational epigenetic inheritance (TEI) — the transmission of epigenetic information from parent to offspring through the germline, such that the offspring's phenotype reflects not just their genome but their parents' or grandparents' epigenetic experience.

The evidence for TEI is strongest and clearest in plants and in *Caenorhabditis elegans*. In *C. elegans*, exposure to pathogenic bacteria can alter small RNA populations in the germline in ways that change immune gene expression in subsequent generations (Rechavi et al., 2011). In plants, stress-induced methylation changes at some loci can be transmitted to subsequent generations. In both cases, the molecular carriers of transgenerational inheritance appear to be small RNA molecules (particularly piRNAs and siRNAs) rather than — or in addition to — DNA methylation changes themselves.

In mammals, the evidence for TEI is more equivocal and mechanistically less clear. Several studies have reported that parental diet, stress, or environmental toxin exposure alters offspring phenotype in ways that suggest epigenetic transmission. However, a key mechanistic challenge is that mammalian embryogenesis involves genome-wide epigenetic reprogramming — the erasure of most parental epigenetic marks at fertilization and again in primordial germ cells. For transgenerational inheritance to occur, some marks must somehow escape these two rounds of reprogramming.

Candidate mechanisms include the incomplete erasure of DNA methylation at certain imprinting control regions, the survival of certain small RNA species through reprogramming, and maternal transmission of protein factors through the egg cytoplasm. None of these mechanisms has been established as a general pathway for TEI in mammals, and considerable skepticism remains in the field about the generality and reliability of TEI in mammalian systems (Heard & Martienssen, 2014).

The conceptual significance of TEI, if real, is substantial: it would mean that the experiences of individuals can, through molecular mechanisms, influence the biology of their descendants. This would be a form of Lamarckian inheritance — the transmission of acquired characters — which neo-Darwinian evolutionary theory has traditionally excluded. We should be careful about overstating the implications: even well-established TEI in *C. elegans* operates over a limited number of generations (typically 1-5) and involves specific molecular pathways, rather than representing a general, unlimited mechanism for inheritance of acquired characters. But the principle — that epigenetic information can carry biological "memory" across generations — is now established in enough systems to take seriously.

## Epigenetic Memory and Cellular Identity

Taking a step back from the controversies, the function of epigenetic memory in the life of individual organisms is not controversial: it is the molecular basis of cellular identity. A liver cell and a neuron have the same genome but radically different phenotypes — they express different genes, have different shapes, perform different functions. This difference is maintained not by genetic differences but by epigenetic differences: different patterns of DNA methylation, histone modification, and chromatin organization that establish and perpetuate tissue-specific gene expression patterns through all the cell divisions of adult life.

This stable, heritable differentiation is a form of cellular memory in the deepest sense: every cell carries in its chromatin state a record of its developmental history, of the signals that pushed its progenitors toward one lineage rather than another, that specification decisions were made long ago that constrain what the cell is now. The neuron cannot decide to become a liver cell; its chromatin state has locked it into neural identity through mechanisms that are resistant to reversal under normal conditions.

The fact that chromatin states can sometimes be reprogrammed — by Yamanaka factors, by somatic cell nuclear transfer, or by certain pathological conditions — shows that this lock is not absolute. But the stability of normal epigenetic memory in adult tissues is remarkable. A cell that divided every 24 hours for 70 years — making over 25,000 generations — would maintain its tissue identity throughout, propagating the same chromatin state generation after generation. That is a form of molecular memory whose fidelity rivals that of DNA sequence itself.
