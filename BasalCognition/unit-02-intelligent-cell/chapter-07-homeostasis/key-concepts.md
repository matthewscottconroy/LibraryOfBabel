# Key Concepts: Chapter 7

---

**Active Inference**
In Friston's free energy principle framework, the process by which a biological system acts on its environment to bring sensory data in line with model-based predictions, rather than simply updating its model to match sensory data. A system performing active inference treats its actions as predictions about what sensory states it should observe, and generates behavior that would produce those predicted sensory states. *Why it matters*: Active inference provides a unified computational framework for understanding both perception (belief updating) and action (world changing) as aspects of a single process — free energy minimization. Applied to cellular biology, it reframes homeostatic regulation as prediction-error minimization.

**Allostasis**
Proposed by Sterling and Eyer (1988), the process by which a biological system achieves physiological stability through change — specifically, by predictively adjusting the set points of regulated variables in anticipation of upcoming demands, rather than simply maintaining fixed set points through reactive negative feedback. Key examples include anticipatory heart rate increases before exercise and morning cortisol peaks before waking. *Why it matters*: Allostasis captures the predictive, proactive character of biological regulation that classical homeostasis misses. It implies that regulatory systems maintain models of anticipated demands and calibrate set points accordingly — a claim with implications for understanding stress, disease, and the regulatory costs of chronic adversity (allostatic load).

**Allostatic Load**
The cumulative physiological "wear and tear" produced by chronic activation of allostatic regulatory responses. When allostatic demands are chronic or unpredictable, the systems involved in allostatic regulation (cardiovascular, neuroendocrine, metabolic, immune) accumulate functional impairment — elevated resting heart rate, dysregulated cortisol rhythms, insulin resistance, chronic low-grade inflammation. *Why it matters*: Allostatic load provides a biological mechanism linking chronic stress and adverse social conditions to disease outcomes. It is a concrete cellular and physiological account of how living conditions "get under the skin."

**Free Energy Principle (FEP)**
Friston's proposal that all living systems are organized around the minimization of variational free energy — an information-theoretic quantity that bounds the surprise (negative log probability) of sensory states. Systems minimize free energy by either updating their internal models to better match sensory data (perception/learning) or acting on the world to change sensory data toward what the model predicts (action). *Why it matters*: The FEP provides a potentially unified mathematical framework for understanding perception, action, learning, and self-organization across all biological systems, from single cells to complex organisms. Its application to non-neural organisms is actively debated.

**Homeostasis**
The maintenance of a physiological variable at or near a preferred set point value through negative feedback control. First articulated by Claude Bernard as the stability of the *milieu intérieur*, and named and systematically studied by Walter Cannon. Key elements: a regulated variable, a set point, error detection, a proportional corrective response, and a feedback loop. Operates at the molecular level (cellular pH, calcium, redox state) through the organismal level (temperature, blood pressure, blood glucose). *Why it matters*: Homeostasis is the most fundamental organizational principle of cellular and organismal biology. Every cell is a homeostatic machine, spending significant energy to maintain its internal variables within the ranges that permit life. The homeostatic framework connects cellular physiology to goal-directed behavior.

**Milieu Intérieur**
Claude Bernard's term (French: "internal environment") for the fluid internal environment of the body — the blood, lymph, and interstitial fluid — whose stability Bernard identified as the condition of a free and independent life. The concept captures the insight that multicellular organisms must maintain the physical and chemical conditions of their internal environment within narrow limits, independent of the variable external world. *Why it matters*: The milieu intérieur concept is the historical foundation of homeostasis theory and the modern understanding of physiological regulation. It frames the organism as an active stabilizer, not a passive object, and identifies the maintenance of internal stability as a fundamental biological imperative.

**Negative Feedback**
A control mechanism in which a deviation of a regulated variable from its set point produces a response that acts to reduce the deviation. The larger the deviation, the stronger the corrective response — and the corrective response itself reduces the deviation, closing the feedback loop. Negative feedback is the mechanistic basis of homeostasis. *Why it matters*: Negative feedback is the simplest implementation of goal-directed behavior at the molecular level. By defining a set point and generating responses proportional to deviation from it, negative feedback systems embody a minimal form of "preference" for particular internal states. All known homeostatic systems use negative feedback at their core.

**Valence**
The property of a state or stimulus of being positive (approach-promoting, rewarding) or negative (avoidance-promoting, aversive). In psychology and neuroscience, valence is typically discussed in the context of subjective experience — how good or bad something feels. In this chapter's framework, valence is recast as a functional property of homeostatic regulatory systems: states that promote homeostasis have positive functional valence (they are approached), and states that disrupt it have negative functional valence (they are avoided). *Why it matters*: Reconceiving valence as a functional property of regulatory systems rather than a subjective experiential property allows it to be applied to non-neural organisms, including bacteria. This grounds the concept of valence in evolutionary biology and provides a mechanistic account of its origin: valence evolved because organisms that were attracted to homeostasis-promoting states and repelled by homeostasis-disrupting states left more descendants.

**Variational Free Energy**
The specific information-theoretic quantity minimized by biological systems in Friston's free energy principle. Mathematically, it equals the negative log probability of sensory data under the agent's model (the "accuracy" term) plus the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the agent's current beliefs and its prior beliefs (the "complexity" term). It provides an upper bound on the "surprise" of sensory observations and can be minimized either by updating beliefs (perception) or by acting to change sensory states (action). *Why it matters*: Variational free energy is the technical core of the FEP, connecting the conceptual framework of surprise minimization to computable quantities that can be used to make specific predictions about neural and biological systems.
