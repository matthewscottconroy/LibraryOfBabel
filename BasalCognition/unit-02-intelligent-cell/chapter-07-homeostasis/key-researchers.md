# Key Researchers: Chapter 7

---

## Walter Cannon
**Institution**: Harvard Medical School (1871–1945)
**Key Contribution**: Cannon coined the term "homeostasis" and provided its first systematic scientific treatment in his 1932 book *The Wisdom of the Body*. His experimental work established the physiological basis of the fight-or-flight response (identifying the role of adrenal hormones in acute stress), demonstrated the role of the autonomic nervous system in visceral regulation, and characterized multiple examples of homeostatic control of body temperature, blood pressure, blood glucose, and other variables. Cannon understood homeostasis not merely as a descriptive term but as a theoretical framework connecting physiology to control theory, and his work laid the conceptual foundation for all subsequent research on biological regulation.

**Key Papers and Works**:
- Cannon, W. B. (1932). *The Wisdom of the Body*. W.W. Norton.

## Claude Bernard
**Institution**: Collège de France, Paris (1813–1878)
**Key Contribution**: Bernard is the intellectual founder of modern physiology and the original articulator of the *milieu intérieur* concept. His experimental work — on the liver's role in glucose metabolism, the vasomotor innervation of blood vessels, the mechanism of action of curare, and the digestive physiology of the pancreas — was conducted with a rigor and conceptual clarity that was far ahead of his time. His philosophical writings, particularly *Introduction to the Study of Experimental Medicine* (1865), articulated the scientific method for biology and established the centrality of controlled experimentation and the stability of the internal environment as organizing principles for biological inquiry.

**Key Papers and Works**:
- Bernard, C. (1865). *Introduction à l'étude de la médecine expérimentale*. J.B. Baillière. [English translation: *An Introduction to the Study of Experimental Medicine*, 1927, Macmillan]

## Peter Sterling
**Institution**: University of Pennsylvania (Perelman School of Medicine)
**Key Contribution**: Sterling, a neuroscientist and physician, proposed the concept of allostasis with Joseph Eyer in 1988 and has spent the subsequent decades developing and refining it. His work challenges the classical homeostatic model of biological regulation, arguing that biological regulatory systems are fundamentally predictive — they adjust set points in anticipation of upcoming demands rather than simply correcting errors after they occur. More broadly, Sterling has been a prominent critic of pharmaceutical approaches to disease that focus on restoring homeostatic variables to "normal" levels without addressing the allostatic dysregulation that drove them from normal in the first place. His book *Allostasis: A Model of Predictive Regulation* (2012) synthesizes three decades of work.

**Key Papers and Works**:
- Sterling, P., & Eyer, J. (1988). Allostasis: a new paradigm to explain arousal pathology. In S. Fisher & J. Reason (Eds.), *Handbook of Life Stress, Cognition and Health* (pp. 629–649). John Wiley & Sons.
- Sterling, P. (2012). Allostasis: a model of predictive regulation. *Physiology & Behavior*, *106*(1), 5–15.

## Karl Friston
**Institution**: University College London (Wellcome Centre for Human Neuroimaging)
**Key Contribution**: Friston is arguably the most influential theoretical neuroscientist of his generation. His free energy principle — the proposal that all living systems minimize variational free energy, or equivalently, minimize surprise about their sensory states — represents an ambitious attempt to unify the fields of computational neuroscience, statistical physics, and philosophy of mind under a single mathematical framework. The FEP has generated a large and growing literature in theoretical neuroscience, and has been extended to encompass perception (predictive processing), action (active inference), learning (model updating), and self-organization (morphogenesis). Whether the FEP is a genuine explanatory theory or a powerful mathematical redescription of existing phenomena is a live debate, but its influence on the field is unquestionable.

**Key Papers and Works**:
- Friston, K. (2010). The free-energy principle: a unified brain theory? *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, *11*(2), 127–138.
- Friston, K. (2013). Life as we know it. *Journal of the Royal Society Interface*, *10*(86), 20130475.

## Antonio Damasio
**Institution**: University of Southern California (Brain and Creativity Institute)
**Key Contribution**: Damasio is a neurologist and neuroscientist who has made foundational contributions to understanding the relationship between emotion, body, and decision-making. His somatic marker hypothesis — that emotional feelings (body states) guide decision-making by providing rapid evaluative signals — challenged the Cartesian separation of reason and emotion and placed the body at the center of rational cognition. His research on patients with ventromedial prefrontal cortex damage (who have impaired emotional responses and make poor real-world decisions despite intact logical reasoning) provided the empirical grounding for this hypothesis. In the context of this chapter, Damasio's framework provides a neural-level account of how homeostatic state deviation generates behavioral motivation — an account that can be scaled down to the cellular level.

**Key Papers and Works**:
- Damasio, A. (1994). *Descartes' Error: Emotion, Reason, and the Human Brain*. Putnam.
- Damasio, A. (1999). *The Feeling of What Happens: Body and Emotion in the Making of Consciousness*. Harcourt.
