# Key Researchers: Chapter 8

## Howard Berg
**Institution**: Harvard University (1934–2021)
**Key Contribution**: Berg was the founding figure of bacterial motility and chemotaxis research. His invention of the three-dimensional tracking microscope allowed the first detailed characterization of *E. coli* swimming behavior, establishing the run-tumble algorithm. He subsequently made major contributions to understanding the flagellar motor — characterizing its rotational mechanism, its power source (proton motive force), and the cooperativity of the flagellar switch. His work with Edward Purcell on the physics of chemoreception established the fundamental limits of bacterial sensing and showed that *E. coli* operates near those limits. His book *E. coli in Motion* (2004, Springer) is the definitive accessible synthesis of the field. Berg's approach — combining exquisite physics with deep biology — exemplified a style of inquiry that this field continues to benefit from.

**Key Papers and Works**:
- Berg, H. C., & Brown, D. A. (1972). Chemotaxis in *Escherichia coli* analysed by three-dimensional tracking. *Nature*, *239*(5374), 500–504.
- Berg, H. C., & Purcell, E. M. (1977). Physics of chemoreception. *Biophysical Journal*, *20*(2), 193–219.
- Berg, H. C. (2004). *E. coli in Motion*. Springer.

## Victor Sourjik
**Institution**: Max Planck Institute for Terrestrial Microbiology, Germany
**Key Contribution**: Sourjik has made seminal contributions to understanding the molecular architecture of bacterial chemotaxis signaling, particularly the receptor clustering that underlies signal amplification. His laboratory developed fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET)-based assays to measure signaling activity in living bacteria with spatial and temporal resolution, allowing direct observation of receptor cluster dynamics, CheY-P levels, and motor switching in real time. This work revealed the quantitative relationships between receptor occupancy, kinase activity, and motor switching that are central to understanding how the chemotaxis network achieves its signal amplification and sensitivity. His reviews of bacterial chemotaxis provide some of the clearest integrative summaries of the field.

**Key Papers and Works**:
- Sourjik, V., & Wingreen, N. S. (2012). Responding to chemical gradients: bacterial chemotaxis. *Current Opinion in Cell Biology*, *24*(2), 262–268.
- Sourjik, V., & Berg, H. C. (2002). Receptor sensitivity in bacterial chemotaxis. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences USA*, *99*(1), 123–127.

## Nathalie Balaban
**Institution**: Hebrew University of Jerusalem
**Key Contribution**: Balaban's laboratory pioneered the quantitative study of bacterial persisters — the drug-tolerant subpopulation of bacterial cultures that survive antibiotic treatment and can re-establish infections. Her landmark 2004 *Science* paper provided the first single-cell characterization of persister formation, showing that persisters arise through stochastic switching between normal and dormant states, not through genetic mutation. This work transformed understanding of antibiotic treatment failure and established persister formation as a paradigm of bacterial bet-hedging. Her laboratory has subsequently characterized the molecular mechanisms of persister formation (particularly toxin-antitoxin modules) and explored therapeutic strategies to eliminate persisters.

**Key Papers and Works**:
- Balaban, N. Q., Merrin, J., Chait, R., Kowalik, L., & Leibler, S. (2004). Bacterial persistence as a phenotypic switch. *Science*, *305*(5690), 1622–1625.

## Uri Alon
**Institution**: Weizmann Institute of Science, Israel
**Key Contribution**: (See Chapter 4; repeated here because his work is equally central to this chapter.) Alon's systematic analysis of network motifs in bacterial transcriptional networks — showing that feedforward loops, negative autoregulation, and other computational motifs are overrepresented in real networks — provides the conceptual backbone for understanding bacterial gene regulatory networks as information-processing systems. His identification of the computational properties of each motif type (temporal filtering, noise reduction, pulse generation) allows bacterial regulatory behavior to be understood in engineering terms without losing biological accuracy.

**Key Papers and Works**:
- Alon, U. (2007). *An Introduction to Systems Biology: Design Principles of Biological Circuits*. Chapman & Hall/CRC.
- Milo, R., Shen-Orr, S., Itzkovitz, S., Kashtan, N., Chklovskii, D., & Alon, U. (2002). Network motifs: simple building blocks of complex networks. *Science*, *298*(5594), 824–827.

## Richard Losick
**Institution**: Harvard University (Department of Molecular and Cellular Biology)
**Key Contribution**: Losick is a pioneer in understanding bacterial developmental biology, particularly sporulation in *Bacillus subtilis*. His laboratory characterized the transcriptional programs controlling sporulation, identified the roles of alternative sigma factors in ordering the sporulation gene expression program, and contributed to understanding the bistability of the sporulation commitment decision. More broadly, Losick has been a champion of the idea that bacteria exhibit genuine developmental complexity — a view that was controversial when he began advocating it — and has written insightfully about stochastic cell fate decisions in bacteria and their relevance to eukaryotic development.

**Key Papers and Works**:
- Losick, R., & Desplan, C. (2008). Stochasticity and cell fate. *Science*, *320*(5872), 65–68.
- Dubnau, D., & Losick, R. (2006). Bistability in bacteria. *Molecular Microbiology*, *61*(3), 564–572.
