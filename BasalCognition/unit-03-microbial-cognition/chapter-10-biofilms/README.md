# Chapter 10: Biofilms — Collective Architecture

## Opening Scene

Run your tongue across the surface of a tooth that hasn't been brushed in 24 hours. That faintly rough film you feel is a biofilm — a community of bacteria numbering in the millions, embedded in a self-produced matrix of polysaccharides, proteins, and extracellular DNA, organized in a three-dimensional structure with water channels, metabolic gradients, and differentiated cell populations. The bacteria in that film are doing something qualitatively different from bacteria swimming freely in saliva. They are organized. They have an architecture. They are, in some sense, more than the sum of their parts.

Dental plaque is perhaps the most familiar biofilm, but biofilms are everywhere: on rocks in streams (the slippery coating you feel on riverside stones), in your gut lining (where they are part of a healthy microbiome), on medical devices (catheters, joint implants, heart valves, where they cause life-threatening infections), in hospital water systems (where *Legionella* lurks in warm water biofilms), and coating the hulls of ships (where marine biofilms initiate the fouling community that eventually reaches barnacles and mussels). Biofilms are the dominant mode of existence for most bacteria in natural environments — the planktonic, freely swimming cell we study so carefully in the laboratory is, in many ways, an artifact of how we culture bacteria.

## The Central Question

Biofilms exhibit properties that individual bacteria do not: three-dimensional architecture, chemical gradients, differentiated cell populations with division of labor, coordinated responses to external threats, and electrical communication waves that coordinate metabolism across centimeters. Do these emergent properties justify describing biofilms as a form of collective cognition? This chapter asks that question carefully.

The central question is: **in what senses is a biofilm a cognitive system — a system that senses, integrates, and responds to information at the collective level?**

This question is not merely rhetorical. Whether one accepts or rejects the "biofilm as cognitive system" framing has implications for how we approach biofilm infection biology, how we think about the evolution of multicellularity, and how we define the conceptual scope of cognition itself.
