# Further Reading: Chapter 10

---

**Prindle, A., Liu, J., Asally, M., Ly, S., Garcia-Ojalvo, J., & Süel, G. M. (2015). Ion channels enable electrical communication in bacterial communities. *Nature*, *527*(7576), 59–63.**
The landmark paper demonstrating potassium wave propagation in *B. subtilis* biofilms. Required reading for understanding biofilm electrical signaling. Clearly written, with excellent methods and supplementary materials that allow readers to understand exactly how the genetically encoded membrane potential sensor works and how the wave dynamics were analyzed. The conceptual implications for biofilm cognition are discussed in the paper's concluding sections.

---

**Flemming, H. C., Wingender, J., Szewzyk, U., Steinberg, P., Rice, S. A., & Kjelleberg, S. (2016). Biofilms: an emergent form of bacterial life. *Nature Reviews Microbiology*, *14*(9), 563–575.**
The most comprehensive recent review of biofilm biology, covering formation, structure, function, resistance, and ecological significance. An excellent synthetic overview written by leading experts. Particularly good on the physical properties and functional diversity of extracellular matrices and on the ecological significance of biofilms as an evolutionary transition in bacterial lifestyle.

---

**Costerton, J. W., Stewart, P. S., & Greenberg, E. P. (1999). Bacterial biofilms: a common cause of persistent infections. *Science*, *284*(5418), 1318–1322.**
A foundational review that established biofilm infections as a distinct clinical problem requiring different therapeutic approaches from planktonic infections. Written by Costerton (the founder of biofilm microbiology), Stewart, and Greenberg. Accessible and historically important; provides an entry point to the clinical significance of biofilm biology.

---

**Stewart, P. S., & Costerton, J. W. (2001). Antibiotic resistance of bacteria in biofilms. *The Lancet*, *358*(9276), 135–138.**
A clear and influential summary of the mechanisms of biofilm antibiotic tolerance. Distinguishes biofilm tolerance from genetic antibiotic resistance and explains the multiple physical and biological mechanisms contributing to the MBEC/MIC discrepancy. Essential background for anyone working on biofilm infections.

---

**Vlamakis, H., Aguilar, C., Losick, R., & Kolter, R. (2008). Control of cell fate by the formation of an architecturally complex bacterial community. *Genes & Development*, *22*(7), 945–953.**
A key paper characterizing cellular differentiation within *B. subtilis* biofilms, showing that the same genetic population gives rise to distinct subpopulations (matrix producers, sporulating cells, motile cells) with different gene expression profiles and spatial distributions. Provides the molecular evidence for division of labor in biofilm communities.

---

**López, D., Vlamakis, H., & Kolter, R. (2010). Biofilms. *Cold Spring Harbor Perspectives in Biology*, *2*(7), a000398.**
An accessible, comprehensive review of biofilm biology from the Kolter perspective, covering formation, structure, the role of quorum sensing, cellular differentiation, and the evolutionary significance of biofilms. Good companion to the more biochemically focused Flemming et al. review; more focused on developmental biology and genetics.

---

**West, S. A., Griffin, A. S., Gardner, A., & Diggle, S. P. (2006). Social evolution theory for microorganisms. *Nature Reviews Microbiology*, *4*(8), 597–607.**
(Listed also in Chapter 9.) Particularly relevant here for its treatment of the public goods problem in biofilm context — why do bacteria produce matrix components (a public good) rather than cheating? The kin selection and spatial structure arguments developed here are essential for understanding the evolution of biofilm cooperation.

---

**Balaban, N. Q., Merrin, J., Chait, R., Kowalik, L., & Leibler, S. (2004). Bacterial persistence as a phenotypic switch. *Science*, *305*(5690), 1622–1625.**
(Listed also in Chapter 8.) Essential background for understanding persister cells in the biofilm context. The persister cells embedded in biofilms are responsible for the recurrence of biofilm infections after antibiotic treatment and device removal — understanding their origin (stochastic phenotypic switching) is key to developing better strategies for biofilm infection management.

---

**Mah, T. F., & O'Toole, G. A. (2001). Mechanisms of biofilm resistance to antimicrobial agents. *Trends in Microbiology*, *9*(1), 34–39.**
An excellent mechanistic review of biofilm antibiotic tolerance, covering the three major mechanisms: diffusion limitation by the matrix, altered metabolic states in the biofilm, and differentiated subpopulations (including persister cells). Accessible and clearly organized; provides the conceptual framework for understanding why biofilm tolerance is so much greater than planktonic tolerance.
