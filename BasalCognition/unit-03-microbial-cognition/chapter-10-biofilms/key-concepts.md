# Key Concepts: Chapter 10

---

**Biofilm**
A community of microorganisms (typically bacteria, but also fungi and other microbes) attached to a surface and embedded in a self-produced extracellular matrix. Biofilms exhibit spatial organization, chemical gradients, cellular differentiation into subpopulations with division of labor, and coordinated collective behaviors that are not present in planktonic (free-swimming) cells. Estimated to account for more than 80% of microbial biomass on Earth and the majority of chronic bacterial infections. *Why it matters*: Biofilms are the dominant mode of bacterial existence in most natural and clinical environments. Understanding biofilm biology is essential for addressing the major challenges of chronic infection, biofouling, and the evolution of antibiotic resistance. Biofilms are also the most sophisticated example of collective cognition in microbial systems.

**Cyclic di-GMP (c-di-GMP) and the Lifestyle Switch**
The intracellular second messenger molecule that controls the planktonic-to-biofilm transition in many bacteria. High c-di-GMP promotes biofilm formation (matrix synthesis, flagellar suppression, adhesin expression); low c-di-GMP promotes planktonic motility. Levels are set by the balance of diguanylate cyclase (synthesis) and phosphodiesterase (degradation) enzymes, which integrate diverse environmental inputs. *Why it matters*: c-di-GMP is the master molecular switch for the biofilm lifestyle decision. Understanding how c-di-GMP levels are regulated and how they control downstream effectors is key to understanding biofilm formation and, potentially, to developing strategies that prevent biofilm formation before it begins.

**Extracellular Matrix (ECM)**
The complex mixture of polysaccharides (EPS), extracellular DNA (eDNA), and proteins produced by biofilm cells and secreted into the space between cells. The ECM provides structural integrity, protects cells from environmental stress and antibiotics, serves as a scaffold for cell attachment, and participates in water retention and nutrient distribution. Different species produce characteristic ECM compositions. *Why it matters*: The ECM is not merely passive structural material — it is an active participant in biofilm physiology. Understanding its composition and properties is essential for understanding biofilm mechanical properties, antibiotic tolerance, and the development of biofilm-disrupting therapies.

**Minimum Biofilm Eradication Concentration (MBEC)**
The minimum concentration of an antibiotic required to kill bacteria in biofilm, as opposed to the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) for planktonic cells. MBEC is typically 10 to 1000 times higher than MIC for the same bacteria, reflecting the multiple mechanisms of biofilm antibiotic tolerance (matrix barriers, metabolic dormancy, metabolic heterogeneity). *Why it matters*: The discrepancy between MIC and MBEC is the fundamental reason why biofilm infections fail antibiotic treatment at doses that would be effective against planktonic infections. Understanding the MBEC concept is essential for appreciating why clinical antibiotic dosing guidelines based on MIC testing are insufficient for biofilm infections.

**Potassium Wave**
A traveling wave of membrane potential depolarization propagated through a biofilm by the sequential release and detection of potassium ions. First described in *B. subtilis* biofilms by Prindle et al. (2015). Initiated when metabolically stressed interior cells open potassium channels, releasing K+ into the extracellular space; this depolarizes neighboring cells, which also open their channels, propagating the wave. Propagates at centimeters per minute. Functions to redistribute metabolic resources from peripheral to interior cells by transiently inhibiting peripheral cell metabolism. *Why it matters*: The potassium wave is the clearest demonstration of long-range electrical communication in bacterial biofilms and the strongest evidence for collective, community-level information processing in a non-neural microbial system. It establishes biofilms as electrically active communities, not merely chemically coordinated ones.

**Regulated Cell Death (in biofilms)**
The programmed lysis of a subpopulation of biofilm cells in response to stress, releasing their contents (including eDNA) into the extracellular matrix. The released material reinforces the matrix, increasing its structural integrity and resistance to antibiotic penetration. Requires regulated autolysis gene expression, triggered by quorum sensing and stress signals. Distinct from random cell death. *Why it matters*: Regulated cell death in biofilms is a form of altruism at the cellular level — individual cells die in ways that benefit the community. It illustrates the extent to which biofilm communities have evolved collective behaviors that sacrifice individual cell interests for community survival, and raises questions about the transition from individual cell survival strategies to community-level survival strategies.

**Sacrificial Cells**
A subpopulation of biofilm cells that undergo regulated lysis or metabolic shutdown in response to antibiotic or other stress, contributing their cellular contents to the matrix and serving as a protective "shield" for surviving cells. Conceptually related to persister cells (which survive by becoming dormant) but distinct: sacrificial cells die, while persisters survive. The existence of sacrificial cells implies that the biofilm community "allocates" some cells to die for the benefit of others. *Why it matters*: Sacrificial cells are a striking example of community-level decision-making that overrides individual cell survival. They raise fundamental questions about the boundaries of the "individual" in biology, and whether the biofilm community is better understood as a single cognitive agent rather than a collection of independent individuals.
