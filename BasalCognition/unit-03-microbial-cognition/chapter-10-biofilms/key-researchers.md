## Gürol Süel
**Institution**: University of California San Diego (Division of Biological Sciences); Salk Institute for Biological Studies (non-resident fellow)
**Key Contribution**: Süel's laboratory made the seminal discovery of electrical communication in bacterial biofilms. Using genetically encoded fluorescent reporters for membrane potential, his group demonstrated that *Bacillus subtilis* biofilms propagate long-range potassium waves — traveling waves of membrane depolarization that coordinate metabolism across the entire biofilm community. His subsequent work has characterized calcium waves in biofilms, the role of ion channel-mediated communication in sporulation coordination, and the possible role of electrical biofilm signaling in attracting neighboring bacteria. His laboratory's work fundamentally transformed the conceptual view of biofilms from passively organized chemical communities to electrically active, dynamically coordinated systems.

**Key Papers and Works**:
- Prindle, A., Liu, J., Asally, M., Ly, S., Garcia-Ojalvo, J., & Süel, G. M. (2015). Ion channels enable electrical communication in bacterial communities. *Nature*, *527*(7576), 59–63.

## Joel Prindle
**Institution**: Northwestern University (Department of Biomedical Engineering)
**Key Contribution**: Prindle was the lead author on the landmark 2015 *Nature* paper demonstrating potassium wave propagation in *B. subtilis* biofilms, which he conducted as a postdoctoral researcher in Süel's laboratory. His current laboratory continues to explore bacterial biofilm communication, with a focus on how electrical signals coordinate community-level behaviors and how this electrical communication might be targeted therapeutically to disrupt pathogenic biofilms.

**Key Papers and Works**:
- Prindle, A., Liu, J., Asally, M., Ly, S., Garcia-Ojalvo, J., & Süel, G. M. (2015). Ion channels enable electrical communication in bacterial communities. *Nature*, *527*(7576), 59–63.

## Roberto Kolter
**Institution**: Harvard Medical School (Department of Microbiology and Immunobiology)
**Key Contribution**: Kolter's laboratory has made fundamental contributions to understanding biofilm formation and bacterial social behavior, particularly in *B. subtilis*. His work with Richard Losick on the developmental biology of *B. subtilis* biofilms — characterizing cellular differentiation, the roles of different cell types, and the gene regulatory networks controlling biofilm formation — established *B. subtilis* as the primary model system for studying biofilm development. He has also been a influential voice for reconceptualizing bacteria as social organisms capable of cooperative behaviors, helping to create the intellectual climate in which biofilm cognition is now seriously discussed.

**Key Papers and Works**:
- Vlamakis, H., Aguilar, C., Losick, R., & Kolter, R. (2008). Control of cell fate by the formation of an architecturally complex bacterial community. *Genes & Development*, *22*(7), 945–953.
- López, D., Vlamakis, H., & Kolter, R. (2010). Biofilms. *Cold Spring Harbor Perspectives in Biology*, *2*(7), a000398.

## J. William Costerton
**Institution**: University of Calgary; Montana State University; University of Southern California (1934–2012)
**Key Contribution**: Costerton was the founding figure of biofilm microbiology. His early electron microscopy of bacteria on surfaces revealed, for the first time, that bacteria in natural and clinical environments are embedded in an extracellular matrix — not merely adhered to surfaces as individual cells. He coined the term "biofilm" and spent decades demonstrating that biofilm-related infections (on medical devices, in lungs of cystic fibrosis patients) behave fundamentally differently from planktonic infections, requiring different diagnostic and therapeutic approaches. His work was initially controversial and took decades to be fully accepted by the medical community. It is now the foundation of infection biology.

**Key Papers and Works**:
- Costerton, J. W., Stewart, P. S., & Greenberg, E. P. (1999). Bacterial biofilms: a common cause of persistent infections. *Science*, *284*(5418), 1318–1322.
- Stewart, P. S., & Costerton, J. W. (2001). Antibiotic resistance of bacteria in biofilms. *The Lancet*, *358*(9276), 135–138.

## Hans-Curt Flemming
**Institution**: University of Duisburg-Essen, Germany
**Key Contribution**: Flemming is the leading authority on the composition, structure, and properties of biofilm extracellular matrices. His laboratory has characterized the matrix components (polysaccharides, proteins, eDNA, lipids) of diverse biofilms and analyzed how matrix composition relates to biofilm mechanical properties, antibiotic tolerance, and water retention. He has also been influential in articulating the evolutionary and ecological significance of biofilms as the dominant mode of microbial life. His review papers provide the most comprehensive synthetic accounts of biofilm biology.

**Key Papers and Works**:
- Flemming, H. C., Wingender, J., Szewzyk, U., Steinberg, P., Rice, S. A., & Kjelleberg, S. (2016). Biofilms: an emergent form of bacterial life. *Nature Reviews Microbiology*, *14*(9), 563–575.
