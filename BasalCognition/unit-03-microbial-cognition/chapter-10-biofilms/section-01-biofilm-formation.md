# Section 1: Biofilm Formation and Architecture

Biofilm formation is not passive — bacteria do not simply stick to surfaces and accumulate randomly. It is a regulated, sequential developmental program that transforms individual planktonic bacteria into a complex, multicellular community through a series of coordinated gene expression changes. Understanding this developmental program is prerequisite to understanding the emergent cognitive properties of the mature biofilm.

---

## The Stages of Biofilm Development

Biofilm development proceeds through recognizable stages, each involving distinct gene expression programs and behavioral transitions:

**Stage 1: Initial reversible attachment**. Planktonic bacteria approaching a surface make initial, transient contact mediated by physical forces: van der Waals interactions, electrostatic interactions, and hydrophobic effects. This initial contact is reversible — bacteria can detach and return to planktonic growth. The decision to commit to surface attachment involves sensing surface contact through mechanosensitive ion channels, pilus-mediated contact forces, and changes in the diffusion of small molecules near the surface. Surface contact triggers rapid changes in gene expression, including upregulation of genes involved in irreversible attachment and downregulation of flagellar genes (since flagella are no longer needed for swimming and their continued expression would waste energy).

**Stage 2: Irreversible attachment and microcolony formation**. Committed bacteria produce adhesins — surface proteins that mediate tight, specific binding to the surface. They suppress flagellar motility and begin producing the first components of the extracellular matrix. Cells divide and expand into microcolonies — small clusters of cells that remain anchored to the surface. Within microcolonies, cells are in close proximity, enabling chemical communication through quorum sensing signals and secreted metabolites at concentrations that would be diluted to ineffectiveness in the open medium.

**Stage 3: Maturation**. As the biofilm grows and quorum sensing threshold concentrations are reached, cells undergo a more global gene expression shift: matrix component production (polysaccharides, proteins, eDNA) is dramatically upregulated; cells begin differentiating into distinct subpopulations with different gene expression profiles; water channels and mushroom-shaped pillars develop, distributing nutrients and oxygen through the biofilm structure.

**Stage 4: Dispersal**. Mature biofilms release cells back into the planktonic state through a process called dispersal, triggered by environmental cues (starvation, mechanical shear, changes in chemical signals). Dispersal involves expression of enzymes that degrade the biofilm matrix, upregulation of flagellar genes, and detachment of motile cells from the biofilm surface. Dispersal is the mechanism by which biofilm infections spread — cells released from a mature biofilm can colonize new surfaces downstream or in new body sites.

## Extracellular Matrix: The Biofilm Building Material

The extracellular matrix (ECM) of biofilms is not an inert scaffold. It is a complex, functionally diverse material that protects cells, maintains biofilm architecture, facilitates nutrient and signal transport, and actively participates in the biofilm's defensive responses.

The major components of the ECM differ between bacterial species but share functional themes:

**Exopolysaccharides (EPS)**: Long-chain polysaccharides are often the bulk of the biofilm matrix. Examples include alginate in *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* mucoid biofilms (particularly important in cystic fibrosis lung infections), Pel and Psl polysaccharides in non-mucoid *P. aeruginosa*, and PNAG (poly-N-acetylglucosamine) in *Staphylococcus aureus* and *S. epidermidis*. EPS provides viscosity and structural integrity, acts as a water reservoir (preventing desiccation), physically impedes the diffusion of antibiotics and host immune effectors into the biofilm, and can bind and sequester divalent cations (calcium, magnesium) that some antibiotics (aminoglycosides) require for activity.

**Extracellular DNA (eDNA)**: Many biofilms contain significant amounts of double-stranded DNA in the ECM, derived from cells that have undergone programmed lysis. eDNA is not merely a structural component; it provides a structural scaffold that bacteria actively use for cell-to-cell attachment, and it appears to contribute to the biofilm's tolerance to antimicrobials by chelating divalent cations and creating a negatively charged barrier.

**Extracellular proteins**: Matrix proteins include structural proteins (like the fibronectin-binding proteins of *S. aureus* that mediate surface attachment), enzymes (proteases, nucleases that can remodel the matrix or degrade host proteins), and surface-displayed proteins that mediate cell-cell adhesion within the biofilm.

## Differentiation Within the Biofilm

One of the most remarkable features of mature biofilms is the differentiation of cells within the community into distinct subpopulations with different gene expression profiles and different functions. This is, in some ways, analogous to the cellular differentiation that occurs in multicellular organisms — different cells in the community specialize for different roles.

In *B. subtilis* biofilms, at least three distinct cell types are found in the mature biofilm: matrix producers (cells that produce the EPS and protein matrix), sporulating cells (committed to forming environmentally resistant spores), and motile cells (retaining flagellar expression for dispersal). The proportions of these cell types are regulated by the quorum sensing system and the Spo0A sporulation commitment switch, and spatial position within the biofilm influences which fate a cell adopts (Vlamakis et al., 2008).

In *P. aeruginosa* biofilms, similar spatial differentiation exists: cells at the surface of the biofilm "mushrooms" are in a different metabolic state from cells at the base; cells in the oxygen-rich outer layers are growing aerobically, while cells in the oxygen-depleted interior may be growing anaerobically or may have entered a dormant state. The diversity of metabolic states within a single biofilm means that the community as a whole is more metabolically versatile than any individual cell.

This cellular differentiation within biofilms is an important step toward multicellularity. The biofilm community has something like division of labor: different cells specialize for different functions, and the specialization is coordinated by chemical signaling within the community. Whether this constitutes "true" multicellularity — or a transitional form of it — is a matter of ongoing discussion.
