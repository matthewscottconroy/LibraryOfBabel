# Study Questions — Unit III: Microbial Cognition

*These questions require close engagement with the molecular mechanisms of bacterial cognition and their theoretical significance. Some questions are deliberately provocative; the goal is to stress-test the conceptual claims of the field.*

---

## Question 1: Chemotaxis as Minimal Cognition

The *E. coli* chemotaxis system is often presented as the paradigm case of basal cognition: a bacterium senses a chemical gradient, integrates the signal through a phosphorylation cascade, stores a short-term "memory" via receptor methylation, and adjusts its behaviour (run/tumble frequency) accordingly.

**(a)** Compare the information-processing strategy of bacterial chemotaxis with how a neuron integrates synaptic inputs. Both systems: (i) receive multiple, competing signals; (ii) produce a graded internal response; (iii) generate a threshold-dependent output; (iv) exhibit adaptation (desensitisation). What is conserved between these two architectures? What fundamentally differs? Does the conservation support or undermine the claim that they are instances of the same kind of cognitive process?

**(b)** The chemotaxis receptor array in *E. coli* exhibits signal amplification of roughly 50-fold: a 0.1% change in ligand concentration produces a near-maximal change in kinase activity. This performance rivals, in some respects, the signal discrimination capacity of sensory neurons. What does this imply about the relationship between molecular complexity and cognitive capacity?

**(c)** The "run/tumble" strategy of *E. coli* is a biased random walk: without a gradient, the bacterium explores randomly; with a gradient, it biases its random walk toward higher attractant concentrations. Is a biased random walk the same kind of thing as directed goal-oriented movement? If so, are all apparently goal-directed behaviours just biased random walks at some level of description?

## Question 2: Quorum Sensing and Collective Decision-Making

Quorum sensing allows bacteria to make collective decisions that no individual cell could make alone: decisions about when to form a biofilm, when to initiate virulence, when to sporulate. The mechanism involves accumulating a signal (autoinducer) to a threshold concentration, then triggering a coordinated gene expression change.

**(a)** Seeley's (2010) work on honeybee democracy shows that bee swarms make house-selection decisions through a quorum-sensing-like mechanism: individual bees scout locations and "vote" via waggle dances until one location achieves a quorum. Is the honeybee quorum mechanism and the bacterial quorum mechanism instances of the same phenomenon? What would it mean if they are? What would it mean if they are not?

**(b)** Quorum sensing is sometimes described as bacteria "counting" their population density. But is this an accurate description of what is mechanistically happening? What would genuine counting require, and does the quorum sensing mechanism have those properties? Does it matter whether bacteria "really" count, or is the functional equivalence sufficient?

**(c)** The *Bacillus subtilis* sporulation decision involves integrating signals about nutrient availability, population density, and cell cycle state. The decision is irreversible: once a cell commits to sporulation, it cannot return to vegetative growth. What does the irreversibility of this decision tell us about the nature of bacterial "decision-making"? Compare it with a decision made by an animal under conditions of environmental stress.

## Question 3: Biofilm Intelligence

A mature biofilm is not a collection of identical bacteria. It is a differentiated community with distinct cell types (matrix producers, motile cells, protective "shield" cells), electrical communication via ion channels, and coordinated resistance to antibiotics. Chapter 10 argues that the biofilm represents a genuinely collective cognitive architecture.

**(a)** Evaluate the claim that a biofilm is more cognitively sophisticated than any individual bacterium. What specific cognitive capacities does the biofilm have that the individual cell lacks? What does "more sophisticated" mean in this context?

**(b)** The biofilm's electrical signalling system (involving potassium ions propagating membrane potential waves) has been compared to neural signalling. Is this comparison illuminating or misleading? What does the comparison reveal, and what does it obscure?

**(c)** Antibiotic resistance in biofilms is often attributed to the "collective intelligence" of the biofilm: persister cells (a small subpopulation that enters a dormant, antibiotic-tolerant state) ensure that even lethal doses of antibiotics do not eliminate the community. Is the production of persister cells an instance of collective decision-making, or is it simply the result of stochastic variation in gene expression? Does the distinction matter clinically? Philosophically?

## Question 4: Viral Decision-Making

The lysis/lysogeny decision in phage lambda is a paradigm case of biological decision-making in a non-cellular system. When lambda infects a bacterium, the virus must "decide" whether to immediately replicate and lyse the host (lytic pathway) or integrate into the host genome and enter a dormant state (lysogenic pathway). This decision depends on the physiological state of the host cell.

**(a)** Is the lambda lysis/lysogeny decision a genuine instance of cognition? Apply Lyon's biogenic framework: does phage lambda sense, integrate, and act? What, if anything, is missing?

**(b)** Phage lambda is not alive in the standard sense (it does not metabolise independently). Does this mean it cannot cognize? Or does the viral case force us to decouple cognition from metabolism?

**(c)** Recent work has shown that certain phages (e.g., *Bacillus subtilis* phage phi3T) communicate with each other using a peptide called "arbitrium" — effectively a quorum sensing system that allows phages to coordinate the lysis/lysogeny decision across multiple infections. How does this inter-phage communication affect the attribution of cognitive capacities to viruses? Is it more or less surprising than bacterial quorum sensing?

## Question 5: Deep Roots and Evolutionary Continuity

Unit III concludes with the claim that microbial cognition is not primitive or degraded cognition but a complete and sophisticated cognitive architecture evolved over billions of years. This claim has implications for evolutionary biology, neuroscience, and philosophy.

**(a)** If bacterial cognition is "complete" in some relevant sense, what does this imply about the evolution of nervous systems? Are nervous systems genuinely novel cognitive inventions, or are they merely quantitative elaborations of capacities already present in bacteria? What evidence would distinguish these two interpretations?

**(b)** The human microbiome contains approximately 10^14 bacterial cells, many of which engage in quorum sensing, chemical communication, and coordinated behaviour within the gut. Given what you know about bacterial cognition, how should we understand the relationship between the microbial communities in our bodies and our own cognitive processes? Is this relationship merely physical-chemical, or does it deserve a richer description?

**(c)** Consider the following argument: bacteria have existed for 3.5 billion years and have evolved extremely robust and adaptive cognitive strategies. Human nervous systems have existed for a few hundred thousand years. In terms of evolutionary "testing," bacterial cognition has been refined for 10,000 times longer than human cognition. Does this mean bacterial cognition is, in some sense, more reliable or optimal than human cognition? What would it mean for a cognitive strategy to be "optimal"?
