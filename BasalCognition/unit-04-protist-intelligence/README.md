They are also some of the most behaviorally sophisticated organisms on Earth, achieving feats of problem-solving, navigation, and adaptation with a single cell that took animals millions of years of neural evolution to replicate."*

## Unit Introduction

The word "protist" is more a term of exclusion than inclusion. It denotes every eukaryote — every organism whose cells have a nucleus — that is not an animal, plant, or fungus. In terms of evolutionary diversity, the protists encompass more variation than all animals, plants, and fungi combined. In terms of cognitive sophistication per neuron (zero neurons, in most cases), they are unmatched in the biological world.

This unit focuses on four groups that are particularly important for basal cognition: amoebae, slime molds, ciliates, and flagellates.

**Amoebae** are the original hunters — single cells that move through their environment, sensing and pursuing prey, navigating obstacles, and responding to conditions with remarkable flexibility. Dictyostelium discoideum, the cellular slime mold that is technically an amoeba, provides one of the most studied models of collective behavior and developmental complexity in the biological world.

**Slime molds** — particularly Physarum polycephalum — have become the celebrity organisms of basal cognition research. Their ability to solve mazes, optimize networks, anticipate periodic events, and implement what look startlingly like computational algorithms has made them the go-to example for demonstrating that intelligence does not require a brain. We examine these claims carefully, with attention to what the evidence actually shows.

**Ciliates** — including Paramecium and Stentor — are single-celled organisms with extraordinarily rich behavioral repertoires. The history of scientific attempts to understand their behavior is, itself, a fascinating story: a century of arguing about whether organisms with no neurons can learn, and what "learning" would mean in such a context.

**Flagellates**, particularly the choanoflagellates, are our closest single-celled relatives — the bridge between the protist world and the animal world. Understanding their cognitive capacities tells us something about what our own ancestors were doing before animals existed.

## The Unit's Central Questions

- How sophisticated can cognition be without any neurons at all?
- What mechanisms implement memory, anticipation, and flexible problem-solving in single-celled eukaryotes?
- How does the transition from single-celled to multicellular life affect cognitive organization?
- What does the cognitive richness of protists tell us about the evolutionary pressures that produced nervous systems?

---

## Why Protists Matter for Basal Cognition

Protists are the organisms that most clearly demonstrate the gap between cognitive capacity and neural complexity. An E. coli bacterium is cognitively active, but it is a prokaryote — a cell type that preceded neurons by over 3 billion years, and whose cognitive architecture is relatively simple. Protists are eukaryotes, with all the molecular complexity that implies: cytoskeleton, membrane-bound organelles, complex gene regulation. But they lack nervous systems entirely. They are, in a sense, the maximum cognitive output of a single eukaryotic cell — and that output is remarkable.
