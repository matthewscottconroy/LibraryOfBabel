# Chapter 12: Key Researchers

## Peter Devreotes
**Institution**: Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine, Department of Cell Biology  
**Key Contribution**: Devreotes has spent his career dissecting the intracellular signaling pathways that enable Dictyostelium to detect and respond to chemoattractant gradients. His work established the central role of G protein-coupled receptors and phosphoinositide signaling in chemotaxis, and he was a principal architect of the Local Excitation / Global Inhibition (LEGI) conceptual framework for understanding gradient sensing. His lab has been instrumental in demonstrating that cells do not passively respond to gradients but actively generate internal asymmetries that are biased by external signals.

**Key Papers**:
- Devreotes, P. N., & Zigmond, S. H. (1988). Chemotaxis in eukaryotic cells: A focus on leukocytes and Dictyostelium. *Annual Review of Cell Biology*, 4, 649–686.
- Parent, C. A., & Devreotes, P. N. (1999). A cell's sense of direction. *Science*, 284(5415), 765–770.
- Devreotes, P. N., & Bhatt, D. (2021). Excitable signal transduction networks in directed cell migration. *Annual Review of Cell and Developmental Biology*, 37, 157–184.

## Günther Gerisch
**Institution**: Max Planck Institute of Biochemistry, Martinsried (emeritus)  
**Key Contribution**: Gerisch is one of the founding figures of Dictyostelium research. Beginning in the 1960s, he established the cellular and biochemical basis of Dictyostelium aggregation, demonstrating the central role of cAMP as the aggregation signal and characterizing the receptor-relay system that allows cAMP waves to propagate through a population of cells. His laboratory pioneered the use of fluorescence microscopy to visualize signaling dynamics in living cells, making it possible to watch the LEGI dynamics in real time.

**Key Papers**:
- Gerisch, G. (1968). Cell aggregation and differentiation in Dictyostelium. *Current Topics in Developmental Biology*, 3, 157–197.
- Gerisch, G., & Wick, U. (1975). Intracellular oscillations and release of cyclic AMP from Dictyostelium cells. *Biochemical and Biophysical Research Communications*, 65(1), 364–370.

## John Tyler Bonner
**Institution**: Princeton University (emeritus)  
**Key Contribution**: Bonner studied Dictyostelium for more than six decades, from his graduate work in the 1940s through a series of books that remain standards in the field. He was primarily a naturalist and developmental biologist rather than a molecular biologist, and his great contribution was establishing the ecological and evolutionary context of Dictyostelium behavior — understanding why the organism does what it does as a biological problem before asking how at the molecular level. His book *The Social Amoebae* (2009) is the most accessible synthesis of what slime molds tell us about the evolution of social behavior and collective organization.

**Key Papers and Books**:
- Bonner, J. T. (1944). A descriptive study of the development of the slime mold Dictyostelium discoideum. *American Journal of Botany*, 31(3), 175–182.
- Bonner, J. T. (2009). *The Social Amoebae: The Biology of Cellular Slime Molds*. Princeton University Press.

## Joan Strassmann
**Institution**: Washington University in St. Louis, Department of Biology  
**Key Contribution**: Strassmann, working with her long-time collaborator David Queller, transformed Dictyostelium research by bringing the conceptual tools of evolutionary biology — particularly kin selection theory, game theory, and evolutionary genetics — to bear on the organism's social behavior. Her research established that natural populations of Dictyostelium contain multiple coexisting genotypes that compete within chimeric aggregates, and that cheater lineages represent a genuine evolutionary threat to cooperation. She also identified the molecular mechanisms of kin discrimination that allow cooperator strains to resist cheater invasion.

**Key Papers**:
- Strassmann, J. E., Zhu, Y., & Queller, D. C. (2000). Altruism and social cheating in the social amoeba Dictyostelium discoideum. *Nature*, 408, 965–967.
- Strassmann, J. E., & Queller, D. C. (2011). Evolution of cooperation and control of cheating in a social microbe. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 108(Suppl 2), 10855–10862.

## David Queller
**Institution**: Washington University in St. Louis, Department of Biology  
**Key Contribution**: Queller is a theoretical and empirical evolutionary biologist who has worked with Joan Strassmann on the social evolution of Dictyostelium. He has contributed both theoretical frameworks for understanding altruism and cooperation in microbes and empirical work on the genetic and molecular basis of kin discrimination. His broader theoretical work on the evolution of social traits in microorganisms has helped establish Dictyostelium as a model system for evolutionary biology, not merely developmental biology.

**Key Papers**:
- Queller, D. C., Ponte, E., Bozzaro, S., & Strassmann, J. E. (2003). Single-gene greenbeard effects in the social amoeba Dictyostelium discoideum. *Science*, 299(5603), 105–106.
- Strassmann, J. E., Zhu, Y., & Queller, D. C. (2000). Altruism and social cheating in the social amoeba Dictyostelium discoideum. *Nature*, 408, 965–967.
