# Section 3: Parasitic Cognition — Entamoeba and the Art of Reading the Host

## Introduction

There is a way of thinking about parasitism that is sanitized to the point of distortion: the parasite as inert chemical vehicle, delivered into a host by accident or by the brute mechanics of transmission, there to multiply until the host is consumed or the immune system prevails. This picture is wrong in ways that matter for our understanding of cellular cognition.

Entamoeba histolytica — the causative agent of amoebic dysentery and amebic liver abscess, infecting perhaps 50 million people per year worldwide — does not behave like an inert vehicle. It reads its environment. It responds to signals from the host immune system. It switches between phenotypic states — a non-invasive form that colonizes the gut asymptomatically, and an invasive form that penetrates tissue and causes disease — in ways that depend on environmental inputs. The switch is not random and it is not genetically determined in any simple sense; it is regulated, responsive, and in some respects adaptive.

Whether we should call this cognition is a question worth taking seriously. By the standards we have been developing throughout this book — behavioral flexibility, signal integration, adaptive phenotypic response — Entamoeba has a stronger claim than its reputation might suggest.

## 3.1 The Biology of Entamoeba

Entamoeba histolytica is an anaerobic protist in the Amoebozoa, the same large clade that includes Dictyostelium. Like its soil-dwelling relatives, it moves by pseudopodia and feeds by phagocytosis — but the prey in its case are not bacteria in the leaf litter; they are the cells of the intestinal epithelium, immune cells, and red blood cells of its human or primate host.

The lifecycle has two stages: the cyst, which is the environmentally resistant transmission form, and the trophozoite, the active feeding form. Cysts are ingested in contaminated food or water, excyst in the small intestine, and the emerging trophozoites colonize the large intestine, typically living in the mucus layer above the epithelium without causing disease. In the majority of infected individuals — estimates suggest approximately 90% — the infection remains asymptomatic (WHO, 1997). In a minority, the parasite penetrates the epithelial layer, invades the submucosa, and causes dysentery; or disseminates through the bloodstream to the liver, where it forms abscesses.

The question of what determines whether an infection remains asymptomatic or becomes invasive has occupied parasitologists for decades. It is not purely a question of parasite genetics: the same isolate can exhibit both non-invasive and invasive behavior in different hosts, or even in the same host at different times. Environment matters. The host matters. And the parasite's own cellular responses to environmental signals matter.

## 3.2 Reading the Host: Sensing Immune Signals

The intestinal environment is not a static chemical milieu. It is a dynamic theater of conflict, in which the host immune system continuously surveys for pathogens and responds to their presence. A successful parasite in this environment must navigate not just the challenge of finding nutrients but the challenge of surviving an immune response calibrated to destroy it.

Entamoeba trophozoites are not passive victims of this immune pressure. They actively detect host immune signals and alter their behavior in response.

**Nitric oxide sensing**: Host macrophages attacking Entamoeba trophozoites release nitric oxide (NO), a potent antimicrobial molecule. Remarkably, Entamoeba responds to NO not merely by dying or surviving, but by altering its gene expression profile. Studies have shown that exposure to physiologically relevant concentrations of NO induces changes in Entamoeba virulence gene expression, including genes encoding cysteine proteases — key virulence factors — and surface lectins involved in host cell adhesion (Ascuitto et al., 2004; other work in this area). The precise regulation of this response remains an active research area, but the phenomenon itself — sensing a host immune molecule and adjusting a gene expression program accordingly — represents a form of adaptive phenotypic response to host signals.

**Complement sensing**: The complement cascade, a key component of the innate immune system, can lyse pathogens through the membrane attack complex. Invasive Entamoeba strains resist complement-mediated lysis far more effectively than non-invasive strains. This resistance is partly constitutive but is also regulated: the expression of galactose/GalNAc lectins and other surface molecules that interfere with complement activation changes in response to complement exposure (Bhatt & Bhatt, 2009). Again, the pattern is one of detecting a host defense signal and modulating surface properties in response.

**Mucin sensing**: The mucus layer of the intestinal epithelium presents both a physical barrier to invasion and a chemical environment rich in information. Entamoeba trophozoites can degrade mucin — the main structural component of mucus — using secreted cysteine proteases, but the secretion of these proteases is itself regulated by mucin exposure. Trophozoites in contact with mucin upregulate protease secretion; trophozoites without mucin contact show lower secretory activity (Kissoon-Singh et al., 2011). This constitutes a response to substrate — a detection of "barrier present" that triggers "increased barrier-degrading activity."

## 3.3 Phenotypic Switching: Invasive and Non-Invasive States

The most striking aspect of Entamoeba cognition — if we are willing to use that word — is the existence of distinct phenotypic states with different behavioral repertoires, and the capacity to switch between them.

In the non-invasive state, Entamoeba trophozoites colonize the mucus layer without directly contacting the epithelium, feed on bacteria in the intestinal lumen, and produce no overt pathology. They can persist in this state for extended periods. In the invasive state, the same organism — or rather, an organism with the same genome — extends pseudopods into the epithelium, kills epithelial cells by contact-dependent mechanisms involving the Gal/GalNAc lectin and amoebapore proteins, and begins actively penetrating tissue.

The transition between these states is associated with major changes in gene expression, including upregulation of virulence genes encoding cysteine proteases (EhCP1, EhCP2, EhCP5), the Gal/GalNAc lectin heavy subunit (Hgl), and amoebapore proteins that lyse cells by inserting pores in their membranes (Bhatt & Bhatt, 2009). These changes are not simple on/off switches; they represent coordinated reprogramming of a substantial portion of the transcriptome.

What triggers the switch? This is incompletely understood, but several environmental factors have been identified:

**Bacterial co-infection**: The intestinal microbiome appears to modulate Entamoeba virulence. Gnotobiotic animal models (animals colonized with defined microbiomes) show that the presence or absence of specific bacterial species dramatically affects whether Entamoeba causes disease (Burgess et al., 2020). Entamoeba can engage in close physical contact with bacteria, engulfing and killing some; it is possible that the composition of the local bacterial community provides signals about environmental conditions that influence virulence gene expression.

**Redox state**: The intestinal environment varies in oxygen tension and redox state depending on location and host condition. Entamoeba is anaerobic but has sophisticated mechanisms for sensing and responding to oxidative stress. These mechanisms include thioredoxin-dependent systems that protect against reactive oxygen species — and there is evidence that the redox state of the environment influences virulence gene expression.

**Physical contact with host cells**: Direct contact between Entamoeba and host epithelial cells rapidly upregulates virulence gene expression, particularly genes involved in cell killing. This contact-dependent induction suggests a sensor-effector loop: detect host cell, upregulate killing machinery, kill, proceed.

## 3.4 The Comparative Cognition of Parasitism

There is a temptation, when discussing parasitic cognition, to frame it in terms of a kind of arms race — host gets more sophisticated, parasite gets more sophisticated in response — that implies increasing cognitive complexity on both sides. This framing captures something real but risks anthropomorphizing in ways that obscure the actual mechanisms.

What Entamoeba does is not, in any interesting sense, strategic. There is no representation of the host immune system, no model of what the complement cascade will do, no plan for how to evade it. What exists is a set of molecular sensors — protein complexes that change shape in response to specific ligands — coupled to signal transduction cascades that modify gene expression in ways that, over evolutionary time, have been selected to increase survival in the host environment.

But the same can be said of your own immune cells. The B cell that adjusts its surface receptor expression in response to antigen stimulation is also not "reasoning" about the pathogen. It is following molecular rules that evolution has refined for survival in a world with pathogens. The difference between Entamoeba and a B cell is one of mechanism, complexity, and history — not of kind.

What is interesting about Entamoeba — what earns it a place in a chapter on amoeboid cognition — is the degree of behavioral flexibility encoded in molecular sensing circuits, and the sophistication of the integration between environmental signals and adaptive phenotypic response. An organism that can detect nitric oxide, modulate its gene expression, alter its surface properties, resist immune attack, and switch between colonization and invasion modes based on environmental inputs is exhibiting a form of adaptive intelligence that, in another cellular context, we would not hesitate to recognize as sophisticated.

Parasitic cognition challenges us because the ends it serves are at odds with the welfare of the host, and it is difficult to avoid projecting hostility onto biological processes that have no experiential character at all. But the adaptive sophistication is real, and ignoring it in the name of moral clarity would be a mistake. Understanding how Entamoeba reads its environment is not only good parasitology; it is a window into the deep evolutionary origins of cellular intelligence.
