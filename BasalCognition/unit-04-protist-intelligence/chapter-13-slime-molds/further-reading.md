# Chapter 13: Further Reading

## Annotated Bibliography

**Nakagaki, T., Yamada, H., & Tóth, Á. (2000). Maze-solving by an amoeboid organism. *Nature*, 407, 470.**
The paper that started it all. Remarkably concise — less than a full page — but the images are striking and the conceptual claim is clearly stated. Reading the original paper is valuable precisely because of its brevity; it forces you to confront how much the authors did and did not claim, and to notice how the popular reception of the paper sometimes went beyond the text.

**Tero, A., Takagi, S., Saigusa, T., Ito, K., Bebber, D. P., Fricker, M. D., ... & Nakagaki, T. (2010). Rules for biologically inspired adaptive network design. *Science*, 327(5964), 439–442.**
The Tokyo rail network paper, which follows the 2000 Nature paper as the second landmark Physarum study. Contains the mathematical model and the quantitative comparison to the actual rail network. The supplementary materials provide enough methodological detail to attempt to reproduce the simulation. Essential reading for understanding what the slime mold is actually doing mechanistically.

**Saigusa, T., Tero, A., Nakagaki, T., & Kuramoto, Y. (2008). Amoebae anticipate periodic events. *Physical Review Letters*, 100(1), 018101.**
The anticipation paper. Appearing in a physics journal reflects the authors' decision to frame the work in terms of oscillator dynamics rather than biological cognition — a framing choice that is itself conceptually interesting. The paper is mathematically somewhat technical in its discussion of mechanisms, but the experimental results are clearly presented.

**Adamatzky, A. (2010). *Physarum Machines: Computers from Slime Mould*. World Scientific.**
The most comprehensive treatment of Physarum as a computing substrate, written by the field's most prolific researcher. Adamatzky's enthusiasm occasionally runs ahead of the evidence, and the reader should engage critically, but the book provides an invaluable survey of the experimental program. Includes demonstrations of many logical and arithmetic operations purportedly implemented in Physarum networks, with extensive photographic documentation.

**Adamatzky, A. (Ed.) (2016). *Advances in Physarum Machines: Sensing and Computing with Slime Mould*. Springer.**
A collection of papers from multiple research groups, providing a broader survey of the Physarum computing field than Adamatzky's single-authored work. Useful for seeing the diversity of approaches and the range of contested results.

**Alim, K., Andrew, N., Pringle, A., & Brenner, M. P. (2017). Mechanism of signal propagation in Physarum polycephalum. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 114(20), 5136–5141.**
A rigorous physical analysis of how perturbations (food stimuli) propagate through the Physarum tube network via cytoplasmic flow. This paper is important for understanding the biophysical mechanism by which information about food sources is integrated across the organism — a mechanism that is more subtle than simple diffusion.

**Oettmeier, C., Brix, K., & Döbereiner, H. G. (2017). Physarum polycephalum — a new take on a classic model system. *Journal of Physics D: Applied Physics*, 50(41), 413001.**
An excellent review of Physarum biology from a physics perspective, covering the life cycle, cytoplasmic streaming dynamics, tube network formation, and the various experimental demonstrations of putative problem-solving. Balanced and technically careful, this is one of the best single-source reviews of the field.

**Tero, A., Kobayashi, R., & Nakagaki, T. (2007). A mathematical model for adaptive transport network in path finding by true slime mold. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*, 244(4), 553–564.**
The mathematical model paper that provides the theoretical foundation for understanding why Physarum finds efficient networks. More mathematically accessible than it sounds; the core reinforcement model is a simple system of differential equations that can be implemented in a spreadsheet.

**Lyon, P. (2006). The biogenic approach to cognition. *Cognitive Processing*, 7(1), 11–29.**
A philosophically careful discussion of what it means to attribute cognition to organisms without nervous systems, written specifically in the context of single-celled organisms. Lyon argues for an operationally grounded approach that focuses on functional properties of cognition rather than substrate. Valuable for situating the Physarum experiments in a broader cognitive science context.

**Ueda, T., Muratsugu, M., Kurihara, K., & Kobatake, Y. (1976). Chemotaxis in Physarum polycephalum: effects of chemicals on isometric tension of the plasmodial strand in relation to chemotactic movement. *Experimental Cell Research*, 100(2), 337–344.**
An early study demonstrating that Physarum exhibits directed movement in chemical gradients — a behavioral capacity predating the modern computational experiments by decades. Reading the historical literature alongside the modern work helps appreciate the continuity of research on this organism and how much recent work was foreshadowed by older observations.
