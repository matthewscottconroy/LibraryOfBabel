# Chapter 13: Key Researchers

## Toshiyuki Nakagaki
**Institution**: Hokkaido University, Research Institute for Electronic Science  
**Key Contribution**: Nakagaki is the primary architect of the Physarum computational biology program. His 2000 Nature paper on maze-solving by Physarum is one of the most cited and widely discussed papers in the field of basal cognition. Together with his collaborators — particularly the mathematician Atsushi Tero — he developed both the experimental demonstrations and the mathematical theory that explains how Physarum network dynamics produce optimization behavior. His group's Tokyo rail network paper (2010) extended the program from a demonstration of principle to a quantitative comparison with human engineering.

**Key Papers**:
- Nakagaki, T., Yamada, H., & Tóth, Á. (2000). Maze-solving by an amoeboid organism. *Nature*, 407, 470.
- Tero, A., Takagi, S., Saigusa, T., Ito, K., Bebber, D. P., Fricker, M. D., ... & Nakagaki, T. (2010). Rules for biologically inspired adaptive network design. *Science*, 327(5964), 439–442.
- Saigusa, T., Tero, A., Nakagaki, T., & Kuramoto, Y. (2008). Amoebae anticipate periodic events. *Physical Review Letters*, 100(1), 018101.

## Atsushi Tero
**Institution**: Kyushu University, Faculty of Design  
**Key Contribution**: Tero is the mathematician in the Nakagaki collaboration who developed the formal mathematical model of Physarum network adaptation. His 2007 paper providing the differential equation model for tube reinforcement dynamics gave the program a rigorous theoretical foundation. He has continued to develop mathematical analyses of adaptive network formation and their relationship to engineering-relevant network design problems.

**Key Papers**:
- Tero, A., Kobayashi, R., & Nakagaki, T. (2007). A mathematical model for adaptive transport network in path finding by true slime mold. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*, 244(4), 553–564.
- Tero, A., Takagi, S., Saigusa, T., Ito, K., Bebber, D. P., Fricker, M. D., ... & Nakagaki, T. (2010). Rules for biologically inspired adaptive network design. *Science*, 327(5964), 439–442.

## Andrew Adamatzky
**Institution**: University of the West of England, Bristol (UWE Bristol), Unconventional Computing Laboratory  
**Key Contribution**: Adamatzky is the leading proponent of Physarum as a computing substrate in the full logical and computational sense. He has published extensively on demonstrations of logical gate operations, robot control systems, and other computational applications using living Physarum cultures. While his interpretations are sometimes contested, his experimental productivity has generated a large body of data on Physarum's behavioral repertoire and has established the field of "slime mold computing" as a recognized, if controversial, area of unconventional computing research.

**Key Papers and Books**:
- Adamatzky, A. (2010). *Physarum Machines: Computers from Slime Mould*. World Scientific.
- Adamatzky, A., & Jones, J. (2010). Road planning with slime mould: If Physarum built motorways it would route M6/M74 through Newcastle. *International Journal of Bifurcation and Chaos*, 20(10), 3065–3084.

## Karen Alim
**Institution**: Technical University of Munich, Theory of Biological Networks  
**Key Contribution**: Alim leads a group working on the biophysics of the Physarum tube network, focusing on how signals propagate through the organism via cytoplasmic flow. Her group has provided rigorous physical analysis of the mechanism by which food stimuli generate perturbations that travel through the network, which underpins the organism's ability to integrate spatial information. Her work bridges the biological observations and the mathematical modeling with careful attention to the actual fluid dynamics of the system.

**Key Papers**:
- Alim, K., Andrew, N., Pringle, A., & Brenner, M. P. (2017). Mechanism of signal propagation in Physarum polycephalum. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 114(20), 5136–5141.
- Alim, K., Amselem, G., Peaudecerf, F., Brenner, M. P., & Pringle, A. (2013). Random network peristalsis in Physarum polycephalum organizes fluid flows across an individual. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 110(33), 13306–13311.
