# Chapter 14: Ciliates — Behavioral Repertoires of Single Cells

## Opening Scene

Herbert Spencer Jennings spent years watching Paramecium through a microscope, and what he saw changed his mind about the nature of animal behavior.

The standard view in the early twentieth century — backed by Loeb's machine theory and the general mechanist program — was that simple organisms were simple automata: apply a stimulus, get a response, end of story. Jennings watched Paramecium bump into an obstacle, back up, turn slightly, advance again; bump again, back up, turn more, advance; and eventually navigate around the obstacle entirely. He watched the animal slow down when it entered a region of optimal temperature and speed up when it left. He watched it exhibit what looked, to any honest observer, like trial-and-error behavior — systematic exploration of a problem space until a workable solution was found.

"If Paramecium were a large animal," Jennings wrote in 1906, "so as to come within the everyday experience of human beings, its behavior would at once call forth the attribution of hunger, of desire, and the like."

This was not naive anthropomorphism. Jennings was a careful, skeptical biologist who spent his career insisting on the difference between what the evidence showed and what it was tempting to conclude. His point was that the behavioral complexity he was observing in a single-celled organism challenged the adequacy of simple stimulus-response models — and that the challenge was real, regardless of whether "desire" was the right word for what Paramecium experienced.

## The Central Question

How much behavioral complexity can a single cell accommodate — and what mechanisms, short of a nervous system, make it possible?

Ciliates are single-celled eukaryotes defined by their carpets of cilia — hair-like cellular appendages, coordinated in waves, that drive locomotion and feeding currents. They are among the most behaviorally complex single-celled organisms known, and they have been subjects of serious behavioral study for more than a century. The Paramecium that Jennings watched is the most famous representative of the group, but it is not the most interesting one.

That distinction arguably belongs to Stentor coeruleus — a trumpet-shaped, sessile ciliate that exhibits a graded, hierarchical behavioral repertoire in response to repeated irritation. The Stentor story is also a story about scientific controversy, failed replication, and the eventual return of a finding — a history as illuminating as the science itself.

## What You Will Learn

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:

**Describe the behavioral repertoire of Paramecium** — its avoiding reaction, its taxis behaviors, and the electrical signaling that underpins its behavior — and evaluate Jennings' interpretation of these behaviors in light of modern cellular neuroscience.

**Explain the Stentor avoidance hierarchy** — what Jennings described, why it was controversial, what the Wood et al. reinvestigation found, and what the existence of hierarchical behavioral responses in a single cell implies about the organization of behavior without a nervous system.

**Understand the unusual genetics of ciliates** — the macronucleus/micronucleus system, programmed DNA rearrangement, and epigenetic inheritance of cortical patterns — and appreciate why these phenomena are relevant to the concept of cellular memory and inheritance of acquired characteristics.

The ciliate story is, at bottom, a story about complexity without nervous systems. How much complexity is possible? What organizes it? What are its limits? These are questions with answers that continue to surprise.
