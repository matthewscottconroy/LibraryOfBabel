# Chapter 14: Further Reading

## Annotated Bibliography

**Jennings, H. S. (1906). *Behavior of the Lower Organisms*. Columbia University Press.**
The foundational text for this chapter. Jennings' prose is clear and his experimental descriptions remain readable more than a century after publication. The book is available in full online through Project Gutenberg and similar archives. Reading it in parallel with modern mechanistic accounts of Paramecium behavior reveals how much the phenomenology Jennings described has been confirmed, even as the mechanistic explanations he proposed have been replaced by molecular ones.

**Eckert, R. (1972). Bioelectric control of ciliary activity. *Science*, 176(4034), 473–481.**
The paper that established the ionic basis of Paramecium behavior, showing that calcium influx drives ciliary reversal and thus the avoiding reaction. This is the mechanistic vindication of Jennings' behavioral observations — the avoiding reaction and its modulation can be understood in terms of ion channel physiology. A landmark in both cell biology and the neuroscience of primitive organisms.

**Dexter, J. P., Prabakaran, S., & Gunawardena, J. (2019). A complex hierarchy of avoidance behaviors in a single-cell eukaryote. *Current Biology*, 29(24), 4323–4329.**
The reinvestigation of Stentor's behavioral hierarchy by the Wood/Gunawardena group. Essential reading for anyone who wants to understand what is actually established about Stentor's behavioral repertoire versus what was historical legend. The paper's discussion of variability and mechanism is admirably careful.

**Beisson, J., & Sonneborn, T. M. (1965). Cytoplasmic inheritance of the organization of the cell cortex in Paramecium aurelia. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 53(2), 275–282.**
The classic paper demonstrating epigenetic inheritance of cortical organization. One of the most elegant experiments in the history of cell biology — the experimental design is simple, the results are clear, and the implications are profound. Sonneborn was one of the great figures in 20th century genetics, and this paper represents one of his most important contributions.

**Mochizuki, K., & Gorovsky, M. A. (2004). Small RNAs in genome rearrangement in Tetrahymena. *Current Opinion in Genetics & Development*, 14(2), 181–187.**
A readable overview of the RNA-guided DNA elimination process in Tetrahymena macronuclear development. For those wanting to understand the molecular basis of the epigenetic inheritance of macronuclear organization, this paper and subsequent reviews in the same vein are essential starting points.

**Fang, W., Wang, X., Bracht, J. R., Nowacki, M., & Landweber, L. F. (2012). Piwi-interacting RNAs protect DNA against loss during Oxytricha genome rearrangement. *Cell*, 151(6), 1243–1255.**
Demonstrates the role of piRNA-class small RNAs in directing macronuclear DNA rearrangement in the spectacular ciliate Oxytricha trifallax, whose macronuclear genome is assembled from scrambled germline sequences guided by parental RNA templates. This is epigenetic inheritance of genome architecture at its most extreme.

**Machemer, H., & Teunis, P. F. M. (1996). Sensory-motor coupling and motor responses. In J. Hausmann & P. C. Bradbury (Eds.), *Ciliates: Cells as Organisms* (pp. 178–208). Gustav Fischer Verlag.**
The most technically detailed treatment of ciliate sensory-motor integration, covering the biophysics of ciliary beat direction control and its regulation by membrane potential and calcium. Dense but rewarding for those who want the mechanistic picture in full.

**Lynn, D. H. (2008). *The Ciliated Protozoa: Characterization, Classification and Guide to the Literature* (3rd ed.). Springer.**
The comprehensive reference on ciliate diversity and systematics. Useful as a backdrop to the behavioral work, which tends to focus on a handful of model species. Understanding how diverse ciliates are — and the range of ecological contexts they inhabit — provides motivation for thinking about how behavioral complexity evolved in the lineage.

**Bonini, N. M., Gustin, M. C., & Nelson, D. L. (1986). Regulation of ciliary motility by membrane potential in Paramecium: a role for cyclic AMP. *Cell Motility and the Cytoskeleton*, 6(3), 256–272.**
Shows that cAMP modulates the calcium sensitivity of Paramecium's ciliary reversal response, adding a layer of behavioral modulation beyond the simple calcium switch. This provides a mechanistic foundation for understanding how Paramecium's behavioral responses can be context-dependent, depending on its internal physiological state.
