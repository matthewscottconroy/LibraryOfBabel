# Chapter 14: Key Concepts

## Glossary

**Avoiding Reaction**
The stereotyped behavioral sequence exhibited by Paramecium in response to an adverse stimulus: reverse ciliary beat (backward swimming), body rotation, then resumption of forward swimming in a new direction. The avoiding reaction is driven by calcium influx through voltage-gated channels in the anterior membrane, triggered by depolarization caused by mechanosensitive or chemosensitive channel activity. The sequence functions as a trial-and-error exploration mechanism: the organism samples new directions until it finds one without the adverse stimulus.

*Why it matters*: The avoiding reaction demonstrates that directed, exploratory behavior is possible without a nervous system. Its mechanism — electrical signaling, calcium as a second messenger, ciliary reversal — is structurally homologous to neural signaling, providing a possible evolutionary precursor to more complex behavioral systems.

---

**Cilia**
Microtubule-based cellular appendages that beat rhythmically to drive locomotion and feeding currents. Cilia are structurally identical to flagella (the distinction is partly historical and partly refers to their context: cilia occur in large numbers per cell and tend to be shorter; flagella are typically few per cell and longer). In ciliates, the coordinated beating of thousands of cilia — organized in metachronal waves — produces smooth swimming. The direction of the ciliary beat determines the direction of locomotion.

*Why it matters*: Cilia are the behavioral actuators of ciliates — the physical structure that converts internal signaling into movement. Understanding ciliary dynamics and their regulation is the key to understanding ciliate behavioral biology.

---

**Macronucleus**
The transcriptionally active, polyploid nucleus of ciliates, produced from the micronucleus through a developmental process of DNA rearrangement, elimination, and amplification. The macronucleus controls the cell's day-to-day gene expression and therefore its physiology and behavior. It is not transmitted intact during sexual reproduction; instead, a new macronucleus is developed from the post-conjugation micronucleus in each generation. The macronucleus thus represents a somatic commitment — a processed version of the germline information optimized for function rather than transmission.

*Why it matters*: The macronucleus/micronucleus distinction makes ciliates a unique model for questions about the relationship between germline and soma, and about epigenetic inheritance of genomic organization.

---

**Micronucleus**
The transcriptionally silent, diploid germline nucleus of ciliates. The micronucleus contains the complete, unmodified genome and is transmitted to offspring during sexual reproduction (conjugation). Its relationship to the macronucleus is analogous (very loosely) to the relationship between the genome (as stored information) and the transcriptome (as expressed information) in conventional cells.

*Why it matters*: The existence of the micronucleus as a separate germline genome — distinct from the functionally active macronucleus — provides the physical basis for the unusual epigenetic inheritance of macronuclear organization, and makes ciliates a model system for understanding epigenetic information transmission.

---

**Programmed DNA Rearrangement**
The process by which the macronucleus is developed from the micronucleus in ciliates, involving RNA-guided elimination of specific DNA sequences, chromosomal fragmentation, telomere addition, and DNA amplification. In some species (notably Oxytricha), the process is so extensive that germline gene segments are assembled in different orders in the macronucleus than they appear in the micronucleus — requiring the organism to "read" an RNA template from the parental macronucleus to guide the rearrangement correctly.

*Why it matters*: Programmed DNA rearrangement is one of the most dramatic examples of epigenetic information transmission known in biology. The parental macronucleus provides, via RNA, a template for the organization of the daughter macronucleus — creating a channel of inheritance that bypasses the DNA sequence.

---

**Behavioral Hierarchy**
An organization of behavioral responses in which simpler, cheaper, or less disruptive responses are attempted before more costly ones, with escalation occurring when lower-level responses fail. The Stentor avoidance hierarchy — bending, ciliary reversal, contraction, detachment — is the canonical example in protist behavioral biology. The hierarchy may be explicitly encoded (there is a rule specifying when to escalate) or emergent (arising from the differential thresholds of different response systems). The distinction between these two mechanisms is an active area of investigation.

*Why it matters*: Behavioral hierarchies are widely found in animals with nervous systems, where they are organized by brain structures that modulate the threshold and priority of different behaviors. The Stentor hierarchy suggests that this organizational principle can exist in a single cell, raising questions about its evolutionary origins and cellular mechanisms.

---

**Epigenetic Inheritance**
Inheritance of information that is not encoded in the DNA sequence. In the ciliate context, this includes both the RNA-mediated transmission of macronuclear genome organization (from parental to daughter macronucleus) and the physical templating of cortical structure (in which existing cortical rows serve as physical templates for the correct assembly of new rows in daughter cells). Epigenetic inheritance demonstrates that the DNA sequence is not the only heritable information in a cell.

*Why it matters*: Epigenetic inheritance challenges the gene-centric view of evolution and inheritance. If acquired characteristics — changes in cellular organization not specified by DNA sequence — can be transmitted to daughter cells, then the evolutionary process may be more complex than a simple model of random mutation and natural selection suggests.

---

**Cortical Inheritance**
The specific mechanism by which the spatial organization of the ciliate cortex — the pattern and orientation of kinetid rows — is passed from mother cell to daughter cells, independently of the DNA sequence. Demonstrated by Beisson and Sonneborn (1965), cortical inheritance occurs because existing cortical structures serve as physical templates for the assembly of new structures in the same position and orientation during cell division.

*Why it matters*: Cortical inheritance is the clearest known example of non-genomic, purely structural inheritance in any organism. It illustrates that heritable information can be encoded in the physical organization of biological structures, not only in molecular sequences.
