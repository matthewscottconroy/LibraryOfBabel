# Section 2: Stentor coeruleus — The Hierarchical Decider

## Introduction

Stentor coeruleus is a large, solitary ciliate — visible to the naked eye at 1–2 mm in length — with a distinctive trumpet or vase shape. It anchors itself to a substrate and extends into the water column, beating its oral cilia in a vortex that draws bacteria and organic particles into its mouth. Unlike Paramecium, Stentor is sessile for much of its life, which means that its behavioral options are constrained: it cannot simply swim away from an irritant. It has to decide — and "decide" is the word that the behavioral literature has, with varying degrees of philosophical comfort, applied to what Stentor does.

What Jennings described, and what the modern reinvestigation has confirmed (with important qualifications), is a graded, hierarchical behavioral response to repeated irritation. This hierarchical organization — in a single cell — is arguably the most striking example of behavioral complexity in the protist world.

---

## 2.1 Jennings' Original Account

Jennings (1906) reported the following behavioral sequence in Stentor exposed to a localized irritant (in his experiments, a fine stream of carmine particles directed at the anterior end of the animal):

1. **Bending away**: On first contact with the irritant, Stentor bends its body away from the stream without detaching from the substrate. If the irritant persists, it transitions to:

2. **Reversing the oral cilia**: The cilia at the mouth reverse their beat direction, attempting to push the irritant away. If this fails (the irritant continues), Stentor transitions to:

3. **Contraction**: The entire body contracts rapidly into a small ball — a dramatic shrinkage enabled by the myoneme protein filaments in the cell's cortex. The contraction removes the oral end from contact with the irritant. After a brief pause, the organism re-extends. If it re-extends into the irritant again, it transitions to:

4. **Repeated contraction**: Multiple cycles of contraction and extension. If the irritant persists through all this, finally:

5. **Detachment and swimming away**: Stentor releases its anchor, becomes free-swimming, and moves away from the irritant location.

This sequence is hierarchical in two senses. First, the responses are ordered from least to most disruptive of normal feeding activity — the organism tries the cheapest option first and escalates only when cheaper options fail. Second, there is apparent learning or habituation within the sequence: after detachment, if Stentor re-settles near the irritant and is exposed again, it may detach more rapidly than the first time — as if it has "learned" that this location is problematic.

Jennings presented this as evidence for trial-and-error behavior in a single cell — the organism systematically trying responses and "deciding" to escalate when they fail. This interpretation was influential but also controversial.

---

## 2.2 Historical Controversy and Failed Replication

Despite Jennings' careful experimental work, subsequent researchers had difficulty reliably reproducing the full hierarchical sequence he described. Early 20th century papers reported the behavior, but also noted its variability: the full five-step hierarchy was not always exhibited, and the order of responses was not always as Jennings described. By mid-century, the Stentor behavioral hierarchy was occasionally cited in textbooks but treated by many researchers as incompletely established — impressive if real, but not replicable with the reliability that should characterize a landmark biological finding.

This is an uncomfortable but important part of the story. The reasons for the variability were not well-understood. Possibilities included: differences in the health and physiological state of the animals; differences in the nature of the irritant used; subtle procedural differences between laboratories; and genuine stochasticity in Stentor's behavioral responses (meaning that the hierarchy describes statistical tendencies rather than a fixed deterministic sequence).

The uncertainty persisted for decades, with the result that the Stentor behavioral hierarchy occupied an awkward position in the literature: too interesting to ignore, too variable to accept uncritically.

---

## 2.3 The Wood et al. Reinvestigation

In 2019, a team led by David Wood at the University of Pittsburgh published a reinvestigation of the Jennings hierarchy in *Current Biology* (Dexter et al., 2019). This study brought modern methods — systematic quantification, video recording, computer-assisted tracking — to the century-old question of what Stentor actually does when irritated.

The results were broadly supportive of Jennings' core observation but importantly qualified in several ways.

**What was confirmed**: Stentor does exhibit the behaviors Jennings described — bending, ciliary reversal, contraction, and detachment — and these behaviors can be observed in response to localized irritation. Individual Stentor cells exhibit multiple responses in sequence rather than a single fixed response, consistent with the hierarchical account.

**What was more complex**: The sequence was not as rigidly ordered as Jennings' account might suggest. Different cells showed different orders of response, and the same cell could exhibit responses in different orders on different trials. This suggests that the "hierarchy" is better understood as a repertoire of responses with different probabilities, whose likelihoods shift over the course of repeated irritation, rather than a strictly ordered escalation sequence.

**On habituation**: The study found evidence for stimulus-specific adaptation — cells became less responsive to a repeated identical stimulus over time — but the interpretation of whether this constitutes "habituation" in the technical sense (as opposed to sensory adaptation or fatigue) was carefully qualified by the authors. The distinction matters for cognitive interpretation: habituation, in the learning literature, implies that the reduced response is specific to the stimulus and recovers with rest, distinguishing it from general fatigue. The evidence for this specificity in Stentor was suggestive but not definitively established in the 2019 study.

**On "decision-making"**: The authors noted that their results were consistent with, but did not require, the interpretation that Stentor makes decisions in any cognitive sense. The variable ordering of responses could reflect genuine decision-making (the cell "choosing" among options based on context) or could reflect stochasticity in the signaling pathways driving each response. Distinguishing these hypotheses would require more detailed mechanistic investigation.

The Wood lab has continued this line of investigation, and subsequent work has begun to probe the molecular mechanisms underlying Stentor's behavioral flexibility.

---

## 2.4 Mechanism: What Controls the Hierarchy?

What cellular mechanisms could produce a hierarchical, ordered behavioral repertoire in a single cell?

At a first approximation, the answer involves the same calcium signaling system we encountered in Paramecium, but with additional layers of regulation specific to Stentor's more complex architecture.

The myoneme system — protein fibers running along the length of the cell that can shorten explosively in response to calcium elevation — underlies the contraction behavior. The oral cilia are a separate mechanical subsystem with their own calcium-sensitive regulation. The anchoring filaments that hold the cell to the substrate have mechanical properties that determine the threshold for detachment.

A plausible (though not fully established) model of the hierarchy involves calcium dynamics with multiple thresholds:

- Low calcium elevation (from mild or distant irritation): activates bending and oral ciliary responses
- Moderate calcium elevation (from stronger or closer irritation): activates myoneme contraction
- High or sustained calcium elevation (from repeated strong stimulation): overwhelms the anchoring system and leads to detachment

Under this model, the hierarchy is not encoded explicitly anywhere; it emerges from the differential calcium sensitivities of different behavioral subsystems. The "decision" to escalate is not made by any decision-making process; it emerges from the dynamics of calcium elevation in response to repeated stimulation.

Whether this mechanistic account is correct in detail remains to be established. What is important is that it demonstrates how hierarchical behavioral organization could exist in a single cell without requiring any computational structure that neuroscientists would recognize. The hierarchy can be a property of the physical chemistry of the cell's signaling systems, not a property of an information-processing architecture.

---

## 2.5 The Significance of Hierarchical Behavior

If the hierarchical behavioral repertoire of Stentor is real — and the balance of evidence, including the Wood et al. reinvestigation, suggests it is, at least in its core features — then it has significant implications for our understanding of cellular cognition.

**For the concept of decision-making**: Stentor's hierarchy is often described as "decision-making" in the biological literature, and there is something right about this. The organism does not produce a fixed response to an irritant; it produces different responses in different contexts (particularly, depending on how long the irritant has persisted), and those responses are ordered in a way that reflects something like cost-benefit logic. Whether this constitutes decision-making in the cognitive sense depends on what we think decision-making requires — and that question is genuinely open.

**For the organization of behavior without brains**: The hierarchy demonstrates that behavioral responses can be organized hierarchically — with cheaper, less disruptive options tried first and more disruptive options reserved for persistent problems — without a nervous system to impose that organization. The organization is a property of the cell's signaling network, not of a separate computational module.

**For evolutionary continuity**: Hierarchical behavioral organization is often treated as a property of animals with nervous systems — the organizational principle that allows reflexes, habits, instincts, and deliberate actions to be appropriately deployed depending on circumstances. The Stentor data suggest that this organizational principle has roots in cellular biology that predate nervous systems. Understanding how it works in a single cell may illuminate how it works in circuits of many cells.
