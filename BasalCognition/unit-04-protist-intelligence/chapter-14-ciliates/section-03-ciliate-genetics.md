# Section 3: Ciliate Genetics — Memory Written in DNA and Cortex

## Introduction

The behavioral complexity we have described in Paramecium and Stentor is remarkable. But ciliates have another kind of complexity that is even more extraordinary, and that bears directly on fundamental questions about memory, inheritance, and the relationship between genotype and phenotype.

Ciliates have two kinds of nuclei. The macronucleus controls the cell's everyday gene expression — it is the working copy of the genome, actively transcribed, responsible for the proteins that run the cell's metabolism and behavior. The micronucleus is the germline nucleus — the copy of the genome that is transmitted to the next generation during sexual reproduction. And the two are not simply copies of each other. The macronucleus is derived from the micronucleus through a process of dramatic genomic rearrangement that, in some ciliate species, eliminates the majority of the germline DNA from the working genome.

This is not an unusual wrinkle in a standard genetic story. It is, as we will see, a window into some of the deepest questions about gene regulation, epigenetics, and the extent to which somatic cells can inherit acquired information — questions that, in more familiar organisms, are difficult to approach directly.

---

## 3.1 The Macronucleus and Micronucleus: A Divided Genome

In most eukaryotes, every cell nucleus contains essentially the same genome (with minor exceptions for specialized cells). The nucleus is the nucleus. Ciliates violate this assumption dramatically.

The **micronucleus** is the germline nucleus. It contains the complete, diploid genome in a form similar to what you would find in a typical eukaryotic nucleus — with the same sequences, the same organization, the same genes. It is transcriptionally largely silent during normal vegetative growth. Its function is reproduction: it is the nucleus that undergoes meiosis during sexual reproduction (conjugation) and is transmitted to offspring.

The **macronucleus** is the somatic nucleus. It is transcriptionally active and contains a highly processed version of the genetic information. The processing involves several extraordinary steps:

**Genome amplification**: During macronuclear development, the micronuclear genome is replicated many times. In Tetrahymena thermophila (the best-studied ciliate), the macronucleus contains approximately 45 copies of each chromosome. This polyploidy ensures that enough template is available for the high rates of transcription needed to support the cell's metabolic activity.

**DNA elimination**: Depending on the species, 15–90% of the micronuclear DNA sequences are eliminated during macronuclear development. What is eliminated? Primarily repetitive sequences and non-coding elements that constitute the "junk DNA" of the germline genome. What remains are the protein-coding genes and their regulatory elements, now present in many copies for efficient transcription.

**Chromosome fragmentation and capping**: The chromosomes are broken into smaller fragments, each ending with telomeric caps. This produces a macronucleus containing thousands of short chromosomal fragments rather than a few long chromosomes.

The result is an organism with two distinct genomes in every cell — one for inheritance, one for function — connected by a developmental process of dramatic restructuring.

---

## 3.2 Programmed DNA Elimination: Reading an Epigenetic Code

The DNA elimination step is not random. In ciliate species studied in detail, the sequences that are eliminated from the developing macronucleus are consistently the same — the same repetitive elements, the same non-coding sequences are excised at every macronuclear development. This means that there is information somewhere about what to eliminate, and that information must be transmitted across generations.

In Tetrahymena and its relatives, this information is encoded in small RNA molecules. The parental macronucleus produces small RNAs — called scan RNAs (scnRNAs) — that are complementary to the sequences that should be retained in the daughter macronucleus. When these scnRNAs scan the developing macronucleus, they find complementary sequences that match retained sequences and mark them for protection. Sequences without matching scnRNAs are recognized as "foreign" and eliminated (Mochizuki & Gorovsky, 2004).

What makes this system relevant to questions of memory and inheritance is the following: the identity of the sequences that are retained in the macronucleus is determined by the content of the parental macronucleus itself, transmitted via scnRNAs. This creates a form of epigenetic inheritance: the parental macronucleus instructs the daughter macronucleus about what to retain, using RNA as the transmission medium.

In the spectacular case of Oxytricha trifallax, the scRNA-mediated templating of macronuclear development is so precise that the parental macronucleus provides not just which sequences to retain but also in what order to join them — specifying the rearrangement of DNA segments that produces functional genes from scattered germline sequences (Fang et al., 2012). This is, in effect, a cellular memory of genome organization that is transmitted epigenetically from mother to daughter cell, via RNA.

## 3.3 Epigenetic Inheritance of Cortical Patterns

The most philosophically provocative example of epigenetic inheritance in ciliates involves not the genome but the cell's outer layer — the cortex.

The ciliate cortex is the cell's outer surface, and it is not a uniform membrane. It is elaborately organized into rows of structures called kinetids — each kinetid containing a cilium and its associated basal body, connected by a system of fibrous connections to neighboring kinetids. The rows run in parallel from the anterior to the posterior of the cell, establishing the cell's body axis and determining the position and orientation of structures like the mouth and the anal pore.

In the 1960s and 1970s, Tracy Sonneborn and his colleagues at Indiana University performed a series of experiments using Paramecium that have been regarded as some of the most important in the history of epigenetics. They surgically altered the cortex of living Paramecium cells — rotating a small patch of the cortical row structure by 180 degrees, so that the cilia in that patch were oriented opposite to the rest. The altered cells survived and continued to divide. And in the daughter cells, the inverted patch was faithfully reproduced — a clone of inverted-patch cells could be maintained through many cell generations (Beisson & Sonneborn, 1965).

This is epigenetic inheritance in its most literal sense: the structure of the cortex — not the DNA sequence — is inherited by daughter cells. The mechanism is not small RNAs or DNA methylation (the usual suspects in epigenetic discussions). It is the physical templating of cortical structure by existing cortical structure: the basal bodies of existing cilia serve as templates for the assembly of new basal bodies in the correct position and orientation.

The philosophical implication is striking: here is a case where the inheritance of a cellular property is independent of the DNA sequence. The DNA has not changed — the inverted-patch cells have the same genome as wild-type cells. What has changed is a property of the cell's physical organization, and that property is inherited through the physical interaction of cortical structures with each other.

---

## 3.4 What Does This Tell Us About Memory and Inheritance?

The ciliate genetic system — the macronucleus/micronucleus division, programmed DNA elimination guided by RNA templates from the parental macronucleus, and the physical templating of cortical structure — collectively constitute a set of mechanisms for heritable information storage that do not depend on DNA sequence in the conventional sense.

This has several implications for the themes of this book:

**Expanded conception of memory**: If heritable information can be encoded in cortical structure and transmitted through physical templating, then the concept of "cellular memory" is richer than we might have thought. The ciliate cortex "remembers" its organization across cell divisions through a mechanism that is physical, not biochemical in the usual sense.

**Expanded conception of inheritance**: The ciliate system provides a concrete example of Lamarckian-style inheritance — inheritance of acquired characteristics — in which a change induced by an experimental intervention (the cortical rotation) is stably inherited for many generations. This does not rehabilitate Lamarck's general theory (which was wrong in its proposed mechanisms), but it does demonstrate that molecular mechanisms for inheritance of somatically acquired traits can exist, in appropriate organisms, without any revision of core evolutionary genetics.

**Challenge to the central dogma**: The RNA-mediated inheritance of macronuclear structure in ciliates represents information flow from the soma (the macronucleus) to the germline (the micronucleus/developing macronucleus) — a direction of information flow that is not usually considered in the framework of the central dogma. It does not violate the central dogma in its narrow sense (it does not involve protein-to-nucleic acid information transfer), but it does demonstrate that somatic information can influence the functional genome of the next generation through epigenetic mechanisms.

These are not just ciliate curiosities. They point toward mechanisms — epigenetic inheritance, physical templating of cellular structure, RNA-mediated transmission of genomic information — that may be more widespread in eukaryotes than currently recognized, and that have implications for how we think about inheritance, memory, and the relationship between genotype and phenotype.
