# Chapter 15: Flagellates and the Origins of Multicellularity

## Opening Scene

Inside a healthy adult human, there are roughly 37 trillion cells — neurons, muscle cells, epithelial cells, immune cells, liver cells — each performing specialized functions, each connected to others through an elaborate network of physical contacts and chemical signals, each subordinating its individual proliferative capacity to the needs of the whole. The arrangement is so ancient, so stable, and so deeply embedded in our biology that it is easy to forget how extraordinary it is.

How did it happen? How did a world of independent cells — each dividing, competing, surviving on its own — become a world of cells cooperating in integrated organisms so seamlessly that individual cell identity is nearly invisible in the collective? This transition — from unicellular life to multicellular life — is arguably the most important organizational event in the history of animal life. It happened multiple times, in multiple lineages, and it left traces in organisms alive today that allow us to study it directly.

The choanoflagellates are the closest living relatives of the animals. They are single-celled, or form small colonies of cells connected by thin extensions of cytoplasm. They feed by beating a single flagellum surrounded by a collar of microvilli, creating currents that draw bacteria toward the cell surface for phagocytosis. And they possess, in their genomes, a remarkable collection of genes — genes encoding proteins involved in cell-cell signaling, cell adhesion, and the coordination of cell behavior — that were once thought to be animal inventions.

They were not inventions. They were inheritances.

## The Central Question

What was present in the unicellular ancestors of animals that made multicellularity not only possible but perhaps, in a sense, inevitable — or at least highly probable once certain conditions were met?

This question has both a molecular answer (what genes and proteins were present?) and a cognitive answer (what behavioral and signaling capabilities were present?). Both answers have been transformed in the last two decades by genomic and behavioral studies of choanoflagellates and other early-branching protists. What has emerged is a picture in which the molecular toolkit for multicellular life — cell adhesion, intercellular signaling, coordinated behavior, cell differentiation — was assembled in unicellular ancestors long before multicellularity itself evolved.

This chapter examines three windows into this transition: choanoflagellates as models for our unicellular ancestors; Volvox and its relatives as a living laboratory for multicellularity evolution in progress; and the theoretical framework of major evolutionary transitions as a guide for understanding what the unicellularity-to-multicellularity transition implies for cognition.

## What You Will Learn

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:

**Describe the relationship between choanoflagellates and animals**, including the genomic evidence for their close relationship and the behavioral evidence suggesting that the molecular toolkit for animal-style cell-cell signaling predates the animal lineage.

**Explain the Volvox system** as a natural experiment in the evolution of multicellularity — how increasing colony size is correlated with increasing cell differentiation, and what the evolution of the germ-soma distinction implies for the concept of individuality at different levels.

**Apply the Maynard Smith and Szathmáry framework of major evolutionary transitions** to the multicellularity transition, understanding what this framework predicts about the conditions under which individuality shifts from the cell to the multicellular collective, and what the cognitive implications of such a shift might be.

The flagellate story is, ultimately, a story about the origins of animal cognition — not in neurons or brains, but in the molecular architecture of the cells that eventually built them.
