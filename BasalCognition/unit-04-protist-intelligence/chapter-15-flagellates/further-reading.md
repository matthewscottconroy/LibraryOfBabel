# Chapter 15: Further Reading

## Annotated Bibliography

**King, N., Westbrook, M. J., Young, S. L., Kuo, A., Abedin, M., Chapman, J., ... & Rokhsar, D. (2008). The genome of the choanoflagellate Monosiga brevicollis and the origin of metazoans. *Nature*, 451(7180), 783–788.**
The landmark genomic paper that established the extent to which "animal-specific" gene families were already present in the unicellular ancestor of animals. Essential reading for anyone interested in the molecular origins of animal multicellularity. The analysis of tyrosine kinase signaling and cell adhesion gene families in Monosiga remains striking.

**Alegado, R. A., Brown, L. W., Cao, S., Dermenjian, R. K., Zuzow, R., Fairclough, S. R., ... & King, N. (2012). A bacterial sulfonolipid triggers multicellular development in the closest living relatives of animals. *eLife*, 1, e00013.**
Shows that colonial (rosette) formation in the choanoflagellate Salpingoeca rosetta is induced by a specific bacterial lipid signal — demonstrating that the unicellular-to-colonial transition in our nearest relatives is a regulated, environmentally responsive behavior. A beautiful paper in a beautiful journal (eLife's open-access, post-publication peer review format).

**Ryan, T. J., & Grant, S. G. N. (2009). The origin and evolution of synapses. *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, 10(10), 701–712.**
A thorough review of the evolutionary origins of the postsynaptic density and the synaptic machinery more generally, including discussion of choanoflagellate synaptic protein homologs. For students wanting to understand how the molecular architecture of synapses relates to pre-neural cellular signaling, this is the essential starting point.

**Maynard Smith, J., & Szathmáry, E. (1995). *The Major Transitions in Evolution*. Oxford University Press.**
The foundational theoretical framework for the chapter's evolutionary discussion. The book is broad, ambitious, and occasionally speculative — but its central framework for thinking about how biological information storage and transmission change at evolutionary transitions has proved enormously productive. The chapters on the origin of multicellularity and on the emergence of social complexity are most directly relevant here.

**Kirk, D. L. (2005). A twelve-step program for evolving multicellularity and a division of labor. *BioEssays*, 27(3), 299–310.**
A readable and insightful review of the genetic changes associated with the evolution of germ-soma differentiation in the Volvocine algae, focusing on the regA gene and its role in committing cells to the soma fate. Kirk's "twelve-step program" provides a useful conceptual ladder from single-celled Chlamydomonas to fully differentiated Volvox.

**Drescher, K., Goldstein, R. E., Michel, N., Polin, M., & Tuval, I. (2010). Direct measurement of the flow field around swimming microorganisms. *Physical Review Letters*, 105(16), 168101.**
The biophysics of Volvox swimming, including quantitative analysis of the flow fields generated by the flagellar beating of individual cells and the resulting collective motility. This paper provides the physical understanding of how locally generated forces sum to produce collective phototaxis.

**Fairclough, S. R., Dayel, M. J., & King, N. (2010). Multicellular development in a choanoflagellate. *Current Biology*, 20(20), R875–R876.**
A brief but important paper documenting the cellular organization of Salpingoeca rosetta rosettes, showing that the colony is not simply an aggregate of cells but has a reproducible internal organization. This structural organization is relevant to thinking about how choanoflagellate colonies differ from and resemble the organized cell layers of animal embryos.

**Jékely, G., Keijzer, F., & Godfrey-Smith, P. (2021). An option space for early neural evolution. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B: Biological Sciences*, 376(1821), 20190761.**
A philosophically sophisticated discussion of how nervous systems may have evolved from pre-neural sensory-effector cells. The paper situates the evolution of neurons within the broader context of multicellular animal evolution and discusses how the option space for neural evolution was constrained by the ancestral cellular toolkit.

**Grosberg, R. K., & Strathmann, R. R. (2007). The evolution of multicellularity: A minor major transition? *Annual Review of Ecology, Evolution, and Systematics*, 38, 621–654.**
A comprehensive review of the evolution of multicellularity across all lineages in which it has evolved independently (at least 25 times). Provides the ecological and evolutionary context for understanding why multicellularity evolves — a necessary supplement to the molecular and developmental picture provided by the King and Kirk work.
