# Section 1: Choanoflagellates — Our Nearest Unicellular Relatives

## Introduction

The relationship between choanoflagellates and animals has been suspected since the nineteenth century, when Ernst Haeckel noted the striking morphological similarity between choanoflagellate cells and the choanocytes — the feeding cells — of sponges. Both have a single flagellum surrounded by a collar of microvilli, creating a feeding apparatus so distinctive that its independent evolution seemed unlikely. Haeckel proposed that the choanoflagellates were the ancestors of the sponges, and through them, of all animals.

This proposal has been confirmed, refined, and enormously extended by molecular phylogenetics. Choanoflagellates are the sister group of the animals — the closest living relatives of the entire animal kingdom, more closely related to us than any other unicellular organisms (King et al., 2008). The divergence between the choanoflagellate and animal lineages occurred approximately 600–800 million years ago. In the time since, choanoflagellates have remained essentially unicellular (or formed simple colonies), while the animal lineage diversified into everything from sponges to arthropods to vertebrates.

What happened? And what did the unicellular ancestor of animals already have in place before the transition to multicellularity?

## 1.1 The Choanoflagellate Toolkit

The sequencing of the Monosiga brevicollis genome in 2008 was a landmark event (King et al., 2008). What the genome revealed challenged a then-prevailing assumption: that the molecular machinery enabling animal-style multicellularity was invented in the animal lineage.

Many of the genes and gene families previously thought to be animal innovations were found in Monosiga:

**Cell adhesion molecules**: Cadherins — the major cell-cell adhesion proteins in animal embryogenesis — are present in choanoflagellate genomes. Animal cadherins mediate cell-cell contacts that are essential for the formation of epithelial layers, for the coordinated movements of cells during gastrulation, and for the maintenance of tissue integrity. The choanoflagellate cadherins are structurally similar to animal cadherins, though their functional role in choanoflagellates is not yet fully understood.

**Receptor tyrosine kinases**: The signaling receptors that mediate many cell-cell communication events in animals — including growth factor receptors, the receptor for insulin, and many developmental signaling receptors — are tyrosine kinases. Monosiga brevicollis has more tyrosine kinases in its genome, proportionally, than any animal so far examined. This is unexpected: tyrosine kinase signaling was thought to be a key innovation of animal multicellularity, used for intercellular communication during development. Finding it in a unicellular organism raises the question of what it is used for.

**Scaffolding proteins and signal transduction components**: PDZ domain proteins, SH3 domain proteins, ankyrin repeats — the molecular scaffolding that organizes signal transduction in animal cells — are all present in choanoflagellate genomes.

**Synaptic proteins**: Perhaps most strikingly, choanoflagellates possess homologs of proteins involved in the postsynaptic density of animal synapses — specifically, components of the MAGUK and Homer/Shank scaffolding complexes (Ryan & Grant, 2009). These proteins are components of the specialized protein architecture that organizes signal reception at the postsynaptic side of chemical synapses. Their presence in choanoflagellates suggests that the molecular scaffold that ultimately enabled synaptic signaling in neurons was in place before neurons existed.

## 1.2 Colonial Behavior and Cell-Cell Signaling

Choanoflagellates are not invariably solitary. Many species form colonies — groups of cells connected by cytoplasmic bridges or extracellular matrix. The most studied of these is Salpingoeca rosetta, which was the focus of research by Nicole King's group at UC Berkeley.

S. rosetta can exist as single cells or as colonial "rosettes" — spherical colonies of cells, each cell contributing its flagellum to the colony's surface, beating in coordinated fashion to generate feeding currents. The transition between single-cell and colonial life is regulated by environmental signals — specifically, by molecules produced by bacteria in the environment (Alegado et al., 2012). A specific bacterium, Algoriphagus machipongonensis, produces chondrioitol-related sulfonolipids that induce rosette formation in S. rosetta. In the absence of this bacterial signal, S. rosetta exists as single cells.

This is a behavioral response to a chemical signal from another organism — a primitive form of chemosensing and behavioral switching that has some structural similarities to quorum sensing in bacteria (though the mechanism is quite different). The colonial form has different properties from the single-cell form: rosette colonies feed more efficiently in some conditions, and they may be better protected from some predators.

The regulation of colonial behavior by environmental signals suggests that the choanoflagellate unicellular-to-colonial transition is not merely a mechanical consequence of incomplete cell division — it is a regulated phenotypic transition, responsive to environmental information. This matters because it means that colonial behavior in choanoflagellates is not equivalent to multicellularity accidentally arising from failed division; it is a controlled, adaptive response that presumably has fitness consequences in natural environments.

## 1.3 Synaptic Protein Origins: Cognition Before Synapses?

The presence of homologs of synaptic scaffolding proteins in choanoflagellates deserves extended discussion, because its implications are significant for the central themes of this book.

The postsynaptic density (PSD) is a specialized protein complex at the receiving end of animal chemical synapses. It consists of hundreds of proteins organized by scaffolding molecules (particularly PSD-95/MAGUK family proteins) into a functional unit that anchors neurotransmitter receptors, organizes downstream signaling molecules, and regulates synaptic plasticity. The PSD is, in a sense, the molecular basis of synaptic memory — changes in PSD composition underlie long-term potentiation and depression, the cellular correlates of learning and memory.

Choanoflagellates have homologs of MAGUK family proteins and other PSD components (Ryan & Grant, 2009). These proteins presumably serve some function in choanoflagellates — possibly organizing signal transduction at the cell surface in response to bacterial or environmental signals, or mediating the organization of colonial cell-cell contacts. But whatever their current function, their deep evolutionary history in unicellular relatives of animals implies that the molecular toolkit for synaptic organization was assembled in a pre-neural context, for non-neural purposes.

This is relevant to questions about the evolution of cognition for the following reason: the synaptic machinery that underlies neural learning did not arise de novo in the animal lineage. It was recruited from a pre-existing toolkit that was present in the unicellular ancestor. The cognitive capacities of neurons are, in this sense, evolutionary elaborations of molecular capacities that already existed in single cells — not entirely new inventions.

This does not mean that choanoflagellates are cognitive in the way neurons are. But it does mean that there is molecular continuity between the information-processing capabilities of our unicellular relatives and the information-processing capabilities of our neurons. The evolution of cognition did not begin with the neuron; it began with whatever ancient molecular circuits the neuron later elaborated.

## 1.4 The Choanoflagellate Model and Its Limits

It is important to be appropriately cautious about how much we can infer from choanoflagellate genomics about the biology of our unicellular ancestors.

Modern choanoflagellates are not frozen in time. They are themselves the products of 600–800 million years of evolution since the divergence from the animal lineage. They may have gained genes (through horizontal transfer from bacteria, for example) and lost genes since the divergence. The genes they share with animals reflect the ancestral toolkit, but we cannot assume that the ancestral context in which those genes were used is identical to their current context in modern choanoflagellates.

Moreover, having a gene is not the same as having its function. The cadherin in a choanoflagellate may have the same protein domain structure as an animal cadherin but function in a different cellular process — one that does not involve cell-cell adhesion in the way that animal cadherins do. Demonstrating that a gene is shared is the first step; demonstrating that its function is also shared requires additional experimental work.

What the genomic data provide is a powerful framework for asking mechanistic questions about animal origins, and a set of molecules whose functions in choanoflagellates are worthy of investigation precisely because those functions may illuminate the evolutionary origins of animal multicellularity and its associated cognitive capabilities.
