# Section 2: Volvox — A Natural Experiment in Multicellularity

## Introduction

Volvox is a spherical green alga, barely visible to the naked eye, that rolls through freshwater ponds and streams — rotating as it swims, propelled by the coordinated beating of flagella on its thousands of cells. It is beautiful, but its beauty is almost incidental to its scientific significance. Within the volvocine algae — a closely related group of species ranging from single-celled Chlamydomonas to the fully multicellular Volvox — we have a living series of transitional forms that illustrate, in organisms available for laboratory study, how the transition from unicellular to multicellular life may have occurred.

The volvocine algae did not descend from each other in any simple linear chain; they represent independent evolutionary lineages that have independently evolved different degrees of multicellularity. But because they are all closely related, their genomic and physiological similarities allow us to ask what changes accompanied the transition to larger colony size and greater cell differentiation. It is a natural experiment in the evolution of multicellularity, replicated multiple times in the same phylogenetic neighborhood.

---

## 2.1 The Volvocine Series

The volvocine algae present a graded series of organization:

**Chlamydomonas**: A single biflagellate cell. It divides, its daughters swim free, and each is an independent individual. All cells are genetically identical (in clonal cultures) and all are simultaneously capable of photosynthesis, reproduction, and motility.

**Gonium**: A flat colony of 8–16 cells connected by cytoplasmic bridges and embedded in an extracellular matrix. All cells are morphologically similar (undifferentiated), each beating its two flagella to contribute to colony motility. Colonies reproduce as wholes: each cell produces a daughter colony, and the original colony fragments into daughter colonies.

**Pandorina / Eudorina**: Spherical colonies of 16–32 cells. Increasing coordination of flagellar beating for rolling locomotion. Some partial differentiation between cells in different positions.

**Pleodorina**: A colony of 64–128 cells with genuine differentiation: cells at the anterior pole are smaller and motility-specialized (they will not reproduce during the colony's life), while cells at the posterior pole are larger and reproduction-specialized (they will produce the next generation).

**Volvox**: Spherical colonies of 500–50,000 cells with complete germ-soma differentiation. The soma cells are small, flagellated, photosynthetically active, but sterile — they contribute to the colony's movement and metabolism but will never reproduce. A small number of much larger cells, embedded within the colony interior, are the germ cells (gonidia) — they will reproduce asexually to produce daughter colonies (themselves), or sexually to produce eggs.

This series represents increasing colony size, increasing coordination of collective behavior, and increasing degree of germ-soma differentiation — the three signatures of the transition to multicellularity.

## 2.2 Collective Phototaxis

One of the most striking behavioral capabilities of Volvox is its ability to perform collective phototaxis — directed swimming toward a light source — coordinated across thousands of cells, each independently beating its flagella.

The cells of the Volvox exterior are each photosensitive: each cell has a light-sensitive organelle (the eyespot) located toward the colony's posterior. When light strikes from one direction, the cells on the illuminated side of the colony detect it and modify their flagellar beating pattern — slowing or changing the effective stroke direction — while cells on the shadowed side beat normally. The resulting asymmetry in flagellar force causes the colony to rotate and re-orient toward the light source (Drescher et al., 2010).

This is collective phototaxis without centralized control. There is no cell in Volvox that receives information from all other cells and issues commands. Each cell acts on its own local light perception. The collective phototaxis emerges from the coordination of 500–50,000 independently photosensitive cells, each following local rules that, in aggregate, produce directed colony movement.

The mechanism is, in structural terms, analogous to the decentralized control of movement in some invertebrate nervous systems — where no single "command neuron" controls behavior but where the collective dynamics of a distributed network produces coordinated output. The analogy is not exact (the cells of Volvox are not neurons), but it illustrates a general principle: collective directed behavior can emerge from distributed local responses without requiring central coordination.

## 2.3 The Evolution of Germ-Soma Differentiation

The most important evolutionary transition in the Volvocine series is the emergence of germ-soma differentiation in Pleodorina and Volvox. This transition is significant because it represents a fundamental change in how individuality is organized: in undifferentiated colonies (Gonium, Pandorina), every cell is equally capable of reproduction; in Volvox, soma cells have permanently renounced reproduction — they are, in evolutionary terms, sacrificing their fitness for the benefit of the germ cells.

This looks like the Dictyostelium altruism problem revisited at a higher organizational level. Why would evolution favor soma cells that cannot reproduce? The answer invokes the same logic as Dictyostelium stalk cell altruism: inclusive fitness. In a clonal Volvox colony (all cells are genetically identical), the soma cells that sacrifice their own reproduction are promoting the reproduction of genetically identical germ cells — effectively reproducing their own genes through a different pathway.

The genetic change underlying germ-soma differentiation in Volvox has been identified. A single gene — regA — appears to be a master regulator of the soma cell fate (Kirk, 2005). The regA gene encodes a transcriptional repressor that, when active, suppresses chloroplast biogenesis and cell growth, preventing soma cells from accumulating the resources needed for reproduction. In Chlamydomonas (the unicellular relative), there is no regA, and all cells can reproduce. In Volvox, regA is expressed in soma cells, locking them into their sterile, flagellated fate.

This is a surprisingly simple genetic basis for a macroevolutionary transition — a single regulatory gene switching a cell between a reproductive and a somatic fate. It suggests that germ-soma differentiation may not require many genetic innovations, only the appropriate deployment of regulatory machinery.

## 2.4 What Volvox Teaches About Cognitive Transitions

The Volvox system is relevant to cognitive transitions in the following way: as the organizational level of individuality shifts from the cell to the colony, the level at which cognitive processes are implemented should also shift.

A Chlamydomonas cell performs phototaxis as an individual — it senses light, it adjusts its flagellar beat, it turns toward the source. The sensing, processing, and response are all properties of a single cell.

A Volvox colony performs phototaxis as a collective — but the sensing is still distributed across individual cells, each responding locally. What has changed is the integration of those local responses into a collective output (directed colony movement). The "phototaxis" of Volvox is a higher-level emergent property, produced by the coordinated behavior of many cells, each of which individually executes a simpler local response.

This is, abstractly, the same structure as the relationship between individual neurons and the circuits they form in animal nervous systems. Individual neurons are not, by themselves, capable of producing the complex behaviors that their circuits produce. The circuit's behavior is emergent — a property of the collective, not of any individual component.

The Volvox system thus provides a concrete biological model for thinking about how cognition scales with organization level: the cognitive properties of a system can be more than the sum of the cognitive properties of its parts. Understanding how this superadditivity works in Volvox — how the coordination of individually simple flagellar responses produces collectively complex phototaxis — is a step toward understanding how the coordination of individually modest neurons produces the collectively extraordinary capabilities of the animal brain.
