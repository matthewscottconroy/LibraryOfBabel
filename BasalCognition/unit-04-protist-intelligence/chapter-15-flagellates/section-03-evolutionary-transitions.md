# Section 3: Evolutionary Transitions and the Cognitive Implications of Multicellularity

## Introduction

In 1995, John Maynard Smith and Eörs Szathmáry published a book called *The Major Transitions in Evolution*. It is one of the most influential works in evolutionary biology of the late twentieth century, and its central insight is elegantly simple: the history of life is punctuated by a small number of transitions in which entities capable of independent replication joined together to form higher-level entities — transitions that fundamentally altered the structure of information, cooperation, and individuality in the living world.

The transitions they identified include: the origin of the first cell (replicating molecules in compartments), the emergence of the chromosome (individual replicators joining in a linked genome), the origin of the eukaryotic cell (symbiosis between formerly independent prokaryotes), the evolution of multicellularity, the evolution of sexual reproduction, and the emergence of language in humans.

What these transitions share, in Maynard Smith and Szathmáry's analysis, is a change in the way information is stored and transmitted. At each transition, the higher-level entity takes on properties — particularly, control over the replication of its lower-level components — that were previously properties of the lower-level components themselves. The individual cell that was formerly a complete individual becomes, after the multicellularity transition, a component of a higher-level individual that controls its proliferation, differentiation, and fate.

For the cognitive sciences, this framework has a direct implication: if individuality can shift from one organizational level to another, perhaps cognition can too.

---

## 3.1 Major Transitions and the Concept of Individuality

The Maynard Smith and Szathmáry framework is precise about what makes a collection of lower-level entities into a higher-level individual: the condition is that the lower-level entities have relinquished reproductive autonomy to the higher-level collective. Cells in a metazoan body are individuals in a very limited sense: they have DNA, they perform cellular functions, they can divide under normal circumstances. But their division is controlled by signals from the organism as a whole — they proliferate when the organism needs them to, they die when the organism needs them to (via apoptosis), and cancer — unconstrained cell proliferation — is precisely the breakdown of this control. The metazoan body is an individual because it controls the reproduction of its cells; the cells are components rather than independent individuals.

Applying this criterion to the Volvox system: Volvox is a higher-level individual because the soma cells have completely relinquished reproductive autonomy (through the action of regA), placing all reproduction in the hands of the germ cells. The soma cells are, in this strict sense, components of the colonial individual rather than independent individuals themselves.

Applying this criterion to Dictyostelium: the situation is more complex. During vegetative growth, individual cells are fully independent individuals — they reproduce, compete, and behave on their own behalf. During aggregation and slug formation, they surrender some autonomy (participating in collective movement, differentiating into stalk or spore according to collective signals) but retain the capacity to abandon the collective (cheating behavior, or simply failing to aggregate in poor conditions). The Dictyostelium collective is a temporary, partial individuality — a point on a continuum between full cellular independence and full colonial integration.

## 3.2 Cognitive Implications: Where Is the Cognizer?

If individuality can shift from one organizational level to another, the question of where cognition is located becomes more interesting.

In a single-celled organism like Paramecium, the cognitive system — the collection of sensors, signal transduction pathways, and behavioral actuators that constitute the organism's ability to detect its environment and respond adaptively — is entirely contained within a single cell. The cognizer is the cell.

In a multicellular organism like a simple Volvox sphere, the situation is already more complex. Each cell has sensory capability (the eyespot for light detection). Each cell has actuator capability (flagellar beating). But the cognitively relevant output — directed colony phototaxis — is a property of the collective, not of any individual cell. In a meaningful sense, the cognizer is the colony, not any individual cell.

In a more complex animal, the situation is even more complex. Individual neurons are not the cognizers; the circuits they form are. And the brain is not, for many cognitive purposes, the cognizer; the whole organism, embedded in its environment and body, is. The cognitive system is, at each level, more than the sum of its parts.

The Maynard Smith and Szathmáry framework suggests that this is not an accidental feature of complex cognition but a general consequence of major transitions in individuality: at each transition, the properties we care about — including cognitive properties — migrate to the higher organizational level. The cell is the cognizer before multicellularity. The organism is the cognizer after. What changes between these is not just the addition of more cells; it is the reorganization of how information is stored, transmitted, and acted upon.

## 3.3 Pre-Neural Cognition and the Origins of the Nervous System

This section invites us to adopt a particular perspective on the evolution of the nervous system: not as the invention of cognition, but as the elaboration and specialization of cognitive functions that were already present in pre-neural cells.

The evidence for this perspective comes from multiple directions:

**Genomic evidence from choanoflagellates**: As we saw in Section 1, the molecular toolkit for neural signaling — including synaptic scaffolding proteins, receptor tyrosine kinases, and cell adhesion molecules — was present in the unicellular ancestor of animals before the nervous system existed.

**Cellular signaling in non-neural cells**: Non-neural cells in animal bodies — epithelial cells, immune cells, endocrine cells — perform signal detection, signal integration, and behavioral responses using molecular machinery that is evolutionarily continuous with the neural machinery. Epithelial cells express glutamate receptors and release neurotransmitters. Immune cells perform signal integration and decision-making about whether and how to attack pathogens. These are pre-neural cognitive functions implemented in non-neural tissues.

**Evolution of the neuron from a sensory-effector cell**: Current theories of nervous system evolution propose that the first neurons evolved from proto-sensory cells — cells that were already specialized for sensing environmental stimuli and coupling that sensing to behavioral responses. The neuron added the ability to transmit information rapidly over distances, but the basic sensing-integrating-responding architecture was present before (Jékely et al., 2021).

What this perspective suggests is that we should not look for the origin of cognition at the origin of the neuron. The neuron is a cognitive specialist — a cell that evolved to do sensory-motor coupling more efficiently and over greater distances than other cells can. But the cognitive work — the sensing, the integration, the behavioral response — was already happening in cells before neurons existed.

The flagellate, with its toolkit of signaling proteins, its colonial responses to environmental signals, its distributed phototaxis — represents something close to the ancestral condition: cognition at the cellular level, not yet specialized into dedicated neural circuits, but already capable of sensing, integrating, and responding.

## 3.4 Closing the Unit: What Protist Intelligence Tells Us

Having spent four chapters examining protist intelligence — in amoebae, slime molds, ciliates, and flagellates — we are now in a position to draw some broader conclusions.

**First**, the behavioral sophistication of protists is real and substantial. Gradient sensing with amplification, collective coordination through wave propagation, network optimization through physical dynamics, hierarchical behavioral repertoires, epigenetic inheritance of cellular organization, synaptic protein precursors — these are not trivial adaptations. They represent genuine complexity in information processing at the cellular level.

**Second**, the mechanisms by which protist behavior is implemented are, in many cases, recognizably related to the mechanisms of neural processing. Ion channel-based electrical signaling, calcium as a behavioral switch, positive feedback and signal amplification, excitable medium dynamics — these are shared features of single cells and neural circuits. The continuity between them is not accidental; it is evolutionary.

**Third**, the cognitive concepts we have inherited from the study of animals — sensation, decision-making, memory, learning, communication — apply to protists in limited, partial ways that require careful translation rather than wholesale import or wholesale exclusion. Protists sense, but not the way we sense. They make decisions, but not the way we decide. The appropriate response is to specify, for each claim, what mechanisms support it and what aspects of the richer concept it does and does not include.

**Fourth**, and most important for the broader themes of this book, the protist data firmly establish that intelligence — adaptive information processing in service of behavioral flexibility — is not an invention of the nervous system. It is a property of life that nervous systems elaborate, not a property that nervous systems create.
