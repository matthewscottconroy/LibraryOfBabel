# Unit V: Plant Cognition — The Other Intelligence

---

> *"Plants have been described as 'the other intelligence' — a form of problem-solving, sensing, and adapting that runs so differently from animal cognition that we have consistently failed to recognize it. A plant does not move through the world; the world moves through it. Its intelligence is not locomotion but architecture, not flight but chemistry, not memory in a hippocampus but memory in a stem."*

---

## Unit Introduction

If you want to understand how deeply our concept of intelligence is shaped by our own experience of it, try to think seriously about plant cognition. Plants do not move in the way we move. Their timescale for decision and response is, for most of their activities, orders of magnitude slower than ours. They have no nervous system, no muscles, no face that we might read for signs of experience. And yet they solve problems of extraordinary difficulty: they find water in drought, orient toward light while managing heat, detect herbivore attack and recruit defenders, communicate across distances, and integrate information from dozens of simultaneous environmental signals.

This unit examines the evidence for plant cognition and the mechanisms underlying it. It is one of the most contested areas of basal cognition research, and we engage with the controversy directly and honestly. The scientific debate between the plant neurobiology community (Baluška, Mancuso, Trewavas) and their critics (Taiz et al., 2019) is examined carefully, with attention to what the evidence actually supports and what is speculation.

## A Note on Controversy

Plant cognition is the most contested area of basal cognition research. The plant neurobiology program has been criticized by prominent plant biologists (Taiz et al., 2019) for using neurological vocabulary inappropriately and for overclaiming on the basis of evidence. Some of the most striking findings — particularly Gagliano's habituation and associative learning experiments — have faced replication failures.

This unit presents the evidence honestly, labels what is contested, names the critics and their arguments, and helps the reader form their own judgment about what the evidence supports. The goal is not to vindicate the plant neurobiology program but to understand what plants actually do — and what that means for the concept of cognition.

*Reference: Taiz, L. et al. (2019). Plants neither possess nor require consciousness. Trends in Plant Science, 24(8), 677–687.*
