# Chapter 16: Further Reading

## Annotated Bibliography

**Baluška, F., Mancuso, S., & Volkmann, D. (Eds.). (2006). *Communication in Plants: Neuronal Aspects of Plant Life*. Springer.**
The manifesto of the plant neurobiology movement — a collection of chapters from researchers who believe the neural vocabulary is appropriate for plant signaling. Essential for understanding the strongest case for plant cognition claims. Read critically: some chapters are more empirically careful than others, and the editorial framing tends to favor the most ambitious interpretations.

**Taiz, L., Alkon, D., Bhatt, D., et al. (2019). Plants neither possess nor require consciousness. *Trends in Plant Science*, 24(8), 677–687.**
The most prominent critical response to plant neurobiology, arguing that neural vocabulary inappropriately implies consciousness and cognitive experience for plants. Written by established plant biologists with deep expertise. The paper stimulated considerable response and debate — read the accompanying commentaries if available through your library.

**Trewavas, A. (2005). Green plants as intelligent organisms. *Trends in Plant Science*, 10(9), 413–419.**
Trewavas' argument for plant intelligence, which should be carefully distinguished from the Baluška-Mancuso position. Trewavas focuses on the adaptive, integrative, and information-processing properties of plant behavior without claiming that plants have neural consciousness. This nuanced position is often caricatured in the controversy.

**Fromm, J., & Lautner, S. (2007). Electrical signals and their physiological significance in plants. *Plant, Cell & Environment*, 30(3), 249–257.**
The most comprehensive and balanced review of plant electrical signaling available. Covers the biophysics of action potentials and variation potentials, the evidence for their functional roles in wound signaling, and the limitations of current understanding. Required reading for anyone wanting to understand the empirical basis of plant electrical signaling.

**Mousavi, S. A. R., Chauvin, A., Pascaud, F., Kellenberger, S., & Farmer, E. E. (2013). GLUTAMATE RECEPTOR-LIKE genes mediate leaf-to-leaf wound signalling. *Nature*, 500(7463), 422–426.**
Demonstrates that GLR3.3 and GLR3.6 — plant homologs of animal glutamate receptors — are required for the propagation of electrical wound signals between leaves in Arabidopsis. This is the strongest evidence to date for a functional role of plant glutamate receptor homologs in long-distance signaling.

**Pearce, G., Strydom, D., Johnson, S., & Ryan, C. A. (1991). A polypeptide from tomato leaves induces wound-inducible proteinase inhibitor proteins. *Science*, 253(5022), 895–898.**
The discovery of systemin — the first plant peptide hormone — and the demonstration of its role in the systemic wound response. A classic paper that established the paradigm for thinking about long-distance chemical signaling in plant defense.

**Whippo, C. W., & Hangarter, R. P. (2006). Phototropism: Bending towards enlightenment. *Plant Cell*, 18(5), 1110–1119.**
An excellent review of phototropism mechanisms, covering phototropin photoreceptors, downstream signaling, and the Cholodny-Went auxin redistribution model. Accessible and comprehensive, suitable for readers at the graduate level.

**Jonsson, H., Heisler, M. G., Shapiro, B. E., Meyerowitz, E. M., & Mjolsness, E. (2006). An auxin-driven polarized transport model for phyllotaxis. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 103(5), 1633–1638.**
A mathematical model demonstrating that phyllotactic patterning (the spiral arrangement of leaves and flowers) can emerge from the self-organizing dynamics of polar auxin transport. Essential for understanding auxin transport as a self-organizing system.

**Ryan, C. A. (1990). Protease inhibitors in plants: Genes for improving defenses against insects and pathogens. *Annual Review of Phytopathology*, 28, 425–449.**
A comprehensive review by one of the pioneers of wound response research, covering the discovery and mechanism of systemic proteinase inhibitor induction. Provides the ecological and biochemical context for the systemin story and the systemic wound response.

**Wildon, D. C., Thain, J. F., Minchin, P. E. H., Gubb, I. R., Reilly, A. J., Skipper, Y. D., ... & Bowes, G. (1992). Electrical signalling and systemic proteinase inhibitor induction in the wounded plant. *Nature*, 360(6399), 62–65.**
Demonstrates that electrical signals precede systemic proteinase inhibitor induction after wounding in tomato — one of the key papers establishing a functional role for plant electrical signals in systemic defense responses.
