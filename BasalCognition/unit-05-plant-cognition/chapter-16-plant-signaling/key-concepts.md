# Chapter 16: Key Concepts

## Glossary

**Action Potential (Plant)**
A rapid, propagating change in membrane potential in plant cells, involving calcium influx and chloride efflux (rather than the sodium/potassium fluxes of animal neurons). Plant action potentials are all-or-nothing events that propagate through phloem sieve tube cells at speeds of approximately 1–40 cm/s. They are triggered by various stimuli including wounding, touch, cold, and electrical stimulation, and they trigger physiological responses in tissues they pass through.

*Why it matters*: Plant action potentials demonstrate that electrical signal propagation is not confined to nervous systems — it is a more general cellular capability that plants implement in a non-neural vascular context. Understanding their mechanism and function is essential for evaluating claims about plant "neural-like" signaling.

---

**Variation Potential**
A slow, graded electrical change in plant tissues, distinct from action potentials, driven by hydraulic pressure changes in the xylem. When vascular tissue is wounded, the sudden release of xylem tension propagates as a pressure wave through the xylem, activating mechanosensitive ion channels along the xylem walls and producing a graded depolarization that travels at the speed of the pressure wave. Variation potentials are faster than phloem action potentials and less electrically stereotyped.

*Why it matters*: Variation potentials represent a different mode of long-distance signaling than electrical action potentials — one based on hydraulic rather than electrochemical propagation. Their mechanism illustrates how the same mechanical structure (the xylem) serves both water transport and signal transmission.

---

**Systemin**
An 18-amino acid peptide hormone produced in response to wounding in Solanaceous plants (tomatoes, potatoes, and relatives). Systemin is produced at wound sites and transported through the phloem to distant tissues, where it activates the jasmonate signaling cascade. The discovery of systemin by Pearce et al. in 1991 established that plants use peptide hormones for long-distance signaling, analogous (though mechanistically distinct) to animal peptide hormones.

*Why it matters*: Systemin is the key component of the systemic wound response — the coordinated defensive response of the whole plant to localized herbivory. Its discovery established the paradigm for understanding plant long-distance defensive signaling.

---

**Jasmonate (JA / JA-Ile)**
Lipid-derived plant hormones that mediate wound and stress responses. Jasmonic acid (JA) and its amino acid conjugate jasmonoyl-isoleucine (JA-Ile) are produced from membrane lipids at sites of damage or stress and activate a transcription factor cascade (COI1/JAZ module) that upregulates defense gene expression. Jasmonate signaling is the primary molecular pathway of the systemic wound response and is also required for reproductive development and responses to various abiotic stresses.

*Why it matters*: Jasmonate is the primary hormonal mediator of plant defense against herbivory — one of the most important adaptive responses in plant biology. Understanding its signaling cascade is essential for understanding how plants coordinate systemic defensive responses.

---

**Glutamate Receptor-Like (GLR)**
Plant proteins homologous to the ionotropic glutamate receptors of animal nervous systems. Plant GLRs are encoded by a family of ~20 genes in Arabidopsis and function as ligand-gated calcium channels activated by glutamate and other amino acids. They are required for the propagation of electrical wound signals between leaves (Mousavi et al., 2013) and have roles in pathogen defense and other stress responses. The evolutionary relationship between plant GLRs and animal glutamate receptors is genuine, but the extent of functional homology is contested.

*Why it matters*: Plant GLRs are the most concrete link between plant electrical signaling and the molecular machinery of animal synaptic transmission. Their characterization has provided the strongest molecular evidence for the "plant neurobiology" position, while their very different cellular context makes direct functional analogy uncertain.

---

**Cholodny-Went Theory**
The theory, formulated independently by Nikolai Cholodny and Frits Went in the 1920s, that tropisms in plants are produced by the lateral redistribution of auxin across stimulated organs, causing differential elongation that produces the growth curvature. The illuminated side of a phototropically stimulated shoot has less auxin (and therefore slower elongation) than the shaded side, producing curvature toward the light. The theory has been substantially confirmed at the molecular level.

*Why it matters*: The Cholodny-Went theory is one of the classic examples of successful model-based reasoning in plant biology — a mechanistic proposal made before the molecular tools to test it existed, subsequently confirmed by molecular genetics. It remains the organizing framework for understanding phototropism and gravitropism.

---

**Phytohormone Cross-Talk**
The mutual regulation of different plant hormone pathways by each other, such that the response at any given tissue location reflects the integrated output of multiple simultaneous signals. The best-characterized example is the antagonistic relationship between jasmonate (JA) and salicylate (SA) pathways: conditions that activate JA tend to suppress SA and vice versa. Cross-talk is mediated by shared transcription factors, shared metabolites, and direct regulation of pathway genes by components of other pathways.

*Why it matters*: Phytohormone cross-talk is the mechanism by which the plant integrates information about multiple simultaneous stresses and resolves conflicts between competing adaptive responses. It is, in a functional sense, the plant's system for prioritizing and integrating information — a role performed in animals by higher brain centers.

---

**Phototropin**
A plant blue-light photoreceptor (encoded by the PHOT1 and PHOT2 genes in Arabidopsis) that mediates phototropism, stomatal opening, and chloroplast movement. Phototropins contain a flavoprotein chromophore that undergoes conformational change upon blue light absorption, activating a kinase domain. Activated phototropin initiates the signaling cascade that redistributes auxin laterally across the illuminated organ, producing the differential elongation that causes phototropic curvature.

*Why it matters*: Phototropin is the sensory receptor of phototropism — the molecular "eye" that allows plants to detect light direction. Its characterization completed the molecular account of phototropism and provided the entry point for understanding all subsequent steps in the Cholodny-Went mechanism.

---

**Amyloplast**
A starch-filled plastid that functions as the gravity sensor in plant statocytes. Amyloplasts are denser than the surrounding cytoplasm and sediment toward the lowest point of the cell when the plant is displaced from vertical. Their sedimentation is detected by mechanosensitive systems in the statocyte (possibly including actin filaments and mechanosensitive ion channels), triggering the lateral auxin redistribution that produces the gravitropic response.

*Why it matters*: The amyloplast is the plant's equivalent of the otolith — a dense structure that sediments in response to gravity, providing the cell with directional gravitational information. Understanding how amyloplast sedimentation is detected and transduced into a hormonal signal is a key problem in plant gravitropism research.
