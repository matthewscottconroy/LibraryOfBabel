# Chapter 16: Key Researchers

## Anthony Trewavas
**Institution**: University of Edinburgh, Institute of Molecular Plant Science  
**Key Contribution**: Trewavas has been the most philosophically sophisticated advocate for the view that plants are "intelligent organisms" in a functional sense — that their adaptive, integrative responses to environmental information constitute a form of intelligence, even without neurons or consciousness. His 2005 *Trends in Plant Science* paper is the clearest articulation of this position, and he has subsequently elaborated it in a book (*Plant Behaviour and Intelligence*, Oxford University Press, 2014). Trewavas carefully distinguishes his position from claims about plant consciousness — he is arguing for adaptive information processing, not sentience. His position is sometimes wrongly grouped with the more extreme claims of some plant neurobiology advocates.

**Key Works**:
- Trewavas, A. (2005). Green plants as intelligent organisms. *Trends in Plant Science*, 10(9), 413–419.
- Trewavas, A. (2014). *Plant Behaviour and Intelligence*. Oxford University Press.

## František Baluška
**Institution**: University of Bonn, Institute of Cellular and Molecular Botany  
**Key Contribution**: Baluška is one of the founding figures of the Society for Plant Neurobiology and a proponent of the view that plants have a nervous system-like organization, with the root apex functioning as a command center analogous to a brain. His work on auxin transport, endocytosis in plant cells, and the actin cytoskeleton has contributed to understanding plant cell biology, while his more speculative proposals about plant neurobiology have been controversial. He has co-edited and co-authored numerous volumes on plant signaling and behavior.

**Key Works**:
- Baluška, F., Mancuso, S., & Volkmann, D. (Eds.). (2006). *Communication in Plants: Neuronal Aspects of Plant Life*. Springer.
- Baluška, F., Mancuso, S., Volkmann, D., & Barlow, P. W. (2004). Root apices as plant command centres. *Biologia*, 59(Suppl 13), 7–19.

## Stefano Mancuso
**Institution**: University of Florence, LINV (International Laboratory of Plant Neurobiology)  
**Key Contribution**: Mancuso is one of the most publicly prominent advocates of plant intelligence, having co-authored a popular book on plant intelligence (*Brilliant Green*, 2015, Island Press) that has reached wide audiences. His research has covered aspects of root electrophysiology, plant communication, and plant responses to gravity and light. He has been an effective communicator of plant biology to non-scientific audiences, though his public-facing claims sometimes outrun the evidence. His scientific work on root electrophysiology is more conservative than his popular writing suggests.

**Key Works**:
- Mancuso, S., & Viola, A. (2015). *Brilliant Green: The Surprising History and Science of Plant Intelligence*. Island Press.

## Clarence A. Ryan (Historical)
**Institution**: Washington State University  
**Key Contribution**: Ryan spent his career studying the biochemistry and molecular biology of wound-induced defense responses in tomato and other solanaceous plants. He discovered systemin — the first plant peptide hormone — and elucidated much of the jasmonate signaling pathway that mediates the systemic wound response. His work established the paradigm for understanding how plants coordinate long-distance defensive responses to herbivory, providing the experimental foundation for much subsequent plant signaling research.

**Key Papers**:
- Pearce, G., Strydom, D., Johnson, S., & Ryan, C. A. (1991). A polypeptide from tomato leaves induces wound-inducible proteinase inhibitor proteins. *Science*, 253(5022), 895–898.
- Ryan, C. A., & Pearce, G. (2003). Systemins: A functionally defined family of peptide signals that regulate defensive genes in Solanaceae species. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 100(Suppl 2), 14577–14580.

## Edward Farmer
**Institution**: University of Lausanne, Department of Plant Molecular Biology  
**Key Contribution**: Farmer has made major contributions to understanding the jasmonate signaling pathway and the electrical signals that propagate wound responses in plants. His group's discovery that plant glutamate receptor homologs (GLR3.3, GLR3.6) mediate electrical wound signaling in Arabidopsis (Mousavi et al., 2013) is one of the most important recent results in plant signaling, providing the molecular basis for plant wound-related electrical signals and connecting plant electrophysiology to the glutamate receptor literature.

**Key Papers**:
- Mousavi, S. A. R., Chauvin, A., Pascaud, F., Kellenberger, S., & Farmer, E. E. (2013). GLUTAMATE RECEPTOR-LIKE genes mediate leaf-to-leaf wound signalling. *Nature*, 500(7463), 422–426.
