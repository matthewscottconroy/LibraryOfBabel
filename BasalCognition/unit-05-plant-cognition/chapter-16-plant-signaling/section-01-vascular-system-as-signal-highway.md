# Section 1: The Vascular System as Signal Highway

## Introduction

Before we can evaluate whether plants have something like a nervous system, we need to understand what they actually have. The plant body is not structurally analogous to the animal body, and importing animal concepts without adjustment produces confusion rather than insight. But starting from what plants actually do — the actual molecular and biophysical events that occur when a plant is wounded, stressed, or stimulated — we find a system of remarkable sophistication.

The plant vascular system has two major components: the xylem and the phloem. Their primary biological functions are the transport of water (xylem, upward) and the transport of photosynthate — sugars and other organic compounds (phloem, bidirectionally). These are nutritional transport functions. But both systems also serve as channels for long-distance signaling, carrying electrical signals, hormones, proteins, RNAs, and other informational molecules between distant parts of the plant.

---

## 1.1 Electrical Signals in Plants

Plants produce and propagate electrical signals. This is a fact well-established by the middle of the twentieth century and has been confirmed and elaborated by decades of subsequent research. What kinds of electrical signals, how fast, and what they accomplish are more nuanced questions.

**Action potentials**: Plants produce action potentials — rapid, all-or-nothing changes in membrane potential that propagate along cells and between cells. The biophysical mechanisms are broadly similar to animal action potentials but differ in important details. In animal neurons, action potentials are driven by sodium and potassium channels. In plants, the primary ions involved are calcium (inward) and chloride (outward), with potassium channels contributing to repolarization (Fromm & Lautner, 2007). The result is a depolarization-repolarization cycle similar in shape to neural action potentials.

Plant action potentials propagate through the phloem, which contains living sieve tube cells connected end-to-end by large pores (sieve plates). Electrical coupling between adjacent sieve tube cells allows action potentials to propagate long distances through the phloem relatively rapidly — at speeds of approximately 1–40 cm per second, depending on the species and conditions (Fromm & Lautner, 2007). These speeds are far slower than propagation in myelinated mammalian neurons (up to 70 m/s) but comparable to some invertebrate neural fibers.

**Variation potentials**: In addition to action potentials, plants produce what are called variation potentials (also called slow wave potentials) — slower, graded electrical changes that differ in amplitude along the length of the plant. Variation potentials are driven by a different mechanism than action potentials: they reflect pressure changes in the xylem — specifically, the sudden release of hydraulic pressure when vascular tissue is damaged — that activate ion channels along the xylem wall through mechanotransduction (Malone, 1996). Because they reflect a xylem pressure wave, variation potentials travel at the speed of pressure propagation in the vascular column — faster than action potentials, and essentially instantaneous for practical purposes.

Both types of electrical signals can trigger downstream responses in the tissues they pass through: activation of defense gene expression, changes in stomatal aperture, alterations in photosynthetic rate. The electrical signal is not just a passenger in the vascular system; it is a functional information carrier that triggers changes in cell behavior wherever it passes.

## 1.2 Systemin and the Systemic Wound Response

The systemic wound response — the subject of the caterpillar scene in the chapter introduction — involves both electrical signals and chemical signals, and their interplay is one of the most studied topics in plant signal biology.

Systemin, discovered in 1991, is an 18-amino acid peptide hormone produced in response to wounding (Pearce et al., 1991). It is the first plant peptide hormone to be discovered and remains one of the best-studied. When a tomato leaf is wounded by an insect or mechanically, systemin is released from the wound site into the phloem and transported to distal tissues. In those tissues, systemin binds to a receptor (SR160, a leucine-rich repeat receptor kinase) and activates a signal transduction cascade that ultimately leads to the synthesis of jasmonic acid (JA) and its amino acid conjugate jasmonoyl-isoleucine (JA-Ile) — the active signaling molecules of the jasmonate pathway (Ryan & Pearce, 2003).

JA and JA-Ile activate a transcription factor cascade (the COI1/JAZ signaling module) that upregulates genes encoding proteinase inhibitors, polyphenol oxidases, and other defensive compounds. These defensive proteins accumulate in cells throughout the plant, making the tissue less digestible and less nutritious for herbivores.

The spatial range of this response is remarkable: in well-studied experiments, wounding a single leaf can induce significant proteinase inhibitor accumulation in leaves throughout the entire plant — leaves that have had no direct contact with the wound signal through the xylem or phloem, and that are separated from the wound site by stem segments (Wildon et al., 1992). This systemic response requires long-distance signaling, and both electrical and chemical signals contribute.

The relative contributions of electrical signals (fast, global) and chemical signals (slower, potentially more specific) to the systemic wound response is still a topic of active research. Current evidence suggests that electrical signals may provide a rapid, coarse "alert" signal that initiates responses in distal tissues, while chemical signals provide more specific and sustained activation of particular defensive programs.

## 1.3 Speed Comparisons: Plant Signals vs. Neural Signals

It is worth being precise about the speed of signaling in plants compared to neural signaling, because speed comparisons are often invoked to dismiss plant electrical signals as irrelevant to any neural-like function.

The comparison is illuminating but should not be taken as the sole measure of functional significance:

- Unmyelinated C fibers in mammals (the "slow" pain fibers): 0.5–2 m/s
- Myelinated A fibers in mammals: 5–70 m/s
- Plant phloem action potentials: 0.01–0.4 m/s
- Plant variation potentials (xylem pressure waves): 0.1–10 m/s or more

Plant electrical signals are substantially slower than myelinated animal nerve fibers, but they are in the same order of magnitude as some unmyelinated fibers. More importantly, the appropriate comparison is not "how fast are plant signals compared to mammalian nociception?" but rather "are plant signals fast enough to serve the adaptive function they are proposed to serve?"

For the wound response, the relevant timescale is the duration of an insect feeding event — typically minutes to hours for a browsing caterpillar. A signal that travels at 1 cm/s will traverse a 1-meter plant in under 2 minutes. Proteinase inhibitor upregulation that reaches distant leaves within 30–60 minutes of the initial wounding is functionally relevant, regardless of whether the propagation speed is slower than a mammalian A fiber.

The speed comparison reveals that plant signaling is slower than fast neural signaling, but this is unsurprising given that plants and animals face different ecological pressures and have different life histories. The appropriate question is whether the signals are fast enough to be adaptive — and for many of the functions they serve, they are.

## 1.4 The Vasculature as Integration System

The plant vascular system is not simply a passive conduit for signals. It is itself a site of signal integration, where inputs from multiple sources are combined and modulated before being transmitted to distant tissues.

The phloem contents at any given location in the plant reflect the integrated output of all source tissues proximal to that location — leaves producing sucrose, wounded tissues producing defense signals, developing organs sending hormones. As phloem sap flows from sources to sinks, it carries a mixture of signals whose composition reflects the plant's overall physiological state.

This integration is not trivial. The phloem sap contains not only small molecules (sugars, hormones, amino acids) but also macromolecules — proteins and small RNAs that can traverse long distances and trigger responses in tissues far from their origin (Turgeon & Wolf, 2009). The discovery that functional RNAs (including small regulatory RNAs and mobile mRNAs) travel through the phloem has added a new dimension to our understanding of plant long-distance signaling: the plant can send not just "I have been wounded" signals but also specific molecular instructions that alter gene expression patterns in distant tissues.

Whether this constitutes something like "information transmission" in a cognitively meaningful sense is a question that different researchers answer differently. What is clear is that the vascular system carries a rich and diverse set of signals, that these signals are integrated from multiple tissue sources, and that they coordinate responses across the whole plant. These are the functional properties we would expect of a nervous system's signal transmission component — implemented in a radically different physical substrate.
