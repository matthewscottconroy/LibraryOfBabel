# Section 3: The Plant Neurobiology Controversy — Language, Science, and the Battle Over Vocabulary

## Introduction

Few debates in contemporary biology have generated as much heat relative to their actual empirical content as the controversy over "plant neurobiology." The controversy is partly scientific (what do plants actually do?) and partly semantic (what language should we use to describe it?). But it is also genuinely philosophical: the choice of vocabulary is not simply a matter of convenience; it shapes what questions researchers ask, what hypotheses they consider, and what the public understands about plant biology.

Understanding both sides of this controversy — fairly, without caricature — is essential for any serious student of basal cognition. Let us try.

---

## 3.1 The Plant Neurobiology Position

The Society for Plant Neurobiology was founded in 2005 and subsequently renamed the Society for Plant Signaling and Behavior (the name change itself reflects the controversy). Its intellectual leaders include Stefano Mancuso (University of Florence), František Baluška (University of Bonn), and Anthony Trewavas (University of Edinburgh). Despite sharing a general position that plant behavior is more sophisticated than commonly acknowledged, these researchers do not form a monolithic group — Trewavas, in particular, has developed his own distinctive account of plant intelligence that differs in important ways from the Baluška-Mancuso program.

The core empirical claims of the plant neurobiology position are:

**Plants produce action potentials** that transmit information about environmental stimuli (wounding, touch, light changes) through the vascular system. This is well-established.

**Plants have glutamate receptor homologs (GLRs)**: Plants possess genes encoding proteins homologous to the ionotropic glutamate receptors that mediate fast synaptic transmission in animal nervous systems. In plants, these GLRs have been shown to function as calcium channels activated by glutamate and other amino acids, and to be involved in wound signaling and pathogen defense (Mousavi et al., 2013). Baluška and colleagues have proposed that these GLRs may function in plant vascular "synaptic-like" signaling. This is an active research area with ongoing debate about the extent to which the plant GLRs actually function analogously to animal glutamate receptors.

**The root apex as an information-processing center**: Baluška's group has proposed that the root apex — specifically the root apical meristem — functions as a plant equivalent of a brain, integrating sensory inputs from multiple modalities and coordinating growth responses (Baluška et al., 2004). This proposal is the most contested aspect of the plant neurobiology program and is rejected by many plant biologists as unsupported by evidence.

**Auxin as a plant "neurotransmitter"**: Some plant neurobiologists have proposed that auxin functions analogously to a neurotransmitter in plants — released by cells, transported directionally, and binding to receptor proteins that alter cell behavior. The PIN-mediated directional transport of auxin does have structural similarities to neurotransmitter uptake and release mechanisms, though the detailed mechanisms and timescales are very different.

## 3.2 The Critics' Position

The most prominent and sustained scientific critique of the plant neurobiology program has come from Lincoln Taiz (University of California, Santa Cruz) and a group of colleagues who published a pointed commentary in *Trends in Plant Science* in 2019 (Taiz et al., 2019).

Taiz et al. make several distinct arguments:

**The neural vocabulary is misleading**: When plant neurobiologists use terms like "memory," "learning," "neurotransmitter," and "synapse" for plant phenomena, they imply that the underlying mechanisms are similar to the neural mechanisms these terms were developed to describe. In most cases, the mechanisms are quite different — plants have no synapses, no dedicated signal-transmitting cells, no neuroplasticity. Using neural vocabulary creates a false impression of mechanistic similarity.

**The evidence for plant learning and memory is weak**: Taiz et al. are specifically critical of claims that plants exhibit associative learning or long-term memory in any sense comparable to animal learning. They argue that the evidence for such claims (including the Gagliano habituation and associative learning papers discussed in Chapter 17) is methodologically insufficient and has not been robustly replicated.

**The root apex is not a brain**: The proposal that the root apical meristem functions as a plant brain is unsupported. The root apex is a growth zone — the site of active cell division and elongation — not an information processing center distinct from the rest of the plant. While it does integrate various sensory inputs (gravity, light, soil chemistry, touch), this integration is not mechanistically different from integration happening elsewhere in the plant, and there is no evidence for the kind of centralized information processing that the "brain" metaphor implies.

**The analogy misleads public and scientific understanding**: Beyond the specific scientific claims, Taiz et al. are concerned that the plant neurobiology program misleads both the scientific community and the public into thinking that plants have cognitive experiences — that they feel, learn, and remember in ways comparable to animals. They argue this is harmful because it distorts public understanding of biology and because it may generate false expectations that corrupt research programs.

## 3.3 Where the Evidence Stands

It is important to separate the semantic/philosophical dispute from the empirical one, because conflating them produces confusion.

**Well-established empirical facts** (agreed upon by both sides):
- Plants produce electrical action potentials and variation potentials
- These signals propagate through the vascular system and trigger physiological responses in distant tissues
- Plants have glutamate receptor homologs (GLRs) that function as ligand-gated ion channels
- Plant GLRs are involved in wound signaling (Mousavi et al., 2013)
- Plants respond to a wide variety of environmental stimuli and coordinate whole-plant responses
- Auxin transport is directional and involves PIN carrier proteins

**Contested empirical claims**:
- Whether plant GLRs function in a synaptic-like manner (current evidence is suggestive but not definitive)
- Whether plant action potentials are functionally analogous to neural action potentials (functional similarity in information transmission is partial; mechanistic similarity is limited)
- Whether the root apex is a special information processing center vs. simply a zone of particular activity (most evidence supports the latter)

**Contested interpretive claims** (these are more philosophical than empirical):
- Whether the neural vocabulary is appropriate for plant signaling phenomena
- Whether plants "learn," "remember," or "make decisions" in any meaningful sense
- What the similarities between plant signaling and neural signaling tell us about the evolution of cognition

---

## 3.4 Finding the Balance

The intellectually honest position, in our judgment, is something like the following:

Plants are sophisticated environmental sensors and signal integrators. Their signaling systems — electrical, hormonal, and mechanical — coordinate whole-plant responses to environmental inputs in ways that are genuinely adaptive and genuinely complex. The mechanisms involved have some structural similarities to neural signaling mechanisms (ion channels, action potentials, glutamate receptors) that reflect shared evolutionary history and convergent functional solutions. These structural similarities are scientifically interesting and worth investigating.

At the same time, the differences between plant signaling and neural signaling are real and important. There are no dedicated signal-transmitting cells, no synapses, no neuroplasticity in any established sense. The timescales of plant signal propagation are much slower than fast neural conduction. The plant's signaling architecture is distributed throughout the vascular system rather than organized into dedicated circuits.

Whether we call this a "nervous system" depends on whether we define "nervous system" by its function (rapid, long-distance integration of sensory inputs and coordination of behavioral outputs) or by its mechanism (dedicated neurons, synapses, action potentials propagating in specific circuits). If function, plants have something in the neighborhood. If mechanism, they clearly do not.

Trewavas (2005) has argued persuasively that plant cognition claims should be understood as being about the functional properties of the plant signaling system — its adaptive, integrative, responsive character — rather than about the presence of neurons or consciousness. By this standard, some form of the cognition vocabulary is defensible. Taiz and colleagues argue that even the functional vocabulary misleads when it imports the full suite of cognitive implications. This is a genuine philosophical disagreement that will not be settled by more empirical data about ion channels.

The appropriate response for a graduate student in this field is not to take sides but to understand the empirical content precisely, hold the interpretive vocabulary lightly, and be explicit about the distinction between them whenever making claims about plant cognition.
