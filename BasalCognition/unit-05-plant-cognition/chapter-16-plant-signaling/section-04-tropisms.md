# Section 4: Tropisms — Sensorimotor Integration Without Muscles

## Introduction

A plant in a window turns toward the light. A root grows downward in a dark soil column. A pea vine's tendril wraps around a wire it encounters. These tropisms — growth movements directed by environmental stimuli — are perhaps the most familiar forms of plant behavior, familiar enough to be taught in elementary school. But their familiarity should not obscure their biological sophistication.

Tropisms represent the integrated output of sensory systems, signal transduction pathways, and growth responses coordinated across the whole plant. They are not passive responses to physical forces (though some growth curvatures have a passive component). They are active processes, driven by differential hormone distribution, that require information about the direction of environmental stimuli to be transduced into spatial patterns of growth. In a real sense, they are sensorimotor integration — the conversion of sensory information about the environment into directed movement — implemented in a body that moves by growing rather than by contraction.

## 4.1 Phototropism: Turning Toward the Light

Phototropism — growth toward (or occasionally away from) light — is driven by blue-light-sensing photoreceptors called phototropins. When a shoot tip is illuminated from one side, phototropins on the illuminated side are activated and trigger a signaling cascade that results in the lateral redistribution of auxin: auxin accumulates on the shaded side of the shoot (Whippo & Hangarter, 2006).

This auxin asymmetry produces differential growth: cells on the auxin-rich shaded side elongate more than cells on the auxin-poor illuminated side. The shoot curves toward the light because it grows more on the side facing away from it. The Cholodny-Went theory, formulated independently by Nikolai Cholodny and Frits Went in the 1920s, correctly predicted this mechanism before the molecular details were known — a triumph of model-based reasoning in plant biology.

The mechanics of phototropism have been worked out in considerable detail. The photoreceptor, phototropin (phot1 and phot2 in Arabidopsis), is a flavoprotein that undergoes conformational change upon blue light absorption. This conformational change activates a signaling cascade involving the kinase NPH3 and the transcription factor ARF7/19, ultimately driving the lateral PIN2-dependent transport of auxin toward the shaded side (Whippo & Hangarter, 2006).

What makes phototropism interesting from a cognitive perspective is that the plant must detect not just the presence of light but its direction. This directional detection is achieved through the gradient of phototropin activation across the stem — more activation on the bright side, less on the shaded side. The stem is essentially a detector of light direction, using differential receptor activation across its cross-section to encode a spatial vector. This is, in functional terms, a form of sensing that encodes directional information — a primitive analog of the directional sensing mechanisms we examined in amoeboid chemotaxis.

## 4.2 Gravitropism: Sensing Down

Gravitropism — growth in response to gravity — is mediated by a dedicated sensory system that is among the most elegant in plant biology. The gravitational sensor is the amyloplast: a heavy, starch-filled plastid that sediments to the lowest point of a specialized cell type called a statocyte (in the root cap and the shoot endodermis).

When a root is displaced from its vertical orientation, amyloplasts shift their position within the statocytes — moving toward the new "bottom" of the cell. This movement is detected by mechanosensitive systems (possibly involving the sedimentation of amyloplasts onto actin filaments, or the activation of stretch-sensitive channels), triggering a signaling cascade that results in lateral auxin redistribution. On the new lower side of the root, auxin accumulates; this inhibits elongation of the lower cells relative to the upper cells, causing the root to curve downward and re-establish the gravitropic set point (Morita, 2010).

The gravitropic response integrates information about the plant's orientation relative to gravity with information from other sensory systems. In roots, auxin inhibits elongation at relatively low concentrations; thus, the accumulation of auxin on the lower side of a displaced root slows elongation there, allowing the upper side to elongate more rapidly and produce downward curvature. In shoots, the opposite is true: auxin promotes elongation, so accumulation on the lower side of a displaced shoot causes upward curvature.

The gravitropic response also involves a "memory" component: the extent of displacement from the gravitropic set point is integrated over time, and the response magnitude reflects this integral rather than simply the instantaneous displacement. This integration means that brief perturbations of plant orientation produce small responses, while prolonged displacement produces large ones — a form of temporal summation that is functionally analogous to the temporal integration of vestibular signals in animal balance systems.

## 4.3 Thigmotropism and Mechanosensing

Thigmotropism — directed growth in response to touch — is exemplified by the tendril-coiling of climbing plants and the twining of morning glory vines around supports. It is, among the tropisms, perhaps the most "behavioral" in the everyday sense: the tendril of a pea vine actively explores its environment with periodic rotational movements (circumnutations), detects a suitable support by touch, and wraps around it.

The detection of touch involves mechanosensitive ion channels in the epidermal cells of the tendril — channels that open when the cell membrane is deformed by contact with an object. Calcium influx through these channels triggers a signaling cascade that modifies auxin distribution, causing differential growth. Cells on the contact side of the tendril elongate more slowly than cells on the opposite side, producing coiling.

Thigmotropism illustrates a general principle of plant mechanosensing: the touch response is not simply "grow away from the contact" (which would be negative thigmotropism) or "grow toward the contact" (positive thigmotropism) in all cases. The response depends on the type of plant, the tissue, the developmental stage, and the context. Morning glory vines wrap around supports; cactus spines are thigmomorphogenetically reinforced by touch; general mechanical stimulation (including wind) triggers systemic thigmomorphogenesis — shorter, stockier stem growth that is mechanically more resistant. The plant integrates touch information and produces context-appropriate responses.

## 4.4 Hydrotropism: Sensing Water

The ability of roots to grow toward water sources — hydrotropism — is the least-studied of the major tropisms but arguably the most ecologically important. Roots navigating a heterogeneous soil environment need to find water-rich zones, and they can do so despite the fact that water cannot be "smelled" at a distance by chemical receptors (unlike bacterial chemotaxis, which involves dissolved chemical attractants).

Hydrotropism appears to be mediated by osmotic gradients detected by the root cap (Takano et al., 2001). The mechanism is distinct from gravitropism and requires the root cap for full hydrotropism — responses are reduced in root-cap-deficient mutants. The molecular basis of the water gradient sensor is not yet fully understood, but it may involve aquaporin proteins (water channels) whose conductance changes with local osmotic conditions, or mechanosensitive channels that respond to the differential swelling of cells in different water potentials.

The directional sensitivity of hydrotropism poses an interesting biophysical puzzle: for a root to grow toward higher moisture, it must detect not just the presence of water but a gradient — a differential in moisture level across its cross-section. For a root tip of perhaps 0.5 mm diameter, the gradient across the tip in a realistic soil moisture field could be extremely shallow. How the root achieves directional sensitivity at this resolution is not yet fully understood and represents an active research frontier.

## 4.5 Tropisms as Sensorimotor Integration

Stepping back from the details of individual tropisms, the general picture is of a plant whose body position and architecture are continuously adjusted in response to multiple environmental stimuli, through a system that integrates sensory information and coordinates growth responses across large distances.

Each tropism involves: (1) a sensory step (detecting the relevant stimulus — light direction, gravity, touch, water gradient); (2) a signal transduction step (converting the sensory input into an internal signal, typically involving calcium and hormone redistribution); (3) a response step (differential growth driven by asymmetric hormone distribution).

These three steps are exactly the components of a sensorimotor loop in an animal nervous system: sensor, integrator, effector. The plant implements this loop using a completely different physical architecture — distributed hormone transport rather than neural circuits — but the functional logic is the same.

The multiple tropisms compete and interact. A root displaced from vertical will respond both gravitropically (to grow downward) and hydrotropically (to grow toward water) simultaneously, and the actual trajectory will reflect the balance of these competing influences. Experiments with rotating stages (which eliminate the effective gravitational signal) have been used to isolate the hydrotropism response — showing that roots actively navigate toward moisture even when gravity is not a confounding cue (Takano et al., 2001).

This competition and integration of tropistic responses is a form of sensory information fusion — combining inputs from multiple modalities into a unified behavioral output. In neural systems, this is accomplished by circuits that weight and sum signals from multiple sensor types. In plants, it is accomplished by the spatial competition of hormone gradients whose sources reflect different sensory inputs. Different mechanism; same functional logic.
