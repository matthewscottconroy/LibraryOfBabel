# Chapter 17: Learning and Memory in Plants

## Opening Scene

A Mimosa pudica plant is suspended above a surface, and a small weight attached to a string drops past it — triggering the rapid folding of its leaflets, the defensive response that makes Mimosa famous. The dropped weight is retrieved and dropped again. And again. And again. After approximately 60 drops, the plant no longer folds its leaves in response to the falling weight. It has, the researchers argue, "habituated" — learned that this particular disturbance is not harmful and stopped wasting energy on an unnecessary response.

If this experiment were performed with a mouse — if a mouse stopped responding to a repeated harmless stimulus after many exposures — we would call it habituation: one of the simplest and most fundamental forms of learning. Habituation is present across the entire animal kingdom, from roundworms to humans. It is so basic that it has been called the gateway to more complex forms of learning.

Can a plant, without a nervous system, habituate?

This is the question posed by Monica Gagliano and colleagues in their 2014 Oecologia paper — and the question has not been settled. The experiments are real. The results are striking. The interpretation is contested. The replication history is troubled. What follows is an honest account of all of this.

## The Central Question

Do plants modify their behavior based on experience — and if so, by what mechanisms?

The question has three layers that must be kept distinct:

**Layer 1: Do plants change over time in response to experience?** Yes, obviously — all living organisms are changed by their histories. This is not interesting without more specification.

**Layer 2: Do plants exhibit behavioral changes that satisfy operational criteria for specific forms of learning?** This is the empirically contested question. The criteria for habituation, associative learning, and related forms of behavioral plasticity are well-defined in the learning literature, and whether plant behavior satisfies those criteria is a testable empirical question.

**Layer 3: Do plants learn in a mechanistically analogous way to animals?** This is a deeper question that may or may not be tractable without new molecular tools. It requires identifying the cellular mechanisms of whatever behavioral plasticity is documented and asking whether those mechanisms share relevant properties with neural learning mechanisms.

This chapter addresses all three layers — with different degrees of confidence at each.

## What You Will Learn

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:

**Describe the Gagliano et al. (2014) Mimosa habituation experiment** — its design, results, and claims — and evaluate the methodological objections and replication failures that have followed.

**Distinguish between habituation, sensory adaptation, and fatigue** as explanations for reduced responding to repeated stimuli, and understand why this distinction is crucial for evaluating plant learning claims.

**Describe the molecular basis of vernalization** — the epigenetic mechanism by which plants remember the experience of winter — as an example of robust, well-established plant memory.

**Evaluate the Gagliano et al. (2016) associative learning claims** with appropriate critical care, understanding both the experimental approach and the substantive reasons for skepticism.

**Explain systemic acquired resistance** as a well-established form of plant immune memory, and understand its molecular basis in chromatin modification.
