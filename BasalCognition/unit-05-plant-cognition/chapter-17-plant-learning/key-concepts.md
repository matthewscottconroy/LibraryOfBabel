# Chapter 17: Key Concepts

## Glossary

**Habituation**
One of the simplest forms of learning: a decrease in behavioral response to a stimulus that is repeated without consequence. As defined by Thompson and Spencer (1966), genuine habituation requires: (1) progressive response decrement with repeated stimulation; (2) spontaneous recovery after rest; (3) stimulus specificity (habituation is specific to the stimulus, not a general reduction in responding); (4) dishabituation (a novel stimulus can restore the habituated response). Habituation is distinguished from sensory adaptation (peripheral receptor desensitization) and fatigue (effector resource depletion) by the specificity and rest-recovery properties.

*Why it matters*: Habituation is the operational criterion by which plant behavior researchers (and critics) evaluate whether Mimosa's reduced folding response constitutes genuine learning. Getting the definition right is essential for evaluating the claims.

---

**Vernalization**
The requirement of many flowering plants for prolonged cold exposure before they can flower, and the process by which that cold experience is remembered as a stable epigenetic state. In Arabidopsis, vernalization is implemented through progressive chromatin silencing of the FLC gene during cold — specifically through H3K27me3 modification catalyzed by Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2) — which is stably maintained through subsequent cell divisions, keeping FLC silenced and allowing flowering to proceed when conditions warm.

*Why it matters*: Vernalization is the clearest, best-characterized example of plant memory — and possibly of epigenetic memory in any organism. Its mechanism demonstrates that long-term, heritable, adaptive memory can be implemented in chromatin modifications without any involvement of a nervous system.

---

**FLC (FLOWERING LOCUS C)**
A gene encoding a MADS-box transcription factor that represses flowering in Arabidopsis when expressed. FLC expression is high in non-vernalized plants, preventing premature flowering. During vernalization, FLC is progressively silenced through chromatin modification. The silencing is stable — FLC remains repressed through subsequent cell divisions — allowing the plant to flower when temperatures warm in spring. FLC is the central molecular mediator of the vernalization response.

*Why it matters*: FLC is the "memory molecule" of vernalization. Understanding its regulation — the noncoding RNAs, the PRC2 complex, the H3K27me3 modification — is equivalent to understanding the molecular mechanism of vernalization memory.

---

**Epigenetic Memory**
Memory encoded in chemical modifications to chromatin (histone modifications, DNA methylation) rather than in DNA sequence changes. Epigenetic memories can be: (a) stable through cell division (as in vernalization); (b) inherited transgenerationally (as proposed for some stress memories); or (c) reversible after specific conditions (as in many stress priming phenomena). Epigenetic memory is distinct from genetic memory (changes in DNA sequence) and neural memory (changes in synaptic connectivity).

*Why it matters*: Epigenetic memory provides a mechanistic account of how plants (and other organisms) can retain information about past experiences without nervous systems. Understanding epigenetic memory is central to evaluating plant learning claims, because it provides the molecular substrate that makes some forms of plant memory unambiguously real.

---

**Systemic Acquired Resistance (SAR)**
The whole-plant increase in disease resistance that follows a localized pathogen infection. SAR is mediated by salicylic acid signaling from the infection site to distant tissues, where it activates the NPR1 transcription factor and upregulates pathogenesis-related (PR) protein genes. The primed state associated with SAR is maintained by chromatin modifications (particularly H3K4me3 at PR gene promoters) that allow faster, stronger defense gene induction upon subsequent pathogen encounter.

*Why it matters*: SAR is a well-established, mechanistically characterized form of plant immune memory — a genuine, adaptive modification of defensive capacity based on prior pathogen experience. It demonstrates that biological immune memory can be implemented in epigenetic chromatin states without the involvement of immunological memory cells or the nervous system.

---

**Priming**
The state of enhanced readiness for a defense response that follows a mild stress or pathogen encounter. A primed plant is not fully activated (which would be metabolically costly) but is poised for faster, stronger activation upon challenge. Priming is associated with stable but reversible chromatin modifications at defense gene promoters that maintain a permissive (but not active) transcriptional state. Priming can be somatic (within an individual's lifetime) or transgenerational (transmitted through seeds).

*Why it matters*: Priming is the mechanism by which the "memory" in SAR and other stress memory phenomena translates into altered behavior. Understanding priming provides insight into how epigenetic states modify functional outcomes — how the stored information in chromatin is "read out" as a behavioral change.

---

**Associative Learning**
A form of learning in which an organism forms an association between two previously unrelated stimuli (classical conditioning) or between a behavior and its consequences (operant conditioning). Operationally, associative learning requires: response to the conditioned stimulus after conditioning; extinction when the CS is presented without the US; stimulus specificity; and timing sensitivity in the CS-US interval. The claim that plants perform associative learning (Gagliano et al., 2016) is contested because the published experiments do not fully establish these criteria against alternative explanations.

*Why it matters*: Associative learning is the cognitive capacity that lies at the heart of the most ambitious plant learning claims. Understanding precisely what associative learning requires — and why those requirements are not trivially met — is essential for evaluating these claims.

---

**Sensory Adaptation vs. Habituation**
Two mechanisms that both produce reduced responses to repeated stimulation, but through different processes: sensory adaptation involves decreased sensitivity of receptor cells due to biochemical changes in the sensory apparatus (rapid, recovers quickly, driven by receptor-level changes); habituation involves decreased response amplitude driven by changes in the nervous system (or analogous integrative machinery) that are specific to the stimulus and recover more slowly. The distinction is crucial for plant learning research because fatigue and sensory adaptation are alternatives to learning for any observation of reduced plant responses.

*Why it matters*: The plant learning field's credibility depends on rigorous exclusion of sensory adaptation and fatigue as explanations for reduced plant responses. The methodological requirements for this exclusion — specific rest periods, novel stimulus tests — are part of standard habituation experimental design and should be reported clearly in any plant learning study.
