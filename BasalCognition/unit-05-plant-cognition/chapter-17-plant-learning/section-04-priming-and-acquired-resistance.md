# Section 4: Priming and Systemic Acquired Resistance — The Solid Ground

## Introduction

We end this chapter with the firmest ground: systemic acquired resistance (SAR) and stress priming. These phenomena are well-established, mechanistically characterized, and widely accepted in the plant biology community. They may not be as philosophically dramatic as associative learning in pea plants, but they represent genuine, robust forms of adaptive modification of plant behavior based on past experience — and their mechanisms illuminate the molecular basis of biological memory more broadly.

---

## 4.1 Systemic Acquired Resistance

Systemic acquired resistance is the increased resistance that plants develop, throughout their bodies, following a localized pathogen infection. It was discovered in the 1960s and has been studied intensively ever since. The phenomenon is unambiguous: a plant that has been locally infected by a pathogen (particularly a biotrophic pathogen that activates the salicylate defense pathway) becomes, within 24–72 hours, more resistant to subsequent pathogen attacks throughout its entire body — including in leaves that had no direct contact with the original infection.

The SAR response is long-lasting (persisting for weeks in some species), systemic (affecting the entire plant, not just the infected tissue), and broad-spectrum (providing resistance to a wide range of pathogens, not just the species that induced it). These properties make SAR a genuine whole-plant immune memory — an acquired change in defensive capacity based on prior pathogen experience.

The mechanism of SAR involves several components:

**Salicylic acid (SA) is the primary signal**: Localized infection activates SA synthesis and accumulation at the infection site, and SA (or a related mobile signal) moves through the phloem to distal tissues, where it activates a defensive gene expression program.

**NPR1 is the primary SA receptor**: In distal tissues, elevated SA binds to the receptor protein NPR1 (nonexpresser of PR genes 1), which then acts as a transcriptional coactivator at the promoters of defense genes — including the pathogenesis-related (PR) protein genes. PR proteins include various antimicrobial enzymes and structural proteins that directly impair pathogen growth.

**Chromatin modifications maintain the primed state**: After the primary infection and initial SA response, the plant enters a "primed" state in which the defense genes are not fully activated but are poised for rapid, strong activation upon subsequent pathogen challenge. This primed state is associated with specific chromatin modifications — particularly histone H3K4me3 (an activating mark) at the promoters of defense genes (Conrath et al., 2015). These chromatin marks are maintained in the absence of ongoing SA signaling, keeping the genes in a state of readiness.

**The role of mobile RNA signals**: More recently, it has been established that mobile small RNAs — produced at the infection site and transported through the phloem — contribute to the establishment of the primed state in distal tissues. These RNA signals can alter gene expression and chromatin state in distant cells, providing a molecular mechanism for the long-distance transmission of "immune memory" information.

---

## 4.2 Priming: The Enhanced Readiness State

The concept of priming deserves extended discussion because it is important for understanding the relationship between plant memory and plant behavior.

A primed plant is not simply an "activated" plant — it is not fully engaged in all its defenses all the time. Instead, it is a plant whose defense system is poised for rapid, amplified activation upon challenge. When a primed plant encounters a pathogen, its PR genes are activated faster and to higher levels than in a naive plant. The immune response is not different in kind from the naive response, but different in speed and magnitude.

This distinction between "activated" and "primed" is functionally important. A plant that maintained full constitutive defense expression all the time would pay a high fitness cost — defense proteins are metabolically expensive, and their constitutive expression would divert resources from growth and reproduction. The priming strategy is more economical: maintain the readiness for defense without paying the full cost, and pay the full cost only when the threat is confirmed.

Priming is, in this sense, a memory of threat that enables faster response without imposing the full cost of the defensive response during the inter-threat interval. This logic is identical to immunological memory in vertebrates: memory B and T cells are not fully active antibody-secreting cells, but they can be rapidly reactivated upon re-exposure to the antigen they were primed against. The logic of priming versus activation applies across the full range of biological immune systems.

## 4.3 Transgenerational Priming

There is evidence that priming can be transmitted from parent plants to offspring through seeds. Plants that experienced pathogen attack produce seeds whose germinated offspring show enhanced resistance to the same or related pathogens — even though the offspring themselves were not directly exposed to the pathogen.

This transgenerational priming has been documented in several species, including Arabidopsis and tomato (Rasmann et al., 2012). The proposed mechanism involves epigenetic modifications — particularly changes in DNA methylation patterns — that are established in the maternal plant in response to pathogen attack and that are transmitted through the seeds to affect gene expression in the offspring.

The transgenerational priming phenomenon is real but more contested in its molecular mechanism and adaptive interpretation than somatic SAR. Key questions include: Are the epigenetic modifications in the seeds actually persistent enough to alter offspring phenotype? Is the altered phenotype in offspring genuinely adaptive given the parent's experience (i.e., do offspring really face the same pathogen threats as their parents)? And can the phenomenon be robustly replicated across different plant-pathogen combinations and experimental conditions?

The evidence answers "yes, sometimes, for some systems" — but the universality and robustness of the phenomenon is still being established.

## 4.4 Plant Memory: A Summary Assessment

Having examined the full range of plant learning and memory claims — from the disputed (Mimosa habituation, pea associative learning) to the established (vernalization, SAR) — we can offer a summary assessment.

**Firmly established**:
- Vernalization memory — molecular, epigenetic, long-lasting
- Systemic acquired resistance — systemic immune memory, chromatin-based priming
- Somatic stress priming — enhanced readiness for stress response based on prior stress experience

**Provisionally supported (additional evidence needed)**:
- Transgenerational stress memory and priming
- Stimulus-specific reduction in Mimosa leaflet folding (the behavioral observation is replicated; the "learning" interpretation depends on mechanistic details not yet established)

**Disputed (not adequately established)**:
- Associative learning in plants (the Gagliano 2016 paper is contested in both methodology and interpretation)

This is a fair summary as of current knowledge. It is a summary that should be periodically updated as the science develops — and students engaging with this field should track the literature actively rather than treating this assessment as permanent.
