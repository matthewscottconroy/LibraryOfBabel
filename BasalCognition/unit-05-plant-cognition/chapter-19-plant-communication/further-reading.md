# Chapter 19: Further Reading

## Annotated Bibliography

**Simard, S. W., Perry, D. A., Jones, M. D., Myrold, D. D., Durall, D. M., & Molina, R. (1997). Net transfer of carbon between ectomycorrhizal tree species in the field. *Nature*, 388(6642), 579–582.**
The landmark paper demonstrating carbon transfer between trees through mycorrhizal networks. This is the paper that launched the "wood wide web" concept, and reading the original is important for understanding what was actually shown — and how far popular accounts have gone beyond it. The paper is careful and measured in its claims; the popular science built on it has been less so.

**Farmer, E. E., & Ryan, C. A. (1990). Interplant communication: airborne methyl jasmonate induces synthesis of proteinase inhibitors in plant leaves. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 87(19), 7713–7716.**
The paper that established methyl jasmonate as a between-plant volatile signal. A landmark in plant chemical ecology and in the understanding of volatile plant communication. Demonstrates clearly that one plant's biochemistry can alter the gene expression of another through an airborne chemical signal.

**Tumlinson, J. H., Turlings, T. C. J., & Lewis, W. J. (1993). Semiochemically mediated foraging behavior in beneficial parasitic insects. *Archives of Insect Biochemistry and Physiology*, 22(3–4), 385–401.**
An important paper on tritrophic chemical communication — the plant-VOC signals that attract herbivore predators. Demonstrates the specificity of herbivore-induced VOC blends in attracting parasitoid wasps that are specific to the attacking herbivore species. Essential for understanding the ecological complexity of plant VOC communication.

**Matsui, K. (2006). Green leaf volatiles: hydroperoxide lyase pathway of oxylipin metabolism. *Current Opinion in Plant Biology*, 9(3), 274–280.**
A clear review of green leaf volatile (GLV) biochemistry — the enzymatic pathway that produces GLVs from membrane lipid peroxides within seconds of tissue damage. Understanding the biochemistry helps evaluate the functional roles and timing of GLV signals.

**Song, Y. Y., Zeng, R. S., Xu, J. F., Li, J., Shen, X., & Yihdego, W. G. (2010). Interplant communication of tomato plants through underground common mycorrhizal networks. *PLOS ONE*, 5(10), e13324.**
One of the studies suggesting that defense-priming signals travel through mycorrhizal networks, providing evidence that mycorrhizal connections may function as information channels as well as nutrient transport channels. Should be read critically alongside the material transfer literature.

**Inderjit, & Duke, S. O. (2003). Ecophysiological aspects of allelopathy. *Planta*, 217(4), 529–539.**
A careful review of allelopathy that distinguishes genuine allelopathic effects from simple resource competition. Covers the chemical basis of allelopathy, the ecological conditions under which it is most important, and the methodological challenges in demonstrating allelopathic effects in field conditions. Essential for understanding allelopathy as a scientifically rigorous concept rather than a popular notion.

**Sheldrake, M. (2020). *Entangled Life: How Fungi Make Our Worlds, Change Our Minds & Shape Our Futures*. Random House.**
A beautifully written popular science book about fungi, including mycorrhizal networks. Sheldrake is a good scientist and a skilled writer, but the book's enthusiastic framing sometimes presents more speculative ideas alongside well-established ones without clear differentiation. Read alongside primary sources for the best educational effect — the book is a gateway, not a destination.

**Simard, S. (2021). *Finding the Mother Tree: Discovering the Wisdom of the Forest*. Alfred A. Knopf.**
Simard's popular memoir combining scientific autobiography with advocacy for forest conservation. The science it describes is real; the "wisdom" and "intelligence" framing goes considerably beyond what the data support. Valuable as a case study in how scientists communicate their work to popular audiences, and worth reading alongside the original papers to assess the distance between primary evidence and popular representation.

**Bais, H. P., Weir, T. L., Perry, L. G., Gilroy, S., & Vivanco, J. M. (2006). The role of root exudates in rhizosphere interactions with plants and other organisms. *Annual Review of Plant Biology*, 57, 233–266.**
Already cited in Chapter 18, but worth reiterating here: the most comprehensive review of root exudate biology, including their roles in allelopathy and in shaping the soil microbiome.
