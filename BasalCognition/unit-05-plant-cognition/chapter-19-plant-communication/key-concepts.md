# Chapter 19: Key Concepts

## Glossary

**Volatile Organic Compounds (VOCs)**
Organic molecules with high vapor pressures that readily evaporate from plant tissues into the surrounding air. Plant VOCs include isoprene, monoterpenes, sesquiterpenes, green leaf volatiles, and numerous other classes. They serve multiple functions: thermoprotection (isoprene), defense against herbivores and pathogens, pollinator attraction, and inter-plant signaling. The herbivory-induced VOC blend is a complex, species-specific mixture whose composition encodes information about the nature of the attacker.

*Why it matters*: Plant VOCs are the primary medium of plant-to-plant aerial communication and of the tritrophic signaling that recruits predators to attack herbivores. Understanding VOC chemistry and ecology is essential for understanding inter-plant communication.

---

**Green Leaf Volatiles (GLVs)**
Six-carbon aldehydes, alcohols, and esters (including hexanal, hexenal, and hexenol) released within seconds of tissue damage, produced from membrane lipid peroxides by a rapid enzymatic cascade involving lipoxygenase and hydroperoxide lyase. GLVs are among the first volatile signals released after wounding and can prime defense responses in neighboring plants through stomatal uptake. They also have direct antimicrobial activity near wound sites.

*Why it matters*: GLVs are the fastest component of the plant wound volatile signal — a nearly instantaneous alarm that begins priming neighbors before the slower, more specific herbivory-induced VOCs accumulate. They represent the "immediate alarm" layer of a multi-layered volatile signaling system.

---

**Methyl Jasmonate (MeJA)**
A volatile derivative of jasmonic acid released by herbivore-damaged plants, capable of traveling through air to neighboring plants and inducing defense gene expression in those plants. The discovery by Farmer and Ryan (1990) that MeJA could prime proteinase inhibitor gene expression in nearby plants demonstrated for the first time that plant hormones could function as airborne inter-plant signals.

*Why it matters*: Methyl jasmonate is the best-characterized airborne plant hormone involved in inter-plant communication. Its characterization established the principle that plant defense signals can operate beyond the individual plant, through the air.

---

**Tritrophic Signaling**
Chemical communication involving three trophic levels: the plant (first trophic level), the herbivore eating the plant (second trophic level), and the predator or parasitoid of the herbivore (third trophic level). In tritrophic signaling, herbivore-induced plant volatiles attract natural enemies of the herbivore, reducing herbivore pressure on the plant. The specificity of the herbivory-induced VOC blend — different for different herbivore species — allows specific predators to be recruited.

*Why it matters*: Tritrophic signaling represents a form of plant behavior that extends beyond simple response to attack — the plant actively manipulates the behavior of third-party organisms (predators) in ways that reduce its own herbivore load. This is one of the most sophisticated forms of plant indirect defense.

---

**Mycorrhizal Network**
A network of mycorrhizal fungal mycelium connecting the roots of multiple plants — often trees — through which nutrients (carbon, phosphate, nitrogen) can move between connected individuals. Mycorrhizal networks are widespread in forest ecosystems and are the basis of the "wood wide web" concept. The established fact of nutrient transfer through these networks is often extrapolated in popular accounts to claims about inter-tree "communication" and "cooperation" that go beyond current evidence.

*Why it matters*: Mycorrhizal networks represent a genuine physical infrastructure through which inter-plant material transfer occurs. Understanding what the networks do and do not transfer — and distinguishing established transfer from speculative signaling — is essential for a scientifically accurate view of plant-plant interaction.

---

**Allelopathy**
The chemical inhibition of one plant (or other organism) by another, mediated by compounds released into the environment through root exudates, leaf leachates, or volatile emissions. Allelopathic compounds include diverse secondary metabolites (quinones, terpenes, phenolic acids, alkaloids) that suppress germination, root growth, or photosynthesis in competing plants.

*Why it matters*: Allelopathy represents a form of competitive chemical behavior that extends a plant's competitive influence beyond the immediate zone of resource competition. It is an active, chemically sophisticated strategy whose effectiveness depends on the precision of target compound design and the selectivity of its effects.

---

**Defense Priming (VOC-mediated)**
The induction of a state of enhanced readiness for defense gene expression in neighboring plants, mediated by volatile compounds (particularly GLVs and methyl jasmonate) released by damaged or threatened plants. A primed plant is not fully defending itself but can activate defense responses faster and more strongly when challenged. VOC-mediated defense priming is distinct from physical contact or root exudate-mediated priming.

*Why it matters*: VOC-mediated defense priming demonstrates that inter-plant volatile signals have functional consequences — they alter the physiology of receiver plants in ways that increase their fitness. This makes the signals genuinely communicative in a functional sense, regardless of whether intentionality is involved.

---

**Tritrophic Communication**
See "Tritrophic Signaling" above. The term emphasizes the communicative aspect — the fact that the plant's VOC blend carries specific information (about the attacking herbivore species) that is read by insect predators and results in appropriate behavior (attraction of the specific predator). Whether the word "communication" is appropriate depends on one's definition, but the specificity and functional consequences of the signal make it one of the strongest candidates for genuine plant communication.

---

**Juglone**
A naphthoquinone allelopathic compound produced by black walnut (Juglans nigra) and released into the soil from roots and decaying leaves. Juglone inhibits many plant species through disruption of cellular respiration. The allelopathic zone around black walnut trees — often conspicuously devoid of other species — is one of the most visible demonstrations of plant allelopathy in temperate ecosystems.

*Why it matters*: Juglone and black walnut allelopathy are among the best-characterized allelopathic systems in plants, providing a concrete, well-studied example of chemical competitive behavior with clear ecological consequences.
