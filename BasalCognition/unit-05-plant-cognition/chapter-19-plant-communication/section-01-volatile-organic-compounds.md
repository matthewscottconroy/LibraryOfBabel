# Section 1: Volatile Organic Compounds — Plant Language in the Air

## Introduction

Plants are extraordinarily prolific producers of volatile organic compounds. Estimates suggest that terrestrial vegetation is responsible for the majority of non-methane VOC emissions to the atmosphere — hundreds of millions of tons per year — with enormous consequences for atmospheric chemistry and climate. Most of this is isoprene and monoterpenes, produced in enormous quantities as metabolic byproducts of photosynthesis and thermoprotective compounds. But embedded in this chemical background noise is a more specific signal: the blends of volatile compounds released by plants in response to herbivory, pathogen attack, and other stresses, which serve as signals detectable by neighboring plants and by herbivore-attacking insects.

---

## 1.1 Green Leaf Volatiles

The first thing you smell when you cut grass is green leaf volatiles (GLVs) — a suite of six-carbon aldehydes, alcohols, and esters (hexanal, hexenal, hexenol, and their acetate esters) released within seconds of leaf tissue damage. GLVs are produced from membrane lipids by a rapid enzymatic cascade activated by tissue damage — the same pathway that produces jasmonate precursors — and their release is nearly instantaneous because they are synthesized from abundant membrane lipid substrates using constitutively expressed enzymes.

GLVs serve multiple functions:

**Antimicrobial**: Several GLVs are directly toxic to bacteria and fungi at the concentrations that accumulate near wound sites.

**Wound-signal propagation**: GLVs can penetrate cells throughout a damaged leaf and trigger early steps in the jasmonate defense pathway, providing rapid intra-plant propagation of the wound alarm — faster than electrical signals in some respects, and reaching all tissues in the leaf simultaneously rather than propagating from a single point.

**Priming of neighboring plants**: GLVs released from a damaged plant can be taken up by neighboring plants through their stomata and leaf surfaces. In neighboring plants, GLVs prime — but do not fully activate — the defense pathway: gene expression patterns are altered in ways that allow faster, stronger activation if the plant itself is subsequently attacked (Matsui, 2006).

The priming effect of GLVs on neighboring plants is well-established and has been demonstrated in multiple plant species and in both laboratory and field conditions. The mechanism is reasonably well understood: GLVs, once inside neighboring plant cells, are metabolized in ways that produce products that interact with the JAZ repressor proteins of the jasmonate signaling pathway, lowering the threshold for jasmonate-dependent defense gene activation.

## 1.2 Methyl Jasmonate and Herbivory Signals

The willow-tree scenario from the chapter opening involves not just GLVs but also methyl jasmonate (MeJA) — a volatile derivative of jasmonic acid that is released from herbivore-damaged plants in larger quantities and over longer distances than GLVs.

MeJA was identified as a plant-to-plant communication signal in work by Farmer and Ryan in the early 1990s (Farmer & Ryan, 1990). They showed that tomato plants exposed to MeJA vapor from a jar of cut plant material in a closed chamber upregulated defense genes including proteinase inhibitor genes. This was a landmark result — one of the first clear demonstrations that volatile plant signals could elicit defensive responses in undamaged neighboring plants.

Subsequent work has shown that MeJA and other volatile jasmonates are produced in response to herbivory and can prime defense responses in neighbors across distances of tens of centimeters in field conditions. In more enclosed environments (inside plant canopies, in dense vegetation), the concentrations are sufficient to fully induce defense responses rather than merely prime them.

The ecological consequences of this between-plant signaling are potentially significant: a plant that detects herbivory on its neighbor has time to prepare its own defenses before the herbivore reaches it. This is a genuine adaptive advantage — not merely a byproduct of shared metabolic pathways.

## 1.3 Specificity and Context-Dependence

One of the most interesting aspects of volatile plant signaling is its specificity. Plants do not release uniform VOC blends in response to all stresses; the blend is specific to the type of stress.

**Herbivore-specific VOCs**: Different insect species produce different oral secretions (elicitors), which trigger different VOC blends when they feed on plants. The VOC blend released by a plant being eaten by caterpillars differs from the blend released by aphids, which differs from the blend from beetles. This specificity is not trivial: the different blends attract different species of predatory insects — parasitoid wasps that specialize on particular herbivore species (Tumlinson et al., 1993). A plant being attacked by caterpillars releases a VOC blend that attracts caterpillar-specializing parasitoids; a plant being attacked by aphids releases a different blend that attracts aphid parasitoids.

This is tritrophic communication: the plant broadcasts a chemical signal about who is eating it, and the signal is read by the predators of its attackers. The plant is manipulating the behavior of third-party organisms (predatory insects) in ways that reduce its herbivore load. This constitutes a form of inter-species communication — though whether to call it "intentional" or "communicative" in any cognitively meaningful sense is a question we return to below.

**Context-dependence**: The VOC blend released by a plant also depends on the plant's own physiological state — its nutrient status, its previous stress history, the developmental stage of the damaged tissue. Primed plants release different VOC blends from naive plants after herbivore attack. The context-dependence of VOC signaling means that the information content of the blend — the extent to which it carries specific information about the nature of the damage — is high.

## 1.4 Is This Communication?

The concept of communication implies, at minimum, a signal produced by one entity and received by another, with some functional consequence for the receiver. By this definition, plant VOC signaling is communication: the signal (VOC blend) is produced, it is received by neighboring plants and insects, and it has functional consequences (primed defense response, parasitoid attraction).

Whether it is "intentional" communication — whether the emitting plant "means" to signal its neighbors — is a different and harder question. The answer almost certainly is no in any rich psychological sense: there is no evidence that plants represent their neighbors, intend to inform them, or experience the communication as meaningful. The VOC release is a metabolic consequence of the wound response, with chemical properties that happen to be detectable by neighboring organisms and that have fitness effects for those organisms.

But this "no intention" answer should not lead us to dismiss the phenomenon. The same is true, at a mechanistic level, of many animal communication signals. Alarm calls in birds are "intentional" in a folk-psychology sense, but at the neural level they are produced by signaling pathways that are engaged by threat detection and produce behavioral outputs without any representation of "I am trying to inform my neighbors." The distinction between "intentional" and "reflex" communication is less sharp at the mechanistic level than it appears at the phenomenological level.

What is distinctive about plant VOC communication — and what might limit its claim to be "genuine" communication — is the absence of any apparent mechanism for the emitting plant to adjust its signal based on the presence or absence of receivers. The plant releases the same VOC blend regardless of whether any neighbors are present to receive it. This differs from some animal communication, where signal production is contingent on the presence of receivers. However, it resembles many other forms of animal chemical communication (pheromones, alarm chemicals) where the signal is released in response to internal state regardless of receiver presence.

The appropriate conclusion is that plant VOC signaling is a form of chemical communication that is real, mechanistically characterized, and ecologically significant — but that it differs from the richest forms of animal communication in ways that reflect the plant's lack of nervous system and representational capacity.
