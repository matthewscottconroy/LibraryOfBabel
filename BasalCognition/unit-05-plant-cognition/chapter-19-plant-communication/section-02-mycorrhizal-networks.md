# Section 2: Mycorrhizal Networks — The "Wood Wide Web" and What It Actually Shows

## Introduction

In 1997, a paper appeared in *Nature* by Suzanne Simard and colleagues that would eventually become one of the most cited and most popularized results in ecology: a demonstration that carbon was transferred between birch trees and Douglas fir seedlings through mycorrhizal fungal networks in a forest ecosystem (Simard et al., 1997). This result, solid and important in itself, was subsequently elaborated in popular accounts into the concept of the "wood wide web" — a vision of forests as interconnected intelligence, trees sharing resources and information through underground fungal networks, the older "mother trees" nurturing younger seedlings through these networks.

The popular vision is romantic, some of it is supported by evidence, and some of it is not. Disentangling what the science actually shows from what popular accounts have made of it is a necessary exercise — one that illustrates more broadly how scientific findings are transmitted to the public and why precision matters.

---

## 2.1 The Mycorrhizal Network: What It Is

Mycorrhizal fungi are fungi that form symbiotic associations with plant roots, growing into root cells (in the case of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, AMF) or around root cell surfaces (in the case of ectomycorrhizal fungi, ECM). The fungal hyphae extend from the colonized roots into the surrounding soil, greatly increasing the absorptive surface area available to the plant for water and phosphate uptake. In exchange, the plant provides the fungus with photosynthetically derived carbon sugars.

The mycorrhizal fungi of different plants can be (and commonly are) genetically continuous — that is, the mycelium of a single fungal individual can simultaneously colonize the roots of multiple trees. This creates a physical network through which materials can, in principle, move from one plant to another via the fungal connection.

The Simard et al. (1997) experiment demonstrated that this physical connection is functional: using isotope tracers (13C for Douglas fir, 14C for birch), they showed that labeled carbon fixed by one tree species moved through the mycorrhizal network and appeared in the other species. The transfer was bidirectional — both trees sent carbon to each other — but net transfer from birch to Douglas fir occurred when the Douglas fir was shaded (and thus carbon-limited), suggesting that the network could equalize carbon availability between connected individuals.

This result is the bedrock of the "wood wide web" story, and it is solid. Carbon does move between trees through mycorrhizal networks. The transfer is real and measurable.

---

## 2.2 What Transfer of Nutrients Implies and Does Not Imply

The temptation, which popular accounts have largely succumbed to, is to interpret the transfer of carbon between trees through mycorrhizal networks as evidence for deliberate "sharing" or "communication" between trees. This interpretation significantly overreaches the data.

**What the nutrient transfer shows**: Carbon and other nutrients (phosphate, nitrogen) can move between mycorrhizally connected plants through the fungal network. The direction of net transfer is influenced by the relative nutrient status of the connected plants — more moves to the more deficient plant. This is consistent with simple physical and chemical gradients driving movement along concentration and electrochemical gradients, without any "decision" by any party.

**What the nutrient transfer does not show**: It does not show that plants are aware of their neighbors, that they "choose" to share carbon, or that the transfers serve the interests of the receiving plant rather than simply reflecting the physical gradient dynamics in the network. The fungal network moves materials down concentration and chemical gradients just as any porous medium does; there is no evidence for active regulation of carbon transfer by the trees or the fungi in the direction of "help thy neighbor."

The key distinction is between passive material transfer (which is what the isotope data directly demonstrate) and active, regulated information transfer (which would require evidence of specific signaling, specificity for plant identity, and regulated response). There is good evidence for the former; the evidence for the latter is considerably more limited.

---

## 2.3 Evidence for Signaling Through Mycorrhizal Networks

Beyond material transfer, is there evidence that mycorrhizal networks transfer information — signals that alter plant physiology beyond what would be expected from simple changes in nutrient supply?

The strongest evidence comes from studies showing that defense priming can spread through mycorrhizal connections faster than could be accounted for by nutrient transfer alone. When one plant in a mycorrhizal network is attacked by a pathogen or herbivore, connected plants appear to upregulate defense genes before direct attack — and in some studies, this upregulation is correlated with the mycorrhizal connection and cannot be explained by volatile VOC transfer (Song et al., 2010). This suggests that some form of defense-priming signal is transferred through the fungal network.

What is this signal? Several candidates have been proposed: small defense-related proteins, hormones (jasmonates, salicylates), RNA molecules, or electrical signals propagating through the fungal mycelium. None of these has been definitively identified as the key signal in mycorrhizal network-mediated defense priming. The phenomenon itself is real enough to have been observed in multiple studies, but the mechanism is not yet characterized.

If defense priming signals do travel through mycorrhizal networks and alter plant defense gene expression in connected plants, this would constitute functional information transfer — a signal with specific content (defense-related information) that alters behavior in a fitness-relevant way in the receiver. This would be genuinely interesting from a cognitive perspective.

---

## 2.4 The Limits of What Has Been Established

To summarize the current state of evidence:

**Well-established**: Mycorrhizal networks physically connect trees in forest ecosystems; nutrients (carbon, phosphate, nitrogen) move through these networks between plants; the direction of net transfer is influenced by the relative nutritional status of connected plants.

**Provisionally supported (needs more evidence)**: Defense-priming signals may travel through mycorrhizal networks and alter connected plant physiology before direct attack; "mother trees" may differentially support kin seedlings through mycorrhizal networks (suggested by some studies but not consistently demonstrated).

**Not established**: That trees deliberately "share" or "communicate" through mycorrhizal networks; that the networks function as an "intelligence" or "forest internet" in any meaningful sense; that the transfers are regulated by plant or fungal "decisions" in contrast to physical gradient dynamics.

Popular accounts of the "wood wide web" — including the influential popular science writing by Merlin Sheldrake (*Entangled Life*, 2020) and the considerable media attention given to Simard's work — have often moved well beyond what the data directly support. This does not diminish the genuine importance of the underlying science, but it does require that students of plant biology learn to distinguish between established results and their popularized interpretations.

Simard herself has been careful in her scientific publications, though her popular writing (*Finding the Mother Tree*, 2021) adopts a more narrative voice that sometimes conflates the established findings with more evocative but less well-supported claims. Engaging with both the primary literature and the popular accounts — critically — is excellent training in scientific literacy.
