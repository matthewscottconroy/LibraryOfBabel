# Section 3: Mycorrhizal Network Communication — What the Evidence Actually Shows

## Introduction

Chapter 18 introduced the mycorrhizal network as part of the rhizosphere cognitive field — a fungal infrastructure through which plant roots exchange nutrients and, apparently, signals. The same network reappears here from the perspective of plant communication: what does the mycorrhizal network actually communicate, and how do we know?

This section approaches the mycorrhizal network with the precision that the evidence demands and the popular narrative too often abandons. The science is genuinely interesting — interesting enough to require no embellishment. The challenge is to separate what has been demonstrated from what has been extrapolated. That separation is itself a lesson in scientific epistemology.

---

## 3.1 The Simard Experiment: What Was Shown

In 1997, Suzanne Simard, then a graduate student at Oregon State University, published a study in *Nature* with her collaborators that would eventually become one of the most cited and most discussed papers in ecology. The experiment was conducted in a forest stand in British Columbia, using isotope labeling to trace the movement of carbon between trees (Simard et al., 1997).

The design was elegant. Simard and colleagues labeled paper birch trees with radioactive carbon-14 (¹⁴C) and Douglas fir seedlings with stable carbon-13 (¹³C). By measuring which isotopes appeared in which trees after a period of growth, they could track whether carbon was moving between species. And it was: labeled carbon from birch appeared in Douglas fir, and labeled carbon from Douglas fir appeared in birch. Net transfer went from birch to Douglas fir, particularly when the Douglas fir was shaded and therefore carbon-limited — which is consistent with gradients driving flow toward the more deficient partner.

The result is important and the experiment was well-designed. Net carbon transfer between trees through mycorrhizal networks in a real forest ecosystem was demonstrated for the first time. The transfer responded to the physiological status of the recipient (stressed, shaded trees received more). This is a solid, important finding.

What the experiment did not do — and what the experimental design was not capable of showing — is demonstrate that the trees were "choosing" to share carbon, that they were aware of their neighbors' needs, or that the transfer served the interests of the forest as a whole in any goal-directed sense. Carbon moved because gradients in a physical network move materials along concentration differences. The fact that those gradients were influenced by the trees' nutritional states does not require that the trees were regulating the transfer intentionally.

## 3.2 Defense Signals Through the Network

Beyond nutrients, a more cognitively interesting question is whether informational signals — signals about threat, not just resources — travel through mycorrhizal networks and alter connected plants' physiology.

Song et al. (2010) addressed this question with tomato plants. They grew pairs of tomato plants connected by mycorrhizal networks and infected one plant with a fungal pathogen. Connected plants showed upregulation of defense genes before they were directly attacked. The defense priming spread through the mycorrhizal connection faster than would be expected if it were carried solely by airborne VOCs or soil diffusion, suggesting that the fungal network was acting as a signal conduit.

This is more provocative than the nutrient transfer results. If defense signals travel through mycorrhizal networks, then the network is not merely a plumbing system for resources; it is carrying information about threats. What is that information? It could be small signaling molecules — jasmonates, salicylates, or related compounds — that the defending plant releases and that happen to enter the fungal network and travel through it. It could be electrical signals propagating through the fungal mycelium (which does conduct electrical signals in some contexts). It could be small RNAs or proteins. The mechanism is not yet characterized.

Babikova et al. (2013) provided additional evidence from a different angle, demonstrating that aphid-attacked plants produced below-ground signals that primed defenses in connected neighbors. Importantly, the Babikova study found that some of this priming occurred even when mycorrhizal connections were physically blocked, suggesting that some signals travel through the soil itself — not exclusively through fungal networks. The implication is that plant-to-plant warning signals have multiple pathways, and the mycorrhizal network is one of several, not the only one.

## 3.3 The Epistemological Challenge: Signaling or Leakage?

The central interpretive challenge in mycorrhizal communication research is distinguishing genuine signal transfer from passive leakage of biologically active compounds.

Consider the null model: a plant attacked by a pathogen upregulates its defense chemistry, producing more salicylate, more jasmonate, and more of many other compounds. Some of these compounds enter the plant's vascular system and circulate. Some enter root exudates and pass into the rhizosphere. Some enter the mycorrhizal fungi that are colonizing the plant's roots. Those fungi are physically connected to the roots of neighboring plants. The compounds diffuse or are transported through the fungal tissue to the neighboring roots, where they activate defense pathways — because they are compounds that activate defense pathways. The neighboring plant is primed.

This scenario involves no deliberate communication, no active regulation of signal transfer, no specificity for identity or content. It is simple chemistry: biologically active compounds spread through a physical network and activate their target pathways wherever they reach. The appearance of "communication" is an artifact of network topology and chemical activity.

How do we distinguish this from genuine, regulated signal transfer? The criteria would be: specificity (specific signals travel, not just any compound); directionality (transfer is regulated, not simply gradient-driven); identity-dependence (the signal transfer depends on the identity of the connected plants, not just their chemical states); and adaptive regulation (the sender adjusts signal output based on the receiver's state). None of these criteria have been clearly demonstrated in the mycorrhizal communication literature to date. The evidence for specificity and regulation is largely absent; the evidence for chemical transfer of biologically active compounds is solid.

This does not make the phenomenon uninteresting. Passive leakage of defense signals through a physical network that connects many plants still produces a functional result — the priming of connected plants' defenses — that has adaptive consequences. Whether we call it "communication" depends on whether we require intentionality, specificity, and regulation as criteria for that word, or whether functional information transfer (signal produced, travels to receiver, alters receiver's behavior) is sufficient.

Gilbert and Johnson (2017) make the case for skepticism in the context of the broader mycorrhizal communication narrative: they argue that much of what is described as "communication" in the mycorrhizal network literature is better described as passive chemical transfer, and that invoking communication without evidence of the specific features that distinguish it from passive diffusion is scientifically misleading. This is a fair challenge. The mycorrhizal network literature would benefit from more explicit engagement with the distinction.

## 3.4 The "Wood Wide Web" Narrative: What It Gets Right and Wrong

The phrase "wood wide web" was coined in a 1997 *Nature* news piece accompanying the Simard et al. paper and has since proliferated through science journalism, documentary films, and popular books. It evokes an image of forests as interconnected intelligence — trees sharing resources and information through underground fungal networks, the older "mother trees" nurturing younger seedlings, the forest a kind of superorganism bound together by chemical communication.

Some of this is supported by evidence. Carbon does transfer between trees. Defense priming may travel through fungal networks. Some studies suggest that ectomycorrhizal connections can differentially benefit connected seedlings over unconnected competitors. The network is real and the transfers are real.

But the narrative as popularly presented overclaims in several respects. "Sharing" implies deliberate transfer toward a recipient's benefit; the evidence supports gradient-driven transfer modulated by concentration differences. "Mother trees nurturing seedlings" implies targeted, identity-based support; the evidence for identity-specific transfer is weak. "Communication" implies regulated, specific information transfer; the evidence supports passive chemical diffusion through a physical network. "Forest intelligence" implies collective cognition at the forest level; the evidence does not support any such attribution.

Popular science writing about mycorrhizal networks — including the widely read work of Merlin Sheldrake and the narrative memoirs of Simard herself — often moves across this interpretive gap without clearly marking the transition from evidence to inference. This is not always deliberate exaggeration; the phenomena are genuinely fascinating, and the temptation to give them the most evocative interpretation is understandable. But for students of basal cognition, the exercise of tracing exactly what each study demonstrated and what it did not is one of the most valuable intellectual habits we can cultivate.

The mycorrhizal network is real, ecologically significant, and cognitively interesting even without the embellishments. The transfer of carbon and possibly defense signals between trees through a physical fungal infrastructure is a remarkable biological phenomenon. Describing it accurately — as a passive but functionally significant chemical exchange network that may also carry biologically active signaling molecules — is less romantic than the wood wide web narrative, but more honest.
