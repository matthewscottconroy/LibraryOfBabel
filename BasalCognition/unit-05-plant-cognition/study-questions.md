# Study Questions — Unit V: Plant Cognition

*These questions engage with one of the most contested areas in basal cognition research: whether plants, with no neurons and no nervous system, exhibit genuine cognitive capacities. The field is divided; these questions require you to take a position and defend it.*

---

## Question 1: Plant Learning and Its Critics

Gagliano et al. (2014) reported associative learning in *Pisum sativum*: plants were conditioned to associate a fan (neutral stimulus) with a blue light (food-associated stimulus), and subsequently oriented their growth toward the fan. The paper generated enormous controversy.

**(a)** The primary methodological criticisms of Gagliano et al. are: (i) the sample size was small; (ii) the effect has been difficult to replicate; (iii) the mechanism (how a plant could form a CS-US association) is unknown. Evaluate each of these criticisms. Which is most damaging, and why? How would a defense of the experiment respond?

**(b)** Gagliano's work has been called "a plant Pavlov" — a direct analog of classical conditioning in an organism with no neurons. If the effect is real and replicable, what would it imply for theories of learning that tie the mechanism of classical conditioning to synaptic plasticity (e.g., Hebbian learning, LTP)? Would it require revising these theories, or abandoning them?

**(c)** *Mimosa pudica*'s habituation to repeated dropping (studied by Gagliano et al., 2014) is better established and more widely replicated. How does the habituation result differ from the associative learning result in terms of its mechanistic plausibility and theoretical significance?

## Question 2: Plant Communication and the "Wood Wide Web"

Plants communicate with each other through at least three channels: airborne volatile organic compounds (VOCs), root exudates in the soil, and mycorrhizal fungal networks. The mycorrhizal network ("wood wide web") has been claimed to enable not just nutrient transfer but also signalling between trees.

**(a)** Critically evaluate the claim that the mycorrhizal network constitutes a communication system. What criteria must a signal transmission process satisfy to count as "communication" rather than mere chemical diffusion? Does the mycorrhizal network meet these criteria?

**(b)** Babikova et al. (2013) demonstrated that aphid-induced volatiles produced by a bean plant were transmitted via mycorrhizal networks to neighbouring plants, which then upregulated defences. Is this a case of "intentional" communication, or is it more accurately described as passive chemical leak? Does the distinction matter for claims about plant cognition?

**(c)** The "wood wide web" has attracted enormous popular interest and some concern from scientists that popular accounts exaggerate the degree of coordination and "intentionality" in forest communication. Where do you locate the boundary between the legitimate scientific content of forest communication research and popular over-interpretation? What are the stakes of getting this boundary wrong, in both directions?

## Question 3: Root Intelligence

Darwin and Darwin (1880) compared the tip of a root to "the brain of one of the lower animals." Modern plant neurobiologists (Baluška, Mancuso, and colleagues) have developed this analogy into the "root brain hypothesis": the meristematic transition zone at the root apex functions as a cognitive centre for integrating environmental signals and directing root growth.

**(a)** The "root brain hypothesis" is highly controversial. What are the strongest empirical arguments for it? What are the strongest arguments against? Is the controversy primarily empirical (do roots really do what the proponents claim?) or conceptual (even if they do, does it constitute cognition?)?

**(b)** The root apex integrates signals about gravity, light, water, nutrients, touch, and chemical composition of the soil, producing directional growth responses. Compare this integration process to the integration of synaptic inputs in a single neuron. What is genuinely analogous, and what is the relevant difference?

**(c)** Plant roots can navigate complex three-dimensional soil environments, find water and nutrients, avoid obstacles, and in some cases find support structures for climbing vines. Is this navigational capacity best explained by: (i) simple tropisms (independent directional growth responses to individual gradients), (ii) a more integrated navigational system that computes a resultant growth direction from multiple gradient inputs, or (iii) something else? What kind of evidence would distinguish these explanations?

## Question 4: Systemic Signalling and Plant-Wide Coordination

When a plant is attacked by a herbivore, it does not respond only locally: a systemic signal propagates from the wounded tissue to distal parts of the plant, triggering defensive gene expression throughout. This systemic acquired resistance (SAR) involves calcium waves, electrical signals, jasmonates, and salicylic acid, travelling at up to several centimetres per minute.

**(a)** The systemic wound response in plants has been compared to the "fight or flight" response in animals, mediated by the sympathetic nervous system. In both cases, a localised threat triggers a body-wide physiological change. How far does this analogy hold? Where does it break down, and what do the breakdowns reveal about the role of nervous systems?

**(b)** The speed of the systemic signal in plants (centimetres per minute) is much slower than neural conduction (metres per second). Does speed matter for cognition? If so, what is the relevant threshold, and on what grounds can it be justified?

**(c)** Some accounts of plant cognition (e.g., Calvo, 2016) propose that plants exhibit something like "anticipation": they prepare for future herbivore attacks on the basis of prior experience. Evaluate this claim carefully. What would constitute genuine anticipation, and how would we distinguish it from a primed immune response (which does not require any representation of the future)?

## Question 5: Ethical Implications of Plant Cognition

If plants are cognitive in a meaningful sense — if they sense, integrate, learn, communicate, and respond in goal-directed ways — this has potential implications for how we treat them.

**(a)** Sketch the best argument for the view that plant cognition grounds some form of moral consideration for plants. What is the argument's structure? What premises does it require? Is the argument stronger or weaker than parallel arguments for the moral consideration of invertebrate animals (e.g., insects)?

**(b)** A common objection to plant moral consideration is: "If plants deserve moral consideration, then so does every living cell, and ethics becomes absurd." Evaluate this "slippery slope" objection. Is there a principled line between cognitive systems that warrant moral consideration and those that do not?

**(c)** The expansion of moral consideration has historically followed the expansion of cognitive recognition: as we have come to recognise cognitive complexity in great apes, cetaceans, and birds, we have extended moral consideration to them. Is there a connection between cognitive attribution and moral status, or does moral status derive from some other property (sentience, capacity for suffering) that is independent of the functional cognitive capacities discussed in this unit?
