# Unit VI: Fungal Intelligence — The Network Mind

> *"Fungi are the great overlooked intelligence of the planet. They built the soils that made land life possible. They run the nutrient networks that sustain forests. They solve optimization problems in their architecture that mathematicians find beautiful. And they do it all without a single neuron, without a single muscle, without anything we would recognize as a brain. They think in mycelium."*

## Unit Introduction

Fungi are strange. They are neither plant nor animal — a third kingdom of eukaryotic life that has been evolving independently for over a billion years. They are mostly invisible: the fruiting bodies we recognize as mushrooms are merely the reproductive tips of an organism that spends most of its life as a network of microscopic threads (hyphae) threading through soil, wood, and living tissue. A single fungal individual can cover acres of forest floor. The Armillaria ostoyae specimen in the Malheur National Forest in Oregon covers approximately 9.6 square kilometers and may be over 8,000 years old — making it one of the largest and oldest organisms on Earth.

What does it think about, this ancient and enormous network?

This is not a frivolous question. Fungal networks — mycelium — exhibit behaviors that parallel the kinds of optimization, resource allocation, and information processing that we associate with cognitive systems. Andrew Adamatzky's work (2022) suggests that fungi generate electrical spike trains that may function as communication signals. Mark Fricker's group at Oxford has shown that mycelial networks implement solutions to resource distribution problems that are mathematically elegant. Toby Kiers' work on mycorrhizal symbiosis shows that the allocation of resources through fungal networks is responsive to cost-benefit signals in ways that resemble market logic.

## A Note on the Wood Wide Web

The popular image of forests communicating through underground fungal networks — the "wood wide web" — has captured public imagination and generated significant oversimplification. The underlying science is real: mycorrhizal networks do transfer carbon and nutrients between trees, and there is evidence that some information (in the form of signaling molecules) also travels through these networks.

But the popular account often overstates what is known. Evidence for "intentional" resource sharing or "deliberate" communication between trees through fungal networks is much weaker than popular accounts suggest. This unit presents what the evidence actually shows — which is interesting enough without embellishment.
