# Chapter 20 Exercises: The Mycelial Mind

---

## Part I: Reflection and Discussion

**1. Distributed vs. Centralized Decision-Making**
The mycelium makes resource allocation decisions — which food sources to commit to, which hyphae to prune — through purely local rules without any central coordinator. What does this imply for how we define "decision-making"? Is a decision only a decision if some agent represents options, compares them, and selects one? Or can something constitute a decision if it reliably achieves adaptive outcomes through distributed mechanisms? Where do you draw the line, and why?

**2. The Individual Problem**
The *Armillaria ostoyae* individual in Oregon covers 900 hectares and may be thousands of years old. In what sense is it one individual? Consider: (a) genetic identity, (b) cytoplasmic continuity, (c) coordination and co-regulation of behavior, (d) boundary with the environment. Which of these criteria are necessary for individuality, and which are merely sufficient? Does the answer change your intuitions about what it would mean for this organism to have experiences, or to have interests?

**3. Network Architecture as Memory**
The physical architecture of a mycelial network — which cords are thick, which anastomoses have formed, which branches have been pruned — reflects the history of the organism's encounters with its environment. In what sense is this architecture a form of memory? How does it compare to memory in neural systems? What would it mean to "forget" in a mycelial network?

**4. The Physarum Comparison**
*Physarum polycephalum* and fungal mycelium both optimize transport networks, but they are from entirely different kingdoms of life and use different mechanisms (oscillatory contraction vs. tip growth and reinforcement). What does this convergence tell us about the problem of network optimization in biological systems? Does convergent evolution of a problem-solving capacity tell us something about the nature of intelligence itself?

**5. Anastomosis and Self-Recognition**
Anastomosis requires that hyphae recognize compatible partners and refuse to fuse with incompatible ones. This is a form of self/non-self discrimination — a capacity also found in the immune system, in colonial tunicates, and at the cellular level in development. Why might self-recognition be important for a computing network? What would happen to the network's computational properties if self-recognition failed and any two hyphae could fuse?

## Part II: Thought Experiments

**1. The Networked Brain**
Imagine a version of the mycelium scaled up to brain size, with electrical signal speeds comparable to neurons but retaining its distributed, leaderless architecture. Could such a system support cognition in a meaningful sense? What features of neural cognition — perception, learning, decision-making — might be achievable by a network of this kind, and what features would be fundamentally out of reach? Be specific about what structural or dynamic properties you think are necessary for each cognitive capacity.

**2. The Pruned Self**
A mycelium that loses its connection to a food source prunes the hyphae that led there, reabsorbing their cytoplasm. Suppose that the pruned hyphae had begun to develop a locally distinct electrical activity pattern — a distinct "signal repertoire" associated with that food source. When those hyphae are pruned, is something like a memory lost? Now extend this analogy: synaptic pruning is a major feature of mammalian brain development, and the pruning of synaptic connections is believed to refine neural circuits for more efficient information processing. Are there interesting parallels between mycelial pruning and synaptic pruning? What does the analogy illuminate, and where does it break down?

**3. Two Mycelial Networks in Contact**
Two mycelial networks from genetically distinct individuals encounter each other. Because they are genetically incompatible, they do not anastomose — instead, they compete for territory, sometimes producing chemical warfare (production of antifungal compounds) and sometimes simply establishing stable boundaries. Now suppose we could design a synthetic "handshake" protocol — an artificial system that would allow controlled fusion between the two networks at a limited number of junction points. What computational properties might the resulting hybrid network have? Would it be one organism or two? One cognitive system or two? How would information from one network's environmental history propagate into the other?

## Part III: Laboratory Investigations

**1. Mapping Mycelial Growth on Heterogeneous Substrates**

*Hypothesis*: Mycelial growth patterns (branching frequency, cord thickness, network topology) will differ significantly on substrates with different spatial distributions of nutrients.

*Materials*: Cultures of *Phanerochaete velutina* or *Rhizopus stolonifer* (common bread mold, which can be cultured safely); petri dishes with agar; materials to create nutrient heterogeneity (e.g., agar with wood chips embedded in different arrangements, or agar with nutrient-rich patches at defined positions).

*Protocol*: Inoculate the center of several petri dishes with equal-sized plugs of mycelium. Vary the arrangement of nutrient sources across dishes: (a) two equal sources equidistant from the inoculation point; (b) one rich source and one poor source equidistant; (c) two rich sources at different distances. Photograph the developing network every 12 hours for 7 days. Measure: total hyphal length, branching index (branch points per unit length), diameter of main cords, and the relative investment (cord thickness × length) in pathways toward each food source.

*Analysis*: Do the growth patterns reflect the value and proximity of food sources? Does the network architecture change over time as the mycelium gains experience with the landscape? Can you detect pruning events — where hyphae visible in earlier photographs disappear in later ones?

**2. Electrical Activity Mapping with Surface Electrodes**

*Hypothesis*: Different regions of a mycelial network will show distinct electrical activity patterns, and these patterns will change in response to localized stimulation.

*Materials*: Actively growing mycelium (several species work; *Ganoderma* species grown on wood blocks are suitable); pairs of stainless steel or silver/silver chloride surface electrodes; a millivolt meter or inexpensive biosignal amplifier; a grounded reference electrode.

*Protocol*: Allow mycelium to colonize a wood block or agar substrate until it forms a network covering at least 10 cm². Place electrode pairs at multiple locations simultaneously (if equipment allows) or sequentially, and record spontaneous electrical activity over 30-minute periods. Then apply localized stimuli: touch one region of the network with a fine probe, apply a drop of concentrated glucose solution to one edge, or expose one region to brief bright light. Record electrical activity from unstimulated regions before, during, and after stimulus. Repeat with the stimulus applied to different locations.

*Analysis*: Is spontaneous electrical activity present? Does it appear as discrete spikes or as continuous oscillations? Do localized stimuli produce signals detectable at unstimulated locations? Is there a detectable latency between stimulation and detection at distant electrodes that is consistent with a propagating signal rather than a simultaneously generated response?

**3. Network Resilience to Targeted Disruption**

*Hypothesis*: Mycelial networks will reroute flow around severed pathways and restore connectivity within a predictable timeframe that depends on the availability of alternative routes.

*Materials*: Established mycelial networks on agar (several days old); fine scissors or a sterile blade; vital dye or tracking particles that can be added to one end of the network and detected at the other (fluorescent dyes that are taken up by fungal hyphae have been used in research settings, though dye penetration and safety should be verified before use).

*Protocol*: Allow a mycelial colony to establish connections between two food sources separated by a gap of sterile medium, so that a clear transport corridor forms. Once this corridor is established (visible as a thickened cord), sever the cord with a sterile cut. Record the network's response over the following 24–72 hours. Does it re-establish connection through alternative routes? What is the time course? Repeat the experiment with the cut made at different positions along the cord — closer to one food source, farther from the other — and measure whether the recovery time or path of reconnection depends on the cut location.

*Analysis*: If the network re-establishes connectivity, what route does it use? Are there detectable changes in the thickness of the alternative routes before or after the cut, suggesting anticipatory reinforcement of redundant pathways? Does the fungus respond differently depending on which side of the cut has the food source?
