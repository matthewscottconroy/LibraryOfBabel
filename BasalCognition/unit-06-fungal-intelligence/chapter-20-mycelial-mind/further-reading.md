# Chapter 20 Further Reading: The Mycelial Mind

---

**Boddy, L. (1999). Saprotrophic cord-forming fungi: Meeting the challenge of heterogeneous environments. *Mycologia*, 91(1), 13–32.**

A foundational paper from one of the leading researchers in fungal foraging ecology. Boddy synthesizes decades of experimental work on how wood-decay fungi explore landscapes, compete for resources, and build and remodel their mycelial cord networks. Essential reading for understanding the ecological context in which mycelial intelligence operates. The paper's clear experimental approach and careful interpretation set a standard for the field.

---

**Fricker, M. D., Heaton, L. L. M., Jones, N. S., & Boddy, L. (2017). The mycelium as a network. *Microbiology Spectrum*, 5(3), FUNK-0033-2016.**

The most comprehensive review of mycelial network structure and function from a network-science perspective. Fricker and colleagues bring quantitative tools from mathematics and engineering to bear on the question of how mycelial networks achieve their characteristic trade-off between efficiency, robustness, and material cost. Accessible to graduate students with basic quantitative background; includes discussion of mathematical models and experimental methods for network analysis.

---

**Tero, A., Takagi, S., Saigusa, T., Ito, K., Bebber, D. P., Fricker, M. D., Yumiki, K., Kobayashi, R., & Nakagaki, T. (2010). Rules for biologically inspired adaptive network design. *Science*, 327(5964), 439–442.**

The famous Physarum-Tokyo-rail paper. While about slime mold rather than fungi, this paper is essential background for understanding biological network optimization and has directly inspired research on fungal network intelligence. The authors show that a simple set of local reinforcement rules, implemented in a biological organism, produces network architectures comparable in efficiency to those designed by human engineers. A landmark paper in unconventional computing and biological intelligence.

---

**Nakagaki, T., Yamada, H., & Tóth, Á. (2000). Maze-solving by an amoeboid organism. *Nature*, 407(6803), 470.**

The brief but enormously influential paper demonstrating that Physarum can find the shortest path through a maze. This paper played a major role in opening the question of non-neural intelligence to experimental investigation and inspired a generation of research on biological computation. Read it alongside the Tero et al. (2010) Science paper for the full arc of the Physarum story.

---

**Steinberg, G. (2007). Hyphal growth: A tale of motors, lipids, and the Spitzenkörper. *Eukaryotic Cell*, 6(3), 351–360.**

An authoritative review of the cellular machinery of hyphal tip growth. Steinberg covers the Spitzenkörper, the role of motor proteins in vesicle delivery, and the molecular mechanisms that convert environmental signals into directed growth. More cell biological than the other readings on this list, but essential for understanding how the macroscopic behavior of the mycelium is grounded in specific molecular machinery.

---

**Adamatzky, A. (2018). Towards fungal computer. *Interface Focus*, 8(6), 20180029.**

Adamatzky's accessible overview of his program of research on fungal electrical signaling and its potential applications in unconventional computing. This paper is clearer about what has and has not been demonstrated than some of his more provocative later publications, and provides a good entry point into his body of work. Readers should approach with critical attention to the distinction between demonstrated electrical phenomena and speculative interpretations.

---

**Sheldrake, M. (2020). *Entangled Life: How Fungi Make Our Worlds, Change Our Minds and Shape Our Futures*. Random House.**

A beautifully written popular science book by a Cambridge-trained biologist. Sheldrake's book is the best general introduction to fungal biology currently available, covering mycelium, mycorrhizal symbiosis, lichen, and the chemical ecology of fungi with both scientific rigor and genuine literary flair. Graduate students will find it a welcome complement to the more technical readings on this list, and will likely finish it with a richer appreciation of why fungi deserve serious scientific attention. Note that Sheldrake occasionally ventures beyond what the evidence strictly supports; read with critical attention.

---

**Prosser, J. I., & Trinci, A. P. J. (1979). A model for hyphal growth and branching. *Journal of General Microbiology*, 111(1), 153–164.**

A classic paper on the mathematical modeling of hyphal growth patterns. Though now decades old, this paper established many of the conceptual tools still used in the field and demonstrates the power of mathematical modeling in mycology. Reading this paper alongside modern agent-based models of mycelial growth (such as Meškauskas et al., 2004) provides a useful sense of how the field has developed.

---

**Hickey, P. C., Jacobson, D., Read, N. D., & Glass, N. L. (2002). Live-cell imaging of vegetative hyphal fusion in *Neurospora crassa*. *Fungal Genetics and Biology*, 37(1), 109–119.**

A technically excellent paper documenting the dynamics of anastomosis — hyphal fusion — using live fluorescence microscopy. The paper reveals that anastomosis is an active, regulated process involving directed growth and precise molecular recognition, not simply a passive collision between hyphae. Essential for understanding how the mycelial network acquires its topological properties.

---

**Rayner, A. D. M. (1997). *Degrees of Freedom: Living in Dynamic Boundaries*. Imperial College Press.**

An unusual and thought-provoking book by a mycologist who argues that fungi represent a distinct and underappreciated mode of being — one characterized by indeterminate, dynamic boundaries rather than the fixed individuality of animal organisms. Rayner's perspective is sometimes speculative, and his philosophical arguments are not universally accepted, but the book asks genuinely important questions about biological individuality that are directly relevant to the study of fungal cognition. Recommended for students interested in the philosophical dimensions of the material in this chapter.
