# Chapter 20 Key Concepts: The Mycelial Mind

---

## Hypha (plural: Hyphae)
The fundamental structural and functional unit of the fungal body. A hypha is a tubular, thread-like filament composed of a cell wall enclosing a cytoplasmic space that may be divided by internal cross-walls (septa) or may be continuous without internal partitioning (coenocytic). Hyphae grow exclusively at their tips, and their branching, fusion, and pruning generate the mycelial network. The hypha is simultaneously a structural element, a transport conduit, a signal pathway, and a site of metabolic activity — a remarkable degree of functional integration in a structure that may be less than five micrometers in diameter.

---

## Mycelium
The collective term for the mass of hyphae that constitutes the body of a fungus. A mycelium can range from a microscopic cluster of hyphae in a soil pore to a genetically unified individual covering hundreds of hectares. The mycelium is not a collection of individual cells in the animal sense; it is a continuous (or semi-continuous) cytoplasmic network enclosed in a branching tubular architecture. The mycelium is where the fungus lives, feeds, and — in the sense central to this chapter — "thinks."

---

## Anastomosis
The fusion of two hyphae from the same or a compatible individual. Anastomosis transforms a branching tree-like network into a true network with cycles and redundant pathways, conferring robustness to damage and enabling efficient transport between network nodes. Anastomosis is an active, regulated process requiring molecular self-recognition; incompatible hyphae (from genetically distinct individuals) typically do not anastomose. The frequency and patterning of anastomosis events during mycelial growth shapes the final topology of the network.

---

## Cytoplasmic Streaming
The directed, bulk movement of cytoplasm through fungal hyphae. Cytoplasmic streaming serves as the mycelium's primary transport mechanism, moving nutrients, signaling molecules, organelles, and even nuclei from one part of the network to another. The direction and rate of streaming are not fixed; they respond to the physiological state of the local network and to conditions at distant locations. In this sense, the pattern of streaming across the network encodes information about the distribution of resources and the metabolic state of different network regions — making it simultaneously a transport mechanism and a signaling system.

---

## Spitzenkörper
A cluster of vesicles located just behind the growing hyphal tip, named from the German for "tip body." The Spitzenkörper acts as a vesicle supply depot, delivering membrane material and cell wall components to the expanding tip. Crucially, the position of the Spitzenkörper within the tip influences the direction of growth: environmental signals that shift the Spitzenkörper to one side cause the tip to curve in that direction. The Spitzenkörper is therefore the key interface between environmental sensing and growth behavior — the site where information becomes action in the growing hypha.

---

## Mycelial Cord
A differentiated, rope-like structure formed by the parallel bundling of multiple hyphae. Mycelial cords develop along productive transport routes — the pathways connecting a colony to nutrient-rich regions — and are adapted for bulk transport of nutrients and water. Cords are typically composed of an outer sheath of thick-walled hyphae surrounding a central core of larger, thin-walled transport hyphae. The formation and maintenance of cords is regulated by cytoplasmic flow: routes that carry high flow develop into cords, while low-flow routes remain as thin individual hyphae or are pruned. Cords are the mycelium's "highways."

---

## Hyphal Pruning
The process by which hyphae that are no longer productive are withdrawn and their cytoplasm reabsorbed into the network. Pruning is the complement of reinforcement in the mycelium's resource allocation strategy: it removes investment from unproductive territories and recycles that investment to more productive areas. Pruning involves the retraction of the Spitzenkörper, reorganization of the cytoskeleton, and enzymatic digestion (autolysis) of the hyphal contents. The result is that the mycelial network is not a permanent record of all growth that has occurred, but a continuously updated map of productive connections.

---

## Network Optimization
In the context of mycelial biology, network optimization refers to the process by which the mycelial network converges, over time, on an architecture that efficiently balances transport performance, robustness to damage, and material cost. This optimization is achieved through distributed local rules — reinforcement of high-flow pathways and pruning of low-flow pathways — without any global plan or central controller. The quantitative demonstration that mycelial networks achieve near-Pareto-optimal solutions to the efficiency-robustness-cost trade-off is among the strongest evidence for functional intelligence in fungal systems.

---

## Septa (singular: Septum)
Internal cross-walls that divide the hyphae of many fungal species into compartments. Septa are not completely solid barriers; they typically contain pores — the dolipore septum of Basidiomycetes is a particularly elaborate example — that allow cytoplasm, organelles, and even nuclei to pass between compartments. The permeability of septa is regulated and can be altered in response to damage or stress. The presence or absence of septa, and the degree of their permeability, affects the kinetics and patterns of cytoplasmic streaming and therefore the dynamics of information flow through the network.

---

## Electrical Spike (in fungi)
A transient, discrete change in the electrical potential across the hyphal membrane, analogous in some structural properties to the action potential of neurons. Fungal electrical spikes have amplitudes in the millivolt range, durations of seconds to minutes, and propagate through the mycelial network at rates of millimeters to centimeters per minute. Unlike neuronal action potentials, which are driven primarily by sodium and potassium ion channels, the ionic basis of fungal electrical spikes is not fully characterized, though potassium and calcium channels are thought to be involved. The functional significance of these spikes — whether they serve a signaling role, and what information they carry — is an active area of research and interpretation.
