# Chapter 20 Key Researchers: The Mycelial Mind

---

## Andrew Adamatzky
**University of the West of England, Bristol — Unconventional Computing Laboratory**

Andrew Adamatzky is a computational scientist whose work spans unconventional computing, reaction-diffusion systems, and biological computing substrates. His background is not in mycology but in computer science and applied mathematics, and he approaches the mycelium as an unconventional computing substrate rather than as a biological organism per se. This perspective has led him to ask questions that traditional mycologists might not have formulated.

Adamatzky's most significant contributions to fungal neuroscience (if one may call it that) involve the documentation of electrical spiking activity in mycelium. Using microelectrode arrays implanted in growing fungal colonies, his group has recorded discrete electrical spikes in several species, characterized their temporal structure, and documented their responses to environmental stimuli. His 2022 paper in *Royal Society Open Science* argued that the pattern of electrical spikes in fungi constitutes a kind of "language" — a claim that has been received with significant skepticism by mycologists but has generated productive debate.

Adamatzky is a prolific and intellectually adventurous researcher whose work should be approached with the critical attention it invites. His experimental observations — that fungi generate patterned electrical signals — appear robust. His interpretations of those observations are more contested. Both aspects of his work are worth engaging with seriously.

## Mark Fricker
**University of Oxford — Department of Plant Sciences**

Mark Fricker is a cell biologist turned network scientist whose work on mycelial networks has established much of the quantitative foundation for the field. Starting from a background in plant calcium signaling, Fricker developed an interest in fungal networks through collaborative work with Lynne Boddy and has since become the leading figure in quantitative analysis of mycelial network architecture.

Fricker's approach is distinctive for its mathematical rigor: rather than describing mycelial networks in qualitative terms, his group has developed image analysis pipelines that extract quantitative network metrics — transport efficiency, robustness, material cost — from time-lapse microscopy data. This has allowed him to ask precise questions about whether mycelial networks are optimized, in what sense, and by what mechanisms.

His work has demonstrated that mycelial networks occupy a Pareto-efficient region of the efficiency-robustness-cost trade-off space — that is, they achieve near-optimal solutions to a genuine engineering optimization problem. This is among the strongest quantitative evidence for functional intelligence in fungal systems, because it demonstrates not just that fungi solve problems but that they solve them *well*, in a measurable and mathematically grounded sense.

## Lynne Boddy
**Cardiff University — School of Biosciences**

Lynne Boddy is one of the world's leading experts on the ecology and behavior of wood-decay fungi. Her work spans more than four decades and covers mycelial foraging strategies, interspecific competition, community ecology, and the role of fungi in forest nutrient cycling.

Boddy's contribution to the question of fungal intelligence is fundamentally ecological and behavioral. Through careful laboratory microcosm experiments, her group has documented how fungi like *Phanerochaete velutina* explore landscapes, make resource allocation decisions, compete with other species, and respond to disturbances. This experimental work provides the empirical foundation on which quantitative network analysis and theoretical modeling are built.

Boddy is notable for her care in interpreting results: she describes fungal behavior in terms of functional outcomes without overclaiming about the underlying mechanisms or the presence of "intelligence" in any mentalistic sense. Her papers model the kind of scientifically rigorous, philosophically careful approach to non-neural cognition that characterizes the best work in this field.

## Toby Kiers
**Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam — Department of Ecological Science**

While Kiers's primary work concerns mycorrhizal symbiosis (covered more fully in Chapter 22), her contributions to the study of fungal resource allocation intelligence make her essential reading at this stage. Kiers and colleagues have investigated how mycorrhizal fungal networks manage the movement of resources between plant hosts, demonstrating that this allocation appears to be regulated in a manner consistent with economic optimization — a finding she describes in terms of "trade" and "market dynamics."

Kiers has introduced sophisticated experimental approaches, including the use of isotopically labeled nutrients to track resource movement through fungal networks in real time. Her work is notable for its willingness to apply the concepts of economic theory to biological systems while maintaining appropriate skepticism about the degree to which the analogy is literal rather than metaphorical.

## Merlin Sheldrake
**Independent researcher — trained at University of Cambridge**

Sheldrake occupies a distinctive position in the world of fungal science: he is a rigorously trained mycologist whose 2020 book *Entangled Life* brought the science of fungi to a wide popular audience. While Sheldrake's primary research contributions are in the area of mycorrhizal ecology and tropical forest ecology, his importance for the field of fungal cognition lies partly in his role as a synthesizer and interpreter of the broader literature.

Sheldrake asks, in a scientifically informed way, questions that more cautious researchers sometimes avoid: What is it like to be a fungus? Does the mycelium have something like agency? What does "intelligence" mean when applied to an organism with no brain and no fixed body plan? His answers are appropriately tentative — he is careful to distinguish established science from speculation — but the questions themselves are worth taking seriously. For graduate students approaching this material for the first time, *Entangled Life* is an excellent entry point.

## Toshiyuki Nakagaki
**Hokkaido University — Research Institute for Electronic Science**

Nakagaki is not a mycologist but a biophysicist, and his primary subject is *Physarum polycephalum* rather than true fungi. Nevertheless, his work is so directly relevant to fungal network intelligence that it would be misleading to omit him. His group published the landmark maze-solving and Tokyo-rail papers that established biological network optimization as a serious research topic and inspired a generation of work on mycelial networks.

Nakagaki's contribution has been methodological as much as empirical: he showed that the tools of network science could be applied to biological systems, that biological network optimization could be quantified, and that the results of that quantification were striking enough to command attention from the mainstream scientific community. The work of Fricker, Boddy, and others on true fungal mycelium owes a substantial intellectual debt to Nakagaki's pioneering studies on Physarum.
