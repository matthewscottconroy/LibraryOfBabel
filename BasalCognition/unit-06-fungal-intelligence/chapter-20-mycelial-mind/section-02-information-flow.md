# Section 2: Information Flow in the Mycelial Network

## The Problem of Communication at Scale

A mycelium faces a problem that should be familiar to any student of biology: the problem of communication at scale. How does information move from one part of a large, distributed organism to another? How does the growing tip at the edge of a colony "know" what is happening at the colony center? How does a nutrient discovery at one location influence the behavior of hyphae meters away?

In animals, this problem is solved in two ways: chemically, through hormones and other signaling molecules that diffuse or are transported through the circulatory system; and electrically, through the action potentials that travel along neurons. Fungi have no circulatory system in the animal sense, no neurons, no synapses. Yet information clearly moves through them. The question is how.

Two mechanisms have been studied in significant detail: cytoplasmic streaming, which moves both materials and signals through the hyphal network, and electrical signals, which propagate through mycelium in ways that bear a functional resemblance to neural transmission. These are not entirely separate systems — they interact, and together they constitute the fungal "nervous system," if we may use that term loosely and with appropriate caution.

## Cytoplasmic Streaming as Signal and Transport

Cytoplasm does not sit still in fungal hyphae. It flows. In young, actively growing hyphae, cytoplasm streams toward the growing tip, delivering the vesicles, organelles, and molecular building blocks that feed growth. In older parts of the network, and especially in the thick "cord" structures that connect major network nodes, cytoplasmic flow can move in either direction and can reverse. The direction and rate of flow are not constant; they respond to the physiological state of the local and distant network.

This streaming serves an obvious transport function: it moves nutrients, synthesized compounds, and signaling molecules from one part of the organism to another. A nutrient captured at the periphery of the colony — a glucose molecule, a nitrogen compound — can be transported back to the growing edge or to reproductive structures through cytoplasmic streaming in the cord network. The mycelium is, among other things, a pipeline.

But cytoplasmic streaming is also a signal. The *direction* and *rate* of flow carry information. When a nutrient source is encountered, streaming toward that source accelerates. When a food source is exhausted, streaming direction reverses. The pattern of streaming across the network is a dynamic map of resource distribution — a map that is simultaneously updated and acted upon. This is information processing, even if it is slow by neural standards: cytoplasmic streaming rates are typically on the order of micrometers to millimeters per second, far slower than neural signals but perfectly adequate for coordination over timescales of hours to days.

Importantly, cytoplasmic streaming in fungal cords can be vectorial — it moves in a consistent direction over extended periods — and this direction responds to conditions at distant points in the network. How a local segment of hypha "knows" what is happening far away well enough to orient its streaming appropriately is not fully understood. Pressure gradients, osmotic differences, and signaling molecules are all likely contributors. But the outcome is a kind of hydraulic communication: the pattern of flow in the network transmits information about the state of distant regions.

## Electrical Signals in Mycelium

The more dramatic discovery — and the one that has generated the most scientific excitement and controversy — is that fungi generate electrical signals that propagate through the mycelial network.

This was not entirely surprising to researchers who came from a comparative physiology background. It has long been known that many organisms without neurons generate electrical signals: plants produce action potential-like events in response to wounding; the single-celled alga *Chara* shows remarkable electrical excitability; and even bacteria generate membrane potential changes that coordinate behavior. The question was whether fungi, similarly organized as a network of electrically connected cells, would show coordinated electrical signaling.

The answer appears to be yes. Early work in the 1990s documented spontaneous oscillations in electrical potential across fungal mycelium. More detailed investigation, particularly by Andrew Adamatzky and colleagues at the University of the West of England Bristol, has used microelectrode arrays to record electrical activity in growing mycelium with much greater spatial and temporal resolution.

What these recordings reveal is striking. Fungal hyphae generate discrete electrical spikes — transient changes in the electrical potential across the hyphal membrane — that propagate through the network. These spikes are not random noise. They are patterned, they repeat at roughly regular intervals, and they spread from one part of the mycelium to others. In their basic properties — discrete events, propagation through a connected network, followed by a period of reduced excitability — they bear a structural resemblance to action potentials in neurons.

The resemblance should not be overstated. Fungal electrical spikes are slower than neuronal action potentials: where a mammalian axon might conduct a signal at tens of meters per second, fungal electrical signals propagate at roughly millimeters per minute. The underlying ionic mechanisms also differ from those of neurons in detail, though they likely involve changes in potassium and calcium ion distribution across the hyphal membrane, as in neurons. And the network architecture through which they propagate is entirely different from an axon. Nevertheless, the functional parallel is suggestive: the mycelium generates propagating electrical signals that could, in principle, coordinate behavior across the network.

## Responses to Environmental Stimuli

More compelling than the existence of spontaneous electrical activity is evidence that electrical signals in mycelium respond to — and apparently transmit information about — environmental events.

Adamatzky and colleagues documented electrical responses to a range of stimuli: chemical attractants and repellents placed at the periphery of a mycelium produced electrical signals that propagated toward the colony center (Adamatzky, 2018). Mechanical stimulation — touching or damaging a hyphal tip — generated propagating electrical disturbances. Light exposure altered the pattern of electrical spiking. In each case, a localized stimulus produced a network-wide change in electrical activity.

This is exactly what we would expect of a signaling system: a local event (stimulus) produces a propagating signal that conveys information about that event to distant parts of the organism. Whether the receiving parts of the organism respond to this signal in ways that constitute "acting on" the information is harder to establish. But there is evidence that fungal electrical signals can trigger changes in growth direction and growth rate — the behavioral repertoire available to a growing hypha (Adamatzky, 2018).

It is worth pausing to consider what "responds to information" means in this context. When we say that a neuron responds to an action potential by adjusting its firing rate, or that a muscle responds to neural input by contracting, we do not require that there be a homunculus inside the cell interpreting the signal. The response is mechanical, chemical, and ultimately deterministic. The same is true of fungal electrical signaling: if an electrical signal in a hypha triggers changes in ion channel opening, which alter cytoplasmic calcium concentration, which affects vesicle trafficking and therefore growth direction — that is an information-processing event, even though no interpretation occurs. The functional architecture is what matters, not the presence of a mental state.

## The Adamatzky Electrical Signal Research

Andrew Adamatzky's work on fungal electrical activity is the most detailed and systematic to date, and it deserves careful examination because it has been both influential and controversial.

Using inoculated wooden blocks and later mycelium grown on agar, Adamatzky's group implanted small electrodes into the growing mycelium and recorded potential differences over extended periods. The signals they recorded showed clear structure: discrete spikes with amplitudes in the millivolt range, durations of seconds to minutes, occurring at intervals of minutes to hours. Unlike random fluctuations, these spikes showed temporal patterning — something closer to a train of pulses than to random noise (Adamatzky, 2022).

Crucially, the spikes were not all the same. Adamatzky documented at least two populations of spikes in species like *Ghost Fungi* (*Omphalotus nidiformis*) and the caterpillar fungus (*Ophiocordyceps*): a frequent class of smaller spikes and a less frequent class of larger ones. The interplay between these spike populations, and the way they responded to stimuli, formed the basis of his 2022 *Royal Society Open Science* paper proposing a "language" of electrical signals in fungi — a controversial claim we will examine in Chapter 21.

For our purposes here, the key finding is simpler and more defensible: mycelium generates structured, propagating electrical signals that respond to environmental stimuli in ways consistent with a signaling function. Whether these signals constitute "language" or "communication" in any meaningful sense is a separate and harder question. That they exist and that they carry information about environmental state is, at this point, reasonably well-supported.

## Calcium Waves and Chemical Signaling

Alongside electrical signals, chemical signaling molecules travel through the mycelial network. Calcium ions deserve particular attention, because calcium signaling is ancient — it appears in virtually every eukaryotic cell type and even in some bacteria — and because calcium dynamics in fungi are closely linked to electrical activity.

In hyphae, cytoplasmic calcium concentration oscillates during active growth. Calcium waves — propagating fronts of elevated calcium concentration — have been documented in growing mycelium and are associated with branching decisions, tip orientation, and the fusion events of anastomosis. The calcium wave may be a mechanism by which electrical signals are translated into growth responses: an electrical spike depolarizes the membrane, opens calcium channels, generates a calcium wave, and the calcium wave triggers downstream changes in the cytoskeleton that alter growth direction.

This is speculative at the molecular level — the detailed transduction chain has not been fully worked out in most fungal species — but the general outline is consistent with what is known about calcium signaling in other eukaryotic contexts. The point for our purposes is that the mycelium possesses multiple, interacting signaling systems — electrical, calcium-based, and cytoplasmic streaming — that together constitute a rich informational infrastructure.

## What Information Processing Requires

Having surveyed the signal types in mycelium, it is worth stepping back and asking: what would information processing require, and do these signals meet those requirements?

At minimum, information processing requires: (1) sensors that detect environmental conditions and convert them into internal signals; (2) pathways through which those signals propagate; (3) integrators that combine signals from multiple sources; and (4) effectors that translate integrated signals into behavior.

The mycelium appears to have all four. Hyphal tips sense chemical, mechanical, and electrical environmental conditions. Electrical signals and cytoplasmic flows propagate through the network. The network structure itself — particularly the junctions where anastomosis occurs — creates integrating nodes where signals from multiple input pathways converge. And the growth machinery at each hyphal tip constitutes an effector: it translates incoming signals into directional growth.

This is not proof of cognition in any deep sense. A thermostat also has sensors, signal pathways, integrators, and effectors. The question of whether mycelial information processing rises to the level of cognition — of representation, of decision-making in a meaningful sense — requires asking additional questions about the complexity and flexibility of the integration, the degree to which past experience (network history) shapes current responses, and the adaptiveness of the outcomes.

These are questions we will return to throughout the unit. For now, it is enough to establish that the mycelium is not a passive structure. It is an active information-processing system, and the signals that move through it are the substrate of whatever intelligence it possesses.
