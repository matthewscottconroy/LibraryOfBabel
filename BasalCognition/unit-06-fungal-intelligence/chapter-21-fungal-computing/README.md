# Chapter 21: Fungal Computing and Unconventional Intelligence

## The Lab at the Edge of Biology

In a laboratory at the University of the West of England Bristol, a cluster of mushrooms grows on a substrate of oat flakes while fine stainless steel needles measure the electrical activity threading through the white webbing of their mycelium. On a computer screen nearby, a trace scrolls — not the smooth sine waves of an oscilloscope testing electronic circuits, but something jagged, irregular, and strangely alive: discrete spikes, separated by irregular pauses, occurring in bursts and then falling quiet. The researcher watching this trace is not a mycologist. He is a computer scientist who has spent his career thinking about computation in unconventional substrates — in liquid crystals, in slime molds, in the pulsing of chemical waves — and what he sees in this trace he describes, cautiously, as something like a signal.

That researcher is Andrew Adamatzky, and his question is both simple and radical: Can the fungal mycelium compute?

Not metaphorically compute. Not compute in the vague sense in which we sometimes say that evolution computes the fitness of genotypes, or that the immune system computes a response to pathogens. But compute in a precise, engineering sense: can the physical network of hyphae perform logical operations on information, implement algorithms, or solve problems that we recognize as computational problems?

---

## The Central Question

The question of fungal computing sits at an intersection of several active debates. The first is empirical: what, exactly, do the electrical signals in mycelium represent? Are they noise? Metabolic byproducts with no signaling function? Or are they structured information carriers — the fungal equivalent of the action potential? The second debate is interpretive: even if these signals are structured, does that structure constitute a "language"? Do the spike patterns encode information about the environment in a way that can be decoded? The third debate is applied: if fungi can perform some form of computation, what might that be good for? What problems could fungal computing solve that electronic computing handles poorly?

These debates are not yet settled. The field is young, the data are genuinely interesting, and the interpretive frameworks are still being worked out. What this chapter offers is not a definitive answer but a careful examination of the evidence and arguments — an attempt to distinguish what we know from what we hope, and what we hope from what we should be skeptical about.

## Chapter Roadmap

Section 1 examines the case for fungi as computing substrates. We look at experimental evidence for memory-like properties in mycelium, the maze-solving abilities of the related organism *Physarum*, and Adamatzky's direct attempts to implement logical operations using fungal growth patterns. We assess what these experiments demonstrate and where their limitations lie.

Section 2 turns to the electrical communication data — the spike trains in mycelium — in detail. We examine the 2022 Adamatzky paper on the "language of fungi," look carefully at what the data actually show, and assess the reception of this work within the broader mycological and neuroscience communities. This section is the philosophical core of the chapter: it asks what it would mean for electrical spike patterns to constitute a language, what evidence would be needed to support that claim, and whether the available data meet that standard.

Section 3 looks forward: to the prospect of unconventional computing paradigms that might incorporate biological substrates, to wetware computing as a research program, and to what fungal computing — if it is real — might offer to artificial intelligence research and to our broader understanding of what computing fundamentally is.

## A Note on Caution

This chapter will be unusually explicit about the limits of the evidence. The reason is not that the science is unimportant — it is that the topic is one where the temptation to over-interpret is particularly strong, and where over-interpretation does real damage to the field.

Fungi are fascinating organisms. The possibility that they compute, that they have something like language, that their electrical activity constitutes something like thought — these are genuinely exciting ideas, and the researchers who have proposed them deserve credit for asking audacious questions. But exciting ideas are not validated by their excitement. They are validated by evidence, and the evidence here requires careful reading.

We will try to give you the tools to read it carefully.
