# Chapter 21 Further Reading: Fungal Computing and Unconventional Intelligence

---

**Adamatzky, A. (2022). Language of fungi derived from their electrical spiking activity. *Royal Society Open Science*, 9(4), 211926.**

The paper that provoked the "fungal language" controversy. Read it closely and carefully: the actual data (spike recordings, inter-spike interval distributions, Zipf analysis) are more modest than the interpretation, and distinguishing the two is an excellent exercise in scientific critical reading. The paper's methodology is described in sufficient detail for replication, and the supplementary materials provide raw data that interested readers can re-analyze.

---

**Adamatzky, A. (2018). Towards fungal computer. *Interface Focus*, 8(6), 20180029.**

A more measured overview of Adamatzky's research program, written before the 2022 controversy. This paper is clearer about the speculative nature of fungal computing as a practical enterprise and provides a better entry point into his experimental methods. The section on logical gate implementation is especially useful for understanding what "fungal computing" means operationally.

---

**Tero, A., Takagi, S., Saigusa, T., Ito, K., Bebber, D. P., Fricker, M. D., Yumiki, K., Kobayashi, R., & Nakagaki, T. (2010). Rules for biologically inspired adaptive network design. *Science*, 327(5964), 439–442.**

One of the most cited papers in biological network science. The Tokyo rail Physarum experiment demonstrated that biological network optimization can produce results comparable in quality to engineered networks, and spawned a field of bio-inspired algorithm research. For computing students, this paper provides the clearest link between biological network dynamics and computational algorithm design.

---

**Saigusa, T., Tero, A., Nakagaki, T., & Kuramoto, Y. (2008). Amoebae anticipate periodic events. *Physical Review Letters*, 100(1), 018101.**

This paper demonstrates that Physarum can "anticipate" periodic environmental stresses — a memory-like phenomenon in an organism without neurons. The finding is directly relevant to the question of memory in mycelial networks and provides a methodological template for experiments designed to demonstrate memory-like properties in fungi. Methodologically rigorous; the experimental design is particularly instructive.

---

**Varela, F. J., Thompson, E., & Rosch, E. (1991). *The Embodied Mind: Cognitive Science and Human Experience*. MIT Press.**

The founding text of enactive cognitive science — the framework that argues that cognition is constituted by the ongoing sensorimotor coupling of an organism with its environment, not by internal representations. For students who want to understand why fungal computing is interesting at a philosophical level, this book provides the theoretical framework within which the distinction between embodied and disembodied computation makes sense. Dense and sometimes difficult, but rewards careful reading.

---

**Tsuda, S., Aono, M., & Gunji, Y. P. (2004). Robust and emergent *Physarum* logical-computing. *Biosystems*, 73(1), 45–55.**

The original demonstration of logical gate implementation using Physarum growth patterns. This paper is the direct predecessor of Adamatzky's work on fungal logical gates and provides the conceptual and experimental framework that subsequent work builds on. Accessible to readers with a basic background in logic circuit design.

---

**Adamatzky, A. (Ed.). (2010). *Physarum Machines: Computers from Slime Mould*. World Scientific.**

An edited volume collecting much of the early work on Physarum as a computing substrate. While the subject is slime mold rather than true fungi, the methods and conceptual frameworks apply directly to fungal computing research. Covers maze-solving, logical gate implementation, network optimization, and the mathematical theory of Physarum dynamics. An invaluable reference for anyone who wants to understand the intellectual foundations of biological unconventional computing.

---

**Nakagaki, T., Yamada, H., & Tóth, Á. (2000). Maze-solving by an amoeboid organism. *Nature*, 407(6803), 470.**

The short but enormously influential maze-solving paper. Essential reading because it established biological network optimization as a serious scientific and computational topic. The brevity of the paper — it is a one-page letter — is itself instructive: extraordinary claims can be made in very little space when the experimental design is elegant and the result is unambiguous.

---

**Shepherd, V. A. (2005). The cytomatrix as a cooperative system of macromolecular and water networks. *Current Topics in Developmental Biology*, 71, 1–46.**

An underappreciated review arguing that the cytoplasm of cells — including fungal hyphae — should be understood as a cooperative, quasi-solid network of macromolecules and water, capable of supporting information processing at the subcellular level. Relevant to the question of how electrical signals in mycelium might interact with cytoplasmic structure to produce information processing. More speculative than the other readings on this list, but asks important questions.

---

**Gorochowski, T. E., Matyjaszkiewicz, A., Todd, T., Oak, N., Kowalska, K., Reid, S., Gudelj, I., Šarić, T., Gorochowski, V., & Arndt, D. (2012). BSim: An agent-based tool for modeling bacterial populations in systems and synthetic biology. *PLOS ONE*, 7(8), e42790.**

Included not for its direct relevance to fungi but as a methodological reference for agent-based modeling of biological systems. Agent-based models are the primary computational tool for understanding how local rules in biological networks (including mycelium) produce global behavior, and this paper provides a good introduction to how such models are implemented and validated. The comparison between biological and computational agent-based systems is directly relevant to the fungal computing question.
