# Chapter 21 Key Researchers: Fungal Computing and Unconventional Intelligence

---

## Andrew Adamatzky
**University of the West of England, Bristol — Unconventional Computing Laboratory**

The central figure in fungal computing research. Adamatzky's background is in computer science and applied mathematics, and he has built his career on the study of unconventional computing substrates — including reaction-diffusion systems, slime molds, and now fungal mycelium. His work is notable for its range and for its willingness to propose audacious hypotheses, including the 2022 "language of fungi" paper that generated widespread attention and significant scientific controversy.

Adamatzky's contributions are most credibly evaluated at the empirical level: his group has documented electrical spiking activity in mycelium with greater detail and rigor than any other research group, and has developed experimental methods for characterizing spike train statistics in fungal networks. His interpretive frameworks — the language hypothesis, the computing substrate idea — are more contested, and students should engage with them critically. He is both the field's most productive researcher and its most provocative voice, and both aspects are worth taking seriously.

---

## Toshiyuki Nakagaki
**Hokkaido University — Research Institute for Electronic Science**

Nakagaki established the scientific credibility of biological network optimization through his landmark studies of *Physarum polycephalum*. His maze-solving (2000) and Tokyo-rail (2010, with Tero and colleagues) experiments are among the most cited results in unconventional computing and were directly responsible for inspiring fungal computing research.

Nakagaki's work is characterized by elegant experimental design and close collaboration with mathematicians and engineers, which has made it possible to interpret biological results in quantitatively rigorous terms. His career demonstrates what becomes possible when biologists and computational scientists work closely together on problems that are simultaneously biological and computational.

---

## Yukio-Pegio Gunji
**Waseda University — Department of Earth and Planetary Sciences (Biocomplexity)**

Gunji is a theoretical biologist and philosopher of science whose work spans biological computing, complex systems, and the philosophy of life. He has contributed to Physarum computing research and has written extensively about the philosophical implications of biological computing — specifically about what it means for a physical system to compute, and how the biological context of computing changes its meaning.

Gunji's perspective is useful for students interested in the philosophical dimensions of fungal computing. His insistence that computing in biological systems cannot be understood independently of the organism's embodied relationship to its environment provides a theoretical counterpoint to Adamatzky's more engineering-oriented perspective.

---

## Suzanne Tsuda
**Independent Researcher — formerly University of the West of England**

Tsuda (working as Soichiro Tsuda in early publications) contributed to the foundational work on Physarum logical gate implementation and was among the first researchers to demonstrate that slime mold growth patterns could implement simple Boolean functions. Her work provided the experimental template that subsequent fungal computing research has built on.

---

## Francisco Varela
**École Polytechnique, Paris (deceased 2001) — Neuroscience and Cognitive Science**

Varela is included not as a fungal computing researcher but as the intellectual architect of the framework within which fungal computing's significance can be understood. Along with Thompson and Rosch, Varela developed the enactivist account of cognition — the view that cognition is constituted by sensorimotor coupling with the environment rather than by internal computation on representations. The mycelium, which integrates sensing, computation, and embodied action in a single physical substrate, is the most extreme biological instantiation of the enactivist vision. Understanding Varela's work is essential for appreciating why fungal computing is philosophically significant beyond its practical applications.

---

## Stephanie Forrest
**Arizona State University — Biodesign Institute**

Forrest is a computer scientist and complex systems researcher whose work has explored biological principles — including immune system function, genetic algorithms, and evolutionary computation — as inspiration for computing architectures. While she has not worked directly on fungal computing, her broad framework of biologically-inspired computing is directly relevant, and her work on self-healing and adaptive computing systems addresses many of the same principles that make fungal computing conceptually interesting. Students interested in the practical applications of biological computing principles to engineering should explore her work.
