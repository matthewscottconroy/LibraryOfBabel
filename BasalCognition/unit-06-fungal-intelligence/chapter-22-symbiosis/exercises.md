# Chapter 22 Exercises: Symbiosis and the Extended Fungal Mind

## Part I: Reflection and Discussion

**1. The Wood Wide Web and Scientific Communication**
The "wood wide web" concept — the idea that trees communicate and cooperate through mycorrhizal networks — has captured enormous popular attention. The scientific evidence for the basic phenomenon (nutrient transfer between trees) is robust; the evidence for richer claims (signal transfer, coordinated behavior, kin recognition) is more contested. How should scientists communicate findings in contested areas to non-specialist audiences? What is lost if researchers are too cautious? What is lost if they are not cautious enough? Use the mycorrhizal network literature as a specific case study.

**2. Parasitic Cognition and Moral Responsibility**
If the zombie ant's behavior is produced by a distribution of cognitive work across the ant and the *Ophiocordyceps* fungus, who or what is responsible for the behavior? Discuss whether the concept of moral responsibility can meaningfully be applied to a distributed cognitive system. Then consider: in human contexts, we sometimes say that a person is not responsible for their behavior if their cognition was manipulated — by drugs, by extreme coercion, by brain disease. Does the zombie ant case illuminate or complicate the human case?

**3. The Lichen Individual**
A lichen is classified as a single species despite being composed of at least two (and possibly more) genetically distinct organisms. What does this tell us about our concept of species? What does it tell us about our concept of individuality? Should the fungal component and the algal component be considered separate individuals or parts of one individual? Does the answer change depending on whether you are asking a question about evolution, about physiology, or about cognition?

**4. Reciprocal Rewards and Intelligence**
Kiers et al. (2011) showed that mycorrhizal fungi preferentially deliver phosphorus to plant partners that provide more carbon. The authors describe this as a "biological market." Is this a useful analogy, or does it import misleading assumptions from economics? What would need to be true for the behavior of the mycorrhizal network to count as economic decision-making rather than as the mechanical outcome of local biochemical interactions? Is there a meaningful distinction?

**5. Extended Phenotypes and Extended Minds**
Compare Richard Dawkins's concept of the extended phenotype (behavioral effects of one organism's genes expressed through another organism's body) with Andy Clark and David Chalmers's concept of the extended mind (cognitive processes that extend outside the boundary of the individual). Are these the same concept, or do they differ in important ways? Use the *Ophiocordyceps* case to illustrate any differences you identify.

## Part II: Thought Experiments

**1. The Lichen Without a Partner**
The photosynthetic component of a lichen is typically a species that can survive independently outside the lichen — as a free-living alga in soil or water. The fungal component typically cannot survive without its photosynthetic partner. Imagine you could separate a mature lichen into its component organisms and grow them independently for several generations. Would the separated organisms be different from wild populations of the same species that had never been in a lichen partnership? Now reverse the question: after many generations of independent growth, could you recombine the separated partners to form a functioning lichen? What would this tell us about how deeply the partners are cognitively integrated — how much of the lichen's adaptive behavior is innate in each partner versus emergent from their interaction?

**2. The Informed Parasite**
Imagine a more sophisticated version of *Ophiocordyceps* — one that, rather than producing a fixed suite of chemical manipulators, could sense the current behavioral state of its host and modulate its chemical output accordingly, producing contextually appropriate behavioral adjustments. How much additional precision of behavioral control might this achieve? What computational resources would the fungus need to implement this more sophisticated manipulation? Now compare this imagined fungus to a real complex parasite with a distributed chemical manipulation system: what is the fungus "computing" when it adjusts its chemical output, and where does that computation take place?

**3. The Mycorrhizal Network as Decision-Maker**
Toby Kiers's work suggests that mycorrhizal networks implement something like a biological market — allocating resources based on the quality of trading partners. Extend this idea to its logical conclusion: could a sufficiently large and complex mycorrhizal network, connecting thousands of plant partners across a forest ecosystem, implement optimization computations that have consequences for the whole ecosystem? What would it look like if it did? What observable predictions would distinguish a "smart" mycorrhizal network — one implementing genuine ecosystem-level optimization — from a "dumb" one in which each local transaction is decided by local biochemistry with no global consequences?

## Part III: Laboratory Investigations

**1. Quantifying Mycorrhizal Network Architecture in Soil Cores**

*Hypothesis*: The density and connectivity of mycorrhizal fungal networks will differ significantly between soils with and without established plant communities, and will correlate with plant species diversity.

*Materials*: Soil cores (10 cm diameter × 15 cm deep) from different plant communities (e.g., a lawn, a mixed herb garden, a tree root zone, and a recently disturbed or bare soil); washing and sieving equipment; a dissecting microscope; staining reagents for fungal hyphae (trypan blue in lactoglycerol, or similar); a compound microscope with an eyepiece graticule for measuring hyphal length.

*Protocol*: Collect paired soil cores from each site. Wash a weighed soil subsample (5 g fresh weight) through a series of sieves to remove coarse particles while retaining hyphae and roots. Stain the retained material with trypan blue to distinguish fungal hyphae from plant material. Mount in glycerol and examine under 100× magnification; count hyphal intersections using the gridline intercept method to estimate total hyphal length per gram of soil.

*Analysis*: Does hyphal density correlate with plant community diversity or biomass? Are there qualitative differences (cord formation, branching patterns) between the networks in different communities? Can you detect any evidence of anastomosis events in the soil hyphal networks?

**2. Testing the Reciprocal Reward Hypothesis in Pot Experiments**

*Hypothesis*: Mycorrhizal fungi will preferentially allocate hyphal growth toward plant partners that provide more carbon, and plant partners that receive more fungal phosphorus will allocate more carbon to the fungal network.

*Materials*: Sterile potting substrate (autoclaved to remove existing mycorrhizal inocula); seedlings of a mycorrhizal host plant species (e.g., leek *Allium porrum* for AM fungi, or a birch seedling for EM fungi); mycorrhizal inocula from a known fungal species; split-pot or Petri dish systems that allow separate measurement of root growth in different compartments; radioactively or stable-isotope-labeled phosphorus solution (if available); a shading system to manipulate photosynthetic output of individual roots.

*Protocol*: Establish seedlings with mycorrhizal inocula and allow colonization for several weeks. Then shade half of each plant's root system while leaving the other half in full light. After two weeks of differential shading, measure hyphal density and, if possible, phosphorus delivery to each root compartment. Use carbon isotope labeling or root exudate measurement to estimate carbon flow from shaded vs. unshaded roots.

*Analysis*: Does the fungus preferentially colonize and deliver phosphorus to the higher-carbon roots? Does shading reduce phosphorus delivery to the shaded half within the same plant? This experiment is a simplified version of the Kiers et al. design and can be adapted to available resources.

**3. Observing Ant Behavior Near *Ophiocordyceps*-Infected Colony Members**

*Note: This experiment requires access to ant colonies in a region where *Ophiocordyceps* or related entomopathogenic fungi occur naturally. It is an observational rather than experimental study.*

*Hypothesis*: Ant workers will show detectable behavioral responses to colony members infected with entomopathogenic fungi, including avoidance, grooming, or carrying behavior, before visible fungal symptoms are apparent.

*Materials*: An ant colony in a forested area where entomopathogenic fungi are present; time-lapse or video recording equipment; a reference collection of images of infected vs. uninfected ants.

*Protocol*: Identify ant trails and observe worker behavior over extended periods during active foraging. Photograph all workers that are visibly isolated, showing unusual locomotion, or that are the focus of attention from nestmates. Follow these individuals over subsequent days to determine whether they develop visible fungal infection. Record the behavioral responses of healthy nestmates to any ants that develop visible infection: are they groomed, carried, or avoided?

*Analysis*: Can behavioral anomalies in pre-symptomatic infected ants be detected? How early in the infection process do nestmates begin responding? Is the response of nestmates more consistent with hygienic removal (carrying infected ants away) or with active grooming (attempting to remove the pathogen)?
