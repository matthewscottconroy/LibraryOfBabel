# Chapter 22 Further Reading: Symbiosis and the Extended Fungal Mind

---

**Kiers, E. T., Duhamel, M., Beesetty, Y., Mensah, J. A., Franken, O., Verbruggen, E., Fellbaum, C. R., Kowalchuk, G. A., Hart, M. M., Bago, A., & Palmer, T. M. (2011). Reciprocal rewards stabilize cooperation in the mycorrhizal symbiosis. *Science*, 333(6044), 880–882.**

The landmark paper demonstrating that mycorrhizal resource exchange is regulated by a reciprocal reward mechanism resembling a biological market. Methodologically elegant and conceptually important; the paper changed how researchers think about the "intelligence" of mycorrhizal networks by demonstrating that the network implements adaptive resource allocation, not merely passive transport. Essential reading.

---

**Simard, S. W., Perry, D. A., Jones, M. D., Myrold, D. D., Durall, D. M., & Molina, R. (1997). Net transfer of carbon between ectomycorrhizal tree species in the field. *Nature*, 388(6642), 579–582.**

The original field study demonstrating net carbon transfer between trees via shared mycorrhizal networks. This paper established the empirical basis for the "wood wide web" concept. Read it carefully alongside more recent meta-analyses that have assessed the generality and quantitative significance of the transfer; the phenomenon is real but its ecological significance continues to be debated.

---

**Sheldrake, M. (2020). *Entangled Life: How Fungi Make Our Worlds, Change Our Minds and Shape Our Futures*. Random House.**

The best general-audience account of fungal biology, including excellent chapters on mycorrhizal networks, lichen biology, and the philosophical implications of fungal cognition. Sheldrake writes with scientific rigor and genuine literary flair; his account of the mycorrhizal network and its contested significance is nuanced and honest about the limits of the evidence. Recommended as a companion to the more technical readings on this list.

---

**Hughes, D. P., Andersen, S. B., Hywel-Jones, N. L., Himaman, W., Billen, J., & Boomsma, J. J. (2011). Behavioral mechanisms and morphological symptoms of zombie ants dying from fungal infection. *BMC Ecology*, 11, 13.**

The definitive study of *Ophiocordyceps* behavioral manipulation mechanisms. Hughes and colleagues describe the morphological changes in infected ants, the behavioral sequence leading to the "death grip," and the evidence (surprising to many) that the fungus may control terminal behavior through direct muscle invasion rather than neural manipulation. Essential reading for Section 3 of this chapter.

---

**Spribille, T., Tuovinen, V., Resl, P., Vanderpool, D., Wolinski, H., Aime, M. C., Schneider, K., Stabentheiner, E., Toome-Samuels, M., & Hollister, J. D. (2016). Basidiomycete yeasts in the cortex of ascomycete macrolichens. *Science*, 353(6298), 488–492.**

The discovery that many lichens contain a third partner — basidiomycete yeasts in the cortex — that had been overlooked for 150 years. This paper is a cautionary tale about the limits of what seems well-understood, and a demonstration that even extensively studied organisms can harbor hidden complexity. Relevant to the question of what a lichen "is" and who participates in the lichen composite's adaptive behaviors.

---

**Smith, S. E., & Read, D. J. (2008). *Mycorrhizal Symbiosis* (3rd ed.). Academic Press.**

The standard technical reference on mycorrhizal biology. Comprehensive, authoritative, and detailed; this is the book you turn to when you need the full scientific picture on the mechanisms of mycorrhizal nutrient exchange, the ecology of mycorrhizal communities, or the molecular biology of the symbiosis. Not light reading, but an invaluable reference for graduate students working in this area.

---

**Clark, A., & Chalmers, D. (1998). The extended mind. *Analysis*, 58(1), 7–19.**

The philosophical paper that introduced the extended mind concept. Clark and Chalmers argue that cognitive processes can extend beyond the skull into the environment — that objects and systems that play the right functional role in cognitive processes count as part of the cognitive system. The paper is accessible and has generated enormous debate. For students interested in the philosophical dimensions of symbiotic cognition, this is essential background.

---

**Dawkins, R. (1982). *The Extended Phenotype*. Oxford University Press.**

Dawkins's argument that the effects of genes extend beyond the organism's body into the environment and into other organisms. The discussion of parasitic manipulation — including the manipulation of host behavior by parasitic genes — is directly relevant to the *Ophiocordyceps* case. Dawkins is a gifted writer and the book is accessible despite its theoretical ambition; this is one of the genuinely important books in evolutionary biology.

---

**Pontoppidan, M. B., Himaman, W., Hywel-Jones, N. L., Boomsma, J. J., & Hughes, D. P. (2009). Graveyards on the move: The spatio-temporal distribution of dead *Ophiocordyceps*-infected ants. *PLOS ONE*, 4(3), e4835.**

A careful field study of the spatial distribution of *Ophiocordyceps*-killed ants in relation to the microhabitat features of the forest floor. Documents the precision with which infected ants are directed to specific microhabitats optimal for fungal spore dispersal, and shows that uninfected ants avoid the "graveyards." Provides essential empirical grounding for the behavioral manipulation discussion.

---

**Redecker, D., Kodner, R., & Graham, L. E. (2000). Glomalean fungi from the Ordovician. *Science*, 289(5486), 1920–1921.**

A short but important paper reporting fossil evidence of mycorrhizal fungi from the Ordovician period — approximately 460 million years ago, predating or coinciding with the colonization of land by plants. The antiquity of the mycorrhizal symbiosis is relevant to understanding its evolutionary significance: this is not a recent or fragile partnership but one that has been integral to terrestrial plant evolution for hundreds of millions of years.
