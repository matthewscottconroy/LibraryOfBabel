# Chapter 22 Key Concepts: Symbiosis and the Extended Fungal Mind

---

## Mycorrhiza (plural: Mycorrhizae)
An intimate symbiosis between a fungus and the roots of a plant, in which the fungus colonizes root tissue and extends hyphal networks into the surrounding soil. Mycorrhizal fungi deliver mineral nutrients — particularly phosphorus and nitrogen — to the plant, and in return receive photosynthetically fixed carbon. Mycorrhizal associations are among the most ancient and widespread symbioses in terrestrial ecology, present in the roots of approximately 80–90 percent of all land plant species. The two major forms — arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) and ectomycorrhizal (EM) — differ in the anatomy of the fungus-root interface and in the ecological distribution of plant hosts.

---

## Lichen
A stable, intimate composite organism formed by the association of one or more fungi (typically Ascomycetes) with one or more photosynthetic partners (green algae, cyanobacteria, or both). The fungal component provides the structural framework (the thallus) that protects the photosynthetic component from desiccation, UV radiation, and mechanical damage; the photosynthetic component provides photosynthetically fixed carbon that the non-photosynthetic fungus cannot produce. Recent research has revealed that many lichens also contain basidiomycete yeasts and bacterial communities as additional partners. Lichens are classified as single species despite their composite nature, and they represent one of the most successful strategies for colonizing extreme environments in the biosphere.

---

## Arbuscular Mycorrhizal (AM) Symbiosis
A form of mycorrhizal association in which the fungal partner (phylum Glomeromycota) penetrates the cells of plant roots, forming highly branched structures called arbuscules at the fungal-plant interface. AM fungi do not form significant extradical hyphal networks (beyond individual hyphae extending into the soil) and colonize the majority of herbaceous plant species as well as many trees. The arbuscule is the site of bidirectional nutrient exchange — phosphorus and nitrogen from fungus to plant, sugars from plant to fungus — across a dramatically amplified contact surface.

---

## Ectomycorrhizal (EM) Symbiosis
A form of mycorrhizal association in which the fungal partner forms a sheath around the plant root (the mantle) and penetrates between (but not into) root cells to form a structure called the Hartig net. EM fungi also extend substantial hyphal cords into the surrounding soil, forming the extradical mycelial networks that constitute the "wood wide web" in forest soils. EM associations are characteristic of trees — particularly oaks, beeches, pines, and birches — and are present in many of the world's most ecologically important forest types.

---

## Biological Market
A framework for understanding the regulation of resource exchange in mutualistic symbioses, in which both partners adjust their investment based on the quality of trading partners, analogous to supply and demand in economic markets. Kiers et al. (2011) demonstrated that the mycorrhizal symbiosis shows properties consistent with a biological market: fungi preferentially deliver phosphorus to plant partners that provide more carbon, and plants preferentially support fungal partners that provide more phosphorus. The biological market framework is a useful conceptual tool for understanding adaptive resource allocation in symbioses without requiring that the partners deliberate or represent the exchange.

## Ophiocordyceps
A genus of parasitic fungi (family Ophiocordycipitaceae) that infect insects and other arthropods, manipulating their behavior in ways that serve the fungus's transmission and reproduction. The most studied species, *O. unilateralis*, infects carpenter ants and directs them to specific microhabitats where, after death, the fungus produces spore-bearing structures. *Ophiocordyceps* is the most dramatic known example of fungal behavioral manipulation and has attracted significant attention as a case study in the question of distributed agency in host-parasite systems.

---

## Parasitic Manipulation
The behavioral control of a host organism by a parasite, achieved through mechanisms (chemical, neural, muscular, or other) that redirect the host's behavior in ways that serve the parasite's adaptive interests at the expense of the host's own fitness. Parasitic manipulation is widespread across host-parasite systems but is most spectacularly exhibited by *Ophiocordyceps* and related fungi. The study of parasitic manipulation raises fundamental questions about the boundaries of individual cognition and agency: if a parasite controls a host's behavior using the host's own nervous system, the cognitive system that produces the behavior is distributed across both organisms.

---

## Extended Phenotype
Richard Dawkins's concept that the phenotypic effects of genes extend beyond the organism's body into the environment and into other organisms. The manipulated behavior of a host infected by a behavioral parasite is part of the parasite's extended phenotype: the behavior is a phenotypic expression of the parasite's genome. The extended phenotype concept is closely related to the extended mind concept but differs in focus: the extended phenotype describes causal relationships between genes and their effects, while the extended mind describes the distribution of cognitive processes across organism boundaries.

---

## Distributed Cognition
The theoretical framework holding that cognitive processes are not confined to individual minds but can be distributed across multiple agents, artifacts, and environmental structures. Distributed cognition was developed primarily in the context of human cognitive systems by Edwin Hutchins, but applies to biological systems wherever adaptive outcomes are produced by the coordinated activity of multiple organisms or components that do not individually possess the cognitive capacity to produce those outcomes. Mycorrhizal networks, lichen composites, and *Ophiocordyceps*-ant systems all illustrate different aspects of distributed cognition in ecological context.

---

## Thallus (in lichens)
The body of a lichen — the composite structure formed by the fungal partner (and, in some cases, yeast partners) that houses the photosynthetic component. The thallus varies enormously in form across lichen species: from flat, encrusting forms on rock surfaces, to leaf-like foliose forms that extend from bark, to shrubby, three-dimensional fruticose forms hanging from tree branches. The structure of the thallus is largely determined by the fungal component and provides the environmental buffering — protection from desiccation, UV shielding, mechanical support — that enables the photosynthetic partner to survive in otherwise inhospitable environments.
