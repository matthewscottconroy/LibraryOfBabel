# Chapter 22 Key Researchers: Symbiosis and the Extended Fungal Mind

---

## Toby Kiers
**Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam — Department of Ecological Science**

Toby Kiers is one of the most important figures in the study of mycorrhizal intelligence. Her research group has developed the quantitative tools and experimental frameworks that have made it possible to ask precise questions about how mycorrhizal networks allocate resources — and to get precise answers.

Kiers's 2011 paper in *Science* established that the mycorrhizal symbiosis is regulated by a reciprocal reward mechanism: both plant and fungal partners preferentially invest in partners that provide more in return. Subsequent work by her group has used increasingly sophisticated experimental approaches — isotope tracing, microfluidics, fluorescent markers — to study resource movement through fungal networks with single-hypha resolution.

Kiers is also notable for her willingness to apply concepts from economics and evolutionary theory to biological systems, while remaining careful about the limits of these analogies. Her framework of "biological markets" is a productive way of thinking about mycorrhizal intelligence that does not require attributing consciousness or deliberation to the network.

## Suzanne Simard
**University of British Columbia — Department of Forest and Conservation Sciences**

Simard is the scientist most responsible for bringing the "wood wide web" concept to public attention. Her 1997 *Nature* paper established that carbon can be transferred between trees via shared mycorrhizal networks, and her subsequent research has explored the ecology of mycorrhizal networks in British Columbia forests over several decades.

Simard's work is ecologically important, and her commitment to long-term field research in a difficult environment is admirable. Her public communications — including a widely-viewed TED talk and her 2021 memoir *Finding the Mother Tree* — have, however, sometimes outpaced the evidentiary support for the more expansive claims about forest "communication" and "cooperation" that she has made. Students should read her research papers carefully and compare them with her popular writings, noting where the scientific hedges are dropped and where they should be restored.

## David Pimentel Hughes
**Pennsylvania State University — Department of Entomology and Biology**

David Hughes is the leading researcher on *Ophiocordyceps* behavioral manipulation. His group has combined field ecology, experimental manipulation, and advanced imaging techniques (including scanning electron microscopy and histology of infected ant tissues) to characterize the mechanisms by which the fungus controls ant behavior.

Hughes's work has been responsible for several major advances in our understanding of fungal behavioral manipulation, including the surprising discovery that the fungus may control terminal biting behavior through direct invasion of jaw musculature rather than through neural manipulation. His research is a model of how to approach a complex biological phenomenon — zombie ant behavior — with methodological rigor and appropriate skepticism about simple explanations.

## Toby Spribille
**University of Alberta — Department of Biological Sciences**

Spribille made the unexpected discovery, published in *Science* in 2016, that many macrolichens contain a third partner — basidiomycete yeasts — in addition to the ascomycete fungus and photosynthetic alga that had been recognized for over 150 years. This discovery fundamentally changed our understanding of lichen biology and serves as a reminder of how much biological complexity can remain hidden even in thoroughly studied systems.

Spribille's work is methodologically noteworthy for its creative use of metagenomics — sequencing all of the DNA in a sample to identify all organisms present — to detect the hidden yeast partner. The approach is increasingly important in symbiosis research more broadly, where hidden partners may play important functional roles that have been overlooked.

---

## Jacobus Boomsma
**University of Copenhagen — Centre for Social Evolution**

Boomsma is an evolutionary biologist whose work on the evolution of eusociality and social insect biology intersects with the *Ophiocordyceps* story through his collaboration with David Hughes on the zombie ant system. His broader research on the evolution of obligate mutualistic partnerships — including the leaf-cutter ant-fungus farming symbiosis — provides important evolutionary context for understanding how deep coevolutionary partnerships between fungi and insects develop.

---

## Albert Bernhard Frank
**Agricultural High School, Berlin (1839–1900)**

Frank is included for historical reasons: he coined the term "mycorrhiza" in 1885 and was the first to describe and systematically study the symbiotic relationship between fungi and plant roots. His work was initially controversial — the idea that plant roots harbored permanent fungal associations was not immediately welcomed — but has since been recognized as one of the foundational contributions to plant biology. Frank's legacy is a reminder that the most important scientific discoveries are often those that force us to reconsider what we thought was fundamental.
