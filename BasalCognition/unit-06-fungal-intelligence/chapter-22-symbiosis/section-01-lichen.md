# Section 1: Lichen — The Original Partnership

## What a Lichen Is

The Swiss botanist Simon Schwendener was not popular when he proposed, in 1867, that lichens are not simple organisms but composite associations of fungi and algae. The mycologists of his day found the suggestion offensive — a challenge to the integrity of their taxonomic categories. Lichens had been classified as plants, and that was that.

Schwendener was right, of course. A lichen is a stable, intimate association between a fungus (almost always an Ascomycete, occasionally a Basidiomycete) and one or more photosynthetic partners — a green alga, a cyanobacterium, or sometimes both. In most lichens, the fungus provides the bulk of the visible structure: the tough, weather-resistant body that we see growing on rocks and bark. The photosynthetic partners live inside this fungal architecture, protected from desiccation and mechanical damage, and in return provide the energy currency — fixed carbon in the form of sugars — that the fungus cannot produce on its own.

This much is settled science. What has become somewhat more interesting — and more complicated — is the question of who else is in a lichen. Metagenomic analyses have revealed that lichens also harbor communities of bacteria (including nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria beyond any lichenized component), other fungi, and sometimes even yeasts. What we thought was a partnership of two has turned out to be a community — though the two-partner model captures the most important nutritional relationship.

Recent work has also demonstrated that some lichens contain basidiomycete yeasts in their cortex — the outermost layer — that appear to be consistent and functionally important partners, not mere contaminants (Spribille et al., 2016). This finding, published in *Science*, challenged the long-standing two-partner model and showed that our understanding of lichen complexity was still incomplete. The lesson: even well-studied symbioses can conceal hidden partners.

## Life in Extreme Environments

The most obvious evidence for whatever adaptive intelligence the lichen composite possesses is its ability to survive, and even to thrive, in environments that would quickly kill most other organisms.

Lichens grow on bare rock in Antarctic dry valleys, where temperatures can drop below -60°C and liquid water is unavailable for most of the year. They grow in deserts where the only moisture comes from overnight fog. They colonize volcanic lava flows where no soil exists and minerals must be extracted directly from rock. They are among the first colonizers of newly exposed substrates after glacial retreat, rockfall, or volcanic eruption.

This extreme hardiness is a property of the composite organism, not of either partner alone. The fungal component provides the tough, hydrophobic outer cortex that protects the photosynthetic partner from desiccation and UV radiation. The photosynthetic component provides the energy supply that allows the composite to survive long periods of desiccation followed by rapid reactivation when moisture is available. Neither partner could achieve this combination alone: the fungus without its photosynthetic partner has no energy source; the alga without the fungal architecture would desiccate and die.

The lichen composite is, in this sense, a cognitive achievement: it solves problems — of energy acquisition, desiccation tolerance, UV protection, mineral extraction — that neither component could solve independently. Whether this constitutes "intelligence" depends on what we mean by that word, but it is unambiguously adaptive problem-solving through partnership.

## Behavioral Plasticity in Lichen

What behavior does a lichen exhibit? At first glance, very little. Lichens grow slowly — sometimes less than a millimeter per year on rock surfaces — and they do not obviously move, respond to predators, or coordinate complex activities. A lichen seems more like a stone than like an organism.

But closer examination reveals a degree of physiological plasticity that is remarkable for an organism without a nervous system. Lichens regulate their own desiccation status: the hydrophobic compounds in the cortex slow water loss but do not prevent it, so that the lichen equilibrates with ambient humidity. When rehydrated, photosynthesis resumes within minutes — a rapid transition that requires coordinated physiological changes in both fungal and algal components.

Lichens also regulate their sun exposure. Some species can control the position of the photosynthetic partner within the thallus by adjusting the structure of the fungal architecture — moving the algal cells deeper in the thallus when light intensity is high, reducing the risk of photoinhibition, and moving them closer to the surface when light is low, maximizing photosynthetic input. This adjustment is made by the composite organism as a whole, not by either partner independently, and it represents a genuine regulatory response to environmental conditions.

## Chemical Ecology and Ecosystem Engineering

Lichens produce an extraordinary diversity of secondary metabolites — chemical compounds that appear to serve defensive and ecological functions. Lichen acids, the most numerous class, make lichen tissue unpalatable to most herbivores. Some lichen compounds are toxic to bacteria and other fungi. Others serve as UV absorbers, protecting the photosynthetic component from radiation damage.

These chemical properties make lichens important actors in their ecosystems — not merely passive inhabitants of rock surfaces but active modifiers of their environments. The slow weathering of rock by lichen acids is a major pathway for soil formation over geological time. In some ecosystems, particularly in high-latitude and high-altitude environments, lichens constitute the primary substrate for reindeer and caribou grazing and are therefore a keystone element of trophic webs.

The question of "intelligence" in lichen is, in the end, a question about what behavioral complexity and environmental responsiveness are sufficient to merit the term. A lichen does not plan. It does not represent its environment. It does not make deliberate decisions. But it does respond to environmental conditions with appropriate physiological adjustments, it does engineer its environment in ways that create conditions favorable to its persistence, and it does achieve, through the partnership of two organisms, capabilities that neither partner possesses alone.

## Distributed Cognition in the Composite

The concept of distributed cognition, as developed by Edwin Hutchins and others, holds that cognitive processes can be distributed across multiple agents, artifacts, and environmental structures — that cognition is not confined to individual minds but can be spread across a system. Hutchins's work focused on human cognitive systems, particularly navigation on ships, but the concept applies readily to biological systems.

The lichen composite is an extreme case of distributed cognition: the "cognitive" work of responding to the environment and regulating the organism's internal state is distributed across two (or more) organisms that do not share a nervous system, do not communicate using anything like a language, and may have divergent evolutionary interests. The coordination between them is achieved through physiological and chemical interfaces that have been refined by coevolution over hundreds of millions of years.

What makes this interesting for our purposes is that the lichen composite achieves adaptive outcomes — survival in extreme environments, physiological regulation, ecosystem engineering — without any individual component being able to achieve them alone, and without any central coordination. The intelligence, if we choose to call it that, is an emergent property of the partnership. It is not located in the fungus or in the alga; it is located in the relationship between them.

This is, in a precise sense, the most distributed form of cognition we have considered so far in this book. The lichen is not a single cognitive agent with a distributed nervous system. It is two cognitive agents whose partnership produces outcomes that neither could achieve independently. The whole, here, is not just greater than the sum of its parts; it is qualitatively different from either part.

## Lichen as Ecosystem Engineers

The concept of ecosystem engineering — the idea that some organisms modify their environments in ways that create or maintain habitats for other organisms — was developed primarily with large animals in mind (beavers, elephants, prairie dogs). But lichens are among the most important ecosystem engineers at the microscale and over geological timescales.

Lichen-covered rock surfaces differ from bare rock in temperature fluctuation (lichens buffer extreme temperature swings), in moisture retention (lichen thalli hold water), in nitrogen content (nitrogen-fixing lichen partner species enrich the substrate), and in organic matter content (lichen decomposition produces organic acids and mineral compounds that begin the process of soil formation). These modifications make lichen-colonized surfaces more hospitable for subsequent colonizers — mosses, vascular plants, invertebrates — than bare rock.

In this sense, the lichen composite is not merely adapted to its environment. It actively shapes its environment in ways that feed back on its own persistence and that facilitate the development of more complex ecosystems. This is engineering intelligence at a geological timescale, and it is achieved without anything resembling a brain.
