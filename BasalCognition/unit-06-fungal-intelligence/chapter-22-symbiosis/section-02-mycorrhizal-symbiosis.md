# Section 2: Mycorrhizal Symbiosis and the Wood Wide Web

## The Basic Biology

The term "mycorrhiza" — coined by the German botanist Albert Bernhard Frank in 1885 — combines the Greek words for fungus and root. Mycorrhizal associations between plants and fungi are among the most ancient and widespread symbioses in biology: fossil evidence suggests they have existed for at least 450 million years, since before plants colonized land (Redecker et al., 2000). Today, mycorrhizal fungi are partners with the roots of an estimated 80–90 percent of all land plant species.

Two major forms of mycorrhizal association are relevant to our discussion.

**Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi** (phylum Glomeromycota) form associations with the majority of herbaceous plants and many trees. In AM symbiosis, the fungal hyphae penetrate the plant root cells, forming characteristic branched structures called arbuscules — highly folded interfaces across which nutrient exchange between plant and fungus takes place. AM fungi do not form significant networks of cord-like hyphae in the soil; they extend individual hyphae that can extend up to 10–15 cm from the root.

**Ectomycorrhizal (EM) fungi** (distributed across multiple fungal taxa, including many Basidiomycetes and some Ascomycetes) form associations primarily with trees — particularly oaks, beeches, pines, and birches. In EM symbiosis, the fungal hyphae surround the root surface and penetrate between (but not into) root cells, forming a characteristic sheath called the Hartig net. EM fungi also extend substantial cord-like hyphal networks into the surrounding soil, and it is these networks that form the basis of the "wood wide web."

The fundamental benefit to the plant is access to mineral nutrients, particularly phosphorus and nitrogen, which the thin fungal hyphae can acquire from microsites in the soil that are inaccessible to plant roots. In return, the fungus receives photosynthetically fixed carbon — up to 20–30 percent of a tree's carbon production may be transferred to its mycorrhizal partners (Smith & Read, 2008).

## Resource Transfer: What Is Well-Established

The transfer of nutrients from fungal partners to plant roots, and of carbon from plant roots to fungal partners, is among the best-established facts in plant biology. Decades of isotope tracer experiments — using radioactively or stable-isotope-labeled carbon, phosphorus, and nitrogen — have documented these transfers in quantitative detail.

What is perhaps surprising is the direction and scale of carbon transfer that sometimes occurs between trees via their shared mycorrhizal networks. Several experimental studies have shown that carbon fixed by one tree can be detected in adjacent trees connected through a shared EM fungal network (Simard et al., 1997). The amounts transferred are typically small relative to each tree's own carbon budget, and the significance of the transfer for tree nutrition is contested. But the physical fact of inter-tree carbon movement through mycorrhizal networks appears robust.

Similarly, phosphorus and nitrogen transferred from fungal hyphae to plant roots through mycorrhizal interfaces has been demonstrated repeatedly. The fungus acquires mineral nutrients from the soil matrix and delivers them across the fungal-plant interface; in return, plant-derived sugars flow in the other direction. This nutritional exchange is mediated by specific transport proteins at the fungal-plant interface that have been identified at the molecular level.

## Resource Allocation Intelligence: Kiers et al. 2011

A particularly important paper for our discussion is Kiers et al. (2011), published in *Science*, which demonstrated that the resource exchange in AM symbiosis is not simply a passive transfer but appears to be regulated in a way that rewards cooperative partners and punishes non-cooperative ones.

The experimental approach was elegant: Kiers and colleagues manipulated the photosynthetic output of individual plant roots by shading them and then measured the allocation of fungal-delivered phosphorus to those roots versus to roots with normal photosynthetic activity. They found that the fungal network preferentially delivered phosphorus to the roots that were providing more carbon — and that roots receiving more phosphorus returned more carbon to the fungus. The exchange appeared to be stabilized by a reciprocal reward mechanism: both partners preferentially invested in partners that provided greater benefits.

The authors described this as a "biological market" — an economic framework in which the allocation of resources is driven by supply and demand rather than by fixed rules. This framing is provocative: it implies that the fungal network is performing a kind of optimization — sensing the return it receives from different plant partners and allocating its resources accordingly.

Whether this constitutes intelligence depends, as always, on how one defines the term. The mechanism need not involve representation or deliberation: the fungal network might implement the observed allocation through purely mechanical means, with carbon availability at a given interface directly and mechanically affecting the rate of phosphorus export across that interface. The adaptive outcome — preferential investment in more productive partners — emerges from local biochemical interactions without any global calculation.

Nevertheless, the result is striking. The mycorrhizal fungal network behaves as if it is optimizing its resource allocation across a network of trading partners — adjusting its investment in each partner based on the returns it receives. This is a form of distributed economic intelligence that operates through the same principles of local reinforcement that we have seen in mycelial network optimization.

## The Wood Wide Web: Promise and Oversimplification

The phrase "wood wide web" — coined as a playful allusion to the World Wide Web — refers to the idea that the mycorrhizal networks connecting trees in a forest constitute an information and resource exchange network analogous to the internet. The concept has become enormously popular in public science communication, most prominently through Suzanne Simard's research and her 2021 memoir *Finding the Mother Tree*.

At its core, the wood wide web concept rests on the demonstrated facts of carbon and nutrient transfer between trees via mycorrhizal networks. Beyond this, a number of more expansive claims have been made, of varying evidentiary support.

**The claim that trees communicate distress signals via mycorrhizal networks** — that a tree attacked by insects, for example, sends chemical warning signals through the network to neighboring trees — is more contested. Some experimental studies report chemical signals (including defense-inducing compounds) moving between plants connected by mycorrhizal networks. Others have failed to replicate such effects, and the quantities and speeds involved make it unclear whether mycorrhizal signal transmission is a functional communication system in the way that airborne volatile signaling (which is well-established) is. The evidence here is genuinely mixed, and popular accounts that treat mycorrhizal chemical signaling as established fact are overstating the case.

**The claim that "mother trees" — large, old trees at the center of a mycorrhizal network — preferentially support their offspring** through the network is based primarily on Simard's own research. Some experiments have found preferential carbon transfer from established trees to seedlings of the same species in shared mycorrhizal networks; others have not. The interpretation of such transfer as maternal "care" or "recognition" is speculative and not well-supported by the mechanistic evidence. The transfer, where it exists, could reflect chemical affinity between genetically related individuals without requiring any form of kin recognition.

What the popular accounts of the wood wide web often miss is how messy and contested the underlying science is. Mycorrhizal networks are genuinely important — for ecosystem function, for forest resilience, for nutrient cycling — and the research of Simard, Kiers, and others has revealed real and fascinating phenomena. But the leap from "carbon moves between trees via fungal networks" to "trees communicate and cooperate through a forest internet" requires many intermediate steps that have not all been validated.

## The Toby Kiers Research Program

Beyond the specific 2011 paper discussed above, Toby Kiers and her group at the Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam have developed a research program that is genuinely important for understanding intelligence in mycorrhizal networks.

Using fluorescent tracers, microfluidics, and quantitative imaging, Kiers's group has been able to watch, in real time, how nutrients move through mycorrhizal networks in response to experimental manipulations. Their work has revealed that fungal networks actively direct nutrient flows — that the direction and rate of resource transport through the network responds to local conditions in ways that produce adaptive outcomes at the whole-network level.

In a striking line of experiments, Kiers and colleagues have used engineered AM fungal networks grown on artificial substrates to manipulate and measure nutrient allocation with unprecedented precision. These experiments have confirmed the "biological market" dynamics described in the 2011 paper and have begun to reveal the cellular and molecular mechanisms behind them. The direction of the flow within individual hyphae can be influenced by nutrient availability at remote locations — consistent with a system in which local information is integrated at network nodes to produce directed transport.

This work is careful, quantitative, and mechanistic. It does not require attributing consciousness or deliberation to the fungal network. But it does demonstrate that the network implements a form of distributed economic intelligence — one that optimizes resource allocation across a complex trading network in ways that have adaptive consequences for both fungal and plant partners.

## What the Evidence Warrants

A clear-eyed assessment of mycorrhizal intelligence must distinguish several levels of claim:

1. **Nutrient transfer between trees via mycorrhizal networks**: Demonstrated; quantitatively established; not contested.

2. **Adaptive resource allocation by the fungal network based on partner quality**: Demonstrated by Kiers et al. (2011) and subsequent work; mechanistic basis under investigation; more robustly supported than many popular accounts suggest.

3. **Chemical signal transfer between plants via mycorrhizal networks**: Evidence exists but is mixed; contested in the scientific community; popular accounts overstate the case.

4. **Coordinated forest-level behavior mediated by mycorrhizal networks**: Speculative; not established by available evidence; requires caution.

The mycorrhizal network is a genuinely remarkable biological system. Whether it constitutes an "extended forest mind" or an "underground internet" depends critically on which of these claims one is entertaining and what standard of evidence one applies. The first two claims are sufficient to establish that mycorrhizal networks perform distributed intelligence in a meaningful sense. The latter two require more and better evidence before they can be accepted.
