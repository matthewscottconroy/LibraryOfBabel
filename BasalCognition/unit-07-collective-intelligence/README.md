## Unit Introduction

Intelligence, we have argued, is not a property of individual cells, or even of individual organisms. It is a property of systems — of organized interactions between components that give rise to capacities no component possesses alone. We have seen this at the molecular level (signal integration networks), at the cellular level (biofilm communities), and at the organismal level (Physarum network optimization). Now we examine it at the social level: the cognition of groups.

The field of swarm intelligence — closely related to but not identical with basal cognition — studies the cognitive capacities that emerge when individual agents, following simple local rules, interact to produce sophisticated collective behavior. Ants find shortest paths through complex environments. Honeybees select optimal nest sites through a democratic process that rivals human deliberative mechanisms. Termites construct architectures of thermodynamic sophistication without any individual understanding the design. These are not metaphors. They are engineering achievements of real animals, explicable in mechanistic terms, and genuinely impressive.

## The Key Conceptual Challenge

The most difficult conceptual challenge in this unit is the question of what counts as a cognitive *agent*. When an ant colony selects a foraging route, is the colony the agent, or are the individual ants? When a honeybee swarm chooses a nest site, is the swarm the decider?

The answer matters because it determines what we count as a cognitive unit. If the colony is the agent, then the colony cognizes. If only the individual ants cognize, and the colony's behavior is merely the sum of their individual cognitions, then the colony-level description is a shorthand, not a genuine attribution of cognition.

We argue for a position that is nuanced on this point: collective systems can be genuine cognitive agents when they satisfy the SIA criteria at the collective level — when the collective, as a unit, senses relevant environmental features, integrates information from distributed sources, and acts in ways that tend to maintain the collective's continued organization. Ant colonies and honeybee swarms appear to satisfy these criteria. We examine the argument and the evidence carefully.

## Connecting to the Rest of the Book

Unit VII is the culminating empirical unit before the book turns to theoretical frameworks. It brings together themes from all previous empirical units — cellular communication (Unit II), bacterial quorum sensing (Unit III), protist aggregation (Unit IV), plant communities (Unit V), fungal networks (Unit VI) — and shows how collective intelligence at larger scales shares common organizational principles with collective intelligence at smaller scales. This convergence motivates the theoretical frameworks of Unit IX, which attempt to identify the common architecture beneath all of these phenomena.
