# Chapter 23 Exercises: Swarm Intelligence and the Emergent Group Mind

---

## Part I: Reflection and Discussion

**1. Weak vs. Strong Emergence**
The chapter argues that swarm intelligence is weakly emergent — in principle predictable from individual behavioral rules — rather than strongly emergent. What would the consequences be, for the study of collective cognition, if this conclusion turned out to be wrong? What evidence could, in principle, show that collective behavior is strongly emergent and not predictable from individual rules? Is strong emergence in animal swarms a scientific hypothesis that can be tested, or is it a philosophical claim that lies beyond empirical investigation?

**2. Quorum Sensing as Decision Mechanism**
Bees use quorum sensing to avoid premature commitment to a nest site: they wait until a threshold number of scouts are committed before triggering the swarm's departure. This mechanism improves accuracy but at the cost of speed — it takes longer to reach a quorum. How do you think the quorum threshold is calibrated by natural selection? What environmental and ecological factors would push the threshold up or down? What would the behavioral consequences be of a colony with too high a threshold? Too low a threshold?

**3. Stigmergy and Human Technology**
The chapter argues that termite mounds are part of the termite colony's cognitive system — that the cognitive boundary of the colony includes the mound. Apply the same logic to human technology. Is the internet part of the human cognitive system? Is a library? Is a smartphone? Where, if anywhere, would you draw the line between a cognitive tool (which is part of the system) and a mere physical tool (which is not)? What criterion distinguishes them?

**4. The Superorganism Concept**
Some researchers describe insect colonies as "superorganisms" — entities that function as single organisms at the colony level, with the individual insects playing roles analogous to cells in a multicellular organism. What are the strongest arguments for this view? What are the strongest objections? Does the comparison to multicellular organisms illuminate something important, or does it create more confusion than it resolves? Consider: what would the multicellular organism analog of swarm intelligence be?

**5. Transfer to Human Institutions**
Thomas Seeley has argued that human democratic institutions might learn from bee swarm decision-making. What specific principles of honeybee democracy does he think are transferable? Are there aspects of human social and cognitive organization that make direct transfer problematic? Conversely, are there aspects of bee swarm decision-making that are actually already present in well-functioning human institutions, perhaps unknowingly?

## Part II: Thought Experiments

**1. The Inverted Colony**
Imagine an ant colony in which, instead of workers having lower thresholds for their specialized tasks, all workers have identical thresholds for all tasks — a perfectly homogeneous colony. What would you expect the colony's behavior to look like? Could it maintain a stable division of labor? What would happen when demand for one task suddenly increased? Now compare this to real colonies: what does the actual heterogeneity of individual thresholds contribute to colony-level intelligence, and why might it have evolved?

**2. The Scout with False Information**
A honeybee scout performs the waggle dance for a nest site that does not actually exist — perhaps it found the site but it was subsequently destroyed, or perhaps (per the thought experiment) it is dancing fraudulently. What would happen to the swarm's decision process? How would the quorum-sensing mechanism respond to a scout that dances vigorously but whose recruits cannot verify the site? Now extend this scenario: is there a selection pressure in natural bee populations for scouts to dance "honestly"? How is honest signaling enforced in the biological market model of the waggle dance?

**3. Scaling Swarm Intelligence**
The chapter discusses swarms at the scale of thousands of bees or ants. Consider what would happen if you scaled up: if 10 million bees could communicate via waggle dances, would their collective decision-making be proportionately better? Or would new problems emerge — information overload, coordination failures, susceptibility to false consensus — that limit the scaling of swarm intelligence? More generally: is there an optimal colony size for swarm intelligence, and if so, what determines it? What does this imply about the limits of distributed cognition as a biological strategy?

## Part III: Laboratory Investigations

**1. Demonstrating the Double Bridge Effect with Ants**

*Hypothesis*: An ant colony will converge on the shorter of two routes connecting nest to food through a mechanism of pheromone-mediated positive feedback.

*Materials*: A colony of common house ants (*Lasius niger* or similar), accessible with a clear trail to a food source; a bridge apparatus constructed from cardboard or thin wood, with two parallel paths between nest and food zone, one shorter than the other; a small food reward (sugar water on a cotton pad).

*Protocol*: Establish a colony foraging to a food source with both bridge paths initially equally accessible. Place one path 5–10 cm shorter than the other (without the ants being able to observe the difference from the nest side). Monitor which path the ants use over the first 30 minutes, counting the number of ants on each path at 5-minute intervals. Repeat the trial three to five times, changing which path is shorter between trials (to control for any positional bias).

*Analysis*: Does the colony consistently converge on the shorter path? What is the time course of convergence — does it happen quickly or slowly? Is the convergence complete (all ants on the shorter path) or does some traffic persist on the longer path? What does partial convergence suggest about the noise in the system?

**2. Mapping the Waggle Dance**

*Note: This experiment requires access to a honeybee observation hive — a hive with a glass wall that allows observation of bees on the comb. Many university agricultural departments have these.*

*Hypothesis*: The duration of waggle dance runs is correlated with the quality of food sources, and the direction of dances changes predictably with sun position.

*Materials*: An observation hive; a stopwatch; a compass; artificial food sources (colored sugar water feeders) at known directions and distances from the hive, and with known concentrations.

*Protocol*: Observe bee dances on the comb and record: (a) the angle of the waggle run relative to vertical, (b) the duration of each waggle run, (c) the number of times the dancer repeats the circuit. Place food sources at different directions and distances; mark individual foragers with small paint dots to identify them. Track the dances of known foragers visiting known food sources.

*Analysis*: Can you decode the dance direction to predict the compass direction of the food source? Does the dance duration correlate with food source distance? Does the number of dance repetitions correlate with sugar concentration or other quality measures? Try to attend a time when the sun moves substantially (early morning or late afternoon) and see if the dance angle shifts to compensate.

**3. Collective Decision-Making in a Simple Animal Group**

*Hypothesis*: A group of fish or other schooling animals will collectively avoid a simulated predator stimulus more effectively than an equivalent number of isolated individuals, as measured by response time and trajectory.

*Materials*: A tank of schooling fish (guppies or zebra fish work well); a visual stimulus that simulates an approaching predator (a moving dark silhouette on a screen, or a shadow moved across the tank); a camera for recording responses; software for tracking individual and collective trajectories.

*Protocol*: Present the predator stimulus to groups of different sizes (1, 5, 10, 20 fish) and to isolated individuals. Measure: (a) latency from stimulus onset to first visible behavioral response, (b) the fraction of group members that respond within 2 seconds, (c) the coherence of the group's escape response (measured by the variance in escape direction among group members).

*Analysis*: Does group size affect response speed? Does group membership affect the fraction of individuals that respond? Is the escape direction more consistent in groups than in isolated individuals? What mechanism could explain any group-size effects — is the group's collective decision to flee faster than any individual's independent decision, and why?
