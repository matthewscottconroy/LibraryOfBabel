# Chapter 24: The Evolution of Cooperation and Group Cognition

## The Problem That Shouldn't Exist

There is something that natural selection should have eliminated long ago: cooperation. If natural selection favors the fittest individuals, and if cooperating with others costs the cooperator something — energy, time, risk — then cooperators should be outcompeted by cheaters who take the benefits of others' cooperation without paying the costs. The evolutionarily stable strategy, in a world of selfish optimization, should be defection: take what you can, give nothing back.

And yet cooperation is everywhere. Bacteria form biofilms, coordinating their behavior and sharing nutrients through a public goods system that is vulnerable to exploitation by non-cooperating strains — and yet the biofilms persist. Worker bees forego reproduction and devote their lives to the colony. Vampire bats share blood meals with hungry colony members who are unrelated to them. Human societies cooperate at scales and degrees of sophistication that dwarf anything observed in other animals, maintaining institutions and agreements that benefit the group at significant cost to many individuals.

How did this happen? How did cooperation evolve in the face of selection against it? And what does the evolution of cooperation have to do with the evolution of collective intelligence?

These are the questions of this chapter. They sit at the intersection of evolutionary biology, game theory, behavioral ecology, and cognitive science — and the answers they have generated are among the most important intellectual achievements of 20th-century biology.

## The Major Transitions Framework

The most productive framework for understanding the evolution of cooperation in a deep sense is the Major Transitions in Evolution, developed by John Maynard Smith and Eörs Szathmáry and published in their landmark 1995 book.

Maynard Smith and Szathmáry identified a series of transitions in the history of life — moments when previously independent entities gave up their independence and merged into larger cooperative units. Replicating molecules became the first cells. Prokaryotic cells were colonized by other prokaryotes to become eukaryotic cells. Eukaryotic cells organized into multicellular bodies. And individual animals, in some lineages, organized into social groups so integrated that they function as superorganisms.

Each of these transitions involved the same fundamental challenge: how do you prevent cheating? When individual replicators merge into a cell, how do you prevent some replicators from outcompeting others within the cell, destroying its integrity? When cells merge into a multicellular body, how do you prevent cancer — the defection of individual cells from the cooperative enterprise? When individuals merge into societies, how do you prevent freeriding?

The answers to these questions — the mechanisms that suppress cheating and stabilize cooperation at each level — are also, in many cases, mechanisms of cognitive integration. The transition from a collection of independent organisms to a cooperative group is, simultaneously, a transition from a collection of independent cognitive agents to a collective cognitive system.

## Chapter Roadmap

Section 1 examines the Major Transitions framework in detail, with particular attention to how each transition constitutes a cognitive event — a moment at which the unit of information processing changes.

Section 2 examines the evolutionary theory of altruism and cooperation: Hamilton's rule and inclusive fitness, reciprocal altruism, multilevel selection, and the debates surrounding them.

Section 3 asks the hardest question: can groups be cognitive agents in their own right? We examine the superorganism concept, the debate between E.O. Wilson and his critics, and what distributed cognition means at the group level.

## A Word on Timescales

The evolution of cooperation takes place on two timescales that are easy to conflate but important to distinguish. Evolutionary timescales — millions of years of selection for or against cooperative behaviors — produce the behavioral repertoires and cognitive architectures that make cooperation possible. Ecological timescales — years, decades, or a single season — produce the specific patterns of cooperation and defection that those architectures generate in actual populations.

When we ask whether groups can be cognitive agents, we are asking a question that applies primarily at the ecological timescale: given the behavioral repertoires that evolution has produced, does a particular group of organisms currently function as a cognitive unit? The answer may vary from group to group and situation to situation, even within a single species. Understanding both levels is essential for understanding what collective cognition is and how it evolved.
