## Part I: Reflection and Discussion

**1. Hamilton's Rule and Its Limits**
Hamilton's rule (rB > C) provides a clean mathematical criterion for the evolution of altruism. But applying it in practice requires measuring r, B, and C in real populations — which is often very difficult. Discuss what empirical challenges arise in applying Hamilton's rule to a specific case: the altruistic warning calls of ground squirrels (who alert nestmates to predators at risk to themselves). What would you need to measure? What assumptions would you need to make? How would uncertainty in your measurements affect the strength of your conclusion about whether kin selection explains the behavior?

**2. The Multilevel Selection Debate**
The debate between kin selection theory (gene-centric view) and multilevel selection theory (group selection view) was among the most contentious in evolutionary biology in the 2000s. Read the core claims of both sides and assess the debate: Is it a substantive scientific disagreement about facts in the world, or is it primarily a disagreement about which mathematical description is more useful? Can an experiment, in principle, decide between the two frameworks? If not, what does that tell us about the nature of the debate?

**3. The Major Transitions and Cognitive Integration**
The chapter argues that each major transition in evolution is also a major transition in the organization of cognition. Evaluate this claim for one specific transition: the origin of multicellularity. What cognitive capacities did the first multicellular organisms have that their single-celled ancestors did not? What new selection pressures for cognitive integration did multicellularity create? What mechanisms of cognitive integration evolved specifically in response to multicellularity?

**4. Group Minds and Moral Status**
If a bee swarm is a cognitive agent with a genuine "group mind," does that give the swarm any moral status? Does it matter whether the swarm experiences anything? Compare the moral status question for bee swarms with the same question for human corporations, which also exhibit distributed cognition. Do corporations have moral status? Do they have obligations? Are they morally responsible for their actions? What do the answers to these questions reveal about the relationship between cognition and moral status?

**5. Reciprocal Altruism and Cognitive Demands**
Trivers's theory of reciprocal altruism requires that cooperating individuals be able to recognize each other, remember past interactions, and respond strategically to defectors. What cognitive capacities are minimally required for a species to maintain reciprocal altruistic relationships? Can you rank the following species roughly by their ability to maintain reciprocal cooperation, and explain your ranking: bacteria, honeybees, vampire bats, ravens, chimpanzees, humans?

## Part II: Thought Experiments

**1. The Selfish Colony**
Imagine a mutation that causes a subset of worker bees in a colony to occasionally lay fertile eggs when the queen is present — defecting from the cooperative enterprise of the superorganism. The defectors gain slightly in direct fitness but impose a cost on the colony's efficiency. Now trace through the evolutionary dynamics: Under what conditions would this mutation spread to fixation (all bees become defectors)? Under what conditions would it be suppressed? What mechanisms of policing already exist in bee colonies that would limit the spread of such a mutation, and how effective are they? What would the colony look like after many generations of selection that permitted partial defection?

**2. The Cognitive Transition Scenario**
Maynard Smith and Szathmáry argue that each major transition involves a change in the unit of information processing. Design a hypothetical sequence of transitions — entirely imaginary but biologically plausible — in which a collection of single-celled organisms evolves, step by step, into a colony-level cognitive agent whose collective intelligence exceeds that of any current social insect. For each step, specify: (a) what new cooperative mechanism evolves; (b) what conflict-suppression mechanism evolves alongside it; (c) what new cognitive capacity the resulting entity possesses that the previous level lacked.

**3. The Colony Without Workers**
Imagine you could engineer a bee colony in which every individual is a "queen" — capable of reproduction — but in which all individuals cooperate as if they were workers. What evolutionary pressures would be required to maintain this cooperation, given that every individual has equal reproductive capacity? What kind of conflict-suppression mechanisms would be needed? Now consider: would such a colony be more or less cognitively integrated than a normal bee colony? Why? What does your answer reveal about the relationship between reproductive inequality (queen/worker distinction) and collective cognitive integration?

## Part III: Laboratory Investigations

**1. Testing Hamilton's Rule in a Microbial System**

*Hypothesis*: Cooperative behavior (public goods production) in bacteria will be maintained at higher frequencies in populations with higher genetic relatedness (more clonal populations) than in populations with lower relatedness.

*Materials*: Two strains of a common laboratory bacterium — a "cooperator" strain that produces an extracellular enzyme that benefits all nearby bacteria (this could be amylase in starch medium, for example) and a "defector" strain that does not produce the enzyme but benefits from its neighbors' production; liquid growth medium with starch or another substrate that requires extracellular enzyme action; culture vessels; OD600 measurement for growth.

*Protocol*: Set up competition experiments in which cooperator and defector strains are mixed at different initial ratios in populations with different spatial structure (well-mixed liquid culture vs. structured soft agar, which limits dispersal and creates local clusters that increase effective relatedness). Measure the frequency of cooperators and defectors over 10–20 generations by plating and counting colonies.

*Analysis*: Does spatial structure (which increases effective relatedness) maintain cooperator frequency at higher levels? Does cooperator frequency decline more rapidly in well-mixed populations? Are there conditions under which cooperators can invade a defector-dominated population?

**2. Observing Colony-Level Learning in Ants**

*Hypothesis*: Ant colonies, but not isolated individual ants, will show improved performance on a maze navigation task when repeatedly exposed to the same maze over time, indicating colony-level learning that exceeds individual learning.

*Materials*: A small ant colony (*Lasius niger* or similar) with access to a simple T-maze or Y-maze that provides a food reward at one end; individual isolated ants of the same species.

*Protocol*: Present the maze to the full colony and to isolated individuals repeatedly (once per day for 10 days). Measure the time required to find the food reward on each trial. Compare learning curves (improvement in latency over trials) between colonies and isolated individuals.

*Analysis*: Do colonies improve faster than isolated individuals? Do colonies reach a higher asymptotic performance level? If colony learning exceeds individual learning, what mechanisms could explain the difference? (Consider: trail pheromone deposition, recruitment of experienced foragers to familiar routes, reduced exploration costs for established paths.)

**3. Manipulating Quorum Size in Bee Swarm Decisions**

*Note: This experiment requires access to a bee swarm and research-grade beekeeping facilities. It should only be conducted by researchers with appropriate training and institutional oversight.*

*Hypothesis*: Artificially reducing the apparent number of scouts at a nest site (by removing scouts from the site during observation periods) will increase decision latency and reduce decision accuracy in bee swarms, consistent with the quorum-sensing mechanism.

*Materials*: An artificial bee swarm in a controlled setting (using marked bees from a hive in a flight cage); two or more artificial nest sites of known quality; a capture device for temporarily removing scouts from sites.

*Protocol*: Allow swarms to begin the decision-making process with known site quality differences. In experimental trials, periodically capture and temporarily remove scouts from the better site (without harming them) during the period when the swarm would normally be accumulating scouts toward a quorum. Return captured scouts after the swarm has made its decision. Compare decision accuracy, latency, and quorum timing between control and experimental swarms.

*Analysis*: Does scout removal delay the decision? Does it reduce the probability that the swarm chooses the best available site? Is the effect proportional to the number of scouts removed?
