# Chapter 24 Further Reading: The Evolution of Cooperation and Group Cognition

---

**Maynard Smith, J., & Szathmáry, E. (1995). *The Major Transitions in Evolution*. W. H. Freeman.**

The foundational text for this chapter and one of the most important books in 20th-century evolutionary biology. Maynard Smith and Szathmáry's framework for understanding evolutionary transitions — and their insight that each transition involves a change in the organization of biological information — has shaped research in evolutionary biology, molecular biology, and cognitive science. The book is intellectually demanding but rewards careful reading; it is one of those rare works that genuinely reorganizes how one thinks about a field.

---

**Hamilton, W. D. (1964). The genetical evolution of social behaviour. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*, 7(1), 1–52.**

The two-part paper that introduced inclusive fitness theory and Hamilton's rule. Among the most cited papers in the history of biology, and for good reason: it resolved a paradox that had troubled evolutionary theory since Darwin. Reading the original paper is worth the effort; Hamilton's prose is dense but precise, and the paper's argument is more nuanced than most textbook summaries suggest. Essential primary literature.

---

**Trivers, R. L. (1971). The evolution of reciprocal altruism. *Quarterly Review of Biology*, 46(1), 35–57.**

Trivers's original presentation of reciprocal altruism theory, with examples from cleaner fish, warning calls, and human cooperation. The paper is remarkably accessible for theoretical biology and makes the key logical points with great clarity. Trivers also discusses the cognitive demands of reciprocal altruism — particularly the importance of cheater detection — in ways that prefigure later work on social cognition.

---

**Wilson, E. O., & Hölldobler, B. (2009). *The Superorganism: The Beauty, Elegance, and Strangeness of Insect Societies*. W. W. Norton.**

Wilson and Hölldobler's magnum opus on eusocial insect societies and the superorganism concept. Comprehensive, beautifully illustrated, and written with evident enthusiasm for the subject matter. Students who want the full case for the superorganism concept, backed by extensive empirical detail, should consult this volume. The conceptual chapters at the beginning and end are particularly valuable; the middle chapters provide extensive species-specific natural history.

---

**Gordon, D. (1999). *Ants at Work: How an Insect Society Is Organized*. Free Press.**

Gordon's earlier, more accessible book on ant colony organization, written for a general audience. The book conveys the excitement of her field research and the intellectual puzzles she is trying to solve, while being careful about the limits of the evidence. A good companion to her more technical *Ant Encounters* (2010), especially for students new to ant biology.

---

**Nowak, M. A., Tarnita, C. E., & Wilson, E. O. (2010). The evolution of eusociality. *Nature*, 466(7310), 1057–1062.**

The controversial paper that Wilson co-authored to argue against inclusive fitness theory as an explanation for eusociality. The paper argues that standard natural selection, without invoking inclusive fitness, can explain the evolution of eusociality. It provoked a strong response from defenders of inclusive fitness theory, and the subsequent debate (involving more than 100 co-signatories of a rebuttal letter) provides a case study in how scientific disputes are conducted. Read the original paper and at least one of the major responses to see how the argument played out.

---

**Hutchins, E. (1995). *Cognition in the Wild*. MIT Press.**

The founding text of distributed cognition theory, based on Hutchins's study of navigation aboard US Navy ships. Hutchins argues that the cognitive processes involved in ship navigation are distributed across the crew, their instruments, and their shared procedures — that no individual has all the cognitive resources required for the task. The book is detailed and sometimes technical, but its core argument — that cognitive systems extend beyond individual minds — is important for understanding how the distributed cognition framework applies to biological collectives.

---

**Nowak, M. A., & Sigmund, K. (2005). Evolution of indirect reciprocity. *Nature*, 437(7063), 1291–1298.**

A key paper on indirect reciprocity — cooperation among individuals who do not interact repeatedly with each other but who are connected through a reputation system. This paper extends the theory of reciprocal cooperation to situations (like large human societies) where direct reciprocity cannot explain cooperation. Relevant to the question of how cognitive and social complexity coevolve in systems with large numbers of interacting individuals.

---

**Szathmáry, E. (2015). Toward major evolutionary transitions theory 2.0. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 112(33), 10104–10111.**

A 20-year update to the major transitions framework by one of its originators. Szathmáry revisits the original transitions, assesses which aspects of the theory have been confirmed and which require revision, and identifies new research directions. A good entry point into the current state of major transitions theory for students who have read the original Maynard Smith and Szathmáry book.
