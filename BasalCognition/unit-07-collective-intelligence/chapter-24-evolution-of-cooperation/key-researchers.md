# Chapter 24 Key Researchers: The Evolution of Cooperation and Group Cognition

---

## John Maynard Smith
**University of Sussex (1920–2004) — School of Biological Sciences**

John Maynard Smith was one of the most influential evolutionary biologists of the 20th century. He made foundational contributions to evolutionary theory, particularly through the development of evolutionary game theory (especially the concept of the evolutionarily stable strategy, or ESS) and, with Szathmáry, the major transitions framework.

Maynard Smith's intellectual style — rigorous mathematical modeling combined with keen biological intuition — shaped the culture of theoretical evolutionary biology. His ability to identify the precise logical structure of biological problems and then bring mathematical tools to bear on them produced a body of work that is both technically sophisticated and conceptually illuminating. The major transitions book represents the culmination of his thinking about the deepest patterns in evolutionary history.

---

## Eörs Szathmáry
**Eötvös Loránd University, Budapest — Parmenides Center for the Conceptual Foundations of Science**

Szathmáry is Maynard Smith's co-author on the major transitions framework and the researcher who has most actively extended and defended that framework in subsequent decades. His research spans the origin of life, the evolution of genetic coding, and the theoretical foundations of evolutionary transitions.

Szathmáry's work is notable for its integration of information theory with evolutionary biology — for his insistence that understanding the major transitions requires understanding how biological information is organized, stored, and transmitted. This perspective connects the major transitions framework directly to questions of cognition, since cognition is fundamentally about information processing.

## William D. Hamilton
**University of Oxford (1936–2000) — Zoology Department**

Hamilton is the originator of inclusive fitness theory and one of the most original and important evolutionary biologists of the 20th century. Beyond kin selection, Hamilton made major contributions to the evolution of sexual reproduction, the immunological theory of mate choice, and the evolution of senescence. His intellectual style was eccentric and bold — he was willing to propose ideas that were well ahead of the available evidence — and his influence on evolutionary biology is hard to overstate.

The 1964 papers on the genetical evolution of social behaviour are among the most important scientific papers of the past century. Reading them reveals a mind wrestling seriously with the deepest problems in evolutionary biology, and produces insights that textbook summaries invariably oversimplify.

## Robert Trivers
**Rutgers University — Department of Anthropology (emeritus)**

Trivers is the originator of reciprocal altruism theory, parental investment theory, and parent-offspring conflict theory — three of the most important concepts in behavioral ecology. His work in the early 1970s established the theoretical foundation for understanding cooperation and conflict among non-kin, and his prediction that cheater detection would be a key cognitive capacity in reciprocating species has been extensively vindicated.

Trivers's career has been marked by intellectual courage — he has proposed controversial ideas and defended them in the face of criticism — and by a sometimes difficult personal history. His scientific contributions, however, are of the first importance, and reading his original papers is worthwhile for any student of social behavior and cognition.

## E. O. Wilson
**Harvard University (1929–2021) — Museum of Comparative Zoology**

E. O. Wilson was the dominant figure in 20th-century myrmecology and a major force in evolutionary biology more broadly. His research on ant communication, caste regulation, and colony organization transformed our understanding of social insects. His theoretical work — particularly *Sociobiology* (1975) and *The Superorganism* (2009) — attempted to synthesize social behavior across all organisms, including humans, within an evolutionary framework.

Wilson's career generated both admiration and controversy. His sociobiology drew strong criticism from social scientists and political activists who objected to its implications for human behavior. His late-career rejection of inclusive fitness theory in favor of group selection generated intense scientific debate. On both fronts, he showed willingness to take positions that made him a target of criticism — a quality that, whatever one thinks of the specific positions, reflects genuine intellectual courage.

## Edwin Hutchins
**University of California, San Diego — Department of Cognitive Science**

Hutchins developed distributed cognition theory through his ethnographic studies of navigation practices on US Navy ships. His insight that cognitive processes extend beyond individual minds into social systems, material artifacts, and environmental structures has been profoundly influential in cognitive science and has direct relevance to the study of collective intelligence in biological systems.

Hutchins's work bridges cognitive science, anthropology, and biology in an unusual way. His framework for understanding distributed cognition as a system property — rather than a property of any individual component — provides the most rigorous conceptual foundation for treating bee swarms, ant colonies, and other collective systems as cognitive agents in a meaningful sense.
