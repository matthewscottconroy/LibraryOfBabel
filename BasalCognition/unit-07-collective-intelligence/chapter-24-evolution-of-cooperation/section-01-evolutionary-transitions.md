# Section 1: The Major Transitions in Evolution

## Maynard Smith and Szathmáry's Framework

In 1995, John Maynard Smith and Eörs Szathmáry published *The Major Transitions in Evolution*, a book that offered a unified framework for understanding some of the most important events in the history of life (Maynard Smith & Szathmáry, 1995). Their framework identified eight major transitions — moments when the way in which biological information is organized, stored, and transmitted underwent a fundamental change:

1. The origin of replicating molecules from a chemical soup
2. The enclosure of replicating molecules in protocells
3. The integration of RNA and protein function
4. The origin of chromosomes (linking of replicating molecules)
5. The origin of eukaryotic cells from prokaryotic ancestors (including endosymbiosis)
6. The origin of sexual reproduction and the separation of germline from soma
7. The origin of multicellularity
8. The origin of eusociality (and of human language, treated somewhat differently)

Each of these transitions shares a common structure: entities that had previously replicated independently gave up some or all of their independence and became parts of a larger, integrated whole. In each case, the transition solved the problem of internal conflict — the tendency of lower-level units to evolve in directions that undermine the higher-level unit's integrity — through mechanisms that either suppressed conflict or aligned the interests of lower-level units with those of the higher-level whole.

What makes this framework relevant to the study of cognition is a point that Maynard Smith and Szathmáry made explicitly: each major transition also involved a change in the way information is organized and transmitted. The transition to eukaryotes centralized genetic information in a protected nucleus. The transition to multicellularity required the development of mechanisms for differentiating cells from a common genome. The transition to eusociality created group-level information processing systems — collective cognitive entities — from individuals with their own nervous systems.

In other words: the major transitions in evolution are also major transitions in cognition.

## The Origin of the Cell: First Integration

The transition from free-floating replicating molecules to enclosed cells is the first major cognitive event in evolutionary history. Before enclosure, replicating molecules were independent competitors in a chemical soup. After enclosure, the molecules within a cell became a community whose individual fitness was tied to the collective fitness of the cell.

This transition created the conditions for the evolution of division of metabolic labor: different molecular species within the cell could specialize for different functions — catalysis, replication, structural support — because their fates were now coupled. The biochemical integration of the cell is the first instance of a general principle: when the fates of previously independent units are coupled, cooperative specialization becomes evolutionarily possible.

From a cognitive perspective, the cell is the first system in which information processing is integrated into a functioning whole. The cell's membrane defines a boundary between inside and outside; signals at the boundary are processed by interior machinery to produce behavioral responses. This is sensing, integration, and response — the minimal architecture of cognition — implemented for the first time in a single bounded system.

## Endosymbiosis: Merger of Disparate Agents

The transition to eukaryotic cells involved the incorporation of formerly independent organisms — the ancestors of mitochondria and chloroplasts — into a host cell (Margulis, 1981). This is perhaps the most dramatic example of the major transitions principle: entire organisms, with their own genomes, replication machinery, and metabolic systems, were incorporated as organelles and progressively reduced to components of a larger whole.

The cognitive implications are striking. The mitochondrion — or rather, its ancestor — was once an independent bacterium capable of chemotaxis, environmental sensing, and metabolic regulation. Integrated into the eukaryotic cell, it retained its metabolic function (oxidative phosphorylation) but lost its behavioral independence. Its sensors and effectors were co-opted into the host cell's regulatory system. The merger created a more complex integrated system — the eukaryotic cell, with its far richer behavioral repertoire — at the cost of the organelle's independence.

This is the pattern repeated in every major transition: merging creates richer integrated systems at the cost of the independence of the components. The "minds" of the components are merged into the "mind" of the whole.

## Multicellularity: The Cognitive Collective Body

The transition to multicellularity is the major transition that is most directly relevant to our understanding of nervous systems and brains, because it is the transition that made nervous systems possible. Neurons are cells; a nervous system is a particular kind of multicellular system in which cells specialize for information processing and communication.

But multicellularity creates a cognitive challenge before it creates a cognitive opportunity. A multicellular body is a collective of genetically identical (or nearly identical) cells. In principle, each cell has the same reproductive interests as every other cell — they share a genome. But in practice, multicellularity creates a new level of potential conflict: individual cells can evolve to replicate faster than their neighbors, at the expense of the body's function. This is cancer — and the evolution of multicellular bodies required the simultaneous evolution of mechanisms for suppressing cancer: programmed cell death (apoptosis), cell cycle checkpoints, DNA repair mechanisms, and immunity.

The cognitive integration of the multicellular body required more than just cells. It required the evolution of intercellular signaling systems — hormones, cytokines, gap junctions, neurotransmitters — that could coordinate the behavior of trillions of cells into a coherent, adaptive whole. The nervous system is the most sophisticated of these coordination systems, but it is not the only one. The immune system, the endocrine system, and the developmental signaling system are all information processing networks that contribute to the integrated behavior of the multicellular body.

When we marvel at the intelligence of an animal's nervous system, we are looking at only one layer of a multilayered cognitive system that has been elaborated over hundreds of millions of years of multicellular evolution.

## The Eusocial Transition: The Collective Mind

The transition to eusociality — the extreme form of social organization in which most individuals forego reproduction to contribute to the colony's collective fitness — is the latest of Maynard Smith and Szathmáry's major transitions, and it is the one that most directly creates what we have been calling collective intelligence.

The eusocial transition has occurred independently many times in evolutionary history: in several lineages of Hymenoptera (ants, bees, wasps), in termites, in naked mole rats, and perhaps in some shrimp. In each case, the transition involved the subordination of individual reproductive interests to the colony's collective interests — a sacrifice of individual fitness for group-level adaptive outcomes.

The cognitive consequences of this transition are the subject of Chapter 23 and of the present chapter. When individual animals subordinate their behavior to the colony's collective decision-making process — when the individual bee's foraging behavior is determined by the colony's food demand rather than the individual's own assessment — the colony becomes the functional unit of cognitive behavior. The colony senses, integrates, decides, and acts. Individual bees and ants are, in Seeley's formulation, the neurons of the colony's brain.

This is not mere metaphor. It is a precise characterization of the functional organization of a eusocial colony. And it raises the possibility — which we will examine in Section 3 — that the eusocial colony is not just functionally but ontologically a cognitive agent: a real mind, distributed across thousands of bodies.

## Cognitive Transitions at Each Level

To summarize the argument of this section: each major transition in evolution is also a major transition in the organization of cognition. At each level, previously independent cognitive agents (molecules, cells, organisms) give up their independence and become components of a larger cognitive system. The higher-level system has cognitive properties — sensing, integration, decision-making, response — that are not possessed by any of the lower-level components individually.

This nested structure of cognition — from molecular sensing at the protein level, through cellular cognition, through tissue-level coordination, through organismal behavior, through colony-level decision-making — suggests that cognition is not a special property of brains. It is a general property of biological organization, elaborated at each level of the biological hierarchy.

This is a radical idea, and it is contested. Some researchers would insist that only neural cognition — cognition implemented in nervous systems — is "real" cognition, and that the "cognition" of cells, colonies, and fungi is merely metaphorical. This position is defensible, but it requires a principled account of what makes neural cognition special. The next two sections of this chapter, and the next three chapters of the book, are largely concerned with building that account.
