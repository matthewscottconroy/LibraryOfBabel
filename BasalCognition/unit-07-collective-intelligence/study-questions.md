# Study Questions — Unit VII: Collective Intelligence

*These questions focus on the conceptual and empirical challenges of attributing cognition to collectives rather than individuals. They require engagement with both the biology of social organisms and the philosophy of collective intentionality.*

---

## Question 1: What Level Does Cognition Occur At?

Seeley's (2010) work on honeybee democracy shows that a bee swarm can make house-selection decisions that are better calibrated to objective quality differences than the decisions of individual bees. The collective is, in some sense, smarter than its members.

**(a)** Is the colony the correct unit of cognitive analysis for social insects, or is it the individual bee? What criteria would you use to answer this question? Consider: the individual bee can sense, integrate, and act; the colony can perform cognitive tasks (like optimal house selection) that no individual could perform. Which is the "real" cognitive agent?

**(b)** Imagine two bee swarms: one makes excellent decisions because its individual bees are highly informed and rational; another makes excellent decisions because its collective processes aggregate the noisy assessments of poorly informed individuals. Are these two swarms cognitively equivalent? What does your answer reveal about the relationship between individual and collective intelligence?

**(c)** Critics of collective intelligence accounts argue that colonies are not genuine agents because they lack a unified perspective or "point of view." This objection is related to Nagel's point about phenomenal experience: is there something it is like to be a colony? Evaluate this objection. Is a unified perspective necessary for cognition?

## Question 2: Stigmergy and the Architecture of Collective Cognition

Stigmergy — the mechanism by which agents modify their environment in ways that guide subsequent agents — is the primary mechanism of collective intelligence in ant colonies, termite mounds, and slime mold networks.

**(a)** Compare stigmergy in ant trail-laying (where pheromones modify the environment to guide subsequent ant movement) with memory in the hippocampus (where synaptic weights are modified to guide subsequent neural activity). Are these mechanisms analogous at any level of description? What is genuinely in common, and what differs?

**(b)** The slime mold *Physarum polycephalum* optimises its network architecture through a process of reinforcing tubes that carry high flow and withdrawing tubes that carry low flow — a form of physiological stigmergy. This mechanism was shown (Tero et al., 2010) to recapitulate the Tokyo rail network. Does this make *Physarum* a collective intelligence, or is it better described as a single distributed computing system?

**(c)** Stigmergic systems produce globally coherent behaviour without any individual agent having access to the global pattern. Is this a feature or a bug of collective intelligence? What constraints does it place on what stigmergic systems can do, and what does it reveal about the relationship between local and global levels of description?

## Question 3: Cooperation and Defection in Microbial Communities

*Dictyostelium discoideum* — the cellular slime mold — provides a striking model of collective behaviour: when starved, individual amoebae aggregate into a multicellular slug, which then forms a fruiting body. Some cells become spores (the reproductive payoff); others become stalk cells (they die, sacrificially enabling spore dispersal). This raises the problem of social evolution: why do stalk cells "cooperate" by dying?

**(a)** Standard evolutionary explanations invoke kin selection (Hamilton's rule): if most cells in the aggregate are closely related, then sacrificing oneself to raise the reproduction of relatives can be evolutionarily advantageous. How does the *csA* gene system in *Dictyostelium* (which mediates selective adhesion between kin) fit into this explanation? Is kin selection sufficient to explain all forms of cooperation in social microbes?

**(b)** The division of labour between stalk and spore cells in *Dictyostelium* has been compared to the division of labour in multicellular organisms (germ cells vs. somatic cells). Some evolutionary biologists argue that *Dictyostelium* represents a transition toward multicellularity. At what point, if any, does a collection of cooperating individuals become a new kind of individual? How does this question relate to the question of what level cognition occurs at?

**(c)** "Defector" strains of *Dictyostelium* exist: they preferentially become spores rather than stalk cells, free-riding on the cooperation of non-defectors. Experiments show that populations of mixed cooperators and defectors eventually evolve stable mixed strategies. What does the existence of these dynamics tell us about the nature of collective intelligence in *Dictyostelium*? Can a system be collectively intelligent if it is vulnerable to social defection?

## Question 4: Does the Whole Equal More Than the Sum of Its Parts?

The claim that collective intelligence is "more than the sum of its parts" is sometimes treated as self-evident, but it deserves scrutiny.

**(a)** In what precise sense is a bee colony more intelligent than the sum of its individual bees? Can you give a formal or semi-formal characterisation of "more than the sum"? One candidate: a collective is more intelligent than the sum of its parts if it can solve problems that no combination of individual parts working independently could solve. Does this characterisation work for ant colonies? For biofilms? For *Physarum*?

**(b)** Camazine et al. (2001) describe many collective intelligence phenomena as "self-organisation": pattern formation through local interactions without centralised control. Is self-organisation sufficient for collective intelligence, or is it merely a necessary precondition? What additional properties, if any, are required?

**(c)** Consider the human city as a collective intelligence system. Cities solve resource allocation problems, generate innovation, maintain complex infrastructure, and survive the deaths and replacements of all their individual members. Is the city cognitively more sophisticated than any individual citizen? What is the relationship between the city's "collective cognition" and the cognition of basal organisms discussed in this unit?

## Question 5: Swarm Intelligence and Artificial Systems

Dorigo and Stützle (2004) formalised ant colony optimisation (ACO) as an algorithmic framework for solving combinatorial optimisation problems. The algorithm mimics the pheromone trail-laying behaviour of ants to find approximately optimal solutions to problems like the travelling salesman problem.

**(a)** Does the success of ACO algorithms support or undermine claims about ant cognitive sophistication? On one view, it supports them (ants solved the problem first; we merely formalised what they do). On another view, it undermines them (the algorithm shows that the ant's behaviour can be decomposed into simple rules, with no need to attribute any genuine intelligence to the ant). Evaluate both views.

**(b)** The field of "swarm robotics" is attempting to build robot systems that exhibit collective intelligence analogous to social insects. When (if ever) would a swarm of robots be cognitively equivalent to a swarm of bees? What properties would the robot swarm need to have?

**(c)** Critiquing the claim that *Physarum polycephalum* "solves" the travelling salesman problem: the organism grows a network that approximates the Tokyo rail network, but it does not solve an arbitrary travelling salesman problem. It solves the specific problem of connecting given food sources in a physical space, using a process constrained by its biology (tube reinforcement by flow). How significant is this distinction? Does it tell us something general about the relationship between biological "intelligence" and computational problem-solving?
