# Chapter 25: The Pre-Neural World — Signaling Before Synapses

## The Parts That Predate the Machine

Here is a fact that should stop you in your tracks: the molecular components of the synapse — the scaffolding proteins that cluster receptors at the postsynaptic membrane, the calcium channels that trigger neurotransmitter release, the signaling molecules that modulate synaptic strength — predate the synapse itself by hundreds of millions of years.

These molecules did not evolve for the synapse. They were already there, doing other things, when the synapse came along. Evolution did not invent the synapse from scratch. It assembled it — took pre-existing molecular machines that had been doing various jobs in non-neural cells, repurposed them, combined them in new ways, and gradually elaborated the result into the astounding biological device that underlies all neural cognition.

This is not unusual in evolution; it is the rule. Complex structures are built from repurposed simpler parts. The vertebrate jaw evolved from gill arches. The mammalian middle ear evolved from jaw bones. The blood clotting cascade assembled from proteins originally involved in other functions. Evolution is a tinkerer, not an engineer.

But the pre-existing history of synaptic molecules is particularly important for our purposes, because it tells us something about the relationship between the neural world we have been approaching in this book and the pre-neural world we have been exploring. The building blocks of the synapse were present in the pre-neural world. The sophisticated machinery of neural signaling was assembled from components that already existed in organisms that had no nervous systems at all.

This means that the pre-neural world was not simply less capable than the neural world. It was qualitatively different — organized around different principles, implementing cognition in different ways. And the transition to neural cognition was not a clean break but a gradual elaboration: the components were already in place; what had to evolve was the architecture that assembled them into a nervous system.

## The Central Question

The central question of this chapter is how the nervous system evolved from a world that had no nervous systems. But to ask this question well, we must first understand what the pre-neural world looked like: what signaling mechanisms animals used before they had neurons, how they coordinated their behavior without synapses, and what the earliest nervous systems were like.

This is harder to study than it might seem. The organisms that existed before the evolution of nervous systems are long gone. We can study their modern descendants — sponges (Porifera), which lack neurons entirely; ctenophores (comb jellies), which have nervous systems of uncertain evolutionary origin; and cnidarians (jellyfish, sea anemones, and Hydra), which have the most primitive nervous systems of any animal group. But how well these living animals represent their ancient ancestors is not always clear.

We can also look at the molecular evidence — at the genes and proteins that are shared between animals with and without nervous systems, and that were therefore present in their common ancestors. This molecular archaeology has produced some of the most striking findings in the evolution of nervous systems, and it forms the backbone of this chapter.

## Chapter Roadmap

Section 1 examines the pre-neural signaling world: peptide signaling, epithelial conduction, and the molecular precursors of the synapse, particularly the striking evidence from choanoflagellates and sponges that synaptic proteins predate the synapse.

Section 2 turns to the earliest nervous systems — those of cnidarians, particularly Hydra and jellyfish — and examines how these nerve nets work, what they can do, and what they reveal about the minimal requirements for neural coordination.

Section 3 addresses one of the most actively debated questions in animal evolution: are nervous systems monophyletic (evolved once from a common ancestor) or polyphyletic (evolved independently multiple times)? The ctenophore nervous system, which appears to have evolved independently from that of other animals, provides the critical test case and remains a subject of genuine scientific controversy.

## Why This Chapter Matters

The pre-neural world is where everything in this book has been leading — and from which everything in the remainder of the book will depart. If basal cognition is real — if the single-celled organisms, plants, and fungi we have been studying do something that deserves to be called cognition — then the emergence of the nervous system was not the invention of cognition from nothing. It was the elaboration of pre-existing cognitive capacities into a new and more powerful form.

Understanding the continuity between pre-neural and neural cognition — where the molecular building blocks came from, how they were recruited into new functions, what the earliest nervous systems retained from their pre-neural past — is essential for understanding what the nervous system fundamentally is and what makes neural cognition special.
