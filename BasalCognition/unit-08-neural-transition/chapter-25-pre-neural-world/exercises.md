# Chapter 25 Exercises: The Pre-Neural World

## Part I: Reflection and Discussion

**1. Molecular Archaeology and Evolutionary Narrative**
The finding that sponges have postsynaptic scaffold proteins despite lacking synapses tells us something about the evolutionary origins of the synapse. But how confident should we be in the narrative constructed from this evidence? What are the alternative explanations for the presence of these proteins in sponges? Design a critical assessment of the Sakarya et al. (2007) finding: what would need to be true for their conclusion (that these proteins had a pre-synaptic function that was later co-opted) to be correct, and what evidence could, in principle, refute it?

**2. The Phylogenetics of the Ctenophore Debate**
The question of ctenophore phylogenetic position — whether they are the sister group to all other animals or more closely related to cnidarians — has major implications for the evolution of nervous systems. Examine the nature of this controversy: what specific features of the molecular data make it difficult to resolve? What systematic biases in molecular phylogenetic methods might affect the result? What kinds of evidence, beyond genome sequences, could help resolve the question? What would it mean for the evolution of cognition if ctenophores are definitively placed as basal to all other animals?

**3. The Functional Adequacy of the Nerve Net**
The cnidarian nerve net, despite its lack of centralization, coordinates complex behaviors like prey capture in *Hydra*. What specific behavioral capabilities does a distributed nerve net provide that pre-neural epithelial conduction does not? What specific capabilities does a centralized nervous system provide that a nerve net does not? Based on this comparison, construct a functional argument for why centralization evolved: what specific selection pressures would have driven the evolution of central ganglia from a distributed nerve net?

**4. Neurotransmitter Divergence in Ctenophores**
Ctenophores appear to lack serotonin, dopamine, and GABA — three neurotransmitters that are highly conserved across cnidarian and bilaterian nervous systems. Discuss two interpretations of this absence: (a) ctenophores independently evolved a nervous system using a different chemical toolkit, and (b) ctenophores lost these neurotransmitters secondarily after diverging from animals that had them. What evidence could distinguish between these interpretations? What would each interpretation imply about the universality of neurotransmitter chemistry in animal nervous systems?

**5. The Continuity Argument**
The chapter argues that there is a deep molecular continuity between pre-neural cellular signaling and neural signaling — that the synapse was assembled from pre-existing molecular components rather than invented from scratch. What does this continuity imply for the question of whether neural cognition is fundamentally different from non-neural cognition? Does the finding that synaptic proteins predate the synapse support or undermine the view that neural cognition is special?

## Part II: Thought Experiments

**1. The Minimal Nervous System**
Design the simplest possible nervous system that could coordinate the following behaviors in an aquatic predator: (a) a rapid whole-body contraction in response to mechanical disturbance; (b) directed movement toward chemical attractants (food odors); (c) capture and ingestion of prey upon contact. What is the minimum number of neuron types, synapse types, and anatomical regions required? What functional capabilities of the cnidarian nerve net are you incorporating, and what additional elements do you need? At what point does your minimal nervous system begin to resemble a real nervous system that exists in nature?

**2. The Chemical Language Question**
The deep conservation of neuropeptide families across animal phyla — and into non-animal organisms — suggests that the chemical language of neural signaling has deep evolutionary roots. Imagine discovering a species of protist that produces molecules chemically identical to vertebrate neuropeptides and uses them for chemotaxis and feeding behavior. What would this finding imply about the relationship between neural and non-neural signaling? Would it change how you think about the distinction between hormonal and neural communication? What experiments would you design to determine whether the protist's use of neuropeptide-like molecules is homologous to their use in animal nervous systems, or convergent?

**3. The Second Nervous System**
Suppose that Moroz's hypothesis is correct and that ctenophore nervous systems evolved independently from those of other animals. Now imagine that a third independent evolution of nervous systems is discovered in a group of macroscopic algae. This hypothetical algal nervous system uses different neurotransmitters than either the ctenophore or the bilaterian/cnidarian nervous system, different ionic channels for action potentials, and different synaptic scaffolding — but produces coordinated, flexible behavior in multicellular algal bodies. What would this discovery tell us about the cognitive potential of non-animal biological organizations? What properties of the nervous system's functional organization would be expected to be shared across all three independent evolutions, and what properties would be expected to differ?

## Part III: Laboratory Investigations

**1. Observing Behavioral Coordination in Sponges**

*Hypothesis*: Sponges will show coordinated whole-body responses to chemical or mechanical stimuli that propagate from the point of stimulation to distant body regions, consistent with epithelial conduction.

*Materials*: Living sponges (appropriate species depend on availability; freshwater sponges like *Spongilla* can sometimes be obtained from biological supply companies, or marine sponges from aquariums or coastal field sites); a stopwatch; a dissecting microscope if available; gentle stimulation tools (fine paintbrush for mechanical stimulation; dilute chemical solutions).

*Protocol*: Allow the sponge to settle undisturbed until oscula are open and water flow is visible. Apply a brief mechanical stimulus (gentle touch with a paintbrush) to a small region of the sponge and record: (a) the latency before any response begins, (b) whether the response is local (the touched region only) or propagates to distant regions, (c) the speed of propagation if it occurs, (d) the duration of the response. Compare responses to mechanical vs. chemical stimulation. Compare the latency and speed of propagation to the known speed of epithelial conduction (approximately 0.1–10 mm/sec).

*Analysis*: Is the response coordinated beyond the immediate point of stimulation? Does the propagation speed match the expected range for epithelial conduction? Does repeated stimulation produce any adaptation (decreased responsiveness)?

**2. Mapping *Hydra* Behavioral Repertoire**

*Hypothesis*: *Hydra* exhibits a greater variety of behavioral states than is apparent from casual observation, and transitions between behavioral states can be influenced by chemical stimuli.

*Materials*: *Hydra* cultures (available from biological supply companies or university biology departments); dissecting microscope; a timer; basic chemicals for stimulation (glutathione, which triggers the feeding response, is classically used; dilute acetic acid as an aversive stimulus).

*Protocol*: Observe individual *Hydra* at rest and categorize the behavioral states you observe (extended/contracted body, tentacles extended/retracted, moving/stationary, feeding/not feeding). Record the frequency and duration of each state over a 30-minute observation period for at least 5 individuals. Then add glutathione to the culture water (at concentrations typically used in the literature, around 0.1 mM) and record the behavioral response: does the *Hydra* show the characteristic "mouth-opening" feeding response? How quickly does the response occur and how long does it last?

*Analysis*: How many distinct behavioral states can you identify? Is there a "spontaneous" pattern of behavioral state transitions that occurs without any external stimulus? Does the glutathione stimulus reliably elicit the feeding response? Is the response all-or-nothing or graded?

**3. Comparative Behavior of Cnidarians and Ctenophores**

*Note: This experiment requires access to a marine aquarium facility or coastal research station with living ctenophores and cnidarians.*

*Hypothesis*: Ctenophores and cnidarians will show qualitatively different behavioral coordination capacities consistent with their different nervous system architectures, despite occupying similar ecological niches as water-column predators.

*Materials*: Living ctenophores (e.g., *Mnemiopsis leidyi* or *Pleurobrachia*) and living hydromedusae (e.g., *Aequorea* or a similar small medusa); standardized stimulus apparatus; video recording equipment.

*Protocol*: Present identical stimuli to ctenophores and hydromedusae and compare behavioral responses. Stimuli should include: (a) mechanical disturbance (gentle water current from a pipette at a known distance); (b) contact with prey or prey-like objects; (c) bioluminescent flashes from the ctenophore itself, if available. Measure: response latency, response type (directional vs. non-directional), response persistence, and any coordination of different body regions.

*Analysis*: Do ctenophores and hydromedusae show qualitatively similar or qualitatively different response patterns to the same stimuli? What differences, if any, are consistent with the hypothesis that their nervous systems are independently evolved and therefore architecturally distinct? What differences might simply reflect ecological differences between the two groups?
