# Chapter 25 Further Reading: The Pre-Neural World

---

**Sakarya, O., Armstrong, K. A., Adamska, M., Adamski, M., Wang, I. F., Tidor, B., Degnan, B. M., Oakley, T. H., & Bhatt, D. L. (2007). A post-synaptic scaffold at the origin of the animal kingdom. *PLoS ONE*, 2(6), e506.**

The landmark paper demonstrating that postsynaptic scaffold proteins predate the synapse. This paper is essential reading for understanding the molecular continuity between pre-neural cellular signaling and neural synaptic signaling. The methods are clearly described and the conclusion — that the postsynaptic molecular toolkit predates the first neurons — is striking and well-supported. One of the most important papers in the evolution of nervous systems.

---

**Moroz, L. L. (2009). On the independent origins of complex brains and neurons. *Brain, Behavior and Evolution*, 74(3), 177–190.**

Moroz's accessible presentation of the case for multiple independent origins of nervous systems, focusing on ctenophores as the key evidence. The paper surveys the neurochemical distinctiveness of ctenophores and discusses the implications for understanding the evolution of neural cognition. Readable by graduate students without extensive background in neuroscience; the conceptual argument is clear and the implications well-drawn.

---

**King, N., Westbrook, M. J., Young, S. L., Kuo, A., Abedin, M., Chapman, J., Fairclough, S., Hellsten, U., Isogai, Y., Letunic, I., & Putnam, N. (2008). The genome of the choanoflagellate *Monosiga brevicollis* and the origin of metazoans. *Nature*, 451(7180), 783–788.**

The genomic analysis of the choanoflagellate *Monosiga brevicollis*, the closest known unicellular relative of animals. This paper established that many proteins associated with cell-cell adhesion, signaling, and even synaptic function in animals have pre-animal origins in choanoflagellates. A key reference for understanding the deep molecular history of animal cell biology and neural signaling.

---

**Pisani, D., Pett, W., Dohrmann, M., Feuda, R., Rota-Stabelli, O., Philippe, H., Lartillot, N., & Wörheide, G. (2015). Genomic data do not support comb jellies as the sister group to all other animals. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 112(50), 15402–15407.**

The major counterargument to the ctenophore-basal phylogenetic hypothesis. Pisani and colleagues argue that the placement of ctenophores as the sister group to all other animals reflects systematic biases in phylogenetic inference rather than the true evolutionary history. Read alongside the papers supporting the ctenophore-basal hypothesis for a balanced view of the controversy.

---

**Margulis, L. (1981). *Symbiosis in Cell Evolution*. W. H. Freeman.**

Lynn Margulis's account of the endosymbiotic origin of eukaryotic organelles — the theory that mitochondria and chloroplasts evolved from formerly independent bacteria that were incorporated into host cells. Relevant to Chapter 25 as a paradigm case of the major transitions principle and as context for understanding how the molecular components of complex biological systems can originate in simpler antecedents. Margulis's advocacy for this theory in the face of initial scientific resistance is also an instructive episode in the sociology of science.

---

**Watanabe, H., Fujisawa, T., & Holstein, T. W. (2009). Cnidarians and the evolutionary origin of the nervous system. *Development, Growth & Differentiation*, 51(3), 167–183.**

A review of what *Hydra* and other cnidarians reveal about the evolutionary origins of the nervous system. Covers the cellular organization of the cnidarian nerve net, the neuropeptide signaling system, and what comparative genomics of cnidarians tells us about the ancestral state of the animal nervous system. Accessible and comprehensive; an excellent entry point into the cnidarian neuroscience literature.

---

**Ruppert, E. E., Fox, R. S., & Barnes, R. D. (2004). *Invertebrate Zoology: A Functional Evolutionary Approach* (7th ed.). Brooks/Cole.**

The standard comprehensive textbook on invertebrate biology. Chapters on Porifera (sponges), Cnidaria, and Ctenophora provide the biological context for the material in this chapter. The treatment is more zoological than neuroscientific, but the descriptions of behavioral repertoires and nervous system organization are thorough and accurate. An essential reference for students who need the full biological picture.

---

**Bhatt, D. L., & Bhatt, D. L. (Eds.). (Ongoing). *Origins of Neural Cell Types.* Cold Spring Harbor Protocols.**

The Cold Spring Harbor series on neural cell type origins includes a number of relevant protocols and review articles on the molecular biology of neural evolution. Particularly useful for students interested in the molecular aspects of neural origin, including the evolution of voltage-gated ion channels and synaptic proteins.

---

**Leys, S. P., & Mackie, G. O. (1994). Electrical recording from a glass sponge. *Nature*, 368(6468), 292–294.**

A short but significant paper documenting electrical signal propagation in a sponge — a result that demonstrated pre-neural electrical coordination in animals and supported the case for epithelial conduction as a precursor to neural signaling. The paper is technically elegant and conceptually important; it shifted the understanding of sponge biology significantly.

---

**Ryan, J. F., Pang, K., Schnitzler, C. E., Nguyen, A. D., Moreland, R. T., Simmons, D. K., Koch, B. J., Francis, W. R., Havlak, P., Smith, S. A., & Putnam, N. H. (2013). The genome of the ctenophore *Mnemiopsis leidyi* and its implications for cell type evolution. *Science*, 342(6164), 1242592.**

The ctenophore genome paper that provided genomic support for the distinctiveness of ctenophore biology and the potential independent origin of their nervous systems. This paper, along with a companion paper on *Pleurobrachia bachei*, established the genomic basis for the Moroz hypothesis and has been a major driver of the subsequent debate. Essential reading for students who want to engage with the primary data underlying the ctenophore controversy.
