# Chapter 25 Key Concepts: The Pre-Neural World

---

## Postsynaptic Density
The electron-dense region of the postsynaptic membrane at a chemical synapse, composed of a highly organized scaffold of proteins that cluster neurotransmitter receptors, signal transduction molecules, and cytoskeletal anchors. The postsynaptic density amplifies and integrates incoming chemical signals and translates them into changes in the receiving neuron's excitability and gene expression. Critically, the molecular components of the postsynaptic density — including scaffold proteins like Shank and Homer — are found in sponges that have no synapses, demonstrating that this molecular machinery predates the synapse itself by hundreds of millions of years.

---

## Choanoflagellate
A single-celled (or colonial) organism that is the closest known living relative of animals. Choanoflagellates are aquatic predators that use a characteristic "collar" of microvilli to capture bacterial prey. Their genomes contain many genes associated with animal cell-cell signaling, adhesion, and even synaptic function, demonstrating that the molecular toolkit for animal multicellular biology and for neural signaling was largely assembled before the origin of the animal kingdom. The study of choanoflagellates illuminates the pre-animal foundations of animal cell biology.

---

## Epithelial Conduction
The propagation of electrical or chemical signals through epithelial cells (cells that line body surfaces and cavities) without the mediation of specialized neurons. Epithelial conduction is found in sponges, where it coordinates the closure of oscula in response to mechanical disturbance, and in some non-neural contexts in cnidarians. It is likely a precursor to neural signal conduction — an ancient mechanism of coordinating cell behavior across tissue distances that was present before the evolution of neurons. Signal propagation through epithelial cells is slower than neural conduction but demonstrates that coordinated signaling across body surfaces does not require neurons.

---

## Nerve Net
A primitive nervous system organization in which neurons are distributed diffusely through the body wall without any centralized ganglion or brain-like structure. Signals propagate from wherever they are initiated, spreading through the network in all directions. Nerve nets are found in cnidarians (jellyfish, sea anemones, Hydra) and represent the most basal form of nervous system organization. Despite their lack of centralization, nerve nets coordinate complex behaviors including whole-body contractions, prey capture, and locomotion. The nerve net is the evolutionary precursor from which more centralized nervous systems evolved.

---

## Neuropeptide
A short peptide (typically 3–40 amino acids) used as a chemical signal in the nervous system. Neuropeptides modulate the excitability of neurons and neural circuits, often acting over longer time periods and wider spatial ranges than classical neurotransmitters. Neuropeptide families are among the most deeply conserved signaling molecules in animal biology — many neuropeptide families are found in cnidarians and even in non-animal organisms — suggesting that peptide-based chemical signaling is an ancient communication modality that predates the evolution of the synapse.

---

## Ctenophore (Comb Jelly)
A member of the phylum Ctenophora — a group of marine, gelatinous predators that use rows of fused cilia (comb rows) for locomotion. Ctenophores have nervous systems with neurons and synapses, but their nervous systems differ from those of cnidarians and bilaterian animals in their neurotransmitter repertoire and, possibly, in their evolutionary origin. The question of whether ctenophores are the sister group to all other animals (making their nervous systems independently evolved) or more closely related to cnidarians (making their nervous systems homologous to those of other animals) is among the most actively debated questions in animal evolution.

---

## Monophyly (of nervous systems)
The hypothesis that all nervous systems in the animal kingdom share a single common ancestor — that neurons and synapses evolved once and all subsequent nervous systems are derived from that single evolutionary origin. Monophyly of nervous systems is consistent with the wide conservation of synaptic molecules and neurotransmitters across animal phyla. It implies that the basic functional principles of neural cognition are universal and homologous across all neural animals.

---

## Polyphyly (of nervous systems)
The hypothesis that nervous systems evolved independently in multiple animal lineages, with at least some (possibly all) "nervous systems" representing convergent evolution of similar functional organizations from non-neural ancestors. The ctenophore data — particularly their distinct neurotransmitter repertoire — have revived the polyphyly hypothesis, which was largely dormant in the second half of the 20th century. If confirmed, polyphyly would imply that neural cognition is not a unique historical event but an organizational solution that evolution can discover repeatedly from the right molecular starting points.

---

## Synapse (Evolution of)
The specialized cell-cell junction that forms the basic unit of neural communication, consisting of a presynaptic terminal that releases neurotransmitters, a synaptic cleft, and a postsynaptic membrane with clustered neurotransmitter receptors. The evolution of the synapse — from pre-existing cell-cell communication junctions with postsynaptic scaffold proteins — is one of the critical events in the origin of nervous systems. The finding that postsynaptic scaffold proteins predate the first animals, and are present in sponges that lack synapses, supports the view that the synapse was assembled from pre-existing molecular components rather than invented as a novel structure.

---

## Apical Organ
A sensory structure found at the top (aboral pole) of ctenophores that contains a gravity-sensing system (a statolith balanced on cilia) and appears to coordinate the ctenophore's swimming behavior relative to gravity. The apical organ is the most centralized neural structure in ctenophores and may function as a primitive integration center. Its presence demonstrates that even primitive nervous systems can evolve specialized sensory-integrative structures, and its distinctive anatomy and connectivity suggest that it has no direct homolog in cnidarian or bilaterian nervous systems.
