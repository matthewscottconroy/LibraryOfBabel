# Chapter 25 Key Researchers: The Pre-Neural World

---

## Leonid Moroz
**University of Florida — Whitney Laboratory for Marine Bioscience**

Leonid Moroz is the most prominent advocate for the hypothesis that ctenophore nervous systems evolved independently from those of other animals, and more broadly for the view that complex nervous systems — and complex brains — may have independent evolutionary origins in multiple animal lineages. His research combines comparative neurochemistry, genomics, and molecular biology to characterize the nervous systems of "basal" animals.

Moroz's work on ctenophores revealed that these animals lack several neurotransmitters (serotonin, dopamine, GABA, classical neuropeptides) that are conserved across cnidarian and bilaterian nervous systems, and that their nervous systems use a partly distinct molecular toolkit. This chemical distinctiveness is the core evidence for his independent origin hypothesis.

Moroz is known for challenging orthodoxies and proposing radical hypotheses about neural evolution. Whether his ctenophore hypothesis ultimately proves correct, his work has revitalized the question of neural origin and demonstrated that the evolution of nervous systems is a more complex story than a single-origin narrative would suggest.

## Nicole King
**University of California, Berkeley — Department of Molecular and Cell Biology**

Nicole King studies choanoflagellates — the single-celled organisms most closely related to animals — and has made foundational contributions to understanding the pre-animal origins of animal cell biology. Her group's genomic studies of choanoflagellates have shown that many proteins associated with animal cell-cell signaling, adhesion, and even synaptic function have pre-animal origins in choanoflagellates.

King's work provides essential context for understanding the molecular continuity between non-neural and neural life. By showing that the molecular toolkit of animal signaling was largely assembled before the origin of the animal kingdom, her research supports the view that the evolution of nervous systems involved repurposing pre-existing molecular machinery rather than inventing new molecular tools.

---

## Olivier Sakarya
**Formerly University of California Santa Barbara — Marine Science Institute**

Sakarya is the first author on the 2007 *PLoS ONE* paper demonstrating that postsynaptic scaffold proteins predate the synapse — one of the most important papers in the evolution of nervous systems. While Sakarya is now less active in academic research, his contribution to the field has been lasting: the finding that the molecular toolkit for building the postsynaptic density is present in sponges fundamentally changed how researchers think about the molecular origins of synaptic function.

---

## Sally Leys
**University of Alberta — Department of Biological Sciences**

Sally Leys is the leading researcher on sponge biology and behavior, particularly on the mechanisms by which sponges coordinate their behavior without neurons. Her work on glass sponge electrical signal propagation (Leys & Mackie, 1994) established that pre-neural electrical coordination is possible in animals, and her subsequent research on *Amphimedon queenslandica* has been central to understanding the molecular toolkit available to the last common ancestor of all animals.

Leys brings both cell biological and evolutionary perspectives to sponge research, asking not just how sponges behave but what their biology tells us about the ancestral state of animal cognition. Her work is methodologically careful and her interpretations appropriately cautious — a model of how to do evolutionary biology on difficult material.

---

## Lynn Margulis
**University of Massachusetts Amherst (1938–2011) — Department of Geosciences**

Margulis is included here for her endosymbiotic theory — the demonstration that mitochondria and chloroplasts evolved from formerly independent bacteria incorporated into host cells. While not directly about nervous system evolution, Margulis's work is profoundly relevant to Chapter 25 as a paradigm case of how complex biological systems are assembled from simpler pre-existing components through evolutionary processes.

Margulis's career was also an instructive episode in the sociology of science: her endosymbiotic theory was initially rejected by the mainstream and later accepted as it accumulated overwhelming support. The story of how a radical hypothesis was vindicated is relevant background for students thinking about other radical hypotheses in evolutionary biology, including the multiple origins of nervous systems.

---

## Gregory Wray
**Duke University — Department of Biology**

Wray is an evolutionary developmental biologist (evo-devo researcher) whose work on the developmental genetics of animal body plans and nervous system evolution provides important context for the material in this chapter. His research on the molecular mechanisms of evolutionary change — how genetic changes produce changes in body plan and nervous system organization — bridges molecular biology and evolutionary biology in ways directly relevant to understanding how nervous systems evolved.
