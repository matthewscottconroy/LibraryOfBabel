# Section 1: Protoneurons and Molecular Precursors

## The Synaptic Scaffold Before the Synapse

In 2007, Olivier Sakarya and colleagues published a paper in *PLoS ONE* with the understated title "A post-synaptic scaffold at the origin of the animal kingdom" (Sakarya et al., 2007). The paper reported the results of a comprehensive search through the genome of the demosponge *Amphimedon queenslandica* — a sponge that has no neurons, no muscles, no synapses — for genes encoding proteins known to be components of the postsynaptic density, the molecular scaffold that clusters neurotransmitter receptors at the postsynaptic membrane of a synapse.

What they found was astonishing: the sponge genome contains orthologs of many of the genes encoding postsynaptic scaffold proteins. The proteins that organize the receiving end of a synapse — proteins like Homer, Shank, and components of the MAGUK family — were present in an animal that has no synapses and has never had them. These proteins must have had a function in sponges before they were recruited into synaptic function in the ancestors of neural animals.

This finding — which has been confirmed and extended by subsequent genomic studies — is one of the most important results in the study of neural evolution. It establishes that the molecular toolkit for building the postsynaptic density existed before the first synapse, and that the evolution of the synapse consisted primarily in assembling a new architecture from pre-existing components, not in inventing new molecular machines from scratch.

What were these proteins doing in sponges? The complete answer is not known, but the leading hypothesis involves cell-cell communication: sponges coordinate the behavior of their cells through chemical signals exchanged at cell junctions, and the scaffold proteins may have served to organize the receiving sides of these non-synaptic signaling junctions. The synapse, on this view, is an elaboration of a more ancient cell-junction communication system.

## Choanoflagellates and the Pre-Animal Signaling World

Even more remarkably, many of the molecular components associated with neural signaling are found not just in non-neural animals like sponges, but in choanoflagellates — single-celled organisms that are the closest known relatives of animals. Choanoflagellates have no nervous systems, no synapses, and no neurons. Yet their genomes encode proteins that are recognizably similar to proteins that play roles in neural signaling in animals.

Nicole King and colleagues at the University of California Berkeley, who have led much of the research on choanoflagellate genomics, have found choanoflagellate orthologs of receptor tyrosine kinases, cadherins, integrins, and other cell-adhesion and signaling proteins that in animals are associated with synaptic function (King et al., 2008). These proteins are used by choanoflagellates for sensing their environment, regulating their behavior, and interacting with bacterial prey.

This tells us something fundamental about the molecular origins of neural signaling: the signaling proteins are not animal innovations. They are eukaryotic (and in some cases prokaryotic) innovations that were co-opted into new functions at the origin of the animal kingdom and then repeatedly elaborated within the animal lineage.

The picture that emerges is one of deep molecular continuity across the transition from non-neural to neural life. The molecules that implement neural cognition were present, in their basic forms, hundreds of millions of years before the first nervous system. They were recruited from pre-existing cellular functions — cell-cell adhesion, environmental sensing, metabolic regulation — into the new architecture of the synapse and the nervous system.

## Epithelial Conduction: Signaling Without Neurons

Before neurons, animal behavior had to be coordinated by other means. One such means, particularly well-documented in sponges and in some non-neural contexts in cnidarians and ctenophores, is epithelial conduction: the transmission of electrical signals through epithelial cells (the cells that line body surfaces and cavities) without specialized neurons.

Sponges, despite lacking neurons, are not behaviorally inert. They respond to mechanical disturbance by closing their oscular pores (the large openings through which water exits the sponge's body). This response is coordinated: when one region of the sponge is mechanically disturbed, the contraction can spread to distant parts of the body. The mechanism appears to involve calcium-based signals propagating through the epithelial cells that line the sponge's canals (Leys & Mackie, 1994).

This epithelial conduction is slower than neural conduction — signals propagate at millimeters per minute rather than meters per second — but it achieves coordination across the body without neurons. It demonstrates that signal propagation and behavioral coordination are achievable with pre-neural cellular machinery, given a sufficiently connected network of epithelial cells.

Epithelial conduction also provides a plausible transitional stage in the evolution of nervous systems. The specialization of some epithelial cells into more efficient signal-conducting cells — protoneurons — could have been driven by selection for faster and more precise behavioral coordination. Over time, these specialized cells would differentiate further into true neurons with axons, dendrites, and synapses.

## Peptide Signaling and the Ancient Chemical Language

Long before neurons existed, organisms communicated through chemical signals — peptides and other small molecules that diffuse through the extracellular space and bind to receptors on target cells, producing changes in cell behavior. This peptide signaling system is ancient: it is found in fungi, in plants, in protists, and in all animal groups, and many of the peptide families used for neural communication in animals are conserved across large evolutionary distances.

Neuropeptides — the class of signaling molecules that modulate neural activity in vertebrates and invertebrates — are among the oldest chemical signals in animal biology. Many neuropeptide families (including peptides like oxytocin-related peptides and FMRFamide-related peptides) have orthologs in cnidarians, which have the most basal nervous systems of any animal; and some of these peptide families have homologs outside of animals entirely.

This deep conservation of neuropeptide families tells us that the chemical language of neural communication was not invented with the nervous system. It was an ancient language that the nervous system adopted and elaborated. The synapse provided a new physical infrastructure — a precisely localized, rapidly regulated junction between cells — that dramatically increased the speed and precision of chemical communication. But the chemical signals themselves, and many of their receptors, were already present in the pre-neural world.

The persistence of this ancient chemical signaling alongside the evolved neural signaling system is not without consequence: many organisms, including humans, have hormone systems that use the same molecular families as neurotransmitters. Insulin, oxytocin, serotonin, and many other signaling molecules function both as hormones in the diffuse endocrine system and as neurotransmitters in the nervous system. The overlap reflects the evolutionary history: neural signaling was layered onto an older hormonal signaling infrastructure, and the two systems remain intertwined in complex ways.

## The Path to the First Synapse

What was the path from pre-neural cell-cell communication to the first synapse? This is a question that direct evidence cannot fully answer, because synapses do not fossilize. But the molecular and comparative evidence constrains the possibilities.

The scenario most consistent with available evidence involves several steps:

1. In early animal ancestors (or pre-animal ancestors), cell-cell signaling junctions formed at contact points between adjacent cells, with scaffold proteins organizing the receptor clusters on the receiving cell.

2. Selection favored increasingly precise localization of the signaling machinery to specific cell-cell contact points, increasing the specificity and efficiency of cell-cell communication.

3. Some cells specialized for signal transmission — acquiring the ability to conduct electrical signals and to release chemical signals at specific release sites.

4. The combination of specialized signal-transmitting cells (proto-neurons) with organized receptor clusters at cell-cell junctions produced the first primitive synapses.

5. Further elaboration of the pre-synaptic machinery (vesicle release), the post-synaptic machinery (receptor clustering, signal integration), and the axonal machinery (fast signal propagation) produced the highly specialized synapses of modern neurons.

Each step in this sequence is plausible given what we know about the molecular components and the selective pressures that could have driven the changes. Whether this is actually what happened, or whether some steps were bypassed or occurred in a different order, is not known.

What the scenario makes clear is that the nervous system is not an alien structure, invented whole from new materials. It is a biological elaboration of pre-existing cellular machinery — the same kind of elaboration that recurs throughout the history of life. Understanding the pre-neural world is not just historically interesting; it is essential for understanding what the nervous system fundamentally is and why it works the way it does.
