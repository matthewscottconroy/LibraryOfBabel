# Section 3: The Multiple Origins Question

## The Most Important Tree Problem in Neuroscience

The question of whether nervous systems evolved once or multiple times is among the most consequential unresolved questions in animal evolution. It is not merely a question of natural history. It determines whether the nervous systems of all living animals share a common functional architecture that reflects a common evolutionary origin, or whether they are independent solutions to the problem of behavioral coordination — solutions that may share some molecular components but differ fundamentally in how those components are assembled.

If nervous systems are monophyletic — evolved once in the common ancestor of all animals with nervous systems — then we can expect neural cognition, in its basic principles, to be universal among neural animals. The synaptic machinery, the ionic basis of action potentials, the basic logic of sensory processing and motor output would all trace back to a single evolutionary origin and would be expected to share deep architectural similarities.

If nervous systems are polyphyletic — evolved independently in different lineages — then neural cognition may come in fundamentally different flavors. The nervous systems of different lineages might use different molecular machinery, different anatomical architectures, and different computational principles, each independently evolved to solve the problem of behavioral coordination.

The debate over monophyletic versus polyphyletic origins of nervous systems has been transformed in the past two decades by a combination of molecular phylogenetics and comparative neuroanatomy. The key player in the controversy is the ctenophore — the comb jelly — and the story of how ctenophores became central to this debate is both scientifically fascinating and methodologically instructive.

## Ctenophores: An Independent Nervous System?

Ctenophores are marine animals superficially similar to jellyfish but belonging to a completely separate phylum (Ctenophora). They are among the most beautiful animals in the sea: transparent, iridescent from the diffraction of their comb rows (rows of fused cilia that they use for locomotion), and predatory. They range from tiny, marble-sized forms to the Venus girdle, which can reach a meter in length.

Ctenophores have nervous systems. They have neurons, they have organized ganglia, they have clear sensory structures including a sophisticated gravity-sensing organ (the apical organ) at the top of the animal. Their nervous systems coordinate swimming, prey capture, and other behaviors. At the gross anatomical level, their nervous systems resemble those of cnidarians in some ways — they have a nerve net — but differ in others.

The controversy is not about whether ctenophores have nervous systems. It is about where ctenophores sit in the animal family tree, and what the implications of that position are for the evolution of nervous systems.

## The Phylogenetic Problem

For much of the 20th century, ctenophores were thought to be closely related to cnidarians — both were considered "radiata" (animals with radial symmetry) and both were placed as basal branches of the animal tree, well below the split between protostomes and deuterostomes. In this traditional view, the nervous systems of ctenophores and cnidarians were assumed to be homologous — derived from the nervous system of a common ancestor that already had neurons.

Beginning in the late 2000s and accelerating through the 2010s, molecular phylogenetic analyses — analyses of the evolutionary relationships among animals based on genomic sequence data — began suggesting a different topology. Several major analyses placed ctenophores as the sister group to all other animals — meaning that ctenophores diverged from the rest of the animal kingdom before sponges, before cnidarians, before bilaterians. If this phylogenetic position is correct, it has radical implications.

If ctenophores are the sister group to all other animals, and sponges are not — if sponges are positioned closer to bilaterian animals than ctenophores are — then the most parsimonious interpretation is that nervous systems evolved independently in ctenophores and in the common ancestor of cnidarians and bilaterians. On this view, ctenophores have a nervous system that is not homologous to the nervous systems of other animals but evolved independently from a non-neural ancestor.

Leonid Moroz, at the University of Florida, has been the most prominent advocate for this hypothesis — the "independent origin" hypothesis for ctenophore nervous systems (Moroz, 2009). His group has shown that ctenophores use a different repertoire of neurotransmitters than most other animals: they appear to lack serotonin, dopamine, norepinephrine, GABA, and the classical neuropeptides used by cnidarian and bilaterian nervous systems. Instead, they rely on L-glutamate and a distinct set of neuropeptides for neural signaling. This chemical distinctiveness is consistent with an independent origin.

Moroz has also shown that ctenophores have a distinct complement of synaptic genes — the genes encoding the proteins that build synapses — that differs from that of other animals in ways that suggest their synapses are not simply degraded versions of bilaterian synapses but are built from a partly different molecular toolkit.

## The Counterarguments

The independent origin hypothesis for ctenophore nervous systems is not universally accepted, and the counterarguments are worth considering carefully.

The first objection is phylogenetic: the placement of ctenophores as the sister group to all other animals is not unanimously supported by molecular phylogenetics. Different analyses, using different data sets and different methods of phylogenetic inference, produce different topologies. Some analyses support the ctenophore-basal position; others support the more traditional placement of sponges as the basalmost animals, with ctenophores more closely related to cnidarians (Pisani et al., 2015; and others). This phylogenetic instability reflects genuine difficulty in resolving the deepest relationships in the animal tree, not settled evidence for any particular topology.

The second objection is biological: the molecular differences between ctenophore and bilaterian nervous systems, while real, do not unambiguously indicate independent origin. Nervous systems can change dramatically over hundreds of millions of years of separate evolution; the chemical distinctiveness of ctenophore neurotransmitters might reflect secondary loss and replacement rather than independent origin. Distinguishing these scenarios requires more detailed molecular analysis than is currently available.

The third objection is philosophical: the parsimony argument for independent origin depends on the assumption that nervous systems are difficult to evolve — that they would not evolve more than once unless forced to by selection. But if the molecular toolkit for nervous systems was widely distributed across early animals (as the Sakarya et al. data on sponge postsynaptic scaffold genes suggest), then nervous systems may have been relatively easy to evolve, in which case independent origins become more plausible — but also less surprising and less informative.

## What the Controversy Reveals

The ctenophore controversy is important for reasons beyond the specific question of ctenophore evolution. It illustrates several things about how evolutionary biology works and what its limits are.

First, molecular phylogenetics is powerful but not infallible. The relationships among basal animal phyla are genuinely difficult to resolve because of long branches, rapid radiation, and the limited number of informative characters available. Different methods and data sets produce different answers, and scientific consensus is hard to achieve when the data are this noisy.

Second, the implications of a phylogenetic placement can be transformative. The difference between "ctenophores are close relatives of cnidarians" and "ctenophores are the sister group to all other animals" is enormous for our understanding of nervous system evolution. This means that the stakes of the phylogenetic question are very high, which is a reason both to pursue it rigorously and to be cautious about any particular answer.

Third, the debate illustrates the importance of multiple lines of evidence. Molecular phylogenetics is one line; comparative neuroanatomy is another; neurochemistry (the neurotransmitter repertoire) is a third; gene expression patterns are a fourth. When these lines converge, conclusions are stronger; when they diverge, the disagreement itself is informative.

## Implications for the Evolution of Cognition

Whatever the ultimate resolution of the ctenophore debate, it has already changed how we think about the evolution of cognition in important ways.

The mere possibility that nervous systems evolved independently more than once — that neural cognition is not a unique evolutionary innovation but a capability that can arise from the right molecular toolkit and selective pressure — strengthens the case for viewing cognition as an organizational property, not as a special biological substance. If evolution can build a nervous system from scratch twice (or more), using partly different molecular components, then there is something about the functional organization of nervous systems that is more fundamental than any specific molecular realization.

This parallels what we saw earlier in the book with mycelial networks and slime molds — different biological substrates implementing similar computational solutions to similar problems. Neural cognition might be one instance of a more general class of distributed information processing systems, some of which happen to use neurons and some of which do not.

Moroz's hypothesis, if it is correct, provides the most dramatic demonstration of this convergence yet encountered: two completely independent evolutions of nervous systems, using partly different molecular machinery, but producing similar functional outcomes — coordinated behavior, sensory processing, and behavioral flexibility. This is the cognitive equivalent of the convergent evolution of eyes — the same functional design evolved in multiple independent lineages because the functional solution is so powerful that selection reliably discovers it.
