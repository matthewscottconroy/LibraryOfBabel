# Chapter 26: Simple Nervous Systems and What They Teach Us

## What Three Hundred Neurons Can Do

A worm lives in the soil. It is barely a millimeter long, transparent, and unsegmented. Its entire body contains exactly 959 cells — an invariant number across all individuals of its species, determined cell by cell during development. Of these cells, 302 are neurons, connected by approximately 7,000 synapses.

By any measure, this is a small nervous system. A single cubic millimeter of human cortex contains roughly 100,000 neurons and a billion synapses. The entire nervous system of *Caenorhabditis elegans* — the worm in question — is smaller than what a cubic millimeter of human brain discards every few minutes.

And yet. The worm navigates chemical gradients, seeking oxygen and food while avoiding toxic compounds. It detects and avoids mechanical threats. It learns, in a basic sense: it habituates to repeated stimuli and shows sensitivity to paired stimuli that alters its subsequent behavior. It has distinct behavioral states — active, quiescent, foraging, fleeing. It mates. It makes choices.

How much behavior can 302 neurons support? Apparently, more than the number would suggest. And the reason why — the gap between the expected and the actual behavioral repertoire — is itself one of the most important lessons in neuroscience.

## The Logic of Simple Systems

The classic approach in neuroscience has been to start with a complex system — the human brain — and try to understand it from the top down. This approach has produced enormous amounts of knowledge, but it has also produced enormous confusion, because the human brain is so complex that identifying causal relationships between specific circuit elements and specific behaviors is extremely difficult.

An alternative approach, which has been extraordinarily productive, is to start with the simplest possible nervous systems and work upward: to use the simplicity of small nervous systems as a tool for identifying the elementary principles of neural computation and behavioral control. This is the logic behind the model organisms that dominate 20th-century neuroscience: *Caenorhabditis elegans* (302 neurons), *Aplysia californica* (approximately 10,000 neurons, many of them huge and individually identifiable), and *Drosophila melanogaster* (approximately 100,000 neurons in the brain, with complete genetic tractability).

Each of these systems has taught us things about the nervous system that could not have been learned — or could not have been learned as clearly — in more complex systems. Each has also taught us something about cognition — about what cognition requires, what it can do with minimal resources, and what it looks like in systems that are just barely adequate for the behaviors they support.

## Chapter Roadmap

Section 1 examines *C. elegans* — its complete connectome, its behavioral repertoire, and the puzzle of how 302 neurons achieve more than naive counting would predict. The key is neuromodulation: the same circuit, tuned to different chemical states, behaves very differently.

Section 2 turns to *Aplysia californica* and Eric Kandel's Nobel Prize-winning work on the molecular basis of memory. Aplysia provided the first cellular and molecular explanation for how experience changes the nervous system — how the neuron changes its properties, and how those changes persist over time.

Section 3 examines the *Drosophila* brain — 100,000 neurons, complete behavioral genetics, and an ongoing connectome project. We focus particularly on the mushroom body, the learning and memory center, and what the genetic dissection of fly behavior has revealed about the circuit basis of cognition.

Section 4 steps back to ask what the convergent evolution of neural structures in different lineages tells us about the possibility space of cognition — what kinds of cognitive capacities reliably evolve when the opportunity arises, and why.

## A Note on the Model Organism Strategy

The use of model organisms in neuroscience is a strategic choice that involves trade-offs worth acknowledging explicitly. The assumption is that principles learned in simple systems will generalize to complex ones — that the molecular basis of memory in *Aplysia* is similar enough to the molecular basis of memory in mammals that understanding Aplysia illuminates mammalian memory. This assumption has been spectacularly vindicated in some cases (the CREB transcription factor story being the most dramatic example) and has proven more limited in others.

The cautious reader should hold the model organism strategy's successes and limitations in mind simultaneously. Simple systems illuminate elementary principles. Whether all the elementary principles of cognition are instantiated in simple systems, or whether some aspects of cognition emerge only in large, complex networks, is a question that the model organism strategy cannot itself answer.
