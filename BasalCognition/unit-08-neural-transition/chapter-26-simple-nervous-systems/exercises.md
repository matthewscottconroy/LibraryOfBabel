# Chapter 26 Exercises: Simple Nervous Systems and What They Teach Us

---

## Part I: Reflection and Discussion

**1. The Neuromodulation Principle**
The chapter argues that neuromodulation dramatically expands the behavioral repertoire of a fixed anatomical circuit by creating multiple functional states from the same physical substrate. This principle applies to *C. elegans* (302 neurons, many behavioral states) and scales to the mammalian brain (86 billion neurons, an almost unlimited range of states). What does this principle imply for our understanding of the relationship between brain size and cognitive capacity? Is the number of neurons a meaningful measure of cognitive complexity, or is the neuromodulatory architecture more important? Can you think of cases where adding more neurons to a circuit increases cognitive capacity in ways that neuromodulation alone could not achieve?

**2. CREB and the Universality of Memory Mechanisms**
CREB-dependent transcription is required for long-term memory in *Aplysia*, in *Drosophila*, and in mammals. What does this molecular conservation tell us about the evolution of long-term memory? Does the conservation of the molecular mechanism imply that the memory formed by CREB-dependent transcription is fundamentally the same kind of thing in all these organisms, or might the same molecular mechanism support qualitatively different cognitive functions in different neural architectures? How would you design an experiment to distinguish these possibilities?

**3. The Connectome and Its Limits**
The complete *C. elegans* connectome has been known since 1986, yet we do not have a complete explanation of all *C. elegans* behaviors in terms of the connectome. What does this gap tell us about the relationship between anatomical connectivity and neural function? What additional information, beyond the connectome, is needed to fully explain behavior? Consider: neuromodulatory state, synaptic strength dynamics, gap junction conductance, extrasynaptic signaling. Is a complete mechanistic explanation of behavior in terms of neural circuit elements a realistic goal, or does the gap between circuit and behavior reflect something fundamental about the nature of cognition?

**4. The Convergent Eye Problem**
The vertebrate eye and the cephalopod (octopus/squid) eye are often cited as a classic example of convergent evolution. Both are "camera" eyes with a lens and a focused image projected onto a photoreceptor array. But they differ in the orientation of the photoreceptors (vertebrate photoreceptors face backward — "inverted retina" — while cephalopod photoreceptors face forward), in the neural processing layers of the retina, and in many molecular details. What does this case of convergent morphology with divergent detailed implementation tell us about the nature of convergent evolution? Is the "same" eye really the same? And what is the cognitive equivalent of this question for convergent learning systems in vertebrates and invertebrates?

**5. Mirror Self-Recognition and Its Meaning**
The mirror self-recognition test has been passed by chimpanzees, dolphins, elephants, magpies, and possibly cleaner wrasse fish. Discuss: (a) What does passing the mirror test tell us about the cognitive capacity of an animal? What does it not tell us? (b) Why might a fish's passing the mirror test be more contested than an elephant's? (c) Is mirror self-recognition a meaningful measure of self-awareness, or is it a narrower test that some animals pass for reasons unrelated to self-awareness? What alternative tests of self-awareness could be designed?

## Part II: Thought Experiments

**1. Scaling Neuromodulation**
Imagine a nervous system with exactly 302 neurons — identical to *C. elegans* in anatomy — but with 100 neuromodulatory systems instead of the 10 or so that the real worm has. Each neuromodulatory system can independently set the excitability of each neuron to a different level. How many distinct functional states could this hypothetical nervous system occupy? What does your answer reveal about the relationship between neuromodulatory complexity and behavioral repertoire? Now compare this to the brain: the human brain has dozens of major neuromodulatory systems, each acting on billions of neurons. Is the behavioral richness of the human brain better explained by the number of neurons or by the complexity of the neuromodulatory architecture?

**2. The Design Problem for a Simple Memory System**
You are given a budget of 100 neurons to build a memory system that can: (a) store associations between arbitrary pairs of stimuli; (b) distinguish recently acquired from older associations; (c) retrieve specific associations when presented with one element of the pair. How would you design the circuit? What are the key trade-offs (memory capacity vs. interference, fast learning vs. stability, generalization vs. specificity)? After designing your circuit, compare it to what is known about the mushroom body of *Drosophila* and the hippocampus of mammals. What solutions did you independently discover? What features of the biological systems did you miss?

**3. The Counterfactual *Drosophila***
Imagine a version of *Drosophila* that evolved in an environment where olfactory learning was not adaptive — where odors were not reliably predictive of food or danger. What would you expect the mushroom body of this hypothetical fly to look like? Would you expect it to be absent entirely, simplified, or merely repurposed for a different function? Now consider the converse: imagine a *Drosophila* in which olfactory learning was vastly more important — where fine olfactory discrimination and memory over weeks or months were critical for survival. What modifications to the mushroom body circuit would you predict natural selection would make? What new molecular machinery might evolve?

## Part III: Laboratory Investigations

**1. Habituation and Sensitization in a Simple Invertebrate**

*Hypothesis*: Non-associative learning (habituation and sensitization) can be demonstrated in a simple invertebrate within a single experimental session.

*Materials*: A culture of earthworms (*Lumbricus terrestris* or similar) or freshwater flatworms (*Planaria*); a standardized stimulus apparatus (a small vibration device or a bright light for flatworms, or a gentle water current for aquatic invertebrates); a camera for recording responses; a timer.

*Protocol*: Establish a baseline response (withdrawal or directional movement) to a standardized stimulus. Apply the stimulus at regular intervals (every 30 seconds) and record the response amplitude (distance withdrawn, or time to response) on each trial. Continue until response amplitude decreases by at least 50% (habituation). Then apply a single intense, aversive stimulus (bright light, strong vibration). Immediately re-test the standard stimulus and measure the response amplitude.

*Analysis*: Does the response habituate over repeated stimulations? What is the time course of habituation — how many trials to reach criterion, and what is the shape of the decline? Does the aversive stimulus produce sensitization — an increase in response to the standard stimulus above the habituated level? If so, how long does the sensitization last?

**2. Olfactory Conditioning in *Drosophila***

*Note: This experiment requires a *Drosophila* culture and basic Drosophila handling equipment, available in many biology departments.*

*Hypothesis*: *Drosophila* will show conditioned avoidance of an odorant paired with electric shock in a standard associative learning paradigm.

*Materials*: *Drosophila* culture; a simple T-maze for measuring olfactory preference; two odorants (octanol and methylcyclohexanol are standard in the literature); a small battery-based shock apparatus (available commercially for this use); timers.

*Protocol*: Use the standard Pavlovian conditioning protocol for *Drosophila*: expose flies to one odorant (CS+) paired with electric shock, then to the second odorant (CS-) without shock. After training, allow the flies to choose between CS+ and CS- odorants in the T-maze and measure the distribution of flies between the two arms. Calculate a performance index (PI) reflecting the proportion of flies that chose CS- over CS+.

*Analysis*: Does the trained group show significant preference for CS- over CS+ compared to untrained control groups? What is the magnitude of the performance index? Does the preference persist over time (measure at 3 hours, 24 hours if possible)? Is short-term memory (3-hour retention) or long-term memory (24-hour retention) more robust in your hands?

**3. Chemotaxis Gradient Tracking in *C. elegans***

*Note: *C. elegans* cultures can be obtained from biological supply companies or maintained easily in a laboratory setting.*

*Hypothesis*: *C. elegans* will track a chemical gradient in a reproducible, quantifiable manner, and the direction and magnitude of chemotaxis will reflect the worm's nutritional state.

*Materials*: *C. elegans* cultures (well-fed and 4-hour-starved); agar plates for chemotaxis assay; attractant chemicals (diacetyl or isoamyl alcohol, diluted to appropriate concentrations, are standard attractants); a dissecting microscope; image analysis software or a counting grid.

*Protocol*: Prepare chemotaxis assay plates with a gradient of attractant established by placing a small amount of undiluted attractant on one side of the plate and a control (ethanol solvent) on the other. Allow gradient to form for 1 hour at room temperature. Place a small group of worms (20–40) at the center of the plate. Allow them to move for 1 hour, then immobilize and count the distribution of worms across the plate.

*Analysis*: Do well-fed worms and starved worms show different degrees of chemotaxis to the attractant? Is the response to a standard concentration graded (intermediate distribution) or all-or-nothing? What does any difference between fed and starved states suggest about the neuromodulatory regulation of chemotaxis in *C. elegans*?
