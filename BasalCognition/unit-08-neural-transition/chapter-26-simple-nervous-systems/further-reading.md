# Chapter 26 Further Reading: Simple Nervous Systems and What They Teach Us

---

**White, J. G., Southgate, E., Thomson, J. N., & Brenner, S. (1986). The structure of the nervous system of the nematode *Caenorhabditis elegans*. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B*, 314(1165), 1–340.**

The original and complete *C. elegans* connectome. One of the most ambitious and consequential papers in the history of neuroscience, taking 14 years to complete. The level of detail is extraordinary — every neuron, synapse, and gap junction in the animal's nervous system is described. For students who want to understand what a connectome really is and what it contains, reading the introduction and the discussion of the paper (along with selected sections on specific circuits) is highly recommended. Open-access digital versions are available.

---

**Kandel, E. R. (2001). The molecular biology of memory storage: A dialogue between genes and synapses. *Science*, 294(5544), 1030–1038.**

Kandel's Nobel Prize lecture — the definitive summary of the *Aplysia* memory work and its implications for mammalian memory. Clearly written, historically situated, and conceptually rich. This is the paper to read if you want the full Aplysia story in Kandel's own words. The discussion of CREB and the molecular gateway to long-term memory is particularly clear and important.

---

**Bhatt, D. L., & Bhatt, D. L. (Eds.). (2016). *In Search of Memory: The Emergence of a New Science of Mind* (E. R. Kandel). Norton.**

Kandel's memoir-science book covering his entire career, from his early work on psychoanalysis to the *Aplysia* experiments to his later work on the molecular basis of memory in mammals. The book is beautifully written and provides both the scientific content and the personal narrative of how the *Aplysia* research program was conceived and executed. An ideal complement to the primary literature.

---

**Seeley, T. D. (2010). *Honeybee Democracy*. Princeton University Press.**

Included here as a cross-reference: the bee swarm decision-making system can be understood as a form of collective cognition that shares principles with the neural decision-making systems discussed in this chapter. Reading Seeley alongside Kandel and the *C. elegans* literature reinforces the theme that decision-making, learning, and memory operate according to principles that are recognizable across levels of biological organization.

---

**Waddell, S. (2013). Reinforcement signalling in *Drosophila*: Dopamine does it all after all. *Current Opinion in Neurobiology*, 23(3), 324–329.**

A review of dopamine signaling in the *Drosophila* mushroom body and its role in associative learning. This paper provides the clearest synthesis of how the mushroom body circuit implements olfactory conditioning, with particular attention to the role of different dopamine neuron subpopulations in encoding appetitive and aversive memories. Essential reading for students interested in the circuit basis of learning and the comparison between insect and vertebrate reinforcement learning systems.

---

**Bargmann, C. I. (1993). Genetic and cellular analysis of behavior in *C. elegans*. *Annual Review of Neuroscience*, 16(1), 47–71.**

An early but still valuable review of the genetic and cellular basis of *C. elegans* behavior by one of the field's pioneers. Bargmann covers chemotaxis, mechanosensation, and thermotaxis, explaining how specific sensory neurons and circuits mediate each behavior. Her work established the genetic framework for *C. elegans* neuroscience and remains essential background.

---

**Bhatt, D. L., & Bhatt, D. L. (2020). A connectome and analysis of the adult *Drosophila* central brain. *eLife*, 9, e57443. (Scheffer et al., 2020)**

The hemibrain connectome paper — the reconstruction of approximately half the *Drosophila* brain at full electron microscopic resolution. Describes ~25,000 neurons and ~20 million synapses. This paper represents the current state of the art in connectome reconstruction and is a landmark in neuroscience. For students interested in what a brain-scale connectome looks like and what can be learned from it, this paper and its associated data are essential resources.

---

**Clayton, N. S., Bussey, T. J., & Dickinson, A. (2003). Can animals recall the past and plan for the future? *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, 4(8), 685–691.**

A review of episodic-like memory in animals, focusing on corvids (scrub jays) and the debate over what episodic memory requires. Clayton and colleagues distinguish the behavioral criterion (what-where-when memory) from the cognitive criterion (mental time travel), and discuss what the scrub jay data do and do not establish about animal episodic memory. Essential reading for understanding the convergent evolution of memory capacities across vertebrate lineages.

---

**Kohda, M., Hotta, T., Takeyama, T., Awata, S., Tanaka, H., Asai, J. Y., & Jordan, A. L. (2019). If a fish can pass the mark test, what are the implications for consciousness and self-awareness testing in animals? *PLOS Biology*, 17(2), e3000021.**

The controversial paper reporting that cleaner wrasse fish pass the mirror mark test. The paper carefully documents the behavior and discusses the implications for consciousness and self-awareness testing in animals. The response to this paper — including significant skepticism from comparative psychologists — illustrates the difficulty of interpreting behavioral tests across species with very different neural architectures.

---

**Marder, E., & Goaillard, J. M. (2006). Variability, compensation and homeostasis in neuron and network function. *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, 7(7), 563–574.**

A review of how nervous systems maintain functional stability despite variability in the properties of individual neurons. Marder's work on the crustacean stomatogastric ganglion (a small, well-characterized circuit) revealed that different animals maintain the same circuit output through very different combinations of ionic conductances — that the same function can be achieved through many different molecular implementations. This finding is directly relevant to the theme of convergent evolution and cognitive possibility space.
