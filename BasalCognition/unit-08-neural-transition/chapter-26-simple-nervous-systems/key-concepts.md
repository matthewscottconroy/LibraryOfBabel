# Chapter 26 Key Concepts: Simple Nervous Systems and What They Teach Us

---

## Connectome
A complete map of all the neurons and their synaptic connections in a nervous system. The term was first applied to the complete wiring diagram of *C. elegans* (White et al., 1986), and has since been extended to partial connectomes of larger nervous systems, including the fly brain (hemibrain) and, in development, the mouse and human brain. A connectome describes the anatomical substrate of neural computation but does not, by itself, determine function: understanding behavior requires additional knowledge of synaptic strength, neuromodulatory state, and the dynamics of neural activity.

---

## Neuromodulation
The modification of the functional properties of neurons and neural circuits by diffusely acting chemical signals — neuropeptides, monoamine neurotransmitters (serotonin, dopamine, octopamine), and other modulatory substances — that act on many neurons simultaneously to change their excitability, response properties, or synaptic strengths. Neuromodulation allows the same anatomical circuit to operate as functionally different circuits in different behavioral states, dramatically expanding the behavioral repertoire achievable by a fixed number of neurons. Neuromodulation is a key mechanism by which the *C. elegans* nervous system achieves state-dependent behavioral flexibility.

---

## Habituation
The simplest form of non-associative learning: a decrease in the strength of a behavioral response upon repeated exposure to a stimulus. Habituation is distinct from sensory adaptation (which reflects peripheral changes in receptor sensitivity) and from fatigue (which reflects peripheral changes in effector performance); it reflects changes in synaptic transmission within the neural circuit mediating the response. Habituation has been demonstrated in virtually every animal nervous system tested, including *C. elegans*, *Aplysia*, *Drosophila*, and mammals. In *Aplysia*, the cellular mechanism of habituation — a decrease in neurotransmitter release from sensory neuron terminals — was the first molecular explanation of a learning process in any organism.

---

## Sensitization
A form of non-associative learning in which a single, strong, or aversive stimulus produces enhanced responsiveness to subsequent stimuli. Sensitization is the complement of habituation: where habituation reflects decreased synaptic transmission after repeated weak stimulation, sensitization reflects increased synaptic transmission after a strong aversive experience. In *Aplysia*, sensitization is mediated by serotonin released by interneurons onto sensory neuron terminals, activating a cAMP-PKA cascade that increases neurotransmitter release. Short-term sensitization lasts minutes; long-term sensitization lasts days and requires new protein synthesis and structural synaptic changes.

---

## CREB (cAMP Response Element Binding protein)
A transcription factor that is a key molecular gatekeeper of long-term memory consolidation. When the cyclic AMP (cAMP) signaling cascade is activated repeatedly — as occurs during strong or spaced training — it activates protein kinase A (PKA), which translocates to the nucleus and phosphorylates CREB. Phosphorylated CREB activates the transcription of genes whose protein products produce structural changes at synapses — new synaptic connections, modified receptor expression — that constitute the long-term memory trace. CREB-dependent transcription is required for long-term memory in *Aplysia*, *Drosophila*, and mammals, demonstrating deep evolutionary conservation of the molecular mechanism of memory storage.

---

## Mushroom Body
A bilateral brain structure in insects that serves as the primary site for olfactory learning and memory, and more broadly as an associative learning center. The mushroom body receives olfactory input from the antennal lobe and modulatory input (including dopaminergic teaching signals) from interneurons conveying information about stimulus valence. Its output neurons project to behavioral control centers. The mushroom body is functionally analogous to (though structurally distinct from and not homologous to) the mammalian hippocampus in its role as an associative memory structure. The *Drosophila* mushroom body circuit has been reconstructed at full connectome resolution and is one of the best-understood memory circuits in any animal.

---

## Kenyon Cells
The principal neurons of the insect mushroom body. Each Kenyon cell receives input from a sparse, semi-random combination of olfactory projection neurons from the antennal lobe, making individual Kenyon cells selective for specific combinations of odorant inputs rather than for single odorants. This combinatorial coding strategy allows the mushroom body to represent a very large number of odor identities with a relatively small number of neurons, and is the basis for the mushroom body's capacity to distinguish among many different learned odors. The Kenyon cell coding strategy is functionally similar to sparse coding strategies used in the mammalian olfactory cortex and hippocampus.

---

## Convergent Evolution (cognitive)
The independent evolution of similar cognitive capacities in distantly related lineages, reflecting common adaptive pressures and the accessibility of certain cognitive solutions from different neural architectures. Cognitive convergence has been documented for tool use (in corvids and primates), self-recognition (in corvids, cetaceans, elephants, and great apes), episodic-like memory (in corvids and mammals), and basic forms of associative learning (across virtually all animal phyla with nervous systems). Cognitive convergence suggests that certain cognitive capabilities solve broadly occurring adaptive problems and can be implemented by neural circuits of diverse architecture.

---

## UAS-GAL4 System
A genetic tool widely used in *Drosophila* neuroscience that allows the expression of any transgene of interest in any genetically defined set of neurons. The system involves two genetic components: a "driver" line expressing the yeast transcription factor GAL4 under the control of a neuron-type-specific regulatory sequence, and an "effector" line expressing a transgene of interest (e.g., an optogenetic channel, a fluorescent marker, or a silencing protein) under the control of a UAS (upstream activation sequence) that binds GAL4. Only in neurons where GAL4 is expressed will the effector transgene be activated. The UAS-GAL4 system, combined with the large library of available driver lines, makes *Drosophila* the most genetically tractable system for circuit-level neuroscience.

---

## Cognitive Possibility Space
A theoretical concept used in this chapter to describe the set of cognitive capabilities that are achievable by biological nervous systems (or non-neural biological systems), given the physical and chemical constraints of biology and the range of selective pressures that can act. Certain cognitive capabilities appear to occupy positions in this space that are highly accessible — easily evolvable under common selective pressures — and therefore evolve repeatedly and independently across many lineages (convergent evolution). Others occupy less accessible positions requiring specific neuroanatomical prerequisites. The concept suggests that the evolution of cognition is constrained not only by phylogenetic history but by the topology of an adaptive landscape of cognitive possibilities.
