# Chapter 26 Key Researchers: Simple Nervous Systems and What They Teach Us

---

## Sydney Brenner
**Medical Research Council (1927–2019) — MRC Laboratory of Molecular Biology, Cambridge**

Sydney Brenner is one of the founding figures of molecular biology — he was involved in the deciphering of the genetic code — but his most important contribution to neuroscience was the establishment of *Caenorhabditis elegans* as a model organism for studying development and the nervous system in the 1960s. Brenner recognized that understanding the nervous system required a simple, genetically tractable organism in which every cell could be identified and traced, and he spent decades making *C. elegans* into the system that could answer his questions.

Brenner received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2002, shared with John Sulston and Robert Horvitz, for discoveries concerning genetic regulation of organ development and programmed cell death in *C. elegans*. The Nobel Prize citation recognized the connectome as a central achievement. Brenner's strategic vision — choosing the right organism for the right question — is one of the great examples of scientific leadership.

## John White
**University of Wisconsin-Madison — Morgridge Institute for Research (formerly MRC Laboratory of Molecular Biology)**

John White is the lead author of the 1986 connectome paper — the individual who, over more than a decade of painstaking electron microscopy, reconstructed the complete wiring diagram of the *C. elegans* nervous system. This work required tracking thousands of synaptic connections through serial sections of embedded tissue, without the computational tools that are now available. The effort and discipline required for this achievement are difficult to overstate.

White has continued to contribute to connectome methodology and to our understanding of the functional significance of the *C. elegans* connectome in subsequent decades. His work on gap junctions and on the development of improved connectome reconstruction methods has extended the original achievement into new territories.

## Eric Kandel
**Columbia University — Zuckerman Institute**

Eric Kandel is the central figure in the modern molecular neuroscience of memory. His choice of *Aplysia* as an experimental system, and his decades of patient investigation of the molecular basis of synaptic plasticity in the gill-withdrawal reflex circuit, produced one of the most coherent and consequential research programs in the history of neuroscience.

Kandel received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2000, shared with Arvid Carlsson and Paul Greengard, for discoveries concerning signal transduction in the nervous system. His Nobel lecture, published in *Science* (Kandel, 2001), remains the clearest account of the *Aplysia* work and its implications for mammalian memory. Beyond the science, Kandel's autobiography *In Search of Memory* is one of the best accounts of a scientific life in neuroscience.

## Cornelia Bargmann
**The Rockefeller University — Lulu and Anthony Wang Laboratory of Neural Circuits and Behavior**

Cornelia ("Cori") Bargmann has been a central figure in *C. elegans* neuroscience for four decades, making foundational contributions to the genetic and cellular analysis of chemosensation, behavior, and the relationship between neuromodulation and behavioral state. Her work established that the same neural circuit can produce different behavioral outputs depending on the neuromodulatory environment — a key insight that explains how 302 neurons can support a rich behavioral repertoire.

Bargmann has also contributed substantially to the broader field of systems neuroscience through her leadership roles at the BRAIN Initiative and the Chan Zuckerberg Initiative. Her ability to integrate genetics, neuroscience, and behavioral biology has made her one of the most influential scientists of her generation.

## Gero Miesenböck
**University of Oxford — Department of Physiology**

Miesenböck is one of the inventors of optogenetics — the technique of using light-sensitive proteins (opsins) to control the activity of specific neurons with light. While several laboratories contributed to the development of optogenetics, Miesenböck's early work using rhodopsin variants to activate *Drosophila* neurons with light was among the first demonstrations that this approach was feasible. Optogenetics has transformed neuroscience by making it possible to activate or silence specific neuron types in behaving animals with millisecond precision.

His work in *Drosophila* on the neural basis of courtship and other behaviors exemplifies the power of combining genetic specificity with optical control to dissect neural circuits at the single-neuron level.

## Nicola Clayton
**University of Cambridge — Department of Psychology**

Nicola Clayton is the researcher who established that scrub jays show episodic-like memory — the ability to remember what food they cached, where, and when. Her work demonstrated for the first time that a non-mammalian animal could pass a behavioral test of episodic memory that was thought to require hippocampal function and possibly conscious recollection. The scrub jay work is one of the clearest examples of convergent evolution of a cognitive capacity in a distantly related lineage.

Clayton's work has also contributed to the debate over what episodic memory requires and whether the behavioral criterion (what-where-when memory) is sufficient to establish the cognitive capacity, or whether additional evidence for mental time travel is needed. Her careful framing of the question — distinguishing what behavior shows from what it implies — is a model of philosophical rigor in comparative cognition.
