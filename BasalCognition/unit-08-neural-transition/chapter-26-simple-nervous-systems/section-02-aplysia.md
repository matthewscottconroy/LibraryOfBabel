# Section 2: *Aplysia* and the Molecular Basis of Memory

## The Sea Slug That Changed Neuroscience

*Aplysia californica* is a marine gastropod — a sea slug — that reaches up to 75 centimeters in length and lives in shallow coastal waters from California to Mexico. It is not, by any obvious measure, a candidate for illuminating the molecular basis of human memory. It is an invertebrate, it has roughly 10,000 neurons (far fewer than almost any vertebrate), and it spends most of its time grazing on algae. It does not navigate mazes, solve problems, or exhibit the behavioral sophistication that intuitively seems relevant to memory research.

Yet *Aplysia* is the organism through which we first understood, at the molecular level, how experience changes the nervous system — how the synapse, that fundamental unit of neural communication, is altered by events in ways that persist over time. The story of *Aplysia* research is the story of how a simple question (how does a nervous system learn?) was answered through the right choice of experimental system, at the right moment in the history of neuroscience.

## The Gill-Withdrawal Reflex

Eric Kandel, who received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2000 for this body of work, chose *Aplysia* because of its giant neurons — individually identifiable cells that could be recorded from repeatedly, that could be manipulated, and whose connections to other neurons could be traced anatomically. In a vertebrate nervous system, almost no neurons are large enough or stable enough to be recorded from repeatedly in the same individual. In *Aplysia*, key neurons in the circuits mediating basic behaviors are large enough to see with the naked eye, individually identifiable, and consistently present across individuals.

The gill-withdrawal reflex was the behavioral entry point. When the siphon (a tube-like structure near the gill) of an *Aplysia* is touched, the animal retracts its gill in a defensive withdrawal response. This reflex is mediated by a circuit of sensory neurons, interneurons, and motor neurons that Kandel and colleagues were able to identify and characterize completely — a connectome of the reflex circuit.

Habituation of the gill-withdrawal reflex was the first target: repeated gentle stimulation of the siphon causes the reflex to diminish and eventually disappear. Kandel's group showed that this habituation reflects a decrease in synaptic transmission between the sensory neurons and the motor neurons: the sensory neuron's presynaptic terminal releases less neurotransmitter per action potential after repeated stimulation. The molecular mechanism involves a decrease in the number of active vesicle release sites at the presynaptic terminal (Kandel, 2001).

This was, at the time, a striking result: it showed that learning — even the simple learning of habituation — involves a specific molecular change at a specific synaptic site. Learning was not mysterious; it was chemistry.

## Sensitization and the Serotonin Story

Sensitization — increased responsiveness following a noxious stimulus — was the next target. After a strong shock to the animal's body wall, the gill-withdrawal reflex to a subsequent gentle siphon touch is dramatically enhanced: the reflex is bigger, faster, and more persistent. This sensitization can last for minutes (short-term sensitization) or days (long-term sensitization), depending on the number of sensitizing stimuli applied.

Kandel's group identified the molecular pathway responsible: the noxious shock activates interneurons that release serotonin onto the sensory neuron terminals. Serotonin binds to receptors on the presynaptic terminal and activates an intracellular signaling cascade involving cyclic AMP (cAMP) and protein kinase A (PKA). PKA phosphorylates potassium channels, reducing their activity and thereby prolonging the action potential in the sensory neuron terminal. This prolonged action potential triggers more calcium entry, which releases more neurotransmitter — producing the enhanced synaptic response that is the cellular basis of sensitization.

Short-term sensitization depends on this PKA-dependent phosphorylation, which is reversible and temporary. Long-term sensitization — sensitization that lasts days — requires something more: it requires changes in gene expression and the synthesis of new proteins at the synapse.

## CREB and the Gateway to Long-Term Memory

The molecular gatekeeper of long-term synaptic change turned out to be CREB — the cyclic AMP Response Element Binding protein. CREB is a transcription factor: a protein that binds to specific DNA sequences and activates or represses the transcription of genes.

When repeated sensitizing stimuli drive sustained activation of the cAMP-PKA cascade, PKA translocates to the nucleus of the sensory neuron and phosphorylates CREB, activating it. Activated CREB then turns on genes whose products — new proteins synthesized in response to the learning experience — produce structural changes at the synapse: the growth of new synaptic connections, the remodeling of existing ones, the insertion of new receptor proteins. These structural changes are the cellular basis of long-term memory.

CREB is not a neuron-specific or even an animal-specific protein: it is present in essentially all eukaryotic cells and plays roles in many cellular processes beyond learning. But its role as the molecular gatekeeper of long-term synaptic change — the switch that converts short-term activity into long-term structural modification — has proven to be conserved across enormous evolutionary distances.

Kandel and colleagues demonstrated that the CREB-dependent molecular cascade is not unique to *Aplysia*. In mammals, the same PKA-CREB pathway is required for long-term potentiation (LTP) — the synaptic strengthening that is the cellular basis of learning in the mammalian brain. Mice with genetic disruption of CREB function show specific impairments in long-term memory while short-term memory remains intact. The molecular details of long-term memory storage, revealed in *Aplysia*, are conserved across hundreds of millions of years of evolution in the brains of vastly more complex organisms (Kandel, 2001).

## From Aplysia to Mammalian Memory

The Aplysia work produced one of the most productive connections in 20th-century biology: the realization that the molecular mechanisms of memory storage are conserved from invertebrates to mammals. This conservation had several important consequences.

It validated the model organism strategy: the investment in understanding memory in a simple organism paid off directly in understanding memory in complex organisms. It identified specific molecular targets — the cAMP cascade, CREB, specific kinases and phosphatases — that could be investigated and potentially manipulated in mammalian systems.

It also revealed something important about the nature of long-term memory: it is not simply a matter of strengthened synaptic connections but a matter of structural growth — the formation of new synaptic connections that persist over time. This structural view of long-term memory has been supported by decades of subsequent work in mammalian systems, using microscopy techniques that allow the tracking of individual synaptic connections in living animals over days and weeks.

For our broader inquiry into the evolution of cognition, the Aplysia work illustrates the deep conservation of molecular mechanisms across the animal kingdom. The basic chemistry of learning — cAMP, PKA, CREB, structural synaptic remodeling — is shared from gastropod mollusks to mammals. This conservation suggests that the fundamental molecular basis of memory was established early in animal evolution and has been maintained through hundreds of millions of years of neural diversification.

## The Limits of the Aplysia Model

The Aplysia model, like all models, has limits that should be acknowledged. The behaviors that have been studied — habituation and sensitization of a simple reflex, and simple associative conditioning — are among the most elementary forms of learning. The molecular mechanisms underlying these elementary forms may not scale straightforwardly to the complex, context-dependent, narrative memory that characterizes human cognition.

More specifically: long-term potentiation in the mammalian hippocampus, while sharing molecular features with long-term facilitation in *Aplysia*, is embedded in neural circuits of enormously greater complexity, with dozens of modulatory systems, reciprocal connections, and computational operations — like pattern completion and pattern separation — that have no obvious counterparts in the *Aplysia* circuit.

This is not a criticism of the model but a calibration. The *Aplysia* model established the molecular elementary unit of memory storage. Understanding how those elementary units are integrated into the complex, flexible, context-sensitive memory systems of vertebrate brains is a separate project, one that requires different methods and different experimental systems.
