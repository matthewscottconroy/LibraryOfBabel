# Section 3: The *Drosophila* Brain — Genetic Dissection of Cognition

## A Hundred Thousand Neurons

The common fruit fly, *Drosophila melanogaster*, has been a central model organism in genetics for over a century — since Thomas Hunt Morgan established the chromosomal theory of heredity using fly crosses in the early 1900s. The fly's short generation time (about two weeks), large broods, small genome (four chromosome pairs), and extraordinary genetic tractability — the ability to make precise genetic manipulations and to express specific genes in specific cells — have made it the premier organism for genetic analysis of complex biological phenomena.

For neuroscience, *Drosophila* offers something that simpler organisms like *C. elegans* cannot: approximately 100,000 neurons in the adult brain (the number varies by region and sex, but this is the rough order of magnitude), supporting a behavioral repertoire that includes navigation, olfactory learning, visual processing, circadian rhythms, aggression, courtship and mating, social behaviors, and rudimentary forms of episodic-like memory.

This is not a simple system in any conventional sense. But it is tractable — far more so than any vertebrate brain — because the genetic tools available in *Drosophila* allow researchers to ask questions about specific neuron types and specific circuits with a precision that is impossible in mammals.

## The Mushroom Body: Learning and Memory Center

The most studied brain region in *Drosophila* is the mushroom body — a bilateral pair of structures in the fly's brain that serves as the primary site for olfactory learning and memory. The mushroom body is named for its resemblance, in histological sections, to a pair of stacked mushroom caps.

The mushroom body receives inputs from the olfactory system and from modulatory neurons that convey information about the rewarding or aversive value of stimuli. Its output neurons project to downstream circuits that control behavior. In a classical olfactory conditioning experiment, a fly is trained by pairing an odorant (CS+, the conditioned stimulus) with an electric shock (the unconditioned aversive stimulus). The fly subsequently avoids the CS+ odorant. This conditioned avoidance is dependent on the mushroom body: flies with mushroom body lesions cannot form olfactory memories.

The mushroom body circuit has been worked out in considerable detail. Olfactory information arrives from the antennal lobe (the fly's version of the olfactory bulb) at the Kenyon cells — the principal neurons of the mushroom body, of which there are approximately 2,000 per hemisphere. Each Kenyon cell receives input from a sparse, semi-random combination of olfactory projection neurons, making Kenyon cells specific to particular combinations of olfactory inputs rather than to single odorants. This combinatorial coding at the Kenyon cell level is what gives the mushroom body its capacity to discriminate among a large number of odors.

The "learning signal" that modifies Kenyon cell output is delivered by dopaminergic neurons — neurons that release dopamine — which innervate the mushroom body in a precise spatial pattern. Different dopamine neuron subpopulations respond to different valences (appetitive vs. aversive) and different phases of the memory trace (acquisition, consolidation, retrieval). By using genetic tools to activate or silence specific dopamine neuron subpopulations while measuring fly behavior and neural activity, researchers have been able to identify, with unprecedented cellular precision, which neurons control which aspects of learning and memory.

## Genetic Dissection of Behavior

The power of *Drosophila* for neuroscience lies in its genetic tools, and it is worth pausing to appreciate what these tools make possible.

The UAS-GAL4 system allows researchers to express any gene of interest in any specific subset of neurons, by using regulatory sequences (GAL4 drivers) that are active only in specific cell types to drive expression of a UAS-linked transgene. This means researchers can selectively activate neurons with light-sensitive channels (optogenetics), silence neurons with temperature-sensitive suppressors, kill specific neurons with toxins, or label specific neurons with fluorescent markers — all in specific, genetically defined cell types — and then measure the behavioral consequences.

This genetic precision has produced an extraordinary level of mechanistic understanding of *Drosophila* behavior. The neurons that control the initiation of flight, the orientation to visual targets, the courtship song, the aggression response, and many other behaviors have been identified and characterized at the single-cell level. The circuit diagrams underlying these behaviors are being worked out at a resolution that would be impossible in any vertebrate system.

The behavioral repertoire that has been dissected genetically includes capacities that one might not expect from a fly with a millimeter-scale brain: spatial memory (flies can remember the position of a rewarded location in a maze), temporal discrimination (flies can learn to associate a stimulus with a specific time of day), and some capacity for second-order conditioning (learning to associate a previously neutral stimulus with a conditioned stimulus, rather than directly with the unconditioned stimulus).

None of these behaviors demonstrates the kind of flexible, abstract reasoning associated with human cognition. But they do demonstrate that 100,000 neurons, properly organized and modulated, can support a much richer behavioral repertoire than one might naively expect.

## The Connectome Project

The White et al. (1986) *C. elegans* connectome took 14 years to complete using serial electron microscopy. Advances in electron microscopy, tissue processing, and computational image analysis have now made it possible to reconstruct connectomes from much larger nervous systems.

The *Drosophila* brain connectome project — several parallel efforts using different combinations of imaging and computational methods — has produced draft connectomes of increasingly large portions of the fly brain. The hemibrain connectome, published in 2020, characterized the synaptic connectivity of approximately half the fly brain — roughly 25,000 neurons and 20 million synapses — at full electron microscopic resolution. The complete fly brain connectome is a near-term target.

What do we learn from a complete connectome of 100,000 neurons? The connectome reveals the detailed circuit architecture of the brain — which neurons connect to which, with how many synapses, and (in combination with imaging of neural activity) what those connections do. It provides the substrate for computational modeling of circuit function and for systematic testing of predictions about how manipulating specific circuit elements will affect behavior.

The mushroom body circuit, which is now known in exquisite detail from the connectome, illustrates what connectome data can offer: a precise structural description of the learning circuit that, combined with genetic manipulation and behavioral measurement, allows precise mechanistic understanding of how olfactory memories are formed and retrieved.

## What *Drosophila* Teaches Cognitive Science

The *Drosophila* brain makes three things clear that are important for cognitive science.

First, sophisticated learning and memory — multiple memory phases (short-term, medium-term, long-term), different valences (appetitive, aversive), context-dependent retrieval — can be implemented in a brain of 100,000 neurons. The organizing principles of learning — dopaminergic teaching signals, sparse odor representations, compartmentalized memory encoding — are not uniquely human or even mammalian; they are general solutions to the problem of learning that evolution has discovered in insects.

Second, the genetic approach to circuit dissection reveals that specific behaviors are implemented by specific, identifiable neural circuits. Behavior is not a property of the brain as a whole but of specific circuit motifs — patterns of connectivity that implement specific computations. Identifying these circuit motifs in *Drosophila* has generated hypotheses about similar circuit motifs in vertebrate brains, some of which have been confirmed.

Third, the gap between the neural substrate and the behavioral output — the mystery of how specific circuit elements give rise to specific cognitive capabilities — is resolvable in principle. In *Drosophila*, we are approaching a level of mechanistic understanding at which the circuit basis of specific learning and memory phenomena can be traced from the sensory input through the modulatory signals through the synaptic changes to the behavioral output. This is what a complete account of neural cognition looks like in a simple system. Achieving comparable accounts in more complex systems is the central challenge of systems neuroscience.
