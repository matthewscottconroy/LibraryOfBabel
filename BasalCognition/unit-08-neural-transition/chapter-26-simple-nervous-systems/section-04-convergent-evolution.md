# Section 4: Convergent Evolution and the Cognitive Possibility Space

## The Lesson of Eyes

The vertebrate eye is a biological wonder: a complex optical instrument, with a lens that adjusts its focal length for near and far vision, a photoreceptor array of extraordinary sensitivity and dynamic range, and a neural processing chain that begins extracting features — edges, motion, color — before the image has even left the retina. It took centuries to understand how such a complex structure could have evolved.

The discovery that complicated things, but enriched our understanding enormously, is that eyes evolved independently at least 40 times in different animal lineages. Insect compound eyes, octopus eyes, starfish eyes, box jellyfish eyes, and vertebrate eyes are all functional optical systems that convert light into neural signals — but they are not homologous. They evolved independently, from different precursor structures, using some of the same molecular components (opsins, in particular, are conserved) but different optical designs.

This is convergent evolution: independent evolution of similar solutions to the same adaptive problem. Convergent evolution, when it occurs, tells us something deep: that the evolved solution is not arbitrary but reflects the structure of the problem. If evolution independently discovers the same solution multiple times, it suggests that the problem space has a shape — that some solutions are more accessible, more stable, or more effective than others, and that natural selection reliably finds them.

## Convergent Evolution of Cognitive Capacities

Convergent evolution is not limited to morphological structures like eyes. It extends to cognitive capacities — to behavioral capabilities that evolve independently in distantly related lineages because they solve common adaptive problems.

Self-recognition in a mirror is a striking cognitive capacity that was long thought to be restricted to great apes. The mirror self-recognition test — in which an animal with a visible mark on its body investigates the mark after seeing itself in a mirror — has now been passed by chimpanzees, bonobos, orangutans, gorillas, bottlenose dolphins, Asian elephants, Eurasian magpies, and (in some experiments, though the interpretation is contested) cleaner wrasse fish (Kohda et al., 2019). These animals represent at least four independent evolutionary lineages — primates, cetaceans, proboscideans, and corvids — and in each case the self-recognition capacity appears to have evolved independently.

Tool use shows a similar pattern. Primate tool use and corvid tool use — New Caledonian crows make and use hook-shaped tools to extract insects from bark — evolved independently in lineages separated by hundreds of millions of years. The cognitive demands of tool manufacture and use (planning, causal understanding of tool-object relationships, motor skill) were apparently met by the neural architectures of both primates and corvids through independent evolutionary pathways.

Episodic-like memory — the ability to recall specific past events in their spatial and temporal context — has been demonstrated in corvids (scrub jays), who remember what food they cached, where they cached it, and how long ago. This was the first non-human demonstration of what was thought to be a specifically human and specifically hippocampus-dependent cognitive capacity.

## What Convergence Tells Us About Intelligence

The convergent evolution of cognitive capacities across independent lineages has two important implications.

The first is functional: certain cognitive capabilities solve adaptive problems that recur across many different ecological contexts. Self-recognition may be adaptive in social species where distinguishing self from others is important. Tool use is adaptive wherever manually inaccessible food sources are available. Episodic memory is adaptive wherever the spatial and temporal context of past events is relevant to current behavior. These capabilities evolve repeatedly because the selective pressures that favor them are broadly present.

The second implication is architectural: the neural architectures that implement these capabilities can be very different and still achieve the same functional outcome. The cerebral cortex that implements self-recognition and tool use in primates is architecturally very different from the pallium that implements self-recognition and tool use in birds. The mushroom body that implements olfactory associative memory in insects is architecturally very different from the hippocampus that implements spatial and episodic memory in mammals. Yet both pairs of structures solve similar computational problems.

This architectural diversity in the face of functional similarity suggests that the cognitive capability is what is under selection — the ability to solve the problem — not the specific neural implementation. Evolution can, and does, build the same functional capability from different structural starting points. This is the cognitive equivalent of the physical principle that the same computation can be implemented in many different physical substrates.

## The Cognitive Possibility Space

The convergent evolution of cognitive capacities suggests the existence of what we might call a cognitive possibility space: the set of cognitive capabilities that are achievable by neural (and non-neural) biological systems, given the physical and chemical constraints of biology and the adaptive pressures that selection can exert.

Some capabilities appear to occupy positions in this possibility space that are highly accessible — they evolve readily under common selective pressures. Basic sensory processing, associative learning, and behavioral flexibility are found across essentially all animals with nervous systems. Other capabilities appear to occupy positions that require more specific neuroanatomical prerequisites — self-recognition and tool use may require a threshold of neural complexity and integration that only certain lineages have crossed.

The capacities we have been studying throughout this book — the chemotaxis of bacteria, the learning of Physarum, the network optimization of mycelium, the collective decision-making of bee swarms — also occupy positions in this cognitive possibility space. They are achieved by very different biological substrates, but they solve recognizable cognitive problems: gradient sensing, memory, optimization, decision-making.

What the convergent evolution of neural cognitive capacities adds to this picture is the observation that the neural architecture itself is variable — that the same cognitive capability can be implemented in different neural structures. This makes the claim that nervous systems have a special role in cognition more precise: it is not that any particular neural architecture is necessary for cognition, but that certain functional organizations — certain patterns of sensory input, integration, modulation, and behavioral output — can be achieved by nervous systems more reliably and at higher levels of sophistication than by the non-neural substrates we have been examining earlier in the book.

## Closing the Circle: Intelligence Before the Brain

This section, and this chapter, bring us to the end of a long journey. We began with the simplest biological entities — individual molecules, single cells, bacterial colonies — and asked what they can sense, compute, remember, and decide. We moved through protists, plants, fungi, and collective animal systems, finding cognitive capacities wherever we looked, implemented in substrates as different as membrane channels and mycelial networks. And now we have arrived at simple nervous systems — at 302 neurons and 100,000 neurons and the molecular basis of memory — and found that the principles that operate here are continuous with the principles that operated in the simpler systems we examined earlier.

The bacterial chemotaxis pathway and the neural chemotaxis circuitry of *C. elegans* use different molecular machinery but implement the same computational logic: compare stimulus concentration at different times, bias movement toward increasing concentrations. The cAMP signaling cascade that mediates sensitization in *Aplysia* is the same molecular machinery used for signal transduction in single-celled organisms. The CREB transcription factor that gates long-term memory in *Aplysia* and in mammalian brains is a ubiquitous eukaryotic protein with functions far beyond neurons. The principles of collective intelligence that operate in bee swarms are recognizable instantiations of the same positive and negative feedback principles that operate in bacterial quorum sensing.

Intelligence before the brain is not a pale shadow of intelligence with the brain. It is the foundation on which neural intelligence was built — a foundation that remains visible in the molecular machinery, the computational logic, and the functional organization of the simplest nervous systems we know.

The brain did not invent intelligence. It elaborated it. And understanding what was there before the brain is essential for understanding what the brain is and what it does.
