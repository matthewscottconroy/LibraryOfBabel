# Section 5: Hydra and the Radially Symmetric Mind

## Introduction

*Hydra* is a small freshwater polyp, a few millimeters long, distantly related to jellyfish and sea anemones, and belonging to the phylum Cnidaria — the oldest animal group known to have neurons. It has no brain, no centralized ganglion, no head-tail axis that would suggest a preferred direction of information flow. Its nervous system is a diffuse net of interconnected neurons distributed across the entire body column, foot, and tentacles. By the classical criteria of neuroscience — centralization, directionality, hierarchical organization — Hydra should barely qualify as having a nervous system at all.

And yet *Hydra* exhibits behavioral capacities that command respect: it coordinates complex whole-body movements for feeding and escape, it habituates to repeated stimuli, and it regenerates its entire body, including its nervous system, from a fragment. The story of what *Hydra* can do with a diffuse nerve net — and what that story tells us about the relationship between neural architecture and cognitive capacity — is one of the most instructive in the field of basal cognition.

---

## 5.1 The Anatomy of a Diffuse Nervous System

The *Hydra* nerve net is not, as early anatomists assumed, a completely uniform web of neurons with no internal organization. It consists of multiple neuronal populations with distinct morphologies, positions, and neurotransmitter profiles — sensory neurons distributed through the epithelium, ganglion neurons in the body column, and peptidergic neurons releasing various neuropeptides. These populations form overlapping networks with different functional properties.

What is absent is any structure analogous to a brain or ganglionic center — any region where the density of neurons is dramatically higher than elsewhere, suggesting a specialized site of integration. This makes *Hydra* an ideal test case: if coordinated whole-body behavior requires a centralized integrating structure, *Hydra* should not be able to produce it. If it can, then centralization is not necessary for coordination.

Passano and McCullough (1964), in a classic series of electrophysiological experiments published in the Journal of Experimental Biology, characterized the electrical activity of the *Hydra* nerve net. They identified propagating electrical discharges that travel across the body wall in patterns correlated with different behaviors. During feeding, a specific pattern of discharges produces the tentacle and mouth movements associated with prey capture. During locomotion (somersaulting), a different pattern produces coordinated whole-body contractions. The nerve net does not simply relay signals from point to point; it generates and propagates patterned activity that coordinates multiple body regions simultaneously.

## 5.2 Calcium Imaging Reveals Functional Organization

Classical electrophysiology gave us the broad outlines of *Hydra* nerve net activity; modern calcium imaging has revealed the fine structure. Dupre and Yuste (2017), using genetically encoded calcium indicators expressed throughout the *Hydra* nervous system and light-sheet microscopy to image the entire animal simultaneously, produced the first whole-animal neural activity maps of *Hydra* during natural behavior.

Their results were striking. Despite the absence of any obvious anatomical brain structure, the *Hydra* nerve net is functionally organized: different neuron populations show coordinated activity during specific behavioral states. During body column contractions, a specific neuronal subpopulation shows synchronized activity. During tentacle movements, a different population is active. During feeding behavior, the two populations interact in a stereotyped temporal sequence.

The functional organization revealed by calcium imaging is not random. It has the character of a distributed computational system in which different neuron subsets are specialized for different behavioral roles, even though no anatomical specialization distinguishes them by location or morphology. The organization is in the pattern of connectivity and activity, not in the gross anatomy.

This finding has important implications for the broader thesis of this chapter and of this book. It suggests that functional cognitive organization — the differential recruitment of neuron subsets for different behavioral functions — does not require centralization. A diffuse nervous system can be functionally organized in ways that produce complex, coordinated behavior. The organizing principle is not spatial (a brain in one location) but temporal (patterns of activity across the distributed network).

## 5.3 Habituation and Simple Learning

*Hydra* habituates. When the water it inhabits is gently disturbed, the polyp contracts rapidly — withdrawing its tentacles and shortening its body column. This is a defensive response. But if the disturbance is repeated at regular intervals without any associated harm, the contraction response diminishes over trials and eventually disappears: the animal has habituated.

This habituation has the canonical properties of the phenomenon as characterized across all nervous systems: it is stimulus-specific (habituation to mechanical disturbance does not generalize to a different stimulus like a change in light), it recovers spontaneously with time, and it can be dishabituated by a strong novel stimulus. The same cellular mechanisms that underlie habituation in *Aplysia* (Section 2) — decreased presynaptic neurotransmitter release — are almost certainly at work here, though the *Hydra* preparation is less amenable to the single-neuron electrophysiology that characterized the *Aplysia* mechanism.

Habituation is the simplest form of learned behavioral modification, and its presence in *Hydra* tells us something important: the capacity for experience-dependent behavioral change was present at the earliest stages of animal nervous system evolution. Habituation does not require a brain; it requires neurons with synapses that can undergo plasticity. That plasticity appears to have been present from the beginning.

## 5.4 Regeneration and the Non-Neural Body Plan

One of the most remarkable properties of *Hydra* is its regenerative capacity. A *Hydra* bisected horizontally into head and foot fragments will regenerate — each piece will grow back the missing half, complete with a functional nervous system — within days. The head fragment regrows a foot; the foot fragment regrows a head and tentacles. This regeneration is not random; the polarity is maintained. The piece that was the top grows back into a head-like structure; the piece that was the foot regrows into a foot.

What guides this regeneration? Crucially, the body pattern information is not stored exclusively in the nervous system. Enucleated *Hydra* fragments — fragments from which all neural cells have been removed — can still regenerate, slowly and imperfectly, but unmistakably: the body plan information is encoded elsewhere, in the non-neural tissue, in the bioelectric patterns and positional gradients that pervade the body column.

This connects directly to Michael Levin's bioelectric framework (Chapter 27). The regenerative target state — the body plan that the tissue is "trying" to restore — is encoded in bioelectric patterns that exist across the tissue as a whole, not specifically in neurons. The nervous system facilitates regeneration and coordinates the behavioral output of the regenerated animal, but it is not the repository of the organizational information that guides regeneration.

For our broader inquiry, this means that even in animals that have nervous systems, the nervous system is not the sole carrier of organismal organizational information. Some of the information that determines what the organism is, and what it will become after injury, resides in the non-neural tissue — in exactly the kind of bioelectric and chemical patterning that we have been examining in plants, slime molds, and single cells throughout this book. The nervous system elaborates, specializes, and accelerates cognitive processes that were, in their rudimentary forms, already present in the non-neural body.
