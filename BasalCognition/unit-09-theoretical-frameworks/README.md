# Unit IX: Theoretical Frameworks — Making Sense of Basal Cognition

---

> *"The empirical findings are remarkable. But remarkable findings demand remarkable theories. This unit examines the major theoretical frameworks that attempt to explain what basal cognition is — not just describe what it does. These are the conceptual tools with which the field is building a new science of mind."*

---

## Unit Introduction

By this point in the book, the empirical picture is rich. We have documented cognitive capacities — sensing, integration, memory, learning, anticipation, collective decision-making — across the full breadth of non-neural life. We have seen that these capacities are not peripheral curiosities but central to what organisms do and why they survive.

But description is not explanation. The empirical finding that E. coli integrates chemical signals and modulates behavior accordingly tells us that E. coli is a cognitive system. It does not tell us *why* this is cognition rather than mere chemistry, or *how* to understand it within a broader theoretical framework that connects it to the cognition of neurons, of organisms, of colonies.

This unit is about that broader theoretical framework. It examines five major theoretical programs that have been applied to basal cognition, each approaching the problem from a different angle and each illuminating different aspects of it.

---

## The Five Frameworks

**Chapter 27 — Autopoiesis and Enactivism**: The most biologically grounded of the frameworks. Maturana and Varela's autopoiesis theory establishes the connection between life and cognition at the organizational level. Enactivism (Varela, Thompson, Rosch) extends this into a comprehensive account of cognition as organism-environment coupling rather than internal representation.

**Chapter 28 — Predictive Processing and the Free Energy Principle**: Karl Friston's free energy principle provides a mathematical framework — arguably the most ambitious in biology — that claims to unify perception, action, learning, and evolution under a single principle: the minimization of surprise (variational free energy). When applied to all biological systems, it makes the striking claim that every living system is, in some sense, a prediction machine.

**Chapter 29 — Integrated Information Theory**: Giulio Tononi's IIT proposes that consciousness is identical to integrated information — a quantity that can, in principle, be calculated for any physical system. Its application to non-neural organisms generates predictions that are both exciting and controversial.

**Chapter 30 — Levin's Cognitive Light Cone and Scale-Free Cognition**: Michael Levin's framework proposes that cognitive processes exist at every level of biological organization — cells, tissues, organs, organisms, colonies — and that the same fundamental cognitive algorithms operate at each level. The "cognitive light cone" specifies the spatial-temporal scale at which a given system's competencies are integrated.

**Chapter 31 — Minimal Cognition Theories**: The philosophical and formal accounts of what constitutes the minimum conditions for genuine cognition — the floor of the cognitive spectrum. Lyon's biogenic approach and Barandiaran & Moreno's MCO framework are examined in depth.

---

## How to Read This Unit

The five frameworks in this unit are not competing theories of the same thing — they approach basal cognition from different angles, ask different questions, and are useful for different purposes. Autopoiesis asks: what is the organizational basis of cognition? Predictive processing asks: what computational principle unifies cognitive behavior? IIT asks: what is the relationship between cognition and experience? Levin's framework asks: at what scales does cognition exist? Minimal cognition theory asks: what is the floor?

A sophisticated understanding of basal cognition requires engagement with all five. They complement more than they contradict.

## A Note on Theoretical Standards

This unit maintains the same standards of intellectual honesty that govern the empirical units. Where theories are well-supported, this is noted. Where they are contested — and all five frameworks are contested to varying degrees — the objections are named and examined. Where empirical predictions are generated, they are stated clearly. Where theories resist empirical test, this is acknowledged as a limitation.

The philosophy of science matters here as much as the science itself. A theory that explains everything and predicts nothing is not a scientific theory. Several of the frameworks in this unit walk close to this edge, and understanding exactly where each framework stands on the spectrum from bold empirical hypothesis to unfalsifiable framework is part of what this unit teaches.
