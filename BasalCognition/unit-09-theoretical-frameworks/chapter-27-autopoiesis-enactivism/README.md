# Chapter 27: Autopoiesis and Enactivism

## The Living Machine That Makes Itself

There is a peculiar riddle at the heart of biology that physics and chemistry alone cannot dissolve. A rock erodes. A flame burns. A crystal grows. But only a living cell *maintains* itself against entropy through its own activity — consuming matter and energy to regenerate the very machinery that does the consuming. A cell is not merely a complicated chemical reaction; it is a process that produces and reproduces itself. This self-production is so fundamental, so unlike anything we find in the non-living world, that two Chilean biologists in the early 1970s coined a new word for it: *autopoiesis*.

Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela were not primarily interested in cognition when they developed the concept. They were trying to answer a question that seemed both obvious and impossibly hard: what is the defining feature that makes something alive? Their answer — that living systems are systems organized to produce themselves — turned out to have profound implications far beyond cell biology. It implied, as they gradually worked out, that cognition is not a special capacity layered on top of life, but is constitutive of life itself. Any system that maintains itself by making distinctions in its environment — by selectively interacting with some things and not others, in ways that preserve its own organization — is already, in some minimal sense, *knowing* its world.

This chapter explores autopoiesis as a theoretical framework and follows its development into enactivism, the broader philosophical and scientific program that Varela, Evan Thompson, and Eleanor Rosch elaborated in *The Embodied Mind* (1991). Enactivism is one of the most influential frameworks in contemporary cognitive science, and it has particular relevance for basal cognition because it does not treat mind as the exclusive property of neural systems. On the enactivist view, sense-making — the organism's active engagement with a world that is, from the organism's perspective, meaningful — is present wherever there is life.

## Central Question

**What does it mean to say that a bacterium "knows" its environment?** Not metaphorically, not by analogy with human knowledge, but in a precise theoretical sense that connects cellular processes to the broader concept of cognition? Autopoiesis and enactivism together offer one of the most carefully developed answers to this question available in contemporary science and philosophy.

## Chapter Roadmap

**Section 27.1 — The Autopoietic Framework** begins with Maturana and Varela's foundational concepts: self-production, organizational closure, and the critical distinction between a system's *organization* (its abstract relational structure) and its *structure* (the actual physical components implementing that organization). It then develops the concept of *structural coupling* — the way autopoietic systems interact with their environments without being instructed by those environments — and explains why Maturana came to identify cognition with life itself.

**Section 27.2 — Enactivism** traces how Varela, Thompson, and Rosch transformed autopoiesis into a full research program in cognitive science. It explains the "four E's" that characterize the enactivist approach (embodied, embedded, enacted, extended), examines the concept of *sense-making* as the minimal unit of cognitive engagement, and applies the framework to unicellular organisms. The section closes with a careful account of the major critiques — from both the philosophy of mind and from empirical biology — and considers how enactivism might be refined in response to them.

## A Note on Two Levels of Claim

Enactivism operates at two distinct levels that are easy to conflate. The first is an *empirical* claim: that cognition cannot be understood as a purely internal process but is always shaped by the organism's body, its sensorimotor history, and its ecological context. This claim has strong experimental support and is now largely mainstream in cognitive science. The second is a stronger *phenomenological* claim: that lived experience is constituted by organism-environment coupling, that there is no mind-independent "content" carried by neural representations, and that subjective experience arises from enactive engagement rather than from internal information processing. This second claim is philosophically contested and remains an active area of debate.

Working scientists studying bacterial chemotaxis or slime mold navigation can productively use the enactivist framework for its empirical insights without committing to its stronger phenomenological theses. This chapter aims to keep these levels clearly distinguished so you can adopt what is useful and think carefully about what remains open.
