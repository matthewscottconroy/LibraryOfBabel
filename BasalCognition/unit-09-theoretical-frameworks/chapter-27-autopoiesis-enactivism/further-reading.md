# Further Reading: Chapter 27 — Autopoiesis and Enactivism

---

**Maturana, H.R., & Varela, F.J. (1980). *Autopoiesis and Cognition: The Realization of the Living*. D. Reidel.**
The foundational text. The first essay, "Biology of Cognition," was written in 1970 and represents Maturana's initial attempt to work out the implications of operational closure for perception and cognition. The second essay, "Autopoiesis: The Organization of the Living," is the formal presentation of the theory. Essential reading, though demanding; Maturana's prose is dense and self-referential in ways that mirror his theoretical commitments.

**Varela, F.J., Thompson, E., & Rosch, E. (1991). *The Embodied Mind: Cognitive Science and Human Experience*. MIT Press.**
The book that transformed autopoiesis into a broad cognitive science research program. Particularly valuable is the dialogue it maintains with Buddhist mindfulness traditions, which Varela saw as a phenomenological partner for enactivist theory. The sections on color perception and on the immune system as a second "self" remain fascinating and underappreciated by mainstream cognitive science.

**Thompson, E. (2007). *Mind in Life: Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of Mind*. Harvard University Press.**
Thompson's major independent work, and arguably the most philosophically rigorous development of enactivism available. The chapters on life and mind, on emotion and feeling, and on consciousness all draw on Hans Jonas, Husserl, and Merleau-Ponty to build a sophisticated biological phenomenology. Graduate students who want to take enactivism seriously as a philosophical position should read this carefully.

**Maturana, H.R., & Varela, F.J. (1987). *The Tree of Knowledge: The Biological Roots of Human Understanding*. Shambhala.**
A more accessible version of the main ideas, intended for a general educated audience. Beautifully illustrated and written with unusual clarity compared to their earlier technical work. A good entry point before tackling *Autopoiesis and Cognition* directly.

**Jonas, H. (1966). *The Phenomenon of Life: Toward a Philosophical Biology*. Harper & Row.**
A philosophical masterpiece that anticipates many enactivist themes. Jonas argues that with metabolism comes a primordial form of concern, of being for whom things can go better or worse, and this normative dimension is irreducible to physics and chemistry. His concept of "organic freedom" — the organism's characteristic mode of relating to its environment by transcending its immediate material constraints — is one of the most profound ideas in twentieth-century philosophy of biology.

**Varela, F.J. (1979). *Principles of Biological Autonomy*. North Holland.**
An earlier, more formal treatment of autopoiesis and autonomy by Varela alone, which introduces the concepts of organizational closure and autonomous systems in more mathematical terms. Valuable for readers with backgrounds in dynamical systems theory who want a more rigorous foundation.

**Di Paolo, E.A., Rohde, M., & De Jaegher, H. (2010). Horizons for the enactive mind: Values, social interaction, and play. In J. Stewart, O. Gapenne, & E.A. Di Paolo (Eds.), *Enaction: Toward a New Paradigm for Cognitive Science* (pp. 33–87). MIT Press.**
A major extension of enactivism into the domains of social cognition and participatory sense-making. Di Paolo and colleagues argue that social interaction constitutes its own kind of autonomous process with properties not reducible to the sense-making of individual organisms. Particularly relevant for thinking about collective intelligence in basal organisms.

**Hutto, D.D., & Myin, E. (2013). *Radicalizing Enactivism: Basic Minds without Content*. MIT Press.**
A provocative development of enactivism that argues most forms of cognition — including all basal forms — do not involve contentful mental states at all. Content, on their view, is a sophisticated achievement of language-using creatures. This "radical" enactivism generates both interesting predictions for basal cognition and significant philosophical problems, and engaging with it sharpens one's thinking about what "content" and "representation" actually mean.

**Luisi, P.L. (2003). Autopoiesis: A review and a reappraisal. *Naturwissenschaften*, 90(2), 49–59.**
A useful scientific review that examines autopoiesis in the context of origin-of-life research. Luisi, a chemist who has worked on protocell research, provides a critical assessment of what the autopoietic framework does and does not contribute to our understanding of the minimal requirements for life. Helpful for connecting philosophical framework to empirical biology.

**Rudrauf, D., Lutz, A., Cosmelli, D., Lachaux, J.P., & Le Van Quyen, M. (2003). From autopoiesis to neurophenomenology: Francisco Varela's exploration of the biophysics of being. *Biological Research*, 36(1), 27–65.**
An overview of Varela's scientific legacy, covering his contributions from autopoiesis through immunology to neurophenomenology. Valuable for understanding how the theoretical framework informed and was informed by Varela's empirical work in neuroscience, and for seeing the connections between the philosophical program and laboratory research.

**Moreno, A., & Mossio, M. (2015). *Biological Autonomy: A Philosophical and Theoretical Enquiry*. Springer.**
A rigorous contemporary philosophical analysis of biological autonomy that draws on and extends the autopoietic tradition. Moreno and Mossio develop a theory of "closure of constraints" that preserves the key insights of autopoiesis while rendering them more compatible with mechanistic biology. Essential reading for anyone who wants to take the core ideas seriously while engaging with recent philosophy of biology.
