# Key Concepts: Chapter 27 — Autopoiesis and Enactivism

---

## Autopoiesis

From the Greek *autos* (self) and *poiein* (to make). A system is autopoietic if its components continuously produce and regenerate the very network of processes that produce those components — if it is, in short, a self-producing entity. The paradigm case is the living cell: its molecular components produce the enzymes that catalyze their synthesis, the membrane that bounds the cell, the ribosomes that assemble proteins, and so on in a closed network of mutual production. Autopoiesis is proposed by Maturana and Varela as the defining property of living systems: a system is alive if and only if it is autopoietic. This definition deliberately excludes viruses (which cannot replicate without host machinery) and crystals (which grow through accretion rather than self-production) while including all cells. See also: *organizational closure*, *structural coupling*.

---

## Organizational Closure

The property of a system whose processes form a closed network: every process in the network both depends on and contributes to other processes in the same network, with no process functioning purely as a unidirectional input or output to an external system. Organizational closure does not mean material isolation — closed systems still exchange matter and energy with their environments — but it means that the *logic* of the system's processes is self-referential: the network as a whole determines what counts as an input and how inputs are processed. In an autopoietic cell, metabolic processes are organizationally closed: enzyme A catalyzes the synthesis of enzyme B, which catalyzes the synthesis of the substrate for enzyme A, and so on. The network refers only to itself. Contrast with *operational closure*, which is a related but somewhat broader concept Maturana uses to describe the nervous system's closed dynamical structure.

---

## Organization vs. Structure

A fundamental distinction in the autopoietic framework. The *organization* of a system is the abstract pattern of relations among its components — the topology of the network — defined independently of what the components actually are. The *structure* of a system is the concrete physical implementation of that organization at a particular time — the actual molecules, their quantities, their spatial arrangement. Organization defines the *kind* of system something is; structure is how that kind is instantiated at a particular moment. Two systems can share the same organization while having completely different structures (biological convergence is a rough analogy). A single system maintains its organization while its structure changes continuously over time (a cell renewing its molecular components). The persistence of organizational identity through structural change is what constitutes biological individuality.

---

## Structural Coupling

The history-dependent relationship that develops between an autopoietic system and its environment (or between two or more autopoietic systems) through repeated interactions. Each interaction perturbs the system, triggering structural changes that may preserve or destroy its autopoietic organization. Over time, systems whose structural changes have been compatible with continued autopoiesis acquire structures that are congruent with the regularities of their environments — not because the environment has been copied into the system, but because the system's structure has been shaped by a history of successful couplings. Structural coupling is the autopoietic account of adaptation, learning, and co-evolution: all three are forms of structural change in the history of repeated interaction. The key point is that structural coupling does not involve the transfer of information from environment to organism; it involves the shaping of organizational structure through a history of perturbations.

## Sense-Making

The enactivist concept for the most basic form of cognitive engagement: the active constitution of significance relative to the organism's own autopoietic organization. Sense-making has three essential features: *perspective* (there is a point of view from which things are significant), *valence* (significance is evaluative — things are better or worse for the organism), and *agency* (significance is constituted through the organism's own activity, not passively received from the world). Sense-making is held to be present in every autopoietic system: any organism that acts to maintain its own organization thereby constitutes its environment as a domain of significance structured around that organization. Sense-making in this technical sense is not the same as conscious meaning-making in the human sense; it is the functional analog from which, enactivists argue, richer forms of meaning emerge in more complex organisms. Proposed by Thompson (2007) as the minimal criterion for cognition.

---

## Umwelt

Borrowed by enactivism from the biologist Jakob von Uexküll (1909), who used it to describe the species-specific perceptual and action world of each organism. The Umwelt is not a representation of the physical environment; it is the meaningful world as enacted from the organism's own perspective. A tick's Umwelt consists of warmth, butyric acid, and mammalian skin — the features of the environment that are action-relevant for this organism given its body and needs. A bacterium's Umwelt is structured by chemical gradients, osmotic pressures, and light intensities that are relevant to its metabolic self-maintenance. Enactivism uses the Umwelt concept to emphasize that different organisms enact different worlds from the same physical environment, and that there is no organism-neutral "view from nowhere" on the environment.

---

## Enaction / Enactivism

The theoretical position holding that cognition is not the processing of a pre-given world by an internal information-processing system, but the active *bringing forth* or *enaction* of a meaningful world through the organism's embodied engagement with its environment. Enactivism denies that perception involves the recovery of pre-existing environmental features (against classical cognitivism) and denies that the mind is located inside the skull (against methodological individualism). The term was introduced by Varela, Thompson, and Rosch (1991) and has since developed into a broad research program. It overlaps with but is not identical to other "4E" positions: it implies embodiment and embeddedness, is friendly to extension, and adds the distinctive claim that cognition enacts rather than represents its object domain.

---

## The Four E's

A collective designation for four related positions in contemporary cognitive science, all of which challenge the classical "cognitivist" view of the mind as an internal information-processing system: *Embodied* (cognition is shaped by the body's morphology and dynamics), *Embedded* (cognition is shaped by ecological context and environmental structure), *Enacted* (the objects of cognition are brought forth through the organism's activity rather than discovered as pre-given), and *Extended* (cognitive processes can literally incorporate elements outside the organism's body). Not all defenders of one "E" accept all others; enactivism in its fullest form incorporates all four. The 4E framework is now well-established enough to be considered mainstream in many areas of cognitive science, though the enacted and extended theses remain philosophically contested.

---

## Perturbation vs. Instruction

A critical distinction in the autopoietic framework for characterizing the relationship between environment and organism. A *perturbation* is an environmental event that triggers a change in the organism's structure without specifying the nature of that change. What happens as a result of a perturbation is determined by the organism's own organizational dynamics, not by the perturbation itself. An *instruction*, by contrast, would be an environmental event that specifies the organism's response — that carries information about what the organism should do. Maturana and Varela deny that environments can instruct autopoietic systems; environments can only perturb them. This distinction challenges input-output models of cognition that treat stimuli as carrying instructions for behavioral responses, and it supports a view in which the organism is always the active determinant of its own behavior.

---

## Neurophenomenology

A research program proposed by Varela (1996) and developed in collaboration with Thompson and others, which attempts to integrate first-person phenomenological descriptions of experience with third-person neuroscientific measurements. Neurophenomenology proposes that phenomenological analysis — the careful first-person investigation of the structure of experience following Husserlian methods — can provide constraints on neuroscientific models, and vice versa. The program has been applied primarily to studies of consciousness, emotion, and perception in humans, but it has implications for basal cognition insofar as it motivates attention to the experiential dimension of biological self-maintenance — the question of what, if anything, it is like to be an autopoietic system at different levels of complexity.
