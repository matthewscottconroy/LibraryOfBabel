# Key Researchers: Chapter 27 — Autopoiesis and Enactivism

---

## Humberto R. Maturana (1928–2021)

Humberto Maturana was a Chilean biologist and neuroscientist who spent most of his career at the University of Chile in Santiago. Trained initially as a physician and then in biology at University College London and MIT, he made early contributions to visual neurophysiology — his 1960 paper on feature detection in the frog retina, co-authored with Lettvin, McCulloch, and Pitts, remains a landmark in neuroscience. But it was his philosophical radicalization of these findings that made him one of the most original thinkers in twentieth-century biology.

Maturana's key insight was that the nervous system does not operate as a device that maps the environment onto internal representations. Studying color perception in pigeons, he found that neural activity was determined by the organization of the nervous system, not by the physical properties of light stimuli. He concluded that all cognition is determined by the structure of the cognizing system, not by any independent reality. From this, he developed first the concept of operational closure, then autopoiesis (with Varela), and finally a comprehensive epistemology that he called "biology of cognition."

In his later years, Maturana took his constructivist epistemology in increasingly radical directions, arguing that there is no observer-independent reality and that all description is constituted by the observer's language. These views remain controversial. His foundational contributions to autopoiesis and the biology-cognition connection, however, are recognized across multiple disciplines.

## Francisco J. Varela (1946–2001)

Francisco Varela was a Chilean biologist, neuroscientist, and philosopher — perhaps the most genuinely interdisciplinary scientist of his generation. Originally Maturana's student at the University of Chile, he developed the formal concept of autopoiesis with Maturana in the early 1970s before pursuing an independent research program that ranged from immunology to neurophenomenology to contemplative science.

Varela's collaborations were unusually diverse: he worked with mathematicians on formal models of biological autonomy, with cognitive scientists on enactivism (culminating in *The Embodied Mind* with Thompson and Rosch), and with Buddhist teachers and scholars in the Mind and Life Institute, which he co-founded with the Dalai Lama. His scientific work on the immune system as a second cognitive "self" — a distributed system that constructs its own identity through self/non-self discrimination — anticipated many themes in current theoretical immunology.

His last major research project involved neurophenomenology: the attempt to link first-person phenomenological descriptions of experience with third-person neuroscientific measurements, particularly of neural oscillations during conscious states. He developed this program despite serious illness — he received a liver transplant in 1997 and died of liver failure in 2001. His intellectual trajectory was one of continuous expansion and integration, and his early death was a significant loss to cognitive science.

## Evan Thompson (1962– )

Evan Thompson is a Canadian philosopher of mind who has been one of the most important developers and defenders of enactivism since the publication of *The Embodied Mind*, which he co-authored with Varela and Rosch. His subsequent work has extended the framework in two major directions: toward a comprehensive biological phenomenology in *Mind in Life* (2007), which remains the most rigorous philosophical development of enactivism available, and toward a critical engagement with contemplative traditions, particularly in *Waking, Dreaming, Being* (2015), which examines questions of self, consciousness, and reality through both neuroscientific and Buddhist philosophical lenses.

Thompson's philosophical training in phenomenology (particularly Husserl and Merleau-Ponty) and his close collaboration with Varela give him an unusual dual perspective: he can engage rigorously with the philosophical literature on consciousness and intentionality while remaining committed to grounding theory in biological reality. His sustained engagement with critics of enactivism — including representationalist, mechanist, and eliminativist positions — has sharpened the framework considerably.

More recently, Thompson has become a thoughtful critic of some positions associated with the "extended mind" tradition, arguing that the boundaries of cognitive processes cannot simply be erased without theoretical loss. His current work explores consciousness, self, and the minimal conditions for subjective experience.

## Ezequiel Di Paolo (1972– )

Ezequiel Di Paolo is an Argentine computational neuroscientist and philosopher currently based in Spain who has been among the most productive developers of enactivism in the twenty-first century. His theoretical contributions have extended the framework in several directions: the concept of "adaptivity" (the capacity to regulate the conditions of one's own viability, which he argues must be added to autopoiesis to get full-blooded agency), the theory of "participatory sense-making" (which extends sense-making to social interactions), and detailed treatments of the relationship between enactivism and dynamical systems theory.

Di Paolo's work is notable for its precision: he is willing to engage with the mathematical details of dynamical systems and agent-based modeling in ways that most philosophical proponents of enactivism have avoided. His computational work on minimal agent models has helped ground enactivist intuitions in explicit formal theory. His book *Linguistic Bodies* (with Rohde and De Jaegher, 2018) represents the most sustained attempt to extend enactivism to language and cultural cognition.

## Eleanor Rosch (1938– )

Eleanor Rosch is an American psychologist at the University of California, Berkeley, best known before her collaboration with Varela and Thompson for her foundational work on human categorization. Her studies of "basic level" categories — demonstrating that people organize their conceptual worlds around a privileged level of abstraction (chairs, not furniture, not rocking chairs) — are among the most influential findings in cognitive psychology (Rosch, 1978).

Her collaboration with Varela on *The Embodied Mind* brought a different perspective than either biology or philosophy could supply: the perspective of an experimental psychologist who had become deeply skeptical of the information-processing paradigm that had dominated cognitive science since the 1960s. Rosch's encounter with Buddhist philosophy — particularly Madhyamaka analysis of the empty, relational nature of categories — reinforced her skepticism about fixed, mind-independent representational content. In *The Embodied Mind*, she contributed both empirical grounding in cognitive psychology and a philosophical perspective shaped by contemplative insight.

## Pier Luigi Luisi (1938– )

Pier Luigi Luisi is an Italian chemist who has spent decades working at the interface between chemistry, biology, and philosophy of life. His experimental work on liposomes, protocells, and the chemistry of self-assembly has provided some of the most concrete laboratory tests of the autopoietic framework. Luisi and collaborators have constructed chemical systems that exhibit some features of autopoiesis — self-producing membranes that can grow and divide — providing existence proofs that the concept is not merely theoretical but corresponds to realizable chemical processes.

Luisi's contributions are important for basal cognition because they connect the philosophical framework to questions about the origin of life and the minimum requirements for autopoietic organization. His work raises the question of whether chemical systems short of full biological cells can exhibit proto-cognitive features, and if so, what this implies about the relationship between chemistry and cognition. His book *The Emergence of Life* (2006) is an excellent introduction to these questions for graduate students with biological backgrounds.
