# Section 27.1: The Autopoietic Framework

## Self-Production and Organizational Closure

In the early 1970s, Humberto Maturana was wrestling with a problem in the philosophy of perception. Working from neurophysiology, he had become convinced that the nervous system does not process external information in the ordinary sense — that it does not passively receive signals from the world and convert them faithfully into internal representations. Instead, the nervous system seemed to be a closed, self-referential system that determined its own states. Sensory perturbations triggered changes in neural activity, but the pattern of those changes was determined by the system's own organization, not by the structure of the world outside. He called this *operational closure*: the defining characteristic of a system whose processes refer only to themselves (Maturana & Varela, 1980).

This insight, unsettling as it was for commonsense notions of perception, led Maturana and his younger collaborator Francisco Varela toward a much deeper question. If the nervous system is operationally closed, then so, presumably, is the cell that contains it. And if that is true, then perhaps operational closure is not a quirk of neural computation but a fundamental feature of life itself. What is a cell, after all, but a collection of molecular processes that produce and regulate one another? The cell membrane is produced by the lipid molecules inside it; the enzymes that catalyze cellular reactions are produced by the ribosomes that the cell itself assembles; the DNA replication machinery is produced by proteins that DNA encodes. The cell is a network of production processes that generates itself as a bounded, distinct entity.

Maturana and Varela named this property *autopoiesis*, from the Greek *autos* (self) and *poiein* (to make). An autopoietic system is one whose components continuously produce, maintain, and regenerate the network of processes that produce those same components. The system generates itself. It is not organized *by* something external — it is organized *as* a self-producing unity (Maturana & Varela, 1980, p. 78).

### Organization vs. Structure

The distinction between *organization* and *structure* is central to autopoiesis and deserves careful attention, because it is easily glossed over and frequently misunderstood.

The **organization** of a system is its abstract relational pattern — the set of relations among components that define it as a particular kind of system. The organization of an autopoietic system is the self-producing network described above: the relations among processes such that each process produces and sustains the others. Crucially, organization is defined independently of what the components actually are. Two autopoietic systems can share the same organization while being made of completely different materials.

The **structure** of a system is the actual, concrete physical implementation of that organization at a particular moment — the specific molecules, the particular membrane composition, the actual concentrations of metabolites. Structure changes continuously. A cell's structure at 9 AM differs from its structure at 9 PM: molecules have been degraded and replaced, the membrane has shifted in composition, proteins have been synthesized and broken down. And yet the cell remains the *same kind of thing* throughout — it maintains its organization even as its structure changes.

This distinction has an immediate implication: the identity of a living system is its organizational identity, not its material identity. A cell is not defined by what atoms it contains but by the relational pattern those atoms instantiate. This is why the classic ship-of-Theseus paradox feels less paradoxical when applied to cells: of course all the molecules in your body can be replaced over years without you ceasing to be you, because *you* are the organization, not the stuff.

### The Boundary of Self

Autopoiesis also specifies, with unusual precision, what counts as inside and outside a system. A crucial feature of autopoietic systems is that they produce their own boundaries. The cell membrane is not something externally imposed on the cell; it is a product of cellular metabolism. The boundary is part of what the system produces, and the system's continued existence requires maintaining that boundary (Varela, Maturana, & Uribe, 1974).

This is remarkable because it means that the distinction between "inside" and "outside" — between the system and its environment — is itself generated by the system's activity. The environment is not simply given; it is constituted as environment *relative to the system's self-defined boundary*. This point will become philosophically crucial when we turn to sense-making and enactivism.

It is worth being precise about what autopoiesis does not claim. It does not claim that cells are isolated from their environments — obviously they are not. Every cell exchanges matter and energy with its surroundings. What it claims is that this exchange is *organized* by the cell's own processes, not by the environment. The environment provides inputs, but the cell determines what those inputs mean for its ongoing self-production. Maturana and Varela called this *organizational closure combined with energetic and material openness*. The logic of interaction is determined from the inside; the material is acquired from the outside.

## Operational Closure and Informational Openness

The concept of operational closure requires careful unpacking because it sounds, at first, like a claim that living systems are isolated from their environments — which is plainly false. What Maturana and Varela mean is something more specific and more interesting.

A system is **operationally closed** if its processes always refer back to other processes within the same system. In an autopoietic system, every molecular process both depends on and contributes to other molecular processes in the same network. There is no process that simply receives an instruction from outside and executes it without any transformation by the system's own dynamics. Every perturbation from the environment is processed, interpreted, and responded to according to the system's own organizational logic.

This does not mean that the environment has no effect. A drastic change in the environment — say, a sudden shift in temperature or osmotic pressure — can destroy the system entirely. But short of destruction, the environment can only *trigger* changes in the system; it cannot *specify* those changes. What happens next is determined by the system's structure, not by the perturbation itself. The same environmental event can produce different responses in the same system at different times, because the system's structure has changed between encounters. And different systems can respond completely differently to the same perturbation.

This is the crucial theoretical move: the environment provides *perturbations* but not *instructions*. The system is informationally open in the sense that perturbations from the environment affect it. But it is operationally closed in the sense that the response to those perturbations is fully determined by the system's own organizational dynamics.

For a bacterium, this means that when a gradient of glucose creates a perturbation at the membrane receptors, what happens next is determined by the entire signaling network of the cell — its current state, its recent history, its metabolic context. The glucose gradient does not instruct the cell to tumble less and run more. Rather, the gradient triggers a change in receptor conformation, which alters the phosphorylation state of CheY, which changes the probability of flagellar motor reversal — all according to the cell's own signaling logic. The environment perturbs; the organism responds according to its own nature (Berg, 2004).

### What Triggers vs. What Determines

The distinction between triggering and determining is among the most important in the autopoietic framework, and it has implications that ripple through the whole of cognitive science. Mainstream cognitive science has often assumed that the environment *specifies* behavior — that the information "in" a stimulus is read out and processed, much as a computer reads data from an input device. Maturana's framework denies this. For an operationally closed system, there is no reading out, only triggering of self-determined responses.

This does not collapse into solipsism. Maturana is not saying that cells (or people) live in hermetically sealed subjective worlds disconnected from reality. He is saying that the connection between organism and world is always mediated by the organism's own organizational logic. There is a world, and perturbations from that world impinge on the system. But the meaning of those perturbations — what they are *for* the system — is determined by the system, not by the perturbations themselves.

This framing has a direct implication for how we should understand bacterial chemotaxis. We might be tempted to say that the bacterium "knows" that glucose is present because its receptors are reliably activated by glucose molecules. But this would be to attribute knowledge to the molecule-receptor interaction rather than to the cell. On the autopoietic view, the cell's response constitutes a kind of knowing — but it is knowing structured by the cell's own organizational closure, not a passive reading of what the world contains.

## Structural Coupling

If operationally closed systems are not instructed by their environments, how do they come to be well-adapted to those environments? How does a bacterium come to respond to glucose in ways that are actually useful, rather than in ways that are random or maladaptive? The answer lies in the concept of **structural coupling**.

Over time, an autopoietic system interacts repeatedly with its environment. Each interaction perturbs the system, triggering structural changes that may or may not preserve the system's autopoietic organization. If the structural change is incompatible with continued autopoiesis — if the cell bursts, or its metabolic network collapses — that lineage of interaction ends. If the structural change is compatible with continued autopoiesis, the system continues and may encounter further perturbations of similar kinds.

Over evolutionary and developmental timescales, this process produces systems whose structures are congruent with the regularities of their environments. The bacterium's chemotaxis network has been shaped by billions of years of selective pressure such that its operationally closed responses to chemical gradients reliably move it toward metabolically useful molecules. The structural coupling between bacterium and chemical environment is the product of that history.

Notice what this account does and does not say. It does not say that the environment has been "copied into" the cell, or that the cell now "represents" the environment internally. It says that the cell's structure has been shaped by a history of successful interactions with environments of a certain kind, producing a congruence between the cell's self-determined responses and the structure of typical environments. The fit is real, but it is produced by a history of coupling, not by any direct transfer of environmental information into the system (Maturana & Varela, 1987).

Structural coupling is not limited to evolutionary timescales. Development and learning are also forms of structural coupling: the individual organism's structure changes through its history of interactions such that its future responses are shaped by its past encounters. A paramecium that has repeatedly encountered a mechanical barrier changes its behavior in ways that reflect that history — not because it has stored a "representation" of the barrier, but because its structure has been modified by the coupling.

### Mutual Structural Coupling

A further important feature of structural coupling is that it is typically mutual. When an autopoietic system repeatedly interacts with another autopoietic system, both are changed by the history of interactions. Each shapes the structural changes in the other. Maturana and Varela called the network of interactions among autopoietic systems that produces mutually congruent structural changes a *consensual domain*, and they argued that language and culture are the richest examples of such domains in humans (Maturana & Varela, 1987).

For basal cognition, the relevance is immediate. Biofilms, mycelial networks, colonial organisms, and ecosystem-level interactions all involve the mutual structural coupling of many autopoietic systems. The "intelligence" of a biofilm — its coordinated responses, its division of labor, its adaptive dynamics — may be understood as a product of mutual structural coupling among individual bacterial cells, rather than as a property any single cell possesses (Watnick & Kolter, 2000).

## Cognition as Sense-Making

The most audacious claim in Maturana and Varela's framework is the identification of cognition with life. In *Autopoiesis and Cognition* (1980), they wrote: "Living systems are cognitive systems, and living as a process is a process of cognition" (p. 13). This is not a metaphor or a loose analogy. It is a theoretical claim that follows, they argued, from the nature of autopoiesis itself.

Here is the argument in brief. An autopoietic system maintains itself through selective interactions with its environment. "Selective" here means that not every perturbation is treated equally — only some perturbations are incorporated into the self-producing network, and the system continuously distinguishes between what pertains to its organization and what does not. This discriminative activity — the continuous making of distinctions that are relevant to the system's own continued existence — is, Maturana and Varela argued, a minimal form of cognition.

On this view, a bacterium swimming up a glucose gradient is genuinely cognizing. It is not merely reacting in the way a thermostat reacts to temperature. The bacterium's reaction is organized by a self-producing system that maintains its own identity by selectively engaging with its environment. The "meaning" of the glucose gradient — its relevance for the bacterium — is constituted by the bacterium's own autopoietic organization. The bacterium, through its activity, transforms a mere chemical gradient into something that is *for it* an opportunity. This is sense-making: the active constitution of significance.

### The Cognitive Domain

Maturana introduced the concept of a *cognitive domain* to describe the range of distinctions that an autopoietic system can make — the scope of its sense-making activity. An organism's cognitive domain is defined by its structural properties: what its sensory surfaces are sensitive to, what internal states it can occupy, what behavioral responses it can produce. The cognitive domain of a bacterium is very different from that of a mouse, but both have cognitive domains, and both are genuinely cognitive systems.

This framework suggests that cognition is not an all-or-nothing property but comes in degrees and kinds. More structurally complex systems have richer cognitive domains; simpler systems have more restricted ones. But the difference is one of complexity and scope, not of kind. There is no sharp line between "cognition" and "mere mechanism" because the most elementary mechanisms of autopoietic self-maintenance already constitute cognition in its minimal form.

This claim is philosophically bold, and it has attracted significant criticism, which we will consider in detail in the following section on enactivism. But it is worth pausing to appreciate what the claim achieves. For centuries, the question of where to draw the line between mind and mechanism has seemed intractable, because any line drawn seems arbitrary. The autopoietic framework proposes a non-arbitrary principled answer: the line is the line between autopoiesis and non-autopoiesis, between self-producing organization and mere mechanism. Everything on the autopoietic side is cognitive; everything else is not.

Whether or not this answer is ultimately convincing, it is a serious theoretical proposal that takes the challenge of minimal cognition seriously. It does not trivialize cognition by extending it everywhere, nor does it arbitrarily restrict it to neural systems.
