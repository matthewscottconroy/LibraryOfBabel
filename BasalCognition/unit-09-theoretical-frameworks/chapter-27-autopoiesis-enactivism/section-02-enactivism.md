# Section 27.2: Enactivism

## From Autopoiesis to a Research Program

By the late 1980s, Francisco Varela had become one of the most creative figures at the intersection of neuroscience, philosophy, and cognitive science. Working with the philosopher Evan Thompson and the cognitive scientist Eleanor Rosch, he undertook a systematic project: to transform the autopoietic framework into a full research program that could engage directly with cognitive science, phenomenology, and the study of experience. The result was *The Embodied Mind* (Varela, Thompson, & Rosch, 1991), one of the most influential books in the philosophy of mind of the last half-century.

The central thesis of *The Embodied Mind* is deceptively simple: cognition is not the processing of a pre-given world by an information-processing brain. Rather, cognition is the enactment of a world — the bringing forth of significance through the ongoing, embodied activity of an organism embedded in its environment. The mind is not inside the skull. It is in the dynamic coupling between organism and world.

This view received a name: *enactivism*. And over the following decades, Thompson in particular elaborated it extensively, most fully in *Mind in Life* (2007), which attempted to bridge the gap between biology, phenomenology, and cognitive science through a careful engagement with the philosophy of Edmund Husserl and Hans Jonas.

## The Four E's

Contemporary philosophy of mind distinguishes a cluster of related views that challenge the classical "cognitivist" picture of the mind as an internal information-processing system. These views are often grouped under the label of the "4E" framework: embodied, embedded, enacted, and extended cognition. While not all defenders of any one "E" accept the others, enactivism in its most developed form incorporates all four.

### Embodied Cognition

The claim that cognition is **embodied** is the most widely accepted of the four. It holds that cognitive processes are not independent of the body that implements them, but are shaped in fundamental ways by the body's morphology, dynamics, and sensorimotor capacities.

The evidence for embodiment in human cognition is now extensive. Studies in cognitive linguistics have demonstrated that abstract conceptual structures are organized around image schemas derived from bodily experience — concepts like "up/down," "inside/outside," "force," and "path" structure our thinking about causation, time, social relations, and mathematics in ways that trace to our bodily engagement with the physical world (Lakoff & Johnson, 1999). Experiments in sensorimotor contingency demonstrate that what visual perception is — what it is for an organism to see a surface, a shape, a color — depends constitutively on the sensorimotor skills available to the organism (O'Regan & Noë, 2001).

For unicellular organisms, embodiment is perhaps trivially true and yet profoundly significant. The bacterium's "cognition" — its discriminative, self-maintaining engagement with its chemical environment — is constituted entirely by molecular processes occurring in and on a body. The spatial organization of that body matters: the clustering of receptors at the cell poles in *E. coli*, the arrangement of signaling components across the membrane, the physical dynamics of flagellar rotation. These morphological facts are not contingent implementational details that could in principle be replaced by any other substrate. They are partly constitutive of what kind of cognition this cell can perform.

### Embedded Cognition

The claim that cognition is **embedded** holds that cognitive processes cannot be understood in isolation from the organism's ecological context. Organisms are adapted to and dependent upon particular kinds of environments, and their cognitive processes are organized around the affordances — the action possibilities — that those environments offer.

This is the least philosophically contentious of the four E's and has substantial support from evolutionary biology and behavioral ecology. An organism's sensory and motor systems are not general-purpose input/output devices but are tuned to the specific action-relevant features of the environments in which they evolved. The Gibsonian concept of *affordance* — the relational property that a feature of the environment presents as an opportunity for action to a particular kind of organism — captures part of what embeddedness means (Gibson, 1979).

For basal cognition researchers, embeddedness is a crucial corrective to a tempting error: the error of studying an organism's cognitive capacities in isolation from the environments in which those capacities evolved and function. A bacterium removed from chemical gradients and placed in a uniform medium is a bacterium whose cognitive system is not functioning — just as a human visual system studied in the absence of textured surfaces and moving objects will miss much of what makes vision what it is.

### Enacted Cognition

The claim that cognition is **enacted** is the most distinctive and philosophically original contribution of enactivism. It holds that the objects of cognition — the meaningful features of an organism's world — are not pre-given properties of a mind-independent environment but are actively brought forth (enacted) through the organism's own activity.

This is a direct application of the autopoietic framework to perception and cognition. Recall that for Maturana and Varela, the environment of an autopoietic system is not simply given but is constituted relative to the system's own boundary-producing activity. Enactivism extends this: the *meaningful* features of an organism's environment — the things that matter to it, that constitute its experienced world — are not discovered but enacted.

Varela, Thompson, and Rosch introduced the term *Umwelt*, borrowed from the biologist Jakob von Uexküll, to describe the organism-specific world of significance that each species enacts (Varela et al., 1991). A tick's Umwelt — its enacted world of significance — consists of warmth, the odor of butyric acid, and mammalian skin. These are not all the physical features of the environment; they are the features that matter to a tick, the features that organize its sense-making activity. The tick does not "perceive" a meadow the way we do. It enacts a different world from the same physical environment.

For unicellular organisms, the enacted world is defined by the organism's metabolic needs and its receptor repertoire. A bacterium's Umwelt is a world of chemical gradients, osmotic pressures, and light intensities that are relevant to its continued existence. This is not a "representation" of the external world. It is a domain of meaning constituted by the bacterium's own autopoietic activity in a particular environment.

### Extended Cognition

The claim that cognition is **extended** — that cognitive processes can literally incorporate elements outside the organism's body — is the most controversial of the four. We will examine it in detail in Chapter 33 when we discuss the Clark-Chalmers extended mind hypothesis. For now, we note only that enactivism is broadly sympathetic to the extended cognition thesis, since it already denies that cognitive processes are bounded by the skull. If cognition is in the organism-environment coupling, then tools, artifacts, and social structures that become part of the relevant coupling may plausibly be part of the cognitive process.

## Sense-Making as the Minimal Unit of Cognition

The concept of **sense-making** is central to enactivism and deserves sustained attention. It was developed most explicitly by Thompson (2007) drawing on the work of Hans Jonas, and it provides the crucial link between autopoiesis and cognition.

Jonas argued that with the emergence of metabolism came the emergence of a new kind of being in the world: the being for whom things can go well or badly, for whom the environment is not neutral but is laden with significance (Jonas, 1966). A rock is indifferent to whether it dissolves or stays intact. A cell is not indifferent. The continuation of its autopoietic organization matters — not to an observer, but to the cell itself in a functional sense. The cell acts to maintain itself, and this acting-to-maintain constitutes a proto-concern, a primordial form of caring about continued existence.

Sense-making, in the technical enactivist sense, is the activity through which an organism constitutes its environment as a domain of significance relative to its own autopoietic organization. Three features define sense-making:

1. **Perspective**: The organism enacts a point of view. Things are significant or insignificant, better or worse, relevant or irrelevant *for this organism*, given its particular autopoietic organization. There is no sense-making without a perspective from which things make sense.

2. **Valence**: Significance is not neutral. For an autopoietic system, some encounters are nutritive and some are toxic; some states are more compatible with continued self-production than others. Sense-making is inherently evaluative: it constitutes a world in which some things are better than others for *this kind of being*.

3. **Agency**: Sense-making is active, not passive. The organism does not simply receive significance from the world; it enacts significance through its activity. The bacterium moving up the glucose gradient is not reading off a meaning that the gradient carries; it is, through its movement, constituting the gradient as food.

These three features — perspective, valence, and agency — are present in every autopoietic system. They constitute, enactivists argue, the minimal form of cognition that any living system possesses by virtue of being alive.

### Sense-Making in Unicellular Organisms

Consider in detail what sense-making means for *E. coli*. This bacterium lives in environments where chemical gradients fluctuate, where nutrients appear and disappear, where chemical repellents and attractants are intermingled. The bacterium's chemotaxis system allows it to navigate this landscape: it runs longer in favorable chemical gradients and tumbles more in unfavorable ones, producing a biased random walk that tends toward nutrient-rich and repellent-poor regions (Berg & Brown, 1972).

From the perspective of classical information processing, we might describe this as: the bacterium receives information about the chemical gradient and processes it to produce appropriate motor output. From the enactivist perspective, the description is quite different: the bacterium enacts a chemical landscape as a domain of significance structured around the gradient of its own metabolic needs. The gradient is not processed; it is engaged. The engagement is the cognition.

The difference between these descriptions is not merely verbal. The information-processing description encourages us to ask: where is the information? What format does it take? Where is it stored? The enactivist description encourages different questions: what is the perspective from which significance is constituted? How does the engagement shape the further structure of the bacterium's world? How does the organism's history of couplings shape its current sense-making?

These are not merely philosophical questions. They have methodological implications for how we study unicellular organisms. An enactivist approach to bacterial cognition would attend not just to the stimulus-response transfer function but to the dynamics of the whole coupling between bacterium and chemical environment — how the bacterium's movement creates gradients that it then encounters, how its metabolic state shapes the significance of particular chemicals, how the history of recent encounters shapes current behavior.

## Critiques and Extensions

Enactivism has attracted both enthusiastic support and vigorous criticism. The criticisms come from several directions, and engaging with them carefully is essential for using the framework productively.

### The Content Problem

One of the most influential criticisms is that enactivism cannot account for the *content* of mental states — the fact that thoughts are about things, that perceptions are of objects, that beliefs have truth conditions. Classical cognitive science appeals to internal representations to explain content: a mental state has the content it does because it represents some feature of the world. Enactivism denies that there are representations in the relevant sense, but critics argue that it thereby loses the capacity to explain what thoughts are about (Fodor & Pylyshyn, 1988).

Enactivist responses to this objection take several forms. Thompson (2007) argues that content emerges from the organism's history of enacted couplings with its environment — that the "aboutness" of a cognitive state is constituted by its role in the organism's ongoing sense-making activity, not by its correspondence to an external world. Others, like Hutto and Myin (2013), have developed more radical "enactivist" positions that deny content to all but the most sophisticated cognitive systems, arguing that basic forms of sense-making do not involve contentful states at all.

For basal cognition, this debate is somewhat beside the point: the question of whether bacterial responses have "content" in the full philosophical sense is arguably less important than the question of how they function. What matters for the study of unicellular cognition is the functional organization of the organism's engagement with its environment, and this is something enactivism illuminates well regardless of how the philosophical debate about content is resolved.

### The Hard Problem

A second, deeper criticism concerns consciousness and phenomenal experience. Enactivism is committed — especially in Varela and Thompson's versions — to explaining subjective experience in terms of organism-environment dynamics rather than purely neural mechanisms. But many critics argue that even if we give a complete functional-dynamic account of an organism's sense-making activity, we will have said nothing about why there is "something it is like" to be that organism (Chalmers, 1996).

This is the hard problem of consciousness, and it is one that no current scientific theory fully addresses. Enactivism's distinctive contribution is to insist that any account of consciousness that ignores the organism's embodied engagement with the world is necessarily incomplete. Whether enactivism also provides the missing piece — whether organism-environment dynamics can ultimately explain phenomenal experience — remains an open question.

For the study of basal cognition specifically, this debate matters because it affects what we are willing to attribute to unicellular organisms. If we accept that sense-making, valence, and perspective are sufficient for there to be "something it is like" to be the organism, then bacteria and paramecia have primitive forms of experience. If we insist that phenomenal consciousness requires neural integration of some kind, then the sense-making of unicellular organisms might be genuinely cognitive without being genuinely experiential in the phenomenal sense. Enactivism tends toward the first view; critics tend toward the second; and the debate remains unresolved.

### Overclaiming and Underclaiming

A practical criticism of enactivism is that it is sometimes deployed in ways that claim too much and sometimes in ways that claim too little. The claim that bacteria are "cognitive" can be used to suggest, misleadingly, that bacteria have rich inner lives comparable to animal consciousness. And the claim that sense-making is "merely" a functional property can be used to deflate the concept so far that it loses its philosophical bite.

The most productive use of the enactivist framework steers between these errors. It takes seriously that sense-making is a genuine theoretical concept with clear conditions of application, and it is willing to attribute sense-making to unicellular organisms on the basis of those conditions — but it remains agnostic about stronger claims concerning the phenomenal character of that sense-making. This is the position Thompson himself has come to endorse in more recent work: a clear affirmation of the cognitive significance of life, combined with genuine uncertainty about the distribution of phenomenal experience.

### Mechanistic Criticisms

Some biologists and philosophers of biology are skeptical of the entire autopoietic framework on mechanistic grounds. They argue that describing cells as "self-producing" imports a kind of teleology that has no place in a properly mechanistic biology: cells don't produce themselves *in order to* maintain their existence; they are the products of blind evolutionary processes that happened to produce stable, self-maintaining systems (Machery, 2012).

The enactivist response, developed by Thompson (2007) at length, appeals to the concept of *teleodynamics* developed by Terrence Deacon and to the philosophical work of Hans Jonas: there is a form of normativity intrinsic to autopoietic systems that is not magical teleology but a natural consequence of the self-producing, self-maintaining organization. The cell doesn't have purposes in the way humans consciously have purposes, but it does have what Jonas called *intrinsic teleology* — a built-in orientation toward its own continued existence that constrains its dynamics in ways that mere chemical reactions are not constrained.

This debate about teleology in biology is one of the deepest in philosophy of biology, and we cannot resolve it here. What we can say is that the autopoietic/enactivist framework is philosophically serious enough to be worth engaging on its own terms, even if one ultimately disagrees with its teleological commitments.

## A Framework for Basal Cognition Research

Despite its philosophical controversies, the enactivist framework offers several concrete contributions to basal cognition research.

First, it provides a **principled criterion for cognition** that does not depend on having a nervous system: the possession of autopoietic organization sufficient for sense-making. This criterion is non-arbitrary and theoretically grounded, and it allows us to ask precise questions about where in the tree of life sense-making begins and what forms it takes.

Second, it provides a **vocabulary for describing organism-environment relationships** that goes beyond the stimulus-response model without requiring us to invoke mysterious internal representations. The concepts of structural coupling, perturbation, and operational closure allow us to characterize the dynamics of unicellular behavior with more theoretical richness than is possible in a purely input-output framework.

Third, it **connects biological and cognitive scientific research** in a productive way. The study of bacterial metabolism, membrane dynamics, and chemotaxis is also, on the enactivist account, the study of bacterial cognition. There is no gap between the biology and the cognition, because cognition just is the life process viewed from the inside.

Fourth — and perhaps most importantly for graduate students entering this field — it offers a **research attitude**: an insistence on studying organisms in their ecological context, attending to the whole dynamics of organism-environment coupling rather than isolating organism variables. This attitude, regardless of one's philosophical commitments, is methodologically sound and has produced important discoveries in behavioral ecology, sensorimotor science, and developmental biology.
