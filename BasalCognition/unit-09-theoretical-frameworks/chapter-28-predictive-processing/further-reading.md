# Further Reading: Chapter 28 — Predictive Processing and the Free Energy Principle

---

**Clark, A. (2016). *Surfing Uncertainty: Prediction, Action, and the Embodied Mind*. Oxford University Press.**
The most readable and philosophically nuanced book-length treatment of the predictive processing framework. Clark is an unusually clear writer on difficult material, and he is willing to take the framework seriously while acknowledging its limitations. The chapters on action, emotion, and the self are particularly valuable. A good starting point for graduate students approaching the literature for the first time.

**Friston, K. (2010). The free-energy principle: A unified brain theory? *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, 11(2), 127–138.**
The most widely cited and accessible of Friston's theoretical papers. This review article presents the FEP as applied to brain function and provides the clearest statement of the relationship between free energy minimization, Bayesian inference, and prediction error minimization. Essential reading for understanding what the framework claims before evaluating whether those claims extend to all life.

**Friston, K. (2013). Life as we know it. *Journal of the Royal Society Interface*, 10(86), 20130475.**
The paper in which Friston explicitly extends the free energy principle beyond the brain to all living systems. He uses the concept of Markov blankets to argue that any self-organizing system with a statistical boundary between internal and external states is, formally, implementing variational Bayesian inference. Challenging mathematically but essential for evaluating the universal FEP claim.

**Hohwy, J. (2013). *The Predictive Mind*. Oxford University Press.**
A philosophical companion to Clark's book, more technically rigorous and more committed to the view that predictive processing vindicates a form of "internalism" — the view that the mind is fundamentally an internal model-building process. Hohwy's disagreements with Clark over the implications of the framework illuminate important philosophical fault lines that are often glossed over. A thorough engagement with this book and Clark's will give you a sophisticated grasp of the framework's philosophical commitments.

**Bruineberg, J., Kiverstein, J., & Rietveld, E. (2018). The anticipating brain is not a scientist: The free-energy principle from an ecological-enactive perspective. *Synthese*, 195(6), 2417–2444.**
A sophisticated critique of the FEP from an enactivist perspective, arguing that Friston's framework is committed to a form of internalism that conflicts with the ecological and embodied character of cognition. The paper argues that the notion of an internal "model" in the FEP is philosophically problematic and that a better account of self-organizing biological systems would emphasize organism-environment coupling rather than internal model representation.

**Colombo, M., & Series, P. (2012). Bayes in the brain — on Bayesian modelling in neuroscience. *British Journal for the Philosophy of Science*, 63(3), 697–723.**
An important methodological critique of Bayesian approaches to neuroscience, asking whether Bayesian models are genuinely explanatory or merely descriptive. The paper argues that the ubiquity of Bayesian models in neuroscience reflects their flexibility rather than any specific empirical claim about neural computation, and that this flexibility raises concerns about the scientific status of the framework. Valuable for developing critical perspective on the FEP.

**Friston, K., & Stephan, K.E. (2007). Free energy and the brain. *Synthese*, 159(3), 417–458.**
A more technically detailed presentation of the FEP applied to neuroscience, connecting the framework to empirical measurements of brain activity, including effective connectivity and neuroimaging data. Useful for graduate students who want to understand how the abstract mathematical framework connects to concrete experimental predictions.

**Nave, K., Deane, G., Miller, M., & Clark, A. (2020). Wilding the predictive brain. *Wiley Interdisciplinary Reviews: Cognitive Science*, 11(6), e1542.**
An examination of the relationship between predictive processing and the "4E" cognitive science tradition. The paper argues that predictive processing and embodied/enactive approaches are more compatible than is often assumed, and outlines a research program for integrating them. Useful for students who have engaged with both Chapter 27 (enactivism) and Chapter 28.

**Seth, A.K., & Bayne, T. (2022). Theories of consciousness. *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, 23(7), 439–452.**
While focused on consciousness in the full sense, this review article provides an excellent comparative evaluation of several theories — including predictive processing — against each other and against empirical evidence. It helps situate the FEP within the broader landscape of theories of mind and provides a model for how to evaluate competing frameworks rigorously.

**Hesp, C., Smith, R., Parr, T., Allen, M., Friston, K.J., & Ramstead, M.J.D. (2021). Deeply felt affect: The emergence of valence in deep active inference. *Neural Computation*, 33(2), 398–446.**
An attempt to extend the active inference framework to account for valence and affect — the positive/negative character of experience. Relevant for basal cognition because it connects the formal FEP machinery to questions about the evolutionary origin of good/bad, which is central to understanding sense-making in minimal organisms.
