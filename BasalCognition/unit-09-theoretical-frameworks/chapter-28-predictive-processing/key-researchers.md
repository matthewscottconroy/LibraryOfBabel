# Key Researchers: Chapter 28 — Predictive Processing and the Free Energy Principle

---

## Karl J. Friston (1959– )

Karl Friston is a British neuroscientist at University College London who is almost certainly the most-cited neuroscientist in the world by some metrics — a consequence of both the breadth of his contributions and the intellectual reach of his theoretical frameworks. He has made foundational contributions to neuroimaging methodology (statistical parametric mapping, voxel-based morphometry), to the theory of brain connectivity (dynamic causal modeling), and to theoretical neuroscience (the free energy principle and active inference framework).

Friston's career illustrates an unusual trajectory: from highly specific technical contributions to neuroimaging statistics toward increasingly grand theoretical claims about the nature of mind and life. The free energy principle in its current form attempts nothing less than a unified account of self-organizing biological systems from the cell to the organism to the social collective. Whether this ambition succeeds or overreaches is one of the central controversies in current theoretical neuroscience and philosophy of mind.

Friston is a demanding writer — his papers are mathematically sophisticated and often presuppose considerable background in Bayesian statistics and statistical physics. But he is also a generous and creative thinker who regularly engages with critics, collaborates broadly, and revises his positions in response to objections. Graduate students who want to engage seriously with the FEP will find the work demanding but rewarding.

---

## Andy Clark (1957– )

Andy Clark is a Scottish philosopher at the University of Edinburgh whose work has shaped the philosophy of cognitive science for three decades. He is the primary philosophical advocate and interpreter of the predictive processing framework, and his book *Surfing Uncertainty* (2016) remains the most accessible and philosophically careful introduction to the framework available.

Clark is notable for his ability to synthesize ideas from multiple disciplines — neuroscience, cognitive science, philosophy of mind, robotics — into coherent theoretical frameworks, and for his willingness to follow arguments where they lead even when the conclusions are surprising. He is also the originator (with David Chalmers) of the extended mind thesis (Chapter 33), and his commitment to embodied, embedded, and extended approaches to cognition makes him an important bridge between the predictive processing tradition and the enactivist tradition.

His earlier book *Being There: Putting Brain, Body, and World Together Again* (1997) remains an excellent introduction to the broader "4E" framework and situates predictive processing within a longer history of embodied cognitive science.

## Jakob Hohwy (1969– )

Jakob Hohwy is a Danish philosopher at Monash University who has been the most rigorous philosophical defender of a "strong" interpretation of predictive processing — one committed to the view that the brain is fundamentally a model-building device implementing hierarchical Bayesian inference. His book *The Predictive Mind* (2013) is essential reading for anyone who wants to understand the philosophical commitments of the framework.

Hohwy's position is interesting precisely because it is explicitly "internalist": he argues that the predictive brain is fundamentally about constructing models of a world it can never directly access. This makes him a philosophically interesting foil for Clark, who draws more embodied and extended conclusions from the same basic framework. The disagreement between Hohwy and Clark about the implications of predictive processing illuminates deep fault lines in the philosophy of perception and action.

## Hermann von Helmholtz (1821–1894)

Hermann von Helmholtz was perhaps the most versatile scientist of the nineteenth century, making fundamental contributions to thermodynamics, electrodynamics, optics, and acoustics. For the history of cognitive science, his most important contribution was the concept of "unconscious inference" in perception: the idea that what we perceive is a construction — the brain's best hypothesis about the external causes of sensory signals — rather than a direct readout of the world.

This idea, embedded in his massive *Treatise on Physiological Optics* (1867), anticipated by more than a century the computational formalization of predictive processing. Helmholtz's insight was that the inverse problem of perception — inferring the three-dimensional world from two-dimensional retinal images — has no unique solution, and the brain must use prior knowledge to select among the infinitely many solutions consistent with the data. His empiricism about the origins of those priors (they are learned from experience, not innate) remains philosophically relevant.

## Rajesh Rao and Dana Ballard

Rajesh Rao (now at the University of Washington) and Dana Ballard (University of Texas at Austin) published the 1999 paper in *Nature Neuroscience* that gave predictive processing its first clear neural implementation. Their "predictive coding" model of the visual cortex proposed that feedback connections from higher cortical areas carry predictions, while feedforward connections carry prediction errors — a simple but powerful re-interpretation of the anatomy of visual cortex that opened an entire research program.

Their paper is among the most influential in theoretical neuroscience and catalyzed a decade of empirical work testing the predictive coding architecture. Rao has since developed computational models of predictive coding in motor and reward systems; Ballard has extended the framework to robotics and motor control. Together they established the empirical research tradition on which the broader FEP program rests.

## Anil Seth (1972– )

Anil Seth is a British neuroscientist at the University of Sussex who has become one of the most prominent public communicators of predictive processing and one of its most productive empirical researchers. His work applies the framework to consciousness, focusing particularly on "interoceptive inference" — the idea that our sense of being a self, of having a body, and of emotional experience are all forms of predictive processing applied to internal bodily signals.

Seth's concept of consciousness as a "controlled hallucination" — the brain's best prediction of the causes of its sensory input, both external and internal — has been widely discussed and has helped bring predictive processing to a broad public audience. His empirical work on disorders of consciousness and on the neural basis of body ownership and emotional feeling connects the theoretical framework to concrete experimental findings. His book *Being You: A New Science of Consciousness* (2021) provides an engaging and scientifically grounded account of predictive processing accounts of consciousness.
