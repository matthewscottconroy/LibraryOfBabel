# Chapter 29: Integrated Information Theory

## The Quantity of Mind

What if consciousness could be measured? Not described, interpreted, or inferred — but actually quantified, given a number, compared across systems? This is the bold proposal at the heart of Giulio Tononi's Integrated Information Theory, which assigns to every physical system a value called phi (Φ) — a measure of the system's integrated information — and identifies that value with the system's degree of consciousness.

The appeal of this proposal is hard to overstate. Philosophy of mind has been stuck for decades on what David Chalmers called the "hard problem" of consciousness: even if we could give a complete account of all the functional and physical processes in a brain, we would not have explained *why* there is "something it is like" to be that brain. Most theories of consciousness sidestep this problem by focusing on the easy problems — the functional correlates of consciousness, the neural mechanisms of attention, the computational processes that distinguish waking from sleep — while acknowledging that the hard problem remains. IIT claims to confront the hard problem directly: it does not try to explain consciousness in terms of something else, but starts from consciousness as a fundamental feature of the world and asks what physical property it corresponds to.

For students of basal cognition, IIT is fascinating and disturbing in equal measure. Fascinating because it implies that consciousness — or at least some nonzero degree of integrated information — may be present wherever there is complex, interconnected physical processing: in neurons, yes, but also in cells, in biofilms, in plants. Disturbing because the same logic implies that a simple feedforward network of transistors — or, more provocatively, a grid of logic gates arranged in a certain way — might have higher Φ than many biological systems, which strikes most people as deeply wrong. The theory is philosophically rigorous, empirically contested, and scientifically provocative. Understanding it requires confronting some of the hardest questions in the philosophy of mind.

## Central Question

**If consciousness is identical to integrated information, what follows for the distribution of experience in the biological world?** Is the theory's pan-psychist implication a reductio ad absurdum, a troubling but potentially true consequence, or something the theory can avoid? And critically: is IIT a scientific theory at all — does it generate testable predictions, or is it a philosophical position dressed in mathematical clothing?

## Chapter Roadmap

**Section 29.1 — IIT: Theory and Claims** presents the theory in its own terms: the five axioms (existence, composition, information, integration, exclusion), the five postulates that connect these to physical systems, the concept of phi, and the bold identification of consciousness with integrated information. It explains what IIT implies about non-neural systems — that they too have consciousness in proportion to their integrated information — and examines the "exclusion" postulate, which is IIT's most distinctive and most criticized feature.

**Section 29.2 — IIT and Basal Cognition** examines what IIT specifically predicts for the organisms at the center of this book: bacteria, plants, fungi, slime molds. It considers IIT's relationship to computationalism — its startling claim that certain arrangements of logic gates might be conscious while biological neurons might not be — and presents the major scientific and philosophical criticisms in detail, including Aaronson's "expander graph" objection, critiques of the axioms, and the empirical challenge of measuring Φ in real biological systems.
