# Further Reading: Chapter 29 — Integrated Information Theory

---

**Tononi, G. (2004). An information integration theory of consciousness. *BMC Neuroscience*, 5(1), 42.**
The founding paper. Shorter and more accessible than later versions, this paper introduces the basic framework and motivates the project of quantifying consciousness. The connection between the phenomenological axioms and the mathematical measure is laid out clearly enough that the logical structure of the argument is visible, making it easier to identify where one might disagree.

**Oizumi, M., Albantakis, L., & Tononi, G. (2014). From the phenomenology to the mechanisms of consciousness: Integrated information theory 3.0. *PLOS Computational Biology*, 10(5), e1003588.**
The most fully developed version of the theory. IIT 3.0 addresses many criticisms of earlier versions, particularly regarding the grain problem and the logic gate objection. Technically demanding but essential for anyone who wants to engage with the current state of the theory rather than a strawman version. The mathematical appendices provide rigorous definitions of all quantities.

**Tononi, G., Boly, M., Massimini, M., & Koch, C. (2016). Integrated information theory: From consciousness to its physical substrate. *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, 17(7), 450–461.**
An accessible review co-authored with Christof Koch, who is IIT's most prominent neuroscientific advocate. The paper reviews the theory's predictions for various neurological states (sleep, anesthesia, disorders of consciousness) and discusses empirical tests. The review provides a good balance between theoretical exposition and empirical engagement.

**Koch, C. (2019). *The Feeling of Life Itself: Why Consciousness Is Widespread but Can't Be Computed*. MIT Press.**
An accessible and enthusiastic defense of IIT by Christof Koch, written for a broad scientific audience. Koch draws out the implications of IIT for artificial intelligence (feedforward networks are not conscious), for animals (many more species than we typically acknowledge have significant consciousness), and for medicine (vegetative state patients might be conscious). The book is valuable for seeing how a committed advocate interprets the theory's implications, even where his arguments remain to be tested.

**Chalmers, D.J. (1996). *The Conscious Mind: In Search of a Fundamental Theory*. Oxford University Press.**
Not specifically about IIT, but essential background for understanding what IIT is trying to achieve. Chalmers' formulation of the hard problem of consciousness and his taxonomy of theories (type-B physicalism, panpsychism, property dualism) provide the philosophical framework within which IIT operates. Understanding IIT requires understanding why the hard problem is hard, which this book explains better than almost any other.

**Cerullo, M.A. (2015). The problem with phi: A critique of integrated information theory. *PLOS Computational Biology*, 11(9), e1004286.**
A careful scientific critique focusing on the technical problems with computing Φ, including the grain problem, the computational intractability, and the sensitivity of Φ values to implementation choices. Required reading for any student who wants to evaluate IIT's scientific status rather than simply accept or reject it on intuitive grounds.

**Bayne, T. (2010). *The Unity of Consciousness*. Oxford University Press.**
An empirically grounded philosophical treatment of consciousness unity that is directly relevant to IIT's integration axiom. Bayne examines cases of apparent disunity — split-brain patients, dissociative disorders, normal experiences of multitasking — and argues that the unity of consciousness is more complex and more fragile than simple integration theories suggest. A valuable corrective to IIT's strong integration axiom.

**Massimini, M., & Tononi, G. (2018). *Sizing Up Consciousness: Towards an Objective Measure of the Complexity of Experience*. Oxford University Press.**
A neuroscientific book focused on the empirical tools IIT has generated for measuring consciousness-related activity in the brain — specifically the Perturbational Complexity Index (PCI), which provides an empirical approximation of integrated information using transcranial magnetic stimulation and EEG. The book discusses applications to disorders of consciousness and anesthesia. Valuable for students interested in how the theoretical framework connects to clinical neuroscience.

**Tononi, G., & Koch, C. (2015). Consciousness: Here, there, and everywhere? *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B*, 370(1668), 20140167.**
A discussion of the panpsychist implications of IIT, addressing the combination problem and the question of how micro-experiences could combine into macro-experiences. The paper is candid about the challenges and represents one of the more philosophically self-aware engagements with the theory's more exotic implications.

**Seth, A.K., & Bayne, T. (2022). Theories of consciousness. *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, 23(7), 439–452.**
An even-handed comparative review of major theories of consciousness including IIT, global workspace theory, higher-order theories, and predictive processing accounts. The paper evaluates each theory against a set of criteria and provides a useful map of the theoretical landscape. Essential for students who want to understand how IIT stands in relation to its competitors.

**Tegmark, M. (2016). Improved measures of integrated information. *PLOS Computational Biology*, 12(11), e1005123.**
A contribution from a physicist rather than a neuroscientist or philosopher, proposing technical improvements to the Φ measure that address some of the mathematical problems with earlier versions. Tegmark's engagement with IIT from a physics perspective raises interesting questions about whether integrated information is a concept that belongs to physics, and whether the framework's mathematical structure is as principled as Tononi claims.
