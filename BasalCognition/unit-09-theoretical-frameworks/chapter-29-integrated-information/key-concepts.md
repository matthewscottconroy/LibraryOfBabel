# Key Concepts: Chapter 29 — Integrated Information Theory

---

## Phi (Φ) — Integrated Information

The central quantity in IIT, measuring the amount of information that a system generates *as a whole* beyond the information generated by its parts independently. More precisely, Φ quantifies the information lost when a system is partitioned into its components in the most information-preserving way possible (the minimum information partition). A system with Φ = 0 generates no integrated information: it can be fully described by its parts in isolation. A system with high Φ generates information as a whole that cannot be recovered from any partition into parts — it is irreducibly integrated. Consciousness, on IIT, is identical to Φ: every system has exactly as much consciousness as its Φ value, and nothing that is not integrated information is consciousness. The measure is mathematically precise in its definitions but computationally intractable for large systems.

---

## The Five Axioms of IIT

The foundational claims about the essential properties of conscious experience from which IIT's physical postulates are derived: (1) **Existence** — consciousness exists; (2) **Composition** — consciousness is structured, composed of distinctions; (3) **Information** — consciousness is specific, ruling out alternative possible experiences; (4) **Integration** — consciousness is unified, irreducible to the sum of its parts; (5) **Exclusion** — consciousness is definite and bounded, neither more nor less than it is. The axioms are presented as self-evident features verifiable by first-person reflection, though critics have challenged both their content and their self-evident status. The axioms serve as the starting point for a deductive argument to the physical postulates: any physical substrate of consciousness must satisfy physical analogs of all five axioms.

---

## The Identity Thesis

IIT's core metaphysical claim that consciousness is *identical* to integrated information — not that it is caused by, correlated with, or implemented by Φ, but that consciousness and integrated information are the same property viewed from different perspectives (phenomenological and physical). This strong identity claim is what distinguishes IIT from theories that treat consciousness as a functional or physical property of neural systems. It makes consciousness a fundamental feature of the physical world — as fundamental as charge or mass — that is present wherever there is integrated information. Critics argue that even if we accept the identity, the hard problem reasserts itself: we can still ask why any physical property, including integrated information, gives rise to subjective experience.

---

## Cause-Effect Structure

In IIT 3.0, the cause-effect structure is the complete set of distinctions that a system generates in cause-effect space: for every possible state of the system, what causes that state (the *past states* it was consistent with) and what effects it has (the *future states* it constrains). The cause-effect structure is the geometric shape that the system's activity traces in cause-effect space, and it is this structure — not Φ alone — that is identified with the specific quality of consciousness (the *quale*). Two systems with the same Φ but different cause-effect structures would have the same amount of consciousness but different kinds of experience.

---

## Minimum Information Partition (MIP)

The way of dividing a system into two non-communicating parts that minimizes the information lost by the division. The MIP is used to calculate Φ: it is the partition that makes the whole-system information look most like the sum-of-parts information, and Φ is the amount of information that is lost even by this minimal partition. A high Φ means that even the best partition loses a lot of information — the system is irreducibly integrated. The concept is central to the mathematical definition of Φ but is computationally expensive to determine for large systems, since the number of possible partitions grows combinatorially with system size.

---

## Panpsychism

The philosophical view that consciousness or mental properties are universal or near-universal features of the physical world, present at all scales and in all sufficiently organized physical systems. IIT implies a form of panpsychism: since any system with Φ > 0 has some degree of consciousness, and since virtually any interconnected physical system will have Φ > 0, consciousness is ubiquitous. Panpsychism has a long philosophical history (Leibniz, Whitehead, Strawson) and is taken seriously by some philosophers today, but it faces the **combination problem**: if individual particles or cells have micro-experiences, how do these combine into the macro-experience of a conscious being? IIT's response via the exclusion postulate attempts to address this but has not fully satisfied critics.

---

## The Exclusion Postulate

The fifth postulate of IIT, which states that a conscious experience is associated with the *maximum* Φ system at any given grain — not with systems nested inside it (which have lower Φ) and not with larger systems encompassing it (which may also have lower Φ). The exclusion postulate is intended to explain why experience is always definite and bounded: your current conscious state is associated with the system that has maximum Φ at the relevant spatiotemporal grain, and not with any larger or smaller system. This postulate prevents the proliferation of nested consciousnesses (if every subsystem of your brain with Φ > 0 were conscious, you would be a hierarchy of overlapping conscious subjects), but it generates counterintuitive implications about which physical systems are conscious and which are not.

---

## The Logic Gate / Expander Graph Problem

The most widely discussed objection to IIT, originating in Scott Aaronson's 2014 blog post. Aaronson pointed out that certain highly regular mathematical graph structures — expander graphs — have very high Φ values by IIT's measure, despite being simple, regular, and devoid of any plausible phenomenological properties. More generally, the objection is that IIT's Φ measure can be maximized by physical systems that have no connection to the kinds of systems we consider conscious, suggesting that Φ does not measure what Tononi claims it measures. IIT 3.0 modified the measure (from simple Φ to the full cause-effect structure) in ways intended to address this concern, but whether the modification fully resolves the problem is debated.

---

## Perturbational Complexity Index (PCI)

An empirical approximation of consciousness-related integrated information, developed by Massimini and Tononi, that uses transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) to perturb the brain and measures the complexity of the resulting EEG response. A conscious brain produces a complex, spatiotemporally differentiated response to TMS; an unconscious brain (during deep sleep or anesthesia) produces a simple, stereotyped response. PCI provides a single number that increases with the complexity of the TMS response and has been shown to discriminate between conscious and unconscious states with high accuracy in clinical populations. PCI is IIT-motivated but does not directly measure Φ; it is better understood as an empirical biomarker of the kind of neural integration that IIT predicts underlies consciousness.
