# Key Researchers: Chapter 29 — Integrated Information Theory

---

## Giulio Tononi (1960– )

Giulio Tononi is an Italian-American neuroscientist and psychiatrist at the University of Wisconsin-Madison who is the originator and primary developer of Integrated Information Theory. His career spans clinical psychiatry (he trained as a physician in Italy before pursuing research), neuroscience of sleep (his early work with Gerald Edelman on neural dynamics and homeostatic sleep regulation remains influential), and theoretical consciousness science.

IIT represents the defining project of Tononi's intellectual life. Beginning with an information-theoretic approach in the early 2000s and developing through three major revisions (IIT 1.0, 2.0, and 3.0), the theory has become increasingly mathematically sophisticated and philosophically explicit. Tononi is unusual among neuroscientists in his willingness to embrace the philosophical implications of his theory, including panpsychism, and to engage directly with philosophers on questions of phenomenology, causation, and ontology.

He has also pursued empirical applications of IIT concepts, particularly in the measurement of consciousness in clinical populations (patients in vegetative states, under anesthesia, during sleep) using tools like the Perturbational Complexity Index. His collaborations with Massimini, Koch, and others have attempted to bridge the gap between theoretical framework and clinical practice. Whether IIT is ultimately vindicated or replaced, Tononi's sustained effort to make consciousness scientifically tractable represents a significant intellectual contribution.

---

## Christof Koch (1956– )

Christof Koch is a German-American neuroscientist who was for decades at Caltech and is currently at the Allen Institute for Brain Science. His long collaboration with Francis Crick on the neural correlates of consciousness established the empirical research program that seeks to identify the specific neural mechanisms associated with conscious states. His subsequent adoption of IIT as his preferred theoretical framework represents a shift from the search for correlates to a principled theory of what consciousness *is*.

Koch's advocacy for IIT is enthusiastic and broad: he has argued that IIT implies consciousness in insects, the internet (which he concludes has very low Φ), and potentially in individual cells. His public engagement with IIT's implications — including a public bet with philosopher David Chalmers about the prospects for consciousness science that ran for 25 years — has helped shape the popular understanding of both consciousness science and IIT.

His book *The Feeling of Life Itself* (2019) represents the most accessible defense of IIT available, and his continued empirical work at the Allen Institute on the cellular and circuit basis of awareness in the mouse provides some of the empirical grounding that the theoretical framework requires.

## Marcello Massimini (1970– )

Marcello Massimini is an Italian neuroscientist at the University of Milan whose empirical work on consciousness has provided some of the most direct experimental engagement with IIT's predictions. Working with Tononi, he developed the Perturbational Complexity Index (PCI) — a measure that uses transcranial magnetic stimulation combined with EEG to assess the complexity of brain responses and thereby estimate the degree of consciousness-supporting integration.

PCI has been applied in clinical studies of patients in vegetative states, minimally conscious states, and locked-in syndrome, and has been shown to discriminate between conscious and unconscious states with high sensitivity. This represents a genuine empirical application of IIT-motivated thinking to clinical neuroscience, providing a test of whether integration-based measures of brain activity correlate with consciousness in the ways the theory predicts.

## Scott Aaronson (1981– )

Scott Aaronson is an American theoretical computer scientist at the University of Texas at Austin who is not a consciousness researcher but whose blog post criticizing IIT — "Why I am not an integrated information theorist" (2014) — became one of the most widely discussed critiques of the theory. Aaronson's objection, focusing on the high Φ values of expander graphs, crystallized the concern that IIT's mathematical measure fails to track intuitive notions of consciousness.

Aaronson's contribution is important precisely because it comes from outside the consciousness research community: a mathematician and computer scientist who found IIT's mathematical claims compelling enough to engage with seriously, and found them to generate unacceptable conclusions. His critique helped prompt revisions in IIT 3.0 and has remained a touchstone in discussions of the theory's mathematical foundations.

---

## Gerald Edelman (1929–2014)

Gerald Edelman was an American biologist who won the Nobel Prize for his work on the chemical structure of antibodies before turning his attention to neuroscience and consciousness. His "neural Darwinism" framework — the theory of neuronal group selection, which proposed that the brain develops through a competitive selection process analogous to natural selection — was an important predecessor to contemporary theories of neural dynamics.

Tononi worked with Edelman for many years, and IIT grew out of that collaboration. Edelman's concept of "re-entrant" neural dynamics — the reciprocal, recursive signaling between different brain regions that he argued was essential to consciousness — directly influenced IIT's emphasis on integration and the importance of recurrent connectivity. Understanding Edelman's work provides important historical context for IIT's theoretical commitments.

## Larissa Albantakis

Larissa Albantakis is a computational neuroscientist in Tononi's laboratory at the University of Wisconsin who has been responsible for much of the technical development of IIT 3.0, including the formal definition of the cause-effect structure and the development of the PyPhi software library that allows researchers to compute Φ for small systems. Her work represents the engineering side of the IIT project: taking Tononi's philosophical-mathematical framework and turning it into computable tools that can be applied to real (small) networks.

Her contributions to formalizing the theory have made it possible to compute Φ for simplified models of biological networks, enabling the first quantitative predictions of IIT for non-neural systems. This includes work on the causal integration of simple biochemical networks and on the relationship between network architecture and Φ values in artificial systems.
