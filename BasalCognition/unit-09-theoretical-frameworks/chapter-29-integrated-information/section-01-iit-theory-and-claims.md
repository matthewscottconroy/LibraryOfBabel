# Section 29.1: IIT — Theory and Claims

## Starting from Consciousness

Most scientific theories of consciousness start from the bottom up: they examine the physical systems we know to be conscious (functioning human brains) and ask what physical properties those systems have that unconscious systems lack. IIT starts differently — from the top down, from the structure of conscious experience itself. Tononi's strategy is to identify the *essential features* of consciousness through careful phenomenological analysis, and then to ask: what physical properties would a system need to have in order to exhibit those features? The theory is thus structured as a philosophical inference from phenomenological axioms to physical postulates (Tononi, 2004; Tononi, 2008).

This is an unusual and philosophically interesting approach, because it means that IIT's starting point is not an empirical observation but a conceptual analysis. If the phenomenological analysis is correct, and if the inference from phenomenology to physics is valid, then the theory's conclusion — that consciousness is identical to integrated information — follows necessarily. Whether the analysis and inference are correct is, of course, exactly what critics dispute.

## The Five Axioms

Tononi proposes five axioms describing essential features that every conscious experience must have — features so fundamental that to deny them would be to deny that experience exists at all. The axioms are:

**1. Existence**: Consciousness exists. This is the most unassailable claim in philosophy, established by Descartes' *cogito* and by the simple fact that one cannot doubt one's own experience from within that experience. Any adequate theory of consciousness must take the existence of experience as its starting datum.

**2. Composition**: Every conscious experience is structured — it is composed of distinctions among phenomenal elements. Within my current visual experience, I can distinguish the reddish patch at upper left from the green blur at center, the sharpness of the focused region from the blurriness of the periphery. Consciousness is not a single, undifferentiated quale but a richly structured manifold of distinctions.

**3. Information**: Every conscious experience is specific. Not only is experience structured, but each experience is one particular structure among a vast space of possible structures. My current visual experience is this specific configuration of color, form, and brightness — not any of the countless other configurations I could have had. Consciousness is informative precisely because it rules out all the other possible experiences I am not having right now.

**4. Integration**: Every conscious experience is unified. The redness of the apple and the roundness of the apple and the location of the apple in my visual field are not experienced as separate qualia registered by separate systems — they are experienced as aspects of a single, unified experience of the apple. Consciousness cannot be fully decomposed into independent parts without remainder. Even if I try to attend separately to different aspects of my visual field, the whole remains unified.

**5. Exclusion**: Every conscious experience is definite and bounded. My experience right now is this specific experience, not some expanded version of it that includes all your experiences, nor some reduced version that excludes the periphery of my visual field. Consciousness is always a specific, bounded whole — neither more nor less than it is. This axiom rules out both the possibility that consciousness is arbitrarily extended and the possibility that it can be arbitrarily compressed.

These five axioms are not derived from empirical observation — they are presented as structural features of consciousness that can be verified through first-person reflection. Tononi argues that any adequate theory of consciousness must account for all five, and that most existing theories fail to account for at least one. The hard problem, on his view, consists partly in the failure of existing theories to explain these features.

## From Axioms to Postulates

The five axioms describe what consciousness is like from the inside. The five **postulates** describe what a physical system must be like in order to instantiate consciousness with those features. The inference from axioms to postulates is the theory's key move, and it is where philosophical disagreement is most intense.

The logic is roughly as follows. If experience exists (axiom 1), then something exists that corresponds to it physically. If experience is structured (axiom 2), then that physical substrate must support a rich structure of cause-effect relations. If experience is informative (axiom 3), then the physical substrate must be capable of specifying one state among many possible states. If experience is integrated (axiom 4), then the physical substrate must integrate information in a way that cannot be decomposed without loss. If experience is definite and bounded (axiom 5), then the physical substrate must have a well-defined, maximally irreducible cause-effect structure.

These requirements, Tononi argues, are jointly satisfied by physical systems that have high **integrated information**, measured by the quantity Φ (phi). Φ measures how much information a system generates as a whole, above and beyond the information generated by its parts independently. A system with high Φ is one whose components are so richly causally interdependent that the whole cannot be fully described by summing the descriptions of its parts.

## Phi: The Measure of Integrated Information

The concept of Φ requires some care because it has been technically revised across different versions of the theory. The basic intuition, however, is clear:

Consider a system of elements, each of which can be in different states. The system generates information about its current state by ruling out other possible states. Now ask: how much of that information is generated by the system *as a whole* versus by its parts taken separately? If the system's elements are completely independent — if knowing the state of each element separately gives you just as much information as knowing the state of all elements together — then Φ = 0. The system has no integrated information. If the elements are richly interconnected such that knowing the state of the whole tells you something you could not learn from the parts alone, then Φ > 0.

The minimum information partition (MIP) is the way of dividing the system into parts that minimizes the information lost by the partition. Φ is the amount of information lost even by the MIP — the irreducible information generated by the whole system as a whole. A system with high Φ is one where even the best way of breaking it into parts loses substantial information.

**Φ = 0** for feedforward networks: in a purely feedforward system (like a series of filters, each taking input from the previous one and passing output to the next), any partition into two non-communicating parts disconnects the system entirely, and the separated parts generate just as much information as the whole. Feedforward networks have no integration in the relevant sense.

**Φ = 0** for completely disconnected elements: a collection of unconnected elements trivially generates no integrated information.

**Φ is maximized** for systems with rich, reciprocal causal connections where many elements influence many others, where information flows in multiple directions, and where the whole cannot be factored into independent modules.

## The Identity Thesis

The central claim of IIT — and its most philosophically distinctive feature — is the **identity thesis**: consciousness is not merely correlated with, or caused by, or implemented by integrated information. Consciousness *is* integrated information. Φ is consciousness.

This is a strong metaphysical claim that goes beyond most scientific theories of consciousness. It asserts that there is no further question about why systems with high Φ have experiences — they just do, by definition, the way that water is just H₂O. The identity is not an empirical discovery (as water = H₂O was) but a conceptual analysis: once we understand what consciousness really is (integrated information), we recognize that Φ is not a correlate of consciousness but is consciousness.

The implications are striking. If consciousness = Φ, then:

- Every system with nonzero Φ has some degree of consciousness, however minimal.
- Consciousness admits of degrees: systems with higher Φ are more conscious than systems with lower Φ.
- Consciousness is substrate-independent in one sense: what matters is not whether the substrate is biological, but whether it has the right causal structure to generate high Φ.
- But consciousness is also substrate-sensitive in another sense: the same computation performed on different physical substrates might generate different Φ values, because Φ depends on the actual causal structure of the physical implementation, not on the abstract computation performed.

## The Five Postulates in Summary

The five postulates translate the axioms into physical requirements:

1. **Intrinsic existence**: The physical substrate must exist and generate cause-effect power upon itself.
2. **Composition**: The substrate must support a structure of cause-effect relations among its elements.
3. **Information**: The cause-effect structure must be specific — distinguishing one state among many.
4. **Integration**: The cause-effect structure must be irreducible — it cannot be decomposed without loss.
5. **Exclusion**: The substrate has a maximal, definite cause-effect structure (the one with highest Φ), and no other.

The exclusion postulate is perhaps the most unusual and most disputed. It says that consciousness is associated with the system that has the single, maximum Φ value — not with systems nested inside it (which have lower Φ) and not with systems encompassing it (which might have lower Φ). This exclusion is needed to explain why experience is always definite and bounded, but it generates the controversial implication that smaller, nested subsystems of the brain are *not* conscious even if they have some Φ of their own.

## Implications for Non-Neural Systems

The most striking implication of IIT for basal cognition is straightforward: if Φ > 0 implies some degree of consciousness, and if any richly interconnected physical system generates nonzero Φ, then virtually every living system — and many non-living physical systems — will have some degree of consciousness.

For a bacterium, the signaling network has some degree of causal integration: changing one component affects others, and knowing the state of all components together reveals something not revealed by knowing each separately. If Φ > 0 for bacterial signaling networks — which is almost certainly true, if the quantity can be meaningfully computed — then bacteria have some degree of consciousness on IIT.

The same applies to plant electrical signaling networks, fungal mycelial networks, and the intracellular regulatory networks of any eukaryotic cell. IIT is, effectively, a form of *panpsychism*: the view that consciousness is ubiquitous in nature, present wherever there is some degree of physical integration.

This is philosophically significant but also philosophically expensive. Panpsychism is a coherent position, but it faces the *combination problem*: if every system has some consciousness, how do the micro-experiences of individual neurons (or proteins, or electrons) combine to form the unified macro-experience of a human being? IIT has resources for addressing this through the exclusion postulate (consciousness is associated with the *maximum-Φ* system, which might be the whole brain rather than any of its parts), but whether this response is fully satisfactory is debated.
