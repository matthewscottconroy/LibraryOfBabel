# Section 29.2: IIT and Basal Cognition

## What IIT Predicts for Non-Neural Organisms

The previous section established that IIT's identity thesis — consciousness = integrated information — implies that any system with nonzero Φ has some degree of consciousness. This section examines what follows from this for the organisms at the center of this book, and then turns to the major scientific and philosophical criticisms that have been raised against IIT.

For basal cognition researchers, the most important implication of IIT is that the question "does this organism have consciousness?" is, in principle, answerable by computing Φ for its physical substrate. This makes the question both more tractable (it becomes a mathematical question about causal structure) and more demanding (we need detailed knowledge of the causal structure of molecular signaling networks to apply the measure).

### Bacteria and Unicellular Organisms

A bacterial signaling network is a network of causally interdependent molecular components: receptors influence kinases, kinases influence response regulators, response regulators influence motor proteins, and so on in a web of regulatory interactions that include feedback loops and cross-talk. In principle, such a network has some nonzero Φ, because its components influence each other in ways that cannot be fully captured by considering each component in isolation.

In practice, computing Φ for a real bacterial signaling network is currently beyond our technical reach: the number of molecular states is astronomical, the interactions are not fully characterized, and the computational complexity of Φ calculation scales superexponentially with system size. What we can say, from the structure of the network, is that it is likely to have some positive Φ, because it contains feedback loops and cross-talk that create the kind of irreducible causal integration that Φ measures.

IIT would therefore predict that *E. coli* has some degree of consciousness — presumably very low compared to mammalian nervous systems, but nonzero. This is a striking prediction, but it is not a very actionable one given current measurement capabilities.

### Plants and Fungi

Plants possess electrical signaling networks (see Chapters 13–14) that share some formal features with neural networks: long-distance electrical propagation, integration of signals from multiple sources, and modulation of response based on systemic state. The degree of causal integration in these networks — and hence the Φ value — is an open question. IIT would predict some consciousness if Φ > 0, but the specific Φ value would need to be computed to evaluate the prediction.

Fungal mycelial networks are particularly interesting from an IIT perspective because they combine widespread physical connectivity (enabling signal propagation across large distances) with integration of nutrient and environmental information from multiple sources. The network topology of a mature mycelium — with its anastomoses, feedback loops, and dynamic restructuring — might support higher Φ than is immediately obvious.

### Slime Molds

*Physarum polycephalum* presents an unusual case for IIT. The organism is a single cell (in its plasmodial stage) with distributed cytoplasmic flow that carries electrical and chemical signals across the entire organism. The integration of this flow — the way that conditions at one end of the plasmodium affect conditions at the other — represents a form of physical integration across a spatially extended single-celled system. If Φ is high for a system whose components are the cytoplasmic flow fields across the organism, IIT would predict some consciousness in *Physarum*. This is speculative but not incoherent.

## Computationalism and the Logic Gate Problem

One of the most discussed implications of IIT — and one of its most counterintuitive — concerns the relationship between IIT and computationalism. Computationalism is the view that mental states are defined by their computational (functional) properties and are independent of the physical substrate that implements them. IIT is, in an important sense, *anti-computationalist*: it holds that Φ depends on the physical causal structure of a system, not on the abstract computation it performs.

This has a dramatic implication. Suppose we take a neural network that processes information in some complex way and implement the same abstract computation on two different physical substrates: (1) a biological neural network with recurrent connections, and (2) a feedforward digital circuit with no recurrent connections but implementing the same input-output function. The computationalist says these two systems implement the same computation and should have the same functional mental states. IIT says they are completely different: the feedforward circuit has Φ = 0 (because feedforward systems have no integrated information) while the recurrent biological network has high Φ. The feedforward circuit, however computationally sophisticated, is not conscious on IIT.

This is interesting because it suggests that biological neural architectures — with their massive recurrence, feedback, and cross-talk — are precisely the kind of physical structure that generates high Φ. Feedforward deep learning networks, however capable, would have very low Φ on IIT. This gives IIT a built-in explanation for why AI systems might be cognitively sophisticated but not conscious: they are feedforward architectures with low integration.

### The Problematic Grid of Logic Gates

The most famous objection to IIT from this direction comes from the computer scientist Scott Aaronson, who pointed out that certain mathematical objects — specifically, large expander graphs — have extremely high Φ values by the IIT measure, even though they are simple, regular mathematical structures that seem to have no phenomenological plausibility as conscious systems. Aaronson's "expander graph objection" challenges IIT on the grounds that Φ measures something mathematical, not something specifically relevant to consciousness (Aaronson, 2014).

Tononi's response was to acknowledge that the concern is genuine but to argue that it reveals a limitation of early versions of IIT that are addressed in the more fully developed IIT 3.0. In IIT 3.0, the relevant measure is not Φ alone but the entire *cause-effect structure* of the system — the specific pattern of distinctions it generates — and simple regular structures like expander graphs would have cause-effect structures that do not match any plausible phenomenology.

The more general version of Aaronson's concern is this: IIT seems to entail that any physical system that happens to have the right causal topology will be conscious, regardless of what it "does" or what it "means." A randomly wired grid of logic gates with the right connectivity pattern would, on IIT, be more conscious than a lightly integrated biological organism. This seems deeply wrong to most people's intuitions. It suggests that IIT is measuring something — causal integration — that is not quite the same as what we mean by consciousness.

## Scientific Criticisms

IIT has attracted scientific criticism from several directions beyond the logic gate objection:

### Computational Intractability

Computing Φ is computationally intractable for systems of any realistic size. The number of possible partitions of a system grows super-exponentially with its size, making exact Φ computation impossible for systems with more than a handful of elements. Approximations exist but are difficult to validate (Tononi et al., 2016). This means that the theory cannot, in practice, generate precise quantitative predictions for real biological systems, which limits its scientific usefulness and makes it difficult to test.

### The Grain Problem

IIT requires specifying the "grain" at which the system is analyzed: the relevant elements and their states. But different choices of grain can yield very different Φ values. Should neurons be analyzed at the level of spiking rates, membrane potentials, ion channel states, protein conformations, or quantum states? Each choice yields a different network with different causal structure and different Φ. The theory does not, in its current form, specify which grain is the right one, and this is not a minor technical detail — it is central to the theory's empirical content (Cerullo, 2015).

### Empirical Disconfirmation Concerns

A group of researchers published a letter in 2023 signed by over 100 scientists arguing that IIT had been elevated to the status of a well-supported theory without adequate empirical evidence, and calling for clearer specification of what evidence could disconfirm it. The letter, and the response from IIT proponents, crystallized a debate about the scientific status of the theory that has not been resolved (Fleming et al., 2023). This reflects a broader concern about whether IIT makes enough specific, testable predictions to count as a scientific theory in good standing.

### The Axiom Problem

The five axioms are presented as self-evident features of consciousness, but critics have challenged both their content and their status as axioms. The integration axiom, for instance, might be false: split-brain patients show that the unity of consciousness can be disrupted by severing neural connections, suggesting that consciousness is more decomposable than the integration axiom implies (Bayne, 2010). If the axioms are not self-evident but controversial empirical claims, then the theory's deductive structure loses its foundation.

## Philosophical Objections

### The Multiple Realization Problem for IIT

A classical philosophical objection to IIT concerns multiple realizability. If consciousness = Φ, then the same Φ value can be realized by physically very different systems. But intuitively, not all physical systems with the same Φ value should have the same experiences. A silicon chip and a biological neuron network with the same Φ should, on IIT, have exactly the same experiences. But this contradicts the intuition that biological and non-biological substrates might have different phenomenological properties even with the same informational structure. This is the same intuition that drives biological naturalism about consciousness (Searle, 1992) and it sits uneasily with IIT.

### Panpsychist Commitments

IIT is committed to a form of panpsychism, and panpsychism faces the **combination problem**: how do the micro-experiences of individual physical elements combine to form the unified macro-experience of a conscious system? IIT's response via the exclusion postulate — that the macro-experience is associated with the maximum-Φ system, not with any of its parts — is intended to address this, but critics argue that it merely relocates rather than solves the problem. Why should the maximum-Φ system be the locus of experience rather than any of the many nested systems with positive but sub-maximal Φ?

### IIT and the Hard Problem

IIT proposes to solve the hard problem of consciousness by identifying consciousness with a physical property (integrated information). But critics argue that even if we accept the identification, the hard problem reasserts itself: why should integrated information, as opposed to some other physical property, give rise to experience? The identification is stipulated by the axiom-to-postulate inference, but stipulation is not explanation. We can ask "why does integrated information feel like anything?" just as we can ask "why does neural activity feel like anything?" (Chalmers, 2016).

## What IIT Offers Basal Cognition Research

Despite its critics, IIT offers several genuine contributions to basal cognition research:

**A principled measure for comparing systems**: Even if Φ cannot be computed exactly for real biological systems, approximations and structural analyses can be compared across systems of different complexity. IIT motivates the question of how integrated different biological signaling networks are, which is a well-defined empirical question independent of the theory's consciousness claims.

**A challenge to neural chauvinism**: IIT's implication that consciousness is not restricted to nervous systems challenges the assumption that neural complexity is the only relevant variable. Even if the theory's specific predictions are wrong, it usefully forces the question of what else besides neural connectivity might matter for whatever it is that brains have and other systems might have in degree.

**A formalism for causal structure**: The mathematical tools IIT has generated for quantifying causal integration in networks are useful beyond the specific context of consciousness theory. Analyzing the causal structure of molecular signaling networks, developmental gene regulatory networks, or ecosystem-level interaction networks using IIT-derived tools can generate useful insights regardless of whether one accepts the identity thesis.

The honest assessment is that IIT is a bold and philosophically serious theory that has generated both important insights and important critiques. It is worth engaging with carefully and critically, neither dismissing it as obviously wrong nor accepting it as established science. For basal cognition, its most useful legacy may be the set of questions it forces us to ask about causal integration in biological systems — questions that are worth pursuing even if the answers do not vindicate IIT's specific claims about consciousness.
