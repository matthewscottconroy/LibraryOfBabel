# Chapter 30: Levin's Cognitive Light Cone and Scale-Free Cognition

## The Problem of the Right Level

When we ask whether a cell is intelligent, we face an immediate methodological problem: compared to what? A single neuron? A cell is smarter. A brain? A cell seems hopelessly inferior. The comparison depends entirely on what level of organization we choose as our reference. And yet in biology, the choice of level is not arbitrary — it is a scientific question. The level at which a biological system exercises *competency* — the ability to achieve goals reliably despite perturbations — is a discoverable fact about the system, not merely a matter of analytical convenience.

Michael Levin, a developmental biologist at Tufts University, has built a theoretical framework around precisely this insight. His **cognitive light cone** concept proposes that every agent operates at a characteristic spatial and temporal scale: it integrates information from within its light cone and acts to achieve goals at that scale. A bacterium's cognitive light cone is small: it integrates local chemical information and pursues goals (nutrient acquisition, avoiding toxins) on timescales of seconds and spatial scales of micrometers. A multicellular organism's cognitive light cone is vastly larger: it integrates information from across its entire body and pursues goals (reaching homeostatic target states, mounting immune responses, completing development) on timescales of hours to years.

What makes this framework powerful is its implication that the distinction between "cognition" and "mere mechanism" is not a kind distinction but a scale distinction. At every level of biological organization — from molecules to cells to tissues to organs to organisms to colonies — there are systems with characteristic cognitive light cones, exercising competencies appropriate to their scale. This is what Levin calls **scale-free cognition**: the same basic cognitive principles apply at every level, but instantiated in different physical substrates and operating at different scales.

## Central Question

**What determines the cognitive light cone of a biological system, and how do light cones at different scales interact?** The answer, Levin argues, involves bioelectricity — the electrical signaling that coordinates cells into coherent, goal-directed collectives. Bodies know what shape to build partly because cells communicate electrically, sharing information that extends individual cellular cognitive light cones into a collective, organismal-scale intelligence. The chapter traces this argument from theory to experiment.

## Chapter Roadmap

**Section 30.1 — The Cognitive Light Cone** introduces the concept formally, explains the competency hierarchy from cells to colonies, examines what "scale-free cognition" means and what it does not mean, and discusses the implications of the framework for understanding cancer, regeneration, and development as cognitive phenomena.

**Section 30.2 — Morphogenetic Intelligence** examines how bodies know what shape to build. It introduces the concept of body plans as attractors in high-dimensional bioelectrical state space, examines regeneration in organisms like planaria as goal-directed problem-solving under novel conditions, and discusses the Xenobot project — the design of living machines from frog cells — as an experiment in re-specifying cognitive targets at the cellular level.
