# Chapter 30 Exercises: Levin's Cognitive Light Cone and Scale-Free Cognition

---

## Part I: Reflection and Discussion Questions

**1. The Grain of Cognition**
The cognitive light cone is defined by the spatial and temporal scale over which a system integrates information and pursues goals. But "scale" is not a fixed property of a system — it depends on the analytical framework applied. A heart, for instance, might be analyzed as a collection of individual cardiac muscle cells (cellular scale), as a coordinated pump (organ scale), or as part of the circulatory system (systemic scale). Does it make sense to say that the heart has a single cognitive light cone, or does it have different light cones depending on the level of analysis? If light cones are level-relative, does the concept lose its empirical bite?

**2. Goal-Directedness and Mechanism**
The chapter distinguishes between "executing a program" and "pursuing a goal," arguing that regeneration involves the latter. But is this distinction principled? A thermostat "pursues the goal" of maintaining temperature by detecting deviations and correcting them — yet we don't consider thermostats to be cognitive agents. What additional features would need to be present in a biological system for its behavior to count as genuine goal-pursuit rather than sophisticated mechanism? Evaluate Levin's criteria (equifinality, flexibility, novel problem-solving) and consider whether they are sufficient to distinguish genuine cognition from very complex reflexes.

**3. Cancer as Cognitive Defection**
Levin proposes that cancer represents a "defection" from multicellular cognitive coordination: cancer cells have shrunk their cognitive light cones to the cellular scale and no longer integrate tissue-level bioelectrical signals. Evaluate this framing. Does it provide explanatory insight beyond the standard genetic account of cancer? Does it generate different therapeutic predictions? What experimental results would support or challenge the bioelectrical account of cancer specifically over the standard genetic-mutation account?

**4. Xenobots and Moral Status**
Xenobots are living entities constructed from frog cells and designed by an evolutionary algorithm to exhibit specific behaviors. If we take seriously the claim that cells exercise genuine cognition — that they pursue goals, integrate information, and exhibit flexibility — then Xenobots are cognitive agents. Should we have moral concern for Xenobots? Consider the following positions: (a) moral status requires sentience (the capacity for pleasure and pain), and there is no reason to believe Xenobots are sentient; (b) moral status tracks cognitive sophistication, and Xenobots have some degree of cognition; (c) the act of deliberately creating and destroying cognitive agents is itself problematic, regardless of the agents' degree of sophistication. Which position do you find most defensible, and what are its implications for future bioengineering?

**5. Scale-Free Cognition and Reductionism**
Levin argues that the competency hierarchy is not reducible in the sense that understanding each level requires its own theoretical framework. Is this a claim about ontological emergence (there are genuinely new causal powers at higher levels) or about epistemic emergence (the higher-level description is indispensable for understanding behavior, even if lower-level mechanisms are complete)? Does the distinction matter for how we should do science? Consider the relationship between Levin's position and Philip Anderson's famous "More is Different" argument about emergent phenomena in condensed matter physics.

## Part II: Thought Experiments

**1. The Cognitively Isolated Organ**
Imagine a kidney perfectly isolated from the body — receiving blood supply and capable of filtering, but disconnected from all hormonal, neural, and bioelectrical communication with the rest of the organism. The kidney continues to function: it filters blood and produces urine. Does the kidney have a cognitive light cone in Levin's sense? What are its goals? Now reconnect the kidney to the organism: does its cognitive light cone change? At what point does the kidney's cognition become the organism's cognition? This thought experiment probes the boundaries between organ-level and organism-level cognition.

**2. The Bioelectrical Body Plan Upload**
Levin and colleagues have shown that the bioelectrical state of an organism contains information about its body plan. Suppose we could completely characterize the bioelectrical state of an organism at a given developmental stage and "upload" it to a different organism — somehow imposing the bioelectrical pattern of a frog on a developing axolotl (a salamander with very different anatomy). According to the attractor framework, the axolotl cells should now pursue the frog body plan as their target. Would the result be a frog? A chimera? An organism that actively struggles between two attractors? This thought experiment probes the relationship between genetic programs and bioelectrical attractors in determining developmental outcomes.

**3. The Minimal Morphogenetic Agent**
Design a hypothetical minimal organism that would demonstrate the characteristic features of morphogenetic intelligence: equifinality, flexibility in the face of novel challenges, and bioelectrically encoded target states. This organism should be as simple as possible — ideally close to the complexity of a protist — while exhibiting these features. What would you need to observe in such an organism to be convinced that it has a morphogenetic cognitive light cone? Can such an organism exist at the unicellular level, or does morphogenetic intelligence necessarily require multicellularity?

## Part III: Laboratory and Computational Investigations

**1. Bioelectric Manipulation of Planarian Head-Tail Polarity**
*Rationale*: Reproduce (at a conceptual and computational level, or in the laboratory if facilities allow) the key experiments demonstrating bioelectrical control of planarian regeneration. Culture planarian fragments and expose them to gap junction blockers or ion channel modulators known to alter the bioelectrical pattern. Predict, based on the literature, what anatomical outcome each manipulation should produce. Then determine experimentally whether the predictions are correct. Key measurements: (a) membrane potential patterns across the wound edge using voltage-sensitive dyes; (b) expression patterns of polarity-determining genes (e.g., *Wnt* signaling components); (c) anatomical outcomes after full regeneration. Analyze whether changes in bioelectrical state predict changes in gene expression and anatomical outcome in the order predicted by the bioelectric-first hypothesis.

**2. Computational Attractor Analysis of Developmental Gene Regulatory Networks**
*Rationale*: Implement a Boolean or continuous-state model of a well-characterized developmental gene regulatory network (e.g., the segment polarity network in *Drosophila*, or the endomesoderm network in sea urchin). Analyze the model's attractor landscape: how many stable attractors does the network have? How deep are their basins of attraction (i.e., how far from the attractor can the system be perturbed and still return to it)? How do the attractors change when key nodes are removed or modified? Compare the attractor structure to the known developmental outcomes of gene perturbation experiments. Does the attractor structure predict experimental phenotypes? This investigation connects Levin's theoretical framework to the concrete molecular biology of developmental networks.

**3. Measuring Competency Hierarchies in Biofilm Formation**
*Rationale*: *Bacillus subtilis* forms biofilms that exhibit complex spatial organization: different cell types (spore-formers, matrix producers, motile cells) occupy specific positions within the biofilm structure. This organization is the result of coordinated gene expression driven by quorum sensing and other cell-to-cell communication mechanisms. Design an experiment to measure the "cognitive light cone" of the biofilm as a whole versus individual cells: how large is the spatial scale over which positional information is integrated? Use fluorescent reporters to track the spatial spread of a signal introduced at one edge of the biofilm and quantify how far the signal propagates and how it influences gene expression in distant cells. Compare the spatial range of biofilm-level coordination to the spatial range of individual-cell sensing, and determine whether the biofilm exhibits competencies (coordinated spatial patterning) that exceed what could be achieved by individual cell-level sensing.
