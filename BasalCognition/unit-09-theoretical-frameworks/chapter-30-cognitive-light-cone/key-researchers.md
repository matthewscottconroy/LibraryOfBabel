# Key Researchers: Chapter 30 — Cognitive Light Cone and Scale-Free Cognition

---

## Michael Levin (1969– )

Michael Levin is a developmental biologist at Tufts University who has become one of the most intellectually ambitious figures in contemporary biology. His laboratory combines experimental work on bioelectricity in development and regeneration with theoretical and computational work on cognitive science and information theory, pursuing an integrated research program that is unusual in its disciplinary breadth.

Levin trained in genetics and developmental biology and has spent decades studying the role of bioelectrical signals — particularly membrane potentials maintained by ion channels and gap junctions — in directing developmental processes. His experimental work has produced striking demonstrations that bioelectrical signals are not merely epiphenomenal accompaniments to gene expression but are causally upstream of major developmental events: they can change which genes are expressed, in which cells, and with what spatial patterns. These findings have forced a reconsideration of the relationship between genetics and development.

Alongside his experimental work, Levin has developed an increasingly ambitious theoretical framework — the cognitive light cone and scale-free cognition — that situates his bioelectricity findings in a broader picture of biological intelligence distributed across multiple levels of organization. He collaborates with philosophers, cognitive scientists, roboticists, and AI researchers, and publishes in journals spanning developmental biology, neuroscience, philosophy of biology, and cognitive science. He is one of the most productive and original scientists working at the intersection of biology and cognitive science today.

## Josh Bongard

Josh Bongard is a computer scientist at the University of Vermont who specializes in evolutionary robotics and morphological computation. His collaboration with Levin on the Xenobot project represents a synthesis of computational design and biological implementation that is unprecedented: using evolutionary algorithms to design the configuration of living cells, and then constructing those configurations using microsurgery on actual embryonic tissue.

Bongard's independent work explores how the body shape of a robot affects its cognitive capacities — the thesis that morphology is a form of computation (discussed further in Chapter 32). His evolutionary algorithms for robot design have produced bodies with unusual and counterintuitive shapes that nonetheless perform well at specific tasks, demonstrating that design space for intelligent embodied systems is far larger than intuition suggests. The Xenobot collaboration extends this work to living systems, exploring whether the principles discovered in robotic design apply to the biological domain.

## Giovanni Pezzulo

Giovanni Pezzulo is a computational neuroscientist at the National Research Council of Italy whose work bridges theoretical neuroscience and theoretical biology in ways that complement Levin's empirical program. His collaborative papers with Levin on "top-down models in biology" have argued for the indispensability of goal-directed explanations in biology — explanations that describe systems in terms of what they are trying to achieve, not just what they are mechanistically doing.

Pezzulo works on active inference and predictive processing (connecting to Chapter 28) and has proposed frameworks for understanding the evolution of cognition in terms of the increasing temporal depth of the states that organisms can model and act upon. His work connects Levin's developmental biology to the broader cognitive science literature and provides theoretical frameworks for understanding how morphogenetic intelligence relates to neural intelligence.

## Lewis Wolpert (1929–2021)

Lewis Wolpert was a British developmental biologist at University College London whose work on positional information provided the foundational framework within which bioelectrical morphogenesis is now understood. His "French flag model" — in which a gradient of a signaling molecule provides positional information to cells, allowing them to determine their identity based on their position — remains the canonical account of how cells know where they are in a developing embryo.

Wolpert's framework is in some ways the foil for Levin's: where Wolpert emphasized the role of chemical gradients as the medium for positional information, Levin has argued for the primacy of bioelectrical signals. The two frameworks are complementary rather than contradictory — bioelectrical signals often work by regulating the expression of signaling molecules — but the emphasis is different. Wolpert was also a prominent public defender of the view that plants are not intelligent and that anthropomorphizing biology is a scientific error, making him an interesting counterpoint to the basal cognition researchers whose work this book surveys.

## Alan Turing (1912–1954)

Alan Turing's contribution to morphogenetics research is often forgotten in accounts that focus on his foundational contributions to computer science and his tragic personal history. His 1952 paper "The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis" is one of the foundational papers in theoretical biology. Turing showed mathematically that two chemicals interacting through diffusion — one an activator that promotes its own production, one an inhibitor that suppresses the activator — can spontaneously generate spatial patterns from uniform initial conditions. This reaction-diffusion mechanism is now recognized as playing a role in a wide range of biological patterning processes, from the stripes of a zebra to the spacing of hair follicles.

Turing's morphogenetic work is relevant to Chapter 30 because it establishes the principle that spatial information can emerge spontaneously from self-organizing chemical dynamics, without requiring a pre-specified genetic blueprint. Levin's bioelectrical morphogenesis builds on this principle: the bioelectrical attractor states that encode body plan information are themselves products of self-organizing dynamics among ion channels and gap junctions. The attractor is emergent, not programmed.

## Leigh Van Valen (1935–2010)

Leigh Van Valen was an American evolutionary biologist at the University of Chicago who proposed the "Red Queen" hypothesis (organisms must continuously evolve just to maintain their relative fitness against coevolving competitors) and made wide-ranging contributions to evolutionary theory. His concept of "morphological species" — species defined by their stable adult morphology rather than by genetic criteria — is relevant to Levin's attractor framework: the adult morphology is, in attractor terms, the stable state that development reliably reaches.

Van Valen's work on the stability of morphology across evolutionary time — the observation that some body plans have remained stable for hundreds of millions of years while others change rapidly — raises the question of what determines the stability of morphogenetic attractors. This question has not been systematically addressed in Levin's framework but represents an important extension: understanding why some body plans are deep attractors while others are shallow would illuminate the evolutionary dynamics of morphogenetic intelligence.
