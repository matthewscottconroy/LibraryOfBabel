# Section 30.1: The Cognitive Light Cone

## What Counts as an Agent?

The question of what counts as a cognitive agent is one of the most contested in the philosophy of mind and cognitive science. Definitions cluster around three kinds of criteria: structural (having a nervous system, or some analog), functional (performing information processing of sufficient complexity), or teleological (having goals, in some robust sense). Each kind of criterion faces difficulties. Structural criteria are too narrow: they exclude obviously goal-directed systems that lack the favored substrate. Functional criteria are too broad: they include thermostats and flood-control systems if interpreted generously. Teleological criteria face the difficulty of distinguishing genuine goals from mere equilibria.

Michael Levin's approach is different. Rather than trying to define "agent" in terms of necessary and sufficient conditions, he proposes a **spatial-temporal criterion**: an agent is a system that integrates information over a characteristic spatial and temporal scale and uses that information to achieve goals at that scale. The cognitive light cone of an agent is the region of space and time over which it exercises such integration and goal-pursuit.

This criterion is deliberately scale-relative. It does not ask "is this system an agent in some absolute sense?" but rather "at what scale does this system exercise agency?" The answer is empirical: we can measure the spatial and temporal range of information integration and goal-directed behavior in any system, and the answer tells us the scale of the system's cognitive light cone.

The term "light cone" is borrowed from physics, where it denotes the region of spacetime that can causally influence or be influenced by a given event. A cognitive light cone is the biological analog: the region of spacetime that an agent integrates over and acts within. Just as a physical event cannot be influenced by events outside its past light cone, an agent cannot respond to perturbations outside its cognitive light cone — by definition, those perturbations are beyond the scale at which the agent integrates information.

## The Competency Hierarchy

One of Levin's most important empirical claims is that biological organization exhibits a **competency hierarchy**: at each level of biological organization, there are systems that exercise competencies — reliably achieving outcomes despite perturbations — that are not simply reducible to the competencies of the level below (Levin, 2019).

The hierarchy, roughly, looks like this:

**Molecular machines**: Individual molecular complexes (ion channels, signaling cascades, transcription factor networks) exercise competencies at the molecular scale. They achieve specific molecular goals — maintaining particular ion gradients, activating specific genes in response to signals — with robust reliability across varying conditions. These are not trivial: a single receptor-kinase signaling complex can implement something analogous to a logic gate, integrating multiple inputs and producing a specific output.

**Individual cells**: A cell integrates molecular signals from across its entire regulatory network and exercises competencies at the cellular scale — navigating chemical gradients, deciding when to divide, choosing between differentiation states, maintaining homeostasis. These competencies exceed what any individual molecular machine can achieve, because they require integration across the entire cellular regulatory network.

**Tissues and organs**: A tissue or organ integrates signals from hundreds of thousands of cells and exercises competencies at the tissue scale — forming correct anatomical structures during development, maintaining tissue homeostasis, mounting coordinated responses to damage. The vertebrate limb bud knows how to form a hand; the liver knows how to regenerate after partial hepatectomy; the immune system knows how to mount a coordinated response to a novel pathogen. These are competencies of the tissue or organ, not of individual cells.

**Whole organisms**: An organism integrates information from across its entire body and pursues goals at the organismal scale — navigating complex environments, learning from experience, executing multi-step behavioral sequences, regenerating entire missing body parts. A planarian flatworm, when cut into pieces, can regenerate an entire organism from each fragment — including the correct brain, gut, and musculature, in the correct spatial arrangement. This is a remarkable organismal-scale competency that requires integration of positional information across the entire worm body.

**Colonies and superorganisms**: Ant colonies, bee hives, and bacterial biofilms exercise competencies at the colonial scale — solving foraging optimization problems, defending against pathogens, constructing elaborate architecture — that no individual organism in the colony can exercise alone. The colony's cognitive light cone encompasses a spatial and temporal scale much larger than that of any individual member.

The key theoretical claim is that each level of this hierarchy is *not reducible* to the level below in the sense that the competencies at each level are not predictable from the properties of the level below without additional understanding of the level's own organizational principles. This is a claim about epistemic reduction, not ontological reduction: Levin does not invoke any mysterious non-physical causation, but he does maintain that understanding each level requires its own theoretical framework.

## What Scale-Free Cognition Claims — and Doesn't Claim

The term "scale-free cognition" is potentially misleading and deserves careful unpacking. It does not claim that cognition is the same at all scales — that a bacterium has the same kind of cognition as a mammal, or that a cell is as smart as an organism. It claims that the *same basic principles* — information integration, goal-directedness, competency in the face of perturbation — apply at every scale, instantiated in different physical substrates and operating over different spatial and temporal ranges.

This is an important distinction. Scale-free fractal patterns appear in many natural phenomena — coastlines, river networks, lung bronchioles — without implying that the physics is the same at every scale. The fractal pattern is produced by similar principles operating at different scales, not by identical mechanisms. Similarly, scale-free cognition proposes that similar principles of information integration and goal-directedness operate at every level of biological organization, even though the specific mechanisms are completely different at each level.

What are these "similar principles"? Levin and collaborators propose that the common thread is the existence of a goal or target state, the ability to integrate information about the current state relative to that target, and the ability to take action to close the gap between current and target states. This is a cybernetic framework — it echoes Norbert Wiener's original notion of feedback control — applied recursively at every level of biological organization (Levin, 2019).

### The Problem of Goal Attribution

The claim that cells "have goals" requires careful handling. It can be understood in two ways:

The weak reading: cells have stable attractor states that they return to after perturbation, and we can usefully describe their dynamics in goal-directed language without claiming any inner sense of striving or purpose. This is compatible with a fully mechanistic biology.

The strong reading: cells have goals in a sense that is continuous with the sense in which we have goals — they are genuinely pursuing outcomes, not merely exhibiting stable dynamics. This stronger claim is philosophically more ambitious and more controversial.

Levin's position is closer to the weak reading in its formal claims, but his language often suggests the stronger reading. He is careful to say that attributing cognitive vocabulary to cells is "agnostic" about whether they have subjective experience — it is a pragmatic attribution of cognitive concepts at the level of organization where those concepts are most useful for understanding and predicting behavior (Levin, 2019). This pragmatic stance is defensible but requires the reader to track carefully what is and is not being claimed.

## Implications for Cancer, Regeneration, and Development

One of the most striking features of Levin's framework is that it generates concrete predictions and explanations for biological phenomena that are not typically discussed in cognitive terms.

### Cancer as Cognitive Failure

On the scale-free cognition view, cancer can be understood as a failure of cognitive integration at the tissue scale. Normal cells integrate bioelectrical and biochemical signals from their tissue neighbors and adjust their behavior — proliferation rate, differentiation state, migration tendency — to maintain tissue homeostasis. In this sense, they are "good citizens" of the tissue: their individual behavior is coordinated with the goals of the whole.

Cancer cells, Levin argues, have "defected" from this integration: their cognitive light cone has shrunk to the cellular scale. They respond to local signals but ignore the tissue-level bioelectrical communication that coordinates normal cells. They pursue cellular-scale goals (nutrient acquisition, proliferation) without regard for the tissue-scale goals of homeostasis and appropriate anatomical structure.

This reconceptualization is not merely metaphorical. Levin and collaborators have shown that manipulating the bioelectrical state of tissues can affect tumor formation and regression in animal models. In *Xenopus* tadpoles, inducing depolarization in a patch of cells that share the bioelectrical signature of tumor cells causes those cells to form disorganized masses resembling tumors — and, conversely, normalizing the bioelectrical state of actual tumor cells can induce them to re-join the normal developmental program (Chernet & Levin, 2013).

This suggests that bioelectrical signals are, in part, what maintains cells' cognitive light cones at the tissue scale, and that disrupting those signals allows cells to "revert" to a more selfish, cellular-scale cognitive mode. Cancer, on this view, is not merely a genetic disease (though genetic mutations play a role) but a failure of the bioelectrical communication that maintains multicellular coordination.

### Regeneration as Goal-Directed Problem-Solving

Regeneration provides some of the most compelling evidence for scale-free cognition at the organismal level. When a planarian flatworm is cut into dozens of pieces, each piece can regenerate an entire organism. But the regenerated organism has the correct anatomy for a full-sized worm, not the anatomy of a tiny piece: it generates a head at the correct end and a tail at the correct end, forms a brain and gut in the correct positions, and achieves the correct proportion of body parts.

This is remarkable because it implies that individual cells or small groups of cells "know" what the target anatomy should be — that they have access to information about the global body plan that they are trying to rebuild. Levin and collaborators have argued that this information is encoded in the bioelectrical state of the tissue: the spatial pattern of membrane potentials across the organism constitutes a "body plan blueprint" that cells use to orient their developmental behaviors (Levin, 2012).

This interpretation is supported by experiments showing that changing the bioelectrical state of planaria — for example, by blocking gap junctions or manipulating specific ion channels — can produce planaria that regenerate with two heads, or with their head replaced by a tail, or with various anatomical abnormalities (Oviedo et al., 2010). These experiments demonstrate that the bioelectrical pattern is causally necessary for correct regeneration and that altering it produces predictable, albeit dramatic, changes in the regenerated anatomy.

Levin interprets this as evidence that regeneration is genuinely goal-directed at the organismal scale: the organism is pursuing the target state encoded in its bioelectrical pattern, and it will use whatever cellular mechanisms are available to achieve that target — including novel compensatory strategies when normal mechanisms are blocked. This is the hallmark of genuine goal-directedness in the cognitive sense: the use of diverse means to achieve a fixed end.

## The Cognitive Light Cone and Evolution

The cognitive light cone framework has interesting implications for evolutionary biology. If organisms at each level of the competency hierarchy are genuinely pursuing goals at that level, then evolution can operate not just on the morphological and behavioral outcomes of development, but on the goals themselves — the target states that biological systems pursue.

Levin has proposed the concept of **goal-directed evolution**: the idea that changes in the target states encoded in bioelectrical patterns can produce rapid, coordinated morphological changes that would be difficult to explain by the slow, gene-by-gene accumulation of mutations that classical population genetics describes. If a mutation changes a bioelectrical set-point, the entire developmental machinery orients toward the new target, potentially producing large morphological changes in a single step.

This is a speculative proposal that connects to ongoing debates in evolutionary developmental biology ("evo-devo") about the role of developmental plasticity in evolution. It does not contradict standard evolutionary theory — natural selection still operates on the outcomes — but it suggests that the units of evolutionary variation include not just genetic sequences but the cognitive targets encoded in bioelectrical states.
