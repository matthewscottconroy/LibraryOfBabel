# Chapter 31: Minimal Cognition Theories

## Where Does Cognition Begin?

Every scientific discipline has its boundary questions — questions about the limits of the domain the discipline studies. For the cognitive sciences, the boundary question is: where does cognition begin? Not where does neural cognition begin — that question, while interesting, is relatively tractable — but where does cognition *simpliciter* begin? Is a bacterium navigating a chemical gradient cognizing? Is an immune cell making a classification judgment when it attacks a pathogen? Is a plant integrating information about herbivory when it changes its chemical defenses?

These questions are not merely semantic. The answers we give shape how we design our experiments, how we interpret our results, and what we take ourselves to have explained when we explain a biological behavior. If cognition requires a nervous system, then the experiments described in this book are about complex molecular mechanisms that are, strictly speaking, outside cognitive science's domain. If cognition requires only the functional properties of information integration and goal-directed behavior, then bacteria and plants are genuine cognitive subjects, and cognitive science must expand its scope dramatically.

The field of **minimal cognition** takes the second option seriously. It asks: what are the minimum conditions necessary and sufficient for a system to count as cognitive? And it develops theoretical frameworks and empirical criteria for answering this question rigorously — without simply announcing that everything is cognitive (which would be too permissive) or that only neural systems are cognitive (which would be too restrictive).

## Central Question

**What is the minimal set of properties that distinguishes a cognitive system from a sophisticated reactive mechanism?** The answer requires confronting questions about representation, agency, intentionality, and the evolutionary origins of the distinction between good and bad that underlies all cognitive valence. This chapter examines the leading proposals and evaluates their strengths and limitations.

## Chapter Roadmap

**Section 31.1 — The Floor of Cognition** introduces the major theoretical frameworks for minimal cognition: the "biogenic approach" (Pamela Lyon's argument that if bacteria are not cognitive, no non-human animal is), the minimally cognitive organization (MCO) program (Barandiaran and Moreno's attempt to specify necessary and sufficient conditions for cognition in dynamical systems terms), and the debate about representation — whether the concept of "representation" is necessary for cognition or whether more primitive concepts (sensitivity, affect, valence) can do the same work.

**Section 31.2 — Agency and Intentionality** examines the philosophical concepts of agency and intentionality and their application to biological systems. It traces Brentano's concept of intentionality through contemporary philosophy of mind to its application in theories of minimal cognition. It examines the distinction between derived and intrinsic intentionality and asks whether the distinction can be maintained. And it develops the concept of **functional intentionality** — the pragmatic application of intentional vocabulary to systems that exhibit the right functional profile — as a working framework for basal cognition research.
