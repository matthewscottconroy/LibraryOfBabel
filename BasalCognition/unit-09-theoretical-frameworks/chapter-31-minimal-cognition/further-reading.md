# Further Reading: Chapter 31 — Minimal Cognition Theories

---

**Lyon, P. (2006). The biogenic approach to cognition. *Cognitive Processing*, 7(1), 11–29.**
The foundational paper for the "biogenic" position in minimal cognition research. Lyon's argument — that any principled criterion for animal cognition applies to bacteria — is clearly stated and philosophically careful. The paper provides an excellent entry point to the minimal cognition literature and raises questions that motivate the more technical frameworks developed by Barandiaran, Moreno, and others.

**Barandiaran, X.E., & Moreno, A. (2008). On what makes certain dynamical systems cognitive: A minimally cognitive organization (MCO) program. *Adaptive Behavior*, 16(5), 293–309.**
The most systematic formal attempt to specify necessary and sufficient conditions for minimal cognition. The paper develops the three MCO conditions (self-determination, adaptive self-regulation, sensorimotor coupling) from dynamical systems theory and the autopoietic tradition. Technically demanding but essential for students who want a rigorous criterion rather than an intuitive appeal to biological precedent.

**Bickhard, M.H. (2009). The biological foundations of cognitive science. *New Ideas in Psychology*, 27(1), 75–84.**
A dense but rewarding paper arguing that the foundational concepts of cognitive science — representation, intentionality, normativity — must be grounded in biological organization rather than functional-computational properties. Bickhard's "interactivism" provides a framework for thinking about the evolutionary emergence of cognition without invoking strong mentalistic concepts. Essential for students interested in the philosophical foundations of the minimal cognition thesis.

**Dennett, D.C. (1987). *The Intentional Stance*. MIT Press.**
The foundational statement of the intentional stance — the practice of interpreting systems as rational agents with beliefs and desires when doing so improves predictive accuracy. Dennett's approach provides philosophical grounding for "functional intentionality" and explains how intentional vocabulary can be applied to systems without committing to strong claims about their inner life. A key conceptual resource for students navigating the debates about intentionality in minimal systems.

**Thompson, E. (2007). *Mind in Life: Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of Mind*. Harvard University Press.**
Particularly relevant for this chapter is Thompson's treatment of life and mind, sense-making and valence, and the relationship between autopoietic organization and the origin of normativity. The chapters on life and mind, and on emotion and feeling, provide philosophical depth for the questions raised in Section 31.2 about the relationship between agency, intentionality, and value.

**Searle, J.R. (1992). *The Rediscovery of the Mind*. MIT Press.**
The most influential defense of the derived/intrinsic intentionality distinction and the claim that computational processes cannot have genuine intentionality. Required reading for understanding the philosophical position that the minimal cognition literature is most directly challenging. Whether or not you find Searle's arguments convincing, engaging with them seriously is essential for developing a sophisticated position on biological intentionality.

**Menary, R. (Ed.). (2010). *The Extended Mind*. MIT Press.**
A collection of essays on the extended mind thesis that includes contributions from proponents and critics. Relevant to this chapter for the essays that probe the relationship between cognitive extension and the criteria for what counts as cognitive. Several essays connect extended mind debates to minimal cognition questions about where the boundary of cognitive systems lies.

**Godfrey-Smith, P. (2016). *Other Minds: The Octopus, the Sea, and the Deep Origins of Consciousness*. Farrar, Straus and Giroux.**
While focused primarily on cephalopod intelligence, this beautifully written book by a philosopher of biology engages directly with the question of how minds evolved and what minimal conditions are required for genuine experience. Godfrey-Smith's treatment of the evolutionary origins of subjective experience and his care about distinguishing different levels of cognitive sophistication make this essential reading for anyone interested in the deep questions about minimal cognition.

**Pattee, H.H. (2001). The physics of symbols: Bridging the epistemic cut. *Biosystems*, 60(1–3), 5–21.**
A technical but important paper on the relationship between physical dynamics and symbolic representation in biology. Pattee argues that the origin of life involved the origin of a fundamental distinction between dynamic (non-symbolic) and symbolic (representational) processes — the "epistemic cut." This distinction is relevant to debates about representation in minimal organisms: if the first living systems already crossed the epistemic cut, then some form of representation is present from the very beginning of life.

**Godfrey-Smith, P. (2001). Environmental complexity and the evolution of cognition. In R. Sternberg & J. Kaufman (Eds.), *The Evolution of Intelligence* (pp. 233–249). Lawrence Erlbaum.**
An argument that cognitive complexity co-evolves with environmental complexity: organisms in more complex, variable, and unpredictable environments evolve more sophisticated cognitive machinery. This provides an evolutionary perspective on the minimal cognition question, suggesting that the question is not just "does this system have cognition?" but "what level of cognitive sophistication is adaptive in this environment?"

**Mossio, M., & Moreno, A. (2010). Organisational closure, adaptation and agency. *Adaptive Behavior*, 18(2), 135–150.**
A follow-up to the MCO paper that develops the relationship between organizational closure, adaptive capacity, and agency. The paper examines how the degree of organizational closure relates to the degree of agency, and what further conditions are needed for genuine agency beyond basic self-maintenance. Valuable for students who want to pursue the technical development of the MCO framework.
