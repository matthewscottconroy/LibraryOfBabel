# Key Concepts: Chapter 31 — Minimal Cognition Theories

---

## Minimal Cognition

The theoretical program concerned with identifying the minimum conditions necessary and sufficient for a system to count as cognitive. Minimal cognition research asks: what is the simplest possible cognitive system? What properties distinguish cognition from sophisticated mechanical response? The program explicitly rejects the assumption that cognition requires a nervous system and attempts to develop principled criteria — based on functional organization, dynamics, or behavioral properties — that can be applied across the full range of biological systems, including bacteria, protists, plants, and fungi. The field is genuinely interdisciplinary, drawing on philosophy of mind, cognitive science, theoretical biology, and empirical biology.

---

## The Biogenic Approach

Lyon's (2006) approach to minimal cognition, named to distinguish it from "neurogenic" approaches that restrict cognition to neural systems. The biogenic approach argues that if we attribute cognition to non-human animals on the basis of behavioral and functional criteria (sensitivity to environmental cues, integration of multiple signals, flexible response, goal-directed navigation), then by those same criteria, bacteria must also be cognitive. The approach does not claim that bacteria have rich inner lives comparable to animals, but that the relevant difference is one of degree (cognitive complexity) rather than kind (presence or absence of cognition). Any principled line between cognition and non-cognition, on this view, must apply at or below the bacterial level of organization.

---

## Minimally Cognitive Organization (MCO)

Barandiaran and Moreno's (2008) formal specification of three necessary and sufficient conditions for minimal cognition: (1) **self-determination** — the system's dynamics are determined by its own internal organization, not merely by external inputs; (2) **adaptive self-regulation** — the system modulates its behavior in response to environmental conditions in ways that serve its continued viability; (3) **sensorimotor coupling** — the system's sensory and motor processes are specifically and non-arbitrarily linked such that sensing is directed toward acting and acting modifies what is sensed. The framework is derived from dynamical systems theory and the autopoietic tradition and is intended to provide operationalizable conditions for minimal cognition that rule out thermostats and simple control systems while including bacteria and unicellular organisms.

---

## Intentionality

The property of being "about" something — of being directed toward an object. Brentano identified intentionality as the defining feature of mental phenomena: all mental states have intentional content (beliefs are about things, desires are for things, perceptions are of objects), while physical events are not intrinsically about anything. In philosophy of mind, the challenge is to explain how intentionality can arise in a physical world — how physical systems can have states that are "about" something beyond themselves. For minimal cognition, the challenge is to explain whether and how biological systems without nervous systems can have intentional states. The concept of functional intentionality addresses this by attributing intentional properties pragmatically, without strong metaphysical commitment to "real" intentionality.

---

## Derived vs. Intrinsic Intentionality

Searle's (1992) distinction between intentionality that a system has because an interpreter assigns it (derived intentionality, possessed by books, thermostats, and computers) and intentionality that a system has in and of itself (intrinsic intentionality, claimed to be possessed by conscious minds). The distinction is intended to show that no purely computational system has genuine intentionality, because computational systems' "meanings" are derived from the human minds that design and interpret them. The distinction is philosophically contested: critics argue that even human intentionality is "derived" in some sense (from evolutionary and developmental history) and that there is no principled non-circular way to distinguish derived from intrinsic intentionality. For minimal cognition, the question is whether biological intentionality — the "aboutness" of a receptor binding its ligand, or a bacterium moving toward glucose — is intrinsic or derived.

---

## Functional Intentionality

The pragmatic attribution of intentional properties (beliefs, goals, desires, representations) to a system when doing so provides the best predictive and explanatory framework for understanding its behavior, without commitment to strong metaphysical claims about whether the system "really" has those properties in a full philosophical sense. Functional intentionality is a methodological stance, not a metaphysical one: it recommends using cognitive vocabulary where it is most useful and remaining agnostic about deeper questions. It is related to Dennett's "intentional stance" and provides working basal cognition researchers with a framework for applying cognitive vocabulary to bacteria, plants, and other minimal organisms without either overclaiming (they are fully cognitive in the human sense) or underclaiming (they are merely mechanical).

---

## Valence

The positive/negative character of organism-world interactions: the fact that for any self-maintaining biological system, some outcomes are better (support continued self-maintenance) and others are worse (threaten it). Valence is proposed by enactivists and minimal cognition theorists as more fundamental to cognition than representation: the first cognitive property to emerge with life is not the ability to represent the world but the fact that the world can be better or worse for the organism. Valence structures the organism's behavior — it is drawn toward positive valence states and repelled from negative ones — without requiring any explicit representation of the world. The evolutionary origin of valence is proposed to coincide with the origin of life: the first autopoietic systems were the first systems for which anything had positive or negative significance.

---

## Reactive vs. Anticipatory Systems

A distinction in cognitive science between systems that respond only to current sensory input (reactive) and systems that maintain internal models of future states and adjust behavior accordingly (anticipatory). Reactive systems have no temporal depth — they process the current moment but not past or future. Anticipatory systems have temporal depth — they use the past to predict the future and use those predictions to guide present behavior. The distinction is relevant to minimal cognition because it identifies a threshold in cognitive sophistication: purely reactive systems may be mechanical responses, while anticipatory systems exercise a minimal form of planning or prediction. Bacteria occupy an intermediate position: their behavior is primarily reactive but includes anticipatory components (receptor adaptation computes the gradient rather than the concentration, which is predictive of near-future conditions).

---

## The Floor of Cognition

A metaphor in the minimal cognition literature for the level of biological organization at which cognition begins. Different theoretical frameworks draw the floor at different levels: the biogenic approach puts the floor at the level of bacterial chemotaxis; the MCO program puts it at the first system satisfying all three MCO conditions; IIT puts it at the first system with Φ > 0; enactivism puts it at the first autopoietic system. These different placements of the floor reflect different theoretical commitments about what is essential to cognition — representation, adaptive regulation, information integration, or organizational closure. The question of where the floor lies is both empirical (what do the simplest candidate cognitive systems actually do?) and philosophical (what would it mean for something at that level to be cognitive?).

---

## Neurocentrism

The assumption, critiqued by Lyon and others in the minimal cognition literature, that cognitive science is fundamentally about neural systems and that non-neural systems can at most serve as analogs or models of "real" cognition. Neurocentrism is not merely the empirical claim that neural systems are the most cognitively sophisticated — this is probably true — but the conceptual claim that nervousness is a necessary condition for cognition. The minimal cognition literature argues that neurocentrism is unwarranted: the criteria we use to attribute cognition to non-human animals apply equally to non-neural organisms if those criteria are functional and behavioral rather than structural. Breaking the assumption of neurocentrism is the first step in extending cognitive science to encompass the full range of living systems.
