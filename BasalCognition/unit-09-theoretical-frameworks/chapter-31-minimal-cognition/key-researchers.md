# Key Researchers: Chapter 31 — Minimal Cognition Theories

---

## Pamela Lyon

Pamela Lyon is an Australian cognitive scientist and philosopher affiliated with the University of Adelaide who has been one of the most important advocates for taking bacterial cognition seriously as a scientific and philosophical claim. Her 2006 "biogenic approach" paper established the minimal cognition research program in its contemporary form and has been widely cited across cognitive science, philosophy of biology, and astrobiology.

Lyon's approach is notable for its combination of philosophical rigor and scientific groundedness: she engages seriously with the behavioral and biochemical literature on bacterial cognition while developing precise philosophical arguments about what cognition requires. Her more recent work has focused on the evolution of cognitive capacities across the tree of life, and she has been involved in interdisciplinary projects connecting minimal cognition research to astrobiology (the question of what cognitive capacities we should expect to find in putative extraterrestrial life).

---

## Xabier Barandiaran

Xabier Barandiaran is a Spanish philosopher and cognitive scientist whose work at the intersection of dynamical systems theory, cognitive science, and philosophy of biology has made important contributions to the minimal cognition literature. His MCO program, developed with Alvaro Moreno, represents the most formally rigorous attempt to specify necessary and sufficient conditions for minimal cognition.

Barandiaran's approach is characteristically precise: he uses the mathematical framework of dynamical systems theory to specify what "self-determination," "adaptive self-regulation," and "sensorimotor coupling" mean in terms that can be operationalized and tested. He has also contributed to the development of the "participatory sense-making" framework (with Di Paolo and others), which extends minimal cognition concepts to social interactions. His broader project aims to provide a mathematically and empirically grounded theory of the minimal conditions for agency and cognition.

---

## Alvaro Moreno

Alvaro Moreno is a Spanish philosopher of biology at the University of the Basque Country who has been one of the leading theorists of biological autonomy, organization, and the emergence of cognition. His long collaboration with Mossio on organizational closure and with Barandiaran on minimal cognition has produced some of the most rigorous formal accounts of what distinguishes living and cognitive systems from non-living physical systems.

Moreno's broader work on biological autonomy traces the conditions under which self-maintaining, self-organizing systems can be said to have genuine interests, goals, and cognitive properties. He situates these questions within the philosophy of biology, examining how the emergence of agency relates to the evolution of multicellularity, metabolism, and the nervous system. His approach is unusual in combining formal rigor (mathematical and dynamical systems frameworks) with deep philosophical engagement (autopoiesis, phenomenology, philosophy of science).

## Franz Brentano (1838–1917)

Franz Brentano was an Austrian philosopher and psychologist whose work on intentionality established one of the central concepts of twentieth-century philosophy of mind. A former Catholic priest who left the church over the doctrine of papal infallibility, Brentano founded an empirical approach to philosophy (as opposed to the speculative metaphysics of his contemporaries) that influenced both phenomenology (through Husserl, who was his student) and analytic philosophy of mind.

His contribution of the intentionality concept — the idea that mental phenomena are distinctive in being "about" things, in having intentional objects — set the agenda for a century of debate about the nature of mind and its relationship to physical reality. The question of how intentionality can arise in a physical world — how anything can be "about" anything else — remains one of the deepest puzzles in philosophy of mind, and it is the question that theories of minimal cognition must confront when they attribute intentional properties to bacteria and other non-neural organisms.

## Daniel Dennett (1942–2024)

Daniel Dennett was an American philosopher at Tufts University who was one of the most influential and controversial philosophers of mind of the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries. His "intentional stance" — the practice of predicting and explaining a system's behavior by attributing to it beliefs, desires, and rational agency — provides one of the most widely used philosophical frameworks for thinking about cognitive attribution to non-human systems.

Dennett's position is "heterophenomenological": he takes seriously the self-reports and apparent experiences of agents, including non-human agents, as data to be explained, without committing to any particular view about the metaphysics of consciousness. His work on the evolution of mind (particularly *Darwin's Dangerous Idea*, 1995) provides an evolutionary framework for thinking about how intentionality and agency emerged from purely physical processes, which is relevant to the minimal cognition question.

---

## Mark Bickhard

Mark Bickhard is an American philosopher and psychologist at Lehigh University whose "interactivist" theory of representation and cognition provides an alternative framework to both classical cognitivism and radical enactivism. His argument that intentionality and normativity must be grounded in biological organization — in the functional relationship between organisms and their environments — connects to both the MCO program and the enactivist tradition.

Bickhard's key contribution is the insistence that representation must be "emergent" from organism-environment interaction rather than built into the organism's internal states: representations are, on his view, functional anticipations of future environmental interactions, not static internal pictures of the world. This framework grounds intentionality in the organism's temporal relationship to its environment rather than in any static internal state, providing a naturalistic account of how intentionality can arise in simple biological systems.
