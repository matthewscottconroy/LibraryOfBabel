# Section 31.2: Agency and Intentionality

## Brentano's Intentionality

In 1874, the Austrian philosopher Franz Brentano proposed what he took to be the defining feature of mental phenomena: **intentionality** — the property of being *about* something, of being directed toward an object. Every mental act, Brentano argued, has intentional content: a belief is a belief *that* something is the case; a desire is a desire *for* something; a perception is a perception *of* an object. This "aboutness" or "directedness" is what distinguishes mental from physical phenomena: physical objects are not about anything, while mental states always are (Brentano, 1874/1995).

Brentano's observation captures something deep about the structure of mind. When I think of Paris, the city of Paris is the intentional object of my thought — my thought is directed toward it. But Paris itself is not "about" anything; it is simply there. The intentionality is in the thought, not in the city.

Contemporary philosophy of mind has taken Brentano's concept seriously but has also subjected it to sustained critical analysis. The main questions are: What grounds intentionality? What makes a mental state be *about* something rather than something else? And can intentionality be explained in purely naturalistic, non-mental terms, or does it require some form of irreducible mentality?

For minimal cognition, the crucial question is: can intentionality — in some form — be present in organisms that lack neural systems? Can bacteria or immune cells have intentional states?

## Derived vs. Intrinsic Intentionality

John Searle drew a widely discussed distinction between **derived** and **intrinsic** intentionality (Searle, 1992). Derived intentionality is the kind possessed by artifacts — books, maps, computers, traffic signs. A word on a page is about something, but only because a human mind has assigned that meaning to it: the word's intentionality derives from the intentional states of the humans who created it and use it. Remove the human interpreters, and the physical marks on the page are not about anything; they are just physical marks.

Intrinsic intentionality, by contrast, is the kind that mental states possess in and of themselves, without requiring any external interpreter. My thought about Paris is about Paris not because some third party has interpreted it as such, but because of some intrinsic feature of my mental state that constitutes its directedness toward Paris.

Searle argued that computers have only derived intentionality: a thermostat's "goal" of maintaining temperature is only a goal relative to the interpretation we give it; the physical state of the thermostat is not intrinsically about temperature. His famous Chinese Room argument extends this to any formal system: no amount of syntactic manipulation constitutes genuine semantic content — intentional states — and therefore no computational system has intrinsic intentionality.

### Does the Distinction Hold for Biology?

The derived/intrinsic distinction seems clear enough when applied to artifacts. But it becomes considerably murkier when applied to biological systems, and the murkiness is philosophically important.

Consider a bacterium's chemoreceptor. The receptor molecule binds glucose and changes its conformation, triggering a signaling cascade. Is the receptor's response *about* glucose in the way that my thought is about Paris? Or is it just a physical-chemical interaction, no more intentional than a key turning in a lock?

The natural intuition is that the bacterium's receptor response is "just chemistry" and has no genuine intentionality — that if it is "about" glucose, this is derived from our interpretation of it, not from any intrinsic property of the molecule. But this intuition deserves scrutiny.

The question is: what would be needed for the receptor response to have intrinsic intentionality? If the answer is "a nervous system" or "consciousness," then the argument is circular: we are defining intrinsic intentionality as a property of neural/conscious systems and then observing that biological systems without neural systems lack it. This does not advance the theoretical question.

Bickhard (2009) and others have argued that the derived/intrinsic distinction cannot be maintained as Searle frames it. On Bickhard's view, the intentionality of human mental states is itself a product of the biological and evolutionary history of the nervous system — it is, in a sense, "derived" from the functional organization of the brain. The difference between human intentionality and bacterial chemoreceptor response is not that one is intrinsic and one is derived, but that they are different degrees and kinds of functional organization with different evolutionary histories.

This point undermines Searle's distinction without embracing the opposite extreme — it does not say that thermostats and bacteria have the same intentionality as humans. It says that intentionality comes in degrees, and that what counts as "intrinsic" vs. "derived" depends on what level of functional organization we are analyzing.

## Functional Intentionality

The concept of **functional intentionality** provides a pragmatic framework for attributing intentional properties to biological systems without resolving the deep metaphysical questions about what grounds intentionality.

The idea, developed in different forms by several theorists, is to attribute intentional properties — beliefs, desires, representations, goals — to a system if and when doing so is the best way to predict and explain the system's behavior, regardless of whether the system has genuine phenomenal consciousness or "really" has those mental states in any philosophically loaded sense. This is a version of Daniel Dennett's "intentional stance" (Dennett, 1987): we adopt the intentional stance toward a system when interpreting it as a rational agent with beliefs and desires gives us better predictive leverage than treating it as a purely physical or functional system.

For minimal cognition, functional intentionality suggests the following approach: attribute cognitive vocabulary to biological systems at whatever level of organization it gives maximal predictive and explanatory power. For a bacterium, the most useful level may be the cellular level: describing the bacterium as "seeking" glucose, "avoiding" toxins, and "deciding" when to tumble predicts its behavior better than a description purely in terms of molecular interactions. For a tissue, the most useful level may be the tissue level: the regenerating planarian "wants" to restore its correct anatomy. For an organism, the organismal level captures the relevant goal-directedness.

This pragmatic approach does not commit us to any metaphysical claims about whether bacteria really have beliefs and desires in the philosophical sense. It simply observes that the cognitive vocabulary is the most predictively useful one for certain levels of biological organization, and uses it accordingly.

### Limitations of Functional Intentionality

Functional intentionality is useful but limited. Its main limitation is that it cannot adjudicate between different levels of description: if the cognitive vocabulary is equally predictive at the molecular level (we can describe receptors as "recognizing" glucose), the cellular level, and the tissue level, which level should we use? The framework gives us no principled answer.

A second limitation is that functional intentionality says nothing about the moral status of the systems to which it is applied. If we attribute goal-directedness to a bacterium purely on predictive grounds, this gives us no reason to think that the bacterium has experiences that matter morally. Moral status requires something more — some degree of sentience, perhaps, or the capacity for welfare — that functional intentionality does not itself provide.

For scientific practice, however, the limitations of functional intentionality are manageable. The important point is that the cognitive vocabulary is not being applied to bacteria as a metaphysical claim about their inner life, but as a scientific heuristic for organizing observations and generating hypotheses. Used in this spirit, the intentional vocabulary is a productive tool for basal cognition research.

## Valence and the Evolutionary Origin of Good and Bad

Perhaps the most fundamental question in minimal cognition is: how did the distinction between good and bad arise? How did the universe come to contain systems for which some outcomes are better than others, some states of the world more desirable than others?

Physical systems have no intrinsic valence: it is not better or worse for a stone to be at the top of a hill or the bottom. The stone is indifferent. The distinction between good and bad is, from the perspective of physics, an addition — something that requires explanation.

The standard answer from evolutionary biology is that natural selection created systems with valence by selecting for behaviors that contribute to reproductive fitness. Behaviors that increase the probability of gene propagation came to be reinforced; behaviors that decrease it came to be inhibited. The result is organisms that are drawn toward some states (food, mates, safety) and repelled from others (toxins, predators, damage) — organisms for which the world has a valence structure.

But this answer describes the *mechanism* by which valence was created without explaining why valence exists at all. The question is not "what did evolution select for?" but "why does the fact of being selected for constitute being *good* for the organism?" A randomly evolved bacterium that happens to move toward glucose is moving toward glucose — but is glucose actually *good* for it in any sense beyond the functional?

### Valence and Autopoiesis

The enactivist answer, drawing on Jonas and Thompson, is that valence is constituted by autopoietic organization: the continuation of autopoietic organization is *intrinsically valuable* for the autopoietic system, because the system's activity is organized around its own continuation. Disruption of autopoiesis is bad in the sense that the system *acts to prevent it* — and this acting-to-prevent is the ground of valence.

On this view, the origin of valence coincides with the origin of life. The first autopoietic systems were the first systems for which anything was good or bad. Before life, there was no good or bad — only physics and chemistry. With life came the first instances of valence in the universe: not morally significant valence, but the most primitive proto-valence from which all more sophisticated forms of value emerge through evolution and development.

This is a powerful philosophical position, but it faces the challenge noted in Chapter 27: it risks conflating stability with value. A crystal that resists dissolution has a kind of "stability maintenance," but we don't say that dissolution is bad *for* the crystal. What distinguishes autopoietic stability from crystalline stability in a way that grounds genuine valence?

Thompson's response is that autopoiesis, unlike crystal growth, involves *activity* directed at maintaining its own conditions of possibility. The autopoietic system doesn't merely resist dissolution passively; it actively produces and regenerates the components that maintain its organization. This active self-production introduces a form of normativity — a reference to what *ought* to happen if the system is to continue — that mere physical stability does not have.

### Valence and Representation

The concept of valence has an interesting relationship to the debate about representation. Some theorists argue that the most fundamental form of intentional content is affective: the first thing that any cognitive system "represents" about the world is its valence — whether a situation is good or bad, approach-worthy or avoid-worthy. More sophisticated cognitive content (representations of specific objects, relations, or facts) is built on top of this affective foundation.

Bickhard (2009) develops this line of thinking by arguing that functional emotions — states that encode the positive or negative valence of situations for the organism — are more fundamental than propositional representations. A bacterium that moves toward glucose is not representing glucose as food in any propositional sense, but it is responding to glucose as approach-worthy — it is exhibiting the most primitive form of positive valence. This positive valence is, Bickhard argues, the most basic form of intentional content.

This suggests that the evolutionary origin of valence and the evolutionary origin of intentionality are the same event: with the first organisms that had goals (that were oriented toward their own continuation), there came the first forms of valence and the first forms of intentional directedness. The two properties are inseparable.

## Agency Without Consciousness

One final distinction deserves attention: the distinction between **agency** and **consciousness**. These concepts are often conflated — we typically attribute agency to things we take to be conscious, and we often assume that consciousness is what makes agency genuine. But the minimal cognition literature suggests that this conflation is unwarranted.

Agency, in the sense relevant to minimal cognition, is the capacity to act to maintain oneself and pursue goals, where those goals are constituted by the system's own organizational dynamics. Consciousness, in the phenomenal sense, is the property of there being something it is like to be the system — subjective experience.

These may come apart. A system can be a genuine agent — with real goals, real information integration, real goal-directed behavior — without there being anything it is like to be that system. Whether bacteria, immune cells, or plant signaling networks are phenomenally conscious is an open question that the minimal cognition literature does not settle. What the literature does suggest is that we should not assume consciousness as a prerequisite for agency: we can attribute genuine agency to systems while remaining agnostic about their phenomenal consciousness.

This is the epistemically honest position for the basal cognition researcher. We know enough to say that bacteria, slime molds, plants, and fungi are genuine agents in the minimal sense — they have goals, they integrate information, they act to maintain themselves, they exhibit valence-structured responses to their environments. We do not know whether they are conscious in any phenomenal sense. The science of basal cognition does not require an answer to the consciousness question: it can proceed productively by investigating the functional structure of minimal agency while acknowledging that the deeper question of phenomenal experience remains open.
