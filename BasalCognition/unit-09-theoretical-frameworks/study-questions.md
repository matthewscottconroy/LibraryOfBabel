# Study Questions — Unit IX: Theoretical Frameworks

*These questions engage with the major theoretical frameworks for understanding cognition in non-neural systems: integrated information theory, predictive processing, enactivism, and dynamical systems theory. They require both technical understanding and philosophical evaluation.*

---

## Question 1: Integrated Information Theory and Basal Cognition

Giulio Tononi's integrated information theory (IIT) proposes that consciousness is identical to integrated information, measured by the quantity Φ (phi): the amount of information generated by a system above and beyond the information generated by its parts.

**(a)** IIT assigns a positive Φ value to any system in which the whole generates more information than the sum of its parts. In principle, a bacterium, a biofilm, or a single neuron could have positive Φ. Does this mean they are conscious on IIT's account? What are the implications of this result — does it show that IIT is too permissive, or does it support basal cognition's claims about the widespread distribution of cognitive-like properties?

**(b)** IIT has been criticised for being computationally intractable: computing Φ exactly for systems larger than a few dozen nodes is infeasible. How does this practical limitation affect the theory's usefulness for basal cognition research? Can you imagine an approximate version of Φ that would be useful for comparing the "cognitive integration" of different biological systems?

**(c)** IIT identifies consciousness with information integration, but Unit 9 distinguishes between functional cognition (sensing, integrating, acting) and phenomenal consciousness (subjective experience). A high-Φ system is, on IIT's account, conscious; a low-Φ system that exhibits sophisticated chemotaxis is not. Evaluate the relationship between IIT's Φ-based account and the biogenic functional account of cognition. Are they compatible? Which is more useful for basal cognition research?

## Question 2: The Free Energy Principle and Active Inference

Karl Friston's free energy principle (FEP) proposes that all biological systems act to minimise their variational free energy — a measure of the divergence between a system's internal model of the world and the sensory evidence it receives. On this account, all biological behaviour is a form of inference.

**(a)** Apply the free energy principle to bacterial chemotaxis: a bacterium moves up an attractant gradient. In FEP terms, what is the bacterium's "generative model"? What counts as "prediction error," and how is it minimised? Is this a useful reframing of chemotaxis, or is it an unfalsifiable post-hoc description?

**(b)** The FEP has been criticised for being "panpsychist" in a problematic sense: if all biological systems minimise free energy, and if free energy minimisation is identical to inference, then all biological systems "believe" things. Does this make the FEP too broad to be scientifically useful? How would a defender of FEP respond?

**(c)** Friston (2013) has extended the FEP to multicellular organisms and even to ecosystems, arguing that evolution can be understood as a process of free energy minimisation. Evaluate this extension: is the FEP a genuinely unified theoretical framework, or is it a family of related analogies held together by shared vocabulary?

## Question 3: Enactivism and the Rejection of Representation

Varela, Thompson, and Rosch (1991) proposed enactivism: the view that cognition is not a matter of processing internal representations but of the ongoing sensorimotor coupling between an organism and its environment. The mind is not in the head; it is enacted in the world through bodily engagement.

**(a)** Apply enactivism to plant cognition. A plant's tropisms, systemic signalling, and communication with other plants involve continuous sensorimotor coupling with the environment. Does this make plants cognitively sophisticated on the enactivist account, or does enactivism still presuppose something (e.g., motor control over movement) that plants lack?

**(b)** A central claim of enactivism is that cognition requires "autonomy": the system must be able to generate its own norms of relevance — to determine what counts as a significant environmental feature. Does a bacterium responding to a chemical gradient have this kind of autonomy? What would it look like for a system to *not* have it?

**(c)** Enactivism is sometimes contrasted with "cognitivism" (the view that cognition is essentially computation over internal representations). The book's Unit 9 suggests that these frameworks may be complementary rather than competing, each capturing different aspects of biological cognition. Is this pluralism intellectually honest, or is it an evasion of the real theoretical conflict? Can both frameworks be true, or does accepting one require rejecting the other?

## Question 4: Dynamical Systems and Attractor Landscapes

Kauffman (1993) developed the Boolean network model of gene regulatory networks, in which cell types correspond to attractor states in a high-dimensional state space. This framework has been extended to explain how cells "decide" their fate during development.

**(a)** In the attractor landscape model, cell differentiation is a form of decision-making: the cell follows a trajectory through state space that leads to a stable attractor corresponding to a specific cell type. Is this description of development as "decision-making" genuinely illuminating, or is it a metaphorical extension of the term that obscures more than it reveals?

**(b)** Michael Levin has proposed that bioelectric signalling modifies the "cognitive landscape" of tissues, shifting the attractors available to cells and directing morphogenesis. Evaluate this proposal: what evidence would support it, and what would falsify it? How does it relate to the dynamical systems framework of Kauffman?

**(c)** The concept of "attractor" is used in two very different senses in basal cognition research: (i) as a description of stable gene expression patterns in individual cells, and (ii) as a description of stable behavioural patterns in organisms (e.g., the "foraging" vs. "sheltering" attractors of a bacterium). Are these uses genuinely analogous, or are they homonymous? What would a unified dynamical systems account of cognition across scales look like?

## Question 5: Theoretical Integration and the Future of Basal Cognition

Unit IX presents four major theoretical frameworks (IIT, FEP, enactivism, dynamical systems), none of which is complete and none of which has achieved consensus. This theoretical pluralism reflects the youth and ambition of the field.

**(a)** Identify the single most important point of agreement among IIT, FEP, enactivism, and the dynamical systems approach as applied to basal cognition. What does this shared commitment tell us about the underlying vision of cognition that motivates the field?

**(b)** Identify the single most important point of conflict among these frameworks. Is the conflict empirical (the frameworks make different, testable predictions) or conceptual (the frameworks are built on incompatible assumptions about what cognition is)? If conceptual, can the conflict be resolved, and if so, how?

**(c)** Design a research programme that would meaningfully discriminate among these theoretical frameworks using evidence from basal organisms (bacteria, plants, slime molds). What experiments would you perform? What data would support each framework and what data would count against it? Be as specific as possible about the predicted outcomes.
