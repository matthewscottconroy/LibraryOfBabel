# Further Reading: Chapter 32 — The Body as Mind

---

**Pfeifer, R., & Bongard, J. (2007). *How the Body Shapes the Way We Think: A New View of Intelligence*. MIT Press.**
The foundational text for morphological computation. Pfeifer and Bongard (the same Bongard of the Xenobot project) survey the evidence that body morphology is an integral part of cognitive architecture, with chapters on passive dynamics, material properties, distributed sensing, and the design of intelligent robots. The writing is accessible and the examples are vivid. Essential reading for the chapter's core claims about morphological computation.

**Damasio, A.R. (1994). *Descartes' Error: Emotion, Reason, and the Human Brain*. Putnam.**
The book that established the somatic marker hypothesis and made the case for the body's role in cognition accessible to a wide audience. Damasio's account of patients with ventromedial prefrontal cortex damage — who retain intellectual capabilities while losing the capacity for sound practical judgment — is one of the most compelling arguments in the embodied cognition literature. The title ("Descartes' Error") signals the book's ambition: to overturn the Cartesian separation of mind and body.

**Damasio, A.R. (2010). *Self Comes to Mind: Constructing the Conscious Brain*. Pantheon Books.**
Damasio's more developed account of the relationship between body, brain, and self. He extends the somatic marker hypothesis into a comprehensive theory of consciousness, arguing that the self is constituted by the brain's continuous monitoring of the body's state. More ambitious and more speculative than *Descartes' Error*, but valuable for understanding the full scope of Damasio's theoretical program.

**Furness, J.B. (2006). *The Enteric Nervous System*. Blackwell.**
The definitive scientific treatment of the enteric nervous system by its leading researcher. Comprehensive but accessible, covering the anatomy, neurochemistry, physiology, and clinical significance of the ENS. The discussion of the ENS's capacity for autonomous function — coordinating gastrointestinal motility without brain input — is directly relevant to the somatic cognition thesis.

**Hochner, B. (2012). An embodied view of octopus neurobiology. *Current Biology*, 22(20), R887–R892.**
A review article by the leading researcher on octopus neurobiology, focusing on the relationship between the octopus's distributed neural architecture and its cognitive capacities. Hochner's "embodied view" — that the octopus's intelligence is inseparable from the physical properties of its arms and skin — connects neurobiological research to the embodied cognition program.

**Ingber, D.E. (1998). The architecture of life. *Scientific American*, 278(1), 48–57.**
An accessible introduction to tensegrity in biology, from the scientist who has done most to develop the concept in the cellular and molecular biology context. Ingber argues that cells, extracellular matrix, and the musculoskeletal system all implement tensegrity principles, and that understanding this architecture is key to understanding biological mechanics and mechanotransduction. Good companion reading to the technical papers for students who want a more accessible introduction.

**McGeer, T. (1990). Passive dynamic walking. *International Journal of Robotics Research*, 9(2), 62–82.**
The original paper on passive dynamic walking, which remains one of the most elegant demonstrations of morphological computation. McGeer's analysis of why a two-legged mechanical system without any control can walk stably on a slight slope reveals how much of locomotion is accomplished by the body's physics rather than by neural computation. A classic paper worth reading in full.

**Metzinger, T. (2003). *Being No One: The Self-Model Theory of Subjectivity*. MIT Press.**
A dense but important philosophical account of how the brain constructs a model of the self, drawing on neuroscientific evidence about bodily self-experience, phantom limbs, out-of-body experiences, and related phenomena. Relevant to this chapter for its treatment of how somatic signals contribute to the construction of bodily self-awareness. Not easy reading, but rewarding for students who want to understand the philosophical implications of the somatic cognition thesis.

**Cryan, J.F., & Dinan, T.G. (2012). Mind-altering microorganisms: The impact of the gut microbiota on brain and behaviour. *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, 13(10), 701–712.**
An influential review of the evidence for gut microbiome influence on brain function and behavior. The paper surveys animal studies showing that germ-free animals (which lack gut microbiota) show abnormal anxiety and cognitive behavior, and that specific bacterial strains can alter brain chemistry and behavior through the gut-brain axis. An important empirical foundation for the claim that somatic cognition extends to microbial populations.

**Varela, F.J., Thompson, E., & Rosch, E. (1991). *The Embodied Mind: Cognitive Science and Human Experience*. MIT Press.**
The foundational text of enactivism (discussed in Chapter 27) but also directly relevant here as the original systematic argument that cognitive science must take embodiment seriously. The sections on color perception, visual experience, and sensorimotor contingency make the embodied cognition argument concretely and with philosophical rigor.

**Seth, A.K. (2021). *Being You: A New Science of Consciousness*. Dutton.**
An accessible account of predictive processing accounts of consciousness, with substantial chapters on interoception and the "beast machine" theory of consciousness — the idea that our experience of self and world is fundamentally shaped by the brain's need to regulate the body. The book brings together ideas from Chapters 28 and 32 in a way that is scientifically rigorous and philosophically serious.
