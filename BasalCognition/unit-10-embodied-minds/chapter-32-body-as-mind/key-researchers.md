# Key Researchers: Chapter 32 — The Body as Mind

---

## Antonio Damasio (1944– )

Antonio Damasio is a Portuguese-American neuroscientist at the University of Southern California who has been among the most influential advocates for the embodied nature of cognition and emotion. His somatic marker hypothesis — developed from years of studying patients with brain lesions affecting emotional processing — proposed that bodily states are essential inputs to rational decision-making and that the traditional separation of reason and emotion is scientifically unsupported.

Damasio's work is notable for combining rigorous neuroscience with philosophical ambition. His books — including *Descartes' Error* (1994), *The Feeling of What Happens* (1999), *Looking for Spinoza* (2003), and *Self Comes to Mind* (2010) — have each advanced his theoretical program while remaining accessible to educated non-specialists. His most recent work has focused on the evolution of feelings and the relationship between biological homeostasis and the emergence of subjective experience, connecting to themes in basal cognition.

---

## Rolf Pfeifer (1947– )

Rolf Pfeifer is a Swiss roboticist and cognitive scientist who directed the Artificial Intelligence Laboratory at the University of Zurich for decades. His central intellectual contribution — developed with colleagues including Josh Bongard — is the concept of morphological computation: the idea that the physical body of an intelligent system is itself a computational resource that offloads work from the control system.

Pfeifer's approach to intelligence is resolutely embodied: he has argued that true intelligence cannot be achieved by disembodied computational systems, and that the design of intelligent robots must take the body's physical dynamics seriously as a cognitive resource. His laboratory has produced robots with biologically inspired bodies that exhibit sophisticated behavior through morphological computation, providing existence proofs for the theoretical claims. *How the Body Shapes the Way We Think* (2007) remains the most comprehensive statement of the morphological computation research program.

---

## Benny Hochner (1949– )

Benny Hochner is an Israeli neuroscientist at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem who has been the leading researcher on octopus neurobiology for decades. His work has combined neurophysiology, behavioral experiments, and comparative neuroscience to illuminate how the octopus's unusual distributed nervous system enables its remarkable cognitive capacities.

Hochner's key contribution is the "embodied" view of octopus cognition: the argument that the octopus's intelligence cannot be understood as simply a nervous system computation, because the physical properties of the octopus's arms — their muscular hydrostat structure, their peripheral innervation, their sensory capabilities — are integral parts of the cognitive architecture. He has shown experimentally that arm extension behavior is controlled by the arm's peripheral nervous system, not the central brain, establishing the octopus as the most dramatic example of peripheral morphological computation in neuroscience.

---

## Donald Ingber (1956– )

Donald Ingber is an American physician-scientist at Harvard University whose decades-long program applying tensegrity principles to cell biology has transformed our understanding of how cells sense and respond to mechanical forces. His discovery that the cytoskeleton is a tensegrity structure — and his subsequent development of this insight into a comprehensive framework for mechanobiology — established the field of cellular mechanotransduction on new theoretical foundations.

Ingber's work is relevant to the body-as-mind thesis because tensegrity architecture in cells implies that mechanical information is processed and distributed across the entire cell simultaneously, not localized to specific mechanosensors. This constitutes a form of morphological computation at the cellular level: the cell's physical structure participates in processing mechanical information, not merely in receiving and transmitting it.

---

## John Furness

John Furness is an Australian physiologist at the University of Melbourne who is the leading authority on the enteric nervous system. His decades of research on the anatomy, neurochemistry, and physiology of the ENS have established it as a remarkably autonomous neural system, capable of coordinating complex gut function independently of the central nervous system.

Furness's work has been important in establishing that the ENS is not simply a peripheral relay for the brain's commands but a semi-autonomous neural system with its own sensory, integrative, and motor functions. His textbook *The Enteric Nervous System* (2006) remains the definitive scientific reference and has been important in bringing the ENS's cognitive significance to the attention of cognitive scientists and philosophers of mind.

---

## Tore McGeer (1954–1999)

Tore McGeer was a Canadian engineer who developed the concept of passive dynamic walking and demonstrated it with the construction of simple mechanical systems that can walk naturally without any control system. His elegant 1990 paper in the *International Journal of Robotics Research* introduced passive dynamic walkers to a broad scientific audience and sparked a research program that continues to influence both robotics and the understanding of human locomotion biomechanics.

McGeer's contribution to the body-as-mind thesis is the demonstration that even human-like locomotion — something we typically think of as requiring sophisticated neural control — can be largely achieved through morphological computation. The passive dynamics of a properly designed body do most of the computational work of walking; the brain's contribution is corrections and energy replenishment, not complete trajectory specification. McGeer died tragically young in an accident, but his intellectual legacy has proved enormously productive.
