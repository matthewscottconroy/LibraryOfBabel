# Chapter 33 Exercises: The Extended Mind

---

## Part I: Reflection and Discussion Questions

**1. The Parity Principle Examined**
The parity principle states: if a process would be cognitive if it occurred inside the skull, then the same process occurring outside the skull is also cognitive. Consider the following challenge: not all processes that occur inside the skull are cognitive. Digestion occurs inside the skull (in the brain's blood supply regulation). The autonomic regulation of heart rate occurs partly inside the skull. These are not typically considered cognitive processes. Does the parity principle need to be qualified by a prior account of which internal processes are cognitive? If so, does this qualification undercut the argument — because we need an independent criterion for cognition before the parity principle can do any work?

**2. Otto, Inga, and Epistemic Authority**
Otto's notebook functions as extended memory, but it differs from Inga's internal memory in at least one important way: its contents can be wrong without Otto knowing it — someone could alter the notebook, or Otto could have written incorrect information and forgotten. Internal memories (while fallible) have a kind of first-person epistemic authority that external notebooks lack. Does this difference matter for the extended mind thesis? Does it show that the notebook is not a genuine part of Otto's cognitive system, or merely that extended cognitive systems have different reliability profiles than internal ones?

**3. Niche Construction and Moral Responsibility**
If organisms extend their cognitive systems into the environments they construct, and if we bear moral responsibility for our cognitive actions, do we bear moral responsibility for the cognitive actions of our constructed cognitive niches? Consider: a social media platform that an organization constructs is used by millions of people as an extended cognitive resource. If that platform constructs a cognitive niche that systematically produces misinformation, biased cognition, or addictive behavior, does the organization that built it bear moral responsibility for those cognitive harms? How does niche construction theory help us think about collective cognitive responsibility?

**4. The Bloat Objection and Non-Human Organisms**
The bloat objection to the extended mind thesis holds that if we accept the functional criterion for cognitive extension, virtually everything in an organism's environment will count as part of its cognitive system. For a bacterium embedded in a biofilm, this seems particularly acute: the entire chemical milieu of the biofilm — all the quorum sensing molecules, metabolites, matrix components — would be part of the bacterium's extended cognitive system. Is this an objection to applying the extended mind thesis to basal organisms, or is it a correct and important insight about how distributed basal cognition actually works? What is being lost or distorted by insisting on the individual bacterium as the unit of cognitive analysis?

**5. Extended Minds and Individual Cognitive Identity**
If Otto's mind genuinely extends into his notebook, then when the notebook is destroyed, something that was part of his mind is destroyed. Does this have moral implications? Should notebooks have the same kind of cognitive protection that we afford to neural tissue? More broadly: if minds regularly extend into constructed environments, and if those environments can be destroyed or altered by others, are there new kinds of cognitive harm — harm to extended cognitive systems — that our ethical frameworks need to account for? Consider specific cases: data deletion, cultural destruction, environmental modification.

## Part II: Thought Experiments

**1. The Biofilm Mind**
Imagine a biofilm of 100 million bacteria, each exchanging quorum sensing molecules, sharing nutrients through membrane channels, and coordinating gene expression through chemical signals. Treat this system as a candidate cognitive agent. What are its sensory states? (The chemical concentrations at its various edges.) What are its internal states? (The gene expression profiles of all cells and the concentration of all signaling molecules throughout the biofilm.) What are its active states? (The motility of edge cells, the secretion of biofilm-disrupting enzymes, etc.) Does this system have a Markov blanket? What is its cognitive light cone? Does the extended mind thesis imply that the cognitive system of this biofilm includes the chemical medium surrounding it? Work through these questions systematically and determine what picture of biofilm cognition emerges.

**2. The Forest Network**
A mature temperate forest contains dozens of tree species all connected by mycorrhizal networks through which carbon, phosphorus, and chemical signals are exchanged. The network is not uniform: some "hub" trees are connected to hundreds of others; some edge trees are connected to only a few. If we treat the forest as a candidate cognitive system — using the niche construction and extended mind frameworks — what is the forest sensing? What is it doing with that information? What is its temporal scale of response? And critically: is there a cognitive system here at all, or is this an aggregation of individually cognitive organisms sharing a physical medium? What criteria would you use to decide?

**3. The Cognitive Ecological Experiment**
You are a researcher who has been given the opportunity to modify the cognitive niche of a group of humans across three generations. Your goal is to produce, through niche construction alone (no genetic modification), measurable improvements in a specific cognitive capacity — say, spatial reasoning, or collaborative problem-solving, or emotional regulation. Design the niche you would construct. What artifacts, practices, institutions, and environments would you create? How would you measure the effect across three generations? This thought experiment makes concrete the relationship between cognitive niche construction and the evolution (or development) of cognitive capacities.

## Part III: Laboratory and Computational Investigations

**1. Measuring Extended Cognitive Resources in Ant Colonies**
*Rationale*: Ant pheromone trail systems are external memory systems: they store information about the location of food sources in the physical environment, making that information available to all colony members. Design an experiment to measure the information content of an ant colony's pheromone trail system. Set up an *Atta* or *Linepithema* ant colony in a foraging arena with multiple food sources of different quality and distance. Allow the colony to establish pheromone trails. Then, at various time points, measure: (a) the pheromone concentration on each trail (using chemical analysis or fluorescent analog); (b) the rate of ant traffic on each trail; (c) the information content of the trail system (using information-theoretic measures of how accurately the trail system encodes the quality and location of food sources). How does the information content of the trail system change over time as the colony optimizes its foraging? Does the trail system store more information than any individual ant could store in its nervous system?

**2. Agent-Based Simulation of Cognitive Niche Construction**
*Rationale*: Implement an agent-based model in which individual agents can modify their shared environment in ways that constitute cognitive niche construction. Each agent should have: (a) a simple behavioral rule that responds to environmental signals; (b) the ability to deposit signals in the environment; (c) a learning mechanism that updates behavioral rules based on outcomes. Run the simulation in an environment with patchy, variable resources. Compare the collective foraging performance of: (i) agents who cannot deposit environmental signals; (ii) agents who deposit signals but whose signals decay rapidly; (iii) agents whose signals persist long enough to form stable trail systems. Analyze how the persistence of environmental signals affects collective cognitive performance. Does the group with persistent signals exhibit emergent behaviors that are not predictable from the individual agents' rules?

**3. Plant Volatile Communication and Extended Sensing**
*Rationale*: Design or reproduce an experiment testing whether plant volatile signals extend the effective sensing range of a plant community. Plant groups of *Nicotiana attenuata* or *Arabidopsis thaliana* in a controlled environment. Damage a subset of plants (simulating herbivore attack) and expose undamaged plants to the resulting volatile plume. Measure: (a) the range at which the volatile signal is detectable (using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry); (b) the degree of defensive gene expression upregulation in undamaged plants at different distances from the damaged plants; (c) whether the undamaged plants' resistance to actual herbivore attack is improved by prior volatile exposure. Use these measurements to calculate the "extended sensing radius" of the plant community — how much farther the community can "sense" a herbivore threat than an individual plant could. Does the community-level sensing radius scale with community density, consistent with the prediction that the plant volatile network is a distributed cognitive resource?
