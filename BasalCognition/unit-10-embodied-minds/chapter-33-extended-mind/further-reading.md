# Further Reading: Chapter 33 — The Extended Mind

---

**Clark, A., & Chalmers, D. (1998). The extended mind. *Analysis*, 58(1), 7–19.**
The founding paper, brief and elegant. Before engaging with any of the literature on or about the extended mind thesis, read this paper in full. The Otto and Inga thought experiment, the parity principle, and the conditions for cognitive extension are all set out clearly enough that the paper repays close reading even after extensive engagement with secondary literature.

**Clark, A. (2008). *Supersizing the Mind: Embodiment, Action, and Cognitive Extension*. Oxford University Press.**
Clark's book-length defense and development of the extended mind thesis, with responses to major objections and applications to a wide range of cases including language, culture, emotion, and collective cognition. Clark is always readable and philosophically careful. The appendix, in which Clark responds directly to Adams and Aizawa's criticisms, is particularly valuable for understanding the state of the debate.

**Menary, R. (Ed.). (2010). *The Extended Mind*. MIT Press.**
A collection of essays that maps the philosophical landscape of the extended mind debate, including the founding Clark-Chalmers paper, major objections, and responses. The collection spans positions from strong extension to strong internalism and gives a comprehensive view of the debate as it stood a decade after the founding paper. Essential reference for students who want to understand the philosophical terrain.

**Adams, F., & Aizawa, K. (2008). *The Bounds of Cognition*. Blackwell.**
The most sustained philosophical critique of the extended mind thesis. Adams and Aizawa argue against both the parity principle and the specific conditions Clark and Chalmers propose, defending a view on which cognition is restricted to processes involving intrinsic mental content — which they argue can only occur in biological neural systems. Reading this alongside Clark's responses provides the sharpest possible presentation of the debate.

**Odling-Smee, F.J., Laland, K.N., & Feldman, M.W. (2003). *Niche Construction: The Neglected Process in Evolution*. Princeton University Press.**
The foundational text of niche construction theory. Dense but comprehensive, covering the formal evolutionary theory, extensive empirical examples across the tree of life, and the implications for our understanding of adaptation, heredity, and the relationship between organisms and environments. Essential reading for anyone who wants to understand niche construction beyond the examples provided in this chapter.

**Sterelny, K. (2012). *The Evolved Apprentice: How Evolution Made Humans Unique*. MIT Press.**
Sterelny's account of cognitive niche construction in human evolution. The central concept — that human cognitive development is scaffolded by accumulated cognitive artifacts and practices that preceding generations have constructed — provides an evolutionary framework for understanding how culture and cognition co-evolve. Philosophically sophisticated and empirically grounded, this book is among the best treatments of the relationship between ecology, evolution, and mind.

**Hutchins, E. (1995). *Cognition in the Wild*. MIT Press.**
A detailed ethnographic and cognitive scientific study of ship navigation that demonstrates how complex cognitive tasks are accomplished by distributed cognitive systems spanning people and artifacts. Hutchins's concept of "distributed cognition" provides an alternative to the Clark-Chalmers framework that emphasizes the social and material distribution of cognitive work. Essential for understanding how real distributed cognitive systems operate.

**Turner, J.S. (2000). *The Extended Organism: The Physiology of Animal-Built Structures*. Harvard University Press.**
Turner's argument that the structures organisms build — nests, mounds, webs, burrows — are properly understood as extended organs of the organisms that build them: part of their physiology, not merely products of their behavior. The treatment of termite mound thermoregulation is particularly detailed and illuminating. This book connects niche construction theory to physiology and provides biological grounding for the extended mind thesis applied to non-human organisms.

**Laland, K.N., & Sterelny, K. (2006). Perspective: Seven reasons (not) to neglect niche construction. *Evolution*, 60(9), 1751–1762.**
A clear and persuasive overview of why niche construction theory matters for evolutionary biology, addressing common objections and clarifying misconceptions. A good entry point into the niche construction literature for students with biological backgrounds who want to understand the evolutionary significance of the concept before engaging with its cognitive implications.

**Godfrey-Smith, P. (2001). On the status and explanatory structure of developmental systems theory. In S. Oyama, P.E. Griffiths, & R.D. Gray (Eds.), *Cycles of Contingency: Developmental Systems and Evolution* (pp. 283–297). MIT Press.**
An evaluation of Developmental Systems Theory, which is closely related to niche construction theory and provides philosophical grounding for the claim that organisms and environments are jointly constructed across generations. Relevant for students who want to understand the theoretical foundations of niche construction in the philosophy of biology.

**Chemero, A. (2009). *Radical Embodied Cognitive Science*. MIT Press.**
An alternative to the Clark-Chalmers extended mind framework from a "radical" embodied cognition perspective. Chemero argues that cognition is not extended but dissolved — that the boundaries between organism and environment dissolve when we properly understand cognition as organism-environment dynamics rather than computation inside or outside the skull. A philosophically stimulating challenge to the extended mind framework and to the assumption that cognitive extension is the right metaphor.
