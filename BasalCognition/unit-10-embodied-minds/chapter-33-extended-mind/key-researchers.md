# Key Researchers: Chapter 33 — The Extended Mind

---

## Andy Clark (1957– )

Andy Clark is a Scottish philosopher at the University of Edinburgh who is co-originator of the extended mind hypothesis (with David Chalmers) and has been its primary philosophical developer and defender over the past twenty-five years. Beyond the extended mind, Clark is a leading advocate for the predictive processing framework (discussed in Chapter 28) and for the "4E" approach to cognition generally.

Clark's intellectual trajectory — from connectionist philosophy of mind to embodied cognition to the extended mind to predictive processing — represents a sustained effort to articulate a vision of cognition as fundamentally embedded in the world rather than isolated inside the skull. His books, including *Being There* (1997), *Mindware* (2001), *Natural-Born Cyborgs* (2003), *Supersizing the Mind* (2008), and *Surfing Uncertainty* (2016), are models of accessible philosophical writing on technically demanding topics. His latest theoretical work integrates predictive processing with extended and embedded cognition, arguing that the brain's prediction-based architecture is specifically designed to exploit cognitive extensions.

---

## David Chalmers (1966– )

David Chalmers is an Australian philosopher at New York University who is best known for formulating the "hard problem" of consciousness — the question of why any physical process gives rise to subjective experience — and for advocating a form of property dualism as the most defensible response to it. His collaboration with Clark on the extended mind thesis represents a different side of his intellectual persona: where the hard problem arguments tend toward philosophical conservatism about consciousness, the extended mind thesis is philosophically radical about the boundaries of mind.

Chalmers has also contributed to the extended mind debate through his advocacy of the "extended consciousness" thesis — the claim that if the mind extends into the world, then conscious experience may extend there too. This is a more controversial claim than the cognitive extension thesis, and Chalmers defends it carefully in essays collected in *Philosophy of Mind: Classical and Contemporary Readings* (2002). His most recent major work, *Reality+* (2022), extends these ideas into the philosophy of virtual reality.

## Fred Odling-Smee

Fred Odling-Smee is a British evolutionary biologist who originated niche construction theory in collaboration with Kevin Laland and Marcus Feldman, and is the primary architect of the formal evolutionary framework that underpins it. His early papers in the 1980s proposed that the standard "one-way" view of evolution — organisms are adapted to environments by natural selection — missed the "other way" in which organisms modify their environments and thereby alter selective pressures.

Niche construction theory, as Odling-Smee formulated it, proposes that niche construction is a fundamental evolutionary process with the same theoretical standing as natural selection and genetic inheritance. Organisms don't just adapt to their niches; they construct them, and the constructed niches constitute a form of ecological inheritance that is passed across generations alongside genetic inheritance. This framework has implications for the evolution of cognitive capacities whenever those capacities are shaped by the cognitive niches organisms construct.

---

## Kevin Laland (1962– )

Kevin Laland is a British behavioral and evolutionary biologist at the University of St Andrews who has been central to the development of niche construction theory and the "extended evolutionary synthesis" — a proposed broadening of the modern evolutionary synthesis to include niche construction, developmental plasticity, epigenetic inheritance, and cultural evolution as fundamental evolutionary processes.

Laland's work combines formal theoretical modeling with empirical studies of animal learning, social behavior, and cultural transmission. His books, including *Niche Construction* (with Odling-Smee and Feldman, 2003) and *Darwin's Unfinished Symphony: How Culture Made the Human Mind* (2017), have made niche construction theory accessible to broad audiences while advancing the formal theory. His empirical work on fish learning and primate cultural transmission connects the theoretical framework to experimental biology.

---

## Edwin Hutchins (1948– )

Edwin Hutchins is an American cognitive anthropologist and cognitive scientist at the University of California, San Diego, who pioneered the "distributed cognition" framework through his ethnographic studies of ship navigation. His fieldwork on the navigation of large ships — documenting in granular detail how navigational computations are distributed across navigators, instruments, charts, and communication protocols — provided the first detailed empirical account of a distributed cognitive system.

Hutchins's distributed cognition framework differs from Clark's extended mind thesis in emphasis: where Clark focuses on the functional equivalence of internal and external cognitive resources, Hutchins focuses on the organizational structure of socially and materially distributed cognitive systems. Both frameworks challenge cognitive individualism — the assumption that cognitive processes occur inside individual minds — but they do so in complementary rather than identical ways.

## Scott Turner (1951– )

Scott Turner is an American physiologist at the State University of New York, College of Environmental Science and Forestry, who has studied the physiology of animal-built structures — particularly termite mounds — and developed the provocative argument that these structures are best understood as extended organs of the organisms that build them. His book *The Extended Organism* (2000) applies concepts from organismal physiology (homeostasis, respiration, thermoregulation) to the analysis of termite mounds, wasp nests, and other biological constructions.

Turner's work connects niche construction theory to organismal physiology in a way that grounds the cognitive extension thesis in concrete biological mechanisms: termite mounds maintain stable temperature and gas composition through physical dynamics that are continuous with the thermoregulatory physiology of individual termites. This continuity between organismal physiology and environmental construction provides the strongest available argument that environmental structures can be parts of biological systems rather than merely products of them.
