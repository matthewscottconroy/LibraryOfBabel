# Section 33.1: Clark and Chalmers — The Extended Mind

## The Original Argument

In 1998, Andy Clark and David Chalmers published "The Extended Mind" in the journal *Analysis* (Clark & Chalmers, 1998). It is among the most cited papers in contemporary philosophy and one of the most-discussed in cognitive science. Its core argument is simple enough to state in a paragraph, though its implications ramify in complex directions.

The argument begins with a **functionalist premise**: mental states are defined by their functional roles — by what they do, not by what they are made of. A memory, for instance, is defined by being a stored representation of past experience that can be retrieved and used to guide future behavior. It doesn't matter whether that memory is stored in hippocampal synapses or in a written diary; what matters is the functional role the storage plays.

The argument then offers two thought experiments to test our intuitions about memory:

**Inga** wants to go to the Museum of Modern Art. She thinks about it, recalls that the museum is on 53rd Street, and walks to the museum. Her memory is in her head.

**Otto** has early Alzheimer's disease. He has the same desire to visit the museum, but he cannot rely on his biological memory. He has instead trained himself to consult a notebook he always carries, which contains all the information he would normally store in his head. He opens the notebook, reads that the museum is on 53rd Street, and walks to the museum.

Clark and Chalmers ask: is there a relevant cognitive difference between Inga and Otto? Inga's memory of the museum's location is in her head; Otto's is in his notebook. Both serve the same functional role: storing information that is retrieved and used to guide behavior. If we accept the functionalist premise, it seems that Otto's notebook is performing the same cognitive function as Inga's memory — and therefore that the notebook is, in the relevant sense, part of Otto's cognitive system.

This is the **parity principle**: if a process that is performed in the head is cognitive, then the same process performed outside the head (using external resources that play the same functional role) is also cognitive. What matters is the functional role, not the location.

### The Conditions for Extension

Clark and Chalmers acknowledge that not every environmental resource should count as part of the cognitive system. A book in a library that Otto has never read does not count as part of his cognitive system. They propose three conditions that a resource must meet to count as an extended cognitive resource:

1. **Availability**: The resource must be reliably available when needed.
2. **Automatic endorsement**: The information from the resource must be automatically endorsed — treated as belief — when retrieved, rather than being subject to additional evaluation.
3. **Easy accessibility**: The resource must be easily accessible; it should not be excessively difficult to consult.

These conditions distinguish Otto's notebook (which he carries, consults automatically, and treats as authoritative) from a book in a library (which he does not carry, may not remember to consult, and would need to evaluate independently). Only resources meeting all three conditions count as parts of the extended cognitive system.

## The Parity Principle and Its Logic

The parity principle is the logical core of the extended mind argument, and it is worth examining carefully. The principle says: if a process would be cognitive if it occurred inside the skull, then the same process occurring outside the skull is also cognitive.

The argument from the principle to the conclusion that external resources can be part of cognitive systems runs as follows:

1. Internal processes P (e.g., retrieving a memory from the hippocampus) are cognitive.
2. External processes Q (e.g., retrieving a notation from a notebook) perform the same functional role as P.
3. By the parity principle, Q is also cognitive.
4. Therefore, external resources that implement Q are part of the cognitive system.

This argument is valid if the parity principle is accepted. But the parity principle can be challenged on several grounds.

## Major Objections

### The Bloat Objection

The most immediate objection is the **bloat objection**: if all external resources that play cognitive functional roles are parts of cognitive systems, then cognitive systems will "bloat" to include an unmanageable range of external objects — books, the internet, other people's memories, the built environment. This seems to imply that the cognitive system of a well-networked modern human encompasses virtually all of human culture, which strikes most people as absurd (Adams & Aizawa, 2001).

Clark and Chalmers respond that their conditions for extension — availability, automatic endorsement, and easy accessibility — are sufficiently restrictive to exclude most of the resources the bloat objection worries about. A book in a library is not available at all times, is not automatically endorsed, and is not easily accessible; it therefore does not qualify as part of the extended cognitive system.

Critics respond that the conditions are still too permissive, and that the problem lies deeper: it is not merely that too many things are included, but that the extension principle conflates the resources a cognitive system *uses* with the resources that *constitute* it. A calculator uses electricity, but electricity is not part of the cognitive system. Why should the extended mind case be different?

### The Mark of the Cognitive

A deeper challenge is the problem of the **mark of the cognitive**: what distinguishes cognitive processes from non-cognitive ones? If the only criterion is "performs a function that would be cognitive if performed in the head," then the criterion is circular — it assumes we know which processes are cognitive before we can apply it.

Adams and Aizawa (2001) proposed that the mark of cognitive processes is that they involve intrinsic mental content — representations with original, non-derived intentionality (recall the derived/intrinsic intentionality distinction from Chapter 31). On this view, external resources like notebooks have only derived intentionality — they are about things only because we have assigned them that meaning — and therefore cannot be genuine parts of cognitive systems.

Clark's response is that internal representations also have their intentionality in virtue of their causal roles — there is no principled reason to treat internal and external representations differently. The "intrinsic" versus "derived" distinction is not as robust as it seems once we examine the mechanisms of mental content in brains.

### The Causal-Constitutive Fallacy

A third objection is the **causal-constitutive fallacy** (Rupert, 2004): the extended mind thesis confuses causal contributions to cognition with constitutive parts of cognitive systems. Many things causally influence cognitive processes — nutrition, the built environment, social support — without being parts of those cognitive processes. The fact that Otto's notebook causally influences his behavior does not show that the notebook is a constituent of his cognitive system rather than a tool that his cognitive system uses.

This objection is philosophically forceful. The response requires showing that there is a principled distinction between causal influences and constitutive parts — and that Otto's notebook is on the "constitutive" side of the divide. Clark has argued that the relevant criterion is whether the resource is so tightly coupled to the cognitive system that removing it significantly degrades cognitive function in ways that are not simply the loss of a peripheral tool. Otto without his notebook has significantly impaired cognitive function, not merely missing one tool among many.

## Extended Minds in Non-Human Organisms

The extended mind thesis was developed primarily with human examples in mind, but the concept applies at least as naturally — perhaps more naturally — to non-human organisms.

### Tool Use

Many non-human animals use tools in ways that extend their cognitive capacities. Crows use sticks to extract insects from tree crevices, extending their reach. Dolphins in Shark Bay use sponges to protect their beaks while foraging on the seafloor, extending their tactile sensing into otherwise inaccessible environments. Chimpanzees use stones as hammers and anvils to crack nuts, extending their mechanical leverage. New Caledonian crows not only use tools but manufacture them to specific designs, fabricating hooks from plant material (Weir et al., 2002).

On the extended mind account, these tools are genuine parts of the cognitive systems that use them: they extend the organism's perceptual and action capacities in ways that the parity principle says are cognitive. The crow's stick is doing cognitive work (environmental perception, manipulation planning) that would count as cognitive if it were implemented in the crow's nervous system.

### Chemical Ecology as Extended Cognition

Perhaps the most interesting application of the extended mind thesis to basal organisms involves chemical ecology. Many organisms — bacteria, fungi, plants, social insects — release chemical signals into their environment that modify the behavior of other organisms. These chemical signals constitute a form of distributed information storage and processing that extends beyond the individual organism.

A bacterial biofilm provides a compelling example. Individual bacteria release quorum sensing molecules that accumulate in the biofilm environment. When the concentration of these molecules reaches a threshold, it triggers coordinated behavioral changes across the entire biofilm — gene expression changes, motility changes, production of extracellular matrix. The quorum sensing signal is stored in the environment (as a concentration of molecules) and influences the behavior of all cells that encounter it. Is this environmental chemical store a part of the biofilm's cognitive system?

The extended mind framework says: yes, if the signal meets the conditions for cognitive extension. The signal is available to all cells in the biofilm (availability), it automatically triggers behavioral changes (automatic endorsement), and cells are in continuous contact with it (easy accessibility). The quorum sensing molecule pool is an external resource that performs a cognitive function — coordinating behavior based on past and current information — and on the extended mind account, it should count as part of the biofilm's extended cognitive system.

This is a radical but theoretically coherent conclusion. It implies that the cognitive boundary of a biofilm extends into the surrounding chemical medium, and that the "mind" of the biofilm includes not just the individual cells but the chemical milieu in which they are embedded.

### Plants and Chemical Signaling

Similar reasoning applies to plants. When a plant is attacked by an herbivore and releases volatile organic compounds that warn neighboring plants of the threat, those volatiles are functioning as external information storage — encoding the fact of attack in the environment. If the neighboring plants reliably receive and respond to those signals (altering their chemistry in anticipation of attack), then the chemical signal constitutes an extended cognitive resource: it is available, automatically endorsed (by the receiving plant's signaling system), and easily accessible.

This analysis makes the extended minds of plants look less like a dramatic philosophical conclusion and more like a straightforward description of how plant communication actually works. Plants don't just have minds; they have extended minds, partly implemented in the chemical medium around them.

## A Critical Note on the Extended Mind Thesis

The extended mind thesis is philosophically stimulating and empirically productive. But we should be honest about what it does and does not establish.

It does not establish that external resources are *phenomenally* conscious — that there is something it is like to be a notebook or a quorum sensing molecule pool. It is possible to accept the functional/cognitive extension claim while denying that the extended system has any richer form of mind than the individual cognitive agent at its core.

It also does not establish that extended cognitive systems have the same epistemic authority as internal cognitive systems. Even if Otto's notebook is part of his cognitive system, its contents might be wrong in ways that his internal memories cannot normally be wrong (internal memories are typically self-consistent; notebooks can contain errors).

What the thesis does establish — or at least argue for — is that the appropriate unit of analysis for cognitive science is not the organism alone but the organism-plus-environment system. Cognitive processes, on this view, regularly extend outside the skull. For basal cognition researchers, this implies that studying an organism's cognition in isolation from the chemical, physical, and social environment in which it is embedded will systematically miss important cognitive processes.
