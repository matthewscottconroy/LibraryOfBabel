# Section 33.3: Extended Minds in Non-Human Organisms

## From Thought Experiment to Biological Reality

Clark and Chalmers built their extended mind argument from a thought experiment — Otto and his notebook — precisely because thought experiments are clean. Real biology is messier. But sometimes the messy reality outstrips the philosophical imagination, and I want to suggest that the biology of non-neural organisms presents cases of extended cognition that are not merely analogous to the Otto case, but in important respects more dramatic.

The organisms we have studied throughout this book — slime molds, ants, plants, bacteria — do not use notebooks. They use chemistry, geometry, and physical construction. But the logical structure is the same: information that would otherwise be unavailable to the organism is stored in the environment in a form that the organism can later retrieve and act upon. The environment becomes, functionally, a part of the organism's cognitive apparatus. If that is not extended cognition, it is very difficult to say what it is.

In what follows, we examine three case studies in detail, applying the extended mind framework with care. For each, we ask: what information does the external structure store? How does the organism retrieve it? Does the external storage satisfy the parity principle? And what happens to the organism's cognitive capacity if the external storage is removed?

---

## Case Study One: Slime Mold and External Memory

The most elegant biological instance of extended cognition may be the navigational memory system of *Physarum polycephalum*, the plasmodial slime mold. *Physarum* has no neurons, no brain, no centralized command structure. Yet it solves mazes, optimizes transport networks, and avoids obstacles — and it does so partly by reading the history of its own movements from the environment.

The key observation is this: *Physarum* deposits a layer of extracellular slime as it moves. This slime is not a passive byproduct of locomotion — it is a functional memory trace. When the organism encounters a decision point, it preferentially extends growth away from regions already covered in its own slime. The slime trail is, in effect, a spatial record of where the organism has already been (Reid, Latty, Dussutour, & Beekman, 2012). This externalizes what would otherwise need to be stored internally: a map of explored territory.

Reid et al. (2012) demonstrated this elegantly by placing *Physarum* in a U-shaped trap and then filling the trap with agar — effectively removing the external slime memory while leaving the organism physically intact. The organism's ability to avoid the trap was dramatically impaired. The external slime trace was not merely correlated with navigational competence; it was causally necessary for it.

Apply the parity principle here. If a cognitive process inside an organism stores information about explored spatial territory, retrieves that information at decision points, and uses it to guide behavior — we call that memory. *Physarum*'s slime deposits store information about explored territory; the organism detects and retrieves that information at decision points; the information guides behavior. By parity, the slime trail is memory. It is just memory that happens to be outside the organism's body.

The Adams and Aizawa bloat objection applies here with diminished force. The slime is not merely causally coupled to the organism's behavior — it is produced by the organism, structured by the organism's past trajectory, read by the organism at later decision points, and degraded by the organism's continued movement. It is a highly organism-specific, organism-produced, organism-readable information structure. This is not the road causing the driver's behavior; this is the organism literally writing to and reading from its own external memory.

## Case Study Two: Ant Colonies and Stigmergic Memory

The second case study comes from social insects. An ant colony maintains a sophisticated collective memory of its environment — resource locations, nest structure, trail networks — encoded not in any individual ant's brain but in the colony's collective behavioral patterns and, crucially, in the chemical traces deposited in the environment.

**Stigmergy** — a term introduced by Pierre-Paul Grassé (1959) and later developed as a general principle of collective intelligence — refers to the coordination of collective action through environmental modification. The principle is simple: an agent modifies the environment in a way that stimulates a subsequent modification by the same or another agent. The environment becomes a communication medium.

For ants, pheromone trails are the primary stigmergic medium. When a forager finds food, it deposits a pheromone trail on the return journey. Other foragers, following this trail, reinforce it with their own deposits. Trails to better food sources receive more reinforcement because foragers return from them faster; better trails become more salient chemical signals, attracting more followers and generating more reinforcement. The trail network that emerges is not designed by any individual ant — it is computed by the colony through the medium of the chemical environment.

From the extended mind perspective, the colony's pheromone field is its collective external memory of food source locations and their relative value. The information is not stored in any individual ant's nervous system; it is stored in the spatial distribution of chemical concentrations in the territory. Individual ants access this memory by following gradients; they write to it by depositing pheromones; they erase it by ceasing to reinforce trails that no longer lead to food.

If a laboratory removes all pheromone traces from an ant colony's foraging territory, the colony must re-explore from scratch. Its navigational knowledge is gone — not because any individual ant has lost a memory, but because the external memory system has been erased. This is the extended cognition equivalent of erasing Otto's notebook: the cognitive capacity vanishes not because the organism's internal machinery is damaged, but because the external cognitive scaffold has been removed.

It is worth noting that this is a case of collective extended cognition — no individual ant has the extended mind; the colony does. This is not a deficiency of the framework; it is an extension of it. Clark and Chalmers wrote from the perspective of individual human cognizers, but the parity principle does not restrict extension to individuals. If a collective cognitive process is implemented across an organism and its environment, that cognitive process is extended, whether the organism is a single animal or a social colony.

## Case Study Three: Plants and the Rhizosphere as Extended Sensory System

The third case study is botanically the most complex. Plants cannot move toward resources or away from threats, and their cognitive apparatus must compensate for this constraint through other means. One of the most striking of these is the construction and maintenance of a chemically rich rhizosphere — the zone of soil immediately surrounding the plant's root system — that functions as an extended sensory and regulatory apparatus.

Plants release a substantial fraction of their photosynthetically fixed carbon through root exudates — organic acids, sugars, amino acids, phenolic compounds, and signaling molecules — into the rhizosphere. This is metabolically expensive; estimates suggest that plants release 20 to 40 percent of their fixed carbon this way (Jones, Hodge, & Kuzyakov, 2004). Why? Because the exudates shape the microbial community, the chemical environment, and the nutrient availability in the root zone in ways that serve the plant's informational and physiological needs.

The rhizosphere is not merely a chemical gradient — it is a structured information environment. Mycorrhizal fungi extending through the rhizosphere provide the plant with real-time information about soil nitrogen and phosphorus levels across spatial scales far beyond the reach of root hairs (Bonfante & Genre, 2010). The plant accesses this information through the mycorrhizal network, responding to fungal signaling by adjusting resource allocation and growth direction. The fungal network is, functionally, the plant's extended sensory system — it samples the environment at spatial scales the plant cannot directly access and relays that information to the plant.

The plant also releases volatile organic compounds (VOCs) into the air above ground, some of which serve as signals to neighboring plants about herbivore attack, drought stress, or pathogen presence (Heil & Karban, 2010). These airborne chemical traces constitute a form of extended social cognition: the chemical environment encodes information about threats that individual plants can read, even when they cannot directly perceive those threats. A plant that responds to a neighbor's stress volatiles by upregulating its own defenses before it is attacked is, in effect, reading environmental information about the local threat landscape — information that is stored not in its own tissue but in the shared chemical medium of the air.

These rhizospheric and atmospheric chemical environments are not merely incidental features of the plant's ecology. They are active constructions, maintained at metabolic cost, that extend the plant's sensing and regulatory reach far beyond its physical boundaries. They satisfy the functional criteria for extended cognition: they are produced by the organism, they carry information relevant to the organism's current situation, the organism reads that information and adjusts its behavior accordingly, and their removal degrades the organism's cognitive performance.

## What the Extended Mind Lens Reveals

Applying the extended mind framework to non-neural organisms reveals something important that the original Clark and Chalmers argument, focused on human individuals and their artifacts, did not emphasize: the use of the environment as a cognitive extension is not a distinctively human achievement. It is a pervasive feature of biological cognition.

*Physarum* externalizes navigational memory in extracellular slime. Ant colonies externalize collective knowledge in pheromone trails. Plants extend their sensory reach through fungal networks and chemical signals. Each of these is a case of cognitive extension — not by analogy with human notebook use, but by the same functional criteria that make notebook use an instance of extended cognition.

The organisms studied in this book routinely use the world as part of their cognitive apparatus. They are not, in this respect, cognitively impoverished compared to the human case. They are, in many ways, more thoroughly extended — their cognitive boundaries are more permeable, their reliance on environmental scaffolds more constitutive of their basic cognitive function. Understanding basal cognition requires understanding not just the organism, but the organism-environment system as the unit of cognitive analysis.
