# Section 33.4: The Boundaries of the Cognitive Self

## A Question That Will Not Stay Put

Where does a cognitive agent end and its environment begin? For most of Western philosophy and cognitive science, this has seemed like a question with an obvious answer: the cognitive agent ends at its skin, or more precisely, at its skull. The mind is in the head. The environment is out there. The boundary between them is the organism's physical boundary.

The extended mind thesis challenges this answer. Niche construction extends it further. And when we consider organisms like *Physarum polycephalum*, an ant colony, or a plant embedded in mycorrhizal networks, the apparent obviousness of the skull-bound answer begins to dissolve entirely. These organisms do not have skulls. They often do not have clearly defined individual boundaries at all. And their cognitive processes — to the extent that we can identify them — are distributed across what we would ordinarily call organism and environment, with no clear line between them.

This is not a problem to be solved by drawing a better line. It is a feature to be understood. The question "where does the cognitive agent end?" may have the wrong shape. The better question, as we will argue here, is: at what scale does the cognitive organization we are interested in operate, and what are the relevant causal boundaries at that scale?

---

## The Markov Blanket Formalism

One of the more mathematically sophisticated attempts to address the organism/environment boundary problem comes from the work of Karl Friston on the free energy principle (Friston, 2013). Friston's framework employs a concept from statistical mechanics called the **Markov blanket** — a construct originally due to Pearl (1988) in the context of Bayesian networks.

A Markov blanket is defined as the minimal set of variables that renders a target variable conditionally independent of all other variables in the system. More intuitively: the Markov blanket of a system is the statistical boundary that separates the system's internal states from its external environment. Everything inside the blanket influences and is influenced by the internal states; everything outside the blanket influences the internal states only through the blanket.

For biological organisms, the Markov blanket corresponds, at least approximately, to the cell membrane (for a cell), the skin (for a multicellular organism), or the boundary of a colonial organism (for a colony). The key insight is that Markov blankets are not fixed structural features but emergent statistical regularities. They define themselves by the pattern of causal dependencies, not by physical boundaries.

This has two important implications. First, Markov blankets can be nested: a cell's membrane constitutes a Markov blanket within the blanket of the organ, which sits within the blanket of the organism, which sits within the blanket of the colony. Cognitive organization at each level is separated from its context by its own boundary, maintaining its own internal states. Second, Markov blankets can be extended: if an organism's behavior reliably depends on environmental states — a biofilm matrix, a pheromone field, a mycorrhizal network — then those states may effectively be within the organism's Markov blanket, functioning as quasi-internal states even though they lie outside the organism's body.

Friston's framework does not resolve the extended mind question so much as it formalizes it. The question of where the cognitive agent ends becomes the question of what the relevant Markov blanket includes — and that question has no universal answer. It depends on the timescale of observation, the kind of causal coupling, and the level of organization we are interested in.

## The Holobiont: Rethinking the Individual

The Markov blanket formalism raises a deeper question: what is the biological individual? For most of biology's history, this has seemed obvious — an individual is a single organism, the product of a single genome and a single developmental process. But this picture has become increasingly untenable.

Consider the human body. The human gut contains approximately as many microbial cells as human cells, and these microbes are not passengers — they perform metabolic functions essential to the host's physiology, modulate the immune system, and produce neurotransmitter precursors that influence behavior (Cryan & Dinan, 2012). The term **holobiont** — originally associated with Lynn Margulis's work on symbiosis — describes the composite entity of a host organism and its associated microbial community as a single biological unit (Margulis, 1991). Gilbert, Sapp, and Tauber (2012) developed this concept as a framework for understanding evolution and development, arguing that the holobiont, not the individual organism, is the primary unit of biological selection.

From the perspective of the extended mind, the holobiont presents a striking case. If cognitive processes are defined by their functional role rather than their substrate, and if the microbial community participates in functional processes that contribute to the host's sensing, integration, and action — then the cognitive boundaries of the host extend into the microbiome. The host's "mind," functionally understood, is already distributed across a community of organisms with different genomes and different evolutionary histories.

This is not merely speculative. There is good evidence that the gut microbiome influences behavior in animals through multiple pathways: the gut-brain axis involves vagal afferents, immune signaling, and serotonin production, all of which depend on microbial activity (Cryan & Dinan, 2012). If these pathways are disrupted — by antibiotics, by dysbiosis, by germ-free conditions — behavior changes in measurable ways. The microbiome is, functionally, part of the cognitive system of its host.

For non-human organisms, the situation is often even more thoroughgoing. A termite cannot digest cellulose without its gut symbionts; a legume cannot fix nitrogen without its *Rhizobium* partners; a coral cannot photosynthesize without its dinoflagellate symbionts. In each case, core metabolic and sensory functions are distributed across what we conventionally treat as distinct organisms. The holobiont framework recognizes this distributed organization as the biological reality.

## Dynamic Boundaries and Cognitive Identity

The Markov blanket formalism and the holobiont framework converge on a common insight: biological boundaries are real but permeable, dynamic, and scale-dependent. They are not given by physics or chemistry alone but emerge from the pattern of causal organization that characterizes a living system at a particular scale.

For cognitive science, this has a significant implication: the concept of a cognitive individual needs to be understood in the same way. A cognitive individual is not defined by having a skin or a skull — it is defined by being a node of cognitive organization: a system that senses, integrates, and acts in a coordinated way that maintains its own functional identity. The boundaries of such a system are the boundaries of that coordination — not the physical boundaries of the organism's body.

This means that cognitive individuality is a matter of degree rather than kind. *Physarum* is more or less an individual depending on whether it is in a single plasmodium or has fragmented into multiple pieces, each navigating independently. An ant colony is more or less a cognitive individual depending on whether its pheromone network is intact or disrupted. A plant embedded in a mycorrhizal network is more or less a cognitive individual depending on how tightly integrated its signaling with the fungal network is. In each case, the relevant question is not "is this an individual?" but "at what scale is the cognitive organization occurring, and how integrated is it?"

## The Book's Synthetic Position

The extended mind thesis began with a thought experiment about a man with a notebook. It has led us, through niche construction and the biology of non-neural organisms, to a general reconceptualization of cognitive boundaries.

The book's position, reached through these chapters and the evidence examined throughout, can be stated simply: **cognitive boundaries are real but permeable; the relevant question is not "where does the agent end?" but "at what scale does the cognitive organization we are interested in operate?"**

This position is not instrumentalist — it does not treat cognitive boundaries as merely convenient fictions. There are real facts about where the cognitive organization of a *Physarum* network is centered, about how the ant colony's pheromone field functions as its extended memory, about how the plant's mycorrhizal network extends its sensing reach. These facts are determined by the causal structure of the system, not by our conceptual convenience.

But the position is also not essentialist — it does not assume that there is a single fact of the matter about where cognition ends, independent of the scale and type of cognitive organization being investigated. At the cellular scale, the Markov blanket of a bacterium runs at its membrane. At the colonial scale, the boundary of the biofilm's collective cognition runs somewhere in the diffusion field of its quorum sensing molecules. At the ecological scale, the cognitive organization of a mycorrhizal network extends across multiple plant partners and kilometers of hyphal growth.

None of these facts is more fundamental than the others. They are descriptions at different scales of a reality that, at every scale, exhibits the same basic structure: sensing, integration, and action, organized by boundaries that are real, dynamic, and open to the world.

The cognitive self, it turns out, has always been more permeable than we imagined. Basal cognition research did not invent this permeability — it revealed it.
