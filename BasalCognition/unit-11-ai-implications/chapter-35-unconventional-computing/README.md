# Chapter 35: Unconventional and Biological Computing

## The Computer That Lives

In 1994, Leonard Adleman sat down with a test tube and solved a computational problem. He did not use a silicon chip, an algorithm, or a programming language. He used DNA — single-stranded oligonucleotides, the molecules of heredity — floating in solution, hybridizing according to Watson-Crick base pairing, and collectively exploring a combinatorial search space with a parallelism that no electronic computer of the era could approach. The problem was a small instance of the Hamiltonian path problem — find a route through a set of cities that visits each city exactly once — and Adleman's DNA solution found it (Adleman, 1994). The paper appeared in *Science* and was received with a mixture of genuine scientific excitement and the particular skepticism that follows when someone does something that looks more like performance art than engineering.

Adleman was right, and so were his skeptics. DNA computing is a genuine computational paradigm, with properties — massive parallelism, energy efficiency, information density — that differ fundamentally from silicon computing. It is also, in its current form, slow, error-prone, and difficult to program. Whether it will ever displace silicon for general-purpose computation is doubtful. Whether it will find important specialized applications — in medicine, in bio-integrated devices, in molecular diagnostics — seems increasingly likely.

But Adleman's experiment points toward something more important than any particular application. It demonstrates that *matter itself can compute* — that the physical and chemical processes of biological molecules can be organized to solve computational problems, without any sharp distinction between hardware and software, between substrate and process. Life has been doing this for four billion years. We are very recently beginning to understand how.

## The Central Question

This chapter asks: what forms of computation are possible in biological and chemical substrates, and what can these forms do that silicon cannot — or can do in ways that silicon cannot approach? The question is not merely technological. It is also philosophical: what is computation, if cells, molecules, and chemical reactions can do it? And it is ethical: if we build computers from neurons, from DNA, from genetic circuits — what obligations do we acquire?

## Chapter Roadmap

**Section 35.1 — Wetware** examines DNA and RNA computing, the extraordinary experiment of neuronal computing using cultured brain organoids (including the DishBrain experiments), and the broader project of using biological tissue as a computational substrate. We trace the development from Adleman's foundational experiment to contemporary work that has begun to blur the line between biology and technology in ways that demand both scientific and ethical attention.

**Section 35.2 — Synthetic Biology as Cognitive Engineering** explores the use of genetic circuits — synthetic sequences of DNA that implement logical operations in living cells — as a platform for engineering cellular intelligence. Beginning with the repressilator (Elowitz & Leibler, 2000), we examine how synthetic biologists have built oscillators, toggle switches, logic gates, and more complex genetic programs, and what this tells us about the information-processing capacity of gene regulatory networks. The living Xenobot, assembled from frog cells, makes an appearance here as an existence proof that designed biological systems can exhibit emergent cognitive behaviors.

**Section 35.3 — Chemical Computing** examines computation in chemical systems, from the Belousov-Zhabotinsky reaction — a chemical oscillator that produces spiral waves capable of performing certain computations — to Turing patterns and the theoretical foundations of morphogenetic computation. Chemistry, it turns out, is not merely the substrate of computation; it is itself a form of computation, one that operates in parallel, exploits physical law directly, and generates complexity from simplicity in ways that illuminate both biology and information theory.
