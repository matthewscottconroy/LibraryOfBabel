# Chapter 35: Further Reading

## DNA and Molecular Computing

**Adleman, L. M. (1994). Molecular computation of solutions to combinatorial problems. *Science*, 266(5187), 1021–1024.**
The founding paper of DNA computing — short, clear, and intellectually exciting. Adleman's presentation is accessible to readers without a background in computational complexity, and his discussion of the future potential of molecular computation remains thought-provoking thirty years later.

**Winfree, E. (1998). Algorithmic self-assembly of DNA. Doctoral dissertation, California Institute of Technology.**
Winfree's thesis, available online, develops the theoretical foundations of DNA computing based on tile self-assembly — a general framework for molecular computation that connects to the theory of cellular automata. Dense but rewarding for technically inclined readers.

**Qian, L., & Winfree, E. (2011). Scaling up digital circuit computation with DNA strand displacement cascades. *Science*, 332(6034), 1196–1201.**
The paper that established strand displacement cascades as a practical DNA computing platform, demonstrating a square root computation in purely DNA-based chemistry. A model of clear scientific writing.

## Synthetic Biology

**Elowitz, M. B., & Leibler, S. (2000). A synthetic oscillatory network of transcriptional regulators. *Nature*, 403(6767), 335–338.**
The repressilator paper — the founding experiment of rational synthetic gene circuit design. Read it alongside the toggle switch paper (Gardner et al., 2000) for the full picture of what synthetic biology began with.

**Endy, D. (2005). Foundations for engineering biology. *Nature*, 438(7067), 449–453.**
A clear and accessible statement of the synthetic biology program — the vision of building biological systems from standardized, characterized parts. Raises the right questions about what it would mean to engineer living systems reliably.

**Andrianantoandro, E., Basu, S., Karig, D. K., & Weiss, R. (2006). Synthetic biology: New engineering rules for an emerging discipline. *Molecular Systems Biology*, 2(1), 2006.0028.**
A comprehensive review of the early synthetic biology toolkit and the engineering principles guiding its development. Discusses modularity, abstraction, and standardization as engineering principles applied to biological circuits.

## Organoid and Neuronal Computing

**Kagan, B. J., Kitchen, A. C., Tran, N. T., Habibollahi, F., Khajehnejad, M., Parker, B. J., ... & Razi, A. (2022). In vitro neurons learn and exhibit sentience when embodied in a simulated game-world. *Neuron*, 110(23), 3952–3969.e8.**
The DishBrain paper. Read critically — the claims about sentience are speculative, as the authors themselves acknowledge — but the experimental data are interesting and the interpretive framework (free energy minimization) is worth engaging with seriously.

**Lancaster, M. A., & Knoblich, J. A. (2014). Organogenesis in a dish: Modeling development and disease using organoid technologies. *Science*, 345(6194), 1247125.**
An accessible review of the brain organoid field as it stood in 2014. The technical landscape has advanced considerably since, but this paper gives a clear conceptual foundation.

## Chemical Computing

**Adamatzky, A. (2010). *Physarum Machines: Computers from Slime Mould*. World Scientific.**
Andrew Adamatzky's monograph on computing with slime molds is one of the most unusual books in the scientific literature — a serious scientific work that is also a kind of art. Demonstrates in detail how *Physarum* can solve graph problems, implement logic gates, and approximate transport networks.

**Adamatzky, A., De Lacy Costello, B., & Asai, T. (2005). *Reaction-Diffusion Computers*. Elsevier.**
A technical but readable treatment of the BZ reaction and other excitable chemical media as computing platforms. Includes detailed descriptions of how to implement logic gates and solve problems using chemical waves.

**Turing, A. M. (1952). The chemical basis of morphogenesis. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London, Series B*, 237(641), 37–72.**
Turing's biological masterpiece — a mathematical analysis of how chemical systems can spontaneously generate spatial patterns. Remarkable both for its scientific prescience and as a window into how a great mathematical mind approached a biological problem.

## Xenobots and Designed Living Systems

**Kriegman, S., Blackiston, D., Levin, M., & Bongard, J. (2020). A scalable pipeline for designing reconfigurable organisms. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 117(4), 1853–1859.**
The original xenobot paper. Read the supplementary materials as well as the main text — the design algorithm and the experimental methods are described in more detail there, and both are fascinating.
