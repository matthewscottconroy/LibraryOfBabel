# Chapter 35: Key Researchers

## Leonard Adleman
**Institution:** University of Southern California, Department of Computer Science and Mathematics
**Primary contributions:** Founded DNA computing with the 1994 demonstration of molecular computation of the Hamiltonian path problem; also known for his contributions to public key cryptography (the "A" in RSA). In biology and computing, Adleman showed that molecular chemistry could be organized to solve combinatorial problems with massive parallelism inaccessible to conventional silicon computers.

**Key works:**
- Adleman, L. M. (1994). Molecular computation of solutions to combinatorial problems. *Science*, 266(5187), 1021–1024.
- Adleman, L. M. (1998). Computing with DNA. *Scientific American*, 279(2), 54–61.

**Characterization:** The mathematician who looked at a test tube and saw a computer — and was right enough to found a field.

## Michael Elowitz
**Institution:** Caltech, Division of Biology and Biological Engineering
**Primary contributions:** Co-invented the repressilator with Stanislas Leibler, founding the gene circuit design approach in synthetic biology; subsequently developed approaches for understanding cellular noise and stochastic gene expression. Elowitz's work demonstrated that gene regulatory networks could be rationally designed to implement specific dynamic behaviors.

**Key works:**
- Elowitz, M. B., & Leibler, S. (2000). A synthetic oscillatory network of transcriptional regulators. *Nature*, 403(6767), 335–338.
- Elowitz, M. B., Levine, A. J., Siggia, E. D., & Swain, P. S. (2002). Stochastic gene expression in a single cell. *Science*, 297(5584), 1183–1186.

**Characterization:** The engineer of living clocks, who showed that cells could be programmed like circuits — and then spent a career exploring the noise and variability that makes cellular computation distinctive.

---

## Andrew Adamatzky
**Institution:** University of the West of England, Unconventional Computing Laboratory
**Primary contributions:** The leading researcher in chemical and biological computing using non-silicon substrates, including the Belousov-Zhabotinsky reaction, slime molds (*Physarum polycephalum*), and fungal mycelium. Adamatzky has systematically characterized these systems as computing media, demonstrating logical operations, maze-solving, and network optimization.

**Key works:**
- Adamatzky, A. (2010). *Physarum Machines: Computers from Slime Mould*. World Scientific.
- Adamatzky, A., De Lacy Costello, B., & Asai, T. (2005). *Reaction-Diffusion Computers*. Elsevier.
- Adamatzky, A. (Ed.). (2016). *Advances in Unconventional Computing*. Springer.

**Characterization:** The most industrious explorer of unconventional computing media, building a growing body of evidence that intelligence does not require silicon.

---

## Josh Bongard
**Institution:** University of Vermont, Morphology, Evolution and Cognition Laboratory
**Primary contributions:** Evolutionary robotics, morphogenetic robotics, and the design of xenobots. Bongard's laboratory uses evolutionary algorithms to explore the space of possible body-brain combinations, producing robots and biological machines with emergent behaviors not explicitly designed. The xenobot work represents his most provocative contribution to the field of designed living systems.

**Key works:**
- Kriegman, S., Blackiston, D., Levin, M., & Bongard, J. (2020). A scalable pipeline for designing reconfigurable organisms. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 117(4), 1853–1859.
- Bongard, J., Zykov, V., & Lipson, H. (2006). Resilient machines through continuous self-modeling. *Science*, 314(5802), 1118–1121.

**Characterization:** An evolutionary roboticist who crossed into biology — designing organisms instead of machines, and discovering that the boundary between them is less clear than he expected.

---

## Erik Winfree
**Institution:** Caltech, Division of Engineering and Applied Science
**Primary contributions:** Developed the theory and practice of DNA computing based on tile self-assembly and strand displacement cascades. Winfree's theoretical work established the computational universality of DNA systems; his experimental work produced increasingly sophisticated molecular computing demonstrations, including neural network-like classifiers in DNA.

**Key works:**
- Winfree, E. (1998). Algorithmic self-assembly of DNA. Doctoral dissertation, California Institute of Technology.
- Qian, L., & Winfree, E. (2011). Scaling up digital circuit computation with DNA strand displacement cascades. *Science*, 332(6034), 1196–1201.

**Characterization:** The computer scientist who took DNA seriously as a computational substrate and spent a career developing the tools to program it.

---

## Brett Kagan
**Institution:** Cortical Labs, Melbourne, Australia
**Primary contributions:** Developed the DishBrain system — cultured human neurons interfaced with a game environment — and demonstrated that neurons modify their activity patterns in response to feedback, exhibiting behavioral adaptation. Kagan's work raises substantive questions about the moral status of neuronal computing systems and the definition of sentience.

**Key works:**
- Kagan, B. J., Kitchen, A. C., Tran, N. T., Habibollahi, F., Khajehnejad, M., Parker, B. J., ... & Razi, A. (2022). In vitro neurons learn and exhibit sentience when embodied in a simulated game-world. *Neuron*, 110(23), 3952–3969.e8.

**Characterization:** The neuroscientist who taught neurons to play Pong and was honest enough to ask uncomfortable questions about what that means.
