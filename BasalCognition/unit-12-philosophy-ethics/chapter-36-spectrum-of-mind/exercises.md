# Chapter 36: Exercises

## Part I: Reflection and Discussion

1. **The line-drawing problem.** Identify three candidate thresholds for the presence of mind: (a) a neural complexity threshold, (b) an information integration threshold (e.g., phi > 0 in IIT), and (c) a behavioral flexibility threshold. For each, identify a specific system — biological or artificial — that falls just below the threshold by your definition. Is there a principled argument that this system lacks mind that does not also exclude systems we intuitively take to be minded? What does this exercise reveal about the adequacy of threshold views?

2. **The functional/phenomenal distinction.** The chapter distinguishes between functional criteria for mind (what a system does) and phenomenal criteria (what it is like to be that system). Can these come apart? Is it possible to have rich functional cognition with no phenomenal experience — or rich phenomenal experience with very limited functional cognition? Give examples (real or hypothetical) and discuss whether the distinction is clean or blurry.

3. **The combination problem's force.** Evaluate the combination problem as an objection to panpsychism. Is it a decisive objection, or does it face a corresponding problem on the other side (the emergence problem for non-panpsychist views)? Can you identify any version of panpsychism that dissolves rather than merely deflects the combination problem?

4. **Basal cognition as evidence.** The chapter suggests that basal cognition evidence is at least consistent with panpsychism. Evaluate this claim carefully. What would count as evidence *for* panpsychism, as opposed to evidence for functional cognition without phenomenal experience? Is there any observation we could make of a bacterium or a slime mold that would genuinely differentiate these hypotheses? What does this reveal about the empirical tractability of claims about mind?

5. **Moral implications of graded mind.** If mind is graded, what follows for moral consideration? Consider three positions: (a) only systems above a certain functional threshold deserve moral consideration; (b) all minded systems deserve moral consideration, in proportion to the richness of their minds; (c) moral consideration should be based on something other than mindedness (ecological membership, relational standing, etc.). Evaluate each position against the basal cognition evidence reviewed in this book.

## Part II: Thought Experiments

1. **The phi machine.** Integrated Information Theory assigns consciousness to any system with phi greater than zero. Consider a thermostat: two components (sensor, switch) interacting in a causal loop. IIT implies the thermostat has phi > 0 and therefore some degree of consciousness. Does this conclusion strike you as absurd, as plausible, or as something in between? If absurd, what does this tell you about IIT? If plausible, what implications follow for how we treat thermostats — and, by extension, all simple feedback systems? If in between, what would it take to convince you one way or the other?

2. **The gradual replacement.** Imagine a thought experiment in which you gradually replace the neurons in a human brain, one by one, with functionally equivalent silicon components. At each step, the brain's behavior and information processing remain identical. Does the subject's experience change? If yes: when does the change occur, and what causes it? If no: does this support functionalism (silicon can be conscious if it has the right function) or eliminativism (there was never really phenomenal experience to preserve)? Does the thought experiment tell us anything about panpsychism?

3. **The spectrum and the alien.** Suppose we encounter a genuinely alien form of life — one that evolved independently and is made of entirely different molecules, with a very different architecture of information processing. How would we determine where it falls on the spectrum of mind? What observations would constitute evidence that it is cognitively richer or poorer than *Physarum polycephalum*? What observations would constitute evidence that it has phenomenal experience? This thought experiment reveals the methodological assumptions built into our assessments of mind — make them explicit.

## Part III: Laboratory and Computational Investigations

1. **Compute phi for simple systems.** Using an available software implementation of Integrated Information Theory (several are available as open-source packages), compute phi for a series of systems of increasing complexity: a simple logic gate, a small neural network, a model of the *E. coli* chemotaxis signaling network, and a model cortical circuit. Characterize how phi relates to the number of components, the nature of their interactions, and the complexity of the system's dynamics. Discuss whether phi, as measured, tracks your intuitive judgments about cognitive richness.

2. **Model the combination problem.** Using a mathematical framework of your choice (information theory, dynamical systems, category theory), attempt to formalize what it would mean for micro-experiential states to "combine" into a macro-experiential state. What mathematical operations correspond to experiential combination? What constraints must those operations satisfy to produce a unified macro-experience? Identify where your formalization succeeds and where it breaks down. Write a brief analysis of what this exercise reveals about the difficulty of the combination problem.

3. **Evolutionary analysis of cognitive complexity.** Using published phylogenomic data and behavioral datasets, construct an analysis of how several candidate measures of cognitive complexity (behavioral repertoire size, neural complexity if applicable, information processing capacity in signaling networks) are distributed across the tree of life. Are these measures correlated? Do they change in concert across evolutionary transitions? What does the distribution tell us about whether mind is best understood as a threshold or a continuum property?
