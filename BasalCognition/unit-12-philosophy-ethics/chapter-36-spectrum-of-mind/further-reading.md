# Chapter 36: Further Reading

## Foundational Philosophy of Mind

**Nagel, T. (1974). What is it like to be a bat? *Philosophical Review*, 83(4), 435–450.**
The paper that made the hard problem of consciousness unavoidable. Nagel's argument that subjective experience has a character inaccessible from any third-person perspective is still the clearest statement of why functionalism and physicalism face deep explanatory difficulties. Required reading for anyone thinking seriously about the spectrum of mind.

**Chalmers, D. J. (1995). Facing up to the problem of consciousness. *Journal of Consciousness Studies*, 2(3), 200–219.**
Chalmers's canonical statement of the hard problem. Distinguishes the "easy problems" (explaining cognitive functions) from the hard problem (explaining why those functions are accompanied by experience), and argues that no solution to the easy problems can, by itself, solve the hard problem. The paper that rehabilitated consciousness as a serious topic in analytic philosophy.

**Strawson, G. (2006). Realistic monism: Why physicalism entails panpsychism. *Journal of Consciousness Studies*, 13(10–11), 3–31.**
One of the clearest statements of the positive case for panpsychism from within a physicalist framework. Strawson's argument — that if everything is physical and consciousness exists, then consciousness must be a physical property, and the most parsimonious version of this is panpsychism — is philosophically rigorous and demanding. The responses by seventeen commentators in the same issue are also worth reading.

## Contemporary Panpsychism

**Goff, P. (2019). *Galileo's Error: Foundations for a New Science of Mind*. Pantheon Books.**
The most accessible and well-argued popular presentation of contemporary panpsychism. Goff traces the hard problem to the exclusion of qualitative properties from the scientific worldview since Galileo, and proposes panpsychism as the natural corrective. Well-written and philosophically careful; a good starting point.

**Goff, P. (2017). *Consciousness and Fundamental Reality*. Oxford University Press.**
Goff's academic treatment, more technically rigorous than *Galileo's Error*. Develops the distinction between micropsychism and cosmopsychism, engages seriously with the combination problem, and defends a sophisticated form of panpsychism called constitutive Russellian panpsychism.

**Chalmers, D. J. (2010). *The Character of Consciousness*. Oxford University Press.**
A comprehensive treatment of consciousness from one of the field's most important contributors. Covers the hard problem, the combination problem, panpsychism, and the relationship between phenomenal and functional properties. Dense but rewarding.

## The Spectrum of Mind

**Godfrey-Smith, P. (2016). *Other Minds: The Octopus, the Sea, and the Deep Origins of Consciousness*. Farrar, Straus and Giroux.**
A beautifully written exploration of the evolution of mind through the lens of cephalopod cognition. Godfrey-Smith uses the octopus as a case study for thinking about how minds of radically different architecture might have evolved, and what this tells us about the distribution of experience in the natural world. A model of philosophical biology.

**Godfrey-Smith, P. (2016). Animal evolution and the origins of experience. In T. Metzinger & J. M. Windt (Eds.), *Open MIND*. MIND Group.**
A more technical companion to *Other Minds*, developing the evolutionary argument for a graduated view of experience. Available freely online through the Open MIND collection.

## Information-Theoretic Approaches

**Tononi, G. (2004). An information integration theory of consciousness. *BMC Neuroscience*, 5, 42.**
The founding paper of Integrated Information Theory, presenting the phi measure of consciousness. More accessible than later technical formulations. Essential for understanding the IIT approach to graded consciousness.

**Oizumi, M., Albantakis, L., & Tononi, G. (2014). From the phenomenology to the mechanisms of consciousness: Integrated information theory 3.0. *PLOS Computational Biology*, 10(5), e1003588.**
The current version of IIT, with detailed mathematical development. Technical but important for understanding both the theory's strengths and its controversial implications (including that any system with phi > 0 has some degree of consciousness).

## Ecological and Relational Ethics

**Leopold, A. (1949). *A Sand County Almanac*. Oxford University Press.**
The foundational text of land ethics. Leopold's argument that the moral community should be extended to include soils, waters, plants, and animals — that a thing is right when it tends to preserve the integrity, stability, and beauty of the biotic community — provides an alternative to sentience-based frameworks for considering the moral status of non-neural organisms.

**Callicott, J. B. (1989). *In Defense of the Land Ethic: Essays in Environmental Philosophy*. SUNY Press.**
A rigorous philosophical development of Leopold's land ethics, engaging with utilitarian, deontological, and virtue-based critiques. Essential for thinking about the ethical implications of basal cognition evidence.
