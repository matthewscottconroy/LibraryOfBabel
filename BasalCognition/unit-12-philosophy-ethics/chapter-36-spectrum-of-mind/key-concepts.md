# Chapter 36: Key Concepts

**Hard Problem of Consciousness** — David Chalmers's term for the question of why physical processes are accompanied by subjective experience: why does information processing feel like anything at all? Distinguished from the "easy problems" (explaining cognitive functions such as attention, memory, and behavioral control), which are hard in practice but not in principle. *Why it matters for basal cognition:* The hard problem sets the limits of what cognitive science can establish about the inner lives of non-neural organisms. Even a complete account of the functional cognition of a bacterium leaves open whether there is anything it is like to be that bacterium.

**Panpsychism** — The philosophical view that mentality or experience is a fundamental and pervasive feature of reality — present, in some minimal form, in all physical things — rather than an emergent property of certain complex systems. *Why it matters for basal cognition:* Panpsychism offers one framework for understanding why cognitive function might be accompanied by experience in simple organisms. It is not proven or disproven by basal cognition evidence, but the evidence is consistent with it and inconsistent with views that sharply restrict experience to neural systems of high complexity.

**The Combination Problem** — The challenge, within panpsychist frameworks, of explaining how many micro-experiences combine to form a single unified macro-experience. If electrons have micro-experiences and brains are made of electrons, how do the micro-experiences of electrons become the unified experience of a human? *Why it matters for basal cognition:* The combination problem is a genuine difficulty for panpsychism, but it has a structural parallel in the emergence problem for non-panpsychist views. Understanding the problem clearly is necessary for evaluating the relative merits of panpsychist and emergentist accounts of mind.

**Micropsychism** — The version of panpsychism in which fundamental physical entities (subatomic particles, quantum fields) have micro-experiential properties, and macro-experiences are composed from or constituted by these micro-experiences. Contrasted with cosmopsychism. *Why it matters for basal cognition:* Micropsychism faces the combination problem acutely — it must explain how the micro-experiences of atoms combine into the experience of a bacterial cell, let alone a human. Working through this problem is illuminating about what "combination" of experience could mean.

**Cosmopsychism** — The version of panpsychism in which the universe as a whole has a unified experience as a fundamental feature, and individual experiential states are not assembled from micro-experiences but are "carved from" or constituted by the cosmic experience. *Why it matters for basal cognition:* Cosmopsychism sidesteps the combination problem by making the direction of constitution run from whole to parts rather than parts to whole, but raises its own questions about how the cosmic experience is structured and individuated.

**Integrated Information Theory (IIT)** — Giulio Tononi's theory of consciousness that identifies consciousness with integrated information — a quantity called phi (Φ) measuring the degree to which a system's causal structure exceeds the sum of its parts' causal structures. On IIT, any system with Φ > 0 has some degree of consciousness. *Why it matters for basal cognition:* IIT implies that graded consciousness is present in virtually any interacting physical system, supporting the spectrum-of-mind view. It is highly controversial — both its philosophical foundations and its empirical implications have been challenged — but it has driven productive debate and spawned measurable predictions.

**The Continuum Hypothesis (of mind)** — The claim that mentality — or the properties relevant to ascribing mind — vary continuously across systems rather than being binary (present or absent). Distinguished from the threshold view, which holds that mind appears at a specific level of complexity. *Why it matters for basal cognition:* The continuum hypothesis is the philosophical expression of what basal cognition evidence suggests empirically: that cognitive function is a graded property distributed across the tree of life. If true, it has significant implications for moral consideration and for how we understand the relationship between neural and non-neural intelligence.

**Phenomenal Consciousness** — The subjective, experiential aspect of mental states — "what it is like" to be in a given state (Nagel, 1974). Distinguished from access consciousness (information being available for use in reasoning, reporting, and behavior control) and from functional consciousness broadly. *Why it matters for basal cognition:* Phenomenal consciousness is the hardest dimension to assess in non-neural organisms. Whether bacteria, plants, or slime molds have phenomenal consciousness — whether there is something it is like to be them — is genuinely unknown, and the tools of third-person science cannot currently answer it.

**Russellian Monism** — The view, associated with Bertrand Russell and developed by Chalmers and Goff, that physics describes the causal structure of the world without specifying the intrinsic nature of physical properties, and that phenomenal properties are (or are grounded in) the intrinsic nature of what physics only describes structurally. *Why it matters for basal cognition:* Russellian monism is one of the most philosophically defensible frameworks for panpsychism, and it connects the science of mind to the foundations of physics in a way that is intellectually provocative and not obviously wrong.
