# Section 1: The Continuum Hypothesis

## The Binary Tradition

Western philosophy and science have generally treated mind as a binary property. Either a system has a mind or it does not. Either there is "something it is like" to be that system — some inner experience, some phenomenal character, some subjectivity — or the system is a philosophical zombie, functionally similar to a minded system but "dark inside." Descartes drew the line at rational thought; Kant at the synthetic unity of apperception; many contemporary philosophers at some threshold of neural complexity or global information integration.

This binary tradition has practical appeal. It gives us a clear target for moral consideration — entities with minds, or with morally relevant forms of experience, deserve consideration; entities without minds do not. It avoids the uncomfortable proliferation of moral patients that a graduated view threatens. It coheres with common sense: we do not generally feel that rocks, rivers, or thermostats deserve moral consideration, whatever functional properties they may have.

But the binary tradition has a serious problem: it struggles to identify any non-arbitrary threshold. Neural complexity? At what level of complexity does the switch flip? Information integration? Phi greater than zero, as Tononi's IIT would have it, is present in virtually any interacting physical system. Recursive self-modeling? Present in rudimentary form in many non-neural organisms. Global workspace integration? Requires definition in terms that don't beg the question.

The difficulty is not merely practical — not merely that we haven't found the right threshold yet. It is principled: given the continuous variation in all the candidate properties, there is no natural place to draw a non-arbitrary line. As Thomas Nagel observed, "it seems unlikely that the line is perfectly sharp" (Nagel, 1979, p. 168). And if the line is not perfectly sharp, then the binary view must either accept arbitrariness or acknowledge gradation.

## The Continuum Hypothesis Stated

The continuum hypothesis, as applied to mind, is the claim that mentality — or more carefully, the properties relevant to ascribing mind — vary in degree rather than being present or absent. Different systems have different quantities, or different kinds, or different combinations, of these properties. There is no single threshold below which mind is absent and above which it is present.

This is not a claim that all systems have the same mind, or that the difference between human experience and bacterial sensing is merely one of degree and therefore trivial. It is the claim that the relevant properties form a continuum, and that wherever we draw a line — as we must, for practical purposes — we should acknowledge that the line is a convention, not a discovery.

The properties that might be graded include:

**Behavioral complexity and flexibility.** Systems can respond to their environments with varying degrees of behavioral variety, context-sensitivity, and generalization. *E. coli* responds to eight or nine chemicals along a single behavioral dimension. An octopus responds to hundreds of stimuli across dozens of behavioral dimensions, including novel stimuli encountered for the first time. Humans respond across virtually unlimited behavioral dimensions, including stimuli that have never existed in our evolutionary history. This property varies continuously, with no obvious discontinuity.

**Temporal integration.** Systems integrate information over different temporal scales. A G-protein coupled receptor responds to its ligand on millisecond timescales with no history. *E. coli*'s chemotaxis system integrates chemical concentration over several seconds, implementing a temporal derivative. Learning organisms integrate over minutes, hours, and lifetimes. The depth of temporal integration — the span of experience that currently influences behavior — is a graded property.

**Self-modeling.** Systems can model themselves with varying accuracy and depth. A homeostatic system maintains itself against perturbation without any explicit model of itself. An organism with proprioception has a rudimentary body map. A mirror-self-recognizing animal has a more elaborate self-model. A human with a detailed autobiographical narrative has the most elaborate known self-model. This too is a graded property.

**Valence.** Systems respond to certain states as aversive and certain states as appetitive — they have something functionally analogous to preferences. Whether this functional valence is accompanied by subjective experience is the hard question; but the functional property itself is graded, from the simple approach/avoidance of a bacterium following a chemoattractant gradient to the complex emotional life of a social mammal.

Taking these graded properties seriously, the continuum hypothesis suggests that mind — if that term refers to the whole cluster of properties, rather than to phenomenal consciousness specifically — is distributed along a continuum that includes not just humans and animals but, to varying degrees, all organisms and perhaps some non-biological systems.

## The Problem of Drawing Lines

If the continuum hypothesis is correct, we cannot avoid drawing lines — for scientific, legal, and ethical purposes, we need to determine which systems count as cognitive and which do not, which deserve moral consideration and which do not. But the lines we draw will be conventions rather than discoveries. This is not as troubling as it first sounds: many important distinctions in science and ethics are conventional in this sense. The distinction between childhood and adulthood, between wellness and illness, between a species and a population — these are all continuous phenomena at which we draw conventional lines for practical purposes.

The philosophical danger is not that the lines are conventional but that we mistake conventions for natural facts. If we say "a system is cognitive if and only if it has X neurons organized in Y way," we may be correct that this is a useful operational definition for some research purposes. But we should not mistake the operational definition for a statement about the natural boundaries of cognition itself.

Pamela Lyon (2006) has argued for what she calls a minimal cognition framework: cognition, minimally construed, is adaptive information processing coupled to action in ways that benefit the organism. On this definition, bacteria are cognitive — they do exactly this. But Lyon's framework does not claim to have solved the threshold problem; it reframes it. The question becomes: what additional properties are present in "more cognitive" systems that are absent in minimally cognitive ones? This is a more productive scientific question than "where is the threshold?" because it invites research into mechanisms rather than line-drawing.

An alternative approach, developed by Godfrey-Smith (2016), appeals to the evolutionary history of cognition as a way of grounding claims about degrees of mind. On Godfrey-Smith's view, the evolution of minds — especially the convergent evolution of sophisticated cognition in vertebrates, cephalopods, and social insects — gives us evidence about what kinds of organizational properties are associated with sophisticated cognitive life. Systems that share those organizational properties to higher degrees are, we have inductive reason to believe, more richly minded. This approach does not dissolve the hard problem, but it grounds our attributions of mind in biological evidence rather than in arbitrary philosophical stipulation.

## Functional vs. Phenomenal Criteria

A crucial distinction, easily blurred, is between functional and phenomenal criteria for mind. Functional criteria concern what a system does — its information processing, its behavioral flexibility, its adaptive responses, its temporal integration. Phenomenal criteria concern what it is like to be that system — whether there is any inner experience, any subjectivity, any "light on inside."

The case for graded mind is strongest on functional criteria. The behavioral and information-processing properties described above clearly vary continuously across biological systems, and the evidence from basal cognition research shows that many of these properties are present in organisms we would not traditionally describe as minded.

The case is much harder on phenomenal criteria. Phenomenal consciousness — the subjective quality of experience — is, by its nature, inaccessible from the outside. We cannot directly observe whether there is something it is like to be an *E. coli*, or a slime mold, or a plant. The argument for graded phenomenal mind must be indirect: if phenomenal mind tracks functional properties, and if functional properties are graded, then phenomenal mind is graded. But the premise — that phenomenal mind tracks functional properties — is precisely what the hard problem of consciousness makes questionable.

Thomas Nagel's famous paper "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974) made this point with great force: the subjective character of experience is not capturable by any functional or physical description. Even if we knew everything about the bat's nervous system, we would not know what echolocation experience is like. This asymmetry — between the accessibility of functional properties and the inaccessibility of phenomenal properties — is the deepest difficulty for the continuum hypothesis.

One response is to bite the bullet and embrace graded phenomenal mind — what philosophers call panpsychism (discussed in Section 36.2). Another is to restrict the continuum hypothesis to functional mind and remain agnostic about phenomenal mind: we can say that systems differ in degree of cognitive function without claiming to know anything about their inner experience. This more modest version of the continuum hypothesis is scientifically respectable and philosophically defensible, though it leaves open the moral questions that phenomenal mind would resolve.

## Implications for Moral Consideration

If mind is graded, what follows for ethics? The most natural inference is that moral consideration should be graded as well — that systems with more cognitive function, or more rich phenomenal experience, deserve more moral consideration, and that this consideration extends, in diminishing degrees, to systems at all points on the cognitive continuum.

This view, while natural, is philosophically contested. Peter Singer's utilitarian framework (1975) grounds moral consideration in the capacity for suffering — which requires phenomenal experience, not merely functional cognition. On this view, the moral question about bacteria is not whether they compute but whether they can suffer, and that question remains radically open. A Kantian approach grounds moral consideration in rational autonomy, which sets an even higher bar.

But we can also ask whether the standard frameworks are adequate for the range of systems now under consideration. A framework developed to adjudicate among human claims, and extended to include other sentient animals, may not be well-suited for entities like bacterial colonies, mycorrhizal networks, or slime molds. The ecological ethics tradition — which grounds moral consideration in membership in biotic communities, in systemic relations, in the value of biodiversity and ecological integrity — offers a different framework, one that does not require sentience and that can accommodate the distributed, relational character of biological cognition (Leopold, 1949; Callicott, 1989).

What seems clear is that the threshold view — with its binary distinction between morally considerable and non-considerable systems — is inadequate if the underlying reality is continuous. We need ethical frameworks adequate to a world of graded cognition. Developing those frameworks is one of the most important philosophical tasks facing the field of basal cognition, and the following two chapters will continue the work.
