# Section 2: Panpsychism Revisited

## The Return of an Old Idea

Panpsychism — the view that mentality or experience is a fundamental and pervasive feature of reality, not an emergent property of certain complex systems — is among the oldest philosophical positions in the Western tradition. Thales attributed a kind of soul to magnets; Spinoza's single substance has attributes including thought; Leibniz's monads each have their own perspective on the universe. By the early twentieth century, the physicist and philosopher Alfred North Whitehead had developed a sophisticated panpsychist metaphysics in which "experience" in some minimal sense is a feature of every actual entity in nature (Whitehead, 1929).

For most of the twentieth century, panpsychism was out of fashion. The rise of scientific materialism, logical positivism, and the computational theory of mind pushed it to the philosophical margins. A respectable philosopher or scientist could not take it seriously without incurring professional risk. The prevailing assumption was that consciousness was either a product of neural computation (functionalism) or somehow nothing more than the brain's information processing (eliminativism) — in either case, a phenomenon restricted to systems with the right kind of neural organization.

This consensus has been under pressure for some decades, and the pressure is now difficult to ignore. David Chalmers's articulation of the "hard problem of consciousness" (1995) — the problem of explaining why any physical process is accompanied by subjective experience — showed convincingly that standard functionalist and materialist accounts of mind leave something crucial unexplained. If physical processes give rise to consciousness, what is the mechanism of that arising? No satisfactory answer has been offered. And if no account of how consciousness emerges from non-conscious matter is forthcoming, one natural response is to question whether consciousness ever needed to emerge — whether it has always been there, in some form, in the structure of reality.

This is the intellectual situation that has generated the current, scientifically informed rehabilitation of panpsychism, represented in accessible form by Philip Goff's *Galileo's Error* (2019) and in more technical form by Galen Strawson (2006) and David Chalmers himself (2010).

## The Core Argument for Panpsychism

The core argument for panpsychism is a form of reductio: the alternative to panpsychism — consciousness emerging from non-conscious matter — faces what appears to be a principled explanatory gap. How does the felt quality of a color, the painfulness of pain, the very existence of subjective experience, arise from electrochemical processes that are, in themselves, presumably not experienced by anyone?

Goff (2019) traces this problem to what he calls "Galileo's error" — the decision, made at the dawn of modern science, to exclude qualitative properties (what things look, feel, sound like) from the domain of objective scientific description. Galileo was right that this exclusion was methodologically productive for physics. But the excluded qualities did not disappear; they remained in experience. The hard problem of consciousness is, on this account, the consequence of two centuries of physics succeeding without explaining the subjective aspect of reality — not because the subjective aspect is supernatural, but because it was deliberately excluded from the scientific description from the start.

Strawson (2006) makes the argument more sharply: if physicalism (the view that everything is physical) is true, and consciousness exists (which it obviously does — we have undeniable first-person access to it), then consciousness must be physical. But if consciousness is physical, and if physical things can have phenomenal properties, then phenomenal properties must be a genuine feature of the physical world — not restricted to brains, but potentially present wherever matter is organized in the right ways. Since the organization of matter is a continuous variable, and since there is no obvious threshold at which phenomenal properties suddenly appear from their absence, the most parsimonious view is that phenomenal properties are fundamental and pervasive.

This argument, while not conclusive, establishes a significant burden of proof for the opposition. The objections to panpsychism are real; they do not dissolve this argument by themselves.

## The Combination Problem

The most serious objection to panpsychism is the combination problem: how do many micro-experiences combine to produce a single, unified macro-experience? If electrons and quarks each have micro-experiences — some minimal "feel" to their existence — how do the micro-experiences of the 10²⁷ particles in a human brain combine to produce the singular, unified experience of seeing red, or feeling pain, or reading these words?

The problem is serious because we have no satisfactory account of combination. It is not enough to say that micro-experiences "add up" — addition is a mathematical operation and does not obviously apply to experiential properties. It is not enough to say that they "interact" — any two physical systems interact, but not any interaction produces unified experience. The combination problem is, in some respects, as hard as the original emergence problem it was supposed to solve.

Philosophers have responded in various ways. Some distinguish between micropsychism (the view that fundamental physical entities have micro-experiences) and cosmopsychism (the view that the universe as a whole has a unified experience that is the ground of all individual experiences, rather than the other way around). On the cosmopsychist view, there is no combination problem: the universe's experience is primary, and individual experiences are "carved out" of it rather than assembled from parts (Goff, 2017). This is metaphysically exotic — it inverts our normal assumptions about part-whole relations — but it avoids the combination problem by denying that individual experiences need to be assembled from micro-experiential parts.

Others have proposed that the unity of experience does not require all parts to contribute equally. On constitutive panpsychism, the integrated, unified experience of a brain is constituted by the experiences of its neural components in the same way that the neural dynamics constitute the information processing. The unity is a property of the whole configuration, not a sum of parts. This does not fully dissolve the combination problem, but it suggests that it may be a technical problem about the metaphysics of constitution rather than a fundamental obstacle to the panpsychist program.

The combination problem is genuinely hard. Acknowledging this is important for intellectual honesty. What is equally important is acknowledging that the standard alternative — consciousness emerging from non-conscious matter — has its own version of the problem: the emergence problem. Why should any physical process give rise to experience, rather than proceeding "in the dark"? Both panpsychism and emergentism face a deep explanatory challenge. Neither has fully met it.

## Panpsychism and Biological Science

What is the relationship between panpsychism as a philosophical position and the empirical science of basal cognition? There are two distinct ways to connect them.

The first connection is evidential. If plants, bacteria, and slime molds exhibit cognitive behaviors that suggest some degree of minded engagement with their environments — if these systems genuinely sense, integrate information, remember, and act adaptively in ways that parallel what we observe in minded animals — then this evidence is at least consistent with the panpsychist view that mentality extends throughout the biological world. The evidence does not prove panpsychism (it is also consistent with sophisticated zombiehood), but it undermines one common argument against it: the argument that attributing any form of experience to non-neural organisms is absurd because they lack the neural architecture associated with mind.

The second connection is theoretical. Several theoretical frameworks for consciousness — most prominently Tononi's Integrated Information Theory (Tononi, 2004) and Friston's Free Energy Principle (Friston, 2010) — have implications that extend mentality to a wide range of biological systems. IIT, in particular, assigns a measure of consciousness (phi) to any system with the right causal structure, and phi greater than zero is present in virtually any interacting physical system. Tononi himself has pointed out that this means even simple biological systems have some degree of consciousness on IIT — though an extremely small degree. Whether IIT is correct is deeply contested; but if it is, panpsychism follows for biological systems.

The scientific program of basal cognition is not committed to panpsychism — one can believe that bacteria are cognitively active without believing that they have subjective experience. But the program is in genuine tension with the view that consciousness is sharply restricted to vertebrates or to mammals. The evidence that cognitive function is pervasive and continuous across the tree of life at least raises the question of whether phenomenal experience might be similarly distributed.

## The Scientific Program Panpsychism Invites

If panpsychism, or something in its neighborhood, is correct, what does a scientific research program look like? Godfrey-Smith (2016) suggests that evolution provides the right lens: the evolution of experience, like the evolution of cognition, can be traced as a biological history. Some organisms have more sophisticated experiential lives than others; the question is how and when greater experiential richness evolved. This research program does not require certainty about what experience is, only that it varies in degree — an assumption that is consistent with common sense even for non-panpsychists.

Levin and colleagues have proposed that consciousness and goal-directedness may be fundamental to matter at some level of description, and that what we call cognitive evolution is a process by which these fundamental properties are organized into more sophisticated, more unified forms at higher levels of biological organization (Levin, 2019). This is not straightforwardly panpsychist — it is more like a functionalist view with a very broad functional base — but it takes seriously the possibility that what we observe in basal organisms is not merely a pale shadow of human cognition, but an expression of something genuine and continuous with it.

The honest position, for a working scientist, is probably this: we do not know whether non-neural organisms have phenomenal experience. The evidence of basal cognition research establishes that they have cognitive function; it does not establish that they have inner experience. Panpsychism offers one way to explain why they might; it also faces serious philosophical difficulties. Keeping the hypothesis on the table, treating it with philosophical rigor rather than dismissal or credulity, and designing experiments that might differentiate it from alternatives is the appropriate scientific response. This is a field at the frontier of inquiry, and the frontier is genuinely open.
