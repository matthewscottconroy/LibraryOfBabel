# Chapter 37: The Ethics of Non-Neural Organisms

## The Uncomfortable Question

You are reading this book, which means you are probably a mammal with a well-developed neocortex, a large behavioral repertoire, and a rich phenomenal life. You are almost certainly capable of suffering in ways that matter morally — you know what pain feels like, and you know that pain is bad. Your moral intuitions have been calibrated, over a lifetime, against organisms like yourself.

Now consider what this book has argued. Bacteria communicate, make collective decisions, learn from experience in limited ways, and respond to their chemical environments with a sophistication that parallels, at the molecular level, what neurons do in nervous systems. Plants emit volatile chemicals that convey information to their neighbors, can be habituated to repeated harmless stimuli, and maintain a distributed electrical signaling network that propagates through their tissues at speeds comparable to the slow neural signals of simple animals. Slime molds solve problems. Mycorrhizal networks transport information and resources across forest floors in ways that shape the behavior of many plants simultaneously.

None of this is sufficient to establish that these organisms can suffer. But it is sufficient to establish that our intuitive framework — in which cognition, experience, and moral consideration cluster tightly together in neural systems and are absent from everything else — does not accurately describe the biological world.

This chapter does not resolve these questions. They are genuinely hard, and intellectual honesty requires acknowledging their difficulty. What this chapter does is lay out the strongest available frameworks for thinking about the moral status of non-neural organisms, examine the practical implications of taking these frameworks seriously, and ask what obligations — if any — the evidence of basal cognition places on us.

## The Central Question

**If cognition is present to some degree in bacteria, plants, fungi, and other non-neural organisms, does this generate any moral obligations toward them?** And if so, what would those obligations look like in practice — in agriculture, in medicine, in ecology, in the design of synthetic biological systems?

## Chapter Roadmap

**Section 37.1 — Moral Consideration Without Neurons** examines the standard framework for moral consideration — sentience as the relevant criterion — and asks what happens when we take seriously the possibility that sentience, or something morally relevant in its vicinity, extends to non-neural organisms. We consider plant ethics, microbial ethics, and fungal ethics as distinct cases with distinct practical implications.

**Section 37.2 — Agricultural and Environmental Implications** draws out the practical consequences of extended moral consideration for specific human practices: industrial agriculture, soil management, forest management, and invasive species control. The goal is not to generate paralyzing conclusions but to identify which practices deserve ethical scrutiny in light of what we now know about non-neural cognition.

**Section 37.3 — Synthetic Biology Ethics** examines the obligations we incur when we design and create cognitive systems — from xenobots to brain organoids — and the regulatory frameworks that do and do not exist to govern this research.
