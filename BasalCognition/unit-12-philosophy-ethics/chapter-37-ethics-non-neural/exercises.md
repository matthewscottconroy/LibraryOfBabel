# Chapter 37: Exercises

## Part I: Reflection and Discussion

1. **The sentience criterion.** Peter Singer's framework grounds moral consideration in the capacity to suffer. Evaluate this criterion in light of the evidence for basal cognition. Is suffering a sufficient criterion for moral consideration? Is it necessary? Can you identify cases in which the sentience criterion gives counterintuitive results in either direction — systems with no apparent sentience that we feel deserve consideration, or systems with apparent sentience that we nonetheless treat as morally unimportant?

2. **The interests question.** Matthew Hall argues that plants have interests — in growth, reproduction, and appropriate environmental conditions — that are morally significant even in the absence of sentience. What is an interest, precisely? Does having an interest require having any form of experience? Can a bacterium have an interest? Can a gene circuit? Identify the conceptual boundaries of interest-attribution and assess whether the biocentric framework (all living things have morally relevant interests) is too broad, too narrow, or approximately right.

3. **Proportionality in practice.** The section argues that the harm we cause to cognitively active organisms should be proportionate to the benefit obtained. Apply this principle to three specific cases: (a) clear-cutting an old-growth forest for timber; (b) applying broad-spectrum herbicides to a suburban lawn; (c) antibiotic treatment of bacterial sepsis in an immunocompromised patient. For each, characterize the harm, the benefit, and the proportionality. Are your conclusions consistent with each other? With standard ethical practice?

4. **The organoid ethics frontier.** A brain organoid grows, over six months, from a small mass of undifferentiated stem cells into a complex structure exhibiting coordinated electrical activity patterns, the rudiments of cortical layering, and spontaneous rhythmic oscillations. At what point, if any, does this system acquire moral status? What criteria are you using? What experiments or observations would change your assessment?

5. **Creator responsibility.** The chapter argues that creating a cognitive system generates creator responsibility that is distinct from the responsibility of harming a naturally occurring cognitive system. Do you find this argument compelling? Consider: do laboratory animal breeders bear greater moral responsibility for the welfare of animals they breed than researchers who use animals sourced from the wild? Does the source of an organism's existence affect the obligations its creators or users bear toward it?

## Part II: Thought Experiments

1. **The plant patient.** A hospital patient is dying of a systemic infection caused by a bacterium. The treatment requires antibiotics that will kill many billions of bacteria. The patient's family insists that their loved one be treated. The hospital ethicist raises the question of whether the bacteria's interests matter. Walk through this dilemma from the perspective of three frameworks: (a) sentience-based ethics; (b) biocentric ethics; (c) ecological ethics. What does each framework say? Which, if any, generates the conclusion that treatment is ethically problematic? Is there a way to honor whatever obligations we have to the bacteria without failing the patient?

2. **The xenobot release.** A synthetic biology company has designed a xenobot — a small mass of frog cells assembled according to an evolutionary algorithm — that is remarkably effective at breaking down microplastic pollution in marine environments. The company wants to release billions of these xenobots into the ocean, where they will move through the water, consume microplastics, eventually break down naturally, and (based on laboratory observations) self-replicate from free-floating frog cells in the environment. Identify every moral consideration that bears on this decision. Who has moral standing in this situation? What regulatory framework should govern the decision?

3. **The silent forest.** Imagine an agricultural policy that completely eliminates all chemical inputs from farming — no pesticides, no herbicides, no synthetic fertilizers — replacing them with intensive crop rotation, biological pest control, and ecological intensification. The policy would dramatically reduce agricultural harm to soil microbiomes, plant communities, and mycorrhizal networks. It would also reduce crop yields significantly in the short term, causing food price increases that disproportionately affect the poorest communities. Work through the ethical trade-offs of this policy from the perspective of each framework discussed in the chapter. Which framework do you find most helpful for navigating this trade-off, and why?

## Part III: Laboratory and Computational Investigations

1. **Soil microbiome comparison.** Using publicly available 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing datasets (available through the NCBI Sequence Read Archive), compare the bacterial diversity of soil samples from conventionally managed agricultural land, organically managed agricultural land, and undisturbed native grassland or forest. Calculate alpha diversity metrics (Shannon index, observed species richness) and beta diversity (Bray-Curtis dissimilarity) for each management type. Discuss whether diversity differences reflect differences in the cognitive community's composition, and what the ethical implications of these differences might be under the frameworks discussed in this chapter.

2. **Animal welfare guidelines analysis.** Obtain the animal welfare guidelines from two or more national or international research ethics bodies (e.g., the US NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals; the UK Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986; the European Directive 2010/63/EU). Analyze how each guideline defines the relevant criterion for moral consideration (sentience, pain experience, etc.), which taxa it covers, and how it handles taxa at the boundary of coverage (e.g., invertebrates). Evaluate whether any of these guidelines could be extended to cover cognitively active non-neural organisms, and what modifications would be needed.

3. **Case study: Neonics and bee cognition.** Neonicotinoid pesticides — widely used insecticides — have been shown to impair the navigation, foraging, and communication behaviors of honeybees at sublethal concentrations (Gill et al., 2012). Honeybees are invertebrates with sophisticated collective cognition (see Chapter 23). Research the evidence for neonicotinoid impacts on bee cognition, the ecological impacts of bee population decline, and the regulatory response in your jurisdiction. Write a 2000-word analysis of the ethical issues this case raises, drawing on the frameworks discussed in this chapter. At what point should cognitive impairment of a non-neural organism constitute sufficient grounds for regulatory intervention?
