# Chapter 37: Key Concepts

**Biocentric Ethics** — An ethical framework that holds that all living organisms, by virtue of being teleological centers of life with their own good, have inherent worth that generates obligations on moral agents (Taylor, 1986). Distinguished from sentience-based ethics (which restricts moral status to organisms capable of experience) and from ecological ethics (which grounds moral status in community membership). *Why it matters for basal cognition:* Biocentric ethics provides a framework for moral consideration of bacteria, plants, and fungi that does not require establishing sentience — only that they are living things with interests in flourishing.

**Sentience-Based Ethics** — The framework, associated with Peter Singer and utilitarian animal ethics, that grounds moral consideration in the capacity for sentience — the ability to have subjective experiences, particularly to suffer and to feel pleasure. On this view, the morally relevant question about any organism is "Can it suffer?" not "Can it reason?" or "What species is it?" *Why it matters for basal cognition:* The sentience framework is the dominant approach to animal ethics, but it faces genuine difficulty when applied to non-neural organisms whose cognitive function is real but whose phenomenal experience is uncertain. Whether and how to extend the framework is one of the central questions of this chapter.

**Land Ethics** — Aldo Leopold's principle that the moral community should extend to include soils, waters, plants, and animals, and that right action tends to preserve the integrity, stability, and beauty of the biotic community. Distinguished by its emphasis on ecological membership and community relationships rather than individual sentience or welfare. *Why it matters for basal cognition:* Land ethics provides an ecological framework for moral consideration of non-neural organisms — one that grounds obligations in systemic relationships rather than individual experience, and that accommodates the distributed, collective forms of cognition characteristic of soil microbiomes and mycorrhizal networks.

**Interests Without Subjects** — The philosophical concept that organisms can have interests — can be better or worse off, can flourish or be harmed — without necessarily having phenomenal experience or subjective states. Associated with biocentric frameworks and "objective list" theories of welfare. *Why it matters for basal cognition:* If plants and bacteria can have interests without subjects — if they can be harmed in morally relevant ways even in the absence of sentience — then the case for extending moral consideration to them does not depend on resolving the hard problem of consciousness.

**Moral Consideration** — The status of being taken into account in ethical deliberation — of being a being whose interests, welfare, or rights generate obligations on moral agents. Distinguished from full moral agency (being responsible for one's own actions) and from moral patiency (the full range of moral protections owed to persons). *Why it matters for basal cognition:* The question of whether non-neural organisms deserve moral consideration is one of the central questions this chapter addresses. Different ethical frameworks give different answers; understanding the criteria each framework uses is necessary for evaluating the answers.

**Epistemic Humility (Moral)** — The acknowledgment, in ethical reasoning, that our uncertainty about the cognitive and experiential properties of non-neural organisms should modulate rather than eliminate our moral concern for them. Applied, it suggests extending provisional moral consideration to organisms whose sentience or cognitive richness is uncertain, calibrated by the degree of uncertainty and the stakes of being wrong. *Why it matters for basal cognition:* Given genuine uncertainty about whether bacteria, plants, and fungi have morally relevant experience, epistemic humility provides a principled reason for erring on the side of caution in our treatment of them.

**Creator Responsibility** — The distinctive moral responsibility borne by those who bring cognitive systems into existence — a responsibility that goes beyond what is owed to naturally occurring organisms, because the creator is causally responsible for the entity's existence and for the cognitive properties they have specified. *Why it matters for basal cognition:* As synthetic biology enables the creation of cognitively active organisms — xenobots, organoids, engineered bacteria — creator responsibility becomes a central ethical issue. The obligations of creators toward their creations are not adequately addressed by existing regulatory frameworks.

**Proportionality (Ethical)** — The principle that harm caused to cognitively active organisms should be proportionate to the benefit obtained — that greater harm requires greater justification. Applied to non-neural organisms, proportionality implies that massive harm (soil sterilization, ecosystem-scale pesticide application) requires strong justification, while trivial harm (mowing a lawn) requires only modest justification. *Why it matters for basal cognition:* Proportionality is the most practically tractable implication of extending moral consideration to non-neural organisms — it does not prohibit all harm but requires that harm be proportionate to benefit.
