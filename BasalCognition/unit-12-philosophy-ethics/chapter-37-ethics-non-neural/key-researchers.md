# Chapter 37: Key Researchers

## Peter Singer
**Institution:** Princeton University, Center for Human Values
**Primary contributions:** Articulated the sentience-based framework for moral consideration in *Animal Liberation* (1975), arguing that the capacity to suffer, not intelligence or species membership, is the morally relevant criterion. His "expanding circle" framework — in which the moral community has expanded historically from the immediate group to broader categories of beings — provides a template for asking whether basal cognitive organisms might be the next expansion.

**Key works:**
- Singer, P. (1975). *Animal Liberation*. Random House.
- Singer, P. (1979). *Practical Ethics*. Cambridge University Press.

**Characterization:** The philosopher who made animal ethics philosophically serious — and whose framework now faces its most demanding test from organisms that challenge the sentience criterion itself.

---

## Matthew Hall
**Institution:** Australian National University, Fenner School of Environment and Society
**Primary contributions:** Developed a philosophical framework for plant moral status in *Plants as Persons* (2011), drawing on process philosophy and Indigenous philosophical traditions to argue that plants have their own forms of subjectivity and deserve moral consideration independent of whether they are sentient in the vertebrate sense.

**Key works:**
- Hall, M. (2011). *Plants as Persons: A Philosophical Botany*. SUNY Press.

**Characterization:** The philosopher who insisted that the question of plant moral status is serious, and took it seriously enough to write a book about it.

---

## Paul Taylor
**Institution:** Brooklyn College and City University of New York (retired)
**Primary contributions:** Developed biocentric ethics in *Respect for Nature* (1986) — the view that all living organisms, by virtue of being teleological centers of life with their own good, have inherent worth that generates obligations on moral agents. Taylor's framework is the most rigorous philosophical defense of moral consideration for all living things, including non-sentient ones.

**Key works:**
- Taylor, P. W. (1986). *Respect for Nature: A Theory of Environmental Ethics*. Princeton University Press.

**Characterization:** The philosopher who took "respect for all life" seriously enough to derive concrete ethical obligations from it — a framework that gains new relevance from basal cognition research.

---

## Suzanne Simard
**Institution:** University of British Columbia, Department of Forest and Conservation Sciences
**Primary contributions:** Discovered and characterized resource transfer through mycorrhizal networks among tree species in natural forests, demonstrating that forest trees exchange carbon, water, and nitrogen through underground fungal networks in ways that benefit neighboring seedlings. Her research on "mother trees" as network hubs with disproportionate ecological influence has generated both scientific and public attention.

**Key works:**
- Simard, S. W., Perry, D. A., Jones, M. D., Myrold, D. D., Durall, D. M., & Molina, R. (1997). Net transfer of carbon between ectomycorrhizal tree species in the field. *Nature*, 388(6642), 579–582.
- Simard, S. (2021). *Finding the Mother Tree: Discovering the Wisdom of the Forest*. Alfred A. Knopf.

**Characterization:** The forest ecologist who showed that trees are not competitive individuals but cooperative communities — and who has been honest about both the science and the public's appetite for that story.

---

## Monica Gagliano
**Institution:** Southern Cross University, Ecology and Evolution
**Primary contributions:** Experimental investigation of plant learning and memory, including demonstrations of habituation in *Mimosa pudica* and controversial claims of associative learning. Gagliano's work has driven discussion about whether plants can learn in ways that require revising standard cognitive and ethical frameworks.

**Key works:**
- Gagliano, M. (2014). Experience teaches plants to learn faster and forget slower in environments where it matters. *Oecologia*, 175(1), 63–72.
- Gagliano, M., Vyazovskiy, V. V., Borbély, A. A., Grimonprez, M., & Depczynski, M. (2016). Learning by association in plants. *Scientific Reports*, 6, 38427.

**Characterization:** The plant biologist who has most boldly pushed the empirical case for plant cognition — and who understands that the ethics follows from the biology.

---

## Aldo Leopold
**Institution:** University of Wisconsin-Madison (historical, 1887–1948)
**Primary contributions:** Wildlife ecologist and author of *A Sand County Almanac* (1949), in which he articulated the "land ethic" — the principle that the moral community should be extended to include soils, waters, plants, and animals, and that a thing is right when it tends to preserve the integrity, stability, and beauty of the biotic community. The land ethic provides an ecological rather than sentience-based framework for moral consideration of non-neural organisms.

**Key works:**
- Leopold, A. (1949). *A Sand County Almanac*. Oxford University Press.

**Characterization:** The ecologist who saw what the environment needed before there was a word for ecology, and wrote one of the most beautiful and influential environmental texts in the English language.
