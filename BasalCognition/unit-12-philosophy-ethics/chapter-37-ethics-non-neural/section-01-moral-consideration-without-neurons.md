# Section 1: Moral Consideration Without Neurons

## The Standard View

The dominant framework for moral consideration in contemporary ethics grounds the moral status of an organism in its capacity for sentience — the ability to experience pleasure and pain, to have positive and negative experiential states. Peter Singer, following Jeremy Bentham's utilitarian tradition, articulated this position with great clarity: "The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" (Singer, 1975, p. 8). On this view, the morally relevant property is not cognitive sophistication, rationality, or language, but the capacity for experience — and specifically for aversive experience. An organism that can suffer has interests that deserve moral consideration; an organism that cannot has no moral claim on our behavior toward it.

This framework has been extraordinarily productive. It drove the animal rights movement, expanded moral concern to hundreds of billions of farm animals previously treated as mere resources, and provided a principled basis for criticizing practices that cause unnecessary suffering to sentient creatures. Its clarity is also its strength: it gives a determinate answer to most moral questions about animal treatment, one that does not depend on contested facts about intelligence, sociality, or personhood.

But the framework has a pressure point: it requires a determination of which organisms are sentient — which organisms can suffer — and that determination is far harder to make than the criterion itself suggests. We are confident that mammals and birds are sentient; we have good reason to think that fish and cephalopods are; we are less certain about insects; and we have essentially no agreed-upon method for determining whether plants, bacteria, or fungi are sentient in the morally relevant sense.

This uncertainty is not merely ignorance that future research will resolve. It reflects a deeper difficulty: sentience involves phenomenal consciousness, and phenomenal consciousness — as the previous chapter established — is not directly observable. We can observe behavior, physiology, and neural activity. But the inference from behavior and physiology to inner experience requires interpretive assumptions that remain contested.

## The Challenge from Basal Cognition

The evidence reviewed throughout this book creates a specific pressure on the sentience framework. Consider what we know:

Bacteria respond to their chemical environment with a sophistication that involves molecular "decisions," temporal memory, and collective behavior. They detect gradients, integrate signals, adjust gene expression, and move adaptively. These behaviors are not random; they are organized with respect to the bacterium's survival interests. Whether there is anything it is like to be a bacterium doing these things is unknown.

Plants detect damage, produce volatile signals that communicate to their neighbors, and can be habituated to repeated stimuli in ways that reduce their defensive responses. The habituation is reversible, context-sensitive, and distinct from simple fatigue (Gagliano, 2014). Monica Gagliano and colleagues have also reported evidence of associative learning in plants — conditioned responses to paired stimuli — though these results remain contested (Gagliano et al., 2016).

Slime molds solve maze problems, anticipate periodic stimuli (Saigusa et al., 2008), and make "decisions" about which food source to approach in ways that factor in multiple variables simultaneously.

None of this establishes sentience. The organisms in question lack neurons; they lack the neural architecture associated with pain in vertebrates; they lack the behavioral repertoire that we use as evidence for subjective states in animals. But the gap between their cognitive function and the cognitive function of organisms we take to be sentient is narrower than we previously thought — and the gap between their molecular machinery and the molecular machinery of sentience-associated neural systems is narrower still. The same ion channels, the same signaling cascades, the same electrical dynamics that appear in our neurons appear in plant cells, bacterial membranes, and slime mold cytoplasm.

Does this mean plants and bacteria are sentient? No — or at least, we cannot say yes with any confidence. Does it mean we should take the question more seriously than we have? Almost certainly yes.

## Plant Ethics

Matthew Hall, in *Plants as Persons: A Philosophical Botany* (2011), argued for a significant revision of the standard framework: plants should be considered as morally significant entities, not because they can suffer in the familiar sense, but because they are living beings with their own forms of subjectivity, their own adaptive engagements with their worlds, their own interests in flourishing. Hall draws on both Western philosophical traditions (particularly process philosophy) and Indigenous philosophical traditions that have long attributed moral status to plants, to argue that the exclusion of plants from moral consideration reflects a culturally specific bias rather than a universal moral truth.

Hall's argument does not depend on the claim that plants are sentient in the vertebrate sense. It depends on a different concept of moral status — one grounded in the living being's having a good of its own, a welfare that can be promoted or harmed, independent of any subjective experience. This is a form of what philosophers call "interests without subjects" — the idea that organisms can have interests (they can be better or worse off) without necessarily having phenomenal experience.

This position has precedent in mainstream environmental ethics. Paul Taylor's "biocentric ethics" (1986) holds that all living organisms, by virtue of being living things with their own teleological centers of life, have inherent worth that imposes obligations on us. On Taylor's view, even bacteria have a good of their own — conditions that allow them to flourish — and this generates at least a prima facie moral obligation not to harm them without sufficient justification.

The practical implications of plant ethics are more modest than the theoretical framework might suggest. Hall and Taylor are not arguing that all plant harm is equivalent to harm to animals, or that we should starve rather than eat vegetables. They are arguing that plant harm requires justification — that it is not ethically neutral — and that practices causing massive, unnecessary plant harm (clear-cutting, soil sterilization, herbicide flooding) deserve ethical scrutiny even if the organisms being harmed cannot suffer in the vertebrate sense.

## Microbial Ethics

The extension of moral consideration to microorganisms is the most philosophically challenging step, and the most practically charged. If bacteria have any form of morally relevant interests — if there is anything about their situation that generates obligations toward them — then antibiotic treatment of bacterial infections, the microbiome-disrupting effects of processed food and pharmaceuticals, and the killing of billions of microorganisms in industrial processes all acquire a moral dimension.

This is, to put it mildly, uncomfortable. It is also not clearly wrong. The cognitive function of bacteria — their signal integration, their collective decision-making, their adaptive behavioral repertoire — is genuinely sophisticated. The molecular machinery of bacterial sensation shares deep evolutionary roots with the molecular machinery of vertebrate sensation. If we accept that the relevant criterion for moral consideration is some form of adaptive engagement with the world — some form of having interests, even without phenomenal experience — then bacteria qualify.

The right response to this discomfort is not to dismiss it with a wave of the hand. It is to think carefully about what minimal moral consideration for microbial life would actually mean in practice. It would not mean that all antibiotic use is wrong; the interests of a person dying of bacterial sepsis outweigh the interests of the bacteria causing it by any reasonable framework. But it might mean that the casual, prophylactic, and agricultural overuse of antibiotics — which has generated a global crisis of antibiotic resistance — is wrong not only for prudential reasons (it creates resistant strains that harm us) but for ethical ones (it causes massive, largely unnecessary harm to cognitively active organisms).

This is not an argument for radical pacifism toward microbes. It is an argument for proportionality: that harm to cognitively active organisms — however different their cognition from ours — requires justification proportional to the harm caused. This is exactly the framework we apply to harm to sentient animals, qualified by uncertainty about the nature and depth of microbial experience.

## Fungal Ethics

The ethics of fungi is perhaps the least developed of these extensions, but it raises genuinely novel questions. Mycorrhizal networks — the underground fungal webs that connect plant roots across forest floors — are the subjects of Suzanne Simard's research on "wood wide web" communication (Simard et al., 1997). Whether these networks have any form of cognitive agency — as Simard's more popular writing sometimes implies — is contested. What is less contested is that mycorrhizal networks are distributed biological systems of enormous ecological importance, whose disruption by land management practices (tilling, fungicide use, soil compaction) has significant ecological consequences.

Merlin Sheldrake's *Entangled Life* (2020) invites the reader to take fungal cognition seriously — not necessarily to attribute phenomenal experience to fungi, but to recognize that mycorrhizal networks are information-processing systems of real complexity, that they respond to environmental conditions in ways that influence the behavior of many organisms, and that their casual destruction in industrial agriculture involves the elimination of biological computation that has taken millennia to establish.

What moral framework would capture these considerations? An ecological ethics framework, rather than a sentience-based one, seems better suited: the mycorrhizal network matters not primarily because of what it experiences (if anything), but because of its role in ecological communities of which we are part, and to which we bear obligations of respect and non-destruction.

## The Importance of Epistemic Humility

A consistent theme in this section is that moral consideration of non-neural organisms does not require certainty about their inner experience. It requires only epistemic humility — an acknowledgment that our uncertainty about inner experience should modulate, not eliminate, our moral concern. The stronger our evidence that a system has morally relevant properties (adaptive engagement with the world, having a good of its own, integration into moral communities), the stronger the presumption against causing it harm without justification.

This epistemic approach is familiar from the precautionary principle in environmental policy: when actions risk serious harm, and when the magnitude of the harm is uncertain, we should err on the side of caution. Applied to moral consideration, the principle suggests: when there is genuine uncertainty about whether an organism has morally relevant properties, and when the harm of treating it as non-considerable (if it is considerable) would be serious, we should extend provisional moral consideration rather than defaulting to exclusion.

This is not a revolutionary conclusion. It is a conservative one: it extends to non-neural organisms the same epistemic charity we extend to organisms whose sentience we take for granted, calibrated by the degree of our uncertainty.
