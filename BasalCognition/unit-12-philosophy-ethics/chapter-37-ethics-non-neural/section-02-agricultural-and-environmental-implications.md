# Section 2: Agricultural and Environmental Implications

## From Principle to Practice

The previous section established that non-neural organisms — plants, microbes, fungi — have some claim to moral consideration, grounded variously in their functional cognition, their having interests of their own, and their membership in ecological communities to which we bear obligations. This section asks what that moral consideration means in practice, in the contexts where human behavior most consequentially affects these organisms: agriculture, soil management, forest management, and ecological restoration.

The goal is not to generate paralyzing conclusions or to suggest that all plant or microbial harm is equivalent to harm to sentient animals. It is to identify which human practices deserve ethical scrutiny in light of what we now know about non-neural cognition — and to ask whether any of those practices might be reformed in ways that reduce harm without eliminating the human goods they provide.

## Plant Cognition and Industrial Agriculture

Modern industrial agriculture is, among other things, an exercise in the elimination of plant cognition. Plants in a monoculture field are denied the chemical communication networks they use in diverse communities; they are separated from the mycorrhizal partners on which they depend for mineral nutrition; their signaling systems are suppressed by herbicides that target molecular pathways shared across the plant kingdom; and they are grown in conditions of soil compaction and chemical saturation that prevent the root exploration and soil interaction that constitutes the plant's primary sensorimotor engagement with its world.

This description is accurate and worth sitting with. Industrial agriculture does not merely kill plants; it creates conditions in which the cognitive capacities of plants cannot function. Whether this matters morally depends on one's framework, but it is at minimum ecologically consequential: plants deprived of mycorrhizal partners and chemical communication networks are less resilient, require more chemical inputs to maintain yields, and are more vulnerable to disease and pest pressure. The ecological and agricultural costs of suppressing plant cognition are real and measurable even if the moral costs remain contested.

From a moral standpoint — taking seriously even a modest extension of consideration to plant welfare — industrial monoculture deserves scrutiny on several grounds. The scale is enormous: approximately 1.5 billion hectares of cropland cover the Earth's surface, much of it in monocultures that substitute chemical inputs for ecological function. The disruption of plant social networks (chemical signaling) and plant symbioses (mycorrhizal networks) is pervasive. Practices like subsoil compaction by heavy machinery cause long-term structural damage to soil architecture that prevents root exploration for years after a single event.

The alternative is not to abandon agriculture but to reform it: agroecological approaches — intercropping, reduced tillage, organic management, preservation of soil microbiome diversity — produce measurable improvements in agricultural resilience and often comparable yields over the long term, while allowing the ecological relationships on which plant cognition depends to function more naturally (Altieri, 1995). These approaches are not new, and their ecological benefits are well-documented. What the perspective of plant cognition adds is a moral dimension: these practices are better not only because they are more ecologically efficient, but because they permit plants to live as the cognitively active organisms they are, rather than as commodities managed by chemical suppression.

## Soil Microbiome Ethics and the Cognitive Community Underground

A teaspoon of healthy agricultural soil contains billions of bacteria and millions of fungal fragments, constituting a community of microbial cognitive agents whose interactions determine soil structure, nutrient cycling, plant health, and carbon sequestration. This community — the soil microbiome — is one of the most complex and least understood biological systems on Earth, and it is under severe pressure from modern agricultural practices.

Industrial agriculture's impact on the soil microbiome is well-documented: synthetic fertilizers, particularly nitrogen, alter the composition of microbial communities by favoring fast-growing, nutrient-opportunistic species and suppressing slower-growing, mutualistic ones; pesticides and herbicides kill non-target microbial species and disrupt soil food webs; tillage physically disrupts the spatial organization of microbial communities and the fungal networks that give soil its characteristic architecture (Montgomery & Biklé, 2016).

From a purely ecological perspective, these impacts matter because healthy soil microbiomes are necessary for sustainable food production — they fix nitrogen, solubilize phosphorus, suppress pathogens, and build the soil organic matter that gives agricultural soil its water-holding capacity and structural stability. The global decline in soil health is, by this measure, an agricultural catastrophe in slow motion.

From the perspective of microbial cognition, the picture is more complex. If microbial communities are cognitively active — if they engage in quorum sensing, collective decision-making, and distributed information processing — then the destruction of soil microbiome diversity is the destruction of biological computation. The cognitive community underground is not merely an ecological service provider; it is, if basal cognition research is right, a collection of cognitively active organisms whose interactions constitute something that might reasonably be called collective intelligence.

This framing does not resolve the question of whether microbial interests generate moral obligations. But it does suggest a different way of thinking about soil health: not merely as a resource to be managed, but as a community of cognitively active organisms whose welfare is at least provisionally relevant to our moral deliberations. This might translate, in practice, into a stronger commitment to practices that preserve soil microbiome diversity — not only for the instrumental benefits they provide, but out of a more general obligation not to cause unnecessary harm to cognitively active organisms.

## Mycorrhizal Networks: Harm by Disruption?

The question of whether disrupting a mycorrhizal network constitutes harm is not straightforward. The network itself — the intertwined fungal hyphae connecting plant roots across a forest floor — is not an individual organism in the sense that a rabbit or a bacterium is. It is an ecological structure: an emergent pattern of biological organization that arises from the interactions of many individual fungal organisms (of one or several species) with the root systems of many plant species.

Disrupting this network by removing the plants to which it is connected, by tilling the soil in which it grows, or by applying fungicides that kill the fungal component causes several kinds of harm: it eliminates the ecological services the network provides (nutrient transfer, water distribution, pathogen suppression); it reduces the resilience of the plant community that depends on it; and it destroys the network as a physical structure — a structure that may have taken decades or centuries to develop and that, if the cognitive community framework is taken seriously, embodies distributed information about the local ecology in the pattern of its connections.

Suzanne Simard's research on carbon transfer through mycorrhizal networks (Simard et al., 1997) showed that "mother trees" — large, centrally connected trees in forest networks — transfer significant quantities of carbon to their seedling neighbors through the mycorrhizal network, particularly in conditions of low light where seedlings cannot photosynthesize enough for their own needs. This is not mere metaphor: carbon labeled with radioactive 14C in the mother tree's leaves shows up in the seedlings, transferred through the fungal network. The network actively redistributes resources from surplus to need — a form of collective resource management that benefits the community as a whole.

Does this constitute harm when the network is destroyed? Under a sentience-based framework: not obviously, unless the organisms involved can suffer. Under a biocentric framework: yes, because the organisms involved have interests in their own flourishing, and the network is essential to that flourishing. Under an ecological framework: yes, because the network is part of a biotic community to which we bear obligations of respect and non-destruction.

## Invasive Species Management: A Reconsideration

The management of invasive species — non-native organisms that establish populations in ecosystems to which they were not introduced and cause ecological harm — is one of the most practically challenging areas of conservation biology. Standard management approaches include mechanical removal, herbicide application, biological control, and, in extreme cases, systemic eradication programs. These approaches cause harm to the individual organisms being managed — often on a very large scale.

Standard ethical frameworks have generally regarded this harm as justified by the ecological harm caused by invasive species — their disruption of native communities, reduction of biodiversity, and alteration of ecosystem function. The cognitive status of the organisms being managed has not been considered morally relevant, because they are typically assumed to lack morally relevant experience.

The evidence of basal cognition complicates this picture without resolving it. If the invasive plants, bacteria, or fungi being eradicated have some form of cognitive function — some degree of morally relevant engagement with their worlds — does this affect the ethics of eradication?

The most defensible position is that it affects the ethics but does not change the conclusion. Eradication of ecologically harmful invasive species remains defensible under most frameworks because the harm caused by the invasive organisms — to native biodiversity, to ecosystem function, to the welfare of native organisms — outweighs the harm to the invasive organisms. But the basal cognition perspective does add a dimension: eradication programs should use the most targeted, least harmful methods available, should be proportionate to the ecological harm caused by the invasive, and should be subject to the same kind of ethical scrutiny we apply to other practices that cause harm to cognitively active organisms.

More specifically: the casual, preventive, or cosmetic use of herbicides and fungicides against plants and fungi that cause no significant ecological harm — the management of weeds in gardens, the control of "undesirable" fungi in lawns, the application of herbicides along road margins for aesthetic reasons — seems harder to justify under a framework that takes plant and fungal cognition seriously. These practices cause harm — chemical disruption, injury, death — to cognitively active organisms, for reasons that are at best instrumental and at worst trivial.

## The Practical Ethics of a Cognitively Active World

The implications of this section can be summarized in a few practical principles:

**Proportionality.** The harm caused to cognitively active non-neural organisms should be proportionate to the benefit obtained. Massive harm (industrial soil sterilization, ecosystem-scale fungicide application) requires strong justification; trivial harm (mowing a lawn) requires only modest justification; intermediate harm falls somewhere between.

**Minimization.** Where practices cause harm to cognitively active organisms, the least harmful alternative that achieves the necessary goal should be preferred. This favors agroecological over industrial approaches, targeted over broad-spectrum herbicide use, and biological over chemical pest management.

**Preservation of ecological relationships.** The cognitive function of non-neural organisms is largely expressed through their ecological relationships — their chemical communication networks, their symbiotic partnerships, their collective behaviors. Preserving these relationships is not merely ecologically beneficial; it is a way of preserving the conditions in which cognitively active organisms can live as the organisms they are.

**Epistemic humility.** We do not know the full extent of the cognitive capacities of non-neural organisms. New evidence continues to emerge. Our ethical frameworks should be responsive to that evidence as it develops, rather than committing permanently to a particular assessment of what organisms can and cannot experience.

None of these principles requires a revolutionary transformation of human behavior. They are extensions of ethical principles we already accept — proportionality, minimization of harm, respect for living things — to a broader range of organisms than we have previously considered. What is required is intellectual honesty about what the science shows, and willingness to take its moral implications seriously.
