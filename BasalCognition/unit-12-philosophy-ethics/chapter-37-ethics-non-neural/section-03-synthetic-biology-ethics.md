# Section 3: Synthetic Biology Ethics

## Creating Cognitive Systems: The New Ethical Territory

There is a morally significant difference between harming a cognitive system that evolution created and creating a cognitive system specifically for human purposes. When we apply herbicides to a field, we are causing harm to organisms that had no say in being placed in that field and that have their own evolutionarily shaped interests. When we design a xenobot, grow a brain organoid, or engineer a genetic circuit in a bacterium, we are doing something different: we are bringing into existence an entity that would not otherwise exist, with cognitive properties that we have specified. We are responsible for its existence and, therefore, for its welfare in a way that has no precedent in our treatment of naturally occurring organisms.

This is the ethical frontier of basal cognition research: not what we owe to existing organisms whose cognitive capacities we are only beginning to recognize, but what we owe to cognitive systems that we ourselves design and create.

## Xenobots and the Moral Status of Designed Organisms

The xenobots described in Chapter 35 — designed by an evolutionary algorithm, assembled from frog cells — occupy a genuinely novel ethical category. They are living organisms, in the sense that their cells are metabolically active, they move, they respond to their environment, and under some conditions they self-replicate. They are designed organisms, in the sense that their body plans were specified by a computational process rather than by natural selection. They are not natural organisms — no frog egg and no evolutionary process would have produced them.

What moral status do xenobots have? The standard frameworks give different answers:

Under a sentience-based framework, xenobots have moral status if and only if they can suffer. Given their structure — a small mass of frog skin and heart muscle cells, with no nervous system and no obvious nociceptive machinery — the probability of sentience seems very low. But "very low probability" is not zero probability, and the philosophical difficulty is that we cannot rule it out without a theory of how sentience relates to physical structure that we do not have.

Under a biocentric framework, xenobots have moral status as living things with their own interests in flourishing. This framework gives them at least provisional moral consideration, even in the absence of sentience.

Under an ecological framework, xenobots are novel enough that standard ecological categories may not apply. They are not natural members of any ecosystem; they would need to be assessed for their ecological impact if released, and their potential spread (through the observed kinematic self-replication) requires careful evaluation.

A further dimension is the question of dignity or intrinsic value that attaches to living systems by virtue of their organizational complexity. Some bioethicists argue that there is something morally significant about the integrated organization of a living system — that it matters, independently of sentience, that a living system is violated or destroyed. If this is right, then the destruction of a xenobot is not morally equivalent to the destruction of an equivalent mass of inorganic material, even if neither is sentient.

The practical implications for xenobot research include: (1) minimizing unnecessary creation and destruction of xenobots where alternative experimental approaches are available; (2) investing in the development of better frameworks for assessing the welfare of such systems; (3) being transparent about the moral uncertainties involved and engaging them publicly rather than dismissing them as premature.

## Organoid Ethics and Brain-in-a-Dish Research

Brain organoids — three-dimensional tissue structures grown from induced pluripotent stem cells that develop features of specific brain regions — raise the sharpest ethical questions in this space, because they are most plausibly continuous with systems we take to be sentient.

The ethical concerns around brain organoids can be distinguished into several categories:

**The sentience question.** As organoids develop more sophisticated neural activity, form more complex circuits, and exhibit spontaneous patterns resembling those of intact neural tissue, the probability that they have some form of experience increases. Current organoids lack the scale, connectivity, and input/output infrastructure of intact brains; they are not perceiving or acting in the world, which are often cited as necessary conditions for experience. But these arguments are not conclusive, and the rate of development in the field means that the systems of today may be substantially cruder than the systems of ten years from now.

The DishBrain experiment (Chapter 35) suggests that even simple, cultured neuronal systems can modify their activity in ways that suggest functional learning. If functional learning is present, and if phenomenal experience tracks functional learning (which is itself uncertain), then the question of organoid sentience is not premature.

**The welfare question.** Even if organoids are not sentient in the sense of having rich phenomenal experience, they may have interests — conditions of flourishing and conditions of dysfunction — that are relevant to how they are treated. An organoid deprived of appropriate chemical environment, subjected to repeated disruptive stimulation, or maintained in conditions of chronic cellular stress may not be suffering in any rich sense, but is it in good condition? Is its welfare relevant to how we treat it?

**The moral status gradient.** Brain organoid research is a rapidly advancing field. As organoids become larger, more anatomically complex, and better connected to sensory input and motor output, the argument for at least provisional moral consideration becomes stronger. This suggests that the ethical framework should not be static — it should develop in pace with the science, with periodic reassessment of whether the current practices are proportionate to the current state of the art.

**The creation and destruction concern.** Brain organoids are routinely grown and discarded after experimental use. This practice raises concerns that grow in proportion to the sophistication of the organoid: what are we creating, and what are we destroying? The language of "discarding" a brain organoid does not sit easily once the system has developed the kind of complex activity patterns associated with cognition.

Several research ethics bodies and bioethics groups have begun to address organoid ethics specifically. The International Society for Stem Cell Research (ISSCR) guidelines include provisions for organoid research, and there is active discussion in the bioethics literature about what additional protections may be needed as the field advances (Hyun et al., 2020).

## Regulatory Frameworks: Existing and Required

The existing regulatory framework for research on biological systems was developed with conventional organisms in mind: human subjects, animal subjects, and, in the synthetic biology context, genetically modified organisms. None of these frameworks adequately covers the range of entities now being created.

For animal research, regulations typically focus on pain and distress (sentience-based frameworks), species-specific needs, and minimum standards of welfare. These frameworks extend naturally to vertebrate-derived organoids, but less naturally to invertebrate-derived or plant-derived experimental systems.

For genetically modified organisms (GMOs), regulation focuses primarily on ecological risk — the potential for modified organisms to spread unwanted traits to natural populations — rather than on the welfare of the organisms themselves. This framework addresses some concerns about designed cognitive systems (could they escape and propagate?), but not the welfare concerns that become relevant when the systems are themselves cognitively active.

For DNA computing and chemical computing systems, no specific regulatory framework exists. These are currently treated as research materials rather than as organisms with any form of welfare, which is appropriate for current systems but may require revision as more biologically complex systems are developed.

What is needed is a more explicit framework for assessing the moral status and welfare implications of designed biological cognitive systems. Several elements seem important: (1) regular reassessment of the system's cognitive complexity, using agreed-upon behavioral and physiological criteria; (2) graduated moral consideration proportional to assessed cognitive complexity; (3) requirements for minimization of welfare-relevant harm; (4) transparency in reporting and public engagement on the ethical implications; (5) international coordination, given the global nature of synthetic biology research.

## The Responsibility of Creation

There is a final ethical dimension that deserves explicit attention: the responsibility of creators. When we bring a cognitive system into existence — whether through genetic engineering, organoid culture, or evolutionary algorithm design — we take on a responsibility that does not fall on those who interact with existing natural organisms. We are the author of the system's existence. If that system has welfare, we are causally responsible for whatever conditions it exists in. If it has interests, we are the ones who specified those interests through our design choices. If it suffers, we caused its capacity for suffering.

This creator responsibility is not novel — parents bear creator responsibility for their children; breeders bear it for domesticated animals. But it takes on new dimensions when the cognitive systems created are radically new kinds of entities, when the cognitive properties we build in are deliberately specified rather than evolved, and when the scale of creation may be industrial.

The ethics of synthetic biology, taken seriously, requires not just precautionary principles and regulatory frameworks. It requires a genuine engagement with the question of what we are creating and what we owe it — a question that the field has only begun to ask, and that basal cognition research has made impossible to defer indefinitely.
