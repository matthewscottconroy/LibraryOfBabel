# Chapter 38: What Basal Cognition Means for Understanding Ourselves

## The Mirror Turned Back

Every scientific revolution eventually reaches the same uncomfortable moment: the point at which the new understanding reflects back on the understanders themselves, and the image is not quite what we expected. Copernicus displaced the Earth from the center of the universe. Darwin dissolved the boundary between humans and other animals. The discovery of the unconscious showed that the mind was not transparent to itself. Each of these reconceptions required not just a revision of specific beliefs but a renegotiation of human self-understanding — a recalibration of what we are.

Basal cognition research is, potentially, another such moment. If cognition — genuine information processing, memory, adaptive behavior, goal-directed action — is present throughout the living world, if it does not require neurons and is not uniquely animal, then what we thought was our most distinctive feature turns out to be a particular instance of something vastly more ancient and more widespread. We are not the inventors of intelligence; we are its elaboration.

This is not a diminishment. The fact that intelligence is ancient and pervasive does not make human cognitive capacities less real or less remarkable. The elaboration is genuine: human cognition, with its language, culture, recursive self-reflection, and capacity to model other minds, is something genuinely new in the history of life. But it is new in the way that a great river is new — continuous with its upstream tributaries, nourished by every stream that joined it along the way, not conceivable without the entire catchment that shaped it.

## The Central Question

**What remains distinctive about human cognition once we accept that cognition extends throughout the living world?** And what are the implications of this reconception for medicine, for psychology, and for how we understand ourselves as biological organisms embedded in larger cognitive communities?

## Chapter Roadmap

**Section 38.1 — Rethinking Human Uniqueness** traces the long history of human exceptionalism and its progressive erosion — from Descartes's rational soul to Darwin's continuity, from Chomsky's universal grammar to the discovery of language-like capacities in apes and cetaceans. We ask what, if anything, remains distinctively human after basal cognition research, and what the psychological challenge of that renegotiation involves.

**Section 38.2 — The Holobiont Self** examines the radical implication that the human self is not a single entity but a composite — a holobiont comprising the human genome and the many microbial genomes that co-inhabit the body. We examine the evidence that gut bacteria influence behavior, mood, and even cognitive development, and the older story of mitochondria as ancient cognitive partners with their own evolutionary history.

**Section 38.3 — Implications for Medicine** draws out practical implications of the distributed, composite, cognitively active nature of the human body for medicine: bioelectric approaches to cancer and regeneration, the gut microbiome as a cognitive system to be treated, psychobiotics, and the reconceptualization of drug targets in light of the body's distributed intelligence.
