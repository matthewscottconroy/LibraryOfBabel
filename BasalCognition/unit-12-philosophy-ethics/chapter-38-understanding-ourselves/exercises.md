# Chapter 38: Exercises

## Part I: Reflection and Discussion

1. **What remains distinctively human?** After reviewing the evidence for basal cognition throughout this book, articulate your best account of what — if anything — is distinctively human about human cognition. Be specific: identify properties, mechanisms, or capacities that are present in human cognition and absent (not merely less developed) in non-neural organisms. Evaluate whether those properties are kind-differences or degree-differences from what we observe in simpler systems.

2. **The holobiont and personal identity.** If the gut microbiome influences behavior, mood, and personality — and if the microbiome changes over time with diet, antibiotics, and health status — then is "your" personality stable across these changes? What does this imply for theories of personal identity that ground identity in psychological continuity? Is there a conception of personal identity that is robust to the holobiont perspective?

3. **Mitochondria as ancestral selves.** Lynn Margulis argued that the eukaryotic cell is a symbiotic composite of different lineages, and that the mitochondrion is a bacterial endosymbiont. Does this make the mitochondrion part of "you," or a co-inhabitant with you? How does the answer depend on your theory of personal identity? Does it matter that the mitochondrial genome is maternally inherited, making your mitochondria direct descendants of your mother's mitochondria?

4. **Cancer as cognitive failure.** Levin's bioelectric framework describes cancer as a failure of tissue-level cognitive governance — cells defecting from the tissue's collective behavior. Evaluate this metaphor. Is it scientifically useful (does it generate testable hypotheses that differ from standard genetic accounts of cancer)? Is it philosophically accurate (does cancer genuinely involve a failure of cognition, or merely a failure of regulation)? Can a framework be useful without being accurate?

5. **Psychobiotics and authenticity.** If a probiotic supplement changes your gut microbiome composition and, as a result, reduces your anxiety and improves your mood, is the change in your emotional state authentically "yours"? Compare this to the question of whether antidepressants produce authentic mood improvement, and to the question of whether caffeine produces authentic alertness. Where, if anywhere, is there a meaningful distinction between "authentic" and "chemically mediated" psychological states?

## Part II: Thought Experiments

1. **The microbiome transplant.** A procedure is developed that can completely replace the gut microbiome of one person with the microbiome of another — more complete and efficient than current fecal microbiota transplantation. The recipient's microbiome is replaced, their gut-brain axis function changes measurably, and their self-reported mood, anxiety levels, and some aspects of behavioral style shift significantly toward those of the donor. Is this a medical treatment, an enhancement, or something else? Does the recipient remain "the same person"? Does the donor bear any moral responsibility for the effects their microbiome has on the recipient? What regulatory framework should govern such a procedure?

2. **The regeneration question.** Michael Levin's group has shown that appropriate bioelectric intervention can induce limb regeneration in frogs that do not normally regenerate limbs. Suppose this approach were translated to humans, and a person who had lost an arm could, through a combination of bioelectric stimulation and supportive pharmacology, regenerate a functional arm over eighteen months. What ethical considerations bear on the development and distribution of such a treatment? Is it enhancement or restoration? How does the cognitive framing of regeneration — as the body's bioelectric system being helped to achieve its target morphology — affect the ethical analysis?

3. **The dysbiosis thought experiment.** Suppose research establishes, with high confidence, that a specific configuration of gut microbiome composition — call it Microbiome A — is reliably associated with reduced empathy, increased risk-taking, and reduced social cooperation, while Microbiome B produces the opposite effects. Suppose further that Microbiome A is more common in populations exposed to a specific widely used herbicide. Work through the ethical and political implications of this finding. Who has obligations to whom? What regulatory responses are warranted? And: does this thought experiment change how you think about the cognitive boundaries of the self?

## Part III: Laboratory and Computational Investigations

1. **Gut microbiome composition analysis.** Using the Human Microbiome Project database or other publicly available 16S amplicon sequencing data, analyze the gut microbiome composition of 50 or more individuals across different health categories (e.g., healthy controls, depression, anxiety, IBS). Identify bacterial families or genera that differ significantly in abundance between groups (using appropriate statistical methods controlling for multiple comparisons). Discuss what the identified associations do and do not tell us about the causal role of the microbiome in the health conditions, and what experimental designs would be needed to establish causality.

2. **Modeling bioelectric tissue governance.** Using a published computational model of bioelectric signaling in epithelial tissue (several are available in the literature from Levin's and related groups), simulate the effect of a local voltage perturbation representing a nascent tumor. Characterize how the perturbation propagates or fails to propagate through the tissue bioelectric network. Now simulate the effect of a pharmacological intervention that restores normal membrane voltage in the perturbed region. Does this restore normal tissue organization in the model? What does this tell you about the therapeutic hypothesis of bioelectric cancer treatment?

3. **Literature review: Psychobiotics.** Conduct a systematic search of randomized controlled trials of probiotic interventions on psychological outcomes (depression, anxiety, perceived stress) published in the last decade. Extract effect sizes, sample sizes, trial quality (using a validated assessment tool), and outcome measures. Calculate an overall effect size estimate and assess heterogeneity across trials. Write a 2000-word critical review of the current state of psychobiotic evidence, clearly distinguishing what is established, what is promising, and what is speculative.
