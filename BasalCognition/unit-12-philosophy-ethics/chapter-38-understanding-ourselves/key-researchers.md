# Chapter 38: Key Researchers

## Lynn Margulis (historical, 1938–2011)
**Institution:** University of Massachusetts Amherst (final appointment)
**Primary contributions:** Developed and vigorously defended the endosymbiotic theory of eukaryotic evolution — the hypothesis that mitochondria, chloroplasts, and other organelles evolved from bacterial endosymbionts incorporated into ancestral eukaryotic cells. Initially rejected by mainstream biology, the theory is now established fact. Margulis also developed the Gaia hypothesis with James Lovelock and was a consistent advocate for viewing organisms as collaborative symbiotic systems rather than competitive individuals.

**Key works:**
- Margulis, L. (1967). On the origin of mitosing cells. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*, 14(3), 255–274.
- Margulis, L. (1981). *Symbiosis in Cell Evolution*. W. H. Freeman.
- Margulis, L., & Fester, R. (Eds.). (1991). *Symbiosis as a Source of Evolutionary Innovation*. MIT Press.

**Characterization:** The biologist who spent a career being right about things that everyone insisted were wrong — and who transformed how we understand the composite nature of life.

## John Cryan
**Institution:** University College Cork, APC Microbiome Ireland; Department of Anatomy and Neuroscience
**Primary contributions:** Leading researcher on the gut-brain axis and psychobiotics. Cryan's laboratory has produced the most influential experimental evidence for microbiome-behavior interactions, including the demonstration that germ-free mice show altered stress responses and social behavior, and that specific bacterial strains can reverse these effects.

**Key works:**
- Cryan, J. F., & Dinan, T. G. (2012). Mind-altering microorganisms: The impact of the gut microbiota on brain and behaviour. *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, 13(10), 701–712.
- Cryan, J. F., et al. (2019). The microbiota-gut-brain axis. *Physiological Reviews*, 99(4), 1877–2013.

**Characterization:** The neuroscientist who took gut bacteria seriously as actors in brain function — and produced the experimental evidence to make others take them seriously too.

---

## Michael Levin
**Institution:** Tufts University, Allen Discovery Center
**Primary contributions:** Bioelectricity, morphogenesis, and regenerative medicine. See also Chapter 40. Levin's work on bioelectric signaling in development, cancer, and regeneration has produced a reconceptualization of the body as a distributed cognitive system, with implications for cancer treatment, regenerative medicine, and our understanding of biological agency.

**Key works:**
- Levin, M. (2021). Bioelectric signaling: Reprogrammable circuits underlying embryogenesis, regeneration, and cancer. *Cell*, 184(8), 1971–1989.
- Levin, M. (2019). The computational boundary of a "self": Developmental bioelectricity drives multicellularity and scale-free cognition. *Frontiers in Psychology*, 10, 2688.

**Characterization:** The developmental biologist who asked not just how bodies are built, but how they know what they are supposed to look like — and found that the answer involves bioelectric intelligence.

---

## Seth Bordenstein
**Institution:** Vanderbilt University, Microbiome Institute
**Primary contributions:** Holobiont theory and the microbiome as a unit of selection. Bordenstein has developed the theoretical and empirical case for the hologenome theory of evolution — the idea that natural selection acts on the holobiont (host plus its associated microbiome) as a unit, not on the host genome alone.

**Key works:**
- Bordenstein, S. R., & Theis, K. R. (2015). Host biology in light of the microbiome: Ten principles of holobionts and hologenomes. *PLOS Biology*, 13(8), e1002226.

**Characterization:** The evolutionary biologist who is working out what it means, for evolutionary theory, that individuals are always also ecosystems.

---

## Michael Tomasello
**Institution:** Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology; Duke University
**Primary contributions:** Comparative developmental psychology and the cultural origins of human cognition. Tomasello has systematically investigated what is and is not distinctive about human cognitive development, focusing on shared intentionality — the capacity for joint attention, cooperative communication, and shared purpose — as the key differentiator.

**Key works:**
- Tomasello, M. (1999). *The Cultural Origins of Human Cognition*. Harvard University Press.
- Tomasello, M. (2008). *Origins of Human Communication*. MIT Press.

**Characterization:** The developmental psychologist who has most carefully mapped the boundary between human and non-human cognition — and taken seriously what both sides of the boundary tell us.

---

## Francisco Varela (historical, 1946–2001)
**Institution:** CNRS Paris, École Polytechnique (final appointment)
**Primary contributions:** Autopoiesis theory (with Maturana), enactivism (with Thompson and Rosch), and the neurophenomenology research program that aimed to integrate first-person experiential reports with third-person neuroscience. Varela's work on the immune system as a cognitive network and on the self as an emergent biological process has direct relevance to the holobiont perspective.

**Key works:**
- Varela, F. J., Thompson, E., & Rosch, E. (1991). *The Embodied Mind*. MIT Press.
- Varela, F. J., Maturana, H. R., & Uribe, R. (1974). Autopoiesis: The organization of living systems, its characterization and a model. *BioSystems*, 5(4), 187–196.

**Characterization:** The biologist-philosopher who insisted that self and world are not separate domains but aspects of a single, ongoing, embodied process — and built one of the most sophisticated frameworks for understanding that process.
