# Section 2: The Holobiont Self

## You Are Not Alone in Here

If someone asked you to count the cells in your body, you might reasonably expect to arrive at a large number — 37 trillion, by recent careful estimates (Bianconi et al., 2013). You would be right. But you would also be answering the wrong question, because the cells in your body that carry your DNA are not the only cells in your body. The human body also hosts approximately 38 trillion microbial cells — bacteria, archaea, fungi, and viruses — with their own genomes, their own metabolisms, and their own evolutionary histories (Sender, Fuchs, & Milo, 2016). The ratio of human to microbial cells is roughly 1:1; the ratio of human to microbial genes is approximately 1:100.

This is not news, strictly speaking — microbiologists have known for decades that the human body is colonized by microorganisms. What is newer is the recognition that these microorganisms are not merely passengers. They are active participants in the physiology, immunology, neurology, and behavior of the host. The human body is not a self-contained individual but a holobiont — a composite of many different genomes, engaged in ongoing co-evolutionary relationships (Margulis & Fester, 1991; Bordenstein & Theis, 2015).

The implications for self-understanding are significant. If the gut microbiome influences behavior, mood, and cognitive development — and there is increasing evidence that it does — then "your" thoughts and feelings are not solely the product of "your" neurons. They are the product of a distributed biological system that includes organisms with their own evolutionary interests, their own communication systems, and their own influence over the neurotransmitter environment of the human brain.

## The Gut-Brain Axis

The gut contains the enteric nervous system — a network of approximately 500 million neurons embedded in the gastrointestinal tract, sometimes called the "second brain" — which communicates bidirectionally with the central nervous system via the vagus nerve, the spinal cord, and blood-borne signals (Gershon, 1998). Gut bacteria influence the gut-brain axis through multiple mechanisms: by producing neurotransmitters and their precursors (serotonin, dopamine, GABA, and others), by modulating enteric nervous system activity, by producing short-chain fatty acids that cross the blood-brain barrier, and by regulating the activity of the vagus nerve directly.

The behavioral consequences of gut microbiome composition are becoming increasingly well-documented. John Cryan and Ted Dinan, two of the leading researchers in this area, reviewed evidence from animal studies showing that germ-free mice — raised without any gut bacteria — exhibit significantly altered stress responses, social behavior, and anxiety-like behaviors compared to conventionally colonized mice; and that some of these behavioral differences can be reversed by colonizing germ-free mice with specific bacterial strains or with the complete microbiome from normal donors (Cryan & Dinan, 2012).

In humans, the evidence is less direct but accumulating. Observational studies have found associations between gut microbiome composition and depression, anxiety, autism spectrum disorder, and cognitive performance (Cryan et al., 2019). Intervention studies using probiotics have demonstrated modest but measurable effects on mood and anxiety in some populations. The psychobiotics field — the study of live bacterial interventions with mental health benefits — is in its early stages but is attracting serious scientific and clinical attention.

The mechanism is not fully understood. The gut microbiome does not control the brain like a puppeteer; the relationship is one of mutual influence in a complex bidirectional system. But the influence is real enough that it is no longer possible to understand mood, cognition, and behavior as products exclusively of the central nervous system. They are products of the holobiont — the whole composite system, including its microbial participants.

## Mitochondria: Ancient Cognitive Partners

The influence of non-human biology on human cognition extends further than the gut microbiome, to the very cells that make up the central nervous system. Every neuron in the human brain contains hundreds to thousands of mitochondria — the organelles responsible for aerobic energy production. These mitochondria are not human inventions. They are the descendants of alpha-proteobacteria that were engulfed by ancestral eukaryotic cells approximately 1.5 billion years ago and, instead of being digested, were incorporated as permanent endosymbionts (Margulis, 1981).

Mitochondria retain their own circular genome — a remnant of the ancestral bacterial genome, with 37 genes in humans — their own ribosomes, their own membrane system, and their own evolutionary interests, though these have been dramatically curtailed by the transfer of most mitochondrial genes to the nuclear genome over evolutionary time. They divide by binary fission, not by the cell cycle machinery of eukaryotes. In a very real sense, every human neuron is a collaboration between a eukaryotic host cell and a bacterial endosymbiont.

The cognitive relevance of this is not metaphorical. Neuronal function requires enormous amounts of ATP — the energy currency of the cell — and that ATP is generated by mitochondria. Neurons that are deprived of mitochondrial function — by mitochondrial mutations, by metabolic disruption, or by excitotoxic damage — cannot maintain membrane potential, cannot fire action potentials, and cannot support synaptic transmission. The entire electrical activity of the brain depends on its mitochondrial partners.

Beyond energy supply, mitochondria have more specific roles in neural function: they regulate calcium dynamics that control synaptic release; they produce reactive oxygen species that serve as signaling molecules; they participate in programmed cell death (apoptosis); and they are dynamically distributed within neurons, moving toward synapses during activity and retreating during rest (Devine & Bhatt, 2018). Mitochondrial dysfunction is implicated in a wide range of neurological conditions, including Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis.

Lynn Margulis spent decades arguing that the endosymbiotic origin of mitochondria (and chloroplasts) was among the most significant evolutionary events in the history of life (Margulis, 1981). She was right, and her view — that the eukaryotic cell is a symbiotic composite — is now mainstream biology. The implication for basal cognition is direct: even at the level of the individual neuron, "cognitive" function is a product of evolutionary collaboration between organisms with different ancestries and different genetic interests.

## The Composite Self

These considerations — the gut microbiome's influence on behavior, the mitochondrial partnership at the heart of every neuron — suggest a reconception of the self that is more distributed, more biological, and more strange than the commonsense picture.

The commonsense picture is this: "I" am my brain, or my nervous system, or perhaps my body as a whole, with the microbiome as a helpful but external service provider. The revised picture is: "I" am a collaborative process involving approximately 30,000 human genes, several million microbial genes, and the ongoing negotiation among all of these different biological actors within the physical boundaries of a body that, at the level of the cell, is partly bacterial in origin.

This is not a reductio ad absurdum — it is not an argument that the self is an illusion, or that personal identity is meaningless. It is a revised account of what the self is: not a single process in a single substrate, but an emergent property of an ongoing biological collaboration. This is consistent with what we know about emergence in complex systems generally: the whole can be real and causally efficacious even when the parts are heterogeneous and have their own dynamics.

What changes is the ecological context of the self. If my gut microbiome influences my behavior, then my behavior is partly a product of organisms that have their own evolutionary interests — interests that may not always align with what is good for me (the host). If my mitochondria are descendants of bacteria, then every act of cognition depends on an evolutionary partnership that has been ongoing for 1.5 billion years. I am embedded in the living world not just ecologically but biologically — at the level of my cells and my genes.

The philosophical tradition of the "extended mind" (Clark & Chalmers, 1998) argued that cognitive processes extend beyond the brain to include notebooks, smartphones, and other external tools. The holobiont self extends this picture further: the boundaries of the cognitive self extend below the brain, into the microbiome and the mitochondria, and outward into the ecological relationships on which the microbiome depends. The self is not a thing; it is a process, and that process has no sharp boundary.
