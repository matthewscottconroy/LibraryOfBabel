# Section 3: Implications for Medicine

## The Body as a Cognitive System

Modern medicine has been enormously successful by treating the body as a machine — a complex assemblage of organs, cells, and molecules that can be diagnosed in terms of structural abnormalities, biochemical imbalances, and pathogen invasion, then repaired by surgery, drugs, and targeted intervention. The machine metaphor has produced antibiotics, vaccines, statins, chemotherapy, and organ transplantation. These are genuine triumphs.

But the machine metaphor is incomplete, and some of its incompleteness is now clinically relevant. The body is not just a machine; it is a collection of cognitively active biological systems — cells, tissues, organs, and microbial communities — each with their own information-processing capacities, their own goal-directed behaviors, and their own adaptive responses to perturbation. Medicine that ignores these cognitive properties — that treats cells as passive recipients of chemical signals rather than active agents with their own decision-making machinery — may be missing important leverage points for therapeutic intervention.

This section examines three areas where the cognitive framing of biological systems has already generated, or is beginning to generate, clinically relevant insights: bioelectric medicine for cancer and regeneration; the gut microbiome as a cognitive system to be therapeutically engaged; and the emerging science of psychobiotics.

## Bioelectric Medicine: Cancer and Regeneration

Cancer is, among other things, a failure of the body's cognitive governance system. Normal cells are embedded in a tissue with its own goals — to maintain the structure and function of the organ — and they generally cooperate in pursuing those goals, dividing when tissues are damaged and ceasing division when the tissue is repaired. Cancer cells have defected from this cooperation: they divide uncontrollably, invade neighboring tissues, suppress immune responses, and commandeer the blood supply to feed their own growth. From Michael Levin's bioelectric perspective, cancer is not merely a genetic disease — it is a behavioral disease, in which cells have ceased to receive or respond to the bioelectric signals that embed them in the tissue-level cognitive community (Levin, 2021).

This reconceptualization has therapeutic implications. Standard cancer treatment aims to kill cancer cells — with surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy. These approaches work, often dramatically, but they also damage normal tissue and frequently fail to prevent recurrence, because they address the symptom (the dividing cancer cells) rather than the underlying failure of bioelectric governance.

Levin and collaborators have demonstrated that resetting the bioelectric state of tumor tissue — using ion channel modulators to restore normal membrane voltage patterns in cancerous tissue — can suppress tumor growth in model organisms without killing the cancer cells (Chernet & Levin, 2013). The idea is that cancer cells, presented with the correct bioelectric environment, may re-integrate into normal tissue behavior: they may stop defecting from the tissue's cognitive community if the signals that defined the community are restored. This is a fundamentally different therapeutic logic — not killing aberrant cells but re-educating them.

This approach remains experimental and has not yet been translated into clinical practice for most cancer types. But it represents a research program that would not be conceivable without the cognitive framing: the idea that cancer is a breakdown of tissue-level governance, and that restoring governance rather than eliminating defectors is a possible therapeutic strategy.

The same bioelectric medicine framework applies to regeneration. Planarian flatworms can regenerate almost any lost body part; salamanders can regenerate limbs, eyes, and heart tissue; humans can regenerate liver tissue and peripheral nerve fibers but not spinal cord or cardiac muscle. The variation in regenerative capacity across species has long been a puzzle. Levin's group has shown that this variation is partly explicable in terms of the bioelectric "memory" maintained in tissue: organisms with high regenerative capacity maintain, in their tissue bioelectric patterns, a reference representation of the target morphology — and can use that representation to guide regeneration after injury (Levin, 2021).

Experimentally manipulating the bioelectric patterns in non-regenerating organisms — using drugs that modify ion channel activity — can induce regenerative behaviors that those organisms do not display spontaneously. Frogs induced to grow a complete (functional, if rudimentary) leg after amputation using a bioreactor that provided appropriate bioelectric signals is a demonstration that the regenerative capacity exists but is suppressed in non-regenerating organisms (Murugan et al., 2022). This is medicine that treats the body's cognitive signaling system rather than its cellular machinery directly.

## The Gut Microbiome as Therapeutic Target

If the gut microbiome influences cognition, mood, and behavior through its effects on the gut-brain axis — as Section 38.2 reviewed — then therapeutic manipulation of the microbiome is a legitimate target for the treatment of neurological and psychiatric conditions. This insight drives the emerging fields of psychobiotics and microbiome-based psychiatry.

Psychobiotics are defined as live microorganisms that, when ingested in adequate amounts, confer a mental health benefit on the host (Dinan, Stanton, & Cryan, 2013). The concept is an extension of the probiotic concept — the use of live bacteria to restore or supplement the gut microbiome — to the specific domain of mental health. Initial evidence for psychobiotics came from animal studies showing that colonization of germ-free mice with specific bacterial strains — particularly *Lactobacillus rhamnosus* — could reduce anxiety-like behavior and alter stress hormone responses in ways that depended on intact vagus nerve signaling (Bravo et al., 2011).

Human trials of psychobiotics have been more modest in their results: well-designed randomized controlled trials have found statistically significant but clinically modest effects of probiotic supplementation on self-reported depression and anxiety in non-clinical populations, and some evidence for effects in clinical populations (Ng et al., 2019). The effects are smaller and more variable than the animal data would suggest, which is not surprising given the greater complexity of the human gut ecosystem and the difficulty of standardizing probiotic interventions in a highly variable microbiome.

The therapeutic potential is real enough to attract serious investment, but the field needs better mechanistic understanding — specifically, which bacterial species have which effects through which mechanisms, in which host populations — before targeted psychobiotic interventions will be genuinely reliable. The science is young, and the commercial enthusiasm around probiotics in general has sometimes outrun the evidence.

Beyond live probiotics, therapeutic manipulation of the gut microbiome can occur through diet (the composition of the diet is the primary determinant of gut microbiome composition), through fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT — transferring the complete microbiome from a healthy donor to a recipient), and through precision prebiotic interventions (compounds that selectively feed beneficial bacterial species). FMT has demonstrated remarkable efficacy for recurrent *Clostridioides difficile* infection and is under active investigation for psychiatric conditions, metabolic disease, and autoimmune conditions (Smits et al., 2016).

The framing that makes these interventions conceptually coherent is the holobiont perspective: the gut microbiome is not a passenger to be managed but a cognitive system to be treated. Its composition and activity are as medically relevant as the composition and activity of any other organ, and interventions that restore it to healthy function are as legitimate as interventions targeting the liver, the heart, or the brain.

## Rethinking Drug Targets

The cognitive framing of biological systems suggests a broader reconceptualization of what drug targets are. Standard drug development targets specific molecular interactions: a drug binds to a receptor, enzyme, or ion channel and modulates its activity. This is often effective, but it treats the molecular target as if it were a passive component in a machine rather than an element in an adaptive cognitive system.

Cells adapt to drugs. When a receptor is blocked chronically, the cell often upregulates the expression of that receptor — a homeostatic response that partially compensates for the pharmacological effect. When a metabolic enzyme is inhibited, the cell may upregulate alternative metabolic pathways. When an ion channel is targeted, cells may adjust the expression of other channels to maintain appropriate excitability. These adaptive responses are not bugs in the system; they are features — the cognitive adaptations of cells and tissues to perturbation. They are also major reasons why drugs that work brilliantly in the short term often lose efficacy over time, or produce tolerance and dependence.

Understanding these cellular adaptations as cognitive responses — as the product of information-processing systems that are responding to the perturbation just as they respond to any other environmental change — suggests different therapeutic strategies. Rather than fighting adaptive responses, therapies might be designed to work with them: using drugs that reset signaling systems to new stable states, rather than chronically blocking or activating them; using temporal dosing patterns that exploit adaptive dynamics rather than ignoring them; or using bioelectric interventions that alter the information-processing context in which molecular targets operate.

The cancer treatment analogy is instructive: rather than killing cells (fighting the symptom), restore the signaling environment (address the cognitive context). This logic applies broadly. Depression, for example, is conventionally treated with drugs that increase synaptic serotonin or dopamine — addressing what appear to be molecular deficits. But the depressed brain is not merely chemically deficient; it is a cognitive system that has reached a dysfunctional stable state, in which patterns of neural activity, connectivity, and information processing reinforce each other in ways that perpetuate the disorder. Treatments that reset the system to a different stable state — electroconvulsive therapy, psychedelic-assisted therapy, deep brain stimulation — may work precisely because they do not merely supplement a chemical but disrupt the cognitive attractor state and allow reorganization (Carhart-Harris et al., 2021).

This is speculative, and the evidence for specific mechanistic claims varies considerably. But the conceptual framework — treating the body's biological systems as cognitive agents, not passive machinery, and designing interventions that work with their adaptive capacities rather than against them — is supported by the full weight of the basal cognition research reviewed in this book. Medicine that takes this framework seriously will look different from medicine that treats the body as a machine. It will be more adaptive, more personalized, more attentive to the temporal dynamics of biological response, and more aware that the system being treated is always trying, in its own way, to achieve a form of health.
