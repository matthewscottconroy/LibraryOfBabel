# Chapter 39: Open Problems in Basal Cognition

## The Privilege of Ignorance

There is a particular kind of intellectual pleasure available at the frontier of a young field — the pleasure of being confronted with questions that are genuinely open, where the honest answer is "we don't know." This is not failure; it is the most valuable epistemic situation a scientist can occupy. The map still has white spaces. The territories adjacent to what is known are full of phenomena not yet described, questions not yet properly posed, and methods not yet invented.

Basal cognition is such a field. The preceding chapters have reviewed a remarkable body of evidence: bacteria that perform temporal differentiation and collective decision-making; slime molds that solve optimization problems and anticipate periodic stimuli; plants that habituate, communicate, and possibly learn; mycorrhizal networks that transport resources and information across forest floors. The evidence is genuine, the phenomena are real, and the scientific community is beginning to take them seriously.

But the theoretical foundations are still being laid. We do not have agreed-upon methods for measuring cognitive function in non-neural systems. We do not fully understand the physical mechanisms underlying most of the behaviors we observe. We do not know how neural cognition evolved from cellular cognition, or whether there were intermediate cognitive forms that are now extinct. And we do not know — cannot know, with current tools — whether any of these non-neural systems have subjective experience.

These are not minor lacunae. They are the central open problems of the field, and working through them honestly is what this chapter is for.

## The Central Question

**What are the deepest unsolved problems in basal cognition research, and what conceptual and methodological advances will be needed to make progress on them?** This is not a rhetorical question. It is the kind of question that should drive a research career.

## Chapter Roadmap

**Section 39.1 — The Measurement Problem** examines how we assess cognitive function in systems that lack nervous systems, asking what criteria should be used, how to avoid anthropocentric baselines, and what information-theoretic approaches might offer.

**Section 39.2 — The Mechanism Problem** asks what physical processes actually implement basal cognition: bioelectricity, chemistry, or mechanics? Are there universal mechanisms? How big is the gap between computational models and biological reality?

**Section 39.3 — The Evolution Problem** investigates how neural cognition evolved from cellular cognition, when subjectivity might have first appeared, and whether some basal cognitive capacities were lost in the transition to neural organization.

**Section 39.4 — The Consciousness Problem** confronts the hardest question directly: is there something it is like to be *Physarum polycephalum*? And what would we need to answer that question?
