# Chapter 39: Further Reading

## The Measurement Problem

**Lyon, P. (2006). The biogenic approach to cognition. *Cognitive Processing*, 7(1), 11–29.**
The founding paper of the biogenic approach to cognition — starting from what simple organisms do and building up, rather than starting from human cognition and asking which organisms approach it. Provides a methodological foundation for avoiding anthropocentric baselines.

**Ginsburg, S., & Jablonka, E. (2019). *The Evolution of the Sensitive Soul: Learning and the Origins of Consciousness*. MIT Press.**
A rigorous and ambitious attempt to identify the evolutionary transition to consciousness by specifying what learning capacity was necessary and sufficient for consciousness to appear. Whether or not you agree with the specific criterion (unlimited associative learning), the approach — taking the measurement problem seriously and proposing a solution — is exemplary.

**Trewavas, A. (2003). Aspects of plant intelligence. *Annals of Botany*, 92(1), 1–20.**
An early and influential argument for extending the language of intelligence to plants, with careful discussion of the behavioral criteria being applied. Valuable for seeing both what the concept of plant intelligence covers and where it is contested.

## The Mechanism Problem

**Bray, D. (2009). *Wetware: A Computer in Every Living Cell*. Yale University Press.**
An accessible and rigorous account of the computational properties of cellular signaling networks. Bray's analysis of bacterial chemotaxis as a form of information processing is particularly relevant to the mechanism problem — it shows how a detailed mechanistic account enriches rather than deflates the cognitive interpretation.

**Tero, A., Takagi, S., Saigusa, T., Ito, K., Bebber, D. P., Fricker, M. D., ... & Nakagaki, T. (2010). Rules for biologically inspired adaptive network design. *Science*, 327(5964), 439–442.**
The paper describing the mathematical model of *Physarum* network optimization and its verification against real *Physarum* behavior. Exemplifies both the power of mathematical modeling in basal cognition research and the gap between the model and the mechanistic implementation.

## The Evolution Problem

**Godfrey-Smith, P. (2016). *Other Minds: The Octopus, the Sea, and the Deep Origins of Consciousness*. Farrar, Straus and Giroux.**
See earlier notes. The evolutionary analysis of consciousness through the lens of cephalopod cognition is essential for thinking about the evolution of experience from non-neural antecedents.

**Ginsburg, S., & Jablonka, E. (2019). *The Evolution of the Sensitive Soul: Learning and the Origins of Consciousness*. MIT Press.**
Also relevant here: the authors identify a specific evolutionary transition — the emergence of unlimited associative learning — as the threshold for consciousness. Whether this criterion is correct is contested, but the evolutionary framing is the right one for thinking about the evolution of subjectivity.

**King, N., Westbrook, M. J., Young, S. L., Kuo, A., Abedin, M., Chapman, J., ... & Rokhsar, D. (2008). The genome of the choanoflagellate *Monosiga brevicollis* and the origin of metazoans. *Nature*, 451(7180), 783–788.**
The key genomic paper showing that the molecular toolkit for neural communication was already present in the closest non-animal relatives of animals. Establishes the evolutionary context for the origin of neurons.

## The Consciousness Problem

**Nagel, T. (1974). What is it like to be a bat? *Philosophical Review*, 83(4), 435–450.**
Essential. See earlier note.

**Chalmers, D. J. (1995). Facing up to the problem of consciousness. *Journal of Consciousness Studies*, 2(3), 200–219.**
Essential. See earlier note.

**Metzinger, T. (2003). *Being No One: The Self-Model Theory of Subjectivity*. MIT Press.**
A technically demanding but intellectually rich theory of consciousness as the product of a self-model — a representation of oneself that is transparent (experienced as real rather than as a model). Metzinger's theory has implications for when in evolutionary history self-models might have appeared and what minimal conditions they require. Not for the faint of heart, but rewarding for those who engage seriously.

**Bayne, T., Shulman, G. L., & Posner, M. I. (2016). Glossary of terms for consciousness and related phenomena. *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 20(12), 901–907.**
A useful reference for the vocabulary of consciousness research. The lack of agreed-upon definitions has been a persistent problem in the field; this glossary represents a careful attempt to address it.

## Philosophical Frameworks

**Strawson, G. (2006). Realistic monism: Why physicalism entails panpsychism. *Journal of Consciousness Studies*, 13(10–11), 3–31.**
Essential. See earlier note.

**Frankish, K. (2016). Illusionism as a theory of consciousness. *Journal of Consciousness Studies*, 23(11–12), 11–39.**
The strongest version of the eliminativist position on consciousness — the view that phenomenal consciousness as we conceive it does not exist, and that the impression of phenomenal consciousness is itself an illusion generated by the brain. An important counterpoint to the panpsychist position and to the view that basal cognition research is studying systems with genuine phenomenal experience.
