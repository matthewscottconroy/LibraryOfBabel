# Section 3: The Evolution Problem

## From Molecules to Minds: The Missing Transitions

The fossil record of brains is sparse. Neurons, unlike bones, do not mineralize, and the soft tissue of nervous systems is rarely preserved in a form that allows detailed reconstruction of its organization. What we have is molecular phylogenetics — the comparison of genetic sequences across living organisms — and some exceptional fossil preservation of nervous system morphology in Cambrian animals. From these sources, we can reconstruct the broad outlines of nervous system evolution: that nervous systems appeared in early metazoans, that complex brains evolved multiple times in different animal lineages, and that the transition from cellular to neural cognition was gradual rather than abrupt.

But the detail we would most like — the intermediate forms, the specific evolutionary steps, the particular mutations and developmental innovations that transformed cellular signaling networks into synaptic networks — is largely unavailable. The evolution of the neuron from the cell, of the neural circuit from cellular signaling, and of the nervous system from isolated neurons is a story that can be sketched but not yet fully told.

This is the evolution problem in basal cognition: **how did neural cognition evolve from cellular cognition?** And the related question: **are there forms of cognitive experience, or cognitive function, that are characteristic of non-neural organisms and were lost or transformed in the evolution of neural systems?**

## The Evolution of the Neuron

The most basic version of the evolution problem is: how did the neuron evolve? What was the precursor to the neuron in the evolution of the metazoa, and what evolutionary pressures drove the specialization of that precursor into the distinctive form of a neuron?

The most plausible answer draws on the molecular toolkit of cellular signaling that predates metazoans. Many of the proteins involved in neural function — voltage-gated ion channels, calcium-regulated secretion machinery, post-synaptic scaffold proteins — are present in organisms that lack neurons entirely. Choanoflagellates — the closest single-celled relatives of animals — have genes encoding proteins with domains found in synaptic scaffold proteins, suggesting that the molecular components of the synapse were present before neurons evolved (King et al., 2008). Filastrea — another animal relative — have genes encoding proteins involved in neuronal secretion and postsynaptic signaling (Sebe-Pedros et al., 2017).

This suggests that the evolution of the neuron was not the invention of new molecular machinery but the repurposing and specialization of molecular components that already existed in non-neural ancestors. The cell's existing machinery for secretion (exocytosis), membrane signaling (ion channels), and cell-cell communication (gap junctions and extracellular ligands) was co-opted and elaborated into the neuron's distinctive apparatus. The cognitive functions that neurons perform may therefore have evolutionary roots in the cellular processes that implemented basal cognition in pre-neural ancestors.

This is consistent with what we observe in the basal cognition of living non-neural organisms. The ion channels that mediate action potentials in neurons are evolutionary relatives of the ion channels that maintain membrane potential in bacteria and control cytoplasmic dynamics in slime molds. The calcium signaling that regulates synaptic vesicle release is an elaboration of the calcium signaling that controls contractility in non-muscle cells. The molecular logic of synaptic plasticity — long-term potentiation requiring NMDA receptor activation, calcium influx, and kinase activation — has structural parallels in the molecular logic of other forms of cellular memory. The neuron is a cell that has specialized in information processing; its molecular toolkit is continuous with the toolkit that all cells use for signaling and response.

## The Evolution of Subjectivity: When Did Experience Begin?

If neurons evolved from non-neural cells by specialization of existing molecular machinery, and if that machinery was implementing some form of cognitive function in pre-neural ancestors, then the evolution of subjectivity — of phenomenal experience — raises a profound question: at what point in the evolution of life did experience first appear?

Three broad positions can be distinguished:

**The neuron threshold view:** Experience requires neurons. Pre-neural organisms may have sophisticated information processing, but they are "in the dark" — there is nothing it is like to be them. Experience appeared with the evolution of neural systems, perhaps requiring a certain neural complexity or the specific architecture of excitatory-inhibitory circuits.

**The cellular threshold view:** Experience, in some minimal form, appeared with the evolution of life — or at least with the evolution of cells capable of minimal cognition. Single cells have minimal experience; neural systems elaborate it into the rich phenomenal life of complex animals. This view is compatible with a form of panpsychism restricted to living things (biopsychism).

**The panpsychist view:** Experience, in some form, is a fundamental property of matter and did not "begin" at any point in evolutionary history. What evolved was not experience itself but the organizational complexity that gives experiences the specific character they have — the elaboration of simple physical proto-experience into the rich, integrated phenomenal life of complex organisms.

We do not have the tools to decide between these views empirically. The third-person methods of science cannot directly access first-person experience. But the views have different implications for the research program of basal cognition: on the first view, basal cognition research is studying sophisticated information processing without experience; on the second and third views, it is studying forms of experience radically different from our own.

## Lost Cognitive Capacities?

One of the most counterintuitive possibilities raised by evolutionary analysis of cognition is that some non-neural organisms may possess cognitive capacities that neural organisms have lost. The evolution of nervous systems was an elaboration of cellular signaling, but elaboration is not always strictly additive — the specialization of neurons for rapid information transmission may have come at the cost of other computational properties that generalist cells possess.

Consider the relationship between speed and flexibility. Neurons are extraordinarily fast information processors — they can fire at rates of hundreds of hertz and transmit signals over long distances in milliseconds. But speed imposes costs: neurons are metabolically expensive, require precise spatial organization, and are vulnerable to damage in ways that diffuse cellular networks are not. Non-neural organisms, with their slower but more distributed signaling, may be more robust to damage, more capable of parallel processing across many dimensions simultaneously, and more adaptable to novel environments in ways that neural systems — with their specialized circuits — are not.

This is speculative, but it is supported by some empirical observations. The regenerative capacity of planarian flatworms — their ability to regrow virtually any missing body part — exceeds that of most neural animals, suggesting that the bioelectric governance capacity underlying regeneration is present in non-neural organisms (or organisms with very simple nervous systems) in ways that are suppressed or lost in more neurally complex animals. The distributed, network-based computation of mycorrhizal networks may implement forms of optimization — minimizing path length, maximizing connectivity, allocating resources across multiple connected nodes — that centralized neural computation cannot approach without enormous architectural overhead.

If non-neural organisms possess cognitive capacities that neural organisms have lost or traded away for other advantages, then basal cognition research is not merely the study of primitive antecedents to "real" cognition. It is the study of a different branch of the cognitive possibility space — one that evolution explored before the neuron, and that may contain solutions to problems that the neuron-based branch has not yet solved.

## Methodological Approaches to the Evolution Problem

Several experimental and comparative approaches are being used to address the evolution problem, though none yet provides the complete picture:

**Comparative genomics** of cognitive genes — examining which components of the neural toolkit are present in non-neural organisms, and which are uniquely neural innovations — gives information about the evolutionary order of molecular events in the transition from cellular to neural cognition.

**Evolutionary developmental biology (evo-devo)** of nervous system development — examining which developmental pathways specify neurons, and how these pathways are related to the signaling pathways of non-neural ancestors — can reveal the developmental logic underlying the evolution of neurons.

**Experimental evolution** — evolving simple organisms under selection pressures that favor cognitive sophistication — can explore what evolutionary paths lead from cellular cognition toward neural-like cognition, and what the intermediate forms look like.

**Phylogenomics of basal animals** — examining organisms at the base of the animal phylogeny (sponges, placozoans, ctenophores) that either lack nervous systems or have the simplest known ones — can identify the evolutionary context in which neurons first appeared and what cognitive capacities existed before them.

None of these approaches is sufficient alone, and all face significant practical challenges. But together they constitute a research program that can make progress on the evolution problem, even if it cannot fully resolve it.
