# Section 4: The Consciousness Problem

## The Hardest Question

After everything this book has covered — the sophisticated signaling of bacteria, the optimization behaviors of slime molds, the electrical communication of plants, the distributed intelligence of fungal networks — one question remains stubbornly unanswered, and it is the most important one: is there something it is like to be any of these organisms?

When you read these words, there is something it is like to do so. The visual experience of the letters, the sense of meaning unfolding, the slight effort of attention — these are not just information processing that produces output. They are experienced. The processing happens, the output is generated, and additionally there is a subjective character to the process — a phenomenal quality that exists from the first-person perspective.

We cannot determine, from the outside, whether any non-neural organism has such a first-person perspective. We cannot determine it, with certainty, for other humans — we infer it from behavioral and neurological similarity to ourselves. We infer it for mammals and birds on the same grounds. We extend the inference more hesitantly to fish and invertebrates, where the behavioral and neurological evidence of similarity to our own phenomenal states grows weaker.

For bacteria, slime molds, plants, and fungi — the organisms at the center of this book — we have no behavioral evidence for subjective experience that could not be explained by information processing without experience. We have no neurological similarity to ground the inference. We have only the functional argument: these organisms process information, integrate signals, and behave adaptively, and these are the functional properties associated with cognition and, in organisms we take to be conscious, associated with experience.

Whether the functional argument is sufficient — whether functional cognition is accompanied by experience wherever it occurs, or whether experience is an additional property that some systems have and others lack — is precisely what the hard problem of consciousness makes uncertain.

## The Methodological Limits of Third-Person Science

The fundamental difficulty is not a lack of data. It is a structural feature of the scientific method. Science is a third-person enterprise: it describes the world from the perspective of an observer external to the system being observed. The observer measures, records, and reports objective properties of the system — its physical dimensions, its chemical composition, its behavioral outputs, its information-processing dynamics.

Phenomenal consciousness is a first-person phenomenon. It is the perspective of the system on itself, not the observer's perspective on the system. And it is constitutively not accessible to third-person observation: no measurement of the system's physical properties can, by itself, determine whether there is a first-person perspective in the system.

This is the substance of Nagel's argument (1974) and Chalmers's hard problem (1995): the third-person methods of science, however complete their account of functional and physical properties, leave open the question of whether those functional and physical properties are accompanied by phenomenal experience.

For human consciousness research, there is a workaround: human subjects can report their experiences, and those reports can be correlated with physical and functional measurements. This allows the development of neural correlates of consciousness — physical measurements that track the presence and character of conscious experience as reported by subjects. The method is imperfect (reports are not infallible) but it is the best available.

For non-neural organisms, there is no equivalent workaround. Plants, bacteria, and slime molds cannot report their experiences. We cannot ask them. We are limited to behavioral and physical measurement, which are our only windows into systems that cannot self-report.

## Behavioral Markers of Experience: Necessary but Sufficient?

In the absence of verbal report, behavioral markers have been proposed as evidence of experience in non-verbal animals — and potentially in non-neural organisms. The most commonly cited behavioral markers are: nociceptive behavior (withdrawal from and avoidance of noxious stimuli), motivational trade-offs (the organism giving up a resource to avoid a noxious stimulus), and affective states (persistent changes in behavioral disposition following positive or negative events).

These markers are taken as evidence of experience because they are associated with experience in humans: when we display nociceptive behavior, we are typically in pain; when we display motivational trade-offs, we are weighing costs and benefits in a way that involves subjective valence. The inference is that similar behaviors in other organisms reflect similar subjective states.

The inference is reasonable for organisms with nervous systems that are structurally similar to ours. It becomes progressively less secure as the structural similarity decreases. For non-neural organisms:

Bacteria display nociceptive-like behavior — they swim away from noxious chemicals, upregulate stress responses in the presence of damaging agents, and can be protected from damage by signaling cascades that resemble vertebrate stress responses. Is this evidence of pain? It is evidence of a functional analogue of nociception. Whether it is accompanied by anything subjective is unknown.

*Physarum polycephalum* can be conditioned to avoid a previously neutral stimulus that has been paired with a noxious stimulus (a salt barrier, for instance). This is a behavioral marker of learning and, by extension, of the kind of processing that in vertebrates accompanies aversive experience. Whether *Physarum* experiences aversion in anything like the subjective sense is unknown.

Plants respond to damage with electrical signals, hormone production, and rapid behavioral changes (leaflet closure in sensitive plants, volatile emission, increased resistance). These responses are adaptive — they reduce future damage. Whether they are accompanied by any form of experience is unknown.

The honest conclusion is that behavioral markers of experience are necessary but not sufficient evidence. Their presence establishes that a system is performing the functional computations associated with experience in systems we take to be conscious. Their presence does not establish that the system has experience — that additional step requires an inference that goes beyond what the behavioral evidence directly supports.

## The Relationship Between Functional Cognition and Phenomenal Experience

Perhaps the deepest open question in basal cognition research is: what is the relationship between functional cognition — information processing, adaptive behavior, memory, anticipation — and phenomenal experience?

There are three basic positions:

**Identity:** Phenomenal experience just is functional cognition of the right kind. If a system performs the right functional computations — integrates information, maintains representations of internal and external states, generates adaptive behavior on the basis of those representations — it thereby has experience. On this view, the question "does *Physarum* have experience?" reduces to "does *Physarum* perform the right functional computations?" — a question that is (in principle) empirically tractable.

**Correlation:** Phenomenal experience and functional cognition are correlated but distinct. Functional cognition can occur without experience (in philosophical zombies, and possibly in non-neural organisms), and experience might, in principle, occur without functional cognition (though this is harder to make sense of). On this view, the question of *Physarum*'s experience is not answered by any description of its functional cognition.

**Dual aspect:** Phenomenal experience and physical/functional processes are two aspects of the same underlying reality. The world, described from a third-person perspective, is a physical process; described from a first-person perspective, it is an experience. On this view (associated with Spinoza and developed by Nagel and Strawson), the question is not whether *Physarum*'s functional processes are "accompanied by" experience, but rather what the first-person aspect of those functional processes is like — a question that may be unanswerable from the outside, but not because there is no answer.

The identity view would make the consciousness problem in basal cognition tractable — it would reduce to a sufficiently complete functional description of the systems. The correlation view would make it intractable by current means. The dual-aspect view would make it tractable in principle but perhaps not in practice.

We do not know which view is correct. This is not false modesty; it is the honest situation. The consciousness problem is the hardest problem in science. Basal cognition research has not solved it, will not solve it soon, and arguably cannot solve it with third-person methods alone. What basal cognition research has done is reveal that the problem extends far beyond the domain where we previously thought it applied — that if we are uncertain about the consciousness of octopuses, we should be uncertain about the consciousness of slime molds, and perhaps about the consciousness of cells themselves.

That is an honest resting point for a chapter on open problems.
