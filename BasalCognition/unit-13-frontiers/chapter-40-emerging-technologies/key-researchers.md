# Chapter 40: Key Researchers

## Georg Nagel
**Institution:** University of Würzburg, Department of Neurophysiology
**Primary contributions:** Identified and characterized channelrhodopsin-2, the light-gated ion channel that made modern optogenetics possible. Nagel's work began in the context of studying phototaxis in *Chlamydomonas* algae — an example of basal cognition research that had transformative consequences for neuroscience.

**Key works:**
- Nagel, G., Szellas, T., Huhn, W., Kateriya, S., Adeishvili, N., Berthold, P., ... & Bamberg, E. (2003). Channelrhodopsin-2, a directly light-gated cation-selective membrane channel. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 100(24), 13940–13945.

**Characterization:** The biophysicist who found a cognitive tool in an alga — and whose curiosity about how a single-celled organism navigates by light gave neuroscience one of its most powerful technologies.

## Karl Deisseroth
**Institution:** Stanford University, Department of Bioengineering and Psychiatry
**Primary contributions:** Developed optogenetics into a general neuroscience tool, adapting channelrhodopsins and related proteins for controlling specific neuron types in intact animals. Deisseroth's laboratory has also developed CLARITY and other tissue clearing and expansion microscopy techniques for whole-brain imaging.

**Key works:**
- Boyden, E. S., Zhang, F., Bamberg, E., Nagel, G., & Deisseroth, K. (2005). Millisecond-timescale, genetically targeted optical control of neural activity. *Nature Neuroscience*, 8(9), 1263–1268.
- Deisseroth, K. (2015). Optogenetics: 10 years of microbial opsins in neuroscience. *Nature Neuroscience*, 18(9), 1213–1225.

**Characterization:** The engineer-neuroscientist who turned channelrhodopsin into a technology — and transformed neuroscience's ability to establish causal relationships between neural activity and behavior.

## Mark Fricker
**Institution:** University of Oxford, Department of Plant Sciences
**Primary contributions:** Computational modeling and network analysis of fungal mycelial growth and transport. Fricker's laboratory has developed the mathematical tools for analyzing mycorrhizal and saprotrophic fungal networks as adaptive computing systems, characterizing their optimization properties and resilience.

**Key works:**
- Fricker, M. D., Heaton, L. L. M., Jones, N. S., & Boddy, L. (2017). The mycelium as a network. *Microbiology Spectrum*, 5(3), FUNK-0033-2016.
- Bebber, D. P., Hynes, J., Darrah, P. R., Boddy, L., & Fricker, M. D. (2007). Biological solutions to transport network design. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*, 274(1623), 2307–2315.

**Characterization:** The plant scientist who builds mathematical models of fungal intelligence — characterizing what networks compute by analyzing how they grow.

## Gürol Süel
**Institution:** University of California San Diego, School of Biological Sciences
**Primary contributions:** Electrophysiology of bacterial communities. Süel's laboratory discovered that bacterial biofilms generate propagating electrical signals — waves of potassium ion concentration that coordinate metabolic activity across the biofilm — providing direct evidence for electrical communication in non-neural systems.

**Key works:**
- Prindle, A., Liu, J., Asally, M., Ly, S., Garcia-Ojalvo, J., & Süel, G. M. (2015). Ion channels enable electrical communication in bacterial communities. *Nature*, 527(7576), 59–63.
- Liu, J., Martinez-Corral, R., Prindle, A., Lee, D. D., Larkin, J., Gabalda-Sagarra, M., ... & Süel, G. M. (2017). Coupling between distant biofilms and emergence of nutrient time-sharing. *Science*, 356(6338), 638–642.

**Characterization:** The physicist-turned-microbiologist who discovered that bacteria talk to each other electrically — and that biofilms are better understood as electrical circuits than as passive aggregates.

## Michael Levin
**Institution:** Tufts University, Allen Discovery Center
**Primary contributions:** See Chapters 38 and 39. In the context of emerging technologies, Levin has been a pioneer in applying bioelectric imaging (GEVIs, voltage-sensitive dyes) and optogenetic control to developing animal tissues, establishing the causal role of bioelectric patterns in morphogenesis.

**Key works:**
- Levin, M. (2021). Bioelectric signaling: Reprogrammable circuits underlying embryogenesis, regeneration, and cancer. *Cell*, 184(8), 1971–1989.
- Pai, V. P., Aw, S., Shomrat, T., Lemire, J. M., & Levin, M. (2012). Transmembrane voltage potential controls embryonic eye patterning in *Xenopus laevis*. *Development*, 139(2), 313–323.

**Characterization:** The biologist who has built the most complete experimental case for bioelectricity as a cognitive signal in developing organisms — using the full toolkit of modern imaging and manipulation to make the argument rigorously.

## Frances Arnold
**Institution:** Caltech, Division of Chemistry and Chemical Engineering
**Primary contributions:** Pioneered directed evolution as a method for engineering proteins with desired properties, demonstrating that evolutionary algorithms could find protein sequences with specific catalytic activities, stability, and binding properties far beyond what rational design could achieve. Nobel Prize in Chemistry, 2018.

**Key works:**
- Arnold, F. H. (2019). Innovation by evolution: Bringing new chemistry to life (Nobel Lecture). *Angewandte Chemie International Edition*, 58(41), 14420–14426.
- Chen, K., & Arnold, F. H. (1993). Tuning the activity of an enzyme for unusual environments: Sequential random mutagenesis of subtilisin E for catalysis in dimethylformamide. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 90(12), 5618–5622.

**Characterization:** The chemist who showed that evolution, not design, is the best strategy for engineering biological function — and won a Nobel Prize for the demonstration.
