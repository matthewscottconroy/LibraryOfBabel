# Section 3: Computational Modeling

## Why We Model

Biological systems are complex. "Complex" here means something specific: the behavior of the whole cannot be predicted by simple summation of the parts, because the parts interact with each other and with their environment in ways that generate emergent properties not present in any individual component. A bacterial colony is more than the sum of individual bacteria. A mycelial network is more than the sum of individual hyphal segments. Understanding these emergent properties requires tools that can capture the dynamics of many interacting components simultaneously — which is what computational models do.

Models serve several functions in science. They allow us to test whether a proposed mechanistic explanation is sufficient to account for observed behavior — whether the proposed mechanism, implemented in a computer, actually generates the behavior we observe. They allow us to explore the behavior of systems under conditions that cannot be achieved experimentally — to ask "what would happen if?" without performing the experiment. They generate quantitative predictions that can be tested experimentally, providing a rigorous check on the mechanistic account. And they can reveal unexpected emergent properties of complex systems that would not be intuitable from the mechanistic description alone.

Each of these functions is valuable in basal cognition research, and computational modeling is central to the field's development.

## Agent-Based Models of Bacterial Communities

Agent-based models (ABMs) simulate a collection of individual agents, each following a set of rules that govern their behavior, and observe the collective behavior that emerges from agent interactions. In the context of bacterial communities, each agent is a bacterium, governed by rules derived from known biological properties: it moves according to a run-and-tumble algorithm, it senses and responds to chemical gradients, it secretes and responds to quorum sensing signals, it divides when nutrient accumulation exceeds a threshold, and it dies when resources are depleted.

ABMs of bacterial communities have been used to study quorum sensing dynamics — how the transition from individual to collective behavior depends on population density, geometry, and signal diffusion — and how biofilm structure emerges from the interactions of individual cells (Xavier & Foster, 2007). These models have revealed that spatial heterogeneity in biofilms is not just a consequence of diffusion gradients but is actively generated by the behavior of individual cells, whose growth and death patterns create the layered structure of the biofilm.

ABMs are particularly well-suited for studying how collective cognitive behaviors emerge from individual cellular cognitive behaviors. A key question in basal cognition research is whether the collective behavior of a bacterial community can be reduced to the summed behavior of its individual members, or whether it constitutes a genuinely emergent property that requires higher-level explanation. ABMs can address this question by implementing the individual-level rules and asking whether the collective behavior that emerges matches what is observed biologically — and what individual-level rules are necessary and sufficient to produce the observed collective behavior.

## Network Models of Mycelial Computation

The mycelial network of a fungus — a graph of hyphal segments connected at branch points and anastomoses — is naturally suited to network-theoretic analysis. Network models represent the mycelium as a graph, with edges representing hyphal segments and nodes representing branch points or resource patches, and model the flow of material and information through the graph as a dynamic process governed by rules derived from the biology of cytoplasmic flow and local hyphal growth.

The most influential class of mycelial network models, developed by Mark Fricker and colleagues, is based on the Physarum-inspired adaptive network model: the conductance of each edge increases when material flux through it is high and decreases when flux is low, with a positive feedback that reinforces high-flux pathways and eliminates low-flux ones (Tero et al., 2010). This model, originally formulated for slime mold, applies remarkably well to fungal network dynamics and has been used to predict the topology of mycelial networks growing through heterogeneous landscapes.

Fricker's group has also developed more detailed mechanistic models of mycelial growth and transport, incorporating the known biophysics of cytoplasmic flow (governed by pressure gradients generated by actomyosin contraction), hyphal tip growth (governed by turgor pressure and wall synthesis), and anastomosis (governed by recognition signals). These mechanistic models generate network architectures that match observed fungal growth patterns, providing evidence that the observed network properties emerge from local biophysical rules rather than from any global plan.

Network analysis can also characterize the information-processing capacity of mycelial networks by computing properties like path redundancy (the number of independent paths between any two nodes), graph efficiency (the average inverse path length, measuring how quickly information can propagate across the network), and community structure (modules within the network that are more internally connected than externally). These properties are directly relevant to basal cognition: a network that efficiently connects all resource patches with minimal total length, while maintaining redundant paths for robustness, is implementing a specific optimization that can be characterized mathematically and compared to the behavior of optimal networks.

## Reaction-Diffusion Models of Morphogenetic Intelligence

Turing's (1952) reaction-diffusion model — a system of partial differential equations describing the concentrations of activating and inhibiting chemical species diffusing through a medium and reacting with each other — has become one of the most powerful frameworks in theoretical biology for modeling the emergence of spatial pattern from homogeneous initial conditions.

Modern reaction-diffusion models have grown substantially more sophisticated than Turing's original two-component system. They now include multiple components, nonlinear kinetics, spatial heterogeneity in diffusion coefficients, mechanical feedback (the pattern of chemical concentration can mechanically deform the medium, which changes the diffusion properties, which changes the pattern), and coupling to other signaling pathways.

In the context of basal cognition, reaction-diffusion models are particularly important for understanding morphogenetic intelligence — the capacity of developing biological systems to generate complex, reproducible body plans from simple initial conditions and local rules. The question of how morphogenesis "knows" what shape to produce is one of the central questions in developmental biology, and reaction-diffusion models provide a principled mechanism: the target morphology is an attractor of the reaction-diffusion dynamics, encoded in the kinetics and diffusion constants of the components.

Testing reaction-diffusion models against biological data requires spatially resolved measurements of the concentrations of the relevant chemical species — exactly the kind of measurements that modern imaging technologies (GCaMP reporters for calcium, transcriptomic mapping for gene expression) can provide. The integration of imaging data with reaction-diffusion models is one of the most productive current directions in developmental biology, and it is directly relevant to the mechanistic question in basal cognition: do reaction-diffusion dynamics implement the information processing underlying morphogenetic intelligence?

## Machine Learning Approaches

Machine learning — specifically deep learning — has become an indispensable tool in biological research, applied to problems including protein structure prediction, genomic sequence analysis, cell type classification from single-cell sequencing data, and image analysis for microscopy. Its application to basal cognition research is at an early stage but is beginning to produce useful results.

**Behavioral classification.** Deep learning models can classify the behavioral state of an organism from video data — identifying periods of directed motion versus random exploration, or detecting specific behavioral signatures like the run-tumble algorithm of bacterial chemotaxis — without requiring the researcher to hand-specify features. Applied to basal organisms, behavioral classification from video allows the extraction of precise quantitative behavioral phenotypes at a scale not achievable by manual annotation.

**Predictive modeling of signaling dynamics.** Given time-series data on the concentrations of multiple signaling components in a cell or tissue, machine learning models can predict future concentrations from past ones — learning the effective dynamics of the signaling network without knowing the underlying biochemistry. This "black box" predictive modeling is not a mechanistic explanation, but it can identify which signaling components are most predictive of behavioral outcomes, guiding the design of more targeted mechanistic experiments.

**Generative models of biological structure.** Generative models — variational autoencoders, generative adversarial networks, diffusion models — trained on large datasets of biological images or molecular structures can generate new examples that capture the statistical properties of the training data. Applied to mycelial network images, plant electrical signal time series, or bacterial colony patterns, these models can reveal what features are characteristic of the biological system and generate hypotheses about the underlying mechanisms.

The limitations of machine learning in basal cognition research should be acknowledged: these models are not explanatory in the mechanistic sense, they require large datasets that are not always available for basal organisms, and they can capture spurious correlations that do not reflect causal biological relationships. But as tools for pattern recognition in complex biological datasets, they are increasingly indispensable and are extending the reach of basal cognition research into data modalities that would otherwise be inaccessible.
