# Chapter 41: Exercises

## Part I: Reflection and Discussion

1. **Testing the common architecture.** The chapter argues that sensing-integration-action is a universal architecture in biological cognition. Choose a biological system not discussed in the chapter (e.g., immune cells, coral reefs, mycorrhizal networks, insect wings during flight) and analyze whether it exhibits this architecture. What does sensing mean in this system? What is integrated, and what does the integration produce? What is the action, and how does it depend on the integration? Does your analysis support the claim of universality, or does it reveal exceptions?

2. **The scale-free hypothesis: levels of conflict.** Levin's scale-free cognition framework predicts that when levels of the competency hierarchy conflict, pathology results. Evaluate this prediction for two cases: (a) cancer, where cellular cognition conflicts with tissue-level cognition; (b) a case of your choice, from a different domain (autoimmunity, social violence, ecological overshoot, addiction). For each case, how well does the "conflict between cognitive levels" framing account for what happens? What does the framing add to standard non-cognitive explanations of the same phenomena?

3. **The continuity thesis and moral status.** If neural cognition is continuous with cellular cognition, and if neural cognition confers moral status, does it follow that cellular cognition also confers moral status? Work through this argument carefully. Where are the weak points? Does the continuity thesis imply that all living organisms have moral status, or is there a principled way to draw a moral threshold within a continuum of cognitive function?

4. **What we don't know: the priorities.** Among the three open questions discussed in Section 41.4 (consciousness, mechanism, ethics), which do you believe is most important to address, and why? What would progress on that question look like — what specific findings or developments would constitute significant advances? What is the most important single experiment that would, if successful, move the needle most?

5. **The synthesis and your view.** Having read this book, state your own view on the following question: is there a unified account of biological intelligence? If yes, articulate it in your own words (not by summarizing this chapter, but by arguing for it from the evidence). If no, explain why the diversity of cognitive mechanisms resists unification, and what a better framework would look like.

## Part II: Thought Experiments

1. **The cognitive spectrum revisited.** Construct your own "cognitive spectrum" for biological systems — a ranking or mapping of cognitive complexity across the systems discussed in this book, from bacteria to humans. What dimensions does your spectrum have? What are you measuring — behavioral flexibility, depth of temporal integration, richness of goal representation, all of these? Where does *Physarum* fall relative to a honeybee? Where does a mycorrhizal network fall relative to an individual plant? What does constructing this spectrum reveal about the concept of cognitive complexity?

2. **The first neuron.** Imagine you could observe the evolutionary transition at which the first neuron-like cell appeared — the first cell that was more specialized for rapid, directed signal propagation than its neighbors, and that formed something like a proto-synapse with another such cell. What cellular cognitive properties would its immediate pre-neural ancestors have had? What new cognitive properties did the neuron's specialization add? What did the specialization cost — what cellular cognitive properties may have been lost or reduced in the specialization? This thought experiment should draw on the molecular and functional evidence discussed throughout the book.

3. **Designing a cognitive ecosystem.** Imagine you are designing a closed ecological system — like a space habitat — with the goal of creating an environment that is maximally cognitively rich at every level of biological organization: cognitively active microbiomes, cognitively active plant communities, cognitively active fungal networks, and cognitively active animals. What species would you include, and how would you design the interactions among them? What would a "cognitively rich" ecosystem feel like to inhabit? And what does the design exercise reveal about the relationship between ecological and cognitive organization?

## Part III: Laboratory and Computational Investigations

1. **Implementing scale-free cognition.** Using a multi-scale computational model (you can use any modeling platform — NetLogo, custom Python, MATLAB), implement a simplified version of the scale-free cognition hierarchy: individual agents (cells) that each follow simple rules, organized into a tissue-level system that has its own goal state, organized into an organism-level system with its own goals. Demonstrate a case in which the levels cooperate (normal function) and a case in which they conflict (disease). Characterize the emergent behavior of the system in each case, and discuss what your model reveals about the relationship between levels in biological cognitive hierarchies.

2. **Cross-taxa cognitive comparison.** Conduct a systematic literature review of the following behavioral capacities across five taxa spanning the range from bacteria to vertebrates: (a) habituation; (b) sensitization; (c) associative conditioning; (d) spatial memory; (e) social learning. For each taxon and each capacity, rate the strength of evidence (strong/moderate/weak/absent) and cite the key paper. Construct a matrix and analyze: which capacities appear to form a developmental sequence (always appearing together in a specific order across taxa)? Which capacities appear independently? What does this pattern tell you about the modularity of cognitive evolution?

3. **Writing your own synthesis.** Write a 3000-word synthesis — not a summary of this chapter, but your own original argument — for or against the following claim: "The cognitive behaviors of non-neural organisms represent a single unified phenomenon, best understood as instances of the same underlying biological intelligence, rather than a collection of superficially similar but mechanistically unrelated adaptive behaviors." Draw on specific evidence from at least five different taxa discussed in this book, and engage seriously with the strongest arguments on the other side of your position.
