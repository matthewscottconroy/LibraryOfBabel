# Chapter 41: Key Concepts

**Unified Theory of Biological Intelligence** — A theoretical framework that would account for cognitive function across all biological systems — from single-celled organisms to complex nervous systems — using a common set of principles, rather than treating different organism types as requiring separate explanatory frameworks. *Why it matters for basal cognition:* The aspiration toward a unified theory is not premature ambition but necessary intellectual discipline: without a unified framework, findings about bacterial cognition, plant signaling, and neural computation remain isolated, preventing the comparative analysis that science requires. The chapter argues that such a theory is not yet available but is taking shape through convergent frameworks.

**Common Cognitive Architecture** — The structural features shared by all biological cognitive systems: sensing (detection of environmental variables), memory (storage of information about past states), anticipation (regulatory organization tuned to expected future states), and valence (functional distinction between states that promote versus threaten organizational continuity). *Why it matters for basal cognition:* The common architecture thesis holds that these four features are not optional add-ons that some organisms have and others lack, but constitutive features of any biological system capable of adaptive behavior — making them as universal as metabolism or replication.

**Scale-Free Cognition** — Michael Levin's proposal that cognitive algorithms — the functional computations by which systems sense, integrate, and act — are instantiated at every major level of biological organization: subcellular, cellular, tissue, organ, organism, and colony. The same algorithmic principles appear at each scale, though implemented in different physical substrates. *Why it matters for basal cognition:* Scale-free cognition dissolves the apparent gap between "simple" and "complex" cognition by showing that what changes across scales is not the logical structure of cognition but its implementation, speed, and integration capacity.

**Competency Hierarchy** — In Levin's framework, the nested structure in which cognitive agents at lower levels of organization are integrated into — and partially constrained by — cognitive agents at higher levels. Individual cells have their own goals (growth, survival, division); tissue-level organization constrains and coordinates these into organ-level goals; organs are coordinated into organism-level goals. *Why it matters for basal cognition:* The competency hierarchy explains how multicellular complexity arises: not by replacing cellular cognition with something else, but by adding coordination layers that give rise to new levels of cognitive agency while preserving the lower-level competencies.

**Continuity Thesis** — The claim that neural cognition is a special case of biological cognition — one that differs from non-neural cognition in implementation, speed, and integration capacity, but not in fundamental logical or ontological kind. The molecular, functional, and evolutionary continuity between pre-neural and neural cognition supports this thesis. *Why it matters for basal cognition:* If the continuity thesis is correct, then basal cognition research is not peripheral to cognitive science but constitutive of it: understanding the deep history and common architecture of biological cognition requires understanding its pre-neural forms.

**Neural Cognition as Special Case** — The characterization of nervous-system-based cognition as occupying a particular region of the broader space of biological cognitive architectures, rather than defining that space. Nervous systems amplify, accelerate, and integrate cognitive processes that exist in proto-form throughout biology; they do not constitute a separate category. *Why it matters for basal cognition:* Recognizing neural cognition as a special case rather than the defining case reorients cognitive science: instead of asking "how much does organism X resemble a brain?" we ask "how does organism X implement the common cognitive architecture, and at what scale?"

**Biological Valence** — The functional property by which a living system distinguishes states that promote its organizational continuity from states that threaten it — attractants versus repellents, growth-promoting versus growth-inhibiting conditions, immune self versus non-self. Valence is functionally attributed without commitment to phenomenal valence (the felt quality of goodness or badness). *Why it matters for basal cognition:* Valence is the functional root of goal-directedness: a system with valence has something at stake in its interactions with its environment. Without valence, sensing and acting are not cognitive in any interesting sense; they are merely reactive.

**Evolutionary Origins of Cognition** — The question of when, in the history of life, each cognitive capacity arose — and whether the history of cognition is a single continuous trajectory from the first cells or a more complex story of multiple independent origins, elaborations, and losses. *Why it matters for basal cognition:* Reconstructing the evolutionary origins of cognition is essential for understanding its deep architecture. The discovery that nervous systems evolved multiple times independently (Moroz, 2012) complicates any simple continuity story while reinforcing the scale-free view: if the same cognitive architecture is reinvented repeatedly, this suggests it is a robust attractor in the space of adaptive biological organization.

**Cognitive Archaeology** — The use of comparative methods, phylogenetic analysis, and fossil evidence to reconstruct the cognitive capacities of ancestral organisms — including organisms that left no direct descendants and for which only molecular or morphological evidence remains. Analogous to behavioral ecology's approach to reconstructing ancestral behaviors from comparative data. *Why it matters for basal cognition:* Cognitive archaeology allows the field to ask when, in evolutionary time, particular cognitive capacities appear — grounding the continuity thesis in concrete phylogenetic evidence and identifying the specific transitions through which cognitive organization increased in complexity.

**Open Problems in Basal Cognition** — The four genuinely unresolved empirical and philosophical questions identified in the book's final synthesis: the consciousness question (is there something it is like to be *Physarum* or a root?); the mechanism question (how exactly does integration work at the cellular level?); the evolutionary question (when did each capacity arise, and how many independent origins did it have?); and the ethical question (what follows for our treatment of non-neural organisms from what we have learned?). *Why it matters for basal cognition:* Identifying open problems with precision is the essential precondition for making progress on them. A field that cannot say what it does not know cannot formulate the experiments needed to find out.
