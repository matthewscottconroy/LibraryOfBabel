# Section 3: The Continuity Thesis

## Against the Discontinuity View

There is a view — call it the discontinuity view — that treats neural cognition as categorically different from whatever precedes or underlies it. On the discontinuity view, there is a threshold at which "real" cognition appears, defined by the presence of neural circuits, or by some threshold of neural complexity, or by some specific architectural feature like the global workspace that enables neural broadcasting. Below this threshold, systems may display sophisticated adaptive behavior, but they are not cognitive in the sense that matters — they are, in the philosophical idiom, zombies or mere robots, processing information without any genuine cognitive character.

The discontinuity view has a certain philosophical appeal. It preserves the intuition that human and animal minds are something special — something not to be found in slime molds or bacteria — without requiring us to maintain that minds are supernatural. It places the specialness of mind in a specific physical feature (neural organization) rather than in a spiritual substance.

The continuity thesis, by contrast, holds that neural cognition is a special case of biological cognition — a particularly powerful and flexible elaboration of computational principles that operate, in simpler forms, throughout the living world. The specialness of neural cognition is real, but it is a difference of degree and elaboration, not of kind. The same fundamental principles — sensing, integration, memory, anticipation, goal-directedness — operate in bacterial cells and in human brains; the difference is in the richness and flexibility of the implementation.

This chapter has been developing the empirical case for the continuity thesis throughout. Here, we state it explicitly and examine its implications.

## The Empirical Case

The empirical case for the continuity thesis rests on the following observations, which have been documented in detail in the preceding chapters:

**Molecular continuity.** The molecular machinery underlying cognitive function in neural systems — ion channels, signaling kinases, calcium-sensitive proteins, actomyosin, gap junctions — has evolutionary antecedents in non-neural organisms. Ion channels are present in bacteria and mediate cognitive-relevant behaviors (potassium channels in biofilm electrical communication). Calcium signaling is universal in eukaryotes and coordinates responses in plants, fungi, and protists. The molecular building blocks of neural cognition were not invented by nervous systems; they were borrowed, elaborated, and specialized from a molecular toolkit that predates nervous systems by hundreds of millions of years.

**Functional continuity.** The functional categories of cognitive science — sensing, integration, memory, decision-making, learning — are applicable to non-neural organisms, not merely by analogy but because these organisms genuinely exhibit the relevant information-processing behaviors. *E. coli* performs temporal differentiation of chemical gradients, implementing a functional analog of derivative computation. *Physarum* solves optimization problems using network dynamics that implement functional analogs of parallel search. Plants integrate electrical, chemical, and mechanical signals to generate context-sensitive responses. These are not pale shadows of neural computation; they are distinct implementations of the same functional class.

**Architectural continuity.** The sensing-integration-action architecture is not a neural invention; it is a biological universal. It is implemented differently at different organizational levels and in different taxa, but the logical structure is the same. This architectural continuity suggests that there is a computational logic underlying all biological cognition, of which neural cognition is one particularly elaborated instance.

**Evolutionary continuity.** Neural systems evolved from non-neural systems. The neurons in the first nervous systems were not invented from scratch; they were specialized cells that had previously performed general cellular functions. Their specialization for rapid, directed signal propagation was a modification of molecular machinery that cells had been using for other purposes. The cognitive functions of the first nervous systems were elaborations of cognitive functions that existed in the unicellular ancestors of animals. There is no evolutionary discontinuity in the causal chain from cellular cognition to neural cognition.

## What the Continuity Thesis Implies for Understanding Mind

If neural cognition is a special case of biological cognition, rather than a categorically new phenomenon, several implications follow for how we understand mind:

**The hard problem of consciousness extends throughout biology.** The question of why physical processes give rise to subjective experience — Chalmers's hard problem — is not specific to neural systems if experience tracks cognitive function. If there is something it is like to be a human brain processing visual information, and if the same organizational principles that make that processing cognitive are present in simpler forms in bacteria and plants, then the hard problem applies wherever those organizational principles are present. The question "why do neural processes give rise to experience?" does not have a more tractable answer than "why do cellular processes give rise to experience?" — unless the answer lies in something specific to neural organization that is absent in cellular organization, which is precisely what the discontinuity view claims and the continuity thesis denies.

**Understanding non-neural cognition illuminates neural cognition.** If the same computational principles operate at multiple biological scales and in multiple taxa, then studying the simpler implementations may reveal the principles more clearly than studying the most complex one. The logic of temporal differentiation in bacterial chemotaxis, studied in detail in a system of six receptor proteins and a handful of kinases, illuminates the temporal processing of neural sensory systems in a way that studying the visual cortex — with its billions of neurons — alone cannot. This is the scientific payoff of the continuity thesis: it licenses cross-system inference, allowing insight from simple systems to inform understanding of complex ones.

**The evolution of mind can be studied empirically.** If mind is continuous with life, then its evolution can be studied as a biological history. We can trace the evolution of cognitive capacities — by comparing cognitive behaviors across taxa, by identifying the molecular innovations associated with cognitive elaboration, by examining the fossil record of nervous system complexity — in the same way that we trace the evolution of any other complex biological trait. The evolution of mind is not a philosophical mystery to be resolved by armchair reasoning; it is a biological question that can be addressed with the tools of evolutionary biology and molecular phylogenetics.

## The Contribution of Pre-Neural Systems

The continuity thesis also implies a specific claim about the relationship between pre-neural and neural cognition: that understanding pre-neural cognition is not peripheral but central to understanding neural cognition. This is a stronger claim than "basal cognition is interesting in its own right," and it deserves explicit defense.

The claim is this: because the molecular machinery of neural cognition evolved from the machinery of pre-neural cellular cognition, and because the organizational principles of neural cognition are elaborations of pre-neural principles, understanding the pre-neural forms is not merely historical background but provides insight into the nature of neural function that studying neural systems alone cannot provide.

This is analogous to the relationship between prokaryotic and eukaryotic cell biology. Understanding the basic cell biology of bacteria — their cell membrane, their cytoskeleton, their energy metabolism — is essential for understanding eukaryotic cell biology, not because eukaryotic cells are simply elaborated bacteria (they are not), but because the fundamental principles of cellular organization are most clearly visible in the simpler system, and because the eukaryotic innovations — the nucleus, the mitochondria, the endoplasmic reticulum — make sense only against the background of the simpler system they elaborate.

The same logic applies to cognition. The computational principles underlying neural cognition — temporal integration, multiscale memory, goal-directed action, anticipation — are most clearly visible in the simpler systems where they are implemented in a handful of molecular components with known properties. The neural elaborations of these principles — the complexity of cortical circuits, the variety of neurotransmitter systems, the plasticity of synaptic connections — make sense as elaborations of a computational logic that was already in place before neurons evolved.

## Against Eliminativism About Basal Cognition

The continuity thesis also implies a rejection of eliminativism about basal cognition — the view that the cognitive vocabulary applied to non-neural organisms is merely a convenient shorthand with no genuine ontological commitment. On the eliminativist view, when we say that a slime mold "solves" a maze or a bacterium "decides" to swim, we are using useful language that does not commit us to the view that anything genuinely cognitive is occurring.

The continuity thesis rejects this: the cognitive vocabulary is not merely convenient shorthand, but reflects a genuine property of the systems being described — a property that is continuous with the property the same vocabulary describes in neural systems. The bacterium's "decision" to swim up a gradient is a genuine decision in exactly the sense that the neuron's "decision" to fire is a genuine decision: both are threshold-crossing events in systems organized to produce adaptive behavior, and both are the output of an information-integration process that combines current signals with stored information about the past.

The threshold events are implemented differently — one by the kinetics of CheY phosphorylation, the other by the accumulation of excitatory post-synaptic potentials — but the organizational logic is the same. Eliminativism about the bacterial case implies eliminativism about the neural case, and eliminativism about the neural case implies eliminativism about human cognition. The continuity of the organizational logic means that eliminativism is either true for all of them or false for all of them.
