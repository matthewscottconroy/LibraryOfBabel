# Section 4: What We Don't Know

## The Value of Honest Ignorance

This book has made many claims. Bacteria perform temporal differentiation of chemical gradients. Slime molds solve network optimization problems by cytoplasmic flow dynamics. Plants generate and propagate electrical signals that coordinate defensive responses. Mycorrhizal networks transfer resources and information among forest trees. These claims are supported by published, peer-reviewed evidence and are as well-established as anything in contemporary biology.

But not everything this book has implied or suggested is equally well-established. Some claims are more contested. Some questions are genuinely open — not because the relevant experiments haven't been done, but because the questions are hard in ways that no experiment of the currently available type can fully address. Ending with intellectual honesty requires being explicit about these open questions — about what we don't know, and why.

This is not a counsel of despair. It is a map of the frontier, drawn as accurately as we can manage, as an invitation to the next generation of researchers who will push it forward.

## The Consciousness Question: Still Open

The hardest open question is the one that has been circling throughout the philosophical chapters of this book: is there something it is like to be any of the non-neural organisms we have studied? Is there subjective experience — phenomenal consciousness — in bacteria, slime molds, plants, or fungi?

We do not know. We cannot know, with currently available methods. The functional evidence is clear: these organisms perform sophisticated information processing, exhibit adaptive behaviors that meet many of our criteria for cognition, and do so through molecular mechanisms that share evolutionary roots with the mechanisms of neural cognition. The phenomenal question is a different question, and it cannot be answered by any functional or physical measurement.

Intellectual honesty requires resisting both the temptation to confidently assert that there is experience in these systems (which overclaims what the evidence supports) and the temptation to confidently deny it (which similarly overclaims). The appropriate position is genuine uncertainty — uncertainty that should make us treat these systems with more respect than our prior default of assuming all interesting cognitive life is neural.

What progress looks like on this front is not a direct observation of experience — that is almost certainly impossible with third-person methods — but the development of better theories of how experience relates to physical organization. If we had a more complete theory of why specific physical configurations are accompanied by experience — one that made quantitative, testable predictions about which systems have experience and which do not — we could apply that theory to non-neural organisms and get at least a constrained answer. No such theory currently exists in a satisfactory form. Building one is one of the most important intellectual tasks facing science in the twenty-first century.

## The Mechanism Question: Still Underspecified

The mechanism question is more tractable than the consciousness question, but still largely unresolved. For most of the basal cognitive behaviors we have described, we have a functional account (what the behavior is, described in cognitive terms) and a rough mechanistic sketch (which molecular and physical processes are likely involved), but not a complete mechanistic explanation — one that specifies every component, every interaction, every quantitative parameter, in a way that fully accounts for the observed behavior.

The *Physarum* network optimization is a paradigm example. The Tero model describes the optimization in terms of a positive feedback between flow and tube diameter, and this model accurately predicts observed network topologies. But what is the molecular mechanism of this feedback? How does the tube wall know about the flux through it? What role does the calcium oscillation play? How does the actomyosin contractility that drives cytoplasmic streaming respond to flow, and through what signaling pathway? These are specific, answerable questions that remain open.

The plant electrical signal propagation is similar: the signals are measurable and clearly correlate with behavioral responses, but the complete signal transduction pathway — from the electrical signal in the phloem or epidermis, through whatever intermediate steps, to the activation of defense gene expression — is not fully characterized.

These are tractable research problems. They require the combination of advanced imaging, genetics, and computational modeling that is becoming increasingly available. The challenge is less conceptual than experimental: the experiments are technically demanding, require careful attention to timescale and spatial resolution, and must disentangle the contributions of many simultaneously active signaling pathways. Progress will be incremental, and each increment will matter.

## The Ethical Question: Still Unresolved

The ethical implications of basal cognition research are genuine and significant, but they are unresolved in the sense that we have not yet developed ethical frameworks adequate to the evidence. The existing frameworks — sentience-based ethics, biocentric ethics, land ethics — all capture something important, but none is fully satisfactory for the range of situations that basal cognition research creates.

The ethics of treating cognitively active non-neural organisms — in agriculture, in medicine, in ecological management — requires frameworks that can accommodate gradations of cognitive complexity and uncertainty about experience. The ethics of designing and creating cognitive systems — xenobots, organoids, synthetic cognitive circuits — requires frameworks for creator responsibility that do not yet exist. The ethics of destroying or disrupting ecological cognitive communities — soil microbiomes, mycorrhizal networks — requires frameworks that connect individual organism welfare to community-level cognitive organization.

These frameworks will not be developed by philosophers alone. They require dialogue between scientists who understand what non-neural organisms can do and philosophers who understand what moral consideration requires. The dialogue has begun, but it is far from complete, and the urgency of completing it is increasing as synthetic biology and ecological disruption advance faster than our moral frameworks.

## The Promise of a New Biology of Mind

Against these open questions, let us close with a statement of the promise. Basal cognition research is not merely a collection of interesting curiosities about organisms we had previously ignored. It is the foundation of a new biology of mind — a biology that studies cognitive function not as the exclusive property of vertebrate nervous systems but as a fundamental feature of living organization.

This new biology will be able to trace the evolutionary history of cognition from its simplest implementations in ancient single-celled organisms through the elaborations of multicellularity, the innovations of neural systems, and the extraordinary elaboration of the human mind. It will be able to identify the general computational principles underlying cognitive function at every level — principles that will inform both our understanding of natural systems and our design of artificial ones. It will connect cognitive science to developmental biology, to evolutionary biology, to ecology, and to the physics of complex systems in ways that are currently only beginning.

This new biology will also change how we see ourselves — as cognitive elaborations of a world that has always been cognitively active, as participants in a living community of cognition that extends from the bacteria in our gut to the mycorrhizal networks beneath the forests to the neural assemblies in the human brain. We are not isolated cognitive agents in a world of mechanisms. We are elaborations of life's most ancient project: making sense of the world in order to act within it.

The project is unfinished. The map has white spaces. The questions are hard. The discoveries have barely begun.

That is not a limitation to mourn. It is an invitation to continue.
