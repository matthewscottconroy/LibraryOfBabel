# Section 2: Linear Algebra

Here is a number that should stop you in your tracks: a typical single-cell RNA-seq dataset contains measurements of 30,000 genes across 10,000 cells — 300 million data points. You cannot look at these data by plotting them. You cannot reason about them intuitively. The only way to find biological structure in 300 million numbers is to use mathematics that was designed specifically to work in high-dimensional spaces. That mathematics is linear algebra.

It turns out that those 300 million numbers do not live in a 300-million-dimensional space at all. They live in a space of maybe 30 or 50 dimensions — because cells of the same type express genes in correlated patterns, and those patterns are shared across the 10,000 cells you measured. The tool that reveals this low-dimensional structure is the singular value decomposition. The conceptual foundation that makes it interpretable is the theory of vector spaces and linear transformations. The computational machinery that makes it feasible on a laptop is sparse matrix algebra.

Linear algebra is the mathematics of linear transformations and their representations as matrices. For computational biologists, this means something concrete: the gene expression matrix is a linear algebraic object. The stoichiometric matrix that encodes your metabolic model is a linear algebraic object. The covariance matrix that describes the correlations in your genomic dataset is a linear algebraic object. The Jacobian that governs the stability of your gene circuit is a linear algebraic object. You cannot do serious computational biology without linear algebra; you would be trying to read without knowing the alphabet.

This section builds linear algebra from the ground up in three subsections, each adding depth to the previous.

**Subsection 1 — Fundamentals** begins with the basic objects: vectors, vector spaces, matrices, and linear transformations. You will learn to think geometrically — a vector is a direction in space, a matrix is a transformation of space, and the product $A\mathbf{x}$ is asking where vector $\mathbf{x}$ lands after that transformation. The key biological example is the stoichiometric matrix, which maps a flux vector to a rate-of-change vector: this is a linear map from reaction space to metabolite space, and the constraint of steady state ($S\mathbf{v} = 0$) is a constraint that the flux vector lives in the null space of this transformation. This section also introduces the determinant and its role in stability analysis — a tool you will use every time you classify a fixed point in a two-dimensional ODE system.

**Subsection 2 — Core Decompositions** develops the two most powerful algorithms in the linear algebra toolkit: eigendecomposition and singular value decomposition. Eigenvalues are numbers that tell you what happens to vectors when you apply a linear transformation repeatedly — and the eigenvalues of the Jacobian at a biological fixed point tell you whether small perturbations grow or decay. The SVD is even more general: it applies to any matrix, not just square ones, and it produces the best possible low-rank approximation. PCA is SVD applied to expression data. Network centrality is eigenvector decomposition applied to adjacency matrices. Stability analysis is eigenvalue computation applied to Jacobians. These are not three separate ideas with a common name — they are the same mathematical operation applied to different biological contexts.

**Subsection 3 — Advanced Concepts** covers three ideas that come up repeatedly in modern computational biology but are not always covered in standard courses: null spaces (with their deep connection to flux balance analysis and metabolic modeling), positive definite matrices (with their role in statistical inference and parameter optimization), and sparse matrix data structures (which make genome-scale computations feasible). These are the bridge between the pure linear algebra of textbooks and the numerical linear algebra of production bioinformatics tools.

By the end of this section, you should be able to look at a bioinformatics paper and identify every place where linear algebra is operating — whether that is labeled as "PCA," "FBA," "regression," or "stability analysis." The mathematics is always the same; only the biological interpretation changes.
