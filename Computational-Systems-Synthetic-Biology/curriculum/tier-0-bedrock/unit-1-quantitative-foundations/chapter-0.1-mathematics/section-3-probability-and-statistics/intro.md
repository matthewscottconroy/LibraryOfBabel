# Section 3: Probability and Statistics

In 1990, the year the Human Genome Project launched, there were no statistical methods capable of analyzing genome-scale data. By 2010, when the first thousand-genome sequencing projects began, a new statistical infrastructure had been built almost from scratch: methods for calling variants from noisy read data, for correcting the massive multiple testing problem in association studies, for modeling overdispersed count distributions from sequencing, for inferring networks from expression profiles. This infrastructure did not emerge because statisticians invented new mathematics — it emerged because biologists learned to apply existing mathematical tools to new biological data types.

The tools they applied came almost entirely from classical probability and statistics. Bayes' theorem, invented in the 18th century, powers modern variant calling. The negative binomial distribution, developed in the 19th century, is now standard for RNA-seq count modeling. The Bonferroni correction, developed for agricultural experiments, became the standard for genome-wide association. The core mathematical ideas are old; what changed was learning which distribution fits which biological measurement, and which inference procedure is appropriate for which question.

This section covers that core statistical machinery in six subsections, moving from foundations to applications.

**Subsection 1 — Probability Theory** lays the axiomatic foundation. The Kolmogorov axioms are three simple rules from which everything else in probability follows. But the real payoff of this subsection is conceptual: you will learn to reason precisely about conditional probability, independence, and Bayes' theorem — and you will see immediately why these matter in biology. Conditional probability is how you compute the probability that a patient carries a disease variant given that they have the disease. Bayes' theorem is how you update your belief about which hypothesis is true as you accumulate evidence. Independence is an assumption that is constantly violated in genomic data and that you must actively check.

**Subsection 2 — Probability Distributions** catalogs the specific distributions that appear most frequently in biological data, and explains *why* each one arises from the underlying biology. The Poisson distribution emerges from counting rare independent events — like mutations or sequencing reads at a position. The negative binomial arises when the Poisson rate itself varies across cells — which it does for almost every gene in every scRNA-seq dataset. The beta distribution naturally models frequencies and fractions. The multivariate normal models correlated gene expression profiles. Understanding the mechanistic origin of each distribution is what allows you to recognize when your data does not fit the assumed model — and to understand what that tells you about the biology.

**Subsection 3 — Statistical Inference** develops the machinery for drawing conclusions from data: maximum likelihood estimation, confidence intervals, hypothesis testing, and the critical problem of multiple testing correction. In genomics, you almost always test thousands of hypotheses simultaneously, and the consequences of not correcting for this — a wave of false discoveries — poisoned the early candidate-gene association literature. The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, which controls the false discovery rate, is now one of the most-cited statistics papers in history.

**Subsection 4 — Regression** covers the workhorse model of quantitative genetics and molecular biology. Linear regression underlies GWAS, differential expression analysis (via GLMs), and network inference (via LASSO). You will learn why regularization is necessary when you have more predictors than samples — a situation endemic to genomics — and how the L1 and L2 penalties produce sparse versus shrinkage solutions.

**Subsection 5 — Bayesian Statistics** presents the Bayesian approach to inference, which asks not "is this data inconsistent with the null?" but "given this data, what should I believe about the parameters?" This is the framework behind GATK variant calling, BEAST phylogenetics, and increasingly the parameter estimation in ODE-based systems biology models. The subsection develops Bayes' theorem in its inferential form, introduces conjugate priors for tractable closed-form computation, and explains the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm and its modern descendants.

**Subsection 6 — Markov Chains** closes the section with one of the most productive mathematical ideas in all of bioinformatics. The hidden Markov model is the mathematical engine behind gene prediction, CpG island detection, chromatin state annotation, and protein secondary structure prediction. Markov chains also underlie MCMC algorithms for Bayesian inference, the chemical master equation for stochastic gene expression, and phylogenetic substitution models. Understanding the theory makes all of these tools interpretable rather than opaque.

The overarching theme of this section is that biological data is always uncertain, and probability theory is the language for reasoning honestly about that uncertainty. Statistics is the science of learning from imperfect observations — which is to say, it is the science of doing biology.
