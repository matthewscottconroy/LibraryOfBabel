# Section 1: General Chemistry

In 1897, J.J. Thomson discovered the electron. Within fifty years, physicists had worked out quantum mechanics and chemists had used it to explain why atoms bond the way they do. By the time Francis Crick and James Watson were piecing together the structure of DNA in 1953, they were drawing on a complete theory of chemical bonds, hydrogen bonding, and the energetics of molecular interactions. The molecular biology revolution was, in a very real sense, a physics revolution applied to chemistry applied to biology. General chemistry is where it all begins.

This section covers the four pillars of physical chemistry that any quantitative biologist must internalize: thermodynamics, equilibrium, acid-base chemistry, and kinetics. These are not separate subjects. They are four perspectives on the same underlying reality — the behavior of molecules at physiological temperatures and concentrations. Thermodynamics tells you whether a reaction can proceed; equilibrium tells you where it will end up; kinetics tells you how fast it gets there; and acid-base chemistry tells you what charge state the participants are in while all of this is happening.

**Thermodynamics** opens with the Gibbs free energy $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$, which is the master criterion for spontaneity in biochemistry. You will learn why ATP hydrolysis drives biosynthetic reactions, why the ratio of ATP to ADP in a cell determines the available free energy, and why thermodynamics is not just theoretical background — it is the physical layer that constrains every flux balance model and every molecular dynamics simulation you will ever run.

**Chemical equilibrium** extends the thermodynamic picture to the dynamic balance between forward and reverse reactions. The key insight — that equilibrium constants are exponentially related to free energies, and that cellular concentrations are typically far from equilibrium — explains why metabolic pathways work in the direction they do even when individual steps appear thermodynamically unfavorable at standard conditions. The transcription factor binding isotherm emerges naturally from equilibrium theory and sets up the Hill functions you will use throughout the rest of the curriculum.

**Acid-base chemistry** might seem like the most purely chemical of these topics, but it turns out to touch almost everything in computational biology. The charge states of amino acid side chains determine the electrostatic landscape of protein active sites; the pKa of a catalytic histidine determines its mode of action; the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation underlies every protonation state assignment in a molecular simulation. At pH 7.4, histidine is 96% neutral — a fact you will use when you think about enzyme mechanism design or model pH-dependent enzyme activity in ODE systems.

**Chemical kinetics** completes the picture by quantifying reaction rates. First-order decay constants determine mRNA half-lives; second-order association rate constants set the speed of protein-protein interaction; the Arrhenius equation explains temperature sensitivity. Most importantly, kinetics introduces the mathematical structure that runs through all of systems biology: rate laws are differential equations, and the steady states of gene circuits emerge from balancing production and degradation, exactly as you will see in the simplest gene expression model.

By the end of this section, you will have the physical and chemical vocabulary to read a biochemical rate law and understand where it comes from — not just as an equation, but as a claim about molecular behavior. That grounding is what separates a modeler who can write equations from one who can judge whether those equations are right.
