# Section 2: Organic Chemistry

In the summer of 1944, a chemist named Selman Waksman and his student Albert Schatz isolated streptomycin — the first antibiotic effective against tuberculosis. They were not random in their search; they knew that certain soil bacteria produced compounds with unusual chemical structures, and they were systematically testing whether those structures could kill pathogens. Their success depended on understanding organic chemistry: the reactive groups on the drug molecule, how it interacted with the bacterial ribosome, why it was toxic to mycobacteria but not to mammalian cells. Organic chemistry is not just a prerequisite — it is what made the antibiotic revolution possible.

The premise of this section is simple: if you want to understand how biomolecules behave, you need to understand the reactive groups they carry and the mechanisms by which those groups react. There is no shortcut. The behavior of an enzyme is determined by the chemistry of its active site; the structure of a membrane is determined by the geometry of its lipid tails; the selectivity of a drug is determined by the shape and charge distribution of its pharmacophore. All of these are organic chemistry.

**Functional groups** are the vocabulary. A carboxylate bears a negative charge at physiological pH and acts as a nucleophile or metal ligand. An amine is a general base and nucleophile. A thiol is a soft nucleophile that coordinates to transition metals. A phosphate ester links nucleotides in DNA and RNA and carries two negative charges at physiological pH. When you read a protein sequence and want to predict its behavior in a molecular dynamics simulation, you are thinking through this list: which residues are charged? Which ones can form disulfide bonds? Which ones are phosphorylated in vivo? Functional groups are the first answer to the question "why does this molecule do what it does?"

**Reaction mechanisms** are the grammar that links functional groups to chemical events. There are really only a handful of fundamental organic reactions — nucleophilic substitution, elimination, addition to carbonyls, and redox — and virtually every enzymatic reaction is a variation on these themes. The serine protease mechanism, the SAM methyltransferase mechanism, the acyl-CoA thioester chemistry: once you recognize the underlying mechanism type, you know what the transition state looks like, what inhibitors would block it, and how you might engineer an alternative activity. Mechanisms are not memorization exercises — they are the tools for thinking about molecular function at a deep level.

**Stereochemistry** completes the section by introducing the three-dimensional dimension that organic chemistry and biology share. Biology is fundamentally chiral: L-amino acids, D-sugars, stereospecific enzyme reactions. The thalidomide story — one enantiomer a sedative, the other teratogenic, and the drug racemizes in vivo — is the most dramatic possible illustration that the three-dimensional arrangement of atoms is not a detail. In computational chemistry, the correct stereochemistry of every molecule in a docking simulation must be specified; an incorrect enantiomer will give incorrect binding predictions. In metabolic modeling, the stereospecificity of reactions like fumarase (L-malate only) must be correctly annotated.

The logical progression in this section is from static structure to dynamic reactivity to three-dimensional consequence: you first build a vocabulary of reactive groups, then learn the mechanisms by which they react, then appreciate how three-dimensional structure controls which reactions are possible. Together, these three subsections give you the organic chemistry foundation that all of biochemistry rests on.
