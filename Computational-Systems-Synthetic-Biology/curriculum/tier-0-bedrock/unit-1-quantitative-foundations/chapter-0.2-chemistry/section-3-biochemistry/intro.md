# Section 3: Biochemistry

In 1926, James Sumner crystallized urease — demonstrating for the first time that an enzyme was a protein. The biochemistry community resisted this conclusion for years. Enzymes were so powerful, so specific, so seemingly magical in their catalytic abilities that many scientists couldn't believe they were "just" proteins. We now know that they are — but the magic hasn't diminished. The fact that a 250 amino acid chain can fold into a shape that accelerates a reaction by $10^{17}$-fold over its uncatalyzed rate, with perfect stereospecificity and exquisite substrate selectivity, is arguably more remarkable than any mystical explanation could be.

This section is where the general chemistry of Section 1 and the organic chemistry of Section 2 meet the molecules of life. Here you encounter the actual cast of characters that populate every biochemical model: proteins, enzymes, nucleic acids, lipids, carbohydrates, and coenzymes. Each subsection adds a layer to the picture of how living cells manage chemistry.

**Amino acids and proteins** introduces the building blocks of the proteome and the hierarchy of protein structure. You will learn which residues are charged, which can form disulfide bonds, which are phosphorylated in vivo, and how these properties determine protein behavior in silico. The structural hierarchy — primary, secondary, tertiary, quaternary — is not just taxonomy: it is the framework for understanding why AlphaFold works, how molecular dynamics simulations represent folded proteins, and which mutations are likely to disrupt function without destroying structure.

**Enzyme kinetics** derives the Michaelis-Menten equation from first principles and extends it to cooperative enzymes through the Hill equation. This is perhaps the most computationally consequential subsection in the entire chapter. The Michaelis-Menten equation is the rate law in virtually every ODE model of a metabolic network; the Hill function is the rate law for gene expression. Understanding where these equations come from — and what the quasi-steady-state approximation requires — determines whether you can trust a model you've built or whether you've made an error that will propagate invisibly through all your downstream analyses.

**Nucleic acid chemistry** covers the structure of DNA and RNA, the thermodynamics of Watson-Crick base pairing, and the nearest-neighbor model for predicting melting temperatures. The payoff is immediate and practical: these are the physical laws underlying PCR primer design, RNA secondary structure prediction, and CRISPR guide RNA design. The mfold and RNAfold algorithms minimize the sum of nearest-neighbor free energies — knowing this, you understand both what these tools are doing and where they can fail.

**Lipids and membranes** explains how amphipathic molecules self-assemble into bilayers, how cholesterol regulates membrane fluidity, and how the Nernst equation governs the equilibrium potential of ions across membranes. The Nernst equation connects membrane chemistry to computational neuroscience (Hodgkin-Huxley models), to drug pharmacokinetics (membrane permeability), and to synthetic biology (designing membrane-anchored biosensors).

**Carbohydrates** introduces the diversity of sugar chemistry, the stereochemical basis for their biological properties, and the complexity of the glycome — the least computationally tractable part of the molecular biology of the cell. Even for computational biologists who will not focus on glycobiology, carbohydrate chemistry appears in metabolic models (glycolysis, pentose phosphate pathway), in the thermodynamics of nucleic acid structure (ribose vs. deoxyribose), and in the regulatory biology of protein glycosylation.

**Coenzymes and metabolic currency** closes the section by describing the small molecules that connect metabolism into an integrated network: ATP, NAD$^+$/NADH, NADPH, FAD, CoA, SAM, and folate. These are the currency of flux balance analysis — the molecules whose stoichiometries appear as constraints in every metabolic model. Understanding why NADH and NADPH are functionally different despite being structurally nearly identical, and why acetyl-CoA is "activated" acetic acid, is essential for building accurate metabolic models.

Together, the six subsections of this section give you the biochemical cast of characters that will populate your models throughout the rest of this curriculum. Every ODE system, every metabolic network, every gene circuit involves these molecules. Knowing their chemistry is knowing why your models behave the way they do.
