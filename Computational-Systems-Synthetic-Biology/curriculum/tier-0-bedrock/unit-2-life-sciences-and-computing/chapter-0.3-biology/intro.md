# Chapter 0.3: Biology

Consider the genetic code. There are 64 possible codons — triplets of the four nucleotide bases — and only 20 amino acids plus three stop signals to assign them to. That means the code is redundant: most amino acids are encoded by two to six synonymous codons. But here is what makes the genetic code remarkable: with only minor exceptions in mitochondria and a handful of unusual organisms, the same code operates in every living cell on Earth. A ribosome from a bacterium that has never seen a eukaryote will correctly translate a yeast mRNA. The genetic code is, for all practical purposes, universal.

This universality is not merely interesting. It is deeply consequential. It means that DNA is a common language — that a gene cloned from a human cell can be expressed in *E. coli*, that a synthetic gene synthesized on a DNA synthesizer can be read by any organism's ribosomes, that the entire project of molecular biology and synthetic biology rests on a shared information-processing substrate that was established over three billion years ago and has not fundamentally changed since. The universality of the genetic code is the biological fact that makes the computational biologist's toolkit possible.

This chapter provides the biological vocabulary and conceptual framework that the rest of the curriculum assumes. It is not a conventional biology textbook — it is biology organized around the questions and concepts that a computational or synthetic biologist actually needs to understand. Every section has been written with an eye toward the downstream applications: ODE models of gene expression, circuit design in bacteria and eukaryotes, evolutionary analysis of genomic data, interpretation of next-generation sequencing experiments.

The chapter is organized into six sections, each addressing a different level of biological description.

Section 1 (Cell Biology) establishes the physical context: what cells are, how they are organized, how they divide, and what the architectural differences between prokaryotes and eukaryotes mean for the dynamics of gene expression. You will learn the numbers that matter — cell sizes, molecular copy numbers, diffusion timescales — and understand why these numbers constrain the models you can build.

Section 2 (Molecular Biology) traces the flow of biological information from DNA to RNA to protein. Here you will find the mechanistic basis of gene expression: the machinery of transcription (promoter recognition, elongation kinetics, termination), the processing steps in eukaryotes (capping, splicing, polyadenylation), translation (the ribosome, initiation mechanisms, codon usage), post-translational modification (phosphorylation, ubiquitination, and the language of signaling), and DNA repair (the sources and correction of mutations). The quantitative parameters introduced here — transcription rates, mRNA half-lives, translation initiation efficiencies, protein degradation rates, mutation rates — are the parameters that gene circuit models are built from.

Section 3 (Genetics and Genomics) covers how biological information is inherited, organized, and regulated at the genomic scale. Classical genetics establishes the formal logic of heredity that underlies GWAS and linkage analysis. Chromosome structure and epigenetics reveal the regulatory layers beyond DNA sequence that control gene accessibility. Genome organization provides the reference context for all sequencing analyses. Microbial genomics introduces the compact, dynamic genomes of bacteria, including the pangenome concept and the tools of metagenomics.

Section 4 (Microbiology) covers the biology of bacteria in depth: growth kinetics, quorum sensing, genetic manipulation tools, the native biology of CRISPR-Cas, and bacteriophage biology. Because bacteria are the primary chassis organisms of synthetic biology, this section is particularly important for anyone who will design or model genetic circuits.

Section 5 (Evolutionary Biology) introduces the four evolutionary forces, the mathematics of population genetics, the tools of molecular evolution, the concept of fitness landscapes, and horizontal gene transfer. Evolution is not merely background — it determines how quickly synthetic constructs degrade in growing cultures, shapes the sequence variation we observe in genomic data, and provides both the inspiration and the mechanism for directed evolution.

Section 6 (Key Biological Systems) introduces the model organisms and canonical circuits that the field is built around: *E. coli*, yeast, fly, worm, mouse; and the lac operon, the lambda switch, the circadian clock, the MAPK cascade, chemotaxis, the p53 network, and the cell cycle. These are the examples that every paper in the field assumes you know, and knowing them deeply — not just descriptively but quantitatively — will accelerate your engagement with the primary literature.

By the end of this chapter, you will have the biological literacy that a computational biologist needs: not a memorized catalog of facts, but a working mental model of how cells process information, how genomes encode and organize that information, how populations of cells evolve, and what the canonical model systems look like in enough detail to evaluate claims about them. This chapter provides the biological half of a foundation; the computer science section that follows provides the other half. Together, they equip you for the computational questions that the rest of the curriculum addresses.
