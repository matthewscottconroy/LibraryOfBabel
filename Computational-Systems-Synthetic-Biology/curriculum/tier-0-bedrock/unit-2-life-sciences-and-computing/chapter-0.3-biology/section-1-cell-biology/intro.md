# Section 1: Cell Biology

The cell is the unit of life. Not the gene, not the protein, not the metabolite — the cell. It is the smallest system that can take in nutrients, convert them to useful work, maintain its own chemical composition against a tendency toward equilibrium, sense its environment, respond to it, and replicate itself. Everything else in biology is either a subsystem of the cell or a relationship between cells. This is why cell biology comes first in this chapter: before you can understand gene circuits, metabolic networks, or the evolution of populations, you need a working mental model of the physical context in which all of these phenomena occur.

What you will find in this section is not a comprehensive cell biology textbook. What you will find are the concepts that a computational biologist needs to build correct mental models and correct mathematical models of cellular processes. The emphasis throughout is on the quantitative: numbers that matter (cell sizes, molecular copy numbers, timescales), architectural principles that constrain what models must include, and physical reasoning about why cells are the way they are.

The section begins with the most fundamental division in cellular life: the boundary between prokaryotes and eukaryotes. This is not merely a taxonomic curiosity. The architectural differences between these two cell types — the presence or absence of a nucleus, the coupling or decoupling of transcription and translation, the organization of the chromosome — have direct consequences for the dynamics of gene expression and the design of gene circuits. A synthetic circuit that works perfectly in *E. coli* may behave very differently when transferred to a mammalian cell chassis, and understanding why requires understanding the architectural differences laid out in the first subsection.

From there, the section moves to the plasma membrane — the cell's interface with the world. Every external signal that a cell responds to arrives at the membrane first, and every computational model of signal transduction begins with the membrane's properties. You will encounter the mathematics of ion channels (the Hodgkin-Huxley equations, one of the most successful quantitative models in biology), the kinetics of transporters, and the structural basis of receptor signaling.

The next subsection covers organelles, the membrane-bounded compartments that give eukaryotic cells their characteristic functional specialization. For computational biology, the key insight is that compartmentalization is not merely descriptive — it has quantitative consequences. The concentration of a signaling molecule in the nucleus versus the cytoplasm, the calcium gradient between the ER lumen and the cytoplasm, the pH gradient across the lysosomal membrane — these numbers enter directly into quantitative models, and getting them wrong produces models that fail to match experiments.

The cytoskeleton subsection introduces a structural and dynamic system that underlies cell mechanics, intracellular transport, and cell division. The polymerization kinetics of actin and tubulin, the dynamic instability of microtubules, and the force-generating properties of motor proteins are all amenable to quantitative treatment — and models of cell mechanics, mitosis, and intracellular transport require exactly this treatment.

The section closes with the cell cycle — arguably the most thoroughly characterized regulatory network in all of biology. The cell cycle is the paradigm case for how a network of molecular switches generates an irreversible, ordered sequence of events. The bistable switch at the heart of the G1/S transition, the checkpoint mechanisms that ensure fidelity, and the cyclin-CDK oscillator that drives the whole cycle forward are canonical examples of systems biology concepts that you will encounter in your own modeling work.

By the end of this section, you will have a quantitative picture of the cell as a physical object — its size, its contents, its internal organization, and the timescales on which its components change. That picture is the foundation on which everything else in the curriculum rests.
