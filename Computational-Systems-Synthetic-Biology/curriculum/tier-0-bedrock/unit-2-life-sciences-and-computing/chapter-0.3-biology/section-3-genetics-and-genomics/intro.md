# Section 3: Genetics and Genomics

Gregor Mendel counted peas. Morgan counted crossovers in fruit flies. Barbara McClintock counted transposon insertions in maize. The history of genetics is, at its core, a history of counting — of finding patterns in the transmission and variation of heritable traits, and of inferring the underlying molecular mechanisms from those patterns. Genomics is what happened when sequencing became cheap enough to count everything at once: not one trait in thousands of crosses, but millions of variants in thousands of individuals simultaneously. The mathematics are now different in scale, but the underlying logic is the same. Genetics and genomics together provide the interpretive framework for understanding how biological information is encoded, organized, and transmitted — and for detecting the signatures that evolution, development, and disease leave in that information.

This section moves from the classical rules of inheritance deduced by Mendel, through the physical organization of chromosomes, the epigenetic layer that controls gene accessibility, the large-scale architecture of eukaryotic genomes, and finally to the compact, dynamic genomes of microbes. Each subsection adds a level of description that a computational biologist working with genomic data must understand.

The section opens with classical genetics: Mendel's laws, the chi-squared test, epistasis, penetrance, linkage mapping, and complementation. You might wonder why a curriculum aimed at computational biology bothers with 19th-century pea genetics. The answer is that the logic of classical genetics is the logic of all modern genetic analysis. GWAS studies apply chi-squared and logistic regression tests to ask Mendel's question at genome scale: is this allele associated with this phenotype? Linkage analysis — the same principle Morgan used to map genes in flies — still drives the identification of disease genes. Complementation tests, recast as interaction screens, are the basis of two-hybrid assays and CRISPR modifier screens. The formalism is old; the applications are current.

Chromosome structure follows, covering the hierarchy from nucleosome to chromosome territory. The key insight here is that the three-dimensional organization of DNA in the nucleus is not a passive storage solution — it is a regulatory layer. TADs, CTCF-anchored loops, and A/B compartments control which enhancers can reach which promoters, and disrupting these structures by structural variants is increasingly recognized as a pathogenic mechanism. For computational biology, this means that Hi-C data analysis, ATAC-seq peak calling, and chromatin state modeling are not separate topics from gene regulation — they are the same topic at different resolutions.

Epigenetics extends the regulatory picture to heritable chemical marks on DNA and histones. The key concepts — DNA methylation and its maintenance through replication, the histone code, reader-writer mechanisms for epigenetic memory — explain how cells with identical genomes maintain different identities across many cell divisions. For synthetic biology, epigenome editing with dCas9 fusions has made these marks directly programmable; understanding the underlying biology is essential for designing these interventions rationally.

Genome organization provides the reference context for all genome-scale analyses. The distribution of protein-coding genes, introns, repetitive elements, and non-coding RNA genes across the human genome is not random, and understanding this distribution is prerequisite for correctly interpreting sequencing experiments. Short-read aligners struggle with repetitive regions; long reads are resolving previously inaccessible repeat arrays; annotating a new genome requires knowing what to look for and where.

The section closes with microbial genomics — the genome biology of bacteria. Bacterial genomes are organized differently from eukaryotic genomes (operons, no introns, extreme gene density), are annotated differently, and evolve differently (pangenomes, horizontal gene transfer). Metagenomics — the sequencing of entire microbial communities — is one of the most active areas of computational biology, and its tools and concepts are entirely grounded in microbial genome organization.

Together, these five subsections give you the biological literacy to work with genomic data at every scale, from a single gene variant to a complete metagenome.
