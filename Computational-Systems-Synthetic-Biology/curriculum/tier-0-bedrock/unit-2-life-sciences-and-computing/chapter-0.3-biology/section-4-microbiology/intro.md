# Section 4: Microbiology

Bacteria are the organisms that synthetic biology was built around. Before there were mammalian cell factories, before there were yeast metabolic engineers, there was *E. coli* and its close relatives — small, fast-growing, genetically tractable, and willing to express almost any gene you put in front of them. The tools of synthetic biology — inducible promoters, standardized ribosome binding sites, recombinase logic gates, CRISPR-based regulators — were all developed and characterized primarily in bacteria. And the concepts at the heart of systems biology — bistable switches, oscillators, perfect adaptation — were first characterized and modeled in bacterial circuits.

This section covers the microbiology that a computational biologist working with bacteria needs to know: not just what bacteria are (covered in the cell biology section) but how they grow, how they communicate, how they can be genetically manipulated, and what their intrinsic immune system looks like before it was repurposed as a genome editing tool. It closes with the bacteriophages — the viruses of bacteria — which are both ecological forces that shape bacterial evolution and exceptional molecular tools that have contributed some of the most important components of the synthetic biology toolkit.

The section opens with bacterial growth kinetics — one of the most quantitatively tractable areas in all of biology. The exponential growth equation, the Monod nutrient-limitation model, and chemostat steady-state analysis are the foundational models of microbial physiology, and they are prerequisite for interpreting almost any experiment done in bacteria. Crucially, growth rate is coupled to cellular physiology in ways that affect gene circuit behavior: faster-growing cells have more ribosomes, shorter generation times, and different proteome compositions than slower-growing cells. A circuit that was designed and characterized at one growth rate may behave differently at another, and the growth law described here explains why.

Quorum sensing follows, covering the LuxI/LuxR system and its relatives as canonical examples of population-level signal integration and bistable switching. Quorum sensing is important both as a biological phenomenon — it underlies biofilm formation, virulence, and antibiotic tolerance in clinically important pathogens — and as an engineering toolkit. The LuxR/lux promoter system is one of the most widely used inducible expression systems in synthetic biology, and the QS architecture (diffusible signal molecule + transcriptional activator with positive feedback) is a reusable design motif for building population-level logic.

The genetic manipulation tools subsection covers the three classical mechanisms by which DNA enters bacteria — transformation, transduction, and conjugation — at the mechanistic and quantitative level needed for experimental design and for modeling HGT. The section also covers the λ Red recombinase system for precise chromosomal editing, which is the basis for essentially all systematic gene knockout collections in bacteria.

CRISPR-Cas in native biology treats the CRISPR immune system as a biological phenomenon before it was an engineering tool. Understanding the native three-stage immune response (acquisition, processing, interference), the role of the PAM as a self/non-self discriminator, and the diversity of CRISPR-Cas systems is essential for understanding why certain guide RNAs work and others don't, why off-target effects occur, and what the biological constraints are on any system derived from CRISPR machinery.

The section closes with bacteriophage biology, covering the lytic/lysogenic life cycle decision, the lambda switch as the canonical bistable circuit, phage diversity, and Lotka-Volterra dynamics of phage-host coevolution. Lambda phage was the original proving ground for bistable switch theory; T7 RNAP is a workhorse of orthogonal transcription systems; phage display is a high-throughput directed evolution platform; and phage ecology is a quantitative modeling challenge that touches population genetics, game theory, and spatial dynamics simultaneously.

By the end of this section, you will understand bacteria not just as containers for circuits but as organisms with their own ecology, physiology, and evolutionary dynamics — organisms whose behavior your circuits must be designed to work within, and whose properties will shape the performance and stability of anything you build in them.
