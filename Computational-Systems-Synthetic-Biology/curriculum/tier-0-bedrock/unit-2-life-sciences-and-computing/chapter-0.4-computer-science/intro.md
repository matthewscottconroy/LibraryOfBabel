# Chapter 0.4: Computer Science

In 1981, Temple Smith and Michael Waterman published a two-page paper in the *Journal of Molecular Biology*. Its title was "Identification of Common Molecular Subsequences." The algorithm it described — what we now call Smith-Waterman local alignment — was a direct adaptation of a technique from optimization theory called dynamic programming, originally developed in the 1950s by Richard Bellman for operations research. Smith and Waterman took a method for finding optimal paths through decision problems and recognized that sequence alignment is exactly that kind of problem.

This kind of borrowing and reinvention defines the history of computational biology. When shotgun sequencing of whole genomes became possible in the 1990s, the assembly problem — how do you reconstruct a genome from millions of overlapping fragments? — turned out to be a variant of a classical graph theory problem: finding an Eulerian path through a de Bruijn graph. When short-read sequencing arrived in the 2000s and genomes needed to be indexed for rapid alignment, the solution came from data compression theory: the Burrows-Wheeler transform, invented in 1994 for text compression, turned out to enable $O(m)$ substring search over a genome of arbitrary size. When thousands of genomes needed to be compared simultaneously, MinHash sketching — a probabilistic technique from locality-sensitive hashing — provided approximate Jaccard similarity in sub-linear space. When protein structures needed to be predicted from sequence, transformer architectures developed for natural language processing became the foundation of AlphaFold2.

Computational biology did not merely borrow computer science. It invented its own solutions when existing ones were insufficient, and those inventions — k-mer counting, de Bruijn assembly, profile HMMs, FM-index alignment — have become foundational to the discipline. The relationship runs in both directions: bioinformatics has produced algorithmic innovations that are now studied in computer science curricula.

The reason for this pattern is the specific structure of biological data. Genomes are strings over a four-letter alphabet, which creates string algorithm problems of unprecedented scale. Biological sequences have evolutionary history, which means that comparing them requires scoring matrices derived from substitution rates, not simple character equality. Reads come from both strands of a double helix, which means that canonical k-mer counting must account for reverse complements. Gene expression matrices from single-cell experiments are sparse, high-dimensional, and too large for memory, requiring specialized array storage formats. Every one of these domain-specific features has driven the development of domain-specific computational solutions.

This chapter introduces the computer science foundations that underlie computational biology — not as abstract theory, but grounded in biological applications at every step.

**Section 1 (Programming)** covers the tool ecosystem: Python with NumPy, SciPy, Pandas, and BioPython for computational work; R with Bioconductor for statistical genomics; bash scripting for pipeline construction; and git for version control and reproducibility.

**Section 2 (Algorithms and Data Structures)** covers the algorithmic infrastructure: fundamental data structures (arrays, hash tables, trees, graphs), algorithm analysis with Big O notation, sorting and searching, dynamic programming for sequence alignment, string algorithms including the BWT/FM-index and de Bruijn graphs, probabilistic data structures for genome-scale counting, and graph algorithms for biological networks.

**Section 3 (Databases)** covers data management: relational databases and SQL for structured biological data, the major biological databases and their programmatic APIs, and NoSQL and array storage for data types that relational models cannot accommodate.

**Section 4 (Software Engineering)** covers the practices that make computational biology work reproducible: code quality and style, systematic testing, documentation, workflow managers and environment management for reproducibility, HPC cluster computing, and performance optimization.

**Section 5 (File Formats)** covers the language of bioinformatics tools: the FASTA, FASTQ, SAM/BAM/CRAM, VCF, BED, GFF3/GTF, and systems biology formats you will encounter in every analysis pipeline.

A word on how to use this chapter. The goal is not to memorize syntax or commands — those are reference material, available in documentation. The goal is to understand why things work the way they do: why generators are the right tool for large-file processing, why the FM-index enables $O(m)$ alignment, why DESeq2 uses a negative binomial model, why `set -euo pipefail` belongs at the top of every bash script. When you understand the why, the how follows naturally, and you can reason about novel problems rather than simply pattern-matching to familiar solutions.
