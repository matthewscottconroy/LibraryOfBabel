# Unit 2: Life Sciences and Computing

Here is a question worth taking seriously: why does computational biology require both biology and computer science, rather than just one of them? The answer is not merely logistical — not simply that "you need to program to do the analysis." It is deeper than that. Biology and computer science are each, in themselves, a way of thinking about information: how it is encoded, stored, transmitted, processed, corrupted, and evolved. When you bring both ways of thinking to bear on a biological system, you can ask questions that neither discipline can formulate on its own. Let us be concrete about what that means.

Biology without computation can discover that the *lac* operon is repressed by LacI and activated by CAP. Computation applied to this system can ask: given the known binding affinities, the copy numbers of LacI and CAP, the diffusion constants, and the transcription rates, what is the predicted distribution of mRNA levels across a population of cells — and how does this distribution depend on inducer concentration? The answer reveals bistability at intermediate IPTG concentrations, which explains why individual bacteria in a population can be either fully induced or fully uninduced even when the population average suggests an intermediate state. Without the biology, you have no system to model. Without the computation, you cannot extract the dynamical prediction that explains the population heterogeneity.

The reverse direction is equally powerful. Computer science without biology gives you a rich theory of networks: how information flows through them, how they can be designed to be robust or sensitive, how their computational complexity can be analyzed. Computational biology applies this theory to biological networks — gene regulatory networks, metabolic networks, protein interaction networks, neural circuits — and discovers that these networks were not designed but evolved, and that evolution has produced solutions to computational problems (signal integration, memory, pattern formation) that are sometimes elegant and sometimes deeply surprising. The biological detail constrains the models; the computational theory reveals the design principles.

This unit provides the two halves of this foundation.

**Chapter 0.3 (Biology)** covers the life sciences vocabulary and conceptual framework that a computational biologist needs. You will learn cell architecture and why it matters for circuit dynamics — specifically, why a gene circuit model for bacteria is structurally different from one for mammalian cells. You will trace the central dogma mechanistically, gaining quantitative intuition for the numbers that parameterize gene expression models: transcription rates, mRNA lifetimes, translation efficiencies, protein degradation rates. You will learn the genetics and genomics needed to interpret sequencing data and design genetic experiments — from Mendel's laws through epigenomics through pangenomics and metagenomics. You will learn the microbiology of bacteria, the primary chassis organisms of synthetic biology, in enough depth to understand how circuits behave in them and how they evolve. And you will learn evolutionary biology as a quantitative discipline — not just natural history but the mathematical framework for inferring selection from sequence data, for designing directed evolution experiments, and for predicting the evolutionary stability of synthetic constructs.

**Chapter 0.4 (Computer Science)** covers the computational foundations that a biologist entering computational work needs. Data structures and algorithms determine whether a bioinformatics analysis runs in seconds or hours on genome-scale data. Complexity theory provides the language for understanding why some computational problems in biology are hard in a fundamental sense — not just currently intractable but provably difficult. Probabilistic models and Bayesian inference are the statistical engines behind sequence alignment, phylogenetic reconstruction, variant calling, and regulatory motif finding. Dynamic programming, graph algorithms, and machine learning each contribute essential tools that appear throughout the computational biology curriculum.

The key insight that motivates bringing both chapters under a single unit is that biology and computer science are not simply "background knowledge" that you acquire once and then apply. They are lenses that you bring to every new problem, and their interaction is generative. When you learn that the spliceosome is a ribozyme that assembles de novo on each intron and catalyzes two transesterification reactions, you gain something beyond a biological fact: you gain a concrete example of a biological "parser" that operates on structured string inputs with specific grammatical rules (splice sites, branch points, polypyrimidine tracts). The computational abstraction illuminates the biology; the biological concreteness grounds the abstraction.

The questions that become answerable when you have both foundations include some of the most exciting open problems in science. How do cells compute cell fate decisions — processing noisy molecular signals and producing precise, heritable, irreversible outcomes? What is the minimal genetic circuit that can produce a spatial pattern from a homogeneous initial state? How much information does a genome actually contain about the organism it encodes? How rapidly does natural selection explore sequence space, and what does this tell us about the evolvability of proteins? How can we design genetic circuits that perform specified computations robustly, in the face of molecular noise and evolutionary pressure to delete them?

These are not rhetorical questions. They are active research programs, and the foundations in this unit are what you need to engage with them seriously.
