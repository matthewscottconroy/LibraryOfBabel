# Section 1: Pairwise Sequence Alignment

Comparative biology has always depended on recognizing similarity — Richard Owen's concept of homology in the nineteenth century, Linus Pauling's discovery of molecular disease in sickle-cell hemoglobin, Francis Crick's sequence hypothesis connecting DNA to protein. But recognizing similarity by eye does not scale. When a newly sequenced gene has 400 amino acids and you want to know whether it resembles any of the 250 million sequences in UniProt, you need a precise, mathematical definition of "resemblance." Pairwise sequence alignment is that definition.

This section lays the algorithmic and conceptual foundations for everything that follows in the chapter. We begin with the alignment problem itself — what it means to align two sequences, why there is a scoring function, and why the problem is non-trivial. Then we build up the mathematical machinery: edit distance as the simplest model of sequence similarity, dynamic programming as the efficient algorithm for optimizing that similarity, substitution matrices as the evolutionary interpretation of scoring functions, and BLAST as the practical implementation that made database search tractable at genomic scale.

These five topics form a logical chain: each one motivates or enables the next. Edit distance explains why alignment is an optimization problem. Dynamic programming explains how to solve that problem exactly. Substitution matrices explain what biological meaning to give the scoring function. BLAST explains how to search databases at scale when exact dynamic programming is too slow. By the end of this section, you will understand not just how to run an alignment or a BLAST search, but what those tools are actually computing and why each design choice was made.

The central insight of pairwise alignment is that sequence similarity can be decomposed into three kinds of events — matches, substitutions, and indels — each with a cost that reflects its frequency in evolution. The alignment problem asks: given these costs, what is the best arrangement of the two sequences? Dynamic programming solves this efficiently by exploiting optimal substructure: the best alignment through any position depends only on the best alignments through nearby positions, so you can build the solution cell by cell without revisiting completed work.

The biological payoff of this machinery is enormous. Before Needleman-Wunsch (1970) and Smith-Waterman (1981), sequence comparison was qualitative. After those algorithms and the practical tools they enabled, it became quantitative. The researcher who pastes an unknown sequence into NCBI BLAST and discovers in under a minute that it shares 43% identity with a characterized enzyme — with an E-value of $10^{-32}$, implying almost certain homology — is relying on every concept in this section. Understanding how that number was produced, and what it means, is the goal.
