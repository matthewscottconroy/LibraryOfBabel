# Section 2: Multiple Sequence Alignment

Two sequences tell you whether a relationship exists. Many sequences tell you what that relationship means.

That is the central promise of multiple sequence alignment. When you align 500 homologs of a protein across the tree of life — bacteria, archaea, fungi, plants, animals — you are not just computing similarities. You are reading the evolutionary record. Positions conserved across 3 billion years of evolution are under selection because they matter. Positions that tolerate change are under no constraint. The pattern of conservation and variation, visible only when many sequences are aligned together, is the most direct window into protein function that sequence data provides.

This section covers the theory and practice of multiple sequence alignment (MSA), building from the conceptual motivation — why pairwise alignment is insufficient and what MSA adds — through the principal algorithmic strategies: progressive methods that align sequences sequentially from most similar to most divergent, and iterative methods that refine alignments by repeatedly realigning subsets to escape local optima. We then examine how to assess alignment quality, because computing an alignment is not the same as computing a good alignment, and because downstream analyses are only as trustworthy as the alignments that feed them. The section concludes with profile Hidden Markov Models — the probabilistic generalization of an alignment that provides the most sensitive known method for detecting distant sequence homologs.

The key computational challenge is that exact multiple alignment is NP-hard: the search space grows exponentially with the number of sequences, and finding the globally optimal alignment is computationally impossible except for very small problems. All practical methods are heuristics, and understanding their failure modes — the circumstances under which early progressive errors cascade through the alignment, or where iterative refinement gets stuck in a local optimum — is essential for using them wisely.

The biological applications of MSA are consequential enough to motivate careful attention to all of this machinery. Phylogenetic trees are built from MSAs; errors in the alignment change the topology of the tree. AlphaFold2 takes an MSA as its primary input; the coevolutionary signal that enables structure prediction lives in the pattern of correlated substitutions across alignment columns. Pfam's ~19,000 protein family profiles, which annotate hundreds of millions of sequences, are each built from a curated MSA. When the alignment is wrong, everything downstream is wrong. Getting MSA right — choosing the right tool, checking quality, trimming uncertain regions — is one of the highest-leverage decisions in a bioinformatics pipeline.
