# Section 4: Short Read Mapping and Assembly

The sequencer produces reads. Biology cares about genomes.

Between these two things — millions of short fragments and the complete genomic sequence — lies the work of this section. Read mapping and genome assembly are the two strategies for bridging that gap, each appropriate for different circumstances. When a high-quality reference genome exists for the organism you are studying, you map reads to it: each read is placed at its position in the reference, and the aggregate pattern of placements reveals variation, expression, accessibility, or modification depending on the experiment. When no reference exists, you assemble: the overlapping structure of the reads is used to reconstruct the original sequence from scratch, without any prior knowledge of what it should look like.

These are not merely technical problems. They are problems with deep mathematical structure. Read mapping, done naively, would require comparing each of 500 million reads to each of 3.2 billion reference positions — an astronomical number of comparisons that would take years. The Burrows-Wheeler Transform, borrowed from data compression, reduces this to a search linear in the query length, regardless of reference size. Genome assembly, done naively, would require finding the Hamiltonian path through an overlap graph — an NP-hard problem. The de Bruijn graph reformulation converts the problem to finding an Eulerian path, which is solvable in linear time. In both cases, the right mathematical representation makes the intractable tractable.

The biological payoff is equally substantial. Short read mapping is the foundation of modern genomics: every whole-genome sequencing study that discovers a disease variant, every RNA-seq experiment that measures gene expression, every ChIP-seq experiment that maps transcription factor binding — all of these require correctly mapped reads. Genome assembly is the foundation of reference genomes, which are in turn the foundation of all reference-based analysis. The quality of the assemblies you use determines the quality of every variant call, expression estimate, and annotation that depends on them.

This section covers the algorithmic basis of read mapping, the principal aligners for different data types (DNA sequencing, RNA-seq, long reads), the de Bruijn graph approach to genome assembly, and the metrics used to evaluate assembly quality. The two topics are closely related: understanding assembly explains why reference genomes have gaps and why certain regions are systematically underrepresented; understanding mapping explains why reference-based analyses inherit the limitations of the reference.
