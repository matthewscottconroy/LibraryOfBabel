# Chapter 1.2: Genomics

In October 2003, the Human Genome Project announced its completion after thirteen years and approximately $3 billion in funding. The project had sequenced all 3.2 billion base pairs of the human genome, involving hundreds of researchers at institutions across twenty countries, and used technology — Sanger sequencing at industrial scale — that required enormous investments in instrumentation, labor, and logistics. The result was extraordinary: the first complete readout of the molecular instructions that build a human being. For a moment, it seemed like the culmination of something.

It turned out to be the beginning.

In the years immediately following the Human Genome Project, sequencing costs began to fall — not gradually, but catastrophically, at a rate that made Moore's Law look sluggish by comparison. The first generation of "next-generation sequencing" platforms arrived around 2005, and the cost per genome dropped from $3 billion to $10 million. By 2010, it was under $50,000. By 2015, it was under $5,000. Today, a human genome can be sequenced for less than $200 — less than a routine blood panel at a hospital — and the number of human genomes sequenced annually is measured in the millions.

This is not merely a story about cheaper technology. Cheaper sequencing changed what questions could be asked. When a genome costs $3 billion, you sequence one, and you learn about human biology in general. When a genome costs $200, you can sequence thousands of patients with the same disease and statistically map the genetic variants that cause it. You can sequence every bacterium in a gram of soil, reconstructing the genomes of organisms that have never been cultured. You can sequence the tumor and the matched normal tissue from every cancer patient in a clinical trial. You can track the evolution of a pathogen in real time during an outbreak. Genomics, in the modern sense, is the entire intellectual infrastructure built to extract meaning from this data deluge.

This chapter covers that infrastructure. We begin with variant calling — the problem of identifying where an individual's genome differs from the reference, which lies at the heart of clinical genomics, cancer genomics, and population genetics. You will learn the spectrum of genomic variation from single nucleotides to chromosomal rearrangements, the GATK best practices pipeline that identifies variants from sequencing reads, the population genetics metrics that make variants interpretable, and the GWAS framework that maps variants to phenotypes at population scale. Special attention is given to somatic variant calling, where tumor heterogeneity, low allele fractions, and the absence of a population reference create unique statistical challenges.

Genome annotation addresses a different but equally fundamental problem: given a genome sequence, determine what it encodes. Ab initio gene prediction uses statistical models of gene structure to find genes from sequence alone. Evidence-based annotation integrates RNA-seq and protein homology data to produce high-quality gene models. Functional annotation assigns biological meaning to predicted genes through database comparisons and ontology frameworks. You will also learn the file formats — GFF3, GTF, GenBank — in which annotations are stored and exchanged, along with the coordinate conventions that, when misunderstood, cause systematic errors in downstream analyses.

Comparative genomics uses the evolutionary signal encoded in sequence conservation to understand function and history. Synteny analysis reveals large-scale chromosomal organization across species. Ortholog and paralog identification distinguishes genes that share function by common descent from those that have diverged after duplication. Whole genome duplication events — key drivers of evolutionary novelty in vertebrates, plants, and fungi — leave distinctive signatures in synteny and synonymous substitution distributions. And pan-genomes capture the reality that no single reference genome can represent the full sequence diversity of a species, particularly relevant in bacteria and agriculturally important plants.

Metagenomics extends genomics beyond the individual organism to the community. Shotgun metagenomics sequences all DNA in an environmental sample, enabling taxonomic profiling, functional annotation, and reconstruction of individual microbial genomes (MAGs) without culturing. Amplicon sequencing focuses on phylogenetic marker genes for cost-effective community profiling. Together, these methods have revealed the hidden majority of microbial life and are providing the tools for characterizing the microbiome in health, disease, and environmental contexts.

Epigenomics addresses the layer of regulatory information that exists above the DNA sequence itself: the chromatin accessibility, histone modifications, DNA methylation patterns, and three-dimensional genome architecture that determine which genes are expressed in which cell types. The epigenome is not static — it is dynamically remodeled during development, disease, and in response to environmental signals — and understanding it requires four interconnected experimental technologies: ATAC-seq, ChIP-seq/CUT&RUN, bisulfite sequencing, and Hi-C.

Finally, a practical workflow section integrates these approaches into an end-to-end computational pipeline, emphasizing not just the commands but the rationale for each step and the workflow management tools that make multi-sample genomics reproducible.

The Human Genome Project cost $3 billion and took thirteen years. The data analyzed in a single modern clinical sequencing center in a year costs a fraction of that and is orders of magnitude larger in scope. The challenge is no longer generating the data. The challenge — the interesting, hard, intellectually rich challenge — is making sense of it. This chapter gives you the tools to start.
