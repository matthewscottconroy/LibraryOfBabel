# Section 1: Variant Calling

The genome sequence you carry is not quite the reference genome in the database. Between any two people, there are approximately 4–5 million nucleotide differences — single-letter changes, small insertions and deletions, large copy number variants, structural rearrangements. These differences are the raw material of evolution and the molecular basis of disease. The field of variant calling is devoted to finding them: given a set of sequencing reads from a new sample, identify every position where that sample differs from the reference, report the nature of the difference, and assign confidence to each call.

This is deceptively difficult. The reads contain errors. The alignment is imperfect. The genome has repetitive regions where reads can map ambiguously. And the signal you are trying to detect — a true variant — can look almost identical to an instrument artifact or an alignment mistake. Early variant callers were naive about these failure modes, and the field learned their consequences the hard way: false variants that didn't replicate, real variants that were missed, and years of wasted effort tracing results that turned out to be computational artifacts.

The current generation of variant calling pipelines — centered on the GATK toolkit — represents the accumulated learning from those failures. Each step of the GATK best practices pipeline exists because a simpler approach failed in a specific way. Base quality score recalibration exists because instruments systematically miscalibrate their quality scores. Duplicate marking exists because PCR inflates apparent evidence without adding information. HaplotypeCaller's local assembly exists because simple pileup-based callers fail at indels and in repetitive regions. Variant quality score recalibration exists because no single quality metric cleanly separates true variants from false positives.

This section builds your understanding of variant calling from the ground up. You will begin with the biological question — what types of genomic variation exist, how do they arise, and why do they matter — before moving to the computational methodology that detects them. You will learn to run the GATK pipeline and, more importantly, to understand why each step is there. You will then move outward to the population genetics context that makes variant data interpretable: allele frequencies, Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, linkage disequilibrium, and the statistical tools for measuring population structure.

From there, the section turns to two specific applications that represent the field's most important use cases. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) use population-scale variant data to map the genetic architecture of complex human traits — using statistical methods that must carefully account for multiple testing, population stratification, and LD structure. And somatic variant calling — the detection of mutations that arose in tumor cells — presents a different and harder set of challenges: low variant allele fractions, tumor heterogeneity, no population reference for filtering, and a direct clinical payoff when driver mutations are correctly identified.

By the end of this section, you should be able to run a complete germline and somatic variant calling pipeline, interpret its QC metrics, understand what can go wrong and why, and situate variant calls in their population genetics context. These skills are foundational: they underlie GWAS, cancer genomics, clinical diagnostics, and population genomics studies.
