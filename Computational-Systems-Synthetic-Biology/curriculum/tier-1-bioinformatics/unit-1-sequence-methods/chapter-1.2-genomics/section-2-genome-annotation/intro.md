# Section 2: Genome Annotation

Imagine that you have just assembled the first complete genome of a newly discovered organism — a deep-sea bacterium with remarkable heat tolerance, or a plant with extraordinary drought resistance. The assembly has run, the contigs have been scaffolded into chromosomes, and you are looking at 450 megabases of DNA. What you have at this moment is impressive but essentially uninterpretable. You know the sequence; you do not know the biology.

Genome annotation is the process of changing that. It is the systematic identification of all the functional elements in a genome sequence: the protein-coding genes, their exon structures, their splice variants, their regulatory elements, and ultimately the biological functions encoded by those elements. Without annotation, a genome sequence is like a book written in an alphabet you can read but a language you do not understand — you can see the letters but not the meaning.

The challenge is that gene prediction from sequence alone is genuinely hard. Unlike in prokaryotes, where genes are densely packed and straightforward to identify, eukaryotic gene structures are complex: genes are interrupted by introns that can span hundreds of kilobases, alternative splicing generates multiple protein isoforms from single loci, and regulatory elements are scattered across non-coding sequence in ways that sequence alone cannot easily reveal. Ab initio prediction tools use statistical models of gene structure — Hidden Markov Models trained on the species-specific signatures of splice sites, codon usage, and hexamer frequencies — to find genes without any experimental evidence. They work, but imperfectly: they miss small exons, get splice boundaries wrong in complex regions, and predict only one isoform per locus.

The practical solution is to combine multiple evidence types. RNA-seq data from the organism provides direct evidence for which sequences are actually transcribed, revealing splice junctions and isoforms with base-pair resolution. Protein sequences from related organisms, aligned to the genome, reveal exon positions even for genes not expressed in your sampled conditions. Ab initio prediction fills in the gaps — regions where no experimental evidence is available. Pipelines like MAKER and BRAKER2 integrate all three evidence types, weighting each by its quality and resolving conflicts to produce the best supported gene model.

The section then turns to functional annotation — assigning biological meaning to the predicted gene products. This relies primarily on sequence similarity to proteins of known function, enriched by domain-level analysis with tools like InterProScan, organized by the Gene Ontology hierarchy, and connected to metabolic pathways through KEGG. Finally, you will learn the file formats in which annotations are stored and exchanged: GFF3, GTF, and GenBank — each with specific coordinate conventions, feature hierarchies, and use cases that you need to understand to avoid systematic errors when combining annotation data with other genomic analyses.

The quality of a genome annotation directly determines the quality of every downstream analysis that uses it. Incorrect exon boundaries corrupt differential expression analyses. Missing genes lead to incomplete pathway reconstructions. Propagated functional annotation errors seed incorrect biological conclusions across the literature. By the end of this section, you will be able to evaluate annotation quality critically, use the major annotation tools, and interpret the annotations you encounter with appropriate skepticism.
