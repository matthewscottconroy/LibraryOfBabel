# Section 3: Comparative Genomics

In 1975, the year before the first complete DNA sequence of any organism was published, Allan Wilson and Mary-Claire King reported that the protein sequences of chimpanzees and humans differ by only about 1%. The finding was striking and counterintuitive: two species so morphologically, behaviorally, and cognitively different shared almost identical proteins. If the proteins are so similar, where does the difference come from?

The answer, we now know, lies largely in the comparison of complete genomes — not just the genes, but the regulatory elements, the structural organization, the copy number differences, and the non-coding sequences between genes. Comparative genomics is the systematic study of how genomes differ across species (and within species), what those differences tell us about function and evolution, and how we can use genomic conservation to identify important sequences we would not otherwise know to look at.

The power of comparative genomics rests on a simple observation: if a sequence has been conserved across millions of years of evolution, selection must have been maintaining it. Non-functional sequences accumulate mutations freely; functional sequences are constrained by purifying selection. When you align the human genome to the mouse genome — separated by ~90 million years of evolution — and find a small sequence that is 98% identical despite all that time, you have strong evidence that this sequence does something important, even if you don't yet know what. This is how the ENCODE project identified thousands of putative regulatory elements: by looking for mammalian conservation in non-coding sequence.

This section covers four interconnected topics in comparative genomics. Synteny analysis examines the large-scale conservation of gene order across chromosomes — revealing chromosomal rearrangements, guiding genome assembly, and enabling annotation transfer between species. Ortholog and paralog identification addresses the fundamental problem of determining which genes in different species are truly homologous through speciation (orthologs) versus through gene duplication (paralogs) — a distinction that determines whether sequence similarity implies functional similarity.

Whole genome duplication is the most dramatic form of genomic rearrangement: the doubling of the entire genome, which has occurred multiple times in vertebrate evolution, in yeast, and repeatedly in plant lineages. Understanding WGD is essential for interpreting gene family sizes, identifying retained ohnologs, and explaining why plants are so extraordinarily tractable for polyploid breeding.

Finally, pan-genomes represent one of the most significant current shifts in the field: the recognition that no single genome can represent the full sequence diversity of a species. Bacteria and plants have particularly open pan-genomes, where different strains or lines can differ dramatically in their gene content. The shift from linear references to graph-based pan-genomes is transforming how we represent, query, and interpret genomic variation at the population level.

Together, these topics provide the conceptual and computational vocabulary for reading evolutionary history from DNA sequences — and for using that history to understand function, predict consequences of variation, and design better biological systems.
