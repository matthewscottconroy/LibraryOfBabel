# Section 5: Epigenomics

Consider two facts that seem, at first, to be in direct tension. First: every cell in your body (with minor exceptions) carries essentially the same DNA sequence, encoding the same roughly 20,000 protein-coding genes. Second: a liver hepatocyte and a hippocampal neuron look completely different, behave completely differently, produce completely different proteins, and respond to their environments in completely different ways. If the genomes are the same, why are the cells so different?

The answer lies in epigenomics — the study of heritable modifications to chromatin that regulate which parts of the genome are accessible, which genes are expressed, and which regulatory elements are active, without changing the underlying DNA sequence. The epigenome is the cell-type-specific software that runs on the genome's hardware. Different cell types have different epigenomes even though they share the same genome, and these differences are maintained through cell division, reversed during reprogramming, and disrupted in disease.

The epigenome has four major layers, each studied by distinct experimental methods. Chromatin accessibility — measured by ATAC-seq — reveals the nucleosome-depleted regions where transcription factors bind and regulatory elements are active. Only 1–3% of the genome is in open chromatin in any given cell type, and this fraction is highly cell-type-specific: it defines the active regulatory landscape that determines which genes can be transcribed. Histone modifications — measured by ChIP-seq and its modern successors CUT&RUN and CUT&TAG — encode the histone code, a combinatorial language of covalent marks on histone tails that specifies whether a region is an active promoter, an active enhancer, a Polycomb-repressed gene, or constitutive heterochromatin. Different modification patterns at the same locus can mean completely different things: H3K27ac marks active enhancers, while H3K27me3 at the same locus marks Polycomb silencing.

DNA methylation — measured by bisulfite sequencing and methylation arrays — is the most stable epigenetic mark: methyl groups added to cytosines in CpG dinucleotides persist through DNA replication and are maintained faithfully over cell generations. Promoter methylation silences gene expression; gene body methylation actually facilitates it. And the three-dimensional architecture of the genome — measured by Hi-C — determines how regulatory elements find their target genes across tens or hundreds of kilobases of intervening sequence. Enhancers, promoters, and insulators interact in three-dimensional space through chromatin loops anchored by CTCF and cohesin, organized into topologically associating domains (TADs) that insulate regulatory interactions.

These four layers are not independent. Open chromatin is enriched for specific histone marks; DNA methylation and chromatin accessibility are inversely correlated; TAD boundaries are enriched for CTCF binding and active transcription. The epigenome is an integrated, multi-layered regulatory system, and understanding it requires reading all four layers simultaneously. That integration is increasingly tractable: the ENCODE project has generated reference epigenomes for hundreds of human cell types, the Roadmap Epigenomics project profiled human tissues and developmental stages, and the single-cell epigenomics revolution is now extending these measurements to individual cells.

The biological payoff is substantial. The majority of GWAS variants for complex diseases fall in non-coding sequence — and their mechanism of action is almost always epigenomic. A variant in an enhancer ATAC-seq peak, disrupting a transcription factor binding site, in a cell type relevant to the disease: this is how most common genetic risk variants work. Epigenomics provides the mechanistic context that converts GWAS associations into biological hypotheses. For cancer, epigenomic disruption is as central as genetic mutation: DNMT inhibitors, HDAC inhibitors, and EZH2 inhibitors are all approved cancer therapies, targeting the machinery that writes and maintains epigenetic marks. And for synthetic biology, understanding the epigenomic state of a chassis organism — which regions of its genome are accessible, which genes are silenced — is essential for predicting where introduced regulatory circuits will function as designed.
