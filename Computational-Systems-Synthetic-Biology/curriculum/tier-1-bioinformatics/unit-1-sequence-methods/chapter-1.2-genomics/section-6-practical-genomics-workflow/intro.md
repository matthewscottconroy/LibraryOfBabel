# Section 6: Practical Genomics Workflow

Everything in the preceding sections — the variant calling theory, the annotation tools, the epigenomic methods — ultimately runs on FASTQ files. Raw reads from a sequencer, paired or unpaired, compressed or not, are the universal input to genomics analysis. Between those raw reads and an interpretable biological result lies a sequence of computational steps that is remarkably standardized across the field: quality control, adapter trimming, alignment, duplicate marking, recalibration, variant calling, annotation. Each step has a purpose; each step has failure modes; each step has best practices refined by years of collective experience.

This section is about building that pipeline — not just knowing the commands, but understanding why they are run in that order, what each step is protecting you from, and how to recognize when something has gone wrong. The goal is not to produce a analyst who can copy-paste commands from a tutorial. It is to produce one who can look at a QC report and know whether their data is clean, look at a Ti/Tv ratio and know whether their variant calls are credible, and look at a VAF distribution and know whether it reflects biology or artifacts.

The practical genomics workflow is also an exercise in computational infrastructure. Real genomics projects are not run by typing commands one at a time. They involve dozens to thousands of samples, heterogeneous compute environments (local servers, cloud platforms, HPC clusters), and the constant danger of interrupted jobs, disk failures, and version changes in tool dependencies. Workflow management systems — Snakemake, Nextflow, WDL/Cromwell — address these challenges by encoding pipelines as formal specifications: directed acyclic graphs of rules with explicit input/output dependencies. When a job fails, the workflow manager knows exactly what needs to be re-run. When you add a new sample, it knows what is already complete. When you want to share your analysis with a collaborator, the workflow file is the definitive, reproducible record of what was done.

The single workflow covered in this section — a short-read germline whole-genome or exome sequencing pipeline — serves as the archetype. Once you understand why each step exists in this pipeline, you can read any other genomics pipeline (RNA-seq, ChIP-seq, ATAC-seq, metagenomics) and recognize the analogous steps and their analogous rationales. The FASTQC-trim-align-markdup-recalibrate-call pattern is not arbitrary. It is the distillation of everything the field has learned about what can go wrong with sequencing data and how to correct it before those errors contaminate your science.
