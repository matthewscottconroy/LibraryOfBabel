# Section 4: Common Analysis Pitfalls

In 2012, a team of statisticians at MD Anderson Cancer Center published a paper that read more like forensic science than biology. Keith Baggerly and Kevin Coombes had spent years reverse-engineering the data behind a series of high-profile genomic medicine papers — papers that had led to clinical trials. What they found was systematic: batch effects that had been ignored, sample labels that had been shifted, statistical analyses that had been conducted without appropriate multiple testing correction. The papers were retracted. The trials were halted. And genomics entered a period of uncomfortable self-examination about whether its methods were rigorous enough to support the clinical applications they were being asked to enable.

The two failure modes Baggerly and Coombes identified — batch effects and multiple testing violations — are not exotic or obscure. They appear in almost every large-scale transcriptomics study conducted without deliberate attention to these issues. They are the most common ways that technically correct-looking analyses produce biologically incorrect conclusions. This section exists to make sure you are immunized against them.

## What You Will Learn in This Section

**Subsection 1: Batch Effects and Normalization** explains what batch effects are, why they arise (varying processing dates, reagent lots, technicians, RNA quality), and why they are so dangerous — they produce systematic, non-random variation that looks exactly like a biological signal. You will learn to detect batch effects using PCA and hierarchical clustering before any DE analysis is run, and to correct them appropriately using ComBat for bulk data and Harmony or scVI for single-cell data. Most importantly, you will learn the critical distinction between batch correction for visualization (appropriate) and batch correction as a preprocessing step before DE analysis (inappropriate and statistically invalid). The correct approach — including batch as a covariate in the DE model — is explained with working code.

**Subsection 2: Multiple Testing and Thresholds** starts from first principles: why is a p-value of 0.05 meaningless when applied to 20,000 simultaneous tests? You will understand the difference between controlling the family-wise error rate (Bonferroni, appropriate for confirmatory testing) and controlling the false discovery rate (Benjamini-Hochberg, appropriate for discovery-oriented genomics), and why FDR is the right choice for differential expression analysis. The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure is derived step-by-step, and the practical question of choosing FDR thresholds (0.05 vs. 0.10 vs. 0.01) is addressed in terms of the biology being done. The p-value histogram is introduced as the single most informative diagnostic plot for detecting statistical problems before interpreting results, with the characteristic shapes of inflation, conservation, and clean data explained.

## The Logical Flow

These two subsections address orthogonal problems that compound when both are present. Batch effects inflate apparent fold changes; multiple testing inflation multiplies the chance of declaring those artificial fold changes significant. A study plagued by both will report a large, statistically significant gene list that is almost entirely artifactual. Read both subsections before conducting any DE analysis, regardless of which specific tools you are using — the statistical principles apply equally to DESeq2, edgeR, limma, and their single-cell equivalents.

This section is short relative to the technical content elsewhere in the chapter, but it is not minor. The judgment to check PCA before running DESeq2, to include batch in the design formula rather than pre-correcting, to report adjusted rather than raw p-values, and to specify thresholds before seeing results — these habits separate reproducible science from the kind that gets retracted.
