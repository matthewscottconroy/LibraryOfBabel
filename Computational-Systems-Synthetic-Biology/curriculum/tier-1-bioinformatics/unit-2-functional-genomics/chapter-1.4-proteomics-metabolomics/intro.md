# Chapter 1.4: Proteomics and Metabolomics

DNA is the blueprint. RNA is the photocopy of the relevant sections. But proteins are what actually runs the cell — the molecular machines that catalyze reactions, transmit signals, build structures, transport molecules, recognize pathogens, regulate gene expression, and execute every other function that constitutes being alive. When a signaling pathway is activated, when a cancer cell becomes drug resistant, when a bacterium responds to an antibiotic, when a neuron fires — in every case, the story is ultimately told in proteins and their small-molecule substrates and products. The proteome and the metabolome are, in a real sense, the cell in action.

This observation is not merely poetic. It has a practical consequence that shapes everything in this chapter: the genome and transcriptome, as informative as they are, are proxies for the biology that actually matters. Measuring mRNA tells you about transcriptional intentions. Measuring proteins tells you about functional deployment. And measuring metabolites — the small molecules that proteins produce and consume — tells you what the cellular machinery is actually doing right now, moment to moment, in response to its environment. The correlation between mRNA and protein levels for the same gene, measured across human cell types, is approximately 0.4. That number should make you pause: it means that genomics and transcriptomics, for all their power, cannot substitute for direct measurement of the molecular machines and chemical currency of life.

Proteomics and metabolomics are the experimental technologies that make these direct measurements possible. They are both, at their core, mass spectrometry technologies — and the first section of this chapter is devoted to understanding why. Mass spectrometry measures the mass-to-charge ratio of ionized molecules with extraordinary precision, and from that measurement, in combination with fragmentation patterns and database matching, it can identify and quantify thousands of proteins or metabolites in a single experiment. The instrument is elegant in conception and fiendishly complex in practice, and understanding its architecture — ion source, mass analyzer, detector — and the consequences of its design choices is the prerequisite for working intelligently with any proteomics or metabolomics dataset.

The second section dives into proteomics proper. The dominant approach is shotgun (bottom-up) proteomics: digest all the proteins in your sample to peptides with trypsin, separate the peptides by nano-liquid chromatography, and identify them by tandem MS. From the peptide identifications, infer the proteins. It is counterintuitive — why destroy the proteins to identify them? — but it works, and it scales: a modern Orbitrap-based instrument can identify 8,000–10,000 proteins in a two-hour run. We cover quantitative strategies (label-free quantification, SILAC, and isobaric TMT labels), the data-independent acquisition revolution that has largely solved the missing-value problem, structural and interaction proteomics methods that reveal how proteins physically behave and associate, and PTM proteomics — the analysis of phosphorylation, ubiquitination, acetylation, and glycosylation that reveals the regulatory layer written on top of protein expression.

The third section turns to metabolomics, where the analytical challenges are, if anything, more severe. The metabolome is chemically diverse beyond easy description: amino acids, lipids, organic acids, nucleotides, secondary metabolites, xenobiotics — molecules spanning twelve orders of magnitude in plasma concentration and four orders of magnitude in molecular weight, all present simultaneously in your sample. The targeted-versus-untargeted choice, the NMR-versus-MS platform decision, the LC-MS workflow from sample quenching to feature alignment and normalization, the annotation problem that leaves 70–90% of untargeted features structurally uncharacterized — these are the challenges that define the field. We close the metabolomics section with metabolic flux analysis: the ¹³C isotope tracing methodology that measures not just what metabolite pools exist but how rapidly they are being turned over, providing a mechanistic, quantitative account of cellular metabolism that no steady-state measurement can substitute for.

The fourth and final section addresses multi-omics integration — the computational synthesis of proteomics, metabolomics, transcriptomics, and genomics data from the same samples. Each omics layer is a different camera angle on the same biology. No single angle is complete; together, they provide a picture that individual layers cannot. The tools for integration range from simple correlation analysis to sophisticated probabilistic latent factor models (MOFA+), from PLS-based supervised methods (mixOmics DIABLO) to network-based approaches (OmicsIntegrator) and causal inference via Mendelian randomization. The TCGA and UK Biobank multi-omics datasets, available to anyone with an internet connection, have already yielded therapeutic targets and patient stratifications that individual omics could not find. The integration of multiple omics layers is increasingly the standard approach for large-scale systems biology studies — because the biology is integrated, and so our measurements must be too.

Two threads run through the entire chapter. The first is the technological: mass spectrometry is the enabling technology, and understanding its physics and engineering constraints tells you what is measurable, what is missing, and what is artifact. The second is the biological: every technique here was developed in response to a specific biological question that simpler methods could not answer. The Warburg effect, the discovery of oncometabolites in IDH-mutant glioma, the identification of kinase substrate networks in cancer signaling, the patient stratification in colorectal cancer that transcriptomics alone could not achieve — these are the payoffs. Keep the biology in mind as you work through the technical details, because the details only make sense in the context of the questions they were designed to answer.
