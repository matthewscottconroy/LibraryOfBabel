# Section 1: Mass Spectrometry Fundamentals

Ask someone who does not work with mass spectrometry what it does, and they will probably say something like "it measures the masses of molecules." That is technically true, but it captures almost none of what makes mass spectrometry the transformative technology at the center of modern proteomics and metabolomics. A more honest answer: mass spectrometry is a machine that can, in a single two-hour experiment, identify and quantify thousands of protein species from a complex biological mixture — distinguishing a phosphopeptide from its unmodified counterpart by 80 Da, resolving two metabolites that differ only in the arrangement of a methyl group, and doing it all with sub-parts-per-million mass accuracy. To do this requires not one but three interconnected physical systems — ion source, mass analyzer, and detector — each with its own engineering constraints, its own characteristic artifacts, and its own set of biological implications.

This section builds the physical and conceptual foundation that all of proteomics and metabolomics stands on. You will not get far in interpreting MS data, troubleshooting experiments, or critically evaluating published papers without understanding how ions are made, how they are separated, and how they are identified. The concepts here are not detours — they are the prerequisites for understanding why certain proteins are always missing from plasma proteomics data, why phosphopeptide identification requires special fragmentation methods, why DIA has largely replaced DDA for quantitative work, and dozens of other practical and conceptual features of the field.

We begin with the mass spectrometer itself: its three-module architecture, the crucial distinction between m/z and mass, and the dynamic range limitations that make plasma proteomics so technically challenging. We then turn to the ion source — the gateway that converts biological molecules from solution into gas-phase ions — and examine why electrospray ionization and MALDI occupy entirely different niches despite both being "soft" ionization methods. The mass analyzer section covers the four major analyzer types (quadrupole, TOF, Orbitrap, ion trap) with enough physical depth to understand why a quadrupole excels at targeted quantification while an Orbitrap dominates untargeted proteomics. We finish with tandem mass spectrometry — the fragmentation and sequencing step that transforms a mass measurement into a molecular identity — including the b/y ion series for peptide sequencing, database searching, and the target-decoy approach to false discovery rate control.

Throughout, the emphasis is on connecting instrumental principles to biological consequences. Every design decision in mass spectrometry involves trade-offs: speed versus sensitivity, resolution versus scan rate, targeted depth versus untargeted breadth. Understanding those trade-offs is what allows you to read an instrument name in a Methods section and immediately understand something about the data quality, the biological question the experiment can answer, and the artifacts that might be lurking in the results.

By the end of this section, you should be able to trace the path of a tryptic peptide from the moment it exits the LC column — ionization in the nanospray emitter, transmission through the quadrupole precursor filter, fragmentation in the HCD cell, detection in the Orbitrap, database matching, and FDR filtering — and understand why each step looks the way it does. That mechanistic understanding is what separates a computational biologist who can truly work with proteomics data from one who is merely operating a pipeline.
