# Section 2: Proteomics

The human genome encodes approximately 20,000 protein-coding genes. The human proteome is something else entirely. Alternative splicing produces multiple protein isoforms from many genes. Post-translational modifications — phosphorylation, ubiquitination, acetylation, glycosylation, and dozens more — diversify each protein's functional states. Protein complex assembly, localization, and turnover rates vary enormously between cell types and physiological conditions. Conservative estimates suggest the functional proteome comprises hundreds of thousands of distinct molecular species, all descended from that genome of 20,000 genes, and all varying dynamically with the cell's state.

If the genome is the instruction manual, the proteome is the cell actually running. And the gap between those two things — between what is encoded and what is present and active — is where most of the interesting biology lives. Drug targets are proteins, not genes. Disease mechanisms are executed by protein activity changes, not sequence mutations alone. Cellular decisions — to divide, differentiate, die, or resist chemotherapy — are implemented through changes in the proteome. This is why proteomics exists: to measure the actual molecular machinery of the cell rather than just the blueprint.

This section covers the major strategies for proteomics at scale, organized around the fundamental questions each approach answers. We start with shotgun (bottom-up) proteomics: the digestion of all proteins to peptides, their separation by liquid chromatography, and their identification by tandem MS. This is the workhorse of global proteome analysis, capable of identifying 8,000–10,000 proteins from a cell line in a single day, and understanding its logic — why trypsin, why nano-LC, why DDA, why the protein inference problem matters — is prerequisite for working with proteomics datasets.

We then address the quantification problem: how do you assign numbers to protein abundances and compare them reliably across conditions? Label-free quantification, SILAC, and TMT labeling each offer a different compromise between throughput, accuracy, sample compatibility, and cost. The choice of quantification strategy shapes the entire experimental design and profoundly affects what you can conclude. We devote particular attention to data-independent acquisition (DIA), which has transformed quantitative proteomics over the past decade by eliminating the stochastic sampling problem of DDA and dramatically reducing missing values in large cohort studies.

The section continues with structural and interaction proteomics — crosslinking MS, affinity purification MS, hydrogen-deuterium exchange, and proximity labeling — which move beyond "what is expressed?" to "what does it touch, and what does it look like?" These techniques are essential for understanding signaling networks, drug mechanisms, and the structural basis of disease. We close with PTM proteomics: the specialized enrichment and analysis strategies for phosphoproteomics, ubiquitinomics, acetylomics, and glycoproteomics that reveal the regulatory layer of the proteome — the dynamic covalent marks that translate protein expression into protein function.

Throughout this section, pay attention to what each approach can and cannot tell you. Shotgun proteomics gives you a census of protein abundance but loses isoform identity. SILAC gives high quantitative accuracy but only works in cell culture. DIA gives reproducible quantification but requires careful spectral library management. PTM proteomics reveals regulatory state but requires enrichment steps that introduce their own biases. No single approach captures the full proteome in its full complexity; the skilled proteomicist understands the coverage and limitations of each strategy and designs experiments — and interprets results — accordingly.
