# Section 3: Metabolomics

Otto Warburg won the Nobel Prize in 1931 for discovering that cancer cells preferentially ferment glucose to lactate even in the presence of oxygen. He spent the rest of his career arguing that this metabolic reprogramming was the primary cause of cancer. He was wrong about causation, but he was right that metabolism is not peripheral to disease — it is central to it. And he was right in a way that became fully clear only much later: the metabolome is where the cell's genetics, epigenetics, signaling, and environment converge into a final common output. If you want to know what a cell is actually doing right now, the metabolome is where you look.

Metabolomics measures the complement of small molecule metabolites in a biological system. These molecules — amino acids, organic acids, lipids, sugars, nucleotides, secondary metabolites — are the substrates and products of enzymatic reactions, the signals that regulate gene expression, the molecules that get secreted into the bloodstream as biomarkers of disease. They are also extraordinarily chemically diverse: spanning twelve orders of magnitude in concentration, covering an enormous range of polarities and molecular weights, and including both well-characterized central metabolites and thousands of poorly characterized compounds that may be genuinely novel.

This chemical diversity makes metabolomics simultaneously the most information-rich omics layer and the most technically challenging. Unlike the proteome, which is encoded and largely enumerable, the metabolome is open-ended: there is no metabolic "genome" that tells you in advance what to expect. Every organism, every cell type, and every physiological state produces its own metabolic fingerprint, and a substantial fraction of the features in any untargeted metabolomics experiment represent compounds that have never been structurally characterized.

This section navigates the major strategies and challenges of metabolomics. We begin with the targeted versus untargeted dichotomy — the central strategic choice in experimental design — and develop the framework for knowing when each approach is appropriate. We then compare NMR and mass spectrometry as analytical platforms, emphasizing their complementarity rather than their competition: NMR provides absolute quantification and structural richness for the abundant metabolome, while LC-MS provides sensitivity and breadth for the trace metabolome. The LC-MS workflow section covers the complete pipeline from sample collection and quenching through chromatographic separation, data processing, normalization, and QC — with particular emphasis on the steps where poorly controlled variability masquerades as biology.

The annotation subsection addresses the most intellectually challenging problem in metabolomics: connecting a detected mass-and-retention-time feature to a chemical identity. The MSI confidence level framework, exact mass matching, spectral library databases, molecular networking with GNPS, and in silico tools like SIRIUS/CSI:FingerID are all covered, along with the honest accounting of how much of a typical untargeted metabolomics dataset remains unidentified. We close with metabolic flux analysis — the methodology that measures not just what molecules are present but how rapidly they are being made and consumed — through ¹³C isotope tracing and model-based flux estimation. Flux analysis is what transforms a static metabolite snapshot into a mechanistic account of how the cell is using its nutrients, and it has been central to understanding both cancer metabolism and the design of metabolic engineering strategies.

Together, these subsections build a complete picture of what metabolomics can offer to systems biology: a direct readout of the cell's biochemical phenotype, a discovery platform for novel biomarkers, and a quantitative window into the metabolic programs that execute cellular decisions.
