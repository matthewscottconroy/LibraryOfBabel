# Section 4: Multi-Omics Integration

Imagine trying to understand a city's economy by studying only its manufacturing output. You could learn a great deal — which factories are running, which products are being made, how output changes with season. But you would miss the financial flows that fund the factories, the workforce availability that determines operating capacity, the regulatory environment that shapes what can legally be produced, and the consumer behavior that determines what gets sold. Each layer of the economy is real and measurable, but each is incomplete without the others. The city's economy is not one of these things; it is all of them simultaneously, entangled.

Biological systems work the same way. The genome determines what proteins can be made. The transcriptome reflects the current transcriptional program. The proteome executes cellular functions. The metabolome reports what enzymatic activities are actually running, what nutrients are available, and what biosynthetic precursors are being generated. Each layer tells you something real. And the correlation between adjacent layers — between mRNA and protein, between protein and metabolite — is much weaker than you might hope: the mRNA-protein correlation across mammalian cells is approximately r = 0.4, meaning mRNA levels explain only ~16% of variance in protein levels. If you measure the transcriptome and call it a proxy for the proteome, you are missing most of the biology.

Multi-omics integration is the discipline of combining multiple omics measurements from the same samples to build a more complete picture of biological state than any single layer can provide. This section covers the conceptual framework and practical tools for doing that integration well.

We begin with the three levels of integration — early fusion (feature concatenation), intermediate fusion (shared latent factors), and late fusion (combine results layer by layer) — and develop the reasoning for when each approach is appropriate. Early fusion is powerful but requires large sample sizes to avoid the curse of dimensionality. Late fusion is safe and interpretable but misses cross-layer coordination. Intermediate fusion, exemplified by MOFA+, is often the best balance: it finds shared biological programs visible across multiple layers without requiring a complete feature matrix.

We cover MOFA+ in detail because it has emerged as the most broadly applicable tool for unsupervised multi-omics analysis: its probabilistic framework handles missing data gracefully, its factor structure is biologically interpretable, and its extension to group-structured data enables comparison of multi-omics profiles across conditions or patient groups. We also cover similarity network fusion (SNF) for patient stratification, Mendelian randomization for causal integration, and correlation-based approaches for discovering which genes are transcriptionally versus post-transcriptionally regulated.

The second subsection catalogs the major software tools: mixOmics for PLS-based supervised integration and multi-omics biomarker discovery, iCluster for joint latent variable clustering of cancer multi-omics, OmicsIntegrator for network-based integration using the protein interactome as a scaffold, and Cytoscape/StringApp for visualization. Each tool is illustrated with working code, not as a recipe to follow blindly, but as a starting point for understanding what the software is actually computing.

Throughout this section, keep in mind the distinction between correlation and causation, and between biological signal and technical artifact. Multi-omics data is high-dimensional and noisy, and spurious correlations across layers can appear convincing precisely because they are consistent across multiple data types. Rigorous analysis requires careful quality control of each individual omics layer before integration, clear hypotheses about what types of cross-layer relationships are expected, and ideally some form of validation — either in an independent cohort, by genetic manipulation, or by Mendelian randomization. Integration is only as strong as the data it integrates.
