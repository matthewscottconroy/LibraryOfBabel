# Chapter 1.5: Structural Bioinformatics

In 1972, Christian Anfinsen won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for demonstrating that the amino acid sequence of a protein contains all the information necessary to determine its three-dimensional structure. He had denatured ribonuclease A — unfolded it completely with a reducing agent and urea — and shown that when you remove those denaturants, the protein refolds spontaneously, without any additional information, into its functional state. The sequence is sufficient. The fold is encoded in the sequence.

This was a beautiful result. It was also, in a sense, a death sentence for half a century of computational biology. If the sequence encodes the structure, then surely you should be able to compute the structure from the sequence? This is the protein folding problem, and it turned out to be one of the hardest problems in all of science. Researchers spent fifty years and enormous computational resources trying to solve it. The CASP competition, launched in 1994 to benchmark structure prediction methods, ran for fourteen rounds before a breakthrough arrived. Each round saw incremental progress. The harder targets — large multi-domain proteins, proteins with few evolutionary relatives, novel folds — resisted prediction with maddening consistency.

Then, in November 2020, a team from DeepMind presented AlphaFold2 at CASP14 and solved the problem. Not perfectly, and not for every category of protein, but at a level of accuracy — median TM-score of 0.92, GDT_TS scores above 90 on the hardest targets — that effectively closed the gap between computational prediction and experimental determination for most globular proteins. Andrei Lupas, a structural biologist who had spent years trying to determine the structure of a difficult target, called AlphaFold2's result for his protein "mind-blowing." A method trained on known protein structures had predicted the fold of a protein no human had solved, at atomic accuracy, in a matter of hours.

The AlphaFold2 story is the defining narrative arc of modern structural bioinformatics, and it threads through this entire chapter. But the story is not just about one algorithm. It is about fifty years of scientific progress that made that algorithm possible: the experimental structures in the Protein Data Bank that AlphaFold2 trained on; the coevolutionary methods that inspired its architecture; the structural classification systems that organize the space it navigates; the homology modeling tradition that defined the problem it solved. And it is about what comes after: the drug discovery applications, the protein design revolution, the new biology made possible by having atomic-resolution structure information for essentially every known protein.

This chapter covers the full landscape of structural bioinformatics through that arc. You will start with the experimental foundation — how structures are actually determined (X-ray crystallography, cryo-EM, NMR), how they are stored and assessed (PDB files, quality metrics), and what it means to work with structural data responsibly. You will move through the computational methods that were state-of-the-art before AlphaFold2 (homology modeling, coevolutionary contact prediction) and understand them not just as historical artifacts but as the conceptual building blocks of what replaced them. You will learn AlphaFold2 in depth — its architecture, its confidence metrics, its genuine limitations — and how to use it wisely rather than naïvely.

You will then explore the applications that structural information enables: comparing and classifying structures to detect remote evolutionary relationships; molecular docking and virtual screening for drug discovery; computational protein design and directed evolution for engineering molecules with new functions. And finally, you will learn to see: the visualization tools that transform atomic coordinates into biological understanding.

By the end of this chapter, you should have two things. First, a practical toolkit — the specific methods, software, and workflows needed to do structural bioinformatics work in the AlphaFold2 era. Second, and equally important, a sense of what structural information actually means: when to trust it, when to question it, and how shape connects to function in the molecular machinery of life. Structure is not just a picture of a protein. It is the mechanistic explanation of how a protein works, the evolutionary record of its history, and the starting point for engineering its future.

The protein folding problem took fifty years to solve. What comes next — understanding how structure shapes function across the proteome, designing proteins for medicine, predicting how mutations alter structure and disease — is the work of the coming decades. This chapter is your entry point.
