# Section 1: The Protein Data Bank

The Protein Data Bank was founded in 1971 with seven protein structures. Today it contains more than 220,000. This growth — from a handful of laboriously determined structures to a quarter-million atomic models — represents one of the great cooperative scientific achievements of the twentieth century. Every structure in the PDB was deposited by researchers who determined it experimentally, written in a file format that has not fundamentally changed since punch card days, and made freely available to the world. The PDB is the foundation on which essentially all of computational structural biology rests.

But the PDB is not just a repository to download files from. It is the source of the experimental ground truth that validates computational predictions, the training data that modern deep learning methods learn from, and the primary means by which structural biologists share their findings with the community. Understanding the PDB — how its structures were determined, how its files are organized, and how to assess whether a given structure is reliable — is the first skill you need before any structural analysis can proceed responsibly.

This section covers three interconnected topics. First, the experimental methods: how do you actually determine the 3D structure of a protein? X-ray crystallography, cryo-electron microscopy, and NMR spectroscopy each operate on completely different physical principles and each has distinct strengths, limitations, and optimal use cases. The method used to determine a structure shapes what you can trust about it, and knowing the methods is the basis for interpreting confidence in structural information.

Second, the file formats: how is structural information encoded, and what are the pitfalls when parsing it? The PDB file format carries forty-five years of historical decisions that create real problems for the unwary — alternate conformations, missing residues, insertion codes, non-standard residues. These are not exotic edge cases; they appear in a significant fraction of PDB structures, and silent mishandling of them is a source of errors in published analyses.

Third, quality assessment: not all PDB structures are equally reliable. Resolution, R-factor, Ramachandran statistics, and B-factors all carry information about how much to trust a model. A 1.5 Å structure with excellent MolProbity statistics and a 3.8 Å cryo-EM reconstruction of a flexible complex are both "protein structures," but they support very different levels of structural inference. Systematic quality assessment before any downstream analysis is not optional — it is the first act of scientific rigor in structural bioinformatics.

Together, these three topics give you the foundation to work with structural data intelligently rather than mechanically: to know not just how to open a PDB file, but what questions to ask before you open it, and what to check before you trust what's inside.
