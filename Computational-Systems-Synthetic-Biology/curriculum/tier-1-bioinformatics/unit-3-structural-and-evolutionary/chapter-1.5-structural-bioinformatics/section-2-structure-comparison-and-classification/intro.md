# Section 2: Structure Comparison and Classification

Once you have protein structures, the next question is: what can you do with them? One of the most fundamental things is comparison. Are these two structures similar? If so, how similar, and in what sense? Does their similarity reflect common evolutionary ancestry, or have two unrelated proteins converged on the same shape through independent routes?

These questions are more subtle than they appear. Similarity is not a single thing. Two proteins can be similar at the level of overall fold while differing substantially in their loops and active site geometry. They can share a superfamily classification while having no detectable sequence similarity. They can have the same RMSD against a reference structure but differ in whether that deviation is localized in one flexible loop or distributed across the entire protein. Understanding the right metric for the right question is the central skill of structural comparison.

This section covers two complementary topics. First, pairwise structure comparison: the quantitative methods for measuring how similar two specific structures are. The RMSD is the most widely reported metric, but it has a critical flaw — length dependence — that makes it nearly uninterpretable without context. The TM-score addresses this limitation and has become the standard metric for evaluating structure prediction accuracy in CASP. Alongside these metrics, the structural alignment algorithms DALI and CE provide the means to align structures even when sequence alignment fails, revealing evolutionary relationships that sequence has long since obscured.

Second, structural classification databases: the hierarchical systems (SCOP, CATH, ECOD) that organize the universe of known protein folds and reveal its deep structure. It turns out that protein fold space is not a continuous cloud but a discrete collection of a few thousand stable architectures, reused throughout evolution in endlessly varying combinations. This organization is one of the deepest insights in structural biology, and the classification databases are the tools that make it computationally accessible.

Together, pairwise comparison and classification give you two complementary views of structural similarity: the quantitative ("how similar are these two specific structures?") and the organizational ("where does this structure fit in the known universe of protein folds?"). Both perspectives are essential for structural bioinformatics practice.
