# Section 3: Homology Modeling

For most of the history of structural bioinformatics, the central practical problem was this: you have a sequence of a protein you care about, but no experimental structure exists and crystallization has failed. What do you do? The answer, developed and refined over the 1990s and 2000s, was homology modeling: exploit the deep principle that protein structure is more conserved than sequence across evolutionary time, find a related protein whose structure is known, and use that structure as a template to build a 3D model of your protein of interest.

Homology modeling was never perfect. The model quality degraded as template sequence identity fell, loops were hard to model reliably, and the most functionally interesting parts of the protein were often the ones that differed most from the template. But it was far better than nothing, and for proteins with close structural homologs, it was good enough to guide mutagenesis, suggest binding modes, and support drug discovery campaigns.

Then, in 2021, AlphaFold2 changed the picture entirely. For most single-chain proteins, AlphaFold2 produces predictions that are better than classical homology models at any sequence identity, and structures for essentially every protein in UniProt are freely available. The era of classical homology modeling as the default approach to unknown protein structures is over.

And yet this section exists, and for good reasons. First, there are still cases where classical modeling is valuable: mutant modeling relative to an experimental structure, situations where AlphaFold2 has low confidence, and specialized applications requiring fine-grained control over the modeling protocol. Second, and perhaps more importantly, understanding how homology modeling works gives you a mental model for understanding what AlphaFold2 is implicitly doing — the concepts of template selection, alignment quality, and loop modeling don't disappear in the deep learning era; they are learned rather than hand-coded. Third, interpreting AlphaFold2's confidence metrics (pLDDT and PAE) is much easier if you understand what they are measuring: roughly, whether the local structural environment is well-constrained by evolutionary information, which is exactly what classical modeling's sequence identity thresholds were trying to capture.

This section covers the decision of when to use homology modeling (and when not to), the step-by-step process of building a model, and the practical software tools available. Read it not only for the methods, but for the underlying conceptual framework — it will pay dividends when you encounter AlphaFold2's limitations and need to reason carefully about what to trust.
