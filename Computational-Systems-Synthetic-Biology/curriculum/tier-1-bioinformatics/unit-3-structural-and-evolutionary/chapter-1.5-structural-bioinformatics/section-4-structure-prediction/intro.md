# Section 4: Structure Prediction

In the 1960s, Cyrus Levinthal made a famous observation that came to be known as Levinthal's paradox: if a protein explored all possible conformations randomly before folding, it would take longer than the age of the universe to fold. Yet proteins fold in microseconds to milliseconds. This implied that proteins must fold through specific pathways, guided by local interactions, rather than exhaustively sampling conformation space. The observation launched a fifty-year research program into protein folding — one of the most sustained and productive efforts in computational biology.

But lurking behind the mechanistic question of how proteins fold was a predictive question: given only the amino acid sequence, can you predict what three-dimensional structure the protein will adopt? This became known as the protein folding problem, and it proved extraordinarily hard. The CASP competition, launched in 1994, provided a rigorous benchmark by withholding experimentally determined structures from the community and evaluating how well computational methods could predict them. For more than two decades, CASP scores improved slowly, incrementally, never decisively. The problem seemed genuinely hard in a way that more compute, more clever algorithms, and more physics couldn't easily overcome.

Then, at CASP14 in 2020, AlphaFold2 solved it. Not in the sense that every prediction is perfect — there are important categories of protein where it still struggles. But in the sense that for most globular single-chain proteins, AlphaFold2 now predicts structures with accuracy comparable to experimental methods. The protein folding problem, as the CASP community had defined it, was solved by a deep learning system that learned to extract three-dimensional structural information from the evolutionary record encoded in sequence databases.

This section covers two topics that are deeply connected. AlphaFold2 — its architecture, its confidence metrics, its limitations, and its practical use — is the main event: the tool that every structural biologist now uses as a first resort. But understanding AlphaFold2 properly requires understanding coevolutionary methods: the statistical analysis of correlated mutations that inspired AlphaFold2's design and that is still operating, in learned form, inside the Evoformer module. The coevolution story is not just historical context — it is the intellectual foundation of the entire approach, the answer to the question "why does AlphaFold2 need a multiple sequence alignment to predict structure?"

Together, these two topics tell the story of one of the great achievements in the history of computational biology, and give you the practical and conceptual tools to use its fruits wisely.
