# Section 7: Structure Visualization

There is a reason that the iconic images of molecular biology are visual: the double helix from Franklin's X-ray photograph and Watson and Crick's model, Perutz's wire model of hemoglobin, the ribosome in cryogenic glory. Structural biology is, fundamentally, a science of shape, and shapes are understood through sight. A column of numbers describing atomic coordinates conveys no immediate insight about what a protein looks like or how it works. A picture does.

This is not just aesthetics. Visualization is a tool for thinking. When you look at a protein structure colored by B-factor, you immediately see which regions are mobile and which are ordered — information that would take minutes to extract from tables of numbers. When you see a binding site with a ligand in stick representation against a transparent protein surface, you can evaluate whether the pose makes chemical sense at a glance. When you overlay an AlphaFold2 model on an experimental structure and color by RMSD, the regions of agreement and disagreement are spatially mapped in a way no metric can communicate.

This section covers the major tools for protein structure visualization: PyMOL, UCSF ChimeraX, VMD, Mol* Viewer, and NGL Viewer. These tools have different strengths and appropriate use cases, and learning when to use which one is part of practical structural bioinformatics competency.

PyMOL is the industry standard for publication figures — the tool whose output appears in essentially every structural biology paper in Nature, Science, and Cell. Its selection algebra and scripting API make it powerful and reproducible. ChimeraX is the tool of choice when cryo-EM density maps are involved, or when you need to visualize very large complexes. VMD is the right tool when you have molecular dynamics trajectories to analyze. Mol* handles the web-based use case and has made structural data accessible to anyone with a browser.

Beyond tool selection, this section provides practical guidance on making publication-quality figures: what representations to use and avoid, how to choose meaningful colors, how to compose figures that communicate the biology rather than just displaying the coordinates. The difference between a figure that makes the science immediately clear and one that leaves the reader confused is rarely a difference in the underlying data — it is a difference in visualization choices. Those choices are learnable skills, and this section teaches them.
