# Chapter 1.6: Phylogenetics

Here is an extraordinary fact: from a few hundred nucleotides of DNA sequence, you can reconstruct the evolutionary history of life on Earth. Not guess at it. Not approximate it. Reconstruct it statistically, with quantifiable confidence, reaching back billions of years to common ancestors that left no other physical trace. Charles Darwin sketched the first tree of life in his 1837 notebook with a tentative "I think" at the top. He had no method for building it from data. The computational phylogenetics you will learn in this chapter is the mathematical fulfillment of that sketch.

The comparative method — using differences between existing sequences to infer the history that produced them — is one of the most powerful ideas in biology. Its power comes from a simple observation: all life shares common ancestry, and the process of evolution leaves a systematic, recoverable signature in molecular sequences. Closely related species have accumulated fewer differences; distantly related ones have accumulated more. The branching pattern of relationships constrains the pattern of differences: if A and B are sisters (most recently common ancestors), they must share variants that neither shares with the outgroup C. By counting differences, modeling the process of substitution, and searching for the tree that makes the data most probable, you can recover the history.

This chapter takes you through the full arc of computational phylogenetics, from the simplest distance-based methods to the most powerful Bayesian and coalescent-based approaches.

**Sections 1** establishes the conceptual foundation: what phylogenetics answers, the vocabulary of trees, and the critical distinction between orthologous and paralogous sequences that must be correct before any analysis can succeed.

**Section 2** introduces distance methods — the fastest and most intuitive approaches. You will learn why the observed fraction of differences underestimates true evolutionary distance (the multiple-substitution problem), how the Jukes-Cantor and Kimura models correct for this, and how UPGMA and Neighbor-Joining convert distance matrices into trees. Understanding why UPGMA fails (it assumes a molecular clock) and how NJ overcomes this (with a clever rate correction in the Q-matrix criterion) gives you intuition for what all phylogenetic methods are attempting.

**Section 3** covers maximum parsimony — the dominant methodology from the 1960s through the 1990s. The Fitch algorithm and the concept of parsimony-informative sites remain conceptually important. But the focus here is on parsimony's critical failure mode: long-branch attraction in the Felsenstein zone, where systematic bias makes parsimony statistically inconsistent. This failure motivated the development of maximum likelihood, and understanding it prepares you to understand what ML is doing right.

**Section 4** is the core of the chapter: maximum likelihood phylogenetics. Felsenstein's pruning algorithm efficiently computes the likelihood of any tree by summing over all possible ancestral states. The GTR substitution model, with gamma rate variation across sites, correctly handles the heterogeneous evolution of real sequences. Empirical amino acid models (LG, WAG) and mixture models address the overparameterization problem for protein sequences. Automated model selection (ModelFinder) identifies the best-fitting model for any dataset. And IQ-TREE2's stochastic search algorithm finds near-optimal trees despite the astronomical space of possible topologies.

**Section 5** extends this to Bayesian phylogenetics. Rather than finding a single best tree, Bayesian methods compute a posterior probability distribution over all trees using Markov Chain Monte Carlo sampling. MrBayes provides the gold standard for topology estimation with posterior probability support. BEAST2, integrated with molecular clock models and fossil calibrations, dates divergence events and reconstructs demographic histories — the analytical engine behind real-time viral phylodynamics during pandemic surveillance.

**Section 6** addresses how to evaluate the confidence you should have in phylogenetic clades. Bootstrap support is the most widely used measure, but it measures data consistency, not the probability of correctness. Concordance factors — site concordance factors (sCF) and gene concordance factors (gCF) — reveal what fraction of the data actually supports each relationship, complementing bootstrap with a measure of genomic consensus.

**Section 7** develops the molecular clock and its calibration. The strict clock is almost always violated, motivating relaxed clock models. Fossil calibrations anchor the molecular timescale to absolute dates, while tip dating exploits sequence collection dates for rapidly evolving organisms. BEAST2 is the standard tool for calibrated dating, producing divergence time estimates with proper uncertainty propagation.

**Section 8** addresses one of the most important and surprising discoveries of the genomics era: different genes in the same organisms frequently tell different evolutionary stories. Incomplete lineage sorting, horizontal gene transfer, gene duplication and loss, and hybridization all cause gene trees to differ from species trees. In rapid radiations — birds, mammals, Drosophila — concatenation-based methods can converge to the wrong species tree even with genome-scale data. Coalescent-based methods, particularly ASTRAL, correctly account for this discordance.

**Section 9** connects the phylogenetic tree to the selective forces that shaped it. dN/dS analysis measures the ratio of nonsynonymous to synonymous substitutions, revealing whether genes are under purifying selection, neutral drift, or positive selection. PAML implements branch, site, and branch-site models for detecting episodic adaptive evolution. The McDonald-Kreitman test, Tajima's D, and haplotype-based sweep statistics extend selection analysis to population genomic data.

Together, these sections constitute a rigorous introduction to computational phylogenetics at the graduate level. By the end, you will have both the conceptual understanding and the practical toolkit to build phylogenies, evaluate their support, date their nodes, account for gene tree discordance, and read the signature of natural selection written into sequences. Darwin's tree, sketched tentatively in 1837, is now a quantitative science. This chapter is your guide to it.
