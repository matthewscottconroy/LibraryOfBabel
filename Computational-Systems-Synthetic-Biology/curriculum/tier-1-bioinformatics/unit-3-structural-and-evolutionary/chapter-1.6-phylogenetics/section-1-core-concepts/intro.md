# Section 1: Core Concepts in Phylogenetics

Before you build a tree, you need to understand what kind of thing a tree is, what questions it can answer, and what conceptual traps are waiting for the unwary. This section establishes the vocabulary and conceptual foundations that every subsequent section depends on.

The first thing to appreciate is the sheer scope of what phylogenetics can answer. The obvious application — reconstructing evolutionary relationships among organisms — is only the beginning. Modern phylogenetics reconstructs the historical migrations of ancient viruses, dates divergence events with millions-of-years precision, resurrects ancient proteins for laboratory study, identifies antibiotic resistance transfer events in real-time hospital outbreaks, and assigns function to proteins of unknown purpose by their evolutionary context. Darwin drew the first tree of life in his notebook in 1837, but the computational tools that make tree-building rigorous from molecular data emerged only in the last few decades. Every method you will learn in this chapter is an answer to a specific limitation of earlier approaches.

The second thing to appreciate is that phylogenetic trees are hypotheses, not facts. A tree represents the best available inference about evolutionary history given the data and the model. It can be more or less well-supported, more or less sensitive to model assumptions, and more or less consistent with trees built from different genes. Understanding the vocabulary of trees — nodes, branches, clades, polytomies, root, topology — is a prerequisite for evaluating the strength and limits of any phylogenetic hypothesis. This vocabulary is not arbitrary jargon; each term encodes a specific biological or statistical concept, and confusing them leads to genuine errors of interpretation.

The third foundational concept is homology. Phylogenetics is built on comparisons between sequences, and those comparisons are only meaningful when the sequences being compared share common ancestry. The distinction between orthologs (related by speciation) and paralogs (related by duplication) is not a technicality — using the wrong type of homolog in a phylogenetic analysis produces incorrect trees. Every genome contains thousands of gene families with complex histories of duplication and loss, and correctly identifying which copies to compare is one of the most practically important challenges in phylogenomics.

By the end of this section, you will have the conceptual framework needed to approach every subsequent topic: what questions phylogenetics answers and why, how to read and correctly interpret the topology of a tree, and how to ensure that the sequences you compare are the right ones — orthologs, not paralogs, identified with rigorous tools rather than naïve sequence similarity.
