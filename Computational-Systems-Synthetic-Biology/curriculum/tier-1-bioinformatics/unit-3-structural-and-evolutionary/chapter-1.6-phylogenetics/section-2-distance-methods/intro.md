# Section 2: Distance Methods

The oldest and most intuitive approach to phylogenetic tree building begins with a simple question: how different are these sequences from each other? If you can summarize all pairwise differences in a matrix of distances, you have a compressed representation of the evolutionary information in your alignment — and from that matrix, you can reconstruct a tree.

Distance methods convert sequences into pairwise distances and then use those distances to infer a tree. They are the fastest phylogenetic approaches available, capable of placing thousands of sequences on a tree in minutes. Their speed makes them invaluable as guide trees for multiple sequence alignment, as quick reference topologies before full ML analysis, and as the only feasible approach when dataset size precludes character-based methods.

But the simplicity comes at a cost. The first and most important cost is information loss: reducing a full alignment to pairwise distances discards all information about which specific sites changed and in what evolutionary context. Two very different evolutionary histories can produce identical distance matrices, and a distance matrix cannot distinguish between them. The second cost is the problem of saturation: the observed fraction of differences between two sequences systematically underestimates the true evolutionary distance, because sites can change multiple times. A site that changed three times appears no different from one that changed once. As divergence increases and back-mutations accumulate, the relationship between observed differences and true evolutionary distance becomes increasingly nonlinear — and eventually breaks down entirely.

This section addresses both problems. The first subsection develops the mathematical machinery for correcting observed divergence into true evolutionary distance — the Jukes-Cantor and Kimura 2-parameter models — and explains both why these corrections are necessary and when they are insufficient. The second and third subsections cover the two principal tree-building algorithms: UPGMA, the simplest possible hierarchical clustering approach that assumes a molecular clock, and Neighbor-Joining, which relaxes the clock assumption by using a clever correction for lineage-specific substitution rates.

You will come away understanding not just how these methods work, but why they fail — and how those failure modes point directly toward the more sophisticated likelihood-based methods that occupy the rest of the chapter.
