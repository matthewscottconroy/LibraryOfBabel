# Section 3: Maximum Parsimony

For nearly three decades — from the mid-1960s through the 1990s — maximum parsimony was the dominant methodology in molecular systematics. The appeal was philosophical as well as practical: Occam's Razor, the principle that entities should not be multiplied without necessity, applied to evolutionary history means preferring the tree that requires the fewest changes to explain the observed data. The approach is elegant, computationally tractable, and intuitive.

Understanding parsimony is not merely historical. You will encounter parsimony trees throughout the literature, particularly from the 1980s and 1990s, and you need to be able to evaluate them critically. More importantly, understanding why parsimony fails — and under precisely what conditions it gives the wrong answer — provides the clearest possible intuition for what maximum likelihood methods are doing right.

Maximum parsimony asks: given the observed character states at the tips of a tree, what is the minimum number of substitutions required to explain the data across all possible tree topologies? The tree requiring the fewest total changes is preferred. The Fitch algorithm computes this efficiently with a single bottom-up tree traversal. The informative sites concept — only sites where at least two taxa share each of at least two different states — clarifies which alignment positions actually contribute information about topology.

The critical failure mode is long-branch attraction, described analytically by Joseph Felsenstein in 1978. When two lineages have evolved rapidly — accumulating many substitutions independently — they accumulate many identical changes by convergence rather than common ancestry. Parsimony cannot distinguish shared ancestry from convergent evolution; it counts all shared character states as evidence for grouping, regardless of whether they arose once or independently. In the Felsenstein zone, parsimony is not just wrong — it is statistically inconsistent, meaning it converges to the wrong tree as you add more data.

This single failure mode motivated the development of maximum likelihood phylogenetics. If you understand long-branch attraction, you understand why ML with rate variation models is superior to parsimony — and you will be prepared to recognize when any phylogenetic method, parsimony or otherwise, might be susceptible to its effects.
