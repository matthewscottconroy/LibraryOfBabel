# Section 4: Maximum Likelihood

In 1981, Joe Felsenstein published the paper that gave phylogenetics its statistical foundation. Rather than asking which tree minimizes changes, or which tree makes sequences look most similar, he asked: which tree makes the observed data most probable, under an explicit probabilistic model of how sequences evolve? This question — the maximum likelihood question — transforms phylogenetics from a collection of heuristics into a rigorous statistical science.

Maximum likelihood phylogenetics consists of three deeply interconnected components, each of which is a section here. The first is the core statistical framework: defining the likelihood of a tree and computing it efficiently with Felsenstein's pruning algorithm, a dynamic programming recursion that sums over all possible ancestral states at internal nodes in linear time. This algorithm is the computational heart of modern phylogenetics, and understanding it reveals exactly what ML is doing that parsimony and distance methods cannot.

The second component is the substitution model. The likelihood function requires an explicit model of how nucleotides or amino acids change over time — the instantaneous rate matrix Q and the resulting transition probabilities P(t) = e^Qt. This section covers the hierarchy of nucleotide models from the simplest (Jukes-Cantor, equal rates) through the most general time-reversible model (GTR), and the critical addition of rate variation across sites (the gamma distribution). For protein sequences, empirical models like LG and WAG capture the complex physicochemical constraints on amino acid substitution from large databases of protein evolution. The choice of model matters: an undersimplified model produces biased branch lengths and potentially wrong topologies; an overparameterized one wastes degrees of freedom.

The third component is the search problem. With the right likelihood function and the right model, you still face an astronomical number of possible tree topologies — more than there are atoms in the universe for datasets of practical size. Heuristic search algorithms — NNI, SPR, TBR — navigate this space without exhaustive enumeration. IQ-TREE2, the current standard for ML phylogenetics, uses multiple starting trees, stochastic perturbation, and parallel computation to find near-optimal trees reliably.

Together with automated model selection (ModelFinder), these components constitute the modern ML workflow — and by the end of this section, you will understand both what each step is doing and why each choice matters.
