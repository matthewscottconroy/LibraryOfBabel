# Section 5: Bayesian Phylogenetics

Maximum likelihood finds the single best tree. But how much better is it than the second-best? Is there a tight optimum with one clearly superior topology, or is the likelihood surface relatively flat with many trees nearly as good? These questions — about uncertainty, not just the best estimate — cannot be answered by maximum likelihood alone. They require a framework for computing probability distributions over trees, not just point estimates. That framework is Bayesian inference.

Bayesian phylogenetics applies Bayes' theorem to the phylogenetic inference problem: the posterior probability of a tree is proportional to the likelihood of the data given the tree, multiplied by the prior probability of the tree. The result is a full probability distribution over tree topologies, branch lengths, and model parameters — the posterior distribution. Every phylogenetic clade gets a posterior probability: the fraction of sampled trees that contain that clade, interpretable directly as the probability that the clade exists given the data and model.

The computational challenge is formidable: the posterior is a distribution over an astronomically large space that cannot be computed analytically. Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) solves this by constructing a random walk through parameter space that, at convergence, samples from the posterior. The Metropolis-Hastings algorithm makes this possible without ever computing the normalizing constant — the normalizing constant cancels in the acceptance ratio. The result is a chain of samples that, after convergence and burn-in removal, represent draws from the posterior.

This section covers three topics in sequence. The first is the Bayesian framework itself — Bayes' theorem applied to phylogenetics, prior distributions, and the interpretation of posterior probabilities vs. ML bootstrap support. The second is MCMC in detail — the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm, proposal moves for trees and parameters, convergence diagnostics (ESS, PSRF, Tracer visualization), and the critical difference between mixing and convergence. The third is the two principal software implementations: MrBayes for topology estimation and hypothesis testing, and BEAST2 for divergence time estimation with molecular clocks, tip dating, and phylodynamics.

The Bayesian framework is particularly powerful for downstream analyses that need to propagate phylogenetic uncertainty — ancestral sequence reconstruction, divergence time estimation, diversification rate analysis — because it naturally represents and propagates the full uncertainty in the tree, rather than conditioning all subsequent analyses on a single point estimate.
