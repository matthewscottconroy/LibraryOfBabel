# Section 7: The Molecular Clock

In 1965, Emile Zuckerkandl and Linus Pauling noted that the number of amino acid differences between species' hemoglobin sequences seemed to correlate with their divergence time from the fossil record. Proteins, they proposed, were evolving at approximately constant rates — like molecular clocks. If this were true, you could use sequence divergence as a proxy for time, transforming phylogenetics from a tool that reveals relationships into one that reveals timelines.

The molecular clock hypothesis, even in its imperfect form, is one of the most consequential ideas in modern biology. It enables evolutionary biologists to date divergence events without fossils, to estimate when ancient viruses first integrated into host genomes, to track the emergence timing of new pathogen variants in real time, and to reconstruct the demographic history of populations through time. Without some form of molecular clock, phylogenetics can tell you the shape of the tree but not when its nodes occurred.

This section addresses two fundamental topics. The first is the nature of molecular clocks themselves: the strict clock, which assumes all lineages evolve at the same rate, and the relaxed clocks that accommodate the biological reality of rate variation. The strict clock is almost always statistically rejected in real datasets — generation time, effective population size, metabolic rate, and life history all affect evolutionary rates, sometimes by orders of magnitude. The uncorrelated lognormal clock (UCLN), implemented in BEAST2, is the standard relaxed clock model: each branch has an independent rate drawn from a lognormal distribution, estimated jointly with the tree topology and substitution model via MCMC.

The second topic is calibration — how molecular rates are anchored to absolute time. Fossil calibrations provide minimum age bounds on specific nodes, incorporated as prior distributions in BEAST2. For rapidly evolving organisms like RNA viruses, tip dating uses the sampling dates of sequences themselves as calibration points, requiring no fossils at all. The quality of calibration is often the limiting factor in molecular clock dating: poorly chosen or overconfident calibration priors produce falsely precise divergence time estimates that propagate errors into all downstream analyses.

Understanding both the clocks and the calibrations is essential for critically reading the literature of divergence time estimation — a literature that has produced both major advances and significant controversies.
