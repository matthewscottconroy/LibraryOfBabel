# Section 8: Gene Trees vs. Species Trees

One of the most important and surprising discoveries of the genomics era is that different genes in the same set of organisms tell different evolutionary stories. This is not noise, not artifact, not error. It is a fundamental consequence of how genes evolve within populations — and it has profound implications for how phylogenomics must be done.

A species tree represents the history of populations and lineages: when they split, how long they remained isolated, whether any gene flow occurred between them. A gene tree represents the genealogical history of a specific locus, which can differ from the species tree for several distinct reasons. The most biologically important is incomplete lineage sorting (ILS): when ancestral populations are polymorphic and speciation occurs rapidly or with large effective population sizes, gene copies from different species may coalesce (find their common ancestor) in deeper ancestral populations than expected — producing gene trees that contradict the species tree. This is not a rare edge case. Many of the most contested phylogenetic problems in evolutionary biology — the relationships among major orders of birds, the placement of turtles within reptiles, the deep nodes of the placental mammal phylogeny — occur in rapid radiations where ILS is rampant.

Other sources of discordance include horizontal gene transfer (common in bacteria, relevant in eukaryotes too), gene duplication and differential loss (producing paralog contamination), and hybridization with introgression (documented between modern humans and Neanderthals, rampant in plants).

The practical implication is serious: concatenating all genes into a single super-alignment and treating the result as if it were a single gene tree produces a statistically inconsistent estimator in the anomaly zone. More data can make the answer worse. The solution is to use methods that explicitly model the multispecies coalescent — ASTRAL for large datasets, *BEAST/StarBEAST3 for small datasets where full Bayesian inference is feasible.

This section covers both the biology of discordance (what causes it, how to recognize it) and the computational methods for handling it correctly. By the end, you will understand why concordance factors complement bootstrap support for revealing the true genomic basis of phylogenetic relationships.
