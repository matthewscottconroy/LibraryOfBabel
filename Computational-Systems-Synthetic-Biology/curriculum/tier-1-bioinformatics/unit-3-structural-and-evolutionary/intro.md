# Unit 3: Structural and Evolutionary Bioinformatics

A protein sequence is a string of letters. But those letters encode two kinds of information that are not visible in the sequence itself: the three-dimensional structure the protein folds into, and the evolutionary history that produced it. Reconstructing both — shape and history — from sequence data is the central project of this unit, and it requires two quite different sets of methods.

Chapter 1.5 covers structural bioinformatics. Chapter 1.6 covers phylogenetics. At first glance, these might seem like unrelated topics bundled together for administrative convenience. But look closer and you will find that they are deeply connected — connected by the same fundamental fact about biological sequences and by the same underlying challenge.

**The fundamental fact**: both structure and evolutionary history are more conserved than sequence. Two proteins can have less than 20% sequence identity and share the same fold. Two genes can be so diverged that no pairwise alignment is detectable, yet they can be traced back to a common ancestor through careful phylogenetic analysis. In both cases, the signal is still present in the sequence — but extracting it requires methods that see past the noise of accumulated mutation to the underlying conserved reality.

**The shared challenge**: in both structural and evolutionary bioinformatics, you are working backwards from observable data (sequence) to infer something that you cannot directly observe (three-dimensional structure; ancestral relationships). Both are inverse problems, and both require explicit models of how sequences change over time and across evolutionary space. The coevolutionary methods at the heart of modern structure prediction are, in a deep sense, phylogenetic in nature — they exploit the statistical patterns in a multiple sequence alignment to extract information about three-dimensional contacts, and those patterns arise precisely because of shared evolutionary history. AlphaFold2 needs phylogenetic diversity in its MSA to work well. Phylogenetic methods that use structural information can detect evolutionary relationships invisible to sequence comparison alone. The two fields are not parallel; they are intertwined.

**Why shape matters**: A protein's sequence tells you what it is made of. Its structure tells you how it works. The active site of an enzyme — the precise geometric arrangement of catalytic residues that positions a substrate for chemical transformation — is a three-dimensional object, not a one-dimensional sequence motif. Drug binding is a three-dimensional phenomenon. Protein-protein interaction interfaces are surfaces, not strings. Understanding mechanism, designing drugs, and engineering proteins all require structural information. Chapter 1.5 covers the full toolkit: experimental structure determination (X-ray crystallography, cryo-EM, NMR), structure quality assessment, comparison and classification, homology modeling, and the AlphaFold2 revolution that made structural information available for essentially every known protein. It culminates in the applications — molecular docking for drug discovery, and computational protein design for synthetic biology — that make structural bioinformatics one of the most practically consequential fields in computational biology.

**Why history matters**: A protein's sequence does not exist in isolation — it is one point in a vast evolutionary trajectory that stretches back billions of years. Understanding where a protein came from, how it is related to other proteins, and how it has changed across species provides a different but equally essential kind of biological insight. Phylogenetics is the science of reconstructing evolutionary history from molecular data. It gives us the tree of life and the timescale of divergence; it detects horizontal gene transfer and convergent evolution; it identifies rapidly evolving viral genes and conserved functional sites; it provides the framework for interpreting any comparative genomic analysis. Chapter 1.6 covers the mathematical and computational foundations of phylogenetics — from distance-based clustering to maximum likelihood and Bayesian inference — with attention to the models of sequence evolution that make statistical phylogenetic inference possible.

**Sequence, structure, evolution — a triad**: This unit sits between the sequence analysis of earlier chapters and the functional analysis of later ones. The picture it builds is this: sequences evolve under evolutionary constraint. That constraint reflects function. Function reflects structure. Structure encodes mechanism. All four — sequence, evolution, structure, mechanism — are facets of the same underlying biological reality, and the computational methods in this unit are the tools for moving between them. By the time you finish this unit, you will be able to look at a protein sequence and ask not just "what is this?" but "what does it look like, how does it work, where did it come from, and how has it changed?" Those are the questions that turn sequence data into biological understanding.
