# 2.5 Multi-Scale and Whole-Cell Modeling

The ultimate goal of systems biology: a predictive model of an entire cell.

---

## Why Multi-Scale Models?

Biological phenomena span orders of magnitude in both time and space:

| Scale | Time | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Chemical reactions | ns–µs | Enzyme catalysis, protein conformational change |
| Gene expression | minutes–hours | mRNA synthesis, protein accumulation |
| Cell cycle | hours–days | DNA replication, division |
| Development | days–weeks | Tissue patterning, organogenesis |
| Evolution | years–millions of years | Adaptation, speciation |

No single modeling framework spans all scales. Multi-scale models stitch together different representations.

---

## Timescale Separation

### The Principle

When processes operate on very different timescales, fast processes can be treated as instantaneous from the perspective of slow processes.

**Mathematical basis:**
- Slow-fast systems: ε(dx/dt) = f(x,y); dy/dt = g(x,y)
- As ε → 0, fast variable x equilibrates instantaneously → quasi-steady-state approximation
- Reduces system dimensionality

**Biological examples:**
- Enzyme kinetics: ES complex equilibrates fast → Michaelis-Menten simplification
- Ion channel gating: fast vs. slow gates in Hodgkin-Huxley model
- Signaling vs. gene expression: signaling (~seconds) vs. gene expression (~hours)

### Hierarchical Models

Build models at each relevant timescale, pass outputs between them:
- Molecular dynamics (ns) → conformational states
- Kinetic Monte Carlo (µs–ms) → enzyme kinetics
- ODE/stochastic models (seconds–hours) → network dynamics
- Agent-based models (hours–days) → cell population behavior

## The First Whole-Cell Model

### Karr et al. 2012 (Science)

First complete computational model of a living organism — *Mycoplasma genitalium* (the smallest known self-replicating organism, 525 genes).

**Key features:**
- Integrates 28 submodels for different cellular processes
- Each submodel uses the most appropriate mathematical framework
- Submodels communicate through shared cellular state variables

**Submodels include:**
- Chromosome replication
- Transcription (kinetic model of RNA polymerase)
- Translation (ribosome availability, tRNA charging)
- Metabolism (FBA)
- Protein folding and secretion
- Cell division

**How submodels communicate:**
- Shared state: metabolite concentrations, protein counts, DNA state
- Time-stepping: each submodel updates its outputs using current shared state
- Coordination: cell mass calculated from all components; triggers division

**What it predicted correctly:**
- Cell cycle duration
- Growth rate sensitivity to genetic perturbations (gene essentiality)
- Metabolite concentrations
- mRNA and protein copy numbers

**Limitations:**
- Took 128 person-years to build
- Only possible for the smallest known free-living organism
- Many parameters estimated or inferred; not all validated

### Lessons from the Whole-Cell Model

1. **Integration is the hardest part**: individual submodels are well-known; making them communicate consistently is the challenge
2. **Parameterization bottleneck**: most parameters for small-molecule metabolism and kinetics are unknown
3. **Framework choice matters**: stochastic gene expression model vs. ODE vs. FBA give different behaviors for different processes
4. **Validation requires global phenotypic data**: gene essentiality screens, growth curves, metabolite profiles

## Vivarium: Compositional Multi-Scale Modeling

### The Problem Vivarium Solves

Traditional simulation: one model, one framework, hard to reuse or combine components.

Vivarium (Agmon et al. 2022): hierarchical, composable simulation framework

**Core concepts:**
- **Process**: a computational component with inputs and outputs (any framework: ODE, stochastic, rule-based)
- **Composite**: tree of processes sharing a common store (state)
- **Store**: named variables accessed by multiple processes
- **Wiring**: connects process ports to store variables

**Example composition:**
```
Cell = Composite(
    metabolism = FBA_process,
    transcription = stochastic_process,
    translation = kinetic_process,
    ...
)
```

Each process only sees the variables it needs. Adding a new process only requires specifying its wiring.

**Why this matters:** allows incremental model building, reuse of validated components, multi-scale assembly.

## ME-Models (Metabolism and Expression)

### Beyond Metabolic Models

Standard GEMs assume unlimited ribosomes and RNA polymerases. ME-models include:
- Ribosome as an explicit resource
- RNA polymerase as an explicit resource
- Enzyme synthesis as part of the optimization
- Protein and RNA degradation

**Constraint:** Total protein synthesis ≤ ribosome capacity × elongation rate

This creates growth-rate-dependent proteome allocation:
- Fast growth: invest more protein in ribosomes
- Slow growth: invest more in metabolic enzymes for substrate scavenging

**ME-model predictions:**
- Proteome composition as function of growth rate (validates against mass spectrometry)
- Optimal allocation without explicit regulation: explains the "Monod" relationship empirically
- Antibiotic sensitivity predictions: drugs targeting ribosomes or metabolic enzymes

**Implementation:**
- COBRAme: Python framework for ME-models
- Requires curated genome-scale data for translation, transcription, complex assembly

## Agent-Based Modeling

### When to Use Agent-Based Models

When individual variation and spatial effects matter:
- Cell migration and tissue formation
- Tumor growth and treatment
- Microbial colony formation and competition
- Immune cell dynamics

### Key Frameworks

**NetLogo**
- Patch-based environment; agents follow rules
- Low-code entry point; good for ecological and epidemiological models
- Visualization built-in

**Mesa (Python)**
- Grid and continuous space; agent classes in Python
- Good for custom models; integrates with scientific Python stack
- Visualization with Solara

**CompuCell3D**
- Cellular Potts model for cell shape dynamics
- Multi-scale: intracellular (ODE/FBA) + intercellular (contact energy) + extracellular (PDE diffusion)
- Applications: morphogenesis, organoid formation, tumor growth
- Each cell can have its own internal ODE model

**iDynoMiCS / BacSim**
- Individual-based biofilm simulators
- Each bacterium grows, divides, moves in 3D space
- Nutrient gradients modeled as diffusion PDEs

### Cellular Potts Model (Glazier-Graner-Hogeweg)

Energy-based model of cell shape and movement:
```
H = Σ J(σ_i, σ_j) + Σ λ_vol(V_σ - V_target)² + Σ λ_sur(A_σ - A_target)²
```
- J: surface energy between cell types
- Volume and surface area constraints
- Metropolis algorithm: accept moves that lower total energy (with thermal fluctuation)
- Captures: cell sorting, tissue surface tension, chemotaxis-driven migration

## Linking Scales: Key Challenges

### The Semantic Alignment Problem

Models from different groups use different:
- Variable names for the same entity
- Units
- Frameworks (ODE vs. Boolean vs. stochastic)
- Time references (absolute vs. relative)

**Solutions:**
- SBML (Systems Biology Markup Language): standard for ODE/stochastic models
- BioPAX (Biological Pathway Exchange): OWL-based; links to biological databases
- SBO (Systems Biology Ontology): terms for model elements
- BioModels Database: curated repository of published models in SBML

### Parameter Uncertainty Propagation

- Each submodel has uncertain parameters
- How do uncertainties in submodels combine at the whole-cell level?
- Global sensitivity analysis across the full model (computationally expensive)
- Uncertainty quantification (UQ): probabilistic treatment of parameter uncertainty

### Emergent Properties

Properties of the whole that are not predictable from individual components:
- Cell cycle timing emerges from interacting oscillators + checkpoints
- Cell polarity emerges from Turing-like mechanisms at membrane
- Bet-hedging (phenotypic diversity) emerges from stochastic switching

Whole-cell models are the tool for studying emergence rigorously.
