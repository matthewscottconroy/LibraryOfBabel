At this moment, a single *E. coli* cell growing on glucose is running roughly 2,500 chemical reactions simultaneously. It is converting glucose to pyruvate through glycolysis, channeling some of that carbon into the pentose phosphate pathway to generate NADPH for biosynthesis, running the TCA cycle to generate electron carriers, pumping protons across its inner membrane to synthesize ATP, and assembling amino acids, nucleotides, lipids, and cofactors at the rates precisely calibrated to double the cell in 20 minutes. All of these processes are happening at once, all are stoichiometrically linked, and the whole system is tuned so finely that when you remove a single metabolic gene, the cell often cannot grow at all. How do you make quantitative sense of a system with this level of complexity?

This chapter answers that question by developing the mathematical and computational toolkit for metabolic modeling — a field that has achieved something remarkable: it can predict how a cell grows, which genes are essential, what products it will secrete, and how its fluxes are distributed, using nothing more than a stoichiometric matrix and some linear algebra.

The opening and most consequential story in the chapter concerns the stoichiometric matrix and flux balance analysis. When the stoichiometric matrix was first used as the basis for optimization in the early 1990s, the idea that you could predict *E. coli* growth rates without any kinetic parameters was considered by many to be too naive to be useful. That skepticism turned out to be wrong. Flux Balance Analysis, as developed by Palsson, Edwards, and their colleagues over the following decade, correctly predicts gene essentiality with ~95% accuracy, reproduces the acetate overflow phenomenon that puzzled microbiologists for years, and correctly predicts how cells shift their metabolism between aerobic and anaerobic conditions. These first two sections lay the mathematical groundwork — the stoichiometric matrix, the steady-state constraint, flux constraints, and the linear programming formulation — and show you how to use them in practice with COBRApy.

From there, the chapter scales up. Genome-scale metabolic models — GEMs for short — apply this framework not to toy networks but to the full metabolic complement of an organism, encoded in the genome. The third section introduces what makes a GEM, surveys the major models available for *E. coli*, yeast, human cells, and gut microbiota, and walks through the reconstruction workflow from genome annotation to phenotypic validation. This is where you learn that a GEM is not just a computational tool but a scientific object, a compressed and testable encoding of metabolic knowledge.

The fourth section extends the constraint-based framework in three important directions: incorporating gene regulatory constraints so that models can distinguish between metabolic states under different environmental conditions; coupling FBA to ordinary differential equations for dynamic simulation of batch cultures; and stacking multiple GEMs into community models that can predict the metabolic interactions between organisms in the gut microbiome or a synthetic co-culture.

Thermodynamics enters in the fifth section, where we ask which flux distributions are not just stoichiometrically feasible but thermodynamically possible. The Gibbs free energy constrains reaction directionality in ways that FBA ignores, and methods like Max-Min Driving Force analysis and enzyme cost minimization connect thermodynamics to metabolic network behavior in quantitatively precise ways that are essential for pathway design.

The sixth section covers ¹³C metabolic flux analysis, the experimental counterpart to FBA. While FBA predicts what a cell should do to maximize growth, ¹³C MFA measures what it actually does — by tracking the fate of isotopically labeled carbon atoms through the network. This section covers the tracer principle, isotopologue distributions, the EMU computational framework that makes the analysis tractable, and the INCA software used in practice.

Finally, the seventh section confronts kinetic modeling: the most mechanistically detailed and the most parameter-hungry approach. You will learn the rate laws, understand the challenges (the parameter crisis is real and important), and encounter ensemble modeling — the probabilistic framework that makes useful predictions even when exact parameters are unavailable.

Taken together, these seven sections constitute a complete toolkit for quantitative metabolism. By the end of this chapter, you will be able to build a stoichiometric model, run FBA and interpret the results, work with genome-scale models, simulate fermentation dynamics, evaluate thermodynamic feasibility, design and analyze isotope tracing experiments, and reason clearly about what kinetic models can and cannot tell you. Metabolism is the machinery of life. These methods are how you learn to read the machine.
