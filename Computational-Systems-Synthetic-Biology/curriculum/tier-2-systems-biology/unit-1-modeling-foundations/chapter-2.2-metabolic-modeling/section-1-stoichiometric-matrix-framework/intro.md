Every metabolic network, no matter how complex, is ultimately a set of chemical transformations connected by shared molecules. Glucose feeds into glycolysis; glycolysis feeds the TCA cycle; the TCA cycle produces NADH; NADH feeds the electron transport chain. These are not vague biological narratives — they are stoichiometric relationships, and they can be encoded with perfect precision in a matrix. That matrix, the stoichiometric matrix **S**, is the foundational data structure of all quantitative metabolic modeling. Before you can optimize, simulate, or engineer, you need to understand what **S** is and what it contains.

This section matters because it is where biology becomes algebra. The stoichiometric matrix is not an approximation or a model assumption — it is a direct encoding of mass conservation laws in a form that a computer can manipulate. Get it right, and every downstream analysis inherits that correctness. Get it wrong, and even the most sophisticated flux balance analysis will produce predictions that are fundamentally unreliable.

But the stoichiometric matrix is more than a bookkeeping device. Its algebraic structure encodes deep biological facts. The null space of **S** tells you the space of all possible steady-state metabolic behaviors. The rank of **S** tells you how many independent constraints govern the system. The left null space reveals conservation laws — things like "total adenylate nucleotides are conserved" — that you might never have guessed from looking at the biology directly. Linear algebra, it turns out, has a remarkably tight relationship with metabolism.

This section develops the stoichiometric framework from the ground up across four subsections. You will first learn how to build **S** from a list of reactions — including how to handle compartmentalization, exchange reactions, and the GPR associations that link reactions to genes. You will then meet the steady-state assumption, **S·v = 0**, the single equation that makes constraint-based modeling tractable. From there you will explore the null space of **S** and understand what it means for a metabolic network to have degrees of freedom. Finally, you will see how flux constraints — irreversibility bounds, nutrient uptake limits, enzyme capacity constraints — transform the abstract null space into a bounded, biologically interpretable feasible region. By the end of this section, the stoichiometric matrix will feel not like a formalism but like a natural language for describing how cells manage the flow of matter.
