In 2010, a team at UCSD used Flux Balance Analysis to predict whether individual genes were essential for growth in *E. coli* — and compared those predictions against a library of 3,979 experimental gene knockouts. FBA got roughly 95% of them right, with no kinetic parameters. No measured enzyme affinities. No rate constants. Just mass conservation and the hypothesis that the cell maximizes its growth rate. That is a stunning result, and it is the best advertisement for why you should invest in understanding FBA deeply.

Flux Balance Analysis matters because it is the most successful quantitative method in metabolic biology for its level of abstraction. Other modeling approaches require kinetic parameters that are unavailable for most reactions, or they can only address small subsystems, or their computational demands make genome-scale application impossible. FBA trades kinetic detail for scope, and what you can do with a genome-scale stoichiometric model and a linear program turns out to be remarkably broad.

The core idea is that evolution has shaped metabolic networks to operate near-optimally — at least under the exponential growth conditions that natural selection favors. If you trust that idea, then finding the optimal flux distribution under stoichiometric and capacity constraints is both computationally tractable and biologically predictive. The optimization problem is a linear program, which means you can solve it for a network with thousands of reactions in milliseconds, get the globally optimal answer, and extract shadow prices that tell you which nutrients are limiting.

This section covers FBA from its mathematical foundations through practical application and careful biological interpretation. You will first work through the linear programming formulation: what the objective function means, why LP is the right tool, and how shadow prices reveal metabolic bottlenecks. You will then learn how to interpret FBA results without over-reading them — understanding when the growth-maximization assumption holds, how parsimonious FBA improves flux prediction, and why acetate overflow is a triumph of the method rather than a failure. The third subsection brings this to practice with COBRApy, the Python library through which all of this is actually done. Finally, you will learn Flux Variability Analysis — the technique that reveals not just one optimal flux distribution but the full space of optimal possibilities. Together, these four subsections constitute the core toolkit for anyone doing constraint-based metabolic analysis.
