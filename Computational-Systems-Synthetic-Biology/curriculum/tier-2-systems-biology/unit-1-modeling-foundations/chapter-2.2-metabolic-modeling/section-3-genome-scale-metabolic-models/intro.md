The first complete genome-scale metabolic model of *E. coli* was published in 2000. It covered 739 reactions, 436 metabolites, and 660 genes — an enormous undertaking at the time, representing years of literature curation by a dedicated research team. Today, the same organism is represented by iML1515, which covers 2,712 reactions, 1,877 metabolites, 1,515 genes, and has been validated against thousands of experimental phenotypes. What changed is not just the scale, but the quality of evidence behind every reaction, the precision of every metabolite's molecular formula, and the fidelity of every gene-protein-reaction association. Genome-scale metabolic models have become the most complete quantitative summaries of cellular metabolism that exist.

This section matters because in practice, you will almost never build a model from scratch. You will begin with a GEM — a carefully curated, community-validated stoichiometric network — and use it as your computational substrate for analysis, engineering, or comparison. Understanding what makes a GEM reliable, which models exist for which organisms, how they were built, and where their gaps lie is the practical knowledge that separates productive modeling from frustrating confusion.

There is also a deeper reason to study GEMs carefully: they are not just computational tools. They are scientific objects that consolidate decades of biochemical research into a consistent, testable, computable form. Every reaction in iML1515 is supported by evidence: a gene, an enzyme, a biochemical measurement. The model is a compressed representation of what the community knows about *E. coli* metabolism. That knowledge can be queried, corrected, extended, and falsified. In that sense, working with a GEM is not just computation — it is engagement with the accumulated understanding of a field.

This section covers genome-scale metabolic models across four subsections. You will begin by understanding what makes a GEM — the hallmarks of genome-scale coverage, compartmentalization, mass balance, GPR associations, and experimental validation that distinguish a high-quality model from a draft. You will survey the major models for organisms you are likely to work with: *E. coli*, *S. cerevisiae*, human cells, gut microbiota, and pathogens. You will then walk through the reconstruction workflow — the multi-stage process from genome annotation through manual curation to phenotypic validation that produces a reliable model. Finally, you will tackle gap filling, the process of identifying and resolving missing reactions, which is both a technical challenge and a source of biological discovery.
