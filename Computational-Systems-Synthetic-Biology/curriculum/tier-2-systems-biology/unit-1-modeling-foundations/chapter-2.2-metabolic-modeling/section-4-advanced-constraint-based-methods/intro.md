Standard FBA is a snapshot: optimal fluxes at one moment, in one environment, assuming the cell can express whatever enzymes it wants. That snapshot is enormously useful — but reality is messier. Cells regulate which genes are expressed based on environmental signals. They grow in environments that change over time as nutrients are consumed. They live in communities with dozens or hundreds of other species, exchanging metabolites and competing for resources in ways that no single-organism model can capture. Addressing these realities requires moving beyond the base FBA formulation.

This section matters because the most important biological phenomena in metabolism are precisely the ones that standard FBA misses. Diauxic growth — where bacteria sequentially consume glucose then switch to a secondary carbon source — is a regulatory phenomenon. The metabolic rewiring that occurs when *E. coli* is shifted from aerobic to anaerobic conditions involves both stoichiometric changes and regulatory responses. The interaction between gut microbiome species and their human host occurs through metabolite exchange in a shared environment. Each of these requires extensions to the basic constraint-based framework.

The three extensions covered here span the major dimensions in which standard FBA is extended for biological realism. Regulatory constraints — via rFBA, transcriptomic E-flux methods, and GECKO enzyme capacity modeling — bring gene expression into the picture. Different methods trade off between granularity and computational cost, from the Boolean simplicity of rFBA to the proteomics-grounded enzyme pool constraint of GECKO. Dynamic FBA couples the metabolic LP to a set of ODEs describing the time-evolving extracellular environment, transforming FBA from a static snapshot into a time-course simulation tool appropriate for batch fermentation. Community metabolic modeling extends the framework to multiple organisms sharing a common environment, enabling computational exploration of cross-feeding, syntrophy, competitive exclusion, and the metabolic division of labor.

Together, these methods represent the frontier of what constraint-based modeling can address. Each subsection introduces not just a technique but a class of biological questions that became accessible with its development — questions about how cells coordinate metabolism with gene regulation, how batch fermentation dynamics emerge from steady-state metabolic principles, and how the emergent metabolism of a microbial community is more than the sum of its individual members.
