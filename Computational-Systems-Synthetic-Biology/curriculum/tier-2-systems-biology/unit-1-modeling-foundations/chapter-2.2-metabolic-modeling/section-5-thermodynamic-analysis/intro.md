Phosphoglucose isomerase — the enzyme that interconverts glucose-6-phosphate and fructose-6-phosphate in glycolysis — has an equilibrium constant of about 0.29 at pH 7. Under standard conditions, the reaction is slightly unfavorable in the forward direction. Yet in a glycolytic cell, this reaction runs forward continuously. The reason is that cells maintain the concentration of G6P about six times higher than F6P, which shifts the thermodynamic balance enough to drive the reaction forward. Stoichiometric models like FBA would have no problem running this reaction in either direction — or both simultaneously in a loop, which is physically impossible. Thermodynamics says which way reactions actually go, and under what concentrations. That is information FBA simply throws away.

This section matters because thermodynamics is not optional. The Gibbs free energy sets hard limits on what metabolism can do — which reactions are reversible, which are committed steps, what concentration ratios are compatible with a given flux direction. Ignoring thermodynamics when designing biosynthetic pathways or interpreting flux models leads to predictions that look reasonable on paper but are physically impossible in the cell. A proposed pathway that looks stoichiometrically feasible might be thermodynamically blocked at the metabolite concentrations that accumulate during production. A reaction assigned as reversible in a model might be essentially irreversible under physiological conditions. Thermodynamic analysis catches these problems before they become failed experiments.

The four subsections here build a progressively more powerful toolkit for thermodynamic analysis. You begin with the fundamentals: $\Delta_r G'$ and what it tells you about reaction directionality under cellular conditions, including the crucial distinction between standard free energy change and physiological free energy change. You then meet eQuilibrator, the computational tool that makes thermodynamic data accessible for thousands of metabolites — transforming what used to be a literature search into a programmatic calculation. From there you learn Max-Min Driving Force (MDF) analysis, which asks not whether individual reactions are thermodynamically feasible but whether a consistent set of metabolite concentrations can make an entire pathway feasible simultaneously — a much harder and much more meaningful question. Finally, enzyme cost minimization (ECM) connects thermodynamics to proteome economics: given a flux distribution, how much enzyme must the cell synthesize, and how does that cost depend on how far from equilibrium each reaction operates?

Together, these methods transform thermodynamics from a constraint you enforce by hand (setting irreversibility bounds) into an active analytical tool for understanding and improving metabolic designs.
