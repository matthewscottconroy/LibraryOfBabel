In the late 1980s, a simple but ingenious experiment was performed: cells were grown on glucose that had been isotopically enriched with ¹³C at specific carbon positions, and the resulting labeling patterns in downstream amino acids were measured by NMR. The patterns were different depending on whether cells used glycolysis or the pentose phosphate pathway to process the carbon. For the first time, it was possible to directly measure the split between two pathways that stoichiometry alone could never distinguish. That experiment established a principle that has since become one of the most powerful quantitative tools in metabolic biology: if you label the carbons, the metabolism labels itself.

This section matters because ¹³C MFA is the only method that gives you actual flux values — not predictions, not optima, but measurements of the real fluxes operating in living cells under specific conditions. FBA tells you the optimal flux distribution; ¹³C MFA tells you what the cell is actually doing. The two often agree, which validates the FBA approach, but they sometimes disagree in instructive ways — the cancer cell that routes enormous carbon flow through the pentose phosphate pathway despite "suboptimal" growth, the industrial strain that runs unexpected overflow metabolism, the plant cell that cycles carbon between chloroplast and cytoplasm in ways no model predicted. These discoveries came from isotope tracing, not computation.

Understanding ¹³C MFA also deepens your understanding of metabolism at a mechanistic level that no steady-state model can provide. To design a labeling experiment, you must think carefully about carbon atom transitions — which carbons in your tracer end up where in your products, and what mixtures of flux routes would produce the labeling patterns you observe. That kind of mechanistic thinking, forced by the method, is one of its hidden pedagogical gifts.
