A single fertilized egg contains roughly the same DNA as every other cell in your body. Yet from that one cell emerges the neuron that fires when you recognize a face, the pancreatic beta cell that releases insulin when your blood glucose rises, the cardiomyocyte that has been contracting rhythmically since you were a fetus in the womb, and nearly two hundred other cell types — each maintaining a distinct identity across decades and thousands of cell divisions, each executing a specialized gene expression program that is appropriate to its function and resistant to perturbation. All of this diversity, all of this precision, all of this stability: produced not by differences in DNA sequence, but by differences in which genes are expressed, when, and in response to what signals.

The machinery that makes these decisions is the gene regulatory network — a web of transcription factors, their target genes, and the regulatory logic that connects them. A transcription factor does not merely switch a gene on or off; it participates in a circuit with dozens of other TFs, all influencing each other, creating feedback loops and feedforward pathways that give the overall network emergent properties no single gene could produce alone. These properties — bistability, oscillation, adaptation, memory, precise threshold detection — are the computational primitives that cells use to make decisions, keep time, respond to signals, and lock in identities.

This chapter develops the quantitative tools for understanding gene regulatory networks at this systems level. The organizing theme is dynamics: not just what genes are expressed, but how regulatory circuits behave over time, how they respond to perturbations, and how their topology determines their dynamical repertoire.

We begin in Section 1 with **network motifs** — the elementary functional modules from which regulatory networks are assembled. Network motifs are small circuit patterns that appear far more frequently in real regulatory networks than in random graphs, a statistical enrichment that reflects selection for circuits with useful computational properties. You will work through autoregulation, feedforward loops, and feedback loops in detail, seeing how the mathematical properties of each topology translate directly into biological functions: faster response times, noise filtering, pulse generation, bistability, oscillation.

Section 2 introduces **Boolean network models** — an abstraction that treats every gene as either ON or OFF, governed by logical update rules derived from regulatory interactions. Despite (or because of) this extreme simplification, Boolean models make powerful predictions about the attractor structure of regulatory networks. You will encounter the Kauffman hypothesis — that cell types correspond to attractors — and examine how it has been validated by large-scale cell cycle models and single-cell genomics data. The section closes with methods for bridging the gap between Boolean and continuous dynamical models, allowing the qualitative predictions of Boolean analysis to be connected to quantitative expression data.

Section 3 turns to the practical problem of **inferring GRNs from gene expression data**. We have enormous amounts of data — single-cell RNA-seq datasets with millions of cells, hundreds of conditions — but transforming that data into regulatory network maps is far from straightforward. You will study information-theoretic methods (ARACNE, mutual information, VIPER), regression-based methods (GENIE3, GRNBoost2, LASSO), and the SCENIC pipeline, which combines expression-based inference with genomic motif validation to identify true direct regulatory interactions. The section concludes with network controllability theory — a graph-theoretic framework that identifies the minimum set of genes that must be externally driven to reprogram a cell from one state to another.

Section 4 extends the regulatory network concept into **epigenetics**: the heritable chromatin modifications that constrain and channel transcriptional regulation. Chromatin state, DNA methylation, and nucleosome positioning are themselves governed by positive feedback dynamics and bistable switches. You will build quantitative models of Polycomb silencing, simulate DNA methylation maintenance through cell division, and understand chromatin accessibility through a thermodynamic competition model between TF binding and nucleosome occupancy.

Section 5 uses the **mammalian circadian clock** as a detailed case study in oscillatory gene regulation — arguably the best-characterized gene regulatory network in existence. Working through the molecular architecture of the clock and the Goldbeter-Leloup kinetic model, you will see how a transcription-translation feedback loop generates robust 24-hour oscillations, how phosphorylation kinetics set the period, and how the secondary REV-ERB/BMAL1 loop provides robustness. The section distills six general design principles from this model system that transfer directly to understanding any other biological oscillator or regulatory circuit.

The chapter closes in Section 6 with **cell fate decision networks** — the circuits that implement commitment and identity in development. You will build the mathematical theory of bistability as decision-making, compute Waddington landscapes from single-cell data, and analyze Yamanaka reprogramming as a network control problem: an experiment in driving a cell from one attractor basin to another using a minimal set of external inputs.

Throughout, the goal is to develop fluency with the dynamical systems perspective on gene regulation — the ability to look at a circuit and ask not just "what does this gene do?" but "what does this topology compute?" That shift in perspective is the entry point to modern quantitative cell biology.
