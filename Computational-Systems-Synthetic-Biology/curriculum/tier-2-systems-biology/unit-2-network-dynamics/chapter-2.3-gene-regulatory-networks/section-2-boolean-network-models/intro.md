A mammalian cell undergoing differentiation does something remarkable: it navigates from a state of broad regulatory potential — a stem cell that could become any of dozens of tissue types — to a fixed, stable identity maintained indefinitely. This transition is not merely a change in which proteins are present; it is a change in the entire regulatory landscape of the cell, a reorganization of gene expression that persists through thousands of cell divisions and resists perturbation. How do you model a system like that?

You could write ordinary differential equations for each gene's expression level, parameterize the Hill functions and degradation rates, and simulate the resulting continuous dynamics. But for a network of 50–100 transcription factors governing a cell fate decision, this approach requires thousands of parameters that cannot currently be measured — and even if they could be, the high-dimensional ODE would be computationally expensive to analyze and difficult to interpret. Before you know which questions the model can answer, you are buried in parameterization.

Boolean network models offer a different approach. They start from a deceptively simple premise: each gene is either ON or OFF, and the state of each gene at the next moment is determined by a logical rule over its regulators. By compressing all the continuous complexity of gene expression into binary variables, Boolean models sacrifice quantitative precision and gain something extremely valuable: the ability to analyze the complete attractor structure of large networks from qualitative regulatory knowledge alone.

This section will walk you through the Boolean framework from foundations to application. The first subsection addresses the question that you should always ask before choosing a modeling approach: when is this the right tool? Boolean models excel for attractor-centric questions — questions about which stable cell states exist, how robust they are, and how the network transitions between them. They are less suited to questions about timing, graded responses, or noise. Understanding this distinction up front will save you from the frustration of applying a model to a question it cannot answer.

The second subsection introduces the mathematical formalism: state spaces, update functions, and the critical distinction between synchronous and asynchronous update schemes. This last point is not a technical footnote — it determines which attractors you find. You will see that the biologically faithful asynchronous scheme avoids artificial oscillatory attractors that arise as artifacts of synchronized updating.

The third subsection presents the attractor hypothesis — the Kauffman conjecture that cell types correspond to attractors of gene regulatory networks. This is one of the most intellectually provocative ideas in systems biology, with strong predictive power and growing experimental support from single-cell genomics. It transforms the question "which genes define this cell type?" into the deeper question "what topological feature of the network creates this attractor?"

The final subsection addresses a practical problem: how do you connect a Boolean model, which lives in a world of 0s and 1s, to experimental data, which comes in the form of continuous expression values? The quasi-Boolean and piecewise-linear formalisms provide a principled bridge, using Boolean topology as structural scaffolding to generate continuous dynamical systems whose parameters can be fit to data.
