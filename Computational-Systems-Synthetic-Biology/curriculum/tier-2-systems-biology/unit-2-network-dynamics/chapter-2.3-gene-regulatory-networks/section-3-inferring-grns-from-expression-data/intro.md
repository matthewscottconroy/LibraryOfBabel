You have a dataset: 50,000 single cells from a differentiating stem cell population, each profiled for the expression of 20,000 genes. Somewhere in those 20,000 genes are the transcription factors that are actually orchestrating the differentiation — activating lineage-specific programs, repressing alternative fates, establishing the stable attractors that correspond to mature cell types. Your task is to reconstruct the regulatory network from this data: to infer which TF regulates which target, which edges are activating and which repressing, and which nodes are the master regulators without which the differentiation cannot proceed.

This is the GRN inference problem, and it sits at the intersection of machine learning, information theory, and molecular biology. It is one of the most important unsolved problems in computational genomics, and also one of the most technically treacherous. The fundamental difficulty is not computational — it is causal. Gene expression data tells you correlations; regulatory relationships are causal. A TF and its target gene are co-expressed not because they are coupled to the same upstream signal, but because the TF directly activates the target. These two hypotheses can look identical in expression data.

This section covers the major classes of inference methods, with attention to both their mathematical underpinnings and their practical performance. The first subsection frames the problem precisely — establishing why it is hard, what different data types can and cannot tell you about regulatory causality, and how prior knowledge (TF binding motifs, ChIP-seq data, evolutionary conservation) can be incorporated to compensate for the fundamental limitations of expression-based inference.

The second subsection covers information-theoretic methods, centered on mutual information as a dependency measure and ARACNE's data processing inequality as a pruning principle for removing indirect interactions. You will also encounter VIPER, which solves the TF activity vs. expression problem by scoring TF activity from the coordinated behavior of its regulon rather than from the TF's own mRNA level.

The third subsection introduces regression-based methods — GENIE3 and GRNBoost2 — which reformulate inference as a supervised machine learning problem and have consistently performed among the best methods across benchmark evaluations. LASSO regression provides an alternative that, unlike tree-based methods, recovers the sign (activating vs. repressing) of regulatory interactions.

The fourth subsection covers SCENIC, the current standard pipeline for single-cell regulatory network analysis. SCENIC's key contribution is combining expression-based inference (GRNBoost2) with genomic validation (RcisTarget motif enrichment) to separate direct from indirect regulatory relationships, producing a regulon activity matrix that characterizes each cell's regulatory state independent of marker gene knowledge.

The section closes with network controllability — a graph-theoretic framework for identifying the minimum set of genes that must be perturbed to steer a cell from one fate to another. This connects inference to design: once you have a GRN, what is the minimal intervention needed to reprogram it?
