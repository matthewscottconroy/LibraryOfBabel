Something extraordinary happens when a fibroblast is grown in a dish for a year. Its gene expression profile drifts. CpG methylation patterns change gradually, in a process that mirrors — in slow motion — the epigenetic changes observed in aged tissue. The cell is not mutating; its DNA sequence is unchanged. What is changing is the layer of chemical modification that sits on top of the DNA, governing which genes are accessible and which are locked away. This drift is not random noise; it is governed by the dynamics of epigenetic maintenance systems that have measurable rate constants and quantitative failure modes.

Until recently, the dominant view of epigenetics was essentially qualitative: chromatin is either open or closed, genes are either active or silenced, and the state of each region is either maintained through cell division or erased during reprogramming. This section replaces that qualitative picture with a quantitative one — treating epigenetic states as dynamical variables that evolve according to differential equations, with parameters that can be measured and outcomes that can be predicted.

The first subsection introduces the conceptual framework: chromatin state as a bistable switch. The reader-writer positive feedback mechanisms that maintain histone modification patterns — exemplified by the PRC2/H3K27me3 system — are mathematically equivalent to the molecular toggle switches covered in the previous chapter. This is not an analogy; it is the same mathematical structure, instantiated in chromatin chemistry rather than transcriptional logic. You will see how the bistability criterion applies to epigenetic maintenance, and why this framework explains phenomena as diverse as cancer epigenetic heterogeneity and the reprogramming barriers that make iPSC generation inefficient.

The second subsection takes up DNA methylation, the best-characterized epigenetic mark and the one with the most complete quantitative framework. The coupled dynamics of DNMT3A/B (de novo methylation), DNMT1 (maintenance methylation), and TET enzymes (active demethylation) can be written as a differential equation system whose steady state, stability, and response to perturbation are analytically tractable. You will work through the mathematical model of methylation dynamics, simulate DNMT1 knockout, and understand why DNMT inhibitors require multiple cell cycles to achieve demethylation — a prediction with direct clinical implications.

The third subsection covers chromatin accessibility — the physical availability of regulatory DNA for protein binding. This is where epigenetic state meets transcription factor function: even a perfectly expressed TF cannot regulate its targets if the regulatory DNA is occluded by a nucleosome. The thermodynamic competition model between TF binding and nucleosome occupancy provides a quantitative framework for predicting which regulatory elements are accessible in a given cell type, and why pioneer transcription factors can access closed chromatin regions that other TFs cannot.

Together, these three layers — histone modifications, DNA methylation, and chromatin accessibility — constitute the epigenetic network that constrains and channels the transcriptional regulatory network. Understanding their dynamics is prerequisite to understanding cell identity, reprogramming, aging, and the epigenetic basis of cancer.
