There is a gene regulatory network in nearly every cell of your body that has been running without interruption since you were an embryo, that adjusts its phase every morning in response to sunlight, that compensates for temperature changes that would double the rate of every biochemical reaction it contains, and that keeps time accurate to within minutes over weeks. This network — the circadian clock — has been studied intensively since the 1970s, and what has accumulated is the most complete quantitative understanding of any gene regulatory network in biology.

For computational systems biology, the circadian clock is a gift. Every other regulatory network you will encounter in this curriculum has some component that is poorly characterized, some interaction whose sign is debated, some parameter that has never been measured. The circadian clock has none of these gaps. We know the molecular components. We have the kinetic parameters. We have decades of time-course measurements, mutant phenotypes, and pharmacological perturbation data. Every modeling technique this curriculum covers — ordinary differential equations, bifurcation analysis, sensitivity analysis, stochastic modeling, parameter estimation — has been applied to the clock, and the results have been validated against independent experimental data. This makes the circadian clock an ideal reference system: if your modeling approach gives wrong answers for the clock, something is wrong with the approach.

The first subsection describes the molecular architecture of the mammalian clock in detail. You will see how a primary negative feedback loop — CLOCK:BMAL1 driving transcription of Per and Cry genes, whose protein products then inhibit CLOCK:BMAL1 — generates sustained oscillations when combined with the critical time delay provided by casein kinase 1 phosphorylation of PER proteins. You will also encounter the secondary loop involving REV-ERB and ROR nuclear receptors, which reinforces the primary oscillation and provides robustness. The clinical connection is immediate: Familial Advanced Sleep Phase Syndrome is caused by a single amino acid substitution at a CK1 phosphorylation site in PER2, and understanding this mutation quantitatively requires understanding the mathematical role of the phosphorylation delay in the feedback loop.

The second subsection works through the Goldbeter-Leloup model — the landmark quantitative ODE model of the mammalian clock that has become a standard in the field. You will simulate the model, extract the oscillation period, and see how manipulating individual parameters reproduces known mutant phenotypes. The model is available from BioModels Database, so you can reproduce all the results yourself.

The third subsection extracts the general lessons from the circadian clock — six design principles that appear throughout biological oscillators and regulatory circuits. These include why strong nonlinearity is necessary for oscillation, how redundant feedback loops provide robustness, why temperature compensation requires compensatory parameter evolution rather than temperature-insensitive components, and why coherent population-level oscillations require intercellular coupling even when individual cells are competent oscillators. These lessons are immediately transferable to the next challenge you encounter in biology, whatever it is.
