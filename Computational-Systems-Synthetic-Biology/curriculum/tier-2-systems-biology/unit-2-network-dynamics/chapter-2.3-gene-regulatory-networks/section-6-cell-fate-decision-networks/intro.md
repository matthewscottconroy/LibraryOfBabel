In 1957, Conrad Waddington drew a picture that would become one of the most influential diagrams in biology: a ball rolling down a hillside divided by ridges into branching valleys. Each valley was a cell fate. Each ridge was a developmental boundary. The act of rolling downhill was differentiation — progressive, irreversible, channeled. Waddington called this the epigenetic landscape, and for fifty years it lived as a beautiful metaphor without a mathematical foundation.

Today, we can compute that landscape. Using single-cell RNA sequencing, dynamical systems theory, and Boolean network models, we can construct quantitative versions of Waddington's picture — maps of the regulatory state space that show where cells are, where they are heading, and what barriers they must cross to change fate. The mathematics that makes this possible is bistability: the property of a dynamical system that has two stable states separated by an unstable threshold. Every sharp cell fate boundary, every irreversible commitment decision, every stable cell identity maintained through thousands of cell divisions — all of these are bistability in molecular form.

This section develops the theory and applications of cell fate decision networks, building from the mathematical foundations of bistability all the way to the clinical and experimental reality of cellular reprogramming.

The first subsection presents the mathematical anatomy of bistable switches and explains why they are the natural substrate for cell fate decisions. The four key properties of cellular commitment — discreteness, robustness, irreversibility, and history-dependence — emerge directly from bistability, not as biological accidents but as mathematical necessities. You will work through the conditions required for bistability, understand hysteresis as the molecular basis of cellular memory, and see how cross-repression between master regulators implements the robust two-state switches found throughout developmental biology.

The second subsection takes up the Waddington landscape — both the conceptual framework and the mathematics needed to compute it. The quasipotential formalism connects the energy landscape metaphor to rigorous stochastic dynamics theory, and you will see how the landscape can be estimated directly from single-cell expression data using kernel density methods. RNA velocity provides the dynamical complement: not just where cells are on the landscape, but which direction they are moving.

The third subsection examines Yamanaka reprogramming as a detailed case study in attractor transitions. From a systems biology perspective, introducing Oct4, Sox2, Klf4, and c-Myc into a fibroblast is an experiment in network control — an attempt to drive the cell from one attractor basin to another using external inputs. The single-cell trajectory of reprogramming, with its bottlenecks, intermediate states, and branching paths, maps directly onto the mathematical picture of a ball climbing over a saddle in the energy landscape. The recent achievement of chemical reprogramming without any TF overexpression provides a powerful confirmation of the attractor model: it does not matter how you provide the energy to cross the barrier, only that the barrier is crossed.
