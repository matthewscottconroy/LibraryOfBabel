In 1987, roughly half of all metastatic melanoma patients were dead within a year of diagnosis. In 2011, a drug called vemurafenib entered clinical trials and produced tumor shrinkage in patients with a specific mutation — BRAF V600E — at a rate that was, by oncology standards, astonishing. The drug worked because researchers understood, at a quantitative mechanistic level, exactly which signaling molecule was mutated, what the mutation did to the kinase's activity, how that kinase's activation cascaded through MEK to ERK, and why ERK's sustained hyperactivation drove the uncontrolled proliferation defining the disease. The drug is not perfect — resistance emerges, often through reactivation of the same ERK pathway through alternative routes — but the partial success and the mechanisms of failure are both comprehensible, and comprehensibility is what makes further progress possible.

Signaling networks are the molecular infrastructure through which cells detect the state of their environment and coordinate appropriate responses. A cell surface receptor binds its ligand, undergoes a conformational change, recruits or activates intracellular effectors, and launches a cascade of molecular events that ultimately alters gene expression, metabolism, cytoskeletal organization, and cell fate. This sounds like a simple relay — and for individual components studied in isolation, it often appears that way. But the network as a whole exhibits behaviors that no single component possesses: amplification of vanishingly small inputs, switch-like responses that snap between two stable states, adaptation that maintains sensitivity across orders-of-magnitude variation in background signal, and temporal integration that distinguishes a five-minute pulse of ERK activity from a five-hour one. These are emergent properties of network architecture, and they are the subject of this chapter.

The chapter is organized around six main themes. We begin with the general principles of signal transduction — amplification, noise filtering, integration, and adaptation — approached from an information-processing perspective. The question "what does signaling achieve?" has a crisp, computational answer, and we develop it before touching any specific pathway. We introduce the MAPK/ERK cascade as the canonical model system: thoroughly characterized, clinically relevant, and rich enough in feedback structure to illustrate virtually every general principle. We then examine zero-order ultrasensitivity, one of the most elegant theoretical results in the field, which shows how switch-like behavior can emerge from simple enzyme kinetics without any allosteric cooperativity.

Section two examines how extracellular signals first make contact with the cell, covering the biophysics of receptor-ligand binding, the GPCR activation cycle and second-messenger signaling, receptor tyrosine kinases and their downstream scaffolds, and the TGF-beta/Smad pathway as an example of a nuclear transcription factor relay. Section three is devoted to calcium as a second messenger — its unique chemical suitability for this role, the IP3-induced release mechanism and the oscillatory dynamics it generates, and the diverse effector proteins that decode calcium signals into specific cellular responses. Section four examines how cells integrate multiple simultaneous signals through AND gate logic, how they maintain sensitivity to changing inputs through adaptation, and how they extract information from the temporal profile of a signal rather than just its magnitude.

The final two sections broaden the view. Section five confronts what might be called the specificity paradox — how can 518 kinases, many of them shared across dozens of pathways, produce specific cellular responses to hundreds of different stimuli? — and introduces phosphoproteomics as the experimental tool that finally made it possible to observe the whole signaling network at once. Section six is a structured reference for the major signaling pathways in mammalian biology: MAPK/ERK, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, Wnt/beta-catenin, Notch, Hedgehog, JAK/STAT, NF-kB, and cAMP/PKA.

Throughout, the approach is the same. Mathematics and biological mechanism are presented together because neither is complete without the other. An equation without its biological meaning is an algebraic exercise; a biological description without its mathematical structure cannot be used to make predictions. The goal is to leave you with a framework rigorous enough to read the primary literature in signaling biology and quantitative enough to model the systems you care about from first principles.
