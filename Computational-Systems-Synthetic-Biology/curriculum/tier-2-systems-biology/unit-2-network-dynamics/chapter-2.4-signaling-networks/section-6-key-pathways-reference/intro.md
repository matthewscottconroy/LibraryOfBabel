You have now built the conceptual architecture for thinking about signaling networks: you understand how individual molecules bind their receptors, how cascades amplify and filter signals, how calcium encodes information in frequency, how AND gates integrate multiple inputs, how adaptation maintains sensitivity across changing backgrounds, how temporal profiles specify distinct cellular fates, and how the same kinase can produce specific outputs in a sea of molecular sharing. This final section is a reference companion — a structured catalogue of the major signaling pathways in mammalian biology, organized so that you can locate any pathway's essential logic quickly.

Each pathway entry follows the same format: canonical activation sequence, key regulatory nodes and the feedback loops that shape the response, major outputs, disease-causing mutations, and approved drugs. The discipline of reading these entries is recognizing what you already know in each new pathway: the NF-kB pathway has a delayed negative feedback loop that creates oscillatory dynamics, just as you would predict from the general principles of negative feedback with delay. The Notch pathway uses a remarkably compact signaling logic — proteolytic release of an intracellular domain that is itself the transcription factor — that requires no second messengers and no kinase cascades. The JAK-STAT pathway threads the needle between those extremes: a receptor-associated kinase that directly phosphorylates a transcription factor, without the multi-tier cascade of MAPK but with more regulatory complexity than a simple Notch cleavage event.

Notice also which pathways have been successfully targeted therapeutically and which remain difficult. MAPK pathway inhibitors (BRAF, MEK, ERK) exist in abundance; direct RAS inhibitors were considered undruggable for decades before the recent breakthrough of KRAS G12C covalent inhibitors. Wnt pathway inhibitors have been elusive because targeting intracellular components risks disrupting normal Wnt-dependent tissue homeostasis. These therapeutic gaps are not random — they reflect the network architecture of each pathway, including how much feedback and redundancy is present, and how much the pathway is required in normal tissues.

When you encounter a pathway in a paper, a clinical report, or a drug label, come here first to orient yourself in the canonical logic before diving into the details. The patterns you have already learned — amplification cascades, feedback loops, nuclear effectors, second messengers — are all present here; this section shows you how they are assembled differently in each biological context.
