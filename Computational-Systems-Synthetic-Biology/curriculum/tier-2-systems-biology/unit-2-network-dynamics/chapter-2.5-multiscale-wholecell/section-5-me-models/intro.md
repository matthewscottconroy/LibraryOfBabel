Bacteria know something that most metabolic models don't: making proteins is expensive. Every enzyme in the cell had to be transcribed by an RNA polymerase, translated by a ribosome, folded by chaperones. The ribosome itself had to be transcribed and translated. The RNA polymerase had to be synthesized. At high growth rates, a substantial fraction of the cell's total protein — sometimes 50% or more — is made up of ribosomes, whose sole function is to make more protein. This creates a deeply self-referential allocation problem: to grow faster, you need more ribosomes; but making more ribosomes requires... ribosomes.

Standard genome-scale metabolic models have no way to represent this. They treat the enzyme complement as unlimited, optimizing fluxes as if you could always have exactly as much of any enzyme as you want. The models are powerful, but they systematically ignore the fundamental resource constraint of cellular life: the proteome has finite capacity, and every enzyme occupies some of it.

ME-models — Metabolism and Expression models — fix this by explicitly including the gene expression machinery as variables in the optimization. Every protein must be transcribed by a finite pool of RNA polymerases, translated by a finite pool of ribosomes. The ribosome is no longer a fixed-cost item in a biomass equation; it is a resource that is produced and consumed and limits growth just as carbon or nitrogen does.

This section presents ME-models through two angles: the elegant biological logic of ribosome allocation, and the concrete mathematical extension of FBA that makes it work. The ribosome allocation argument — that maximum growth rate is set by a competition between the cell's need for ribosomes and its need for metabolic enzymes — is one of the most illuminating quantitative arguments in microbiology. It explains why bacteria at high growth rates commit so much of their proteome to ribosomes, why overflow metabolism (secreting acetate aerobically) is rational rather than wasteful, and why antibiotics targeting ribosomes are most effective under conditions of rapid growth.

The computational implementation, COBRAme, extends the LP framework of COBRApy to handle the additional coupling constraints. You will see how the growth rate $\mu$ appears inside the constraint matrix — creating a quasi-linear problem that must be solved iteratively — and how this structure produces the quantitative predictions about proteome composition that can be tested against mass spectrometry data. The ME-model of *E. coli*, iOL1650-ME, correctly predicted ribosomal protein fractions across 12 growth conditions with $R^2 > 0.9$ — a prediction that the standard GEM, treating ribosomes as a fixed cost, cannot make at all.
