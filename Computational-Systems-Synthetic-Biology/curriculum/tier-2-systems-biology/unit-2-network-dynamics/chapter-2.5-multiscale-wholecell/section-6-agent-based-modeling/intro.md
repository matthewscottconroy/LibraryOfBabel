A biofilm is not a well-mixed population. Near the surface, cells have abundant oxygen and nutrients, divide rapidly, and express genes tuned for fast growth. A hundred microns deeper, oxygen is depleted, nutrients are scarce, cells are dormant, and their gene expression profile is completely different. A population-level ODE model — which treats all bacteria as identical and well-mixed — cannot represent this. It computes an "average" bacterium that does not exist anywhere in the actual biofilm. The spatial structure, the nutrient gradients, the cellular heterogeneity — these are not complications to be averaged away. They are the biology.

This section introduces agent-based models, the computational framework designed for exactly this situation: when individual variation and spatial structure matter so much that population averages become actively misleading.

The first subsection answers the fundamental question of when to use ABMs at all. The decision turns on whether individual variation generates qualitatively different population behavior — whether knowing the distribution of cell states, rather than just the mean, changes your predictions in ways that matter. You will work through a decision framework that distinguishes cases where ABMs are genuinely necessary (rare-event statistics, spatial patterning, emergent tissue morphology) from cases where a simpler ODE or PDE model gives you everything you need at a fraction of the computational cost. The honest truth is that ABMs are expensive in every dimension — computationally, analytically, and in terms of parameterization — and they are only justified when the biology demands it.

The second subsection surveys the landscape of ABM frameworks used in computational biology: NetLogo for teaching and rapid prototyping, Mesa for flexible Python-based research models, CompuCell3D for problems where cell shape and contact-dependent signaling matter, and iDynoMiCS for biofilm and microbial community modeling. These tools embed different assumptions, and matching the right tool to the right biological question is itself a scientific decision.

The third subsection goes deep on the Cellular Potts Model — the physics-based approach that underlies CompuCell3D and much of computational morphogenesis. The CPM treats cells as extended objects on a lattice that minimize a Hamiltonian encoding adhesion energy, volume elasticity, and surface tension. Through a Monte Carlo Metropolis algorithm, this produces realistic cell shape dynamics and emergent tissue organization without requiring explicit simulation of cytoskeletal mechanics. You will see the full CPM Hamiltonian, implement the Metropolis update algorithm in Python, and understand how differential adhesion, chemotaxis, and tumor invasion are captured in this framework.
