A cell is not a bag of molecules in equilibrium. It is a dynamical system — perpetually out of equilibrium, constantly integrating signals, making decisions, maintaining commitments, and adjusting its behavior to a changing environment. The proteins and RNA molecules inside a cell are not static building blocks but participants in a continuous computational process: gene regulatory networks read the epigenetic state and environmental signals to determine which genes are expressed; signaling networks transduce those signals from the cell surface to the nucleus in milliseconds; and all of these molecular processes are somehow coordinated across the spatial and temporal scales that separate a single phosphorylation event from the behavior of a whole tissue.

Understanding that computation — how cells process information and produce coherent behavior — is the central challenge of modern systems biology. This unit addresses it directly by developing the mathematical and computational tools for studying biological networks as dynamical systems.

**Chapter 2.3: Gene Regulatory Networks** is the foundation of the unit. It opens with network motifs — the recurring circuit elements from which regulatory networks are built — and establishes the key principle that network topology determines dynamical function: autoregulation can produce either speed or bistability depending on its sign; feedforward loops filter noise or generate pulses; feedback loops generate homeostasis or oscillation. From these elementary building blocks, the chapter scales up to Boolean network models and their remarkable prediction — the Kauffman attractor hypothesis — that the stable cell types accessible to an organism correspond to the attractors of its gene regulatory network. You will learn to infer gene regulatory networks from single-cell expression data using SCENIC and related tools, model the epigenetic layer that underlies transcriptional regulation, and study the mammalian circadian clock as the best-characterized oscillatory gene circuit in biology. The chapter closes with cell fate decision networks — bistability, the Waddington landscape, and Yamanaka reprogramming as an experimentally validated attractor transition.

**Chapter 2.4: Signaling Networks** extends the dynamical systems perspective to the faster-timescale networks that transmit information from the cell surface to the nucleus. Signaling operates on timescales of seconds to minutes — far faster than the transcriptional changes it produces — and uses mechanisms that are qualitatively different from gene regulation: protein phosphorylation cascades, second messengers, receptor activation, and signal integration at molecular coincidence detectors. You will develop quantitative models of receptor-ligand interactions and downstream signaling, examine calcium oscillations as a case study in second messenger dynamics, and study how cells integrate signals from multiple pathways simultaneously. The chapter includes a reference treatment of the major signaling pathways — MAPK, PI3K/Akt, Wnt, Notch, Hedgehog, JAK/STAT — as dynamical systems, providing the mechanistic foundation for interpreting pathway perturbation experiments.

**Chapter 2.5: Multiscale and Whole-Cell Models** confronts the hardest problem in computational cell biology: how to bridge the gap between molecular mechanisms (happening at the nanometer and millisecond scale) and cellular behavior (happening at the micrometer and hour scale). The chapter introduces the conceptual framework of multiscale modeling, with timescale separation as the key mathematical tool for managing complexity across scales. It covers the whole-cell model of Mycoplasma genitalium — the first complete computational model of a living cell — as a landmark in the field, and introduces the Vivarium simulation framework and ME (metabolism and expression) models as approaches for building modular, composable whole-cell models. Agent-based modeling provides a complementary approach for systems where cell-to-cell heterogeneity and spatial organization matter. The chapter closes by examining how scale-linking methods — from quasi-steady-state approximations to information-theoretic approaches — allow inference about higher-scale behavior from molecular-level data.

Together, these three chapters build the quantitative vocabulary for thinking about the cell as a computing machine: one that processes information through networks whose topologies determine their dynamical behaviors, across timescales from milliseconds to development, and at spatial scales from individual protein complexes to whole-cell behavior.
