# 3.4 Metabolic Engineering

Redesigning cellular metabolism to produce useful compounds — the bridge between systems biology and commercial biotechnology.

---

## Core Objectives

**TRY: the three metrics of metabolic engineering success**
- **Titer**: product concentration in the culture (g/L or mg/L)
- **Rate**: volumetric productivity (g/L/h)
- **Yield**: product per unit substrate (g product/g glucose; or mol/mol)

All three matter for commercial viability; they often trade off against each other.

## Pathway Design

### Identifying Biosynthetic Routes

**Retrobiosynthesis**: work backward from target molecule to available precursors
1. Identify target molecule
2. Find reactions in metabolic databases that produce target
3. Trace precursor requirements back to central metabolic pathways
4. Identify gaps: reactions not present in chassis organism

**Databases for Pathway Design:**
- **KEGG**: enzyme-reaction-compound connections; pathway maps
- **MetaCyc**: curated metabolic pathways from experimental literature
- **BRENDA**: enzyme kinetic parameters; Km, kcat, inhibitors
- **BiGG**: standardized reaction/metabolite IDs linked to GEMs

**Automated Retrobiosynthesis Tools:**
- **RetroPath2.0**: KNIME workflow; explores chemical space around target
- **BNICE.ch**: predicts novel enzymatic transformations using biochemical rules
- **novoStoic**: identifies non-natural pathways from biochemical reactions

### Enzyme Selection Criteria

For each reaction in the pathway, select the best enzyme considering:
- **Substrate specificity**: accept your actual substrate, not analogs
- **kcat**: catalytic rate — high kcat means less enzyme needed
- **Km**: affinity — low Km (tight binding) needed if substrate is limiting
- **Thermodynamics**: ΔG of reaction — unfavorable reactions need coupling
- **Expression compatibility**: codon usage, G/C content for expression in chassis
- **Side reactions**: off-target activity wastes substrate
- **Cofactor requirements**: NADPH vs. NADH has different availability

### Cofactor Balancing

Most biosynthetic pathways require:
- **ATP**: energetic currency; replenished by central metabolism
- **NADPH**: reducing equivalents for biosynthesis; mainly from pentose phosphate pathway
- **NADH**: often produced in catabolic pathways; electron transport chain
- **CoA**: activated carrier for C2 units (acetyl-CoA is central precursor)

**Common imbalances:**
- NADPH limitation: fatty acid synthesis, terpenoid synthesis — may need to overexpress G6PDH or alter glycolytic flux
- Acetyl-CoA availability: many terpenoids, polyketides — ATP-citrate lyase or pyruvate dehydrogenase bypass

**Cofactor engineering strategies:**
- Overexpress NADPH-regenerating enzymes: glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, NADH kinase
- Replace NADH-dependent enzyme with NADPH-dependent isozyme (transhydrogenase engineering)
- Rewire pentose phosphate pathway flux

## Strain Optimization Strategies

### Knock-Out of Competing Pathways

Identify pathways that consume your target metabolite or precursor:
- FBA/FVA: predict which knockouts increase flux toward product
- Optknock (COBRApy): find knockout combinations that couple growth to production
- MOMA (Minimization of Metabolic Adjustment): predict post-knockdown flux redistribution

**Examples:**
- Ethanol production: knockout of competing fermentation products (acetate kinase, lactate dehydrogenase)
- Isobutanol: knockout of competing amino acid pathways beyond the branch point
- 3-HP: overexpress MCR (malonyl-CoA reductase), knockout fatty acid synthesis competing for malonyl-CoA

### Overexpression of Rate-Limiting Enzymes

Identify the rate-limiting step:
1. Metabolomics: accumulation of intermediate → downstream enzyme is limiting
2. Proteomics: which enzyme is at lowest level relative to its flux requirement?
3. FBA with enzyme cost (GECKO): predict enzymatically constrained bottlenecks

**Overexpression methods:**
- Strong constitutive promoter (J23119 or equivalent)
- High-copy number plasmid (ColE1 origin, ~50–100 copies)
- Chromosomal integration + gene multiplication
- RBS optimization to match ribosome availability to need

### Dynamic Metabolic Engineering

**The growth-production tradeoff:**
During exponential growth: resources go to biomass
During stationary phase: resources available for production, but cells are stressed
**Solution**: dynamically switch from growth mode to production mode at high cell density

**Quorum sensing-based switches:**
- Detect population density via AHL accumulation
- Switch promoter activity at threshold density
- Example: glucose → cell growth → switch to production mode when dense

**Two-stage bioprocesses:**
- Stage 1: optimize for biomass accumulation (growth-supporting conditions)
- Stage 2: induce production (nutrient shift, temperature shift, inducer addition)

**Biosensors for dynamic control:**
- Metabolite-responsive promoters or riboswitches: sense pathway intermediate
- Feedback to control upstream flux when intermediate accumulates (avoid toxicity)
- malonyl-CoA sensor (FapR): controls expression of malonyl-CoA consuming enzymes

## Key Case Studies

### Artemisinic Acid (Keasling Group, UC Berkeley)

**Target**: artemisinin, antimalarial drug; expensive to extract from *Artemisia annua*
**Pathway**: acetyl-CoA → mevalonate → FPP → amorphadiene → artemisinic acid
**Host**: *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* (initial), *E. coli* (partial pathway)

**Engineering steps:**
1. Introduce complete mevalonate pathway from *E. coli* into yeast
2. Overexpress key genes: HMG-CoA reductase (rate-limiting)
3. Introduce amorphadiene synthase (plant terpene synthase)
4. Express CYP71AV1 (cytochrome P450) for 3-step oxidation to artemisinic acid
5. Balance NADPH supply; optimize fermentation conditions

**Result**: 25 g/L artemisinic acid in engineered yeast (>1000× over initial strains)
**Commercial impact**: 100 million doses of artemisinin produced annually via fermentation

### 1,3-Propanediol (DuPont, first commercialized metabolic engineering product)

**Target**: 1,3-PDO; polymer precursor for Sorona fiber
**Strategy**: glycerol → 3-HPA → 1,3-PDO using glycerol dehydratase + 1,3-PDO oxidoreductase
**Engineering**: first company to use metabolic engineering commercially (2000)
**Lesson**: regulatory approval, process scale-up, and cost-competitiveness require years beyond strain engineering

### Isobutanol (Liao Group, UCLA)

**Target**: isobutanol; drop-in biofuel
**Pathway**: 2-keto acids from amino acid biosynthesis → 2-ketoisovalerate → isobutyraldehyde → isobutanol
**Key insight**: redirect valine biosynthesis (IlvBNCD) + add KDC + ADH
**Titer**: >20 g/L isobutanol in engineered *E. coli*

### Non-Ribosomal Peptides and Polyketides

**Targets**: antibiotics, immunosuppressants (rapamycin, erythromycin, epothilone)
**Challenge**: multi-enzyme complexes (NRPS, PKS) with modular architecture
**Engineering approach**: swap modules between different NRPS/PKS to generate new compounds (combinatorial biosynthesis)

## Adaptive Laboratory Evolution (ALE)

### Principle

Let evolution solve the optimization problem:
1. Start with engineered strain producing target compound
2. Culture under selection pressure that correlates with desired phenotype
3. Serial dilution or continuous culture for hundreds of generations
4. Sequence evolved strains to identify beneficial mutations
5. Understand mechanisms → re-engineer better starting strains

### When to Use ALE

- After rational engineering hits diminishing returns
- When the target phenotype is survival or growth-linked
- When the genetic basis of a phenotype is unknown (then reverse-engineer from evolved mutations)
- To improve tolerance to toxic products or substrates

### ALE for Tolerance

Many metabolic products are toxic to producer cells:
- Organic acids (lactic acid, succinic acid): decrease cytoplasmic pH
- Alcohols: membrane disruption
- Terpenoids: hydrophobic; membrane intercalation

ALE under product stress → evolved strains with improved tolerance
Common mechanisms found: membrane composition changes, efflux pump upregulation, altered proton gradient management

### Limitation

ALE is powerful but:
- Growth selection ≠ production selection in all cases
- Evolved beneficial mutations may improve growth at expense of production yield
- Mechanism discovery requires careful genomics and reverse engineering

## Protein Scaffolds and Metabolic Channeling

### Substrate Channeling

When enzymes of a pathway are co-localized, the product of one enzyme is directly transferred to the next:
- No dilution into bulk cytoplasm
- Less intermediate accumulation (avoids toxicity)
- Higher effective local concentrations → better kinetics

### Synthetic Scaffolds

**Protein scaffolds (Dueber et al. 2009)**
- Fusion protein with multiple protein-protein interaction domains (GBD, SH3, PDZ)
- Enzyme tagged with cognate peptide ligand → recruits to scaffold
- Optimal stoichiometry by adjusting domain copy number

**DNA scaffolds**
- Enzymes fused to zinc finger proteins targeting specific DNA sequences
- Arrange enzymes in defined geometry on synthetic DNA scaffold

**RNA scaffolds**
- Aptamer sequences in RNA fold to bind specific proteins
- Enzymes tagged with RNA-binding domains → recruited to RNA scaffold
- Easily programmable RNA sequence

### Compartmentalization

**Bacterial microcompartments (BMCs)**
- Proteinaceous shells in bacteria enclosing enzymes
- Carboxysome: CO2-fixing enzyme complex
- Engineering: load heterologous enzymes into engineered shells
- Applications: concentrate toxic intermediates, channeling

**Organelle engineering (yeast)**
- Lipid droplets, vacuoles, mitochondria: distinct chemical environments
- Route pathway enzymes to specific organelles via targeting peptides
- Peroxisome: ideal for terpenoid synthesis (available acetyl-CoA, reducing equivalents)

## Fermentation and Scale-Up

### Laboratory Fermentation

**Batch culture**: add all nutrients at start; grow until limited
**Fed-batch**: add nutrients continuously; maintain optimal concentration; most common industrial

**Key parameters:**
- Dissolved oxygen (DO): monitor and control with stirring/aeration
- pH: neutralize organic acids produced
- Temperature: often optimal at lower temperature for heterologous protein stability
- OD (optical density): proxy for biomass; correlated with cell count

### Common Media

- LB (Lysogeny Broth): rich; fast growth; poor for production (too much amino acid supply → unregulated metabolism)
- M9 minimal medium: defined; controllable; better for metabolic flux analysis
- MOPS medium: well-defined; good for model organism studies
- TB (Terrific Broth): higher yield than LB for E. coli protein production

### Scale-Up Challenges

- Oxygen transfer: harder at large scale (diffusion becomes limiting)
- Mixing: gradients form in large tanks; cells experience heterogeneous environments
- Heat removal: metabolic heat production
- Contamination: sterility harder to maintain at scale
- Product inhibition: higher product concentrations at scale

**Computational fluid dynamics (CFD)**: model mixing and gradients in bioreactors; predict scale-up behavior
