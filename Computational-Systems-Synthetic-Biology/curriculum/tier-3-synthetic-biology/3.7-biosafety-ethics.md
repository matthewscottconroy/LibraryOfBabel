# 3.7 Biosafety, Biosecurity, and Ethics

Responsibility is not optional — it is part of the practice.

---

## Why This Matters

Synthetic biology creates powerful capabilities. The same knowledge and tools that allow:
- Engineering microbes to produce medicines
- Developing disease diagnostics
- Restoring degraded ecosystems

Also create risks for:
- Accidentally releasing engineered organisms
- Misuse to create pathogens or toxins
- Unequal access to benefits
- Unforeseen ecological consequences

Understanding both dimensions is required for responsible practice.

---

## Biosafety

### Biosafety Levels (BSL)

Risk classification of biological agents and corresponding containment requirements:

**BSL-1**: Minimal risk agents; no known disease in healthy adults
- Examples: E. coli K-12, S. cerevisiae, non-pathogenic cell lines
- Requirements: standard microbiological practices; lab coat, gloves; open benchtop work

**BSL-2**: Moderate risk; agents causing disease if exposed
- Examples: S. aureus, Salmonella, most human cell lines, HIV (in certain work)
- Requirements: BSL-1 + limited access, sharps precautions, biohazard symbol, face protection for aerosols

**BSL-3**: Serious or potentially lethal disease via inhalation
- Examples: M. tuberculosis, SARS-CoV-2, West Nile virus, Brucella
- Requirements: BSL-2 + access control, HEPA-filtered air, two sets of self-closing doors, PPE

**BSL-4**: High risk of life-threatening disease; no vaccine or treatment
- Examples: Ebola, Marburg, Lassa, smallpox
- Requirements: BSL-3 + full-body pressure suit or class III biosafety cabinet, dedicated building, air lock

### NIH Guidelines

- NIH Guidelines for Research Involving Recombinant or Synthetic Nucleic Acid Molecules (NIH Guidelines)
- Applies to all federally funded recombinant DNA research in the US
- Risk Group (RG) classification of host organisms: RG1–RG4
- Experiments requiring IBC (Institutional Biosafety Committee) approval
- Experiments requiring RAC (Recombinant DNA Advisory Committee) review

### Institutional Biosafety Committees (IBCs)

- Required at any institution doing recombinant DNA research (for NIH funding)
- Register all experiments involving rDNA
- Approve protocols before work begins
- Ensure training and PPE requirements are met

### Risk Assessment Framework

For any new experiment:
1. **Agent characterization**: pathogenicity, transmissibility, infectious dose
2. **Laboratory procedures**: aerosol-generating steps, sharps use
3. **Scale**: volume of culture, concentration
4. **Host-vector system**: containment features of the biological system
5. **Personnel**: training, immunocompromised status

## Biosecurity

### Dual-Use Research of Concern (DURC)

DURC: life sciences research reasonably anticipated to create, transfer, or use enhanced potential pandemic pathogens (ePPP), or knowledge/technologies that could be misused.

**7 categories of concern (NSABB, NIH):**
1. Enhance transmissibility of a pathogen
2. Enhance virulence or lethality
3. Increase resistance to therapeutics
4. Alter host range
5. Enhance ability to evade diagnostic detection
6. Enable weaponization or production
7. Cause significant disruption to ecosystems

**Policy responses:**
- Fink Report (2004): defined the dual-use dilemma; established NSABB
- NSABB review: advisory board for significant DURC cases
- Gain-of-function (GOF) research moratorium (2014–2017); current policies under HHS/NIH review

### Select Agents Program

- CDC/USDA list of highest-risk biological agents
- Registered facilities required for possession/transfer
- Personnel security risk assessment (suitability determination)
- Examples: Anthrax, Ebola, Smallpox, Botulinum toxin, F. tularensis

### Gene Synthesis Screening

DNA synthesis companies screen orders against databases of dangerous sequences:
- IARPA FELIX: pathogen sequence database for synthesis screening
- GenBank/NCBI Blast: checked against known pathogen sequences
- Customers ordering concerning sequences may be flagged, orders refused

International Gene Synthesis Consortium (IGSC) standards: ~80% of synthetic DNA volume covered

### Biocontainment

Engineering organisms so they cannot survive or spread outside intended context:

**Auxotrophic containment**
- Engineer dependency on synthetic amino acid not found in nature
- Without supply: cell dies
- Weakness: evolution of escape mutants over time

**Semantic containment**
- Recode essential genes with non-standard genetic code
- Engineered organism cannot use natural ribosomes to read these genes
- Requires co-delivery of orthogonal translation machinery

**Kill switches**
- Toxin-antitoxin systems: remove inducer of antitoxin → toxin kills cell
- Triggered by: absence of synthetic small molecule, temperature change, population density
- Problem: evolution frequently inactivates kill switches (selection against containment)

**Multi-layered containment**
- Multiple orthogonal containment mechanisms simultaneously
- Each can fail; combination dramatically reduces escape probability
- Wang et al. 2016: five-layer biocontainment in E. coli

## Community Standards and Governance

### iGEM (International Genetically Engineered Machine)

- Undergraduate/graduate synthetic biology competition
- Established community norms for biosafety
- White list (allowed parts) and required safety forms
- iGEM Safety Committee reviews all projects
- Strong community learning effect: newcomers learn safety culture through participation

### DIY Biology and Community Labs

- Growing community of citizen scientists doing biotechnology
- Community labs: Genspace (NYC), BioCurious (Bay Area), Hackuarium (Switzerland)
- BioCoder: code of ethics for independent researchers
- FBI Bioterrorism Prevention: outreach to community biology labs
- Tension: open access to knowledge vs. security concerns

### International Governance

- Biological Weapons Convention (BWC): 1975; prohibits development, production, stockpiling of biological weapons; 183 states parties
- Weakness: no verification mechanism
- Australia Group: export controls on dual-use materials
- WHO Pandemic Preparedness: surveillance and response coordination

## Ethics

### Equity and Access

- Who benefits from synthetic biology products?
- Artemisinin via fermentation: initially raised concerns about impact on farmers in developing countries growing *Artemisia annua*
- Gene drives: proposed to eliminate malaria mosquitoes or invasive species; consent issues in affected ecosystems/communities
- Biosafety regulations: compliance burden disadvantages low-income countries; innovation gap

### Environmental Release

- Engineered organisms released intentionally (gene drives, biopesticides, bioremediation)
- Environmental risk assessment: extremely difficult for novel organisms in complex ecosystems
- Reversibility: gene drives are by design irreversible
- Staged testing: contained field trials before open release
- Indigenous and local community consent: affected communities must be engaged

### Synthetic Organisms and Moral Status

- As organisms become more synthetic, what moral status do they have?
- Minimal genomes: does a living cell made of >50% synthetic DNA have different status?
- Chimeric organisms: human neural organoids in mice; pig embryos with human cells
- No consensus; active philosophical debate

### Responsible Innovation Framework

1. **Anticipate**: project possible consequences before research begins
2. **Reflect**: consider values, assumptions, motivations
3. **Engage**: involve public and affected communities
4. **Act**: respond to concerns; adjust research direction

## Practical Biosafety for Computational/Systems Biologists

Even if you work primarily computationally:

- **Sequence data**: downloading or sharing sequences of dangerous pathogens may have restrictions
- **Collaboration**: co-authoring with experimental colleagues puts you in contact with regulated materials
- **Publication**: describing how to enhance pathogen properties requires DURC review
- **Software tools**: designing guide RNAs for pathogens, designing enhanced toxins

**When in doubt**: consult your institution's biosafety office or IBC before proceeding.
