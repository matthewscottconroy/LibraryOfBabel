In 2006, a team at UC Berkeley and Amyris Biotechnologies announced that they had engineered *E. coli* and then yeast to produce artemisinic acid — the precursor to artemisinin, an antimalarial drug previously extractable only from the sweet wormwood plant *Artemisia annua*, and available only in quantities too small and too expensive to treat the hundreds of millions of people who needed it. The engineered yeast could produce artemisinic acid from glucose by assembling a metabolic pathway from parts drawn from three different organisms, including a cytochrome P450 from *A. annua* that had never been expressed in yeast before. A decade later, derivatives of this work were producing artemisinin at commercial scale, and the approach was saving lives.

Around the same time, other groups were engineering bacteria to serve as living sensors that could detect and report the presence of heavy metals in drinking water. Still others were designing human T cells to carry synthetic receptors — CAR-T cells — programmed to recognize and kill specific tumor cell types with a precision that traditional chemotherapy cannot approach. The field had a name by then: synthetic biology.

Synthetic biology is the engineering discipline that emerged from systems biology. The relationship between the two is clean and important. Systems biology asks: *how does this cell work?* It builds mathematical models of gene regulatory networks, metabolic fluxes, and signaling cascades, and uses those models to understand the emergent behaviors — oscillations, bistability, ultrasensitivity — that arise from the structure of biological circuits. Synthetic biology takes those models and asks the next question: *how do we build a cell that works differently?* It takes the same quantitative understanding of how biological components behave, and uses it to design new components and new circuits that give cells new capabilities.

The connection to systems biology is not superficial. The toggle switches and oscillators that synthetic biologists build are direct implementations of the mathematical archetypes — bistable systems, limit cycle oscillators — that systems biologists study in natural networks. The metabolic pathways that synthetic metabolic engineers construct must satisfy the same flux balance constraints that systems metabolic models predict. The gene circuits that drive CAR-T cell killing behavior use the same transfer functions, Hill coefficients, and feedback topologies that systems biologists have characterized in natural signaling circuits. Synthetic biology is, in a deep sense, applied systems biology — the discipline of taking quantitative understanding and turning it into design.

This tier is organized around three units that trace the arc from foundational tools to advanced applications. Unit 1 covers the building blocks and their composition: the genetic parts (promoters, ribosome binding sites, terminators, regulatory RNA, insulators) that are the atoms of any genetic design, and the circuits built by connecting those parts in specific topologies. You will learn how to characterize parts quantitatively, how to design them computationally, how to assemble them into devices using modern Golden Gate and MoClo standards, and how to reason about the circuit-level behaviors that emerge from their composition. This unit provides the vocabulary and grammar of synthetic biology — the component knowledge and design principles you need to read and write in the language of genetic engineering.

Unit 2 turns from the construction of new genetic programs to the modification of existing genomes. Genome editing — CRISPR-based tools in particular — has transformed what it means to modify a living cell. Rather than introducing foreign DNA on a plasmid, you can now rewrite the genome itself: correct mutations, knock in new genes at defined loci, regulate endogenous genes without altering their sequence, or perform genome-wide screens that identify every gene affecting a phenotype of interest. Unit 2 covers these tools, then turns to the broader engineering strategies that use them: metabolic engineering, the redesign of cellular biochemistry for production of valuable compounds; and directed evolution, the use of selection rather than rational design to discover protein and pathway variants that achieve desired functions.

Unit 3 addresses applications that push the boundaries of what synthetic biology can do. Cell-free systems — transcription and translation reactions performed outside intact cells — bypass the constraints of living organisms and enable new paradigms for prototyping circuits, manufacturing proteins, and deploying biological sensors in point-of-care settings. The unit closes with a rigorous treatment of biosafety and ethics: the ecological risks of engineered organisms, the regulatory frameworks governing their use, and the ethical obligations that attach to a technology with the power to redesign life. These are not peripheral concerns. They are part of what it means to practice synthetic biology responsibly, and the field's continued progress depends on getting them right.

Together, these three units give you a comprehensive view of synthetic biology as it is practiced today: grounded in quantitative molecular understanding, committed to principled design over trial and error, and engaged seriously with both the transformative possibilities and the genuine risks of engineering living systems.
