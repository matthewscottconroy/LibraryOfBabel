Most of what we know about biological sensing is about detection: does the signal exceed a threshold? Is the receptor bound? Does the gene turn on? But many of the most important biological decisions are not threshold decisions — they are range decisions. Insulin signaling depends on blood glucose being within a particular range; too low and different signaling is triggered than too high. NF-κB activates different target genes at different stimulation intensities. The concentration of a morphogen during development must fall within a specific window to specify one cell fate rather than another. The biological question is not "is it there?" but "is it there in the right amount?"

This is the band-pass problem, and it is surprisingly difficult to solve with the regulatory elements available in a cell. A simple activating promoter produces a monotone response that rises with input and never comes back down. A simple repressing circuit falls monotonically. Getting a response that rises, peaks, and then falls as input concentration increases requires at least two regulatory interactions with different thresholds and opposing effects on the output — which is exactly the design principle behind synthetic band-pass filters.

This section covers the design and implementation of band-pass concentration filters in genetic circuits. The material has direct therapeutic relevance: a significant obstacle in precision medicine is that a therapeutic agent must be effective within a concentration window that is effective but not toxic, which requires sensing whether the relevant biomarker is in the right range rather than simply above a threshold. Band-pass biosensors that detect the "Goldilocks zone" of a disease marker concentration — neither absent nor dangerously elevated — are an active area of clinical development.

The single subsection in this section develops the design principle in full: the two-threshold framework, the mathematical description of the activating and repressing arms, and the composed output function. You will work through both bacterial implementations (using characterized AHL receptor pairs with different sensitivities) and mammalian implementations (using Tet-On activators and KRAB repressors competing at the same synthetic promoter). A detailed case study on copper sensing in *E. coli* makes the design process concrete. The section also introduces the selectivity metric — a Q-factor analog borrowed from electronics — that quantifies how sharply a given band-pass design discriminates between concentrations inside and outside the pass-band. By the end, you should be able to specify the molecular components needed to detect any input molecule within a defined concentration window, provided you have appropriate regulatory elements with sufficiently separated K values.
