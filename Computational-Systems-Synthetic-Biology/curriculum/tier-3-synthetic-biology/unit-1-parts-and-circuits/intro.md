If cells are computing machines — and there is good reason to think of them that way — then the obvious next question is: can we reprogram them? Can we rewrite the instructions, add new logic, wire up new connections between inputs and outputs? For most of the history of molecular biology, the answer was a qualified yes: you could delete genes, you could overexpress proteins, you could introduce foreign genes from other organisms. But deliberate design — specifying a computation you want the cell to perform and building the genetic program that achieves it — was largely beyond reach.

That changed at the turn of this century. In 2000, two papers appeared in *Nature* within weeks of each other. One described a genetic toggle switch built in *E. coli* — a bistable memory element that could be flipped between two stable states by brief pulses of inducer, and that would remain in whichever state it was set until pushed to the other. The other described a genetic oscillator, a Repressilator, in which three repressors chasing each other's tails produced spontaneous cycling of gene expression with a period of several hours. Neither of these behaviors exists in wild-type *E. coli*. Both were designed from scratch, composed of parts whose individual behaviors were understood quantitatively enough to predict the circuit-level behavior that would emerge from their combination. The era of synthetic genetic circuits had begun.

This unit is about the two foundational layers of that enterprise. The first, covered in Chapter 3.1, is the parts themselves: the genetic elements — promoters, ribosome binding sites, terminators, regulatory RNAs, and insulators — that are the building blocks of any genetic circuit. Understanding these parts means understanding not just what they do but how to quantify what they do, how to design them for specified properties, and how to combine them in ways that do not produce unexpected interactions. The chapter traces the evolution of assembly standards from the original BioBrick system to modern Golden Gate and MoClo approaches, examines the molecular mechanisms underlying each part class in enough detail to reason about failures and workarounds, and confronts the central practical problem of context dependence — the fact that parts do not always behave the same way when moved between constructs.

The second layer, covered in Chapter 3.2, is circuits: the logic that emerges when multiple parts and devices are connected in specific topologies. You will see how the toggle switch and oscillator from those landmark 2000 papers arise from the mathematics of gene regulation networks — how bistability and oscillation are properties not of individual genes but of the feedback structure connecting them. Chapter 3.2 develops the quantitative circuit design toolbox: transfer functions and load, the Boolean logic gates that implement digital computation in cells, the analog circuits that perform arithmetic on gene expression signals, and the design principles that determine whether a composed circuit will behave as predicted or dissolve into unexpected cross-talk and context sensitivity.

The distinction between parts and circuits maps onto a classical engineering divide: components versus architecture. A thorough understanding of components does not automatically give you good circuits — you need to understand how components interact in topology, how feedback changes stability properties, and how the composition of individually well-characterized parts can produce collective behaviors that none of the parts exhibit alone. This unit provides both levels of understanding, in sequence, building from the molecule to the system.

By the end of this unit, you will have the conceptual and quantitative tools to read the synthetic biology literature with genuine comprehension — to understand why a particular circuit design was chosen, what assumptions it relies on, and where the likely failure modes lie. More ambitiously, you will have the foundation to begin designing circuits yourself: starting from a desired function, working backward to a parts list, predicting the behavior from known part parameters, and anticipating the engineering challenges in implementation.
