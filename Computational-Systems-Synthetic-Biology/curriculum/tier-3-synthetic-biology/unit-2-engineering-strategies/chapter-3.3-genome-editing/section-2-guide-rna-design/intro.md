The most common task in a CRISPR experiment is also one of the most consequential: choosing which 20-nucleotide sequence to use as a guide. It seems trivial at first. You're picking a short sequence from a region of the genome, and there are usually dozens of candidates. But the difference between a good guide and a poor one spans an order of magnitude in editing efficiency, and the difference between a specific guide and an unspecific one can mean the difference between a clean scientific result and an artifact, or between a safe therapeutic and a dangerous one.

Guide RNA design matters precisely because Cas9's specificity is imperfect. The system tolerates mismatches — some positions more than others — which means that every guide RNA has a landscape of potential genomic off-target sites that can be cut at varying frequencies. Choosing the right guide means simultaneously maximizing on-target efficiency and minimizing off-target risk, under a set of molecular constraints that are quantitatively well understood.

This section covers guide design in three linked subsections. The first establishes the design principles for on-target efficiency: PAM site selection, GC content, poly-T avoidance, seed region rules, and the position-specific nucleotide preferences captured in the Doench Rule Set 2 score. These are not arbitrary conventions — each rule has a mechanistic explanation, and knowing the mechanism is what allows you to apply the rules intelligently when you encounter a difficult genomic target.

The second subsection addresses off-target prediction: how Cas9's mismatch tolerance is captured by the CFD score, how tools like Cas-OFFinder enumerate all genomic sequences within a defined mismatch threshold, and why chromatin accessibility is a critical variable that sequence-based predictions alone miss. It also covers the three gold-standard experimental methods — GUIDE-seq, CIRCLE-seq, and Digenome-seq — for detecting off-target sites that computational tools might miss.

The third subsection turns to mitigation: the molecular strategies available when a guide has unacceptable off-target risk. High-fidelity Cas9 variants, paired nickases, truncated guides, chemical sgRNA modifications, and RNP delivery each reduce off-target activity through a distinct mechanism, and the section explains when each is appropriate. Together these three subsections equip you to design guides that work efficiently and specifically — which is, ultimately, the prerequisite for every other section in this chapter.
