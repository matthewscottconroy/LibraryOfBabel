A common misconception when first learning CRISPR is that there is one Cas9 — SpCas9 from *Streptococcus pyogenes*, 1,368 amino acids, NGG PAM, blunt-ended cuts. In practice, that description captures one member of a large and still-expanding family of RNA-guided nucleases that differ in size, PAM requirement, cut geometry, species of origin, and catalytic mechanism. The reason this family has grown so rapidly is straightforward: SpCas9 is excellent for many applications, but not for all of them. Its PAM requirement excludes some genomic positions. Its size strains the packaging capacity of adeno-associated virus, the dominant vector for in vivo therapeutic delivery. Its specificity, while impressive, can be improved. Each of these limitations has driven engineering efforts that produced a new variant or motivated the search for a smaller, more PAM-flexible, or more specific ortholog.

Understanding the variant landscape matters because selecting the right nuclease for a given application is now a standard step in experimental design — analogous to choosing between restriction enzymes in classical cloning. The section covers this landscape in a single subsection, organized by the problem each variant solves.

You'll encounter the engineered SpCas9 PAM variants — VQR, xCas9, and SpRY — that expand the range of targetable genomic sequences by relaxing PAM stringency, and you'll understand the specificity cost that comes with that flexibility. You'll learn why SaCas9 and CjCas9, derived from pathogens rather than *Streptococcus*, exist primarily because they are smaller and fit more easily into AAV, and what their more restrictive PAMs mean for target site selection. You'll encounter Cas12a, which cuts with staggered ends and processes its own crRNA arrays — properties that make it especially useful for multiplexed editing and diagnostics. And you'll understand Cas13, which targets RNA rather than DNA and underlies some of the most sensitive nucleic acid detection systems ever built.

Perhaps most importantly, this section introduces dCas9 — catalytically dead Cas9 — as the programmable DNA-binding scaffold that makes CRISPRi, CRISPRa, and epigenome editing possible. Once you remove the catalytic activity from Cas9, you are no longer constrained to cutting DNA: you can recruit any molecular function to any genomic position simply by changing the guide RNA. That versatility is what allows a single molecular platform to serve as both a gene editor and a gene regulator, and it is the conceptual foundation for the genome-wide screens covered in section 7.
