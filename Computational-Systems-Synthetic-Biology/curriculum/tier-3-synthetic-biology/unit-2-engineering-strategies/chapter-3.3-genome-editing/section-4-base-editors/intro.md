There is something uncomfortable about introducing a double-strand break into a human cell as a therapeutic intervention. The cell treats that break as a serious emergency: dozens of proteins converge within seconds, repair pathways compete, and the outcome — even with a perfect repair template — involves a degree of cellular stress and stochastic variation. For many therapeutic applications, particularly those requiring precise correction of a single nucleotide in a non-dividing cell, the ideal would be a tool that simply reaches in and chemically changes the base, without the drama of a break, without cell-cycle restrictions, without a separate repair template. That is exactly what base editors do.

Base editors are a conceptual departure from the CRISPR paradigm of cutting and repairing. Instead of inducing a break and waiting for the cell to fix it, a base editor nicks one strand (to signal repair machinery) and directly deaminates a target base in the R-loop — chemically converting cytosine to uracil (which reads as thymine) or adenine to inosine (which reads as guanine). The edit is written directly by a fused enzyme, not by the cell's repair machinery. The result is a C-to-T or A-to-G transition at a specific genomic position, at efficiencies that can reach 80% in cells where HDR would achieve 3%.

This section covers cytosine and adenine base editors in a single subsection that explains the chemistry and protein architecture of each, the concept of the editing window that restricts which bases can be targeted, and the variants that have improved efficiency and reduced off-target activity since the original BE3 and ABE7.10. You'll see why approximately 58% of known pathogenic point mutations — the ones that are single-nucleotide transitions — are in principle addressable by base editing without HDR, and you'll understand the specific example of progeria, where CBE has corrected the causative C-to-T mutation in mouse models with near-complete efficiency.

The section also covers the real limitations: bystander editing (when multiple cytosines or adenines fall in the editing window), guide-independent RNA deamination by the deaminase domain, and the fact that base editors cannot install transversions, insertions, or deletions. Understanding these boundaries is what makes the transition to prime editing — covered in the next section — feel not arbitrary but necessary.
