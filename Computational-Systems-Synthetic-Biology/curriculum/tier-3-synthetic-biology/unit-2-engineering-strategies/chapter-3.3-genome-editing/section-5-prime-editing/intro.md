After base editors, you might think the precision genome editing problem is largely solved. Transitions can be installed with 40–80% efficiency, no double-strand breaks needed, no HDR template required. But then you check: the mutation you need to correct is a transversion — adenine to thymine, say. Base editors can't help. Or you need to insert three amino acids at a specific position. Base editors can't help with that either. Or you need to create a specific 15-nucleotide deletion. Still nothing. For roughly 42% of known pathogenic point mutations — the transversions — and for essentially all insertions and small deletions, the precision tools available before 2019 remained inadequate in non-dividing cells.

Prime editing changes the picture by introducing a genuinely new operating principle. Instead of deaminating a base in the R-loop, prime editing uses a reverse transcriptase tethered to Cas9 to directly write new sequence into the nicked strand, using a template embedded in an extended guide RNA. The information encoding the desired edit travels with the editing machinery — no separate donor DNA, no HDR required. This approach is borrowed from retroviral biology, but targeted with the precision of Cas9-guided R-loop formation.

This section covers prime editing in four subsections that follow the natural logic of the technology. The first introduces the conceptual innovation and the PE2 components — the nCas9-H840A-RT fusion protein and the pegRNA with its spacer, RT template, and primer-binding site — explaining the design logic for each element and why PE2 achieves 2–25% efficiency as a baseline. The second covers the seven-step mechanism in molecular detail, tracing the edit from target binding through nick formation, PBS hybridization, reverse transcription, flap resolution, ligation, and the all-important mismatch repair step where half of all prime editing events can revert. Understanding this mechanism is what makes the improvements that follow comprehensible rather than arbitrary.

The third subsection covers PE3 and PE5 — the efficiency improvements that address specific mechanistic bottlenecks — along with epegRNAs and PEmax. These are not simple add-ons; each targets a specific failure mode in the mechanism, and the PE5 efficiency table shows the cumulative improvement from each layer of optimization. The final subsection addresses limitations honestly: where base editors still outperform prime editing, why insertions beyond 44 bp are currently inaccessible, and how the size and complexity of the prime editor protein creates delivery challenges that are still being actively solved. Together, the four subsections give you both a mechanistic understanding of how prime editing works and a practical framework for deciding when it is the right tool.
