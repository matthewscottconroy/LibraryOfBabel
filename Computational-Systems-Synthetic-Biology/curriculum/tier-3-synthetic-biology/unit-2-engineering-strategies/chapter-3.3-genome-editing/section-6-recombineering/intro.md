The CRISPR revolution has so thoroughly dominated the genome editing narrative that it's easy to forget an equally important technology developed nearly a decade earlier: recombineering. In 2000, Datsenko and Wanner described a simple one-step method for making precisely defined genetic modifications in *E. coli* using PCR products with 40-nucleotide homology arms and three proteins from bacteriophage lambda. No restriction enzymes, no cloning, no kilobase-scale homology regions — just a short synthetic oligonucleotide and an induced expression system. Recombineering became ubiquitous in bacterial genetics and metabolic engineering almost immediately, and it remains the method of choice for bacterial chromosome engineering today, even in the CRISPR era.

The reason recombineering persists is that it solves a slightly different problem than CRISPR. CRISPR introduces a double-strand break and relies on cellular repair; recombineering works at the replication fork, incorporating synthetic sequence directly into newly synthesized DNA. For bacteria, which lack robust HDR and for which NHEJ introduces lethal chromosomal instability, recombineering is often the only practical way to make a precise, scarless modification. And when combined with CRISPR — as covered in the second subsection of this section — the combination is remarkably powerful, because Cas9 lethality can be used to select against unmodified cells, enriching the population of recombinants from 0.01% to near 100%.

The first subsection covers the lambda Red system in detail: the three proteins (Gam, Exo, Beta) and why each is necessary, the mechanism by which single-stranded oligonucleotides are incorporated at the replication fork, and the extensions to multiplex automated genome engineering (MAGE) that scaled the approach to the genome level. You'll understand why seamless recombineering with counter-selection is the gold standard for scar-free bacterial genome modification, and why MAGE was used to recode all 321 TAG stop codons in *E. coli* — a project that would be essentially impossible by traditional cloning.

The second subsection covers CRISPR-assisted recombineering: the logical coupling of Cas9 lethality with lambda Red repair, the design rules for ensuring the repair template disrupts the guide RNA target, and the extensions to CAGE (CRISPR Accelerated Genome Engineering) and multiplex yeast editing. For metabolic engineering — where dozens of sequential modifications are often required — this section is highly practical: CRISPR-assisted recombineering is what makes the strain construction workflow manageable.
