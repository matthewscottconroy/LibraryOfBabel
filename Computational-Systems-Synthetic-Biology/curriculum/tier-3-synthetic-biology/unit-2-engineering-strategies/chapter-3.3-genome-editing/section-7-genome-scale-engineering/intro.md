Individual gene editing is impressive. But biology operates at the level of networks — dozens or hundreds of genes interacting through metabolic pathways, regulatory circuits, and feedback loops. If you want to reengineer a cell's metabolism to produce a valuable chemical at high yield, modifying a single gene is rarely enough. You need to knock out competing pathways, overexpress rate-limiting enzymes, tune expression levels across an entire synthetic pathway, and do this iteratively, guided by computational models of flux. Similarly, if you want to know which genes are essential for a specific cellular phenotype — drug resistance, cancer cell proliferation, the response to a toxin — you can't test one gene at a time. You need to test all of them.

This section explores genome editing at the scale where the full power of CRISPR becomes apparent: not as a tool for changing one position in one gene, but as a platform for systematically rewiring entire genomes and screening the function of every gene simultaneously.

The first subsection focuses on metabolic engineering — arguably the most mature application of CRISPR at the genome scale. It covers the sequential knockout strategy for eliminating competing pathways, the concept of safe harbor integration sites for overexpressing pathway enzymes, and the connection to flux balance analysis and OptKnock that makes rational strain design possible. The lycopene production example illustrates how a dozen CRISPR modifications, guided by computational metabolic modeling, can be executed in weeks rather than months. You'll also see how CRISPR has opened up industrially relevant organisms — *Corynebacterium*, *Aspergillus*, *Clostridium*, microalgae — that were previously inaccessible to systematic genetic manipulation.

The second subsection covers CRISPRi/a genome-wide screens: the use of dCas9-KRAB and dCas9-VPR fusions with pooled guide RNA libraries to systematically repress or activate every gene in the genome and observe phenotypic consequences. This is functional genomics at its most powerful — the ability to ask, in a single experiment, which of the 20,000 genes in a human cell is required for a specific phenotype. The MAGeCK and BAGEL analysis pipelines that extract ranked gene lists from these screens are covered in sufficient depth to enable you to interpret and design your own screen.

The third subsection reaches the logical endpoint of genome-scale engineering: synthesizing genomes from scratch. JCVI-syn3.0's 473-gene minimal cell, the Church lab's complete *E. coli* genome recoding with 3 compressed codons, and the Sc2.0 synthetic yeast chromosome project each represent a different answer to the question of what it means to design a genome. These are not just engineering demonstrations — they are experiments with deep biological implications about the minimal requirements for life and the evolvability of the genetic code.
