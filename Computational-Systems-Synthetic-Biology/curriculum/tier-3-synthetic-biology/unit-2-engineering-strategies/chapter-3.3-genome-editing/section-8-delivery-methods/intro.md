A guide RNA that perfectly specifies your target, a Cas9 variant optimally matched to your application, a delivery format with minimal off-target risk — all of this work is wasted if the editing machinery cannot get inside the right cells. Delivery is routinely the limiting factor in CRISPR applications, yet it receives surprisingly little attention in treatments of the molecular biology. For basic research with transformed cell lines, transfection with plasmid DNA is adequate. But the moment you step into primary cells, into tissues, into living animals, delivery becomes the dominant engineering challenge.

The problem has three components. First, the components themselves — Cas9 protein (or its encoding RNA), the guide RNA, and optionally a donor template or pegRNA — are large, charged, fragile molecules that cells are actively designed to exclude and degrade. Second, the tissues and cell types that matter most therapeutically — neurons, cardiomyocytes, hematopoietic stem cells, hepatocytes — present very different barriers to entry and respond to perturbation differently. Third, the delivery format (plasmid, mRNA, ribonucleoprotein) determines how long Cas9 is active in the cell, which in turn affects both the probability of editing and the accumulation of off-target cuts. Getting all three right simultaneously is a genuine engineering problem.

This section addresses delivery in a single comprehensive subsection that covers the major platforms — RNP electroporation, lipid nanoparticles, adeno-associated virus, and standard lipofection — from the perspective of their mechanisms, practical trade-offs, and approved or advanced clinical applications. The examples grounding the section are real: the FDA-approved Casgevy therapy for sickle cell disease, which uses ex vivo RNP electroporation of hematopoietic stem cells; and the Intellia NTLA-2001 trial for transthyretin amyloidosis, which uses systemic LNP delivery to achieve durable in vivo gene editing in liver. These are not hypothetical applications — they represent the current frontier of what has been demonstrated safe and effective in humans, and they illustrate precisely why delivery choices cannot be separated from therapeutic design.
