Every metabolic engineering project begins with the same blank-slate question: how do you get from here — glucose, acetyl-CoA, pyruvate, whatever the cell already makes — to there, the molecule you want to produce? The answer is not always obvious. Some targets have well-known natural biosynthetic routes that can simply be transplanted into a new host. Others have no known natural route and must be assembled from parts, guided by thermodynamic principles and databases of enzymatic reactions. And even when a route exists on paper, the choice between multiple candidate routes — each with different enzyme requirements, cofactor balances, and thermodynamic properties — can determine whether a project succeeds or stalls.

Pathway design is the phase of metabolic engineering where computational tools pay their greatest dividends. The retrobiosynthesis approach — starting from the target molecule and working backward to available cellular metabolites — was once done by hand, requiring weeks of literature search and expert judgment. Today, tools like RetroPath2.0 can explore thousands of candidate routes in hours, ranking them by thermodynamic feasibility, number of heterologous steps, and enzyme availability. The difference between a three-step route requiring two heterologous enzymes and a twelve-step route requiring ten is not marginal; it may determine whether the project is feasible at all.

This section covers three interconnected aspects of pathway design. The first subsection addresses how to identify biosynthetic routes — the retrobiosynthesis approach, the key databases (KEGG, MetaCyc, BRENDA, BiGG), and the computational tools that automate route discovery. You will see how thermodynamic evaluation, using tools like eQuilibrator, filters candidate routes to those that are actually feasible under intracellular conditions, and how the worked example of lycopene production illustrates the tradeoffs involved in route selection.

The second subsection addresses enzyme selection — the step where you populate each enzymatic node in your chosen route with a specific protein from a real organism. Multiple homologs exist for almost every enzyme class, and the differences between them in kcat, Km, cofactor specificity, and expression compatibility are often the difference between a pathway that works and one that does not. The decision framework developed here will give you a systematic way to evaluate enzyme candidates before committing to synthesis.

The third subsection addresses cofactor balancing — one of the most commonly underestimated challenges in metabolic engineering. Most biosynthetic pathways consume NADPH, and NADPH supply is frequently limiting in high-flux contexts. Understanding how to identify cofactor imbalances, either by stoichiometric analysis of the pathway or by FBA with cofactor constraints, and how to address them through PPP overexpression, transhydrogenase engineering, or cofactor-specificity switching, is often the difference between a strain that stalls at low titer and one that achieves commercial relevance.

Pathway design sets the ceiling on what any subsequent optimization can accomplish. The best strain optimization in the world cannot overcome a fundamentally flawed route — one that requires impossible cofactor balances, generates toxic intermediates at every step, or depends on enzymes that do not function in the chosen chassis. Getting the design right, before any construction begins, is the single highest-leverage activity in the entire metabolic engineering process.
