You have designed the pathway, selected the enzymes, expressed them in your chosen host, and run your first fermentation. The product is there — but the titer is 10-fold below where you need it to be. What happens next is strain optimization: the iterative, systems-guided process of identifying what is limiting your production and making targeted changes to relieve those limitations. This is not guesswork, and it is not random mutagenesis. It is a structured engineering cycle that uses metabolomics, proteomics, and computational modeling to tell you precisely where to intervene.

The fundamental insight of strain optimization is that production is limited, almost always, by a combination of three classes of problems: too much flux going somewhere other than your product (competing pathways), too little catalytic capacity at a specific step (rate-limiting bottlenecks), and a static genetic program that cannot adapt between growth and production phases. Each of these problems has specific diagnostic signatures and specific solutions.

This section covers three complementary strategies for strain optimization. The first subsection addresses the knockout of competing pathways — using flux balance analysis and tools like OptKnock to identify which reactions divert precursors away from your target, and how to eliminate them without killing the cell. The worked example of succinate production illustrates how a carefully chosen set of four knockouts can push yield from near zero to 93% of theoretical maximum. The deeper point is that competing pathway knockouts are not just about removing competition — they can couple growth and production in ways that make the cell's own evolutionary drive work for you rather than against you.

The second subsection addresses the overexpression of rate-limiting enzymes — how to identify which step in your pathway is the true bottleneck, using the converging evidence of metabolomics (intermediate accumulation), proteomics (enzyme levels), and enzyme-constrained FBA (GECKO modeling). The stepwise lycopene optimization example traces exactly how you move from "something is wrong" to "this specific enzyme, expressed at this level, is the constraint" to a resolved bottleneck — and how resolving one bottleneck simply reveals the next one, requiring another iteration.

The third subsection introduces dynamic metabolic engineering — the recognition that static gene expression programs are inherently suboptimal because the conditions for growth and the conditions for production are in fundamental conflict. Quorum sensing-based switches, metabolite-responsive biosensors like the FapR system, and two-stage bioprocesses all address the same problem in different ways: how do you allow the cell to grow rapidly during the growth phase and then redirect all available resources toward production once sufficient biomass has accumulated? The ODE models that govern quorum sensing dynamics, and the design principles for avoiding oscillation and evolutionary escape, are developed in enough detail to guide actual circuit design.

Strain optimization is where the systematic character of metabolic engineering most clearly shows. It is not enough to make changes and hope for the best. It is necessary to know why a change is expected to help, what you expect to measure if it works, and how to interpret the result to design the next iteration. This section gives you both the diagnostic framework and the specific intervention toolkit to do that well.
