Principles without examples are abstract; examples without principles are anecdotes. The case studies in this section occupy a unique position in this chapter — they are where the engineering strategies developed in the previous sections come alive in specific biological systems, specific failures, and specific breakthroughs that changed what people believed was possible. Each case study has been chosen not just because it is historically significant but because it illustrates a distinct dimension of metabolic engineering that the other cases do not.

The artemisinic acid project — the Keasling lab's decade-long effort to produce the antimalarial drug precursor artemisinin in engineered yeast — is the canonical example of complex terpenoid pathway engineering. It shows what 10 years of iterative DBTL cycles looks like when applied to a genuinely difficult target: a product that requires membrane-associated P450 enzymes with their own electron supply chains, a competing essential pathway that cannot be eliminated, product toxicity that demands active management, and a rate-limiting enzyme that required 8 chromosomal copies to express at sufficient levels. The journey from 0.025 mg/L amorphadiene in 2003 to 25 g/L artemisinic acid in 2013 is a roadmap of every major strain optimization strategy in action. It also illustrates — without sugar-coating — what realistic timelines look like for complex pathway engineering at global health scale.

The 1,3-propanediol case demonstrates something different: that metabolic engineering can achieve commercial competitiveness with petroleum chemistry, not just pharmaceutical niche markets. DuPont's 18-year journey to commercial production of bio-based PDO establishes the template for commodity chemical metabolic engineering — multi-organism pathway assembly, the willingness to introduce 30 genes to solve a cofactor problem, the importance of fermentation process engineering alongside strain engineering, and the brutal reality that regulatory approval, process scale-up, and economic analysis each add years to the timeline.

The isobutanol case introduces the concept of metabolic retrosynthesis with non-obvious precursors — the insight that the cell is already making, in amino acid biosynthesis, the exact carbon skeleton you need, and that the most elegant engineering solutions exploit this existing infrastructure rather than building around it. The Liao group's 2008 paper changed how engineers approach biofuel design by showing that two heterologous enzymes added at a branch point in valine biosynthesis could yield 22 g/L of a compound the cell had never produced.

The final case study, on nonribosomal peptides and polyketides, confronts the limits of modular engineering. The NRPS and PKS assembly-line concept — that you can rearrange biosynthetic modules like Lego blocks to generate new antibiotics on demand — has been enormously motivating and only partially validated. Understanding why domain and module swaps so often fail, and what computational and structural tools are slowly making them more reliable, is essential context for anyone working on natural product engineering.

Together these four cases span the range from proof-of-concept to commercial production, from simple two-enzyme additions to 30-gene cofactor engineering, from individual protein-engineering choices to structural biology-guided combinatorial biosynthesis. They are the foundation for understanding what metabolic engineering can realistically accomplish, and at what cost.
