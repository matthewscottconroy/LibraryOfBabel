Evolution cannot act on what does not exist. Before selection can find an improved enzyme, the improved enzyme has to be somewhere in the library you generate. This sounds obvious, but it has a profound consequence: the quality of the library you create — how comprehensively it covers the relevant regions of sequence space, how evenly it distributes mutations, what fraction of library members retain folded and functional proteins — largely determines the quality of the result you can achieve, regardless of how sophisticated your selection strategy is. No assay, however clever, can find a variant that was never synthesized. Generating diversity well is therefore not a technical afterthought; it is where directed evolution experiments succeed or fail at the level of design.

This section covers the four main approaches to generating sequence diversity for directed evolution, each suited to a different stage of the optimization process and a different type of question. The methods differ in where they introduce mutations, how they sample the space of possible changes, and what prior knowledge they require or exploit.

Error-prone PCR is where most directed evolution projects begin. It is deliberately unsophisticated: a PCR reaction run under conditions that make the polymerase sloppy, introducing random mutations across the entire gene at a controlled average rate. Its great virtue is that it requires no knowledge of which positions matter — you let selection figure that out. The Poisson statistics of epPCR, the effects of mutation rate on library quality, the biases in the mutation spectrum that limit which amino acid changes are accessible — all of these have practical consequences for how you design the experiment and interpret the results.

Saturation mutagenesis takes a different approach: instead of random mutations everywhere, it asks the question of specific positions exhaustively. At one or more targeted sites, every possible amino acid is represented in the library. This is the right tool for the later stages of an optimization campaign, when prior rounds or structural analysis have identified residues that clearly matter but where you do not know which amino acid change will work best. The section covers the mathematics of complete coverage (how many clones you need to ensure you have seen every amino acid), the degenerate codon schemes that encode all twenty amino acids efficiently, and strategies like CAST that extend saturation mutagenesis to multiple positions without combinatorial explosion.

Chemical mutagenesis shifts the scale entirely from single genes to whole genomes. EMS, MNNG, and related compounds damage DNA bases throughout the chromosome, making them the tools of choice for adaptive laboratory evolution and for phenotypes that involve many genes and regulatory elements. The section also covers mutator strains — bacteria engineered to replicate their DNA sloppily — and the remarkable PACE system, where chemical mutagenesis in a flowing culture enables hundreds of generations of evolution per day.

Recombination methods address the deepest limitation of mutagenesis: the inability to jump between distinct fitness peaks. Stemmer's DNA shuffling, StEP, SCHEMA-guided recombination, and virtual recombination via machine learning prediction all share the same underlying principle — combining beneficial mutations from separate lineages to produce combinations that sequential mutagenesis could never reach by single steps. These methods embody the same logic that makes sexual reproduction so powerful in natural populations: the ability to assemble novel combinations from existing diversity.

Understanding these four approaches as a toolkit — when to use each one, when to combine them, and what their limitations tell you about the shape of the fitness landscape you are navigating — is the foundation of intelligent directed evolution experimental design.
