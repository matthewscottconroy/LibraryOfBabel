Engineering biology requires making choices that nature never faced. Evolution solves problems slowly, iteratively, without foresight — accumulating small improvements over millions of years, guided by nothing but differential reproduction. Human engineers face a different set of constraints: you have a specific goal, a specific timeline, and a specific set of tools. The central tension in synthetic biology's engineering strategy is between rational design — specifying the desired change and implementing it precisely — and evolutionary approaches, where you generate enormous diversity and let selection do the work of finding what you want. Both strategies are powerful. Both have limits. The most sophisticated approaches combine them.

This unit covers the strategies used to engineer biological systems at multiple scales, from single nucleotides to entire genomes to the evolutionary dynamics of populations.

Chapter 3.3 covers genome editing — the toolkit for making precise, targeted changes to DNA sequences. CRISPR-Cas9 is the centerpiece: a programmable RNA-guided nuclease that can cut any genomic sequence flanked by a short motif, modified only by changing 20 nucleotides in a synthetic guide RNA. The chapter builds from molecular mechanism to clinical application, covering how Cas9 finds and cuts its target, how cells repair the resulting break (and how you can control which pathway they use), how guide RNAs are designed for efficiency and specificity, and how the toolkit has expanded to include base editors for chemical conversion of single nucleotides without breaks, prime editors for any small sequence change, and Cas9 variants that address limitations in delivery size and PAM accessibility. The chapter ends at the frontier: genome-scale CRISPR screens that interrogate the function of every gene simultaneously, and genome synthesis projects that design and build entire chromosomes from scratch. This is rational design operating at its most precise — knowing exactly what sequence change you want and having the molecular tools to make it.

Chapter 3.4 covers metabolic engineering — the redesign of cellular biochemistry to produce valuable chemicals, fuels, and pharmaceuticals. Here, the engineering challenge is not just making a precise genetic change but understanding how that change propagates through a complex network of enzymatic reactions with competing fluxes, feedback regulation, and thermodynamic constraints. The chapter covers the core objectives of metabolic engineering: maximizing product yield, titer, and productivity while maintaining cell viability; the tools for pathway design including flux balance analysis and genome-scale metabolic models; and the strategies for strain optimization that go beyond the initial design, including metabolic flux analysis, protein scaffolds for pathway channeling, and adaptive laboratory evolution. Key case studies — artemisinin, lycopene, succinate — illustrate how the rational design-build-test cycle works in practice, and a final section on fermentation and scale-up connects laboratory engineering to industrial application.

Chapter 3.5 covers directed evolution — the deliberate application of evolutionary principles to engineer proteins and nucleic acids with desired properties. Where rational design requires detailed structural and mechanistic knowledge to specify a beneficial mutation, directed evolution explores sequence space broadly and relies on selection to identify functional variants. The chapter covers the generation of diversity (random mutagenesis, recombination, synthetic libraries), the critical distinction between selection (growth-linked) and screening (individual assay), and how the two strategies differ in their throughput-sensitivity trade-off. A dedicated section on machine learning-guided directed evolution explains how predictive models trained on sequence-function data can focus experimental effort on the most promising sequence neighborhoods, dramatically accelerating evolution. The chapter closes with characterization methods for the evolved proteins and the conceptual lessons that apply when evolution encounters epistasis, fitness landscapes, and the combinatorial explosion of sequence space.

Together these three chapters span the full spectrum of biological engineering strategy: the precision of targeted genome editing, the systems-level complexity of metabolic network redesign, and the exploratory power of evolution. No single approach suffices for all problems — the cell you want to correct has a mutation that requires prime editing; the biosynthetic pathway you want to install requires both rational design and adaptive laboratory evolution; the enzyme you need doesn't exist in nature and must be evolved. Understanding when to apply each strategy, and how to combine them, is the core competency this unit develops.
