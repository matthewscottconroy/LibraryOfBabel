Translation is biology's most elaborate machine. A ribosome is not one protein — it is a complex of two large RNA molecules and over 50 proteins, all folded and assembled in a precise geometry, threading mRNA through an active site that catalyzes peptide bond formation at a rate of roughly 15 amino acids per second. Surrounding it are dozens of accessory factors — initiation factors that load the ribosome onto the message, elongation factors that deliver charged tRNAs and drive translocation, release factors that recognize stop codons, recycling factors that dismantle the complex afterward — each a small molecular machine in its own right. And powering all of it: 20 aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, each exquisitely specific for one amino acid, charging its cognate tRNA and feeding the system with activated substrates.

For decades, translating a gene in a test tube meant using a crude cell extract — a lysate containing all of this machinery along with thousands of other proteins whose presence was necessary, tolerated, or simply unavoidable. In 2001, Takuya Shimizu and colleagues in the Ueda lab changed that. They purified every one of the required components individually, combined them in defined proportions, and showed that protein synthesis — complete, faithful translation of an mRNA into a full-length protein — could be reconstituted from scratch with 36 characterized components and nothing else. The PURE system was born.

This section focuses exclusively on the PURE system: its complete component inventory, the functional role of each part, how the energy regeneration system keeps the reaction running, and what the system's performance characteristics look like in practice. The reason this system deserves its own dedicated treatment is not merely that it is technically impressive (though it is). It is that PURE is the only cell-free platform that gives you complete control over every molecule in the reaction. With a crude extract, you inherit the complexity of the cell — thousands of unknown proteins, variable from batch to batch, impossible to fully characterize. With PURE, you know exactly what is present. That knowledge is the prerequisite for every experiment that requires removing a specific component (to study its role), replacing it with an engineered variant (to expand the genetic code), or modeling the kinetics quantitatively (because the system is fully defined).

The single subsection in this section walks through the PURE system's component list in functional order: ribosomes, initiation factors, elongation factors, termination and recycling factors, aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, tRNAs, and the energy regeneration system. For each category, you will learn not just what the component is but why it is necessary — what specific biochemical problem it solves in the translation cycle. The section closes with a quantitative picture of PURE system performance and a discussion of the commercial variants available to researchers who want to use the system without purifying every component themselves.

The payoff for this level of mechanistic detail is practical. When you later encounter non-natural amino acid incorporation — where RF1 must be removed and an orthogonal aaRS/tRNA pair must be added — you will understand exactly why that works and what the expected yield consequences are. When you encounter quantitative models of translation kinetics, you will know which parameters to look up from the PURE system literature. And when you encounter debates about whether a given protein's expression in cell-free is limited by transcription, translation initiation, or elongation, you will have the molecular vocabulary to reason through the argument.
