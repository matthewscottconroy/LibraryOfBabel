There is a particular kind of error that sophisticated scientists are especially prone to: mistaking a tool's capabilities for the tool's limitations, and then forgetting to ask what the tool cannot do at all. Cell-free systems are powerful enough, and their advocates enthusiastic enough, that this error is easy to make. A researcher who has spent months becoming skilled at cell-free prototyping can develop a kind of tunnel vision — everything starts to look like a problem that cell-free can solve. This section is the corrective.

Understanding what cell-free systems cannot do is not a grudging appendix to the chapter. It is essential knowledge for using the technology well. Every time you design an experiment using cell-free, you are making an implicit claim about the applicability of the result: that what you observe in a test tube is informative about what happens inside a cell. That claim is sometimes correct, sometimes approximately correct, and sometimes simply wrong. Knowing which category you are in — before you run the experiment — is what distinguishes a cell-free practitioner from a cell-free enthusiast.

The two subsections here divide the problem into two distinct types of limitation. The first covers categorical limitations: things cell-free systems simply cannot do, regardless of how clever your protocol. Cells grow; cell-free does not. Biofilms form; cell-free cannot replicate this. Membrane receptor signaling cascades operate at the plasma membrane with all its lateral organization, asymmetry, and associated scaffolds; cell-free reactions have none of this. Eukaryotic gene regulation is controlled by chromatin state — nucleosome positioning, histone acetylation, DNA methylation — and cell-free transcription from naked plasmid DNA bypasses all of it. The decision table at the end of this subsection is worth committing to memory: a quick checklist for asking whether your question is even addressable in a cell-free format.

The second subsection addresses something subtler and in some ways more dangerous than categorical limitations: quantitative differences. Cell-free systems can perform the same reactions that cells perform — transcription, translation, protein-protein interaction — but the numbers are systematically different. Expression levels in cell-free are 100–1000 times higher than in vivo. Kinetics are 4–10 times faster, because growth dilution is absent. Resource competition operates through different mechanisms and different quantitative parameters. These differences do not mean cell-free data is useless for predicting in vivo behavior — it means you need a calibration model that accounts for the systematic offsets. Building that calibration model is not difficult; understanding why it is necessary requires the mechanistic reasoning covered in this subsection.

Together, these two subsections give you the complete picture of where cell-free sits in the experimental toolkit: indispensable for rapid screening and quantitative part characterization, but always a step removed from the cellular context that ultimately determines whether an engineered design will function as intended.
