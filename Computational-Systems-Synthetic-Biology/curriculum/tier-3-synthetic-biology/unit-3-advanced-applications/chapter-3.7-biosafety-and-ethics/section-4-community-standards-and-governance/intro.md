Regulations are necessary but not sufficient. A field governed only by rules and enforcement is a field that will always be one step behind its own capabilities — rules take years to develop, technologies emerge faster than that. What actually shapes how researchers behave, day to day and decision by decision, is culture: the shared norms, expectations, and values that determine what counts as acceptable practice. Synthetic biology has been remarkably deliberate about building that culture, and this section is about the institutions that have done the building.

iGEM is the most important of them. What began as five MIT students experimenting with biological oscillators in 2003 grew into the global synthetic biology competition and community of practice that has trained more synthetic biologists than any other initiative in the field's history. iGEM's technical legacy — the BioBrick standard, the Registry of Standard Biological Parts, the InterLab measurement study — is well documented. But its cultural legacy may matter more: the requirement that every team submit a Safety Form before competing has introduced biosafety thinking to tens of thousands of student researchers at the stage in their careers when professional habits are first being formed.

Outside institutions, DIY biology presents a different kind of challenge. Community labs like Genspace in Brooklyn and BioCurious in San Jose have genuine claims to legitimacy — they democratize access to tools that were previously restricted to well-funded institutions, they train diverse communities, and they have contributed to open-source biology tools and citizen science. But they operate without the institutional oversight (IBC, BSO, IRB) that academic labs rely on for safety review. The question of how to extend appropriate oversight to community biology without destroying what makes it valuable is genuinely unsettled, and this section treats it honestly.

Beyond any single community or institution, synthetic biology faces governance challenges that no national framework can fully address. Gene drives that spread across borders, engineered organisms shipped between countries, sequence data accessed globally from public databases — these all require international coordination. The Biological Weapons Convention, the Cartagena Protocol, the Nagoya Protocol on benefit sharing, and the WHO International Health Regulations each address part of the picture while leaving significant gaps. Understanding where those gaps are — and why filling them is so difficult — is part of understanding the real governance environment synthetic biology operates in.
