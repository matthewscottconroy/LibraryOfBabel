There are questions about synthetic biology that the regulatory frameworks in the previous sections cannot answer. Whether a gene therapy that costs $2.1 million per dose represents a triumph of biotechnology or a failure of equity is not a biosafety question. Whether a gene drive that could eliminate malaria should be released into wild mosquito populations in countries that did not design or fund the technology is not a question that any single regulatory agency has authority over. Whether the creation of a synthetic organism from digital sequence data raises moral obligations toward that organism is not covered by any existing legal framework. These are ethical questions — and they are unavoidable.

This section addresses four of them. The equity and access question asks who benefits from synthetic biology and who bears its risks, and examines the intellectual property landscape that determines those distributions. The $475,000 CAR-T cell therapy and the $2.1 million gene therapy for spinal muscular atrophy are presented not as aberrations but as features of a development system that prioritizes returns on investment; the question is whether different choices — different IP strategies, different licensing arrangements, different research priority-setting — could produce a synthetic biology that distributes its benefits more equitably.

The environmental release question focuses particularly on gene drives — the most extreme case of irreversible environmental modification currently on the table. A gene drive is designed to spread through wild populations by overriding Mendelian inheritance. Once released into a geographically unconstrained species, it cannot be recalled. The ethical framework for such decisions must account not just for probable outcomes but for the full distribution of possible outcomes, including low-probability catastrophic ones. The precautionary and proactionary principles offer competing guidance; neither is obviously correct.

The moral status question asks whether the organisms we create have moral claims on us — and whether synthetic origin changes the answer. For bacteria, the answer under most ethical frameworks is no. But as synthetic biology moves toward more complex organisms, chimeric human-animal combinations, and designed cognitive properties, the question becomes genuinely difficult, and this section engages it seriously rather than dismissing it.

Finally, the responsible innovation framework brings all of these threads together: what does it mean to develop synthetic biology in a way that is not just safe but genuinely good? The AREA framework — anticipation, reflexivity, inclusion, responsiveness — provides practical tools for thinking about this at the level of an individual laboratory, and the He Jiankui case provides the most instructive available example of what happens when all four principles are violated simultaneously.
