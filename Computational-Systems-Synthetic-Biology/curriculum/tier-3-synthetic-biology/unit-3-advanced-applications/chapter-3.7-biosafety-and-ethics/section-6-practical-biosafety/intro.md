Most of the biosafety framework you have encountered so far in this chapter was developed with the experimental biologist in mind: the researcher handling live cultures, generating aerosols, working with clinical isolates, injecting animals. The default assumption was that biosafety risks were physical — organisms and biological materials that needed to be kept inside containment. But computational biologists in synthetic biology create a category of risk that the original frameworks simply did not anticipate: the risk that computational outputs — sequence designs, fitness prediction models, structure-function analyses — are themselves biosecurity-relevant, even when no organism is ever handled.

This section exists because your biosafety responsibilities as a computational synthetic biologist are real, but they are not the same as those of your wet-lab colleagues, and the difference matters for how you practice.

The core concept is the "sequence-first problem": in the post-genomics era, biological information and biological materials are not as separate as traditional biosafety frameworks assumed. A published genome sequence can be synthesized. A machine learning model that predicts which mutations make a pathogen more transmissible can be misused to guide gain-of-function engineering. A gene synthesis screening algorithm, described in sufficient detail, can be reverse-engineered to understand how to evade it. These are not hypothetical risks — they are the specific biosecurity concerns that have emerged as computational biology has become more powerful.

The practical content covers what data you can safely access, what computational work carries dual-use implications and how to handle it, what security practices apply to your working environment, and when you should consult your institution's biosafety officer even as a computational researcher. It also situates you within the bigger picture: computational biologists are not just potential sources of biosecurity risk but among the most important contributors to biosecurity solutions — through sequence screening algorithms, pandemic surveillance systems, and structural tools that accelerate vaccine design. The goal is not to make you anxious about your work. It is to make you the kind of researcher who thinks carefully about both sides of that ledger.
