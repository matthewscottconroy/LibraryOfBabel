In February 1975, roughly 140 scientists from around the world gathered at the Asilomar Conference Center on California's Monterey Peninsula. They were molecular biologists — among the best in the world — and they had come to ask a question that scientists almost never ask publicly: should we stop doing what we are doing?

Paul Berg had been planning an experiment. He wanted to insert DNA from a tumor-causing monkey virus (SV40) into a strain of E. coli, creating the first recombinant organism capable of colonizing the human gut while also carrying viral oncogenes. His colleagues pulled him aside before he could run it. The experiment was not obviously dangerous — but it was not obviously safe either. No one knew. And that uncertainty, applied to an organism that could replicate indefinitely inside the human body, was enough to give the field pause. Berg canceled the experiment. He called for a voluntary moratorium. And then the molecular biology community did something that has no real parallel in the history of science: it organized a conference to decide, collectively, whether and how to proceed.

The result of Asilomar was not a permanent ban. It was a framework — a set of principles for proportioning the level of physical containment to the potential hazard of the research, implemented through a standing advisory committee and a set of guidelines that would be revised as evidence accumulated. Those NIH Guidelines, first published in 1976, have governed recombinant DNA research ever since. The Asilomar process is now invoked as a model every time a new biological technology raises similar questions. It was invoked when the poliovirus was synthesized from scratch in 2002. It was invoked during the H5N1 gain-of-function controversy in 2011. It was invoked, too late, after He Jiankui announced the birth of CRISPR-edited babies in 2018.

The Asilomar model is not perfect — it was a gathering of insiders, largely excluding the public, making decisions about technologies with global consequences. But what it got right was the fundamental insight: biosafety and ethics are not afterthoughts to be addressed once a technology has matured. They are integral to the science. The researchers who paused in 1975 were not being timid or anti-scientific. They were being thorough. They were extending their scientific rigor to the question of consequences — asking not just "can we do this?" but "should we do this, and if so, how?"

Consider, as a contrasting case, the dual-use dilemma raised by the reconstruction of the 1918 influenza pandemic virus. In 2005, Jeffery Tumpey's team at the CDC reconstructed the virus from sequences recovered from the preserved lung tissue of victims, working in a BSL-3 laboratory with full regulatory approval. The science was extraordinary: they identified the genetic features that made the 1918 strain so deadly. That knowledge informs pandemic preparedness to this day. It also, unavoidably, provided a blueprint for reconstructing one of the deadliest pathogens of the 20th century. The sequence is in GenBank. Anyone with access to a gene synthesis company and appropriate institutional registration can, in principle, start from that sequence. The dual-use dilemma cannot be resolved — it can only be governed. And governing it requires researchers who understand both the science and the ethics.

This chapter is about that governance. It covers the practical infrastructure of biosafety — the four biosafety levels and their containment requirements, the NIH Guidelines that codify which experiments require oversight and which are exempt, the Institutional Biosafety Committees that translate federal policy into laboratory practice, and the risk assessment framework that allows researchers to evaluate novel engineered organisms for which no pre-existing classification exists. It covers biosecurity — the Select Agent Program that regulates possession of the most dangerous biological agents, the dual-use research of concern (DURC) policy that identifies experiments requiring enhanced oversight, the gene synthesis screening systems that prevent dangerous sequences from being ordered through commercial channels, and the biocontainment strategies that can prevent engineered organisms from surviving outside their intended environments. It covers community governance and standards — the role of iGEM in building a global culture of responsible synthetic biology practice, the DIY biology movement and its distinct biosafety challenges, and the patchwork of international treaties and agreements that attempt to manage synthetic biology risks across national borders. And it covers ethics in the deepest sense — equity and access questions about who benefits from and who bears the risks of synthetic biology, the irreversibility of environmental release decisions and the governance frameworks for gene drives, the philosophical questions about moral status raised by the creation of synthetic organisms, and the responsible innovation framework that asks not just whether research is safe but whether its development process is inclusive, reflexive, and responsive to the communities it will affect.

The chapter ends with a section specifically for you — the computational biologist — because the biosafety landscape looks different when your primary tool is a laptop rather than a pipette, and the specific responsibilities that follow from working with biological sequences, predictive models, and synthesis-enabling algorithms deserve their own treatment.

None of this material is peripheral to the practice of synthetic biology. It is the framework within which responsible synthetic biology becomes possible at all.
