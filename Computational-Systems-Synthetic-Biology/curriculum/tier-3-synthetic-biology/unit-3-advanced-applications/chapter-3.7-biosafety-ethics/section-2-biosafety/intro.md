A laboratory is not just a workspace — it is a contained environment, deliberately designed to keep the organisms inside it from reaching the world outside. This containment is not invisible infrastructure that you can take for granted. It is a set of deliberate choices made in the design of the building, the protocols you follow, the equipment you use, and the way you think about the things you are growing. The biosafety section of this chapter is about understanding those choices and why they are made the way they are.

The centerpiece is the biosafety level system — four levels of ascending containment requirements, matched to the potential hazard of the organisms being studied. BSL-1 is for organisms that pose no meaningful risk to healthy adults: you can work with E. coli K-12 on an open bench. BSL-4 is for agents like Ebola and Marburg, for which there is no vaccine or treatment and no room for error: researchers wear full-body pressurized suits and shower out of the facility before leaving. The progression between these levels is not arbitrary — it reflects decades of epidemiological data on laboratory-acquired infections and the specific features (aerosol transmission, case fatality rate, treatment availability, facility design) that determine how hazardous a particular organism is in a laboratory context.

For synthetic biologists, the BSL system has an important subtlety: engineered organisms do not come with pre-assigned BSL designations. When you insert a toxin gene into E. coli K-12, you create something new that must be evaluated on its own merits. That evaluation process — the four-factor risk assessment framework — is the subject of its own subsection. Understanding risk assessment is one of the most practically valuable things you will take away from this chapter.

Connecting the BSL system and risk assessment to the laboratory reality are two institutional structures: the NIH Guidelines, which define which experiments require oversight and which are exempt, and the Institutional Biosafety Committee, which implements those guidelines at your institution. The IBC is not a bureaucratic obstacle — it is the body that reviews your risk assessment, provides authoritative guidance when you are uncertain, and ensures that the containment level you have chosen is appropriate for what you are doing. The Biological Safety Officer who staffs the IBC is one of the most useful people in any research institution. This section introduces you to both and explains how to work with them effectively.
