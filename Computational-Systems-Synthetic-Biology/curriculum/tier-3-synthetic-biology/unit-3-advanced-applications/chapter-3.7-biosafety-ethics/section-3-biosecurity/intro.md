Biosafety is about protecting researchers from the organisms they study. Biosecurity is about protecting the world from the possibility that biological research could be misused. The distinction matters: biosafety assumes good intentions and focuses on accidents and exposures. Biosecurity must also reckon with deliberate misuse — with the possibility that the same techniques that make synthetic biology powerful could be turned toward harm.

The central challenge of biosecurity in synthetic biology is that it cannot be solved by restricting access to physical materials alone. Before the genomics era, controlling access to dangerous pathogens was the primary biosecurity tool: if you needed the actual organism to study it, then controlling the organism controlled the capability. The synthesis of poliovirus from sequence data in 2002 ended that logic permanently. Today, the primary biosecurity concern is information — sequence data that can be converted directly into physical biological material through commercial gene synthesis. Governing that conversion is the central problem this section addresses.

Four subsections cover the landscape. The dual-use research of concern (DURC) policy identifies the specific categories of experiments — enhancing pathogen transmissibility, broadening host range, reconstituting extinct pathogens — that require enhanced oversight because their results could be misused. The Select Agent Program is federal law: it governs physical possession of the most dangerous agents and toxins, with criminal penalties for violations. Gene synthesis screening addresses the information-to-material conversion problem, describing how synthesis companies screen orders against databases of dangerous sequences and what practitioners need to know about that process. And biocontainment strategies cover the engineering solutions — auxotrophic dependence, kill switches, semantic containment through genome recoding — that make it possible to build organisms that die when they leave their intended environment.

Together, these four subsections define the biosecurity landscape that synthetic biologists work within. Understanding this landscape is not optional: if your research involves pathogen sequences, toxin genes, novel organisms with enhanced properties, or gene drives with potential for population-level effects, you are working in territory that biosecurity policy was specifically designed to address. Knowing the rules — and knowing why they exist — is part of working in this field responsibly.
