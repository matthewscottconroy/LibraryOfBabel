# International Governance of Synthetic Biology

Here is a governance problem with no clean solution: the most advanced synthetic biology capabilities are concentrated in a handful of countries, but the risks those capabilities create — engineered pandemics, gene-drive-modified ecosystems, weaponizable biological agents — do not respect national borders. A gene drive released in West Africa to suppress malaria-transmitting mosquitoes could spread to North Africa and Southern Europe within years, without any authorization from the affected populations. A laboratory accident with an engineered pathogen in one country becomes everyone's problem within weeks, as SARS-CoV-2 demonstrated. Yet the regulatory frameworks governing synthetic biology are almost entirely national in character — the NIH Guidelines in the U.S., the GMO directives in the EU, each country with its own rules, its own oversight bodies, and its own threshold for what risks are acceptable. The gap between the global scale of synthetic biology's potential consequences and the national scale of its governance is one of the central problems the field must solve.

Synthetic biology is a global enterprise: research occurs across dozens of countries, gene synthesis orders cross international borders daily, and engineered organisms — whether released intentionally or accidentally — do not respect national boundaries. The governance frameworks developed within individual nations (the NIH Guidelines in the U.S., the GMO directives in the EU) are insufficient on their own because they create regulatory fragmentation: research prohibited in one country can often be conducted in another with less restrictive rules, and products developed under different oversight frameworks may interact unpredictably in international trade and ecology.

## The Biological Weapons Convention (BWC)

The **Biological Weapons Convention** (BWC, formally the *Convention on the Prohibition of the Development, Production and Stockpiling of Bacteriological (Biological) and Toxin Weapons and on their Destruction*) is the foundational international treaty governing biological weapons. Opened for signature in 1972, entered into force 1975. As of 2024, 183 states are party to the convention.

**Key provisions**:
- Article I: prohibits development, production, or stockpiling of biological agents "of types and quantities that have no justification for prophylactic, protective, or other peaceful purposes"
- Article II: requires destruction of existing biological weapons stockpiles
- Article III: prohibits transfer of biological weapons or related technology to any state

**Critical weakness**: the BWC has **no verification mechanism**. There is no international inspections regime (unlike the Chemical Weapons Convention, which has the OPCW). States parties are trusted to self-report compliance. The former Soviet Union maintained the largest offensive biological weapons program in history (the **Biopreparat** program) in explicit violation of the BWC from 1975 until the program was disclosed after the Soviet collapse — demonstrating the limitation of a treaty without verification.

**Relevance to synthetic biology**: Article I of the BWC covers biological agents with "no justification for peaceful purposes" — which includes, in principle, laboratory-synthesized agents engineered for enhanced virulence or transmissibility. Whether current DURC research on pathogen enhancement falls within the scope of the BWC is a matter of ongoing legal debate.

**BWC meetings**: the BWC meets for annual Meetings of Experts and quinquennial Review Conferences. Synthetic biology was explicitly addressed as an emerging technology of concern in the BWC Review Conferences of 2016 and 2022, with discussions of whether the convention's provisions adequately cover synthetic nucleic acid molecules, de novo organism construction, and gene drives.

## The Cartagena Protocol on Biosafety

The **Cartagena Protocol on Biosafety** is a supplementary agreement to the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD), in force since 2003. As of 2024, 173 parties (not including the U.S., which has not ratified the CBD).

**Scope**: regulates the transboundary movement, transit, handling, and use of **Living Modified Organisms (LMOs)** — organisms modified through modern biotechnology that may have adverse effects on biodiversity.

**Key provisions**:
- **Advance Informed Agreement (AIA)**: prior to the first intentional transboundary movement of an LMO intended for deliberate release into the environment, the exporting party must notify the importing country and receive consent.
- **Biosafety Clearing-House**: a mandatory database where countries post information about LMOs subject to AIA procedures.
- **Precautionary principle**: importing countries may refuse entry of an LMO based on scientific uncertainty, even without proof of harm (Article 10.6).

**Relevance to synthetic biology**: the Cartagena Protocol's definition of LMOs — organisms modified through biotechnology "in a way that does not occur naturally through mating and/or natural recombination" — directly applies to engineered bacteria, algae, yeast, and plants used in synthetic biology applications. Export of bioremediation organisms, engineered crops, or environmental sensing organisms across borders triggers Cartagena Protocol obligations.

**The Nagoya-Kuala Lumpur Supplementary Protocol (2010)**: extends the Cartagena Protocol to address liability and redress for damage from LMOs — assigning financial responsibility for transboundary damage caused by LMO release.

## The Nagoya Protocol on Access and Benefit Sharing

The **Nagoya Protocol on Access to Genetic Resources and the Fair and Equitable Sharing of Benefits** (2014) is a CBD supplementary agreement addressing a fundamental equity issue: many high-value biological resources are found in biodiversity-rich developing countries, but their value is largely captured by companies and researchers in wealthy countries.

**Provisions relevant to synthetic biology**:
- Access to genetic resources (including microorganism samples) from provider countries requires **prior informed consent (PIC)** and **mutually agreed terms (MAT)** — typically a benefit-sharing agreement
- **Digital sequence information (DSI)**: a contentious question — whether the Nagoya Protocol applies to sequence data uploaded to public databases, and whether synthetic biologists who construct organisms using published sequences from biodiversity-rich countries owe benefit-sharing obligations. This is unresolved as of 2025.

**Practical implications**: researchers accessing microbial samples from international collections or biodiversity hotspots, or collaborating with institutions in countries that have implemented Nagoya Protocol access regulations, must ensure their access agreements comply with Nagoya requirements.

## The WHO International Health Regulations

The **International Health Regulations (IHR, 2005)** establish a framework for global health security — including requirements for nations to report public health emergencies of international concern (PHEICs) within 24 hours of detection. The COVID-19 pandemic exposed significant gaps in IHR implementation.

**Relevance to synthetic biology**: if a laboratory accident involving an engineered organism caused a disease outbreak that met PHEIC criteria, the IHR would require notification and response coordination. The IHR also governs international transfer of pathological specimens — relevant for research collaborations involving biological materials from pathogen sources.

## Regional Governance: The European Union

The EU's GMO regulatory framework is the most comprehensive regional governance system for synthetic biology-relevant organisms:

**Directive 2001/18/EC** (deliberate release of GMOs into the environment): requires pre-market authorization, mandatory environmental risk assessment, public consultation, and post-market monitoring. This directive has made commercial release of engineered organisms in the EU significantly more difficult than in the U.S.

**Directive 2009/41/EC** (contained use of GMOs): regulates GMO research in contained laboratory settings. More permissive than the deliberate release directive; analogous to the NIH Guidelines.

**New Genomic Techniques (NGT) regulation (2023 proposal)**: the EU has proposed a distinction between "NGT1" plants (modifications that could occur naturally or through conventional breeding) and "NGT2" plants (more complex modifications), with NGT1 subject to minimal regulation and NGT2 subject to existing GMO rules. This is controversial — NGT1 is essentially a partial exemption for precise CRISPR edits.

## OECD and Harmonization Efforts

The **Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD)** has published non-binding guidance on biosafety that member countries use to harmonize national policies:

- **OECD Guidance on Best Practices for the Safe Use of Industrial Biotechnology**: technical guidance for contained industrial use of engineered organisms
- **OECD Good Developmental Practices for Synthetic Biology**: principles for responsible development and governance of synthetic biology applications
- **Blue Biotechnology safety series**: guidance for marine environments

OECD guidance does not have treaty force but influences national regulations in member states through a "soft law" mechanism.

## Governance Gaps and Emerging Issues

**Gene drives**: no international governance framework specifically addresses gene drives. The CBD has discussed gene drives but has not agreed on binding rules. A 2020 CBD decision recommended assessment of risks before field releases but stopped short of a moratorium.

**De-extinction**: reconstructing extinct species using ancient DNA and CRISPR is not explicitly covered by any international framework. The woolly mammoth revival project (Colossal Biosciences) would release engineered mammoth-elephant hybrids into the Arctic — with no clear governance pathway.

**AI-designed organisms**: computationally designed proteins and organisms with no natural sequence homologs could potentially evade sequence-based screening and regulatory categories based on biological identity.

**Unilateral action problem**: a country or actor willing to violate international norms can develop and deploy biological agents or release engineered organisms regardless of treaties. Governance depends on verification, enforcement, and norm internalization — all of which are incomplete.

## Why This Matters

International governance of synthetic biology is where scientific capabilities, national interests, ecological risks, and equity concerns converge most visibly. For researchers, international governance affects practical decisions: Can I collaborate with a researcher in country X? Can I export this engineered organism to a field site? Do I owe benefit-sharing obligations for using microbial sequences from a biodiversity-rich country? More broadly, the international governance frameworks for synthetic biology will determine whether the field's ecological and public health risks are managed effectively or whether the regulatory patchwork allows high-risk applications to proceed in the least-regulated jurisdiction. Building robust international governance — with verification mechanisms, equity provisions, and coverage of emerging capabilities — is one of the central policy challenges of the synthetic biology era.
