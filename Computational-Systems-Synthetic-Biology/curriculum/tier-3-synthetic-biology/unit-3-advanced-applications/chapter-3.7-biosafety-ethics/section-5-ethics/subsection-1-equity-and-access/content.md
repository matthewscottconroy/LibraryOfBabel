# Equity and Access in Synthetic Biology

In 2019, the FDA approved Zolgensma — a gene therapy for spinal muscular atrophy in children, a devastating neurodegenerative condition that without treatment is fatal in infancy. The therapy worked: a single injection could halt disease progression and allow children who would have died before age two to survive and develop. The price was $2.1 million per dose. This made Zolgensma, for a brief moment, the most expensive drug in history. The manufacturer's justification was standard: the price reflected the cost of development and the value delivered over the patient's lifetime. The reality was that in the 193 countries with per-capita GDPs below $12,000, the drug was, for most families, effectively non-existent. The same biotechnology that represented a triumph of synthetic biology and molecular medicine was simultaneously, for the majority of the world's population, a demonstration of who the tools of synthetic biology are built for.

The transformative potential of synthetic biology — affordable medicines, improved agricultural yields, biosensors for environmental monitoring, cell therapies for genetic diseases — is unevenly distributed. The same structural inequities that shape access to healthcare, education, and technology broadly also shape who benefits from synthetic biology and who bears its risks. Addressing equity in synthetic biology is not peripheral to the field — it is a matter of scientific integrity, social responsibility, and the long-term sustainability of the field's social license.

## Defining Equity in the Context of Synthetic Biology

**Equity** is distinct from **equality**: equality means everyone receives the same resources; equity means everyone receives what they need to achieve comparable outcomes, accounting for historical disadvantages and structural barriers. In synthetic biology, equity operates at multiple levels:

**Access to therapeutic applications**: gene therapies, CRISPR-corrected cell transplants, and engineered biologics are among the most expensive medical treatments ever developed. Tisagenlecleucel (CAR-T therapy) costs ~$475,000 per treatment; zolgensma (gene therapy for spinal muscular atrophy) costs $2.1 million. These are priced in part to recoup development costs and provide returns to investors — but they are effectively inaccessible to most of the world's population.

**Access to agricultural benefits**: engineered crops (Bt cotton, Roundup Ready soybean) have been transformative for productivity — but access depends on IP licensing terms. Terminator seeds and proprietary herbicide packages can impose ongoing costs on farmers that undermine the net benefit of higher yields, particularly for small-scale farmers in developing countries.

**Access to diagnostic tools**: synthetic biology-enabled diagnostics (SHERLOCK-based COVID-19 tests, toehold switch Zika diagnostics) are potentially low-cost and deployable in resource-limited settings — but only if the IP is accessible. Proprietary lock-in of diagnostic technologies by private companies has been a recurring problem in global health (HIV diagnostics, HCV tests).

**Participation in research**: who gets to do synthetic biology research? Research capacity is heavily concentrated in wealthy countries. As of 2020, the U.S., EU, and China account for >80% of synthetic biology publications and >90% of venture capital investment. This geographic concentration shapes which problems are prioritized (diseases of affluent populations, agricultural problems of large-scale commercial farming) and which are neglected.

## The IP Landscape and Its Equity Implications

Intellectual property is the central mechanism by which synthetic biology is financed — and a central barrier to equitable access.

**The CRISPR patent situation**: as of 2025, CRISPR-Cas9 IP is primarily held by two consortia:
- Broad Institute (MIT/Harvard): patent rights for CRISPR in eukaryotic cells
- UC Berkeley (Doudna/Charpentier): patent rights for the biochemical CRISPR-Cas9 system

Both have granted licenses — but primarily to commercial entities in wealthy countries. Access in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) depends on voluntary licensing agreements or government compulsory licensing under TRIPS provisions. The Gates Foundation negotiated humanitarian-use licenses for CRISPR applications in agriculture for developing countries, but therapeutic applications remain largely inaccessible.

**Open access alternatives**: several initiatives attempt to make synthetic biology tools freely accessible:
- **Addgene**: a non-profit plasmid repository that distributes research materials for academic use at cost (~$75–100 per plasmid); has distributed >3 million samples to researchers in >100 countries
- **iGEM Registry**: openly licensed biological parts for non-commercial use
- **OpenCRISPR**: community efforts to develop CRISPR tools not encumbered by the major patent families (e.g., CasΦ from bacteriophages, which may not be covered by existing patents)
- **BIOS (Biological Innovation for Open Society)**: licensing framework adapted from open-source software licensing for biological innovation

## Neglected Tropical Diseases and the Priority Gap

A striking example of the equity deficit: **neglected tropical diseases (NTDs)** affect over 1 billion people globally, primarily in Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and Latin America. Diseases like schistosomiasis, trypanosomiasis (sleeping sickness), leishmaniasis, Chagas disease, and lymphatic filariasis cause enormous morbidity but affect populations with limited purchasing power.

Synthetic biology has the technical capability to address NTDs:
- Engineered yeast producing cheap artemisinin analogs for malaria (the artemisinic acid work has direct NTD extensions)
- Cell-free toehold switch diagnostics deployable without cold chains
- CRISPR-based gene drives targeting Anopheles mosquitoes for malaria elimination

But the market incentives are weak: pharmaceutical companies cannot recover development costs from populations without purchasing power. The result: research investment in NTDs is a tiny fraction of investment in diseases of wealthy countries.

**Addressing the priority gap**:
- **CEPI (Coalition for Epidemic Preparedness Innovations)**: public-private partnership that funds vaccine development for epidemic-prone diseases with insufficient market incentives
- **Medicines for Malaria Venture (MMV)**: product development partnership for antimalarials
- **DNDi (Drugs for Neglected Diseases initiative)**: develops treatments for NTDs through non-profit models
- **Push-pull mechanisms**: government-funded advanced market commitments (AMCs) guarantee a market for successful products, reducing commercial risk

## Global Research Capacity and Participation

Equity in synthetic biology also requires equity in who does the research:

**Brain drain**: talented researchers from LMICs often train in high-income countries and do not return, concentrating expertise where it is already concentrated. This is partly driven by differential compensation and research infrastructure.

**Capacity building initiatives**:
- **H3Africa** (Human Heredity and Health in Africa): NIH/Wellcome Trust initiative to build genomics research capacity in African institutions
- **iGEM as a global platform**: iGEM explicitly includes teams from LMICs and has been a training ground for researchers who go on to build research capacity in their home countries. Teams from sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and Latin America have won medals and built lasting programs.
- **Virtual labs and remote training**: the COVID-19 pandemic accelerated development of virtual laboratory training tools that can partially address the resource gap in experimental training

**Participatory research design**: increasingly, research ethics frameworks require that communities affected by research participate in its design and governance — not just as research subjects, but as collaborators. For synthetic biology research with direct local impacts (gene drives targeting local mosquito populations, agricultural GMOs for local crops), this means meaningful engagement with local communities before research begins.

## Responsible Licensing and the Access-by-Design Principle

An emerging framework in synthetic biology ethics is **access by design**: incorporating equitable access into the IP and licensing strategy from the earliest stages of development, rather than trying to retrofit access agreements after commercial development is underway.

Practical approaches:
- **Two-tier pricing**: lower prices for LMIC markets from launch (as practiced by some pharmaceutical companies for HIV antiretrovirals after the 2001 Doha Declaration)
- **Humanitarian licenses**: free access for non-commercial, non-profit applications in defined LMICs (as negotiated by the Gates Foundation for agricultural CRISPR applications)
- **Patent pools**: centralized patent portfolios licensed collectively to generic manufacturers (as implemented by the Medicines Patent Pool for HIV, HCV, and COVID-19 therapeutics)
- **Open-source development**: projects deliberately conducted without IP capture, making outputs freely available (as with some open-source CRISPR tools and Addgene-distributed constructs)

## Why This Matters

The history of technology repeatedly shows that transformative tools amplify existing inequalities as often as they reduce them. Synthetic biology has the potential to produce treatments for cancers and genetic diseases that are beyond the reach of most of the world, diagnostic tools that are locked behind IP barriers that prevent their use in resource-limited settings, and agricultural innovations that concentrate economic benefit among large agribusinesses while imposing costs on small farmers. This is not inevitable — it is a consequence of choices about IP, research priorities, and who has a voice in governance. Researchers who understand the equity implications of their work, and who actively participate in designing more equitable access frameworks, contribute to a version of synthetic biology that delivers its benefits broadly rather than narrowly. This is not altruism — it is what sustainable, socially legitimate science looks like.
