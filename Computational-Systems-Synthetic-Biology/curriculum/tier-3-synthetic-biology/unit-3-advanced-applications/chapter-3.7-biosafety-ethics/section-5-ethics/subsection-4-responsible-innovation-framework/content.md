# Responsible Innovation Framework for Synthetic Biology

In November 2018, He Jiankui stood up at a gene editing summit in Hong Kong and announced that he had used CRISPR to edit the genomes of twin girls who had already been born. He had targeted the CCR5 gene, which encodes a co-receptor for HIV entry into cells, hoping to confer HIV resistance. The room was silent. Then came the questions — from David Baltimore, from Jennifer Doudna, from the assembled community of researchers who had spent years building the scientific and ethical case for careful, consensual, reviewed development of human germline editing. The questions were not about the technique. They were about everything He Jiankui had skipped: the scientific consensus that said this was premature, the peer review that had been bypassed, the consent process for the parents that had been designed to obscure rather than clarify, the governance frameworks that had been deliberately circumvented. Within a year, He had been convicted by a Chinese court of illegal medical practice and sentenced to three years in prison. His case has become the most studied example in synthetic biology of what responsible innovation is not.

**Responsible innovation** is a governance concept that asks not just whether a technology is safe and effective, but whether the process of developing it is responsive to societal values, inclusive of diverse stakeholders, reflexive about its own assumptions, and anticipatory of future consequences. It emerged from science and technology studies and policy research as an alternative to purely regulatory approaches — recognizing that regulations are reactive (addressing harms after they occur), while responsible innovation aims to be proactive (shaping development before harms become manifest).

## The AREA Framework

The most influential responsible innovation framework is the **AREA framework** (Responsible Research and Innovation in Science and Innovation Policy, Stilgoe, Owen, Macnaghten, 2013):

### Anticipation

**Definition**: systematic thinking about what kinds of futures might emerge from this technology — including futures not currently visible and ones we would not choose.

**In synthetic biology**:
- What happens if artemisinic acid production in engineered yeast succeeds commercially? (Answer: farmer livelihoods in regions producing natural artemisinin may be disrupted)
- What happens if gene drives for malaria control are released? (Answer: mosquito population dynamics change in ways we cannot fully predict; possibly predators that depend on mosquitoes are affected)
- What happens if CRISPR human germline editing becomes routine? (Answer: genetic stratification of society; new equity issues around access to genetic enhancement)

Anticipation does not require certainty — it requires structured thinking about a range of plausible futures, including negative ones. Tools include scenario planning, futures analysis, and expert elicitation.

### Reflexivity

**Definition**: the capacity to recognize and question one's own assumptions, values, and blind spots in research.

**In synthetic biology**:
- Are our "biosafety" risk assessments focused on the risks that trained laboratory scientists find easy to measure (individual LAI probability), at the expense of risks that are harder to quantify (ecological disruption, long-term evolution of engineered organisms)?
- Are our "benefit" analyses narrowly focused on scientific or economic metrics, at the expense of distributional concerns (who benefits? who bears risks)?
- Is our definition of "success" for a project shaped by our own cultural context in ways that may not apply to other contexts?

Reflexivity requires researchers to actively seek perspectives different from their own — from ethicists, social scientists, community representatives, and people from different cultural and economic backgrounds.

### Inclusion (Engagement)

**Definition**: broadening participation in research and innovation decisions to include those who will be affected by the technology.

**Weak inclusion**: informing the public about technology decisions already made (public communication, risk communication).

**Strong inclusion**: involving affected communities in making technology decisions — through deliberative processes, participatory design, or co-governance arrangements.

**In synthetic biology**:
- Community engagement for gene drive releases: the Target Malaria project's multi-year engagement process with communities in West Africa is an example of strong inclusion
- Patient involvement in gene therapy development: patient advocacy groups now participate in FDA advisory committee meetings and in study design for clinical trials
- Indigenous community consultation for organisms with cultural significance (e.g., gene drives targeting invasive species that are culturally significant to indigenous communities)

The challenge is that strong inclusion is time-consuming, expensive, and may slow innovation. Against this, failing to include affected communities undermines legitimacy — resulting in stronger backlash when harms occur and slower eventual adoption even if the technology is beneficial.

### Responsiveness

**Definition**: the capacity to change the direction of research in response to new information, values, or stakeholder input.

**In synthetic biology**:
- The Fouchier and Kawaoka H5N1 experiments were responded to by the NIH with the P3CO framework — a direct responsiveness to community and policy concerns
- The 2018 He Jiankui case prompted rapid responses from scientific societies (condemning the experiment), from China's government (new regulations on clinical CRISPR use), and from international bodies (a call for a global moratorium on heritable genome editing)
- The COVID-19 pandemic prompted rapid changes in biosafety risk assessment for SARS-CoV-2 research — demonstrating that risk assessments can and must be updated as evidence evolves

Responsiveness requires governance structures that can actually respond — not locked-in regulatory frameworks that take decades to update.

## The He Jiankui Case as a Responsible Innovation Failure

The 2018 CRISPR babies case is the most prominent example of responsible innovation failure in synthetic biology. It violated every AREA principle:

**Anticipation failed**: He did not adequately consider the range of futures enabled by his action — including the prospect of mosaic editing (the embryos had different edit patterns in different cells), the medical risks from off-target editing, and the social consequences of establishing heritable editing as a clinical practice without appropriate consent or oversight.

**Reflexivity failed**: He was apparently not aware of (or chose to ignore) the dominant view in the international scientific community that heritable germline editing was premature. His interpretation of the "medical need" (preventing HIV transmission from an HIV-positive father) was disputed — standard IVF techniques could have achieved the same goal without germline modification.

**Inclusion failed**: the informed consent process for the parents was inadequate. The parents did not have the scientific background to evaluate the risks; the consent documents were written in a way that obscured rather than clarified what was being proposed; and the broader communities affected by the precedent (all future humans, all other prospective parents, society as a whole) had no meaningful input.

**Responsiveness failed**: He was aware that the broader scientific community would disapprove of his research. He pursued it anyway by concealing it until the experiment was completed — specifically preventing the response process from functioning.

## Operationalizing Responsible Innovation in a Research Laboratory

The AREA framework can be applied at the laboratory level:

**Pre-project responsible innovation checklist**:
1. **Anticipation**: Have you identified the three most concerning potential negative futures from this research? Have you done a dual-use assessment?
2. **Reflexivity**: Have you consulted with a bioethicist, social scientist, or community representative about your framing of the research problem?
3. **Inclusion**: Who will be affected by this research? Have they been informed? Have they had any input?
4. **Responsiveness**: What would cause you to stop or change direction? Is there a pre-specified decision rule for halting?

**Ongoing practice**:
- Include a brief ethical review as a standing agenda item in lab group meetings
- When publishing, include an ethics statement that honestly addresses dual-use potential, equity implications, and known limitations
- Engage with policy processes that affect your research area (NSABB advisory comments, NAS workshops, congressional testimony when invited)

## The Role of Scientific Societies

Professional societies have a role in establishing and enforcing responsible innovation norms:

- **ASMS, ASM (American Society for Microbiology)**: publish biosafety and biosecurity guidelines; take positions on policy issues (opposing restrictions on scientific communication that are disproportionate to the biosecurity benefit)
- **CRISPR community statements**: following He Jiankui, the International Commission on the Clinical Use of Human Germline Genome Editing (NAS/NAM/Royal Society) established specific preconditions for any future clinical germline editing — including regulatory approval, clear medical benefit, absence of reasonable alternatives, and long-term monitoring
- **ACS, ACS CHAS**: chemical safety, applied to laboratory biological safety
- **iGEM Safety Committee**: establishes norms for student synthetic biology — a formative influence on practitioners' understanding of what responsible practice looks like

## Responsible Communication and Publication

A specific aspect of responsible innovation is **responsible communication**: how researchers publish and communicate results with dual-use potential.

**Redaction policies**: for DURC research, journals may require that methods sections be redacted or modified to reduce dual-use risk. This is practiced by *Science*, *Nature*, *PNAS*, and others for select papers.

**Pre-publication NSABB review**: for research meeting DURC criteria, the NSABB may review manuscripts before publication upon request from journals or authors. This adds time but provides legitimacy.

**Proactive communication**: researchers who anticipate that their results will be misinterpreted (either over-estimated for risk or under-estimated) have a responsibility to communicate actively with journalists, policymakers, and the public to provide accurate framing.

## Why This Matters

Responsible innovation is not a set of constraints that slow down synthetic biology — it is the framework that makes synthetic biology's social license sustainable. A field that produces transformative technologies while ignoring who bears the risks, fails to include affected communities in decisions, and responds inadequately to harms will ultimately be constrained by the society it operates within — through regulation, public opposition, or loss of funding. A field that takes responsible innovation seriously — anticipating harms, reflecting on its own assumptions, including diverse voices, and responding to new information — builds the trust that makes broader development and deployment possible. The history of biotechnology (including the Asilomar process, the GMO labeling debates, and the CRISPR babies crisis) shows repeatedly that the failure to engage proactively with ethical and societal concerns creates larger backlash than the proactive engagement would have required. Responsible innovation is not altruism — it is enlightened self-interest, and it is the professional standard that the best practitioners of synthetic biology aspire to.
