Biology is spatial. Cells are not well-mixed reaction vessels — molecules diffuse, gradients form, waves propagate, and the geometry of the container shapes the dynamics within it. A morphogen produced at one end of a tissue does not instantly flood the entire tissue; it diffuses outward while being degraded, forming a graded concentration profile that encodes positional information for developing cells. A calcium signal initiated at one synapse travels as a wave down the dendrite, activating receptors at distances determined by the cable properties of the neuronal membrane. Bacteria in a microfluidic device respond to a chemical gradient that spans the entire device length. None of these phenomena can be described by ODEs. They require partial differential equations.

A PDE describes how a quantity — morphogen concentration, membrane potential, chemical species — varies across both space and time simultaneously. This is a fundamentally different mathematical object from an ODE, and solving it numerically requires new ideas. Where an ODE lives in a finite-dimensional state space (one number per species), a PDE lives in an infinite-dimensional function space: the solution is a function of position at every moment in time. Numerically, we approximate this by discretizing space, reducing the PDE to a (typically very large) system of ODEs that can then be solved with the methods you already know. The challenge is how to perform that spatial discretization faithfully and efficiently.

This section introduces three approaches to PDE discretization, each suited to a different biological context. The finite difference method (FDM) is the most accessible: replace spatial derivatives with simple differences between neighboring grid points, transforming the PDE into a large ODE system via the method of lines. For smooth 1D problems — morphogen gradients, 1D Turing patterns, calcium diffusion along a fiber — FDM is fast to implement and provides second-order accuracy in space. You will see exactly why the CFL stability condition matters and why implicit time-stepping is preferred for fine spatial grids.

The finite element method (FEM) is necessary when the geometry is not a rectangle. Neuronal dendrites, tumor boundaries, wing disc geometries, microfluidic channels — all of these require an unstructured mesh, and FEM was designed for exactly this purpose. The section walks through the weak form derivation and basis function expansion that underlie all FEM formulations, then shows how FEniCS, a powerful Python framework, makes expressing these problems as natural as the underlying mathematics.

Spectral methods take a different approach entirely: instead of approximating the solution locally (at nearby grid points), they represent it globally as a sum of smooth basis functions. For problems with smooth solutions on periodic domains, spectral methods achieve exponential convergence — errors that decrease faster than any power of the number of modes. For Turing patterns, periodic biological oscillations, and fluid mechanical problems in biology, this makes spectral methods extraordinarily accurate and efficient. The Dedalus framework brings modern spectral methods to Python with minimal overhead.

Together, these three approaches cover the vast majority of PDE problems you will encounter in computational biology.
