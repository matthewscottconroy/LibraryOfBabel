In November 2020, DeepMind announced that AlphaFold2 had achieved something structural biologists had described as the holy grail of computational biology: it could predict the three-dimensional structure of a protein from its amino acid sequence with accuracy comparable to experimental methods. For fifty years, the protein structure prediction problem had resisted the best efforts of the field, because the space of possible structures is astronomically large and the physical forces governing folding are complex and interdependent. AlphaFold2 solved it, not by simulating physics, but by learning the evolutionary and geometric constraints encoded in hundreds of millions of sequences and tens of thousands of experimentally determined structures, using a deep learning architecture — the Evoformer — built around multi-head self-attention.

AlphaFold2 is the most visible example of a broader revolution: machine learning has become the dominant paradigm for making sense of high-dimensional biological data, and the pace of progress is unlike anything the field has seen before. In the same years that AlphaFold2 was published, deep learning models reached clinical-grade accuracy in predicting the splicing consequences of genetic variants (SpliceAI), predicted gene expression levels from hundreds of kilobases of regulatory sequence (Enformer), classified cell types from single-cell RNA-seq data with greater reproducibility than manual annotation, and generated de novo protein sequences that fold into designed structures and bind therapeutic targets with sub-nanomolar affinity (RFdiffusion, ProteinMPNN). Protein language models trained on hundreds of millions of sequences now predict thermostability, catalytic activity, and pathogenicity of mutations without any labeled training data — zero-shot, from the evolutionary information alone.

This chapter gives you the conceptual and practical tools to engage with this revolution intelligently: not just to run existing models, but to understand why they work, when to trust them, and how to adapt or build them for new biological problems.

We begin, deliberately, with classical machine learning. Random forests, gradient boosted trees, support vector machines, k-means clustering, and PCA are not outdated relics to be quickly dispensed with. They are the right tools for the tabular, relatively small biological datasets that most researchers actually work with — clinical metabolomics, patient-level proteomics, drug sensitivity screens — and empirical benchmarks consistently show that gradient boosted trees outperform deep learning on this class of data. Classical methods also provide the interpretability that biological science often requires: feature importance scores that directly identify which genes, metabolites, or variants drive a prediction. The UMAP plots that appear in virtually every single-cell paper are rooted in dimensionality reduction methods that this section demystifies.

Deep learning takes over where the data is unstructured and high-dimensional enough to justify it. The deep learning section builds from first principles — what a neural network computes, how backpropagation trains it, why regularization matters on small biological datasets — and develops the architectures that have been most transformative: 1D CNNs for sequence motif detection, LSTMs and their replacement by transformers, protein language models that distill evolutionary information into dense representations, graph neural networks that encode molecular topology, and generative models that design new proteins and small molecules. Each architecture is presented with the biological motivation that drove its development and the biological problems it has reshaped.

The application-specific section zooms in on three problems of direct practical importance: predicting regulatory elements and gene expression from DNA sequence, interpreting the functional consequences of genetic variants, and predicting drug-target binding affinities. These are the applications that have had the most direct clinical and scientific impact — they are where published models like Enformer, SpliceAI, AlphaMissense, and DeepDTA came from — and working through them in detail will show you how the general architectural principles are applied to real biological questions with real constraints.

The chapter closes with what many textbooks leave until the end and then abbreviate: evaluation and pitfalls. Data leakage is the most pervasive source of unreliable published results in computational biology, and the patterns it takes in biological data — sequence homology between train and test sets, temporal dependencies, feature normalization errors — are specific enough to deserve dedicated treatment. Proper cross-validation for biological data requires understanding when groups of samples must stay together, when temporal splits are mandatory, and when nested cross-validation is required to avoid reporting inflated performance. Interpretability methods — SHAP values, saliency maps, attention visualization — complete the toolkit by turning model predictions into biological hypotheses.

By the end of this chapter, you will have the foundation to read the current deep learning literature in computational biology critically, to select the right method for a given dataset and question, to implement models from scratch or adapt pretrained ones, and to evaluate all of it honestly.
