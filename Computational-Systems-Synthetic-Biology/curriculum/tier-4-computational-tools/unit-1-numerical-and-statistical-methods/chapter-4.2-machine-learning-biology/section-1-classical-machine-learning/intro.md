There is a persistent myth in computational biology that "classical" machine learning — random forests, support vector machines, logistic regression — is something you learn before moving on to the real tools. This myth costs researchers time, resources, and scientific clarity. The reality is that for the data that most biologists actually collect, classical methods are not a stepping stone. They are often the destination.

Think about the typical omics experiment. You have perhaps 200 patient samples, each profiled with 5,000 proteins. You want to predict who will respond to treatment. You could, in principle, fine-tune a transformer. But you would need months of compute time, careful regularization tuned to your specific dataset, and you would end up with a black box that cannot tell you which proteins matter. Or you could run a random forest in thirty seconds, get competitive performance, and immediately rank your proteins by their contribution to the prediction. For this problem — and for the majority of supervised learning problems in quantitative biology — classical methods are the right tools.

This section builds the foundation you need to use these methods rigorously. We begin by asking why classical methods remain competitive despite the deep learning revolution, grounding the answer in empirical benchmarks and the nature of tabular biological data. The subsection on decision trees and ensembles covers the two methods you will actually use most: random forests and gradient boosted trees. You will learn how they work, how to tune them, and crucially, how to extract biological insight from their feature importance scores — including the important distinction between biased impurity-based importance and the more reliable permutation-based alternative.

Support vector machines occupy a special place in the history of computational biology: they were the dominant approach for sequence-level classification tasks for nearly two decades before deep learning. Understanding SVMs teaches you a way of thinking about classification that will remain useful even as you move to neural networks — the idea that the right representation can make a hard problem easy. The kernel trick, in particular, is a profound insight that shows up repeatedly in more modern methods.

Clustering and dimensionality reduction round out the section. These are the tools you will reach for in exploratory analysis, when you have new data and no labels. k-means and hierarchical clustering, applied with appropriate distance metrics, reveal structure in proteomics and single-cell data that guides the design of subsequent experiments. UMAP and t-SNE are now standard in every single-cell RNA sequencing paper published — but they are routinely misinterpreted. You will learn not just how to run them, but how to read the resulting plots critically: what neighborhood structure is preserved, what distances mean (hint: in UMAP, they mean nothing), and why you should always cluster in PCA space even when you visualize in UMAP space.

By the end of this section, you will have a complete, rigorous toolkit for the majority of supervised and unsupervised learning tasks you will encounter in biological research — and you will understand exactly when to apply each method.
