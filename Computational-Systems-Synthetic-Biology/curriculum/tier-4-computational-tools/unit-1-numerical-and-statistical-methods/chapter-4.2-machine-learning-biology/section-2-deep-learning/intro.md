In 2015, a team at Google DeepMind trained a convolutional neural network called DeepBind to predict where transcription factors bind DNA. They fed it raw sequence — four letters, one-hot encoded — and asked it to learn the binding rules directly from ChIP-seq data, without any prior knowledge of motifs or binding chemistry. It beat every existing method, including those built on decades of carefully curated position weight matrices. Three years later, SpliceAI predicted the splicing consequences of genetic variants with clinical-grade accuracy by reading the sequence around an exon-intron boundary. In 2021, Enformer predicted gene expression from 200 kilobases of DNA sequence, capturing the long-range regulatory logic of entire chromosomal neighborhoods. And in 2022, AlphaFold2 solved the protein structure prediction problem, predicting the three-dimensional conformation of virtually any protein from sequence alone.

Deep learning did not just improve on existing biological sequence methods. It replaced them, in problem after problem, by discovering features that human experts had not thought to look for.

This section explains how. We start from the foundations: the neural network as a parameterized function, backpropagation as the algorithm that trains it, and the loss landscape that the optimizer navigates. These fundamentals are not optional background — they are the diagnostic language you need to understand why your model is failing and how to fix it. When your validation loss plateaus while training loss continues to decrease, you are overfitting. When gradient norms collapse to near zero, you have vanishing gradients. Knowing what these mean and how to respond is the difference between a competent practitioner and someone who runs black-box models and hopes for the best.

From the foundations, we move to the architectures that have been most transformative in biology. Convolutional neural networks learned to scan DNA sequences for binding motifs the way a biologist scans a sequence alignment for conserved positions — but more systematically and at a scale no human could match. Recurrent neural networks and LSTMs brought memory to sequence processing, encoding the idea that what matters at position $i$ depends on what came before; you will understand both why they work and why transformers eventually replaced them for most tasks. The transformer section covers self-attention from first principles, building the mathematical machinery behind ESM-2, DNABERT, Enformer, and AlphaFold2's Evoformer.

Protein language models represent the current state of the art in sequence-based biology: models trained on hundreds of millions of protein sequences that have learned the evolutionary grammar of proteins well enough to predict stability, function, and variant effects in zero-shot settings. Graph neural networks bring a fundamentally different inductive bias — operating on molecular topology rather than sequence — and have become the default approach for drug discovery applications. The section closes with generative models: VAEs, GANs, and diffusion models, now producing de novo protein designs with sub-nanomolar therapeutic binding affinities.

This is the toolkit that is reshaping biological science. Understanding it deeply will let you not just use these tools, but adapt them, critique them, and extend them.
