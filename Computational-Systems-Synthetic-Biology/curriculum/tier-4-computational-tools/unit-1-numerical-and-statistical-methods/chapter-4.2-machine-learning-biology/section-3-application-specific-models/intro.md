The general-purpose architectures you learned in the previous section — CNNs, transformers, protein language models, graph neural networks — become most powerful when they are applied to specific biological problems with domain-specific training data, carefully designed input representations, and evaluation metrics matched to the biology. This section is about three of those applications: predicting regulatory elements from DNA sequence, predicting the functional consequences of genetic variants, and predicting whether a drug molecule will bind its protein target.

These are not arbitrary choices. They represent the areas where machine learning has had the most direct impact on biological understanding and clinical practice. The regulatory element prediction problem sits at the heart of the non-coding genome — the 98% of our DNA that does not encode protein but nonetheless controls every aspect of gene expression. Most genetic variants that cause common disease map to this regulatory region, not to protein-coding sequences. Models like DeepBind, DeepSEA, and Enformer have made it possible, for the first time, to ask what a specific non-coding sequence change does to gene regulation — computationally, across hundreds of cell types simultaneously, without running a single wet lab experiment.

Variant effect prediction extends this to the clinical problem of variant interpretation. Every year, clinical genetic laboratories classify thousands of variants of uncertain significance — changes in patient DNA whose consequences are unknown. The tools covered in this section — SpliceAI, AlphaMissense, ESM-1v, CADD — are now embedded in clinical variant curation pipelines, influencing real treatment decisions. Understanding how they work, what data they were trained on, and what their failure modes are is increasingly a prerequisite for responsible use in clinical genomics.

Drug-target interaction prediction addresses the earliest stage of drug discovery, where the question is not whether a known drug works but which candidate from a library of billions of compounds is worth synthesizing and testing. The computational throughput of modern ML-based screening is genuinely staggering: a GNN trained on ChEMBL can score a billion compounds against a target in hours on a single GPU cluster, a process that would take decades by experimental assay.

Each subsection develops both the conceptual framework and the practical implementation: how the biological problem is framed as a machine learning task, what architectural choices that framing motivates, how to use the available pretrained models, and how to build and train your own when the pretrained options do not cover your biological question. Pay particular attention to the evaluation sections — for all three applications, naive performance metrics are misleading in ways that reflect the non-random structure of biological data, and understanding the correct evaluation approach will help you both build better models and critically read the published literature.
