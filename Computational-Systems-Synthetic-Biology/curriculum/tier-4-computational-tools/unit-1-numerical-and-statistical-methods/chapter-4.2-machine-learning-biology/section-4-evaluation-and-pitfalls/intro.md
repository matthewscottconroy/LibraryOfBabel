A paper reports that their new model predicts protein-ligand binding affinity with an $R^2$ of 0.92 on the test set. Impressive — but before you decide to use the model, there are questions you need to ask. How was the test set constructed? Were test compounds clustered away from training compounds by scaffold, or just split randomly? Were the binding affinity measurements collected in a single lab, or across multiple assays that might introduce batch effects? Was cross-validation used to select the architecture and then reported as if it were an independent evaluation? Any one of these issues could turn a $R^2$ of 0.92 into something much worse when the model encounters truly novel compounds in your own drug discovery pipeline.

The ability to critically evaluate machine learning claims is as important as the ability to build models. This section covers the three pillars of that critical evaluation: data leakage, proper cross-validation, and interpretability.

Data leakage is, without exaggeration, the dominant source of inflated performance claims in computational biology. Biological data violates the independence assumption that underlies standard machine learning in systematic ways: protein sequences cluster into families, patient samples come from the same hospitals and cohorts, genomic assays batch in experimental runs. When train and test sets contain samples from the same biological clusters, models learn to recognize family members rather than to generalize, and performance metrics become meaningless. The data leakage subsection covers the three main variants — sequence homology leakage, temporal leakage, and feature leakage — with concrete code for detecting and preventing each.

Proper cross-validation takes these ideas and builds them into a rigorous evaluation protocol. You will learn when to use stratified k-fold, when group k-fold is necessary, when leave-one-group-out is the right choice, and when nested cross-validation is required to get an honest estimate of generalization performance after hyperparameter selection. The distinction between standard and nested CV — and the optimism bias that results from confusing them — is subtle but consequential: it is the difference between a reported AUC that reflects your model's true performance and one that reflects your model's fit to the CV procedure.

Interpretability closes the section not as a luxury but as a scientific tool. When a SHAP analysis reveals that your drug resistance predictor relies primarily on a housekeeping gene that is highly expressed in the specific cell line you trained on, that is not a bad prediction in a narrow sense — it is a model that has learned an artifact and will fail on any other cell line. Saliency maps can reveal that a CNN has learned to detect experimental batch effects encoded in the flanking sequence of ChIP-seq peaks rather than true TF binding motifs. Attention visualization in protein language models can identify the residues that drive a functional prediction, generating testable hypotheses. These methods turn a model from an oracle into a scientific collaborator.

Together, these three subsections equip you to build ML models you can trust, evaluate them honestly, and explain what they have learned.
