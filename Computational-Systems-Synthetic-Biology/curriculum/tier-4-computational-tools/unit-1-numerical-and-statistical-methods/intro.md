Modern biology generates data faster than traditional statistics can analyze it. A single RNA-seq experiment measuring gene expression across thousands of cells produces millions of numbers in an afternoon. A high-content imaging screen with a hundred thousand compound-treated cells generates terabytes of image data in a day. A genome-scale molecular dynamics simulation produces trajectory data at gigabytes per hour. The human genome contains three billion base pairs; comparing it to other genomes, or to a library of patient genomes, requires computational methods that can operate on sequences of that length in reasonable time. Biology in the twenty-first century is not data-limited — it is analysis-limited. The bottleneck is not collecting observations but extracting meaning from them.

The numerical and statistical methods in this unit are the engine that closes that gap. They are not decorative additions to biological knowledge; they are the means by which biological knowledge is produced and tested. Without ODE solvers, there is no quantitative systems biology. Without optimization, there is no parameter fitting and no flux balance analysis. Without machine learning, there is no single-cell trajectory analysis, no protein structure prediction from sequence, no automated image analysis of cellular phenotypes. These are not separate disciplines bolted onto biology from the outside — they are part of biology itself, as integral to the modern practice of the field as pipettes and PCR.

This unit covers the methods in two chapters. Chapter 4.1 on scientific computing develops the numerical infrastructure for mechanistic modeling and high-performance computation. You will learn to solve ordinary and partial differential equations that describe the dynamics of biological systems — from the repressilator's gene expression oscillations to morphogen gradient formation in a developing embryo. You will learn to formulate and solve optimization problems: fitting ODE models to data, predicting metabolic behavior through flux balance analysis, and designing gene circuits with genetic algorithms. And you will learn to make computational biology fast — through profiling, vectorization, JIT compilation, parallelization, and differentiable programming with JAX. These skills transform computations that would take days into computations that take minutes, and computations that would be impossible into computations that are tractable.

Chapter 4.2 on machine learning addresses the data-driven side of modern biology. Where mechanistic models encode prior knowledge in differential equations, machine learning extracts structure from data — classifying cell types from expression profiles, learning latent representations of genomic variation, predicting regulatory grammar from DNA sequence, inferring cell trajectories from single-cell measurements. You will encounter supervised learning (classification, regression), unsupervised learning (dimensionality reduction, clustering), and deep learning architectures (convolutional networks for imaging, transformers for sequences) applied to the specific challenges of biological data: high dimensionality, technical noise, batch effects, and the need to distinguish biological variation from experimental artifact.

The two chapters are complementary, not alternatives. Mechanistic models bring interpretability and prior knowledge; machine learning brings flexibility and the ability to learn patterns that no one thought to look for. The most powerful computational biology draws on both — using data-driven methods to suggest hypotheses, mechanistic models to test them, and the numerical tools in this unit to do both at scale.
