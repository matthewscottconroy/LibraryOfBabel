In 2012, a simulation published by the Shaw group showed, in atomic detail, how a beta-blocker drug molecule approached a GPCR receptor, diffused across the surface, slid into the orthosteric binding pocket, and settled into its final bound pose — all over a timescale of microseconds, in explicit solvent, at body temperature. The binding pathway included multiple intermediate binding poses that no crystallographic study had observed, some of them involving contacts with residues outside the binding pocket entirely. The experiment it most resembled was an NMR titration, but no NMR experiment could have shown you the atoms moving. This was something genuinely new: a movie, not a snapshot, of a biologically critical molecular event at atomic resolution.

Molecular dynamics simulation lets you watch proteins move — every atom, every femtosecond. The fundamental idea is almost startlingly simple: treat every atom as a classical particle, compute forces from an empirical potential energy function, apply Newton's second law, and advance time in tiny steps. Repeat half a billion times, and you have a microsecond trajectory. The simplicity of the algorithm obscures the sophistication of what it produces — detailed hypotheses about conformational dynamics, allosteric mechanisms, binding pathways, and the molecular basis of disease mutations that no other method can generate.

The experimental context makes MD's value concrete. X-ray crystallography gives you atomic-resolution structure but a single static snapshot, averaged over millions of molecules, at cryo or room temperature conditions that may or may not reflect the biologically active state. NMR gives you dynamics through relaxation parameters and chemical shift perturbations, but the relationship between these observables and specific atomic motions requires interpretation. Cryo-EM gives you near-atomic structure of large complexes in multiple conformational states, but "multiple states" means a handful of distinct populations, not a continuous trajectory. MD is the only method that produces a continuous, atomic-resolution, time-resolved record of a molecular machine in action.

What can you actually learn from an MD trajectory? The chapters of this unit illustrate the range. You can watch the binding pocket of a kinase open and close on a nanosecond timescale, revealing cryptic sites invisible in any crystal structure — sites that have become the targets of allosteric drugs. You can observe how an ion channel gate responds to membrane voltage or ligand binding, providing mechanistic detail that explains decades of electrophysiology data. You can measure the binding free energy of a drug candidate to within 1 kcal/mol using free energy perturbation — predictive accuracy sufficient to guide which analogs to synthesize. You can observe the spontaneous assembly of a lipid bilayer in coarse-grained simulations, or measure the lateral diffusion coefficient of a membrane protein, or watch how a titin Ig domain unfolds under mechanical force in a steered MD simulation whose force-extension curve matches atomic force microscopy experiments. MD is not a replacement for experiment — it is a complement that can see what experiments cannot, and be calibrated against what they can.

This chapter builds the theoretical and practical foundations needed to run, analyze, and interpret MD simulations with genuine understanding. Section 1 develops the theory: the physical basis of MD as numerical integration of Newton's equations, the force fields that define atomic interactions, the integrators that advance time, the time step constraints imposed by atomic vibrations, periodic boundary conditions that eliminate surface artifacts, and the Particle Mesh Ewald algorithm that handles long-range electrostatics exactly. Section 2 covers the practice: system preparation (downloading structures, assigning protonation states, solvating with explicit water, adding ions), energy minimization and multi-stage equilibration, and the crucial choice of thermostat and barostat that determines which statistical ensemble the simulation samples. Section 3 moves to trajectory analysis — the structural metrics (RMSD, RMSF, radius of gyration) that characterize stability and flexibility, secondary structure dynamics tracked by DSSP, contact maps and interaction fingerprints that reveal residue-level changes, and the free energy calculations (MM-PBSA, FEP, umbrella sampling) that convert trajectories into quantitative thermodynamic predictions.

Sections 4 and 5 extend the toolkit. Enhanced sampling methods — replica exchange MD and metadynamics — attack the fundamental timescale problem: many biologically important processes occur on microsecond-to-millisecond timescales that brute-force simulation cannot reach, and these methods intelligently accelerate exploration without compromising the thermodynamic validity of the results. Coarse-grained MD, centered on the MARTINI force field, trades atomic detail for a thousand-fold speedup in accessible timescale and length scale, opening the door to membrane biophysics, capsid assembly, and large-scale conformational dynamics. Section 6 surveys the major MD packages — GROMACS, AMBER, NAMD, and OpenMM — and explains how to choose between them.

The goal throughout is not to make you an expert who has memorized the GROMACS manual. It is to give you enough theoretical understanding that you can diagnose problems when simulations go wrong, enough practical knowledge that you can set up a production-quality simulation from a PDB file, and enough analytical literacy that you can extract meaningful biological conclusions from the trajectory you produce.
