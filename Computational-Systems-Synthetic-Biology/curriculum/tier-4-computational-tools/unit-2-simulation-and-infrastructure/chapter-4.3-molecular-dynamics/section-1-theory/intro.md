Before you can trust a single number that comes out of a molecular dynamics simulation, you need to understand what the simulation is actually doing. That sounds obvious, but the history of computational biology is littered with artifacts confidently published as biology by researchers who did not ask hard enough questions about the machinery underneath their results. This section builds the theoretical foundation that lets you ask those questions — and answer them.

The starting point is Newton's second law, applied to every atom simultaneously. This is the audacious simplification at the heart of MD: ignore quantum mechanics, ignore electrons, treat each atom as a classical point mass, and compute forces from an empirical potential energy function. The result is a set of coupled ordinary differential equations, one for each of the three spatial coordinates of each atom, which the computer integrates forward in time. Subsection 1 explains precisely what this means — what a trajectory is, what timescales it can and cannot access, and how to think about the relationship between the simulation ensemble and a real experimental measurement.

From there, the theory branches into the specific components that determine simulation accuracy. Subsection 2 examines force fields — the mathematical objects that define how atoms interact. The functional form is universal across nearly all biomolecular force fields: harmonic springs for bonds and angles, periodic potentials for dihedral rotations, Lennard-Jones terms for van der Waals interactions, and Coulomb terms for electrostatics. But the parameters differ between force field families (AMBER, CHARMM, OPLS), and those differences matter. Understanding force fields means understanding where they come from, what they get right, and where their classical approximations break down.

Subsection 3 addresses the integrator — the numerical algorithm that takes forces and produces the next set of positions and velocities. The velocity Verlet and leapfrog algorithms are the workhorses of MD; understanding why they are preferred over more familiar methods like Runge-Kutta comes down to two properties: time-reversibility and symplecticity. Both are non-trivial concepts that have real consequences for how reliably you can extract thermodynamic averages from long simulations.

The time step (subsection 4) and periodic boundary conditions (subsection 5) are practical parameters you will set for every simulation you run. The time step determines how much simulated time you can afford per wall-clock hour; it is constrained below by the fastest atomic motion in the system (C-H bond stretches at ~11 fs period) and optimized above by hydrogen mass repartitioning and constraint algorithms. Periodic boundary conditions are the mathematical trick that turns a finite simulation box into an approximation of bulk solution; knowing the minimum image convention and the artifacts PBC can introduce is essential for correctly interpreting simulation results.

The section closes with long-range electrostatics (subsection 6), specifically Particle Mesh Ewald — the algorithm that makes accurate treatment of Coulomb interactions computationally feasible. The physical argument for why a naive cutoff is catastrophically wrong for electrostatics is compelling: the Coulomb interaction decays only as $1/r$, so truncating it introduces errors that scale with system size. PME solves this with a combination of real-space and Fourier-space summations that reduce the cost from $O(N^2)$ to $O(N \log N)$. It is not an optional refinement — it is the reason modern MD simulations of charged and polar systems are physically meaningful.
