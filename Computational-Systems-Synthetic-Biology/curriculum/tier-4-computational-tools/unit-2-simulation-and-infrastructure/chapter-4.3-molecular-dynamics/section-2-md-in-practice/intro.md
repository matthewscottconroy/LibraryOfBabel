Knowing the theory of MD is necessary but not sufficient. The gap between understanding Newton's equations and actually running a simulation that tells you something true about biology is wider than it looks, and most of the pitfalls live in the practical steps: preparing the system, getting the temperature and pressure right, choosing thermostats and barostats that sample the correct statistical ensemble. This section is about closing that gap.

The first practical challenge is system preparation (subsection 1). You start with a protein structure — perhaps from the PDB, perhaps predicted by AlphaFold2 — and you need to transform it into a simulation-ready system: hydrogens added, protonation states assigned, solvated in explicit water, neutralized with ions, enclosed in an appropriately sized periodic box. Each step requires genuine biochemical judgment. Histidine residues have three protonation states, and the wrong choice at an active site will produce a simulation where the catalytic mechanism is energetically inaccessible. Missing loops must be modeled. Alternate conformations must be resolved. The code here is not difficult; the decisions require thought.

Once the system is built, it is not ready to simulate. Crystal structures are not force-field energy minima, and a freshly solvated system contains steric clashes and geometrical imperfections that would immediately destabilize MD. Energy minimization (subsection 2) resolves these clashes without dynamics — finding a nearby local minimum by moving atoms downhill along the force gradient. Then comes equilibration: first NVT (constant volume and temperature) to let solvent relax around the protein backbone, then NPT (constant pressure and temperature) to let the box density equilibrate to the correct value. Only after density and temperature have settled to their stationary values is it appropriate to collect production data. The common mistake is impatience — starting production too early and then discarding the resulting trajectory as "equilibration" without any principled criterion for when equilibration was actually complete.

Thermostats and barostats (subsection 3) complete the picture. The choice of thermostat is not merely technical: different algorithms sample different statistical ensembles. The Berendsen thermostat is easy to implement and quick to converge, but it suppresses kinetic energy fluctuations and does not generate the correct Boltzmann distribution. Use it to equilibrate, but use V-rescale or Nosé-Hoover for production. The same principle applies to pressure coupling: Berendsen barostat for equilibration, Parrinello-Rahman for production. These distinctions matter most when you are computing thermodynamic quantities — free energies, heat capacities, entropy — where the distribution of sampled configurations is the quantity of interest, not just the mean.

Taken together, the three subsections in this section trace the complete path from a PDB file to a production-quality trajectory. The theory section told you what MD computes; this section tells you how to make sure it is computing it correctly.
