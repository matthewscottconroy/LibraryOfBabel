A trajectory file is not an answer — it is a question. After the simulation ends and you have a hundred nanoseconds of atomic coordinates saved to disk, the scientific work has barely begun. Every frame in that trajectory is a configuration of the system consistent with your force field and your simulation conditions; it is your job to extract from that enormous dataset the few numbers, patterns, or mechanistic insights that actually matter biologically. This section is a systematic tour of how to do that.

The foundation is structural analysis (subsection 1): RMSD, RMSF, and radius of gyration. These are the first things any experienced computational biologist computes on a new trajectory, and they answer the most basic questions about whether the simulation makes sense. RMSD quantifies global structural drift from a reference — if it climbs monotonically over the entire trajectory, the simulation has either not equilibrated or is undergoing a large conformational change that needs to be interpreted. RMSF maps per-residue flexibility across the sequence, revealing which loops are mobile, which secondary structure elements are rigid, and where the protein breathes. Radius of gyration tracks overall compactness, providing a sensitive indicator of partial unfolding or domain rearrangement. Taken together, these three numbers let you characterize the trajectory before committing to more expensive analyses.

Secondary structure analysis (subsection 2) moves from global metrics to local backbone conformation. The DSSP algorithm reads hydrogen bond geometry to assign each residue at each frame to one of eight classes — alpha helix, beta strand, turn, coil, and others. Applied over an entire trajectory, DSSP produces a two-dimensional matrix (time × residue) that shows secondary structure dynamics in vivid detail: helices that fray at their termini, loops that sample multiple distinct conformations, beta strands that transiently zip and unzip. This analysis is particularly important for disordered proteins and for detecting induced-fit binding, where a ligand causes a loop or terminal region to adopt a new secondary structure conformation.

Contact maps and interaction analysis (subsection 3) take a different view — instead of asking how much the protein has moved, you ask which residues are touching each other and how often. A contact map is simply the pairwise matrix of contact frequencies between all residue pairs, accumulated over the trajectory. Difference contact maps between apo and holo simulations reveal allosteric pathways: the set of contacts that change when a ligand binds far from the active site, transmitted through the protein structure. Hydrogen bond tracking identifies which backbone and side chain H-bonds are persistent versus transient. Salt bridge analysis monitors electrostatic interactions that stabilize or gate conformational transitions. For protein-ligand simulations specifically, the interaction fingerprint — the per-residue frequency of ligand contact — is a direct output for medicinal chemistry: which residues drive binding affinity, and which could be targeted by a more potent analog.

The section concludes with free energy calculations (subsection 4), which are the highest-value analyses MD can produce. MM-PBSA gives rapid, approximate estimates of binding free energy by combining molecular mechanics energies with continuum solvation models — useful for ranking compounds but systematically inaccurate for absolute affinities. Free energy perturbation (FEP) with the Bennett Acceptance Ratio estimator provides near-experimental accuracy by slowly transforming one molecule into another through non-physical intermediate states; this is the method behind modern computational drug discovery pipelines that correctly predict binding affinities to within 1 kcal/mol. Umbrella sampling maps the free energy along a reaction coordinate — ion permeation, membrane insertion, ligand dissociation — by running many simulations with harmonic restraints and reconstructing the full potential of mean force. Together, these methods transform MD from a qualitative exploration tool into a quantitative predictive instrument.
