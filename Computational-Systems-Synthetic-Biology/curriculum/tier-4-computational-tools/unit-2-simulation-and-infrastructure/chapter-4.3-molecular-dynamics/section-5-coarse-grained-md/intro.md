At some point, the biological question you want to ask becomes bigger than the resolution you need to answer it. When you want to know whether two proteins will spontaneously form a complex in a lipid bilayer, you do not care which specific hydrogen atoms are involved — you care about the collective behavior of thousands of lipid molecules and dozens of protein residues over microseconds. When you want to understand how a viral capsid assembles from 60 identical subunits, atomic-resolution detail would be like reading a book one electron at a time. The appropriate level of description for a scientific question depends on what the question actually is, and for many of the most important questions in cell biology, a coarser description is not a compromise — it is the right answer.

Coarse-grained molecular dynamics formalizes this intuition. By grouping multiple atoms into single interaction sites and replacing the detailed force field with simpler effective potentials, CG models reduce the number of degrees of freedom by roughly a factor of four (for MARTINI) to thousands (for polymer-level models), while retaining the essential physics of the process being studied. The result is not an approximation of all-atom MD — it is a different model tuned to a different regime of biology.

This section focuses on MARTINI, the dominant CG force field for biomolecular systems. MARTINI maps approximately four heavy atoms to a single bead, assigns each bead a chemical type (charged, polar, nonpolar, or apolar), and describes interactions through Lennard-Jones and Coulomb potentials parameterized to reproduce experimental observables — partition coefficients, lipid bilayer structure, protein folding thermodynamics. MARTINI 3, the current version, corrected systematic errors in protein-protein interaction energetics that had made MARTINI 2 unreliable for studying protein aggregation and complex assembly. The practical tooling — martinize2 for protein coarse-graining, insane.py for membrane building, CHARMM-GUI for automated system preparation — has matured to the point where setting up a MARTINI simulation is now comparable in effort to setting up an all-atom simulation.

The payoff is extraordinary. A one-day MARTINI simulation on a modern GPU covers the same conformational space as a year of all-atom MD — a thousand-fold speedup arising from fewer degrees of freedom, larger time steps, and smoother free energy landscapes. This section quantifies that speedup carefully and honestly, including the MARTINI time-scaling factor ($\tau^* \approx 4$) that maps CG simulation time to effective physical time. It also covers Go-like models — the extreme end of coarse-graining, where each residue is a single bead and all information about native contacts is encoded directly in the potential — which remain the gold standard for studying protein folding kinetics and mechanical unfolding. And it describes backmapping: the procedure for taking an interesting conformation identified in a CG simulation and restoring it to all-atom resolution for high-resolution analysis. Used together, CG and all-atom MD are complementary tools, not competing ones.
