Think about what it means to "understand" a protein. You might know its structure, its catalytic mechanism, its binding affinity for its substrate. But if you knock it out of a cell, does the cell die? The answer almost never comes from the protein itself — it comes from its neighbors. Is it the only protein performing its function, or do five paralogs back it up? Does it sit at a crossroads connecting two critical pathways, or is it a peripheral cog whose loss ripples nowhere? These are questions about relationships, not properties. And to answer them systematically, you need a mathematical language for describing relationships at scale. That language is graph theory, and this section introduces it through the lens of biological networks.

Why does this section matter? Because every tool in the rest of this chapter — centrality analysis, community detection, motif detection, disease module identification — rests on a foundation of understanding what kinds of networks biologists work with and what those networks actually represent. A protein-protein interaction network and a gene regulatory network are both graphs, but they ask different questions, are built by different experiments, and have different interpretive pitfalls. A metabolic network connects metabolites through reactions; a genetic interaction network connects genes through their combined effects on fitness. Treating all of these as interchangeable "networks" leads to shallow analysis. Understanding what each one means leads to biology.

This section surveys the major classes of biological networks that you will encounter throughout this chapter and in the research literature. The first subsection introduces the graph abstraction itself: what nodes and edges represent, how directionality and weights modify interpretation, and how to load and summarize a network in Python using NetworkX. You will build intuition about the basic vocabulary — degree, density, connected components, path length — that underpins all network analysis. You will also see code for pulling data from real databases like STRING and for visualizing networks in a way that exposes biological structure rather than obscuring it. This is not abstract mathematics; by the end of the first subsection you will have loaded a real subset of the human interactome and computed its first summary statistics.

The subsection on biological network types then surveys the terrain: protein-protein interaction networks (where edges are physical binding); gene regulatory networks (where edges are transcriptional control, signed and directed); metabolic networks (where nodes can be either metabolites or reactions, and the bipartite structure matters enormously); signaling networks (cascades of modifications transmitting information from receptor to effector); co-expression networks (where edges are statistical correlations in expression data); and genetic interaction networks (where edges encode the combined effect of two mutations on fitness). Each type is a window onto a different aspect of cell biology. Each has characteristic topological properties that you will learn to recognize and interpret. All of them will appear again in later sections of this chapter.
