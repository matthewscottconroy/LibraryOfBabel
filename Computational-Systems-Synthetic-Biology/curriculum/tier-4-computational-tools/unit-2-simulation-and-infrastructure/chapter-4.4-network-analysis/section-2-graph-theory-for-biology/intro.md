Before you can ask interesting questions about a biological network, you need to be able to describe it. Not qualitatively — "this is a complex network with many hubs" — but quantitatively, in terms of numbers that have precise mathematical definitions and that, it turns out, carry real biological information. Graph theory provides this vocabulary. It is the part of mathematics that was invented precisely to reason about structure and connectivity, and it fits biology with surprising naturalness. The degree of a node is just the count of its connections, but in a PPI network it predicts essentiality. The clustering coefficient of a node tells you whether its neighbors know each other, which in a protein network tells you whether they form a complex. The average path length between any two nodes tells you how quickly a signal can traverse the entire system. These numbers are not incidental — they encode the design principles that evolution has built into biological networks over billions of years.

This section is about building fluency with that vocabulary. The five subsections move from the most local and elementary properties to increasingly global ones, and each introduces both the mathematical definition and the biological meaning. By the time you reach the final subsection on centrality, you will have a toolkit of metrics that can take any biological network — PPI, regulatory, metabolic, co-expression — and produce a ranked list of the nodes that matter most, by several different definitions of "matter."

The first subsection establishes the basics: degree, density, connected components, path length, and the adjacency matrix. You will implement a complete analysis pipeline that computes all of these for any network you load. The second subsection extends degree into the degree distribution — the question of how connectivity is distributed across all nodes — and introduces the power-law hypothesis along with the statistical tools needed to test it rigorously (not just by looking at a log-log plot). The third subsection covers the clustering coefficient, which quantifies local cohesion and, in biological contexts, is one of the primary signatures of protein complex membership. The fourth subsection ties together clustering and path length through the small-world framework of Watts and Strogatz, showing how to test whether a real biological network exhibits the characteristic combination of high clustering and short path lengths — and why this combination is believed to be functionally important. The fifth and final subsection covers centrality: betweenness, closeness, eigenvector centrality, and PageRank, each of which captures a different sense in which a node can be "important" in a network. You will implement the Brandes algorithm for efficient betweenness computation and learn to recognize the biologically distinct node types — hubs, bottlenecks, bridge nodes — that different centrality metrics reveal.
