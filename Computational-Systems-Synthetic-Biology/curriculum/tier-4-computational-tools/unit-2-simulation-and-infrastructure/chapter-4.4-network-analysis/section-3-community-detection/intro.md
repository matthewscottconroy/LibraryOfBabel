Here is a thought experiment that captures why community detection matters. Imagine you have been handed a list of 15,000 protein interactions in yeast, printed in two columns. Gene A interacts with Gene B. Gene C interacts with Gene D. Fifteen thousand rows. What can you learn from this list? Not much directly — the information is there, but it is not interpretable in that form. Now imagine that a community detection algorithm has grouped those 15,000 interactions into 200 clusters, each labeled with the GO terms most enriched in that cluster: "ribosome biogenesis," "DNA damage response," "spindle assembly checkpoint," "fatty acid synthesis." Now you are doing biology. The raw topology has been transformed into a biological map.

This is what community detection does. It takes the implicit modular structure of a network — the tendency of functionally related proteins to interact more with each other than with proteins outside their functional group — and makes it explicit, producing a partition of nodes into communities that you can name, enrich, and reason about. The biological significance of this is not just interpretive convenience. The disease module hypothesis, which we will explore in Section 8, rests entirely on the observation that disease genes cluster in communities. Community detection in regulatory networks reveals co-regulated gene programs. Community detection in metabolic networks identifies functionally coherent reaction modules. It is, in a practical sense, the primary method by which network topology is converted into biological knowledge.

This section has three subsections. The first establishes why modularity is not just a computational convenience but a biological principle: it is how cells achieve functional specialization, how genomes maintain evolvability, and how disease processes are compartmentalized. It introduces the modularity score Q — the central metric for community quality — and explains both what it measures and what it cannot measure, including the resolution limit that makes small communities invisible to modularity-based methods. The second subsection covers the main algorithms: Louvain, Leiden, Girvan-Newman, and Infomap. You will understand what each one optimizes, how they differ, and crucially when to use each one — Louvain for speed on large networks, Leiden when you need guaranteed community connectivity, Infomap for directed regulatory networks. You will also learn to tune the resolution parameter to detect communities at the biologically relevant scale, whether that scale is individual protein complexes or broad pathway modules. The third subsection is the most important for rigorous practice: evaluating communities. You will learn to distinguish topological quality (high Q) from biological validity (GO enrichment, correspondence to known complexes), and you will implement stability analysis to determine whether the communities your algorithm finds are genuine structure or algorithm-dependent artifacts.
