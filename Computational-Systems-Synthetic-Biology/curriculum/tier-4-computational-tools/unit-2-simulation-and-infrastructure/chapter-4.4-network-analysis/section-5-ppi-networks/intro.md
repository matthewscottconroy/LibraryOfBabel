Protein-protein interaction networks sit at the center of modern systems biology. They are the substrate on which community detection finds functional modules, on which centrality analysis identifies essential proteins, on which disease module analysis localizes pathological processes, on which drug target identification finds therapeutic leverage points. Almost every quantitative method in this chapter, and in the field at large, ultimately relies on a PPI network as its input. Which makes it all the more important to understand what a PPI network actually is — which is to say, to understand how the data were generated, what biases those methods introduce, and how to construct a network that is fit for purpose rather than simply available.

This matters deeply, and not in an abstract methodological sense. Consider TP53. In the BioGRID database, TP53 has over 3,000 reported interactions — more than any other human protein. Is TP53 genuinely the most connected protein in the human interactome? Probably not entirely. TP53 is also the most studied protein in human biology. Every laboratory that discovers a new protein checks whether it interacts with TP53. The correlation between a protein's degree in the interactome and the number of publications about that protein is disturbingly strong. If you build a network from raw BioGRID data and compute betweenness centrality, you will recover a list dominated by the 50 most-studied proteins in cell biology — which is a description of the sociology of biomedical research, not the structure of the human interactome.

This section contains one subsection that confronts this problem directly. You will survey the major PPI databases — BioGRID, STRING, IntAct, and HuRI — understanding what each one collects, how interactions are scored, and what their respective strengths and limitations are. You will examine the major experimental methods that generate PPI data: yeast two-hybrid, affinity purification mass spectrometry, co-fractionation, and proximity labeling. Each method has a false positive rate, a false negative rate, and a systematic bias toward particular classes of proteins. You will implement a bias assessment function that tests whether degree correlates with publication count, and you will learn how to build a high-confidence network by combining evidence from multiple databases with appropriate filtering thresholds. The goal is not to make you skeptical of PPI data — it is genuinely informative — but to make you a sophisticated consumer of it, so that your network analyses rest on foundations you understand and can defend.
