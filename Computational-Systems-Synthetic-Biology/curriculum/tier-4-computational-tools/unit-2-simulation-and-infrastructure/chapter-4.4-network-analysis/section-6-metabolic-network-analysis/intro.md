Metabolism is, in a certain light, the most tightly constrained network in all of biology. A protein can acquire a new interaction partner through a simple binding site mutation. A transcription factor can acquire a new target through a single nucleotide change in a promoter. But metabolism is governed by thermodynamics, mass conservation, and stoichiometry — you cannot produce ATP without consuming ADP and phosphate, regardless of what mutations the cell carries. This means that metabolic networks are not just topological structures; they are constraint structures, and the constraints are physical laws. Network analysis of metabolism is therefore simultaneously a graph problem and a linear algebra problem, and getting the representation right is the prerequisite for everything else.

Why does this section exist alongside the PPI and co-expression sections? Because metabolic networks require fundamentally different treatment. In a PPI network, an edge between two proteins means they bind each other — a symmetric, binary relationship. In a metabolic network, an edge means a metabolite participates in a reaction as either a substrate or a product — an asymmetric, stoichiometrically weighted relationship. The appropriate representation is a bipartite graph with two node types, not the unipartite graphs used for most other network types. And there is the currency metabolite problem: ATP, NADH, and water participate in hundreds of reactions. In a metabolite-metabolite graph, they become super-hubs that connect all of metabolism into a single dense neighborhood, making path lengths meaningless and centrality scores dominated by artifact. Learning to handle currency metabolites correctly is one of the first practical skills in metabolic network analysis.

This section has three subsections. The first covers representation: the stoichiometric matrix, the bipartite graph, and the metabolite-metabolite projection (with currency metabolites handled appropriately). You will build metabolic graphs from COBRA models and analyze the pathway-level connectivity structure of a genome-scale reconstruction. The second subsection covers elementary flux modes — the mathematically rigorous definition of "metabolic pathway" as the minimal combination of reactions that can operate at steady state. EFMs are the atoms of metabolism: every feasible flux distribution is a combination of them, and the EFM with the highest yield defines the theoretical ceiling for any metabolic engineering project. You will implement EFM computation for small networks and use the EFMtool interface for medium-scale analyses. The third subsection covers flux sampling — the method that explores the full space of feasible metabolic states without assuming any single objective. Flux sampling reveals which reactions are fixed by stoichiometric constraints and which are genuinely flexible, identifies coupled reaction pairs, and enables rigorous comparison of metabolic states between conditions. Together, these three subsections give you a complete toolkit for metabolic network analysis that respects the physical constraints that make metabolism different from every other network type in biology.
