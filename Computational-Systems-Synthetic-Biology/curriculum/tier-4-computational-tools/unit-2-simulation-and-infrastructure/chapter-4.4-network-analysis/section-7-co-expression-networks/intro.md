There is something remarkable about what RNA-seq data contains that is not in the differential expression analysis. When you compare a disease group to a control group and find 500 differentially expressed genes, you have learned something: these genes go up or down in disease. But you have discarded most of the information in the data. What you have not asked is: which of these 500 genes move together? Which sets of genes are so consistently co-regulated across samples that they almost certainly share regulatory architecture or functional role? This is the question that co-expression network analysis addresses, and the answer — expressed as a network of gene-gene correlations — can reveal the modular structure of the transcriptome in ways that no single-gene analysis can.

Co-expression networks matter for the same reason that all biological networks matter: the relationships between elements carry information that the elements themselves do not. A gene module identified by WGCNA is not just a list of correlated genes — it is a data-driven hypothesis about co-regulated functional units. When that module's eigengene (its first principal component across samples) correlates with patient survival at r = 0.65, you have a biomarker that is more robust and more biologically interpretable than any single gene could be. When the same module is enriched for "immune cell infiltration" GO terms, you have evidence that immune biology is a major driver of survival variance in your cohort. This kind of insight, extracted from a single RNA-seq dataset without any additional experiments, is the promise of co-expression network analysis.

This section has two subsections. The first covers the construction of co-expression networks from RNA-seq data: preprocessing (CPM normalization, log transformation, quantile normalization), the Pearson correlation network, and then WGCNA — the weighted gene co-expression network analysis method that has become the field standard. You will implement soft threshold selection (the procedure that chooses a power transformation to make the network approximately scale-free), compute the Topological Overlap Measure that stabilizes module detection, and run hierarchical clustering to identify gene modules. You will then compute module eigengenes and correlate them with external traits — the final step that connects the mathematical output (gene clusters) to the biological question (what drives the phenotype you care about). The second subsection addresses gene regulatory network inference using mutual information rather than Pearson correlation. Mutual information detects nonlinear dependencies, which are common in regulatory biology where transcription factors operate near saturation. You will implement the ARACNE algorithm's Data Processing Inequality pruning step — the principled procedure for removing transitive correlations and recovering direct regulatory relationships. Together, these two subsections give you a complete toolkit for extracting regulatory network structure from expression data, from the simplest correlation approach through the most principled information-theoretic inference method.
