The traditional model of drug discovery treats the genome as a list. You identify a disease gene, you find its protein product, you screen for molecules that inhibit it. This approach has produced important drugs, but it has also produced a long trail of clinical failures — compounds that worked perfectly on the target but failed to help patients because the disease was not actually caused by that target alone, or because the cell compensated by activating an alternative pathway. The insight that network biology offers is that disease is almost never about one gene in isolation. It is about a network neighborhood. Genes that cause the same disease cluster in the interactome. Drugs that treat the same disease act on proteins in the same network module. And the therapeutic leverage that actually changes patient outcomes comes not from hitting any single node, but from disrupting communication within the pathological subnetwork in a way the cell cannot easily compensate for.

This final section of the chapter applies all the network analysis tools you have accumulated to the most practically important problem in biomedical network biology: finding drug targets. The three subsections each represent a distinct strategy, and together they span the conceptual range from network topology to population genetics to experimental functional genomics.

The first subsection covers the disease module hypothesis — the framework that systematizes the observation that disease genes are not randomly distributed in the interactome, but cluster in connected neighborhoods. You will implement the statistical test for disease module significance (comparing the observed cluster size to randomly sampled gene sets), the module expansion procedure that discovers novel disease gene candidates at the periphery of the known module, and the module separation score that predicts comorbidity between diseases. The second subsection covers network propagation — arguably the most practically impactful single algorithm in network medicine. Random walk with restart spreads a signal from seed genes across the entire interactome, ranking every gene by its network proximity to the seeds. You will implement both iterative and analytical solutions, learn to correct for hub bias through degree normalization, and apply propagation to the problem of drug repurposing: finding diseases whose network neighborhoods overlap with the targets of an existing drug. The third subsection covers synthetic lethality networks — the framework behind some of the most successful precision oncology drugs developed in the past two decades. You will understand the genetic interaction score, load and analyze the global yeast genetic interaction network, identify cancer-specific synthetic lethal partners of driver mutations, and see how genome-wide CRISPR screens are extending this framework to systematic therapeutic discovery across hundreds of cancer cell lines.
