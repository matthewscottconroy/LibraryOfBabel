In 2016, a survey by the journal Nature asked 1,576 researchers across disciplines whether they had tried and failed to reproduce published results. More than 70 percent said yes. When asked about their own work, over 50 percent admitted they had failed to reproduce one of their own previous results. The survey generated significant attention, and much of the subsequent discussion focused on underpowered studies, publication bias, and flexible statistical analysis — legitimate concerns, especially in the biomedical sciences. But computational biology faces a distinct and arguably more tractable version of the same crisis. A substantial fraction of computational reproducibility failures are not caused by bad statistics or insufficient sample sizes. They are caused by missing software, undocumented code, inaccessible data, and unspecified computational environments. The science was done correctly. The analysis was performed. The results are real. But no one can verify this, because the tools to do so are gone.

This is not speculation. Studies attempting to reproduce published bioinformatics analyses have found that a majority cannot be executed: the code is unavailable or incomplete, the software versions are unspecified, the intermediate data files are missing, or the described methods are ambiguous in ways that allow many different implementations. A 2018 study of 62 bioinformatics papers found that only 26 could be reproduced from the information provided. A survey of RNA-seq differential expression analyses found that different choices of normalization, testing framework, and filtering threshold — each reasonable and described in methods sections — could generate dramatically different results from the same data. These failures are not willful. They emerge from research cultures and incentive structures that reward publications over the infrastructure that makes publications verifiable.

The practical response to this is the subject of this chapter. Software engineering practices developed in industry — version control, automated testing, containerization, dependency management — translate directly into reproducibility practices for computational research. This chapter covers seven areas, each addressing a different dimension of the problem.

We begin with reproducibility itself as a framework: a taxonomy of what reproducibility means, four pillars that define reproducible computational research, and the concrete project structure that implements those pillars from day one. This section establishes the conceptual vocabulary that the rest of the chapter elaborates.

The second section covers model exchange formats — the infrastructure that allows mathematical models of biological systems to be shared, simulated, and reused across tools and labs. SBML provides a universal XML representation for any ODE model of biochemical reactions, with direct support in the BioModels Database and every major simulation tool. Antimony provides a human-readable alternative that compiles to SBML, making models accessible to readers as well as to machines. BioNetGen extends the framework to systems where combinatorial complexity — the exponential proliferation of molecular states in signaling networks — makes explicit ODE formulations impossible.

The third section covers workflow management systems: Snakemake and Nextflow. These tools transform multi-step analyses from sequences of manual commands into automated, dependency-tracked pipelines that rerun only the steps affected by any change in input data or parameters. A Snakemake pipeline submitted with a paper provides reviewers with a single command that reproduces the entire analysis from raw data.

The fourth section covers containerization: Docker for development and cloud computing, Singularity/Apptainer for high-performance computing environments. Containers package complete software environments into portable, versioned artifacts that run identically across platforms and time.

The fifth section covers Python package development: the project structure, configuration files, and testing practices that transform ad-hoc analysis scripts into installable, testable, publishable software.

The sixth section covers data management: long-term archiving with Zenodo and figshare, efficient storage formats for large biological datasets with HDF5 and Zarr, and data version control with DVC.

The seventh section covers publication-quality figure design: configuring matplotlib for print, choosing colorblind-safe palettes, and building multi-panel figures at journal-specified dimensions.

Each of these is a practical skill, learnable and applicable. The investment required is real but bounded: none of these practices requires redesigning how science is done, only how its products are organized and preserved. The payoff is both personal and collective. Personally, reproducible infrastructure makes your own future work easier — when you need to rerun an analysis six months later, or respond to reviewer requests, or hand a project to a student, the infrastructure you built protects your past work and accelerates your future work. Collectively, science that can be independently verified and built upon is the only kind that earns the name.
