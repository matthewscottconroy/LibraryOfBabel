In 2015, a team at Bayer HealthCare reported that they had attempted to replicate 67 published preclinical studies in oncology and cardiovascular disease — and found that only 20 to 25 percent of them could be reproduced. Among the factors they identified was a category of failure that doesn't make it into the headlines: the original authors, when contacted, could not provide the exact analytical procedures they had used. Not because they had hidden anything, but because those procedures had lived in scripts on a laptop that had been replaced, or in the memory of a graduate student who had moved on, or in a version of software that no longer existed. The science was done in good faith. But without the infrastructure to preserve it, it was effectively gone.

This section addresses reproducibility not as a philosophical ideal but as an engineering problem. What does it actually mean for a computational biology analysis to be reproducible? What are the concrete steps that make the difference between an analysis that can be re-executed by a stranger in five years and one that cannot? The answers are surprisingly practical: version-controlled code, archived data with stable identifiers, containerized software environments, and automated workflows that document every processing step.

The single subsection here — "Reproducibility as a First-Class Concern" — lays out the foundational framework for everything that follows in this chapter. You'll encounter the reproducibility spectrum, which distinguishes between bit-for-bit identical replication and the more achievable standard of numerical reproduction. You'll see the four-pillar framework that organizes all reproducibility practices around code, data, environment, and workflow — and you'll get the concrete project structure and tooling that implements those pillars. The checklist at the end is not decoration: it is the exit criterion for any computational analysis you intend to share or publish. Every subsequent section of this chapter — version control, model formats, workflow management, containerization, data archiving — is an elaboration of one or more of these four pillars. Understanding the framework first means that when you encounter Snakemake or Docker later, you know exactly what problem each tool is solving.
