Edward Tufte, whose books on the visual display of quantitative information have influenced scientific visualization for four decades, opens one of his chapters with a question: "What is the purpose of a graph?" His answer: to communicate ideas about data with the maximum clarity and minimum distortion possible. A graph is not a decoration. It is not evidence that analysis was performed. It is a visual argument — a structure designed to make one specific claim about data immediately and unmistakably visible.

The gap between an exploratory matplotlib plot and a publication-quality figure is not primarily aesthetic. It is functional. An exploratory plot serves its creator, who knows what the data means and what to look for. A publication-quality figure serves readers who are encountering the data for the first time, who are skeptical, who are busy, and who will form their judgment of the paper's quality partly from the quality of its figures. The technical requirements that flow from this are concrete: font sizes that remain legible at print resolution, figure widths that match the journal's column width, color palettes that work for colorblind readers and for grayscale printing, axis labels that describe variables with units rather than saying "Value" or "Result", and data representations that show distributions rather than hiding them behind bar heights.

This section covers a single but comprehensive subsection on publication-quality figure design. You'll work through configuring matplotlib's rcParams with a style dictionary that implements the typographic standards of Nature, Cell, and PNAS. You'll use the Okabe-Ito palette — designed by color vision researchers to be distinguishable under all forms of color deficiency — and see why the default matplotlib color cycle fails this test. You'll build a multi-panel figure using GridSpec with proper panel labels, consistent axis styling, and significance annotation. You'll learn the difference between rasterized formats for submission (PNG at 300 DPI for preprints, TIFF at 600 DPI for journals that require it) and vector formats (PDF and SVG) that remain resolution-independent and editable. And you'll see the practical argument for generating all figures programmatically: a figure generated by a Python script is not just a visual artifact — it is reproducible output, auditable, updatable when data changes, and verifiable as accurately representing the underlying data.
