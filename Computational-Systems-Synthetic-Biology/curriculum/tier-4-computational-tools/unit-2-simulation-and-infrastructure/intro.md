Computational biology is unusual among the sciences in that its primary research output is often not a dataset, not a figure, not an equation — it is code. A molecular dynamics simulation is code that produces a trajectory; a network analysis is code that produces a graph; a reproducible research pipeline is code that makes every result regenerable from raw data. The algorithms, the software engineering choices, the infrastructure decisions — these are not support work for the real science. They are the science. And yet they are treated by many biologists as unfortunate technical necessities, learned reluctantly and practiced carelessly. This unit takes the opposite view: the computational tools of modern biology deserve the same rigorous treatment as its experimental methods, because they are what determine whether the conclusions drawn from large-scale data are reliable.

The three chapters in this unit cover the simulation and infrastructure tools that define computational biology at its most technically demanding.

Chapter 4.3 builds the theory and practice of molecular dynamics simulation from the ground up. MD is the method that lets you watch proteins move at atomic resolution — every femtosecond, every hydrogen atom. The opening material establishes the physical foundations: Newton's equations, force fields, integrators, time step selection, periodic boundary conditions, and the Particle Mesh Ewald algorithm for electrostatics. From there the chapter moves to practice: how to prepare a system for simulation (protonation states, solvation, ion placement), how to run the multi-stage equilibration protocol that ensures the system has settled to the correct thermodynamic state before data collection begins, and how to choose thermostats and barostats that sample the correct statistical ensemble rather than a subtly wrong one. Trajectory analysis occupies a substantial portion of the chapter — structural metrics, secondary structure dynamics, contact maps, and free energy calculations that convert atomic coordinates into quantitative biological insights. Enhanced sampling methods (metadynamics, replica exchange MD) extend MD to processes that occur on biologically relevant but computationally inaccessible timescales, and coarse-grained models extend it to biological length scales that all-atom resolution cannot reach. A survey of the four major MD packages — GROMACS, AMBER, NAMD, and OpenMM — closes the chapter with practical guidance on choosing the right tool for each project.

Chapter 4.4 addresses network analysis, which is the natural language for systems-level questions in biology. Protein-protein interaction networks, gene regulatory networks, metabolic networks, and signaling pathways are all graphs, and graph theory provides a rich vocabulary for their analysis: degree distributions that reveal hub proteins, shortest path algorithms that identify critical regulatory routes, community detection algorithms that find functional modules, and centrality measures that rank nodes by their importance to network function. The chapter connects these mathematical abstractions to concrete biological questions: how does the deletion of a hub gene cause lethality, while the deletion of a peripheral gene has no phenotype? How does a drug that targets a single node in a signaling network produce systemic effects on multiple pathways? How do disease mutations cluster in specific network modules?

Chapter 4.5 turns to the software engineering practices that make computational biology research reproducible and scalable. The scientific computing literature has a replication crisis of its own — results that cannot be reproduced because the code was lost, the environment was undocumented, the analysis was performed interactively and never formalized, or the pipeline was fragile to changes in input data. This chapter addresses these problems with the tools that software engineering has developed for managing complexity: version control, containerization, workflow management systems, automated testing, and the documentation practices that make code comprehensible to future collaborators (and to yourself, six months later). These are not optional refinements for large collaborative projects — they are baseline hygiene for any computational research that claims to produce reproducible science.
