# 5.2 Research Craft

The discipline of doing science well — often underdocumented but separates good researchers from great ones.

## Reading the Literature

### Staying Current

**Preprints:**
- bioRxiv: most biology preprints appear here first, 6–18 months before publication
- Read preprints critically: not peer-reviewed; methods and conclusions may change

**Alert systems:**
- PubMed email alerts: search term → email when new papers match
- Google Scholar alerts: follow authors, track papers that cite a paper you care about
- Twitter/X lists: many labs post preprints immediately; follow key groups in your area
- Semantic Scholar: AI-powered recommendations based on your reading history

**Structured reading (weekly habit):**
- Table of contents alerts: Nature, Science, Cell, Nature Biotechnology, Molecular Systems Biology
- Dedicate 1-2 hours/week to reading current papers in your area
- Keep notes: paper title, key claim, method, your critique, relevance to your work

### Literature Mapping

**Connected Papers** (connectedpapers.com)
- Build visual map of papers related to a seed paper
- Identifies: classic foundational papers, recent developments, unexplored connections

**Semantic Scholar**
- Citation network exploration; AI-generated summaries
- Identify: highly cited papers in an area; papers citing your target paper

**Systematic review protocol:**
1. Identify seed papers (1-3 high-quality reviews or landmark papers)
2. Follow all references in seed papers
3. Identify authors publishing in this area → follow their other work
4. Search forward: who cites your seed papers?
5. Read enough that you stop seeing new names/concepts

### Evaluating Papers Critically

**For experimental papers, ask:**
- What are the controls? Are they appropriate?
- Sample size: powered to detect the claimed effect?
- How many times was the experiment repeated? Biological replicates vs. technical?
- Are the statistics correct? Is the test appropriate? Multiple comparisons corrected?
- Could confounders explain the result?
- Is the claimed mechanism supported, or is it one of several interpretations?

**For computational papers, ask:**
- Can you reproduce the results from the described methods?
- Are the benchmarking datasets appropriate? No train/test leakage?
- How sensitive are conclusions to parameter choices?
- Is the source code available? Is it readable and documented?
- Is the comparison to baseline fair?

## Experimental Design

### Controls

No experiment is complete without controls:

**Positive control**: condition known to produce the expected result
- Confirms: assay is working; detection limit is appropriate
- Without a positive control, you cannot distinguish a real negative from a failed assay

**Negative control**: condition known to produce no effect
- Confirms: background levels; specificity of your assay

**Isogenic controls**: strains/cell lines identical to experimental except for the single variable
- Critical for: genetic screens, engineered cell lines
- Without isogenic control: genetic background differences may confound result

**Vehicle control**: treatment with solvent/carrier without active compound
- Critical for: any experiment using DMSO, ethanol, or other carrier solvents

### Replicates

**Biological replicates**: independent biological samples (different organisms, cultures, patients)
- Capture: biological variability; required for statistical inference
- Minimum: 3 for most experiments; more for high-variance readouts

**Technical replicates**: same sample measured multiple times
- Capture: measurement variability only
- Modern instruments are precise: technical replicates often unnecessary
- Do not mistake technical replicates for biological replicates in statistics

### Statistical Power

Before running an experiment, ask: what effect size do I need to detect, and how many replicates?

```r
library(pwr)

# For a t-test: what n for 80% power to detect Cohen's d = 0.5?
pwr.t.test(d = 0.5, sig.level = 0.05, power = 0.8, type = "two.sample")
# n = 63.77 → 64 per group

# For RNA-seq: RNASeqPower or pwr2
library(RNASeqPower)
rnapower(depth = 20, n = 3, cv = 0.4, effect = 2, alpha = 0.05)
```

**Underpowered studies:**
- Cannot reliably detect real effects
- Positive results may be false positives (winner's curse)
- Waste resources when conclusion is negative

### Randomization and Blinding

- **Randomization**: randomly assign subjects/samples to treatment groups; prevents systematic bias
- **Blinding**: analyst unaware of group assignment during analysis; prevents unconscious bias
- Double-blind when possible; single-blind (blinded analysis) when full double-blind is impractical

## Scientific Writing

### Structure of a Research Article (IMRaD)

**Introduction**
- What is the biological question? Why does it matter?
- What is known? What is not known? (gap identification)
- Brief statement of approach and findings
- Last sentence: "Here we show/demonstrate/report..."

**Methods**
- Sufficient detail to reproduce: specific strains, plasmids, antibodies, reagents with catalog numbers
- Software versions; database versions; exact parameters
- Statistical tests: which test, software, threshold
- Key: another researcher should be able to reproduce your results

**Results**
- Present findings in logical order; each section = one result
- Lead with the finding, not the experiment: "The repressilator oscillated with a period of 160 ± 30 min (Fig. 1)"
- Figures drive the narrative: text explains and interprets figures

**Discussion**
- Interpret results: what do they mean?
- Relate to existing literature: agree? Disagree? Extend?
- Limitations: what could go wrong? What didn't you test?
- Future directions: what questions remain?
- Do not repeat results in Discussion; interpret them

### Writing Principles

**Precision over ambiguity:**
- Wrong: "We observed an increase in expression"
- Right: "Expression increased 3.2-fold ± 0.4 (mean ± SD; n=4 biological replicates; p=0.003, Welch's t-test)"

**Active voice, past tense for experiments:**
- "We measured..." not "Measurements were taken..."
- "The FBA model predicted..." not "The model was used to predict..."

**Define on first use:**
- Acronym: write out on first use — "differential gene expression (DGE)"
- Technical terms: define in context; do not assume reader's knowledge

**One idea per paragraph:**
- Topic sentence: state the claim
- Body: evidence, data, reasoning
- Conclusion: interpretation or transition

**Be precise about statistics:**
- Report: test name, test statistic (F, t, χ²), degrees of freedom, exact p-value
- Report: error bars (SD, SEM, or CI; state which)
- Report: n (biological replicates, not total measurements)

### Figure Design (Tufte Principles)

**Data-ink ratio**: maximize data per unit ink
- Remove: chartjunk, unnecessary gridlines, 3D effects, decorative backgrounds
- Keep: data, axis labels, units, scale bar

**Choosing the right plot:**
- Distribution: violin plot or beeswarm (not bar+error for small n)
- Change over time: line plot
- Correlation: scatter plot (with regression line + CI)
- Parts of whole: bar chart (not pie chart)
- Comparison across many groups: box plot or violin

**Colorblind accessibility:**
- 8% of males have red-green color blindness
- Use: viridis, cividis, plasma (perceptually uniform, colorblind safe)
- Never rely on red vs. green alone (use shape or pattern as second encoding)
- Test: run figures through colorblindness simulator (Coblis, Sim Daltonism)

**Consistent aesthetics:**
- Consistent font size across panels
- Same color scheme throughout the paper for same biological entity
- Consistent axis ranges when comparing related panels

## Research Habits

### Lab Notebooks

**Every experiment gets an entry:**
- Date, purpose, materials (exact lot numbers and sources), protocol, raw data, observations, interpretation
- Data should be reproducible from notebook alone

**Electronic notebooks**: Benchling (biology-focused), Jupyter, Notion
- Advantage: searchable, shareable, linkable to data files
- Disadvantage: data ownership concerns with cloud providers

### Data Management

- Raw data: never modify; keep in original format
- Processed data: document every processing step (scripts in version control)
- File naming: date-project-sample-condition (e.g., 20240315_repressilator_aTc10nM_rep1.csv)
- Backups: 3-2-1 rule (3 copies, 2 media types, 1 offsite)

### Time Management for Research

- Morning blocks: deep work (analysis, writing, reading)
- Afternoon: experiments, meetings, email
- Weekly: review goals; update task list; read 2-3 new papers
- Monthly: assess progress toward milestones; course-correct

### When You're Stuck

1. State the problem precisely in writing — often clarifies the path forward
2. Talk to a colleague — often they see what you're missing
3. Sleep on it — diffuse thinking during downtime is often the most productive
4. Consult the literature — has someone solved this already?
5. Simplify the problem — can you reproduce the issue in a minimal case?

## Presenting Research

### The 10-Minute Talk

Structure:
1. **Hook** (30 sec): why does this matter?
2. **Background** (1.5 min): what is known; what is the gap
3. **Question** (30 sec): one sentence
4. **Approach** (1 min): how did you address it; key methods
5. **Result 1** (2 min): most important finding; one figure
6. **Result 2** (2 min): supporting finding; one figure
7. **Summary** (1 min): what you showed; what it means
8. **Conclusion** (30 sec): where does this go next

**Slide design:**
- One main point per slide; state it in the title
- Remove all text that you will say verbally
- Large font (24+ pt for text; 18+ pt for labels)
- Figures: larger than you think necessary

### Poster Presentations

- Abstract in one sentence at top
- Five panels: Background, Question, Methods (brief), Results, Conclusion
- Designed to be read in 5 minutes by someone who doesn't know your field
- Add a QR code linking to preprint or supplementary data
