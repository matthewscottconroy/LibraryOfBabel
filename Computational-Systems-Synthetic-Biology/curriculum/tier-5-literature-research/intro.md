What does it mean to "know" a field?

One answer: you know its facts. You can name the transcription factors that regulate the cell cycle, recall the mechanism of CRISPR-Cas9, explain how flux balance analysis works. This kind of knowledge is necessary. But a student who has only this — who can pass a comprehensive exam but has never read the primary literature, never wrestled with an argument in its original form, never confronted the messy space between what a paper claims and what its evidence actually shows — does not know the field in the way a scientist knows it. They know about the field.

A second answer: you know its methods. You can run a DESeq2 analysis, implement a toggle switch model, compute gene essentiality from an FBA model, interpret an AlphaFold pLDDT score. This knowledge is also necessary. A computational biologist who cannot implement the tools of the field is not yet a practitioner. But methods knowledge without intellectual context — without understanding why the methods were developed, what assumptions they carry, and under what conditions they fail — is fragile. It breaks the first time you encounter data that doesn't fit the tutorial, a result that doesn't make sense, or a reviewer who asks why you chose this approach rather than that one.

A third answer: you know the field's literature. Not as a list of papers you have read, but as a living intellectual ecosystem you can situate yourself in. You know which papers established which ideas. You know which results are solid and which are contested. You know where the field came from and, therefore, where it is going. This is the knowledge that lets you read a new preprint and immediately understand what it is claiming, why it matters, and whether the evidence is adequate. It is the knowledge that lets you design an experiment that will actually advance the field rather than repeat what has already been done.

Tier 5 is where this curriculum closes the loop between learning and doing. You have spent four tiers building quantitative tools (Tiers 1 and 2), biological knowledge (running through all tiers), and computational skills (Tiers 3 and 4). The foundations are in place. Tier 5 asks you to use those foundations to engage with the actual scientific literature — to read it with discipline and critical intelligence, to situate new results in intellectual context, and to begin thinking about what it would mean to contribute to it.

This is the tier where you stop learning about the field and start participating in it.

The tier contains a single unit, Unit 1: Scientific Practice. The choice to build the entire tier around a single unit is deliberate. Scientific practice — the set of skills required to engage with and contribute to a scientific discipline — is not a collection of isolated techniques. It is an integrated set of capacities: reading deeply, evaluating critically, writing clearly, situating your work in context, and understanding the standards by which claims are judged. These capacities are interlocked. You cannot evaluate a paper well without understanding its statistical methods. You cannot write a paper well without having read enough papers to internalize the genre. You cannot situate your work in context without knowing the literature. The unit is single because the subject is unified.

Unit 1 contains two chapters. Chapter 5.1, covered in this tier, focuses on the foundational papers of computational and systems biology — the primary documents in which the field's conceptual vocabulary was established. It begins with a section on how to read papers (strategies, critical evaluation frameworks, paper types) before proceeding through the canon of the field: systems biology dynamics and network motifs, metabolic modeling, synthetic biology, bioinformatics, statistical methods, and the required textbooks. Reading these papers carefully, with the critical apparatus developed in the opening section, is the core intellectual work of the tier.

Chapter 5.2 will address scientific writing — the reciprocal skill, and the transition from consuming the literature to contributing to it.

The question at the top of this introduction — what does it mean to know a field? — does not have a single answer, because knowing a field is not a state you reach but a process you sustain. Scientists who have worked in a field for thirty years still read papers every week, still discover results that surprise them, still find themselves revising their understanding of what they thought they knew. The curriculum you have been working through has prepared you to begin that process. Tier 5 is where you begin.
