In 1990, when Altschul and colleagues published BLAST, a typical sequence database search took hours on the fastest computers available. By 1995, BLAST searches took minutes. Today, you can search a database of trillions of nucleotides in seconds on a laptop. The biology of sequence similarity has not changed; what changed was the algorithm, and the algorithm changed because someone thought hard about what was actually being asked and found a smarter way to ask it.

Bioinformatics is, at its core, an ongoing argument between biological questions and computational constraints. Every algorithm in this section exists because a biological question was important enough to pursue and the naive approach was too slow, too memory-intensive, or too statistically ill-defined to be useful. Needleman and Wunsch needed a principled way to align two protein sequences; dynamic programming provided it. Smith and Waterman needed to find conserved domains in proteins that otherwise differed enormously; a two-line modification of Needleman-Wunsch solved it. Altschul and colleagues needed to search databases of millions of sequences without waiting a day for each query; a clever heuristic that traded off a small amount of sensitivity for three orders of magnitude in speed made genomic biology routine. Li and Durbin needed to align billions of short reads to the human genome during an era when the human genome had just been sequenced; the Burrows-Wheeler transform, borrowed from data compression, made clinical whole-genome sequencing computationally feasible.

The papers in this section define the algorithmic foundations that underpin virtually every analysis in computational biology. They are organized into three subsections.

The first covers the sequence analysis papers: the canonical Needleman-Wunsch global alignment algorithm, the Smith-Waterman local alignment, BLAST, and the BWA short-read aligner. These are the tools you use every time you compare a sequence to a database, every time you map reads to a reference genome, every time you look for a domain in a protein of unknown function. Understanding them as algorithms — not just as software — gives you the reasoning to interpret unexpected results and to know when a tool is appropriate for your question.

The second covers the genomics papers: the dual human genome papers of Lander et al. and Venter et al. from 2001, the Mardis review of next-generation sequencing technologies, and the Love, Huber, and Anders DESeq2 paper on RNA-seq differential expression. These papers define what it means to work at genomic scale — the reference sequences, the sequencing platforms, and the statistical frameworks that make genome-scale data interpretable.

The third covers structure and function: the Barabási and Albert scale-free network paper, which provided the theoretical framework for understanding why hub proteins in interaction networks tend to be essential and evolutionarily conserved, and the Jumper et al. AlphaFold2 paper, which solved protein structure prediction and eliminated a bottleneck that had defined structural biology for five decades.

Together, these three subsections give you the algorithmic and conceptual foundations to work intelligently with biological sequences, genomes, transcriptomes, and protein structures. The papers are varied in length and mathematical depth, but all of them reward careful reading — not just for the results they report, but for the questions they defined.
