There is a test you can run on any statistical result in the literature called the GRIM test — it checks whether the reported means are mathematically consistent with the reported sample sizes. If a paper reports a mean of 4.23 from a sample of 20 subjects measured on a 7-point integer scale, the GRIM test will tell you that no combination of 20 integers from 1 to 7 can produce that mean. The result is arithmetically impossible. When researchers applied the GRIM test to psychology and social science papers, they found inconsistencies in roughly 50% of the papers they examined. Not all of these are evidence of fraud — many reflect reporting errors, different sample sizes, or excluded subjects. But some of them are not consistent with any innocent explanation.

You might wonder what this has to do with systems biology. The answer is that the same statistical failures that have plagued psychology — inadequate sample sizes, inappropriate tests, multiple testing without correction, selective reporting — are pervasive in molecular biology too. RNA-seq analyses without proper normalization. Single-cell UMAP plots whose cluster structure is reported as biological fact when it depends entirely on parameter choices. Differential expression analyses that discover hundreds of genes without asking how many false positives the correction procedure allows. These are not rare pathologies; they are standard practice in papers published in top journals. Understanding the statistical foundations is not optional for a scientist who wants to read the literature critically and produce results that will hold up.

This section covers the key statistical papers that underpin modern computational biology. There is a single subsection here, but it is one of the most practically important in the entire chapter.

The subsection covers three foundational contributions. The Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate paper (1995) gives you the tool for multiple testing correction that is appropriate for the discovery-oriented, high-dimensional analyses of genomics — more powerful than Bonferroni correction, and designed precisely for the situation where you are testing thousands of hypotheses simultaneously and are willing to tolerate a controlled proportion of false positives among your reported findings. The DESeq2 paper of Love, Huber, and Anders provides both the statistical model (negative binomial, with empirical Bayes dispersion shrinkage) and the practical workflow for RNA-seq differential expression — and this subsection supplements the genomics section's account with a deeper treatment of the statistical machinery. The van der Maaten and Hinton t-SNE paper introduces the dimensionality reduction method that transformed the visualization of single-cell data — and provides the mathematical basis for understanding its well-known capacity to mislead, if you ask it questions it cannot answer.

The section also points toward several essential statistical references: Cohen's power analysis framework, Gelman and Hill on multilevel models, and Efron and Hastie on modern statistical inference. These are not papers but textbooks, and they belong in the same mental category as Alon and Strogatz — foundations you build into your thinking over time, not results you look up once.

Statistical literacy in computational biology is not about having memorized the procedures. It is about understanding why each procedure exists — what failure mode it was designed to address — so that when you encounter a new analysis type or a new kind of data, you can ask the right questions about how to treat it. The papers in this subsection give you that understanding for the three most consequential statistical issues in modern biological data analysis.
