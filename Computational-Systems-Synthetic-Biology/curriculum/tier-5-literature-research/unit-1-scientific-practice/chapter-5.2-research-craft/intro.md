Here are two scientists. The first ran an experiment that worked beautifully. The repressilator oscillated. The period matched the theoretical prediction to within 15%. The flow cytometry data were clean, the replicates were consistent, the figures assembled themselves almost without effort. When asked, at the lab meeting, why this result was important — what it established, what the implications were, how it related to the broader question of genetic circuit design — the student paused, looked at the data, and said, "It's a pretty good result, right?" The second scientist ran an experiment that did not work at all. The protein wouldn't express. The fourth attempt produced a faint band that was almost certainly a nonspecific artifact. In the notebook, though, she had written: "Hypothesis: expression fails because the RBS strength is too low for this GC-rich sequence. Test: compare RBS variants using the RBS Calculator predictions." She had turned a failed experiment into a specific, testable question. Six weeks later, the experiment worked — but the more important thing was that she understood why it had failed, and that understanding was the difference between one publishable result and the beginning of a deeper mechanistic story.

Research craft is what separates these two scientists. It is not intelligence; both are smart. It is not motivation; both are working hard. It is a collection of skills — asking questions precisely enough to design experiments that can answer them, evaluating the literature critically enough to know where your work fits, designing experiments rigorously enough to produce results you can trust, documenting your work honestly enough that you and others can build on it, and communicating your findings clearly enough that the community can evaluate and use them. These skills are learnable. They are also, curiously, among the least formally taught aspects of scientific training. Most graduate students acquire them by osmosis — from advisors who model good habits, from lab meetings where bad statistics get caught, from the accumulated experience of having papers rejected for reasons that turn out to be methodological. This chapter is an attempt to make those skills explicit, so that you can develop them deliberately rather than accidentally.

The chapter is organized into five sections, each addressing a distinct domain of research craft. The first is reading the literature — not just reading papers, but building a navigable map of a field, staying current with a sustainable system that doesn't collapse under its own weight, and evaluating what you read with calibrated skepticism rather than either credulity or reflexive cynicism. The literature is the conversation that science is having with itself, and participating in that conversation — rather than merely citing papers as decorative references — is what it means to be a scientist rather than a technician.

The second section is experimental design, which is the discipline that determines whether an experiment is capable of answering its question before a single measurement is taken. Controls define what a result means; without them, observations cannot be interpreted. Replicates — and the distinction between biological and technical replicates — determine whether statistics are valid or illusory. Statistical power determines whether the experiment has a reasonable probability of detecting the effect it is looking for. Randomization and blinding prevent the class of systematic bias that no increase in sample size can correct. These are not peripheral refinements; they are the foundations that make experimental results trustworthy.

The third section is scientific writing, addressed at the level of both large-scale structure and sentence-level craft. The IMRaD format is not an arbitrary bureaucratic convention — it is the logical architecture of scientific argument, and understanding it transforms both your writing and your reading. Writing principles — precision, active voice, the one-idea paragraph, complete statistical reporting — are the mechanism by which ideas become communicable. Figure design is the discipline that determines whether your data speaks for itself or requires verbal translation.

The fourth section is research habits — the daily and weekly practices that determine whether a scientific career is sustainable and productive. Lab notebooks, data management, time allocation, and the productive response to being stuck are not glamorous topics, but they account for a remarkable fraction of the variance in scientific output. The researcher who documents work carefully, manages data intelligently, protects their best cognitive hours, and responds to blocks with diagnostic precision rather than anxious repetition will, over five years, produce more reliable science than one who does not — even if the undisciplined scientist works longer hours.

The fifth section is presenting research: the 10-minute conference talk, which is a genre that demands selection above all else, and the poster presentation, which is the most productive networking format in science if worked well. The skills of presentation are translation skills — the ability to convert complex, contextual scientific work into a form that an audience can follow, evaluate, and remember. They are learned through practice and feedback, and they matter more than most graduate programs acknowledge, because a result that cannot be communicated clearly is a result that cannot fully enter the scientific conversation.

The goal of this chapter is not to transform you into a perfect scientist in the abstract. It is to give you concrete, immediately applicable tools for doing the work of science well — thoughtfully, rigorously, and in a way that produces results the community can trust.
