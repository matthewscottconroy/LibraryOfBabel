In 2011, a collaborative group of scientists at Bayer HealthCare published a quiet bombshell in the journal Nature Reviews Drug Discovery. Their scientists had spent years attempting to reproduce key findings from published oncology papers before committing to drug development programs. The conclusion: 65% of published targets could not be confirmed. Not because the original scientists were dishonest. Not because the experiments were poorly executed. But because experimental designs that looked complete on paper had invisible structural flaws — underpowered sample sizes, absent controls, confounded comparisons, unrecognized batch effects — that made results unrepeatable. The problems were not aberrations. They were systematic, and they were traceable to decisions made at the design stage of the experiment.

Experimental design is the discipline that determines, before you touch a pipette or write a line of code, whether your experiment is capable of answering your question. This is not a peripheral concern. It is the central act of science. An experiment that lacks adequate controls can produce data but cannot produce interpretable conclusions. An experiment that conflates biological and technical replicates can generate p-values that are literally false — not because of error in the computation, but because the computation is applied to the wrong n. An experiment run without randomization and blinding can produce results that are entirely consistent within the lab that generated them and entirely irreproducible everywhere else. These are not rare mistakes made by careless scientists; they are endemic, well-documented failure modes that affect even careful, well-intentioned research groups.

The four subsections here address the four pillars of rigorous experimental design. The first is controls — the comparisons that define what your experimental result actually means. You will encounter positive controls, negative controls, vehicle controls, and isogenic controls, and you will understand precisely why each category exists and what specific interpretive failure its absence produces. The second is replicates — the distinction between biological and technical replicates, why confusing them constitutes pseudoreplication, and what the actual minimum n is for common biological experiments. These numbers are larger than you probably think, and the gap between conventional practice (n = 3) and statistically adequate practice is the explanation for a substantial fraction of published biology that cannot be reproduced.

The third subsection covers statistical power — the probability that your experiment will detect a real effect if one exists. Power analysis is the tool that turns "how many replicates should I use?" from a question answered by tradition into a question answered by calculation. The R packages and G*Power software demonstrated here are not difficult to use; they simply require knowing that the calculation needs to be done before the experiment, not after. The fourth subsection covers randomization and blinding — the design-level defenses against systematic bias, the class of error that increasing n will never fix. Systematic bias does not average out; it must be designed out.

Together, these four subsections provide the vocabulary and tools to design experiments whose results are interpretable, whose statistics are valid, and whose conclusions are likely to be true.
