Consider two graduate students, five years into their PhDs. Both are intelligent; both work hard; both have produced solid experimental results. One finishes on time, publishes during the final year, and transitions smoothly to their next position. The other spends an extra two years, produces roughly the same body of work at the end, and arrives at their defense exhausted, with a data archive that a successor in the lab has difficulty interpreting because the documentation was written hastily from memory during the final months. The difference between them is rarely intelligence or scientific creativity. It is habits — the thousands of small, daily decisions about documentation, data organization, time allocation, and response to being stuck that accumulate, over five years, into wildly different outcomes.

Research habits are the infrastructure of a scientific career. They are not glamorous. No one gives a prize for maintaining a rigorous lab notebook or for consistently naming data files with ISO-format dates. But these habits determine whether your research is reproducible, whether your data is accessible when a reviewer asks for it three years after collection, and whether the five-day detour you spent troubleshooting a protocol in year two can be avoided by your successor in year six. The habits described in this section are the ones that separate productive, well-organized scientists from those who generate data without the infrastructure to use it fully.

The first subsection covers lab notebooks, which are the foundational record of experimental science. You will encounter the specific elements that belong in every experiment entry, the failure modes that most commonly degrade notebook quality — retroactive writing, undocumented decisions, data without interpretation — and the conventions for maintaining a computational lab notebook in Jupyter and Git. The notebook is not a bureaucratic requirement. It is the mechanism by which your thinking, as it evolves, becomes a recoverable record.

The second subsection covers data management: file naming, directory structure, version control with Git, and backup strategy. These are the decisions that determine whether your data is accessible in three years or silently corrupted. The 3-2-1 backup rule and the ISO date file naming convention are simple enough to implement on day one of a project; not implementing them creates problems that cannot always be solved on day 500.

The third subsection covers time management — not as a collection of productivity hacks, but as a principled framework for protecting cognitive capacity. The distinction between deep and shallow work, the case for time-blocking and Sunday planning, and the specific trap of thesis-writer procrastination are addressed directly. The fourth subsection confronts being stuck — the inevitable experience of technical failure, intellectual deadlock, and occasional demoralization that every scientist encounters — and provides a taxonomy of stuck types with specific, evidence-based responses for each. Together these four subsections describe not what you should do in the laboratory, but how you should inhabit a research career.
