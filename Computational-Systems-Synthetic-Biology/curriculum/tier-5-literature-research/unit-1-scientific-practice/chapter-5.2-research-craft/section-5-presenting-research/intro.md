There is a particular kind of disappointment that everyone who attends scientific conferences recognizes. A researcher takes the stage, and it is clear within the first two minutes that the work behind the talk is genuinely interesting — a clever experimental design, a surprising result, a computational approach that others in the room would immediately want to use. But the slides are too dense to read, the background section runs five minutes long, the most important figure is hurried through in 45 seconds because time is running out, and the final slide appears while the speaker is still mid-sentence. The work deserved better. So did the audience. And the tragedy is that the quality of the science had nothing to do with the quality of the talk — they are independent skills, and the second must be deliberately cultivated.

Presentation is translation. The experiment happened in a specific lab, with specific organisms, over a specific span of time that the researcher lived through in all its complexity. The audience at a conference or seminar was not there. They have different backgrounds, different preoccupations, different amounts of prior knowledge about the specific subfield. The presenter's job is to translate a piece of scientific work into a form that an audience can understand, remember, and be genuinely moved by — moved to curiosity, to follow-up questions, to new collaborations, to the realization that what they are hearing changes how they think about their own work. That translation requires skill. It can be learned.

This section covers two presentation formats that most researchers will encounter repeatedly throughout their careers. The first is the 10-minute conference talk — a format so demanding that it deserves its own treatment. The fundamental challenge is not compression; it is genre. A 10-minute talk is not a shortened version of a longer talk. It is a different form that requires selecting one story, building only the background necessary to understand that story, designing slides that work at a distance and make claims rather than labels, and knowing your ending cold enough to land it cleanly with 30 seconds to spare. The specific anatomy described — 8 slides, the one-story rule, the slide title as a claim rather than a descriptor — is not arbitrary preference. It reflects what actually works in this format, in this time budget, for audiences who have just sat through three other 10-minute talks.

The second is the poster presentation, which is a fundamentally different mode of communication. In a talk, you control the narrative; in a poster, visitors navigate non-linearly, stopping at whatever catches their eye, spending 10 seconds or 20 minutes depending on their interest. The skills that matter here — visual hierarchy by reading distance, the 30-second pitch and the 3-minute pitch, working the session by reading badges and letting visitors look before you speak, the 48-hour follow-up email that converts a hallway conversation into a lasting professional connection — are practical and teachable. The poster session is, in some ways, the most high-value time at any conference, because the conversations are real in a way that a lecture hall does not permit. Learning to use it well is not a minor skill. It is central to the collaborative, social nature of science.
