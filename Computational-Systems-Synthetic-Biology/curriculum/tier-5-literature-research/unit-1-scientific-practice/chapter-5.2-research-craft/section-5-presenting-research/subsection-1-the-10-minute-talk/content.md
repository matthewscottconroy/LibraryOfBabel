# The 10-Minute Talk

The first time most scientists give a 10-minute conference talk, they prepare by taking their full project and compressing it. Fourteen slides become twelve. Twelve become ten. The font gets slightly smaller. The speaker talks faster. By minute four, the audience has lost the thread; by minute eight, the speaker is skipping slides; the questions session opens with someone asking "so, what is this work actually about?" The 10-minute talk is the most demanding format in science. A 45-minute seminar gives you room to tell a nuanced story, accommodate side questions, and build gradually toward your punchline. A 10-minute slot — standard at many conferences, departmental symposia, and rotation presentations — gives you time for one story told well. Nothing more.

The most common failure mode is trying to fit a full research project into 10 minutes. **Ten minutes is not a compressed 45-minute talk. It is a different genre.**

## The Fundamental Arithmetic

Ten minutes at a comfortable pace is approximately 1,200–1,400 spoken words, or roughly 8 slides at 75 seconds each. That is the budget. Every slide you add steals time from another slide, and the slide you are most likely to rush is the one showing your most important data.

A workable target: **8 slides maximum**, with the distribution described below. If you find yourself with 12 slides and a 10-minute slot, the problem is not that you need to talk faster — it is that you have not yet decided what your talk is about.

## Anatomy of the 10-Minute Talk

**Slide 1 — Title (30 seconds).** State your name, institution, and what you study. The title should convey both the system and the question: not "Studies in genetic circuit engineering" but "Tuning oscillation period in the repressilator through promoter strength ratio." The audience should know what the talk is about before you say a word.

**Slides 2–3 — Background (2 minutes).** Introduce one essential concept the audience needs to understand your contribution. One. Not the entire history of the field, not three parallel developments, not a tour of every paper that inspired you. Find the single conceptual gap your work fills and set it up efficiently. One striking figure or analogy is more memorable than three explanatory slides. End the background with a question statement: a single sentence beginning with "Here we ask..." or "The central question is..."

**Slides 4–7 — Results (5 minutes).** Three to four data figures, each delivering one clear message. State the message in the slide title — not "Figure 3" or "Induction Assay" but "Genetic circuit X achieves 100-fold induction at 10 nM aTc." The title is not a label; it is a claim. The audience who half-heard you will still know what to conclude from that slide.

Use annotations on your figures: arrows pointing to the effect, text boxes highlighting the key value, circles around the relevant comparison. Never rely on verbal description alone for a complex figure — an audience member who misses your sentence about which bar to look at is lost. Make the figure legible without you.

Order your results logically, not chronologically. The first experiment you ran is often not the most important one. Tell the story in the order that makes the most sense to someone hearing it for the first time.

**Slide 8 — Conclusions and Acknowledgments (1.5 minutes).** Three bullet points summarizing your main findings. One sentence on future directions. Acknowledgments listing collaborators and funding — names matter, funding agencies matter, and acknowledging correctly is professional practice, not optional formality. End explicitly: "Thank you" followed by a quiet pause signals that you are done and questions can begin.

## The One-Story Rule

**A 10-minute talk tells one story, not three side stories.** If your audience cannot summarize your talk in a single sentence after you finish, you presented too many narratives. The test: hand someone a blank card after your talk and ask them to complete the sentence "This talk showed that..." If they cannot do it, or if they write different things, the talk had no through-line.

Side projects, interesting negative results, tangential findings, and "by the way, we also found..." moments are for the 45-minute talk. In 10 minutes, cut everything that does not advance the one story you chose to tell. The audience will not miss what was never there; they will lose the thread if you give them too many threads to follow.

## Slide Design

Visual clarity is not aesthetic preference — it is information transmission. A cluttered slide is a slide that doesn't work.

**Maximum 3 colors** per slide. A white background, one primary color for data, one accent color for annotations is sufficient for almost any slide. Color should convey meaning, not decoration.

**Sans-serif font, body text at 24pt minimum, figure labels at 18pt minimum.** If you must squint to read a label in PowerPoint on your laptop, the audience in row 10 cannot read it at all. Resize the figure before the talk, not after.

**White space is not wasted space.** A figure with breathing room is processed faster than one surrounded by text. Every word of text on a slide is a word the audience is reading instead of listening to you. Remove everything from the slide that you will say aloud. Leave only what you will not say: the y-axis label, the sample size, the statistical annotation.

Bullet-point slides — slides consisting primarily of text bullets — are the lowest-information format available to a speaker. They are a script you are reading to an audience that can read faster than you can speak. Replace bullet points with data, diagrams, or images whenever possible. If you must use text, limit it to 3–5 words per line, not full sentences.

## Timing Your Talk

Dry runs matter more than slide design. Most talks run 20% longer when practiced alone than when timed with a stopwatch and an actual listener. Practice aloud — not in your head — with a timer running. The first run through will be rough; that is the point. The rough edges are what you need to find before the conference.

Practice in front of at least one real person before the presentation. The questions a live listener asks are almost always the same questions the conference audience will ask. A friend from outside your subfield is particularly valuable: if they understand the background section, a specialist in an adjacent area will follow you easily.

**Know your ending cold.** The last 30 seconds of a talk are where many speakers trail off, add one more caveat, check the clock awkwardly, and stumble to a stop. Rehearse the conclusion until it is automatic: what your three findings are, what they mean, and the clean "Thank you." A confident ending signals mastery of the material.

## Handling Questions

Take a 3-second pause before answering any question. This signals deliberate thought rather than reflexive panic, and it gives you time to actually understand what was asked before you begin answering. Many conference questions are poorly formed and benefit from being restated: "If I understand you correctly, you're asking whether the feedback delay affects period or amplitude — is that right?" Restatement confirms understanding and often sharpens the questioner's thinking.

"I don't know, but here's how we would find out" is an excellent answer. It is honest, it demonstrates scientific maturity, and it often opens a productive conversation about experimental design. An invented answer that falls apart under follow-up damages your credibility far more than an honest acknowledgment of the limit.

Skip "that's a great question." It is a filler phrase that signals you were not listening and are now buying time. Everyone in the room knows it is a filler phrase. Answer the question directly.

## Managing Nervousness

Physiological arousal — elevated heart rate, heightened attention, faster breathing — is identical whether the triggering emotion is anxiety or excitement. The cognitive reframe that works is to **label the feeling as excitement rather than nervousness.** Research by Alison Wood Brooks (Harvard Business School) showed that this simple reframe measurably improves performance on anxiety-provoking tasks. You are not nervous; you are ready.

The audience wants you to succeed. They are not cataloging your errors. They are hoping you will show them something interesting. Remembering that the audience is on your side before you walk to the podium changes the experience of the first 90 seconds, which are the hardest.

## Takeaway

The 10-minute talk demands selection above all else. Choose one story, build the minimal background needed to understand it, show 3–4 figures each making exactly one claim, and close with three findings and a clean acknowledgment. Design slides for projection at distance, practice with a timer and a real listener, and prepare honest answers to the hardest questions you can anticipate. Everything else — polish, transitions, aesthetic details — is secondary to clarity and selection.
