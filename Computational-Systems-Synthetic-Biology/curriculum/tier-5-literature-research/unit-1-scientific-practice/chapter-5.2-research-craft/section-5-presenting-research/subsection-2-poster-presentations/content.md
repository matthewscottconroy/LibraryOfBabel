# Poster Presentations

Walk into a large poster session at a biology conference — ISMB, ASHG, SynBioBeta — and the chaos is real: hundreds of mounted printouts in rows, scientists moving between them with coffee cups, a low roar of simultaneous conversations in a dozen accents. Zoom in on any single poster exchange and what you see is something quite different from a lecture hall: a researcher explaining their work to a virologist who wandered over from the next row, a grad student sketching an alternative hypothesis on the back of a conference program, two scientists from different continents discovering they have been working on adjacent problems for years. Unlike a talk, a poster enables genuine conversation: one-on-one or small-group discussion where visitors set the pace, ask real questions, and follow up on details. The speaker-driven narrative of a talk becomes an audience-driven exploration. Mastering poster presentations means mastering a fundamentally different mode of scientific communication.

## Poster vs. Talk: The Core Difference

In a talk, you control what the audience sees and when. In a poster, **visitors navigate non-linearly** — they scan the figures first, then the title, then maybe the abstract, and they stop at whatever catches their attention. Your job shifts from narrator to guide. Someone who approaches your poster might spend 10 seconds and move on, or they might spend 20 minutes asking detailed questions. You need to be able to serve both.

This non-linear navigation has design implications: each figure panel needs to communicate its main message on its own, without requiring the visitor to have read the surrounding text first. Panels that only make sense in sequence, or figures whose conclusion is buried in a paragraph of text, lose the casual passerby who might have become a collaborator.

## Standard Dimensions and Conference Requirements

**In the United States:** the most common poster format is 48 inches wide by 36 inches tall (landscape orientation). Some conferences use 36"×48" portrait, or 56"×36" landscape for large-format boards.

**In Europe:** A0 portrait (841 mm × 1,189 mm, approximately 33"×47") is standard.

**Check the conference requirements before you design anything.** Printing a portrait poster when the session uses landscape boards, or printing at 56" when the board is 48", requires last-minute reprinting at conference prices, which are brutal. Most conference websites specify dimensions in the registration materials. If they don't, email the organizers. The constraint on poster size is real — do not assume.

## Layout Principles

**Visual hierarchy by reading distance** is the organizing principle of poster design:

- **Title:** readable at 10 feet. Use 72–96pt font. This is the only way to attract visitors who are scanning across the room. A title that requires approaching to 4 feet is a title that will be skipped.
- **Main figures:** readable at 4 feet. Figure panels should be large enough that a visitor standing at a normal conversational distance can see data clearly without leaning in. Axis labels, data points, and legends at 24–28pt minimum.
- **Body text:** readable at 2 feet. Body text in a poster is not a manuscript excerpt — it should be brief, large (18–20pt minimum), and directly tied to an adjacent figure.

**Reading order:** Visitors expect left-to-right, top-to-bottom. If your layout is unconventional — a circular arrangement, branching paths, reversed flow — use numbered panels (1, 2, 3, 4, 5) in large font at the corner of each panel. Do not make the visitor solve a navigation puzzle.

**Three-column landscape layout** is the most reliable grid for standard US posters: left column for background and question, center column for results (one or two large figures), right column for additional results and conclusions. Two-column portrait works well for A0 format with one large figure per column.

**Methods:** write a short paragraph or a compact numbered list. A full methods section belongs in a manuscript, not a poster. If detailed methods are necessary, offer a QR code linking to a preprint or protocol. Visitors who want methods details will ask you to walk through them verbally — that is better than consuming your visual real estate with fine-print protocols.

**30% white space minimum.** The impulse to fill every inch of the poster is understandable — you did a lot of work and you want it visible. Resist it. White space is not emptiness; it is visual breathing room that directs attention toward what is there. A cluttered poster forces the visitor's eye to work to find the important content. A well-spaced poster delivers the important content immediately.

## The Elevator Pitch

Before the session, prepare two versions of your pitch: a **30-second version** and a **3-minute version**. Practice both until they flow without stumbling.

The 30-second version covers: what system, what question, what finding. "We engineered E. coli to produce artemisinin precursors using a redesigned mevalonate pathway, and we achieved 2x the yield of prior published efforts by optimizing enzyme expression ratios rather than enzyme sequence." That is it — system, approach, result. This is for the visitor who is scanning the room, paused momentarily, and may keep walking. If that sentence is interesting, they stay.

The 3-minute version adds: why the question matters, what the key experiment looked like, what the quantitative result was, and what it means. This is the version you give to someone who stopped and said "tell me about this." It should end with an invitation to discuss: "Does this connect to anything you're working on?"

**The most common mistake in poster pitches is starting with background rather than the finding.** Visitors are time-limited and sophisticated. They want to know what you found before they want to know why the field exists. Start with the result; earn the background.

## Working the Session

Greet people as they approach. Make eye contact, not intense focus on your laptop while they try to read past you. **Read their conference badge** for affiliation and name — knowing that your visitor is from a lab working on related circuitry versus a clinician interested in therapeutic applications lets you calibrate immediately.

Ask early: "Have you heard about this work before?" This prevents you from giving a full introduction to a reviewer who has read your preprint, and prevents you from skipping context a first-timer needs.

**Let the visitor look at the poster for 30–60 seconds before launching into a pitch.** People who have already oriented themselves to the poster have better questions and more productive conversations. Jumping into a pitch the moment they approach can feel like a hard sell.

For visitors who seem interested, walk through the poster in the order that makes logical sense — not necessarily the layout order. Adapt to their questions. If they want to spend five minutes on a single figure, spend five minutes on that figure. The conversations you have at that figure are often the most valuable ones of the conference.

**Prepare honest answers for the three questions you will always get:**
1. "What's next?" — have a clear next experiment and why it would matter.
2. "Why didn't you try X?" — either you did (show the data) or you have a reason (be specific about the cost or the alternative you chose instead).
3. "How does this compare to [competing method/paper]?" — know the literature well enough to give a real comparison with honest acknowledgment of limitations on both sides.

## Digital Tools for Poster Production

**PowerPoint and Keynote** are the most common tools because everyone knows them and print shops accept the output. Set the slide size to the exact poster dimensions (File → Page Setup in PowerPoint) before you begin — scaling after the fact degrades image quality.

**Adobe Illustrator** is the professional standard for publication-quality vector graphics. The learning curve is steep but the output is scalable at any print resolution without pixelation.

**BioRender** is excellent for generating biological illustrations — cell diagrams, pathway schematics, protein structures in schematic form — that look professional without requiring illustration skills. It is subscription-based and widely used in biology.

**Inkscape** is a free, open-source vector graphics editor that handles most of what Illustrator does. The interface is less polished but it produces true vector output and is adequate for poster production.

**Export as PDF for printing**, not PNG or JPEG. PDF is vector-based and resolution-independent; it will print crisply at any size. Before sending to the printer, confirm whether the printer requires CMYK or accepts RGB color mode — screen colors (RGB) sometimes shift noticeably when converted to print (CMYK), particularly in blues and greens.

## The QR Code and Follow-Up

**Every poster should include a QR code** linking to your preprint, paper, GitHub repository, or lab website. Put it in the lower right corner in at least 1.5"×1.5" size. Many interested visitors will not remember your name or affiliation but will scan a code immediately. Link to the most complete version of your work that is publicly accessible.

Print the poster number clearly in 36pt+ font somewhere visible — people navigating a conference hall by poster number are common.

**Follow up within 48 hours.** Collect the email addresses or business cards of visitors who expressed genuine interest. Within two days of the session, send a brief email: "It was great to talk with you at [conference]. Here is the link to our preprint — happy to discuss further if you have questions." That follow-up converts a brief hallway conversation into a professional connection. Most researchers never send it; sending it puts you in a small minority who maintains the relationship.

## Takeaway

A great poster is legible at 10 feet, navigable without explanation, and built around 2–3 large figures that each make exactly one claim. Design for the visitor who has 30 seconds and for the one who has 20 minutes: prepare a polished short pitch and a detailed walk-through, then adapt based on what the visitor wants. Follow up quickly, include a QR code, and treat every poster session as 3 hours of structured networking with the people most likely to care about your work.
