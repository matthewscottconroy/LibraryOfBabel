Reading papers is a skill, not just a habit. Writing papers is a craft, not just a chore. These two statements may seem obvious, but they contain an implication that most graduate programs never make explicit: both can be learned deliberately, and most scientists never learn them as well as they could.

The typical route to literacy in the scientific literature is passive immersion. You read papers because you need to. Your advisor sends you PDFs; journal clubs require presentations; review articles point you to the key papers in a new area. Over years, you absorb the conventions of the genre — how abstracts are structured, how results are framed, what the discussion is supposed to do. But this absorption is inefficient, and it leaves large gaps. Most scientists read papers passively, accepting the framing the authors provide, skimming the methods, and arriving at the discussion with an impression of understanding that does not survive a colleague's pointed question. Most scientists write papers reactively, translating their results into the genre conventions they have absorbed, without a clear model of what a good paper actually does and why.

This unit is designed to address both gaps. It is organized around two chapters that treat engagement with the scientific literature as a learnable skill with identifiable components.

Chapter 5.1 is about reading. Specifically, it is about reading the foundational papers of computational and systems biology — the actual documents in which the conceptual vocabulary of the field was established. These are not case studies chosen for pedagogical convenience. They are the papers that researchers in this field cite when explaining what the field is and where it came from: the network motifs paper of Milo and Alon, the FBA papers of Varma and Palsson, the repressilator of Elowitz and Leibler, the CRISPR biochemistry of Jinek and Doudna, the AlphaFold2 paper of Jumper and Hassabis. Reading them well — with the three-pass method, with critical evaluation, with attention to how arguments are constructed and evidence is deployed — is a different activity from reading for information. It is reading for understanding of how a field thinks.

The chapter begins with a section on reading strategies and critical evaluation, which provides the tools needed for every subsequent section. It then moves through the systems biology canon, the synthetic biology canon, the bioinformatics canon, the statistical foundations, and the required textbooks. By the end of Chapter 5.1, you will have read many of the most important papers in the history of the discipline and will have the critical apparatus to continue engaging with the literature as it develops.

Chapter 5.2 is about writing. The skills of scientific writing are the reciprocal of the skills of scientific reading: understanding what makes a paper's argument clear, its evidence compelling, and its presentation honest is both a criterion for evaluating others' work and a guide for producing your own. That chapter covers the structure of scientific papers, the craft of presenting quantitative results, the ethics of reporting, and the mechanics of scientific communication — all grounded in the same foundational papers you will read in Chapter 5.1.

The two chapters are complementary in a deep sense. The best way to learn to write a scientific argument is to study the arguments you admire — and to understand precisely what makes them work. The best way to evaluate someone else's writing is to have attempted the same thing yourself. This unit asks you to do both.
