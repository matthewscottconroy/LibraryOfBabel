# Unit 01: Real Analysis Essentials

Real analysis is the rigorous foundation beneath every calculus argument. Where a first calculus course teaches you to compute limits, derivatives, and integrals, real analysis asks what those operations actually mean and when they can be trusted. For differential equations, this distinction is not pedantic: the central theorems — existence, uniqueness, continuous dependence on initial conditions — are all theorems of analysis, and their proofs require precisely the tools built in this unit.

## Structure of the Unit

The unit proceeds through seven chapters, each establishing a layer of the analytic infrastructure needed for differential equations.

**Chapter 1: Logic and Proof** establishes the formal language used throughout mathematics. Propositions, quantifiers, and logical connectives are introduced not for their own sake but because every theorem in the subsequent chapters is a statement in this language, and every proof is a sequence of valid inferences. The chapter covers direct proof, proof by contrapositive, proof by contradiction, and mathematical induction.

**Chapter 2: Real Numbers and Completeness** constructs the real line axiomatically. The field axioms and order axioms characterize the algebraic and ordering structure that $\mathbb{R}$ shares with $\mathbb{Q}$, but the Completeness Axiom — every nonempty set bounded above has a least upper bound — is what makes the real line genuinely continuous. From completeness, the Archimedean Property follows, ruling out infinitely large or infinitely small real numbers and giving a firm basis for approximation arguments.

**Chapter 3: Sequences and Limits** develops the epsilon-delta definition of convergence for sequences, proving the central theorems: every convergent sequence is bounded and has a unique limit; the limit of a sum is the sum of the limits. Cauchy sequences are introduced as sequences whose terms grow arbitrarily close to one another without reference to a limit value, and the Cauchy Criterion — a sequence converges if and only if it is Cauchy — is established as a direct consequence of completeness. The Monotone Convergence Theorem, which guarantees convergence for bounded monotone sequences, completes the chapter.

**Chapter 4: Series** lifts the convergence theory of sequences to infinite sums. The major convergence tests — comparison, ratio, root, integral, alternating series — are developed and applied. The distinction between absolute and conditional convergence determines what algebraic manipulations on series are valid; this becomes important when power series are rearranged. Power series, and particularly the formula for the radius of convergence, are covered in detail, since series solutions to ODEs are built on exactly this theory.

**Chapter 5: Continuity** defines continuity at a point and on an interval, characterizes it in terms of limits, and proves its basic preservation properties. The Intermediate Value Theorem establishes that continuous functions on closed intervals attain all intermediate values — a result used in existence proofs. The Extreme Value Theorem guarantees that a continuous function on a compact interval achieves its maximum and minimum. Uniform continuity, a stronger property than pointwise continuity, ensures that integrals of continuous functions can be approximated uniformly, and connects to a key condition in the Picard existence theorem.

**Chapter 6: Differentiation** defines the derivative as a limit and develops its algebraic properties: linearity, product rule, quotient rule, chain rule. The Mean Value Theorem connects the derivative at a point to the behavior of a function over an interval and is the workhorse of inequality arguments throughout analysis. L'Hopital's Rule resolves indeterminate forms. Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange remainder form quantifies how well a polynomial approximates a smooth function, providing error bounds that matter whenever truncated series are used.

**Chapter 7: Riemann Integration** constructs the integral via upper and lower sums, establishes the Riemann integrability criterion, and proves the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus in both its forms. Standard integration techniques — substitution, integration by parts — are justified rigorously. Improper integrals, defined as limits of proper integrals, close the chapter; they will recur in Laplace transform theory.

## How This Unit Fits into the Broader Course

Every existence and uniqueness theorem for ODEs is proved by constructing a sequence of functions that converges to a solution. Picard iteration, for instance, produces a sequence of approximate solutions; the proof that this sequence converges uses the Completeness Axiom, the Cauchy criterion, and uniform convergence — all developed here. The continuity and differentiability theory of Chapters 5 and 6 supplies the conditions under which solutions exist, and the power series theory of Chapter 4 supplies the method of series solutions. Students who invest in this unit will find that the analytic arguments in later chapters are recognizable variations on techniques first encountered here.
