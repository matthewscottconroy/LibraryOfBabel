# Chapter 01 Vectors in Rn

The starting point for all of multivariable calculus is the observation that physical and mathematical quantities often require more than a single number to describe them. A position in space, a velocity, a force, a color in an image — each of these has several independent components. The mathematical abstraction of this idea is the vector, and the first task is to establish the algebraic and geometric structure that makes vectors useful.

## Core Ideas and Organization

This chapter builds the theory of vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ from the ground up, introducing structure in the order that each piece of structure is needed.

**Section 1 (Vector Algebra)** defines vectors as ordered $n$-tuples of real numbers and introduces the two fundamental operations: addition and scalar multiplication. A vector $\mathbf{v} = (v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_n)$ can be added componentwise to another vector, and multiplied by a real number componentwise. These operations satisfy eight axioms that make $\mathbb{R}^n$ a vector space over $\mathbb{R}$. The standard basis vectors $\mathbf{e}_1, \mathbf{e}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{e}_n$ allow every vector to be decomposed as $\mathbf{v} = v_1 \mathbf{e}_1 + \cdots + v_n \mathbf{e}_n$. This section also treats vector subtraction and linear combinations, and introduces the Euclidean norm $\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{v_1^2 + \cdots + v_n^2}$, which measures length.

**Section 2 (Dot Product and Angle)** introduces the dot product $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = u_1 v_1 + \cdots + u_n v_n$ and establishes its central geometric meaning via the formula $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between the vectors. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality $|\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}| \leq \|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|$ is proved and used to verify that the angle formula is well-defined. Orthogonality — two vectors being perpendicular — is expressed elegantly as $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$.

**Section 3 (Cross Product in $\mathbb{R}^3$)** introduces an operation specific to three dimensions. The cross product $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}$ produces a vector perpendicular to both $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$, with magnitude equal to the area of the parallelogram they span and direction determined by the right-hand rule. Unlike the dot product, the cross product is antisymmetric ($\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = -\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{u}$) and fails to satisfy associativity, but it satisfies the Jacobi identity. The scalar triple product $\mathbf{u} \cdot (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w})$ equals the signed volume of the parallelepiped spanned by the three vectors and will reappear as a determinant.

**Section 4 (Projections and Components)** synthesizes the dot product into one of its most important applications. The projection of $\mathbf{u}$ onto $\mathbf{v}$ is $\text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{u} = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|^2} \mathbf{v}$, the component of $\mathbf{u}$ in the direction of $\mathbf{v}$. The complementary component $\mathbf{u} - \text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{u}$ is perpendicular to $\mathbf{v}$, giving an orthogonal decomposition of $\mathbf{u}$. Projections underlie the Gram-Schmidt process for constructing orthonormal bases and will appear in the computation of work (force projected onto displacement) and in least-squares problems.

## Key Theorems in This Chapter

The two foundational inequalities are the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the triangle inequality $\|\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\| \leq \|\mathbf{u}\| + \|\mathbf{v}\|$, the latter following from the former. These justify the use of the norm as a distance function and make $\mathbb{R}^n$ a metric space. The formula $\|\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}\| = \|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|\sin\theta$ for the cross product magnitude, dual to the dot product formula, is proved using the identity $\|\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}\|^2 + (\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v})^2 = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2\|\mathbf{v}\|^2$.

## How the Sections Connect

The chapter has a clear internal logic: each section builds on the previous. The norm in Section 1 is computed using the dot product formula from Section 2 (retroactively, the norm is $\sqrt{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v}}$). The cross product in Section 3 can be seen as encoding both perpendicularity (captured by $\mathbf{u} \cdot (\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}) = 0$) and area, both of which were set up in Section 2. Section 4 draws directly on the dot product to define projection, and the perpendicularity of $\mathbf{u} - \text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{u}$ to $\mathbf{v}$ is verified using the dot product. By the end of the chapter, the reader has a complete set of tools for doing algebra and geometry in $\mathbb{R}^n$.
