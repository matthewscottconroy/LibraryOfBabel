# Chapter 02 Analytic Geometry

Analytic geometry is the discipline of describing geometric objects — points, curves, surfaces, solids — using algebraic equations and, in particular, using the vector language developed in Chapter 1. In two dimensions, a student learns to describe lines by slope-intercept equations and circles by equations of the form $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$. This chapter carries that project into three and higher dimensions, where the variety of geometric objects is vastly richer and vector methods are indispensable.

## What This Chapter Covers

**Section 1 (Lines in $\mathbb{R}^n$)** shows that the most natural description of a line in any dimension is parametric: a point $\mathbf{p}$ on the line and a direction vector $\mathbf{d}$ together determine the line as the set $\{\mathbf{p} + t\mathbf{d} : t \in \mathbb{R}\}$. This parametric approach generalizes directly to $\mathbb{R}^n$ in a way that the slope-intercept form does not. The section also treats symmetric equations for lines in $\mathbb{R}^3$, intersection of lines, and the distance from a point to a line.

**Section 2 (Planes in $\mathbb{R}^3$)** characterizes planes by their normal vectors. A plane through a point $\mathbf{p}_0$ with normal $\mathbf{n}$ is the set of all points $\mathbf{p}$ satisfying $\mathbf{n}\cdot(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{p}_0) = 0$, i.e., the scalar equation $ax + by + cz = d$. This is the key formula for the gradient as a normal to a level surface (developed in Unit 2). The section covers finding the plane through three points, the angle between planes (computed via the angle between their normals), and the distance from a point to a plane.

**Section 3 (Quadric Surfaces)** classifies the surfaces defined by second-degree polynomial equations in $x$, $y$, $z$. Just as the conic sections — ellipses, hyperbolas, parabolas — are the curves defined by second-degree equations in two variables, the quadrics are the surfaces defined by such equations in three variables. The primary examples are ellipsoids, hyperboloids of one and two sheets, elliptic and hyperbolic paraboloids, and cones. Understanding these surfaces geometrically — through their traces (cross-sections with coordinate planes) and through recognizing their standard forms — is essential for later work with level surfaces and with integration over three-dimensional regions.

**Section 4 (Coordinate Systems)** introduces polar coordinates in $\mathbb{R}^2$ and cylindrical and spherical coordinates in $\mathbb{R}^3$. These are not new geometric objects but new ways of labeling points in space. Polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$ relate to Cartesian coordinates by $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$. Cylindrical coordinates $(r, \theta, z)$ extend polar by adding a $z$-axis. Spherical coordinates $(\rho, \theta, \phi)$ — where $\rho$ is the distance from the origin, $\theta$ is the azimuthal angle, and $\phi$ is the polar angle from the positive $z$-axis — are particularly well suited to problems with spherical symmetry.

## How the Sections Build on Each Other

The sections are roughly independent in terms of content, but they share the language of dot products and vectors from Chapter 1. The normal vector to a plane (Section 2) is computed using the cross product of two vectors in the plane; the cross product appears again in Section 1 when computing distances from points to lines. The quadric surfaces in Section 3 are most naturally understood by recognizing them as level sets of quadratic functions, a connection that becomes explicit in Unit 2 when level sets and gradients are discussed. The coordinate systems in Section 4 are used throughout Unit 3 when evaluating double and triple integrals, where choosing the right coordinate system can reduce a difficult integral to a straightforward one.

## How This Chapter Fits into the Course

Analytic geometry in three dimensions is not merely background material; it is the setting in which the rest of the course takes place. The domains over which multiple integrals are computed are often regions bounded by planes, cylinders, spheres, and quadric surfaces. The constraint sets in optimization problems are often planes and surfaces. The level sets of functions of several variables — which the gradient is perpendicular to — are surfaces. Students who can readily visualize and write equations for planes, cylinders, and spheres will find the later material significantly more concrete and accessible.
