# Simply Connected Regions

The vortex field $\mathbf{F} = (-y, x)/(x^2+y^2)$ is curl-free everywhere on $\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}$, yet it has nonzero circulation $2\pi$ around the unit circle. This field is irrotational but not conservative. The discrepancy is not a flaw in our theory — it is a precise indicator of topology. The domain $\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}$ has a hole, and that hole is exactly what allows a curl-free field to escape being conservative. The correct setting for the full equivalence of the four conditions in the previous section requires the domain to be **simply connected**.

## Definition: Simply Connected

A connected open subset $D \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is **simply connected** if every closed curve in $D$ can be continuously deformed to a point within $D$.

Informally: $D$ has no "holes" that a loop could get caught around.

**Examples of simply connected regions:**
- $\mathbb{R}^n$ (for any $n$).
- Any open convex set (open balls, rectangles, half-spaces).
- The interior of a sphere or ellipsoid.
- $\mathbb{R}^3 \setminus \{\text{a single point}\}$ — removing a point from $\mathbb{R}^3$ does not create a hole that loops can encircle in three dimensions (though it does for $n = 2$).

**Examples of domains that are NOT simply connected:**
- $\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{(0,0)\}$: any loop encircling the origin cannot be contracted to a point.
- An annulus $\{(x,y): 1 < x^2+y^2 < 4\}$: loops around the inner hole.
- $\mathbb{R}^3 \setminus \{\text{a line}\}$: loops linking the line.
- A torus (the surface of a donut): two independent families of loops that cannot be contracted.

## The Full Equivalence Theorem

**Theorem.** Let $D \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ be an open simply connected domain, and let $\mathbf{F} = P\,\mathbf{i} + Q\,\mathbf{j}$ be $C^1$ on $D$. The following are equivalent:

1. $\mathbf{F} = \nabla f$ for some $f \in C^2(D)$.
2. $\partial P/\partial y = \partial Q/\partial x$ on $D$ (equivalently, $\nabla\times\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$).
3. $\int_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}$ is path-independent on $D$.
4. $\oint_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = 0$ for all piecewise smooth closed curves $C$ in $D$.

On non-simply-connected domains, $(1) \Rightarrow (2), (3), (4)$ and $(3) \Leftrightarrow (4)$ still hold, but $(2)$ alone does not imply $(1)$.

**Proof sketch of $(2) \Rightarrow (1)$ on simply connected domains.** The key step is using Green's Theorem: for any simple closed curve $C$ bounding a region $E \subset D$, Green's Theorem gives $\oint_C P\,dx + Q\,dy = \iint_E (\partial Q/\partial x - \partial P/\partial y)\,dA = 0$ (since $\partial Q/\partial x = \partial P/\partial y$). Zero circulation around every simple closed curve implies path independence (by the earlier argument), which implies the existence of a potential.

The step uses the fact that any closed curve in a simply connected domain bounds a region inside $D$. This fails when $D$ has holes: a loop around the hole does not bound any region within $D$, so Green's theorem cannot be applied.

## The Topology Behind the Obstruction

The failure of curl-free to imply conservative on non-simply-connected domains is measured by the **de Rham cohomology** group $H^1(D)$: roughly, the space of curl-free vector fields modulo conservative fields. On simply connected domains, $H^1 = 0$, meaning every curl-free field is conservative. On $\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{0\}$, $H^1 \cong \mathbb{R}$, generated by the vortex field (normalized to have circulation $1$ around the origin). On a domain with $k$ holes, $H^1 \cong \mathbb{R}^k$.

This is the beginning of algebraic topology's interaction with calculus. The "periods" of a curl-free field — the circulations around the independent loops of the domain — are the topological invariants that obstruct conservativity.

## Practical Consequences

**In $\mathbb{R}^3$:** The condition for conservativity is richer. $\mathbb{R}^3 \setminus \{\text{a point}\}$ is simply connected (as a 3-dimensional domain, a point is too small to obstruct loops). But $\mathbb{R}^3 \setminus \{\text{a line}\}$ is not simply connected — loops can link the line. The magnetic field of an infinite straight wire, $\mathbf{B} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$, has $\nabla\times\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{0}$ away from the wire but nonzero circulation around loops linking the wire.

**Testing a domain's simple connectivity:** In practice, a domain $D \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ is simply connected if and only if its complement $\mathbb{R}^2\setminus D$ is connected (i.e., the complement has no bounded connected components). The complement of a disk is the exterior plus infinity — connected. The complement of an annulus includes the central disk — a bounded component separate from infinity — hence the annulus is not simply connected.

## Working Around Non-Simply-Connected Domains

When a domain is not simply connected, conservativity must be checked directly by computing circulation around generators of $H^1(D)$:

1. Identify the "holes" (the bounded connected components of the complement).
2. For each hole, choose a simple closed curve in $D$ that encircles it exactly once.
3. Compute the circulation of $\mathbf{F}$ around each such curve.
4. $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative on $D$ if and only if all these circulations are zero.

**Example.** On $D = \mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{(0,0), (3,0)\}$ (two holes), there are two generators: a small circle around the origin and a small circle around $(3,0)$. A field $\mathbf{F}$ with $\nabla\times\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$ on $D$ is conservative if and only if both circulations are zero.

## Summary

Simple connectivity is the geometric condition on a domain that makes the curl test for conservativity both necessary and sufficient. On simply connected domains, the four conditions — conservative, curl-free, path-independent, zero circulation — are fully equivalent. On domains with holes, curl-free fields may fail to be conservative, with the failure measured by the circulations around the holes. This topological dependence of analysis on geometry is one of the deepest themes in vector calculus and is systematically explored in the theory of differential forms and de Rham cohomology.
