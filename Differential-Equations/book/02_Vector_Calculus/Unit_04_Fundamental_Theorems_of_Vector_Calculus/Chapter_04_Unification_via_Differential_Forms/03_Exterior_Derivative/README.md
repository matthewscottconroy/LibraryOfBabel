# The Exterior Derivative

The exterior derivative $d$ is a linear operator that takes $k$-forms to $(k+1)$-forms. It unifies the gradient, curl, and divergence into a single operator, and its fundamental property $d \circ d = 0$ (the exterior derivative of an exterior derivative is always zero) is the algebraic reason why "curl of gradient $= 0$" and "divergence of curl $= 0$" are true.

## Definition

For a smooth $k$-form $\omega$ on an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$, the exterior derivative $d\omega$ is defined as follows:

**For a 0-form (scalar function) $f$:**

$$df = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,dx + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\,dy + \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\,dz = \nabla f \cdot (dx, dy, dz).$$

This is the gradient, expressed as a 1-form.

**For a 1-form $\omega = P\,dx + Q\,dy + R\,dz$:**

$$d\omega = dP\wedge dx + dQ\wedge dy + dR\wedge dz.$$

Expanding $dP = P_x\,dx + P_y\,dy + P_z\,dz$ and collecting terms:

$$d\omega = (R_y - Q_z)\,dy\wedge dz + (P_z - R_x)\,dz\wedge dx + (Q_x - P_y)\,dx\wedge dy.$$

The coefficients $(R_y-Q_z, P_z-R_x, Q_x-P_y)$ are exactly the components of $\nabla\times\mathbf{F}$ where $\mathbf{F} = (P,Q,R)$. So $d$ applied to a 1-form gives the curl, expressed as a 2-form.

**For a 2-form $\omega = P\,dy\wedge dz + Q\,dz\wedge dx + R\,dx\wedge dy$:**

$$d\omega = dP\wedge dy\wedge dz + dQ\wedge dz\wedge dx + dR\wedge dx\wedge dy.$$

Expanding: $dP\wedge dy\wedge dz = (P_x\,dx + P_y\,dy + P_z\,dz)\wedge dy\wedge dz$. Since $dy\wedge dy\wedge dz = 0$ and $dz\wedge dy\wedge dz = 0$, only $P_x\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz$ survives. Similarly for the other terms:

$$d\omega = (P_x + Q_y + R_z)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz = (\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F})\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz.$$

So $d$ applied to a 2-form gives the divergence, expressed as a 3-form.

**For a 3-form $\omega = f\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz$:**

$d\omega$ would be a 4-form on $\mathbb{R}^3$, which is zero (there are no nonzero 4-forms on $\mathbb{R}^3$).

## The Dictionary

$$\begin{array}{ccccccc}
0\text{-form }f & \xrightarrow{d} & 1\text{-form }\omega & \xrightarrow{d} & 2\text{-form }\eta & \xrightarrow{d} & 3\text{-form }\\
\text{scalar }f & \xrightarrow{\nabla} & \text{vector }(P,Q,R) & \xrightarrow{\nabla\times} & \text{vector }(P,Q,R) & \xrightarrow{\nabla\cdot} & \text{scalar}
\end{array}$$

The exterior derivative $d$ simultaneously generalizes gradient, curl, and divergence. The correspondence is:

| Form | Vector calculus operation |
|---|---|
| $d$ on 0-forms | Gradient $\nabla f$ |
| $d$ on 1-forms | Curl $\nabla\times\mathbf{F}$ (as 2-form) |
| $d$ on 2-forms | Divergence $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}$ (as 3-form) |

## The Fundamental Identity: $d^2 = 0$

**Theorem.** For any smooth form $\omega$, $d(d\omega) = 0$.

**Proof for 0-forms.** $df = \sum_i\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^i}\,dx^i$. Then

$$d(df) = \sum_{i,j}\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^j\partial x^i}\,dx^j\wedge dx^i = \sum_{i<j}\left(\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^j\partial x^i} - \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^i\partial x^j}\right)dx^j\wedge dx^i = 0,$$

by Clairaut's theorem (mixed partials commute for smooth $f$, so the antisymmetrization vanishes).

**Consequences:**

$d(df) = 0$ translates to: $\nabla\times(\nabla f) = \mathbf{0}$. Curl of gradient is zero.

$d(d\omega) = 0$ for a 1-form translates to: $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{F}) = 0$. Divergence of curl is zero.

Both classical identities follow from the single algebraic fact $d^2 = 0$.

## Product Rule (Leibniz Rule)

For a $k$-form $\alpha$ and $l$-form $\beta$:

$$d(\alpha\wedge\beta) = (d\alpha)\wedge\beta + (-1)^k\,\alpha\wedge(d\beta).$$

The sign $(-1)^k$ arises because $d$ must "pass through" the $k$-form $\alpha$ to reach $\beta$, picking up a sign for each basis element it passes.

**Example.** For $f$ (0-form) and $\omega$ (1-form): $d(f\omega) = df\wedge\omega + f\,d\omega$. This is the product rule for the curl: $\nabla\times(f\mathbf{F}) = (\nabla f)\times\mathbf{F} + f(\nabla\times\mathbf{F})$, which appears in the vector identity Section 5.1.

## Closed and Exact Forms

A form $\omega$ is **closed** if $d\omega = 0$. It is **exact** if $\omega = d\alpha$ for some form $\alpha$.

Since $d^2 = 0$, every exact form is closed. The question of when the converse holds is the content of de Rham cohomology:

$$H^k(D) = \frac{\ker(d: \Lambda^k \to \Lambda^{k+1})}{\text{im}(d: \Lambda^{k-1} \to \Lambda^k)} = \frac{\text{closed } k\text{-forms}}{\text{exact } k\text{-forms}}.$$

On contractible domains (like $\mathbb{R}^n$), $H^k = 0$ for $k > 0$ (Poincaré's lemma): every closed form is exact. On domains with topology (holes), $H^k$ may be nontrivial, encoding the topological obstruction.

**In vector calculus language:** $H^1(D)$ measures whether curl-free fields on $D$ are conservative. If $D = \mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{0\}$, then $H^1(D) \cong \mathbb{R}$, generated by the vortex field — this is the algebraic explanation of why the vortex field is curl-free but not conservative.

## The Exterior Derivative in Coordinates

For a general $k$-form $\omega = \sum_{I}\omega_I\,dx^I$ (where $I = (i_1 < i_2 < \cdots < i_k)$ is a multi-index and $dx^I = dx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{i_k}$):

$$d\omega = \sum_I d\omega_I \wedge dx^I = \sum_I\sum_j\frac{\partial\omega_I}{\partial x^j}\,dx^j\wedge dx^I.$$

Terms where $j \in I$ vanish (repeated index). The remaining terms give a $(k+1)$-form.

## Summary

The exterior derivative $d$ unifies gradient, curl, and divergence into a single coordinate-free operator on differential forms. Its fundamental property $d^2 = 0$ — following from Clairaut's theorem — algebraically explains both classical "vanishing identities" of vector calculus. Closed forms ($d\omega = 0$) generalize irrotational fields and divergence-free fields; exact forms ($\omega = d\alpha$) are the conservative fields and curl fields. The quotient (de Rham cohomology) measures topological obstruction. The exterior derivative is the key ingredient in the Generalized Stokes' Theorem.
