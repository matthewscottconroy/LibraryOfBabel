# Ordinary Differential Equations

An ordinary differential equation is a relation involving an unknown function of a single independent variable and one or more of its derivatives. The subject sits at the intersection of analysis, algebra, and geometry, and it provides the mathematical language for nearly every quantitative science. Newtonian mechanics, population dynamics, electrical circuit theory, chemical kinetics, and quantum mechanics all express their fundamental laws as differential equations, making fluency in this subject essential for any serious student of mathematics or the physical sciences.

## Scope and Organization

This module covers ordinary differential equations from the ground up, assuming only a solid background in single-variable and multivariable calculus together with basic linear algebra. The treatment is rigorous without being terse: theorems are stated precisely, proofs or proof sketches accompany the most important results, and worked examples connect abstract machinery to concrete problems.

The material is organized into eight units that build on each other in a logical sequence. Unit 1 establishes the vocabulary and foundational existence theory. Units 2 and 3 develop solution methods for first-order and second-order equations, which together cover the equations most frequently encountered in applications. Unit 4 introduces power series and the Frobenius method, extending the reach of analytic techniques to equations whose coefficients are not constant. Unit 5 develops the Laplace transform, a powerful operational method that converts differential equations into algebraic ones and handles discontinuous forcing functions with particular elegance. Unit 6 addresses systems of differential equations, including both linear systems solved by eigenvalue methods and nonlinear systems analyzed through the geometry of the phase plane. Unit 7 covers numerical methods, which are indispensable when closed-form solutions are unavailable. Unit 8 closes the module with boundary value problems and Sturm-Liouville theory, which connects ordinary differential equations to the spectral theory that underlies the analysis of partial differential equations.

## Central Themes

Several unifying ideas recur throughout the module. The first is the interplay between existence and uniqueness: before solving an equation, one must ask whether a solution exists and whether it is the only one. Picard's theorem answers this question for a broad class of initial value problems, and understanding its hypotheses clarifies why certain equations behave in unexpected ways.

The second theme is linearity. Linear equations admit a superposition principle that makes their solution space a vector space, and the theory of linear operators provides both the structural understanding and the computational tools for handling them systematically. The contrast between linear and nonlinear equations is one of the organizing principles of the entire subject.

The third theme is the geometric picture. A first-order equation $y' = f(x, y)$ defines a vector field in the plane, and solutions are curves that follow that field. For autonomous systems, the phase plane captures the qualitative behavior of all solutions simultaneously. This geometric perspective reveals phenomena, such as limit cycles and separatrices, that purely algebraic methods can miss.

The fourth theme is transform methods. The Laplace transform, and later the Fourier transform in the context of partial differential equations, converts problems in one domain into simpler problems in another. Understanding why these transforms work, and not just how to apply them mechanically, builds intuition that transfers to many other areas of analysis.

## Prerequisites and Connections

A student beginning this module should be comfortable with differentiation, integration (including integration by parts and partial fractions), sequences and series, and the basics of matrix algebra including eigenvalues. The material here lays essential groundwork for partial differential equations, functional analysis, dynamical systems theory, and applied mathematics at the graduate level.
