# Fourier Analysis

The heat equation offers one of the most compelling entry points into Fourier analysis. Suppose a metal rod of length $L$ has an initial temperature distribution $f(x)$ and its ends are held at zero temperature. The differential equation governing the evolution of heat $u(x,t)$ is $u_t = \alpha^2 u_{xx}$, subject to boundary and initial conditions. Separation of variables yields solutions of the form $\sin(n\pi x / L) \cdot e^{-\alpha^2 n^2 \pi^2 t / L^2}$, and the full solution is a superposition of these modes. But what makes such a superposition possible, and how do we find the coefficients? The answer lies in Fourier analysis.

Fourier analysis is the study of how functions can be decomposed into sinusoidal components, and how that decomposition can be used to solve differential equations, process signals, and understand the structure of function spaces. It is one of the most powerful and broadly applicable areas of mathematics, sitting at the intersection of analysis, linear algebra, and applications ranging from physics to engineering to number theory.

## What This Unit Covers

This unit develops Fourier analysis from its classical foundations through its modern formulations, organized into three major units.

**Unit 01: Fourier Series** begins with the foundational question of how periodic functions can be represented as infinite sums of sines and cosines. Before writing down a single coefficient formula, we build the proper conceptual framework: periodic functions, the notion of an inner product on a space of functions, and orthogonality of the trigonometric basis. With these tools in hand, the Fourier series coefficients emerge naturally as projections. We study when these series converge, what happens at discontinuities (the Gibbs phenomenon), and how the energy of a function is encoded in its Fourier coefficients (Parseval's identity). We then specialize to sine and cosine series for functions defined on an interval, and close with the elegant complex exponential form, which unifies the theory and prepares the ground for the Fourier transform.

**Unit 02: Fourier Transform** extends the decomposition from periodic functions to functions defined on all of $\mathbb{R}$. Taking the period to infinity in a Fourier series, discrete frequencies become a continuous spectrum, and the sum becomes an integral. The Fourier transform $\hat{f}(\xi) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x) e^{-2\pi i \xi x}\,dx$ carries a function into its frequency representation, and the inverse transform recovers it. We develop the key algebraic properties: linearity, shifting, scaling, differentiation, and the convolution theorem. We examine the deep symmetry expressed by Plancherel's theorem, which says the transform preserves the $L^2$ norm. We discuss tempered distributions, which extend the transform to objects like the Dirac delta. The unit closes with the discrete Fourier transform and the Fast Fourier Transform algorithm, which make computational Fourier analysis practical.

**Unit 03: Other Integral Transforms** surveys the family of integral transforms that arise naturally from different symmetries and geometries. The Laplace transform is revisited here as an integral transform, illuminating its relationship to the Fourier transform through analytic continuation. The Mellin transform, connected to multiplicative structure and the theory of Dirichlet series, appears in analytic number theory and the study of special functions. The Hankel transform arises when solving partial differential equations in cylindrical coordinates, where Bessel functions play the role that sines and cosines play in Cartesian settings. Finally, the $Z$-transform is the discrete-time counterpart of the Laplace transform, essential for the analysis of digital filters and difference equations.

## Prerequisites and Connections

A student approaching this material should be comfortable with single and multivariable calculus, including improper integrals. Familiarity with complex numbers and Euler's formula is essential. The unit on ordinary differential equations (earlier in this course) provides important motivation: Fourier series appear naturally as solution methods for PDEs, and the Laplace transform was already introduced as a tool for ODEs. Students who have seen linear algebra will find that the language of inner products and orthogonality used here directly generalizes the familiar Euclidean setting.

The material here forms the analytic backbone for the study of partial differential equations, which is the subject of Unit 05 of this course. It also connects to probability and statistics (characteristic functions are Fourier transforms of probability densities), to quantum mechanics (where the Fourier transform relates position and momentum representations), and to numerical analysis (where the FFT is one of the most important algorithms ever devised).
