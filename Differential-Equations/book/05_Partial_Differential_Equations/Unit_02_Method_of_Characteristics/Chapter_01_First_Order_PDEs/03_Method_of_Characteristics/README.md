# The Method of Characteristics: Full Development

The method of characteristics reaches its fullest generality for nonlinear first-order PDEs of the form

$$F(x, y, u, u_x, u_y) = 0, \tag{1}$$

where $F$ is a given smooth function of five arguments. Setting $p = u_x$ and $q = u_y$, the equation is $F(x,y,u,p,q) = 0$. The unknowns are not only the values of $u$ but also the values of its partial derivatives $p$ and $q$, which must simultaneously satisfy (1) and the compatibility condition $p_y = q_x$ (since $p = u_x$ and $q = u_y$ and mixed partials are equal).

The characteristic equations for (1) are **Charpit's equations**:

$$\frac{dx}{dt} = F_p, \quad \frac{dy}{dt} = F_q, \quad \frac{du}{dt} = pF_p + qF_q, \quad \frac{dp}{dt} = -(F_x + pF_u), \quad \frac{dq}{dt} = -(F_y + qF_u).$$

These five equations propagate a **characteristic strip** $(x(t),y(t),u(t),p(t),q(t))$ — a curve in 5-dimensional jet space — along which the data is compatible. The solution surface $z = u(x,y)$ is generated by sweeping out these strips from an initial strip lying in the surface.

## Derivation of Charpit's Equations

The idea is to find curves along which the PDE reduces to an ODE. Differentiate $F(x,y,u,p,q)=0$ with respect to $t$ along a candidate curve $(x(t),y(t))$:

$$F_x \dot{x} + F_y \dot{y} + F_u(p\dot{x}+q\dot{y}) + F_p \dot{p} + F_q \dot{q} = 0.$$

Separately, differentiating $p = u_x$ with respect to $t$:

$$\dot{p} = p_x\dot{x} + p_y\dot{y} = u_{xx}\dot{x} + u_{xy}\dot{y}.$$

Similarly $\dot{q} = u_{xy}\dot{x} + u_{yy}\dot{y}$. To avoid the second derivatives $u_{xx}$, $u_{xy}$, $u_{yy}$, we choose $\dot{x} = F_p$ and $\dot{y} = F_q$. Then:

$$\dot{p} = u_{xx}F_p + u_{xy}F_q = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(u_x F_p + u_y F_q) - u_x(F_p)_x - u_y(F_q)_x = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(\text{something}) - \ldots$$

A cleaner derivation uses the fact that $F(x,y,u,p,q)=0$ implies, upon differentiating with respect to $x$ and $y$ (holding the surface in mind):

$$F_x + p F_u + u_{xx}F_p + u_{xy}F_q = 0 \implies \dot{p} = u_{xx}F_p + u_{xy}F_q = -(F_x + pF_u).$$

Similarly $\dot{q} = -(F_y + qF_u)$. The equation for $\dot{u}$ is simply $\dot{u} = p\dot{x} + q\dot{y} = pF_p + qF_q$ (the chain rule). This yields all five Charpit equations.

## The Eikonal Equation

The eikonal equation $|\nabla u|^2 = u_x^2 + u_y^2 = 1$ (with $c^2 = 1$ for simplicity) arises in optics (where $u$ is the phase of a wave, and its level curves are wavefronts) and in the shortest-path problem (where $u$ is travel time).

Here $F = p^2 + q^2 - 1$, so $F_p = 2p$, $F_q = 2q$, $F_x = F_y = F_u = 0$.

Charpit's equations become:

$$\dot{x} = 2p, \quad \dot{y} = 2q, \quad \dot{u} = 2(p^2+q^2) = 2, \quad \dot{p} = 0, \quad \dot{q} = 0.$$

So $p$ and $q$ are constant along characteristics; the characteristics are straight lines $x = x_0 + 2p_0 t$, $y = y_0 + 2q_0 t$; and $u$ increases at rate $2$ along each characteristic.

**Geometric interpretation.** The vector $(2p, 2q)$ has magnitude $2\sqrt{p^2+q^2} = 2$ (since $p^2+q^2=1$). The characteristics are rays perpendicular to the wavefronts $\{u=\text{const}\}$ — exactly the rays of geometric optics. The eikonal equation is thus the mathematical expression of the principle that wavefronts propagate perpendicular to themselves.

## Hamilton-Jacobi Equations

The Hamilton-Jacobi equation $u_t + H(x, \nabla_x u) = 0$ is a fully nonlinear first-order PDE of enormous importance in classical mechanics, optimal control, and differential games. Writing $y = t$ and $p = u_x$, $q = u_t$, the equation is $q + H(x,p) = 0$, so $F(x,t,u,p,q) = q + H(x,p)$.

Charpit's equations give:

$$\dot{x} = H_p(x,p), \quad \dot{t} = 1, \quad \dot{u} = pH_p - H, \quad \dot{p} = -H_x(x,p), \quad \dot{q} = 0.$$

The equations $\dot{x} = H_p$ and $\dot{p} = -H_x$ are Hamilton's equations of classical mechanics (with $H$ the Hamiltonian, $x$ the position, and $p$ the momentum). The characteristics of the Hamilton-Jacobi equation are the trajectories of particles in the corresponding mechanical system. This profound connection — between the wave-like Hamilton-Jacobi PDE and the particle-like Hamiltonian ODEs — was at the heart of the development of quantum mechanics.

## The Complete Integral

A **complete integral** of $F(x,y,u,p,q) = 0$ is a two-parameter family of solutions $u = \phi(x,y,a,b)$ (depending on two arbitrary constants $a$ and $b$) such that the Jacobian $\det\bigl(\partial(\phi_a,\phi_b)/\partial(x,y)\bigr) \neq 0$. From a complete integral, one can obtain the general solution and all singular solutions by envelope constructions:

- **General solution:** eliminate $a$ from $\phi_a = 0$ and $u = \phi$, obtaining a one-parameter family; then eliminate $b$ similarly. The resulting relation is the general solution.
- **Singular solution:** the envelope of the family, obtained by eliminating both $a$ and $b$ from $u = \phi$, $\phi_a = 0$, $\phi_b = 0$.

## Worked Example: Clairaut's Equation

The PDE $u = xu_x + yu_y + f(u_x, u_y)$, i.e., $u = xp + yq + f(p,q)$, is Clairaut's equation for PDEs.

A complete integral is $u = ax + by + f(a,b)$ for constants $a$ and $b$ — a two-parameter family of planes. Check: $u_x = a = p$, $u_y = b = q$, and $xp + yq + f(p,q) = xa + yb + f(a,b) = u$. The singular solution is the envelope of this family of planes, found by differentiating with respect to $a$ and $b$:

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial a} = 0: \quad x + f_a(a,b) = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial u}{\partial b} = 0: \quad y + f_b(a,b) = 0.$$

These two equations implicitly define $a$ and $b$ as functions of $(x,y)$, and substituting back gives the singular solution. Geometrically, the singular solution is the envelope of the family of planes, which is a surface tangent to all the planes.

## Summary of the Full Method

To solve $F(x,y,u,p,q)=0$ with Cauchy data $u=\phi(x)$ on $y=0$:

1. From the initial data, determine $p_0 = \phi'(x_0)$ and $q_0$ from $F(x_0, 0, \phi(x_0), p_0, q_0) = 0$ (there may be multiple choices; each gives a different local solution branch).

2. Solve Charpit's equations with initial conditions $(x_0, 0, \phi(x_0), p_0, q_0)$.

3. The resulting family of characteristic strips sweeps out the solution surface.

4. Verify the strip condition: $du/dt = p\,dx/dt + q\,dy/dt$ must hold on the initial strip (this is a compatibility condition on the initial $p_0, q_0$).

The method is systematic and general, but the computations can become involved. For quasilinear and linear equations, the simpler forms of the characteristic equations (from the previous sections) are preferred in practice.
