# Unit 2: Method of Characteristics

When a particle moves through a medium carrying some quantity — temperature, concentration, vorticity — the evolution of that quantity is governed by a first-order PDE. The method of characteristics is the geometric tool that reduces this PDE to a family of ODEs, one for each path (characteristic curve) along which the quantity is transported. It is among the oldest and most powerful techniques in PDE theory, and its reach extends far beyond first-order equations: characteristic theory underlies the classification of second-order PDEs, the theory of shock waves and conservation laws, and the numerical method of characteristics used in computational fluid dynamics.

## The Central Idea

Consider the transport equation $u_t + c u_x = 0$ with initial data $u(x,0) = \phi(x)$. The equation says that the material derivative $du/dt = u_t + c u_x$ is zero along curves where $dx/dt = c$ — the characteristic lines $x - ct = \text{const}$. Along each characteristic, $u$ is constant, so $u(x,t) = \phi(x - ct)$. The PDE has been reduced to a trivial ODE.

For more general first-order equations $F(x,y,u,u_x,u_y) = 0$, the characteristics are curves in the $(x,y,u)$-space along which the solution surface is generated by propagating initial data. The method provides an explicit algorithm: parametrize initial data along a curve, transport it along characteristics (which requires solving a system of ODEs), and piece the results together to form the solution surface.

## Unit Overview

This unit develops the method of characteristics through two chapters.

**Chapter 1: First-Order PDEs** treats the simplest case — first-order PDEs — in increasing generality. Linear first-order equations are handled by integrating factor methods and simple characteristics. Quasilinear equations (where the characteristic directions depend on the solution itself) require solving a coupled system of ODEs. The full method of characteristics for general first-order equations (Hamilton-Jacobi type) is developed via the Charpit equations.

**Chapter 2: Shock Waves and Conservation Laws** addresses what happens when characteristics of a quasilinear equation cross — a singularity forms in the solution, and the classical smooth solution breaks down. This is not a failure of the method but a genuine physical phenomenon: shock waves form when neighboring characteristics converge. The Rankine-Hugoniot jump conditions determine the shock speed from the underlying conservation law, and the entropy (or admissibility) conditions select the physically correct weak solution from the multiple candidates that the algebra allows.

## Why Characteristics Are Fundamental

The method of characteristics is not just a solution technique — it is the geometric expression of the fundamental property of hyperbolic equations: information propagates along well-defined curves at finite speed. The characteristics of a first-order PDE are the trajectories along which signals travel. For a quasilinear conservation law $u_t + f(u)_x = 0$, the characteristic speed $f'(u)$ depends on the solution itself, so different parts of a wave packet travel at different speeds. When faster-moving parts catch up to slower-moving parts, the wave steepens and eventually breaks — shock formation.

This unit thus sets the stage for the entire theory of hyperbolic PDEs and explains, from first principles, why discontinuous solutions (shocks, rarefaction waves) arise naturally and must be accommodated in any complete theory of conservation laws.
