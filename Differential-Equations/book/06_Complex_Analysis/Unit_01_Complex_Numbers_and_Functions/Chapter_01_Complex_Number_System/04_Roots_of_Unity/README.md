# Roots of Unity

The $n$-th roots of unity are the solutions to the equation $z^n = 1$. Their study sits at the intersection of complex algebra, group theory, and geometry, and they serve as a prototype for the general problem of extracting roots in $\mathbb{C}$. The structure of roots of unity underlies the discrete Fourier transform, cyclotomic field theory, and several classical techniques for summing series using residues.

## Definition and Explicit Form

**Definition.** For a positive integer $n$, the $n$-th roots of unity are the $n$ complex numbers satisfying $z^n = 1$.

By the polar root formula derived in the previous section, with $w = 1 = e^{i \cdot 0}$, the $n$-th roots of unity are
$$\omega_k = e^{2\pi i k/n}, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots, n-1.$$

We denote the primitive root $\omega = \omega_1 = e^{2\pi i/n}$. Then $\omega_k = \omega^k$, so all $n$-th roots of unity are powers of $\omega$.

**Theorem.** The set $\mu_n = \{\omega^k : k = 0, 1, \ldots, n-1\}$ forms a cyclic group of order $n$ under multiplication, generated by $\omega = e^{2\pi i/n}$.

**Proof.** The set is closed under multiplication: $\omega^j \cdot \omega^k = \omega^{j+k} = \omega^{(j+k) \bmod n}$, which again belongs to $\mu_n$. The identity is $\omega^0 = 1$. The inverse of $\omega^k$ is $\omega^{n-k}$. Since $\omega$ generates all elements, $\mu_n$ is cyclic. $\square$

## Geometry

The $n$-th roots of unity lie on the unit circle $|z| = 1$, equally spaced at angular intervals of $2\pi/n$. They form the vertices of a regular $n$-gon inscribed in the unit circle.

For small values of $n$:
- $n = 1$: $\{1\}$.
- $n = 2$: $\{1, -1\}$, the vertices of a diameter.
- $n = 3$: $\{1, e^{2\pi i/3}, e^{4\pi i/3}\} = \{1, -\tfrac{1}{2} + i\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, -\tfrac{1}{2} - i\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\}$, an equilateral triangle.
- $n = 4$: $\{1, i, -1, -i\}$, a square.
- $n = 6$: $\{1, e^{i\pi/3}, e^{2i\pi/3}, -1, e^{4i\pi/3}, e^{5i\pi/3}\}$, a regular hexagon.

## Primitive Roots

**Definition.** An $n$-th root of unity $\zeta$ is called primitive if $\zeta^k \neq 1$ for $k = 1, 2, \ldots, n-1$, i.e., if $\zeta$ generates $\mu_n$.

The primitive $n$-th roots of unity are exactly those of the form $e^{2\pi i k/n}$ where $\gcd(k, n) = 1$. The number of primitive $n$-th roots of unity is $\varphi(n)$, Euler's totient function.

**Examples.**
- For $n = 6$: the primitive roots are $e^{2\pi i/6} = e^{i\pi/3}$ and $e^{2\pi i \cdot 5/6} = e^{5i\pi/3}$, since $\gcd(1,6) = \gcd(5,6) = 1$. Indeed $\varphi(6) = 2$.
- For $n = p$ prime: every root other than $1$ is primitive, giving $\varphi(p) = p - 1$ primitive roots.

## Algebraic Properties: Cyclotomic Polynomials

**Definition.** The $n$-th cyclotomic polynomial is
$$\Phi_n(z) = \prod_{\substack{k=1 \\ \gcd(k,n)=1}}^{n} (z - e^{2\pi i k/n}).$$

This is the minimal polynomial over $\mathbb{Q}$ of any primitive $n$-th root of unity. The cyclotomic polynomials factor $z^n - 1$ completely:
$$z^n - 1 = \prod_{d \mid n} \Phi_d(z).$$

The first few are: $\Phi_1(z) = z - 1$, $\Phi_2(z) = z + 1$, $\Phi_3(z) = z^2 + z + 1$, $\Phi_4(z) = z^2 + 1$, $\Phi_6(z) = z^2 - z + 1$.

## The Orthogonality Relation

**Theorem.** For any integers $m$ and $n \geq 1$:
$$\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} e^{2\pi i mk/n} = \begin{cases} n & \text{if } n \mid m, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$

**Proof.** If $n \mid m$, each term equals $1$ and the sum is $n$. Otherwise, let $\omega = e^{2\pi i m/n} \neq 1$. The sum is a geometric series:
$$\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \omega^k = \frac{\omega^n - 1}{\omega - 1} = \frac{(e^{2\pi i m/n})^n - 1}{\omega - 1} = \frac{e^{2\pi i m} - 1}{\omega - 1} = \frac{1 - 1}{\omega - 1} = 0. \quad \square$$

This is the discrete analogue of the orthogonality of complex exponentials in Fourier analysis, and it is the foundation of the discrete Fourier transform.

## Application: Sum of Roots of Unity

**Worked example.** Show that the sum of all $n$-th roots of unity is zero for $n \geq 2$.

The roots of unity are the roots of $z^n - 1 = 0$. By Vieta's formulas, the sum of the roots equals the negative of the coefficient of $z^{n-1}$ divided by the leading coefficient. Since $z^n - 1$ has no $z^{n-1}$ term, the sum is $0$.

Alternatively: $\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \omega^k = \frac{\omega^n - 1}{\omega - 1} = \frac{0}{\omega - 1} = 0$ for $\omega \neq 1$. $\square$

## Application: Selecting Every $n$-th Term of a Series

The orthogonality relation provides an elegant method for isolating every $n$-th term in a power series. If $f(z) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty a_k z^k$, then
$$\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} f(\omega^j z) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} a_{kn} z^{kn},$$
where $\omega = e^{2\pi i/n}$. This is because $\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \omega^{jm} = \mathbf{1}_{n \mid m}$, so only the terms with $n \mid k$ survive.

**Worked example.** Express $1 + \frac{x^3}{3!} + \frac{x^6}{6!} + \cdots$ in closed form.

Apply the above with $f(z) = e^z$, $n = 3$, $z = x$, and $\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$:
$$\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^{3k}}{(3k)!} = \frac{1}{3}\bigl(e^x + e^{\omega x} + e^{\omega^2 x}\bigr) = \frac{1}{3}\left(e^x + 2e^{-x/2}\cos\!\left(\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}x\right)\right).$$

## Roots of Unity in Residue Computations

In Unit 04, we will use residues to evaluate sums of the form $\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} f(n)$. The poles of $\pi \cot(\pi z)$ at the integers are the key tool, but roots of unity also appear in the selection of pole contributions when $f$ has a periodic structure. Understanding $\mu_n$ thoroughly now pays dividends in those later computations.
