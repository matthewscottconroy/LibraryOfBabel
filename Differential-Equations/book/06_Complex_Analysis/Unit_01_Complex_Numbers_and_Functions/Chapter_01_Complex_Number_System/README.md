# Chapter 01: The Complex Number System

The complex number system $\mathbb{C}$ can be introduced in two equivalent ways: as a formal algebraic extension of $\mathbb{R}$ obtained by adjoining a root of $x^2 + 1 = 0$, or concretely as the set $\mathbb{R}^2$ equipped with a carefully chosen multiplication rule. Both approaches yield the same structure, and familiarity with both is valuable. This chapter develops $\mathbb{C}$ carefully, emphasizing the interplay between its algebraic properties and its geometry as the Euclidean plane.

## Section 01: Algebra of Complex Numbers

A complex number is a pair $(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2$, written as $z = x + iy$, where addition is component-wise and multiplication is defined by $(x_1 + iy_1)(x_2 + iy_2) = (x_1 x_2 - y_1 y_2) + i(x_1 y_2 + x_2 y_1)$. This multiplication rule is forced by the requirement that $i^2 = -1$ and that the distributive law holds.

**Key theorem:** $\mathbb{C}$ is a field. Every nonzero complex number $z = x + iy$ has a multiplicative inverse:
$$z^{-1} = \frac{x - iy}{x^2 + y^2} = \frac{\bar{z}}{|z|^2}.$$
The conjugate $\bar{z} = x - iy$ and modulus $|z| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ are the primary tools for working with reciprocals and quotients.

## Section 02: Geometric Representation

Identifying $z = x + iy$ with the point $(x,y)$ in the plane produces the **Argand diagram** or complex plane. Geometric operations correspond to algebraic ones: addition is vector addition, multiplication by $i$ is a $90^\circ$ counterclockwise rotation, and multiplication by a general complex number $w = |w|e^{i\arg w}$ is a rotation by $\arg w$ combined with scaling by $|w|$.

**Key theorem (Triangle inequality):** For all $z, w \in \mathbb{C}$,
$$|z + w| \leq |z| + |w|.$$
A consequence is the reverse triangle inequality $\bigl||z| - |w|\bigr| \leq |z - w|$, which is frequently used in estimation arguments.

The **argument** of a nonzero complex number, $\arg z$, is the angle $\theta$ such that $z = |z|(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)$. It is defined modulo $2\pi$. The principal argument $\mathrm{Arg}\, z \in (-\pi, \pi]$ is the standard single-valued selection.

## Section 03: Polar Form and de Moivre's Theorem

Once the complex exponential is defined (rigorously justified in Unit 02), the polar form $z = r e^{i\theta}$ makes multiplication transparent:
$$z_1 z_2 = r_1 r_2\, e^{i(\theta_1 + \theta_2)}.$$
**De Moivre's theorem:** For any integer $n$ and any argument $\theta$,
$$(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^n = \cos(n\theta) + i\sin(n\theta).$$
This theorem, once proved formally via the polar form and properties of the exponential, gives an efficient method for computing powers and for deriving trigonometric identities.

## Section 04: Roots of Unity

The $n$-th roots of unity are the solutions to $z^n = 1$. By de Moivre's theorem these are exactly $\omega_k = e^{2\pi i k/n}$ for $k = 0, 1, \ldots, n-1$. Geometrically they are evenly spaced on the unit circle, forming the vertices of a regular $n$-gon.

**Key theorem:** The $n$-th roots of unity form a cyclic group of order $n$ under multiplication, generated by the primitive root $\omega = e^{2\pi i/n}$.

More generally, the $n$-th roots of any $w = r e^{i\phi}$ are
$$z_k = r^{1/n} e^{i(\phi + 2\pi k)/n}, \quad k = 0, 1, \ldots, n-1,$$
forming a regular $n$-gon of radius $r^{1/n}$ centered at the origin.

## Preview of Key Ideas

This chapter establishes the algebraic and geometric scaffolding on which the rest of complex analysis rests. The polar form and the group structure of the $n$-th roots of unity are not merely computational conveniences; they foreshadow the deep role that the exponential function plays throughout the subject. The conjugate $\bar{z}$ and modulus $|z|$ appear in the Cauchy-Riemann equations, in estimates for contour integrals, and in the statement of the Schwarz reflection principle.
