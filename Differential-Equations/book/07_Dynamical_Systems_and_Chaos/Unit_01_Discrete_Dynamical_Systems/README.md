# Unit 1: Discrete Dynamical Systems

A discrete dynamical system is defined by a set $X$ and a function $f: X \to X$. The dynamics are generated by repeated application of $f$: starting from an initial point $x_0 \in X$, one produces the sequence $x_0, x_1 = f(x_0), x_2 = f(x_1), \ldots$, called the orbit of $x_0$ under $f$. The central question of the theory is: what can be said about the long-term behavior of orbits?

## From Simple Rules to Complex Behavior

The surprising message of discrete dynamical systems theory is that very simple functions can generate orbits of extraordinary complexity. The logistic map $f_r(x) = rx(1-x)$ on the interval $[0,1]$ depends on a single parameter $r \in [0,4]$ and is a quadratic polynomial. Yet as $r$ increases from 0 to 4, the qualitative behavior of typical orbits undergoes a sequence of dramatic changes: stable equilibria give way to stable oscillations of period 2, then period 4, then 8, eventually transitioning to chaos in which nearby orbits diverge exponentially.

This behavior is not a quirk of the logistic map. It is universal, appearing in any smooth one-parameter family of maps of the interval with a single quadratic maximum. The mathematical explanation lies in the renormalization theory developed by Feigenbaum, Coullet, and Tresser in the late 1970s.

## Chapter Overview

**Chapter 1: Iteration and Maps** lays the foundational vocabulary. Fixed points are values satisfying $f(x^*) = x^*$, and their stability is determined by the derivative $|f'(x^*)|$. Periodic orbits are fixed points of iterates $f^n$, and the same stability criterion applies. The chapter develops graphical analysis (cobweb diagrams) as a tool for visualizing orbit behavior and introduces the logistic map as the primary running example.

**Chapter 2: Route to Chaos** traces the mechanism by which stable periodic behavior transitions to chaos. Period doubling bifurcations occur at a sequence of parameter values $r_1 < r_2 < r_3 < \cdots$ converging to a critical value $r_\infty$. The ratio $(r_n - r_{n-1})/(r_{n+1} - r_n)$ converges to the Feigenbaum constant $\delta \approx 4.669$. Beyond $r_\infty$, the dynamics include intervals of chaos interspersed with windows of periodic behavior. Strange attractors—sets of fractal dimension on which orbits appear to wander without pattern—provide the geometric picture of chaotic dynamics.

## Key Concepts and Techniques

The primary analytical tools in this unit are:

- **Linearization.** Near a fixed point, the map looks like multiplication by $f'(x^*)$. Stability follows if $|f'(x^*)| < 1$.
- **The chain rule for iterates.** The derivative of $f^n$ at a periodic point decomposes as a product of derivatives along the orbit, enabling stability analysis of periodic orbits.
- **Bifurcation analysis.** Qualitative changes in dynamics occur when eigenvalue conditions are violated, and the implicit function theorem governs when fixed points can be continued as parameters vary.
- **Symbolic dynamics.** Orbits can be encoded as sequences of symbols (e.g., 0 if $x < 1/2$, else 1), reducing the study of complicated orbits to combinatorics of symbol sequences.

## Connections to Continuous Systems

Although this unit focuses on maps, the theory connects tightly to continuous dynamical systems. Every autonomous ODE in $\mathbb{R}^n$ generates a Poincaré map on a transversal hypersurface, reducing the study of periodic orbits in the flow to fixed point problems for a discrete map. The period-doubling route to chaos observed in the logistic map appears in experimental data from driven oscillators, chemical reactions, and fluid convection—systems governed by differential equations. The universality of the Feigenbaum constants accounts for why the same numerical values appear across such different physical contexts.
