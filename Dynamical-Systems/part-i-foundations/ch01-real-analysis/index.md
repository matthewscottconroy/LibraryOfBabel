# Chapter 1 — Real Analysis

> *The language of dynamical systems is analysis. Before orbits, attractors, and entropy, there must be limits, compactness, and continuity.*

---

Every subject in mathematics has a native language, and for dynamical systems that language is real analysis. This is not about forcing elegance for its own sake. It's practical: the objects we'll eventually study — orbits, attractors, invariant measures, Lyapunov exponents — are defined using limits, norms, and topologies. Without fluency in those concepts, the deeper theorems are just incantations.

This chapter builds the metric space framework that everything else lives in. If you've seen calculus and a little linear algebra, you already have the right intuitions. What we're doing here is making those intuitions precise enough to use in genuinely difficult arguments.

We assume you're comfortable with calculus, a semester of linear algebra, and naive set theory — meaning you know what a set, function, and equivalence relation are, without worrying about the foundations of mathematics.

**What this chapter builds:**

The metric space framework is the lingua franca of dynamical systems — nearly every object we study is a metric space or a map between metric spaces. From there we develop compactness, the single most powerful property a space can have, and the source of most existence theorems in dynamics. The Banach and Hilbert spaces in the later sections are the infinite-dimensional arenas where transfer operators and spectral methods live. And the Baire Category Theorem, which closes the chapter, is the primary tool for proving that "generic" dynamical systems have specified properties — it's how we say "most maps behave this way" with mathematical precision.
