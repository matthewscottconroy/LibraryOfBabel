# Chapter 2 — Measure Theory and Probability

> *Probability is measure theory with a normalization condition. Ergodic theory is the study of measure-preserving maps. Before either, there must be measures.*

**Prerequisites:** Chapter 1 (metric spaces, Banach spaces).

**What this chapter builds:** The rigorous foundation for probability, integration, and the $L^p$ spaces on which dynamical systems act. The Radon-Nikodym theorem and conditional expectation are essential for entropy theory. The law of large numbers is the classical predecessor of the Birkhoff Ergodic Theorem.

---

## 2.1 σ-Algebras and Measurable Spaces

### 2.1.1 σ-Algebras

**Definition 2.1.1.** A *σ-algebra* on a set $\Omega$ is a collection $\mathcal{F} \subseteq 2^\Omega$ satisfying:
1. $\Omega \in \mathcal{F}$
2. If $A \in \mathcal{F}$ then $A^c = \Omega \setminus A \in \mathcal{F}$ (closed under complements)
3. If $A_1, A_2, \ldots \in \mathcal{F}$ then $\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n \in \mathcal{F}$ (closed under countable unions)

The pair $(\Omega, \mathcal{F})$ is a *measurable space*. Elements of $\mathcal{F}$ are *measurable sets*.

**Remark 2.1.2.** From (1)-(3) we deduce: $\emptyset \in \mathcal{F}$, and $\mathcal{F}$ is closed under countable intersections (by de Morgan's law). The word "countable" is crucial: uncountable unions of measurable sets need not be measurable.

**Examples 2.1.3.**
- The *trivial σ-algebra* $\{\emptyset, \Omega\}$ — the coarsest possible
- The *discrete σ-algebra* $2^\Omega$ — the finest possible
- The *Borel σ-algebra* $\mathcal{B}(X)$ on a topological space $X$: the smallest σ-algebra containing all open sets. Its elements are *Borel sets*.

**Proposition 2.1.4.** For any collection $\mathcal{C} \subseteq 2^\Omega$, there is a smallest σ-algebra containing $\mathcal{C}$, called the *σ-algebra generated by $\mathcal{C}$*, denoted $\sigma(\mathcal{C})$.

*Proof:* Take the intersection of all σ-algebras containing $\mathcal{C}$ (there is at least one: $2^\Omega$). An intersection of σ-algebras is a σ-algebra.

**Definition 2.1.5.** A function $f: (\Omega, \mathcal{F}) \to (Y, \mathcal{G})$ is *measurable* if $f^{-1}(B) \in \mathcal{F}$ for all $B \in \mathcal{G}$.

For real-valued functions: $f: \Omega \to {\mathbb R}$ is $\mathcal{F}$-measurable if $f^{-1}((a, \infty)) \in \mathcal{F}$ for all $a \in {\mathbb R}$, equivalently if $f^{-1}(B) \in \mathcal{F}$ for all Borel sets $B \subseteq {\mathbb R}$.

## 2.2 Measures and Their Construction

### 2.2.1 Measures

**Definition 2.2.1.** A *measure* on $(\Omega, \mathcal{F})$ is a function $\mu: \mathcal{F} \to [0, \infty]$ satisfying:
1. $\mu(\emptyset) = 0$
2. If $A_1, A_2, \ldots \in \mathcal{F}$ are pairwise disjoint, then $\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\right) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu(A_n)$ (*countable additivity*)

The triple $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)$ is a *measure space*. If $\mu(\Omega) < \infty$, $\mu$ is a *finite measure*. If $\mu(\Omega) = 1$, $\mu$ is a *probability measure*.

**Examples 2.2.2.**
- *Lebesgue measure* $\lambda$ on $({\mathbb R}, \mathcal{B}({\mathbb R}))$: $\lambda([a,b]) = b - a$. More generally, $\lambda^n$ on ${\mathbb R}^n$ assigns to each box $[a_1,b_1] \times \cdots \times [a_n,b_n]$ the measure $(b_1-a_1)\cdots(b_n-a_n)$.
- *Counting measure* on $({\mathbb N}, 2^{\mathbb N})$: $\mu(A) = |A|$ (the cardinality of $A$).
- *Dirac measure* $\delta_x$ concentrated at $x$: $\delta_x(A) = 1$ if $x \in A$, else $0$.
- *Discrete probability measure*: $\mu = \sum_i p_i \delta_{x_i}$ where $p_i \geq 0$ and $\sum p_i = 1$.

**Proposition 2.2.3 (Properties of Measures).**
1. *Monotonicity:* $A \subseteq B$ implies $\mu(A) \leq \mu(B)$.
2. *Subadditivity:* $\mu(\bigcup_n A_n) \leq \sum_n \mu(A_n)$.
3. *Continuity from below:* If $A_1 \subseteq A_2 \subseteq \cdots$, then $\mu(\bigcup_n A_n) = \lim_n \mu(A_n)$.
4. *Continuity from above:* If $A_1 \supseteq A_2 \supseteq \cdots$ and $\mu(A_1) < \infty$, then $\mu(\bigcap_n A_n) = \lim_n \mu(A_n)$.

### 2.2.2 Carathéodory's Extension Theorem

**Theorem 2.2.4 (Carathéodory).** Let $\mathcal{A}$ be an algebra on $\Omega$ (closed under finite unions and complements) and $\mu_0: \mathcal{A} \to [0, \infty]$ a countably additive function on $\mathcal{A}$. Then $\mu_0$ extends to a measure on $\sigma(\mathcal{A})$. If $\mu_0(\Omega) < \infty$, the extension is unique.

*Application:* Lebesgue measure is constructed by defining length on intervals (an algebra) and extending via Carathéodory's theorem to the Borel σ-algebra, then completing to the Lebesgue σ-algebra.

**Definition 2.2.5.** A measure space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)$ is *complete* if: whenever $A \in \mathcal{F}$, $\mu(A) = 0$, and $B \subseteq A$, we have $B \in \mathcal{F}$. The *completion* of any measure space is obtained by adjoining all subsets of null sets.

## 2.3 Integration

### 2.3.1 The Lebesgue Integral

**Step 1: Simple Functions.**
A function $f = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i \mathbf{1}_{A_i}$ (with $A_i$ measurable, $a_i \geq 0$) is *simple*. Define $\int f\,d\mu = \sum_i a_i \mu(A_i)$.

**Step 2: Nonnegative Measurable Functions.**
For $f \geq 0$ measurable, define
$$\int f\,d\mu = \sup\left\{\int \varphi\,d\mu : 0 \leq \varphi \leq f,\; \varphi \text{ simple}\right\}.$$

**Step 3: Integrable Functions.**
Write $f = f^+ - f^-$ where $f^+ = \max(f, 0)$ and $f^- = \max(-f, 0)$. If both $\int f^+\,d\mu$ and $\int f^-\,d\mu$ are finite, define $\int f\,d\mu = \int f^+\,d\mu - \int f^-\,d\mu$.

**Definition 2.3.1.** $f$ is *integrable* (or $f \in L^1(\mu)$) if $\int |f|\,d\mu < \infty$.

### 2.3.2 Convergence Theorems

These are the core computational tools of analysis.

**Theorem 2.3.2 (Monotone Convergence Theorem).** If $0 \leq f_1 \leq f_2 \leq \cdots$ are measurable and $f_n \to f$ pointwise, then $\int f\,d\mu = \lim_n \int f_n\,d\mu$.

**Theorem 2.3.3 (Fatou's Lemma).** If $f_n \geq 0$ measurable, then $\int \liminf_n f_n\,d\mu \leq \liminf_n \int f_n\,d\mu$.

**Theorem 2.3.4 (Dominated Convergence Theorem).** If $f_n \to f$ pointwise $\mu$-a.e., and $|f_n| \leq g$ for some integrable $g$, then $f$ is integrable and $\int f\,d\mu = \lim_n \int f_n\,d\mu$.

**Application in Dynamics:** These theorems are used constantly. When proving a sequence of invariant measures converges, or when passing a limit through an integral, dominated convergence is invoked. Fatou's lemma gives the key inequality in the proof of the Birkhoff Ergodic Theorem.

## 2.4 $L^p$ Spaces

**Definition 2.4.1.** For $1 \leq p < \infty$, the space $L^p(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)$ consists of (equivalence classes of $\mu$-a.e.-equal) measurable functions $f$ with $\int |f|^p\,d\mu < \infty$, normed by
$$\|f\|_p = \left(\int |f|^p\,d\mu\right)^{1/p}.$$
For $p = \infty$: $L^\infty(\mu)$ consists of essentially bounded functions with $\|f\|_\infty = \text{ess sup}|f|$.

**Theorem 2.4.2 (Hölder's Inequality).** If $1/p + 1/q = 1$ (conjugate exponents), then for $f \in L^p$ and $g \in L^q$:
$$\int |fg|\,d\mu \leq \|f\|_p \|g\|_q.$$

*(proof sketch)* Young's inequality: $ab \leq a^p/p + b^q/q$ for $a, b \geq 0$. Apply to $a = |f|/\|f\|_p$ and $b = |g|/\|g\|_q$.

**Theorem 2.4.3 (Minkowski's Inequality).** $\|f + g\|_p \leq \|f\|_p + \|g\|_p$.

**Theorem 2.4.4 (Riesz-Fischer).** For $1 \leq p \leq \infty$, $L^p(\mu)$ is a Banach space.

**Theorem 2.4.5 (Duality).** For $1 \leq p < \infty$, the dual of $L^p(\mu)$ is $L^q(\mu)$ where $1/p + 1/q = 1$: every bounded linear functional on $L^p$ has the form $f \mapsto \int fg\,d\mu$ for a unique $g \in L^q$.

**Special Case:** $L^2(\mu)$ is a Hilbert space with inner product $\langle f, g \rangle = \int fg\,d\mu$. This is the natural function space for spectral analysis of dynamical systems.

## 2.5 Radon-Nikodym Theorem

This theorem is fundamental to the definition of conditional expectation and to entropy theory.

**Definition 2.5.1.** A measure $\nu$ is *absolutely continuous* with respect to $\mu$ (written $\nu \ll \mu$) if $\mu(A) = 0$ implies $\nu(A) = 0$. $\nu$ is *singular* with respect to $\mu$ (written $\nu \perp \mu$) if there exists $A$ with $\mu(A) = 0$ and $\nu(A^c) = 0$.

**Theorem 2.5.2 (Radon-Nikodym).** Let $\mu, \nu$ be σ-finite measures on $(\Omega, \mathcal{F})$ with $\nu \ll \mu$. Then there exists a unique (a.e.) nonnegative measurable function $h = d\nu/d\mu$ such that
$$\nu(A) = \int_A h\,d\mu \quad \text{for all } A \in \mathcal{F}.$$

The function $h = d\nu/d\mu$ is the *Radon-Nikodym derivative* or *density* of $\nu$ with respect to $\mu$.

**Theorem 2.5.3 (Lebesgue Decomposition).** Every σ-finite measure $\nu$ can be uniquely written as $\nu = \nu_{\text{ac}} + \nu_{\text{sing}}$ where $\nu_{\text{ac}} \ll \mu$ and $\nu_{\text{sing}} \perp \mu$.

**Application in Dynamics:** The Radon-Nikodym derivative appears in:
- The definition of Lyapunov exponents (the Jacobian $|\det Df|$ is $d(f_*\mu)/d\mu$)
- The construction of SRB measures (absolutely continuous conditional measures on unstable manifolds)
- Entropy theory: the information function $I(A|\mathcal{G})$ is defined via Radon-Nikodym derivatives

## 2.6 Probability Theory

### 2.6.1 Probability Spaces and Random Variables

**Definition 2.6.1.** A *probability space* is a measure space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ with $P(\Omega) = 1$. A *random variable* is a measurable function $X: \Omega \to {\mathbb R}$ (or into any measurable space). The *distribution* of $X$ is the probability measure $\mu_X = X_*P$ on ${\mathbb R}$ defined by $\mu_X(B) = P(X^{-1}(B)) = P(X \in B)$.

### 2.6.2 Independence

**Definition 2.6.2.** Events $A_1, \ldots, A_n \in \mathcal{F}$ are *independent* if $P(A_{i_1} \cap \cdots \cap A_{i_k}) = P(A_{i_1}) \cdots P(A_{i_k})$ for all subcollections. Random variables $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ are independent if $P(X_1 \in B_1, \ldots, X_n \in B_n) = \prod_i P(X_i \in B_i)$ for all Borel sets $B_i$.

Equivalently, $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ are independent if the σ-algebras $\sigma(X_1), \ldots, \sigma(X_n)$ they generate are independent.

### 2.6.3 Convergence Concepts

**Definition 2.6.3.** A sequence $(X_n)$ of random variables converges to $X$:
- *almost surely (a.s.)* if $P(\lim_n X_n = X) = 1$
- *in probability* if $P(|X_n - X| > \varepsilon) \to 0$ for all $\varepsilon > 0$
- *in $L^p$* if $E[|X_n - X|^p] \to 0$
- *in distribution* if $E[f(X_n)] \to E[f(X)]$ for all bounded continuous $f$

The implications: a.s. $\Rightarrow$ in probability $\Rightarrow$ in distribution; $L^p \Rightarrow$ in probability. None of the converse implications hold in general.

### 2.6.4 Conditional Expectation

**Definition 2.6.4.** Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ be a probability space, $X \in L^1(P)$, and $\mathcal{G} \subseteq \mathcal{F}$ a sub-σ-algebra. The *conditional expectation* $E[X | \mathcal{G}]$ is the unique (a.s.) $\mathcal{G}$-measurable random variable satisfying
$$\int_G E[X | \mathcal{G}]\,dP = \int_G X\,dP \quad \text{for all } G \in \mathcal{G}.$$

*Existence:* The measure $\nu(G) = \int_G X\,dP$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $P|_\mathcal{G}$, so Radon-Nikodym gives $d\nu/d(P|_\mathcal{G}) = E[X|\mathcal{G}]$.

**Properties of Conditional Expectation:**
1. $E[E[X|\mathcal{G}]] = E[X]$ (tower property)
2. If $X$ is $\mathcal{G}$-measurable, then $E[X|\mathcal{G}] = X$
3. If $X$ is independent of $\mathcal{G}$, then $E[X|\mathcal{G}] = E[X]$
4. $E[\cdot|\mathcal{G}]$ is the orthogonal projection from $L^2(\mathcal{F})$ onto $L^2(\mathcal{G})$
5. Tower property: $E[E[X|\mathcal{F}_1]|\mathcal{F}_2] = E[X|\mathcal{F}_2]$ if $\mathcal{F}_2 \subseteq \mathcal{F}_1$

**Application in Dynamics:** Conditional expectation is the key to defining the *information function* $I_\mu(\xi | \eta)$ of a partition $\xi$ given a partition $\eta$, which is central to the definition of entropy.

## 2.7 The Law of Large Numbers and Its Ergodic Generalization

### 2.7.1 Laws of Large Numbers

**Theorem 2.7.1 (Weak Law of Large Numbers).** Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ be i.i.d. with $E[X_1] = \mu < \infty$. Then $(X_1 + \cdots + X_n)/n \to \mu$ in probability.

**Theorem 2.7.2 (Strong Law of Large Numbers).** Under the same hypotheses, $(X_1 + \cdots + X_n)/n \to \mu$ almost surely.

*(proof sketch — Etemadi)* Assume $X_n \geq 0$. By Borel-Cantelli applied to $P(X_n > n) < \infty$ (since $E[X_1] < \infty$) and a blocking argument, one shows that the truncated sums converge.

**The Ergodic Perspective:** The SLLN applies to *independent* random variables. The Birkhoff Ergodic Theorem (Chapter 7) generalizes this to *stationary* random variables: the time average still converges to the space average, but with ergodicity as the hypothesis instead of independence.

### 2.7.2 The Central Limit Theorem

**Theorem 2.7.3 (CLT).** Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ be i.i.d. with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2 \in (0, \infty)$. Then
$$\frac{(X_1 + \cdots + X_n) - n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{n}} \xrightarrow{d} N(0, 1).$$

The CLT has dynamical analogues. For mixing dynamical systems, time-averages satisfy a CLT with a variance that depends on the correlation structure of the system.

## 2.8 Measures on Topological Spaces

When $X$ is a topological space, we want the measure to be compatible with the topology.

**Definition 2.8.1.** A *Borel measure* on a topological space $X$ is a measure on $(X, \mathcal{B}(X))$. A *Radon measure* is a Borel measure that is finite on compact sets, inner regular (every measurable set is approximated from within by compact sets), and outer regular (approximated from outside by open sets).

**Definition 2.8.2.** A sequence of Borel probability measures $(\mu_n)$ *converges weakly* (or *in the weak-* topology*) to $\mu$ if $\int f\,d\mu_n \to \int f\,d\mu$ for all bounded continuous functions $f$.

**Theorem 2.8.3 (Prokhorov's Theorem).** A family $\{\mu_\alpha\}$ of probability measures on a complete separable metric space $X$ has a weakly convergent subsequence if and only if it is *tight*: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a compact $K \subseteq X$ with $\mu_\alpha(K) > 1 - \varepsilon$ for all $\alpha$.

**Application in Dynamics:** Prokhorov's theorem is the primary tool for proving existence of invariant measures. The sequence of *Cesàro averages* $\mu_N = (1/N)\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} f^n_*\mu$ is always tight on compact spaces, so a convergent subsequence exists; any limit point is $f$-invariant.

## 2.9 Product Measures and Fubini's Theorem

**Definition 2.9.1.** Given measure spaces $(\Omega_1, \mathcal{F}_1, \mu_1)$ and $(\Omega_2, \mathcal{F}_2, \mu_2)$, the *product measure* $\mu_1 \otimes \mu_2$ on $(\Omega_1 \times \Omega_2, \mathcal{F}_1 \otimes \mathcal{F}_2)$ is defined on measurable rectangles by $(\mu_1 \otimes \mu_2)(A \times B) = \mu_1(A) \cdot \mu_2(B)$, extended by Carathéodory.

**Theorem 2.9.2 (Fubini-Tonelli).** Let $f: \Omega_1 \times \Omega_2 \to {\mathbb R}$ be measurable.
- *(Tonelli)* If $f \geq 0$: $\int\!\!\int f\,d(\mu_1 \otimes \mu_2) = \int \left(\int f(x,y)\,d\mu_1(x)\right) d\mu_2(y) = \int \left(\int f(x,y)\,d\mu_2(y)\right) d\mu_1(x)$.
- *(Fubini)* If $f \in L^1(\mu_1 \otimes \mu_2)$: all three iterated integrals exist, are equal, and the iterated integrals converge a.e.

**Application:** Product measures model independence. The *Bernoulli shift* $(\{0,1\}^{\mathbb Z}, \mathcal{B}, p^{\otimes {\mathbb Z}})$ is the prototype of an independent process, and its ergodic properties (mixing, entropy computation) are foundational.

## Chapter Notes

Measure theory is covered in Folland's *Real Analysis* (Chapters 1-3) and Royden-Fitzpatrick's *Real Analysis*. For probability theory, Durrett's *Probability: Theory and Examples* is the standard graduate text; for the foundations, see Billingsley's *Probability and Measure*.

The Radon-Nikodym theorem (Section 2.5) and conditional expectation (Section 2.6) are the measure-theoretic tools that recur most frequently in ergodic theory. Conditional entropy $H(\xi | \eta)$ is built from $E[I(\xi)|\sigma(\eta)]$, where $I(\xi)$ is the information function — see Chapter 22.

Prokhorov's theorem (Section 2.8) is the compactness theorem for probability measures on Polish spaces. It is the measure-theoretic analogue of the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem and is used in essentially every proof of existence for invariant measures.
