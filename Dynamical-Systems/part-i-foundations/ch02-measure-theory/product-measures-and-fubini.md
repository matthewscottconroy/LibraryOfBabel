# 2.9 Product Measures and Fubini's Theorem

We close this chapter with the product construction — how to build a measure on a product space from measures on the factors — and Fubini's theorem, which lets you compute integrals on product spaces by iterated integration.

**Definition 2.9.1.** Given measure spaces $(\Omega_1, \mathcal{F}_1, \mu_1)$ and $(\Omega_2, \mathcal{F}_2, \mu_2)$, the *product measure* $\mu_1 \otimes \mu_2$ on $(\Omega_1 \times \Omega_2, \mathcal{F}_1 \otimes \mathcal{F}_2)$ is defined on measurable rectangles by $(\mu_1 \otimes \mu_2)(A \times B) = \mu_1(A) \cdot \mu_2(B)$, extended by Carathéodory.

The product $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{F}_1 \otimes \mathcal{F}_2$ is the $\sigma$-algebra generated by the measurable rectangles $A \times B$ with $A \in \mathcal{F}_1$, $B \in \mathcal{F}_2$.

The product measure is the unique measure on the product that assigns $\mu_1(A) \cdot \mu_2(B)$ to rectangles. For probability measures, this is exactly the joint distribution of two independent random variables: $P(X \in A, Y \in B) = P(X \in A) P(Y \in B)$.

**Theorem 2.9.2 (Fubini-Tonelli).** Let $f: \Omega_1 \times \Omega_2 \to \mathbb{R}$ be measurable.
- *(Tonelli)* If $f \geq 0$: $\int\!\!\int f\,d(\mu_1 \otimes \mu_2) = \int \left(\int f(x,y)\,d\mu_1(x)\right) d\mu_2(y) = \int \left(\int f(x,y)\,d\mu_2(y)\right) d\mu_1(x)$.
- *(Fubini)* If $f \in L^1(\mu_1 \otimes \mu_2)$: all three iterated integrals exist, are equal, and the iterated integrals converge a.e.

What this is really saying: for nonneg functions, you can always switch the order of integration (both sides may be infinite, but they're equal). For integrable functions, you can switch order and get finite answers a.e.

Fubini's theorem is not about cleverness — it's about legitimacy. In calculus, we routinely switch the order of integration; Fubini's theorem is the theorem that says this is legal (under the right conditions). The condition for Fubini (as opposed to Tonelli) is that $f$ is integrable with respect to the product measure — but Tonelli tells you how to check this: compute either iterated integral; if it's finite, the function is integrable.

**Application.** Product measures model independence. The *Bernoulli shift* $(\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}, \mathcal{B}, p^{\otimes \mathbb{Z}})$ — independent coin flips at each integer time — is the prototype of an independent process, and its ergodic properties (it's strongly mixing, and its entropy is computable explicitly) are foundational for the theory. Fubini's theorem is used constantly when computing things like the correlation between observables at different times, or the distribution of a function of the orbit.

The product construction also underlies the *skew product* construction of dynamical systems: given a base map $T$ on $(X, \mu)$ and a family of fiber maps, the skew product is a map on $X \times Y$ with a product-like measure. This is the framework for studying random dynamical systems and group extensions, both of which appear in later chapters.
