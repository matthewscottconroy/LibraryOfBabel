# 2.1 σ-Algebras and Measurable Spaces

## 2.1.1 σ-Algebras

Before we can assign sizes to sets, we need to decide which sets are allowed to have sizes. This turns out to be a subtle question — not every collection of subsets can support a consistent notion of measure (the Vitali construction in Exercise 2.2 demonstrates this). The $\sigma$-algebra is the structure that specifies which sets are "measurable" — the ones we're allowed to assign numbers to.

**Definition 2.1.1.** A *σ-algebra* on a set $\Omega$ is a collection $\mathcal{F} \subseteq 2^\Omega$ satisfying:
1. $\Omega \in \mathcal{F}$
2. If $A \in \mathcal{F}$ then $A^c = \Omega \setminus A \in \mathcal{F}$ (closed under complements)
3. If $A_1, A_2, \ldots \in \mathcal{F}$ then $\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n \in \mathcal{F}$ (closed under countable unions)

The pair $(\Omega, \mathcal{F})$ is a *measurable space*. Elements of $\mathcal{F}$ are *measurable sets*.

The word "countable" in condition (3) is doing essential work. Uncountable unions of measurable sets need not be measurable — this is not a deficiency of the framework but a deliberate constraint that makes the theory work. Countable additivity (coming in Section 2.2) is what gives integration its power; it requires only countably many operations.

**Remark 2.1.2.** From conditions (1)-(3) we can deduce the rest: $\emptyset = \Omega^c \in \mathcal{F}$, and $\mathcal{F}$ is closed under countable intersections (by de Morgan's law: $\bigcap A_n = (\bigcup A_n^c)^c$).

Here's the range of $\sigma$-algebras you'll encounter:

**Examples 2.1.3.**
- The *trivial $\sigma$-algebra* $\{\emptyset, \Omega\}$ — the coarsest possible. Only the whole set and the empty set are measurable.
- The *discrete $\sigma$-algebra* $2^\Omega$ — the finest possible. Every subset is measurable.
- The *Borel $\sigma$-algebra* $\mathcal{B}(X)$ on a topological space $X$: the smallest $\sigma$-algebra containing all open sets. Its elements are *Borel sets*.

The Borel $\sigma$-algebra is the one you'll work with most. It's generated by the open sets, so every open set, closed set, $G_\delta$ (countable intersection of opens), $F_\sigma$ (countable union of closeds), and so on, is a Borel set. In $\mathbb{R}$, Borel sets include all intervals, all countable sets, all "nice" subsets you'd naturally encounter.

The key property of $\sigma$-algebras is that you can generate one from any collection:

**Proposition 2.1.4.** For any collection $\mathcal{C} \subseteq 2^\Omega$, there is a smallest $\sigma$-algebra containing $\mathcal{C}$, called the *$\sigma$-algebra generated by $\mathcal{C}$*, denoted $\sigma(\mathcal{C})$.

*Proof:* Take the intersection of all $\sigma$-algebras containing $\mathcal{C}$ (there is at least one: $2^\Omega$). An intersection of $\sigma$-algebras is a $\sigma$-algebra.

This proposition is used to show that $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}) = \sigma(\text{open intervals})$, and that the Borel $\sigma$-algebra on a product space is generated by the "rectangles" — see Exercise 2.1.

Measurability of functions is defined to make preimages behave well:

**Definition 2.1.5.** A function $f: (\Omega, \mathcal{F}) \to (Y, \mathcal{G})$ is *measurable* if $f^{-1}(B) \in \mathcal{F}$ for all $B \in \mathcal{G}$.

For real-valued functions: $f: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ is $\mathcal{F}$-measurable if $f^{-1}((a, \infty)) \in \mathcal{F}$ for all $a \in \mathbb{R}$, equivalently if $f^{-1}(B) \in \mathcal{F}$ for all Borel sets $B \subseteq \mathbb{R}$.

The definition is chosen so that compositions of measurable functions are measurable, and so that pointwise limits of measurable functions are measurable — both facts that fail if "measurable" is defined too weakly. The $\sigma$-algebra is exactly the right structure to make this work.

With measurable spaces in place, we can now assign numbers to measurable sets — that's a measure.
