# 3.5 Smooth Manifolds

Most dynamical systems in physics and geometry live on smooth manifolds — spaces that locally look like $\mathbb{R}^n$ but can have nontrivial global topology. The sphere, the torus, the group of rotations: these are all smooth manifolds. The differential structure (smoothness of coordinate changes) is what makes it possible to differentiate functions, define vector fields, and set up ODEs.

## 3.5.1 Definition and Charts

**Definition 3.5.1.** A *smooth $n$-manifold* is a Hausdorff second-countable topological space $M$ with a *smooth atlas*: a collection of homeomorphisms $\varphi_\alpha: U_\alpha \to \hat{U}_\alpha \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ (called *charts* or *coordinate maps*) with $M = \bigcup_\alpha U_\alpha$, such that the *transition maps* $\varphi_\beta \circ \varphi_\alpha^{-1}: \varphi_\alpha(U_\alpha \cap U_\beta) \to \varphi_\beta(U_\alpha \cap U_\beta)$ are smooth ($C^\infty$).

Think of it this way: a manifold is a space you can coordinatize in overlapping patches, and the transitions between patches are smooth. Each chart gives you local coordinates, and calculus works in each chart. The smoothness of transition maps ensures that calculus is coordinate-independent: a function that's smooth in one coordinate system is smooth in any other.

The essential examples:

**Examples 3.5.2.**
- $\mathbb{R}^n$: a single chart, the identity map. The trivial manifold.
- $S^n$: the $n$-sphere, covered by two stereographic projection charts (one from the north pole, one from the south).
- $\mathbb{T}^n = (\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^n$: the $n$-torus, locally homeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^n$ via local angle coordinates. Compact, and the natural phase space for angle variables.
- $GL(n, \mathbb{R})$: the group of invertible matrices, an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^{n^2}$ with the subspace smooth structure.

## 3.5.2 Tangent Bundle and Vector Fields

To do dynamics on a manifold, we need vector fields — infinitesimal generators of motion. To define a vector field, we need a notion of "tangent vector" at each point.

**Definition 3.5.3.** The *tangent space* $T_pM$ at a point $p \in M$ is the vector space of derivations on smooth functions at $p$, or equivalently the space of equivalence classes of smooth curves through $p$ (same first derivative in any chart). The *tangent bundle* is $TM = \bigsqcup_{p \in M} T_pM$ with its natural smooth structure.

The tangent bundle $TM$ is itself a smooth manifold of dimension $2n$. A point in $TM$ is a pair $(p, v)$ where $p \in M$ and $v \in T_pM$.

**Definition 3.5.4.** A *vector field* $X$ on $M$ is a smooth section of $TM$: a smooth map $X: M \to TM$ with $X(p) \in T_pM$ for each $p$.

A vector field assigns to each point a tangent vector — the "direction and speed" of a particle at that point. The fundamental theorem connecting vector fields and flows:

**Theorem 3.5.5.** A vector field $X$ on a compact smooth manifold $M$ generates a *flow* $\Phi: \mathbb{R} \times M \to M$ satisfying:
1. $\Phi(0, p) = p$ (identity at $t=0$)
2. $\Phi(t+s, p) = \Phi(t, \Phi(s, p))$ (group homomorphism property)
3. $\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0} \Phi(t, p) = X(p)$ (vector field generates the flow)

The maps $\Phi_t = \Phi(t, \cdot): M \to M$ are diffeomorphisms.

**Remark 3.5.6.** This theorem is essentially the Picard-Lindelöf theorem (Chapter 4) on manifolds. Compactness ensures the flow is *complete* (exists for all time). On non-compact manifolds, solutions may escape to infinity in finite time — think of the ODE $\dot{x} = x^2$ on $\mathbb{R}$, where solutions blow up.

## 3.5.3 Lie Groups

Some manifolds carry group structure as well as smooth structure:

**Definition 3.5.7.** A *Lie group* is a smooth manifold $G$ that is also a group, with smooth group operations (multiplication and inversion). Its *Lie algebra* $\mathfrak{g} = T_eG$ carries the bracket $[X, Y] = XY - YX$ (as left-invariant vector fields).

**Examples 3.5.8.**
- $GL(n, \mathbb{R})$ with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{gl}(n) = M_n(\mathbb{R})$ (all $n \times n$ matrices).
- $SO(n)$: the group of rotations, a compact Lie group with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ = skew-symmetric matrices.
- $SL(n, \mathbb{R})$: matrices of determinant 1, Lie algebra = traceless matrices.
- $\mathbb{T}^n = (\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^n$: the compact abelian Lie group, the natural home of angle variables.

The Lie algebra is the "infinitesimal" version of the group — the tangent space at the identity. The exponential map $\exp: \mathfrak{g} \to G$ connects the two: it integrates the left-invariant vector field generated by $X \in \mathfrak{g}$ to get a one-parameter subgroup in $G$. This is the Lie group version of the flow-vector field relationship.

Lie groups are the natural symmetry groups of dynamical systems. When a system has a symmetry, the Lie group structure provides conserved quantities (Noether's theorem) and reduces the dimension of the problem.
