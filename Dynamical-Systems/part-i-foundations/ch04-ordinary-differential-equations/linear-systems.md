# 4.3 Linear Systems

Linear systems — where the right-hand side of the ODE is a linear map — are the one class of systems we can solve completely and explicitly. They're also the foundation for understanding nonlinear systems near equilibria, because linearization converts a nonlinear problem into a linear one.

## 4.3.1 The Matrix Exponential

The linear IVP $\dot{x} = Ax$, $x(0) = x_0$ (with $A \in M_n(\mathbb{R})$) has the explicit solution:
$$x(t) = e^{tA} x_0, \quad \text{where } e^{tA} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{t^k A^k}{k!}.$$

This series converges for all $t$ and all matrices $A$. The function $t \mapsto e^{tA}$ is the one-parameter group generated by $A$ — it's the matrix version of the scalar exponential $e^{at}$.

Key properties of the matrix exponential:
- $e^{0} = I$
- $\frac{d}{dt} e^{tA} = A e^{tA}$
- $e^{(t+s)A} = e^{tA} e^{sA}$
- $\det(e^{tA}) = e^{t \cdot \text{tr}(A)}$ (Jacobi's formula — this determines when the flow is volume-preserving)
- If $A = PJP^{-1}$ (Jordan form), then $e^{tA} = Pe^{tJ}P^{-1}$

The last property is the key to computation: to find $e^{tA}$, put $A$ in Jordan form, exponentiate the Jordan blocks (which have simple formulas), and conjugate back.

## 4.3.2 Stability of Linear Systems

**Definition 4.3.1.** The equilibrium $x = 0$ of $\dot{x} = Ax$ is:
- *stable* if for all $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $\delta > 0$: $\|x_0\| < \delta \Rightarrow \|e^{tA}x_0\| < \varepsilon$ for all $t \geq 0$
- *asymptotically stable* if stable and $e^{tA}x_0 \to 0$ as $t \to \infty$ for all initial conditions
- *exponentially stable* if $\|e^{tA}\| \leq Ce^{-\lambda t}$ for some $C, \lambda > 0$

The complete stability picture is determined by eigenvalues:

**Theorem 4.3.2 (Stability Classification of Linear Systems).** Let $\sigma(A) = \{\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n\}$ be the spectrum of $A$ (eigenvalues counted with multiplicity).

- $x = 0$ is **exponentially stable** iff all $\text{Re}(\lambda_i) < 0$.
- $x = 0$ is **stable** iff all $\text{Re}(\lambda_i) \leq 0$ and eigenvalues with $\text{Re}(\lambda_i) = 0$ have Jordan blocks of size 1.
- $x = 0$ is **unstable** otherwise.

What this is really saying: stability is determined by the real parts of eigenvalues. Eigenvalues in the left half-plane correspond to decaying modes; in the right half-plane, to growing modes; on the imaginary axis, to oscillatory modes. The Jordan block condition for the marginal case (imaginary axis eigenvalues) matters because a size-2 Jordan block with $\lambda = 0$ gives a solution $te^{0 \cdot t} = t$ that grows linearly — not decaying, hence not stable.

**Definition 4.3.3.** $A \in M_n(\mathbb{R})$ is *hyperbolic* if no eigenvalue has zero real part, i.e., $\sigma(A) \cap i\mathbb{R} = \emptyset$.

For hyperbolic $A$, $\mathbb{R}^n = E^s \oplus E^u$ where $E^s$ = span of generalized eigenvectors for eigenvalues with $\text{Re}(\lambda) < 0$ (stable subspace) and $E^u$ = span of those with $\text{Re}(\lambda) > 0$ (unstable subspace).

The stable subspace $E^s$ consists of initial conditions that converge to 0 under the flow; the unstable subspace $E^u$ consists of initial conditions that diverge. For hyperbolic $A$, there's a clean dichotomy: no initial condition is neither stable nor unstable (there are no "marginal" modes). This clean dichotomy is what makes hyperbolic systems tractable — and the next section shows how this structure propagates to nonlinear systems near hyperbolic equilibria.
