# Chapter Notes — Chapter 7

A mentor's guide to the ergodic theory literature — what to read, in what order, and why.

---

**Start here.** Walters' *An Introduction to Ergodic Theory* (Springer, 1982) remains the best first book. It is complete, rigorous, and readable. The first six chapters cover everything in this chapter and nothing you don't need. Read it straight through at least once before branching out.

**The Soviet classic.** Cornfeld-Fomin-Sinai's *Ergodic Theory* (Springer, 1982) is the classical Russian treatment. It has a different flavor — more examples, a stronger measure-theoretic emphasis, and the historical context of Kolmogorov's school. Worth reading after Walters to see the same material from a different angle. The chapter on entropy (Chapter 10) is especially good.

**The modern research text.** Einsiedler and Ward's *Ergodic Theory with a View Towards Number Theory* (Springer, 2011) is outstanding. It covers everything in this chapter plus Ratner's theorem, equidistribution, and connections to homogeneous dynamics that are active research areas. The writing is clear and the examples from number theory are genuinely illuminating. If you intend to do research near this area, this is your book.

**The Birkhoff theorem.** Halmos' *Lectures on Ergodic Theory* (Chelsea, 1956) contains the cleanest proof of Birkhoff's theorem using the maximal ergodic inequality. It's short — barely 100 pages — and can be read in a weekend. The proof there is due to Garsia (1965) and is the one that has stood the test of time.

**Ornstein's theorem.** Ornstein's own book *Ergodic Theory, Randomness, and Dynamical Systems* (Yale, 1974) develops the $\bar{d}$-metric and the isomorphism theorem from scratch. It's technical but self-contained. The result is so deep that it's worth understanding the proof at least once. A more modern presentation is in Shields' *The Ergodic Theory of Discrete Sample Paths* (AMS, 1996).

**The connection to information theory.** This is not a digression — it's a deep parallel. The KS entropy of a dynamical system and the Shannon entropy of a stochastic process are the same object, viewed from different angles. The Shannon-McMillan-Breiman theorem (Chapter 23 of this book) makes this precise: it is the "ergodic AEP," saying that typical sequences have probability exponentially close to $2^{-h_\mu(f) \cdot n}$. Cover and Thomas' *Elements of Information Theory* gives the information-theoretic side; this chapter gives the dynamical side. Reading both in parallel is rewarding.

**Joinings.** The theory of joinings is Furstenberg's contribution, developed in *Recurrence in Ergodic Theory and Combinatorial Number Theory* (Princeton, 1981). That book is extraordinary — it proves Szemerédi's theorem via ergodic theory and along the way introduces joinings, multiple recurrence, and the connections to additive combinatorics that are still being actively developed. It's not an easy read, but it rewards effort.
