# Chapter Notes — Chapter 11

## On Lorenz and the Primary Sources

Start with the source: Lorenz's 1963 paper *Deterministic Nonperiodic Flow* in the *Journal of Atmospheric Science* is short (15 pages), beautifully written, and rewards careful reading. Lorenz was not doing mathematics — he was doing meteorology, and the fact that mathematicians had to reconstruct the rigorous framework around his observations took decades. That delay is itself instructive about how science works across disciplines.

Tucker's 2002 computer-assisted proof (*Foundations of Computational Mathematics*) that the Lorenz attractor is real and hyperbolic is a landmark. It is also a model for how rigorous interval arithmetic can be used to prove genuinely new mathematical theorems, not just verify known ones. If you are interested in computer-assisted proof as a research method, this is one of the best examples to study.

## On Fractal Geometry

Falconer's *Fractal Geometry: Mathematical Foundations and Applications* is the standard text, and it is good. It is careful, covers Hausdorff dimension thoroughly, and handles self-similar sets elegantly. For multifractal analysis specifically, Pesin's *Dimension Theory in Dynamical Systems* (Chicago Lectures in Mathematics) goes deeper and is the right reference for anyone who wants to do research in this area.

The survey paper by Eckmann and Ruelle — *Ergodic theory of chaos and strange attractors* in *Reviews of Modern Physics* (1985) — is an indispensable document. Written by two of the central figures in the field, it synthesizes the connections between Lyapunov exponents, entropy, dimensions, and SRB measures. Read it early; return to it often.

## On the Logistic Map and Universality

Feigenbaum's discovery of the period-doubling universality constants (1978) is covered in his original papers in the *Journal of Statistical Physics*. For a textbook treatment at the right level, Collet and Eckmann's *Iterated Maps on the Interval as Dynamical Systems* is rigorous and comprehensive. The connection to renormalization group theory is explored further in Chapter 13 (renormalization in complex dynamics) and is one of the deepest ideas in the subject.

## On Chaos and Information Theory

The connection between chaos and information production (Section 11.7) is deep and explicit through Pesin's formula: the Kolmogorov-Sinai entropy equals the sum of positive Lyapunov exponents. The original Pesin paper (1977, translated from Russian) is the mathematical source. Ruelle's *Thermodynamic Formalism* provides the broader context.

This connection will be made even more precise in the ergodic information theory of Chapter 23. If you are primarily interested in the information-theoretic side of chaos, that chapter is the destination; this chapter builds the tools you need to get there.

## Real Entry Points for Research

If you want to enter research through chaos theory, here are the live areas: the rigorous theory of non-uniformly hyperbolic systems (extending Pesin theory beyond Axiom A), where Ledrappier-Young dimension theory is central; the Kaplan-Yorke conjecture in its open cases; and the statistical properties of chaotic systems (decay of correlations, CLTs for observables). Benedicks-Carleson's work on the Hénon map is the model for how one proves results about genuinely non-hyperbolic systems, and it repays careful study.
