# 12.2 Subshifts

Most interesting symbolic systems are not the full shift. They are *subshifts*: closed, shift-invariant subsets of the full shift, where some sequences are forbidden. The constraint might be local (no two consecutive 1s), or global (the frequency of 0s must equal $1/\sqrt{2}$), or somewhere in between. Different types of constraints give different dynamical properties.

The first order of business is to understand how subshifts are characterized — and the answer is satisfying: a subshift is completely determined by its language of allowed finite words.

**Definition 12.2.1.** A *subshift* is a pair $(X, \sigma|_X)$ where $X \subseteq \mathcal{A}^{\mathbb Z}$ is a nonempty closed shift-invariant set ($\sigma(X) = X$).

The *language* of a subshift $X$ is:
$$\mathcal{L}(X) = \{w \in \mathcal{A}^* : w \text{ appears as a finite word in some } x \in X\}.$$

The language is the full combinatorial fingerprint of the subshift. Two subshifts are equal iff they have the same language. And every set of finite words that is "factorial" (subwords of allowed words are allowed) and "extensible" (every allowed word can be extended to a longer allowed word) arises as the language of some subshift.

**Theorem 12.2.2 (Characterization of Subshifts).** A nonempty closed set $X \subseteq \mathcal{A}^{\mathbb Z}$ is a subshift if and only if it is defined by a collection of *forbidden words*: there exists $\mathcal{F} \subseteq \mathcal{A}^*$ such that:
$$X = \{x \in \mathcal{A}^{\mathbb Z} : \text{no word from } \mathcal{F} \text{ appears in } x\}.$$

What this is saying is: every subshift is a "rule" — a list of forbidden patterns. No sequence may contain any word on the forbidden list. The forbidden list may be finite or infinite.

**Examples 12.2.3.**

- *The even shift:* $\mathcal{A} = \{0,1\}$, sequences where between any two consecutive 1s there is an even number of 0s. The forbidden words are $\{10^{2n+1}1 : n \geq 0\}$ — an infinite list of forbidden patterns of odd length. The even shift is a classic example of a subshift that is *not* of finite type (it requires infinitely many forbidden words) but is *sofic* (its language is regular).

- *The Thue-Morse shift:* The orbit closure of the Thue-Morse sequence $0110100110010110\ldots$, defined by $t_0 = 0$, $t_{2n} = t_n$, $t_{2n+1} = 1 - t_n$. This is a minimal subshift (every orbit is dense) and a prime example of a "morphic" system — generated by a substitution rule. The Thue-Morse sequence was originally discovered in the study of combinatorics on words and is now known to have connections to number theory, automata theory, and spectral theory.

- *The Fibonacci shift:* Defined by the single forbidden word $\{11\}$. No two consecutive 1s. The number of allowed words of length $n$ grows as the Fibonacci numbers — which is why it is named as it is. The Fibonacci shift is the prototypical subshift of finite type with golden-ratio entropy.

The language of a subshift is the combinatorial key to all of its dynamical properties. In the next section, we focus on the class with the simplest possible languages: subshifts of finite type.
