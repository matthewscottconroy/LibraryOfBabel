# Chapter Notes — Chapter 14

## The Beautiful Books

Arnold's *Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics* (Springer, 2nd ed.) is probably the most beautiful mathematics textbook ever written. This is not hyperbole — it is a view shared by many mathematicians. Arnold develops Hamiltonian mechanics from the perspective of differential geometry and symplectic topology, with a style that is simultaneously rigorous, geometric, and literary. It is also quite hard. Read it slowly, with a pencil.

For a more modern and comprehensive treatment: McDuff and Salamon's *Introduction to Symplectic Topology* (Oxford) covers symplectic geometry at a graduate level. It goes well beyond what this chapter covers and into modern Floer homology and symplectic capacities.

## KAM Theory

The original papers are Kolmogorov (1954, Doklady), Arnold (1963, Russian Mathematical Surveys), and Moser (1962, Nachrichten). All three are worth reading, though Kolmogorov's is the most terse. The modern rigorous treatment — with complete proofs and careful quantitative statements — is in de la Llave's lecture notes (available on his website, University of Texas) and in Chierchia and Gallavotti's work. For the Whitney differentiability of the surviving tori (they are $C^\infty$ but not analytic), the clearest treatment is in Pöschel's work.

One practical warning: the "small $\varepsilon$" in the KAM theorem is often *very* small in practice — so small that KAM provides essentially no information for physical parameters. The standard map has its last torus at $K_c \approx 0.97$, but the KAM theorem guarantees survival only for much smaller $K$. The numerical $K_c$ comes from Greene's criterion (1979), a beautiful piece of applied mathematics that connects the KAM threshold to the convergence of rational approximants to the golden ratio.

## Aubry-Mather Theory

Mather's original papers (1982, 1984) are in *Topology* and *Ergodic Theory and Dynamical Systems*. Aubry's approach (developed in the physics literature in the context of the Frenkel-Kontorova model of crystal dislocations) is expounded in Aubry and Le Daeron (1983). The survey by Mather and Forni (*Action Minimizing Orbits*, 1994) gives the full picture.

The Frenkel-Kontorova model is the original physical motivation: atoms in a crystal lattice interacting with each other and with an external periodic potential. Aubry-Mather theory describes the ground states of this system. The mathematics of cantori is the mathematics of incommensurate crystal structures.

## Arnold Diffusion

The original Arnold example (1964) is in *Doklady*. The modern treatment — proving generic Arnold diffusion — is in Cheng-Yan (*Annals of Mathematics*, 2004) and Bernard-Kaloshin-Zhang (*Inventiones*, 2011). The survey by Kaloshin and Levi is a good entry point. Arnold's own treatment in Arnold-Kozlov-Neishtadt's *Mathematical Aspects of Classical and Celestial Mechanics* (Springer Encyclopaedia of Mathematical Sciences) is authoritative.

## Quantum Chaos

Haake's *Quantum Signatures of Chaos* (Springer, 4th ed.) is the physicist's bible. Zelditch's papers on quantum ergodicity are the mathematical reference — his survey *Recent Developments in Mathematical Quantum Chaos* (arXiv, 2009) is a good starting point. Lindenstrauss's proof of quantum unique ergodicity for arithmetic surfaces (*Annals of Mathematics*, 2006) is the Fields Medal work and is accessible if you know some ergodic theory.

The Bohigas-Giannoni-Schmit conjecture (1984) connecting classical chaos to GUE statistics is one of the great open problems at the intersection of dynamics and mathematical physics. Understanding it would require a deep connection between the trace formula (relating eigenvalues to classical periodic orbits) and random matrix theory. The field goes by the name "spectral statistics of quantum chaotic systems" and is very active.
