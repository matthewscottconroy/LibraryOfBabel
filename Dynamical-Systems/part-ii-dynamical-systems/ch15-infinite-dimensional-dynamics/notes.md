# Chapter Notes — Chapter 15

## Semigroups and Operator Theory

For $C_0$-semigroups and the Hille-Yosida theorem, there are two standard references. Pazy's *Semigroups of Linear Operators and Applications to Partial Differential Equations* (Springer, 1983) is the classic, concise and clean. Engel and Nagel's *One-Parameter Semigroups for Linear Evolution Equations* (Springer, 2000) is more comprehensive and modern, with a beautiful treatment of spectral theory for generators. If you need the theory for concrete PDEs (heat, wave, Schrödinger), Pazy is the right starting point; if you want the abstract theory in depth, Engel-Nagel is the reference.

## Global Attractors and Infinite-Dimensional Dynamics

Temam's *Infinite-Dimensional Dynamical Systems in Mechanics and Fluid Dynamics* (Springer, 1997) is the comprehensive treatment of global attractors for dissipative PDEs, including the Navier-Stokes equations. It is rigorous and thorough, covering both the existence theory and the dimension estimates.

Robinson's *Infinite-Dimensional Dynamical Systems: An Introduction to Dissipative Parabolic PDEs and the Theory of Global Attractors* (Cambridge, 2001) is more accessible and is the right entry point for students. It covers the abstract theory of attractors and inertial manifolds at a level that does not require knowing functional analysis in advance.

The dimension estimate $\dim_H(\mathcal{A}) \lesssim (L/\ell_d)^2$ for the 2D Navier-Stokes attractor is from Temam and Foias-Manley-Temam. The connection to Kolmogorov's theory of turbulence (the exponent 2 versus Kolmogorov's 4/3 for 3D turbulence) is discussed in Temam's book.

The spectral gap condition for inertial manifolds (Foias-Sell-Temam, 1988) is the key sufficient condition. It is satisfied for the Chladni equation, the Kuramoto-Sivashinsky equation (in a suitable parameter range), and some reaction-diffusion equations. For the 2D Navier-Stokes equations, whether an inertial manifold always exists is open.

## Stochastic Calculus

The accessible introduction to SDEs is Øksendal's *Stochastic Differential Equations: An Introduction with Applications* (Springer, 7th ed.). It is well-organized, covers Itô's formula, the Fokker-Planck equation, and applications to finance and biology. For a rigorous and comprehensive treatment, Karatzas and Shreve's *Brownian Motion and Stochastic Calculus* (Springer) is the standard reference.

For the connection between SDEs and PDEs (Feynman-Kac, backward equations, generators), the right reference depends on your background. Rogers and Williams' two-volume *Diffusions, Markov Processes, and Martingales* is the probabilist's reference; Evans' *An Introduction to Stochastic Differential Equations* (AMS) is shorter and aimed at analysts.

## Random Dynamical Systems

Arnold's (Ludwig Arnold) *Random Dynamical Systems* (Springer, 1998) is the comprehensive treatment and the right reference for anyone who wants to go deep into the theory of cocycles, random attractors, and the multiplicative ergodic theorem in the random setting. It is long (600+ pages) but thorough.

The theory of random attractors was developed mainly by Crauel, Flandoli, and others in the 1990s. Crauel and Flandoli's paper *Attractors for Random Dynamical Systems* (*Probability Theory and Related Fields*, 1994) is the foundational reference for the pullback attractor concept.

## Stochastic Bifurcation Theory

The distinction between P-bifurcation and D-bifurcation was clarified by Arnold and collaborators. The book *Random Dynamical Systems* (Arnold) contains the detailed theory. For specific examples and physical applications, the survey by Horsthemke and Lefever *Noise-Induced Transitions* (Springer, 1984) is the physicist's perspective.

## Research Entry Points

The most active areas accessible from this chapter: (1) the regularity and dimension of attractors for 3D Navier-Stokes (a Millennium Prize problem); (2) the existence of inertial manifolds for physically relevant systems; (3) random dynamical systems driven by colored noise or non-Markovian processes; (4) stochastic PDEs (SPDEs), where the noise is spatially distributed — these require combining the semigroup theory of Section 15.1 with the Itô calculus of Section 15.4. Da Prato and Zabczyk's *Stochastic Equations in Infinite Dimensions* (Cambridge) is the authoritative reference for SPDEs.
