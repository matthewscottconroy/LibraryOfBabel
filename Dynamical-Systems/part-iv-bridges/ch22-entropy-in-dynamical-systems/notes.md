# Chapter Notes — Chapter 22

## On the variational principle

The variational principle (Section 22.3) is one of the most important theorems in the subject, and its proof history is worth knowing. The upper bound — that $h_\mu \leq h_{\text{top}}$ for every invariant measure — was proved by Goodwyn in 1969, and the lower bound (the supremum is achieved) by Dinaburg in 1970. Bowen's 1971 paper unified the approach and introduced the separated-set definition that makes the information-theoretic interpretation transparent. For the complete proof, see:

- **Walters**, *An Introduction to Ergodic Theory*, Chapter 9 — the standard reference, self-contained and complete
- **Katok and Hasselblatt**, *Introduction to the Modern Theory of Dynamical Systems*, Chapter 3 — more context, harder going

Start with Walters. Chapter 9 is thirty pages and contains everything.

## On Pesin's entropy formula

Pesin's formula (Section 22.6) appears in Pesin's 1977 paper "Characteristic Lyapunov exponents and smooth ergodic theory" (*Russian Mathematical Surveys*). The paper established the entire Pesin theory of non-uniformly hyperbolic systems, of which the entropy formula is one theorem among many. The most readable modern treatment is:

- **Katok and Hasselblatt**, Chapter 9 — thorough and well-motivated
- **Mañé**, *Ergodic Theory and Differentiable Dynamics* — a beautiful book, somewhat harder to find but worth the effort

The Ruelle inequality (general maps, any invariant measure) was proved by Ruelle in 1978. The characterization of equality as precisely the SRB condition is due to Ledrappier and Young (1985), in a major pair of papers in the Annals of Mathematics.

## On the pressure function and equilibrium states

The pressure function and equilibrium states are the heart of Bowen's *Equilibrium States and the Ergodic Theory of Anosov Diffeomorphisms* (Lecture Notes in Mathematics, 1975). This is a short book — about 110 pages — and it is a model of mathematical writing: every theorem is motivated, every proof is clean, and the whole edifice is constructed from first principles. If you are going to read one specialized book in the area, make it this one.

The connection to statistical mechanics goes back to Ruelle's *Thermodynamic Formalism* (1978), which places dynamical systems entropy firmly in the tradition of Gibbs measures and the transfer matrix method.

## On zeta functions

The Ruelle zeta function (Section 22.7) is studied in Ruelle's papers from the 1970s and 1980s, and the connection to the spectrum of the transfer operator was made rigorous by Ruelle, Pollicott, and others using techniques from functional analysis. For a modern treatment using anisotropic Banach spaces (the current state of the art), see:

- **Baladi**, *Positive Transfer Operators and Decay of Correlations* (2000)
- **Giulietti, Liverani, Pollicott** — the 2013 Annals paper extends this to Anosov flows

The analogy with the Riemann zeta function is not just aesthetic. Selberg's zeta function for hyperbolic surfaces — a function defined via closed geodesics on a compact hyperbolic surface — is literally a dynamical zeta function for the geodesic flow. The spectral theory of this function is deeply connected to the spectral theory of the Laplacian on the surface, and through it to the Riemann hypothesis in the function field setting.
