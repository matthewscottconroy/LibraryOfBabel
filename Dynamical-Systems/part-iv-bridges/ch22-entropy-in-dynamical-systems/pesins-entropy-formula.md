# 22.6 Pesin's Entropy Formula

Everything so far has been about counting orbits and measuring their complexity. Pesin's formula is different: it connects the information production rate of a dynamical system directly to the *geometric* rate at which nearby orbits diverge.

The setup: a smooth diffeomorphism $f$ on a compact manifold, preserving a smooth measure $\mu$. At each point $x$, the derivative $Df_x$ tells you how fast the map stretches and contracts. The Lyapunov exponents $\lambda_1(x) \geq \lambda_2(x) \geq \cdots$ measure the exponential growth rates of tangent vectors under iteration: a tangent vector in the "most expanding" direction grows like $e^{\lambda_1(x) \cdot n}$ after $n$ steps.

Positive Lyapunov exponents mean nearby orbits diverge. And orbit divergence means information production: if two points that start $\varepsilon$ apart are $\varepsilon e^{\lambda n}$ apart at time $n$, then specifying the orbit to time $n$ at precision $\varepsilon$ requires $\lambda n / \log 2$ more bits than the initial precision did.

Pesin's formula makes this intuition exact.

**Theorem 22.6.1 (Pesin 1977).** For a $C^2$ diffeomorphism $f$ on a compact manifold preserving a smooth measure $\mu$ (absolutely continuous w.r.t. Lebesgue):
$$h_\mu(f) = \int_X \sum_{\lambda_i > 0} \lambda_i(x)\,d\mu(x),$$
where $\lambda_i(x)$ are the Lyapunov exponents at $x$.

The right-hand side is the average total positive Lyapunov exponent — the average rate of volume expansion in the unstable directions. Pesin's formula says this equals the KS entropy exactly.

Plain language: **the rate of information production equals the rate of exponential divergence of nearby orbits**. Every bit of information generated by the system corresponds to one bit of sensitivity to initial conditions. Chaos and information are the same thing, measured in the same units.

This is the statement you suspected was true since you first learned about Lyapunov exponents: a system is chaotic if it has positive Lyapunov exponents, and a system has positive entropy if it generates information faster than it receives it. Pesin's formula says these are literally identical (for smooth invariant measures).

**Ruelle Inequality (general):** For any $C^1$ map and any $f$-invariant measure:
$$h_\mu(f) \leq \int \sum_{\lambda_i > 0} \lambda_i\,d\mu.$$
Equality holds iff $\mu$ is SRB (absolutely continuous on unstable manifolds).

The Ruelle inequality is a general bound: the information production rate is at most the expansion rate, for any invariant measure. Equality — Pesin's formula — holds only for measures that are "smooth in the expanding direction." Measures that are concentrated on sets with lower dimension than the unstable manifolds have less entropy than their Lyapunov exponents would suggest.

The SRB measure, recall from Section 22.5, is the equilibrium state for the potential $-\log|Df|$ — the physical measure. Pesin's formula characterizes SRB measures by the equality $h_\mu(f) = \int \sum_{\lambda_i > 0} \lambda_i \, d\mu$. This is one of the most useful practical tests: if you compute entropy and Lyapunov exponents numerically and they agree, the system is likely behaving "as if" it has an SRB measure.

Let's see this in a familiar example. For the doubling map $T: x \mapsto 2x \pmod 1$, every point has a single Lyapunov exponent $\lambda = \log 2$ (the map expands by a factor of $2$ at every point). The KS entropy with respect to Lebesgue measure is $h = \log 2$. Pesin's formula checks out: $h = \lambda$. One bit per iterate of information production, because the orbit divergence rate is exactly one doubly-per-step.

For the cat map $A: \mathbb{T}^2 \to \mathbb{T}^2$ with $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$: the eigenvalues of $A$ are $\lambda_\pm = \frac{3 \pm \sqrt{5}}{2}$, so the positive Lyapunov exponent is $\log \lambda_+ = \log\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}$. Pesin's formula says $h_{\text{Lebesgue}}(A) = \log \lambda_+$, and this is indeed the topological entropy (since Lebesgue is the MME for the cat map, being an Anosov diffeomorphism).
