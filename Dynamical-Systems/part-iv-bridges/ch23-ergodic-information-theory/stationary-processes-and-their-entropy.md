# 23.1 Stationary Processes and Their Entropy

Before the theorem, we need to be clear about what kind of sources we're allowing. The i.i.d. assumption — each symbol independent of all others — is clean and tractable, but it doesn't describe most real sources. Natural language has grammar. Financial data has momentum and mean-reversion. DNA sequences have codon structure. The question is how far we can generalize the AEP beyond the i.i.d. case.

The right framework is stationarity. A stochastic process is stationary if its statistics don't change over time — not necessarily independent, but invariant under time shifts.

**Definition 23.1.1.** A *stationary stochastic process* $(X_n)_{n \in {\mathbb Z}}$ is one where the joint distribution of $(X_{n+k_1}, \ldots, X_{n+k_m})$ is independent of $n$ for any $m$ and $k_1, \ldots, k_m$.

Stationarity is a symmetry condition: the process looks the same at any starting time. It allows arbitrary correlations — $X_n$ can depend on all past and future symbols — as long as those correlations are time-invariant. Every ergodic measure-preserving transformation gives rise to a stationary process:

**Key Example:** Any MPT $(X, \mathcal{B}, \mu, f)$ with a measurable function $\varphi: X \to \mathcal{A}$ (an alphabet) defines a stationary process $Y_n = \varphi(f^n(x))$ via the *natural extension*.

The process is stationary because $\mu$ is $f$-invariant. It is ergodic if $\mu$ is an ergodic measure for $f$. The shift map on the orbit space $(A^{\mathbb Z}, \sigma, \mu_\varphi)$ is the induced transformation. So every ergodic MPT — every system from Parts I and II of this book — defines a stationary ergodic source.

**Definition 23.1.2.** The *entropy rate* of a stationary process $(X_n)$ is:
$$h = \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{n} H(X_1, \ldots, X_n).$$

The limit exists by subadditivity. Also $h = \lim_n H(X_n | X_{n-1}, \ldots, X_1)$ (the conditional entropy stabilizes).

The subadditivity argument: $H(X_1, \ldots, X_{n+m}) \leq H(X_1, \ldots, X_n) + H(X_{n+1}, \ldots, X_{n+m}) = H(X_1, \ldots, X_n) + H(X_1, \ldots, X_m)$ (by stationarity). So $a_n = H(X_1, \ldots, X_n)$ is subadditive, and by Fekete's lemma, $a_n/n$ converges to $\inf_n a_n/n$.

The equivalence with conditional entropy is informative: $h = \lim_n H(X_n | X_{n-1}, \ldots, X_1)$ says the entropy rate is the per-symbol conditional uncertainty given the entire past. For i.i.d. sources, $H(X_n | X_{n-1}, \ldots, X_1) = H(X_1)$ — the past gives no information. For Markov chains, $H(X_n | X_{n-1}, \ldots, X_1) = H(X_n | X_{n-1})$ — only the last symbol matters.

**Theorem 23.1.3.** For an ergodic MPT $(X, \mathcal{B}, \mu, f)$ with generating partition $\xi$, the entropy rate of the process $(Y_n = \xi(f^n(x)))$ equals the KS entropy $h_\mu(f)$.

This is the keystone. The KS entropy — defined via partitions and limits of partition entropies — is exactly the entropy rate of the stochastic process generated by observing the orbit through the partition. The ergodic theory definition and the information theory definition agree.

This means everything we prove about entropy rates of stationary ergodic processes is a theorem about KS entropy of ergodic MPTs, and vice versa. The SMB theorem — our next destination — is simultaneously a result in ergodic theory (Birkhoff for the information function) and in information theory (almost-sure AEP for ergodic sources).
