# Chapter Notes — Chapter 31

## On Furstenberg's Work

Furstenberg's ergodic proof of Szemerédi's theorem is in *Ergodic Behavior of Diagonal Measures and a Theorem of Szemerédi on Arithmetic Progressions* (J. d'Analyse Mathématique, 1977). This is the paper that started the program. Read it — it's long but the ideas are clearly motivated, and you can see Furstenberg working out the structure theory as he needs it.

His book *Recurrence in Ergodic Theory and Combinatorial Number Theory* (Princeton University Press, 1981) is the definitive reference for the full correspondence principle and multiple recurrence theory. The chapter on compact extensions is demanding, but the payoff — Szemerédi's theorem from ergodic principles — is worth the effort.

The Furstenberg-Katznelson proof of the Hales-Jewett theorem is in *A density version of the Hales-Jewett theorem* (J. d'Analyse Mathématique, 1991). This paper is harder than the 1977 paper, and it requires the full structure theory.

## On the Green-Tao Theorem

The Green-Tao theorem is in *The primes contain arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions* (Annals of Mathematics, 2008). The paper is long (approximately 60 pages) but contains a clear outline of the argument at the start. Gowers's earlier work on *A new proof of Szemerédi's theorem for arithmetic progressions of length four* (Geometric and Functional Analysis, 1998) developed the uniformity norms (now called Gowers norms) that underpin Green-Tao.

Ben Green has several expository articles on the Green-Tao theorem available online; they provide good entry points before tackling the original paper.

## On Normal Numbers

The connection between normal numbers and ergodic theory is classical; it appears in most ergodic theory textbooks. Champernowne's original paper *The construction of decimals normal in the scale of ten* (J. London Math. Soc., 1933) is accessible.

For the current state of the normality problem for specific constants like $\pi$ and $e$, Bailey and Crandall's *Random generators and normal numbers* (Experimental Mathematics, 2002) surveys the evidence and the obstacles.

## On the Collatz Conjecture

Lagarias's survey *The $3x+1$ Problem and its Generalizations* (American Mathematical Monthly, 1985) is the essential starting point, covering the history, the heuristics, and the connections to 2-adic analysis. The more comprehensive *The Ultimate Challenge: The $3x+1$ Problem* (AMS, 2010), edited by Lagarias, collects the major contributions to the problem.

Tao's 2022 paper *Almost all orbits of the Collatz map attain almost bounded values* (Forum of Mathematics, Pi) is the strongest unconditional result: with probability 1 under the natural density, orbits eventually descend below any fixed bound. The paper uses probabilistic and Fourier-analytic methods, and Tao has written excellent blog posts explaining the main ideas.

## On the Connection to Nilpotent Groups

The deeper structure underlying Weyl's polynomial equidistribution theorem and its generalizations involves nilmanifolds — quotients of nilpotent Lie groups. The ergodic theory of nilflows (flows on nilmanifolds) is the right setting for studying polynomial sequences, and it underlies the Host-Kra structure theorem, which is the key ingredient in the modern proof of the Green-Tao theorem. Host-Kra's *Nonconventional ergodic averages and nilmanifolds* (Annals of Mathematics, 2005) is the reference.
