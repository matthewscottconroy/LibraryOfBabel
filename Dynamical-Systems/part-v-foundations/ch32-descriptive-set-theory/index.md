# Chapter 32 — Descriptive Set Theory and Dynamical Systems

> *Borel sets, analytic sets, and the hierarchy of definable sets form the foundation of modern analysis. The Borel hierarchy classifies the complexity of dynamical properties. Turbulence is the obstruction to smooth classification — some equivalence relations are so complex that no assignment of invariants can tame them.*

**Prerequisites:** Chapter 1 (metric spaces, Baire category), Chapter 2 (measure theory), Chapter 6 (topological dynamics).

---

Here is a question that looks simple: can we classify ergodic measure-preserving transformations? By "classify" we mean: find a complete invariant — some object you can assign to each system such that two systems get the same object if and only if they are isomorphic. For Bernoulli shifts, Ornstein's theorem gives a complete answer: the invariant is entropy, and entropy is all you need.

For all ergodic systems, the answer is no. Not "no, not yet" — provably, logically, in principle. There is no Borel function from ergodic measure-preserving transformations to any standard Borel space that classifies them up to isomorphism. The isomorphism relation is not "smoothly classifiable."

This is the business of descriptive set theory: understanding the complexity of equivalence relations, and in particular distinguishing those that can be classified by Borel invariants from those that cannot. The Borel hierarchy gives a precise language for this complexity. Hjorth's turbulence theory gives the definitive obstruction to smooth classification. And the Foreman-Rudolph-Weiss theorem (2011) shows that ergodic theory lies on the "unclassifiable" side of the line.

This chapter is where the logic underlying all of our dynamical questions becomes explicit. The tools developed here — Borel sets, analytic sets, the complexity of equivalence relations — apply not just to dynamics, but to the foundations of mathematics more broadly. You are now doing logic and analysis simultaneously.
