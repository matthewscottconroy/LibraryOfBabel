# 33.2 Dye's Theorem and the Amenable Case

The first major theorem in orbit equivalence theory is Dye's theorem, and it is genuinely shocking. Every free ergodic action of $\mathbb{Z}$ — no matter what entropy it has, no matter whether it's mixing or weakly mixing or neither — produces the same orbit equivalence relation. The doubling map on the circle and the irrational rotation by $\sqrt{2}/3$ are orbit equivalent. They look nothing alike dynamically. But their orbit structures are identical.

**Theorem 33.2.1 (Dye's Theorem, 1959, 1963).** All free ergodic measure-preserving actions of ${\mathbb Z}$ are mutually orbit equivalent. In particular, all free ergodic ${\mathbb Z}$-actions are OE to all other free ergodic ${\mathbb Z}$-actions, regardless of their entropy or mixing properties.

Ornstein and Weiss extended this twenty years later to a theorem that staggers the imagination.

**Theorem 33.2.2 (Ornstein-Weiss, 1980).** All free ergodic measure-preserving actions of any countably infinite amenable group $\Gamma$ are orbit equivalent to each other (and to ${\mathbb Z}$-actions). The orbit equivalence class is *unique* for all amenable groups.

**Corollary 33.2.3.** The orbit equivalence class of a free ergodic action of an amenable group is completely independent of the group $\Gamma$ — only the "orbit structure" (the hyperfinite equivalence relation) matters.

So the integers, $\mathbb{Z}^{1000}$, the lamplighter group over $\mathbb{Z}$, all solvable groups, all nilpotent groups — every free ergodic action of any of these produces exactly the same equivalence relation. From an orbit-equivalence point of view, all these groups are indistinguishable.

What is amenability? Recall the definition.

**Remark 33.2.4 (Amenability).** A group $\Gamma$ is *amenable* if it admits a finitely additive left-invariant probability measure. Examples: abelian groups, nilpotent groups, solvable groups, ${\mathbb Z}^d$. Non-examples: free groups $F_2$, lattices in $SL(n, {\mathbb R})$ for $n \geq 2$.

Amenability is the property that makes groups "approachable from finite subsets" — you can average over the group in a consistent way. And for orbit equivalence, this means all the richness of the group action collapses: all you're left with is the single hyperfinite equivalence relation.

The orbit equivalence relation for amenable groups is called *hyperfinite* because it can be approximated by equivalence relations with finite classes — roughly, you can almost partition the orbits into finite chunks and get almost all the orbit structure that way. This finite approximability is exactly what amenability buys you.
