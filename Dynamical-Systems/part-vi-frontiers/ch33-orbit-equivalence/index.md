# Chapter 33 — Orbit Equivalence Theory

> *Two dynamical systems are orbit equivalent if their orbit structures are the same — even if the systems themselves differ. This is a coarser equivalence than isomorphism but captures more of the "dynamical structure." For countable group actions, the theory is dominated by a single theorem: all free ergodic actions of amenable groups are orbit equivalent.*

**Prerequisites:** Chapter 7 (ergodic theory, Ornstein's theorem), Chapter 28 (groupoids, C\*-algebras), Chapter 32 (Borel equivalence relations).

---

## What This Chapter Is About

Here is a genuinely radical idea: two dynamical systems can be "the same" even when the groups acting on them are completely different. Not the same as isomorphic group actions — not conjugate, not even close in the usual sense — but the same in a coarser sense that only asks: which points end up in the same orbit?

This is orbit equivalence. And the theory that surrounds it is one of the most surprising in all of modern mathematics.

The first major theorem — Dye's theorem from 1959 — says that all free ergodic actions of $\mathbb{Z}$ are orbit equivalent to each other. Not just the simple ones. Not just the ones with matching entropy. All of them: the doubling map, the rotation by $1/3$, the shift on a Cantor set. All orbit equivalent. The integers acting in any free ergodic way produce the same orbit structure.

Ornstein and Weiss extended this in 1980 to a jaw-dropping conclusion: every countably infinite amenable group has this property. The lamplighter group, $\mathbb{Z}^{100}$, the solvable Baumslag-Solitar groups — all their free ergodic actions produce the same orbit equivalence relation, a single equivalence relation called the hyperfinite equivalence relation.

Then the 1990s and 2000s brought the rigidity revolution. Furman, Gaboriau, and Popa showed that non-amenable groups behave completely differently. For groups like $SL(n, \mathbb{Z})$, orbit equivalence carries enormous information about the group. The OE class is not just one point in some classification — it actually remembers the group, sometimes down to finite index. Popa's cocycle superrigidity program pushed this further still: for actions of groups with property (T), orbit equivalence sometimes implies isomorphism.

This chapter tells both stories. The amenable case, where everything collapses to one. The non-amenable case, where orbit equivalence becomes a precision instrument.
