# Unit 1: Foundations and Historical Survey — Unit Survey

## Central Questions

This unit asks: what is epistemology, and what questions has it asked across the history of Western philosophy? These questions are not merely historical curiosities. The assumptions embedded in ancient, medieval, and early modern epistemology continue to shape the terms of contemporary debate. To understand why philosophers argue as they do today about justification, scepticism, and the analysis of knowledge, one must understand the conceptual vocabulary they inherited — a vocabulary forged over two and a half millennia.

The foundational question is definitional: what is knowledge? Plato's Meno and Theaetetus raised this question in a form that proved extraordinarily durable. The Meno's distinction between knowledge and true opinion — illustrated through the paradox of inquiry and the simile of tied statues — anticipates the tripartite analysis that would dominate analytic epistemology for most of the twentieth century. The Theaetetus, more diagnostically, tests three candidate definitions of knowledge (perception, true belief, true belief with an account) and finds each wanting, leaving the reader with the aporia that is philosophy's most fertile condition.

The historical survey is not a detour from the main road — it is the main road. Medieval epistemology developed the Aristotelian theory of abstraction and introduced the distinction between *scientia* (demonstrative knowledge from first principles) and *opinio* (probable belief), a distinction that informed early modern debates about certainty. The early modern period saw the emergence of new methodological problems: the challenge of Cartesian scepticism, the rationalist claim that certain substantive truths are knowable through reason alone, and the empiricist insistence that all knowledge is grounded in sensory experience. The survey of this history is not optional; it is the key to understanding why contemporary problems have the shape they do.

## Map of Major Positions and Their Logical Relations

**The Classical Analysis.** Plato in the Meno proposes, and subsequent tradition canonizes, the view that knowledge is justified true belief (JTB). The three conditions — truth, belief, and justification — are individually necessary and jointly sufficient. Each condition has a philosophical rationale: truth because you cannot know falsehoods; belief because knowing requires taking something to be the case; justification because mere lucky true belief is not knowledge.

**Rationalism.** Descartes, Spinoza, and Leibniz hold that certain substantive knowledge is grounded in reason alone, independent of experience. The primary exemplar is mathematical knowledge. Rationalists typically hold that the intellect has access to innate ideas or self-evident first principles from which demonstrative knowledge can be derived. This requires a faculty of rational intuition.

**Empiricism.** Locke, Berkeley, and Hume hold that all ideas and ultimately all knowledge derives from experience. Locke's *Essay* argues against innate ideas and proposes that the mind is a tabula rasa. Hume extends this to a strict empiricism that raises severe doubts about causal inference, induction, and the very concept of necessary connection — generating the central problem of induction that Kant would later try to solve.

**Kantian Synthesis.** Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* attempts to reconcile rationalism and empiricism. Sensory intuition provides the raw material of experience, but the understanding structures that material through a priori categories (substance, causality, unity). The result is *synthetic a priori* knowledge — knowledge that is both informative about the world and knowable independently of particular experiences. This constitutes a third major position: the view that the form of knowledge is partly a contribution of the knowing subject.

**The Methodological Question.** Beneath these first-order positions lies a second-order question: how should epistemology be done? Should we begin with ordinary knowledge claims and work backwards to their conditions (the analytic method)? Begin with first principles and derive everything (the synthetic method)? Test claims by their practical consequences (pragmatism)? The debate between top-down (foundationalist) and bottom-up (coherentist or naturalist) methodology runs throughout the history.

## Sub-Chapter Contributions

**Unit 1: Introduction to Epistemology.** Establishes the discipline's central questions — what is knowledge, what are its sources, what justifies belief, what is the relationship between knowledge and certainty? Chapter 1 argues that epistemology is not merely academic: our epistemic standards govern what counts as evidence in law, medicine, and policy. Chapter 2 draws the fundamental distinction between knowing and believing, previewing the analysis of knowledge. Chapter 3 introduces the core distinctions — a priori vs. a posteriori knowledge, analytic vs. synthetic propositions, necessary vs. contingent truth — that structure all subsequent discussion.

**Unit 2: Ancient and Medieval.** Covers Plato's Meno and Theaetetus, Aristotle's theory of scientific knowledge (*Posterior Analytics*) and abstraction (*De Anima*), and the transition to medieval epistemology through Augustine, Aquinas, and Ockham. Plato's theory of recollection (anamnesis) and his doctrine of the Forms as objects of genuine knowledge are examined critically. Aristotle's departure from Platonic forms — his insistence that knowledge is of the universal in the particular, not in a separate realm — receives detailed attention. Medieval discussions of illumination theory, the reliability of the senses, and the nature of abstract concepts are treated as genuine philosophical contributions, not merely as transitions to the early moderns.

**Unit 3: Early Modern.** Covers Descartes's method of doubt and the Cogito, rationalist responses to scepticism, Locke's empiricism and its internal tensions, Hume's radical empiricism and its consequences (the problem of induction, bundle theory of the self), and Kant's critical philosophy. Hume's fork — the distinction between relations of ideas and matters of fact — is treated as a pivotal document, because it either threatens to reduce all a priori knowledge to triviality or to make all empirical knowledge merely probable.

**Unit 4: Nineteenth and Twentieth Century.** Traces the emergence of epistemology as an academic discipline — the neo-Kantian movement, the rise of pragmatism (Peirce, James, Dewey), early analytic philosophy (Moore, Russell), and the logical positivist program of the Vienna Circle (Schlick, Carnap, Neurath). The unit closes with the dissolution of logical positivism and the emergence of the contemporary analytic epistemology that subsequent units address.

## Key Philosophers and Arguments

**Plato (428–348 BCE).** The Meno raises the paradox of inquiry: if you know what you are looking for, you do not need to inquire; if you do not know, you cannot recognize the answer. The response — the theory of recollection, that learning is remembering — is a philosophical myth, but it identifies a genuine structural feature of inquiry: it requires some prior cognitive access to its target. The Theaetetus's failure to define knowledge positively has been interpreted as deliberately aporetic, inviting the reader to recognize that knowledge cannot be reduced to any simpler concept.

**Aristotle (384–322 BCE).** The *Posterior Analytics* analyses scientific demonstration (apodeixis): syllogistic reasoning from first principles that are themselves known directly, without demonstration. This requires a faculty of nous — intellectual intuition — that apprehends first principles directly. The regress problem for foundationalism is already here: if every claim needs demonstration, we face infinite regress; the solution is non-demonstrative knowledge of first principles.

**Descartes (1596–1650).** The Meditations employs hyperbolic doubt as a methodological device: anything that can possibly be doubted is set aside, to see what cannot be doubted. The Cogito — *cogito ergo sum*, "I think therefore I am" — survives the methodological doubt because the very act of doubting confirms the existence of the doubter. The certainty thus achieved is the model for Cartesian epistemology: clear and distinct perception guarantees truth. The dream argument and the evil demon are not sincere sceptical conclusions but heuristics for identifying what is genuinely indubitable.

**Locke (1632–1704).** The *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* opens with a sustained attack on innate ideas, arguing that all ideas are derived from sensation or reflection. Locke's account of sensitive knowledge — knowledge of the external world — is notably weaker than his account of intuitive or demonstrative knowledge, generating the problem of whether empiricism can vindicate ordinary perceptual knowledge.

**Hume (1711–1776).** The *Treatise of Human Nature* and the *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* press the empiricist program to its limits. The problem of induction — the impossibility of justifying inductive inference by either deduction or induction without circularity — is Hume's deepest legacy. His bundle theory of the self and his account of causation as constant conjunction rather than necessary connection remain central to discussions of personal identity and causal knowledge.

**Kant (1724–1804).** The *Critique of Pure Reason* introduces the distinction between *analytic* and *synthetic* judgments, and argues that mathematics and the principles of natural science are *synthetic a priori* — knowable independently of experience yet genuinely informative. The transcendental deduction argues that experience is only possible if the categories of the understanding apply to sensory intuition, grounding the a priori framework of knowledge in the conditions of possible experience.

## Essential Readings

1. **Plato, *Meno* and *Theaetetus*** (c. 385–369 BCE). The founding documents of epistemology as a discipline. The Meno introduces the problem of the definition of knowledge and the paradox of inquiry; the Theaetetus tests three candidate definitions systematically and finds each wanting. Together they define the agenda of the field for two and a half millennia. Translations by Myles Burnyeat (*Theaetetus*) and G.M.A. Grube (*Meno*) are recommended.

2. **Descartes, *Meditations on First Philosophy*** (1641). The locus classicus of modern epistemology. The method of hyperbolic doubt, the Cogito, and the epistemological role of God's veracity are the primary subjects. The Second and Sixth Meditations are the most important for epistemological purposes. Cottingham's translation (Cambridge) is the standard.

3. **Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*** (1748). More accessible than the *Treatise* and cleaner in its arguments. Section IV (the problem of induction), Section XII (sceptical doubts and their mitigations), and the notorious Section X (on miracles) are the most important for epistemology. Beauchamp's edition (Oxford) provides critical apparatus.

4. **Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, Prefaces and Introduction** (1781/1787). The Introduction to the B edition sets out the analytic/synthetic and a priori/a posteriori distinctions with precision and argues for the existence of synthetic a priori knowledge. The two Prefaces situate the *Critique* within the history of rationalism and empiricism. Guyer and Wood's translation (Cambridge) is authoritative.

5. **Chisholm, Roderick, *Theory of Knowledge***, 3rd ed. (1989). The standard analytic epistemology textbook through the 1970s and 1980s. Systematically treats the JTB analysis, Gettier's challenge, and the foundationalism/coherentism debate from within the broadly Cartesian framework that the discipline inherited. An indispensable bridge between historical and contemporary epistemology.

## Open Questions and Live Debates

**Is the tripartite analysis the right starting point?** Timothy Williamson's *Knowledge and its Limits* (2000) argues that knowledge is itself a primitive — that the analysis of knowledge in terms of belief plus conditions is fundamentally misguided. This "knowledge-first" turn is one of the most significant methodological shifts in recent epistemology and bears directly on the historical question of whether the JTB analysis captures a genuine structure in the concept of knowledge.

**What is the epistemological significance of a priori knowledge?** The traditional rationalist claim that there is substantive a priori knowledge faces pressure from naturalized epistemology (W.V.O. Quine, "Two Dogmas of Empiricism") on one side and from neorationalist defenders of a priori knowledge in ethics and mathematics (George Bealer, Laurence BonJour) on the other. The status of a priori knowledge is also central to debates about concept empiricism and the semantics of natural kind terms.

**Can the history of epistemology be reconstructed charitably?** Recent work in early modern philosophy (Descartes scholarship by Margaret Wilson, Hume scholarship by Don Garrett) has challenged the textbook narrative. The sceptical reading of Hume and the rationalist reading of Descartes are both more contested than introductory treatments suggest. Historical precision has philosophical consequences.

**Is the analytic-synthetic distinction coherent?** Quine's attack in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) challenged both the analytic-synthetic distinction and the related notion of a priori knowledge. Subsequent defenses (Grice and Strawson, Kripke's modal arguments) have complicated the picture. The status of the distinction matters for the Kantian project of explaining mathematical and logical knowledge.

## Connections to Other Units

This unit provides the historical and conceptual background for the entire subject:

- **Unit 2 (Nature of Knowledge):** The JTB analysis, introduced here via Plato and the analytic tradition, is the direct target of Gettier's 1963 challenge. The post-Gettier literature makes no sense without the classical analysis as its starting point.

- **Unit 4 (Justification and Warrant):** The foundationalism/coherentism debate is Kant's response to Hume in a new idiom. Internalism vs. externalism about justification is the contemporary version of the dispute between rationalist and empiricist accounts of epistemic access.

- **Unit 5 (Skepticism):** Cartesian scepticism originates here. The evil demon, the dream argument, and their descendants in brain-in-vat scenarios are directly inherited from the historical material of Unit 3.

- **Unit 8 (Formal Epistemology):** Bayesian epistemology is, in part, a response to the problem of induction that Hume identified. The question of how inductive inference can be rational is reframed in probabilistic terms.

- **Unit 9 (Social Epistemology):** Testimony as a source of knowledge raises questions about the transmission of justification that have a medieval and early modern pedigree (Reid's principle of credulity, Locke's empiricism about testimony).
