# Key Figures

## Plato (428–348 BCE)
Plato is the originator of systematic epistemology in the Western tradition. His *Theaetetus* examines and rejects three accounts of knowledge (perception, true belief, true belief with an account), while his *Meno* introduces the paradox of inquiry and proposes that knowledge is distinguished from true belief by being "tethered" — stable and explanatorily grounded. Plato's rationalism, his doctrine of recollection, and his distinction between the intelligible and sensible realms have shaped epistemology ever since.

## Aristotle (384–322 BCE)
Aristotle developed a systematic account of scientific knowledge (*episteme*) as demonstrated knowledge — knowledge through causes. In the *Posterior Analytics* he argues that genuine knowledge requires demonstration from first principles, which are themselves known through *nous* (intellectual intuition). His distinction between demonstrative knowledge and the intuition of first principles anticipates debates about foundationalism and the a priori.

## René Descartes (1596–1650)
Descartes transformed epistemology by placing certainty at its center. His method of hyperbolic doubt, the *cogito*, and the attempt to rebuild knowledge on a secure foundation define modern epistemology. His distinction between the clear and distinct and the obscure, his arguments for God's existence as epistemic guarantor, and his dualism between thinking substance and extended substance all remain touchstones.

## John Locke (1632–1704)
Locke's *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* is the foundational empiricist text. He denied innate ideas and argued that all knowledge derives from experience — from sensation and reflection. His distinctions between primary and secondary qualities, his analysis of personal identity, and his account of the degrees and limits of human knowledge established the agenda for British empiricism.

## Immanuel Kant (1724–1804)
Kant's "Copernican revolution" in epistemology holds that objects conform to our faculties of knowledge, not the other way around. His distinction between phenomena and noumena, his account of the synthetic a priori, and his deduction of the categories of the understanding are among the most influential ideas in the history of epistemology. Kant saw himself as limiting knowledge to make room for rational faith.

## Edmund Gettier (1927–2021)
Gettier's three-page paper "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?" (1963) overturned the dominant analysis of knowledge that had held since Plato. His counterexamples showed that a person can have justified true belief without having knowledge. This simple observation reshaped analytic epistemology and launched decades of attempts to find the missing condition.

## Timothy Williamson (1955–)
Williamson's *Knowledge and Its Limits* (2000) proposed inverting the traditional analysis: instead of analyzing knowledge in terms of belief, truth, and justification, we should take knowledge as primitive and analyze belief, evidence, and justification in terms of it. His knowledge-first program is one of the most influential contemporary approaches.
