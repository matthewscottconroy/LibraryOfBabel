# Thought Experiments

## Meno's Paradox (Plato)
**The scenario**: In Plato's *Meno*, Meno asks Socrates how they can inquire into what virtue is: if they already know what virtue is, there is no need to inquire; if they don't know what it is, they wouldn't recognize it even if they found it. So inquiry seems impossible.

**The philosophical point**: The paradox is designed to motivate Plato's doctrine of recollection — that learning is really remembering. More generally, it raises the question of how inquiry can begin at all: you need some prior understanding to guide the search, but not so much as to make the search unnecessary. This illuminates the structure of knowledge-seeking and the relationship between prior understanding and new discovery.

## The Theaetetus Trilemma (Plato)
**The scenario**: Socrates and Theaetetus test three accounts of knowledge: (1) knowledge is perception; (2) knowledge is true belief; (3) knowledge is true belief with an account (*logos*). Each account is shown to be deficient: perception fails to distinguish knowledge from momentary experience; true belief is compatible with accidentally correct opinion; true belief with an account seems to require an account of the account, leading to regress.

**The philosophical point**: The dialogue establishes the basic analytical strategy for epistemology — examine candidate accounts of knowledge against test cases — and identifies the main axes of disagreement (the role of perception, the role of justification, the problem of regress) that structure subsequent inquiry.

## The Justified True Belief Standard (Socrates on "Tethering")
**The scenario**: In the *Meno*, Socrates distinguishes between a person who knows the road to Larissa and a person who merely has a correct opinion about it. Both will arrive; but the knower's belief is stable because it is tied down by reasoning, while the true believer's opinion might wander away.

**The philosophical point**: This is the original statement of the justified true belief analysis — knowledge is true belief that is stable because it is grounded by reason. The tethering metaphor captures the idea that justification "anchors" a belief to the truth, distinguishing lucky guesses from genuine knowledge.

## The Matrix Scenario (contemporary)
**The scenario**: Imagine that you are living in a computer simulation indistinguishable from reality. All your experiences are generated by a computer; the world you take yourself to inhabit does not exist. You have no way of detecting this from the inside.

**The philosophical point**: This contemporary version of Cartesian skepticism asks whether our ordinary concept of knowledge requires ruling out such scenarios. If we cannot rule them out, and if knowledge requires truth about the external world, then perhaps we know nothing. The thought experiment also raises the question of what "reality" is: if the simulation is fully coherent and contains all our practical knowledge, in what sense is it less real?

## Epistemic Akrasia (Henry the Inconsistent Believer)
**The scenario**: Henry carefully examines evidence and concludes that P. He also believes, on the basis of reliable self-knowledge, that his reasoning about P-type questions is unreliable. He maintains both: P, and I am not justified in believing P.

**The philosophical point**: This illustrates *epistemic akrasia* — believing what you believe you shouldn't believe. It illuminates the relationship between first-order and higher-order evidence, and raises the question of what epistemically "self-aware" rational agency requires. Should Henry revise P downward? Or maintain P on the strength of his first-order evidence, while acknowledging the meta-level concern?
