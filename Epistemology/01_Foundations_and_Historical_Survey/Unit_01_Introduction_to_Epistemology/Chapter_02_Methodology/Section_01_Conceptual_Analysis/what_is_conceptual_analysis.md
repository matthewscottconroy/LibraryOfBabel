# What Is Conceptual Analysis?

For most of the twentieth century, conceptual analysis was the primary method of analytic philosophy, including analytic epistemology. Understanding what it is — and why it has attracted both devotion and criticism — is essential to understanding how epistemology works.

## The Basic Idea

Conceptual analysis aims to break down a concept into its necessary and sufficient conditions. To analyze the concept *knowledge*, for instance, is to identify conditions C1, C2, C3 ... such that: a subject S knows that P if and only if C1, C2, C3 ... all hold. The analysis aims to capture not just what happens to be true of most cases of knowledge, but what is *necessarily* true — what must be the case for any knowledge claim to succeed.

The classic result of this kind of analysis for knowledge is the *justified true belief* account: S knows that P if and only if (1) P is true, (2) S believes P, and (3) S is justified in believing P. Whether this analysis succeeds is a central question of this course — spoiler: Edmund Gettier showed in 1963 that it doesn't, at least not without modification.

## Conceptual Analysis and the A Priori

Conceptual analysis is typically thought of as an *a priori* method — one that doesn't require empirical investigation. You figure out the conditions for knowledge not by running experiments, but by thinking carefully about the concept and testing it against possible cases (real and imagined).

This is why thought experiments play such a central role in epistemology. You imagine a case — someone who believes something true but for crazy reasons — and ask: does this count as knowledge? If your intuition says no, that tells you something about the conditions knowledge requires.

## How It Works in Practice

The methodology works through a cycle of proposal and counterexample:

1. **Propose**: Here is an analysis of concept X: X is the case when conditions C1, C2, C3 hold.
2. **Counterexample**: Here is a case where C1, C2, C3 all hold but X doesn't seem to obtain (or vice versa).
3. **Revise**: Modify the analysis to handle the counterexample — add a new condition, revise an existing one.
4. **Repeat**: The revised analysis faces new counterexamples.

This process can feel Sisyphean, but it's not futile. Even failed analyses teach us something about the concept. The centuries-long effort to analyze knowledge has clarified what the conditions for knowledge are *like* — even if no perfectly tight analysis has been produced.

## The Role of Intuitions

A crucial and somewhat awkward feature of conceptual analysis is its reliance on *intuitions* — our immediate, pre-theoretical judgments about cases. When Gettier presents his cases and we judge that the protagonist doesn't know, we're relying on intuition. The analysis is supposed to systematize these intuitions, explaining why we have them.

But intuitions can be wrong, biased, culturally variable, or simply inconsistent. If intuitions are our primary data, then epistemology faces the same problem as every empirical inquiry: the data might be unreliable. We'll return to this problem when discussing experimental philosophy and the challenges to intuition-based methodology.

## Objections and Alternatives

Critics of conceptual analysis raise several concerns:

**The Paradox of Analysis**: If an analysis spells out what a concept already contains, the result seems either trivially true or circular. If it says something non-trivial, it risks being false.

**The Gettier Industry**: The persistent failure to produce a successful analysis of knowledge suggests that perhaps the method itself is the problem. Maybe knowledge can't be analyzed into more basic components.

**Holism**: Some philosophers argue that concepts don't have determinate necessary and sufficient conditions — they're embedded in webs of belief that are revised together. On this view, the project of conceptual analysis is misguided from the start.

Despite these objections, conceptual analysis remains central to much philosophical work. Its alternatives — fully naturalized philosophy, ordinary language analysis, pragmatism — each have their own problems. Understanding what conceptual analysis is aiming for, even while being sensitive to its limitations, is an essential part of the epistemologist's toolkit.
