# Key Figures

## Socrates (470–399 BCE)
Socrates developed the *elenctic* method — a dialectical procedure of question and answer that exposes the inconsistencies in interlocutors' beliefs and moves toward better-defined concepts. The Socratic method is not a direct route to knowledge but a negative procedure that eliminates false beliefs, making room for genuine understanding. His claim to know only that he knows nothing reflects an epistemological self-awareness foundational to philosophical methodology.

## G.E. Moore (1873–1958)
Moore's method of *analysis* — breaking complex concepts into their simpler components — was a founding approach of analytic philosophy. His "open question argument" (is "good" the same as "natural property X"?) is a paradigm of analytical methodology. Moore also pioneered the use of common-sense beliefs as a check on philosophical theories, arguing that philosophical conclusions which contradict obvious truths about the everyday world are more likely to indicate defective premises than to overturn common sense.

## John Rawls (1921–2002)
Rawls developed *reflective equilibrium* as the central method for political philosophy and ethics. The procedure involves moving back and forth between particular considered judgments (specific intuitions about cases) and general principles, revising both until they cohere. Wide reflective equilibrium extends the coherence constraint to background theories. This method has been exported to epistemology as a general account of how philosophical inquiry works.

## Timothy Williamson (1955–)
In *The Philosophy of Philosophy* (2007), Williamson defended the use of thought experiments and conceptual analysis as genuine routes to philosophical knowledge, against skeptics who doubt that armchair reflection can yield substantive results. He argues that philosophical methodology is continuous with scientific methodology: both involve formulating hypotheses and testing them against cases.

## Jonathan Weinberg (contemporary)
Weinberg, along with Stich and Nichols, pioneered *experimental philosophy*, arguing that philosophical methodology should be empirically informed. Their studies of cross-cultural variation in epistemic intuitions challenged the assumption that philosophical thought experiments track universal judgments. Weinberg argues that philosophy cannot rely uncritically on its data — intuitions — without investigating their reliability and sources.

## Wilfrid Sellars (1912–1989)
Sellars's *Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind* (1956) introduced the "myth of the given" critique, which targeted methodological foundationalism — the assumption that some beliefs are self-justifying and provide an unquestionable starting point. This critique transformed how epistemologists think about the structure of justification and the relationship between experience and conceptual thought.
