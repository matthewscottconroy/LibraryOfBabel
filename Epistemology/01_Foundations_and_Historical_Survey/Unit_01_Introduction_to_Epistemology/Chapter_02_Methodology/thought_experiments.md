# Thought Experiments

## The Elenctic Refutation (Socrates)
**The scenario**: Socrates asks an interlocutor — say, Meno — "What is virtue?" Meno gives an example: courage for a man, obedience for a woman, temperance for a child. Socrates pushes: these are different things called "virtue," not the definition of virtue. What do they all share? Meno gives a general account; Socrates shows it has counterexamples. The process continues until the interlocutor confesses ignorance.

**The philosophical point**: The elenctic method uses cases to test general claims. It models a central epistemological methodology: begin with apparent knowledge, expose its inconsistencies through counterexamples, and move toward more adequate understanding. It also illustrates that beginning from intuitions about cases and working toward principles is a legitimate — indeed indispensable — method of inquiry.

## The Trolley Problem and Methodological Leverage (Foot/Thomson)
**The scenario**: A runaway trolley will kill five people on the track ahead. You can divert it to a side track, where it will kill one person. Should you divert? Most people say yes. Now: a surgeon has five patients who will die without organ transplants. She can kill one healthy patient and harvest their organs to save the five. Should she? Most people say no.

**The philosophical point**: This pair of cases illustrates how thought experiments work methodologically. The cases are structurally similar (kill one to save five) but generate opposite intuitions. This divergence is data: it tells us that "kill one to save five" cannot be the relevant moral principle — something distinguishes the cases. The philosopher's task is to identify what. Thought experiments leverage intuitive verdicts to constrain and refine general theories.

## Mary's Room — Methodological Use (Jackson)
**The scenario**: Mary is a color scientist confined to a black-and-white room. She knows everything physical about color vision. When she leaves and sees red, does she learn something new?

**The philosophical point**: As a methodological illustration, Mary's Room shows how thought experiments function as *controlled cases* that test theoretical claims. The physical completeness assumption is stipulated by the experimenter; the question is whether intuition delivers a verdict about learning. Whatever the correct verdict, the reasoning from it to metaphysical conclusions requires methodological reflection: Are our intuitions about Mary reliable? Are they tracking what matters?

## The Violinist (Thomson)
**The scenario**: You wake up connected to a famous violinist who needs your circulatory system for nine months to survive. The Society of Music Lovers connected you without your consent. You can disconnect, killing the violinist.

**The philosophical point**: Originally an argument about abortion, this thought experiment also illustrates a methodological point: thought experiments are used to isolate specific morally relevant features by stipulation. By constructing a case that matches the logical structure of an issue (unwanted dependency, moral requirement to sustain life) while differing in emotionally confounding ways, the experiment tests whether a principle applies uniformly.

## The Changing Concept (Wittgenstein's family resemblance)
**The scenario**: Try to define "game." Chess, tag, solitaire, Olympic athletics — they share some features but no single feature runs through them all. They are related by overlapping similarities, like the resemblances between members of a family.

**The philosophical point**: This thought experiment challenges the methodological assumption that concepts have necessary and sufficient conditions. If family resemblance rather than definition characterizes many of our concepts, then the analytic method — of finding the essence of a concept — may be misguided. This has methodological implications: perhaps epistemological concepts like knowledge, justification, and evidence resist precise definition.
