# Explicit and Tacit Knowledge

Michael Polanyi, a Hungarian-British philosopher and scientist, made one of the most memorable observations in twentieth-century epistemology: "We can know more than we can tell." This deceptively simple claim opens up the category of *tacit knowledge* — knowledge that is real, operative, and important, but not fully articulable.

## The Tacit Dimension

Polanyi's examples are illuminating. Consider how you recognize a friend's face. You can identify your friend instantly and reliably from a vast range of angles, distances, and lighting conditions. But if someone asks you to articulate *how* you do this — what features you're looking for, what rules you're applying — you'll struggle. The knowledge is exercised fluently but resists explicit formulation.

The same applies to balance when riding a bicycle, to a wine expert's ability to identify a vintage, to a surgeon's sense of how much pressure to apply, to a native speaker's grammatical intuitions. These are all forms of knowing — real, reliable, and often very sophisticated — that operate largely below the level of explicit articulation.

Polanyi's point is not just psychological: he isn't simply noting that some knowledge is hard to put into words. His deeper claim is that all explicit knowledge rests on a tacit substrate. Even when we articulate rules and principles, we rely on tacit skills to apply them. A logical rule like *modus ponens* can be stated explicitly, but using it correctly requires a kind of know-how that isn't itself fully rule-governed.

## Explicit Knowledge and Its Limits

Explicit knowledge is propositional — it can be formulated in language, encoded in documents, transmitted through instruction. The periodic table, the rules of chess, the proof of Pythagoras' theorem: these are explicit. They can be written down, taught, looked up.

The assumption that all knowledge is (at least in principle) explicit was dominant in early cognitive science: on the classical computational model, thinking is rule-governed manipulation of symbolic representations, and all knowledge is ultimately encoded as propositions. Polanyi's challenge to this view anticipates debates that became central in philosophy of mind and AI.

## The Hubert Dreyfus Critique

Drawing on Polanyi and Heidegger, Hubert Dreyfus developed one of the most powerful critiques of classical AI. The attempt to build intelligent systems by encoding explicit rules — the so-called *knowledge representation* approach — was bound to fail, Dreyfus argued, because the relevant knowledge is fundamentally tacit. Expert performance doesn't consist of consulting explicit rules; it consists of fluent, context-sensitive engagement that can't be fully captured in a rule system.

Dreyfus's critique has held up surprisingly well. Early AI systems were stymied by exactly the problem he identified: they could handle well-defined formal domains but failed catastrophically in open, real-world contexts where tacit knowledge does the work. Contemporary deep learning systems, interestingly, acquire something that looks more like tacit knowledge — statistical competence that works without explicit rules — but whether this is genuinely the same phenomenon Polanyi had in mind is contested.

## Tacit Knowledge and Expertise

The study of expertise has been significantly shaped by the tacit/explicit distinction. The developmental trajectory from novice to expert, documented by researchers like Dreyfus and Dreyfus (Hubert Dreyfus's brother Stuart), involves a shift from rule-following to holistic pattern recognition — from explicit, laboriously applied principles to fluid, situation-sensitive performance.

Expert intuitions are a form of tacit knowledge. When a chess grandmaster sees that a position is losing without explicitly calculating lines, they're drawing on deep tacit competence built through thousands of hours of practice. This has implications for how we think about expertise, how we teach skills, and what it means to "know" something in a practical domain.

## Epistemological Implications

The concept of tacit knowledge matters for epistemology in several ways:

- It suggests that the scope of knowledge is broader than the scope of articulable propositions.
- It challenges purely intellectualist accounts of knowledge-how.
- It raises questions about whether all knowledge can be justified (since articulating a justification seems to require making knowledge explicit).
- It connects to debates about the foundations of knowledge: if basic beliefs must be foundational, and foundational beliefs must be directly justifiable, what happens to knowledge that doesn't admit of explicit justification at all?

Polanyi's insight reminds us that knowledge is not merely a relationship between minds and propositions — it is embedded in practice, skill, and embodied engagement with the world.
