# Three Kinds of Knowledge: Propositional, Procedural, and Acquaintance

When philosophers talk about knowledge, they often focus on one specific kind: *knowing that* something is the case. But ordinary language suggests that knowledge comes in at least three importantly different forms, and the distinctions between them have generated rich philosophical discussion.

## Knowing That (Propositional Knowledge)

Propositional knowledge is knowledge *that* some proposition is true. You know *that* the Earth orbits the Sun, *that* two plus two equals four, *that* water is composed of hydrogen and oxygen. This is the form of knowledge that epistemology has spent most of its energy analyzing.

The knowledge is called propositional because its object is a *proposition* — a content that can be true or false. The proposition is what follows "that" in claims like "S knows that P." Propositions are the things that can be believed, known, doubted, or asserted.

Most of epistemology's central questions — about justification, reliability, truth, and the analysis of knowledge — are framed in terms of propositional knowledge. The traditional justified true belief analysis is an analysis of propositional knowledge. Gettier's counterexamples target propositional knowledge. The question "can we know anything about the external world?" is a question about propositional knowledge.

## Knowing How (Procedural Knowledge)

Gilbert Ryle drew a sharp distinction, in his 1949 book *The Concept of Mind*, between knowing *that* and knowing *how*. Knowing how to ride a bicycle, swim, play chess, or speak a language is fundamentally different, Ryle argued, from knowing propositional facts. A person who knows how to ride a bicycle might not be able to articulate the relevant propositions — the physics of balance, the correct sequences of muscle movements — but their knowledge is real and practically operative.

Ryle's point was partly negative: he wanted to resist what he called the *intellectualist legend*, the assumption that all intelligent activity must be guided by internally rehearsed propositions. When a skilled athlete moves gracefully through a complex sequence of actions, there's no time for conscious propositional computation. The skill is embodied in the activity itself.

Whether knowing how is truly irreducible to knowing that is controversial. *Intellectualists* (notably Jason Stanley and Timothy Williamson) have argued that knowing how to do X just is knowing a proposition of the form "way W is a way to do X." The debate is lively and has implications for understanding intelligence, expertise, and what artificial systems can and cannot do.

## Knowledge by Acquaintance

Bertrand Russell distinguished propositional knowledge from *knowledge by acquaintance* — a direct, immediate cognitive relationship to an object. You are acquainted with your current visual experience, your headache, the particular shade of red you're perceiving. You are acquainted with yourself insofar as you have direct access to your own mental states.

Russell contrasted this with *knowledge by description*, which is indirect: you know about Julius Caesar, the far side of the Moon, and electrons through descriptions, not through direct acquaintance. You've never met Caesar; you know about him via historical testimony and inference.

The epistemological significance of acquaintance is that it offers a kind of direct, unmediated access that some philosophers have taken as the foundation for all other knowledge. If I am directly acquainted with my own sense data, these are beyond doubt — the incorrigible bedrock on which everything else must be built. This thought is central to foundationalist epistemology in its strongest forms.

Contemporary philosophers have grown skeptical of strong acquaintance-based foundationalism, but the underlying insight — that there is something importantly different about direct cognitive access — remains philosophically live, particularly in philosophy of mind.

## Why the Distinctions Matter

These three types of knowledge aren't just academic categories. They bear on questions about expertise and skill (how much of expertise is propositional?), about machine cognition (can computers have procedural knowledge?), about consciousness (is phenomenal experience a form of acquaintance?), and about the foundations of epistemology (should we found knowledge on acquaintance with experience?).

Keeping the distinctions clear helps avoid confusions that run through multiple philosophical debates. When someone says "AI systems don't really know anything," they might be making a point about one type of knowledge while ignoring others. When a philosopher says "all knowledge reduces to experience," the appropriate question is: which kind of knowledge, and which kind of experience?
