# The A Priori and the A Posteriori: Definitions and Examples

Among the most fundamental distinctions in epistemology is the one between *a priori* and *a posteriori* knowledge. The distinction concerns the *source* or *mode of justification* for our beliefs — specifically, whether justification depends on experience or can be achieved independently of it.

## Definitions

**A priori knowledge** is knowledge that can be justified (or known) independently of experience. The phrase "a priori" is Latin for "from the earlier" — the idea being that such knowledge precedes, or is independent of, sensory experience. Mathematical truths are the paradigm examples: you don't need to run experiments to know that 2 + 2 = 4. You can know it through pure reasoning.

**A posteriori knowledge** (sometimes called *empirical knowledge*) is knowledge that can only be justified through experience. "A posteriori" means "from the later" — such knowledge comes *after* experience has furnished the relevant information. That Paris is the capital of France, that water boils at 100°C at sea level, that there are mountains on the Moon — these are things you can only know through observation and empirical inquiry.

## Clear Cases

The distinction is most intuitive when we look at clear cases on both ends.

Clear cases of *a priori* knowledge:
- Mathematical truths: that there are infinitely many prime numbers, that the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the other sides.
- Logical truths: that every proposition of the form "if P then P" is true, that it cannot rain and not rain simultaneously.
- Conceptual truths: that all bachelors are unmarried, that all triangles have three sides.

Clear cases of *a posteriori* knowledge:
- Scientific facts: the speed of light, the mass of the electron, the age of the universe.
- Historical facts: that World War II ended in 1945, that Napoleon was exiled to Elba.
- Perceptual facts: that there is a cup on the table in front of you right now.

## A Subtlety: Knowledge vs. Justification

The a priori/a posteriori distinction is primarily a distinction about *justification*, not about knowledge per se. A belief is a priori justified if it can be justified without appeal to experience; it is a posteriori justified if experience is required.

This is important because all knowledge requires *some* experience in a trivial sense: we need experience to acquire the concepts that figure in our beliefs. A child who hasn't yet mastered the concept of "bachelor" can't know that all bachelors are unmarried, even though this is an a priori truth. The relevant sense of "independent of experience" is not that no experience whatsoever is involved, but that *justification* doesn't depend on particular experiences of how the world is.

## The Importance of the Distinction

The a priori/a posteriori distinction has been one of the organizing axes of epistemology for centuries:

- **Rationalists** (Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz) maintained that genuine knowledge is a priori — that reason, not the senses, is the primary source of knowledge about reality.
- **Empiricists** (Locke, Hume, the logical positivists) argued that all substantive knowledge about the world is a posteriori — that a priori truths are merely analytic (true by definition) and tell us nothing about reality.
- **Kant** tried to carve a middle path by arguing for the possibility of *synthetic a priori* knowledge — knowledge that is both a priori and genuinely informative about the structure of experience.

Each of these positions generates a distinctive picture of what we can know and how. Understanding the a priori/a posteriori distinction is therefore not just a matter of learning terminology — it's essential to navigating the central debates of the epistemological tradition.
