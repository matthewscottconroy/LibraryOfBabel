# The Analytic-Synthetic Distinction

Alongside the a priori/a posteriori distinction, the analytic/synthetic distinction is one of the foundational dividing lines of epistemology. The two distinctions are often conflated — but separating them is philosophically important, and Kant's decision to treat them as independent produced one of the most fruitful and contested ideas in the history of philosophy.

## Analytic and Synthetic Propositions

**Analytic propositions** are those that are true (or false) in virtue of the meanings of their constituent terms alone. The classic example is "all bachelors are unmarried." You don't need to go out into the world and investigate bachelors to know this is true. The predicate ("unmarried") is already contained in the subject ("bachelor"). Knowing the meanings of the words is sufficient to know the truth.

Kant's original formulation: a proposition is analytic if the concept of the predicate is *contained in* the concept of the subject. Alternative formulations: analytic truths are those whose denials are self-contradictory, or those that are true by definition, or those that are true in every possible world solely in virtue of meaning.

**Synthetic propositions** are those in which the predicate adds something not already contained in the subject. "All bachelors are unhappy" is synthetic — even if it happened to be true (which it doesn't), knowing what "bachelor" means wouldn't tell you whether it is. You'd need to go check. Most empirical claims are synthetic: "water is H₂O," "the Earth is approximately 4.5 billion years old," "Napoleon was short."

## The Relationship Between the Two Distinctions

The analytic/synthetic distinction is *conceptual* or *semantic* — it's about meaning and the containment of concepts. The a priori/a posteriori distinction is *epistemic* — it's about how we justify beliefs.

The standard empiricist assumption is that the two distinctions align perfectly: analytic truths are a priori, synthetic truths are a posteriori. A priori knowledge (like mathematics and logic) is, on this view, just knowledge of analytic truths — truths that hold by definition. Synthetic truths about the world require empirical investigation.

Kant rejected this alignment. He famously argued for the existence of *synthetic a priori* knowledge: claims that are both genuinely informative (not merely true by definition) and knowable without recourse to experience. His examples include arithmetic ("7 + 5 = 12," which he thought was synthetic because the concept of 12 is not contained in the concepts of 7, 5, and addition) and the principles of geometry and Newtonian physics.

## The Logical Positivist View

The logical positivists of the early twentieth century adopted a sharp version of the empiricist alignment. Their *verification principle* held that a meaningful statement must be either analytic (true by definition) or empirically verifiable (confirmable by experience). All genuine knowledge falls into one of these two categories; metaphysical claims that fit neither category are meaningless.

On this view, mathematics and logic are analytic — they are tautologies, empty of factual content. All substantive knowledge about the world is empirical. The synthetic a priori is an illusion; what Kant took to be a priori knowledge of geometry was simply a priori knowledge of *Euclidean* geometry as a formal system, not knowledge of the actual structure of physical space.

## Quine's Attack

In "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951), Quine launched a devastating attack on the analytic/synthetic distinction itself. His argument was that no principled line can be drawn between analytic and synthetic truths, because there is no theory-neutral notion of meaning that could support it.

The concept of analyticity seemed to rely on synonymy: a proposition is analytic if it can be reduced to a logical truth by substituting synonyms for synonyms. But synonymy in turn needs definition, and all proposed definitions seem to presuppose analyticity. The alleged distinction, Quine concluded, is not well-founded.

Quine's critique remains controversial. Many philosophers think he set the bar too high — that we can have a useful working notion of analyticity even if it lacks the sharp metaphysical foundations he demanded. But the critique permanently altered the landscape, making philosophers more cautious about invoking "mere verbal truth" and "pure conceptual analysis" as if these notions were unproblematic.

## The Significance of Synthetic A Priori

If the synthetic a priori is possible — if there are truths that are both knowable without experience and genuinely informative about reality — then a priori knowledge can extend into the fabric of the world, not just the fabric of our definitions. This would be immensely significant: it would mean that reason alone can tell us something substantive about how things are, not just about how we've chosen to use words.

Kant thought the synthetic a priori was the key to understanding how mathematics and physics can be both necessary and informative. Contemporary philosophers debate whether the synthetic a priori is genuinely possible, and if so, what its extent and limits are. The answer has implications for the epistemology of mathematics, ethics, and metaphysics.
