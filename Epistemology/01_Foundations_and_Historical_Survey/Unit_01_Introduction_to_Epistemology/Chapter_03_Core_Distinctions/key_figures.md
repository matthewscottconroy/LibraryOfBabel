# Key Figures

## Immanuel Kant (1724–1804)
Kant is the architect of the core distinctions that structure modern epistemology. His *Critique of Pure Reason* separates the a priori from the a posteriori (based on whether knowledge depends on experience), and the analytic from the synthetic (based on whether the predicate is contained in the concept of the subject). His most controversial claim is the existence of *synthetic a priori* judgments — judgments that are both informative about the world and knowable without empirical investigation. Mathematics and the principles of physics, he argued, are synthetic a priori.

## Saul Kripke (1940–2022)
Kripke's *Naming and Necessity* (1972/1980) demonstrated that the a priori/a posteriori and necessary/contingent distinctions are orthogonal — that there are necessary truths that can only be known a posteriori (e.g., "water is H₂O"), and contingent truths that appear to be knowable a priori (e.g., "the standard meter is one meter long"). This separation transformed the philosophy of language and metaphysics and had major epistemological implications.

## Gilbert Ryle (1900–1976)
Ryle's distinction between knowing-that (propositional knowledge) and knowing-how (practical knowledge) in *The Concept of Mind* challenged the intellectualist assumption that all knowledge is propositional. Ryle argued that knowing how to ride a bicycle is not merely knowing a set of propositions about bicycle riding; it is a practical capacity irreducible to propositional knowledge. This has generated ongoing debate about the relationship between theoretical and practical knowledge.

## Bertrand Russell (1872–1970)
Russell introduced the distinction between knowledge by acquaintance (direct, non-inferential knowledge of an object through experience — sense data, universals, oneself) and knowledge by description (indirect knowledge of something via a description that uniquely identifies it). This distinction tracks different epistemic relationships to objects and shaped analytic epistemology's understanding of how reference and knowledge interact.

## W.V.O. Quine (1908–2000)
Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" attacked the analytic/synthetic distinction as a "dogma" of empiricism without clear foundation. He argued that the distinction relies on a notion of synonymy that cannot be made precise without circularity. For Quine, no statement is immune to revision in the face of recalcitrant experience — the distinction between conceptual and empirical truth is a matter of degree, not kind. Quine's critique forced defenders of the a priori to rethink its foundations.

## Jason Stanley (1969–) and Timothy Williamson (1955–)
Stanley and Williamson's "Knowing How" (2001) argued against Ryle that knowing how to do something is a species of knowing-that — specifically, knowing that a certain way of doing something is a way of doing it. This intellectualist account sparked a major debate about whether practical and theoretical knowledge are genuinely distinct.
