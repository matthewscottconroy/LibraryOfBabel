# Thought Experiments

## The Bicycle Rider (Ryle)
**The scenario**: Consider someone who knows, in a propositional sense, every fact about bicycle riding — the physics of balance, the biomechanics of pedaling, the geometry of turning. They could ace a written exam on bicycle riding. But they have never ridden a bicycle and, when placed on one, fall immediately.

**The philosophical point**: This illustrates Ryle's distinction between knowing-that and knowing-how. The complete propositional knower does not yet have the practical knowledge — the skill — of riding. Knowing-how seems to be something over and above any set of propositions. Whether this means knowing-how is irreducible to knowing-that, or whether it means the subject lacks the right *kind* of knowing-that (knowing de se, in action, not theoretically), is debated.

## Water Is H₂O (Kripke)
**The scenario**: In 1750, chemists didn't know the molecular composition of water. "Water is H₂O" was unknown — it required scientific discovery. Yet, apparently, it couldn't have been otherwise: necessarily, if something is water, it is H₂O. How can a necessary truth be discovered a posteriori?

**The philosophical point**: This thought experiment separates the necessary/contingent and a priori/a posteriori distinctions. "Water is H₂O" is both necessary (true in all possible worlds) and a posteriori (known only through empirical discovery). Kripke calls this a *necessary a posteriori* truth. It challenges the assumption that what is necessary can be known without looking at the world.

## The Standard Meter (Kripke)
**The scenario**: There is a physical rod in Paris — "the standard meter" — which was used to define the unit "meter." By definition, the standard meter is one meter long. Can we know this a priori, just from the definition? And is it necessarily true?

**The philosophical point**: Kripke argues it is a *contingent a priori* truth: we can know it a priori (by definition) but it is not necessary (the rod could have been made shorter). This cases illustrates that some definitional truths are contingent — the definition fixes reference but doesn't make the sentence metaphysically necessary. The a priori and the necessary are again shown to be distinct.

## The Analytic/Synthetic Distinction Under Pressure (Quine)
**The scenario**: Consider the statement "All bachelors are unmarried." It seems to be analytic — true by definition, requiring no empirical investigation. Now suppose a sociologist reports that in some culture, bachelors marry secretly and widely. Should we revise our claim?

**The philosophical point**: Quine argues that even apparently analytic truths can be revised in the face of sufficient theoretical pressure. The "meaning" that makes "bachelor" analytically imply "unmarried" is not a hard constraint but a relatively central commitment in our web of beliefs. This challenges the idea that there is a sharp line between truths of meaning and truths of fact — a line that the a priori/a posteriori distinction seems to depend on.

## Knowing How to Swim vs. Knowing That Swimming Involves Arm Movements
**The scenario**: Two people are asked "do you know how to swim?" One says "Yes — I know that swimming involves coordinated arm and leg movements, that you should keep your face up, that..." but sinks when put in the water. The other says little but swims expertly.

**The philosophical point**: This illustrates the gap between propositional knowledge and practical competence. It also raises the question of what the *truth conditions* of "knowing how" statements are. If knowing how is just a special case of knowing-that, we need to identify *which* propositions. The thought experiment motivates the idea that there are at least two importantly different kinds of epistemic achievement.
