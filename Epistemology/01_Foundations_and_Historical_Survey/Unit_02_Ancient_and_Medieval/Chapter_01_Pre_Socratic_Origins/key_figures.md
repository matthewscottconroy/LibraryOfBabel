# Key Figures

## Heraclitus of Ephesus (c. 535–475 BCE)
Heraclitus held that the world is in constant flux — "you cannot step into the same river twice" — and that the *logos* (the rational principle of order) underlies all apparent change. For epistemology, the key question is: if everything is always changing, is stable knowledge possible? Heraclitus believed the logos was accessible to reason, but that most people fail to perceive it, trapped by the testimony of their senses without understanding the unity behind appearances.

## Parmenides of Elea (c. 515–450 BCE)
Parmenides drew the sharpest possible distinction between the way of truth and the way of opinion. The Way of Truth establishes that being is one, unchanging, and ungenerated — it cannot not-be. The Way of Opinion describes the sensory world but is inherently deceptive. Parmenides's epistemological legacy is the sharp separation between rational insight into necessary being and fallible perception of the changing world — a separation that shaped Plato's Forms.

## Protagoras of Abdera (c. 490–420 BCE)
Protagoras is the first recorded relativist: "Man is the measure of all things — of things that are, that they are, of things that are not, that they are not." This claim, as interpreted by Plato in the *Theaetetus*, entails that truth is relative to the perceiver: what seems true to someone is true for them. Plato's refutation of Protagoras — the "self-refutation argument" — remains a classic argument against epistemological relativism.

## Xenophanes of Colophon (c. 570–475 BCE)
Xenophanes was one of the first thinkers to reflect on the limits of human knowledge. He observed that if oxen and horses had gods, those gods would look like oxen and horses — an early argument for the relativity of religious belief to cultural perspective. On knowledge generally, he held that even if we happened to state the truth, we could not know that we had done so. This anticipates fallibilism and the problem of external world skepticism.

## Democritus of Abdera (c. 460–370 BCE)
Democritus, the atomist, developed an early form of skepticism about the senses. He distinguished between "genuine knowledge" — the knowledge of atoms and void accessible to the intellect — and "bastard knowledge" — the deliverances of sight, hearing, smell, taste, and touch, which reveal only appearances. The atoms themselves are colorless, tasteless, and odorless; sensory qualities are projections of the mind onto a quantitative world. This proto-scientific skepticism about secondary qualities anticipates Locke's distinction between primary and secondary qualities.
