# Recollection and Innate Knowledge in the Meno

Plato's dialogue the *Meno* is one of the most important texts in the history of epistemology, not because it provides a finished theory of knowledge, but because it dramatizes a genuine puzzle and proposes a solution that has resonated — and provoked controversy — ever since.

## Meno's Paradox

The dialogue begins with a question about virtue, but it quickly arrives at a more fundamental epistemological problem. Meno challenges Socrates with what has become known as *Meno's Paradox* (or the *Paradox of Inquiry*):

> "How will you inquire into something, Socrates, when you don't know at all what it is? For if you happen upon it, how will you know it is the thing you didn't know?"

The paradox is this: inquiry requires that you don't already know what you're looking for (otherwise you'd have nothing to inquire about). But if you don't know what you're looking for, you can't recognize the answer when you find it. Therefore, inquiry is impossible.

This seems absurd — inquiry obviously happens and sometimes succeeds. But the logical puzzle is real. What makes the answer *the answer*? What allows you to recognize that you've found it? There must be some prior cognitive grasp of what would count as an answer, or else inquiry is blind luck.

## The Theory of Recollection

Plato's solution, offered in the dialogue as a kind of myth but supported by a famous demonstration, is the *Theory of Recollection* (*anamnesis*). The soul, before birth, had direct acquaintance with the eternal truths — the Forms — and knowledge of them. At birth, this knowledge is "forgotten" (more precisely, suppressed). Learning is not acquiring new knowledge from outside; it is *recollecting* — recovering what the soul already knows.

Plato demonstrates this by having Socrates guide an unlettered slave boy through a geometry problem. The boy, who claims no knowledge of geometry, is led by careful questioning to discover a truth about the relationship between the side of a square and the side of a square double its area. Plato takes this as evidence that the boy already "had" the knowledge — it needed only to be elicited, not taught.

## Philosophical Assessment

The demonstration is less convincing than Plato intends. There's a difference between having the *capacity* to reason geometrically when guided (which may just be a general reasoning ability) and having *stored knowledge* that awaits recollection. Plato's demonstration shows the former; his theory requires the latter.

Nevertheless, the theory addresses the paradox. If we have some prior cognitive contact with the relevant truths — even if that contact is not presently conscious — then inquiry can be self-directed: we use implicit knowledge to guide the search for explicit knowledge. The theory of recollection is a precursor to later *innatism* — the view that some knowledge or cognitive structures are innate, not learned from experience.

## Innatism and Its Descendants

Plato's recollection theory is the ancestor of a long tradition of rationalist innatism. Descartes held that some ideas (the idea of God, mathematical concepts) are innate — stamped in the mind by God at creation, not derived from experience. Leibniz held that the mind contains all truths in the form of "virtual knowledge" that needs only to be made explicit.

In the twentieth century, Chomsky's argument for an innate language acquisition device revived the idea in a more scientifically tractable form. The child's rapid acquisition of complex grammar from impoverished input suggests, Chomsky argues, that the deep structure of language is somehow pre-given to the mind. This is not Platonic recollection, but it's in the same family.

The Meno's deeper legacy is the question it raises: How does inquiry get started? What makes it possible to learn things you don't already know? Any satisfactory epistemology needs to address this, whether by invoking innate knowledge, general cognitive capacities, or some account of how new concepts can be genuinely acquired.
