# Knowledge as True Belief with an Account

The third and final proposal in the *Theaetetus* is that **knowledge is true belief with an account** (*logos*). This is the ancient ancestor of the modern "justified true belief" analysis, and engaging with it carefully helps explain why the analysis initially seemed promising — and why it is vulnerable to objections.

## What Is an "Account"?

The Greek word *logos* is multiply ambiguous: it can mean reason, account, explanation, argument, definition, or formula. Plato explores three interpretations of what it means to have a *logos* accompanying a true belief.

**Logos as expression in speech**: To have an account is simply to be able to put your belief into words. This is immediately dismissed: being able to say what you believe doesn't add to its epistemic status. Someone can articulate a false belief clearly, and someone can have genuine knowledge that they struggle to express.

**Logos as enumeration of elements**: To have an account is to be able to list all the constituent parts of the thing known. Plato uses the example of knowing a syllable versus knowing its letters. This interpretation has more promise but faces a regress problem: do the elements themselves require accounts? If so, we either regress indefinitely or hit elements that can be known without an account — which undermines the requirement.

**Logos as specification of the differentiating mark**: To have an account is to identify what distinguishes the thing from everything else — its unique characteristic. This is the most sophisticated interpretation, but Socrates argues it doesn't work either: identifying a differentiating mark is itself something you either know or believe, and if you believe it, we still need to know whether you know it.

## The Dialogue's Conclusion

The *Theaetetus* ends in *aporia* — without a definitive account. This is philosophically honest. Plato doesn't pretend to have solved the problem. What he has done is eliminate three proposals, and in so doing, revealed the difficulty.

What would a successful account look like? It would need to identify a condition that:
- Is present in all genuine cases of knowledge
- Is absent in cases of mere true belief (lucky guesses, jury cases)
- Doesn't generate a regress
- Applies to all the things we ordinarily take ourselves to know

That is a tall order. The subsequent history of epistemology — including the entire post-Gettier industry of the twentieth century — can be read as an extended attempt to satisfy it.

## The Modern Inheritance

The modern justified true belief (JTB) analysis doesn't appear explicitly in Plato. But the *Theaetetus* contains all its ingredients: the truth condition (knowledge requires truth), the belief condition (knowledge involves a mental state of holding-as-true), and the sense that a third condition is needed to rule out lucky true beliefs and jury-style cases.

When Gettier published his famous three-page counterexample to JTB in 1963, he was operating within a tradition that Plato's dialogue had established over two millennia earlier. The puzzle of the *Theaetetus* — what distinguishes knowledge from true belief? — is still the puzzle.

## Why Aporia Is Productive

It might seem like ending a philosophical inquiry without a conclusion is a failure. But Platonic aporia serves an important function: it generates *genuine puzzlement* — the recognition that what seemed obvious is actually deeply problematic. Socrates' midwifery metaphor captures this: he doesn't fill students' minds with conclusions, but rather delivers — through questioning — the genuine problems they already, in some sense, carry.

The reader of the *Theaetetus* is supposed to leave the dialogue not satisfied but provoked: more aware of the difficulty, better equipped to recognize bad answers, and motivated to think harder. That, Plato suggests, is how philosophical understanding begins.
