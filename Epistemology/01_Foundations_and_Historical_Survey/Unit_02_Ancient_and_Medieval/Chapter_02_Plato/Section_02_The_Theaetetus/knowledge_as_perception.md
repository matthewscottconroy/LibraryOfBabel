# Knowledge as Perception: Theaetetus's First Proposal

The *Theaetetus* is Plato's most sustained and technically sophisticated epistemological dialogue. Unlike the Meno, it doesn't offer a positive theory at the end — it ends in *aporia*, without a definitive conclusion. But what it accomplishes through its three failed attempts to define knowledge is enormously valuable: it clarifies what knowledge cannot be, and in doing so, narrows the space of possibilities in ways that still structure the debate.

The young mathematician Theaetetus's first proposal is simple and natural: **knowledge is perception** (*aisthesis*). What you see, hear, feel — this is what you know.

## The Protagorean Connection

Socrates immediately connects this proposal to the famous doctrine of Protagoras: "Man is the measure of all things — of things that are, that they are; of things that are not, that they are not." If knowledge is perception, and perception is relative to the perceiver, then whatever appears true to someone is true *for that person*. There is no perspective-independent truth, no single fact of the matter about how things are.

This is a form of *relativism* — the view that truth varies with the perceiver. It sounds epistemically permissive: everyone's perceptions are equally authoritative, and no one can be wrong about what they perceive.

Socrates raises two devastating objections to this view.

## The Argument from Expertise

If whatever appears true to someone is true for them, then no one has more knowledge than anyone else. The doctor's opinion about medicine is no more true than the patient's. The navigator's opinion about sailing is no more true than the landlubber's. But this is obviously false: expertise is real, and some people know more about some subjects than others.

The relativist might respond that the doctor's treatments are better — more effective, more useful — even if not more "true." But this concession reintroduces objective evaluation: effectiveness and usefulness are properties things have (or lack) independent of what anyone perceives. The relativist cannot make sense of better and worse epistemic positions without undermining the relativism.

## The Self-Refutation Argument

Even more powerfully, Socrates argues that Protagorean relativism is self-refuting. If every belief is true for the person who holds it, then the belief that not every belief is true — the belief that Protagoras is wrong — is also true for those who hold it. Protagoras must, on his own theory, concede that his critics are right (for them). The theory undermines itself.

This is one of the earliest uses of the self-refutation argument in philosophy, and versions of it have been used against relativism ever since.

## The Problem of Past and Future

Socrates also raises a temporal problem. Knowledge is typically of things that are stable: you know that 7 × 8 = 56 now, but you also knew it yesterday and will know it tomorrow. Perception is fleeting: what you see changes from moment to moment. If knowledge were simply present perception, there would be no knowledge of past or future — but we clearly do know things about the past (what happened) and the future (what will happen in predictable ways).

## The Problem of the Senses Themselves

Perhaps most fundamentally, Socrates points out that certain objects of knowledge are not perceivable at all. You know that honesty is a virtue, that 5 is a prime number, that justice is better than injustice. None of these is perceived through the senses. The knowledge that connects different sensory modalities — that something is *both* loud and hot, say — cannot come from either sense alone. Some cognitive operations, including the ones that yield the most important knowledge, transcend mere perception.

## The Residue

The failure of the perception theory doesn't mean perception is irrelevant to knowledge. It means that perception alone is insufficient: it doesn't have the right features (stability, range, concern with truth and falsity) to constitute knowledge. What distinguishes knowledge from perception is not yet clear — that's what the rest of the *Theaetetus* tries to determine. But the discussion has done important work: it has shown that any adequate account of knowledge must go beyond what the senses deliver.
