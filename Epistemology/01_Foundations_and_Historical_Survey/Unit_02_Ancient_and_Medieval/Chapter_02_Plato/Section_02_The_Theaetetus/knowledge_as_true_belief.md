# Knowledge as True Belief

Having shown that knowledge cannot be identified with perception, Socrates and Theaetetus turn to a second proposal: **knowledge is true belief** (*alethes doxa*). This is a more plausible suggestion — it captures the intuition that knowledge requires getting things right, while being broader than mere perception.

## The Proposal and Its Immediate Appeal

The proposal has obvious virtues. It rules out lucky guesses (or does it?), it rules out false beliefs, and it doesn't tie knowledge to any particular cognitive faculty. Moreover, it allows for the kind of knowledge that matters practically: you know how to get from Athens to Corinth if you have a true belief about the route.

Plato had already noted, in the Meno, that true belief can be practically useful: if you have a true belief that will guide you to Larissa, you'll reach Larissa just as reliably as someone with knowledge. True belief, for practical purposes, seems equivalent to knowledge.

## The Wax Tablet and Aviary: Models of Mind

Before directly attacking the true belief account, Socrates introduces two extended analogies about the structure of the mind. These are fascinating in their own right.

The **wax tablet** model pictures the mind as a block of wax. Perceptions and thoughts leave impressions on the wax, like a seal ring pressed into soft material. Knowledge is having a clear, accurate impression; error is having a blurred, mistaken, or wrongly-placed impression. We confuse two things (say, two people we have met) when their impressions get confused in the wax.

The **aviary** model pictures the mind as a large aviary containing birds representing different pieces of knowledge. To know something is to have the relevant bird in the aviary; to actively know it — to have it available — is to have caught the bird and be holding it. Error is possible when you reach for one bird and catch another.

These models are ingenious attempts to explain how belief and error are possible. They also anticipate later cognitive science's interest in memory and knowledge retrieval.

## Attacking True Belief: The Jury Case

The main attack on the true belief account is elegant. Socrates asks us to imagine a jury that has been persuaded by a clever lawyer. The jury acquires true beliefs about what happened — they end up believing correctly who committed the crime. But the lawyer's arguments weren't reliable: they were persuasive rhetoric, not evidence-based reasoning. Has the jury gained *knowledge*?

Plato's answer is no. Knowledge requires that you arrive at true belief through the right kind of process — through genuine reasoning or direct access, not through persuasion that merely happens to arrive at the truth. A true belief formed through an epistemically unreliable process isn't knowledge even if it's true.

## Why This Matters

This argument anticipates the central post-Gettier insight: **truth and belief are not enough for knowledge; the process by which the belief is formed, or the connection between the belief and the truth, matters too**. The lawyer who persuades the jury with clever rhetoric achieves the right *output* (true belief) through the wrong *process* (non-evidential persuasion).

Plato's jury case is, in a sense, proto-Gettier: it shows that justification — or at least the right kind of connection between belief and truth — is required. The difference between Plato's case and Gettier's is subtle: in Plato's case, the deficiency is in the process of belief-formation; in Gettier's cases, the problem is that even an apparently justified true belief can come apart from knowledge in more exotic ways.

## The Residue

True belief fails as an account of knowledge because it doesn't capture *how* the true belief was reached. Knowledge seems to require not just truth and belief, but something about the epistemic credentials of the belief — what we might call *justification*, or a reliable connection to the truth.

This sets up the third proposal, which will prove both more plausible and more easily attacked: that knowledge is justified true belief. That proposal, as we'll see, is itself vulnerable — but the justification condition it adds represents a genuine philosophical advance over the bare true belief account.
