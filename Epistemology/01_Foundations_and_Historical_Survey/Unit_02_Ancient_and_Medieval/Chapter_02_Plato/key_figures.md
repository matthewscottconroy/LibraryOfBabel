# Key Figures

## Plato (428–348 BCE)
Plato is the central figure of ancient epistemology. His theory of Forms posits mind-independent abstract objects — perfect exemplars of qualities like Beauty, Justice, and Equality — as the proper objects of genuine knowledge. The sensible world is a realm of becoming, only imperfectly approximating the Forms; it is an object of belief (*doxa*), not knowledge (*episteme*). Knowledge of the Forms is achieved through reason and recollection, not through perception. The Divided Line and Allegory of the Cave dramatize the ascent from sensory appearance to rational knowledge. His account of knowledge as infallible, of its object as necessarily real, and of justification as rational understanding remains a touchstone.

## Socrates (470–399 BCE)
Through Plato's dialogues, Socrates appears as the paradigmatic philosophical questioner. His method of elenchos — systematic cross-examination leading to the exposure of inconsistency — models the critical examination of beliefs that Plato took to be necessary for philosophical progress. Socrates's insistence that the examined life is the only life worth living connects epistemology to ethics: right action requires right knowledge.

## Parmenides (c. 515–450 BCE)
As a character in Plato's *Parmenides*, Parmenides subjects the young Socrates's theory of Forms to rigorous criticism, including the "Third Man Argument" — if particular beautiful things participate in the Form of Beauty, and both particulars and the Form are beautiful, then there must be a further Form to explain what they share, and so on infinitely. This argument, which Plato never fully answered, continues to be discussed.

## Theaetetus (c. 415–369 BCE)
The historical Theaetetus was a mathematician; Plato chose to immortalize him as the interlocutor who proposes and defends candidate definitions of knowledge in the *Theaetetus*. The dialogue's three proposals — knowledge as perception, as true belief, and as true belief with an account — are argued for by Theaetetus and refuted by Socrates. Theaetetus represents the intelligent student who must have their initial, confident views subjected to rigorous examination.

## Meno (historical, 5th century BCE)
Meno, as represented in Plato's dialogue, begins confidently able to define virtue and ends in *aporia* — genuinely puzzled. His dialogue with Socrates introduces the paradox of inquiry, the hypothesis of recollection, and the distinction between knowledge and true belief (via the road to Larissa example). Meno represents the transition from pre-philosophical confidence to genuine philosophical puzzlement.

## J.M.E. McTaggart (1866–1925) / F.H. Bradley (1846–1924)
These British Idealists, though not Plato scholars, developed neo-Platonic themes: that genuine reality is coherent and rational; that the sensory world is appearance rather than ultimate reality; that knowledge requires grasping the logical structure of an integrated whole. Their work represents the most sustained neo-Platonic epistemology in modern Western philosophy.
