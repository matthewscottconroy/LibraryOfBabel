# The Posterior Analytics: Demonstration and Scientific Knowledge

The *Posterior Analytics* is Aristotle's systematic treatment of scientific knowledge and demonstration. It is a demanding and technical work, but its central ideas have had enormous influence on philosophy, logic, and the philosophy of science.

## The Project

Aristotle's project in the *Posterior Analytics* is to explain what it means to have *genuine* understanding of something — not just to believe it, not even just to believe it truly, but to understand *why* it is the case. He calls this kind of understanding *apodeixis* — demonstration — and the knowledge it produces *episteme*.

## The Structure of a Demonstration

A demonstration is a deductive argument whose premises are known with certainty and whose conclusion necessarily follows. But Aristotle adds specific requirements for the premises:

**Necessity**: The premises must state what holds necessarily — what could not be otherwise. You can derive the fact that the angles of this particular triangle sum to 180° from a general necessary truth about all triangles. But the particular fact is demonstrated; the general truth is not (it is known immediately by *nous*).

**Priority and Cause**: The premises must be prior to — and explanatorily more basic than — the conclusion. They must state the *cause* (aitia) of what the conclusion states. Demonstrating that something is the case by deriving it from its cause is what distinguishes genuine understanding from merely knowing the fact.

**Universal Predicates**: The premises must involve universals predicated of kinds essentially — as part of what it is to be that kind of thing. "Triangles have angles summing to 180°" is about triangles essentially; "triangles are drawn on blackboards" is not.

## The Two-Stage Structure of Science

Aristotle's picture of a mature science has two stages.

The first stage consists of **immediate first principles**: premises that are known directly by *nous*, without demonstration. These include definitions (of the subject-matter and its essential properties), axioms (principles common to all sciences, like the law of non-contradiction), and postulates (claims specific to the science in question that are assumed without proof).

The second stage consists of **demonstrated theorems**: propositions derived from the first principles by valid deduction. These are known through demonstration, not directly.

A fully articulated science maps a field of knowledge into this hierarchical structure: first principles at the base, demonstrable conclusions building upward.

## The Concern About Circularity

Aristotle explicitly addresses a potential objection: if knowledge requires demonstration, and demonstration requires premises that are known, then how are the premises known? He considers and rejects two unsatisfactory answers:

**Circular demonstration**: Some predecessors argued that you can demonstrate P from Q and Q from P. Aristotle dismisses this: circular demonstration doesn't explain anything; it just moves in a loop.

**Infinite regress**: If every premise requires its own demonstration from prior premises, the regress never ends and no knowledge is possible.

The only way out is **first principles** — premises that are known immediately and non-demonstratively, by *nous*. This is Aristotle's foundationalism.

## The Meno Problem Revisited

Aristotle addresses the *Meno* paradox directly: how can we learn through demonstration if we must already know the premises? He distinguishes two kinds of prior knowledge:

- Universal knowledge: knowing that all triangles have a certain property
- Particular knowledge: knowing that this figure is a triangle

When we demonstrate a particular conclusion from a universal premise, we already know the universal. The demonstration gives us particular knowledge by applying the universal to the particular case. There is no viciously circular prior knowledge of the conclusion — only prior knowledge of what gets applied to yield it.

## Legacy and Influence

The *Posterior Analytics* shaped medieval scholasticism's understanding of science: Aquinas's discussion of *scientia* draws directly on it. It influenced the early modern period through its ideal of deductive science — an ideal that Descartes aspired to and that Newton partially achieved. It also provoked sustained reactions: empiricists challenged the idea that scientific knowledge requires necessary premises; twentieth-century philosophers of science largely abandoned the demonstration model in favor of hypothetico-deductive testing.

But the underlying questions Aristotle raises — about what understanding consists in, what makes an explanation a good explanation, what the structure of scientific knowledge is — remain alive and contested in contemporary philosophy of science.
