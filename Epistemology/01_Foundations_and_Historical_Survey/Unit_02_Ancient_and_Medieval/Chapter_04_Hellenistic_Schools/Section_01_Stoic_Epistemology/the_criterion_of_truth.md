# The Criterion of Truth: Stoic Epistemology in Context

The Greek phrase *kriterion tes aletheias* — criterion of truth — refers to the standard by which genuine knowledge can be distinguished from mere belief or false impression. The debate over the criterion was one of the defining controversies of Hellenistic philosophy, and it shapes epistemological discussion to this day.

## Why the Criterion Problem Arises

The problem emerges from a very natural place: we can be wrong. We've had experiences we took to be veridical that turned out to be false — hallucinations, illusions, mistaken identifications. How do we avoid systematically being wrong? We need some standard for distinguishing reliable from unreliable cognitions.

But this generates a problem. Any proposed criterion must itself be evaluated — it must be checked against some further standard. That further standard must in turn be evaluated, and so on. Either there is a regress, or we must adopt a criterion that certifies itself — which seems like question-begging.

## The Stoic Position Elaborated

The Stoics held that the kataleptic impression is both the criterion of truth and the foundation of knowledge. In their epistemological scheme, there are three stages of epistemic achievement:

**Katalepsis** (comprehension): The cognitive grasp of reality achieved when one assents to a kataleptic impression. This is a kind of direct, reliable cognitive contact with things as they are.

**Episteme** (knowledge): A condition in which one's comprehensions are organized into a systematic, stable whole — a state that the wise person possesses and ordinary people lack. Knowledge is not just having kataleptic impressions; it's having them in a way that is rationally organized and unshakeable.

**Common notions** (*koinai ennoiai*): Certain basic conceptual truths that are accessible to all rational beings simply in virtue of their rationality. These include basic logical principles and foundational normative claims. They are a kind of innate potential that experience activates.

## Epicurean Alternatives

The Epicureans offered a different approach. For them, all sense-perception is infallible: the sense organs accurately transmit information about the world, and errors arise only at the level of inference and interpretation. If you misidentify a stick as bent, the fault lies in your inference, not in the perception itself.

This has a certain elegance: by making perception itself infallible, Epicureans avoid the problem of distinguishing veridical from non-veridical impressions at the perceptual level. But the elegance comes at a cost: if all perception is reliable, then hallucinations and illusions seem inexplicable, unless they're recharacterized as misinterpretations rather than misperceptions.

## The Skeptical Response: Suspending Judgment

The Academic Skeptics — Arcesilaus, Carneades, Philo — argued that there is no criterion of truth. No phenomenal character, no internal mark, can guarantee the truth of an impression. They proposed *epoché* — suspension of judgment — as the appropriate response to this conclusion.

Carneades offered a sophisticated middle ground: even without knowledge, we can act on *probable* (or "persuasive") impressions — those that seem clear, stable, and consistent with other impressions. This is not knowledge, but it's sufficient for practical guidance. Carneades' position anticipates modern probabilism and fallibilism.

## The Significance for Later Philosophy

The criterion debate established several ideas that continued to shape epistemology:

**Self-evident truths**: The Stoic idea that some cognitions carry their own guarantees of truth anticipates Descartes' clear and distinct ideas and the foundationalist tradition's appeal to incorrigible basic beliefs.

**The regress of justification**: The demand for a criterion that is itself certified generates an infinite regress that foundationalists, coherentists, and infinitists have each proposed to handle differently.

**Probability without certainty**: Carneades' probabilism anticipates the probabilistic epistemology developed by Hume, the logical positivists, and contemporary Bayesians.

The Hellenistic debate about the criterion was in some ways more explicitly epistemological and technically sophisticated than anything in Plato or Aristotle. It is a direct ancestor of the modern epistemological tradition.
