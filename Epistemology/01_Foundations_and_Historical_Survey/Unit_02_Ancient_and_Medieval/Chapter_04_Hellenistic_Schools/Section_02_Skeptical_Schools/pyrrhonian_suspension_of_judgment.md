# Pyrrhonism: Suspension of Judgment and Tranquility

Pyrrhonism is one of the most radical and philosophically interesting positions in the history of epistemology. Named after Pyrrho of Elis (c. 360–270 BCE), it is primarily known to us through the extensive writings of Sextus Empiricus (second century CE), who systematized Pyrrhonian arguments in the *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* and the *Against the Mathematicians*. Pyrrhonism is not just a skeptical position — it is a way of life, an attempt to achieve tranquility through the practice of suspending judgment.

## The Pyrrhonian Method

The Pyrrhonist does not claim to know that nothing can be known — that would itself be a claim to knowledge, and it would be inconsistent. Instead, the Pyrrhonist notes that for every argument in favor of a position, an equally compelling argument against it can be produced. When faced with opposed arguments of equal force, the appropriate response is *epoché* — suspension of judgment.

This is the distinctively Pyrrhonian move. You don't conclude that the truth is unknowable; you simply find that you cannot determine which way the argument goes, and so you refrain from committing to either side. The suspension is not a conclusion but a state — you end up *not believing* rather than believing that believing is impossible.

## The Ten Modes of Aenesidemus

The arguments for suspension were systematized into groups called *tropes* (modes). The most famous collection, attributed to Aenesidemus, contains ten modes. They provide reasons to suspend judgment about appearances by noting the variety and relativity of how things seem:

1. **Animals**: Different animals perceive the same objects differently (honey seems sweet to humans, bitter to those with jaundice).
2. **Humans**: Different people perceive the same things differently due to differences in constitution, upbringing, circumstance.
3. **Senses**: The same object appears differently across different senses — a painting looks three-dimensional but feels flat.
4. **Circumstances**: The same thing looks different depending on circumstances — an oar looks bent in water.
5. **Positions and distances**: The same tower looks round from far away, square from close up.
6. **Admixtures**: Nothing is perceived in isolation but always mixed with something (air, water, light).
7. **Quantities and compositions**: Sand feels smooth individually but rough in a pile; wine consumed in moderation is pleasant, in excess harmful.
8. **Relativity**: Everything appears relative to the perceiver and the circumstances.
9. **Frequency**: Unusual things seem more impressive (an eclipse vs. the sun rising).
10. **Customs and beliefs**: What counts as proper, beautiful, just varies across cultures.

## From Suspension to Tranquility

The practical payoff of Pyrrhonism — and this is what distinguishes it from mere philosophical skepticism — is *ataraxia*, tranquility. Pyrrho reportedly observed that our anxieties and disturbances arise from our beliefs: we fear what we believe to be harmful, desire what we believe to be good. Suspend judgment about what is really good and really bad, and the emotional disturbances associated with those beliefs dissolve.

Tranquility, on the Pyrrhonian picture, follows suspension of judgment the way a shadow follows a body — not as something aimed at, but as the natural result of abandoning the dogmatic attempt to determine how things really are.

## Practical Action Without Belief

An obvious objection: if you suspend judgment about everything, how do you act? Sextus's answer appeals to the *phenomena* — appearances that present themselves without any commitment to their accuracy. You follow four guides:

1. **Nature**: Natural impulses like hunger and thirst.
2. **Senses**: How things appear, without asserting that they are as they appear.
3. **Laws and customs**: Socially transmitted norms, followed without endorsing them as objectively correct.
4. **Arts and skills**: Practical competences that don't require philosophical views.

This is a minimalist picture of practical life — a life governed by appearances and habits without the distorting overlay of metaphysical commitment.

## The Pyrrhonian Legacy

Pyrrhonism was largely forgotten through the Middle Ages but was dramatically revived in the sixteenth century when Sextus Empiricus's writings became available. Montaigne, Erasmus, and others drew on Pyrrhonism to argue for intellectual humility. Descartes' method of doubt is a response to Pyrrhonism as much as to any other skeptical tradition. Hume's philosophy has deep Pyrrhonian elements. Contemporary epistemology returns repeatedly to Pyrrhonian themes — the problem of the criterion, the regress of justification, the structure of skeptical challenges — as live and unresolved.
