## Zeno of Citium (c. 334–262 BCE)

Founder of Stoicism and originator of the Stoic epistemological framework, Zeno introduced the pivotal concept of the *phantasia kataleptike* (kataleptic impression)—an impression of such clarity and distinctness that it "grips" the mind and cannot originate from what is not the case. For Zeno, the criterion of truth is not bare sensation but the specific class of impressions that carry their own guarantee of veridicality. His debate with Academic skeptics, particularly Arcesilaus, established the terms of Hellenistic epistemological controversy for two centuries.

## Chrysippus of Soli (c. 279–206 BCE)

The third head of the Stoa and arguably its greatest systematic thinker, Chrysippus elaborated and defended Zeno's epistemology against Academic objections. He refined the doctrine of common notions (*koinai ennoiai*)—concepts that develop in all human minds through natural maturation without special instruction—and argued that these notions, alongside kataleptic impressions, provide the foundations of knowledge. His ten-volume work on dialectic and his defence of *synkatathesis* (assent) as a voluntary rational act were central to the Stoic picture of responsible epistemic agency.

## Epicurus (341–270 BCE)

Epicurus developed a thoroughgoing empiricist epistemology centred on the "canon" (kanon) or rule of truth, consisting of three criteria: sensations (*aistheseis*), prolepses (*prolepseis* or preconceptions), and feelings (*pathe*). On his view, all sensations are infallible at the level of bare reception—error enters only with the judgment we add to them. Prolepses are general concepts that arise from repeated sensory experience and function as criteria for recognizing instances of types; his treatment anticipates later debates about natural kinds and concept formation.

## Arcesilaus of Pitane (c. 316–241 BCE)

As head of Plato's Academy, Arcesilaus transformed it into a skeptical institution, arguing against the Stoic claim that any impression compels rational assent. His key move was to argue that for every kataleptic impression there is an indistinguishable non-kataleptic one, so the Stoic criterion is unworkable. He advocated *epoche* (suspension of judgment) as the appropriate response to this underdetermination, and argued controversially that suspension is compatible with practical action guided by what is "persuasive" (*to eulogon*). He wrote nothing; his views are reconstructed from Sextus, Cicero, and Numenius.

## Carneades of Cyrene (c. 214–129 BCE)

The most formidable of the Academic skeptics, Carneades developed a probabilist epistemology as a practical surrogate for knowledge. He introduced the criterion of the *pithanon* (the convincing or probable impression), which is not truth-tracking but merely useful for deliberation. His arguments against Stoic epistemology included the famous "lazy argument" and a systematic attack on divination and theological knowledge. His disagreement with his pupil Clitomachus about whether he genuinely endorsed probabilism or merely deployed it dialectically remains a contested interpretive question.

## Pyrrho of Elis (c. 360–270 BCE)

The founding figure of Pyrrhonism, Pyrrho reportedly taught that things are "indeterminate, unmeasurable, and inarbitrable," leading to the prescription of withholding assertion (*aphasia*) and achieving mental tranquillity (*ataraxia*). He left no writings; his philosophy is known through his disciple Timon of Phlius and, much later, through Sextus Empiricus. Whether Pyrrho was concerned with epistemology in the systematic sense or with a practical ethos of non-commitment remains debated, but the tradition that bears his name made him the exemplar of radical suspension of judgment.

## Sextus Empiricus (c. 160–210 CE)

The principal surviving expositor of Pyrrhonism, Sextus was a physician who organized the accumulated skeptical arguments of the tradition into two major surviving works. His presentation of the ten modes of Aenesidemus (arguments from the variability of perceptual experience) and the five modes of Agrippa (arguments targeting the structure of justification itself) constitutes the most sophisticated ancient challenge to the possibility of epistemic foundations. His work was rediscovered in the sixteenth century and exercised enormous influence on early modern philosophy, particularly on Montaigne, Descartes, and Hume.
