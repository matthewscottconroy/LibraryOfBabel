# Analogy and Divine Knowledge in Aquinas

One of the most technically sophisticated aspects of Aquinas's epistemology concerns the problem of how we can meaningfully speak and reason about God. This is not merely a theological problem — it connects to fundamental questions about language, meaning, reference, and the limits of human cognition.

## The Problem of Theological Language

Any serious theology faces a dilemma. If our language about God is *univocal* — if "wise" means the same thing whether applied to a human or to God — then God is assimilated to human categories and reduced to something we can comprehend. But God, on the theological view, transcends human comprehension; describing God as simply "very wise" fails to capture the infinite gulf between divine and human wisdom.

On the other hand, if our language about God is purely *equivocal* — if "wise" means something entirely different when applied to God — then theological language becomes empty noise. We can't use it to make any claims about God that we could understand.

## Aquinas's Solution: Analogy

Aquinas's solution is the doctrine of **analogy**. Terms applied to God and creatures are neither univocal nor equivocal but analogical: they apply to both, but in ways that are related to each other rather than identical.

Aquinas distinguishes two types of analogy:

**Analogy of attribution**: One term is primary and the others are derivative. "Healthy" is said primarily of organisms, derivatively of urine (which is healthy *in the sense of indicating* health), food (which is healthy *in the sense of producing* health), and a complexion (which is healthy *as a sign of* health). The term has different applications, but all are related to the primary instance.

**Analogy of proportionality**: The term applies in different proportions to different types of things. "Being" applies to substances (which are beings primarily), accidents (which are beings secondarily), and God (who is Being itself). The same general content applies, but scaled to the different levels of being.

When we say that God is "wise," we are not saying that God has the same kind of wisdom as a human sage, only more of it. We are saying that there is in God the source and cause of what we find as wisdom in creatures — a wisdom related to ours as the model to the image, the original to the reflection.

## The Via Negativa

The negative way (*via negativa* or *apophatic theology*) is complementary to analogical predication. We can say what God is not: not finite, not material, not temporal, not composed, not passible. These negations are reliable because they rest on our understanding of what is incompatible with perfect, infinite, immutable being.

Pseudo-Dionysius (c. 500 CE), an important influence on Aquinas, went further and argued that the negative way is more reliable than the positive way. Our positive concepts are derived from creaturely experience and always risk distorting what they apply to; the negations simply remove inappropriate limitations.

Aquinas accepts this priority of the negative way for knowing *what God is like* but insists that the positive way is necessary for knowing that God *exists* and *acts*.

## The Cognitive Situation of Theology

Aquinas's framework generates a rich picture of the human cognitive situation with respect to theology. We know that God exists (through the five ways), we know that God has perfections (goodness, wisdom, power) in an eminent mode, and we know what God is not. But we don't comprehend God — we don't have the kind of exhaustive, direct understanding of the divine essence that God has of itself or that the blessed have through the *beatific vision*.

This is a form of what contemporary philosophers of religion call *cognitive humility* about theological knowledge. We can have real knowledge of God — enough to guide life and worship — without having comprehensive knowledge. The limits of our knowledge are themselves informative.

## The Epistemological Legacy

Aquinas's doctrine of analogy contributed to several important debates:

- The debate about the univocity of being (Scotus argued that "being" must be univocal across God and creatures for natural theology to be possible at all — a significant disagreement with Aquinas).
- The philosophy of language: the claim that language can be *analogical* — that terms can be meaningfully applied across different categories — has implications for metaphysics and semantics.
- Contemporary religious epistemology: discussions of how to evaluate religious claims, how religious language functions, and what knowledge of God is possible all engage with the Thomistic framework, whether to accept, modify, or reject it.
