# Intellect and Sensory Knowledge in Aquinas

Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274) is the greatest philosopher-theologian of the medieval period. His epistemology represents a synthesis of Aristotelian philosophy and Christian theology, and it is more systematically developed than anything before it in the medieval tradition. Where Augustine privileged reason over sense and illumination over natural cognition, Aquinas rehabilitates sensory experience as a genuine and essential source of knowledge.

## The Aristotelian Starting Point

Aquinas's epistemology is fundamentally Aristotelian. Against the Platonic-Augustinian tradition that regarded sensory experience with suspicion, Aquinas insists: *nihil est in intellectu quod prius non fuerit in sensu* — nothing is in the intellect that was not first in the senses.

This is the empiricist foundation of Aquinas's epistemology. The human intellect, in its earthly existence, acquires knowledge through and from sensory experience. Abstract concepts — the concept of substance, cause, being, justice — are derived by the intellect's abstractive operation on sensory material. There are no innate ideas; all knowledge, including metaphysical and theological knowledge, is built up from sensory materials.

## The Structure of Cognitive Process

Aquinas articulates a detailed model of the cognitive process that moves from sense perception to intellectual understanding.

**Sensory cognition** begins with the external senses (sight, hearing, touch, taste, smell), which receive the sensible forms of objects. The internal senses — the common sense, imagination, memory, and the *vis aestimativa* (evaluative power) — integrate, retain, and compare sensory representations.

**Intellectual cognition** requires the operation of the **agent intellect** (*intellectus agens*) on sensory representations. The agent intellect abstracts the intelligible form from the sensory image — it strips away the material and particular conditions and yields the universal, intelligible content that the **possible intellect** (*intellectus possibilis*) can receive and know.

This is Aquinas's reading of Aristotle's active/passive intellect distinction. The agent intellect is not a separate divine entity (as some Islamic philosophers, notably Averroes, had argued) but a natural power of the human intellect itself.

## Knowledge of God: Analogy and Via Negativa

Aquinas's epistemology becomes particularly interesting — and philosophically important — in his treatment of theological knowledge. Can we know God? And if so, how?

Aquinas's answer invokes the distinction between the *via affirmativa* (positive way), *via negativa* (negative way), and *via eminentiae* (way of eminence). We can attribute properties to God positively (God is wise, good, powerful), negatively (God is not finite, not material, not composed of parts), and by eminence (God is supremely wise, infinitely good).

Crucially, these attributions are *analogical* rather than *univocal*. When we say that God is wise and that Socrates is wise, we're not using "wise" in exactly the same sense. There is a genuine relationship — the wisdom of creatures is a participated, derivative form of God's wisdom — but the analogy is not simple identity of meaning.

This doctrine of analogy is a significant contribution to the philosophy of language and to epistemology. It identifies a third way between literal univocal predication (impossible across a categorical difference) and pure equivocation (which would make theological claims meaningless). Whether the analogical doctrine is successful remains disputed.

## Natural Reason and Revelation

Aquinas distinguishes between what natural reason can know about God and what requires revelation. The existence of God, that God is one, simple, good, and omnipotent — these are, Aquinas argues, knowable by natural reason through the famous "five ways." But the Trinity, the Incarnation, the Resurrection — these are *mysteries* that exceed natural reason and are known only through revelation.

This creates a two-tier epistemology: *praeambula fidei* (preambles of faith), known by reason, and *articuli fidei* (articles of faith), known by faith. The two domains are not in conflict; they are complementary. Natural theology provides the rational foundation for the possibility of revelation; revelation fills in what reason cannot reach.

Aquinas's synthesis — accepting sensory experience, exercising natural reason, and integrating both with revealed faith — is one of the most ambitious epistemological programs in the history of philosophy.
