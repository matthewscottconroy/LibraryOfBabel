# William of Ockham and Nominalism

William of Ockham (c. 1287–1347) is one of the most important and intellectually distinctive philosophers of the medieval period. His nominalism — the view that universal terms do not correspond to real universal entities in the world — had profound epistemological implications, and his methodological principle of parsimony ("Ockham's Razor") became one of the most widely cited principles in the history of philosophy.

## The Problem of Universals

Medieval philosophy inherited from ancient philosophy a fierce debate about the status of universals — the general terms and concepts we use to classify things. When we say that Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle are all human, does the word "human" pick out some real entity (humanity, human nature) that they all share? Or is it merely a convenient label we attach to a group of individuals that resemble each other?

**Realists** (following a broadly Platonic line) held that universals are real entities — either existing in a transcendent realm (Plato) or inhering in particular things (Aristotle). What makes Socrates human is his participation in or instantiation of the real universal human nature.

**Nominalists** denied this. Only individual things exist. Universal terms are just names (*nomina*) — they don't pick out real entities, only groups of individuals. What makes Socrates human is not his relationship to a universal but simply the fact that he is similar to other things we call human.

Ockham was the most rigorous and consequential medieval nominalist.

## Ockham's Razor

Ockham's methodological principle is usually stated as "entities should not be multiplied beyond necessity" (*entia non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem*). In application to universals: if we can explain everything without positing real universal entities, we should not posit them.

Ockham's argument is that everything that needs to be explained about human knowledge and classification can be explained by positing:
- Individual things in the world
- Mental concepts (*intentiones animae*) that function as natural signs of groups of individuals
- Conventional spoken and written terms that express these concepts

No additional entities — universals existing in the world — are needed. Positing them multiplies entities unnecessarily and thereby violates rational economy.

## Intuitive and Abstractive Cognition

Ockham's epistemology distinguishes two fundamental modes of cognition:

**Intuitive cognition** is direct perceptual awareness of individual existing things. When you see this red apple in front of you right now, you have intuitive cognition of it. Intuitive cognition is what grounds existential judgments: "this exists," "this is red."

**Abstractive cognition** is cognition that abstracts from whether the thing exists or has a certain property. When you think about apples in general, or recall a past apple, or imagine a fictional apple, you're engaged in abstractive cognition. It operates on the basis of prior intuitive cognitions but does not itself require the present existence of its object.

Knowledge of universals, on Ockham's view, is abstractive: it involves mental terms (concepts) that represent many individuals but doesn't involve apprehending a real entity called "applehood." The concept is the universal — but it exists only in the mind, not in the world.

## Epistemological Implications

Ockham's nominalism has important epistemological consequences:

**Empiricism**: Since our knowledge of individual things comes through intuitive cognition — direct perceptual awareness — empirical observation is the foundation of knowledge. We can't know general truths prior to knowing individual instances.

**The Separation of Faith and Reason**: Ockham pushed further than Aquinas in separating natural knowledge from revealed theology. Many theological claims — the Trinity, God's attributes, the immortality of the soul — are not demonstrable by natural reason and must be accepted on faith alone. This *via moderna* (new way) contributed to later Protestant theology and to the eventual separation of theology and natural philosophy.

**The Voluntarist Theology**: Ockham's God is radically free — able to command anything, including what seems morally repugnant. Moral truths are not discovered through natural reason but revealed by divine command. This voluntarism had implications for moral epistemology: moral knowledge is not rational insight into a moral order but obedient reception of divine commands.

Ockham's influence was immense, particularly through the late medieval *via moderna* tradition. Indirectly, his nominalism and his separation of faith and reason contributed to the conditions that made the Scientific Revolution possible.
