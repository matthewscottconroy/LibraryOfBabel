# Duns Scotus: Individuation, Intuition, and the Univocity of Being

John Duns Scotus (c. 1266–1308) is one of the most technically sophisticated medieval philosophers — the "Subtle Doctor," as he was known. His epistemology makes several contributions that are important both in their own right and as targets of Ockham's later criticisms.

## The Problem of Individuation

One of Scotus's major contributions is his solution to the *problem of individuation*: what makes an individual the particular individual it is? What distinguishes *this* horse from *that* horse, if they share all the same universal properties (being a horse, being brown, being a certain size)?

Scotus's answer is the *haecceity* (from Latin *haec*, "this") — the "thisness" of an individual, a non-qualitative property that makes it the particular thing it is. Haecceity is not any of the thing's universal properties; it is what grounds the individual's uniqueness and cannot be shared.

Epistemologically, this raises the question: how do we cognize haecceity? Scotus's answer involves his distinction between intuitive and abstractive cognition — a distinction later refined by Ockham.

## The Univocity of Being

Perhaps Scotus's most famous epistemological-metaphysical claim is that "being" is *univocal* — it applies to God and creatures in exactly the same sense. This directly contradicts Aquinas's analogical account.

Scotus's argument: for natural theology to be possible, we must be able to know certain things about God using concepts derived from creaturely experience. We form the concept "wise" from experience of creaturely wisdom and then apply it to God. If the concept applies entirely differently when applied to God, we have no cognitive grasp of what we're attributing to God when we call God wise. Natural theology collapses into meaninglessness.

For natural theology to work, the concepts we use must apply univocally to God and creatures — in exactly the same sense, even if God instantiates these properties in a radically higher degree. The concept of being must apply univocally; then further specification distinguishes finite being (creatures) from infinite being (God).

This is a significant epistemological claim: it asserts the *possibility* of genuine natural knowledge of God by insisting on the applicability of human concepts to divine reality.

## Intuitive Cognition in Scotus

Scotus's account of intuitive cognition is more complex than Ockham's later version. For Scotus, intuitive cognition (cognition of an existing individual as present and existing) is possible even in the absence of the object — through a special divine intervention that produces a "relative intuitive cognition" in the mind without an object.

This has a controversial implication: there could be an experience that is phenomenologically indistinguishable from veridical perception but lacks a physical cause. Ockham develops this possibility further, arguing (apparently as a thought experiment) that God could supernaturally cause us to have intuitive cognitions of non-existent objects — which would have the immediate implication that our perceptions might not be reliable guides to what exists.

## Scotus and the Problem of Error

Scotus is aware of the skeptical problem. How can we trust our cognitions? His response appeals to the self-evident character of the most basic truths: principles like the law of non-contradiction are known with certainty because denying them is self-defeating. Experience, combined with the intellect's natural reliability, provides knowledge of contingent truths with high (but perhaps not maximal) confidence.

Scotus thus occupies a middle ground between Augustinian divine illumination and Ockham's more naturalistic epistemology. He maintains that the human intellect has a natural capacity for truth, grounded in its creation by God, while acknowledging the possibility of error and the need for careful epistemic discipline.

The interplay between Scotus and Ockham on intuitive cognition, universals, and the reliability of perception represents one of the richest and most technically detailed epistemological debates in the history of philosophy.
