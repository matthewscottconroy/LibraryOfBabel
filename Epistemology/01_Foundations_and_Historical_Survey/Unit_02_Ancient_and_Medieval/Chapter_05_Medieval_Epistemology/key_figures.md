## Augustine of Hippo (354–430 CE)

Augustine synthesized the Platonic theory of recollection with Christian theology to produce the doctrine of divine illumination: the human mind does not grasp eternal truths through its own powers but only through the illumination of God, who functions as the "intelligible sun" that makes the forms of things visible to the intellect. In *Against the Academicians* he argues against Academic skepticism that the cogito-like certainty of one's own existence and thought is immune to doubt. His account of the *verbum mentis* (inner word) and the soul's triune structure (memory, intellect, will) as image of the Trinity shaped the entire subsequent Latin tradition.

## Anselm of Canterbury (1033–1109 CE)

Anselm pursued what he called *fides quaerens intellectum* (faith seeking understanding): theology does not replace reason but requires rational articulation of what is believed. His ontological argument for God's existence—the inference from the concept of "that than which nothing greater can be conceived" to the necessity of that being's existence—is simultaneously a theological and an epistemological claim about the relationship between conceptual content and existence. Anselm's analysis of truth in *De Veritate* identifies truth not merely with propositional correctness but with the rectitude or fittingness of a judgment to its object, anticipating later debates about correspondence theories.

## Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274 CE)

Aquinas integrated Aristotelian epistemology with Christian theology in a comprehensive synthesis. Against Augustinian illuminationism, he argued that the human intellect abstracts intelligible species from sensory phantasms through the activity of the agent intellect—a natural cognitive faculty, not a divine intervention. He maintained that we can know God only analogically: divine attributes such as goodness and wisdom apply to God neither univocally (in the same sense as to creatures) nor equivocally (in a completely different sense) but analogically, tracking a relationship of causal dependence. This account of analogical predication remains the standard scholastic framework for theological language.

## William of Ockham (c. 1287–1347 CE)

Ockham's nominalism denied the reality of universals, holding that only particular individuals exist and that general terms are merely mental or linguistic signs that stand for collections of individuals. His epistemology turns on the distinction between *intuitive cognition* (direct acquaintance with a present existing particular, yielding contingent knowledge) and *abstractive cognition* (grasping the content of a concept independently of whether its referent exists). Controversially, he argued that God could, by absolute power, cause intuitive cognition of a non-existent object—a claim that raised the specter of radical skepticism and influenced Descartes's evil demon hypothesis.

## Avicenna / Ibn Sīnā (980–1037 CE)

Avicenna developed a thoroughgoing Neoplatonized Aristotelianism in which knowledge arises through the emanation of intelligibles from the Active Intellect, identified with the angel Gabriel in Islamic theology. His *floating man* argument—a thought experiment in which a person created in mid-air, deprived of all sensory input, can still affirm "I exist"—anticipates the Cartesian cogito and argues for the self-transparency and immateriality of the soul. Avicenna's psychology and theory of abstraction, transmitted to Latin Europe through translations, were among the most influential philosophical texts of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries.

## Averroes / Ibn Rushd (1126–1198 CE)

Averroes, known in Latin scholasticism as "the Commentator," wrote the definitive medieval commentaries on Aristotle and defended a controversial reading of Aristotle's *De Anima* in which the material intellect is single and shared by all humans, while individual souls contribute only the particular sensory images (phantasms) on which that unified intellect operates. This doctrine threatened individual intellectual immortality and was condemned in Paris in 1277, but it forced Latin thinkers—including Aquinas—to develop more careful accounts of how intellection is individual. His rationalism, insisting on the autonomy of philosophical inquiry even in matters touching theology, influenced the Latin Averroist tradition and debates about the double truth.

## Roger Bacon (c. 1214/1220–1292 CE)

Bacon combined Augustinian illuminationism with an empirical orientation unusual for the period, arguing that experience (*experientia*) and mathematics are the two foundations of genuine science. His concept of *scientia experimentalis* distinguished routine inductive inference from transformative first-person experience, and he insisted that even theological knowledge requires experiential confirmation beyond what authority and argument alone can provide. His epistemological writings—particularly in the *Opus Majus*—represent an important precursor to the early modern emphasis on experiment and observation as sources of knowledge.
