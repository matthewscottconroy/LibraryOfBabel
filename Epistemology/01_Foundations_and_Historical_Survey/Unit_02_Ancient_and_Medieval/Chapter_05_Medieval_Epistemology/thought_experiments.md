## Thought Experiments in Medieval Epistemology

---

**The Inner Teacher**

**The scenario:** In *De Magistro*, Augustine considers what happens when a teacher communicates knowledge to a student by speaking words. The teacher utters "tree," and the student who does not know Latin hears only a sound. Even a student who knows Latin hears only a sign pointing to a kind of object. But to recognize whether the word is applied correctly—to judge whether what is said is true—the student must already have some cognitive access to trees themselves, not merely to the word. So the teacher's spoken words cannot, by themselves, cause knowledge in the student's mind.

**The philosophical point:** Augustine concludes that all genuine cognitive illumination comes from Christ, the *verbum* or Word of God dwelling within the mind as an interior teacher. Human teachers can occasion or prompt inquiry, but they cannot directly deposit knowledge into another mind. This raises a profound question about the nature of testimony and instruction: if words are merely signs, and signs only illuminate when a prior cognitive capacity is activated, what exactly does teaching accomplish? The thought experiment anticipates Wittgenstein's reflections on ostensive definition and the impossibility of a purely private linguistic instruction.

**The Floating Man**

**The scenario:** Avicenna asks us to imagine a perfect human being created ex nihilo and suspended in mid-air, deprived of all sensory information: blind, unable to hear, with limbs arranged so they touch nothing, not even one another. This person has received no sensory experience whatsoever. Nevertheless, Avicenna contends, such a person would still be able to affirm the existence of herself as a thinking, self-aware being.

**The philosophical point:** The floating man argument is designed to establish two things: that the soul is immediately self-transparent and does not require sensory input to know its own existence, and that the soul is therefore something other than the body or any material thing (since the body has been deliberately excluded from the conditions of the experiment). The resemblance to Descartes's cogito is striking, and scholars debate the extent of Avicenna's influence on Descartes. The experiment also bears on the question of whether self-knowledge is epistemically prior to knowledge of the external world—a question that still animates first-person debates in contemporary epistemology.

**Ockham's Supernaturally Deceived Perceiver**

**The scenario:** Ockham considers whether God, by absolute divine power, could cause a person to have an intuitive cognition—the direct, vivid, experiential awareness ordinarily caused by a present object—of an object that does not actually exist. Since God is not bound by the natural order he has established, and since the causal chain from object to mind runs through the natural order, it seems God could "short-circuit" this chain and produce the same cognitive state without the object.

**The philosophical point:** Ockham accepted this implication, drawing the conclusion that intuitive cognition does not *necessarily* certify the existence of its object—God could deceive us. This was a startling concession, since intuitive cognition was supposed to be the most direct and reliable form of knowledge. The thought experiment introduces a kind of theological evil-demon scenario: if the reliability of our most immediate experience depends on God's contingent choice not to deceive, then the certainty of our empirical knowledge rests on divine guarantee, not on the character of the experience itself. The parallel with Descartes's *malin génie* argument is explicit and historically significant.

**The Analogy of the Sun**

**The scenario:** Augustine, following Plato's analogy of the sun in the *Republic*, asks us to consider how physical sight works: the eye has the capacity for vision, there are colored objects in the world, but neither the eye nor the objects produce vision by themselves—they require the light of the sun as a third factor. Now consider intellectual cognition: the mind has the capacity to grasp truths, and there are intelligible objects (eternal truths, mathematical necessities), but neither the finite mind nor these objects suffice to explain our grasp of them.

**The philosophical point:** God functions as the intelligible sun, illuminating the eternal truths and making them visible to the mind. The thought experiment is used to motivate the thesis that there is a third factor in cognition beyond the subject and the object—a divine enabling condition. Critics of illuminationism (like Aquinas) accepted the sun analogy but argued that the agent intellect, a natural cognitive faculty, plays the illuminating role; God's illumination is required for creation and maintenance of the natural order but not for each individual act of cognition. The debate between Augustinian and Thomistic epistemology thus turns on this thought experiment's intended scope.

**The Universal in the Mind**

**The scenario:** Consider two white objects—a swan and a sheet of paper. When I perceive them in succession, I form a concept of whiteness that applies to both. Where does this concept reside? Realists say that whiteness itself is a real property shared by both objects; conceptualists say the universal exists in my mind as an abstracted form; nominalists (like Ockham) say it is merely a mental sign or natural resemblance-grouping with no ontological correlate beyond the particulars.

**The philosophical point:** This scenario—the medieval problem of universals—is not merely a metaphysical dispute but an epistemological one. It concerns how general knowledge is possible: if there are no real universals, how can a mental concept of "whiteness" genuinely apply to an open-ended range of objects, including ones I have never encountered? Ockham's answer is that the concept functions as a natural sign that resembles all its instances by virtue of their causal similarity in producing the same type of mental act. The debate anticipates modern debates about natural kinds, concept empiricism, and the semantics of predicates.
