## Primary Sources

- René Descartes, *Meditations on First Philosophy*, trans. John Cottingham, Cambridge University Press, 1996. The foundational text of early modern rationalism and the locus classicus of methodological doubt, the cogito, the proof of God, and the vindication of clear and distinct perception.

- René Descartes, *Discourse on the Method*, trans. John Cottingham, in *The Philosophical Writings of Descartes*, Vol. 1, Cambridge University Press, 1985. Accessible introduction to Descartes's four rules of method and the autobiographical account of how he arrived at his epistemological program.

- René Descartes, *Rules for the Direction of the Mind (Regulae)*, trans. John Cottingham, in *The Philosophical Writings of Descartes*, Vol. 1, Cambridge University Press, 1985. The early methodological treatise, left unpublished, that develops Descartes's account of intuition, deduction, and enumeration as the tools of genuine science.

- Baruch Spinoza, *Ethics (Ethica)*, trans. Edwin Curley, Princeton University Press, 1994. The geometrical method applied to metaphysics and epistemology: the three kinds of knowledge (imagination, reason, intuitive science) are presented as part of a deductive system from definitions and axioms.

- Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, *New Essays on Human Understanding*, trans. Peter Remnant and Jonathan Bennett, Cambridge University Press, 1996. Leibniz's sustained commentary on Locke's *Essay*, providing the most detailed exposition of his doctrine of innate ideas as virtual dispositions and of the distinction between truths of reason and truths of fact.

- Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, *Monadology* and *Discourse on Metaphysics*, trans. Roger Ariew and Daniel Garber, Hackett, 1989. Key texts for Leibniz's metaphysical framework and his doctrine that the monad's perceptions unfold from within, with implications for his rationalist epistemology.

- Nicolas Malebranche, *The Search after Truth (De la recherche de la vérité)*, trans. Thomas M. Lennon and Paul J. Olscamp, Ohio State University Press, 1980. Develops the occasionalist epistemology of "seeing all things in God" and provides the most thoroughgoing Cartesian-rationalist account of divine illumination in the early modern period.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Williams, Bernard, *Descartes: The Project of Pure Enquiry*, Penguin, 1978. The most philosophically rigorous analytical study of the *Meditations*, examining the cogito, certainty, and the role of God in Descartes's epistemological project.

- Cottingham, John, *Descartes*, Blackwell, 1986. An excellent introductory overview of all aspects of Descartes's philosophy, with clear chapters on the method of doubt, innate ideas, and the structure of clear and distinct perception.

- Frankfurt, Harry G., *Demons, Dreamers, and Madmen: The Defense of Reason in Descartes's Meditations*, Bobbs-Merrill, 1970. A careful study of the skeptical arguments and the cogito; argues that the circle (Cartesian circle) is not vicious because it operates at two different levels of epistemic claim.

- Garber, Daniel, *Descartes' Metaphysical Physics*, University of Chicago Press, 1992. Places Descartes's epistemology in the context of his natural philosophy, showing how the theory of clear and distinct ideas functions in the development of a new science.

- Della Rocca, Michael, *Spinoza*, Routledge, 2008. An analytically sophisticated introduction to Spinoza's philosophy that takes seriously the geometric method's implications for epistemology, including the account of adequate ideas and the three kinds of knowledge.

- Bennett, Jonathan, *A Study of Spinoza's Ethics*, Hackett, 1984. A rigorous analytical examination of the *Ethics* that clarifies Spinoza's epistemological claims, particularly the distinction between clear and adequate ideas and the role of the second and third kinds of knowledge.

- Jolley, Nicholas, *Leibniz and Locke: A Study of the New Essays on Human Understanding*, Clarendon Press, 1984. Examines Leibniz's critique of empiricism and defence of innate ideas, clarifying the differences between Leibnizian innatism and the Cartesian version.

- Nadler, Steven, *Malebranche and Ideas*, Oxford University Press, 1992. A careful study of Malebranche's theory that all ideas exist in God and that human cognition consists in the mind's access to God's ideas; connects this to broader Cartesian debates about representation.

- Hatfield, Gary, *Descartes and the Meditations*, Routledge, 2003. A close reading of all six *Meditations* with attention to historical context, Descartes's intentions, and the relationship between his epistemology and his natural philosophy.

- Nadler, Steven (ed.), *A Companion to Early Modern Philosophy*, Blackwell, 2002. Essays by leading scholars covering all major rationalist and empiricist figures; particularly useful for comparative assessments of rationalist epistemological methods.
