## René Descartes (1596–1650)

Descartes set the agenda for modern epistemology by making the question of certain knowledge foundational and methodologically prior to all other inquiry. His method of hyperbolic doubt—suspending assent to anything that admits the slightest possibility of error—was not intended as genuine skepticism but as a purification procedure to identify beliefs that survive all conceivable challenge. The cogito (*cogito ergo sum*) established a single indubitable starting point: the very act of doubting proves the existence of the doubter as a thinking thing. Descartes then used the veracity of God to warrant the reliability of clear and distinct perception, constructing a foundationalist epistemology whose problems—including the Cartesian circle and the relationship between mind and body—defined the subsequent century of philosophical inquiry.

## Baruch Spinoza (1632–1677)

Spinoza's epistemology is inseparable from his monist metaphysics: there is only one substance, and the human mind is a mode of God or Nature under the attribute of thought. His three kinds of knowledge—imagination (confused and inadequate, based on images and association), reason (adequate knowledge of common notions and universal relations), and intuitive science (immediate grasp of things under the aspect of eternity)—represent not just different sources but different levels of adequacy and power. Spinoza held that adequate ideas are self-certifying: a true idea corresponds to its object not by resemblance but by the internal property of adequacy, and the third kind of knowledge culminates in the intellectual love of God, which is simultaneously the highest cognitive and affective achievement.

## Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716)

Leibniz defended a nuanced version of innatism against Locke's empiricism: the mind is not blank at birth but is pre-formed with dispositions and tendencies that the appropriate experience occasions rather than creates. His doctrine of *petites perceptions* (minute unconscious perceptions) provided a mechanism: the mind receives infinitely many imperceptible inputs that combine to produce conscious experience, and these inputs are internally generated by the monad's nature. He distinguished truths of reason (necessary, knowable a priori, whose negation is a contradiction) from truths of fact (contingent, knowable a posteriori, whose negation is possible), a distinction that anticipates Hume's fork and Kant's analytic/synthetic distinction.

## Nicolas Malebranche (1638–1715)

Malebranche developed the most thoroughgoing rationalist epistemology after Descartes by arguing that the human mind has direct access only to God's ideas—we see all things in God, not in some independently real external world or in our own mental representations. This "vision in God" doctrine held that ideas are not modifications of the human mind but eternal objects in the divine understanding, to which God grants our minds a kind of participatory access. Malebranche's occasionalism—the view that God is the only genuine causal agent—was motivated in part by epistemological concerns: only direct access to divine ideas could guarantee the truth-tracking character of our cognition.

## Antoine Arnauld (1612–1694)

Though principally a theologian and logician, Arnauld was Descartes's most incisive philosophical correspondent and, later, Malebranche's principal critic. His objections to the *Meditations* (the Fourth Objections) identified the "Cartesian circle"—the charge that Descartes used clear and distinct perception to prove God and then used God to validate clear and distinct perception. His polemic against Malebranche defended a Cartesian representationalist view in which ideas are genuinely mental modifications that nonetheless represent external objects; his debate with Malebranche over the nature of ideas remains a defining controversy of seventeenth-century epistemology.

## Blaise Pascal (1623–1662)

Pascal occupies an ambiguous but important position in early modern rationalism: trained as a mathematician who made significant contributions to projective geometry and probability theory, he nonetheless argued in the *Pensées* that reason alone is insufficient for the most important knowledge—knowledge of God and of the human condition. His famous wager is an epistemological as much as a pragmatic argument: it concerns what attitude to adopt when the evidence is insufficient to ground rational conviction. Pascal's contrast between the "esprit de géométrie" (systematic deductive reasoning) and the "esprit de finesse" (intuitive, holistic perception of complex situations) anticipates later distinctions between explicit and tacit knowledge.
