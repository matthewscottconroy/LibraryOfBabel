# Locke Against Innate Ideas: The Empiricist Starting Point

John Locke's *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1689) opens with a sustained attack on the doctrine of innate ideas — the rationalist claim that the mind comes equipped with ideas or principles prior to experience. This attack sets the agenda for the entire empiricist tradition and for much of modern epistemology.

## The Target: Innate Ideas and Principles

Locke's target is somewhat broader than Descartes or Leibniz specifically. He attacks the general thesis that there are "innate principles" — intellectual and moral truths — that all human minds possess by nature, independent of experience.

Two types of innateness are commonly claimed:
1. **Innate theoretical principles**: Logical principles like "whatever is, is" and "it is impossible for the same thing to be and not to be."
2. **Innate practical principles**: Moral truths like "do unto others as you would be done by."

Locke attacks both.

## The Consent Argument and Its Problems

The standard argument for innatism, which Locke attributes to his opponents, is the *argument from universal consent*: certain principles are assented to by all humans, which is best explained by their innateness.

Locke demolishes this in two moves. First, he denies the premise: there is no universal consent to any principle. Children and "idiots" (his term) don't assent to logical principles. Different cultures have very different moral beliefs. Second, even if there were universal consent, that wouldn't establish innateness — universal consent might be explained by some universal feature of human experience, or by the immediate self-evidence of the principles, without requiring innate imprinting.

## The Blank Tablet

Locke's positive doctrine is that the mind at birth is like "white paper, void of all characters" — a blank slate (the Latin *tabula rasa*). All the contents of the mind come from two sources: **sensation** (experience of the external world via the senses) and **reflection** (experience of the mind's own operations — perceiving, thinking, doubting, believing, willing).

This is the empiricist credo: the two sources of all ideas, and ultimately all knowledge, are sensation and reflection. No content is in the mind that didn't come from these two sources.

## Simple and Complex Ideas

Locke elaborates this picture with his distinction between simple and complex ideas. **Simple ideas** are those that come directly from sensation or reflection and cannot be further analyzed — the idea of red, of sweet, of round, of pleasure, of thinking. The mind is purely passive in receiving simple ideas; it can't create or destroy them.

**Complex ideas** are built up from simple ideas by the mind's active operations: combining, relating, abstracting. The idea of gold, of substance, of causation, of infinity — these are complex ideas constructed from simpler materials. Here the mind is active and creative, but it's always working with materials provided by experience.

## The Attack on Universals

Locke's empiricism leads naturally to a nominalist position about universals, similar to Ockham's. General terms — "horse," "red," "just" — don't pick out real universal entities in the world. They are applied to groups of individuals on the basis of resemblance. The mind forms *abstract general ideas* by noticing that certain simple ideas recur in multiple objects and separating them from their particular context.

This is a famous and controversial part of Locke's philosophy. Berkeley attacked the concept of abstract general ideas as incoherent — he couldn't form an idea of "color in general" or "triangle in general" that wasn't some specific color or shape. This attack sparked a sustained debate about the nature of abstraction and general concepts.

## The Significance of Locke's Starting Point

Locke's attack on innate ideas established the empiricist program: all knowledge is founded on experience, and the philosopher's task is to trace the genealogy of ideas back to their experiential sources. Ideas that cannot be traced back to sensation or reflection are either confused or empty.

This genealogical method — asking "where did this idea come from?" — became a powerful critical tool. Hume used it to devastating effect against the ideas of causation, substance, and the self. The logical positivists' verification principle is a descendant: meaningful claims must be verifiable by experience. Even contemporary naturalized epistemology — the view that philosophy should be continuous with empirical science — owes something to Locke's empiricist starting point.
