# Primary and Secondary Qualities: The Structure of Perceptual Knowledge

One of Locke's most influential epistemological contributions is his distinction between **primary qualities** and **secondary qualities**. This distinction bears on questions that remain central in philosophy of science and philosophy of perception: which features of the world as we experience it are real features of objects themselves, and which are products of the interaction between objects and human sensory systems?

## The Distinction Defined

**Primary qualities** are features that belong to objects themselves, independent of any perceiver. They include:
- Solidity
- Extension (size and shape)
- Motion and rest
- Number
- Bulk

These qualities exist in objects whether or not they are perceived. A stone is extended and solid even in a world with no perceivers.

**Secondary qualities** are *powers* in objects to produce certain sensory ideas in perceivers, but the ideas themselves don't resemble anything in the objects. Colors, tastes, sounds, smells, and thermal sensations (hot and cold) are secondary qualities. The redness of a tomato, the sweetness of honey, the sourness of lemon — these are experiences produced in us by objects, but the objects don't actually *have* those experienced properties as we perceive them.

## The Corpuscularian Background

Locke's distinction is grounded in the "corpuscularian" physics of his time — the view (associated with Boyle and inspired by ancient atomism) that the physical world consists of minute material particles with only a few basic properties: size, shape, motion, and number. All other observed properties are explained by the arrangement and motion of these particles.

The sweetness of sugar is produced by the particular shape and arrangement of its particles interacting with our tongue; the redness of a surface is produced by how its particles reflect light to our eyes. The sugar itself, at the corpuscularian level, isn't sweet in the way it tastes; it's just a certain arrangement of particles with a certain shape and size.

## The Epistemological Implications

The primary/secondary distinction has significant epistemological implications.

For **primary qualities**, Locke holds that our ideas *resemble* the qualities themselves. The idea of the stone's roughly round shape corresponds to an actual, mind-independent roundness in the stone. Perception of primary qualities is, in this sense, reliable and revelatory: what we perceive actually characterizes the object.

For **secondary qualities**, our ideas do *not* resemble anything in the objects. The tomato doesn't have redness the way it has extension; it only has the *power* to produce a certain visual experience in creatures with our kind of visual system. The color exists only in the experience, not in the object-independent world.

## Berkeley's Response

George Berkeley used Locke's arguments about secondary qualities against Locke's claim that primary qualities are objective. Berkeley argued that the arguments Locke used to show secondary qualities are mind-dependent apply equally to primary qualities:

- Primary qualities like shape and size are just as variable across perceivers and conditions as secondary qualities.
- We can't form an idea of extension without any color (which is secondary).
- If secondary qualities are ideas in minds, primary qualities — which are always experienced along with them — should be too.

Berkeley's conclusion: everything is mind-dependent. The external material world that Locke thought primary qualities represent doesn't exist. Esse est percipi — to be is to be perceived.

## The Contemporary Debate

The primary/secondary quality distinction remains active in contemporary philosophy of science and philosophy of perception. The question of which features of our experience are mind-independent is closely related to debates about:

- **Scientific realism**: Does physics describe the world as it is, or only as it appears to us? Are fundamental physical properties (mass, charge, spin) primary qualities in Locke's sense?
- **Color realism**: Are colors real properties of surfaces, or only features of experiences? Modern debates involve complex positions — dispositionalism (colors are real but response-dependent), physicalism (colors are objective reflectance properties), eliminativism (the folk concept of color is incoherent).
- **Secondary qualities in ethics**: Some metaethicists (like John McDowell) have argued that moral properties are "secondary qualities" — response-dependent in a way that makes them real but not mind-independent in the same way physical quantities are.

Locke's distinction, though developed in a specific corpuscularian context, opened a debate that runs through the heart of modern philosophy.
