# Representative Realism: Locke's Account of Perceptual Knowledge

Locke's theory of perception is a form of **representative realism** (sometimes called **indirect realism** or the **veil of perception theory**). The core thesis: we don't directly perceive external objects themselves; we perceive *ideas* that represent external objects. This view is philosophically natural, scientifically motivated, and philosophically disastrous — it sets up problems that neither Locke nor his successors have been able to fully solve.

## The Representative Theory

On Locke's view, when you perceive a red apple:
1. There is a real external object (the apple).
2. The object causes, through light and the visual system, a certain neural process.
3. This process gives rise to an *idea* in your mind — a sensory representation.
4. You perceive the idea, not the apple directly.
5. The idea represents the apple — in some respects accurately (primary qualities), in some respects inaccurately (secondary qualities).

The "veil of perception" label captures the problem: the ideas stand between us and the objects, like a veil. We never get behind the veil to the objects themselves. We only ever perceive ideas.

## The Problem of the External World

If all we ever directly perceive are ideas in our own minds, how do we know there is an external world at all? How do we know our ideas accurately represent anything?

Locke is honest about this difficulty. He concedes that we cannot have *demonstrative* knowledge (the highest grade) that external objects exist. He claims only a weaker "sensitive knowledge" — a kind of direct assurance, short of demonstration, that accompanies present perception. When I have a lively, vivid, coherent idea, I have sensitive knowledge that it's caused by a real external object.

Critics find this unsatisfying. If the veil is genuinely impenetrable, then whatever confidence we have about the external world is not explained by Locke's theory — it's merely stipulated. Locke seems to be helping himself to the conclusion he needs without arguing for it.

## Berkeley's Idealist Response

Berkeley (see his own section) drew the logical conclusion from Locke's premises: if we only directly perceive ideas, and ideas can only be like other ideas, then the external material world that Locke posits as the cause of our ideas is either unknowable or nonexistent. Better to say that all we know are ideas in minds — there is no mind-independent material world behind the veil, because the veil is all there is.

This is the reductio ad absurdum of representative realism: if you are a consistent empiricist and accept the representative theory, you end up as an idealist.

## Kant's Transformation

Kant acknowledged the force of both Locke's problem and Berkeley's response, and attempted to chart a new course. Like Locke, he accepted that experience involves mental representations. Unlike Locke, he held that these representations constitute the phenomenal world — the world as it appears to us — and that this world is the proper object of knowledge. Knowledge of the "thing in itself" — the mind-independent reality behind representations — is impossible.

This "transcendental idealism" avoids the veil-of-perception problem by relocating what knowledge is of: we know the phenomenal world (which is partly constituted by our cognitive apparatus) rather than the noumenal world (which lies beyond our cognitive reach). Whether this is a solution or a redescription of the problem is a matter of debate.

## Contemporary Responses

Contemporary philosophy has largely abandoned the Lockean framework, but the issues it raised are alive. Several options are on the table:

**Naive realism / Direct realism**: We do directly perceive external objects; there is no veil. The representative theory was a mistake. (This faces the challenge of explaining perceptual error and hallucination.)

**Disjunctivism**: Genuine perception is direct contact with the object; hallucination is something different and separate. The two are *disjuncts*, not instances of a common type.

**Representationalism**: We do perceive representations, but representations are not a veil — they are intentional states that are directly of the world, not intermediary objects we see.

Each of these is a response to the same problem Locke identified: how does mind hook onto world? The representative theory is no longer the standard answer, but the question remains.
