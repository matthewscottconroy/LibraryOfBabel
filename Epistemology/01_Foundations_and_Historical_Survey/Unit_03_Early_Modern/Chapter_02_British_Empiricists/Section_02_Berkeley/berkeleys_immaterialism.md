# Berkeley's Immaterialism: Defending the View

Berkeley's immaterialism often strikes students as a philosophical joke — a view too counterintuitive to take seriously. Dr. Johnson's famous "refutation" — kicking a large stone and exclaiming "I refute it thus!" — captures the common reaction. But Berkeley's philosophy is much more careful and interesting than this dismissal suggests, and taking it seriously illuminates questions that matter deeply in contemporary epistemology and philosophy of mind.

## The Commonsense Defense

Berkeley famously claimed that his view doesn't contradict common sense — it vindicates it. On the materialist view (Locke's representative realism), what you directly know is ideas in your head, and the external world is forever beyond your epistemic reach. Berkeley's view, by contrast, gives you *direct access* to what you ordinarily take yourself to perceive: the tree, the table, the sky.

On Berkeley's view, the tree *is* the collection of ideas you perceive. When you say "I see the tree," you're right — you perceive the tree, not a representation of it. The materialist introduces an unnecessary layer of mediation and then can't account for how we know what lies behind it. Berkeley eliminates the layer.

This is philosophically significant: Berkeley is, in a sense, the first direct realist, despite being an idealist. His direct realism is achieved by making the objects of direct perception not material things but collections of ideas. Whether this counts as a genuine improvement over representative realism or as a clever trick depends on how you assess the resulting picture.

## The Coherence of the Phenomenal World

Berkeley needs to explain why our experience has the structure it has — why ideas occur in regular patterns, why some sequences are predictable, why there's an intersubjectively consistent world. His answer: God has established the laws of nature, which are the regular patterns in which God produces ideas in finite minds. Scientific laws are descriptions of these regularities — not descriptions of the behavior of matter, but of God's consistent manner of producing ideas.

This means that science, for Berkeley, is perfectly legitimate — it discovers the patterns of God's productive activity. Natural philosophy is, in a sense, reading the grammar of God's language of nature.

## Minds and Their Peculiar Status

Berkeley's ontology divides into two categories: minds (spirits) and ideas. But this immediately raises a puzzle: how do we know about other minds? You can't perceive another mind as you perceive ideas — minds are active, not passive. Berkeley says we have "notions" of minds — a different kind of cognitive grasp than the ideas we have of sensible qualities.

This distinction between ideas and notions is not fully elaborated by Berkeley. But it marks an interesting epistemological boundary: there are things we know (other minds, ourselves as minds, God) in a way that differs from sensory experience. This anticipates discussions of self-knowledge and knowledge of other minds that will occupy subsequent philosophy.

## Berkeley and the Development of Idealism

Berkeley's idealism fed directly into subsequent idealist traditions. Kant's distinction between phenomena (experience) and noumena (things-in-themselves) echoes Berkeley's concern that material substance is inaccessible to experience. German idealism (Fichte, Schelling, Hegel) developed idealistic positions far more radical than Berkeley's — they denied not just matter but the entire framework of subject-object opposition.

The idealist tradition is sometimes dismissed as a dead end in the history of philosophy, but it raised real and persistent questions: What is experience? How does mind relate to world? Is there a mind-independent reality, and if so, how is it knowable? These questions are not answered by dismissing idealism — they require genuine philosophical engagement.

## The Contemporary Relevance

Contemporary philosophy of perception continues to grapple with questions Berkeley raised. The disjunctivist view — that veridical perception is fundamentally different from hallucination — is partly motivated by the desire to avoid the representative theory's veil-of-perception problem, just as Berkeley was. The phenomenal concept strategy in philosophy of mind — the claim that consciousness is not explicable in physical terms — has structural similarities to Berkeley's claim that ideas are irreducibly mental.

Berkeley's extreme position serves as a useful limiting case: it shows where the logic of empiricism and anti-representationalism can lead, and forces us to be clear about what assumptions we're making when we say that we know about an external world.
