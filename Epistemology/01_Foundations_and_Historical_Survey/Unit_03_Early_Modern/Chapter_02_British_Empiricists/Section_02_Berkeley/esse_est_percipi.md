# Esse Est Percipi: Berkeley's Immaterialist Epistemology

George Berkeley (1685–1753), Bishop of Cloyne, proposed one of the most counterintuitive and philosophically rigorous positions in the history of epistemology: **immaterialism** — the view that material substance does not exist. His slogan *esse est percipi* — to be is to be perceived — captures the core commitment: things exist only insofar as they are perceived.

This is not a position he arrived at through mysticism or ignorance. It is the result of applying the empiricist method with unusual consistency.

## The Empiricist Critique of Matter

Berkeley's starting point is strictly empiricist: all knowledge comes from experience; experience consists of ideas; ideas can be like only other ideas. Locke had used this framework to distinguish primary and secondary qualities, arguing that secondary quality ideas don't resemble their causes but primary quality ideas do.

Berkeley's attack: how could you know that your ideas of primary qualities resemble their external causes? To know this, you'd need to compare your idea of extension with extension-as-it-really-is. But to compare them, you'd need to perceive both — and you can only perceive your own ideas. You can never step outside your ideas to compare them with reality. The very claim that ideas represent something beyond experience is, strictly speaking, impossible to verify by experience.

Moreover, Berkeley argues, the very concept of "material substance" — a something-I-know-not-what that underlies qualities — is incoherent. What is it supposed to be? Not any quality (those are ideas). Not nothing (then it doesn't exist). The concept is empty.

## Only Minds and Ideas

Berkeley's positive picture: the world consists of **minds** (spirits) and **ideas**. Minds are active, perceiving, thinking things — not ideas. Ideas are passive, fleeting, and can exist only in minds. The objects of ordinary experience — trees, chairs, mountains — are collections of ideas. A tree is a complex of visual ideas, tactile ideas, olfactory ideas, that regularly occur together and conform to predictable patterns.

The green of a leaf exists only when perceived. The hardness of wood exists only when felt. The sound of thunder exists only when heard. Between perceptions, objects don't continue to exist — they cease to exist, or rather, the ideas that constitute them are not "in" anyone's mind.

## God as the Guarantor

Berkeley quickly faces the objection: this makes the world radically unstable and discontinuous. When you leave the room, does the chair cease to exist? What about the unobserved depths of the ocean, the interior of the Sun, the far side of the Moon?

Berkeley's answer involves God. God perceives everything continuously. The contents of God's infinite mind are the permanent, public "objects" of experience — the predictable, law-governed ideas that all finite minds perceive when they open their eyes. The chair continues to exist when you leave the room because God is still perceiving it (or more precisely, God's mind contains the idea of the chair, available to be caused in finite minds whenever they are appropriately situated).

This theological move is often regarded as a weakness: it seems to let materiality back in through the divine back door. But Berkeley's point is that God's ideas are not material — there is no mind-independent matter anywhere in the account.

## Arguments Against the Existence of Matter

Berkeley offers several arguments:

**The master argument**: Can you conceive of an unperceived tree? Surely yes — you just imagined one. But wait: to conceive of it, you had to *perceive* it (mentally). The "unperceived tree" you conceive is an idea in your mind. You cannot conceive of something genuinely unperceived, because conceiving is a form of perceiving. Therefore, the concept of an unperceived object is incoherent.

**The relativity argument**: The same water feels hot to one hand and cold to the other. The water cannot really be both hot and cold; so neither sensation represents the water's intrinsic temperature. If we extend this reasoning, no perceived quality represents an intrinsic quality of the object — because all perceived qualities are relative to the perceiver and conditions.

**The abstraction argument**: Locke claimed we can form abstract general ideas — the idea of extension without any particular color or shape. Berkeley denies this: all ideas are particular and determinate. There is no "extension in general" — only this particular extension of this particular thing. The concept of material substance as a featureless substratum is one such illegitimate abstraction.

## The Legacy

Berkeley's immaterialism was almost universally rejected — but the arguments are harder to answer than most philosophers admit. His critique of abstract general ideas influenced Hume. His challenge to the notion of material substance was absorbed into Kant's agnosticism about things-in-themselves. His use of the master argument is discussed in contemporary philosophy of perception. And the combination of his arguments poses a genuine dilemma: either accept representative realism with its insuperable skeptical problems, or find a direct realist account that can respond to the argument from illusion.
