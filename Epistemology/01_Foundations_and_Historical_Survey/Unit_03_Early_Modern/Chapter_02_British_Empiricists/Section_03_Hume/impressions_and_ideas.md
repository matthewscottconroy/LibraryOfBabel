# Hume on Impressions and Ideas: The Empiricist Foundation

David Hume (1711–1776) is the most radical and systematically consistent of the British Empiricists. He begins from a strict empiricist starting point — all knowledge derives from experience — and follows it wherever it leads, even when it leads to conclusions that are deeply unsettling. His distinction between impressions and ideas is the foundation of his entire philosophy.

## The Two Categories of Perception

Hume's *Treatise of Human Nature* (1739) begins with a fundamental division of all "perceptions of the mind" (everything present to consciousness) into two categories:

**Impressions** are the original, vivid, forceful deliverances of experience: what you see, hear, feel, taste, smell; what you feel emotionally (love, hatred, desire, aversion). Impressions are lively and strong — they are the direct impact of experience on the mind.

**Ideas** are copies or faint images of impressions. When you think about the smell of coffee without currently smelling it; when you recall the vivid blue of a summer sky; when you imagine a centaur — these are ideas. Ideas are weaker and less vivid than impressions, though in some cases (vivid memory, intense imagination) they can approach impressions in force.

## The Copy Principle

Hume's central methodological principle is the **Copy Principle**: all ideas are copies of corresponding impressions. Every genuine idea must trace back to an impression from which it was derived. An idea without a corresponding impression is either a compound of ideas that do have impressions, or it's a meaningless pseudo-idea.

This principle is Hume's weapon for philosophical criticism. When a philosopher uses a term — substance, causation, self, obligation — Hume asks: what impression does this idea copy? If no impression can be identified, the term is philosophically suspicious.

## Simple and Complex Perceptions

Like Locke, Hume distinguishes simple and complex perceptions. A simple impression or idea is one that admits no distinction or separation — the pure sensation of red, the simple feeling of pleasure. A complex perception combines simpler ones.

Hume's Copy Principle applies strictly to simple ideas: every simple idea is a copy of a corresponding simple impression. Complex ideas can be original — you can imagine a golden mountain without ever having perceived one — because the complex idea is composed of simple ideas (gold, mountain) that do correspond to impressions.

## Relations of Ideas and Matters of Fact

Hume's most important epistemological contribution is his *fork* — the division of all genuine knowledge into two categories:

**Relations of Ideas**: Claims that are demonstrably certain — their denial involves a contradiction. Mathematics and logic fall here. "The square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the other sides" is a relation of ideas; denying it would be contradictory. These are known purely by reason, without empirical investigation.

**Matters of Fact**: Contingent truths about the world, known through experience. "The sun rises in the east" is a matter of fact. Its denial involves no contradiction — it can be clearly imagined and conceived. These are known through experience, not pure reason.

The *fork* is sharp and exhaustive: any genuine claim is one or the other. "The world as a whole has a cause" seems to be neither a demonstrative truth (its denial involves no contradiction) nor empirically establishable (we haven't observed the world as a whole). If it's neither, it's cognitively empty — what Hume calls "sophistry and illusion."

## Hume's Critical Applications

The fork and the Copy Principle together constitute a powerful critical toolkit:

- **Causation**: We don't perceive necessary connection between cause and effect — only regular succession. The idea of necessary causation lacks a corresponding impression. (Hume examines this at length — see the next section.)
- **Substance**: The idea of an underlying material substance is the idea of a something-I-know-not-what. What impression does it copy? None identifiable. The concept is empty.
- **The self**: We don't find a unified, persisting self in introspection — only a bundle of passing impressions. (See the section on the bundle theory.)
- **God and metaphysics**: Traditional metaphysical and theological concepts largely fail the Copy Principle test.

Hume's empiricism, taken seriously, generates a form of skepticism that is not about whether we know the external world — Hume accepts that we act as if we do and thinks this is psychologically inevitable — but about whether our confident beliefs about causation, substance, self, and more have the rational foundations we think they do.
