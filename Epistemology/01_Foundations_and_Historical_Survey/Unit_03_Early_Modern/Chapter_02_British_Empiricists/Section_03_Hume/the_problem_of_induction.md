# The Problem of Induction: Hume's Most Lasting Puzzle

The problem of induction is one of philosophy's most important and enduring puzzles. Hume identified it with characteristic clarity, and while hundreds of proposed solutions have been advanced in the nearly three centuries since, none has achieved consensus. Understanding why the problem is so hard is essential to understanding the limits of empirical knowledge.

## What Inductive Reasoning Is

Inductive reasoning is reasoning from observed instances to general conclusions or to unobserved instances. The most familiar form:

- All the emeralds I've ever observed have been green.
- Therefore, all emeralds are green. (Or: the next emerald I observe will be green.)

This kind of inference underlies virtually all empirical science and everyday reasoning. When a doctor concludes that aspirin relieves headaches, they're generalizing from many observed cases. When a physicist postulates that the laws of nature are constant throughout the universe, they're extrapolating from observations in a small corner of space and time.

## Hume's Problem

Hume asks: what justifies inductive inference? What makes it rational to expect the future to resemble the past, or unobserved cases to resemble observed ones?

Two possible justifications present themselves:

**Deductive justification**: Inductive inference is logically necessary. Given that all observed emeralds have been green, the next emerald *must* be green. But this is false — it's perfectly conceivable that the next emerald will be blue. There is no logical necessity connecting past observations to future ones. Deductive justification fails.

**Inductive justification**: Inductive inference has been reliable in the past (we've observed that the sun rises every day, and it keeps rising; we've generalized that water boils at 100°C, and it does). So it's probably reliable in the future. But this is circular: it justifies induction by *using* induction. It assumes that because induction worked before, it will work again — which is precisely the kind of inductive inference whose justification is in question.

There is no non-circular justification of induction. Deductive justification fails; inductive justification begs the question. Hume concludes that inductive inference cannot be *rationally* justified — it rests entirely on *habit* and *custom*, the psychological tendency to expect similar outcomes from similar situations.

## The Implications

Hume's conclusion is radical. If inductive inference cannot be rationally justified, then:

- No empirical generalization (scientific law, everyday expectation) is rationally grounded.
- Causal claims — "A causes B" — are merely reports of observed regularities, not rationally grounded predictions.
- The entire structure of empirical science rests on psychological habit, not rational foundation.

Hume himself emphasizes that this doesn't mean we *should* stop making inductive inferences — we can't help making them, and they work well enough for practical purposes. But they lack the rational justification we thought they had.

## Proposed Solutions

The problem of induction has generated several major families of response:

**Pragmatic vindication** (Reichenbach): If any method can determine truth about frequencies and regularities, induction can. Even without a proof that induction is reliable, we can show that if any method works, induction will — so adopting induction is pragmatically rational.

**Bayesian responses**: We start with prior probabilities and update on evidence via Bayes' theorem. The justification of induction becomes the justification of Bayesian conditionalization. But this faces its own foundational challenges (where do priors come from?).

**Reliabilism**: Induction is justified if it reliably produces true beliefs — and it does. Justification doesn't require a proof of reliability; being reliable is enough. But this seems to beg a version of Hume's question: what grounds the claim that induction is reliable?

**Dissolving the problem** (Wittgenstein, Strawson): The demand for a "justification" of induction rests on a misunderstanding. Induction is part of our basic epistemic practice; asking for a justification of it is like asking why a proof is valid. At some point, justification comes to an end.

**Popper's falsificationism**: Scientific method doesn't rely on induction but on falsification — proposing bold hypotheses and trying to refute them. We never confirm a scientific theory; we only fail to falsify it.

None of these fully resolves Hume's challenge. The problem of induction remains one of epistemology's central open questions.
