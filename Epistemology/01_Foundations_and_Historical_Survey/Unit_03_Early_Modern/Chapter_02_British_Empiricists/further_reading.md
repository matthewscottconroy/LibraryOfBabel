## Primary Sources

- John Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, ed. Peter H. Nidditch, Oxford University Press, 1975. The founding document of British empiricism, offering the first systematic treatment of the tabula rasa, the distinction between primary and secondary qualities, and the four degrees of knowledge from intuition through sensitive knowledge.

- George Berkeley, *A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge*, ed. Jonathan Dancy, Oxford University Press, 1998. Berkeley's systematic defence of immaterialism: the claim that material substance is a philosophical fiction and that to be is to be perceived or to be a perceiving mind.

- George Berkeley, *Three Dialogues Between Hylas and Philonous*, ed. Jonathan Dancy, Oxford University Press, 1998. The more accessible presentation of Berkeley's immaterialism in dialogue form; contains the famous master argument for the inconceivability of mind-independent objects.

- David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature*, ed. L. A. Selby-Bigge, rev. P. H. Nidditch, Oxford University Press, 1978. Hume's systematic attempt to apply Newtonian method to the science of human nature; the epistemological core is in Books I–II, covering impressions and ideas, causation, personal identity, and the limits of reason.

- David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, ed. Tom L. Beauchamp, Oxford University Press, 1999. The more polished and accessible restatement of the epistemological parts of the *Treatise*; Section IV contains the classic statement of the fork between relations of ideas and matters of fact, and Section XII the mitigated skepticism Hume endorses.

- John Locke, *Two Treatises of Government*, ed. Peter Laslett, Cambridge University Press, 1988. Though primarily political, Book II depends on Locke's epistemology of natural law and natural rights; it illustrates the applied dimensions of his theory of knowledge.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Ayers, Michael, *Locke: Epistemology and Ontology*, 2 vols., Routledge, 1991. The definitive scholarly treatment of Locke's epistemology, paying close attention to primary/secondary qualities, the doctrine of real essences, and Locke's response to the Cartesian tradition.

- Lowe, E. J., *Locke on Human Understanding*, Routledge, 1995. A more accessible analytical reconstruction of Locke's main epistemological positions, suitable for advanced undergraduates with an analytical orientation.

- Dancy, Jonathan, *Berkeley: An Introduction*, Blackwell, 1987. A clear analytical introduction to Berkeley's idealism and the epistemological arguments that support it, including a careful assessment of the master argument.

- Winkler, Kenneth P., *Berkeley: An Interpretation*, Clarendon Press, 1989. A rigorous philosophical study of Berkeley's idealism, examining the theory of ideas, the nature of spirits, and the relationship between immaterialism and theism.

- Stroud, Barry, *Hume*, Routledge, 1977. The most philosophically sophisticated introductory study of Hume, covering the copy principle, causation, induction, personal identity, and mitigated skepticism with sustained attention to the arguments' validity.

- Beauchamp, Tom L. and Rosenberg, Alexander, *Hume and the Problem of Causation*, Oxford University Press, 1981. A detailed study of Hume's multiple analyses of causation and their relationship to his broader epistemological project; distinguishes the logical, psychological, and ontological dimensions of his account.

- Bennett, Jonathan, *Locke, Berkeley, Hume: Central Themes*, Clarendon Press, 1971. A classic analytical treatment of the three empiricists in relation to one another; notable for its critical rigour and willingness to find errors as well as insights.

- Garrett, Don, *Cognition and Commitment in Hume's Philosophy*, Oxford University Press, 1997. An important interpretive study arguing that Hume's epistemology is more systematic and less merely psychological than is often assumed; key for the uniformity and separability principles.

- Chappell, Vere (ed.), *The Cambridge Companion to Locke*, Cambridge University Press, 1994. A reliable collection of essays covering all aspects of Locke's epistemology including ideas, language, knowledge, and faith.

- Wright, John P., *The Sceptical Realism of David Hume*, Manchester University Press, 1983. Argues that Hume held a form of causal realism about external objects and that his scepticism is compatible with a robust scientific realism, against the purely phenomenalist reading.

- Walmsley, Jonathan, *Locke's Essay Concerning Human Understanding: A Reader's Guide*, Continuum, 2012. Provides a reliable chapter-by-chapter guide to the *Essay*, suitable for readers approaching Locke for the first time.

- Fogelin, Robert J., *Hume's Scepticism in the Treatise of Human Nature*, Routledge, 1985. A careful study of the skeptical dimensions of Hume's *Treatise*, arguing that his skepticism is more radical and less mitigated than is standardly supposed.
