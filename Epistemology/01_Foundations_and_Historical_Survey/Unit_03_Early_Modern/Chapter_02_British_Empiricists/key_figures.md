## John Locke (1632–1704)

Locke opened the *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* with a sustained attack on the doctrine of innate ideas, arguing that there are no propositions to which all humans universally assent, and that even apparently necessary truths are acquired through the operation of cognitive faculties on sensory experience. His constructivist account of concept formation—simple ideas are furnished by sensation and reflection; complex ideas are assembled from simple ones by combination, comparison, and abstraction—set the empiricist agenda for the following century. His distinction between primary qualities (solidity, extension, figure, motion, number—which resemble their ideas in objects) and secondary qualities (color, taste, smell, sound—whose ideas do not resemble anything in objects) was his most influential contribution to the epistemology of perception, and remains a live issue in philosophy of mind and physics.

## George Berkeley (1685–1753)

Berkeley accepted Locke's empiricism as his starting point but argued that it committed Locke to an incoherent doctrine of material substance. If all we ever directly perceive are ideas in the mind, and if ideas can only resemble other ideas, then the Lockean claim that our ideas of primary qualities resemble qualities of a mind-independent matter is unintelligible. Berkeley concluded that the very concept of material substance was not merely unverifiable but strictly inconceivable. His *esse est percipi* (to be is to be perceived) makes all existence either mental or the act of a perceiving mind; God's continuous perception guarantees the stability and intersubjective regularity of the "external" world. Berkeley's immaterialism was not idealism in the German sense—he took himself to be defending common sense against the artificial puzzles of corpuscularian science.

## David Hume (1711–1776)

Hume is the most rigorous and far-reaching of the British empiricists. His copy principle—that every simple idea is a copy of a prior impression—served as an empiricist criterion of meaningfulness: any term whose corresponding idea cannot be traced to an original impression is meaningless. Applying this criterion to the concept of causation, Hume found no impression of necessary connection between cause and effect; what we actually perceive is constant conjunction and the psychological habit of expectation. His problem of induction—that no amount of observed uniformity can rationally justify the expectation of future uniformity, since such a justification would either be deductive (invalid) or inductive (circular)—remains the central unsolved problem in the philosophy of science. His bundle theory of personal identity, denying any observable self underlying the stream of experience, was equally radical and equally contested.

## Thomas Reid (1710–1796)

Reid founded the Scottish Common Sense school as an explicit response to what he saw as the skeptical consequences of Hume's empiricism. He argued that Hume's difficulties arose from acceptance of "the way of ideas"—the representationalist framework common to Descartes, Locke, and Berkeley—in which the direct objects of perception are always mental intermediaries (ideas) rather than external things themselves. Reid's direct realism insisted that perception gives us immediate access to the external world through the "natural signs" built into our cognitive constitution. His systematic inventory of the "first principles of common sense"—beliefs so basic that no argument for or against them is possible—influenced later reliabilist and externalist epistemologies.

## Francis Bacon (1561–1626)

Though preceding the canonical empiricist tradition, Bacon established the methodological framework that made the British empiricist project intelligible. His critique of the four "idols" (idols of the tribe, den, marketplace, and theatre) diagnosed the systematic sources of cognitive error in natural human tendencies and in received intellectual frameworks. His method of eliminative induction—proceeding by systematic exclusion of hypotheses from a "natural history" of instances, absences, and comparative cases—anticipated the hypothetico-deductive method and John Stuart Mill's methods of experimental inquiry. For Bacon, reliable knowledge required not the introspective certainty of the rationalists but the disciplined organization of sensory experience and the correction of our natural cognitive biases.
