## Thought Experiments in British Empiricism

---

**Molyneux's Problem**

**The scenario:** William Molyneux posed the following question to Locke, which Locke included in the second edition of the *Essay*: Suppose a man born blind who has learned by touch to distinguish a cube from a sphere of the same metal and size. He is then given sight. Upon seeing the cube and sphere placed before him, without touching them, could he distinguish which is the cube and which is the sphere?

**The philosophical point:** Molyneux and Locke both answered "no," and Berkeley agreed. The argument bears directly on the empiricist account of ideas: if the idea of shape derived from touch and the idea of shape derived from sight are distinct ideas with distinct sensory origins, there is no reason to expect that visual experience of a cube would automatically deliver the same concept as tactile experience of a cube, unless they are either innately connected or learned through correlation. The thought experiment thus tests the atomism of empiricist concept formation and the assumption that cross-modal integration requires experience rather than being innately given. It has been revisited by cognitive neuroscience (surgically restored sight cases) and remains contested.

**Berkeley's Master Argument**

**The scenario:** Berkeley challenges the reader to try to conceive of a material object existing without being perceived by any mind. Pick any object—a tree, a stone, a book—and try to form a genuine conception of it existing unseen, unthought-of, unperceived in any way. Berkeley's claim is that this is impossible: the very act of forming a conception of the object constitutes a perception of it in one's own mind, so one can never succeed in conceiving of a genuinely mind-independent object.

**The philosophical point:** This argument purports to show that the concept of matter—of something that exists without being the content of any mind—is literally unintelligible. Critics (most famously Kant) charged that Berkeley conflated the act of conceiving with the content conceived: I can form a concept of a tree while recognizing that the concept's content is of something that would exist even if no mind apprehended it. Whether Berkeley's argument succeeds thus depends on contested questions in the philosophy of mind about the relationship between intentionality and the possibility of mind-independent objects—precisely the issues revisited in contemporary debates between direct realists and representationalists.

**The Missing Shade of Blue**

**The scenario:** Hume, in a famous concession in the *Treatise*, considers whether his copy principle admits exceptions. Suppose someone has seen every shade of blue except one particular shade, which lies in the middle of the continuum from lightest to darkest. Could they form an idea of that missing shade by observing the gap in the sequence and imaginatively extrapolating from the adjacent shades?

**The philosophical point:** Hume acknowledges that "one might possibly perform this," thus apparently allowing that simple ideas need not always be derived from prior impressions—contradicting the copy principle he relies on throughout. The case is philosophically significant for several reasons: it suggests that the mind has generative, not merely receptive, capacities; it raises the question of whether the copy principle is a definition of "simple idea" or an empirical hypothesis about human cognition; and it invites the question of how much constructive imagination is required even for ordinary concept formation. Hume sets the case aside as not worth pressing, but commentators have seen it as revealing a deep tension in his empiricism.

**Hume's Two Suns**

**The scenario:** Hume uses the following illustration to demonstrate the role of constant conjunction in generating causal beliefs: we have always observed that when one billiard ball strikes another, the struck ball moves. But suppose we saw a billiard ball strike another and then witnessed, simultaneously, the sun rise. Would we form a causal belief connecting the collision with the sunrise? No—and the reason is that we have no prior experience of that constant conjunction, regardless of how spatially contiguous or temporally immediate the events are.

**The philosophical point:** The example isolates the element of *repeated regular conjunction* in Hume's account of causation and shows that spatial contiguity and temporal succession alone do not generate causal judgments. The mind forms the idea of a necessary connection only once the pattern of conjunction has been internalized as a habit. This supports Hume's conclusion that causal necessity is not a feature of the objects perceived but a projection of the mind's habitual expectations onto the world. The example anticipates later discussions of the "problem of induction" in the philosophy of science and of the projection theory of causation defended by Simon Blackburn and others.

**The Bundle Theory and the Elusive Self**

**The scenario:** Hume invites us to turn our attention inward and try to catch ourselves in the act of existing independently of any perception. Introspect as carefully as you like: can you find any impression of a persisting self—something distinct from the successive stream of impressions and ideas that makes up your mental life? Whenever you "enter most intimately" into yourself, you stumble upon some particular perception or other—a sensation of warmth, a feeling of discomfort, an image, a thought—but never a simple impression of *self*.

**The philosophical point:** Since there is no impression of self, the concept of a persisting, unified self cannot be legitimately derived from experience, and the idea must be dismissed as an illegitimate philosophical fiction. What we call the "self" is merely a bundle of perceptions held together by relations of resemblance and causation—a fiction generated by the mind's tendency to mistake the close succession of related perceptions for a genuine identity. This poses an immediate problem for Hume's own epistemology: the "skeptic" who makes these claims must be someone—but who? Hume acknowledges the difficulty in the Appendix to the *Treatise*, admitting he cannot satisfactorily explain personal identity and expressing genuine perplexity. The bundle theory remains the starting point for contemporary discussions of personal identity, particularly in the neo-Humean tradition of Derek Parfit.
