# The Synthetic A Priori: Kant's Central Puzzle

The question that drives the *Critique of Pure Reason* is: "How are synthetic a priori judgments possible?" This question presupposes the coherence of the category "synthetic a priori" — a presupposition that was immediately controversial and remains so. But the question itself is profound: how can there be knowledge that is both genuinely informative (not merely definitional) and knowable without empirical investigation?

## The Setup: Three Kinds of Judgment

Kant begins by establishing three categories of judgment:

**Analytic judgments**: The predicate is contained in the concept of the subject. "All bachelors are unmarried." True by analysis of concepts; a priori (knowable without experience); not genuinely informative (tells you nothing about the world beyond what you already knew from the concepts).

**Synthetic a posteriori judgments**: The predicate adds something not contained in the subject; known through experience. "The morning star is the evening star." Genuinely informative; but requires empirical investigation.

**Synthetic a priori judgments**: The predicate adds something not contained in the subject; but the judgment is known without experience. This is the problematic category.

## Why There Must Be a Synthetic A Priori

Kant insists that mathematics and natural science contain synthetic a priori knowledge:

**Mathematics**: "7 + 5 = 12" seems obvious, but Kant argues it's synthetic. The concept of 12 is not contained in the concepts of 7, 5, and addition — you have to go beyond the concepts and construct the result. And arithmetic is not known through experience (you don't need to count 7 and 5 things to know their sum).

**Natural science**: "Every event has a cause" is a foundational principle of Newtonian physics. It's not analytic — it's not part of the concept of "event" that it has a cause. And it can't be known purely through experience (Hume showed that). Yet it seems necessarily true within the domain of experience.

If these claims are genuinely synthetic a priori, they represent a special kind of knowledge that neither pure rationalism nor pure empiricism can accommodate.

## The Solution: The Mind's Contributions

Kant's answer: synthetic a priori knowledge is possible because it concerns the mind's own forms and categories — the a priori structures through which the mind constitutes experience. Arithmetic is synthetic a priori because it concerns the pure form of intuition (time, for Kant). Causation is a priori because it is one of the categories of the understanding — a rule the mind applies to experience.

Since these forms and categories are features of the mind's activity in constituting experience, we can know them a priori (by understanding our own cognitive operations) and they are genuinely informative about the world as experienced (because the world as experienced is structured by these operations).

## The Revolutionary Implication

This implies that synthetic a priori knowledge is not knowledge of mind-independent reality but of the structure that the mind imposes on experience. We can know, a priori, that all events in experience have causes — but only because causation is one of the categories we bring to experience. We can't know whether causation applies to things-in-themselves.

This is Kant's answer to Hume: causation is necessary within experience, but not because it's read off from reality. It's necessary because it's a condition of the possibility of experience as an objective, ordered whole.

## Controversy and Aftermath

The concept of the synthetic a priori has been controversial from the start:

**Hume's heirs** (the logical positivists): All necessary truths are analytic (true by convention); all synthetic truths are contingent and a posteriori. There is no synthetic a priori. What Kant took to be synthetic a priori is really just the structure of our conceptual schemes, subject to revision.

**Quine**: Undermined the analytic/synthetic distinction itself, calling into question whether the categories Kant needs are coherent.

**Contemporary defenders**: Some philosophers (Bonjour, Peacocke) have defended a moderate rationalism that includes something like synthetic a priori knowledge — knowledge of principles that are not merely definitional but are knowable through rational insight.

Whether the synthetic a priori is a genuine category or a philosophical illusion is one of the live questions of epistemology. But Kant's formulation of the question — how is non-trivial, non-empirical knowledge possible? — remains indispensable.
