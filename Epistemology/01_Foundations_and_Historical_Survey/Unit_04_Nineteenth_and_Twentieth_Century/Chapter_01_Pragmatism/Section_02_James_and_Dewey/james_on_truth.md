# William James on Truth: What Works Is True

William James (1842–1910) took Peirce's pragmatism in a more radical and more accessible direction. Where Peirce's pragmatism is primarily a theory of *meaning* — what a concept means is its practical consequences — James developed a pragmatic theory of *truth*. The result is one of the most provocative and widely criticized positions in the history of epistemology.

## The Pragmatic Theory of Truth

James's central claim: "The true is only the expedient in the way of our thinking, just as the right is only the expedient in the way of our behaving." Truth is what it is expedient to believe — what "works" in practice, what "pays" in terms of guiding successful action and fitting with our other beliefs.

More carefully: a belief is true if it is "good in the way of belief" — if believing it is useful, if it leads to successful practical consequences, if it helps us navigate experience, coordinate with others, and satisfy our intellectual and practical needs.

This is not the view that truth is whatever you *want* to be true. James is clear that a belief must actually work — it must face the test of experience, cohere with other beliefs, and prove its worth in practice. Not all convenient beliefs are true; a convenient but false belief will eventually fail the test.

## The Cash Value Metaphor

James uses the metaphor of "cash value" to describe what truth means. An idea is true if it has cash value — if it can be "cashed out" in terms of actual experience and practical success. Abstract claims that make no difference to experience have no cash value and are therefore empty (echoing Peirce's maxim).

This connects to James's broader philosophical program: he is suspicious of abstract metaphysics that floats free of experience. The question to ask of any philosophical claim is: what concrete difference does it make?

## Religious Belief and the Will to Believe

One of James's most discussed and controversial applications of pragmatism concerns religious belief. In "The Will to Believe" (1896), he argues that in certain cases — where a question is "genuine" (forced, live, and momentous) and where evidence is insufficient to decide it — it is rational to allow our non-evidential interests and desires to guide belief.

Applied to theism: the question of whether God exists is genuine (forced, live, momentous). The evidence is insufficient to decide it either way. In this situation, choosing to believe in God — if doing so brings significant practical benefits (moral orientation, meaning, motivation) — can be epistemically rational.

This argument has been widely criticized as a license for wishful thinking. James's defenders point out that the argument is more constrained than it appears: it applies only in cases of genuine evidential insufficiency, and it doesn't license belief in just anything comforting.

## The Objections to James's Truth

James's theory of truth attracted immediate and sustained criticism:

**The objection from error**: If true means "works," then false beliefs that happen to work are true. But this seems wrong — false beliefs can be useful and true beliefs can be useless. Truth and usefulness come apart.

**The objection from moral beliefs**: Many beliefs in morally false but socially stabilizing doctrines have "worked" in the pragmatic sense. But we don't want to call them true.

**The Peirce objection**: Peirce himself criticized James for psychologizing pragmatism — for making truth a matter of individual benefit rather than the long-run convergence of inquiry.

**Russell's objection**: The belief that God exists might lead to eternal happiness (if God rewards believers). Does that make it true? Russell thought James's view made truth too subjective and easily manipulable.

James responded to some of these objections but never fully resolved them. His theory of truth is more a provocative stimulus to rethinking what truth is than a finished account.

## James's Legacy

Despite the objections, James's pragmatism has been enormously influential:

- His emphasis on the concrete, practical stakes of philosophical questions remains a healthy antidote to purely abstract theorizing.
- His psychology (*Principles of Psychology*, 1890) founded American psychology and introduced concepts — stream of consciousness, the will, habit — that influenced both psychology and philosophy of mind.
- His radical empiricism — the view that relations between things are as directly experienced as the things themselves — anticipated some themes in process philosophy and phenomenology.
