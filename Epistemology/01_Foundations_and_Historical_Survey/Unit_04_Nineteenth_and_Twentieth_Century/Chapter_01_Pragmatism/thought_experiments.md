## Thought Experiments in Pragmatist Epistemology

---

**The Squirrel Around the Tree**

**The scenario:** William James describes a dispute among a group of campers. A squirrel is clinging to a tree trunk. A man walks around the tree, trying to see the squirrel, but the squirrel also moves around the trunk to keep the trunk between itself and the man. After the man has gone completely around the tree, the question is debated: has the man gone around the squirrel? One group says yes—he has gone north, east, south, and west of it, encircling its position in space. The other group says no—the man was always facing the squirrel's belly and never succeeded in getting to its back.

**The philosophical point:** James uses this apparently trivial dispute to illustrate the pragmatic method. The question "has he gone around the squirrel?" has no single correct answer because "going around" is ambiguous. Once we distinguish the two meanings—encircling the spatial position versus cycling through relative orientations—there is no remaining dispute. The pragmatic method dissolves philosophical questions by identifying what practical differences different answers would make; if two positions agree on all conceivable practical consequences, they are not really different positions. This has major implications for ontology and metaphysics: many apparently deep metaphysical disputes may be mere verbal disagreements that dissolve once we apply the pragmatic maxim.

**Peirce's Diamond in the Dark**

**The scenario:** Peirce considers whether a diamond that has never been touched and never will be touched (suppose it comes into existence and is immediately annihilated) should be described as "hard." By the scholastic realist tradition, the diamond has the property of hardness regardless of whether this is ever manifest. By a strict operationalist reading of the pragmatic maxim, the claim that an untested diamond is hard is empty, since no tests will ever be performed on it.

**The philosophical point:** This thought experiment is a locus classicus of the debate between pragmatic operationalism and dispositional realism. Peirce himself was a scholastic realist about generals and properties, and he argued that the dispositional claim—"if this diamond were scratched, it would not be scratched"—is genuinely true in virtue of the diamond's real internal constitution, even if the test never occurs. The thought experiment thus reveals a tension within pragmatism between the strict operationalism of the pragmatic maxim and the realism about dispositions and generals that Peirce also endorsed. Contemporary debates about dispositional properties and possible worlds bear directly on this case.

**James's Will to Believe: The Mountain Trail**

**The scenario:** James describes a man lost in the mountains who comes to a precipice from which the only escape is a difficult leap. He has never performed such a leap before, and the evidence is genuinely inconclusive about whether he can make it. If he hesitates and waits for more evidence, he will freeze; if he tries, he may succeed or fail. James observes that believing confidently in one's ability to make the leap—even without sufficient evidence—produces the bodily steadiness and decisiveness that makes success more likely.

**The philosophical point:** James uses this case to argue that in certain conditions—where action is required, the evidence is genuinely inconclusive, and belief itself affects the probability of success—voluntarily chosen belief is epistemically rational. The case is designed to show that the evidentialism of W. K. Clifford ("it is always wrong, everywhere, for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence") is itself a practical policy with consequences, and that in forced options with high stakes, the policy of withholding belief has its own costs and risks. The mountain leap has been extensively analyzed in debates about epistemic rationality, belief voluntarism, and the relationship between practical and theoretical reason.

**Dewey's Inquiry as Surgical Operation**

**The scenario:** Dewey frequently uses medical and biological examples: a physician faced with a patient presenting ambiguous symptoms does not simply "observe" raw data and then "infer" a diagnosis by mechanical rule-application. The physician brings theoretical knowledge of pathology, prior experience with similar cases, intuitions formed through practice, and hypotheses that direct further observation. Tests are performed not to accumulate facts for their own sake but to refine or refute specific hypotheses. The "problem" the physician faces is itself partly constituted by the theoretical framework she brings.

**The philosophical point:** Dewey's medical analogy illustrates several features of his account of inquiry: that problems are not simply given but are diagnosed and constituted through expert judgment; that observation and theory are not independent stages but mutually conditioning; that the goal of inquiry is the resolution of a practical difficulty, not the accumulation of correspondence with antecedent fact; and that the standards of good inquiry are internal to the practice and cannot be derived from a theory-neutral standpoint. The model undermines both foundationalism (which seeks a theory-neutral starting point) and spectator theories of knowledge (which treat the knower as passive receptor rather than active problem-solver).

**Rorty's Conversation as the Medium of Knowledge**

**The scenario:** Rorty asks us to imagine two communities with radically different conceptual frameworks—different background assumptions about what counts as evidence, what kinds of questions are important, what conclusions are acceptable. A physicist and a shaman discuss the cause of a disease. Each finds the other's claims unintelligible or question-begging. There is no neutral "observation language" to which both can appeal, and no shared set of rational standards that could adjudicate between their frameworks from a position outside both.

**The philosophical point:** Rorty draws the anti-foundationalist moral that there is no "God's eye view"—no framework-independent standpoint from which we can assess our beliefs for correspondence with reality. Knowledge is not a matter of the mind accurately mirroring the world but of the knower successfully participating in the practices of justification maintained by her community. The implication is not relativism in a simple sense—within a community, better and worse reasons can still be distinguished—but it is the rejection of epistemological foundationalism and the "mirror" metaphor. Realist critics (Putnam, McDowell) have argued that this conflates the impossibility of a view from nowhere with the claim that all views are equally good, and that Rorty's position cannot account for the normativity of inquiry.
