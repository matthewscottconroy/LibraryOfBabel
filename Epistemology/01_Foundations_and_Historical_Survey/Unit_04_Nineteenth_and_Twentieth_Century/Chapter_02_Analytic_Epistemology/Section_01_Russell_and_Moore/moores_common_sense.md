# Moore's Common Sense Defense of Commonsense Epistemology

G.E. Moore (1873–1958) was Russell's closest philosophical ally and one of the founders of analytic philosophy. But where Russell's epistemology engaged with skepticism by trying to show that it can be answered by philosophical argument, Moore took a different and philosophically significant approach: he argued that commonsense beliefs are *more certain* than any philosophical premises that would be used to undermine them.

## The Defense of Common Sense

In his essay "A Defence of Common Sense" (1925), Moore states that he knows with certainty a long list of propositions about the external world: that the Earth has existed for many years, that his body exists, that other people exist and have minds, that there are material objects in the external world. He doesn't try to prove these from more secure premises — he simply asserts them as things he knows.

Moore's point: philosophical arguments for skepticism must start from premises. But those premises are never more certain than the commonsense propositions they're used to undermine. If an argument concludes that I don't know I have hands, then I'm more certain that I have hands than I am of any premise of the argument. The rational response is to reject the argument (in one of its premises) rather than accept the conclusion.

This is what has been called **Moorean shifting**: reversing the direction of the argument, using the certainty of the conclusion's denial to impugn one of the premises.

## The Proof of an External World

In his famous 1939 lecture "Proof of an External World," Moore offers a proof of the existence of the external world: "Here is one hand, and here is another hand. Therefore, there are at least two external objects." This proof is valid (it has the right form), and Moore claims the premises are known with certainty.

The proof seems trivially question-begging: the skeptic will simply deny that Moore knows his premises. But Moore's point is more subtle. He's not trying to convince the skeptic from premises the skeptic accepts. He's pointing out that he does know his premises — more certainly than he knows any philosophical claim that would undermine them. The burden is on the skeptic to show that Moore's commonsense certainties are unjustified.

## Wittgenstein's Development

Wittgenstein, in *On Certainty* (written shortly before his death in 1951), developed Moore's insight in a more systematic direction. Moore's achievement, Wittgenstein suggests, is not a proof but an illustration of the structure of our practices of justification.

Some propositions function as "hinge propositions" — they are not themselves justified by evidence because they are the *framework* within which evidence operates. "I have hands" is not something I arrive at by inference; it's part of the bedrock of my epistemic practice. Doubting it would require undermining the very practices of checking and testing that make doubt meaningful.

We'll examine Wittgenstein's hinge propositions in detail in the unit on Skepticism.

## Moore on Analysis

Moore's other major contribution to analytic epistemology is methodological. He insisted on the value of careful conceptual analysis — identifying the necessary and sufficient conditions for concepts. His analysis of the concept of "good" in *Principia Ethica* (1903) — his argument that "good" is a simple, unanalyzable concept — launched twentieth-century analytic metaethics.

In epistemology, Moore's insistence on distinguishing what we mean by "knowledge" from what we mean by "justified belief," and his attention to the structure of epistemic concepts, helped establish the conceptual analysis approach that dominated mid-twentieth-century analytic epistemology.

## The Legacy

Moore's commonsense defense remains influential. The Moorean response to skepticism — that commonsense certainties are more reliable than philosophical arguments against them — has been developed by philosophers like Chisholm and, in modified forms, by virtue epistemologists. The claim that there is a *hierarchy* of certainties, with commonsense beliefs at the top, challenges the Cartesian assumption that the right method is to start from scratch and work upward from certain foundations.
