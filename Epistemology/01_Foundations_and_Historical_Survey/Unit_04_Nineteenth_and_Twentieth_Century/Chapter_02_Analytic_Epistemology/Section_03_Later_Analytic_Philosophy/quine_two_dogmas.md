# Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism": A Turning Point

W.V.O. Quine's 1951 paper "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" is one of the most influential philosophical papers of the twentieth century. It attacks the two central assumptions of logical positivism and, in doing so, reshapes the entire landscape of analytic epistemology.

## The Two Dogmas

The first dogma: there is a meaningful analytic/synthetic distinction — a division between truths that hold by virtue of meaning alone (analytic) and truths that depend on the facts (synthetic). This distinction was central to logical positivism: mathematics and logic are analytic (and therefore a priori but empty of factual content), and all substantive knowledge is synthetic (empirical).

The second dogma: **reductionism** — the view that every meaningful statement is equivalent to, or reducible to, a statement about immediate experience. Each statement faces the "tribunal of experience" individually, one by one.

Quine attacks both.

## Against the Analytic/Synthetic Distinction

Quine's argument against analyticity is intricate. The concept of analyticity seems to depend on the concept of synonymy (two expressions being synonymous). But synonymy itself is defined in terms of analytic truth: two expressions are synonymous if replacing one with the other preserves the truth-value of sentences containing them. The definitions circle.

Alternative characterizations of analyticity also fail:
- Truth by definition: definitions are either lexical (reporting existing usage — not normative) or stipulative (arbitrary).
- "No statement is analytic" is self-refuting: is that itself analytic?
- Logical truth extended by synonymy: But synonymy is itself in question.

Quine concludes that no principled, non-circular characterization of analyticity is available. The distinction is a metaphysical article of faith, not a defensible philosophical distinction.

## Against Reductionism: The Web of Belief

Quine replaces the reductionist picture with a **holistic** one. Our beliefs don't face experience individually, each tied to a specific set of confirming or disconfirming experiences. Instead, the "unit of empirical significance is the whole of science" — our beliefs form an interconnected web, and experience impinges on the web as a whole.

When experience conflicts with our beliefs, we have a choice about which beliefs to revise. The choice is constrained by practical considerations — minimize disruption, preserve simplicity, maintain logical consistency — but there is no algorithm. Any statement can be held true in the face of any experience, by making sufficient adjustments elsewhere. And no statement is immune to revision.

This includes *logic*. Quine famously suggests that even the laws of logic could in principle be revised in light of recalcitrant experience (though this would require maximal disruption). The scientific web of belief has no absolutely immune propositions.

## The Pragmatic Upshot

Quine's picture is pragmatist: beliefs are instruments for predicting experience and navigating the world. The distinction between "factual" and "conventional" components of belief dissolves — there is no pure factual component to be compared with reality, independent of our conceptual framework.

This has implications for epistemology:
- **Naturalism**: Philosophy is continuous with science; there is no first-philosophy standpoint from which to judge science.
- **Holism**: Justification is always of the web as a whole, not of individual statements.
- **Revisability**: All beliefs, including those in logic and mathematics, are in principle revisable.

## Criticisms

Quine's attack on analyticity has been highly influential but not universally accepted:

- **Grice and Strawson** argued that the concept of synonymy is perfectly serviceable and that Quine demands a standard of philosophical rigor that nothing meets.
- **Boghossian** distinguished metaphysical analyticity (truth in virtue of meaning alone) from epistemic analyticity (known through understanding alone) and argued that the latter is defensible even if the former is not.
- **Semantic externalism** (Putnam, Kripke) complicated the picture: if meaning isn't fully in the head, the notion of "truth by virtue of meaning" becomes more complex.

Whatever its ultimate verdict, "Two Dogmas" permanently altered analytic philosophy and made holism and naturalism serious philosophical positions.
