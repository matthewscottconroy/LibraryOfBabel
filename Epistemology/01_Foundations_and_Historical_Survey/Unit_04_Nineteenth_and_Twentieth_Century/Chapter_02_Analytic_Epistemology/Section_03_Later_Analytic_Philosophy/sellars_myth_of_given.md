# Sellars and the Myth of the Given

Wilfrid Sellars's "Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind" (1956) — known as EPM — is widely regarded as one of the most important philosophical works of the twentieth century. Its central target is what Sellars calls the "Myth of the Given": the foundationalist assumption that there are immediate, non-inferential, epistemically basic experiences that can justify all other beliefs.

## The Myth of the Given Defined

The "Given" is the epistemological tradition's name for what is simply *presented* to the mind — what the mind receives passively, without any conceptual mediation or interpretive contribution. Sense data (for Russell, Ayer), qualia, phenomenal appearances — these are candidates for the Given. The idea: before any inference, before any conceptual framing, there is something simply given to the mind. This given is supposed to be epistemically basic: it justifies other beliefs without itself requiring justification.

The myth, for Sellars, is not that these experiences exist but that they can play the epistemic role assigned to them. The claim that there is given, non-inferential, epistemically basic content that justifies other beliefs — this is the myth.

## The Dilemma

Sellars poses a dilemma for advocates of the Given:

Consider a sense impression or immediate experience — say, the appearance of something red. This experience is supposed to justify the belief "this is red."

**Option 1**: The experience is a conceptual state — a state that has logical relationships to other states, that can be evaluated as correct or incorrect, that contributes to the logical space of reasons. If so, it can justify beliefs — but then it's not "given" (immediate, unconceptualized) but involves conceptual capacities and therefore requires its own justification.

**Option 2**: The experience is a non-conceptual state — a purely causal occurrence, a matter of natural fact. If so, it's pre-conceptual and cannot, in itself, justify anything. Natural occurrences don't have logical relationships; they don't show that anything is the case.

The dilemma: something in the space of reasons (justified, justifying) cannot be the raw material of the Given; something outside the space of reasons cannot provide justification.

## The Sellarsian Conclusion

Sellars concludes: there is no epistemological Given. All knowledge is inferential in the sense that all claims exist within the space of reasons and are accountable to other claims. There are no foundational certainties that require no justification and can serve as the ultimate basis of justification.

This doesn't mean all knowledge is equally uncertain — it means that the structure of knowledge is not foundationalist (resting on absolutely basic unconceptualized givens) but coherentist (mutually supporting within the space of reasons).

## Perception and Concepts

Sellars's view has important implications for the epistemology of perception. Perceptual knowledge — "I see that the object in front of me is red" — involves conceptual capacities. To perceive something *as* red is to apply the concept red, to classify the experience within a conceptual framework.

This doesn't mean perception doesn't involve non-conceptual content — there may be "looks-red" experiences that have a distinctively phenomenal character. But the epistemic work — the justificatory work — is done by the conceptual classification, not by the raw experience.

## Influence and Controversy

Sellars's critique has been enormously influential:

**John McDowell** developed Sellars's insight in *Mind and World* (1994): perceptual experience itself must be conceptually structured — concepts must extend all the way out to the world — or the relationship between mind and world collapses into either the Myth of the Given or a form of coherentism that loses contact with the world.

**Robert Brandom** extended Sellars's "inferentialism" — the view that the content of concepts is determined by their inferential roles — into a comprehensive philosophy of language and mind.

**The Direct Realist Response**: Defenders of naive realism and direct realism have argued that Sellars's critique doesn't target their view — they don't claim there is an unconceptualized Given; they claim that perceptual experience is already experience of the world, not of an intervening representation. Disjunctivism, in particular, responds to the Sellarsian challenge by insisting that genuine perception is fundamentally different from mere impression.

The "Myth of the Given" debate shaped much of the second half of twentieth-century analytic epistemology and remains a live controversy.
