## Primary Sources

- Bertrand Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, Oxford University Press, 1912 (repr. 1998). Russell's most accessible epistemological work; introduces the distinction between knowledge by acquaintance and knowledge by description, the problem of induction, and the theory of sense-data.

- Bertrand Russell, *Our Knowledge of the External World*, Allen and Unwin, 1914. Develops logical atomism as an epistemological and logical method; argues for the construction of the physical world from sense-data using the resources of modern logic.

- G. E. Moore, *Philosophical Studies*, Routledge, 1922. Contains "The Refutation of Idealism" and other key early papers; representative of Moore's common-sense approach to epistemological questions.

- G. E. Moore, "Proof of an External World," in *Philosophical Papers*, Allen and Unwin, 1959. Moore's famous paper presenting a "proof" that external objects exist by holding up his hands; the paper frames the question of what kind of certainty is available to common sense.

- Rudolf Carnap, *The Logical Structure of the World (Aufbau)*, trans. Rolf A. George, University of California Press, 1969. The definitive Vienna Circle contribution to epistemology; attempts to construct all empirical knowledge from elementary experiences using the resources of the new logic.

- A. J. Ayer, *Language, Truth and Logic*, Gollancz, 1936 (repr. Dover, 1952). The English popularization of logical positivism; presents the verification principle as a criterion of meaningfulness and applies it to metaphysics and ethics.

- Ludwig Wittgenstein, *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus*, trans. D. F. Pears and B. F. McGuinness, Routledge, 1961. The picture theory of meaning and its implications for the limits of what can be said; the epistemological implications are concentrated in the discussion of showing versus saying.

- Ludwig Wittgenstein, *Philosophical Investigations*, trans. G. E. M. Anscombe, Blackwell, 1953. The critique of the private language argument, the rule-following considerations, and the dissolution of epistemological problems through attention to how language actually functions.

- Ludwig Wittgenstein, *On Certainty*, trans. G. E. M. Anscombe and G. H. von Wright, Blackwell, 1969. Wittgenstein's final investigations into certainty, hinge propositions, and the framework presuppositions of our epistemic practices; a direct engagement with Moore's common-sense epistemology.

- Edmund Gettier, "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?", *Analysis*, 23 (1963), pp. 121–123. The three-page paper that refuted the tripartite analysis of knowledge and launched the contemporary epistemological research program.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Ayer, A. J., *Russell and Moore: The Analytical Heritage*, Harvard University Press, 1971. A reliable overview of early analytic epistemology by one of its principal practitioners; situates Russell and Moore in relation to the broader development of the analytic tradition.

- Coffa, J. Alberto, *The Semantic Tradition from Kant to Carnap: To the Vienna Station*, Cambridge University Press, 1991. A magisterial intellectual history tracing the development of the semantic and epistemological concerns that led to logical positivism; essential for understanding the analytic tradition's roots.

- Hacker, P. M. S., *Wittgenstein: Meaning and Mind*, Blackwell, 1990. Part of Hacker's multi-volume commentary on the *Philosophical Investigations*; provides detailed exegesis of the epistemologically relevant sections including the private language argument.

- Soames, Scott, *Philosophical Analysis in the Twentieth Century*, 2 vols., Princeton University Press, 2003. A comprehensive survey of analytic epistemology from Russell and Moore through Gettier and beyond; serves as both a history and a philosophical assessment.

- Chalmers, David, Manley, David, and Wasserman, Ryan (eds.), *Metametaphysics: New Essays on the Foundations of Ontology*, Oxford University Press, 2009. Contains papers on the analytic tradition's treatment of the relationship between epistemology and metaphysics in the post-Gettier period.

- Hylton, Peter, *Russell, Idealism, and the Emergence of Analytic Philosophy*, Clarendon Press, 1990. A detailed study of how Russell's break with British Idealism shaped the analytic epistemological program; traces the origins of the acquaintance/description distinction.

- Friedman, Michael, *Reconsidering Logical Positivism*, Cambridge University Press, 1999. A revisionist study arguing that logical positivism was more continuous with the Kantian and Neo-Kantian tradition than the standard "death of positivism" narrative suggests.

- Glock, Hans-Johann, *What Is Analytic Philosophy?*, Cambridge University Press, 2008. An examination of the defining features and internal diversity of the analytic tradition, with extended discussion of its epistemological concerns and methods.
