## Bertrand Russell (1872–1970)

Russell's contribution to analytic epistemology is twofold: the logical method and the epistemological framework. Logically, he developed the theory of descriptions, showing that apparent names like "the present King of France" are disguised descriptions whose analysis dissolves puzzles about non-existent objects. Epistemologically, he distinguished knowledge by acquaintance—direct, unmediated awareness of sense-data, universals, memory-data, and (controversially) the self—from knowledge by description, which is indirect and mediated by descriptive content. His principle that all genuine knowledge must ultimately be grounded in acquaintance with particulars drove both his logical atomism and his theory of the construction of the physical world from sense-data, making him the bridge between the idealist tradition he rejected and the logical empiricism that followed.

## G. E. Moore (1873–1958)

Moore's common-sense philosophy was a sustained defence of the view that ordinary perceptual and introspective knowledge is more certain than any philosophical argument that might be brought against it. His "proof" of the external world—holding up his hands and saying "here is one hand, and here is another"—was not a trivial gesture but an argument that we have more certainty in these ordinary perceptual claims than in any philosophical premise that could be used to doubt them. His analysis of the "naturalistic fallacy" in ethics has an epistemological dimension: he was similarly suspicious of philosophical reductions that claimed to define common-sense concepts in terms that were actually less clear than the original.

## Rudolf Carnap (1891–1970)

Carnap was the most technically sophisticated member of the Vienna Circle and the leading figure in the development of logical empiricism as a research program. His *Aufbau* attempted to construct the entire empirical world from elementary experiences using purely logical relations, combining Humean empiricism with Russellian logic. In his mature work, he distinguished sharply between "internal" questions (questions posed within a chosen linguistic framework, which have determinate answers) and "external" questions (questions about which framework to adopt, which are pragmatic choices without factual answers). This distinction was designed to dissolve traditional metaphysical disputes, but Quine's critique of the analytic/synthetic distinction undermined the framework that made it possible.

## Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889–1951)

Wittgenstein's two major works represent successive transformations of the analytic tradition's epistemological assumptions. The *Tractatus* attempted to show that all meaningful language pictures the facts and that what cannot be said (logical form, ethics, aesthetics) can only be shown. The *Philosophical Investigations* rejected the entire framework of the *Tractatus* and argued that philosophical problems arise from the misuse of language—from taking words out of their natural home in practice and misapplying them. His private language argument—that a genuinely private, ostensively defined language would be uninterpretable—challenged both Cartesian epistemology and phenomenalistic sense-datum theories. *On Certainty* argued that knowledge claims are embedded in a framework of "hinge propositions" that function not as justified beliefs but as unquestioned background conditions of inquiry.

## Edmund Gettier (born 1927)

Gettier's three-page paper "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?" published in 1963 is one of the most philosophically productive short papers ever written. By constructing two counterexamples to the tripartite analysis of knowledge as justified true belief, Gettier showed that a belief can be true, believed, and well-justified yet still not qualify as knowledge—because the truth of the belief and the justification are accidentally connected. The paper launched a research program lasting more than sixty years attempting to add a fourth condition to the analysis. Though Gettier himself published very little else, his challenge has generated reliabilism, safety conditions, sensitivity conditions, no-false-lemmas approaches, and eventually the knowledge-first movement that abandoned the analytic project entirely.

## Wilfrid Sellars (1912–1989)

Sellars's "Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind" (1956) identified the "myth of the given"—the foundationalist assumption that there are certain epistemically prior, theory-neutral episodes of immediate experience that justify all other beliefs. Sellars argued that no merely causal relation between a sensory state and a belief can confer justification; justification is a normative, language-involving practice that cannot be grounded in pre-conceptual, pre-linguistic "givens." His critique was devastating for classical empiricism and sense-datum theories, and it paved the way for coherentism, inferentialism (Brandom), and broader attacks on foundationalism in the subsequent decades.

## Roderick Chisholm (1916–1999)

Chisholm was the leading mid-century figure in the analytic theory of knowledge, working in the tradition of careful conceptual analysis applied to epistemic concepts. His distinctions between epistemic states—certainty, incorrigibility, indubitability, prima facie justification, and epistemic acceptance—provided a precise vocabulary for epistemological discussion. His "problem of the criterion" revived the ancient question of whether we must first know which things we know before we can know what the criteria of knowledge are, or vice versa; his own "particularist" answer—that we start with particular instances and work toward general criteria—was influential in the development of reflective equilibrium methodology in epistemology.
