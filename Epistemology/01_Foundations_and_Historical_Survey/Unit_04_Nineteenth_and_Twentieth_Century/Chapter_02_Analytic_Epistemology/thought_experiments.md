## Thought Experiments in Analytic Epistemology

---

**Moore's Two Hands**

**The scenario:** In "Proof of an External World," Moore holds up his hands and says: "Here is one hand"—and then says—"and here is another. Therefore, at least two external objects exist." He claims this is a rigorous proof because the premises are known to be true and validly entail the conclusion. He knows he has two hands at least as well as he knows any philosophical premise that a skeptic might use against him.

**The philosophical point:** Moore's proof is not intended as a discovery but as a diagnosis: it reveals that the skeptic's philosophical premises (that we cannot know perceptual claims are true) are less certain than ordinary perceptual knowledge, not more. This is the Moorean shift in reasoning: instead of accepting a philosophical argument from P1 and P2 to the conclusion that we know nothing, we run the argument the other way, taking the certainty of our knowledge as evidence that the philosophical premises must be wrong. Wittgenstein, in *On Certainty*, engaged closely with Moore's proof and argued that while Moore was right that his hands exist, calling it "knowledge" misapplies that concept—it is rather a background hinge, a presupposition of any inquiry that cannot itself be doubted or verified.

**Russell's Five-Minute World Hypothesis**

**The scenario:** Russell, in *The Analysis of Mind*, entertained the hypothesis that the world sprang into existence five minutes ago—complete with all the physical traces, memories, documents, and fossils that make it appear to have a much longer history. Every piece of evidence we have for the past is compatible with this hypothesis, since that evidence is itself present-tense data (present memories, present records, present physical states) that could have been produced by a very recent creation event.

**The philosophical point:** The five-minute world hypothesis illustrates both the epistemological problem of the past and a general feature of inductive reasoning: our evidence for any claim about unobserved states of the world is present-tense and compatible with multiple hypotheses about the past or about unobserved parts of the present. It shows that even the most entrenched historical beliefs are not "proofs" in the deductive sense but best explanations of present evidence. The hypothesis also highlights the role of theoretical simplicity and coherence in epistemological justification: we reject the five-minute world hypothesis not because it is incoherent or contradicted by evidence, but because it is grotesquely less simple than the ordinary hypothesis, and we take simplicity to be an epistemic virtue.

**The Verification Principle Turns on Itself**

**The scenario:** The Vienna Circle's verification principle held that a statement is meaningful if and only if it is either (a) analytically true (true by definition) or (b) empirically verifiable in principle. The Circle used this principle to dismiss metaphysical claims (about God, absolute being, things-in-themselves) as cognitively meaningless. A philosopher then applies the principle to itself: is the verification principle itself either analytic or empirically verifiable?

**The philosophical point:** It is neither: it is not a definition, and no empirical observation verifies it. By its own criterion, the verification principle is meaningless. This self-refutation objection was pressed forcefully by critics, including Karl Popper and others, and it was a major factor in the collapse of logical positivism in its original form. The difficulty points to a general problem for any criterion of meaningfulness or demarcation: the criterion must be stated in a way that does not itself fall on the wrong side of its own demarcation. Carnap attempted to reformulate the principle as a proposal about which language to adopt—making it pragmatic rather than factual—but this concession substantially altered the positivist project.

**Wittgenstein's Private Diary**

**The scenario:** Wittgenstein asks us to imagine someone who wishes to name a recurring private sensation by making a note "S" in a diary every time the sensation occurs. The intention is to use "S" as a name for *that* sensation—the particular quale—so that the name's meaning is fixed by the inner ostension. Later, when the sensation recurs, the person writes "S" again. But what determines whether the second use of "S" correctly refers to the same sensation as the first? The person can only appeal to their memory of the first sensation—but any standard of correctness that is just "what seems right to me" is no standard at all.

**The philosophical point:** The private language argument challenges the foundation of classical empiricism: the idea that the ultimate ground of meaning and knowledge is the direct acquaintance with private mental particulars. Wittgenstein argues that without a public, shared standard of correctness, there is no difference between using a term correctly and merely feeling as though one is using it correctly. The argument has wide epistemological implications: it undermines sense-datum theories, challenges the idea that first-person authority over one's own mental states is epistemically foundational, and supports an externalist, social account of meaning and justification.

**Gettier's Stopped Clock**

**The scenario:** A woman glances at a clock on the wall to check the time. The clock shows 12:10, which is in fact the correct time. She forms the justified belief that it is 12:10. However, the clock stopped exactly twelve hours ago and has been showing 12:10 ever since; she has simply happened to look at it at the right moment when it happens to be displaying the correct time.

**The philosophical point:** The stopped clock case (a close relative of Gettier's original examples) is a Gettier-type counterexample to the JTB analysis. The woman's belief is true, she believes it, and her belief is justified—clocks on walls are generally reliable and she has no reason to doubt this one. Yet the connection between her justification (this reliable-looking clock shows 12:10) and the truth of her belief (it really is 12:10) is purely coincidental: her justification would have been exactly the same if the time were any other and the clock were still showing 12:10 from twelve hours before. She does not *know* what time it is, even though her belief meets the three traditional conditions. The case diagnoses the problem with JTB: justification and truth must be appropriately *connected*, not merely coinstantiated, for knowledge to be present.
