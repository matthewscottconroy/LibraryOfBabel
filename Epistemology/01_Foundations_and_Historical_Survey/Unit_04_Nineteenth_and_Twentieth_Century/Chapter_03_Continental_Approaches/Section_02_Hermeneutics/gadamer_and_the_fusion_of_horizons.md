# Gadamer and the Fusion of Horizons

Hans-Georg Gadamer (1900–2002) is the principal figure of philosophical hermeneutics — the study of interpretation and understanding. His major work, *Truth and Method* (1960), argues that understanding is not a methodological procedure that can be made fully explicit but a happening, an event, that requires the active participation of the interpreter's entire situation.

## Hermeneutics: Beyond Text Interpretation

Hermeneutics began as the study of biblical and legal interpretation. Schleiermacher and Dilthey gave it a broader scope: it became the methodology of the human sciences (*Geisteswissenschaften*) — history, literature, philology, law. Understanding texts and understanding human actions and artifacts require a different method from the natural sciences, these thinkers argued.

Gadamer radicalizes hermeneutics further: understanding, for him, is not a special method of the human sciences but the fundamental mode of human being-in-the-world (following Heidegger). All cognition involves interpretation; all interpretation involves a "fusion of horizons."

## The Horizon Concept

A horizon is the range of understanding and significance available from a given standpoint. Your epistemic horizon is determined by your language, culture, tradition, education, concerns — all the background that shapes what you can see and understand. The horizon is not a limitation but a condition of understanding: you can only see anything at all from *somewhere*, from a position with a horizon.

When you encounter a text (or a tradition, or another person), they have their own horizon — shaped by their time, culture, assumptions. Understanding involves a *fusion of horizons* (*Horizontverschmelzung*): your horizon and the text's horizon are brought into productive contact, neither simply absorbing the other but producing a new, expanded understanding that encompasses both.

This is different from "eliminating" your own horizon in order to achieve purely objective understanding — Gadamer argues that this is impossible and that the attempt to eliminate one's own perspective produces not objectivity but impoverishment.

## Prejudice Rehabilitated

One of Gadamer's most provocative moves is his rehabilitation of *Vorurteil* (prejudice, fore-judgment). The Enlightenment ideal was to eliminate all prejudice — all inherited assumptions — in favor of autonomous rational judgment. Gadamer argues this is incoherent: you cannot think without some prior understanding, some background of inherited assumptions that makes interpretation possible.

Prejudices are not necessarily wrong or harmful; they are the enabling condition of understanding. The hermeneutic task is not to eliminate prejudice but to become aware of it — to bring our pre-understandings to light, test them against the text or tradition, and revise them where revision is required.

This connects to the hermeneutic circle: understanding a whole requires understanding its parts, and understanding parts requires understanding the whole. The circle is not vicious but productive — it describes the actual movement of interpretation, which spirals toward deeper and richer understanding.

## The Linguisticality of Understanding

Gadamer's principle that "Being that can be understood is language" captures his view that understanding and language are inseparable. We don't first have pre-linguistic thoughts that we then translate into language — understanding *is* linguistic through and through. The world as we understand it is always already a linguistically mediated world.

This has implications for epistemology:
- There is no view from nowhere, no language-independent grasp of reality.
- Different linguistic traditions may give rise to genuinely different understandings of the world.
- Translation between frameworks is possible but is always a kind of fusion, never a perfect mapping.

## The Application to the Natural Sciences

Gadamer's hermeneutics is most obviously applied to the humanities. But he extends it to the natural sciences as well: scientific understanding also involves interpretation, tradition, and the application of inherited conceptual frameworks. Kuhn's account of paradigm-governed science has deep affinities with Gadamer's hermeneutics — scientific revolutions are shifts in the framework that governs interpretation, not simple responses to neutral data.

This doesn't mean science is arbitrary or that the natural and human sciences are epistemically equivalent. But it does mean that the ideal of a fully objective, context-free scientific methodology is a philosophical fiction — even in the natural sciences, understanding is always situated.
