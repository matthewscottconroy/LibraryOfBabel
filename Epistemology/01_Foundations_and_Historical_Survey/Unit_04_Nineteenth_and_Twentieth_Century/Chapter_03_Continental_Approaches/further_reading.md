## Primary Sources

- Edmund Husserl, *Logical Investigations*, 2 vols., trans. J. N. Findlay, Routledge, 1970. Husserl's first major systematic work, laying the foundations of phenomenology; the sixth Investigation, on the categorial intuition, is epistemologically central.

- Edmund Husserl, *Ideas: General Introduction to Pure Phenomenology (Ideas I)*, trans. W. R. Boyce Gibson, Allen and Unwin, 1931. The classic statement of transcendental phenomenology; introduces the epoché, the reduction, and the analysis of intentional structures as the method for epistemological investigation.

- Edmund Husserl, *Cartesian Meditations*, trans. Dorothea Cairns, Martinus Nijhoff, 1960. Husserl's most accessible summary of transcendental phenomenology, delivered as lectures in Paris; grapples with the problem of solipsism and the constitution of other minds.

- Martin Heidegger, *Being and Time*, trans. John Macquarrie and Edward Robinson, Harper & Row, 1962. The foundational text of existential phenomenology; the analysis of *Dasein*, being-in-the-world, thrownness, the ready-to-hand/present-at-hand distinction, and the critique of the traditional subject-object epistemology.

- Maurice Merleau-Ponty, *Phenomenology of Perception*, trans. Colin Smith, Routledge, 1962; new trans. Donald Landes, Routledge, 2012. The central text of embodied cognition in the phenomenological tradition; argues that perception is not a mental event but a bodily engagement with the world.

- Hans-Georg Gadamer, *Truth and Method*, trans. Joel Weinsheimer and Donald G. Marshall, Crossroad, 1989. The systematic statement of philosophical hermeneutics; argues that understanding is always conditioned by tradition, language, and the "fusion of horizons" between interpreter and text or object.

- Paul Ricoeur, *The Rule of Metaphor*, trans. Robert Czerny, University of Toronto Press, 1977. Extends hermeneutics to the epistemological dimensions of figurative language and the role of metaphor in the constitution of meaning.

- Michel Foucault, *The Order of Things (Les Mots et les Choses)*, trans. Alan Sheridan-Smith, Pantheon Books, 1970. Introduces the concept of the episteme—the unconscious structure of knowledge that determines what counts as knowledge in a given era—through historical analysis of the human sciences.

- Gaston Bachelard, *The Formation of the Scientific Mind (La Formation de l'esprit scientifique)*, trans. Mary McAllester Jones, Clinamen Press, 2002. Develops the concept of the "epistemological obstacle"—the resistance that pre-scientific common sense and imagery poses to genuine scientific understanding.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Moran, Dermot, *Introduction to Phenomenology*, Routledge, 2000. The most comprehensive and reliable guide to the phenomenological tradition from Husserl through Merleau-Ponty and Ricoeur; excellent for tracing the epistemological dimensions of each thinker.

- Dreyfus, Hubert L., *Being-in-the-World: A Commentary on Heidegger's Being and Time, Division I*, MIT Press, 1991. The most influential analytical reading of Heidegger's phenomenology; argues that Heidegger's analysis of skilled practice and coping provides a critique of representationalist AI and cognitive science.

- Gadamer, Hans-Georg, *Philosophical Hermeneutics*, trans. David E. Linge, University of California Press, 1976. Essays making hermeneutical themes accessible, including the analysis of prejudice, tradition, and the universal claim of hermeneutics.

- Eribon, Didier, *Michel Foucault*, trans. Betsy Wing, Harvard University Press, 1991. A scholarly biography that contextualizes Foucault's archaeological and genealogical methods within broader epistemological debates.

- Dreyfus, Hubert L. and Hall, Harrison (eds.), *Heidegger: A Critical Reader*, Blackwell, 1992. Essays by leading analytic and continental philosophers addressing Heidegger's epistemological innovations, including the critique of Descartes and the analysis of ready-to-hand cognition.

- Zahavi, Dan, *Husserl's Phenomenology*, Stanford University Press, 2003. A reliable and philosophically rigorous introduction to Husserl's method and its applications; covers intentionality, time-consciousness, intersubjectivity, and the epoché.

- Bernstein, Richard J., *Beyond Objectivism and Relativism: Science, Hermeneutics, and Praxis*, University of Pennsylvania Press, 1983. Connects the continental tradition—especially Gadamer and Arendt—to debates in Anglo-American philosophy of science and pragmatism.

- Gutting, Gary, *Michel Foucault's Archaeology of Scientific Reason*, Cambridge University Press, 1989. A careful analytical reading of Foucault's epistemological work, particularly the concept of the episteme and its relationship to philosophy of science.
