# Essay Questions: Unit 1 — Foundations and Historical Survey

---

**1. The Paradox of Inquiry and its Epistemological Implications**

Plato's Meno introduces a paradox: if you know what you are looking for, inquiry is unnecessary; if you do not know, you will not recognize the answer when you find it. Assess Plato's theory of recollection as a solution to this paradox. Does the theory of recollection explain how prior cognitive access to the Forms is possible, or does it merely relocate the problem? How do contemporary epistemologists address the structural issue the paradox identifies — the requirement that inquiry have an epistemic target it does not yet fully possess?

---

**2. Rationalism, Empiricism, and the Status of A Priori Knowledge**

Descartes and Leibniz hold that certain substantive knowledge is accessible through reason alone, independently of experience. Locke and Hume deny this, arguing that all knowledge is derived from sensation and reflection. Kant attempted a synthesis by distinguishing the a priori from the merely analytic and arguing for synthetic a priori knowledge. Critically assess the Kantian response to both positions. Does Kant's concept of synthetic a priori knowledge succeed? What would it mean for Quine's attack on the analytic-synthetic distinction to undermine the Kantian project?

---

**3. Hume's Problem of Induction and its Epistemological Significance**

Hume argued that the inference from observed regularities to unobserved cases cannot be justified by reason (which would require necessary connections between events) or by experience (which would be circular). Assess the scope and severity of this problem. Is the problem of induction a genuine threat to empirical knowledge, or does it merely show that knowledge of the future cannot be demonstrated? Evaluate at least two proposed solutions: Popper's falsificationism, Bayesian conditionalization, or reliabilist accounts of inductive inference. Which solution, if any, meets Hume's challenge?

---

**4. Descartes on Method and the Foundations of Knowledge**

Descartes's method of hyperbolic doubt is designed to identify what cannot be doubted and hence can serve as the foundation of knowledge. Critically assess this foundationalist methodology. Does the Cogito — "I think, therefore I am" — survive the methodological doubt, and does it provide a secure foundation from which Descartes can reconstruct knowledge of the external world? Is Descartes's reliance on God's veracity as a guarantee of clear and distinct perception viciously circular (the "Cartesian Circle"), or does the structure of his argument avoid this objection?

---

**5. The Emergence of Analytic Epistemology from the Historical Tradition**

Analytic epistemology as practiced from the 1960s onward is often presented as a clean break from its historical antecedents. Assess this claim. To what extent does the Gettier problem (1963) represent a transformation of a debate that is already visible in Plato's Theaetetus and Descartes's Meditations? In what ways does the contemporary debate about internalism and externalism about justification recapitulate the historical debate between rationalism and empiricism? Is the history of epistemology best understood as a series of paradigm shifts or as a continuous interrogation of the same set of questions?

---

**6. Kant's Copernican Revolution and Its Legacy**

Kant's "Copernican Revolution" — the claim that objects conform to the conditions of our knowledge, rather than our knowledge conforming to objects — was intended to make synthetic a priori knowledge possible. Critically evaluate the cogency of this strategy. What work is the transcendental deduction doing, and does it succeed in showing that the categories necessarily apply to experience? How does Kant's response to Hume differ from pragmatist and reliabilist responses, and which is more satisfactory?

---

**7. The Relationship between Historical and Systematic Epistemology**

The study of historical epistemology — Plato, Aristotle, Descartes, Hume, Kant — is sometimes presented as a propaedeutic to the "real" epistemological work done by contemporary analytic philosophy. Evaluate this relationship critically. Does the history of philosophy provide genuine philosophical insights unavailable through systematic contemporary analysis, or is it primarily valuable as intellectual history? Defend a substantive view about what epistemologists should learn from reading primary historical sources.
