# Unit 2: The Nature of Knowledge — Unit Survey

## Central Questions

This unit addresses the question that has dominated analytic epistemology since 1963: what is knowledge? The classical analysis — knowledge is justified true belief — seemed, before Edmund Gettier's three-page paper, to be approaching settled consensus. Gettier's counterexamples shattered that consensus with extraordinary efficiency and initiated a research program that continues today without a universally accepted conclusion.

The central questions are: what distinguishes knowledge from merely accidentally true justified belief? Is there a fourth condition that, added to JTB, yields knowledge? Or does the very methodology of seeking such conditions misconceive the structure of knowledge? Are the post-Gettier theories — reliabilism, tracking theories, safety conditions, virtue epistemology, knowledge-first — genuine improvements, or do they each face analogous difficulties? And does the Gettier problem reveal something deep about the concept of knowledge, or is it a symptom of a misguided approach to epistemological analysis?

The unit is organized around the dialectic between the classical analysis, its destruction, and the main reconstructive responses. Each reconstructive theory attempts to capture the intuition that in Gettier cases, the agent's true belief is true "by accident" — but cashing out that notion proves surprisingly difficult. The sub-chapters move from the positive JTB account to the specific Gettier cases, to the family of post-Gettier theories, and finally to the contextual and knowledge-first approaches that question the presuppositions of the analysis project itself.

## Map of Major Positions and Their Logical Relations

**The Classical JTB Analysis.** Knowledge = justified true belief. The three conditions are individually necessary (no one knows falsehoods; one cannot know what one does not believe; true belief by luck is not knowledge) and jointly sufficient. The philosophical work is done by the "justified" condition, which is why the literature on justification (Unit 4) is so extensive.

**Gettier Counterexamples.** Gettier's three original cases establish that JTB is not sufficient for knowledge. Each case describes a situation in which an agent has a justified true belief but clearly does not know. The general structure: the agent infers q from a false justified belief p; by coincidence, q is true, but not for the reason the agent thinks.

**No False Lemmas (Harman).** A fourth condition: S does not know that p if S's justified inference to p passes through a false intermediate belief. This handles Gettier's original cases but fails against "Gettier cases without false lemmas" — cases where the agent infers truly all the way but the inference is still accidentally connected to the truth.

**Causal Theory (Goldman 1967).** S knows that p iff p is causally connected to S's belief in an appropriate way. Handles perceptual knowledge naturally. Fails for mathematical and other non-causal knowledge, and faces the problem of specifying which causal connections are "appropriate."

**Relevant Alternatives (Dretske, Goldman 1976).** S knows that p iff S can discriminate p from all relevant alternatives to p. The concept of "relevance" does the work. Introduces the fake barns case: if the fake barns are not relevant alternatives, one can know. If they are, one cannot. Connects naturally to contextualism.

**Tracking Theory (Nozick).** S knows that p iff:
- p is true
- S believes that p
- If p were false, S would not believe that p (sensitivity)
- If p were true (in nearby possible worlds), S would believe that p (adherence)

The sensitivity condition explains why in Gettier cases the belief would still be held even if p were false. Faces the problem that tracking does not transmit across entailment: one can track each of several propositions without tracking their conjunction.

**Safety Condition (Sosa, Williamson, Pritchard).** S knows that p iff S's belief that p is safe: in nearby possible worlds in which S believes that p in the same way, p is true. Safety is the converse of sensitivity and arguably more plausible. Safety-based accounts align naturally with virtue epistemology.

**Reliabilism (Goldman 1979).** S knows that p iff S's belief that p is produced by a reliable belief-forming process. The account is externalist: justification depends on the reliability of the process, not on the agent's access to that reliability. The generality problem: how to individuate processes affects which processes count as reliable.

**Virtue Epistemology (Sosa, Greco, Zagzebski).** S knows that p iff S's belief that p is an intellectual achievement — it is true because of S's exercise of intellectual virtue. Zagzebski argues that Gettier problems are inescapable for any analysis that treats knowledge as true belief plus a separable positive epistemic condition; virtue epistemology escapes this by treating knowledge as a unified achievement rather than a conjunction of independent conditions.

**Knowledge-First (Williamson).** Knowledge is a primitive mental state that cannot be analyzed in terms of belief, truth, and a further condition. The JTB analysis is misguided from the start. The correct order of explanation goes from knowledge to justification, not vice versa: a belief is justified iff it is produced by a process that would typically produce knowledge.

## Sub-Chapter Contributions

**Unit 1: Classical Analysis.** Three chapters reconstruct the positive JTB account. Chapter 1 traces the analysis from Plato's Meno through the contemporary analytic tradition, establishing the three conditions and the philosophical case for each. Chapter 2 presents Gettier's 1963 paper in detail, covering all three original cases plus the stopped clock case (Russell's predecessor) and the sheep in the field (Chisholm/Gettier variant). Chapter 3 surveys the major post-Gettier responses: no false lemmas, the causal theory, defeasibility analyses.

**Unit 2: Types of Knowledge.** Distinguishes propositional knowledge (knowing that p) from ability knowledge (knowing how to do something, Ryle's distinction) and acquaintance knowledge (knowing a person, place, or thing directly). Debates whether "knowing how" is reducible to "knowing that" (Jason Stanley and Timothy Williamson argue it is; Mark Fantl demurs). Also treats the distinction between occurrent and dispositional belief and its significance for the analysis.

**Unit 3: Knowledge and Context.** Covers contextualism, the knowledge-first turn, and pragmatic encroachment. Contextualism (Cohen, DeRose, Lewis) holds that the truth conditions of "S knows that p" vary with the epistemic standards operative in the attributor's context. This accommodates sceptical pressure without conceding to the sceptic. Pragmatic encroachment (Hawthorne, Stanley, Fantl and McGrath) holds that practical stakes affect whether a true belief counts as knowledge.

## Key Philosophers and Arguments

**Edmund Gettier (1927–2019).** "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?" (*Analysis*, 1963) is the most influential three-page paper in the history of analytic philosophy. Gettier's three cases all share the same structure: S justifiably infers q from a false proposition p that S justifiably believes; as it happens, q is true, but not for the reason S believes. The paper does not propose a positive theory; it demolishes the received one.

**Case 1.** Smith has strong evidence that Jones will get the job (the boss said so) and that Jones has ten coins in his pocket. Smith infers: "the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket." Unknown to Smith, Smith himself will get the job — and Smith also has ten coins in his pocket. Smith has a justified true belief in q, but does not know q.

**Case 2.** Smith has strong evidence that Jones owns a Ford and infers "Jones owns a Ford or Brown is in Barcelona" — a disjunction where the second disjunct is chosen arbitrarily. In fact, Jones does not own a Ford, but Brown happens to be in Barcelona. Smith has a justified true belief in the disjunction but does not know it.

**Case 3.** Sheep in the field: S sees what looks exactly like a sheep in a field and justifiably believes there is a sheep in the field. The animal in S's visual field is in fact a large dog (or, in some versions, a rock). However, there is a sheep behind a bush in the same field. S has a justified true belief but does not know.

**Alvin Goldman (1938–).** His 1967 paper "A Causal Theory of Knowing" proposed that knowledge requires an appropriate causal connection between the fact known and the belief. His 1976 paper "Discrimination and Perceptual Knowledge" introduced the fake barns case (S drives through a region filled with barn facades and looks at the only real barn — does S know it is a barn?) and the relevant alternatives framework. His 1979 paper "What Is Justified Belief?" shifted the focus from knowledge to justification, proposing process reliabilism as the core of a positive epistemological theory.

**Robert Nozick (1938–2002).** *Philosophical Explanations* (1981) proposed the tracking account, deploying the subjunctive conditionals that became central to subsequent discussions. Nozick's "method" refinement — tracking is indexed to methods of belief formation — handles many counterexamples but adds complexity. His argument that closure fails for knowledge (because tracking does not transmit across entailment) generated one of the most contentious debates in post-Gettier epistemology.

**Ernest Sosa (1940–).** Developed virtue epistemology and the safety condition over several decades. *A Virtue Epistemology* (2007) distinguishes *animal knowledge* (belief that is accurate, adroit, and apt — true because of competence) from *reflective knowledge* (apt belief that the agent also reflectively endorses). Sosa's safety condition holds that a belief is safe iff it could not easily have been false: in nearby possible worlds, if the belief is held in the same way, it is true.

**Timothy Williamson (1955–).** *Knowledge and Its Limits* (2000) argues that knowledge is the most general factive stative attitude and that justification is to be explained in terms of knowledge, not vice versa. The "knowledge norm of assertion" (assert only what you know) and the "knowledge norm of action" (act only on what you know) have generated large literatures. Williamson's critique of the luminosity of mental states (there is no condition such that whenever one is in it, one knows one is in it) challenges both internalist accounts of knowledge and higher-order epistemic requirements.

**Linda Zagzebski (1946–).** "The Inescapability of Gettier Problems" (*Philosophical Quarterly*, 1994) argued that any analysis of knowledge as true belief plus a separable positive epistemic condition will face Gettier-type problems: one can always describe a case where the positive condition is met by luck. The solution is virtue epistemology: knowledge is understood as a unified achievement of intellectual virtue, not a conjunction.

## Essential Readings

1. **Gettier, Edmund L., "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?", *Analysis*, 23:6 (1963), pp. 121–123.** Three pages that changed everything. Essential reading. The argument is as simple as the effect was profound: read it before reading anything else in this unit.

2. **Nozick, Robert, *Philosophical Explanations*, Chapter 3: "Knowledge"** (1981, Harvard). The tracking account in full detail, including the method-relative formulation, the defense of the failure of closure, and the response to scepticism. The most systematic post-Gettier positive account before virtue epistemology.

3. **Williamson, Timothy, *Knowledge and Its Limits*, Chapters 1–4** (2000, Oxford). The knowledge-first manifesto. Chapters 1–2 argue that knowledge is a mental state irreducible to belief. Chapters 3–4 develop the luminosity argument. Difficult but essential for understanding the contemporary state of the field.

4. **Sosa, Ernest, *A Virtue Epistemology*, Chapters 1–4** (2007, Oxford). Introduces the animal/reflective knowledge distinction, the safety condition, and virtue epistemology's response to scepticism. More accessible than the earlier papers and more systematic than Zagzebski.

5. **Zagzebski, Linda, "The Inescapability of Gettier Problems," *Philosophical Quarterly*, 44:174 (1994), pp. 65–73.** The most important single paper arguing that the entire post-Gettier analysis project is on the wrong track. Short, clear, and philosophically decisive. Motivates the shift to virtue epistemology and to knowledge-first approaches.

## Open Questions and Live Debates

**Can the analysis project be salvaged?** Shope's 1983 survey of the first twenty years found no successful fourth-condition analysis. More recent proposals (safety, proper function) have fared better but face their own counterexamples. The general question — whether a reductive analysis of knowledge in terms of belief and non-epistemic conditions is possible — remains open.

**Does the knowledge-first turn succeed?** Williamson's critique of the analysis project is influential but contested. Critics (Jonathan Jenkins Ichikawa, John Hawthorne) argue that knowledge-first epistemology faces its own analogs of the problems it identifies in JTB analyses. The debate is partly methodological: is knowledge too basic to analyze, or is it simply difficult?

**What is the significance of the fake barns case?** The case — introduced by Goldman and developed by Dretske — remains a litmus test for different theories. Virtue epistemologists (Sosa, Greco) disagree about whether the barn-perceiver has animal knowledge. The case bears on whether safety conditions are sufficient for knowledge and on the contextualist claim that "knowledge" attributions shift with the relevance of alternatives.

**Does pragmatic encroachment collapse the epistemic/practical divide?** Hawthorne and Stanley argue that what one knows depends on one's practical situation (the "bank cases"). Traditionalists (Fantl and McGrath partially agree; Kornblith and DeRose resist) worry that collapsing the distinction between theoretical and practical rationality misrepresents the concept of knowledge. The debate connects to virtue ethics and to debates about the relationship between epistemology and action theory.

## Connections to Other Units

- **Unit 1 (Foundations):** The JTB analysis that Gettier targets was Plato's candidate in the Theaetetus. The history of that analysis provides essential context.
- **Unit 4 (Justification):** The reliability of belief-forming processes, central to process reliabilism, is a theory of justification as much as a theory of knowledge. Internalism vs. externalism debates are continuous with the debates here.
- **Unit 5 (Scepticism):** Nozick's tracking account is explicitly designed to respond to scepticism by denying epistemic closure. Contextualism emerges partly from the need to explain why sceptical arguments seem compelling even though ordinary knowledge claims seem warranted.
- **Unit 7 (Philosophy of Mind):** The knowledge-first approach requires that knowledge be a genuine mental state. The nature of belief and the relationship between belief and other propositional attitudes is examined in Unit 7.
- **Unit 8 (Formal Epistemology):** Probability and credence approaches to knowledge try to formalize what it means for a belief to be sufficiently well-supported. The relationship between binary belief (S believes p) and graded belief (S has credence n in p) affects the analysis of knowledge.
