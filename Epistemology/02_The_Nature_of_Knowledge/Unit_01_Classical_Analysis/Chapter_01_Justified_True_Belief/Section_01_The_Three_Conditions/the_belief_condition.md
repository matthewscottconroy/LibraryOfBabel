# The Belief Condition: Knowledge Requires Believing

The second condition in the traditional analysis of knowledge is the **belief condition**: S knows that P only if S believes that P. This condition seems obvious — how could you know something you don't believe? — but it has attracted significant philosophical discussion and has been challenged from several directions.

## The Case for the Belief Condition

The most direct argument: knowledge is a cognitive achievement; it must involve taking a stance toward the known proposition. If you have no attitude toward P — neither believing it, disbelieving it, nor suspending judgment — you're simply not in any epistemic relationship with P. Knowledge requires a positive cognitive endorsement.

Furthermore, knowledge is typically shown in assertion and action. If you know that the train leaves at noon, you'll assert this when asked and act accordingly (arrive at the station by noon). The knowledge-supporting behavior reflects the belief.

## The Challenge: Knowledge Without Belief?

Several cases have been used to challenge the belief condition:

**The nervous examinee**: A student who has thoroughly mastered the material but, through test anxiety, disclaims knowledge and says "I don't know" when asked. Intuitively, they do know — the competence is there. But do they believe?

**Unconscious knowledge**: We know countless things we never consciously entertain — facts we have learned and not forgotten but which are not currently active. Do we believe them? In what sense?

**Ryle's cases**: A skilled cyclist doesn't believe anything particular while cycling. Their knowledge (of how to cycle) doesn't seem to consist in beliefs.

These cases suggest that the belief condition is either obviously satisfied (the student does believe, though they deny it), or the concept needs refining (knowledge-how is different from knowledge-that), or there are forms of knowledge that don't involve belief.

## Doxastic States and Degrees of Belief

Contemporary philosophy distinguishes between different types of doxastic (belief-related) states:

- **Full belief** (or outright belief): A binary attitude of acceptance — you either believe P or you don't.
- **Degrees of belief** (credences): A continuous measure of how confident you are in P, from 0 (certain it's false) to 1 (certain it's true).

The traditional analysis uses full belief. But some philosophers argue that knowledge is better analyzed using credences: you know that P if you have a sufficiently high credence in P, formed in the right way. This allows for a more nuanced picture of epistemic states.

## The Dispositional View

One way to handle the "unconscious knowledge" challenge: beliefs are dispositions, not occurrences. To believe that water is H₂O is to be disposed to act on this information when relevant, to assert it when asked, to draw the right inferences. The disposition can be present without being currently activated.

On this view, the belief condition is satisfied by dispositional beliefs even when they are not currently conscious. The student who "doesn't know" during the test has the disposition — formed through study — even if anxiety temporarily suppresses its activation.

## The Significance for the Analysis

The belief condition, however we refine it, plays an important role in the analysis of knowledge. It ensures that knowledge is a *cognitive* achievement — something that the knower has attained, not just a fact about their environment. This is connected to the attributive role of knowledge claims: when we say "S knows that P," we're saying something about S's cognitive relationship to P, not just about P.

The belief condition, combined with the truth condition, gives us "true belief." What remains is to specify what makes true belief *knowledge* — the third condition (justification) and the post-Gettier search for more.
