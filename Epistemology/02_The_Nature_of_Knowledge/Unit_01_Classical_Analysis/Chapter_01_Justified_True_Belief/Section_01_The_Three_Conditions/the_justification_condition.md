# The Justification Condition: Epistemic Credentials for Knowledge

The **justification condition** is the most philosophically contested component of the traditional analysis: S knows that P only if S is justified in believing P. Adding justification to true belief is supposed to rule out lucky true beliefs — cases where someone believes something true but has no good reason for the belief.

## Why Justification Is Needed

The jury case (from Plato's *Theaetetus*) illustrates the need: a jury persuaded by rhetorical manipulation may end up with a true belief about who committed the crime, but their belief is not based on evidence — it's the product of a non-epistemic process. Intuitively, they don't *know* even though they truly believe.

The general point: true beliefs can be accidentally true — formed by processes that don't track truth. Superstition, wishful thinking, unfounded intuition, lucky guessing — these can produce true beliefs. What distinguishes knowledge from lucky true belief is that knowledge has the right *epistemic credentials*: the belief is formed or held for reasons that actually support its truth.

## What Is Justification?

This is where the analysis becomes contentious. "Justification" is a term of art, and different epistemologists mean different things by it.

**Deontological justification**: A belief is justified if the believer has fulfilled their epistemic duties — they have been epistemically responsible, has responded correctly to their evidence, hasn't been reckless or negligent in forming the belief. This is a normative conception focused on the believer's conduct.

**Evidentialist justification**: A belief is justified if it is supported by the believer's evidence. The evidence might be perceptual experience, testimony, memory, or prior beliefs. The stronger the evidence, the more justified the belief.

**Reliability-based justification**: A belief is justified if it is formed by a reliable process — a process that tends to produce true beliefs. Perception, properly functioning memory, and valid deductive reasoning are reliable processes. Wishful thinking and superstition are not. (See the unit on Externalism.)

## Internalism vs. Externalism About Justification

One of the deepest fault lines in the theory of justification:

**Internalists** hold that justification depends only on factors *accessible* to the believer from their own cognitive perspective. What justifies a belief is something the believer can, in principle, recognize — their evidence, their reasons, the coherence of their belief system.

**Externalists** hold that justification can depend on factors *outside* the believer's perspective — like whether the belief-forming process is actually reliable, whether the belief is causally connected to the fact that makes it true, or whether it was formed by a virtue. The believer need not know or be able to recognize that the belief is formed in a reliable way.

The New Evil Demon problem motivates internalism: if an evil demon were systematically deceiving you, your beliefs would be unjustified on the externalist view (they're unreliably formed) but would seem justified from your perspective (you have exactly the evidence and reasons you'd have without the demon). Internalists say that if your internal perspective is the same, your justification is the same.

## The Justification Condition and Gettier

The justification condition was supposed to rule out lucky true beliefs. Gettier showed that it doesn't — not because justification doesn't matter, but because justified true belief still isn't sufficient for knowledge. You can have a justified true belief that is Gettiered — accidentally true in a way that disrupts the connection between justification and truth.

The lesson: whatever justification requires, it needs to be *connected to the truth* in the right way. Justification that happens to coexist with truth isn't enough — the justification must be related to the truth in a way that explains why the belief is true. Getting the right belief for the right reasons, where "right reasons" connects to the actual fact — that's the post-Gettier challenge.
