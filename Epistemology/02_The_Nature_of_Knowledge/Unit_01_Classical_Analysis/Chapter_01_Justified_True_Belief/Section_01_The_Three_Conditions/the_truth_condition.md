# The Truth Condition: Why Knowledge Requires Truth

The simplest component of the traditional analysis of knowledge is the **truth condition**: S knows that P only if P is true. You cannot know something false. This seems obviously correct, but examining why it's correct and what it implies reveals important features of the concept of knowledge.

## Why Truth Is Required

The requirement seems obvious: if someone claims to know that the Earth is flat, and the Earth is not flat, we simply say they don't know — they believe something false. Knowledge, unlike mere belief, is factive: knowing that P entails P.

More precisely, knowledge is a **factive mental state**: it is a mental state whose possession entails the truth of its content. Other factive states include seeing, hearing, remembering (genuine memory of an event entails the event occurred), and regretting (you can only genuinely regret something that actually happened).

Contrast belief: belief is not factive. You can believe that the Earth is flat; that belief doesn't make it true. You can believe all sorts of false things, and people often do.

## The Normative Role of the Truth Condition

The truth condition does more than just define knowledge — it plays an important normative role. Knowledge attributions serve as endorsements: when you tell someone they *know* how to solve a problem, you're endorsing their competence. When you say someone doesn't know the answer, you're withholding that endorsement, perhaps indicating they should check further.

If knowledge didn't require truth, this normative role would collapse. Saying "S knows that P" would not guarantee P, and the knowledge attribution would lose much of its practical value as an endorsement.

## Knowing and Lucky Truth

One subtlety: the truth condition is necessary but not sufficient. True belief is not enough for knowledge — the truth must be connected to the belief in the right way. You can have a true belief formed by a lucky guess, wishful thinking, or unreliable reasoning. The truth condition is satisfied (the belief happens to be true), but we intuitively withhold the knowledge attribution.

This is the motivation for adding the justification condition and for the post-Gettier search for a fourth condition. The truth condition rules out cases where the belief is simply false. But there are problematic cases where the belief is true but true in the wrong way — cases where the truth condition is satisfied but something else is missing.

## The Infallibilist Temptation

A stronger claim than "knowledge requires truth" is **infallibilism**: if S knows that P, then it's impossible for P to be false — S's justification or grounds must guarantee truth, not merely probabilify it. This is a demanding standard. Most of our ordinary knowledge claims involve evidence that makes a claim very likely but doesn't guarantee it.

Descartes was an infallibilist: he required certainty for genuine knowledge. This led him to conclude that we know very little — only the cogito and whatever can be derived with certainty from it. Contemporary epistemologists mostly reject infallibilism in favor of **fallibilism**: knowledge requires truth, but the justification for the belief need not guarantee truth. The belief might have been false, but it happens to be true.

Fallibilism is more permissive and allows most of what we ordinarily call knowledge to count. The trade-off: fallibilism makes knowledge more widespread but also more easily disrupted — even a reliably formed true belief might not be knowledge if things had gone slightly differently (the problem of epistemic luck, central to Gettier cases and their successors).
