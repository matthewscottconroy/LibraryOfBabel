# Plato and the Original Account of Knowledge

The justified true belief (JTB) analysis of knowledge is often presented as if it were the received view before Gettier demolished it in 1963. But tracing the JTB analysis back to its historical sources reveals a more complicated story — and one that illuminates what the analysis is trying to achieve.

## The Theaetetus Revisited

As discussed in the historical section, Plato's *Theaetetus* considers and rejects three proposals for defining knowledge, the last of which is "true belief with an account" (logos). This is often cited as the ancient forerunner of JTB. But the fit is imperfect.

Plato's third proposal uses *logos* — which Plato himself notes is ambiguous between several interpretations. None of them quite matches what twentieth-century epistemologists mean by "justification." The three Platonic interpretations (ability to articulate, enumeration of parts, specification of difference) are all rejected by Socrates. The dialogue ends in aporia — without a positive account.

This means that Plato doesn't actually *endorse* JTB. He considers something in the vicinity and finds it wanting. The historical narrative that attributes JTB to Plato and the Gettier problem to its refutation is a simplification — but a useful one.

## The Twentieth-Century Formulation

The version of JTB that Gettier explicitly targets was formulated by philosophers like Chisholm, Ayer, and Roderick Firth in the mid-twentieth century. Their formulations vary:

**Chisholm**: S knows that P if and only if (1) P is true, (2) S accepts P, and (3) S has adequate evidence for P.

**Ayer**: S knows that P if and only if (1) P is true, (2) S is sure that P, (3) S has the right to be sure that P.

**Roderick Firth** and others offered similar accounts with variations in how the third condition was spelled out.

These formulations agree on the basic structure: knowledge = truth + something like belief + something like justification. The variations concern what exactly the second and third conditions require.

## Why JTB Was Attractive

The JTB analysis is attractive for several reasons:

**It captures something right**: Most cases of knowledge do involve true, justified beliefs. The analysis gets the paradigm cases right.

**It explains failures**: False beliefs fail the truth condition; true beliefs formed unreliably fail the justification condition. The analysis explains why beliefs that fall short in one of these ways don't count as knowledge.

**It grounds epistemic evaluation**: By requiring justification, the analysis connects knowledge to epistemic responsibility and proper reasoning.

**It is theoretically simple**: JTB has an elegant, three-part structure that is easy to understand and apply.

## Why JTB Was Vulnerable

The analysis is vulnerable precisely because of what it doesn't specify: the *relationship* between justification and truth. JTB requires that the belief be justified and that it be true, but it doesn't require that the justification be *connected* to the truth in the right way.

Gettier exploited this gap: he showed that you can have a justified belief, formed in a reasonable way, that happens to be true — but where the truth is accidental given the justification. In Gettier's cases, the justification supports the belief for reasons that have nothing to do with why the belief is true.

This shows that the third condition needs to be strengthened or supplemented — the justification must somehow be appropriate to the truth, not just accidentally accompanying it.

## The Legacy of the Historical Narrative

Even if the narrative that "Plato invented JTB and Gettier refuted it" is a simplification, the narrative is productive: it focuses attention on the right question. What distinguishes knowledge from true belief? Whatever the answer, it must explain both the paradigm cases of knowledge and the edge cases where we withhold the attribution. The JTB analysis, even though it fails, represents genuine philosophical progress — it identifies the relevant dimensions (truth, belief, justification) and shows us where the remaining work needs to be done.
