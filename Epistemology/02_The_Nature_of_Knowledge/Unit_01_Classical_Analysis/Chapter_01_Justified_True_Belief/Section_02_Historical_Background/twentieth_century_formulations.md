# Twentieth-Century Formulations of JTB

The justified true belief analysis of knowledge became the standard view in analytic epistemology during the mid-twentieth century. Understanding the specific formulations that were in play when Gettier published his paper helps clarify what he was targeting and why his counterexamples were so effective.

## The Landscape Before Gettier

By the late 1950s and early 1960s, analytic epistemology had converged on something like JTB as the standard analysis of propositional knowledge. The main variations concerned how to formulate the justification condition.

**Clarence Irving Lewis** (1946): Lewis required that a belief count as knowledge only if it is "terminating" in direct experience — ultimately grounded in immediate, incorrigible sensory experiences. His formulation is foundationalist and internalist.

**A.J. Ayer** (1956): In *The Problem of Knowledge*, Ayer proposed that knowledge is true belief accompanied by "the right to be sure." The right to be sure is characterized in terms of the reasons available to the knower — good reasons, appropriate circumstances.

**Roderick Chisholm** (1957): In *Perceiving: A Philosophical Study*, Chisholm offered a sophisticated internalist account requiring "adequate evidence." Evidence adequacy is assessed from the believer's first-person perspective.

**Bertrand Russell** had earlier (1912) discussed knowledge in terms that anticipate JTB without quite settling on it, emphasizing the role of acquaintance and description in the epistemic basis of beliefs.

## What the Formulations Share

Despite their differences, these formulations share the basic JTB structure:

1. **Truth**: The proposition believed must be true.
2. **Belief**: The subject must believe the proposition.
3. **Justification/Evidence/Right**: The belief must be supported by adequate reasons, evidence, or grounds that the subject has or possesses.

All these formulations treat justification as primarily the subject's internal epistemic situation — what reasons and evidence they have available. This is the internalist orientation that Gettier's cases leave intact while undermining the sufficiency claim.

## Gettier's Strategy

Gettier's approach was elegant: show that all three conditions can be satisfied while knowledge fails. His strategy:

1. Begin with a justified belief in a false proposition (call it Q). Since Q is false, this doesn't give knowledge.
2. Derive from Q a second proposition (P) that the believer is also justified in believing (justification is transmitted by valid deduction).
3. Arrange for P to be true — but for a reason unrelated to Q.

The result: a justified true belief that is not knowledge, because the belief is true for reasons that have nothing to do with the justification.

## Why the Formulations Were Vulnerable

The pre-Gettier formulations were vulnerable because they made an implicit assumption: if a belief is justified and true, the justification is *relevant* to the truth. Gettier's cases show this assumption is false. You can justify a belief through a chain of reasoning that leads, by accident, to a true conclusion — but the truth of the conclusion has nothing to do with the reasoning.

The cases showed that the three conditions are individually necessary but jointly insufficient. What's missing is something about the *connection* between justification and truth — something that ensures the justification is responsible for the truth, not merely co-present with it.

## Post-Gettier Directions

The responses to Gettier fall into several families, all of which accept the three conditions as necessary while proposing additional or modified conditions:

- **No-false-lemmas**: Rule out cases where the justification passes through a false belief.
- **Defeasibility**: Rule out cases where the justification would be defeated if the believer had all the relevant information.
- **Causal theories**: Require a direct causal connection between the fact and the belief.
- **Reliability theories**: Require that the belief be formed by a reliable process.
- **Safety and sensitivity**: Require appropriate modal relationships between the belief and the truth.

All of these represent refinements of the JTB analysis, motivated by the need to connect justification to truth in a way that the original formulations did not require.
