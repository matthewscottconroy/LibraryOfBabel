## Thought Experiments in the JTB Analysis

---

**The Tethered Statues**

**The scenario:** Plato, in the *Meno*, describes a collection of statues made by Daedalus, the mythological craftsman who gave his statues the power of movement. These statues are extraordinarily valuable—but only if tethered, for if left free they will simply walk away. Plato uses this as an analogy: true beliefs are like the statues. If you simply happen to hold a true belief, it may be correct right now, but it is unstable—you may be talked out of it, or circumstances may lead you to abandon it. Knowledge is true belief that has been "tethered" by an account: it is stable, reliable, and cannot easily be dislodged.

**The philosophical point:** The metaphor captures the intuition that there is an epistemically significant difference between someone who believes something true by luck or coincidence and someone who believes it through genuine understanding. A person who happens to give correct directions to Larissa without ever having been there has true belief, but it could equally have been false. A person who gives correct directions from memory of actually having traveled the road has knowledge. The tethering metaphor motivates the justification condition: what converts true belief into knowledge is something that anchors the belief to the truth in a non-accidental way. What exactly this "tethering" amounts to—whether it is a causal connection, a reliably-formed process, the possession of adequate evidence, or something else—is precisely the question that subsequent epistemology has tried to answer.

**The Blind Lottery Winner**

**The scenario:** Consider a person who knows she has bought a lottery ticket. Before the draw, she has no relevant evidence about the outcome beyond knowing that her ticket has a 1-in-a-million chance of winning. She says: "I believe I will win." Her belief, let us suppose, turns out to be true—she does win. Was her pre-draw belief a justified true belief that constituted knowledge?

**The philosophical point:** Most epistemologists say no, and the lottery case thus illuminates the justification condition. The person's belief was true and she believed it, but the evidence available to her (a 1-in-a-million chance) was clearly insufficient to justify the belief. The case reveals that the justification condition is not just a matter of whether the belief happens to be true but whether the epistemic grounds available to the believer are adequate to the claim being made. This connects the analysis to debates about the threshold of justification, the relationship between probability and knowledge (Williamson argues that lottery beliefs cannot constitute knowledge even if true), and the role of stakes in determining what counts as adequate justification.

**The Map in the Dark**

**The scenario:** A hiker is lost in the forest and picks up a map from the ground. The map is not labeled, and she has no idea whether it represents the forest she is in or some other territory entirely. She navigates by the map and happens to reach her destination safely—the map, as it turns out, was an accurate map of the very forest she was in. Did she know where she was going?

**The philosophical point:** The hiker has a true belief (formed from accurate information), she believes it, but her belief lacks the right kind of justification: she had no reason to think the map was an accurate representation of her environment. The case illustrates that the justification condition must require not merely that the evidence used is in fact accurate but that the believer is in a position to recognize it as adequate evidence. This motivates a distinction between reliable processes (which a reliabilist might care about) and accessible justifications (which an internalist would insist on). The case also previews Gettier problems: the hiker's success is partly a matter of luck, and her true belief does not seem to constitute knowledge.

**Degrees of Knowledge: The Expert Witness**

**The scenario:** Three people form the belief that the defendant was at the scene of the crime. The first is an eyewitness who saw him there and recognized him clearly. The second is a friend who inferred his presence from circumstantial evidence—the defendant's car outside, a witness who saw someone of similar build. The third has heard the rumor from a colleague and has no independent assessment of its reliability. All three believe the same proposition, and it is true.

**The philosophical point:** This scenario illustrates Locke's interest in degrees of knowledge and the gradations of justification. It shows that "justification" is not a binary property but admits of degrees, and that these degrees matter for epistemology even if we accept a binary distinction between knowledge and non-knowledge. The eyewitness seems to know; the inferential believer has a justified belief that may or may not rise to knowledge depending on the adequacy of the inference; the rumor-holder has a belief with insufficient justification regardless of its truth. The scenario raises the question of whether there is a sharp threshold at which justified true belief becomes knowledge, or whether knowledge itself admits of degrees—a question that bears on the relationship between the JTB analysis and theories of epistemic rationality.
