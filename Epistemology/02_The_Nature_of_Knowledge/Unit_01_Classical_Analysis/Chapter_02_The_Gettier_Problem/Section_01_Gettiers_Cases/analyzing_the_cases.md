# Analyzing Gettier's Cases: What Goes Wrong?

After reading Gettier's cases, the next step is to ask: exactly what goes wrong? The cases produce the judgment "that's not knowledge," but *why* is it not knowledge? Different diagnoses lead to different repair strategies.

## Diagnosis 1: The False Lemma

In both Gettier cases, Smith infers his true belief from a false belief. He believes Jones will get the job (false), or that Jones owns a Ford (false), and derives his true belief from this false premise.

This suggests a diagnosis: knowledge requires that the belief not be derived from a false intermediate belief — a false *lemma* in the reasoning. If you arrive at a true belief by first believing something false and inferring from it, you don't know.

This diagnosis has initial appeal. It explains what goes wrong: the inference chain passes through a false step, contaminating the conclusion with that falsehood.

The response strategy: add a "no-false-lemma" condition to JTB. The resulting analysis: S knows that P iff S has a true, justified belief in P, and S's justification for P does not depend on any false lemma.

Problems with this diagnosis: cases can be constructed in which the false lemma can be removed without affecting the justification. And there are Gettier-style cases that don't seem to involve false lemmas at all (the barn facade case, coming up).

## Diagnosis 2: The Belief Could Easily Have Been False

Another diagnosis: what's wrong with Gettier cases is that the belief is only *accidentally* true. Had things been slightly different — had the company given the job to someone else, or had Brown been in Rome rather than Barcelona — the belief would have been false. The truth is a matter of luck.

This suggests that knowledge requires something stronger: not just that the belief is true, but that it *couldn't easily have been false* — it's safe, or it tracks the truth in the right way.

Response strategies include **safety** (the belief couldn't easily have been false), **sensitivity** (if P were false, S wouldn't believe P), and **tracking theories** more generally.

## Diagnosis 3: The Justification Isn't Connected to the Truth

A third diagnosis: in Gettier cases, the justification supports the belief, and the belief is true, but the justification doesn't explain why the belief is true. The truth and the justification are, in a sense, accidentally cooccurrent.

Consider Gettier's second case: Smith's justification (his evidence about Jones and the Ford) is good evidence for the disjunction, but the disjunction's truth has nothing to do with Jones's car — it's true because Brown happens to be in Barcelona. The justification and the truth are running on entirely separate tracks.

This diagnosis suggests that knowledge requires the justification to be *connected* to the truth — to explain, in some sense, why the belief is true. The various reliabilist, causal, and tracking approaches can all be seen as attempts to specify what this connection amounts to.

## Diagnosis 4: The Causal Chain

Alvin Goldman's causal theory (discussed separately) provides a specific version of the connection requirement: the subject's belief must be appropriately causally connected to the fact that makes it true. In Gettier cases, the belief is causally connected to something other than what makes it true.

In Case I: Smith's belief that the man with ten coins will get the job is causally connected to his counting Jones's coins and the president's statement. But the truth of the belief is explained by Smith's own coins — something causally unrelated to the evidence.

## The Proliferation of Gettier Cases

An important feature of the post-Gettier literature is that new Gettier-style cases proliferated. For every proposed solution, new cases were constructed that the solution failed to handle. This led to increasing sophistication in the cases and eventually to the recognition that no simple fourth condition could solve the problem — something more fundamental needed to change.

The barn facade case (coming up) is particularly important because it shows that Gettier problems don't require false intermediate beliefs at all. The cases illuminate not just a flaw in JTB but something deep about the relationship between knowledge and luck.
