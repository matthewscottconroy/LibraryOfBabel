# The Original Gettier Paper: Three Pages That Changed Philosophy

Edmund Gettier's 1963 paper "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?" is three pages long. It contains two counterexamples to the justified true belief analysis of knowledge. It is one of the most influential philosophy papers ever published.

## The Paper's Context

Gettier was a young philosophy professor at Wayne State University. He had been warned by a senior colleague that he needed to publish or his career would be in jeopardy. He reportedly did not think much of the paper but submitted it anyway. Its reception was electric: the counterexamples were immediately recognized as devastating to the JTB analysis, and the paper sparked decades of debate about how to repair the analysis.

The paper's economy is remarkable. Two paragraphs establish the target — the JTB analysis as formulated by Chisholm and Ayer — and two brief cases refute it. No philosophical prolixity, no lengthy preamble.

## Case I: Smith and Jones's Job

Smith and Jones have applied for the same job. Smith has strong evidence — from what the company president told him — that Jones will get the job. He also knows (having counted Jones's coins) that Jones has ten coins in his pocket. Smith justifiably believes: "Jones will get the job, and the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket."

Now suppose the following: Jones does not get the job; Smith does. Moreover, Smith himself has ten coins in his pocket, though he doesn't know this.

Smith's belief "The man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket" is:
- **True**: Smith gets the job, and Smith has ten coins.
- **Believed by Smith**: He inferred it from his belief about Jones.
- **Justified**: Based on good evidence (the president's statement, the coin count).

Yet Smith doesn't know that the man who will get the job has ten coins. He believes it for the wrong reasons: he thinks it's because of Jones, but it's actually because of him.

## Case II: Smith Believes Jones Owns a Ford

Smith has strong evidence that Jones owns a Ford — he has seen Jones driving a Ford for years, Jones has always owned Fords, and Jones just offered Smith a ride in a Ford. Smith concludes a disjunction: "Either Jones owns a Ford, or Brown is in Barcelona." (Smith doesn't know where Brown is; he just constructed the disjunction.)

Now suppose Jones in fact doesn't own a Ford — he's been driving a rental. But by an extraordinary coincidence, Brown *is* in Barcelona.

Smith's belief "Either Jones owns a Ford, or Brown is in Barcelona" is:
- **True**: Brown is in Barcelona.
- **Believed by Smith**: He inferred the disjunction from his belief about Jones.
- **Justified**: His belief about Jones was well-supported.

Yet Smith doesn't know that Brown is in Barcelona or Jones owns a Ford. He has a lucky true belief.

## What the Cases Establish

Both cases share a common structure:
1. Smith justifiably believes Q (a false proposition).
2. Smith infers P from Q, where P is also justified (justification is transmitted by deduction from justified belief).
3. P is true, but for a reason unrelated to Q.
4. Smith doesn't know P.

The cases establish that JTB is not sufficient for knowledge: there can be justified true beliefs that are not knowledge because the truth is accidental given the justification.

## The Philosophical Significance

The cases are significant for several reasons:

**They show the necessity of a fourth condition**: Whatever knowledge is beyond JTB, something additional or different is needed to handle Gettier cases.

**They reveal the structure of epistemic luck**: Gettier cases are cases of *epistemic luck* — cases where the belief is true but where it could easily have been false. The luck is what disrupts knowledge.

**They reveal the limits of the JTB analysis**: The JTB analysis was supposed to capture what knowledge requires. Gettier's cases show that the analysis is incomplete. They don't tell us what's missing — that's the work of the subsequent literature.

**They demonstrate the method of counterexample**: Gettier's approach — finding a case where the conditions are satisfied but the analysandum (knowledge) is not — is the standard form of philosophical refutation. The paper is a perfect example of the method in action.
