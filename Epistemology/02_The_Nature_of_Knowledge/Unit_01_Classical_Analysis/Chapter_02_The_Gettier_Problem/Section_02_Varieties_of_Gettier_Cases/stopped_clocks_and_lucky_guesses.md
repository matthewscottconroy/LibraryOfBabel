# Stopped Clocks, Lucky Guesses, and the Range of Gettier-Style Cases

The phenomenon Gettier identified — justified true belief that is not knowledge because the truth is accidental — appears in many forms. Surveying the variety of cases helps clarify what's essential to the problem and what successful analyses must handle.

## The Stopped Clock

You look at a clock on the wall. It shows 3:47. You form the belief: "It is 3:47." Your belief is true — it is indeed 3:47. Your evidence is good: the clock has been reliable for years, the lighting is good, your vision is fine.

But the clock stopped exactly twelve hours ago. It has been showing 3:47 since 3:47 last night.

You don't know that it is 3:47. You have a justified true belief formed through a normally reliable process. But the reliability of your belief formation has nothing to do with the truth of the belief in this instance. You got lucky: the clock stopped showing the right time, and you happened to look at it at the right moment.

This case is instructive because the usual source of reliability — the clock — has ceased to be reliable. Yet from your perspective, nothing is amiss. Your justification is the same as it would be if the clock were working. The Gettier problem arises even without false lemmas and without any malicious manipulation of your environment.

## The Reliable Thermometer

A patient is in the hospital. A nurse takes their temperature with a thermometer. The thermometer reads 101.5°F. The nurse reports: "The patient has a fever." 

Unknown to the nurse, the thermometer is broken — it always reads 101.5°F regardless of the actual temperature. However, the patient does in fact have a 101.5°F fever, as a different, working thermometer would confirm.

The nurse's belief is true and based on her usual evidence (thermometer reading). But she doesn't know that the patient has a fever. Her normally reliable instrument happens to be giving the correct reading for the wrong reason.

## The Reliable Process, Unreliable Instance

A broader pattern: a normally reliable cognitive process or instrument can malfunction in a particular case while still producing a true belief. In such cases:
- The process is generally reliable (supports justified belief).
- The process is not reliable in this instance.
- The belief happens to be true anyway.

This pattern generates Gettier cases. It shows that reliability must be understood not just generally but in the specific circumstances of the belief's formation.

## Pure Lucky Guesses

What about cases with no reliability at all? Consider: you know nothing about the capital of Burkina Faso. You guess "Ouagadougou." You're right.

This is not a Gettier case in the strict sense — you lack justification, so the JTB conditions aren't all satisfied. But it illustrates the same underlying problem: true belief without the right epistemic connection to the truth. The guess case shows why truth alone isn't enough for knowledge. Gettier cases show why justified true belief isn't enough either.

## The Hierarchy of Luck

These cases reveal a hierarchy of epistemic luck:

**Bare luck**: No justification, no reliability — just a lucky guess. Everyone agrees this isn't knowledge.

**Gettier luck**: Justified belief, apparently reliable process, but the truth is accidentally true given the justification. The Gettier cases. Most agree this isn't knowledge.

**Environmental luck**: Reliable process, but in an environment where that process usually fails (barn façade). Contested whether this is knowledge.

**Methodological luck**: Your method is reliable, and the conditions are right, but you could have used a different (unreliable) method and gotten the same belief. More contested still.

## Why the Cases Matter

These cases collectively show that the epistemologically significant question is not just "does the subject have good evidence?" but "is the subject's belief appropriately connected to the truth?" The connection must be more than accidental. Identifying what makes the connection appropriate — causal history, reliability in circumstances, modal properties, virtue-theoretic status — is the central challenge of post-Gettier epistemology.
