# The Fallout for Analysis: Can Knowledge Be Analyzed?

One response to Gettier's cases is to try to patch the analysis — add a fourth condition. Another, more radical response is to question whether the whole project of analyzing knowledge into more basic conditions is well-conceived. The history of the post-Gettier literature suggests that the patching strategy faces deep difficulties. This has led some philosophers to question the analysis itself.

## The Fourth-Condition Industry

The decades following Gettier saw an explosion of proposed fourth conditions. Each proposed condition was met with counterexamples, which motivated refinements, which were met with new counterexamples. This cycle has continued for sixty years.

Some notable proposals and their problems:

**No-false-lemmas**: Rules out Gettier's original cases but fails for barn façade cases and others where no false lemma is involved.

**Defeasibility conditions**: The belief is undefeated — there is no true proposition that, if added to the subject's evidence, would defeat the justification. Faces problems with cases where the defeating information is misleading or irrelevant.

**Causal conditions**: The belief must be appropriately causally connected to the fact. Faces problems with mathematical and modal beliefs, and with cases of deviant causal chains.

**Tracking conditions**: If P were false, S wouldn't believe P (sensitivity). If P is true, S would believe P (adherence). Faces problems with cases where the subject would use a different reliable method in the counterfactual.

**Safety conditions**: The belief couldn't easily have been false. Faces problems with specifying "easily" and with cases where safety seems too demanding.

## The Paradox of Analysis

One reason the analysis of knowledge is so difficult is the *paradox of analysis*: a successful analysis of a concept X must be informative (it must say something non-trivial about X) without being circular (it can't use X in its own analysis). Finding a genuinely informative, non-circular, and extensionally adequate analysis of knowledge has proved extraordinarily difficult.

The Gettier problem might be a symptom of this deeper difficulty. Perhaps the concept of knowledge has complex, context-sensitive, or holistic features that resist capture by any finite list of necessary and sufficient conditions.

## The Knowledge-First Response

Timothy Williamson's *Knowledge and Its Limits* (2000) proposed a radical alternative: abandon the project of analyzing knowledge. Instead, take knowledge as *primitive* — as the basic epistemic concept from which other concepts (justification, evidence, certainty) should be explained.

On Williamson's view:
- Justification is explained in terms of knowledge: your evidence just *is* what you know.
- The traditional analysis reversal: instead of "knowledge = JTB + X," we have "JTB = knowledge, roughly speaking, modulo luck."

This "knowledge-first" approach has attracted significant attention and controversy. It has the virtue of explaining why the JTB analysis fails (knowledge is not built from more basic components) while preserving the normative importance of knowledge.

## The Ordinary Language Response

Another response: perhaps the proliferation of counterexamples reveals that "knowledge" is a vague, context-sensitive ordinary language term, not a technical concept that admits of precise analysis. We should study how the word is actually used — including in the context of philosophical thought experiments — rather than assuming there is a precise analysis to be found.

This connects to the contextualist view: "knows" expresses a context-sensitive relation, and what counts as knowledge depends on the standards in play. On this view, Gettier cases aren't counterexamples to any precise analysis — they're cases where our intuitions about the term vary with context.

## What We Learned

Even if the project of analyzing knowledge has failed in its strict ambitions, the post-Gettier literature has produced genuine philosophical progress:

- A precise taxonomy of kinds of epistemic luck and their relationship to knowledge.
- A clearer understanding of the role of reliability, tracking, and cognitive virtues in knowledge.
- A sophisticated debate between internalist and externalist accounts of justification and knowledge.
- New appreciation for the complexity of the ordinary concept of knowledge and the difficulty of capturing it in precise conditions.

The Gettier problem has been one of epistemology's most productive failures.
