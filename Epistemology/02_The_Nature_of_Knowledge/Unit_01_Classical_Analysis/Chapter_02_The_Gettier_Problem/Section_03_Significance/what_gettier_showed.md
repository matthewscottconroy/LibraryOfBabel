# What Gettier Really Showed

Gettier's paper is often described as refuting the JTB analysis of knowledge. This is correct, but it understates what the cases show. A closer look reveals that Gettier's cases illuminate something deep about the structure of epistemic achievement — and that the problem they pose cannot be solved by adding a simple fourth condition.

## The Surface Lesson: JTB Is Not Sufficient

At the surface level, the lesson is clear: justified true belief is not sufficient for knowledge. The three traditional conditions can be satisfied while knowledge fails.

But this is a refutation of the *sufficiency* claim, not the conditions themselves. The conditions may still all be *necessary*:
- Knowledge still requires truth (you can't know something false).
- Knowledge still requires belief (you can't know something you don't believe).
- Knowledge still requires justification (lucky true beliefs that you have no reason for aren't knowledge).

The question is what to add — or whether a more fundamental revision is needed.

## The Deeper Lesson: The Accidentality Problem

The deeper lesson is about *epistemic luck* — specifically, about what kind of luck is incompatible with knowledge.

In Gettier cases, there are two separate ways in which the belief is true: the way the subject takes it to be true (related to their evidence and reasoning), and the way it is actually true (unrelated to their evidence). These two come apart. The subject's justification supports the belief for reason R, but the belief is true for reason Q ≠ R.

This reveals that knowledge requires not just that the belief is justified and true, but that the justification and the truth are *connected* — that the justification is a guide to the truth, not merely accompanying it. What the analysis needs is some condition that ensures this connection.

## The Proliferation of Counterexamples

A striking feature of the post-Gettier literature is the proliferation of counterexamples, not just to JTB, but to every proposed repair. For almost every fourth condition proposed, philosophers found cases that satisfied all four conditions while knowledge still seemed absent.

This suggested that the problem might not be fixable by adding conditions at all. Perhaps the analysis of knowledge into more basic conditions is fundamentally misguided — perhaps knowledge is unanalyzable.

This was one motivation for Timothy Williamson's "knowledge-first" approach: rather than trying to analyze knowledge into more basic components, take knowledge as the primitive from which other concepts are explained. We'll discuss this in the final unit.

## The Epistemic Luck Taxonomy

The Gettier problem has generated a sophisticated taxonomy of kinds of epistemic luck:

**Veritic luck**: The belief is accidentally true — the believer's situation could easily have been such that the belief was false. Gettier cases are cases of veritic luck.

**Reflective luck**: The believer is not in a position to recognize whether their belief-forming process is reliable (as in the barn façade case).

**Cognitive luck**: The believer happens to form a belief by using their cognitive faculties correctly, though in slightly different circumstances they might not have.

Not all epistemic luck is incompatible with knowledge. You might luckily come to investigate a question that you otherwise wouldn't have, and from that investigation gain genuine knowledge. The luck that blocks knowledge is specifically veritic luck — the kind where the truth of the belief is itself lucky.

## The Return to Virtue Epistemology

Another response to the depth of the Gettier problem is to reconceptualize what knowledge requires at a more fundamental level. Virtue epistemologists argue that what's missing from JTB is the right kind of *credit* — the belief must be true because of the believer's cognitive competence, not merely accidentally.

In Gettier cases, the belief is true, but the truth is not *to the credit of* the believer's cognitive abilities. Ernest Sosa's formulation: for a belief to constitute knowledge, it must be *apt* — accurate (true) because *adroit* (formed by exercise of cognitive ability). In Gettier cases, the accuracy and the adroitness come apart.

This virtue-theoretic perspective shifts the analysis from conditions on the belief to conditions on the believer — a significant reorientation of epistemological inquiry.
