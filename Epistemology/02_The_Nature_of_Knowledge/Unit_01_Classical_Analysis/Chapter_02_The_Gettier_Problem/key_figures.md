## Edmund Gettier (born 1927)

Gettier's two original counterexamples established a template for Gettier-type cases that has been replicated and varied hundreds of times: a person forms a justified belief by reasoning from a justified but false premise, and the belief happens to be true for reasons independent of that reasoning. Case I: Smith justifiedly but falsely believes Jones will get the job and has ten coins in his pocket. Smith infers: "the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket." Smith gets the job and, unknown to him, he also has ten coins in his pocket. The conclusion is true, the inference valid, the premise justified—yet Smith does not know the conclusion. Case II follows an analogous structure. These brief cases sufficed to overturn a two-millennia tradition.

## Alvin Goldman (born 1938)

Goldman's response to Gettier—the causal theory of knowing—proposed that a belief constitutes knowledge only if it is appropriately causally connected to the fact believed. This explains why Gettier cases fail: in Smith's case, the truth of the conclusion is causally independent of Smith's justified inference. Goldman later developed the "fake barns" case to show that causal connections can exist without knowledge: Henry, driving through a region filled with barn facades, happens to look at the one genuine barn and forms a true belief that it is a barn. The causal connection is present—a real barn causes his belief—but Henry does not know, because he is in an environment riddled with would-be defeaters he cannot detect.

## Roderick Chisholm (1916–1999)

Chisholm catalogued a wide range of Gettier-type cases in *Theory of Knowledge* and proposed various fourth-condition analyses to block them. His work demonstrated the difficulty of specifying the additional condition: each proposal was subject to new counterexamples, generating what Shope called the "Gettier industry." Chisholm's careful taxonomy of epistemic states and his analysis of epistemic defeat—the ways in which additional information can undermine justification—remain important tools for engaging the problem, even if his particular proposed solutions did not achieve consensus.

## Gilbert Harman (1938–2021)

Harman's "no false lemmas" approach proposed that knowledge fails whenever the inference to the justified true belief passes through a false intermediate step. This explains some Gettier cases neatly: Smith's false belief about Jones was the lemma through which he arrived at his (accidently true) conclusion. However, Harman recognized that the approach faces difficulties with "misleading defeater" cases—situations where knowledge seems to persist even though the believer's justificatory chain would have been disrupted by readily available (but not encountered) defeaters. His work on these cases refined the defeasibility approach and influenced subsequent discussions of knowledge and defeat.

## Keith Lehrer (1936–2022) and Thomas Paxson Jr.

Lehrer and Paxson's collaborative paper "Knowledge: Undefeated Justified True Belief" (1969) developed the most systematic early defeasibility analysis of the Gettier problem. They distinguished "basic" knowledge (where justification is not defeated by any true proposition) from "non-basic" knowledge and proposed that for knowledge to be present, the justification must survive the addition of all true propositions to the believer's evidence. The central challenge they faced—and which their paper helped identify—is distinguishing "genuine" defeaters (which genuinely undermine justification) from "misleading" defeaters (which seem to undermine but actually mislead), a problem that has generated substantial literature.

## Linda Zagzebski (born 1946)

Zagzebski's 1994 paper "The Inescapability of Gettier Problems" provided a general proof that Gettier-type counterexamples will arise for *any* analysis of knowledge as true belief plus some additional (fallible) condition. The proof proceeds by showing that any analysis of the form "S knows p iff p is true, S believes p, and condition C" can be subverted by constructing a case in which C is met, p is believed, but p's truth is accidentally disconnected from the satisfaction of C. This result motivated her subsequent development of virtue epistemology as the only way to avoid the problem: if knowledge is constituted by an intellectual virtue (rather than an external condition added to true belief), the accidental luck characteristic of Gettier cases is intrinsically excluded.

## Timothy Williamson (born 1955)

Williamson's response to the Gettier problem takes a radically different direction: instead of seeking a fourth condition to add to the JTB analysis, he argues in *Knowledge and Its Limits* (2000) that the entire project of analyzing knowledge in terms of more basic conditions is misconceived. Knowledge is itself a primitive mental state, not analyzable as belief plus further conditions. This "knowledge-first" approach explains why the Gettier industry has failed to produce a satisfactory analysis—it is attempting to reduce what is in fact a fundamental epistemic category. Gettier cases show not that the JTB analysis needs a fourth condition but that knowledge is not constituted by justified true belief at all.
