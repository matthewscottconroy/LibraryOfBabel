## The Core Gettier Cases and Their Descendants

---

**Gettier's Original Case I: The Man with Ten Coins**

**The scenario:** Smith has strong evidence that Jones will get a certain job, and also knows that Jones has ten coins in his pocket (he has counted them). He infers and believes: "The man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket." Unknown to Smith, it is actually Smith himself who will get the job—and, by coincidence, Smith also has ten coins in his pocket (he did not know this). The proposition Smith believes is true, his belief is justified, and he genuinely believes it—but the truth of the proposition is independent of and accidental relative to the grounds on which he formed the belief.

**The philosophical point:** Smith's belief meets all three conditions of the JTB analysis: it is true, believed, and justified by good evidence. Yet he clearly does not know who will get the job with ten coins in their pocket, because he was relying on a false belief about Jones. The case shows that justification and truth can coincide accidentally—the truth of the conclusion is "screened off" from the justification by the falsity of the intermediate premise. This reveals a gap in the JTB analysis: it does not require that the justification and the truth be appropriately connected.

---

**Gettier's Original Case II: Jones Driving a Ford**

**The scenario:** Smith has strong evidence that Jones owns a Ford: he has seen Jones driving it, Jones has always owned a Ford, and so on. Smith infers (from the disjunction introduction rule) that either "Jones owns a Ford, or Brown is in Barcelona"—where Smith has no evidence whatsoever about Brown's location. Unknown to Smith, Jones does not currently own a Ford (the car he was seen in was a rental). But, by sheer coincidence, Brown is in Barcelona. The disjunctive belief is true, believed, and justified by the (false) evidence about Jones.

**The philosophical point:** This case is more unsettling than Case I because the true disjunct (Brown is in Barcelona) is entirely epistemically irrelevant to Smith's justification. The disjunction is justified by the false disjunct (Jones owns a Ford), yet the disjunction's truth is secured by the other disjunct. Smith could not be said to know where Brown is, or even to know the disjunction, despite having a justified true belief in it. The case illustrates that logical inference from justified beliefs does not always preserve knowledge—justificatory closure and knowledge closure can come apart in Gettier situations.

**The Fake Barns**

**The scenario:** Henry is driving through a region of the country where, unbeknown to him, there are numerous papier-mâché barn facades erected along the roadside—each indistinguishable from a real barn at normal viewing distance. Henry happens to look at the one genuine barn in the region and forms the belief: "That is a barn." His belief is true (it is a genuine barn), he believes it, and his perceptual grounds are in normal circumstances perfectly adequate.

**The philosophical point:** Goldman's fake barns case introduced a new type of Gettier problem: one where the false premises and inferential structure of the original cases are absent, but the belief is still intuitively not knowledge. Henry's perceptual process is functioning perfectly, the belief is true, and ordinary perceptual justification is present—yet he does not know, because he is in an environment where his belief-forming process is unreliable (given the prevalence of fakes). The case shows that knowledge requires not merely that the belief be formed reliably in this instance, but that the belief-forming process is reliably discriminating in the *actual* environment. This motivates the relevant alternatives framework: knowledge requires ruling out the relevant alternative possibilities (nearby fake barns), not just all conceivable alternatives.

**The Sheep in the Field**

**The scenario:** A man is walking in the countryside and looks into a field, seeing what he takes to be a sheep. He forms the belief "there is a sheep in the field." Unknown to him, what he actually sees is a large dog cleverly resembling a sheep. However, there is a real sheep in the field—just hidden behind a hedge, out of his sight. His belief (there is a sheep in the field) is true, justified by what appears to be clear perceptual evidence, and sincerely held.

**The philosophical point:** This case by Roderick Chisholm (drawing on a version by Carl Ginet) is particularly instructive because the object that justifies the belief (the dog) and the object that makes the belief true (the hidden sheep) are entirely different. The man's perceptual justification is not a response to the relevant fact—it is a response to an irrelevant simulacrum. This highlights that for knowledge, the justification must be appropriately connected to the *truth-maker* of the belief, not merely to something that causally induces the relevant belief content. The case also illustrates how knowledge can fail even when the environment is not pervasively deceptive—a single deceiving object suffices if the believer forms her belief in response to that object rather than the truth-making one.

**The Stopped Clock**

**The scenario:** A person glances at a wall clock to check the time. The clock displays 3:47 PM, which is in fact the current time. However, the clock stopped exactly twelve hours earlier; it has been displaying 3:47 since 3:47 AM, and she has simply happened to consult it at the moment when its stopped display coincidentally matches the current time.

**The philosophical point:** The stopped clock case was identified by Russell as a precursor to Gettier-type thinking and was later formalized in the Gettier literature. It is distinctive because the belief-forming process (consulting a clock) is normally highly reliable, and the agent has no positive evidence against the reliability of this particular token. The case shows that even a normally reliable process can fail to produce knowledge when, in the specific instance, the connection between process and truth is broken. This motivates reliability conditions that are sensitive not just to the type of process but to its correct functioning in the particular instance. Nozick's sensitivity condition and Sosa's safety condition both handle this case by requiring that the belief not only be formed by a reliable process but that the belief would not have been formed in the same way if the proposition were false.
