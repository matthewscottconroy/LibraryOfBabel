# Defeasibility Approaches to the Gettier Problem

Defeasibility theories add a condition to JTB that addresses the role of *defeating* information: true information that, if the subject possessed it, would undermine their justification. The basic idea is that knowledge requires not just having good reasons, but having reasons that would survive contact with all the relevant truth.

## The Basic Defeasibility Condition

Keith Lehrer and Thomas Paxson (1969) introduced the defeasibility approach. Their condition: S knows that P only if there is no true statement Q such that, if S were to learn that Q, S's justification for P would be defeated (that is, S would no longer be justified in believing P).

In Gettier's Case I: There is a true statement — "it is Smith, not Jones, who will get the job" — that, if learned, would defeat Smith's justification for his belief. So Smith doesn't know.

In the barn façade case: There is a true statement — "you are in Fake Barn County" — that, if learned, would defeat Henry's justification. Henry doesn't know.

The defeasibility approach handles both original Gettier cases and environment-based cases. This is an advance over the no-false-lemmas approach.

## The Problem of Misleading Defeaters

Defeasibility theories face a well-known problem: **misleading defeaters**. Some true statements would, if learned, undermine justification — but only because they are misleading. They point in the wrong direction; the original justification was actually fine.

Example (due to Marshall Swain): Mrs. Grabit's Nephew Case. Tom Grabit steals a book from the library; you see him do it. You know Tom Grabit stole the book. Unknown to you, Tom's mother (Mrs. Grabit) has told the police that Tom was not in the library that day and that it was Tom's identical twin brother John who did it — a complete lie. Mrs. Grabit is mendacious.

Now, if you were to learn that Mrs. Grabit testified that Tom's twin committed the theft, your justification would (at least temporarily) be defeated — this is strong testimony against Tom's presence. Yet intuitively you do know that Tom stole the book. Mrs. Grabit's testimony is false and irrelevant; it's a misleading defeater.

The defeasibility approach incorrectly predicts that you don't know, because there is a true proposition (that Mrs. Grabit so testified) that would defeat your justification.

## Refined Defeasibility: The Epistemic Luck Exclusion

Various refinements attempt to handle the misleading defeater problem. Lehrer later distinguished between "indefeasible" justification (not defeated by any true proposition) and "undefeated" justification (not defeated by the combination of true propositions in the subject's total epistemic situation).

Another refinement: distinguish between **genuinely defeating** information (information that reveals a genuine flaw in the subject's epistemic position) and **merely misleading** information (information that points in the wrong direction and would be corrected by further investigation). Knowledge requires justification undefeated by the former but not necessarily the latter.

Making this distinction precise without circularity is the challenge. What makes a defeater "genuine" rather than "merely misleading"? One answer: the defeating information is genuine if it is positively connected to the actual epistemic situation (not just accidentally defeating). But this seems to presuppose a prior understanding of what the genuine epistemic situation is — which is close to presupposing what we're trying to analyze.

## The Connection to Epistemic Luck

Defeasibility theories are best understood as attempts to rule out epistemic luck by requiring that the subject's justification be robust — capable of surviving contact with the full truth about their situation. Knowledge is stable under the acquisition of further true information.

This connects defeasibility to the broader project of understanding what distinguishes knowledge from accidentally true belief: knowledge is belief that is "cognitively safe," not merely contingently true given a partial epistemic picture.

Whether defeasibility approaches can be made precise and adequate continues to be debated. They represent an important strand in post-Gettier epistemology, and the concept of defeasibility has influenced work on prima facie justification, epistemic warrant, and the structure of justified belief.
