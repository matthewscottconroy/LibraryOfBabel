# Goldman's Causal Theory of Knowledge

Alvin Goldman's causal theory (1967) represents a significant departure from the internalist JTB framework. Rather than adding another condition to JTB, Goldman proposes a fundamentally different approach: knowledge requires an appropriate *causal connection* between the fact that makes a belief true and the belief itself.

## The Causal Theory Stated

Goldman's proposal: S knows that P if and only if the fact that P is causally connected to S's believing that P in an appropriate way.

What counts as appropriate? Goldman mentions:
- **Perception**: The fact causes the belief through normal perceptual mechanisms.
- **Memory**: The original perception causes the belief to persist through memory.
- **Inference**: A causal chain runs through appropriate reasoning: the fact causes S's belief in some Q, and Q causes (via inference) S's belief in P.
- **Combination**: Some combination of these.

The key is that the belief is causally connected to the fact it represents. In Gettier cases, this connection is absent: Smith's belief is causally connected to Jones's coin count and the president's statement, not to the actual fact (that Smith, who has ten coins, will get the job).

## How the Causal Theory Handles Gettier Cases

**Case I**: Smith's belief is caused by his counting Jones's coins and learning that Jones will get the job. The fact that makes the belief true is that Smith has ten coins and will get the job. No causal connection between this fact and Smith's belief. No knowledge.

**Case II**: Smith's belief about the disjunction is caused by his evidence about Jones's Ford. The fact that makes it true is Brown's location in Barcelona. No causal connection. No knowledge.

**Barn façade**: Henry's belief is caused by his perception of the real barn. The fact that there is a barn is causally connected to his belief through vision. The causal theory predicts: Henry does know. (This is controversial — many think the causal theory gives the wrong verdict here.)

## Advantages of the Causal Theory

The causal theory explains knowledge in terms of natural causal relations rather than purely epistemic relations. This is attractive to naturalistically minded philosophers: knowledge becomes a natural-world phenomenon — a matter of causal connectedness — not an irreducibly normative one.

The theory elegantly handles the original Gettier cases without requiring a precise account of "justification" — the causal connection condition does the work.

## Problems for the Causal Theory

**The problem of necessary truths**: Mathematical truths are not caused by anything — they're necessarily true. But we know them. The causal theory seems to exclude mathematical knowledge.

Goldman's response: knowledge of mathematical truths involves a different kind of causal connection — through proofs, instruction, and the cognitive processes that constitute mathematical understanding. But this response makes the causal requirement more attenuated.

**The problem of deviant causal chains**: Suppose the fact that P causes S's belief in P, but through a bizarre causal route — through a telepathic transmission, or through a chain that passes through a hallucination. The causal connection exists but seems wrong. What makes a causal connection "appropriate"?

This is the classic problem for causal theories: deviant causal chains. The appropriate causal connection must be spelled out more precisely than "P causes S to believe P."

**The barn façade problem**: Many philosophers' intuition is that Henry doesn't know in Fake Barn County. The causal theory says he does — the real barn causally connects to his belief. This is a problem for the theory if the barn façade intuition is correct.

## Goldman's Later Reliability Theory

Goldman later moved from causal theories to reliabilism — the view that knowledge requires belief formed by a reliable process. This can be seen as a generalization of the causal approach: what matters is not specifically the causal history but whether the process that produced the belief is reliable.

Process reliabilism (discussed in the justification section) represents the mature development of Goldman's approach to knowledge and justification.
