# Nozick's Tracking Theory: Knowledge as Truth-Tracking

Robert Nozick's *Philosophical Explanations* (1981) contains one of the most sophisticated and influential post-Gettier analyses of knowledge. Nozick proposes that knowledge requires the belief to *track* the truth — to be responsive to the truth in both actual and counterfactual situations.

## The Tracking Conditions

Nozick's analysis: S knows that P if and only if:
1. P is true
2. S believes that P
3. **Sensitivity**: If P were false, S would not believe P
4. **Adherence**: If P were true, S would believe P

The third condition (sensitivity) is most important: it requires a counterfactual connection between the truth of P and S's belief. If P were false, S's belief would be absent — S is sensitive to the falsity of P.

The fourth condition (adherence) is less central but handles cases where S happens to have the right belief by luck in the actual world but would lose it under slight variation.

## How Tracking Handles Gettier Cases

**Stopped clock**: If it were not 3:47, would Smith not believe it's 3:47? No — Smith would still look at the stopped clock and still believe whatever it shows. The sensitivity condition fails. No knowledge.

**Barn façade**: If there were no barn there, would Henry not believe there is a barn? No — there are many barn façades, and Henry would form the same belief. The sensitivity condition fails. No knowledge.

The tracking theory gives the right verdict in barn façade cases — which the causal theory did not.

## Closure Denial

One of the most controversial implications of Nozick's theory is its denial of the **closure principle**: the principle that if you know P and know that P implies Q, then you know Q.

Nozick shows that sensitivity can generate closure failures. Consider:
- You see your hand in front of you. You know: P = "I have hands."
- Q = "I am not a handless brain in a vat."
- P implies Q.
- Do you know Q?

On Nozick's theory: if you were a brain in a vat, you would still believe you have hands (because the vat would generate that experience). So you're not sensitive to the falsity of "I have hands" in the brain-in-a-vat world. You know "I have hands" (in normal circumstances, your belief tracks the truth). But you don't know "I am not a brain in a vat" (because you'd believe this even if it were false).

This gives Nozick a response to skepticism: the skeptic's inference from "you don't know you're not a BIV" to "you don't know you have hands" fails because closure fails. You can know the ordinary claim while not knowing the negation of the skeptical hypothesis.

## Problems for the Tracking Theory

**The lottery case**: I hold one of a million lottery tickets. I don't know that I'll lose before the drawing. But: if I were going to win (if it were false that I'll lose), I wouldn't believe I'll lose — no relevant information would change. The sensitivity condition seems satisfied. But surely I don't know I'll lose just from holding one ticket in a million.

**Methods**: Nozick himself introduces the complication that sensitivity should be evaluated relative to the method by which the belief is formed. This helps with some cases but complicates the analysis considerably.

**Safety vs. Sensitivity**: Later theorists have argued that safety (the belief couldn't easily have been false) is more defensible than sensitivity (the belief would be absent if false). Safety and sensitivity come apart in important cases, and safety seems to give better verdicts in many of them.

**Knowledge from competence**: In cases where I know something through a reliable competence (a chess grandmaster's intuition), the tracking conditions may be satisfied for the wrong reasons. Virtue theorists argue that what matters is the competence, not the counterfactual properties.

Nozick's tracking theory is one of the most innovative and philosophically productive positions in post-Gettier epistemology, even if it faces these challenges.
