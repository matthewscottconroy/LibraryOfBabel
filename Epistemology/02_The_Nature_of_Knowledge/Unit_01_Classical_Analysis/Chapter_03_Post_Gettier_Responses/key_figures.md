## Robert Nozick (1938–2002)

Nozick proposed a subjunctivist analysis of knowledge: S knows p if and only if (1) p is true, (2) S believes p, (3) if p were false, S would not believe p (the sensitivity condition), and (4) if p were true, S would believe p (the adherence condition). The sensitivity condition is designed to exclude Gettier cases and to handle radical skepticism: Nozick argued that we cannot know we are not brains in vats (the sensitivity condition is not met for such beliefs) but that this does not threaten ordinary perceptual knowledge, because the sensitivity condition is met for ordinary beliefs. His analysis also motivated contextualism, since the relevant counterfactual circumstances for evaluating sensitivity vary with context.

## Alvin Goldman (born 1938)

Goldman's reliabilism replaced the justification condition with a reliability condition: a belief constitutes knowledge if and only if it is produced by a belief-forming process that reliably produces true beliefs. This externalist move—making the epistemic status of a belief depend on factors external to the believer's perspective—was designed to solve Gettier problems by requiring a reliable causal connection, not merely contingent justification. His later "process reliabilism" distinguished reliable types of cognitive processes (perception, memory, testimony) from unreliable ones, making the analysis applicable to a wide range of cases. The challenge of "generality problem"—specifying which level of description picks out the relevant type of process—has generated an extensive critical literature.

## Ernest Sosa (born 1940)

Sosa's virtue epistemology developed through two stages. His early "intellectual virtues" approach defined knowledge as belief produced by a reliable intellectual virtue (such as perception, memory, or introspection). His later "apt belief" framework distinguishes three levels: accuracy (the belief is true), adroitness (the belief is produced by a competent exercise of an intellectual virtue), and aptness (the accuracy is because of the adroitness). Knowledge is apt belief, and the safety condition—that the belief could not easily have been false—explicates what it means for aptness to hold. This framework provides a virtue-theoretic response to both Gettier cases (which fail the aptness condition) and skeptical scenarios (where aptness fails globally).

## Timothy Williamson (born 1955)

Williamson's "knowledge-first" epistemology argues that the post-Gettier research program is fundamentally misconceived. Knowledge is a primitive, unanalyzable mental state—the most general factive mental state—and attempts to analyze it in terms of justified true belief plus further conditions are doomed because knowledge is not constituted by those elements but rather constitutes the framework within which they are understood. His safety condition (S knows p only if, in nearby possible worlds in which S forms the belief in the same way, p is true) provides a necessary but not sufficient condition. More importantly, his knowledge-first approach treats the norm of assertion as "assert only what you know" and argues that knowledge plays an ineliminable role in epistemology, action theory, and the theory of practical rationality.

## John Greco (born 1961)

Greco's agent reliabilism is a virtue-theoretic alternative to Goldman's process reliabilism. Rather than asking whether the belief-forming *process* is reliable, Greco asks whether the belief is produced by the agent's *intellectual virtue*—a stable, reliable cognitive disposition that is partly constitutive of the agent's character. Knowledge requires that the true belief be attributable to the agent as an achievement: when someone knows, they deserve cognitive credit for their epistemic success. This credit condition handles Gettier cases by noting that in them, the knower's success is not wholly attributable to her intellectual virtue but partly to epistemic luck. Greco's account connects epistemology to broader debates about agency, responsibility, and moral luck.

## Keith DeRose (born 1962)

DeRose's contextualist response to post-Gettier epistemology developed from his work on safety conditions. He argued that Nozick's sensitivity condition (while correctly identifying that knowledge fails in certain ways) needs to be replaced by a safety condition that is explicitly context-sensitive: whether a belief constitutes knowledge depends on which alternative possibilities are relevant in the conversational context. This approach handles both Gettier cases (where nearby possible worlds in which the belief is false are salient) and skeptical arguments (where the relevance of far-fetched possibilities is artificially raised). DeRose's contextualism has been influential in debates about the bank cases and the relationship between ordinary knowledge claims and skeptical challenges.
