## Thought Experiments on Post-Gettier Analyses

---

**Nozick's Variation on the Grandmother**

**The scenario:** A grandmother sees her grandson before her. He looks healthy, and she believes he is healthy. This belief is sensitive: if he were not healthy—if he were ill and pale—she would perceive the difference and update her belief accordingly. Now contrast: relatives, not wanting to upset her, have agreed that if the grandson were ill they would still assure her that he was healthy. In this scenario, if the grandson were ill, the grandmother would still believe he was healthy (because of the assurances). Her actual belief (formed by direct perception) is true and sensitive; yet if we imagine the alternative scenario where he is ill, the sensitivity condition fails.

**The philosophical point:** Nozick uses this and similar cases to show that the sensitivity condition must be applied at the level of the specific *method* of belief formation. The grandmother's visual belief is sensitive; her testimony-based belief (in the counterfactual variant) would not be. This refinement—tracking sensitivity relative to method—handles a range of cases but creates new complications: how do we individuate methods? And can we always cleanly separate the method used in the actual case from the method that would be used in the counterfactual scenario? The case shows that the sensitivity condition requires a sophisticated account of method-individuation to avoid either over-attribution or under-attribution of knowledge.

**The Barn Facade County Re-Examined**

**The scenario:** Revisit the fake barns case. Now consider two sub-cases: (A) Henry forms his belief about the barn through careful scrutiny—he gets out of his car, touches the walls, notes the depth of shadows, and confirms that it is three-dimensional. He happens to be right because it is a genuine barn. (B) Henry drives past and forms a quick visual belief that it is a barn. In both sub-cases, Henry is in the barn facade county, and his belief is true. Does careful scrutiny in sub-case (A) rescue Henry's belief as knowledge?

**The philosophical point:** This variation tests the difference between sensitivity/safety conditions and virtue-theoretic conditions. Under Nozick's sensitivity condition, both sub-cases may fail similarly—the nearby worlds in which the barn is fake would have deceived even careful Henry. Under a virtue-theoretic account, careful Henry is exercising his intellectual virtues more robustly; whether this counts as knowledge turns on whether knowledge requires merely reliability of the specific process or an appropriate relation between the exercise of virtue and the true belief. The case also illustrates that the "environment" for assessing reliability is not just the physical environment but the broader epistemic situation including the distribution of epistemic risks.

**Goldman's Clairvoyant**

**The scenario:** Norman has a reliable clairvoyant power he does not know about. He forms the belief that the President is in New York—a belief that is in fact true and produced by this reliable clairvoyant faculty. But Norman has no evidence that he has such a faculty, no evidence that his belief is reliably produced, and from his own perspective, the belief seems to arise groundlessly.

**The philosophical point:** This case, introduced by BonJour as a counterexample to reliabilism, challenges Goldman's process reliabilism. The belief is reliably produced and true—yet most epistemologists find it intuitively not knowledge, or at least not *justified*. If reliabilism simply says "reliable process → knowledge," it seems to imply that Norman knows—a conclusion that strikes many as wrong. The case shows that reliabilism must either (a) require the believer to have some access to the reliability of their belief-forming process (making it internalist), (b) distinguish the reliability required for justification from that required for knowledge, or (c) bite the bullet and accept that Norman does know (the externalist response: Norman's belief is reliable, and our intuitive discomfort stems from expecting subjective accessibility, which is not epistemically required).

**Williamson's Anti-Luminosity Argument**

**The scenario:** Williamson asks us to consider a marginally cold morning. The temperature is gradually rising by a tiny amount over time. At each moment, you consider whether you feel cold. There will be a sequence of moments in which, at first, you clearly feel cold; then, toward the end, you clearly do not. But for any two adjacent moments in this sequence, your phenomenal states are indistinguishable. Suppose there is a last moment at which you feel cold (call it *t*). At *t*, you feel cold. But at *t+1*, a moment phenomenally indistinguishable from *t*, you do not feel cold. Could you know at *t* that you feel cold?

**The philosophical point:** Williamson argues that you cannot reliably know this, because at the moment just after you stop feeling cold—a moment phenomenally indistinguishable from the cold moment—you would still believe you feel cold. The belief would be false. So the sensitivity condition is not met: if you did not feel cold (at *t+1*), you would still believe you did. The argument undermines "luminosity"—the thesis that if a condition obtains, one can know it obtains—and has implications for the knowledge-first program: even facts about one's own current mental states are not always luminously knowable, challenging the idea that introspective knowledge has a special privileged status.

**Sosa's Dreamer**

**The scenario:** A person is asleep and dreaming that she is facing a red cube. She forms the belief "there is a red cube before me." While dreaming, she cannot detect that she is dreaming. Her dream-belief may be formed "adroitly"—by the exercise of her normally reliable visual faculties—but the accuracy of the belief is not due to the adroitness: there is no red cube, and the adroitness of her visual system is not what makes the dream-content what it is.

**The philosophical point:** Sosa uses the dreaming case to distinguish adroit belief (belief formed by an intellectual competence) from apt belief (belief whose accuracy is due to that competence). The dreamer exercises her visual competences in forming her belief—the process is the same as in waking life—but the belief is not accurate because of that competence. Knowledge requires aptness, not merely adroitness. This distinction handles cases where a reliable process misfires in unusual circumstances: the fake barn cases, the demon-world cases, and the dreaming case all involve adroit but inapt belief. The framework also addresses nested self-knowledge: one may know that one's belief is apt (reflective knowledge), providing a virtue-theoretic account of the difference between unreflective animal knowledge and fully reflective human knowledge.
