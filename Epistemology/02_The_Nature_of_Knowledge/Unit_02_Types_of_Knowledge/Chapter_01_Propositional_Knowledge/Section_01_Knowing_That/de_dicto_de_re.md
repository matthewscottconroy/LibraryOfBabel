# De Dicto and De Re: The Two Faces of Propositional Knowledge

The distinction between de dicto and de re knowledge is one of the most important structural distinctions in the epistemology of belief and knowledge. It concerns whether our cognitive attitudes are directed toward things under descriptions or toward things themselves.

## The Latin Labels

**De dicto** means "of the word" or "of the saying" — the cognitive attitude is about what is said or described, not directly about the object. "I believe the richest person in the world is generous" — if I don't know who the richest person is, my belief is about whoever satisfies that description.

**De re** means "of the thing" — the cognitive attitude is directly about a particular object, not mediated by a description. "I believe, of Bill Gates, that he is generous" — if someone asks me about Gates specifically, I can confirm or deny. The belief is about Gates the individual, not whoever happens to be richest.

## How They Come Apart

De dicto and de re beliefs can diverge dramatically:

**Case 1**: Oedipus believes (de dicto) that "the man he will marry his mother" is wrong. He also believes (de re) of his mother, that she is a fine woman he might marry. His de dicto and de re beliefs conflict.

**Case 2**: I believe (de dicto) that "the spy in the department is dangerous." I have no de re beliefs about the spy (I don't know who they are). If I'm introduced to the spy without knowing it, I have no de re belief about them related to their danger.

**Case 3**: I believe (de re) of Julius Caesar that he crossed the Rubicon — but this seems odd, since I've never met Caesar. Here, de re belief is not about acquaintance in the direct sense, but about a particular individual picked out by the right kind of epistemic relationship (learning about historical individuals).

## Epistemological Significance

The de dicto/de re distinction has several epistemological implications:

**Opacity and transparency**: In de dicto contexts, substituting one co-referential term for another can change the truth of the belief report. "Jones believes the morning star is a planet" might be true while "Jones believes the evening star is a planet" is false, even though "morning star" and "evening star" refer to the same object. In de re contexts, co-reference substitution preserves truth.

**Reference and knowledge**: Kripke's and Putnam's semantic externalism holds that the reference of names and natural kind terms is fixed not by descriptions in the believer's head but by causal-historical chains. This suggests that much of what we think is de dicto (described) is actually de re (directly about the object). "Water is H₂O" is not just a definition; it's a genuine discovery about the stuff that causes our water experiences.

**Self-knowledge**: First-person knowledge is paradigmatically de re — you know things about yourself in a distinctive way that doesn't depend on describing yourself under some third-person description. This has implications for the epistemology of self-knowledge and personal identity.

**Epistemic responsibility**: Whether you are epistemically responsible for your de re beliefs may differ from your de dicto beliefs. If you have de re beliefs about individuals who have properties you'd find morally significant (if only you knew who they were), the epistemological and ethical dimensions of those beliefs become complex.

## The Psychological Reality

Both de dicto and de re belief attribution are common in everyday psychological explanation. We say both "Mary believes that the tallest building is in Dubai" and "Mary believes, of Burj Khalifa, that it is extremely tall." Understanding when to use which attribution, and what the theoretical implications are, is an ongoing project in philosophy of mind and epistemology.
