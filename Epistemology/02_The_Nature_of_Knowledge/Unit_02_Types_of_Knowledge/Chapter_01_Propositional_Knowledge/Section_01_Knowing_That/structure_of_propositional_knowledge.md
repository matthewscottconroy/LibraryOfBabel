# The Structure of Propositional Knowledge

Propositional knowledge — knowing *that* something is the case — is the central focus of epistemology. Understanding its structure means understanding what propositions are, how they relate to cognitive states, and what distinguishes knowing a proposition from merely believing or understanding it.

## Propositions as the Objects of Knowledge

A **proposition** is the content of a belief — what is believed. When you believe that the sky is blue, the proposition is the sky-being-blue: the fact, the content, the thing that can be true or false. Propositions are abstract objects (or abstract structures) that are the bearers of truth-value.

Different theories of propositions exist: structured propositions (involving objects and properties as constituents), possible-worlds propositions (sets of possible worlds in which they are true), and more. For epistemological purposes, what matters is that propositions are the kinds of things that can be true or false and that can be believed, known, doubted, or asserted.

## De Dicto and De Re Knowledge

An important structural distinction: *de dicto* knowledge and *de re* knowledge.

**De dicto** (of the word/saying): You know something under a specific description. "I know that the tallest building in the world is in Dubai" — your knowledge is of the proposition under that description.

**De re** (of the thing): You know something *of* a particular object, in a way that doesn't depend on any specific description. "I know, of this building, that it is over 800 meters tall" — your knowledge is directly about the building.

The distinction matters because de dicto and de re knowledge can come apart. You might know de dicto that "the tallest building in Dubai is tall" without knowing which building this is. You might know de re about Burj Khalifa that it is extremely tall without knowing it is in Dubai.

In epistemology, most of the analysis of knowledge-that has focused on de dicto propositional knowledge, but de re knowledge raises interesting additional questions about the relationship between knowledge and direct reference.

## First-Person and Third-Person Knowledge

An interesting asymmetry: first-person knowledge (knowledge about yourself) seems to have special features. You know your own mental states in a way that doesn't require observational evidence — you don't need to watch yourself to know that you have a headache. This asymmetry — and its limits — is discussed in the section on introspection and self-knowledge.

## The Normative Dimension

Propositional knowledge is not merely a cognitive state but carries normative significance. Asserting something you know is epistemically appropriate. Asserting something you merely believe invites the question "how do you know?" These normative dimensions of knowledge — what knowledge licenses in terms of assertion, action, and reasoning — have been explored by Timothy Williamson (knowledge norms of assertion) and others.

The knowledge norm of assertion: one should assert P only if one knows P. This norm, if correct, connects the speech-act of assertion to the epistemic state of knowing in a way that is both normatively significant and epistemologically illuminating.
