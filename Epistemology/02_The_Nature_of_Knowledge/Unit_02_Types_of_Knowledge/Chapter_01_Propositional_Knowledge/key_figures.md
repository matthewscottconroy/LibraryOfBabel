## Gottlob Frege (1848–1925)

Frege's distinction between sense (*Sinn*) and reference (*Bedeutung*) is foundational for the theory of propositional knowledge. The sense of an expression is the "mode of presentation" of its referent—the way the object is given to the mind. Two co-referring expressions (like "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus") differ in sense, which is why "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is informative and "Hesperus is Hesperus" is trivial. Propositions, on Frege's account, are structured senses: the object of a propositional attitude is a thought (*Gedanke*)—an abstract, mind-independent entity that determines truth conditions. This framework allows for a fine-grained individuation of the objects of knowledge: one can know that Hesperus is a planet without knowing that Phosphorus is the same planet, even though the same object (Venus) is involved.

## Bertrand Russell (1872–1970)

Russell's theory of propositional knowledge distinguishes knowledge of truths from knowledge of things, and within the former, distinguishes knowledge by acquaintance from knowledge by description. His analysis of belief as a "multiple relation" between a subject and the components of a proposition—rather than a relation between a subject and a single propositional object—was designed to avoid the commitment to false propositions as objects of thought. His distinction between logically proper names (which contribute only their referent to the proposition) and ordinary names and descriptions (which contribute their sense or description) anticipates the de re/de dicto distinction.

## Saul Kripke (1940–2022)

Kripke's *Naming and Necessity* effected a revolution in the theory of propositional content by arguing that proper names are rigid designators—they refer to the same individual in every possible world in which that individual exists—and that natural kind terms function similarly. This creates a category of necessary a posteriori knowledge: we know that water is H₂O not by a priori analysis of the concept "water" but by empirical discovery, yet the identity is necessary. The discovery is a propositional knowledge claim of a distinctive type—de re rather than de dicto—that challenges the traditional equation of necessity with a priority.

## David Kaplan (born 1933)

Kaplan's theory of indexicals—expressions like "I," "here," and "now"—introduced a crucial distinction between character (the rule for determining content in a context) and content (the proposition expressed). Indexical knowledge claims like "I am in danger" cannot be reduced to non-indexical descriptions without loss: the belief expressed by "I am in danger" has a different cognitive role from "Kaplan is in danger" even if both are true and I am Kaplan. This bears on the structure of propositional knowledge by revealing that not all propositions are context-independent abstract objects: some essentially involve the perspective of the knower, motivating Lewis's notion of de se knowledge.

## David Lewis (1941–2001)

Lewis's treatment of de se belief proposed that propositional attitudes are best understood not as relations to classical Fregean propositions (sets of possible worlds) but as relations to properties: to believe that you are in Paris is to self-ascribe the property of being-in-Paris. This captures the irreducibly first-personal character of beliefs involving "I," "here," and "now"—what is sometimes called the "essential indexical." For the theory of propositional knowledge, this suggests that some knowledge claims cannot be fully captured in terms of knowing that a proposition is true; they require a self-locating component. Lewis's framework has influenced debates about the epistemology of self-location and about cases like Sleeping Beauty.

## Alvin Plantinga (born 1932)

Plantinga's work on propositions, possible worlds, and the epistemology of modality has been central to the theory of propositional knowledge. His account of propositions as abstract, possibly-true-or-false entities whose modal properties are determinate regardless of human conceptualization grounds an epistemology in which a priori knowledge is knowledge of necessary propositions and a posteriori knowledge tracks contingent ones. His defence of proper functionalism—that knowledge requires belief-forming faculties functioning as they were designed to function in appropriate environments—provides a theistic version of reliabilism that also engages the structure of propositional knowledge.
