## Thought Experiments in Propositional Knowledge

---

**Frege's Morning Star and Evening Star**

**The scenario:** The ancient astronomers who named "Hesperus" (the evening star, visible in the western sky at dusk) and "Phosphorus" (the morning star, visible in the eastern sky at dawn) did not know that these names referred to the same object—the planet Venus. An astronomer might know that Hesperus is the brightest object in the evening sky without knowing that Phosphorus is, even though the two descriptions apply to the same thing. The discovery that Hesperus is Phosphorus (that the evening star is the morning star) was a genuine astronomical achievement.

**The philosophical point:** The scenario establishes the need for a fine-grained theory of propositional content. If propositions were merely sets of possible worlds (or truth conditions), then "Hesperus is Hesperus" and "Hesperus is Phosphorus" would express the same proposition—since they are both necessarily true. But they differ cognitively: one can believe one without believing the other. This shows that the objects of propositional knowledge must be more finely individuated than truth conditions alone allow. Frege's solution—that senses (modes of presentation) are the objects of propositional attitudes—gives the right result: the two sentences have different senses even though they have the same reference and the same truth value at every world.

**The Essential Indexical: "I Am Making a Mess"**

**The scenario:** John Perry describes a situation in which he is pushing a shopping cart at a supermarket, noticing a trail of sugar on the floor. He follows the trail, looking for the shopper who is making a mess. Eventually he realizes that the trail loops back to his own cart—there is a hole in the bag in his cart. At this point, he gains a new piece of knowledge: "I am making a mess." This belief prompts him to change his behavior immediately (he stops pushing the cart). Before the realization, he believed "the shopper making the mess is making a mess"—a true belief—but this did not guide his action in the same way.

**The philosophical point:** Perry's case shows that de se propositional knowledge—knowledge about oneself as oneself, using "I" irreducibly—plays a distinctive functional role that cannot be replaced by any de dicto description, however complete. Even if Perry believed an exhaustive description of himself under some non-indexical description ("the person with the blue coat pushing the cart in aisle 5"), this belief would not guide action in the same way as the irreducibly first-personal "I am making a mess." This demonstrates that the objects of propositional knowledge are not simply sets of possible worlds but include self-locating elements; and it raises the question of whether there is a distinctive species of propositional knowledge (de se knowledge) that standard accounts of propositions cannot accommodate.

**Kripke's Puzzle About Belief**

**The scenario:** Pierre, a monolingual French speaker, has heard that London is beautiful and comes to believe "Londres est belle" (London is beautiful). He then moves to London but, in the dreary district where he ends up, comes to believe "London is not beautiful." He does not realize that London and Londres are the same city. Does Pierre believe that London is beautiful, that London is not beautiful, or both?

**The philosophical point:** Kripke's puzzle about belief is a puzzle about the individuation of propositional attitudes. If belief is a relation between a person and a proposition, and if the proposition expressed by "London is beautiful" and "Londres est belle" is the same (since both sentences are about the same city), then Pierre both believes and disbelieves the same proposition. But Pierre is not irrational—he is simply ignorant of an empirical identity. The puzzle shows that propositional knowledge cannot be simply a binary relation between a person and a classical Russellian proposition (a set of individuals and properties); the mode of presentation through which the proposition is grasped must also matter for individuating the attitude. This places pressure on direct reference theories and on accounts of propositional knowledge that ignore the cognitive perspective of the knower.

**De Re versus De Dicto: The Suspicious Spinach**

**The scenario:** An agent believes that the tallest person in the room will be served first. She has no idea who the tallest person is. Unknown to her, the tallest person in the room is her long-lost sister. Does she believe, de re of her sister, that her sister will be served first?

**The philosophical point:** The de dicto reading of her belief is unproblematic: she believes that whoever satisfies the description "tallest person in the room" will be served first. But on the de re reading—whether she has a belief about her sister specifically—the answer is less clear. De re knowledge of an object requires some appropriate epistemic relationship to that object (acquaintance, causal history, or similar), not merely a true description that happens to pick it out. The case illustrates why de re knowledge attributions are "opaque" to substitution: one can know that the tallest person will be served first without knowing anything about one's sister, even if one's sister is in fact the tallest person. This opacity—the failure of co-referential substitution in belief contexts—is the central puzzle that motivates both the Fregean and the de se approaches to propositional attitudes.
