# The Relationship Between Knowing How and Knowing That

Ryle's distinction has been debated intensely. Some philosophers accept a strong version of the distinction — knowing-how is irreducible to knowing-that — while others (particularly Stanley and Williamson) argue that the distinction is less radical than Ryle thought. The debate illuminates deep questions about the nature of cognition, ability, and intelligence.

## The Reduction to Propositions: Stanley and Williamson

Jason Stanley and Timothy Williamson's influential paper "Knowing How" (2001) defends intellectualism: knowing how to do something *just is* knowing a certain proposition. Specifically: S knows how to φ if and only if there is a way w such that S knows that w is a way for S to φ.

The key feature: the propositional knowledge is about a *way* of doing something, and it is essentially *practical* — the way is entertained under a practical (first-person, action-guiding) mode of presentation, not a theoretical one.

On this view, the cyclist knows how to ride because they know, of a certain way of riding, that it is a way for them to ride — and they know this under a practical mode of presentation that connects to their practical capacities.

## The Mode of Presentation Move

The Stanley-Williamson view preserves Ryle's insight that knowing-how is different from mere propositional knowledge by invoking the mode of presentation: the cyclist's knowledge is practical because it is entertained under a practical, first-person mode, not because it lacks propositional content.

This is a clever maneuver: it allows knowing-how to be propositional (satisfying the intellectualist desideratum) while acknowledging that practical knowledge has distinctive features (satisfying the anti-intellectualist insight).

## Objections to the Intellectualist View

Critics have pressed several objections:

**The regress survives**: Even if knowing-how is knowing a proposition, there must be some practical grasp of how to apply that propositional knowledge — and that grasp is itself a form of knowing-how that isn't obviously reducible to knowing-that.

**Dissociation cases**: Brain damage can selectively disrupt knowing-that while leaving knowing-how largely intact (procedural memory is more robust than declarative memory in some amnesias). This dissociation suggests genuine psychological distinctness.

**Phenomenology**: The experience of exercising a skill feels different from the experience of recalling or applying a proposition. Skilled action has a fluid, unreflective character that propositional representation seems unable to capture.

**Linguistic evidence**: The grammar of "know how" differs from "know that" in ways that suggest semantic distinctness.

## A Middle Path

A plausible middle path: knowing-how involves both propositional and non-propositional elements. There is a propositional component — you must know something about how to accomplish the task. But there is also an irreducibly practical component — the ability to apply this knowledge in action, which is not itself just more propositions.

This view preserves Ryle's insight that mere possession of propositions doesn't entail ability, while accepting Stanley and Williamson's point that knowing-how has propositional content. It suggests that the debate has illuminated a complex rather than a simple distinction.

## Implications for Cognitive Science

The knowing-how/knowing-that debate connects to the distinction in cognitive science between *implicit* (procedural) and *explicit* (declarative) memory and knowledge. Implicit knowledge is manifested in skilled performance; explicit knowledge can be verbally reported. The two are implemented in different neural systems and can be dissociated by brain damage.

Whether this cognitive-scientific distinction exactly mirrors the philosophical distinction is contested, but the convergence between philosophical analysis and empirical findings is at least suggestive.
