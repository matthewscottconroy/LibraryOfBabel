# Ryle's Distinction: Knowing How vs. Knowing That

Gilbert Ryle's distinction between knowing-how and knowing-that, introduced in *The Concept of Mind* (1949), is one of the most important and productively controversial moves in twentieth-century philosophy. It challenges a deep assumption about the nature of intelligence and challenges the idea that all knowledge is propositional.

## The Intellectualist Legend

Ryle targets what he calls the "intellectualist legend": the assumption that intelligent action is always the expression of prior intellectual operations — that before acting intelligently, we must first know a proposition that guides the action.

On the intellectualist picture, when a chess player makes a good move, they first think of the rule "knights move in an L-shape," apply it to the current position, and then execute the move. Intelligence = propositional knowledge + application.

Ryle's objection: this generates a regress. If every application of a rule requires prior knowledge of a rule-applying rule, and that application requires prior knowledge of another rule, we never reach the end. And if we must reach the end somewhere — if at some point we just act — then that final, rule-free action is itself a case of knowing-how that doesn't reduce to knowing-that.

## The Distinction Defined

**Knowing-how** is the ability to perform actions, exercises competences, and deploy skills. The expert cyclist knows how to ride a bicycle. The chef knows how to make a soufflé. The virtuoso knows how to play a Beethoven sonata. These are genuine forms of knowledge, manifest in successful performance, that are not (or not simply) the possession of propositional beliefs.

**Knowing-that** is possessing a true belief about a proposition. I know that Paris is the capital of France. I know that the Earth orbits the Sun. These are propositional.

Ryle's claim: knowing-how and knowing-that are fundamentally different and knowing-how is not reducible to knowing-that.

## Evidence for the Distinction

**The Competent but Inarticulate Expert**: A master chess player may not be able to articulate general principles behind their choices; they simply see that a position is good or bad. A native speaker knows how to speak grammatically but may know no grammar rules. The knowledge is manifested in performance, not in propositional articulation.

**Knowing-that Without Knowing-how**: You can know that bicycle riding involves leaning into turns without being able to ride a bicycle. You can know that a soufflé must be folded gently without knowing how to make one. Propositional knowledge does not imply practical competence.

**Knowing-how Without Knowing-that**: You can ride a bicycle without knowing any propositional account of how balance works. Much practical expertise consists in know-how that outstrips propositional understanding.

## The Philosophical Significance

Ryle's distinction has implications for:

- **Philosophy of mind**: If knowing-how is irreducibly distinct from knowing-that, then not all cognitive states are representational. Some cognitive achievements are dispositional — they are ways of being disposed to act — rather than ways of representing.

- **AI and cognitive science**: The classical computational model of intelligence (rules + data) faces Rylean challenges. Expertise may be more holistic, contextual, and embodied than the propositional model suggests.

- **Education**: If much valuable knowledge is knowing-how rather than knowing-that, then education cannot consist simply in transmitting information. Practical training, apprenticeship, and experiential learning may be irreducible.

- **The analysis of knowledge**: If knowing-how is not reducible to knowing-that, then the JTB analysis (which targets knowing-that) is incomplete as an account of knowledge generally.
