# Anti-Intellectualism: Defending the Irreducibility of Knowing-How

Anti-intellectualism about knowing-how maintains that knowing how to do something is genuinely distinct from knowing any proposition — that the Rylean insight is correct, and that intellectualist reductions fail.

## The Core Anti-Intellectualist Argument

The fundamental anti-intellectualist argument: someone can possess all the relevant propositional knowledge and still lack the corresponding skill. The novice swimmer who has read extensively about swimming technique, who can articulate every principle of efficient movement in the water, who knows that you should kick from the hips and reach forward — this person might still drown. The gap between propositional knowledge and practical competence is real.

More carefully: even if Stanley and Williamson are right that knowing-how involves knowing a proposition under a practical mode of presentation, that practical mode of presentation is itself a form of knowing-how — knowing how to have or deploy the proposition in action. The intellectualist has not eliminated knowing-how but has relocated it.

## Dreyfus and the Phenomenology of Skill

Hubert Dreyfus (drawing on Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty) provides a phenomenological argument against intellectualism. Expert performance is characterized by:

- **Immediacy**: The expert doesn't deliberate; they respond fluently to the situation.
- **Wholism**: The expert sees the situation as a whole, not as a collection of features to which rules are applied.
- **Context-sensitivity**: The expert responds appropriately to subtle differences in situations that are hard to articulate propositionally.
- **Tacit knowledge**: The expert knows more than they can tell; their knowledge outstrips their propositional articulation.

These features suggest that expertise is not a sophisticated system of propositional representations but a different kind of cognitive achievement — one that is embodied, practiced, and not fully intellectualizable.

## Cognitive Science Evidence

Cognitive science offers some empirical evidence for the anti-intellectualist position:

**Implicit learning**: Humans can acquire complex cognitive skills (grammar of an artificial language, regularities in sequences) without being able to articulate what they've learned. The learning is in the performance, not in the explicit representation.

**Double dissociation**: Patients with certain forms of amnesia retain procedural memories (skills) while losing episodic and semantic memories (propositional knowledge). Other conditions can reverse the dissociation. This suggests distinct neural systems for knowing-how and knowing-that.

**Paralysis by analysis**: When experts are asked to articulate what they're doing while performing a skill, their performance often deteriorates. Propositional interference disrupts knowing-how, which would be paradoxical if knowing-how were just a form of knowing-that.

## The Embodied Cognition Connection

Anti-intellectualism about knowing-how connects naturally to the embodied cognition movement in philosophy of mind and cognitive science. On the embodied view, cognitive processes are not confined to the brain/central processor; they extend through the body and into the environment. Skills are literally embodied — implemented in the musculoskeletal system, the perception-action loop, the body-environment coupling.

If skills are embodied in this sense, it's not surprising that they can't be fully captured in the propositional representations that characterize "central" cognitive processes. Knowing-how is genuinely different in kind from knowing-that.

## The Persistence of the Debate

The intellectualism debate is unlikely to be settled soon. Both sides have serious arguments, and the territory is genuinely difficult: it requires getting clear on what propositions are, how they guide action, what modes of presentation are, and how to interpret the relevant empirical evidence.

What is not in dispute: knowing-how and knowing-that are at least *practically* different — acquiring them requires different learning processes, different forms of teaching, and different forms of exercise. Whether this practical difference reflects a deep ontological difference or merely different ways of relating to the same propositional substrate remains an open and productive question.
