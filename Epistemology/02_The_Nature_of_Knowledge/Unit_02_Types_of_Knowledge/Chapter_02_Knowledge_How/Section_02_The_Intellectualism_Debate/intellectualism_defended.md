# Intellectualism Defended: The Propositional Account of Ability

Intellectualism about knowing-how — the view that knowing how to do something reduces to knowing certain propositions — has been defended most forcefully by Jason Stanley and Timothy Williamson. Their view deserves a careful look, both for what it gets right and for the challenges it faces.

## The Case for Intellectualism

Stanley and Williamson's key argument: the surface grammar of "S knows how to φ" is strikingly parallel to "S knows that P." Both embed a complement clause (a "how" clause and a "that" clause, respectively). This grammatical parallel suggests semantic similarity — knowing-how may be a variety of knowing-that, with the embedded clause describing the object of knowledge.

More substantively: there are clear cases where knowing-how and knowing-that are intimately linked. A new driver who has mastered the propositional knowledge of driving rules — who knows that you brake before turning, that you check mirrors, that parallel parking involves specific sequences of wheel movements — seems to be approaching knowing-how through knowing-that. The practical knowledge and the propositional knowledge seem to be building toward the same thing.

## The Practical Mode of Presentation

The intellectualist must explain why knowing that *a certain way is a way to ride* doesn't guarantee the ability to ride. Stanley's answer: the propositional knowledge must be held under a practical, first-person mode of presentation.

A practical mode of presentation is one that directly connects to action. Knowing that "moving the handlebars this way" (where "this way" is demonstrated in a practical context, from the first-person perspective of riding) is a way to ride — this is practical knowledge that connects directly to the action. Merely knowing the proposition in an academic, theoretical mode is not enough.

This makes the intellectualist position more nuanced: it's not just about which proposition is known but *how* it is known — under what mode of presentation.

## The Dispositional Challenge

A significant challenge: skills are *dispositional* — they are stable tendencies to respond appropriately in relevant circumstances, even when you're not currently thinking about them. When a skilled cyclist rides, they don't consult any propositions; the skill is just exercised.

The intellectualist must explain how propositions — which need to be entertained, accessed, applied — can constitute a disposition. How does possessing a proposition translate into the automatic, unreflective exercise of a skill?

One answer: the propositions are held in a way that automatically guides behavior without requiring conscious access. The skill consists in having propositions accessible in a practically operative way — ready to be "consulted" by the action system, as it were, even if not consciously articulated.

Whether this saves intellectualism or merely redescribes the problem is debated.

## The Unity of Intellectual and Practical Virtue

A philosophically interesting dimension: intellectual virtues (open-mindedness, epistemic care, intellectual honesty) resemble practical virtues (courage, temperance, justice) in being stable dispositions that manifest in appropriate circumstances without requiring conscious deliberation.

If intellectual virtues are forms of knowing-how — knowing how to reason well, knowing how to be open-minded — then intellectualism about knowing-how would require these virtues to reduce to propositional knowledge. But this seems to miss what is essentially dispositional and character-based about virtue.

This connection between the knowing-how debate and virtue epistemology suggests that the dispute has implications that go beyond the analysis of skills to the structure of epistemic excellence more broadly.
