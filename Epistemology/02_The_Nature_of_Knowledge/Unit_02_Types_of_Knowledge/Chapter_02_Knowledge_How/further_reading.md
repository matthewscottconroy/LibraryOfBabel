## Primary Sources

- Ryle, Gilbert, *The Concept of Mind*, Hutchinson, 1949. Chapter 2 introduces the distinction between knowing-how and knowing-that; argues against the "intellectualist legend" that knowing-how is constituted by or reducible to knowledge of propositions.

- Stanley, Jason and Williamson, Timothy, "Knowing How," *Journal of Philosophy*, 98:8 (2001), pp. 411–444. The most influential defence of intellectualism about knowing-how; argues that knowing how to F is a form of propositional knowledge—knowing, of a way w, that w is a way to F.

- Stanley, Jason, *Know How*, Oxford University Press, 2011. A book-length development of the Stanley-Williamson position, with responses to anti-intellectualist objections and a defence of the claim that skill and practical competence are constituted by propositional knowledge.

- Fantl, Jeremy, "Knowing-How and Knowing-That," *Philosophy Compass*, 3:3 (2008), pp. 451–470. A clear survey of the debate between intellectualism and anti-intellectualism about knowing-how; useful for mapping the dialectical landscape.

- Bengson, John and Moffett, Marc A. (eds.), *Knowing How: Essays on Knowledge, Mind, and Action*, Oxford University Press, 2011. A comprehensive anthology collecting the major contributions to the debate; includes both pro-intellectualist and anti-intellectualist papers.

- Polanyi, Michael, *Personal Knowledge: Towards a Post-Critical Philosophy*, University of Chicago Press, 1958. The founding text of tacit knowledge theory; argues that all knowledge involves a personal, irreducibly practical component that cannot be made fully explicit.

- Polanyi, Michael, *The Tacit Dimension*, Doubleday, 1966. A shorter and more accessible statement of the tacit knowledge thesis; the opening chapters on "tacit knowing" are essential for the relationship between knowing-how and tacit knowledge.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Snowdon, Paul, "Knowing How and Knowing That: A Distinction Reconsidered," *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society*, 104:1 (2003), pp. 1–29. A defence of the Rylean distinction against Stanley and Williamson; argues that their intellectualist analysis fails on its own terms.

- Sgaravatti, Daniele and Zardini, Elia, "Knowing How to Establish Intellectualism," *Grazer Philosophische Studien*, 74:1 (2007), pp. 217–261. A critical examination of the Stanley-Williamson argument, focusing on the semantics of infinitival complement constructions.

- Brown, Jason, "Knowing How: Intellectualism Defended," *Philosophical Studies*, 166:3 (2013), pp. 597–620. Develops and defends a refined version of intellectualism against the most powerful anti-intellectualist objections.

- Fridland, Ellen, "Skill and Motor Control: Intelligence All the Way Down," *Philosophical Psychology*, 30:3 (2017), pp. 293–315. Argues that skilled action is intelligently controlled at multiple levels and that this supports a nuanced intellectualism about skill and knowing-how.

- Dreyfus, Hubert L. and Dreyfus, Stuart E., *Mind over Machine: The Power of Human Intuition and Expertise in the Era of the Computer*, Free Press, 1986. Develops the five-stage model of skill acquisition and argues that expert performance is non-representational—a challenge to any intellectualist account of knowing-how.

- Pavese, Carlotta, "Knowledge How and Gradability," *Philosophical Review*, 126:3 (2017), pp. 345–383. Argues that knowing-how admits of degrees in a way that knowing-that does not, and draws implications for the intellectualist debate.

- Wiggins, David, "Deliberation and Practical Reason," *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society*, 76:1 (1975–76), pp. 29–51. Examines the relationship between practical knowledge, skilled action, and deliberative reasoning in a way that bears on the intellectualism debate.

- Millar, Alan, "What Is It To Know How?", *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society*, Suppl. Vol. 83 (2009), pp. 183–205. A careful anti-intellectualist analysis that argues knowing-how is constituted by reliable capacities, not by propositional knowledge.
