## Gilbert Ryle (1900–1976)

Ryle introduced the knowing-how/knowing-that distinction in *The Concept of Mind* as part of his critique of Cartesian dualism and the "intellectualist legend." The intellectualist legend holds that every intelligent performance requires prior knowledge of a rule or proposition that guides it—so knowing how to ride a bicycle requires knowing that balancing requires leaning into a turn, and so on. Ryle argued that this generates a vicious regress: if knowing how requires knowing that some proposition is true, and if applying that propositional knowledge is itself a performance requiring its own guide, we never reach the level of competent performance. Knowing-how, for Ryle, is a non-propositional dispositional capacity—an ability to do things correctly across a range of circumstances—and is not reducible to or constituted by any amount of propositional knowledge.

## Jason Stanley (born 1969)

Stanley, in collaboration with Timothy Williamson and subsequently in his book *Know How*, is the principal contemporary defender of intellectualism about knowing-how. Their analysis holds that "S knows how to F" means something like "there is a way w such that S knows, of w, that it is a way to F"—where this knowledge is entertained under a practical mode of presentation. Stanley argues that this captures the context-sensitivity of knowing-how attributions, explains the linguistic behavior of "knowing-how" constructions, and is supported by the semantics of embedded question complements. The "practical mode of presentation" clause is designed to explain why propositional knowledge of a way need not suffice for the corresponding ability (a paralyzed but knowledgeable swimming teacher knows how to swim but cannot do it).

## Timothy Williamson (born 1955)

Williamson's collaboration with Stanley on knowing-how represents a natural extension of his knowledge-first epistemology: if knowledge-that is a primitive state, and if knowing-how is a species of knowing-that, then the distinction between propositional and non-propositional knowledge is less fundamental than the Rylean tradition supposes. His subsequent work in *Knowledge and Its Limits* develops the thesis that knowledge is a mental state prior to the distinction between practical and theoretical knowledge, with implications for how the knowing-how debate relates to the broader theory of knowledge. His safety-condition epistemology also applies to practical knowledge: safe practical competence is competence that does not easily misfire.

## Hubert Dreyfus (1929–2017)

Dreyfus was the leading philosopher of anti-intellectualism about skill, drawing on Heidegger's phenomenology of absorbed coping and Merleau-Ponty's embodied perception. His five-stage model of skill acquisition (novice, advanced beginner, competent, proficient, expert) argues that expertise involves a progressive transition from explicit rule-following to holistic, non-representational pattern recognition. Expert performance is not guided by the application of propositional knowledge but by a direct perception of the situation and a context-sensitive response that has become habitual and embodied. Dreyfus applied this model to critique classical AI (which modeled cognition as explicit rule-following), arguing that human intelligence is fundamentally non-propositional and non-representational in its skilled dimension.

## John Bengson (born 1982) and Marc Moffett

Bengson and Moffett developed a "neo-Rylean" account of knowing-how that avoids both the crude ability account (knowing-how = having the ability to F) and Stanley-Williamson intellectualism. Their "objectual" account holds that knowing-how is acquaintance with a way of doing something—where this acquaintance is a form of non-propositional direct cognitive contact with a mode of action. This preserves the insight that knowing-how is not merely ability (since the paralyzed expert still knows how to swim) while also denying that it reduces to propositional knowledge. Their anthology *Knowing How* (2011) is the most comprehensive resource for the current state of the debate.

## Ellen Fridland

Fridland's work on skill and motor control has developed a sophisticated account of the relationship between intelligence and automaticity in skilled action. Against both strong anti-intellectualism (which treats skilled performance as non-intelligent automatic response) and crude intellectualism (which treats it as explicit proposition-following), she argues that skill involves hierarchically organized control structures in which higher-level intelligent guidance is continuously integrated with lower-level automatic execution. This "nuanced intellectualism" holds that the intelligence of skilled action is real but not propositional in the traditional sense—it involves action-oriented representations and intelligent monitoring that is not reducible to the application of explicit rules.
