## Thought Experiments on Knowing-How

---

**The Intellectual-Athlete Regress**

**The scenario:** Ryle invites us to consider an Olympic gymnast who performs a perfect vault. The intellectualist says she can do this because she knows that a good vault requires generating sufficient angular momentum, controlling her center of mass, timing her release precisely, and so on. But executing these principles itself requires skill: she must know how to apply each rule. Does applying the rule "generate sufficient angular momentum" require knowing that there is a further rule for applying the angular-momentum rule? This process seems to generate an infinite regress of required propositional knowledge.

**The philosophical point:** Ryle's regress argument is designed to show that any account that makes knowing-how consist in the application of propositional knowledge leads to a vicious infinite regress: the application of knowledge-that always requires an additional piece of knowledge-how, so propositional knowledge cannot be sufficient for practical competence. The only way to stop the regress is to allow some competence that is not constituted by the application of propositional knowledge—i.e., genuine knowing-how. Stanley and Williamson responded by denying that their account requires the gymnast to consciously apply propositions; the propositional knowledge involved in knowing-how is exercised in the performance itself, not prior to it. The debate about whether this response blocks the regress without collapsing the intellectualist position into Ryle's own remains active.

**The Paralyzed Swimming Teacher**

**The scenario:** A former Olympic swimmer, through injury, has become paralyzed from the neck down. She now coaches swimmers professionally—diagnosing faults, prescribing exercises, explaining technique. She demonstrates deep understanding of what good swimming is and how to achieve it. She cannot, however, swim. Does she know how to swim?

**The philosophical point:** This case is a central tool in the intellectualism debate. Anti-intellectualists who identify knowing-how with having the relevant ability must say she does not know how to swim—a conclusion that seems wrong, given her superior understanding. Stanley and Williamson use the case to argue that knowing-how is not identical to ability: she knows how to swim (she knows of the relevant ways of moving through water that they are ways to swim) even though she lacks the ability. They add that her propositional knowledge is entertained under a "practical mode of presentation" that differs from theoretical knowledge—though critics note that this clause does much work and requires more elaboration than the original paper provides.

**The Book-Taught Swimmer**

**The scenario:** Someone has read every book ever written about swimming. She knows every biomechanical principle, every technique, every coaching method. She has perfect theoretical knowledge of swimming. She has never been in water. She jumps in.

**The philosophical point:** The book-taught swimmer case tests the claim that knowing-how is propositional knowledge. If Stanley and Williamson are right, she should know how to swim: she knows all the relevant propositions about swimming under whatever mode of presentation is required. Yet almost everyone judges that she does not know how to swim, at least not in the sense relevant to actual performance. Anti-intellectualists take this as strong evidence that knowing-how requires more than propositional knowledge—it requires what Dreyfus calls the "embodied know-how" acquired only through bodily practice and feedback. The case forces the intellectualist to specify more precisely what the practical mode of presentation amounts to, and whether book-learning could in principle provide it.

**Dreyfus's Novice Chess Player**

**The scenario:** A novice chess player has been taught the rules and some basic strategic principles: "control the center," "develop your pieces early," "do not expose your king." She follows these rules consciously and laboriously when she plays. A grandmaster who plays the same position "sees" the correct move immediately and holistically, without applying rules. The grandmaster cannot fully articulate why she chose the move she chose; asked to explain, she might give a post-hoc rationalization that does not accurately describe the phenomenology of her decision.

**The philosophical point:** Dreyfus argues that the grandmaster's performance illustrates that expert knowing-how is not propositional: it does not consist in the application of chess rules (however implicit), but in a direct, non-inferential, gestalt-like perception of the relevant features of the position. The novice has propositional knowledge of chess principles; the expert has transcended that level and achieved a non-representational competence. This challenges intellectualism by showing that the highest levels of skill involve a *reduction* in the role of explicit propositional guidance, not merely a shift to implicit or unconscious propositional knowledge. The case also connects to broader debates in cognitive science about the architecture of expert performance and whether neural network models (which do not use explicit rules) better capture expertise than rule-based models.

**The Knowledgeable Alien**

**The scenario:** An alien being has perfect knowledge of all the physical facts about riding a bicycle—every force, every neural signal, every muscle movement involved in maintaining balance. It has computed the correct motor response for every possible situation involving a bicycle and a rider. It has never had a body and has no capacity for embodied practice. If it were given a human body, could it ride a bicycle immediately?

**The philosophical point:** This thought experiment (in the tradition of Jackson's Mary) presses the knowing-how question at its sharpest. Even if we concede that the alien's propositional knowledge is complete, it seems that practical competence requires more: the capacity to execute, to feel the feedback through the body, to integrate sensory information with motor output in real time. Anti-intellectualists argue that this additional requirement shows that knowing-how is irreducibly practical and embodied. Intellectualists must either argue that the alien does know how to ride (but lacks the ability to execute) or that the "practical mode of presentation" is precisely what the alien lacks—which pushes the question to what exactly this mode of presentation consists in.
