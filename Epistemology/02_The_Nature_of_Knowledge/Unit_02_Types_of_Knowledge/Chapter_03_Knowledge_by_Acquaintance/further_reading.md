## Primary Sources

- Russell, Bertrand, *The Problems of Philosophy*, Oxford University Press, 1912. Chapter 5 provides the canonical statement of the acquaintance/description distinction; Russell identifies the objects of acquaintance (sense-data, universals, self, memory-data) and the principle that all knowledge of truths ultimately rests on acquaintance.

- Russell, Bertrand, "Knowledge by Acquaintance and Knowledge by Description," *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society*, 11 (1910–11), pp. 108–128. The original paper presentation of the distinction; slightly different in emphasis from the *Problems of Philosophy* account.

- Russell, Bertrand, *Our Knowledge of the External World*, Allen and Unwin, 1914. Develops the implications of the acquaintance/description distinction for the construction of the external world; shows how acquaintance with sense-data grounds knowledge of physical objects through description.

- Jackson, Frank, "Epiphenomenal Qualia," *Philosophical Quarterly*, 32:127 (1982), pp. 127–136. Introduces Mary's Room as an argument for qualia and the claim that phenomenal knowledge (knowledge by acquaintance with phenomenal properties) is not exhausted by physical/functional knowledge.

- Jackson, Frank, "What Mary Didn't Know," *Journal of Philosophy*, 83:5 (1986), pp. 291–295. A briefer follow-up to the original Mary's Room paper; clarifies the knowledge argument and distinguishes it from related arguments.

- Chalmers, David J., *The Conscious Mind: In Search of a Fundamental Theory*, Oxford University Press, 1996. Extends the Mary's Room argument into a systematic two-dimensional framework and a defence of phenomenal consciousness as a non-physical entity; directly relevant to acquaintance with phenomenal properties.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Fumerton, Richard, *Metaepistemology and Skepticism*, Rowman & Littlefield, 1995. A defence of acquaintance-based foundationalism in contemporary epistemology; argues that knowledge by acquaintance provides the ultimate foundation for all empirical justification.

- Conee, Earl, "Phenomenal Knowledge," *Australasian Journal of Philosophy*, 72:2 (1994), pp. 136–150. Examines the relationship between phenomenal consciousness and knowledge by acquaintance; argues that acquaintance with phenomenal qualities is sufficient for knowing them.

- Gertler, Brie, "In Defense of Mind-Body Dualism," in *Thinking about the Mind*, ed. Joel Feinberg and Russ Shafer-Landau, Wadsworth, 2005. Develops an acquaintance-based argument for property dualism; argues that we have direct acquaintance with phenomenal properties that cannot be identified with any physical property.

- Nemirow, Laurence, "Physicalism and the Cognitive Role of Acquaintance," in *Mind and Cognition*, ed. William Lycan, Blackwell, 1990. Defends the ability hypothesis against the knowledge argument: what Mary gains is not propositional knowledge but knowing-how—the ability to recognize, remember, and imagine the relevant experiences.

- Lewis, David, "What Experience Teaches," in *Papers in Metaphysics and Epistemology*, Cambridge University Press, 1999. Lewis's ability hypothesis: Mary's Room shows not that there is a new fact she learns but that she gains new abilities (to recognize red, to imagine it), and these abilities can be explained without dualism.

- Tye, Michael, *Consciousness, Color, and Content*, MIT Press, 2000. Defends a physicalist account of phenomenal consciousness that includes a response to the Mary case; argues that phenomenal properties are representational properties that can in principle be known independently of acquaintance.

- Dokic, Jérôme and Proust, Joëlle (eds.), *Simulation and Knowledge of Action*, John Benjamins, 2002. Includes essays on the relationship between simulation, phenomenal acquaintance, and practical knowledge.

- Sosa, Ernest, *A Virtue Epistemology*, Oxford University Press, 2007. Engages the role of acquaintance in epistemological foundationalism and argues for a virtue-theoretic alternative that does not require acquaintance as a foundational epistemic relation.

- Fumerton, Richard, "Acquaintance with and without Justification," in *Internalism and Externalism in Semantics and Epistemology*, ed. Sanford Goldberg, Oxford University Press, 2007. Examines how acquaintance can serve as a foundational justificatory relation; distinguishes different conceptions of acquaintance and their epistemological roles.
