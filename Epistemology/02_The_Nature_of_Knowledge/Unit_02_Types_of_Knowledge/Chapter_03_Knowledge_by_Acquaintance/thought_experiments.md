## Thought Experiments on Knowledge by Acquaintance

---

**Mary's Room**

**The scenario:** Mary is a neuroscientist who has spent her entire life in a black-and-white room. She has learned all the physical facts about color vision—every wavelength, every neural pathway, every behavioral disposition associated with seeing red. She knows, in complete physical detail, what happens in a human brain when a person sees a ripe tomato. One day, Mary leaves the room and sees a red tomato for the first time. Jackson's question: does she learn something new?

**The philosophical point:** Jackson argues that Mary does learn something new—what it is *like* to see red—and that this new knowledge cannot be a new physical fact (since she already knew all the physical facts). Therefore, physicalism is false: phenomenal knowledge (knowledge of what it is like to have an experience) is a category of knowledge that cannot be captured by any physical description, however complete. The knowledge argument specifically implicates knowledge by acquaintance: Mary gains acquaintance with the phenomenal property of redness, and this acquaintance-based knowledge is what was missing from her purely descriptive physical knowledge. Defenders of physicalism respond with the ability hypothesis (she gains new abilities, not new propositional knowledge), the phenomenal concepts strategy (she gains new concepts of the same facts), and the old fact/new way account.

**What Is It Like to Be a Bat?**

**The scenario:** Nagel asks us to consider a bat, which navigates its environment through echolocation—a perceptual modality utterly unlike human senses. We can describe the physical processes involved in echolocation precisely; we can describe the bat's behavior; we can give a complete functional account of how the bat's sonar system works. But can we know what it is like to experience echolocation from the inside—the subjective character of the bat's perceptual experience?

**The philosophical point:** Nagel argues that the subjective character of experience—what he calls the "what it's like" aspect—is not capturable by any objective, third-person physical or functional description. This is not merely an epistemic limitation of our current science but a structural feature of the relationship between objective description and subjective experience. The bat's experience is accessible only from the bat's point of view, through the kind of immediate, first-personal acquaintance that constitutes phenomenal knowledge. The thought experiment supports the claim that knowledge by acquaintance is genuinely irreducible and that there are truths about consciousness that cannot be captured in the vocabulary of third-person science.

**Russell's Bismarck**

**The scenario:** Russell considers knowledge of the historical figure Bismarck. Those who met Bismarck personally had a kind of knowledge of him—acquaintance with the man as perceived—that is unavailable to us. We know Bismarck only through descriptions: "the first German Chancellor," "the man who unified Germany," "the man who introduced social insurance." No description we form, however detailed and accurate, captures what it is like to be acquainted with Bismarck himself. In contrast, Bismarck, knowing himself from the inside, knew himself directly and immediately.

**The philosophical point:** The Bismarck case illustrates Russell's claim that acquaintance-based knowledge of individuals is richer and more direct than description-based knowledge, and that description always leaves open the question of whether the description is uniquely satisfied. It also raises the privacy problem for acquaintance: what one person knows by acquaintance is in principle inaccessible to others, who must rely on descriptions. This creates an asymmetry between first-person and third-person knowledge that poses a challenge for public epistemology: if the bedrock of knowledge is private acquaintance, how can we communicate about it, or how can we know that others' acquaintance-based knowledge matches our own?

**The Color Scientist Who Never Sees Red**

**The scenario:** Consider a complete inversion of Mary's case: a physicist who knows every physical fact about perception has, through some unusual visual condition, never experienced the color red—perhaps she is chronically unable to open her eyes in red-containing environments. She is an expert on the physics and neuroscience of color. She meets a normal-sighted person and asks: "Is what you call 'seeing red' like anything in particular?" The sighted person says: "Yes, it's that distinctive quality you get when you see a fire engine." The scientist cannot form any conception of what this quality is like.

**The philosophical point:** This variant of the Mary case focuses attention on the privacy problem for acquaintance. The scientist's situation reveals that descriptions of phenomenal properties ("the distinctive quality you get when you see a fire engine") are not sufficient to convey acquaintance with those properties. Even an accurate description of the cause of the experience does not give the scientist acquaintance with the phenomenal character of the experience. This suggests that acquaintance with phenomenal properties cannot be achieved through descriptive means—no matter how accurate the description—and that the knowledge conveyed by acquaintance is genuinely distinct from any propositional knowledge that could be expressed in language.

**The Self-Acquaintance Puzzle**

**The scenario:** Russell in 1912 held that we are acquainted with ourselves as the subject of our experiences. But consider: when I try to introspect on the self that is doing the introspecting, what do I find? Hume famously found only particular perceptions, never a continuing self. Am I acquainted with a subject, or only with a bundle of experiences? If I am only acquainted with the latter, then the self to which I refer when I use "I" is not an object of acquaintance but only of description—"the subject of these experiences."

**The philosophical point:** This scenario places pressure on Russell's claim that the self is an object of acquaintance. If there is no distinct self over and above the stream of experiences, then the foundationalist picture—in which knowledge by acquaintance with the self grounds first-person knowledge—collapses into the Humean bundle theory. If there is a distinct self that we are acquainted with, it must be accessible in a way that the introspective evidence seems not to support. Russell himself later abandoned the claim of acquaintance with the self, substituting acquaintance with particular mental acts. The puzzle connects knowledge by acquaintance to debates about the unity of consciousness, personal identity, and the epistemology of the first-person pronoun.
