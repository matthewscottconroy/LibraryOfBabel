# We Know More Than We Can Tell: Polanyi's Tacit Dimension

One of the most intuitively compelling observations in twentieth-century philosophy of knowledge came from Michael Polanyi, a physical chemist turned philosopher, who noticed something obvious that had been largely overlooked: people know vastly more than they can articulate. This observation, developed in *Personal Knowledge* (1958) and *The Tacit Dimension* (1966), became the foundation of Polanyi's epistemology and a persistent challenge to purely propositional accounts of knowledge.

## The Basic Observation

Consider face recognition. You know your friend's face. You would recognize it immediately in a crowd, in different lighting, after twenty years, in photographs, from angles you've never seen before. Yet if someone asks you to describe your friend's face so precisely that a stranger could pick them out, you will fail. The knowledge you have — the deep, reliable, discriminating knowledge that grounds correct recognition — outstrips anything you can put into words. You know more than you can tell.

Polanyi called this kind of knowledge *tacit knowledge*. It is not mere ignorance or the failure to have learned something. It is positive, operative knowledge that guides recognition, judgment, and skilled performance. It is knowledge that cannot be made fully explicit without remainder.

## The Structure of Tacit Knowledge

Polanyi analyzed tacit knowledge in terms of a distinction between *focal* and *subsidiary* awareness. When you hammer a nail, you are focally aware of the nail and subsidiary aware of the feel of the handle in your palm. The subsidiary awareness of the handle guides your action, but it is not the object of your attention. When you think about the hammer — when you make the subsidiary focal — you disrupt the action. The tacit dimension is always the subsidiary stratum.

This structure appears everywhere. When you read a sentence, you are focally aware of the meaning and subsidiary aware of the individual words. When you drive, you are focally aware of the traffic and subsidiary aware of the feel of the steering wheel. Tacit knowledge operates precisely by not being attended to directly.

The move from subsidiary to focal awareness is not a simple process of articulation. In many cases it is destructive. When the wine expert tries to analyze analytically what they are tasting, the holistic judgment dissolves into components that no longer add up to the original perception. When the musician thinks explicitly about their fingering, the performance suffers. Tacit knowledge resists full translation into focal, explicit form.

## Personal Knowledge and the Knower

Polanyi was deeply opposed to the ideal of a fully explicit, impersonal science — the idea that genuine knowledge is knowledge from nowhere, purged of all personal, tacit, and contextual elements. He argued that this ideal is both unattainable and distorting. All knowledge, including scientific knowledge, involves a personal coefficient: the tacit skills, judgments, and background commitments of the knower.

A scientist "sees" that an experimental result is significant, judges that a hypothesis is worth pursuing, integrates observations into a novel interpretation. These acts of seeing, judging, and integrating are tacit skills, not explicit inferences. They are acquired through training and apprenticeship. They cannot be fully reduced to rules that a non-scientist could apply without that training.

This is not relativism or irrationalism. Polanyi held that tacit knowledge is genuine knowledge — reliable, testable, and often more trustworthy than explicit knowledge. The expert's tacit judgment is frequently better than an explicit calculation. But it is personal in the sense that it requires a person with the relevant capacities to exercise it.

## The Transmission Problem

If knowledge is partly tacit, how is it transmitted? You can tell students the rules of chess and physics and grammar; you cannot directly transmit tacit knowledge by telling them anything. Tacit knowledge is acquired through practice, imitation, and apprenticeship — through sustained engagement with a tradition under the guidance of a master.

This has profound implications for education. There are forms of knowledge that simply cannot be conveyed in textbooks, lectures, or explicit instruction. They must be acquired by doing — by being immersed in a practice long enough for the tacit dimension to become internalized. Polanyi's view here anticipates later discussions of situated learning, communities of practice, and the limits of formal instruction.

## Critiques and Developments

Polanyi's critics have asked whether "tacit knowledge" is really knowledge or just reliable dispositions. If you can't articulate it, how do we know it's knowledge rather than mere competence? And if competence suffices, why bring in the epistemological machinery of knowledge at all?

Others have argued that Polanyi understates the extent to which tacit knowledge can be made explicit — or at least approximated — through careful reflection and analysis. The wine expert may not be able to describe the wine fully, but they can communicate quite a lot. The complete inaccessibility of tacit knowledge may be exaggerated.

Nevertheless, the core insight has proven durable. Cognitive science has confirmed that enormous amounts of human competence are processed outside conscious, articulable attention. The explicit and the tacit are distinct dimensions of human knowledge, and a full epistemology must grapple with both.
