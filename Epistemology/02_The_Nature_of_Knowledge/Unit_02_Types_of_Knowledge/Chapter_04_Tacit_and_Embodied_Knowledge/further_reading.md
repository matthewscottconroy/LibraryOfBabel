## Primary Sources

- Polanyi, Michael, *Personal Knowledge: Towards a Post-Critical Philosophy*, University of Chicago Press, 1958. The foundational text of tacit knowledge theory; argues that all knowledge has a personal, irreducibly practical dimension and that the ideal of impersonal, explicit, "objective" knowledge is a distortion.

- Polanyi, Michael, *The Tacit Dimension*, Doubleday, 1966. The most concise statement of the tacit knowledge thesis; the opening sentence—"We can know more than we can tell"—captures the central insight that knowledge outruns its articulation.

- Merleau-Ponty, Maurice, *Phenomenology of Perception*, trans. Colin Smith, Routledge, 1962 (new trans. Donald Landes, Routledge, 2012). The most sustained philosophical account of embodied knowledge; the analysis of body schema, motor intentionality, and the acquisition of motor skills through practice constitutes the phenomenological foundation for tacit knowledge theory.

- Dreyfus, Hubert L. and Dreyfus, Stuart E., *Mind over Machine: The Power of Human Intuition and Expertise in the Era of the Computer*, Free Press, 1986. Develops the five-stage model of skill acquisition and argues that expert performance transcends any propositional or rule-based representation.

- Ryle, Gilbert, *The Concept of Mind*, Hutchinson, 1949. Chapter 2's distinction between knowing-how and knowing-that is the conceptual ancestor of tacit knowledge theory; Ryle's dispositional analysis of intelligent performance is a precursor to Polanyi's and Dreyfus's accounts.

- Collins, Harry, *Tacit and Explicit Knowledge*, University of Chicago Press, 2010. A systematic taxonomy of types of tacit knowledge from a sociological perspective; distinguishes relational tacit knowledge (that could in principle be made explicit), somatic tacit knowledge (bodily), and collective tacit knowledge (distributed across communities).

## Key Secondary Sources

- Nonaka, Ikujiro and Takeuchi, Hirotaka, *The Knowledge-Creating Company: How Japanese Companies Create the Dynamics of Innovation*, Oxford University Press, 1995. Applies Polanyi's tacit/explicit distinction to organizational knowledge and knowledge management; the SECI model (socialization, externalization, combination, internalization) is widely used in knowledge management theory.

- Reber, Arthur S., *Implicit Learning and Tacit Knowledge: An Essay on the Cognitive Unconscious*, Oxford University Press, 1993. Reviews the experimental psychology of implicit learning—the acquisition of complex competences without conscious awareness—and connects it to Polanyi's tacit knowledge.

- Johnson, Mark, *The Body in the Mind: The Bodily Basis of Meaning, Imagination, and Reason*, University of Chicago Press, 1987. Argues that cognitive schemas—structures of embodied experience—underlie abstract thought; develops a theory of image schemas that provides a cognitive-linguistic analog to tacit bodily knowledge.

- Varela, Francisco J., Thompson, Evan, and Rosch, Eleanor, *The Embodied Mind: Cognitive Science and Human Experience*, MIT Press, 1991. Develops an "enactivist" account of cognition that draws on phenomenology and cognitive science; argues that cognition is embodied, environmentally embedded action.

- Clark, Andy, *Being There: Putting Brain, Body, and World Together Again*, MIT Press, 1997. Develops an "embodied, embedded" cognitive science that emphasizes the role of the body and environment in cognition; relevant to tacit and embodied knowledge as forms of distributed cognitive competence.

- Wittgenstein, Ludwig, *Philosophical Investigations*, trans. G. E. M. Anscombe, Blackwell, 1953. The rule-following considerations (§§138–242) bear directly on tacit knowledge: following a rule is a practice that cannot be grounded in an explicit rule for applying the rule; mastery is a kind of tacit competence.

- Turner, Stephen, *The Social Theory of Practices: Tradition, Tacit Knowledge and Presuppositions*, University of Chicago Press, 1994. Examines the social dimensions of tacit knowledge from a sociological and philosophical perspective; engages with Wittgenstein, Polanyi, and Bourdieu.

- Hubert Dreyfus, *Being-in-the-World: A Commentary on Heidegger's Being and Time, Division I*, MIT Press, 1991. Connects Heidegger's analysis of absorbed coping and the ready-to-hand to the cognitive science debate about tacit and explicit knowledge.
