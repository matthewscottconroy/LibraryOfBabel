## Michael Polanyi (1891–1976)

Polanyi, a scientist turned philosopher, developed tacit knowledge theory as a critique of what he saw as the positivist ideal of impersonal, explicit, fully articulable knowledge. His central claim—"we can know more than we can tell"—identifies a dimension of practical competence, skilled judgment, and personal commitment that underlies and exceeds any explicit formulation. The distinction between focal and subsidiary awareness is central: when a surgeon uses a scalpel, she attends *focally* to the tissue being cut and only *subsidiarily* to the pressure of the instrument in her hand; the subsidiary awareness guides the focal act without being itself the object of attention. For Polanyi, this structure pervades all knowledge: we always know from something (subsidiary) toward something (focal), and the subsidiary dimension is irreducibly tacit.

## Maurice Merleau-Ponty (1908–1961)

Merleau-Ponty's phenomenology of perception provides the philosophical foundation for theories of embodied knowledge. His concept of the "body schema"—the pre-reflective, practical understanding of one's body's capacities and orientation in space—describes a form of knowledge that is neither conscious propositional knowledge nor unconscious mechanical process but a bodily intentionality that orients action without explicit representation. The body schema is not a mental map of the body but the body's actual way of being organized toward a world of practical possibilities. His account of motor habit—the way a new skill is incorporated into the body schema—describes the phenomenology of tacit knowledge acquisition in terms more precise than Polanyi's provides.

## Hubert Dreyfus (1929–2017)

Dreyfus is the foremost philosopher of anti-intellectualism about skill and the most philosophically rigorous critic of representationalist cognitive science. His five-stage model of skill acquisition (novice, advanced beginner, competent, proficient, expert) describes a progressive transformation of the relationship between explicit rules and embodied performance: the novice follows explicit rules; the competent practitioner begins to perceive situations holistically; the expert responds directly and non-inferentially to the situation as a whole, without applying rules. Dreyfus argued that this final stage of expertise is genuinely non-representational—the expert's performance cannot be captured in any set of rules or propositions—and that this poses a principled objection to classical AI and to any intellectualist account of tacit knowledge.

## Harry Collins (born 1943)

Collins, a sociologist of science, has developed the most systematic taxonomy of tacit knowledge available. He distinguishes three types: *relational* tacit knowledge (knowledge that could in principle be made explicit but has not been, for social or practical reasons); *somatic* tacit knowledge (knowledge embedded in bodily skills, which in principle could not be fully articulated because the body's processing exceeds conscious access); and *collective* tacit knowledge (knowledge distributed across social practices and communities that cannot be extracted from those practices and made explicit by any individual). Collins's analysis of the transmission of scientific knowledge—particularly his studies of gravitational wave detection—shows how much scientific knowing depends on tacit, socially embedded competences.

## Mark Johnson (born 1949)

Johnson, working in the tradition of cognitive linguistics with George Lakoff, argues that the structures of embodied sensorimotor experience—what he calls "image schemas"—form the basis of abstract conceptual thought through metaphorical extension. Schemas like CONTAINMENT, BALANCE, FORCE, and PATH arise from recurring patterns of bodily experience and are "projected" onto abstract domains, organizing our understanding of causation, time, morality, and logic. This is a theory of tacit embodied knowledge as cognitively foundational: abstract reasoning is not independent of bodily experience but is shaped by and continuous with it.

## Andy Clark (born 1957)

Clark's work on the "extended mind" and embodied cognition argues that cognitive processes are not confined to the skull but extend into the body and even into the environment. His account of "scaffolded" cognition—the way bodily action and environmental props take over cognitive tasks that would otherwise require explicit mental representation—provides a cognitive-scientific framework for tacit embodied knowledge. The expert's competence is not fully "in the head" but is distributed across bodily skills, environmental affordances, and tool use; this resonates with Polanyi's emphasis on the role of equipment and instruments in tacit knowing.
