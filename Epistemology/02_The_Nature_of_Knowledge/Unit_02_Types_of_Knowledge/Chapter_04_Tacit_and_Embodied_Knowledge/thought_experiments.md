## Thought Experiments on Tacit and Embodied Knowledge

---

**Polanyi's Probe**

**The scenario:** A blind person explores a surface with a stick. At first, she is aware of the pressure and motion of the stick in her hand—the stick itself is an object of attention. With practice, the stick becomes transparent: the person attends *through* the stick to the surface being explored, as though her sense of touch extended to the stick's tip. She is now subsidiarily aware of the stick and focally aware of the surface. The tacit knowledge encoded in her bodily relationship to the stick enables this perceptual extension.

**The philosophical point:** Polanyi uses this example to illustrate the structure of all tool-use and all tacit knowing: the instrument (or skill, or conceptual framework) through which we know something becomes transparent when we know it well enough. We attend from it to its distal object. This focal/subsidiary structure cannot be reversed without disrupting the knowing: if the blind person begins to attend focally to the pressure in her palm, she loses the sense of the distant surface. The example generalizes to scientific instruments, theoretical frameworks, and conceptual tools: the expert scientist does not attend to her conceptual framework as an object but through it to the phenomena she is studying. Making tacit subsidiary awareness explicit necessarily destroys its functional role in knowledge.

**The Bicycle Rider Who Cannot Explain**

**The scenario:** Ask a skilled cyclist to explain exactly what she does to maintain balance. She may say "I lean into turns" or "I adjust my center of gravity." But if she actually tried to execute explicit balance-maintaining instructions consciously—"now lean 7 degrees to the left, now apply 2 kg of pressure to the left pedal"—she would fall off. The explicit knowledge she can articulate does not capture, and if followed exclusively would disrupt, the tacit embodied knowledge she actually deploys.

**The philosophical point:** This case illustrates the priority and irreducibility of tacit embodied knowledge over its explicit articulation. The tacit knowledge of balance is not "implicit" propositional knowledge waiting to be made explicit—it is a mode of bodily organization that operates below the level of propositional representation and that resists faithful translation into propositions. This has implications for debates about knowing-how: if cycling-knowledge is not propositional, then Stanley-Williamson intellectualism is undermined by the fact that the propositional knowledge one can articulate differs systematically from the practical knowledge one deploys. It also connects to Dreyfus's critique of classical AI: any system that tries to cycle by applying explicit rules will fall off.

**The Novice and the Expert Surgeon**

**The scenario:** A novice surgical trainee learns to perform a laparoscopic procedure from an instruction manual and a demonstration. She knows the steps: make the incision here, angle the instrument at 30 degrees, cut along this line. When she actually operates, she performs each step consciously, checking it against her memorized instructions, moving cautiously. A senior surgeon operating nearby performs the same procedure in a smooth, continuous flow, adjusting continuously and intuitively to the patient's individual anatomy without conscious deliberation.

**The philosophical point:** Dreyfus's five-stage model is illustrated: the novice is at stage one (following explicit rules), the senior surgeon at stage five (expert non-representational response). The senior surgeon's knowledge is more than the novice's and cannot be fully transmitted by the manual that the novice follows. The tacit knowledge of the expert—her feel for tissue, her calibrated sense of how much pressure is appropriate, her immediate recognition of anomalous presentations—is acquired through thousands of hours of practice and embodied in her perceptual and motor system in a way that cannot be extracted as an instruction set. This raises questions about the transmission of expertise: how do we train surgeons if the crucial knowledge is irreducibly tacit? Polanyi's answer is through apprenticeship—prolonged, supervised practice in the presence of a master—not through the study of explicit knowledge alone.

**Merleau-Ponty's Typist**

**The scenario:** An experienced typist is asked where the letter "Q" is on the keyboard. She hesitates—she does not immediately know the answer propositionally. Yet she types fluently at 80 words per minute, "finding" each key without conscious search. If she pauses to deliberately locate "Q," she may trace out the keyboard with her fingers before she can say it is in the top-left area. Her knowledge of the keyboard layout is in her hands, not in her head.

**The philosophical point:** Merleau-Ponty uses examples like this to show that skilled motor knowledge is organized in the body schema and may not be accessible to reflective, propositional thought. The typist's knowledge is genuinely embodied—it resides in a motor organization of the fingers that coordinates their movements in response to intention-to-type-the-letter without mediating any conscious spatial representation. The fact that the typist cannot immediately answer "where is Q?" without motor consultation shows that the knowledge is organized for action, not for description. This supports Polanyi's claim that tacit knowing is irreducibly different from explicit knowing, and challenges any account that treats the typist's motor knowledge as a disguised form of propositional knowledge.

**Wittgenstein's Rule-Following and Tacit Grasp**

**The scenario:** Wittgenstein considers a pupil who has been taught to continue a mathematical series: 2, 4, 6, 8, ... When she reaches 1000, she writes: 1004, 1008, 1012 ... She has followed a different rule, we say. But what rule did she follow to 1000? And what makes it the case that she was following "add 2" rather than "add 2 up to 1000, then add 4"? No explicit formulation of the rule specifies its own application; the application is always a further step not determined by the formulation alone.

**The philosophical point:** Wittgenstein's rule-following considerations bear directly on tacit knowledge: the competence to follow a rule correctly is not constituted by any explicit grasp of the rule's formulation. If it were, we would need a further rule for applying the first rule, and a further rule for applying that, generating an infinite regress. Instead, competent rule-following is a practice—a way of going on that is trained into us through participation in a form of life. The "correct" application is determined not by any explicit standard but by the practice of the community. This Wittgensteinian point connects to Polanyi's emphasis on the community of inquiry and to Collins's collective tacit knowledge: rule-following competence is irreducibly social and tacit, not fully articulable by any individual member of the practice.
