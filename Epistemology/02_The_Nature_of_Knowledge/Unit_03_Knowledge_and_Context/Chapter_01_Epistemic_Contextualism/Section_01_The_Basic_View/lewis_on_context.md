# David Lewis on Context and Knowledge

David Lewis's 1996 paper "Elusive Knowledge" is one of the most elegant and influential contributions to epistemic contextualism. It opens with a frank admission: "We know a lot. I know what food I had for breakfast, many common truths of logic and mathematics, countless facts about the world around me... Yet there is a lot I do not know: I have not yet read today's newspaper... I do not know the exact ratio of the circumference of a circle to its diameter." So far, so ordinary. But then the skeptical challenge: "how can I know the food I had for breakfast if I might be dreaming? how can I know even simple truths of logic if I might be deceived by a malicious demon?"

Lewis's answer is a sophisticated form of contextualism that he calls *fallibilism-plus-contextualism*, and it centers on a rule about which alternatives must be "properly ignored."

## The Elimination Account

Lewis works within what he calls the "eliminativist" or "relevant alternatives" picture of knowledge: "S knows that P iff S's evidence eliminates every possibility in which not-P." But immediately he adds: except for possibilities that may be properly ignored. The crucial move is that what can be properly ignored varies with context. Knowledge is almost always possible, because in normal contexts many possibilities are properly ignored. The skeptic is right that we can't rule out the demon — but in ordinary contexts, the demon possibility is properly ignored. Raise the demon in conversation, and you destroy that possibility's being properly ignored; knowledge claims can then fail.

## The Rules of Relevance

Lewis proposes a set of rules governing which possibilities may be properly ignored. The most important:

**The Rule of Actuality**: The actual world is never properly ignored. Whatever is actually the case must be considered; you cannot rule out by ignoring what is in fact true.

**The Rule of Belief**: Possibilities that the subject believes to obtain, or should believe to obtain, cannot be ignored. If you believe it might be raining, the rainy possibility is relevant.

**The Rule of Resemblance**: Possibilities that closely resemble actual or believed possibilities are salient. If I am actually being deceived about my car's location, possibilities involving deception about similar things become relevant.

**The Rule of Attention**: Here is the key rule for Lewis's contextualism: *a possibility that is being attended to cannot be properly ignored.* If skeptical scenarios are mentioned in the conversation — if the brain-in-a-vat hypothesis has been put on the table — then those possibilities are attended to and cannot be ignored. Knowledge attributions that required ignoring them are now false.

This explains the elusiveness of knowledge that Lewis finds so striking. The moment you try to examine whether you know P against the backdrop of radical skepticism, you attend to the skeptical possibilities, they become relevant, and knowledge slips away. Knowledge is "elusive" precisely because attending to skeptical hypotheses destroys the context in which knowledge claims are true.

## Fallibilism and the Lottery

Lewis uses his framework to explain a persistent puzzle about lotteries. You seem to know many things that are far less probable than winning a lottery. Yet you don't seem to know that you will lose a lottery before the draw. Why not?

Lewis's answer: the possibility that you will win is salient in a way that equally-improbable alternatives in other domains are not. The lottery makes the alternative possibility vivid and attended-to. So you can't properly ignore the possibility that your ticket wins, even though it is very unlikely. Lottery propositions are examples of propositions where we cannot claim knowledge even with high probability, because the improbable alternative is always attended to.

## "Elusive Knowledge" and the Semantic Framework

One philosophically important feature of Lewis's account is that he is explicit about what kind of claim he is making. He is not saying that in skeptical contexts we merely *speak loosely* when we deny knowledge. He is saying that "knows" is genuinely a context-sensitive term, and that the context determines which possibilities must be eliminated for the attribution to be true. This is a semantic claim with real teeth.

Lewis also acknowledges a peculiar consequence: writing this paper may have destroyed some of the author's knowledge. By attending carefully to radical skeptical possibilities throughout the paper, Lewis has himself entered a context in which those possibilities are no longer properly ignored. When he sets the paper down and goes about his day, the context shifts back, and ordinary knowledge is restored. This is the "elusive" quality — knowledge comes and goes with context, not with any change in one's evidence or the facts.

## Influence and Criticism

Lewis's paper generated enormous discussion. Critics have pressed the standard contextualist objections: the no-disagreement problem (skeptic and commonsense speaker are just in different contexts), the blindspot problem (ordinary speakers don't know they're using a context-sensitive term), and the charge that the skeptical challenge is being deflected rather than answered.

Others have praised Lewis for giving a precise, formally tractable account of the intuition that epistemic standards shift with context. His rules of relevance, especially the Rule of Attention, remain widely cited in discussions of how skeptical scenarios affect ordinary knowledge claims.
