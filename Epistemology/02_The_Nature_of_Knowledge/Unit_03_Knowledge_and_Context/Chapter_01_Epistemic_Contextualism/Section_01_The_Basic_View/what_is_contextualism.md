# What Is Epistemic Contextualism?

Epistemic contextualism is the view that the word "knows" — and related knowledge-attributing language — is context-sensitive. Just as "tall" means something different when said of a basketball player and a five-year-old, "knows" shifts its meaning depending on the conversational context in which it is used. This might seem like a minor semantic claim, but it has sweeping implications for epistemology, and in particular for how we should respond to skeptical arguments.

## The Core Idea

The contextualist's central observation is that ordinary knowledge attributions seem perfectly fine in everyday life but seem false when philosophers raise skeptical scenarios. You know where your keys are — obviously. But do you know you're not a brain in a vat? That seems much harder. The skeptic says: if you don't know you're not in a vat, and you'd have all the same experiences even if you were, then how can you know anything about the external world? Yet dismissing all ordinary knowledge claims seems absurd.

Contextualism offers a resolution: both the everyday verdict and the skeptical verdict are correct, but they are operating in different contexts where "knows" means something different. In ordinary low-stakes contexts, the standards for knowledge are modest, and you do know where your keys are. In high-standards philosophical contexts, where skeptical possibilities have been made salient, the standards rise, and the same attributions can truthfully be denied. The word "knows" is doing different work in different contexts, just like "flat" does different work when you're describing a road versus a precision-machined surface.

## The Relevant Alternatives Framework

Contextualism is often developed within the framework of relevant alternatives: you know that P only if you can rule out the relevant alternatives to P. What counts as a relevant alternative shifts with context. In ordinary contexts, the alternative "the keys are in the ignition rather than on the hook" might be relevant; the alternative "you are a Cartesian demon's victim" is not. In a philosophical seminar raising radical skeptical hypotheses, the latter alternative becomes relevant, and cannot be ruled out.

The key move is that relevance is not fixed — it varies with what has been mentioned, what is at stake, and what alternatives have been brought into conversational focus. Contextualism makes this variation semantic: the extension of "knows" literally changes.

## The Semantics of Context-Sensitive Terms

Contextualism locates this variation in the semantics of "knows" itself. Compare:

- *Gradable adjectives*: "tall," "flat," "empty," "heavy" — terms whose application depends on a contextually determined standard.
- *Indexicals*: "here," "now," "I" — terms whose reference shifts systematically with context.
- *Epistemic modals*: "might," "must," "probably" — these seem to shift with conversational context in ways that have been explored in formal semantics.

Contextualists typically align "knows" with gradable adjectives. Just as it can be true and false in different contexts to say "the table is flat" (false when a micrometer is available; true when the tablecloth is being laid), it can be true and false in different contexts to say "she knows where her keys are."

## Why Contextualism Matters

The attraction of contextualism is that it allows the epistemologist to hold onto two things simultaneously: first, that ordinary knowledge claims are mostly correct — we do know a great deal; second, that the skeptic is right to raise doubts in the contexts they raise them. There is no single fact of the matter about whether you "know"; there is only what is true relative to a context.

This also captures something important about ordinary epistemic practice. We do adjust our knowledge-attributing standards depending on the situation. Before surgery, we demand much more certainty than before a casual dinner decision. These adjustments feel natural, and contextualism explains them semantically rather than treating them as mere looseness in ordinary speech.

## Some Initial Worries

Not everyone is persuaded. The most immediate worry is *semantic blindspot*: ordinary speakers don't seem to think they are using a context-sensitive term when they use "knows." When someone says "I know I left my keys on the table," they seem to mean something absolute, not something relative to a context. If they are using a context-sensitive term, they don't seem to know it.

A second worry concerns the diagnosis of disagreement. If you say "she knows where her keys are" in an everyday context and the skeptic says "she doesn't know" in a philosophical context, are they disagreeing? Contextualism implies they are talking past each other — but they seem to be in direct conflict. This is the "no-disagreement problem."

A third worry is that contextualism deflates the significance of the skeptical challenge rather than addressing it. The skeptic who raises the brain-in-a-vat hypothesis is not merely changing conversational standards; they seem to be raising a genuine question about the relationship between experience and the world. Contextualism re-describes this as a context-shift rather than engaging it.

These worries will be explored in detail as we examine specific forms of contextualism and their rivals.
