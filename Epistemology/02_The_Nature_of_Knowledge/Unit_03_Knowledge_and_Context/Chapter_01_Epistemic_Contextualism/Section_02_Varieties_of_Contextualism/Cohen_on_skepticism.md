# Stewart Cohen on Contextualism and the Skeptical Problem

Stewart Cohen is one of the most systematic defenders of epistemic contextualism, and his work is notable for its tight focus on what contextualism can offer as a response to philosophical skepticism. Where DeRose emphasizes the bank cases and stakes-sensitive knowledge attribution, Cohen focuses on the philosophical import: why does the skeptical argument seem to work in philosophy class, and why should we not be troubled when we leave and resume ordinary life?

## The Skeptical Argument Revisited

The core of the Cartesian skeptical argument runs something like this:

1. I don't know that I'm not a brain in a vat (BIV).
2. If I don't know that I'm not a BIV, then I don't know that I have hands.
3. Therefore, I don't know that I have hands.

The argument seems valid, and each premise seems plausible. But the conclusion seems absurd. Ordinary people know all kinds of things. How do we resist this argument without committing to either dogmatism (dismissing the skeptical scenario without justification) or skepticism (accepting the conclusion)?

Cohen argues that contextualism offers the most satisfying response available. In the ordinary context, you do know you have hands — and you don't need to be able to rule out BIV scenarios, because those scenarios are not relevant alternatives in that context. In the philosophical context where the argument is being raised, the BIV scenario has been made relevant, and in that context premise 2 is true, and the argument goes through. But this doesn't threaten ordinary knowledge, which is evaluated in a different context.

## The Core Mechanism

Cohen's version of contextualism focuses on which alternatives are contextually relevant. An alternative is relevant if it is "live" in the conversation — if it has been mentioned, if there is reason to think it might obtain, if the context makes it salient. The set of relevant alternatives is not fixed by nature but by the epistemic practices and conversational norms of a community.

This is closely related to the relevant alternatives tradition in epistemology: knowledge requires the ability to discriminate the actual situation from relevant alternatives, but not from all conceivable alternatives. What makes an alternative relevant is partly a semantic and partly a social-pragmatic matter, and Cohen thinks the contextualist account of how standards shift with context provides the right way to understand it.

## Epistemic Standards and Communities

Cohen emphasizes that epistemic standards are not just individual or contextual — they are also community-relative. There are communities with high epistemic standards (scientific communities verifying experimental results, legal communities demanding proof beyond reasonable doubt) and communities with low standards (casual conversational contexts). The word "knows" adapts its meaning to the standards operative in the community or context of utterance.

This has an interesting corollary. Cohen argues that low-standards communities are not thereby epistemically inferior to high-standards communities. Whether knowing-in-context-C is more valuable than knowing-in-context-C* depends on what the standards are tracking. High standards track more demanding epistemic ideals but don't reveal that low-standards knowledge claims are false — they were true in their own context.

## Cohen and Closure

One of Cohen's important contributions is his treatment of the closure principle. Closure says: if you know P, and you know that P entails Q, then you know Q. Applied to skepticism: if you know you have hands, and you know that having hands entails not being a BIV without hands, then you know you're not a BIV. But if you don't know you're not a BIV, and you know the entailment, then you don't know you have hands — the skeptical argument again.

Cohen's contextualism preserves closure within a context. Within an ordinary context, if "knows" is set to a low standard, closure holds: from knowing you have hands, you can derive knowledge that you're not a BIV (at the ordinary standard). Within a philosophical context, if "knows" is set to a high standard, closure also holds, but now the BIV skeptical possibility is not ruled out, so you don't know you have hands — correctly.

This is elegant: closure is preserved universally, and yet the skeptical argument doesn't threaten ordinary knowledge, because the argument operates in a context with elevated standards.

## Criticisms and Responses

The standard objections apply here as well. Timothy Williamson and others press the claim that when contextualists say "you know in the ordinary context," they are conceding too little. We want to say that you genuinely, robustly know — not just that you satisfy some contextually relative standard. Cohen's response: there is no context-independent notion of "genuine" knowledge that goes beyond what contextualism provides. The urge for context-transcendent knowledge is a philosophical illusion.

Another objection: if the skeptic's argument is sound in the skeptical context, isn't the skeptic's conclusion true? And doesn't that mean something troubling? Cohen's response is that the truth in a high-standards context doesn't have any practical or epistemic import for everyday life, which operates in ordinary contexts. The skeptic can be right in their context and yet leave ordinary knowledge entirely unthreatened.

This response is either satisfying or deeply unsatisfying depending on whether you think the skeptic's challenge is really about context-relative standards or about something deeper.
