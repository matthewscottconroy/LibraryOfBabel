# DeRose and the Bank Cases

Keith DeRose's contribution to epistemic contextualism is among the most carefully developed in the literature. His 1992 paper "Contextualism and Knowledge Attributions" introduced a pair of thought experiments — the bank cases — that have become canonical illustrations of the contextualist intuition. DeRose also developed a sophisticated version of contextualism that attempts to respond to the most pressing objections.

## The Bank Cases

DeRose imagines two pairs of situations, differing only in what is at stake:

**Low Stakes**: It is Friday. You and your wife are driving home and discuss whether the bank is open on Saturdays. You recall having been to the bank on a Saturday two weeks ago. You say: "I know it's open on Saturdays." The claim seems fine.

**High Stakes**: Same setup, but now you have an important check that must be deposited by Saturday or you will miss a mortgage payment. Your wife points out that banks change their hours, and this could be a serious problem. You now say: "I'd better go check — I don't know that it's open." This also seems fine.

The point is not merely that you are less confident in the second case. You seem to be denying knowledge in a context where, if the circumstances were less urgent, you would claim knowledge. The same epistemic position — the same evidence, the same memory, the same probability of error — seems to underwrite knowledge in one context and fail to underwrite it in another.

DeRose's contextualism says: this is because "knows" shifts its meaning with context. The epistemic standards governing knowledge attribution are higher in the high-stakes case, and those higher standards are not met.

## DeRose's Contextualist Analysis

DeRose develops contextualism in terms of what he calls the *strength of epistemic position*. The strength of your epistemic position with respect to P is roughly how sensitive your belief-forming processes are to the truth of P — how reliably your belief tracks P across nearby possible worlds. "Knows" is context-sensitive in that the amount of epistemic strength required for knowledge attributions to be true varies with context.

When the stakes are high — or when skeptical possibilities have been raised — the threshold of required epistemic strength increases. When the threshold rises above your actual strength of position, "knows" is false of you. When it falls below, "knows" is true of you.

This is a form of contextualism about the word "knows," not merely about some epistemic standard that floats free of language. The semantics of the word encodes the context-sensitivity. Different people in different conversational contexts who both utter "S knows that P" are saying something true with different truth conditions.

## The Role of Skeptical Hypotheses

DeRose uses his framework to explain why skeptical arguments seem compelling in philosophical contexts but not in everyday life. When a philosopher raises the hypothesis that we might be deceived by a Cartesian demon or living in a simulation, this raises the epistemic standards in that conversational context. In that context, the standards for "knows" are set so high that our ordinary evidence cannot meet them. And so the skeptic's conclusion — that we don't know much — is true in that context.

But when you leave the philosophical seminar and go to buy groceries, the standards drop back. In that ordinary context, your evidence is more than adequate, and "I know where the grocery store is" is true.

This is DeRose's resolution of what he calls the "Skeptical Puzzle": skepticism seems compelling because the skeptical argument genuinely succeeds in its own context; ordinary knowledge seems defensible because it succeeds in ordinary contexts. There is no single answer about whether you "really" know.

## Objections DeRose Considers

DeRose takes seriously the objection that contextualism gives the skeptic too much. If the skeptic is right in their context, that seems to validate the skeptical intuition rather than dismissing it. DeRose responds that the skeptic's context is a special, artificially constructed one that doesn't reveal any interesting limitation on our ordinary cognitive achievements. The skeptical standards are not a better, more rigorous standard that we fail to meet; they are merely higher standards, and there is no privilege in height.

He also addresses the *no-disagreement objection*. If the bank-case high-stakes and low-stakes speakers are just operating in different contexts, they aren't really disagreeing. DeRose acknowledges this is a genuine cost but argues it is offset by the theoretical gains — the resolution of the skeptical puzzle and the natural account of the bank cases.

## Legacy and Influence

DeRose's bank cases have become a staple reference in epistemology. They are used both by contextualists (who see them as evidence for semantic context-sensitivity) and by rivals. The Subject-Sensitive Invariantism of John Hawthorne and Jeremy Fantl/Matthew McGrath accepts the intuitions the bank cases generate but relocates the sensitivity from the semantics of "knows" to what the subject's practical environment determines the subject to know.

The debate between these views hinges on fine-grained semantic and pragmatic questions: Is the variation in knowledge attributions due to genuine semantic context-sensitivity, or due to some pragmatic mechanism that doesn't change truth conditions? The bank cases don't settle this, but they make the phenomenon vivid.
