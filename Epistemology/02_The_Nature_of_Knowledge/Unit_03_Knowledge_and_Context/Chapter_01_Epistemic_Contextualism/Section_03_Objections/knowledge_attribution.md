# Knowledge Attribution and the Pragmatics of "Knows"

Even if one finds contextualism attractive as a diagnosis of the bank cases, there remains the question of how exactly the context-sensitivity works. When we attribute knowledge — when we say "she knows," "he doesn't know," "I know" — what exactly are we doing, and what factors govern whether we get it right? Attending to the pragmatics of knowledge attribution reveals complexity that neither simple invariantism nor simple contextualism handles cleanly.

## The Basic Pragmatics

Speech act theory distinguishes between what is said (the semantic content of an utterance) and what is communicated (what the speaker conveys, including implicature, presupposition, and contextual enrichment). Knowledge attributions can be analyzed at both levels.

At the level of semantics, the question is whether "knows" has fixed or variable truth conditions. At the level of pragmatics, the question is what speakers communicate when they use "knows" in particular contexts — what they presuppose, what they implicate, what contextual enrichments they invite.

Contextualists typically work at the semantic level: the truth conditions of "knows" shift with context. But many philosophers have argued that the variation in knowledge-attributing practice is best explained pragmatically — the truth conditions stay fixed, but what is *communicated* varies.

## Conversational Implicature

H.P. Grice's framework of conversational implicature offers one way to explain stakes-sensitivity without semantic context-sensitivity. In high-stakes situations, asserting "I know P" implicates that your epistemic position is strong enough for the practical decisions at hand. In low-stakes situations, this implicature is absent or weaker. The semantic content of "I know P" is the same in both cases, but what is pragmatically communicated differs.

This explains the bank cases: in the high-stakes scenario, saying "I know the bank is open" would implicate a stronger epistemic position than you actually have, so you decline to say it. This is not because "knows" has changed meaning — it is because asserting "I know" in this context would mislead.

The challenge for this view is explaining why the variation feels like a variation in truth rather than in appropriateness of assertion. The people in the bank cases don't seem to be merely declining to say something misleading; they seem to be acknowledging that they *don't* know.

## The Assertion-Guiding Function of "Knows"

Timothy Williamson's knowledge norm of assertion — the view that one must assert only what one knows — connects to the pragmatics of knowledge attribution. If assertion requires knowledge, then declining to say "I know" in high-stakes contexts is not merely a matter of pragmatics; it reflects a genuine epistemic shortfall. You decline to attribute knowledge to yourself because you genuinely recognize that your evidence doesn't meet the standard for assertion in that context.

But this returns us to the question: is the standard a fixed property of "knows" or a variable one? Williamson takes "knows" to be invariant and the norm to be absolute. The apparent variation is explained by the fact that different contexts call attention to different ways the norm might be violated — not by any change in what the norm requires.

## Perspective-Shifting and Third-Person Attribution

One important consideration is the asymmetry between first-person and third-person knowledge attribution. When you say "I know that P," you are staking a claim about your own epistemic position. When you say "she knows that P," you are making a claim from an external perspective. Contextualism is typically formulated in terms of the context of the attributor, not the subject.

But this generates puzzles. Suppose you are in a high-stakes conversational context and you attribute knowledge to someone who is in a low-stakes situation: "she knows the bus leaves at noon" (she's just curious; you need to catch it to make a surgery). Is the attribution true or false? Contextualism says: false, because you're in a high-stakes context. But intuitively, whether she knows seems to depend on her epistemic position, not yours.

This puzzle about *attributor-sensitivity* versus *subject-sensitivity* is one of the motivations for Subject-Sensitive Invariantism (SSI), which locates the relevant practical facts with the subject rather than the attributor.

## The Diagnostic Value of Attribution

Even if contextualism's semantic claims are wrong, the focus on knowledge attribution has been diagnostically valuable. It has shown that epistemologists can't treat "S knows that P" as a simple relation between a person and a proposition. The social, conversational, and practical dimensions of knowledge attribution are philosophically significant, and any adequate epistemology must account for them — whether at the semantic or pragmatic level.
