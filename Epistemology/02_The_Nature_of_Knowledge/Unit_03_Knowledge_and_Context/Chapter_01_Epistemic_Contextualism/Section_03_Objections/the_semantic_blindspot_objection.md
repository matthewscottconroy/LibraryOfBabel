# The Semantic Blindspot Objection to Contextualism

One of the most persistent objections to epistemic contextualism targets not its practical implications but its semantic claims. The objection is simple to state: ordinary speakers, the people who actually use the word "knows," don't behave as if "knows" is a context-sensitive term. They don't acknowledge that their knowledge attributions are relative to a context; they seem to think they are making claims with fixed, context-independent truth conditions. If contextualism were right, we would expect speakers to be aware of this — but they aren't.

This is the *semantic blindspot objection*, and it has been pressed by Timothy Williamson, John Hawthorne, and others.

## The Structure of the Objection

The argument goes like this. If "knows" were genuinely context-sensitive — like "tall" or "here" — we would expect speakers to have some implicit understanding of this. When you call someone tall, you know that whether that's true depends on the comparison class: tall for a jockey, tall for a basketball player. When you say "here," you know you're pointing to your current location. Speakers have implicit semantic competence that tracks the context-sensitivity of these terms.

With "knows," no such implicit competence is evident. When a speaker says "she knows where the keys are," she doesn't hedge: "well, relative to our current context-standards." She says it flatly, as if making an absolute claim. And when she later says "oh, but she doesn't know — given what she has to do today," she doesn't describe this as a change of context; she describes it as a revision of her assessment.

If contextualism is right about the semantics, speakers are massively ignorant of how their own most-used epistemic vocabulary works. They suffer a systematic semantic blindspot about "knows." This is, at the very least, an uncomfortable result for a semantic theory to have.

## The Charge of Speaker Error

Contextualists have several responses. One response is to accept that speakers have a blindspot: they are simply wrong about the semantics of their own term. This kind of semantic error is not unprecedented. Speakers are often wrong about the meaning of their own words in ways that linguists and philosophers can identify. Contextualism might just be the correct semantic theory even if speakers don't recognize it.

This response has a cost: if we identify the correct semantic theory of a term by looking at how competent speakers use and understand it, then massive divergence between the theory and speaker understanding is evidence against the theory. The contextualist must explain why speaker understanding is systematically misleading on this particular issue.

## The Invariantist Alternative

The blindspot objection motivates *invariantism* — the view that "knows" has a fixed semantic content that doesn't shift with context. There are two main versions:

**Low-standards invariantism** (sometimes called *permissivism*) holds that "knows" always means something like "has adequate evidence by ordinary standards," and that the ordinary claims people make are largely true. The skeptic's claims are false and reflect an unreasonable elevation of standards.

**High-standards invariantism** (or *skeptical invariantism*) holds that "knows" always sets very high standards, and that the skeptic is right — we know very little. The ordinary knowledge claims are false, though we speak loosely.

Neither alternative is fully satisfying. Low-standards invariantism has trouble explaining why skeptical arguments seem compelling. High-standards invariantism has trouble explaining why we feel entitled to our ordinary knowledge claims.

## Pragmatic Rescue?

A more sophisticated contextualist response tries to separate the semantic from the pragmatic. Perhaps "knows" has invariant semantic content, but there are pragmatic mechanisms — implicature, relevance, conversational norms — that explain why we say different things in different contexts. The stakes don't change what "knows" means; they change what is conversationally appropriate to say. High-stakes contexts generate an implicature of greater certainty; low-stakes contexts don't require that implicature.

This move is associated with Jason Stanley's *Subject-Sensitive Invariantism* (discussed elsewhere) and with pure pragmatic accounts. The challenge for purely pragmatic accounts is explaining why the bank-case variations feel like variations in *truth* rather than variations in *what it is appropriate to say*.

## The Metasemantic Question

Underlying this debate is a metasemantic question: what determines the semantic content of a term? If content is determined by the dispositions of competent speakers, then the blindspot objection is damaging. If content is determined by some deeper theory of meaning that goes beyond what speakers consciously know, the contextualist can resist the objection.

The blindspot objection thus connects to broader debates about semantic methodology — about whether intuitions about truth conditions should be the primary data for semantic theory, and about how much weight speaker ignorance should carry.
