## Primary Sources

- DeRose, Keith, "Contextualism and Knowledge Attributions," *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research*, 52:4 (1992), pp. 913–929. The paper that introduced the bank cases and articulated the basic contextualist thesis that knowledge attributions are context-sensitive in a semantically significant way.

- DeRose, Keith, *The Case for Contextualism: Knowledge, Skepticism, and Context*, Oxford University Press, 2009. The most comprehensive book-length defence of epistemic contextualism; responds to objections from subject-sensitive invariantism, relativism, and skepticism.

- Lewis, David, "Elusive Knowledge," *Australasian Journal of Philosophy*, 74:4 (1996), pp. 549–567. Develops the relevant alternatives framework into a contextualist epistemology; proposes seven rules (including the rule of actuality, the rule of resemblance, and the rule of attention) governing which alternatives must be eliminated for knowledge.

- Cohen, Stewart, "Knowledge, Context, and Social Standards," *Synthese*, 73:1 (1987), pp. 3–26. An early and influential defence of contextualism; argues that contextual variations in epistemic standards explain how we can know some things in ordinary contexts while failing to know in skeptical contexts.

- Cohen, Stewart, "Contextualism, Skepticism, and the Structure of Reasons," *Philosophical Perspectives*, 13 (1999), pp. 57–89. Develops the contextualist response to skepticism; argues that in ordinary low-stakes contexts, skeptical alternatives are legitimately ignored.

- Dretske, Fred, "Epistemic Operators," *Journal of Philosophy*, 67:24 (1970), pp. 1007–1023. Develops the relevant alternatives framework prior to its contextualist elaboration; argues that knowledge does not require ruling out all possibilities but only relevant ones.

- Nozick, Robert, *Philosophical Explanations*, Harvard University Press, 1981. Chapter 3's tracking analysis provides the subjunctivist precursor to contextualism; the context-sensitivity of relevant alternatives is implicit in the assessment of sensitivity conditionals.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Preyer, Gerhard and Peter, Georg (eds.), *Contextualism in Philosophy: Knowledge, Meaning, and Truth*, Oxford University Press, 2005. A collection of essays by major contributors to the contextualism debate, covering both linguistic and epistemological dimensions.

- Schiffer, Stephen, "Contextualist Solutions to Skepticism," *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society*, 96 (1996), pp. 317–333. A critical examination of contextualist anti-skeptical strategies; argues that contextualism faces a "now you see it, now you don't" problem with respect to the status of skeptical claims.

- Hawthorne, John, *Knowledge and Lotteries*, Oxford University Press, 2004. The principal defence of subject-sensitive invariantism as an alternative to contextualism; argues that the data that motivates contextualism is better explained by appeal to the practical interests of the *subject* rather than the *attributor*.

- Macfarlane, John, "The Assessment Sensitivity of Knowledge Attributions," in *Oxford Studies in Epistemology*, Vol. 1, eds. Tamar Gendler and John Hawthorne, Oxford University Press, 2005. Develops assessor relativism as an alternative to contextualism; argues that knowledge attributions are sensitive not to the context of utterance but to the context of assessment.

- Stanley, Jason, *Knowledge and Practical Interests*, Oxford University Press, 2005. Defends subject-sensitive invariantism against contextualism; argues that the truth conditions of "S knows p" include S's practical interests, but the standards relevant are those of the subject, not the attributor.

- Rysiew, Patrick, "The Context-Sensitivity of Knowledge Attributions," *Noûs*, 35:4 (2001), pp. 477–514. Challenges contextualism by arguing that apparent context-sensitivity of knowledge attributions can be explained as pragmatic implicature without appealing to semantic context-sensitivity.

- Feldman, Richard, "Skeptical Problems, Contextualist Solutions," *Philosophical Studies*, 103:1 (2001), pp. 61–85. A critical assessment of contextualist anti-skeptical strategies, focusing on the question of whether contextualism genuinely resolves skeptical problems or merely changes the subject.

- Williams, Michael, *Unnatural Doubts: Epistemological Realism and the Basis of Scepticism*, Princeton University Press, 1991. Argues that traditional epistemological skepticism is not a genuine problem but a product of "epistemological realism" (the view that there is a unique, context-independent epistemic status); anticipates themes in contextualism.
