## Fred Dretske (1932–2013)

Dretske's relevant alternatives theory (1970) is the most important precursor to epistemic contextualism. He argued that knowledge does not require ruling out all logical alternatives to what is known but only those alternatives that are *relevant* in the context of inquiry. Knowing that my car is in the parking lot does not require ruling out the possibility that it was stolen by a sophisticated thief who replaced it with an exact replica—this alternative is simply not relevant. Dretske drew the non-closure consequence: we can know p without knowing all the entailments of p, since those entailments may require ruling out irrelevant alternatives. This framework, developed before explicitly contextualist semantics was available, anticipated the central moves of later contextualism.

## David Lewis (1941–2001)

Lewis's "Elusive Knowledge" applied his possible worlds semantics to epistemic contextualism in the most elegant way. Knowledge is defined as the elimination of all possibilities in which p is false, *except those that may properly be ignored*. The context determines which possibilities may be ignored through a system of seven rules: the rule of actuality (actual possibilities cannot be ignored), the rule of belief (possibilities the subject believes obtain cannot be ignored), the rule of resemblance (possibilities resembling actual or believed ones cannot be ignored), and the rule of attention (possibilities attended to in the conversation cannot be ignored). Skeptical arguments work by forcing us to attend to previously ignored possibilities; once attended to, they cannot be ignored, and knowledge claims become false. But in ordinary life, these possibilities are legitimately ignored, and ordinary knowledge claims are true.

## Stewart Cohen (born 1952)

Cohen was one of the first to develop an explicit semantic contextualism about knowledge attributions. His 1987 paper argued that the word "knows" is context-sensitive: it expresses different relations depending on the standards operative in the conversational context. In high-standards contexts (where skeptical alternatives are salient), "S knows p" is false; in low-standards ordinary contexts, it is true. This explains the intuitive validity of both ordinary knowledge claims and skeptical challenges without inconsistency. Cohen argued that contextualism dissolves rather than solves the traditional problem of skepticism: the skeptic raises standards, making "I know" false in the skeptical context, but this does not show that ordinary knowledge claims (made in low-standards contexts) are false.

## Keith DeRose (born 1962)

DeRose is the most systematic and prolific defender of epistemic contextualism. His bank cases are the canonical example: in the low-stakes version, someone claims to know the bank will be open Saturday (having visited last Saturday); in the high-stakes version (where it is crucial that the bank be open), the same person withdraws the claim to know because of the possibility that hours have changed. DeRose argues that what changes between cases is the standard operative in the attributor's context, not any facts about the subject's epistemic position. He developed a "rule of sensitivity"—that an assertion of "S knows p" is warranted only when the standards for knowledge include requiring that S's belief be sensitive in the relevant way—connecting contextualism to the tracking tradition.

## David Sosa (born 1967)

Sosa (son of Ernest Sosa) and, independently, Stephen Schiffer have pressed the "now you see it, now you don't" objection to contextualism: if "I know" is true in ordinary contexts and false in skeptical contexts, then the skeptic's conclusion that "I don't know I'm not a brain in a vat" is true *in the skeptical context*. But is it then true in a more neutral context? Contextualism seems unable to give a context-independent verdict on skeptical claims, making it unclear whether it is a realist or anti-realist position with respect to skepticism. This objection has been developed into a series of challenges about whether contextualism is a substantive epistemological thesis or merely a semantic observation.

## John MacFarlane (born 1968)

MacFarlane developed "assessor relativism" as a rival to DeRose's contextualism. Where contextualism makes the truth of "S knows p" relative to the *speaker's* context (the context in which the attribution is made), MacFarlane makes it relative to the *assessor's* context (the context in which the attribution is evaluated). This allows MacFarlane to explain why past knowledge attributions can be retracted as false in a new context—something contextualism struggles with. Assessor relativism connects epistemology to the broader debate about relativism in the philosophy of language and has implications for the semantics of epistemic modals and evidential markers.
