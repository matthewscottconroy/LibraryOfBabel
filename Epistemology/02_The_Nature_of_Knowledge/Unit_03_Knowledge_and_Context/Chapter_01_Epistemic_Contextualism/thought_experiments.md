## Thought Experiments in Epistemic Contextualism

---

**DeRose's Bank Cases**

**The scenario:** Case A (low stakes): It is Friday afternoon. A couple have driven past the bank and are discussing whether it will be open on Saturday. The wife says "I was there last Saturday and it was open; I know it will be open this Saturday." Her husband says "Fine, let's drive by tomorrow." Case B (high stakes): The same couple, but now there is an important financial matter that absolutely requires them to complete a transaction at the bank on Saturday. The wife says "I was there last Saturday—do you think we can count on it?" The husband says "They may have changed the hours. We'd better not count on knowing it will be open."

**The philosophical point:** In Case A, the wife's claim to know seems true and appropriate; in Case B, the withdrawal of knowledge seems appropriate despite the wife's epistemic position being identical (she was there last Saturday in both cases). DeRose argues that what changes is the standard for knowledge operative in the attributors' context: the high stakes of Case B raise the epistemic standard, making "knows" express a stronger relation. The cases motivate contextualism: the truth conditions of "knows" are context-dependent. Invariantists respond that what changes between the cases is not the truth conditions of knowledge attributions but what it is appropriate to *assert*—the pragmatics of assertion change, not the semantics of "knows."

**Lewis's Conversation-Stopping Demon**

**The scenario:** A group of friends are having an ordinary conversation in which they make various knowledge claims: "I know the train leaves at 9," "I know she's in the office today." A philosopher enters and says: "But you can't rule out that you're a brain in a vat being stimulated to believe all of this." The conversation is derailed. Suddenly the friends feel they cannot claim to know anything.

**The philosophical point:** Lewis's rule of attention explains this: the possibility of a brain-in-a-vat scenario is now "salient"—it has been explicitly raised in the conversation and therefore cannot be "properly ignored." This makes all ordinary knowledge claims false *within the current context*, since knowledge requires eliminating all non-ignorable alternatives. When the philosopher leaves and the conversation reverts to normal, the ordinary knowledge claims become true again—the demon possibility recedes from salience and is once again properly ignored. This illustrates both the power of contextualism to explain why skeptical arguments seem compelling in philosophical contexts and its controversial implication that skeptical arguments do not provide context-independent reasons to doubt ordinary knowledge claims.

**Cohen's Airport Case**

**The scenario:** Two passersby at an airport overhear a conversation. Traveler A says she knows her connecting flight is at gate 32—she looked at the board. Traveler B, for whom missing the connection would be catastrophic, says she needs to check again even though she looked at the same board; she cannot just rely on what she "knows." A third bystander, C, says to A about B: "She's being irrational—she knows it's at gate 32, so she should just go there."

**The philosophical point:** Cohen uses this case to illustrate how stakes affect knowledge attributions. When high stakes are at issue for the subject (Traveler B), the standards for knowledge attribution are raised: the contextualist says that in B's context, "I know the gate is 32" is false even with the same evidence that makes it true in A's context. The bystander C attributes knowledge to B from a low-stakes context—and this attribution is true *from C's perspective* even if it is false from B's perspective. This creates a puzzle for invariantism: if knowledge has a fixed truth condition, how can C's attribution be true and B's denial also be appropriate? The contextualist answer is that the truth conditions of "knows" differ across contexts; the subject-sensitive invariantist answer is that both are tracking a context-independent fact about B's epistemic position, but B's practical interests change what that fact is.

**Dretske's Zebras and Painted Mules**

**The scenario:** At a zoo, a visitor looks at an animal in the zebra enclosure and forms the belief "that is a zebra." Dretske's question: does she need to rule out the possibility that the zoo has painted mules to look like zebras in order to know that the animal is a zebra? Common sense says no—this possibility is too remote to be relevant. Now suppose the visitor is a zoo inspector suspicious of fraud. For her, the painted mule hypothesis is a relevant alternative that must be eliminated before she can know it is a zebra.

**The philosophical point:** Dretske uses this case to argue that which alternatives are relevant to knowledge depends on the context of inquiry. The visitor in an ordinary zoo context need not rule out painted mules; the suspicious inspector must. This shows that knowledge attributions are sensitive to the practical and investigative context in a way that a fixed, context-invariant set of conditions for knowledge cannot capture. Contextualists develop this into a semantic thesis: "knows" picks out different relations in different contexts. The case also illustrates the connection between contextualism and anti-skeptical strategy: the brain-in-a-vat possibility is, in ordinary contexts, as irrelevant as painted mules—we know ordinary things because we are not required by our ordinary epistemic context to rule out such remote possibilities.

**The Detective and the Defense Attorney**

**The scenario:** A detective investigating a murder reaches the conclusion: "I know Jones committed the crime—the evidence is overwhelming." In a court of law, the defense attorney challenges each piece of evidence, raises alternative hypotheses, and argues that the jury cannot know the defendant is guilty. After the argument, can the detective still claim to know Jones is guilty? Can the jury?

**The philosophical point:** This case tests whether standards can be lowered after they have been raised. Once the defense attorney has raised skeptical alternatives (the evidence might have been planted, there might have been an accomplice), the standard for knowledge in the courtroom is elevated. Lewis's rule of attention says these alternatives cannot then be re-ignored. But in ordinary life—outside the courtroom—the detective's colleagues might accurately say "we know Jones did it." Contextualism explains this: "knows" picks out different relations in the courtroom context (high standards, skeptical alternatives salient) and in the investigative context (lower standards, different alternatives relevant). This has implications for legal epistemology and for the relationship between philosophical epistemology and the practical standards of inference in institutional settings.
