# MacFarlane on Assessor Relativism and Knowledge

John MacFarlane has developed one of the most technically sophisticated forms of epistemic relativism, which he calls *assessor-relativism* or *relativism about truth*. His work draws on the formal semantics of natural language and attempts to capture the intuition that knowledge attributions are in some way perspective-dependent — without collapsing into the obvious problems that afflict naive relativism.

## The Semantic Framework: Context of Use vs. Context of Assessment

MacFarlane's key innovation is distinguishing between two kinds of context that bear on the truth of an utterance:

- **Context of use**: The situation in which a sentence is uttered — the speaker, time, location, conversational context.
- **Context of assessment**: The situation from which the truth of the utterance is being evaluated — the perspective of someone assessing whether what was said is true.

Most semantic theories allow context of use to affect truth conditions (indexicals like "I" and "here" work this way). MacFarlane argues that for some expressions — including, on his account, epistemic modals and knowledge attributions — the *context of assessment* also affects truth.

This is the move that distinguishes his view from both standard invariantism (where neither context of use nor context of assessment affects truth) and standard contextualism (where only context of use affects truth).

## The Application to Knowledge

Applied to knowledge attributions, the idea is roughly this. When you assess whether someone knows that P, your assessment is sensitive to the epistemic standards operative in *your* context of assessment, not just in the original speaker's context of use. So if you are in a high-standards context evaluating whether a speaker in a low-standards context spoke truly when they said "she knows where the keys are," the answer depends on *your* standards, not theirs.

This creates a kind of sliding, perspective-relative truth: what the speaker said can be true relative to one context of assessment and false relative to another. The utterance doesn't have a single, fixed truth value — it has a truth value relative to each context of assessment.

## Distinguishing Assessor-Relativism from Contextualism

Contextualism (in DeRose's or Lewis's version) is an *index-sensitive* semantic theory: the truth of a knowledge attribution depends on the context of use (primarily the attributor's context). MacFarlane's assessor-relativism makes truth depend additionally on the context of assessment.

The practical difference is clearest in cases of *retraction*. Suppose you said yesterday in a low-stakes context "I know the bank is open Saturday." Today you are in a high-stakes context and you reassess. On contextualism, your yesterday-statement was true in the yesterday-context, so there is nothing to retract — you were right given the standards then operative. On assessor-relativism, you can coherently say "I was wrong" because the truth of your yesterday-statement is being assessed from today's context, and by today's standards, it is false.

MacFarlane argues that the retraction data actually support relativism over contextualism. Speakers do sometimes retract their past knowledge claims when their epistemic context changes, saying things like "I thought I knew, but I didn't really know." If contextualism were right, these retractions would be unnecessary — the past claims were correct in their context. The felt need to retract suggests that truth is indexed to the context of assessment, not fixed by the context of use.

## Epistemic Modals

MacFarlane's relativism was originally developed for epistemic modals — expressions like "might," "must," "probably" — before being extended to knowledge attributions. When you say "it might be raining," the truth of this seems to depend on the epistemic state of those assessing it: if the person evaluating your claim knows it isn't raining, your statement seems false, even if it was reasonable given your information.

This is a striking result: the same utterance can be true relative to one assessor's perspective and false relative to another, with both assessors making correct evaluations. This is the core of MacFarlane's relativism.

## Criticisms

The assessor-relativist view faces several objections:

*The explosion of truth values*: If every utterance has as many truth values as there are contexts of assessment, the notion of truth seems to be eroded. Propositions and utterances seem designed to be true or false, not true-for-some-and-false-for-others.

*The communication problem*: If the truth of what you say depends on who is assessing it, how does communication work? What exactly are you telling me when you say "she knows"?

*The reductio*: The relativist framework can seem to multiply mystery rather than resolve it. Instead of explaining why different people assess knowledge differently, it simply builds the difference into the semantics.

MacFarlane has responses to each objection, and the debate continues actively in the philosophy of language and formal semantics. His framework has influenced discussions of taste predicates ("that's tasty"), aesthetic attributions, and moral assertions — wherever perspective-dependence seems irreducible.
