# Epistemic Relativism: Knowledge Relative to Standards

Epistemic relativism is the view that knowledge, justification, or rationality is relative to something — to a person, a community, a conceptual scheme, or a set of background standards. Not all versions of epistemic relativism are the same: some are mild and widely accepted, others are radical and philosophically controversial. Understanding the range of positions is essential to evaluating relativism as a serious epistemological view.

## The Range of Relativist Positions

At the mild end, epistemic relativism simply notes that different communities have different epistemic practices and that no single set of practices is universally binding. This is descriptive relativism: an empirical claim about variation in epistemic norms across communities. Almost everyone accepts this.

More philosophically significant is *normative relativism*: the view that what you are epistemically justified in believing is relative to your community's standards or practices — and that there is no perspective-independent fact about which community's standards are correct. On this view, a belief that is justified according to community A's standards is justified simpliciter for members of that community, even if community B's standards would condemn it.

The most radical form is *truth relativism*: the view that what is true is itself relative to a framework, and that there is no framework-independent truth. This is close to the view associated with Protagoras — "man is the measure of all things" — which Plato argued against in the *Theaetetus*.

## Bloor, Barnes, and the Strong Programme

In the sociology of knowledge, the "strong programme" associated with David Bloor and Barry Barnes argues that all knowledge — including scientific knowledge — is to be explained by social causes, and that there is no asymmetry between successful and unsuccessful science: the same kinds of social factors explain both. This is sometimes taken to imply relativism: scientific "knowledge" is knowledge relative to the standards and interests of a scientific community, but there is no context-independent fact about whether those standards are correct.

Philosophers of science have pushed back vigorously. The strong programme tends to conflate the sociological explanation of how beliefs come to be held with the normative question of whether they are justified. The fact that beliefs are caused by social factors doesn't mean they are not also answerable to the world.

## Conceptual Scheme Relativism

A philosophically sophisticated form of relativism is associated with Kant and later with W.V.O. Quine: knowledge requires a conceptual scheme, and what falls into our conceptual categories determines what we can know. In its Kantian form, the categories of the understanding determine the structure of possible experience and hence the limits of knowledge. In its Quinean form, our "web of belief" determines what counts as evidence for what, and there is no scheme-neutral vantage point from which to evaluate the web as a whole.

Donald Davidson's response to conceptual scheme relativism is notable: he argued that the very notion of distinct conceptual schemes is incoherent, because the conditions for translation/interpretation are universal. You cannot have a scheme so different from your own that you cannot in principle interpret it — and if you can interpret it, it shares enough with your scheme to preclude radical relativism.

## The Self-Refutation Problem

The most famous objection to epistemic relativism is that it appears self-refuting. If "knowledge is relative to a framework," is that claim itself relative to a framework? If yes, it only tells us what is the case relative to some framework, not what is actually the case. The relativist cannot claim that relativism is objectively true without abandoning their position. If they retreat to saying relativism is true-for-them or true within their framework, the claim loses the force needed to convince anyone.

Defenders of relativism have developed responses. One is to distinguish between first-order knowledge claims (which are relative) and the second-order claim that knowledge is relative (which might be argued on non-relativist grounds). Another is to accept that relativism is itself a framework-relative claim and embrace the position as a kind of dialectical starting point rather than an absolute conclusion.

## Epistemic Relativism in Contemporary Debates

Contemporary discussions of epistemic relativism appear in several areas:

In **feminist epistemology**, standpoint theorists argue that knowledge is always produced from a particular social position and that pretensions to a "view from nowhere" are ideologically suspect. This is sometimes called a form of relativism, though standpoint theorists typically resist the label, arguing instead for a kind of perspectivism that acknowledges situated knowledge without collapsing into the view that all standpoints are equally valid.

In **social epistemology**, questions about whether the standards of scientific communities are justified, and from what perspective, connect to relativist concerns.

In **philosophy of language**, John MacFarlane's work on "assessment-sensitive" truth has developed a new form of relativism that is technically sophisticated and less vulnerable to the self-refutation objection. We examine this in the next section.
