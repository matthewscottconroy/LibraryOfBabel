# Stakes and Knowledge: Subject-Sensitive Invariantism

Subject-Sensitive Invariantism (SSI) — also called "interest-relative invariantism" or "pragmatic encroachment" — is a view that accepts the intuitions behind the bank cases and other stakes-sensitive scenarios while rejecting the contextualist's semantic explanation. SSI agrees that what you know depends on what's at stake for *you* — but locates this sensitivity in the facts about knowledge itself, not in the meaning of the word "knows."

## The Basic Idea

Where contextualism says that "knows" shifts meaning with the attributor's context, SSI says that what someone actually *knows* shifts with the subject's practical situation. The bank cases show a genuine difference in what the person knows, not a difference in what the word "knows" means. When the stakes are high, you genuinely don't know the bank is open — your epistemic position, combined with your high-stakes practical situation, fails to constitute knowledge. When the stakes are low, you do know.

This means that two people with exactly the same evidence, the same belief, the same reliability of belief formation — but in different practical situations — can differ in what they know. Knowledge is sensitive to the subject's interests and practical stakes, not just their evidence.

## Hawthorne's Lottery Puzzle

John Hawthorne develops SSI in *Knowledge and Lotteries* (2004) through a careful examination of why we seem unable to know lottery propositions even when the probability of winning is minuscule. You know you will not buy a new car this year; you don't know your lottery ticket will lose (before the draw), even though the probability of losing is much higher.

Hawthorne argues that closure principles force us to say that if we knew lottery propositions, we would also know many ordinary propositions from them, and if we don't know lottery propositions, we can't know things that entail winning the lottery. But this creates tension: ordinary knowledge seems to entail not winning the lottery. Hawthorne argues that the practical salience of lotteries — their being *live* practical possibilities for people who enter them — explains why lottery propositions resist knowledge. The solution requires sensitivity to practical context built into the conditions for knowing, not into the semantics of "knows."

## Fantl and McGrath

Jeremy Fantl and Matthew McGrath develop a version of SSI in *Knowledge in an Uncertain World* (2009). Their central claim is the *pragmatic condition on knowledge*: you know P only if P is justified enough to act on in your current practical situation. This directly links knowledge to practical stakes: the more important the decision and the more hinges on getting it right, the more justification is required to know.

This is "pragmatic encroachment" — practical factors encroach on the traditionally purely epistemic domain of justification and knowledge. Epistemic and practical rationality are intertwined: what you know depends not just on your evidence but on what you are about to do with it.

Fantl and McGrath argue that this is not an ad hoc addition to epistemology but rather flows from the role of knowledge in practical reasoning. Knowledge licenses action; what counts as knowledge must therefore be calibrated to what action you are contemplating. High-stakes actions require more certainty; hence high-stakes situations require more epistemic strength for knowledge.

## SSI vs. Contextualism

The key contrast with contextualism:

- **Contextualism**: "knows" shifts meaning; two people in different contexts using "knows" are saying different things.
- **SSI**: "knows" has a fixed meaning; two people in different practical situations genuinely *know different things* (or one knows and one doesn't), even with the same evidence.

SSI avoids the semantic blindspot objection (ordinary speakers don't need to know that "knows" is context-sensitive, because it isn't). It also preserves the intuition that the bank-case subjects genuinely differ in what they know.

SSI does generate its own puzzles. If two people with identical evidence differ in what they know merely because one has more at stake, many philosophers find this result deeply counterintuitive. Knowledge is supposed to be a purely epistemic achievement — making it depend on practical factors seems to corrupt its nature. Why should what you know change simply because the stakes for you go up?

## The Spirit-Level Objection

Critics argue that SSI makes knowledge implausibly sensitive to factors that seem epistemically irrelevant. Imagine two people, same evidence for "the bridge is safe," standing before the same bridge. One is in no hurry; the other will miss a life-saving operation if she doesn't cross quickly. Does she *know* less than him? SSI seems to imply yes — or at least, that the standard for her knowing is higher. But surely whether the bridge is safe doesn't depend on how much is at stake for her.

Defenders of SSI respond that the issue is not whether the bridge is safe, but whether her epistemic position constitutes *knowledge* given her practical situation. Knowledge is a practical concept as well as an epistemic one — it is knowledge that licenses action, and what licenses action must be calibrated to the action's stakes. The apparent counterintuitiveness reflects an overly pure conception of knowledge as practice-independent.

The debate between contextualism, SSI, and invariantism remains one of the liveliest in contemporary epistemology, cutting through questions about the nature of knowledge, the semantics of natural language, and the relationship between epistemic and practical rationality.
