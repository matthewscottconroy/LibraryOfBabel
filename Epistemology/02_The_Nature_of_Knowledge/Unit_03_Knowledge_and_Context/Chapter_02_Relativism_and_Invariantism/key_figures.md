## John Hawthorne (born 1964)

Hawthorne's *Knowledge and Lotteries* is the definitive statement of subject-sensitive invariantism (SSI). Against contextualism, Hawthorne argues that the truth conditions of "S knows p" do not vary with the context of the attributor but do vary with the practical interests of the *subject* S. In high-stakes situations, the subject genuinely fails to know things she would know in low-stakes situations, because knowledge is partly constituted by having evidence adequate to the practical demands of one's situation. His lottery case is central: "I know my ticket will lose" seems false even though the probability of losing is extremely high—and the falsity is not due to the attributor's context but to the nature of lottery propositions and their impracticality as action guides.

## Jason Stanley (born 1969)

Stanley's *Knowledge and Practical Interests* develops SSI under the label "interest-relative invariantism." He argues that the alternatives that must be eliminated for knowledge are interest-relative: which alternative possibilities are relevant to whether one knows depends on one's practical situation and what is at stake for one in being wrong. Stanley's linguistically sophisticated version of SSI draws on contextualist semantics about the content of "knows" but locates the variability in the subject's context rather than the attributor's. His account is designed to explain the bank case data: what differs between DeRose's high-stakes and low-stakes bank cases is not the standard operative for the attributor but the subject's practical interests, which partly determine what she knows.

## John MacFarlane (born 1968)

MacFarlane's assessor relativism occupies a distinctive position: unlike contextualism (which makes the truth of a knowledge attribution depend on the attributor's context) and SSI (which makes it depend on the subject's context), MacFarlane makes it depend on the assessor's context—the context of the person evaluating the attribution. This allows him to explain "retraction" cases: if I say "I knew the bank was open" and then subsequently find myself in high-stakes circumstances, I can legitimately retract my past self-attribution of knowledge, saying "I didn't really know." Contextualism cannot easily explain this retraction (since the past utterance was true relative to the past attributor's context); MacFarlane's relativism handles it because the retraction is evaluated relative to the current assessor's (elevated) standards.

## Jeremy Fantl and Matthew McGrath

Fantl and McGrath's joint work on "pragmatic encroachment" develops a version of SSI grounded in the knowledge norm for practical reasoning: S knows p only if S's evidence warrants p enough to justify acting on p. This norm links knowledge to action in a direct way: what one knows is constrained by what one's evidence warrants one to do. In high-stakes situations, the evidence required to justify action is greater, so knowledge requires stronger epistemic grounds. Their approach connects epistemology to decision theory in a systematic way, and their book *Knowledge in an Uncertain World* provides the most sustained development of pragmatic encroachment as a philosophical position.

## Stewart Cohen (born 1952)

Cohen defends classical contextualism against SSI, arguing that the data motivating both positions—the variability of appropriate knowledge attributions across contexts—is better explained by variability in the attributor's context than in the subject's interests. His objection to SSI is that it creates counterintuitive results in "third-party" cases: if a low-stakes attributor correctly says "S knows p" of a high-stakes subject, that seems wrong on SSI (since the high-stakes subject's knowledge should be lower), but contextualism handles it smoothly (the attributor's low-stakes context makes the attribution true from the attributor's perspective). Cohen argues that attributor-contextualism better respects the linguistic data about when we ascribe knowledge to others.

## Brian Weatherson (born 1968)

Weatherson has developed one of the most sophisticated responses to pragmatic encroachment and SSI. He argues that the intuitions motivating SSI can be explained without treating practical interests as constitutive of knowledge. His "interest-relative" account of what it is appropriate to assert or act on does not require revising the truth conditions of knowledge; instead, it identifies a pragmatic norm of assertion and action that is sensitive to stakes without making stakes epistemically relevant to knowledge itself. This "pragmatic" rather than "semantic" response to the high-stakes cases is the invariantist's most powerful tool, and Weatherson has pressed it against both DeRose's contextualism and Hawthorne's and Stanley's SSI.
