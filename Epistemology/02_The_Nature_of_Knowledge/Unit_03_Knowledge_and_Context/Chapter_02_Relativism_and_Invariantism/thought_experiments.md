## Thought Experiments on Relativism, Invariantism, and Stakes

---

**Hawthorne's Lottery**

**The scenario:** You hold a lottery ticket. The lottery has one million tickets; one will win. You believe, with good evidence, that your ticket will lose. Can you claim to know it will lose? Most philosophers (following Hawthorne) say no: even though the probability of losing is 0.999999, and even though your degree of confidence is entirely rational, you cannot claim to know your ticket will lose in the way you know ordinary things about the world.

**The philosophical point:** The lottery case reveals an asymmetry that SSI is designed to explain. The subjective epistemic position (high confidence, good evidence) is excellent, yet knowledge seems absent. Hawthorne argues this is because lottery propositions cannot function appropriately as action guides: if you claimed to know your ticket would lose, you could legitimately sell it for a trivial amount (since you "know" it is worthless), plan to use your money elsewhere, and so on. But you cannot rationally do these things while holding an undrawn ticket. The knowledge norm for practical reasoning—act on what you know—explains why claiming to know lottery propositions is inappropriate: the stakes make such action unjustified. The lottery case also creates a closure problem for invariantism: if I know I have hands, and if I know that if I have hands then I have not won the lottery (my hands exist only in the real world, not a lottery-replaced world), it seems I should know I will not win the lottery. But I don't know this. This "lottery puzzle" motivates both contextualism and SSI.

**The Knowledgeable But Cautious Agent**

**The scenario:** Sarah is a doctor who, in her professional capacity, knows that a certain medication is safe for her patient: she has checked the literature, the patient's history, and the current guidelines. Prescribing the drug is clearly the right action given what she knows. Now she is told: "The liability if this drug causes harm is enormous—one misstep and your career is over." Does she still know the drug is safe?

**The philosophical point:** SSI predicts that the introduction of enormous stakes *reduces* what Sarah knows, because what counts as adequate epistemic grounds for action is now much higher. But this seems counterintuitive: why should external threats to her career change what she *knows*? Her evidence base is unchanged. Critics of SSI (including invariantists and contextualists) use cases like this to argue that practical stakes should not be part of the truth conditions for knowledge. The invariantist says Sarah still knows but that what it is appropriate for her to *assert* or *act on* changes with stakes. MacFarlane's relativism handles the case differently: Sarah's self-attribution of knowledge ("I know it's safe") is true when assessed from her pre-warning context but false when assessed from the post-warning high-stakes context—without requiring any change in her actual epistemic position.

**MacFarlane's Retraction Case**

**The scenario:** Monday: Maria says "I know the restaurant has vegetarian options—I ate there last month." Friday: Maria discovers the restaurant has changed ownership and revamped its menu. She says "I didn't know it had vegetarian options—I only knew it had them last month." Has she correctly retracted her Monday claim?

**The philosophical point:** This case challenges both contextualism and SSI. For contextualism, the Monday claim was true in Monday's context (where Maria's evidence was adequate by Monday's standards); for SSI, Monday's claim may have been true because Monday Maria's practical interests were adequately served. But both say the Monday claim was true, making Friday's retraction misleading (if the claim was true, retracting it implies it was false). MacFarlane's assessor relativism handles this smoothly: the Monday claim is being assessed from Friday's context, where the relevant alternatives now include the possibility of menu change. The retraction is correct because the assessment context has changed, not because the Monday utterance was false when made. This shows that relativism can handle a type of case that neither contextualism nor SSI handles gracefully.

**Stakes and the Reasonable Person**

**The scenario:** Alex is driving and needs to turn left. She believes the road is clear, based on a quick check of the mirrors. In a low-stakes case (going to the supermarket), she turns. In a high-stakes case (transporting an injured person to the hospital where a wrong turn could be fatal), she hesitates, checks twice, and asks her passenger to look. Philosophers then ask: did Alex *know* the road was clear in the low-stakes case?

**The philosophical point:** This case is used to test whether it is *knowledge* that varies with stakes or merely *standards for appropriate action*. SSI predicts that in the hospital case, Alex genuinely fails to know the road is clear by the standards relevant to her practical situation, while she did know it in the supermarket case. Invariantists respond that Alex knows equally in both cases—the same evidence grounds the same knowledge—but that what it is reasonable to act on differs. This connects to the relationship between epistemology and practical rationality: SSI makes them interdependent; invariantism keeps them distinct. The case is also illuminating for the knowledge norm of practical reasoning: if action should be based on knowledge, and if knowledge varies with stakes, then what counts as safe enough to act on varies with context—a result that SSI embraces and invariantism avoids.

**The Invariantist's Response: Pragmatic Explanation**

**The scenario:** Two people are discussing a mutual friend: "Does she know the party is tonight?" One says "Yes, she knows—I told her this morning." The other says "Well, she has a terrible memory; we should remind her." The first speaker does not retract: "Fine, but she does know—she was told."

**The philosophical point:** This case is used by invariantists to argue that what changes across high-stakes and low-stakes contexts is not the truth conditions of "knows" but what it is *relevant to assert* or *prudent to rely on*. The first speaker is right: knowledge was acquired. Whether to act on it—whether to trust the knowledge or take additional precautions—is a practical question about reliability and risk management, not an epistemic question about whether knowledge is present. The invariantist says this case shows that contextualism and SSI confuse pragmatics with semantics: the context affects what it is useful to say and what it is prudent to rely on, but not whether the word "knows" expresses a different relation. This invariantist response, if correct, would show that all the data motivating contextualism and SSI can be explained without revising the truth conditions of knowledge attributions.
