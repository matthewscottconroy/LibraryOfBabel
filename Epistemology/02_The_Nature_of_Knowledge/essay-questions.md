# Essay Questions: Unit 2 — The Nature of Knowledge

---

**1. The Gettier Problem and the Fourth-Condition Program**

Assess the claim that Gettier cases show that knowledge requires a fourth condition beyond justified true belief. Reconstruct at least two of Gettier's original three cases precisely. Which of the main post-Gettier fourth-condition proposals — no false lemmas, the causal theory (Goldman 1967), the defeasibility analysis (Klein, Lehrer) — is most successful in handling the full range of Gettier-style cases? Is Zagzebski's argument that Gettier problems are inescapable for any JTB-plus-condition analysis persuasive?

---

**2. Nozick's Tracking Theory: Sensitivity, Adherence, and Closure**

Robert Nozick proposed that S knows p iff S's belief tracks the truth of p: if p were false, S would not believe p (sensitivity); if p were true in nearby worlds, S would believe p (adherence). Carefully assess the tracking theory. Does the sensitivity condition handle Gettier cases satisfactorily? Does the failure of closure — the tracking theorist's claim that knowledge does not transmit across entailment — represent a genuine advantage or an unacceptable cost? Evaluate Hawthorne's argument (*Knowledge and Lotteries*, 2004) that the denial of closure leads to unacceptable violations of everyday knowledge ascriptions.

---

**3. Safety, Virtue, and the Analysis of Knowledge**

Both safety-based accounts (Sosa, Williamson, Pritchard) and virtue-epistemological accounts (Greco, Zagzebski) are offered as improvements on earlier post-Gettier theories. Compare these two approaches. In what sense does a belief's safety (it could not easily have been false) explain why knowledge is not present in Gettier cases? Does virtue epistemology — the claim that knowledge is true belief arising from the agent's intellectual competence — provide a more unified account? Is the distinction between "animal knowledge" and "reflective knowledge" in Sosa's virtue perspectivism philosophically motivated?

---

**4. Knowledge-First Epistemology**

Timothy Williamson's *Knowledge and Its Limits* argues that knowledge is a primitive mental state and that justification should be explained in terms of knowledge, not vice versa. Critically assess this "knowledge-first" reversal of the traditional order of explanation. Does Williamson's luminosity argument (no condition is such that whenever one is in it, one is in a position to know one is) succeed? Can knowledge-first epistemology account for the evidential role of experience without presupposing the JTB framework? What, if anything, would be lost if epistemologists accepted that knowledge is unanalyzable?

---

**5. Propositional, Ability, and Acquaintance Knowledge**

Gilbert Ryle's distinction between knowing that and knowing how has been challenged by Stanley and Williamson, who argue that ability knowledge ("knowing how to ride a bicycle") is a form of propositional knowledge with an essentially first-person, practical mode of presentation. Critically evaluate the Stanley-Williamson argument. Does the account of practical modes of presentation adequately capture the difference between knowing a fact about riding and actually being able to ride? What does the debate about "knowing how" reveal about the relationship between knowledge and action?

---

**6. Contextualism about Knowledge Attributions**

Contextualism holds that "S knows that p" has truth conditions that shift with the epistemic standards operative in the context of the attribution. Critically assess DeRose's contextualism. Does contextualism provide a genuine solution to the puzzle of scepticism (knowledge attributions can be true at ordinary standards even if false at sceptical standards), or does it merely restate the puzzle in semantic terms? What is the most powerful objection to contextualism — the "semantic blindspot" objection, the objection from practical reasoning, or the invariantist response?

---

**7. The Epistemological Significance of the Fake Barns Case**

Goldman's fake barn case: a subject drives through a county filled with barn facades and, by chance, looks at the one real barn. Does the subject know there is a barn? Analyse this case in detail. Different theories yield different verdicts: what does each verdict commit one to? Is the case most naturally handled by safety conditions, reliabilist conditions, or virtue-epistemological conditions? Does the verdict depend on contested intuitions about "knowledge," and what methodological conclusions should we draw if intuitions conflict about this case?
