# Unit 3: Sources of Knowledge — Unit Survey

## Central Questions

If Unit 2 asks what knowledge is, Unit 3 asks where it comes from. The question of sources is not merely psychological or empirical — it is epistemological, because different sources have different epistemic profiles: they produce beliefs in different ways, with different degrees of reliability, under different conditions of defeater-susceptibility, and with different relationships to the agent's rational self-governance. Understanding the epistemic character of perception, memory, introspection, testimony, and a priori reasoning is indispensable for assessing whether the beliefs they generate constitute knowledge or merely lucky true belief.

The central debates in this unit concern the epistemic status of each source. For perception: does perceptual experience provide its evidential support directly (phenomenological approaches) or via a representational intermediary (representationalism)? Is perception "inferentially opaque" — is the evidential force of experience not dependent on any inference? For memory: does memory justify by preserving original justification (conservatism) or by producing new justification on each occasion (a more active role)? For introspection: how accurate is introspective access to one's own mental states? Is self-knowledge privileged, or does it face the same reliability questions as other sources? For testimony: is testimony a basic, irreducible source of justification (anti-reductionism), or must testimonially-based beliefs be traceable to individual perception and memory (reductionism)? For a priori knowledge: is it grounded in rational intuition, in conceptual competence, or in a reliable faculty of some other kind?

These questions are not merely academic. The epistemology of testimony is central to debates about deference, authority, and the transmission of knowledge through social structures. The epistemology of perception is central to disputes about the directness of perceptual contact with reality and to debates in philosophy of mind about the nature of experience. The a priori/a posteriori distinction bears on philosophy of mathematics, modal epistemology, and the status of philosophical reasoning itself.

## Map of Major Positions and Their Logical Relations

**Perception.** Three families of view:

*Naïve realism* holds that in normal perceptual experience, the perceiver is in direct cognitive contact with mind-independent objects and their properties. No representational intermediary interposes itself. Proponents: John McDowell, Bill Brewer, Mike Martin.

*Representationalism* holds that experience has representational content — it represents the world as being a certain way — and the epistemological role of experience is mediated by this content. Proponents: Tim Crane, Christopher Peacocke, Michael Tye.

*Disjunctivism* holds that the category of "experience" encompasses two fundamentally different kinds: veridical experiences (in which one perceives the fact itself) and non-veridical experiences (hallucinations, illusions). These are not experiences of the same kind falling under a common highest type. J.M. Hinton, Paul Snowdon, McDowell.

**Introspection.** Two main families:

*Privileged access* views hold that introspection provides especially secure access to one's own mental states — more secure than access to anything external. The classical source is Descartes's claim that the mental is the transparent. Contemporary descendants include Richard Moran's account of first-person authority and John Canfield's analysis of Wittgenstein on private language.

*Reliabilist and critical views* hold that introspection is simply another cognitive process, subject to the same reliability questions as perception. Eric Schwitzgebel argues that introspection is deeply and systematically unreliable. Peter Carruthers defends a high-level theory of introspective access — we introspect by perceptual-like processes applied to our own mental representations.

**Memory.** Three main positions:

*Trace theory:* memory works by storing and retrieving traces of past experience. The epistemological question is how reliable storage and retrieval are.

*Simulation theory:* memory is not purely a retrieval process but involves reconstruction. This explains both the flexibility and the fragility of memory.

*Preservative vs. generative:* Does memory merely preserve justification originally generated by perception (the preservative view, Pollock), or does it generate new justification each time a belief is recalled (the generative view)?

**Testimony.** Two main positions:

*Reductionism* (Hume, Fricker): testimonially-based beliefs are justified only insofar as the hearer has independent (perception-and-memory-based) evidence for the reliability of the testifier. Testimonial justification reduces to other sources.

*Anti-reductionism* (Reid, Coady): testimony is a basic source of justification that does not require independent positive support for each testimonial belief. The natural starting point is to trust testimony; defeasors defeat that presumptive trust.

**A Priori Knowledge.** Three main positions:

*Rationalism* (traditional): there is a faculty of rational intuition that apprehends necessary truths directly. The truths of logic, mathematics, and some metaphysics are accessible through reason alone.

*Concept empiricism / conceptualism*: a priori knowledge is grounded in our mastery of concepts. To know that all bachelors are unmarried is to know what "bachelor" means. The a priori is ultimately grounded in stipulative definition or conceptual content, itself acquired empirically.

*Reliabilism about a priori knowledge* (Bealer, Sosa): a priori knowledge is justified by a reliable faculty of rational intuition. The faculty is not infallible but produces mostly accurate judgments about modal and logical truths.

## Sub-Chapter Contributions

**Unit 1: Perception.** Develops the epistemology of perceptual knowledge from phenomenological and representationalist perspectives. Chapter 1 analyses the structure of perceptual experience and its relationship to perceptual knowledge claims. Chapter 2 examines the epistemic role of phenomenal character: does the "what it's like" quality of experience provide evidence, or is only the representational content epistemically relevant? Chapter 3 treats cognitive penetrability: can higher-level cognitive states (beliefs, desires, expectations) alter the content of perceptual experience, thereby either boosting or undermining its evidential force?

**Unit 2: Memory.** Chapter 1 examines the basic epistemology of memory — how does the fact that I once saw p give me current justification to believe p? Chapter 2 addresses the reliability of memory and the epistemological implications of empirical research on memory distortion, false memories, and the constructive nature of recollection. Chapter 3 treats testimony-like transmission through memory: when I remember that someone told me p, do I inherit the original testimonial justification?

**Unit 3: Reason and A Priori.** Chapter 1 analyses the concept of the a priori and surveys the main candidate categories (logic, mathematics, conceptual truths, some metaphysics). Chapter 2 examines the phenomenology of rational intuition and the question of its reliability. Chapter 3 addresses challenges to the a priori from Quine's holism and from cognitive science accounts of mathematical cognition.

**Unit 4: Testimony.** Chapter 1 presents Coady's systematic defence of anti-reductionism in *Testimony* (1992). Chapter 2 examines the reductionist responses and their difficulties. Chapter 3 treats the extended social epistemology of testimony: the epistemology of deference, the ethics of inquiry, epistemic injustice in testimonial exchange, and the question of transmission vs. generation of epistemic properties through testimony.

**Unit 5: Introspection and Self-Knowledge.** Chapter 1 presents the classical doctrine of privileged access and its contemporary defenders. Chapter 2 examines Schwitzgebel's empirical case against introspective accuracy. Chapter 3 addresses second-order knowledge (knowing that one knows, knowing that one believes) and its relationship to higher-order epistemic requirements.

## Key Philosophers and Arguments

**John McDowell (1942–).** *Mind and World* (1994) argues that perception places the perceiver in direct rational contact with how things are in the world. Experience has propositional content — the fact that things are thus and so — which can stand in justifying relations to judgments. McDowell's disjunctivism and his critique of the "Given" (the idea that experience has an epistemic force independent of conceptual capacities) are central to contemporary philosophy of perception.

**C.A.J. Coady (1939–).** *Testimony: A Philosophical Study* (1992) provided the most systematic anti-reductionist defence of testimony as a basic epistemic source. Coady argues that reductionism is empirically incoherent: human beings could not form the body of memory-and-perception-based evidence needed to justify testimonial beliefs before accepting testimony, and our ordinary concept of perception is partly built from testimony. The book transformed testimony from a marginalized into a central topic.

**Paul Boghossian and Timothy Williamson** on a priori knowledge: Boghossian defends conceptual truth as the ground of a priori knowledge, connecting it to inferentialist accounts of meaning. Williamson challenges the entire framework, arguing that there are no analytic truths and that a priori knowledge must be explained by the contingent reliability of rational faculties.

**Miranda Fricker (1966–).** *Epistemic Injustice* (2007) introduced the concept of *testimonial injustice* — the wrong done to a speaker by unjustly deflating their credibility, typically on the basis of identity prejudice. Fricker's framework connects the epistemology of testimony to social and political philosophy and has generated a large literature in feminist epistemology and social epistemology (Unit 9).

**Elizabeth Fricker (no relation to Miranda Fricker, 1953–).** Developed a sophisticated local reductionism about testimony: hearers need not have prior general reasons for trusting testimony, but they must monitor for defeaters. Local reductionism is a middle position between Hume's strict reductionism and Reid/Coady's anti-reductionism.

**Eric Schwitzgebel (1968–).** *Perplexities of Consciousness* (2011) makes the empirical case that introspection is deeply unreliable — that people systematically misreport their emotional and phenomenal states. This challenges both Cartesian privileged access and the reliability of phenomenological method in philosophy of mind.

**Tyler Burge (1946–).** *Origins of Objectivity* (2010) provides an account of perception's epistemic role grounded in anti-individualism: perceptual states represent mind-independent objects, and this representational content is determined not by the internal states of the perceiver but by causal relations to the environment. Burge's anti-individualism has implications for the epistemology of testimony and introspection.

## Essential Readings

1. **Coady, C.A.J., *Testimony: A Philosophical Study*** (1992, Oxford). The foundational text for contemporary anti-reductionism about testimony. Part I (critique of reductionism) and Part II (positive account of testimony's epistemic role) are essential. The most influential single contribution to this area.

2. **McDowell, John, *Mind and World*, Lectures I–III** (1994, Harvard). Sets out the argument that perception provides non-inferential rational warrant by placing perceivers in direct contact with facts. The concept of the "Given" and McDowell's critique of it are the most important contributions. Demanding but essential for understanding contemporary philosophy of perception.

3. **Fricker, Miranda, *Epistemic Injustice*, Chapters 1–4** (2007, Oxford). Introduces testimonial and hermeneutical injustice as forms of epistemic wronging. Chapters 1–2 on testimonial injustice are the most important for this unit; Chapters 3–4 address hermeneutical injustice and connect to Unit 9.

4. **Peacocke, Christopher, *A Study of Concepts*, Chapter 1** (1992, MIT). Develops the conceptualist approach to a priori knowledge: a priori truths are grounded in the possession conditions for concepts. The most systematic contemporary defence of conceptualism.

5. **Schwitzgebel, Eric, "The Unreliability of Naive Introspection,"** *Philosophical Review*, 117:2 (2008), pp. 245–273. The most accessible presentation of Schwitzgebel's case against introspective accuracy. Pairs usefully with Descartes (Unit 1) and Williamson's luminosity argument (Unit 2).

## Open Questions and Live Debates

**Is cognitive penetrability epistemically damaging?** If expectation and belief can alter the content of perceptual experience (Susanna Siegel's argument in *The Rationality of Perception*, 2017), does this mean that perception is a less reliable guide to reality than it appears? Or does cognitive penetrability merely show that there is no sharp boundary between perception and reasoning?

**Is local reductionism about testimony stable?** Elizabeth Fricker's middle position — monitoring for defeaters without requiring positive prior evidence — has been challenged as collapsing either into global reductionism (if the monitoring requires antecedent reliability evidence) or into anti-reductionism (if the monitoring is merely defeasibility-checking). The debate about the exact formulation of local reductionism is ongoing.

**Can the a priori survive Quinean holism?** Quine's argument that no statement is immune from revision in light of experience (the web of belief, "Two Dogmas of Empiricism") has been resisted by defenders of the analytic/synthetic distinction and by reliabilist accounts of rational intuition. The question of whether there are genuine epistemic differences between empirical and a priori justification remains contested.

**What is the relationship between introspection and phenomenology as a method?** If Schwitzgebel is right that introspection is unreliable, the phenomenological method (making claims on the basis of careful introspective attention to experience) is epistemically undermined. But defenders of phenomenology argue that trained, disciplined introspection has different reliability properties than naive introspection. The methodological debate is central to philosophy of mind.
