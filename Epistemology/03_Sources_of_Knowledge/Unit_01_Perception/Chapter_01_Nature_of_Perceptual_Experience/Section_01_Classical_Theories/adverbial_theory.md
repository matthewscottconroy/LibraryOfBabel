# The Adverbial Theory of Perception

The adverbial theory emerged in the mid-twentieth century as an alternative to sense datum theory that tried to capture what was right about the sense datum account while avoiding its problematic ontology of private mental objects. The key move is grammatical and ontological: instead of saying that when you hallucinate red you perceive a red mental object (a sense datum), the adverbial theory says you are experiencing *redly* — "red" modifies the experience as an adverb modifies a verb, not as a predicate describing an object.

## The Grammatical Turn

Roderick Chisholm and Wilfrid Sellars developed adverbial theories, though the core idea has antecedents in C.J. Ducasse's earlier work. The basic insight is this: when we describe perceptual experience, we needn't construe "red" as a predicate applying to an object of experience. We can instead construe it as modifying the experience itself.

Compare:
- "He senses a red triangular sense datum" (sense datum formulation)
- "He senses redly and triangularly" (adverbial formulation)

Both descriptions apply to the same experiential state. But the adverbial formulation does not commit us to any entity — the sense datum — that is red and triangular. The experience has the character of being red-triangular-ly without there being any object that is red and triangular within the experience.

This is the ontological advantage: we avoid positing a private universe of mental objects. The adverbial theory is a *one-entity* theory (there is only the experiencing subject) rather than a *two-entity* theory (the subject plus the sense datum).

## Modifications and Properties of Experience

The adverbial theorist holds that perceptual experiences are intrinsic mental events that have what we might call *qualitative modes*. When you see red, your experience is of the red-sort; when you see blue, it is of the blue-sort. These "sorts" are not objects within experience but properties of the experience itself.

Some philosophers formulate this in terms of *phenomenal properties*: experiences have properties that characterize how they are, and these properties are intrinsic to the experience, not relational properties connecting the experience to an object. The experience is itself red-seeming, without there being anything that it seems to be red-that-is.

## The Problem of the Many Properties

The adverbial theory faces a significant challenge known as the "many properties" problem, raised by Frank Jackson. Suppose you have an experience of a red square and a blue circle simultaneously. On the sense datum view, there are two sense data: a red square and a blue circle. On the adverbial view, the experience is "redly and squarely and bluely and circularly." But how do we distinguish this experience from one of a blue square and a red circle? In the adverbial formulation, the properties are simply listed without any structure indicating which color goes with which shape.

To handle this, the adverbialist must introduce complex modifiers: "red-squarely and blue-circularly" as opposed to "blue-squarely and red-circularly." But this seems to reinstate the problematic structure of sense data: now the modifiers have object-like complexity, and we may as well have posited sense data.

## Modifications of the Adverbial Strategy

Later adverbialists have refined the position to handle the many-properties problem. One strategy is to say that experiences have *structured* qualitative characters that encode the groupings of properties without requiring objects. Another is to move to a "looks" or "appears" formulation: "it looks to me as if there is a red square and a blue circle" is a sentential claim whose complexity is handled by the embedded sentence, not by positing objects.

This latter move connects to intentionalist theories of perception (discussed in the next section): the content of experience is representational, not object-involving. The debate between adverbialism and intentionalism is partly a debate about how to capture the correctness conditions of experience — whether they require objects or only propositional contents.

## The Historical Significance

The adverbial theory was an important step in the philosophy of perception because it demonstrated that the problems raised by illusion and hallucination — the original motivation for sense data — do not strictly require positing private mental objects. The same phenomena can be described in terms that modify the subject's experiencing without multiplying the ontology.

Whether adverbialism ultimately succeeds is a separate question from whether it provides a useful alternative framework. Most contemporary philosophers of perception have moved toward intentionalist or naïve realist accounts, but adverbialism remains a philosophically serious position that shaped the debate.
