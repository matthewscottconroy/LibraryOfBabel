# Sense Datum Theory

For much of the first half of the twentieth century, the sense datum theory dominated the epistemology of perception. The idea is intuitive: when you perceive, you are directly aware of something mental — a private, subjective item called a sense datum. The redness and roundness that you experience when you see a tomato are not properties of the tomato itself but of a sense datum: an internal, experiential object that represents or is caused by the external world. Understanding this theory — its appeal, its machinery, and its difficulties — is essential background for all subsequent discussions of perception.

## The Basic Account

A sense datum is an object of immediate perceptual awareness. It has the properties that the experience presents: if you seem to see something red and round, you are directly acquainted with a red, round sense datum. The existence of this sense datum is certain — you cannot be wrong that you are having this experience, even if you can be wrong about whether any external red round object exists.

Physical objects, on this view, are not directly perceived. They are inferred or constructed from the evidence provided by sense data. When you see a table, you directly perceive a series of tabular-shaped, brownish, spatially extended sense data; the conclusion that there is a table there is an inference beyond what is given in immediate experience.

The main motivation comes from cases of perceptual variation and error:

- An elliptical coin looks round from directly above. Which shape is the sense datum? The round one or the elliptical one? The coin cannot itself be both — so what you immediately see must be a sense datum.
- In hallucination, you seem to see something that isn't there. What are you seeing? Not a physical object — so you must be seeing a sense datum.
- In illusion (the bent stick in water, the Müller-Lyer arrows), the thing looks one way while being another. What is the immediate object of experience?

These cases suggest that the immediate objects of perception have subjective, experience-relative properties that physical objects lack. Sense data are posited to explain why.

## The Philosophical Context

Sense datum theory connects directly to the tradition of representative realism associated with Descartes and Locke. On this picture, the mind directly perceives its own ideas or impressions, and the external world is known indirectly through these mental intermediaries. The sense datum is the twentieth-century refinement of this older notion.

Bertrand Russell, G.E. Moore, and H.H. Price all developed versions of sense datum theory. Russell used it in his foundationalist epistemology: sense data are what we are acquainted with, and all knowledge of the external world is constructed from this foundation. Moore analyzed perception in terms of the awareness of sense data while attempting to preserve some form of direct realism.

## The Phenomenological Reading

One distinction is between *act* and *object*. The act of perceiving is a mental event directed at an object. On sense datum theory, the object in perceptual acts is a sense datum, not a physical thing. This preserves the intentionality (directedness) of perception while making its immediate objects mental.

Price developed a particularly careful version: sense data are "invasive" in that they can appear in the same visual field as physical objects. When you look at a table, the sense datum of the table's surface is both the direct object of awareness and, in some sense, in front of you in ordinary visual space.

## Problems with Sense Datum Theory

The theory faces formidable objections:

**The appearing/being distinction**: How do we know that sense data have the properties they appear to have? If sense data are the bedrock of experience, what guarantees that experience of them is accurate? And if experience of sense data can be mistaken, we need a further level of intermediary — which begins a regress.

**The problem of location**: Where are sense data? They seem to be in the mind (they are mental objects), but they also seem to be spatially extended and located. Mental items are not usually thought to have spatial properties.

**Inflation of the mental**: Sense datum theory multiplies the entities in the world by positing a vast array of private mental objects. Every perception generates new sense data. This proliferation is theoretically costly.

**Naive realism is more natural**: Ordinary experience presents physical objects, not private mental intermediaries. Phenomenologically, you seem to see the tomato, not an experience of the tomato. Sense datum theory is in tension with the first-personal character of experience.

**The adverbial challenge** (discussed in the next section): Instead of positing sense data as objects, we can describe perception adverbially — "seeing redly" rather than "seeing a red sense datum" — without the problematic ontology.

These objections collectively motivated the search for alternative theories of perceptual experience.
