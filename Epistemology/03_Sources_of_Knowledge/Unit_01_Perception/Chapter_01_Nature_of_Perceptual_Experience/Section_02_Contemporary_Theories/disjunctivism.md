# Disjunctivism about Perceptual Experience

Disjunctivism is one of the most distinctive and widely discussed positions in contemporary philosophy of perception. Its central claim is that veridical perception (seeing something as it really is) and hallucination (seemingly seeing something that is not there) are fundamentally different kinds of mental state — not two species of a common genus, but genuinely distinct experiences that merely share a first-person phenomenological indistinguishability.

## The Core Claim

Most theories of perception — sense datum theory, adverbialism, intentionalism — are *highest common factor* theories. They hold that there is a kind of mental state that veridical perception and hallucination share: in both cases, you have an experience of the same type, and the difference is only external (whether the experience is caused by the right kind of object). The "highest common factor" is the mental state that perception and hallucination have in common.

Disjunctivism denies this. It says that the logical form of "perceptual experience" is disjunctive: perceptual experience = (genuine perception) OR (hallucination/illusion). These are not two species of a shared genus; they have different natures. In veridical perception, the subject stands in a direct, constitutive relationship to the perceived object — the object itself is partly constitutive of the experience. In hallucination, no such relation obtains; the experience is a different, merely internal state.

The two states may be subjectively indistinguishable from the inside, but they are genuinely different kinds of event.

## J.M. Hinton and the Development of Disjunctivism

The disjunctivist position was articulated by J.M. Hinton in the 1960s and later developed by Paul Snowdon and Michael Martin. The key insight is that the indistinguishability of veridical perception from hallucination does not imply that they share a common mental component. Indistinguishability is a first-person, phenomenological relation; it doesn't entail metaphysical identity of type.

Imagine two situations: in one, you see a red apple on the table. In the other, a neuroscientist stimulates your visual cortex to produce a state exactly like the one you would have if there were an apple there. These situations may be phenomenologically identical from your perspective, but they are not the same type of event. In the first, the apple is there, and your experience is constituted partly by your relation to the apple. In the second, you are undergoing a brain event with no perceptual relation to any apple.

## Naïve Realism and Disjunctivism

Disjunctivism connects naturally to *naïve realism* about perception: the view that in genuine perception, we are directly aware of the external world, and the world itself features in the character of our experience. Naïve realism says that when you see the red apple, the apple's redness and shape are not merely represented by your experience — they are present in it.

This is a strong claim. It means that the phenomenal character of perceptual experience — what it is like — is partly determined by the external objects perceived. The experience is not purely internal; it is world-involving. Martin has developed this view under the label of "naïve realism" and connects it to disjunctivism by arguing that the highest common factor view mischaracterizes what genuine perception is.

## The Problem of Hallucination (and Disjunctivism's Response)

The traditional argument from hallucination runs: since hallucination can be phenomenologically indistinguishable from perception, the immediate object of perception must be something that can exist in both cases — a mental intermediary (sense datum, phenomenal state). Disjunctivism's response is to deny that indistinguishability entails a common mental constituent. The two cases are just different in nature, and our first-person inability to tell them apart doesn't show they are the same type of state.

This response has been criticized as appearing to deny something obvious: surely in the hallucination case, something is going on mentally that is experientially real. Disjunctivists can grant this — the hallucination involves a genuine mental state — while denying that veridical perception is also just an internal state. The hallucination is unusual; it mimics the look of perception without being perception.

## Epistemological Implications

Disjunctivism has significant epistemological implications. If veridical perception directly involves the external world, then perception can provide a kind of direct epistemic access to the world that highest-common-factor theories cannot accommodate. The epistemological worry about being trapped behind a "veil of perception" — knowing only your own mental states and inferring the world from them — does not arise in the same way. You don't need to infer from mental states to the world; you are in direct contact with the world in perception.

This makes disjunctivism attractive to those who want a non-inferential, direct epistemology of perception. It is also challenged by the observation that if you cannot know from the inside whether you are perceiving or hallucinating, your epistemic situation seems insecure regardless of which one you're actually doing.
