# Intentionalism about Perceptual Experience

Intentionalism — also called *representationalism* — is currently the dominant approach in philosophy of perception. It holds that perceptual experience is essentially representational: to have a perceptual experience is to be in a mental state with a representational or intentional content. What it is like to have an experience is entirely determined by the content of that experience — what the experience represents to be the case.

## The Core Thesis

Intentionalism's central claim: the phenomenal character of experience — what philosophers call its *qualia* or *phenomenal feel* — is identical to, or supervenes on, its intentional content. There is no aspect of what it is like to have an experience that goes beyond what the experience represents.

When you see a red apple, your visual experience represents a red, roughly spherical object in a certain location. The phenomenal character of the experience — the redness you seem to see, the shape, the spatial structure — is exhausted by this representational content. Two experiences that have exactly the same representational content are phenomenally identical; two experiences that differ phenomenally differ in their content.

This is the *strong* or *pure* intentionalist thesis. A weaker version allows that phenomenal character supervenes on (but is not identical to) content, leaving room for phenomenal properties that play no representational role.

## Motivations for Intentionalism

Intentionalism is attractive for several reasons:

**Unification with the rest of cognition**: Beliefs, desires, and memories are all representational mental states. Intentionalism extends this framework to perception, unifying our account of mind. There is no need for a special category of "raw feels" that resist representational description.

**A natural account of illusion and veridicality**: Experiences can be veridical or illusory because they represent the world as being a certain way, and the world either is or isn't that way. An experience of a bent stick in water is an illusory experience because it represents the stick as bent when it isn't. This mirrors how beliefs can be true or false.

**The transparency of experience**: When you introspect your visual experience, you seem to look through the experience to the represented scene. You attend to the redness, the shape, the objects — not to your mental state as such. This "transparency" suggests that the character of experience is constituted by its representational content, not by some additional non-representational ingredient.

## Fred Dretske and Representationism

Fred Dretske developed an influential externalist version of intentionalism. On his view, the phenomenal properties of experience are properties of the objects represented, not intrinsic properties of the experience. When you see red, redness is a property of the object your experience represents; the experience itself is a transparent window onto its content. The qualia are "out there" in the represented world, not "in here" in the mind.

This makes the phenomenal character of experience entirely world-involving: change what is represented, and you change the phenomenal character. The "what it's like" is determined by what the experience is about.

## Ned Block's Qualia Critique

One of the most important objections to intentionalism comes from Ned Block's distinction between *phenomenal consciousness* (P-consciousness) and *access consciousness* (A-consciousness). P-consciousness is the "raw feel" of experience — what it's like. A-consciousness is the availability of information for reasoning, report, and action control.

Block argues that these can come apart: you can have phenomenal character without access, or access without full phenomenal representation. If phenomenal character can exceed representational content — if there are "overflow" qualia that don't enter into the representational economy — then intentionalism is false, or at least incomplete.

Intentionalists respond by fine-graining the representational content: perhaps the richness of perceptual experience is matched by an equally rich representational content that picks out fine-grained properties that we have trouble putting into words.

## The Inverted Qualia Problem

A classic challenge to intentionalism is the inverted qualia thought experiment: suppose your color experiences are systematically inverted relative to mine — what I experience as red, you experience as green, and vice versa — but we have the same functional organization and behave identically. Would our experiences have different phenomenal characters despite having the same representational content?

If inverted qualia are possible, phenomenal character outruns representational content, and intentionalism is false. The intentionalist response is typically to deny that inverted qualia are genuinely possible once we have a sufficiently fine-grained account of representational content. This is contested.

## Intentionalism and Epistemology

Intentionalism connects perception to knowledge through the concept of *accuracy conditions*. An experience is accurate (veridical) if the world is as the experience represents it to be. This provides a natural account of when perception justifies belief: a belief formed on the basis of an accurate experience is well-grounded, and the justification flows from the representational link between experience and world.

This is the foundation of many reliabilist and externalist epistemologies of perception: what matters for justification is not the intrinsic properties of experience but how reliably experience tracks the world.
