## Primary Sources

- Bertrand Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, Oxford University Press, 1912. The classic introduction to sense-datum theory, arguing that we are directly acquainted only with private sense-data rather than physical objects.
- G. E. Moore, *Some Main Problems of Philosophy*, Allen & Unwin, 1953. Moore's lectures develop and refine his version of sense-datum theory, distinguishing the act of sensing from its object.
- C. D. Broad, *Scientific Thought*, Kegan Paul, 1923. Systematic defense of sense-datum theory with careful attention to the relations between appearing and physical objects.
- Roderick Chisholm, *Perceiving: A Philosophical Study*, Cornell University Press, 1957. Introduces the adverbial analysis of perception, analyzing "sensing redly" rather than "sensing a red sense-datum."
- Wilfrid Sellars, "Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind," in *Science, Perception and Reality*, Routledge, 1963. Attacks the myth of the given while developing an adverbial-style account of experience compatible with conceptualism.
- Fred Dretske, *Seeing and Knowing*, University of Chicago Press, 1969. Foundational work distinguishing epistemic and non-epistemic senses of perception and introducing informational approaches.
- Fred Dretske, *Naturalizing the Mind*, MIT Press, 1995. Develops the representationalist thesis that phenomenal properties are fully explained by representational content.
- Michael Tye, *Ten Problems of Consciousness*, MIT Press, 1995. PANIC theory: phenomenal character is intentional content that is poised, abstract, and non-conceptual.
- Michael Tye, *Consciousness, Color, and Content*, MIT Press, 2000. Extends and defends representationalism against objections, with particular attention to color experience.
- M. G. F. Martin, "The Transparency of Experience," *Mind and Language* 17, 2002. Argues that the transparency of perceptual experience supports naïve realism and disjunctivism rather than representationalism.
- Paul Snowdon, "Perception, Vision and Causation," *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society* 81, 1980. Influential early formulation of the disjunctivist approach to perception and hallucination.
- J. L. Austin, *Sense and Sensibilia*, Oxford University Press, 1962. Devastating ordinary-language critique of the philosophical presuppositions behind sense-datum theory.
- A. D. Smith, *The Problem of Perception*, Harvard University Press, 2002. Comprehensive treatment defending naïve realism against sense-datum and representationalist alternatives.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Tim Crane, "The Intentional Structure of Consciousness," in Aleksandar Jokic and Quentin Smith (eds.), *Consciousness: New Philosophical Perspectives*, Oxford University Press, 2003. Clear overview of intentionalist theories and their rivals.
- Alex Byrne and Heather Logue (eds.), *Disjunctivism: Contemporary Readings*, MIT Press, 2009. Essential anthology collecting the central papers in the disjunctivism debate with useful editorial introduction.
- Jonathan Dancy, *Introduction to Contemporary Epistemology*, Blackwell, 1985. Chapter on perception provides an accessible survey of sense-datum, adverbial, and direct realist theories.
- Bill Brewer, *Perception and Its Objects*, Oxford University Press, 2011. Defends object-involving direct realism against both representationalism and sense-datum views.
- Fiona Macpherson and Dimitris Platchias (eds.), *Hallucination: Philosophy and Psychology*, MIT Press, 2013. Cross-disciplinary collection examining hallucination and its implications for theories of perception.
- Charles Travis, "The Silence of the Senses," *Mind* 113, 2004. Influential attack on the idea that perceptual experience has intentional or representational content, supporting naïve realism.
- Ned Block, "Inverted Earth," *Philosophical Perspectives* 4, 1990. Thought experiment challenging purely representationalist accounts by separating functional role from qualitative character.
- Christopher Peacocke, *A Study of Concepts*, MIT Press, 1992. Introduces the notion of scenarios and proto-propositions in a systematic theory of perceptual content.
