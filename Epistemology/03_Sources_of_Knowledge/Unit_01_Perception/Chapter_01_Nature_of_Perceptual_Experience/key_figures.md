## Bertrand Russell (1872–1970)

Russell introduced the sense-datum theory to analytic philosophy in *The Problems of Philosophy* (1912), arguing that what we are immediately acquainted with in perception are private, mind-dependent objects — sense-data — rather than physical things. His distinction between knowledge by acquaintance and knowledge by description set the agenda for twentieth-century discussions of direct versus indirect realism. Russell's framework, while influential, raised the problem of how sense-data relate to the external physical world.

## G. E. Moore (1873–1958)

Moore developed a careful version of sense-datum theory that distinguished the act of sensing from the sense-datum that is its object, insisting that sense-data are neither mental nor physical in the ordinary sense. His famous analysis in "The Refutation of Idealism" and later lectures established the conceptual vocabulary — particularly the term "sense-datum" itself — that dominated the debate for decades. Moore's scrupulous attention to how we use perceptual language helped define what a satisfying theory of perception would need to explain.

## Roderick Chisholm (1916–1999)

Chisholm developed the adverbial theory as an alternative to sense-datum accounts, arguing that perceptual reports such as "I see a red square" should be analyzed as descriptions of how one is appeared to — "redly and squarely" — rather than as reports of a relation to a special entity. In *Perceiving* (1957) and subsequent work, he connected adverbialism to his broader epistemological program of foundationalism and the notion of self-presenting mental states. Chisholm's analysis attempted to preserve the epistemic role of experience while avoiding the ontological commitments of sense-data.

## Fred Dretske (1932–2013)

Dretske pioneered the information-theoretic and representationalist approaches to perception, arguing in *Seeing and Knowing* that perception is fundamentally a matter of carrying natural information about the environment. His later work in *Naturalizing the Mind* advanced the thesis that phenomenal properties of experience are identical with representational properties — that how an experience feels just is a matter of what properties it represents. This "strong" representationalism became a central target for both naïve realists and sense-datum theorists.

## Michael Tye (born 1950)

Tye is the most systematic contemporary defender of representationalism about phenomenal consciousness. His PANIC theory (Poised, Abstract, Nonconceptual, Intentional Content) argues that phenomenal character is exhausted by a distinctive kind of representational content that is available to guide rational thought and action. Tye has defended this view across a series of books — *Ten Problems of Consciousness*, *Consciousness, Color, and Content*, *Consciousness and Persons* — systematically responding to objections from inverted qualia, absent qualia, and the phenomenology of bodily sensation.

## M. G. F. Martin (born 1962)

Martin is the leading contemporary defender of naïve realism and disjunctivism. His paper "The Transparency of Experience" argues that when we try to introspect on the character of a perceptual experience, we find only the properties of the objects perceived — a result he takes to support the naïve realist view that mind-independent objects are constitutive of experience itself. Martin's work has shaped an entire research program investigating what it would mean for experience to be object-dependent and how this can accommodate the facts about hallucination.

## J. L. Austin (1911–1960)

Austin's posthumously published *Sense and Sensibilia* subjected the philosophical arguments for sense-datum theory — particularly A. J. Ayer's — to a sustained ordinary-language critique. Austin argued that the philosophical case for sense-data rests on systematic misuse and over-generalization of ordinary perceptual vocabulary, and that the "argument from illusion" commits the fallacy of treating the abnormal case as the key to understanding the normal. Though not himself offering a positive theory, Austin's critique cleared the ground for later direct realist and disjunctivist approaches.
