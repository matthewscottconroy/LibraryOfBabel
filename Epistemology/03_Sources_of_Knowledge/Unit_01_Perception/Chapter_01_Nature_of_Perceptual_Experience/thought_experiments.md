## The Bent Stick in Water

**The scenario:** A straight stick is partially immersed in water. Viewed from an angle, it appears bent. You know the stick is straight — you can feel it with your hands, and you know the physics of light refraction. Yet visually the stick still looks bent.

**The philosophical point:** This classic case is standardly invoked in defense of sense-datum theory. If we directly perceive the stick, and the stick is straight, what are we seeing when we see the bend? The sense-datum theorist concludes we must be perceiving a non-physical, bent entity — a sense-datum. The intentionalist responds that we simply represent the stick as bent; no bent object need exist. The disjunctivist denies that this case is structurally the same as veridical perception, and the naïve realist notes that what we see just is the straight stick seen in unusual circumstances.

## Inverted Qualia

**The scenario:** Imagine a person — call them Z — who has been neurologically "inverted" so that whenever they see what we call red, they have the type of color experience that we have when we see green, and vice versa. Z behaves in every way as we do (stopping at red lights, calling ripe tomatoes "red"), and there is no functional difference detectable from outside. The inversion is systematic and undetectable by any behavioral test.

**The philosophical point:** The inverted qualia scenario is a pressure test for representationalist theories. If two experiences can have the same representational content (representing the same surface as red) while differing in phenomenal character (what it is like for the perceiver), then phenomenal character cannot be fully reduced to representational content. Tye and Dretske respond by arguing that the scenario is less coherent than it appears. The thought experiment also raises questions about what sense-datum and adverbial theories can say about the identity conditions for qualitative states.

## The Complete Hallucination

**The scenario:** A subject, call her Helen, takes a drug that induces a perfectly realistic hallucination of a coffee cup on a desk. Every aspect of her visual experience is qualitatively indistinguishable from a genuine perception of a coffee cup — the color, shape, apparent solidity, and spatial location are all exactly as they would be if a cup were present. There is no cup.

**The philosophical point:** The case forces a choice between two views. The common factor theorist (including traditional sense-datum theorists and representationalists) holds that Helen's experience must have the same fundamental nature as a real perception, since no intrinsic difference exists. The disjunctivist denies this: a veridical perception and a hallucination are of fundamentally different kinds, even if subjectively indistinguishable. The debate turns on whether indistinguishability implies shared nature — a question with broad ramifications for epistemological foundationalism.

## Mary's Room and Perceptual Knowledge

**The scenario:** Mary is a brilliant neuroscientist who has lived her entire life in a black-and-white room. She knows every physical and functional fact about color vision — every wavelength, every firing pattern, every causal connection. One day she leaves the room and sees a ripe tomato for the first time.

**The philosophical point:** Frank Jackson's knowledge argument (originally aimed at physicalism) has direct implications for theories of perceptual experience. The thought experiment suggests that the phenomenal character of perceptual experience — what it is like to see red — is not captured by any purely functional or representational account. For theories of perception, this raises the question of whether intentionalist or adverbial analyses can account for the "what it is like" dimension of experience, or whether something more — something like naïve realist acquaintance with a property — is required.

## Peacocke's Scenario Diagram

**The scenario:** You look down a straight road. The road itself has the same width throughout its length. Yet in your visual experience, the sides of the road appear to converge toward the horizon. Your experience simultaneously presents the road as having uniform width (at the level of representational content) and as having converging sides (at the level of phenomenal appearance).

**The philosophical point:** Christopher Peacocke uses this case to argue that we need two levels of perceptual content: a "scenario" level that captures the fine-grained spatial structure of experience, and a propositional or conceptual level. Representationalists must explain which level carries the phenomenal character. The case also challenges adverbial theories to provide an adequate account of spatial properties of experience without positing objects, and provides evidence against purely propositional accounts of perceptual content.
