# Direct Realism about Perception

Direct realism is the view that in ordinary perception we are immediately aware of the external physical world — of mind-independent objects and their properties — without any mental intermediary. You don't perceive your own experience and then infer the world from it; you perceive the world directly. This view captures the naive phenomenology of perception: when you see a red apple, it genuinely seems to you that you are seeing the apple itself, not some inner representation of it.

## The Core Claim

Direct realism is sometimes called "naïve realism" when formulated as the commonsense view that perception puts us in direct contact with the external world. More technically, it is the denial that the immediate objects of perception are always mental entities — sense data, ideas, or phenomenal representations. The immediate object of perception is the physical object or some aspect of it.

This is to be distinguished from questions about whether our perceptual beliefs are infallible or whether illusion is possible. Direct realism is consistent with the possibility of perceptual error; what it denies is that perceptual errors require positing mental intermediaries. When you see the bent stick in water, you directly (but inaccurately) perceive the stick — it looks bent even though it is straight. You are not perceiving a bent sense datum; you are misperceiving the straight stick.

## Historical Background

Direct realism has ancient roots in Aristotle's account of perception, according to which the sense faculty takes on the form (but not the matter) of the perceived object. The eye takes on the color of what is seen; the ear, the sound. This is a metaphysically loaded form of direct realism.

In the modern period, direct realism was defended by Thomas Reid against the dominant representative realism of Descartes and Locke. Reid argued that the "theory of ideas" — the view that we immediately perceive mental ideas and only mediately know external objects — generates skepticism and is philosophically unmotivated. The natural view, Reid thought, is that perception puts us directly in contact with the world.

## Contemporary Direct Realism

Contemporary direct realism takes several forms:

**Relational direct realism** (connected to disjunctivism): The perceptual relation is a three-place relation between a subject, an act of perceiving, and the perceived object. The object is a genuine constituent of the perceptual state — it is not merely represented but present in the experience. This is the naïve realist position developed by Michael Martin and others.

**Representationalist direct realism**: Some philosophers maintain that perception involves representational content but deny that this makes the immediate object a mental entity. You can represent the world through your experience without the world thereby becoming indirect or mediated. The content of the representation is about external objects, and you thereby have direct access to them through the content.

**Disjunctivist direct realism**: In genuine perception (as opposed to hallucination), you are directly aware of the external object; the object is a constitutive part of the experience. This preserves direct realism for the good case while acknowledging that hallucination is different in nature.

## Epistemic Advantages

Direct realism has important epistemological attractions. If you directly perceive the external world, your perceptual beliefs can be directly justified by what you perceive — without the need to first have justified beliefs about your own mental states, and then infer from those to the external world. This sidesteps the threat of the veil-of-perception skepticism that besets representative realism.

On a direct realist view, your perceptual belief that "there is an apple there" is based directly on seeing the apple. The justification is immediate in the sense that it does not run through a prior epistemic access to a representation of the apple. This makes direct realism naturally congenial to epistemological theories that take perceptual justification to be non-inferential and foundational.

## Challenges

Direct realism faces several challenges:

**The argument from illusion**: Illusions and hallucinations seem to show that the immediate objects of perception can differ from the physical world. If the bent-stick case shows that you perceive bent-stickness even when no bent stick is there, then what you immediately perceive cannot always be the physical object.

Direct realists respond in various ways: by distinguishing perception from misperception, by arguing that illusions are a matter of representing properties inaccurately rather than perceiving false objects, or by embracing disjunctivism (genuine perception is direct; hallucinations are different).

**The causal argument**: Physical objects affect us causally through chains of intervening events (light, neural signals, brain activity). The immediate cause of experience is a brain state, not the distal object. How can you be in direct perceptual contact with something so causally remote?

Direct realists respond that causal mediation doesn't entail epistemic mediation. A causal chain can connect you directly to an object even if it runs through intermediate events.
