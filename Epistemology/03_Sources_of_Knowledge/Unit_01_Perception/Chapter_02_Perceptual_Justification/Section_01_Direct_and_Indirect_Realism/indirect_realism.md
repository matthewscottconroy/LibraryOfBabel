# Indirect Realism and the Veil of Perception

Indirect realism — also called *representative realism* or *causal realism* — holds that we never perceive the external world directly. What we immediately perceive are always mental representations: ideas, sense data, or phenomenal appearances. The external world is known only indirectly, through these inner intermediaries that represent or are caused by external objects. Indirect realism is in many ways the default theoretical option in Western philosophy from Descartes through the twentieth century.

## The Core Position

On indirect realism, perception involves three terms: the subject, the mental representation (sense datum, percept, phenomenal state), and the external object. The subject is directly aware of the representation; the external object is only mediately known — it is inferred or postulated as the best explanation of the representations we have.

The external world is real (this is the "realism" part) but accessed only indirectly through the veil of our representations. This distinguishes indirect realism from idealism (which denies the independent reality of the external world) and from direct realism (which denies the veil).

## Motivations from the Argument from Illusion

The main philosophical motivation is the argument from illusion. Physical objects have stable, objective properties — the stick is straight, the coin is round. But perceptual experience presents them differently in different conditions — the stick looks bent in water, the coin looks elliptical from an angle. Whatever you immediately see must have the properties you see it having. So what you immediately see cannot be the physical object, which has different (true) properties. You must be immediately seeing something else — a sense datum that represents the object.

The argument from hallucination strengthens this. In hallucination, you seem to perceive something that doesn't exist. In that case, you cannot be immediately perceiving any external object — there is none. So you must be immediately perceiving something mental. And if perception in the hallucination case is immediate awareness of a mental object, then by the highest-common-factor argument (perception and hallucination can be qualitatively identical), perception in the veridical case is also immediate awareness of a mental object.

## Locke and the Primary/Secondary Quality Distinction

Locke's version of indirect realism is philosophically interesting because it is integrated with a theory of which properties of our experience accurately represent the external world and which do not. Primary qualities (shape, size, motion, solidity, number) are features of external objects that our ideas more-or-less accurately represent. Secondary qualities (color, taste, smell, warmth) are features of our ideas that do not resemble anything in the external world; they are produced in us by the microscopic primary qualities of objects.

On Locke's account, we have reasonably good knowledge of the primary qualities of objects through our ideas of them, but no knowledge of what secondary qualities are "in" objects beyond the power to produce our experiences. The external world, as it is in itself, is a world of colorless, tasteless, odorless corpuscles in motion.

## The Epistemological Problem: Skepticism

The central difficulty for indirect realism is that it seems to entail or at least invite radical skepticism. If the immediate objects of experience are always mental representations, then knowledge of the external world requires an inference from those representations to external causes. But how can we justify that inference?

Descartes saw this problem clearly. His solution was to prove God's existence and then use God's veracity as a guarantee that our clear and distinct ideas accurately represent reality. This was widely regarded as unsatisfying.

Later philosophers tried other strategies: Berkeley argued that the problem dissolved because there was no external material world to worry about (idealism). Kant argued that we impose the structure of experience on the phenomenal world, so we can be certain of the world as it appears. Hume held that the inference from experience to external world is not rationally justifiable but is a matter of natural belief and custom.

## Representative Realism in Contemporary Philosophy of Mind

Contemporary indirect realism typically takes the form of representationalism in philosophy of mind: mental states have representational contents that stand in satisfaction or accuracy relations to the external world. On this view, the immediacy of experience consists in having a phenomenal state with a certain content; whether that content is accurate depends on whether the world matches it.

The key difference from classical sense datum theory is that contemporary representationalism often maintains that the content of experience is *externally individuated* — what your experience represents is partly determined by your history and environment, not just by the internal state of your brain. This "semantic externalism" about mental content (associated with Putnam and Burge) means that the representative content is world-involving even if the experience is not.

## The Permanent Tension

Indirect realism occupies an unstable position: it is realist about the external world but skeptic-friendly in structure, because knowledge of the external world must be mediated through mental representations. Philosophers have tried to stabilize this position by limiting the epistemic gap, arguing that our representations are reliably caused by external objects and that this reliability provides justification. But this reliabilist move depends on knowing something about the external world — how it causes our representations — which seems to require the very knowledge we are trying to justify.
