# Internalist Accounts of Perceptual Justification

Internalism about perceptual justification holds that what justifies perceptual beliefs must be accessible to the believer — it must be something internal to the mind, available to rational reflection. Perceptual experiences, on this view, justify beliefs because the believer can be aware of their experiences and recognize them as grounds for believing. The justification flows from what the believer has access to, not from external facts about the reliability of their perceptual processes.

## The Appeal of Internalism

The internalist picture captures something intuitive about epistemic responsibility. Justification seems to be the kind of thing that should guide belief: if you have good reasons to believe P, you should believe P. But you can only be guided by reasons you can access. If your perceptual processes are reliable without your knowing it, this fact can't guide your believing; you're just lucky to be reliable.

Internalism also explains the apparent symmetry between perception and inference: in both cases, you recognize that your evidence supports your conclusion, and this recognition is what justifies the conclusion. The difference is only that perceptual evidence is experiential rather than propositional.

## Chisholm and the Given

Roderick Chisholm developed a foundationalist internalism in which perceptual experience provides immediately justified "basic" beliefs. When you have a sensory experience, you are justified in believing propositions like "I seem to see something red" — reports of how things seem to you — because these are self-presenting states that are directly accessible. From these basic beliefs about how things appear, you can form justified beliefs about external objects.

This is often called "the given" — there is a stratum of experiential data that is simply given in consciousness, not inferred, and that forms the secure foundation for all empirical knowledge. Chisholm's system is deontological: you are epistemically obligated to proportion your beliefs to your evidence, and evidence that you have direct access to is the most basic kind.

## Laurence BonJour and the Need for Awareness

BonJour's early coherentism is an internalist view that extends to perception. On his account, even perceptual beliefs require the believer to be aware of reasons that support them. A belief cannot be foundationally justified merely by being reliably caused; the believer must be able to recognize that the belief is reliably produced (or that the experience is a good indicator of its content).

This makes perceptual justification much more cognitively demanding. You must have some conception of your perceptual faculties as reliable to be justified by using them. Critics charge that this is too demanding: small children and animals seem perceptually justified without meeting this condition.

## The Appearance-Reality Distinction and Justification

A key move in internalist accounts is to distinguish between appearance beliefs (about how things seem) and reality beliefs (about how things are). Appearance beliefs are given directly by experience and are indubitable; reality beliefs are justified by their relation to appearance beliefs.

This is the traditional "given and inferred" structure of foundationalism. The problem — Sellars's "myth of the given" — is that even appearance beliefs are not justified merely by being caused by experiences; they require the believer to understand concepts, recognize the experience as a ground for the belief, and situate the belief in a web of conceptual commitments. The "given" is not epistemically innocent; it already involves conceptual processing.

## The Problem of Defeaters

Internalism handles defeaters — information that undermines a justified belief — naturally. If you learn that you have been given a hallucinogenic drug, your justified perceptual belief "there is a red apple on the table" becomes unjustified, because you now have access to undermining information. This fits the internalist picture: your justification depends on what you have access to, and new accessible information can defeat old justification.

## Internalism and the Epistemology of Perception Today

Contemporary internalist accounts of perception often focus on the evidential role of perceptual experience: experiences are evidence for beliefs, and what makes them evidence is accessible to the believer (the character of the experience is introspectively available). This preserves the spirit of internalism while acknowledging that not all internalists need endorse the Cartesian picture of certainty as the standard for genuine justification.

Many epistemologists today hold a moderate internalism: while the justification-making features must be accessible to the believer, this does not require certainty or infallibility, only the availability of reasons that adequately support the belief.
