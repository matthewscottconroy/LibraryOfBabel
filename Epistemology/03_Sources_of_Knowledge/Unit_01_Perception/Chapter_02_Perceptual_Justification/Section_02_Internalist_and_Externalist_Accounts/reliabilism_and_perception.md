# Reliabilism and Perceptual Justification

Reliabilism about perceptual justification holds that perceptual beliefs are justified when they are produced by reliable perceptual processes — processes that tend to produce true beliefs in the conditions in which they operate. Whether you are justified in believing "there is a red apple here" doesn't depend on whether you can recognize that your perceptual faculties are reliable; it depends on whether they actually are. This is the core of the externalist approach to perceptual justification.

## Goldman's Process Reliabilism Applied to Perception

Alvin Goldman's process reliabilism is the most developed externalist account of justification and applies naturally to perception. Perceptual beliefs are justified when they are produced by a reliable belief-forming process — one that yields a high proportion of true beliefs across the range of conditions in which it operates.

Vision is typically reliable in ordinary conditions: in good lighting, with nearby objects, for normally sighted perceivers. So visual beliefs formed under ordinary conditions are typically justified. In deceptive conditions — poor lighting, distant objects, abnormal environments — visual processes may be unreliable, and beliefs formed then may be unjustified even if they happen to be true.

The reliabilist account has an advantage over internalism in not requiring that the believer have any particular access to the reliability of their faculties. Animals and small children with no reflective grasp of their epistemic situation can still have reliably-formed, and hence justified, perceptual beliefs. This matches our intuition that a dog who sees its owner approaching is in some sense perceptually justified in its response.

## Proper Function and Perception

Alvin Plantinga's *proper function* view is a variant of reliabilism applied to cognitive faculties. A perceptual belief is warranted (Plantinga's preferred term) when it is produced by cognitive faculties that are functioning properly — operating according to their design plan in the environment for which they were designed. Normal human vision, functioning as evolution or God intended, produces warranted visual beliefs.

This account adds a normative dimension to reliability: it's not just that the faculties tend to produce truths, but that they are supposed to (by their nature or design) and are doing so in the right environment. A perceptual faculty that reliably produces false beliefs due to a bizarre quirk of the environment is not functioning properly and does not produce warranted beliefs.

## The Generality Problem

Reliabilism about perception faces the generality problem: the reliability of a belief-forming process depends on how narrowly or broadly we individuate it. The process that produces the belief "there is something red" can be described as "visual color perception" (very reliable), "visual perception in poor lighting" (less reliable), or "visual perception of this particular scene at this moment" (no clear statistics). Which description is relevant?

The problem is that the same token process — this particular perceptual event — can instantiate many types, each with different reliability profiles. Reliabilism needs a principled way of choosing the relevant type. Goldman and others have proposed constraints (naturalness of the type, the causal history of the particular event), but no fully satisfactory solution is agreed upon.

## The Clairvoyance Objection

BonJour's clairvoyant thought experiment challenges reliabilism specifically about perception. Suppose Norman has a reliable faculty of clairvoyance about the President's location. His clairvoyant beliefs are reliably true. Does he know where the President is? BonJour argues no: Norman has no reason to think his clairvoyant beliefs are reliable; from his perspective, they are groundless. And a belief formed without any accessible grounds seems unjustified, regardless of its external reliability.

Reliabilists respond that BonJour's intuition conflates justification with what would *seem* justified from the inside. If reliability is what matters, and Norman's faculties are reliable, he is justified. The appearance of groundlessness is misleading if the ground (reliability) really exists.

## New Evil Demon and the Access Objection

The new evil demon problem asks us to consider a subject who is deceived by a demon into having systematically false perceptual beliefs. The demon makes the world look exactly like our world, but the subject's beliefs are mostly false. Reliabilism implies this subject is unjustified — their beliefs are unreliably formed. But the subject seems to be doing everything epistemically right; their epistemic situation from the inside is identical to ours.

This challenges the reliabilist picture: if two subjects can be internally identical yet differ in justificatory status, something seems wrong. The internalist says: justification must be the same in both cases, because justification is determined by the internal situation.

Reliabilists can respond that the demon case shows our ordinary concept of justification is confused between an internal, subjective sense and an external, objective sense, or that the internally identical subject does have something — a kind of "subjective justification" — even if they lack full objective reliability.

## The Value of Reliable Perception

Reliabilism connects perception to knowledge in a direct way: reliably-produced perceptual beliefs are justified, and justified true beliefs produced by reliable processes constitute knowledge (modulo Gettier). This makes perceptual knowledge natural and widespread — most people in ordinary conditions know a great deal about their immediate environment through perception, without any reflective certification of their faculties.
