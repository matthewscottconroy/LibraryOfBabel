## Primary Sources

- Roderick Chisholm, *Theory of Knowledge*, 3rd ed., Prentice-Hall, 1989. Classic internalist account grounding perceptual justification in self-presenting states that are certain and provide the foundation for empirical knowledge.
- Alvin Goldman, "What Is Justified Belief?" in George Pappas (ed.), *Justification and Knowledge*, Reidel, 1979. Founding paper for reliabilism, arguing that a belief is justified if and only if it is produced by a reliable cognitive process.
- Alvin Goldman, *Epistemology and Cognition*, Harvard University Press, 1986. Full development of process reliabilism with extensive application to perception as a paradigm case of a reliable belief-forming mechanism.
- Michael Huemer, "Compassionate Phenomenal Conservatism," *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* 74, 2007. Defends the thesis that if something seems to be the case, the subject thereby has prima facie justification for believing it.
- Michael Huemer, *Skepticism and the Veil of Perception*, Rowman & Littlefield, 2001. Develops phenomenal conservatism as an alternative to both foundationalism based on sense-data and coherentism, applying it specifically to perceptual justification.
- Wilfrid Sellars, "Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind," in *Science, Perception and Reality*, Routledge, 1963. The attack on the "myth of the given" challenges any foundationalist account of perceptual justification, arguing that experience lacks intrinsic epistemic authority.
- John McDowell, *Mind and World*, Harvard University Press, 1994. Defends the view that perceptual experience has conceptual content, making it capable of standing in justificatory relations to beliefs without invoking a non-conceptual given.
- Laurence BonJour, *The Structure of Empirical Knowledge*, Harvard University Press, 1985. Argues against foundationalism and for coherentism, challenging the idea that perceptual experience provides non-inferential justification.
- James Pryor, "The Skeptic and the Dogmatist," *Noûs* 34, 2000. Defends dogmatism about perception — the view that merely having a perceptual experience gives immediate, non-inferential justification for believing its content.
- Alvin Plantinga, *Warrant and Proper Function*, Oxford University Press, 1993. Develops a proper-function account of warrant as an alternative to reliabilism and internalism, with substantial implications for perception.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Richard Fumerton, *Metaepistemology and Skepticism*, Rowman & Littlefield, 1995. Defends acquaintance-based foundationalism and critiques reliabilist approaches to perceptual justification.
- Ernest Sosa, *A Virtue Epistemology*, Oxford University Press, 2007. Develops virtue perspectivism, distinguishing animal knowledge from reflective knowledge and applying this to perception.
- William Alston, *Perceiving God*, Cornell University Press, 1991. Uses the analogy of perceptual justification to defend the rationality of religious experience, but contains important independent analysis of how perception confers justification.
- Ram Neta and Guy Rohrbaugh, "Luminosity and the Safety of Knowledge," *Pacific Philosophical Quarterly* 85, 2004. Examines the connection between luminosity, safety, and perceptual justification.
- Roger Scruton, *Modern Philosophy*, Sinclair-Stevenson, 1994. Chapter on perception provides an accessible overview of direct and indirect realism and their consequences for justified belief.
- Matthias Steup and Ernest Sosa (eds.), *Contemporary Debates in Epistemology*, Blackwell, 2005. Contains exchanges on the nature and grounds of perceptual justification, including direct exchanges between defenders of different views.
- Jonathan Vogel, "Reliabilism Leveled," *Journal of Philosophy* 97, 2000. Influential critique of process reliabilism focusing on the generality problem and its implications for perception.
- Earl Conee and Richard Feldman, *Evidentialism*, Oxford University Press, 2004. Collects papers defending evidentialism, including treatment of how perceptual experience constitutes evidence.
- Timothy Williamson, *Knowledge and Its Limits*, Oxford University Press, 2000. The knowledge-first approach; challenges the assumption that justification or evidence is prior to knowledge, with implications for perceptual epistemology.
