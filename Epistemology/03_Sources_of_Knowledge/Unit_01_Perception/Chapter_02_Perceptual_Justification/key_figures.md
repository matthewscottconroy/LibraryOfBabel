## Roderick Chisholm (1916–1999)

Chisholm's internalist foundationalism grounds perceptual justification in "self-presenting" mental states — states that, if they occur, are certain and directly accessible to the subject. His hierarchy of epistemic terms in *Theory of Knowledge* identifies perceptual appearances as providing the lowest but indispensable rung of the evidential ladder. Chisholm's framework preserves the classical empiricist insight that experience is the foundation of empirical knowledge while avoiding the view that we directly perceive physical objects. His concept of appearing-states has been enormously influential even among epistemologists who reject his Cartesian approach to certainty.

## Alvin Goldman (born 1938)

Goldman's reliabilism, introduced in "What Is Justified Belief?" (1979) and developed in *Epistemology and Cognition* (1986), defines justification in terms of the reliability of the processes that produce beliefs rather than the subject's internal reflective access to reasons. Perception is Goldman's paradigm case: visual beliefs are justified because the human visual system is, under normal conditions, a reliable generator of true beliefs about the environment. Goldman's framework opened epistemology to empirical psychology by making the causal and statistical properties of cognitive processes directly relevant to epistemic evaluation.

## James Pryor (born 1971)

Pryor's dogmatism about perceptual justification holds that the mere occurrence of a perceptual experience as of p gives the perceiver immediate, non-inferential, and defeasible justification for believing p. This "dogmatist" position — named without pejorative intent — is deliberately modest: it claims only that experience is a source of prima facie justification, not that the resulting beliefs are certain or unrevisable. Pryor's account has been widely discussed because it attempts to respect the epistemic access that experience seems to provide while avoiding the regress problems that face accounts requiring prior justification of the reliability of one's senses.

## Michael Huemer (born 1969)

Huemer's phenomenal conservatism is the thesis that if something seems to a subject to be the case — including in perceptual seemings — then the subject has prima facie justification for believing it is the case. Unlike Pryor's dogmatism, Huemer's account is meant to apply to all seemings, not just perceptual ones, making it a general epistemological principle. Huemer argues in *Skepticism and the Veil of Perception* that phenomenal conservatism is the only satisfying stopping point in the regress of justification: if we require justification for trusting our seemings, we face an infinite regress or circularity.

## Wilfrid Sellars (1912–1989)

Sellars's "myth of the given" argument in "Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind" challenged the idea that perceptual experience can serve as a foundation for knowledge by itself, without the contribution of conceptual capacities. Sellars argued that a state can only justify a belief if it stands in rational or inferential relations to other states, and mere sensory goings-on — unconceptualized experience — do not stand in such relations. This critique forced foundationalists to reformulate their views and inspired the coherentist tradition in epistemology, while McDowell's subsequent response attempted to preserve a role for experience by insisting that perceptual states have genuinely conceptual content.

## John McDowell (born 1942)

McDowell's *Mind and World* argues that the solution to the oscillation between the "myth of the given" and rampant coherentism lies in recognizing that perceptual experience already has conceptual content — that it is not raw sensory input but world-involving rational awareness. On his view, experiences can justify beliefs precisely because they are already conceptually structured in a way that makes them apt for standing in inferential relations. McDowell's approach has generated substantial controversy about whether all perceptual content is conceptual and how to account for perceptual justification in non-human animals.
