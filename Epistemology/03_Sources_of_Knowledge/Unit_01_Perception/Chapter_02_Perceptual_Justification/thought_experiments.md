## The Speckled Hen

**The scenario:** You look at a hen that has 48 speckles on one side. Your visual experience is of a speckled hen, but you almost certainly do not have a precise experience as of exactly 48 speckles — the speckles are too many and too similar to be individually tracked in a single glance. Yet if perceptual experience is to justify beliefs, and if the experience has some determinate content, there must be some specific number that it represents. It seems forced to say your experience represents exactly 48 speckles; equally, it is odd to say it has no determinate content about number.

**The philosophical point:** The speckled hen problem, raised by Roderick Chisholm against sense-datum accounts and revived against phenomenal conservatism, targets any view on which perceptual justification flows from the precise qualitative character of experience. If the experience does not determinately represent 48 speckles, then the subject is not justified by perception alone in believing there are exactly 48 speckles. This seems right — but the problem generalizes: if we cannot trust experience to fix determinate content about quantity, why should we trust it to fix determinate content about other properties? The case forces precision about what perceptual experiences do and do not represent.

## The New Evil Demon

**The scenario:** Imagine an exact phenomenal duplicate of you who lives in a world created by an evil demon. Every experience your duplicate has is qualitatively identical to yours, but none of them are veridical — the demon ensures that the beliefs formed are systematically false. Your duplicate forms perceptual beliefs in exactly the way you do, with exactly the same internal states.

**The philosophical point:** The new evil demon problem is a challenge to reliabilism about perceptual justification. Intuitively, your demon-world duplicate is just as justified as you are in their perceptual beliefs — they are doing everything right epistemically. But if justification requires reliability, and the demon ensures unreliability, the duplicate's beliefs are unjustified. Goldman's reliabilists respond by distinguishing between being justified and having justified beliefs in an "evil demon possible world" (weak versus strong senses of justification), or by invoking normal-world reliability standards. The problem sharpens the debate between internalist and externalist accounts of perceptual justification.

## Clairvoyance and Reliable Belief Formation

**The scenario:** Norman has a reliable clairvoyant faculty that delivers true beliefs about the location of the President of the United States. Norman has no evidence for or against his having this faculty, but the faculty is in fact perfectly reliable. Norman "just finds himself" believing that the President is in New York, with no conscious process of reasoning or perception.

**The philosophical point:** BonJour's clairvoyance case is a challenge to reliabilism. It seems that Norman is not justified in his belief about the President's location, despite the reliability of his belief-forming process, because he has no awareness of or reason to trust this process. The case is applied to perception by asking whether a perceiver who knows nothing about the reliability of their senses is justified by perceptual experience. For internalists, justification requires that the subject have access to reasons; for reliabilists, reliability is enough. The case crystallizes the internalism/externalism divide in the epistemology of perception.

## The Deceived Subject's Testimony

**The scenario:** A doctor is shown a perfectly forged patient file that reports a high cholesterol reading. The file looks entirely normal and was produced by a trusted source; there is no reason to suspect forgery. Based on the file, the doctor forms the belief that the patient has high cholesterol.

**The philosophical point:** This variant of the fake barn case probes perceptual justification in a context where the causal chain from world to perceiver has been interfered with. The doctor's belief-forming process (reading reliable reports) is ordinarily reliable, but in this particular instance it fails to be truth-conducive. The case raises the question of whether justification depends on the type of process (reading medical files), the token process (reading this particular file), or the situation's safety features. It forces precision on what level of description defines a belief-forming process for reliabilist purposes — Goldman's notorious "generality problem."

## The Inverted Spectrum and Functional Equivalence

**The scenario:** Suppose you learn that your entire life, your visual experience of the sky has been phenomenally like what others experience when they look at ripe tomatoes — your "blue experience" is their "red experience" — though you have always applied the word "blue" correctly, because the inversion was in place from birth.

**The philosophical point:** This scenario, adapted for the question of perceptual justification, asks whether phenomenally different experiences can provide the same justification for the same perceptual beliefs. If justification depends on the functional or representational role of experience (as reliabilists and representationalists hold), your beliefs are just as justified as those of normal perceivers. If justification tracks the qualitative character of experience (as certain internalists hold), there may be a difference. The thought experiment thus tests whether the epistemic weight of perceptual experience is carried by its phenomenal quality or by its content and causal role.
