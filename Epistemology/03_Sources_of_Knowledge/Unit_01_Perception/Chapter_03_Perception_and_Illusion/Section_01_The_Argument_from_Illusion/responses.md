# Responses to the Argument from Illusion

The argument from illusion has provoked a rich variety of philosophical responses, ranging from outright rejection of its premises to sophisticated reformulations that accept the phenomena while resisting the sense datum conclusion. Understanding these responses illuminates the underlying commitments of different theories of perception.

## The Phenomenal Concepts Response

One response is to distinguish between *perceiving something as having a property* and *perceiving a thing that has that property*. When you see the bent stick in water, you perceive it as bent — your experience has a certain character — without this requiring that any object is bent. The experience has a phenomenal property that might be described as "bent-seeming" but this does not entail there is a bent object within the experience.

This is related to the adverbial reformulation: the "bent" in "the stick looks bent" modifies the experience or the mode of perceiving, not an object that is bent. The argument from illusion trades on a grammatical ambiguity: "there is something bent in my experience" can mean either "there is an object in my experience that is bent" or "my experience has a bent-presenting character." Only the second is forced on us by the phenomena.

## The Intentionalist Response

Intentionalists argue that perceptual experience has representational content that can be accurate or inaccurate. The illusion case is simply a case of inaccurate representation: your visual system represents the stick as bent, but this representation is false. There is no bent object in your experience; your experience just represents the stick (accurately) as being in water and (inaccurately) as being bent.

This preserves the intentionality of experience without positing sense data. Illusions are like false beliefs: a false belief that the stick is bent doesn't require there to be a bent stick; it just represents there being one. Similarly, an illusory experience of a bent stick doesn't require a bent sense datum — it just represents the stick as bent.

The argument from illusion, on this view, involves a category error: it treats the content of representation as if it were an object within the experience.

## The Disjunctivist Response

Disjunctivists target the argument's implicit highest-common-factor assumption. The argument assumes that veridical perception and illusion must share a common mental component, because they can be phenomenologically indistinguishable. Disjunctivism denies this: indistinguishability from the inside doesn't entail sharing a common nature.

In veridical perception, you are directly related to the physical object; the object features in your experience. In illusion, you are in a different kind of mental state — one that mimics the appearance of perception but involves no direct relation to the external world. The two cases are not explained by the same account; they are genuinely different kinds of event that happen to be subjectively indistinguishable.

This response is controversial because it seems to leave the nature of illusory experience mysterious. If a hallucination is not a perception-of-something and not an inner-representation-of-something, what exactly is it?

## Austin's Linguistic Critique

J.L. Austin's *Sense and Sensibilia* (1962) mounted a careful and sometimes caustic critique of the argument from illusion. Austin argued that the argument rests on a misunderstanding of ordinary perceptual language. When we say "the stick looks bent," we are not reporting the presence of a bent mental object; we are using a perceptual vocabulary that philosophers systematically misconstrue.

Austin challenged the assumption that perceptual "looks" reports always correspond to properties of mental objects. He also challenged the move from "things sometimes appear other than they are" to "we are never directly aware of physical things." This inference is far too quick: the fact that perception is sometimes unreliable doesn't show that perception never puts us in contact with the world.

Austin's critique was methodologically important: it directed philosophers to attend to the nuances of ordinary perceptual language before imposing theoretical frameworks.

## Reid's Common Sense Response

Thomas Reid had anticipated many of these moves. He argued that the argument from illusion invokes a "theory of ideas" — the view that we immediately perceive mental representations — which is itself a philosophical construction, not a delivery of common sense. The argument's second premise (that there must be something having the perceived properties) is not obvious but is driven by a questionable philosophical assumption.

Reid's counter: trust perception as it presents itself — as revealing the external world — unless there is compelling reason not to. The fact that perception is sometimes unreliable is not compelling reason to think we are never in direct contact with the world.

## Evaluating the Responses

No single response has achieved consensus. What has become clearer is that the argument from illusion, as originally formulated, relies on a set of contestable assumptions about what perception is of, what it means for experience to have properties, and what indistinguishability entails about underlying nature. Modern philosophy of perception has moved away from the sense datum theory the argument originally motivated while continuing to grapple with the underlying issues about the relationship between perceptual experience and the external world.
