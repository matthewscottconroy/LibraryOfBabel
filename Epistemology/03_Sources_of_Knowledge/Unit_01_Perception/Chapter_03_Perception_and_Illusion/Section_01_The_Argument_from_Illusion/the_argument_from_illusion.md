# The Argument from Illusion

The argument from illusion is one of the most historically significant arguments in the philosophy of perception. It has been used to motivate sense datum theories, to challenge naïve realism, and to raise general questions about what perception really puts us in contact with. The argument takes various forms, but its core structure is the same: appeal to cases where perception is misleading to draw conclusions about what perception is always about.

## The Basic Argument

Here is a classic formulation:

1. In cases of perceptual illusion, things appear to have properties they do not have. (The stick in water looks bent though it is straight; the red tile in orange light looks orange; distant objects look smaller than nearby objects of the same size.)

2. In these cases, there is something that really does have the properties that appear. (There really is something that looks bent, something that looks orange, something that looks smaller.)

3. That something cannot be the physical object, which lacks those properties.

4. So there must be a mental object — a sense datum — that has those properties.

5. Veridical perception (perception that is not illusory) is phenomenologically indistinguishable from illusory perception.

6. Therefore, in veridical perception too, what we immediately perceive is a mental object, not the physical world.

The conclusion is that perception always involves immediate awareness of mental intermediaries. We never directly perceive physical objects.

## The Varieties of Perceptual Distortion

The argument draws on a range of cases:

**Perspectival variation**: A round coin viewed from an angle looks elliptical. A square seen in perspective looks trapezoidal. What you see appears to have a shape different from the shape of the object.

**Conditions-relative variation**: The same tomato looks different colors in different lighting conditions. The same wine tastes different before and after dessert.

**Media-induced distortion**: The stick in water looks bent. Objects seen through thick glass look distorted.

**Size-distance phenomena**: The moon on the horizon looks larger than the moon overhead, though both are the same angular size. Distant objects look small.

The argument uses these cases to suggest that the immediate objects of perception have the variable, observer-relative properties, not the stable, objective properties of the external object.

## The Sense Datum Conclusion

If immediate perceptual awareness is always awareness of something that really has the presented properties — and if that something cannot be the physical object in illusion cases — then in illusion we immediately perceive a mental object (sense datum). And since veridical and illusory experiences can be phenomenologically identical, the same analysis must apply to all cases: we always immediately perceive sense data.

This argument was widely influential in the early and middle twentieth century, driving sense datum theories in Russell, Moore, Ayer, Price, and others.

## Responses to the Argument

Contemporary philosophers have developed several responses:

**The appeared-to formulation**: The stick doesn't look bent — it looks like a straight stick in water that appears bent due to refraction. We can describe the experience without implying that any object is bent. The "apparent properties" are properties of the experience, not properties an object needs to actually have.

**Denying premise 2**: There is no object that is bent in the stick case. "The stick looks bent" does not commit us to there being anything bent. It says how the stick appears, not what the stick is like.

**The disjunctivist response**: The argument moves too quickly from the possibility of illusion to conclusions about all perception. In veridical perception, you do perceive the object directly; in illusion, you are in a different kind of state. The cases are not analyzed by the same account.

**Adverbial reformulation**: We should say "the subject is sensing bent-ly" rather than "the subject perceives a bent object." This avoids the problematic ontology while accommodating the phenomenological fact that experience represents things as bent.

**The perspective objection to the argument**: Even if the coin looks elliptical from an angle, you can know that the coin is round and that you are perceiving its elliptical profile. There is no need to posit a mental elliptical object — you can acknowledge that perception represents the coin as having an elliptical apparent shape without this committing you to a mental entity with that shape.

## The Argument's Lasting Impact

Despite these responses, the argument from illusion has been productive: it forced philosophers to be precise about what perception is of, what properties appear in experience, and how to distinguish what is immediately given from what is inferred. Even those who reject its conclusion typically accept its lesson: the epistemology of perception cannot simply assume that perception always gives us unmediated access to the physical world. The conditions under which perception can be a source of knowledge require careful analysis.
