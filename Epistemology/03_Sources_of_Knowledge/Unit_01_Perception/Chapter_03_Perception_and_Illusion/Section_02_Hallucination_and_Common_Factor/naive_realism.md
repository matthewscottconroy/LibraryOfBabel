# Naïve Realism: The World in Experience

Naïve realism, sometimes called "direct realism with teeth," is the view that in genuine perception, the external world is literally present in experience — that the objects, properties, and relations of the physical world are themselves constituents of perceptual experience. It is not merely that experience represents the world; the world features in experience. This is the most radical form of direct realism, and it has generated some of the most interesting work in contemporary philosophy of perception.

## The Core Thesis

The naïve realist's central claim is that the phenomenal character of veridical perception — what it is like to see a red apple — is partly constituted by the apple itself, its redness, and its shape. The apple is not merely represented in the experience; it is present in it. Remove the apple from the picture, and you change the phenomenal character of the experience.

This is in sharp contrast to representationalist views (whether direct or indirect), which hold that the phenomenal character of experience is determined by its internal representational content, independent of whether the represented objects exist. On representationalism, you could have the same phenomenal experience whether or not the apple is there. Naïve realism denies this: in genuine perception, the apple's presence is partly constitutive of what the experience is like.

## The Philosophical Background

Naïve realism draws on phenomenological insights — on how experience presents itself to the perceiver. Ordinary experience presents a world of objects, not a world of representations. When you look at a sunset, you seem to see the clouds and the colors — not a mental film of clouds and colors. The naïve realist takes this first-personal presentation at face value: in perception, you are in direct contact with the world.

Philosophers like John McDowell, Michael Martin, and Bill Brewer have developed naïve realist positions in recent decades, drawing on Wittgenstein's late philosophy and rejecting the Cartesian model that dominated twentieth-century philosophy of mind.

## Responding to the Argument from Hallucination

The hardest challenge for naïve realism is the argument from hallucination. If the character of genuine perception is partly constituted by external objects, what explains the fact that hallucinations can be phenomenally identical to genuine perceptions? If the apple is absent in hallucination, the phenomenal character should differ — but it doesn't (by hypothesis).

Naïve realists typically embrace disjunctivism in response. They deny that phenomenal identity entails shared constitution. Genuine perception and hallucination are different kinds of event: in the first, the phenomenal character is partly constituted by the world; in the second, it is constituted by something else — perhaps a defective internal state that mimics the appearance of world-involving experience.

The key move: the subjective indistinguishability of genuine perception from hallucination is possible *because* the hallucinatory state is designed (by whatever process — drugs, demons, stimulation) to mimic the appearance of perception. The mimicry is real, but it doesn't show that the two states share a mental constituent.

## Martin's "Impersonation" Account

Michael Martin develops this point through the concept of impersonation. The hallucinatory experience impersonates the veridical experience — it passes for it from the inside — without being it or sharing its constitutive structure. The relationship between hallucination and perception is like the relationship between a very good forgery and the genuine article: subjective identity doesn't imply metaphysical identity.

Martin develops an additional claim: for any candidate mental state that might be the common factor of perception and hallucination, we can argue that it fails to capture what is phenomenologically distinctive about genuine perception. Genuine perception is sui generis — it cannot be adequately characterized in terms of any internal state common to it and hallucination.

## The Epistemological Attractiveness of Naïve Realism

Naïve realism is epistemologically attractive because it offers a genuinely non-skeptical account of perceptual knowledge. If the external world is literally present in perceptual experience, then perceptual knowledge is not mediated by internal representations that might fail to connect to the world. You are in direct epistemic contact with the world in genuine perception.

This addresses what John McDowell calls the "frictionless spinning" objection to coherentist epistemologies: if beliefs can only be justified by other beliefs, the whole edifice of belief might be disconnected from the world. Naïve realism grounds perceptual beliefs in direct world-involvement, providing the "friction" that connects mind and world.

## Challenges

Critics press the question of what happens at the neural level. If naïve realism is correct, the same brain state could in principle occur in the hallucinatory and veridical cases, but the experiences would be different in nature. How can the same neural event be a different type of experience? And if the neural states differ (which they need not in principle), what makes the world-involving experience different from the brain-state-involving one?

These questions about the relationship between the physical and the phenomenal remain open, and naïve realism's answers are still being developed.
