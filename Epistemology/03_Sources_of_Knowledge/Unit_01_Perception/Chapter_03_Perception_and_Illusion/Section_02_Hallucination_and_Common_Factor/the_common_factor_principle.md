# The Common Factor Principle and the Argument from Hallucination

The argument from hallucination is a close companion to the argument from illusion, but it cuts deeper. Illusion involves perceptual distortion; hallucination involves perceiving something that does not exist at all. If you can have a subjectively indistinguishable experience of a pink elephant that is not there, this seems to require that there is something in your experience that appears pink and elephant-shaped — and that something cannot be a physical elephant. The common factor principle is the philosophical thesis that drives this argument and connects to some of the most fundamental debates in the philosophy of perception and mind.

## The Common Factor Principle

The common factor principle states: if a veridical perceptual experience and a hallucination are subjectively indistinguishable from the inside, then they must share a common mental component — a "highest common factor" — that explains their phenomenological similarity.

The principle seems intuitively obvious. If two experiences are phenomenally identical — if there is no difference in what it is like to have them — surely there must be some mental feature they share. And that shared feature must be what explains the subjective similarity. 

Applied to hallucination: your veridical experience of a tree and your hallucination of a tree can be phenomenally identical. They share a common mental component. That component — whatever is common to both — is the immediate object of both experiences. In hallucination, it is clearly not the physical tree (there is none). So in veridical perception too, the immediate object is a mental representation, not the tree itself.

This is the highest-common-factor argument for indirect realism or sense datum theory.

## Why the Principle Is Attractive

The common factor principle has intuitive support from the concept of supervenience. Mental states supervene on brain states: if your brain is in exactly the same state, you have exactly the same mental experience. But a hallucinatory brain state and a veridical perceptual brain state can be (in principle) identical. Since the brain state is the same, the mental state is the same. So the mental component of hallucination and veridical perception is the same — there is a common factor.

This "brain in a vat" variant of the argument is particularly compelling because it appeals to physical supervenience, not just phenomenological indistinguishability. Even if somehow the experiences felt slightly different, if the brain states are identical, the mental states would be identical.

## Challenging the Principle: Disjunctivism's Attack

The most direct challenge to the common factor principle comes from disjunctivism. Disjunctivists deny that subjective indistinguishability entails a common mental nature. They argue that the common factor principle confuses two questions:

- *What is the epistemically accessible character of the experience?*
- *What kind of event the experience is?*

These can come apart. Two events can have the same epistemically accessible character without being the same kind of event. A perfectly convincing fake and the real thing might be indistinguishable to the observer, but they are not of the same kind.

Applied to perception: veridical perception and hallucination can have the same epistemically accessible character (you can't tell from the inside which you're having) without being the same kind of mental event. In veridical perception, the external object is a constitutive part of the experience; in hallucination, it isn't. The common factor principle mistakes access-identity for metaphysical-identity.

## Martin on Naïve Realism and the Argument

Michael Martin has developed a sophisticated response that accepts much of the phenomenological data while rejecting the common factor conclusion. Martin's position — *naïve realism* — holds that in genuine perception, experience has its character in virtue of the perceived objects and properties. The experience is constituted by its relation to the world.

But Martin denies that this requires a common mental component with hallucination. In hallucination, there is no experience-constituting relation to the world; the phenomenal character must be explained differently. The two cases are fundamentally different even if they are indistinguishable from the first-person perspective. Martin calls the relationship between perceptual and hallucinatory experience one of "impersonation" — the hallucination impersonates the experience of perceiving without being it.

## Epistemological Implications

The common factor principle has direct epistemological implications. If hallucination and veridical perception share a common mental component, then your epistemic access to the world in perception must go through this mental intermediary. You cannot directly know whether you are perceiving or hallucinating; you only have access to the shared mental component. This re-opens the door to skeptical worries: since you can't distinguish perceiving from hallucinating from the inside, how can perception be a direct source of knowledge about the external world?

Disjunctivism avoids this by denying that you are always in the position of the internal state. In genuine perception, you have direct access to the world; in hallucination, you are in a degraded epistemic situation. The skeptic's scenario (you might be hallucinating) is possible but doesn't undermine the distinctively good epistemic position you occupy in genuine perceptual cases.
