## Primary Sources

- A. J. Ayer, *The Foundations of Empirical Knowledge*, Macmillan, 1940. Ayer's formulation of the argument from illusion as establishing that we perceive sense-data rather than physical objects became the target of subsequent disjunctivist and ordinary-language responses.
- J. L. Austin, *Sense and Sensibilia*, Oxford University Press, 1962. The definitive ordinary-language critique of Ayer's argument, exposing the argument's reliance on tendentious philosophical vocabulary and a false principle of like cases.
- Paul Snowdon, "Perception, Vision and Causation," *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society* 81, 1980–81. Founding paper for disjunctivism, arguing that genuine perception and matching hallucination belong to logically distinct categories.
- M. G. F. Martin, "The Transparency of Experience," *Mind and Language* 17, 2002. Argues that the phenomenological transparency of veridical experience supports a naïve realist disjunctivism.
- M. G. F. Martin, "On Being Alienated," in Tamar Gendler and John Hawthorne (eds.), *Perceptual Experience*, Oxford University Press, 2006. Develops the negative epistemic conception of hallucination central to sophisticated disjunctivism.
- Howard Robinson, *Perception*, Routledge, 1994. Extended defense of sense-datum theory with careful analysis of the argument from illusion and responses to critics.
- Frank Jackson, *Perception: A Representative Theory*, Cambridge University Press, 1977. Defends a representative realist version of sense-datum theory and the claim that the argument from illusion succeeds.
- Alva Noë and Evan Thompson (eds.), *Vision and Mind: Selected Readings in the Philosophy of Perception*, MIT Press, 2002. Useful anthology including key papers on illusion and hallucination from multiple perspectives.
- Gregory Currie and Ian Ravenscroft, *Recreative Minds*, Oxford University Press, 2002. Explores the common factor principle in the context of imagination and hallucination, raising questions about the relationship between perception and mental imagery.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Alex Byrne and Heather Logue (eds.), *Disjunctivism: Contemporary Readings*, MIT Press, 2009. The essential collection for the disjunctivism debate, with papers by Martin, Snowdon, McDowell, Byrne, and critics.
- Bill Fish, *Perception, Hallucination, and Illusion*, Oxford University Press, 2009. Systematic defense of naïve realism against the argument from hallucination, with detailed treatment of illusion.
- Fiona Macpherson and Dimitris Platchias (eds.), *Hallucination: Philosophy and Psychology*, MIT Press, 2013. Cross-disciplinary collection examining the nature of hallucination and its philosophical implications.
- Michael Tye, "Visual Qualia and Visual Content Revisited," in David Chalmers (ed.), *Philosophy of Mind: Classical and Contemporary Readings*, Oxford University Press, 2002. Representationalist response to the argument from hallucination.
- Susanna Siegel, *The Contents of Visual Experience*, Oxford University Press, 2010. Argues for rich representational content and examines how illusion and hallucination bear on what is represented.
- Tim Crane, "The Case for Intentionalism," *Topoi* 25, 2006. Defends intentionalism as providing the correct diagnosis of what goes wrong in illusion and hallucination.
- Fred Dretske, "Experience as Representation," *Philosophical Issues* 13, 2003. Argues that illusions are misrepresentations rather than perceptions of non-physical objects, defending the representationalist framework.
- Christopher Peacocke, "Analogue Content," *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society* Supplementary Volume 60, 1986. Explores how illusory experiences can be accommodated within a representational theory of content.
- Dominic Gregory, "Seeing If Not Seeing As," *Philosophical Quarterly* 60, 2010. Nuanced analysis of the relationship between illusion and seeing-as, relevant to Austin's critique.
