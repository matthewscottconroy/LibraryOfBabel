## A. J. Ayer (1910–1989)

Ayer gave the most influential twentieth-century formulation of the argument from illusion in *The Foundations of Empirical Knowledge* (1940). He argued that in cases of illusion and hallucination, what we are directly aware of cannot be the physical object (which may not have the properties it appears to have), and so we must be aware of private sense-data. Ayer then applied the common factor principle: since illusory and veridical experiences can be phenomenally indistinguishable, what we are aware of in the veridical case must be the same type of thing — a sense-datum. Austin identified Ayer's argument as the primary target of *Sense and Sensibilia*.

## J. L. Austin (1911–1960)

Austin's posthumous *Sense and Sensibilia* is the most celebrated attack on the philosophical argument from illusion. Austin methodically identified the equivocations, false generalizations, and loaded vocabulary — terms like "directly" and "really" — that, in his view, make the argument appear compelling but are in fact confused when applied to ordinary perceptual discourse. His approach is negative rather than constructive: he does not offer a positive theory of perception but instead shows that the argument does not succeed on its own terms, clearing the ground for a less theoretically encumbered approach to the phenomena.

## Paul Snowdon (born 1947)

Snowdon introduced the disjunctivist response to the argument from illusion in a series of papers beginning in 1980. He argued that the logical form of perceptual reports is disjunctive: "there is something which looks red to S, or S is having a red experience in the relevant sense," where the two disjuncts refer to fundamentally different kinds of state. This move blocks the inference from illusion or hallucination to a common factor in veridical perception, because the disjunctivist denies that the surface grammar of perceptual reports reveals a single, common perceptual relation.

## M. G. F. Martin (born 1962)

Martin developed the most sophisticated contemporary version of disjunctivism under the label "naïve realism" or the "openness view." In "On Being Alienated" and related papers, he argued that the correct characterization of hallucination is negative: a hallucination is an experience that merely appears to be, but is not, a case of perceiving. On this view, a hallucination is genuinely indistinguishable from perception from the inside — but this is because it borrows or mimics the structure of genuine perception rather than sharing its fundamental nature. Martin's account has been enormously influential and prompted new work in both philosophy of mind and epistemology.

## Howard Robinson (born 1945)

Robinson is the most persistent contemporary defender of the sense-datum theory and the argument from illusion against disjunctivist challenges. In *Perception* (1994) and subsequent work, he argues that the phenomenological facts about illusion and hallucination genuinely support the conclusion that we perceive private inner objects, and that disjunctivist responses either change the subject or fail to address the argument's key premises. Robinson's work has helped keep the sense-datum tradition alive as a serious philosophical option at a time when disjunctivism has become dominant.

## Susanna Siegel (born 1974)

Siegel's work on the richness of perceptual content has important implications for the epistemology of illusion. In *The Contents of Visual Experience* (2010), she argues that perceptual experience represents high-level properties such as natural-kind membership and causation, not merely low-level properties such as color and shape. Illusions thus become cases of systematic misrepresentation of these richer properties. Her account challenges both sparse representationalism and naïve realism, and raises the question of whether top-down cognitive influences on perception — such as those that produce some perceptual illusions — infect the evidential standing of experience.
