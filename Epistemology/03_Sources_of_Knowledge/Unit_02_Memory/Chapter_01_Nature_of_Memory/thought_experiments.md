## The Causal Deviance Problem

**The scenario:** A traveler, Victor, visits a foreign city and has a vivid experience of a distinctive red fountain in the town square. His experience leaves a memory trace. Later, however, a neurological event destroys that trace. By a remarkable coincidence, the same neurological event produces in Victor a different but accurate belief that there was a red fountain in the town square — not caused by the original experience but by the neurological event which happens to produce an accurate representation.

**The philosophical point:** This case, adapted from discussions by C. B. Martin and Max Deutscher, shows that the causal theory of memory requires not just any causal connection between the original experience and the later memory state, but the right kind of causal connection. Victor satisfies the requirement of having had the experience and of having an accurate representation of it, yet intuitively he does not genuinely remember the fountain. The case parallels analogous problems in the causal theory of knowledge and of reference, illustrating that the concept of memory is normatively constrained in a way that goes beyond mere causal co-occurrence.

## Quasi-Memory and Personal Identity

**The scenario:** Sydney Shoemaker introduces the concept of "quasi-memory": a state that has all the features of genuine memory except that it need not be caused by an experience the subject herself had. Imagine that through some future brain procedure, your quasi-memory system is imprinted with accurate records of another person's past experiences. You now have vivid, apparently memorial representations of experiences you never had.

**The philosophical point:** The quasi-memory thought experiment was designed by Shoemaker and developed by Derek Parfit to separate the concept of memory from the concept of personal identity. Ordinary memory presupposes that the rememberer is the person who had the original experience; quasi-memory does not. Parfit uses this distinction to argue that personal identity is not a deep metaphysical fact but a matter of psychological continuity — and that quasi-memory can constitute that continuity. The case forces the question of whether memory's epistemic role depends on the subject's identity with the past experiencer, or only on a causal chain of the right type.

## Bartlett's War of the Ghosts

**The scenario:** Bartlett presented English undergraduates in the 1920s with a Native American folktale containing culturally unfamiliar narrative structures, motivations, and supernatural elements. He then asked subjects to recall the story immediately and at progressively longer intervals afterward. He found that subjects systematically omitted unfamiliar details, substituted culturally familiar equivalents, rationalized unexplained actions, and shortened the narrative to conform to Western storytelling conventions.

**The philosophical point:** Bartlett's findings show that memory is not a passive reproduction of stored representations but an active reconstruction guided by schematic expectations. Each recall is shaped by the subject's current knowledge, conceptual frameworks, and background assumptions. For epistemology, this means that memory beliefs inherit not only the evidential properties of the original experience but also the distorting influence of all subsequent cognitive activity. This raises the question of whether memory can serve as a reliable transmitter of past-acquired justification, or whether its reconstructive character systematically undermines its epistemic credentials.

## The Flashbulb Memory

**The scenario:** You have a vivid, detailed, highly confident memory of exactly where you were and what you were doing when you heard about a major public event. The memory seems photographically precise — you can "see" the room, feel your emotional state, recall the exact words you heard. Yet subsequent research compares your account with a record made shortly after the event and finds significant discrepancies: details have shifted, emotions have been reattributed, and the narrative has been revised.

**The philosophical point:** Flashbulb memories — highly confident, emotionally salient memories that subjects treat as especially reliable — are in fact no more accurate than ordinary memories, as empirical research by Ulric Neisser and others has shown. The philosophical significance is that phenomenal vividness and high subjective confidence are poor guides to memory accuracy. This dissociation between subjective credence and objective reliability is a direct challenge to internalist accounts that ground the epistemic standing of memory in how the memory state presents itself to the subject. It also raises practical questions about the social and institutional reliance on eyewitness testimony.

## The Engram and the Absent Trace

**The scenario:** Neuroscientist Karl Lashley spent decades searching the rat brain for the physical substrate of a learned behavior — the memory trace or "engram." He systematically destroyed different brain regions, expecting to find that removing a specific area would eliminate the relevant memory. Instead, he found that memories were broadly distributed, with no single localized trace for a given memory.

**The philosophical point:** Lashley's experiments (though later complicated by subsequent findings) raise the philosophical question of whether there must be a specific physical trace corresponding to each memory. If memory traces are distributed, multiply realized, or dynamic rather than static, this has implications for the causal theory of memory: the causal chain required for genuine remembering cannot be a simple connection between a localized engram and a later memory state. It also complicates the relationship between the philosophy of memory and cognitive neuroscience, suggesting that the "storehouse" metaphor fundamentally misleads us about the nature of memory representation.
