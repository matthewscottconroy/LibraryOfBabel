# Memory as Preservative: The Epistemic Status of Memorial Beliefs

One of the central questions in the epistemology of memory is whether memory is a *source* of justification or merely a *preserver* of it. The preservative view — dominant in the literature — holds that memory cannot generate epistemic justification on its own; it can only preserve justification that beliefs had when they were first formed through perception, inference, or other primary sources. This view has significant consequences for understanding how memory interacts with the rest of our epistemic architecture.

## The Preservationist Thesis

The core claim of preservationism: if you were justified in believing P at time t1 (by perception, testimony, inference), and you later recall that P at time t2, your belief at t2 is justified — but *only derivatively*, in virtue of the original justification being preserved through memory. Memory carries the epistemic status of the belief forward through time; it does not add new justification.

If the original belief was unjustified, then the memorial belief is unjustified too. Memory cannot launder epistemic defects. If you originally formed the belief on the basis of wishful thinking or inadequate evidence, remembering it doesn't improve its epistemic status.

This view is associated with Thomas Reid's "principle of memory": memory creates a presumptive entitlement to memorial beliefs, but this entitlement depends on the original source of the belief being reliable. Pryor's "dogmatist" position on memory extends this: memory generates prima facie justification for remembered propositions, but the justification is derivative from the original source.

## Evidence for Preservationism

Preservationism seems to capture the common sense view of how memory works epistemically. If you remember that you locked the door this morning, your justification for this belief derives from having seen (or felt) the lock click. The memory is a conduit for that perceptual justification. Without a reliable original source, no amount of vivid, confident remembering generates justification.

This is confirmed by our practices of testimonial corroboration: when someone's memory claim is important, we look for corroborating evidence — witnesses, records, physical evidence. This suggests we don't treat memory itself as an independent justification-generating source but always trace justification back to original sources.

## The Generationist Alternative

Some philosophers have argued that memory has a kind of justification-generating capacity of its own — that memorial states can provide prima facie justification even when no original justified belief is traced. This would make memory more analogous to perception: just as current perception can justify current beliefs regardless of causal history, current memory could justify current beliefs based on the character of the memorial experience itself.

The generationist view is motivated by cases where you cannot recall how you originally came to believe something. You remember that Paris is the capital of France but have no recollection of learning this. If preservationism is right, your current justification depends on some original learning event, even if you can't access it. This seems plausible — some original learning presumably occurred. But if you try to trace justification all the way back to original sources, you face epistemic regress problems for all long-standing beliefs.

## The Problem of Forgotten Evidence

A variant of the generationist concern: you originally believed P on the basis of good evidence, but the evidence is now forgotten while the belief persists. Is the belief still justified? Preservationists say yes — the original justification is preserved even if the evidence that generated it is no longer accessible. But this seems to mean that your memorial belief floats free of any accessible evidence, justified only by a long-past epistemic episode you can't access.

Critics argue this makes justification too easy: any belief once justified becomes permanently epistemically proper as long as memory functions. But surely beliefs that have become evidentially isolated — disconnected from any accessible evidential basis — lose their justification over time.

## Memory and Rational Belief Revision

The preservative view connects to questions about belief revision. If you receive new evidence that conflicts with a memorial belief, you should update. But the update should be informed by the weight of the original source: a belief based on strong, direct evidence should resist revision by weak counterevidence; a belief based on weak inference should revise more readily. The preservative view implies that the rational weight of a memorial belief is a function of its entire history, not just its current memorial feel.

This is epistemically demanding: tracking the evidential history of all your beliefs is beyond ordinary cognitive capacity. In practice, we rely on heuristics — the vividness and confidence of memories, the plausibility of their content, the coherence with related beliefs — to approximate the ideal of tracking original epistemic status.
