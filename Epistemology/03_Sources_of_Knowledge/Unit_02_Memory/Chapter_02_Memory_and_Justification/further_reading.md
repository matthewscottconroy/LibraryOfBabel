## Primary Sources

- Bertrand Russell, *The Analysis of Mind*, Allen & Unwin, 1921. Contains the five-minute hypothesis — the suggestion that the world was created recently complete with apparent memory traces — as a skeptical challenge to memory-based knowledge.
- Sydney Shoemaker, "Personal Identity: A Materialist's Account," in Shoemaker and Swinburne, *Personal Identity*, Blackwell, 1984. Develops quasi-memory and argues for the deep connection between memory and personal identity in the context of personal persistence.
- John Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, Book II, Chapter 27, 1689; ed. P. H. Nidditch, Oxford University Press, 1975. The classic statement that personal identity through time consists in memory continuity, the starting point for all subsequent debates.
- Derek Parfit, *Reasons and Persons*, Oxford University Press, 1984. Part III develops psychological continuity theory with quasi-memory and challenges the view that personal identity is what matters in survival.
- Laurence BonJour, *The Structure of Empirical Knowledge*, Harvard University Press, 1985. Chapter 6 addresses memory within a coherentist framework, arguing that memory beliefs require coherentist support rather than autonomous foundational status.
- Peter Fumerton, *Metaepistemology and Skepticism*, Rowman & Littlefield, 1995. Defends an acquaintance-based foundationalist treatment of memory, arguing for a direct connection between memorial experience and epistemic access to the past.
- Elizabeth Loftus, *Eyewitness Testimony*, Harvard University Press, 1979. Documents how memory distortion — through misinformation, leading questions, and post-event suggestion — affects the reliability of testimony.
- Michael Huemer, "The Problem of Memory Knowledge," *Pacific Philosophical Quarterly* 80, 1999. Examines whether memory can generate new justification or only preserve justification acquired at the time of the original experience.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Sven Bernecker, *Memory: A Philosophical Study*, Oxford University Press, 2010. Comprehensive treatment of memory epistemology, covering preservationism, generationism, and the role of memorial states in justifying beliefs.
- Sven Bernecker and Kourken Michaelian (eds.), *The Routledge Handbook of Philosophy of Memory*, Routledge, 2017. Definitive reference covering all topics in memory epistemology including transmission of justification, false memory, and skepticism.
- Kourken Michaelian, *Mental Time Travel: Episodic Memory and Our Knowledge of the Personal Past*, MIT Press, 2016. Develops a simulationist account with direct implications for the epistemic status of memory, arguing that memory's reliability is epistemically relevant even if it is not a simple "replay."
- Adam Pautz, "Is There an Integrated Science of Memory?" *Philosophical Psychology* 26, 2013. Examines how the cognitive science of memory bears on epistemological questions about memorial justification.
- Christopher Peacocke, "Judgement, Principles, and the Past," in A. Clark et al. (eds.), *The Legacy of Wittgenstein*, Blackwell, 1986. Discusses memory and the epistemological requirement of taking one's past judgements as having authority.
- Carl Ginet, *Knowledge, Perception, and Memory*, Reidel, 1975. Careful foundationalist treatment of memory within an internalist framework.
- Don Locke, *Memory*, Macmillan, 1971. Philosophical analysis of memory distinguishing types of memory and their respective epistemic significance.
- Jennifer Lackey and Ernest Sosa (eds.), *The Epistemology of Testimony*, Oxford University Press, 2006. Contains chapters relevant to the overlap between memory and testimonial justification — particularly the question of how remembered testimony transmits knowledge.
- Jonathan Sutton, "Stick to What You Know," *Noûs* 39, 2005. Examines whether we can know on the basis of memory even when the stored information has degraded, with implications for memory's transmissive vs. generative status.
