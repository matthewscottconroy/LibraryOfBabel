## Bertrand Russell (1872–1970)

Russell's *The Analysis of Mind* (1921) raised the most radical skeptical challenge to memory-based knowledge: the five-minute hypothesis. He argued that no evidence available to us could rule out the possibility that the world was created five minutes ago, complete with all the apparent traces of a past — physical records, neural states, and seemingly memorial experiences — that would normally be produced by a much longer history. Russell used this not to advocate global skepticism but to show that memory's epistemic warrant cannot be self-validating, and that any justification of memory beliefs must appeal to structural features of the world rather than to the content of the memories themselves.

## John Locke (1632–1704)

Locke's memory theory of personal identity in *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1689) established the framework within which subsequent discussions of memory and justification occur. Locke argued that a person at one time is the same person as a person at another time if and only if the later person can remember the experiences of the earlier. This strong connection between memory and personal identity raises the epistemic question of what grounds our trust in memory: if the very concept of a persisting self depends on memory continuity, then memory cannot straightforwardly be validated by appeal to the persistence of the self that has the memories.

## Sven Bernecker (born 1965)

Bernecker has produced the most systematic contemporary philosophical treatment of memory epistemology. In *Memory: A Philosophical Study* (2010) and *The Metaphysics of Memory* (2008), he carefully distinguishes preservationism — the view that memory transmits but does not generate justification — from generationism — the view that the memorial experience itself provides new justification. Bernecker defends a version of preservationism according to which justified belief is preserved through memory as long as the causal chain from original experience to memory belief is of the right type, and the subject has not acquired defeating evidence in the interim.

## Kourken Michaelian (born 1975)

Michaelian's *Mental Time Travel* (2016) argues that episodic memory is best understood not as a reproductive process that "replays" stored experiences, but as a constructive, imaginative simulation of the past. This simulationist account challenges traditional preservationism: if memory is constructive, the relationship between original epistemic standing and memorial epistemic standing is not one of simple transmission. Michaelian argues that memory can still be epistemically reliable in a way relevant to justification, but the reliability is that of a well-calibrated simulation rather than a fidelity-to-original-trace.

## Elizabeth Loftus (born 1944)

Loftus's experimental work on the misinformation effect and false memory has transformed our understanding of memory's reliability as a source of justified belief. Her studies show that subjects confidently "remember" events that never occurred — a phenomenon particularly relevant to the epistemology of testimony. Her research on eyewitness testimony has practical implications for the law, but its deeper epistemological significance is the demonstration that high confidence and apparent phenomenological vividness are poor indicators of memorial accuracy, undercutting internalist accounts that rely on the subject's own access to the quality of their memorial states.

## Michael Huemer (born 1969)

Huemer's "The Problem of Memory Knowledge" examines whether memory merely preserves justification originally acquired through perception, or whether memorial experience can itself generate new justification for a belief about the past. He defends a version of phenomenal conservatism applied to memory: if something seems to you to have been the case — if you have a memorial seeming — then you thereby have prima facie justification for that memory belief, justification that is not merely inherited from the original perceptual episode. This generationist position has significant implications for cases in which the original perceptual experience may not have been justified, or where the subject cannot recall whether their original belief was based on good evidence.
