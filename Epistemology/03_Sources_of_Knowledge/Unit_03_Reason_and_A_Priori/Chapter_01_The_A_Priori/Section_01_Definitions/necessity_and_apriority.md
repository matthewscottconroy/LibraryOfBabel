# Necessity and Aprioricity: Two Dimensions of the A Priori

The relationship between necessity and a priority is one of the most subtle and consequential issues in epistemology and metaphysics. For centuries they were treated as coextensive — necessary truths are knowable a priori, and a priori knowledge is of necessary truths. Kripke's work in the 1970s disentangled them, revealing each as an independent dimension that requires separate analysis.

## The Traditional Equation

The traditional view had a clean architecture: the analytic, the a priori, and the necessary all coincided, and the synthetic, the a posteriori, and the contingent coincided on the other side. Knowing 2 + 2 = 4 requires no empirical verification because it is necessarily true — it could not be otherwise — and necessarily true because it is analytic — it follows from the definitions of the concepts involved.

This picture is appealing because it explains each dimension in terms of the others. Why is a priori knowledge possible? Because the truths known a priori are necessary, and necessary truths are what logic and reason reveal. Why are necessary truths knowable without experience? Because they are analytic, and analytic truths are knowable by conceptual analysis.

Kant's synthetic a priori was already a crack in this picture: he proposed truths that are a priori and necessary but not analytic. But even Kant assumed that the a priori and the necessary coincide.

## Kripke's Disentanglement

Kripke's achievement was to show clearly that necessity and aprioricity are distinct dimensions of propositions, belonging to different categories: necessity is a metaphysical property of propositions (their modal status), aprioricity is an epistemic property (how they can be known). These can come apart.

**Necessary a posteriori truths** are truths that hold in all possible worlds but that can only be known through empirical investigation. The paradigm cases involve natural kind terms and identity statements:

- "Water is H₂O" — necessarily true (water just is H₂O, in every possible world; anything that lacks this chemical composition is not water) but known a posteriori (through chemistry).
- "Hesperus is Phosphorus" — necessarily true (they are the same planet) but known a posteriori (through astronomical observation).
- "Gold is the element with atomic number 79" — necessarily true if correct but discovered empirically.

These cases rest on Kripke's theory of rigid designation: "water," "gold," and "Hesperus" rigidly designate their referents across possible worlds; their identity with specific natural kinds or individuals is therefore necessary. But we find out which things are identical to what — which natural kind is water — through empirical inquiry.

**Contingent a priori truths** are truths that are knowable without experience but that might have been false. Kripke's example: if you introduce the name "one meter" by the stipulation that "one meter = the length of this stick" (pointing to the standard meter), then "this stick is one meter long" is knowable a priori (by the stipulation) at the moment of introduction. But it is contingently true — the stick might have been longer or shorter.

More philosophically significant: "I exist" seems to be knowable a priori (by Descartes' cogito) but is contingently true — I might not have existed.

## Implications for the Philosophy of Language

The necessary a posteriori has major implications for the philosophy of language. The traditional view was that the meaning of a term determines its reference (descriptions fix meaning and meaning determines reference). Kripke attacked this with his account of rigid designation: names directly refer to individuals, and descriptions associated with a name by speakers need not be constitutive of meaning.

This separates semantic from epistemic questions. What makes "water is H₂O" necessary has to do with the essential nature of water (what water *is* across possible worlds), not with what speakers associate with the word "water." Our concepts can fall short of our words' referents.

## Implications for Epistemology

The disentanglement of necessity from aprioricity has epistemological consequences:

1. **A priori knowledge is not automatically metaphysically significant**: knowing something a priori does not show that it is necessary; it might be a contingent a priori truth.

2. **Empirical knowledge can be metaphysically deep**: necessary truths about natural kinds are known empirically. This challenges the idea that metaphysics is the province of pure reason.

3. **The scope of the a priori is uncertain**: if some necessary truths (like "water is H₂O") are a posteriori, then necessity does not automatically signal a priori accessibility. The extent of genuine a priori knowledge cannot be read off from a catalog of necessary truths.

4. **Two-dimensional semantics** has developed as a framework for handling these distinctions: it distinguishes between the "primary" (epistemic) intension of a term (which determines a priori truths) and the "secondary" (metaphysical) intension (which determines necessary truths).
