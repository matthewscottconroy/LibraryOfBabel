# What Is A Priori Knowledge?

A priori knowledge is knowledge that is independent of experience in its justification. You know it without needing to go out and check the world — the warrant for it comes "from reason alone," as the rationalists put it, rather than from sensory observation or empirical inquiry. The concept is one of the most important and contested in all of epistemology, sitting at the heart of disputes about rationalism and empiricism, necessity and contingency, logic and mathematics.

## The Classic Characterization

The Latin phrase *a priori* means "from what is prior" — prior, that is, to experience. The contrasting term *a posteriori* ("from what is later") refers to knowledge that is justified through experience, specifically through sensory perception and empirical inquiry.

The canonical examples of a priori knowledge are mathematics ("2 + 2 = 4") and logic ("if it is raining and it is cold, then it is raining"). You don't need to count apples or verify truth tables empirically to know these things. They can be known by reasoning alone.

Kant provided a classic formulation: a priori knowledge is characterized by necessity and universality. Empirical knowledge is always contingent — things might have been otherwise — and particular. A priori knowledge holds necessarily and universally: it could not be otherwise, and no exceptions are possible.

## The Epistemic Sense vs. The Metaphysical Sense

It is important to distinguish two things that might be meant by "a priori":

**The epistemic sense**: A proposition P is knowable a priori if it can be justified without appeal to experience — by reflection, conceptual analysis, or rational intuition alone. This is the primary sense in epistemology.

**The metaphysical sense**: A proposition is necessary if it could not be otherwise — if it is true in all possible worlds. Necessary truths (like mathematical truths) are often thought to be knowable a priori, and the connection is not coincidental. But the two come apart in Kripke's work (see below).

Keeping these distinct prevents the confusion of thinking that a priori knowledge must be about necessary truths, or that all necessary truths are known a priori.

## Kripke and the Separation of Necessity from Aprioricity

Saul Kripke's *Naming and Necessity* (1972) was a watershed. Kripke argued that a priori and necessary are orthogonal dimensions that can come apart in both directions:

**Necessary a posteriori**: "Water is H₂O" is (if true) necessarily true — water is necessarily H₂O in all possible worlds. But we know this a posteriori, through chemistry, not through reflection on the concept of water. The necessity is metaphysical; the justification is empirical.

**Contingent a priori**: "The standard meter stick in Paris is one meter long" (at the moment of its serving as the standard) is knowable a priori — if you stipulate that "one meter" means the length of that stick, you know without measurement that it is one meter long. But it is contingently true — the stick might have been shorter.

These cases disrupted the traditional equation of the a priori with the necessary and the a posteriori with the contingent. Epistemologists and metaphysicians now have to keep the two dimensions carefully separate.

## Kant's Synthetic A Priori

Before Kripke, Kant made another crucial distinction. He divided propositions into analytic (where the predicate is "contained in" the subject — "all bachelors are unmarried") and synthetic (where the predicate adds something to the subject — "the ball is red"). He then proposed a puzzle: are there *synthetic* a priori propositions — judgments that extend our knowledge (are not merely analytic) but are known a priori?

Kant thought yes. Mathematical propositions like "7 + 5 = 12" are synthetic (they are not merely definitional) but a priori (not empirical). The same applies to the fundamental principles of science like "every event has a cause." These synthetic a priori truths are the most philosophically significant: they show that pure reason can give us genuine, substantive knowledge of the world.

The logical positivists rejected this: they argued that all a priori truths are analytic (true by definition or logical truth), and that apparent synthetic a priori propositions are really analytic. This "linguistic turn" made a priori knowledge less mysterious — it is simply knowledge of how language works — but it has been widely criticized.

## Rationalism and Empiricism about the A Priori

The historical debate between rationalism and empiricism is largely a debate about how much a priori knowledge there is and what its source is. Rationalists (Descartes, Leibniz, Kant) believed that a priori knowledge is extensive, substantive, and irreducible to empirical knowledge. Empiricists (Locke, Hume, the Vienna Circle) tried to limit a priori knowledge to the trivial (analytic truths) or eliminate it altogether (Quine).

Contemporary epistemology does not map neatly onto this historical debate. Most philosophers accept that there is some a priori knowledge (logic, mathematics, perhaps some conceptual truths) while disagreeing about its extent, its nature, and its relationship to necessity.
