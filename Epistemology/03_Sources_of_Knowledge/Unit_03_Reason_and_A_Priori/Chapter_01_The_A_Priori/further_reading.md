## Primary Sources

- Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, trans. Norman Kemp Smith, Macmillan, 1929 (orig. 1781/1787). The locus classicus for the a priori / a posteriori distinction and the introduction of the synthetic a priori as a fundamental epistemological category.
- W. V. O. Quine, "Two Dogmas of Empiricism," *Philosophical Review* 60, 1951. The landmark attack on the analytic/synthetic distinction and, by extension, on the standard empiricist rationale for a priori knowledge.
- W. V. O. Quine, *Word and Object*, MIT Press, 1960. Develops holistic empiricism, arguing that no statement is immune from revision in light of experience, directly challenging the possibility of genuinely a priori knowledge.
- Saul Kripke, *Naming and Necessity*, Harvard University Press, 1980 (lectures 1970). Disentangles the a priori/a posteriori distinction from the necessary/contingent distinction and the analytic/synthetic distinction, arguing that necessity and a priority come apart in important cases.
- Philip Kitcher, *The Nature of Mathematical Knowledge*, Oxford University Press, 1983. Defends an empiricist account of mathematical knowledge as not genuinely a priori, arguing that all warrant ultimately traces to experience.
- Laurence BonJour, *In Defense of Pure Reason*, Cambridge University Press, 1998. Systematic defense of a rationalist conception of a priori justification as arising from rational insight into necessary connections.
- Albert Casullo, *A Priori Justification*, Oxford University Press, 2003. Careful analytical treatment of the concept of a priori justification, its varieties, and the challenges facing both rationalist and empiricist accounts.
- Tyler Burge, "Content Preservation," *Philosophical Review* 102, 1993. Develops a notion of a priori entitlement relevant to understanding the epistemic status of logical and mathematical knowledge.
- Gottlob Frege, *Foundations of Arithmetic*, trans. J. L. Austin, Blackwell, 1950 (orig. 1884). The attempt to reduce arithmetic to logic (logicism), with the consequence that mathematical knowledge would be analytic a priori if the reduction succeeds.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Albert Casullo and Joshua Thurow (eds.), *The A Priori in Philosophy*, Oxford University Press, 2013. Contemporary collection addressing the nature, scope, and epistemological significance of a priori knowledge.
- Paul Boghossian and Christopher Peacocke (eds.), *New Essays on the A Priori*, Oxford University Press, 2000. Essential anthology with papers by leading epistemologists and philosophers of logic on the a priori.
- Penelope Maddy, *Realism in Mathematics*, Oxford University Press, 1990. Argues for a naturalistic account of mathematical knowledge that is compatible with a modified empiricism.
- Hartry Field, *Science Without Numbers*, Blackwell, 1980. Defends nominalism about mathematics, arguing that mathematical knowledge need not be a priori if mathematics is not genuinely true.
- George Bealer, "A Priori Knowledge and the Scope of Philosophy," *Philosophical Studies* 81, 1996. Argues that philosophy requires a priori knowledge and defends modal intuition as a source of a priori justification.
- Thomas Nagel, *The Last Word*, Oxford University Press, 1997. Defends reason and a priori knowledge against relativist and naturalist challenges, arguing that logical and rational norms are objective.
- Paul Boghossian, *Fear of Knowledge*, Oxford University Press, 2006. Defends objective knowledge against relativism and constructivism, with implications for the status of a priori knowledge.
- Jaakko Hintikka, *Knowledge and Belief*, Cornell University Press, 1962. Formal analysis of epistemic logic relevant to understanding the structure of a priori knowledge claims.
