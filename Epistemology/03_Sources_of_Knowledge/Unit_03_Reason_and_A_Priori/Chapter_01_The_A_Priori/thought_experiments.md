## The Standard Meter and Contingent A Priori Knowledge

**The scenario:** By convention, the standard meter was originally defined as the length of a particular platinum-iridium bar in Paris (the "standard meter"). The rule fixes the reference: whatever length that bar has at a certain time just is one meter. The person who makes this stipulation seems to know a priori — without measuring — that the bar is exactly one meter long, since the very stipulation makes this true.

**The philosophical point:** Kripke uses this case in *Naming and Necessity* to argue for the existence of contingent a priori knowledge. The proposition "The standard meter bar is one meter long" is known a priori (without needing to measure), but it is not necessarily true — the bar could have been a different length. This directly challenges Kant's assumption that all a priori knowledge concerns necessary truths, and Quine's assumption that a priority and analyticity go together. The case opens space for a priori knowledge that is neither analytic nor necessary, transforming the epistemological landscape.

## Arithmetic and the Proof of 7 + 5 = 12

**The scenario:** A child — or a philosopher — is confronted with the claim that 7 + 5 = 12. Kant famously argued that this is synthetic rather than analytic: the concept of 12 is not contained in the concepts of 7, 5, and addition; we need to go beyond the component concepts to arrive at the sum. Yet we seem to know this truth with complete certainty and without any empirical investigation.

**The philosophical point:** Kant's claim that arithmetical identities are synthetic a priori is contested. Frege argued that arithmetic is analytic because it can be derived purely logically from definitions; if he is right, our knowledge of arithmetic does not extend our knowledge in the way Kant supposed. Quine, by contrast, argued that even logical and mathematical truths face the tribunal of experience — they are just very firmly held beliefs that we would be extremely reluctant to revise. The example crystallizes the core dispute about the extent and nature of a priori knowledge: is it merely our grip on our own concepts, a special rational faculty, or just the hardest part of our empirical web of beliefs?

## Quine's Web of Belief

**The scenario:** Imagine a scientist who, when her carefully designed experiment produces anomalous results, considers three options: (a) revise her theory, (b) revise her assumption that the instruments were functioning, or (c) revise her acceptance of classical logic by abandoning the law of the excluded middle. Quine argues that any of these options is in principle available — no statement in the scientist's "web of belief" is absolutely immune from revision.

**The philosophical point:** Quine's holism implies that even logical and mathematical principles could be abandoned in the face of sufficiently global pressure from experience — they are just maximally central nodes in the web, not a priori truths in any philosophically robust sense. This challenges the idea that there is a principled distinction between a priori and a posteriori knowledge: there are only more or less easily revisable beliefs. Critics respond that the pragmatic centrality of logic is not the same as its being merely contingently held, and that logical revision is conceptually incoherent in a way that ordinary empirical revision is not.

## Necessary A Posteriori: Water Is H₂O

**The scenario:** We discover, through chemical investigation, that water is H₂O. This discovery required empirical research — we could not have known it a priori, just by thinking about what we mean by "water." Yet it seems that water could not possibly fail to be H₂O: in every possible world where water exists, it has this molecular composition.

**The philosophical point:** Kripke argues that this case establishes necessary a posteriori knowledge: the truth is both necessary and discoverable only through experience. This challenges the traditional picture in which necessary truths are exactly the a priori ones. For the epistemology of a priori knowledge, the upshot is that modal status (necessity) and epistemological status (a priority) are independent dimensions. Empirical investigation can reveal what is necessarily true, and not all necessary truths are within reach of pure reason. This reshapes the debate by showing that rationalist claims about the scope of a priori knowledge cannot simply appeal to the concept of necessity as their guide.

## The Parallel Postulate and Non-Euclidean Geometry

**The scenario:** For centuries, Euclid's parallel postulate — that through a point not on a given line, exactly one parallel line can be drawn — was treated as a self-evident a priori truth. In the nineteenth century, mathematicians showed that consistent geometries exist in which this postulate is false. Einstein's general relativity then suggested that the geometry of actual spacetime is non-Euclidean.

**The philosophical point:** The history of geometry is standardly cited as the decisive empirical refutation of Kant's claim that Euclidean geometry is synthetic a priori. If what was taken as a self-evident a priori truth turned out to be false of actual space, then the deliverances of rational intuition are not guaranteed to be true. Rationalists respond that the parallel postulate was never truly self-evident — that what is a priori is purely formal mathematics, not applied geometry — and that Kant's error was to conflate intuition about conceptual structure with intuition about physical space. The case remains one of the most important historical tests for theories of a priori knowledge.
