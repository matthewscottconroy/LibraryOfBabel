## Primary Sources

- René Descartes, *Meditations on First Philosophy*, trans. John Cottingham, Cambridge University Press, 1996 (orig. 1641). The foundational text for modern rationalism, arguing that clear and distinct perception by the intellect provides the model for genuine knowledge, with innate ideas as the ultimate source of a priori content.
- René Descartes, *Rules for the Direction of the Mind*, in *The Philosophical Writings of Descartes*, vol. 1, trans. Cottingham, Stoothoff, Murdoch, Cambridge University Press, 1985. Articulates the method of intuition and deduction as the only reliable paths to knowledge.
- G. W. Leibniz, *New Essays on Human Understanding*, trans. P. Remnant and J. Bennett, Cambridge University Press, 1996 (orig. written c. 1704, pub. 1765). The sustained rationalist response to Locke, arguing that necessary truths are grounded in innate intellectual principles rather than derived from experience.
- Baruch Spinoza, *Ethics*, trans. E. M. Curley, Princeton University Press, 1994 (orig. 1677). Demonstrates, in geometrical form, a system of metaphysics and ethics derived from definitions and axioms known a priori, the most ambitious rationalist systematic project.
- Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, trans. Norman Kemp Smith, Macmillan, 1929 (orig. 1781/1787). Kant's synthesis of rationalist and empiricist elements, with the forms of intuition and the categories of the understanding as the a priori structures of knowledge.
- Laurence BonJour, *In Defense of Pure Reason*, Cambridge University Press, 1998. The most sustained contemporary rationalist defense, arguing for rational insight as an irreducible source of a priori justification.
- George Bealer, "Intuition and the Autonomy of Philosophy," in Michael DePaul and William Ramsey (eds.), *Rethinking Intuition*, Rowman & Littlefield, 1998. Defends a rationalist epistemology based on modal intuitions.
- Christopher Peacocke, *A Study of Concepts*, MIT Press, 1992. Develops a theory of concept possession that grounds a priori knowledge in the constitutive conditions for having a concept.
- Christopher Peacocke, *Being Known*, Oxford University Press, 1999. Full epistemological theory grounding a priori knowledge in a rationalist account of understanding and concept possession.

## Key Secondary Sources

- John Cottingham, *Rationalism*, Paladin, 1984. Clear and accessible introduction to the rationalist tradition from Descartes through Leibniz and Spinoza.
- Bruce Aune, *Rationalism, Empiricism, and Pragmatism*, Random House, 1970. Survey of the main epistemological traditions with careful attention to the defining characteristics of rationalism.
- Georges Dicker, *Descartes: An Analytical and Historical Introduction*, Oxford University Press, 1993. Systematic analysis of Descartes's epistemology with particular attention to the innate ideas doctrine and the method of doubt.
- Nicholas Jolley, *Leibniz and Locke: A Study of the New Essays on Human Understanding*, Oxford University Press, 1984. Detailed comparative study of the rationalist/empiricist dispute as it played out between Leibniz and Locke.
- Ralph Walker, *Kant*, Routledge, 1978. Accessible introduction to Kant's epistemology, including the synthetic a priori and the transcendental deduction.
- Peter Strawson, *The Bounds of Sense*, Methuen, 1966. Influential analytical reading of Kant's first Critique, separating the productive from the "transcendental psychology" elements.
- Jaegwon Kim and Ernest Sosa (eds.), *A Companion to Metaphysics*, Blackwell, 1995. Contains entries on rationalism, innate ideas, and related topics.
- Paul Boghossian and Christopher Peacocke (eds.), *New Essays on the A Priori*, Oxford University Press, 2000. Essential anthology on contemporary rationalist and empiricist approaches.
- Michael Devitt, *Coming to Our Senses: A Naturalistic Program for Semantic Localism*, Cambridge University Press, 1996. Naturalistic empiricist critique of rationalist appeals to conceptual truth and innate content.
