## René Descartes (1596–1650)

Descartes is the father of modern rationalism and its most influential representative. His *Meditations* develop the method of doubt as a procedure for identifying those beliefs that can withstand skeptical scrutiny, and conclude that clear and distinct intellectual perception — most fully exemplified in our grasp of simple natures such as thought, extension, and mathematical relations — is the model for genuine knowledge. Descartes argued that the mind is furnished with innate ideas — including the ideas of God, the self, and geometrical objects — that are not derived from experience but are implanted by God and provide the content for a priori reasoning. His legacy includes both the rationalist tradition and the epistemological problematic of the external world.

## G. W. Leibniz (1646–1716)

Leibniz's New Essays on Human Understanding (written as a response to Locke) articulate the rationalist conception of innate ideas with greater sophistication than Descartes, arguing that the mind contains innate "tendencies" or "dispositions" toward certain truths that experience may occasion but not produce. Leibniz drew the crucial distinction between truths of reason — necessary truths knowable a priori and true in all possible worlds — and truths of fact — contingent truths knowable only a posteriori. His principle of sufficient reason and his logic of possible worlds became major resources for subsequent rationalist philosophy, and his claim that arithmetic and geometry are analytic helped set the agenda for Frege's logicism.

## Immanuel Kant (1724–1804)

Kant's "Copernican revolution" attempted to reconcile rationalist and empiricist insights by arguing that knowledge requires both sensibility (the passive reception of intuitions) and understanding (the active application of concepts). The forms of intuition — space and time — and the categories of the understanding — substance, causality, and the rest — are contributed a priori by the mind and impose structure on all possible experience. Kant's transcendental idealism was designed to explain how synthetic a priori knowledge is possible while avoiding both Humean skepticism and Leibnizian dogmatic metaphysics. The first Critique remains the indispensable reference point for any discussion of rationalism's scope and limits.

## Laurence BonJour (born 1943)

BonJour's *In Defense of Pure Reason* (1998) is the most systematic contemporary defense of rationalism as an epistemological position. BonJour argues that rational insight — the direct intellectual apprehension of necessary connections between concepts or properties — is a genuine source of justification that is neither infallible nor unrevisable but is irreducible to any form of empirical evidence. Against Quine, he argues that logical holism cannot account for the modal character of a priori truths; against Kitcher, he argues that no account of mathematical knowledge that traces warrant purely to experience can explain why mathematical beliefs are about necessity. BonJour's moderate rationalism has defined the contemporary debate.

## George Bealer (born 1944)

Bealer is a leading contemporary rationalist who defends the view that philosophical knowledge, particularly modal knowledge, derives from rational intuitions that have a distinctive epistemic status. In "Modal Epistemology and the Rationalist Renaissance" and related work, Bealer argues that intuitions are a form of intellectual seeming that are evidence for the truth of what is intuited — not merely psychological reactions but genuine epistemic inputs. He defends a "basic sources" epistemology on which rational intuition and perception are the two fundamental and independent sources of evidence, challenging the naturalist view that all evidence is ultimately empirical.

## Christopher Peacocke (born 1950)

Peacocke has developed the most technically sophisticated contemporary version of rationalism through his theory of concept possession and its relation to a priori knowledge. In *A Study of Concepts* (1992) and *Being Known* (1999), he argues that a priori knowledge can be explained by the constitutive conditions for possessing concepts: to possess a concept is to be disposed to make certain transitions in thought that are thereby underwritten as a priori. This "possession condition" approach connects a priori epistemology with philosophy of language and mind, grounding rational insight in the structure of thought itself rather than in a free-standing faculty of pure reason.
