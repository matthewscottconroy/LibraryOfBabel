## Descartes's Piece of Wax

**The scenario:** Descartes takes a piece of wax fresh from the beehive. It has a distinctive smell, a particular shape, color, and hardness — all clearly perceptible properties. When the wax is brought near a fire, every one of these properties changes: the smell dissipates, the shape melts, the hardness gives way. Yet we continue to judge that it is the same wax throughout the transformation. What, then, do we perceive when we perceive the wax?

**The philosophical point:** Descartes uses the wax example to argue that our knowledge of physical objects is not delivered by the senses but by the intellect alone. The senses report only the changing properties; it is the intellect that grasps the wax as a persisting material thing capable of extension in indefinitely many shapes. This is the rationalist thesis: genuinely knowing what something is requires intellectual judgment, not mere sensory experience. The example prefigures Kant's doctrine of the categories by showing that the concept of substance is not extracted from experience but is brought to it by the judging mind.

## Leibniz's Response to the Blank Tablet

**The scenario:** Locke had argued that the mind at birth is a blank tablet — a *tabula rasa* — on which experience writes all its knowledge. Leibniz responded with a counter-image: the mind is not a blank tablet but a block of marble with veins running through it that predispose the sculptor (experience) to certain forms rather than others. Experience does not create knowledge from scratch but activates latent dispositions toward necessary truths.

**The philosophical point:** Leibniz's image captures the moderate rationalist position that innate ideas are not fully formed concepts present in the mind from birth (a view he explicitly disavows) but rather innate capacities or dispositions to recognize certain truths when experience prompts reflection. This blocks the empiricist objection that animals and infants show no awareness of the allegedly innate principles of logic and mathematics: they lack the capacity for reflection, not the underlying dispositions. The thought experiment also highlights the methodological difficulty of settling the nativism debate — behavior may underdetermine whether a cognitive capacity is innate or learned.

## The Blind Mathematician and Mathematical Intuition

**The scenario:** A mathematician who has been blind from birth works in geometry. She develops a highly sophisticated understanding of spatial relationships — she can prove theorems about Euclidean space, visualize (in some sense) three-dimensional figures, and work with non-Euclidean geometries — all without any visual experience of spatial arrangements.

**The philosophical point:** This case is relevant to rationalist claims about mathematical knowledge. If a blind mathematician can achieve genuine knowledge of geometrical truths, this supports the rationalist's contention that such knowledge derives from reason rather than from sensory experience. It challenges empiricist accounts on which mathematical knowledge is grounded in perceptual experience with spatial arrangements. However, critics may argue that the blind mathematician's knowledge still depends on experience indirectly — through tactile and motor experience that provides spatial content — and that this is enough to satisfy a sophisticated empiricist account of mathematical cognition.

## Kant's Transcendental Deduction and the Unity of Experience

**The scenario:** Imagine an experience so chaotic and unstructured that no pattern, object, or event is recognizable — a succession of unconnected sense-impressions with no continuity, no spatial arrangement, and no causal structure. Kant asks whether such a succession could count as experience at all, and whether it could ground any knowledge claims.

**The philosophical point:** Kant's answer is no: what makes experience possible at all is the application of the a priori categories of the understanding — substance, causality, unity — to the manifold of intuition. This transcendental argument concludes that the categories must be applied necessarily to experience, not because the world must conform to them but because our experience must exhibit this structure if it is to be experience rather than undifferentiated chaos. The argument is rationalism's most ambitious: it derives a priori principles not merely from the analysis of concepts but from the conditions of the possibility of experience itself.

## BonJour's Colour Incompatibility

**The scenario:** Consider the claim: nothing can be simultaneously red all over and green all over. This seems to be known with certainty, without any need to empirically test individual objects. We cannot even coherently imagine what it would be like to perceive an object that satisfies both conditions. The certainty seems to arise from mere reflection on the concepts of red, green, and simultaneity.

**The philosophical point:** BonJour uses this and similar cases in *In Defense of Pure Reason* to argue that rational insight — the direct intellectual apprehension of necessary incompatibility — is a genuine and irreducible epistemic source. The empiricist cannot explain our certainty here by appeal to the frequency with which we have observed red-and-green objects (we have never observed any), nor by the analytic containment of one concept in another. Something, BonJour argues, is directly "seen" by the intellect. This constitutes a prima facie case for the existence of genuine a priori knowledge that is both non-trivial and not reducible to logical tautology.
