# George Bealer on Intuitions and A Priori Knowledge

George Bealer is one of the most forceful contemporary defenders of the epistemic status of philosophical intuitions. In a series of papers from the 1990s and 2000s, he argued that intuitions have a distinctive evidential status in philosophy that cannot be explained away by appealing to mere trained judgment, theoretical bias, or pragmatic utility. His work provides an important starting point for understanding what intuitions are supposed to do in philosophical methodology.

## The Autonomy of Philosophy

Bealer begins from an observation about philosophical methodology. Philosophy proceeds by constructing arguments, formulating theories, and testing them against cases. The tests are evaluations of how well a theory handles our intuitions about cases. But if philosophical intuitions are not evidence — if they are merely expressions of bias or convention — then philosophy has no subject matter distinct from empirical science, and no method that yields knowledge.

Bealer wants to defend what he calls the *autonomy* of philosophy: philosophy is a discipline with its own methods and its own domain, and those methods produce knowledge. The backbone of his defense is the claim that intuitions have genuine evidential value.

## Modal Reliabilism

Bealer's positive account is called *modal reliabilism*. The claim: there is a reliable connection between the intuitions generated by the properly functioning faculty of reason and modal truths — truths about what is necessarily, possibly, and actually the case.

When a rational being has a genuine intellectual intuition — not a sensory seeming, not an emotional response, but a genuine intellectual seeming — this intuition is reliably connected to modal facts. It is not infallible, but it tracks modal truth well enough to provide prima facie justification.

Bealer distinguishes between *intuitions* proper (genuine intellectual seemings) and mere *appearances*, *hunches*, or *biases*. Only genuine intuitions have evidential value. The challenge — which Bealer acknowledges — is distinguishing genuine intuitions from mere appearances in practice.

The modal aspect is important: intuitions are evidentially valuable because they track what is necessarily and possibly the case. This explains why they are particularly useful in philosophy, which deals with necessary truths and conceptual analysis, rather than contingent empirical facts.

## Dealing with Intuition Conflict

Different people sometimes have different intuitions about the same case. This is the evidential base of the experimental philosophy movement's challenge to philosophical methodology. Bealer's response:

Not all apparent intuitions are genuine intuitions. Some are the result of philosophical error, cognitive bias, cultural conditioning, or misunderstanding of the case. When intuitions conflict, the epistemically responsible task is to identify which intuitions are genuine — by examining them for stability, independence, universality, and coherence with other intuitions.

Philosophical expertise matters: trained philosophers, who have systematically explored the conceptual terrain, are better positioned to distinguish genuine intuitions from pseudo-intuitions. This is analogous to how trained scientists are better positioned to interpret experimental data.

Critics object that this response is self-serving: philosophers are using their own intuitions to certify which intuitions count as evidence, which is circular. Bealer responds that there is no alternative to using one's cognitive faculties to certify their own deliverances — the same circularity applies to perception and memory.

## The Role of Intuitions in Philosophical Argument

On Bealer's account, a philosophical argument has the following structure:
1. Premise P1 (supported by intuition or derivation)
2. Premise P2 (supported by intuition or derivation)
3. Conclusion C (derived from P1 and P2)

But if C conflicts with a strong intuition I, then the argument can be *modus tollensed*: the intuition against C provides grounds to reject P1 or P2. Whether to accept C or reject a premise depends on the relative strength of the intuitions supporting the premises and the intuition against the conclusion.

This shows how intuitions can constrain deductive arguments: they function as evidence that can be weighed against the support for premises. This is not irrationalism — it is the recognition that deductive validity alone doesn't settle philosophical disputes, because the premises are themselves subject to evidential evaluation.

## Limitations and the Experimental Challenge

Bealer's modal reliabilism faces the challenge of explaining the psychological mechanisms that underlie genuine intuitions. If intuitions track modal truth, there must be some explanation of how the human mind comes to be reliably connected to necessary truths. The philosophical account demands a psychological/neuroscientific complement.

The experimental philosophy movement provides data suggesting that intuitions are less uniform and less stable than Bealer assumes. If this is correct, the reliable connection between intuition and modal truth must be more fragile than he acknowledges — or the class of genuine intuitions (as distinguished from mere appearances) must be smaller and harder to identify than his framework implies.
