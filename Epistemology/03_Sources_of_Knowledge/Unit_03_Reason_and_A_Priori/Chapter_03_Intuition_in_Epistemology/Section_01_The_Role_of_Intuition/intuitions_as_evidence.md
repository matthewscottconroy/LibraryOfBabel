# Intuitions as Evidence in Philosophy

Philosophical argument has always relied heavily on intuitions — on what seems obviously true or obviously false when we consider a case or a principle. When Gettier imagined Smith and Jones applying for a job, he relied on the intuition that Smith doesn't know who will get the job, even though his belief is justified and true. When Thomson imagined the violinist plugged into your body for nine months, she relied on intuitions about bodily autonomy and the limits of obligation. Intuitions are doing epistemological work throughout philosophy — but what kind of work, and is it legitimate?

## What Are Philosophical Intuitions?

Philosophical intuitions are spontaneous, non-inferential intellectual seemings — states in which a proposition seems true (or false) without explicit reasoning to that conclusion. When you consider a thought experiment and immediately judge "that's clearly wrong" or "that's clearly knowledge" — before reasoning about why — you are exercising intuition.

Intuitions are distinct from mere gut feelings or prejudices. They are cognitive responses of a particular kind: they present themselves as tracking truth, they are accessible to rational scrutiny, and they can be refined by careful reflection. But they are also distinct from considered judgments: they occur prior to deliberate reasoning, and their authority cannot be reduced to the reasoning they precede.

In philosophical practice, intuitions function as data: they are what a successful theory must explain and accommodate. A theory that generates massively counterintuitive implications faces a strong objection — the intuitions tell against it even if the argument for the theory seems valid.

## The Evidential Value of Intuitions

The standard philosophical view, defended explicitly by George Bealer and others, is that intuitions have evidential value. They provide prima facie justification for the propositions that are intuited. This is not infallible: intuitions can be overridden by sufficiently strong reasoning or by other intuitions. But absent such override, an intuition provides genuine reason to accept what is intuited.

The argument for this view: if intuitions had no evidential value, philosophical methodology would be bankrupt. Almost everything philosophers argue about depends on intuitions at some level — about cases, about principles, about what is possible. If intuitions provide no evidence, then the entire tradition of philosophical analysis collapses into arbitrary assertion.

Moreover, the attempt to *refute* intuitions typically appeals to other intuitions. If you want to show that a thought experiment is misconceived, you argue that relevant intuitions point in a different direction. Even anti-intuition arguments rely on intuitions about the limits and unreliability of intuitions.

## Intuitions and Thought Experiments

Philosophical thought experiments are tools for generating and testing intuitions. They work by constructing cases that isolate the relevant features — removing confounding factors, exaggerating or simplifying to make the essential structure clear. The trolley problem, the brain-in-a-vat, the Chinese room, the Gettier cases — all work by presenting a situation and asking for a judgment.

The power of a well-constructed thought experiment lies in its ability to induce a clear and stable intuition that constrains theory. If a theory implies that you know you are not a brain in a vat, and this seems clearly false, the intuition provides strong evidence against the theory (or against some combination of it with other claims).

The challenge is that thought experiments can also mislead: the features highlighted by the construction may not be the relevant ones, and the intuition may respond to incidental rather than essential features of the case.

## The Selection Problem: Which Intuitions Count?

Not all intuitions are evidentially equal. Some are more stable across reflection, more widely shared, more immune to explanatory debunking. The philosophical task is to identify which intuitions carry evidential weight.

Criteria that are often invoked:
- **Stability under reflection**: Does the intuition survive careful consideration of counterarguments and competing considerations?
- **Independence from bias**: Is there reason to think the intuition might be a product of irrelevant factors (emotional response, in-group identification, etc.)?
- **Cross-cultural robustness**: Is the intuition widely shared across different cultural and philosophical traditions?
- **Modal robustness**: Does the intuition persist across variations of the case, or is it sensitive to small changes that should be irrelevant?

These criteria are not algorithmic; applying them requires judgment. And they are themselves contested: experimental philosophers argue that many "stable" intuitions are actually unstable across populations, and that philosophical practice has been biased by using the intuitions of an unrepresentative group (professional philosophers from specific traditions).

## Reflective Equilibrium

John Rawls's method of *reflective equilibrium* provides a systematic account of how intuitions and principles are brought into balance in philosophical theorizing. You begin with considered judgments (carefully filtered intuitions about cases), formulate general principles that explain and systematize them, then revise both principles and judgments as needed to achieve coherence.

This method treats intuitions as genuine evidence that constrains theory, while also acknowledging that intuitions can be revised when they conflict with well-supported principles. Neither the principles nor the particular judgments are sacrosanct; both are provisional.

Reflective equilibrium represents a kind of philosophical empiricism about intuitions: treating them like observational data — real evidence, but fallible and in need of systematic integration.
