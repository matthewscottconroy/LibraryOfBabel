# Cultural Variation, Intuitions, and the Philosophy of Conceptual Analysis

One of the most philosophically significant findings of experimental philosophy is that intuitions about philosophically central cases vary systematically across cultural groups. This raises deep questions about whether conceptual analysis reveals universal truths about concepts like knowledge, causation, and moral responsibility, or whether it reflects the particular conceptual practices and associated intuitions of a specific cultural tradition.

## The Cross-Cultural Data

The Weinberg, Nichols, and Stich (2001) studies found cross-cultural variation in Gettier-like intuitions. Subsequent studies have found variation in:

- **Knowledge attributions**: Whether true belief with justified but false reasoning constitutes "real" knowledge varies across Western, East Asian, and South Asian subjects.
- **Moral responsibility**: Attributions of moral responsibility in deterministic worlds show cultural variation.
- **Causation**: Judgments about which event "caused" an outcome vary by culture and context.
- **Reference**: Some studies suggest that attributions of reference vary in ways relevant to debates about descriptivism vs. causal theories of reference.

If these findings hold up — and they are contested — they suggest that what philosophers have taken to be universal conceptual intuitions are actually culturally parochial responses.

## The Philosophical Significance

The significance of cultural variation in intuitions depends on what intuitions are supposed to reveal. If they reveal the concept's extension in a particular community, then variation shows that different communities have somewhat different concepts — which is not philosophically catastrophic, just restricts the scope of each analysis. If they are supposed to reveal necessary and universal truths about the conceptual structure, variation is more damaging.

The most common philosophical response is to argue that variation shows some intuitions are epistemically unreliable — perhaps influenced by cultural factors, schemas, or background assumptions — and that the philosophical task is to identify the reliable core. But this requires independent criteria for reliability, and the worry is that those criteria will simply privilege the intuitions of professional (mostly Western) philosophers.

## Contextualism about Concepts

One philosophical response to cross-cultural variation is to embrace a form of contextualism about concepts: concepts like "knowledge" and "cause" may function differently in different contexts and communities, and there is no context-independent fact about the "correct" concept. On this view, philosophical analysis is not discovering universal truths but articulating the conceptual practices of a particular tradition.

This sits uneasily with the realist ambitions of many philosophical projects. If "knowledge" is not a universal concept, then epistemology's claims about the nature of knowledge are relative to a particular conceptual practice. This motivates looking for invariants across cultures or arguing that cross-cultural variation is a surface phenomenon that obscures deeper shared concepts.

## Experimental Philosophy's Positive Program

The positive program in experimental philosophy takes cross-cultural variation not as a refutation of conceptual analysis but as data about how concepts actually function. Rather than assuming a fixed concept and analyzing it, philosophers should investigate how people actually deploy epistemic and moral concepts, what factors influence their judgments, and how the psychological mechanisms underlying concept deployment work.

This connects philosophy to cognitive science and produces a richer, empirically-informed picture of concepts. Jonathan Weinberg has argued that even if this means giving up on some traditional epistemological projects (like finding necessary and sufficient conditions for knowledge), it opens new ones: understanding how epistemic norms emerge and function in human communities, how they vary and why, and how they can be improved.

## The Linguistic Relativity Connection

The cultural variation findings connect to broader debates about linguistic relativity — the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis that different languages and cultures structure reality differently. Strong versions of linguistic relativity have been largely rejected in linguistics (languages differ in vocabulary and structure but humans can think thoughts their language doesn't lexicalize). But moderate versions are well-supported: language and cultural practice influence habitual patterns of attention and categorization.

If cultural differences in epistemic language and practice influence conceptual intuitions, this doesn't mean that truth itself varies across cultures. It means that different communities organize their epistemic practices differently, and that philosophical analysis must be sensitive to this variation rather than assuming that any one tradition's intuitions provide the universal standard.
