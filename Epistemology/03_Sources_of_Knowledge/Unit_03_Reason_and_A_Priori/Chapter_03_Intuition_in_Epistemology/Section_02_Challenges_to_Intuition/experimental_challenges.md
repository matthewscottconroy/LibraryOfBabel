# Experimental Philosophy and the Challenge to Intuition

The experimental philosophy (x-phi) movement, which emerged in the early 2000s with work by Jonathan Weinberg, Shaun Nichols, Stephen Stich, and Joshua Knobe, challenges the epistemological role of intuitions in philosophy by bringing systematic empirical data to bear. If philosophical intuitions are supposed to serve as evidence, they need to be stable, consistent, and independent of epistemically irrelevant factors. Experimental philosophers argue that the evidence shows intuitions fail these criteria.

## The Variability Studies

The first wave of experimental philosophy focused on showing that philosophical intuitions vary across populations in ways that they should not if they reflect genuine modal or conceptual truths. Weinberg, Nichols, and Stich's influential 2001 study found significant cross-cultural variation in responses to Gettier-like cases.

In their study, East Asian, South Asian, and Western subjects were presented with cases similar to Gettier's originals and asked whether the protagonist "really knows" or "only believes" the relevant proposition. Western subjects predominantly said "only believes" (matching the standard philosophical verdict). East Asian and South Asian subjects showed higher rates of saying the protagonist "really knows."

If knowledge attributions vary systematically by cultural background, and if these attributions are supposed to track the concept of knowledge, then either the concept of knowledge differs across cultures (which poses problems for universal philosophical analysis) or the intuitions are tracking something other than the concept (like cultural norms, implicit theories, or associative responses).

## The Framing Effects Problem

Further studies showed that intuitions about the same case can be changed by epistemically irrelevant features:

- **Order effects**: Which case you consider first affects your response to later cases.
- **Framing effects**: Whether a scenario is described positively or negatively affects the attribution of knowledge and free will.
- **Stakes**: The emotional stakes of the scenario affect probability and knowledge attributions.
- **Affect**: Cases involving emotionally charged scenarios produce different intuitive responses than structurally identical cases involving neutral content.

If philosophical intuitions are genuine insights into conceptual or modal truth, they should not be affected by these irrelevant factors. The fact that they are suggests that what we call "intuitions" are actually responses generated by cognitive systems that are sensitive to many features beyond philosophical relevance.

## Nichols and Knobe on Free Will

Joshua Knobe and Shaun Nichols demonstrated that folk attributions of moral responsibility (which depend on intuitions about free will and determinism) vary significantly depending on whether cases are described abstractly or concretely. Abstract descriptions of determinism lead people to say free will is compatible with determinism; concrete cases of someone doing something terrible lead them to say it is not. This suggests that moral intuitions about free will are driven at least partly by affective responses rather than conceptual analysis.

## Philosophical Responses

The mainstream philosophical response has been nuanced rather than simply dismissive:

**The expertise defense**: Professional philosophers, with extensive training in conceptual analysis and reflective scrutiny of intuitions, have more reliable intuitions than naive subjects. The experimental data from undergraduate or lay populations does not threaten the evidential status of experts' intuitions.

Critics of this response: if expertise matters, why do expert philosophers also disagree substantially? And why should philosophical training produce more reliable modal intuitions rather than simply better-socialized philosophical instincts?

**The negative program vs. positive program distinction**: Experimental philosophers distinguish between a negative program (debunking the use of intuitions) and a positive program (using experimental data to enrich philosophical theorizing). The positive program is less controversial: data about how people deploy concepts can inform philosophical analysis of those concepts.

**The calibration response**: Rather than abandoning intuitions, we should calibrate our reliance on them based on empirical knowledge of when they are reliable. Intuitions about structurally simple cases in stable, familiar domains may be reliable; intuitions about exotic thought experiments or cross-cultural cases may not be.

## The Status of the Debate

The experimental philosophy movement has had a significant, if contested, impact on philosophical methodology. It has:
- Forced philosophers to be more explicit about which intuitions they are relying on and why
- Raised awareness of potential sources of bias in philosophical intuition
- Generated fruitful empirical work on how people actually use epistemic and moral concepts

But it has not established that philosophical intuitions in general have no evidential value. The debate about the appropriate role of intuitions in philosophy continues, with increasing methodological sophistication on both sides.
