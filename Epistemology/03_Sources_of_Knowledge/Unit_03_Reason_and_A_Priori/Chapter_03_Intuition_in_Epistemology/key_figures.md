## John Rawls (1921–2002)

Rawls popularized reflective equilibrium as a philosophical method in *A Theory of Justice* (1971), arguing that the proper procedure for constructing a theory of justice is to move back and forth between particular considered judgments (our intuitions about specific cases) and general principles, revising each in light of the other until a coherent fit is achieved. Although Rawls's primary application was moral and political philosophy, the method has been widely adopted in epistemology, where reflective equilibrium between our intuitive verdicts about specific epistemic cases and general epistemological theories is standardly taken to be the appropriate methodology.

## George Bealer (born 1944)

Bealer has been the most persistent defender of the evidential status of philosophical intuitions in the contemporary literature. He distinguishes intuitions — which he characterizes as intellectual seemings with a distinctive phenomenological character — from mere opinions, guesses, or cognitive biases. For Bealer, an intuition that p is evidence that p is true, not merely evidence about the intuiter's psychology. He has argued against naturalist critiques that appeal to cognitive error by maintaining that rational intuitions, properly characterized, are self-correcting in ways that make them reliable evidence about modal and conceptual matters.

## Jonathan Weinberg (born 1970), Shaun Nichols (born 1966), and Stephen Stich (born 1943)

The trio of Weinberg, Nichols, and Stich launched the experimental philosophy movement's challenge to philosophical intuitions with their 2001 paper "Normativity and Epistemic Intuitions." They reported evidence that subjects from different cultural backgrounds have systematically different intuitions about standard epistemological cases — including variants of Gettier cases and fake barn scenarios — concluding that if intuitions vary by cultural background, they cannot be reliable evidence about objective epistemic facts. Their work triggered a major methodological debate about whether the evidential use of intuitions in epistemology is undermined by their cultural variability.

## Timothy Williamson (born 1955)

Williamson's *The Philosophy of Philosophy* (2007) defends philosophical methodology against the experimental philosophy critique, but does so by denying that philosophy relies on a special faculty of intuition. Instead, he argues that philosophers engage in the same kind of counterfactual reasoning that everyone uses in everyday life: thought experiments are simply devices for activating our general ability to evaluate what would be the case in hypothetical scenarios. Williamson thus defends the evidential value of philosophical methodology while rejecting the intellectualist picture of philosophical intuition as a special capacity requiring special justification.

## Herman Cappelen (born 1966)

Cappelen's *Philosophy Without Intuitions* (2012) takes the direct view that the widespread assumption — shared by both defenders and critics of intuition-based methodology — that analytic philosophers rely on intuitions as evidence is simply false. Cappelen argues that careful reading of paradigmatic philosophical texts (Moore, Gettier, Kripke, and others) shows that the claims being made are not presented or defended as intuitions but as straightforward assertions, supported by argument. This surprising conclusion, if correct, dissolves the experimental philosophy challenge by denying its premise.

## Ernest Sosa (born 1940)

Sosa has worked to provide a precise epistemological account of intuitions as intellectual seemings — states with phenomenological character in which something seems to be the case independently of reasoning or inference. In *Reflective Knowledge* (2009) and related papers, he argues that intuitions have prima facie evidential weight for what they represent, analogously to perceptual seemings in the phenomenal conservative tradition, and that this weight is not defeated merely by the discovery that intuitions are sometimes wrong or culturally variable. Sosa's virtue epistemology provides a framework for explaining why intellectual seemings can be reliable in the relevant domains.
