# What Counts as Testimony?

Testimony, in the epistemological sense, is the transmission of information from one person to another through assertion. When you tell me that it's raining outside, and I believe you, I have acquired knowledge (if it is indeed raining) through your testimony. This seems straightforward — but determining what exactly falls within the category of "testimony" has turned out to be philosophically complex, with consequences for how we understand the justification of testimony-based beliefs.

## The Broad and Narrow Views

Philosophers have proposed both broad and narrow conceptions of testimony.

The *narrow view* restricts testimony to sincere, first-person assertions — statements like "it is raining," "the train leaves at noon," "I saw him do it." These are cases where a speaker represents themselves as vouching for a truth, presenting themselves as an epistemic authority on what they are saying. The epistemic relation is explicit: the speaker invites the hearer to form a belief based on their say-so.

The *broad view* extends testimony to include any communication that is designed to produce true belief in another person: written texts, signs, maps, mathematical notation, visual representations like diagrams and charts. On this view, the road sign telling you the speed limit is a form of testimony; the textbook describing the French Revolution is testimony; the scientific paper reporting experimental results is testimony.

## Assertion as the Core Case

Most epistemologists focus on assertion as the paradigm case of testimony. An assertion is a sincere, first-person claim that the world is thus-and-so. When someone sincerely asserts P, they:

1. Represent themselves as believing P
2. Represent themselves as having grounds for P
3. Invite the hearer to form the belief P on the basis of their assertion

This is the speech act structure that makes testimony epistemically significant. The speaker's sincerity and epistemic standing are relevant to whether the hearer is justified in believing on the basis of the testimony.

Non-assertion cases are more complex. A guidebook asserting distances may have been written with care or carelessness; a sign may be official or unofficial, accurate or misleading. The epistemic norms surrounding non-assertion forms of "testimony" are different from those surrounding face-to-face first-person claims.

## Memory as Testimony to Oneself

An interesting borderline case is memory. When you "remember" something, you might be thought of as accessing your past self's assertion or perceptual record. Some philosophers (Mark Rowlands, John Sutton) have proposed that memory is a form of testimony — self-testimony — in which your present self receives information from your past self through the medium of memory traces.

This is philosophically suggestive: it connects the epistemology of memory to the epistemology of testimony, suggesting they might share structural features. But it is also contested: memory seems different from testimony because there is no distinct speaker and hearer, and the reliability of memory is not obviously analogous to the trustworthiness of a witness.

## Written and Technological Testimony

The internet, print media, and digital communication have vastly expanded the range and volume of testimony-based belief. Reading a news article, consulting a database, watching a documentary, using a navigation app — all of these involve receiving information transmitted by others. Are these all testimony?

The epistemological interest in this question concerns justification: the same framework that applies to face-to-face testimony (when is it rational to trust what someone says?) should apply to mediated testimony. But mediated testimony introduces additional questions: the chain from original source to final consumer is longer, with more opportunities for error, distortion, and manipulation. The "speaker" whose sincerity and epistemic standing are relevant may be a complex institution rather than an individual.

## The Scope of Testimony-Based Knowledge

One striking implication of reflecting on testimony is how vast the scope of testimony-based knowledge is. Almost everything we know about history, science, geography, medicine, and the social world was acquired through testimony. The direct perceptual experience of any individual is a tiny fraction of their total knowledge base. We are, in this sense, epistemically dependent on the testimony of others — past and present — to an extraordinary degree.

This raises what Alvin Goldman calls the "epistemic division of labor": different people have expertise in different domains, and we depend on them to transmit reliable information across domains. Understanding when and how this system of epistemic dependence works well or breaks down is a central task of social epistemology.
