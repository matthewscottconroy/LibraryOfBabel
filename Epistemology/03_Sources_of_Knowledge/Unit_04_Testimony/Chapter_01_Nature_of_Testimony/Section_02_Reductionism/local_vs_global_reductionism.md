# Local vs. Global Reductionism about Testimony

The internal debate within reductionism — between global and local versions — is as philosophically significant as the debate between reductionism and anti-reductionism. Both global and local reductionists agree that testimonial justification ultimately derives from more basic sources (perception, induction, inference to the best explanation). They disagree about the structure and locus of that derivation.

## Global Reductionism: Hume's Legacy

Global reductionism holds that our justification for accepting testimony derives from a general, prior inductive basis: we have accumulated experience showing that testimony is reliable, and this general experience justifies us in accepting particular testimonial claims.

The structure of justification: I am justified in believing what witness W says on topic T because I have inductive evidence that witnesses like W speaking on topics like T tend to be accurate. The inductive generalization is established by correlating testimony with fact across many cases.

The advantage of global reductionism is that it is not excessively demanding: individual testimonial acceptances don't require case-by-case verification. The general track record does the work.

The disadvantage — Coady's challenge — is that establishing the inductive base requires independent verification of enough testimonial claims to support a general correlation. For claims about distant times, places, or specialized domains, this is impossible. We cannot check enough testimonial claims independently to establish the inductive base for those domains.

## Local Reductionism: Fricker's Account

Elizabeth Fricker's local reductionism holds that for each particular testimony-based belief, the hearer must have positive reasons from the specific context to justify accepting that belief. The "reduction" is not to a general prior inductive base but to contextual reasons present in the particular epistemic situation.

Fricker distinguishes between two epistemic positions:

- **Type 1**: A hearer has positive reasons, specific to this context, to trust this speaker. She has monitored the speaker's reliability, assessed their expertise and sincerity, found their testimony coherent with other evidence.
- **Type 2**: A hearer merely accepts the speaker's word without such contextual assessment.

Fricker holds that only Type 1 epistemic positions yield justified testimonial beliefs. Type 2 acceptance — naive credulity, accepting testimony without monitoring — is epistemically irresponsible, even if the testimony happens to be true.

## The Monitoring Requirement

The core of local reductionism is what Fricker calls the *monitoring requirement*: hearers have a general epistemic obligation to monitor the trustworthiness of speakers, assessing contextual features that bear on the likelihood that testimony is true. Cues include:

- **Expertise indicators**: Does the speaker have relevant expertise? Are they citing credentials or demonstrating knowledge?
- **Sincerity indicators**: Does the speaker have incentives to deceive? Are there signs of discomfort or evasiveness?
- **Coherence with background knowledge**: Does the testimony fit with what you already know? Does it conflict with established facts?
- **Track record**: Have you interacted with this speaker before? How reliable have they been?

Fricker does not require exhaustive investigation for every testimonial claim; that would be impossibly demanding. But she requires that hearers be sensitive to these cues and update accordingly — neither accepting nor rejecting testimony automatically.

## Objections to Local Reductionism

**The child's epistemic situation**: Children accept vast amounts of testimony without the monitoring Fricker requires. If local reductionism is right, children's testimonial beliefs are unjustified — which seems to conflict with the observation that much of what children are told is both true and known by them.

Fricker's response: children may be a special case where the epistemic norms are different, or where the monitoring requirement is waived by the special trust-relationship between caregiver and child. But this response risks ad hocness.

**The opacity of context**: Many contextual cues are not transparent to hearers. You cannot always determine whether a speaker has relevant expertise, whether they have incentives to deceive, or whether their testimony is coherent with facts you don't know. Local reductionism can demand more contextual information than is available.

**The regress problem**: Monitoring the reliability of testimony often requires relying on further testimony (about the speaker's expertise, credentials, or track record). If that further testimony is itself subject to monitoring requirements, a regress looms.

## The Hybrid Terrain

The debate between global and local reductionism reflects a deeper tension in epistemology between demanding high epistemic standards for knowledge and recognizing the practical constraints on what is achievable. Most epistemologists today occupy hybrid positions: requiring some degree of contextual sensitivity (local reductionism's insight) while not demanding full-scale verification for each testimonial claim (global reductionism's more permissive stance).
