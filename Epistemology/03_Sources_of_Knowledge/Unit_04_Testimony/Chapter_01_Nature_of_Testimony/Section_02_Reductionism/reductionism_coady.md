# Reductionism about Testimony: Hume and Coady

The central question in the epistemology of testimony is whether testimony provides an *independent* source of justification — one that requires no further grounds — or whether it is a *dependent* source that derives its epistemic legitimacy from more basic sources like perception and induction. Reductionism is the view that testimony is dependent: we are justified in believing testimony only because we have inductive evidence from perception and memory that testimony is generally reliable.

## Hume's Reductionist Account

David Hume articulated the reductionist position in the *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (Section X, "Of Miracles"). Hume's concern was with miraculous testimony — reports of violations of the laws of nature — but his analysis is general. He holds that our justification for believing testimony is entirely derived from experience:

> "The reason why we place any credit in witnesses and historians, is not derived from any connexion, which we perceive a priori, between testimony and reality, but because we are accustomed to find a conformity between them."

On Hume's account, we justify reliance on testimony by observing the correlation between testimony and fact: people's assertions typically match how things actually are, we have observed this correlation frequently, and we rely on it inductively. If our experience revealed that testimony was systematically unreliable, we would be unjustified in accepting it.

This is a **global reductionism**: the justification for testimony in general derives from our overall inductive experience of testimony's reliability. We don't need case-by-case verification; the general correlation established by experience justifies acceptance of particular testimonial claims, absent specific defeating factors.

## C.A.J. Coady's Defense and Critique

C.A.J. Coady's *Testimony: A Philosophical Study* (1992) is the most thorough examination of the reductionism/anti-reductionism debate in testimony epistemology. Coady defends anti-reductionism but begins with a careful reconstruction of the reductionist position.

Coady poses a challenge to Humean reductionism: how do we establish the inductive correlation between testimony and fact that Hume's account requires? We would need to verify testimonial claims independently of testimony — by perception, inference, and other non-testimonial sources. But this is impossible for the vast majority of what we believe through testimony. We cannot independently verify that ancient battles occurred, that mathematical theorems proved by others are correct, or that distant places exist as described.

If the inductive correlation could only be established for a small fraction of testimonial claims (those we can verify independently), then Humean reductionism cannot justify the vast range of testimony-based beliefs we actually hold. The inductive basis is too narrow to support the conclusion.

## Local Reductionism

One response to Coady's challenge is **local reductionism** (associated with Elizabeth Fricker). Local reductionism does not require a general prior inductive basis for all testimony. Instead, for each particular testimony-based belief, the hearer must have positive reasons — from the specific context — to trust the particular speaker in the particular situation.

You need not derive these reasons from a prior inductive base about testimony in general; you can form them from contextual evidence in the specific case: the speaker's apparent expertise, their incentives for truth-telling, their track record in the domain, the coherence of their testimony with what you know.

This is more demanding than global reductionism: it requires active assessment of each testimony rather than a default acceptance backed by a general track record. But it is also more targeted: you don't need to justify testimony in general, only to justify this particular testimonial belief.

## The Cost of Local Reductionism

Local reductionism is epistemically demanding. Consider: a child begins acquiring knowledge through testimony from their caregivers and teachers. The child has no independent evidence for the reliability of these particular informants; they simply accept what they are told. If local reductionism is right, much of children's testimony-based belief is unjustified.

This seems to imply that the epistemic foundations of ordinary knowledge are shakier than we think — a counterintuitive result. Anti-reductionists argue that it shows local reductionism is too demanding and that testimony provides justification without requiring positive reasons for each case.

## The Reductionism Debate's Stakes

The debate between reductionism and anti-reductionism about testimony has implications beyond technical epistemology:

- For **education**: if learning through testimony requires independent verification of teachers' reliability, education faces an epistemological problem.
- For **trust in institutions**: if reductionism is right, our trust in media, government, and scientific institutions requires positive inductive grounds — which are often absent or contested.
- For **the transmission of knowledge** across generations: if each new generation must independently verify the knowledge passed to them, cultural transmission of knowledge becomes epistemically precarious.
