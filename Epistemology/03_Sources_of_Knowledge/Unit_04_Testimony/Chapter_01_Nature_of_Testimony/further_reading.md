## Primary Sources

- C. A. J. Coady, *Testimony: A Philosophical Study*, Oxford University Press, 1992. The most comprehensive philosophical treatment of testimony, defining its nature, distinguishing it from other epistemic sources, and defending a non-reductionist account of its justificatory force.
- Elizabeth Fricker, "Against Gullibility," in B. K. Matilal and A. Chakrabarti (eds.), *Knowing from Words*, Kluwer, 1994. Defends local reductionism about testimony and argues that listeners have an epistemic duty to monitor speakers for signs of unreliability.
- Jennifer Lackey, *Learning from Words*, Oxford University Press, 2008. Develops a hybrid account of testimonial knowledge and raises important challenges to both pure reductionism and anti-reductionism.
- Tyler Burge, "Content Preservation," *Philosophical Review* 102, 1993. Argues for a priori entitlement to accept testimony, grounding it in a principled connection between assertion and truth.
- Alvin Goldman, *Knowledge in a Social World*, Oxford University Press, 1999. Develops social epistemology and places testimonial knowledge within a broadly reliabilist framework, emphasizing the role of social institutions in knowledge transmission.
- Miranda Fricker, *Epistemic Injustice*, Oxford University Press, 2007. Examines how social and structural factors — particularly hermeneutical and testimonial injustice — affect whose testimony is credited and how.
- Thomas Reid, *Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man*, ed. Derek Brookes, Pennsylvania State University Press, 2002 (orig. 1786). Articulates the principle of credulity — that we have a natural and rational disposition to believe what we are told — as a basic principle of common-sense epistemology.
- David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, Section X, ed. Tom Beauchamp, Oxford University Press, 1999 (orig. 1748). Hume's treatment of testimony in the context of miracles is the classic statement of a reductionist approach: testimony's credibility must be grounded in our experience of its track record.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Axel Gelfert, *A Critical Introduction to Testimony*, Bloomsbury, 2014. Accessible and comprehensive introduction covering the nature of testimony, its epistemic analysis, and its social dimensions.
- Jennifer Lackey and Ernest Sosa (eds.), *The Epistemology of Testimony*, Oxford University Press, 2006. Essential anthology with papers representing all major positions in the debate, including pieces on the nature of testimony, reductionism, and the transmission of knowledge.
- Peter Graham, "Conveying Information," *Synthese* 116, 1998. Analyzes testimony as a form of information transmission and considers how the nature of testimony affects its epistemic role.
- John Hardwig, "Epistemic Dependence," *Journal of Philosophy* 82, 1985. Argues that epistemic dependence on testimony is pervasive and philosophically significant, challenging the assumption that individual autonomy is the ideal in epistemology.
- Philip Kitcher, *Science in a Free Society*, Harvard University Press, 1993. Examines how the division of epistemic labor in science creates deep testimonial dependence, with consequences for individual and collective knowledge.
- Sanford Goldberg, *Relying on Others: An Essay in Epistemology*, Oxford University Press, 2010. Defends an account of testimonial knowledge that extends epistemological evaluation to the entire chain of testimony.
- Elizabeth Fricker, "Testimony: Knowing Through Being Told," in Ilkka Niiniluoto et al. (eds.), *Handbook of Epistemology*, Kluwer, 2004. Overview of the main positions in testimony epistemology with a defense of local monitoring requirements.
