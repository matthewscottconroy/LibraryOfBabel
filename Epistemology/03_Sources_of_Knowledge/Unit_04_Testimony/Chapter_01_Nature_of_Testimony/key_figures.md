## C. A. J. Coady (born 1939)

Coady's *Testimony: A Philosophical Study* (1992) is the book that transformed testimony into a major topic in analytical epistemology. Coady argues for a broad conception of testimony encompassing any sincere assertion made by a speaker to a listener about how things are, and insists that testimony's pervasiveness makes it epistemically indispensable: virtually all our knowledge of history, geography, science, and other minds depends on it. Coady's anti-reductionist conclusion — that we have a basic non-inferential entitlement to accept testimony — was a direct challenge to the Humean tradition that requires inductive support for each act of testimonial belief.

## Thomas Reid (1710–1796)

Reid's *Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man* (1786) articulates what he called the principle of credulity — the natural and rational disposition to believe what we are told in the absence of specific grounds for doubt. Reid treated this principle as a basic feature of human cognitive architecture, given to us by God or nature as part of our epistemic equipment. He saw testimony and perception as structurally similar: just as we are constitutionally disposed to believe what we perceive without first inferring the reliability of our senses, so we are constitutionally disposed to believe what we are told. Reid's principle of credulity became the touchstone for subsequent anti-reductionist positions.

## David Hume (1711–1776)

Hume's treatment of testimony in the Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding — primarily in the context of miracles — articulates the reductionist position: our credence in testimonial reports should be proportional to our past experience of the correlation between such reports and the facts they report. Hume argued that the credibility of testimony is always derived, never primitive: it is supported by inductive evidence about human honesty and the reliability of informants. While Hume's specific application to miracles is contested, his reductionist principle — that the epistemic standing of testimony depends on prior evidence of its reliability — has been the dominant view against which anti-reductionists have argued.

## Jennifer Lackey (born 1971)

Lackey's *Learning from Words* (2008) challenges both pure reductionism and pure anti-reductionism about testimony with carefully constructed cases. Her "zombie case" suggests that anti-reductionism allows the transmission of knowledge from speakers who lack genuine knowledge, while her "creationist teacher" case suggests that a hearer can gain knowledge from a speaker who sincerely asserts a proposition she does not believe. These cases motivate Lackey's hybrid view — that testimonial knowledge requires both positive reasons on the hearer's side and a reliable testimonial mechanism — making testimony epistemically analogous to perception in requiring both a reliable process and some positive epistemic connection on the hearer's part.

## Miranda Fricker (born 1966)

Fricker's *Epistemic Injustice* (2007) transformed the discussion of testimony by introducing a social and ethical dimension. Her central concept of "testimonial injustice" — the wrong done to a speaker when a hearer gives them insufficient credibility due to an identity prejudice — bridges epistemology and social philosophy, showing that who is believed is not simply a matter of rational evidence assessment but is shaped by social power. Fricker's work has expanded the scope of testimony epistemology to include questions of credibility, structural inequality, and the epistemic responsibilities of listeners and institutional structures.

## Sanford Goldberg (born 1963)

Goldberg's *Relying on Others* (2010) develops the most sophisticated account of how testimonial knowledge depends on the entire chain of epistemic transmission rather than just the immediate speaker-hearer interaction. He argues that a hearer's knowledge depends not only on whether the immediate speaker knows but on whether the epistemic standards appropriate to the type of assertion were met at every point in the chain through which the information traveled. This "extended epistemology" has important implications for understanding how testimonial knowledge is possible in complex, multiply-mediated information environments such as journalism, scientific communication, and online information systems.
