## The Wired-In Historian

**The scenario:** Imagine that all your apparent memories of historical events — the French Revolution, the moon landing, the works of Shakespeare — are in fact directly produced by a sophisticated computer program that was connected to your brain at birth, feeding you accurate information about history. You have never read a book, spoken to a historian, or accessed any testimonial source. The computer's information is entirely accurate and reliably produced.

**The philosophical point:** This case tests what is essential to testimonial knowledge. If you have accurate beliefs about historical events that were caused by a reliable process, does it matter that the process did not involve the assertion of a person? Those who hold a narrow view of testimony — requiring sincere assertion between persons — will say you have knowledge of history but not testimonial knowledge. Those with broader views may say that this is a limiting case of information-transmission. The scenario highlights the question of what distinguishes testimony as an epistemic source from other forms of information transfer, and whether the interpersonal character of assertion is epistemically relevant or merely incidental.

## Coady's Three Witnesses

**The scenario:** Three witnesses observe the same crime from different vantage points and give mutually corroborating accounts to the police. The reductionist — following Hume — claims that the corroboration of the three accounts strengthens our grounds for believing their common content. But suppose the three witnesses all got their information from a single initial observer who told them all the same story, and their apparent independence is a coincidence of shared informational origin.

**The philosophical point:** Coady uses cases like this to argue that the reductionist's appeal to corroboration to ground testimonial credibility is circular in practice: the independence required for corroboration to provide genuine inductive support is typically unverifiable in real testimonial chains. This supports his anti-reductionist conclusion that our acceptance of testimony must be presumptive rather than grounded in case-by-case inductive support. The case also illustrates the difficulty of applying Humean principles to the actual social epistemology of testimony transmission.

## Fricker's "Meddling Middleman"

**The scenario:** A hearer asks a reliable, knowledgeable friend about the time of a concert. The friend accurately tells her "8pm." Unknown to both parties, a malicious third person has at some earlier point interfered in the chain through which the friend received this information — though the interference happened to preserve the truth. The friend does not know this happened.

**The philosophical point:** This case, related to Sanford Goldberg's extended epistemology, raises the question of whether testimonial knowledge depends on the epistemic properties of the entire chain of transmission or only on the immediate speaker. If the hearer acquires knowledge here, it seems that causal chains extending well beyond the immediate conversational context are epistemically irrelevant. But if the malicious interference had resulted in false information, we would say the hearer lacks knowledge. This asymmetry suggests that the standards for testimonial knowledge are sensitive to the history of transmission in ways that our folk practice may not recognize.

## The Expert and the Layperson

**The scenario:** A patient with no medical training is told by her doctor that she has a particular condition requiring a specific treatment. She believes the diagnosis. Later, she learns that her doctor is the world's leading specialist in this condition, that his diagnosis is confirmed by three independent specialists, and that the treatment is supported by multiple randomized controlled trials. Alternatively: she later learns that her doctor was deregistered the week after the diagnosis. In neither case does she have independent grounds for evaluating the medical claim.

**The philosophical point:** The expert-layperson relationship is the most philosophically significant form of epistemic dependence in modern society. The case raises the question of whether, and how, a layperson can acquire knowledge from an expert whose claims she cannot independently verify. Anti-reductionists like Coady and Burge argue that there is a prima facie entitlement to accept sincere assertion; the news that the doctor was deregistered is a defeater, but the original entitlement did not require positive evidence. Reductionists require that the patient have evidence of the doctor's reliability — but this seems to set an impossibly high bar for ordinary people navigating expert testimony in science, medicine, and law.

## Lackey's Zombie Speaker

**The scenario:** An anti-reductionist holds that a hearer acquires testimonial knowledge whenever a knowledgeable speaker sincerely asserts the relevant proposition and the hearer accepts it without positive evidence of unreliability. Lackey imagines a "zombie speaker" who lacks any mental content — she produces the sounds associated with a true and known proposition, but purely mechanically, without belief or knowledge. The hearer, who cannot tell the difference, accepts the assertion.

**The philosophical point:** If the anti-reductionist's conditions are met (a hearer accepts an assertion without any positive reasons for doubt), but the speaker is a zombie with no knowledge, do we want to say the hearer acquires testimonial knowledge? Intuitively, no — but this means that the anti-reductionist account that requires nothing from the hearer is insufficient. Lackey concludes that testimonial knowledge requires something more from both parties: the speaker must genuinely know, and the hearer must be appropriately epistemically connected to the assertion. This motivates hybrid accounts that place conditions on both the speaker and the hearer.
