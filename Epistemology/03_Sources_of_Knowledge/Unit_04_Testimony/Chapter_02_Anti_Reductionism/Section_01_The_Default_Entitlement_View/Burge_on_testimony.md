# Tyler Burge on Testimony, Content, and Epistemic Entitlement

Tyler Burge's account of testimony epistemology is one of the most philosophically demanding in the literature. It is anti-reductionist but avoids grounding testimonial justification in vague notions of natural trust. Instead, Burge develops a principled account rooted in his broader philosophy of mind — particularly his externalism about mental content — and in a careful analysis of the rational norms governing sincere assertion.

## The Content Preservation Principle

Burge's central claim, the "content preservation" thesis, states: when a thinker receives a sincere, intelligible communication, the thinker has a prima facie entitlement to accept the content of that communication. This entitlement is not derived from inductive experience; it is a priori — it flows from what it is to receive a sincere communication as such.

The key word is "entitlement" rather than "justification." Burge distinguishes between *epistemic entitlements* (positive epistemic statuses that a thinker has even without reasons that can be cited in support of the belief) and *epistemic justifications* (positive epistemic statuses grounded in accessible reasons). Perceptual beliefs are entitled, not justified in the full sense: you don't need to cite reasons for your perceptual beliefs; you are entitled to them by virtue of being a perceiver in a functioning environment.

Testimonial beliefs, on Burge's account, are similarly entitled: you don't need to cite inductive reasons; you are entitled to them by virtue of being a rational recipient of sincere communication.

## The Rational Norm of Sincere Assertion

Burge grounds this entitlement in the rational norm governing sincere assertion: when someone sincerely asserts P with understanding, they represent themselves as having adequate warrant for P and as communicating that warrant to the hearer. This is the communicative practice governed by norms of veracity and epistemic responsibility.

The hearer, in receiving a sincere assertion, is in a relationship mediated by these norms. The norms do not guarantee truth, but they create a presumption of reliability — not inductive but constitutive of what sincere assertion *is*. Accepting a sincere assertion is participating in a rational practice that carries its own epistemic norms.

This is a kind of transcendental argument: the practice of assertion constitutively involves norms of truth-telling, and participation in this practice constitutively entitles the hearer to accept what is asserted. There is no further justification needed because the entitlement flows from the nature of the practice.

## The Role of Externalism

Burge's externalism about mental content plays a role in his testimony epistemology. Externalism holds that the content of mental states is partly determined by factors outside the individual's head — by the environment and social context. The meaning of your words, and hence the content of your beliefs, is partly fixed by the semantic norms of your linguistic community.

On the testimonial side, this means that when you accept testimony, you accept content that is individuated externally. The hearer's belief acquires its content partly through the communicative relationship with the speaker. This "communal" character of mental content connects to the anti-reductionist position: our epistemic dependence on testimony is not just practical but partly constitutive of what our thoughts are about.

## Testimonial Knowledge vs. Testimonial Belief

Burge addresses the question of whether testimonial acceptance produces knowledge. His answer: it does, in cases where the speaker knows what they assert and communicates it with understanding. The hearer's entitled acceptance of a speaker who knows P constitutes a transfer of knowledge from speaker to hearer.

This "transmission" model of testimonial knowledge — knowledge passes from speaker to hearer — is anti-reductionist in spirit. The hearer doesn't generate knowledge from their own resources; they receive it. The conditions for this reception are: the speaker has knowledge, the communication is sincere and intelligible, and the hearer has no defeater.

Critics of the transmission model ask: what if the speaker knows P but communicates it carelessly or in a way the hearer cannot fully evaluate? Is knowledge still transmitted? And what about cases where the chain of testimony is long — you read it in an article which cited a study whose results have been reanalyzed? Burge's framework requires tracing the entitlement through the entire chain.

## Limitations of the Approach

Burge's account is primarily a priori and normative — it describes the ideal case of testimonial knowledge transmission. Critics have pointed out that it may not do full justice to the diversity of testimonial situations: unreliable experts, motivated communicators, ideologically distorted media. For these cases, the hearer's entitlement is easily defeated, and it's not clear that the content preservation principle tells us enough about when and how to update appropriately.

Burge's framework is most illuminating for well-functioning epistemic communities where the norms of sincere assertion are widely observed. It is less helpful for diagnosing and navigating the broken or distorted epistemic environments that social and political epistemology must address.
