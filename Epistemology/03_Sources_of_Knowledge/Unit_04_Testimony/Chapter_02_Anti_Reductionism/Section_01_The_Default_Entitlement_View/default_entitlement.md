# Default Entitlement and Non-Reductionism about Testimony

Anti-reductionism about testimony holds that testimony provides an independent source of epistemic justification — one that does not derive from prior inductive grounds or require contextual verification. The core claim: in the absence of positive reasons for distrust, hearers are entitled by default to believe what they are told. This position is associated with Thomas Reid in the early modern period and, in contemporary philosophy, with Burge, Coady, Williamson, and others.

## The Principle of Credulity

Thomas Reid formulated what he called the "principle of credulity": there is a natural, rational disposition to believe what others sincerely tell us, and this disposition is justified — not merely natural. Reid contrasted this with the principle of veracity (the natural tendency to tell the truth): the two principles are correlative, designed to support the transmission of knowledge through testimony.

Reid's position is not naive credibility. He acknowledges that experience can undermine the default trust. What the principle insists is that the *default* epistemic position with respect to testimony is trust, not suspicion. The burden of proof lies with distrust, not with trust.

This is in direct contrast to Humean reductionism, which requires positive inductive grounds for trust. Reid says: we don't need to earn the right to trust testimony; we need reasons to withhold trust.

## Tyler Burge and Preservative Anti-Reductionism

Tyler Burge's "Content Preservation" (1993) offers a philosophically precise anti-reductionist account. Burge's argument: when a person sincerely asserts a proposition with understanding, the hearer is entitled to accept it, provided the hearer has no specific reason to distrust the speaker. This entitlement is not derived from inductive experience but is a fundamental norm of rational epistemic practice.

Burge grounds this in a principle he calls *the a priori entitlement to understand content*: when you receive an intelligible, sincere assertion, you are entitled a priori to accept it, because the conditions of content-transmission guarantee a minimal connection between assertion and truth. Understanding a sincere assertion as expressing a particular content already involves a rational engagement with a truth-aimed speech act.

This is not to say all sincere assertions are true. It is to say that the rational practice of assertion — governed by norms of sincerity and knowledge — creates a presumption of reliability that entitles hearers to accept without prior inductive justification.

## The Asymmetry Between Trust and Distrust

Anti-reductionism often appeals to an asymmetry in the burden of proof. When you receive testimony, you have two options: accept it (with a default entitlement to do so) or reject/withhold (requiring a reason for doing so). The anti-reductionist says that the default is acceptance: in the absence of defeat, the assertion of a sincere, competent speaker justifies belief.

This asymmetry makes epistemic sense given the function of testimony in social life. Testimony is a norm-governed practice: speakers are supposed to assert only what they know, and hearers can reasonably assume this norm is being followed absent specific signals of violation. The norm creates a prima facie presumption of reliability.

The reductionist can respond: the asymmetry exists in practice (we do default to trust) but is not epistemically justified unless backed by inductive grounds. Anti-reductionists insist that the practical default reflects an appropriate epistemic default.

## Children and the Epistemology of Testimony

Anti-reductionism handles the child's epistemic situation more naturally than reductionism. Children learn through testimony from the very beginning of their epistemic lives, before they have accumulated inductive grounds for trusting their informants. If anti-reductionism is right, children are entitled by default to believe what their caregivers tell them, and this provides the epistemic foundation for their early learning.

This seems to match our intuitive assessment: children's testimony-based beliefs are generally in good epistemic standing, not epistemically defective for lacking inductive verification. Anti-reductionism explains this; reductionism struggles with it.

## Critical Pressure on Anti-Reductionism

The main objection to anti-reductionism: if we are entitled by default to believe testimony, how do we explain the epistemic failure of trusting unreliable or deceptive testifiers? The reductionist says: you were wrong to trust without inductive grounds. The anti-reductionist must say: you had a prima facie entitlement that was defeated by factors you didn't recognize. The absence of defeat is not the same as the absence of defeat-relevant features — hearers can fail to recognize defeaters that are present.

This suggests that anti-reductionism's default entitlement is conditional on the absence of defeaters — but the question of when defeaters are present, and how the hearer is supposed to recognize them, requires careful articulation. The border between anti-reductionism and local reductionism can become quite thin at this point.
