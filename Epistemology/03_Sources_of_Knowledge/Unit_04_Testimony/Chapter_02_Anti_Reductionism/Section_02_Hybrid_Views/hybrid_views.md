# Hybrid Views: Between Reductionism and Anti-Reductionism

The debate between reductionism and anti-reductionism about testimony has generated a productive middle ground. Hybrid views accept elements from both sides: like anti-reductionism, they hold that testimony provides a genuine prima facie justification that doesn't require prior inductive grounds for each acceptance; like reductionism, they hold that this prima facie justification can be defeated by the absence of positive indicators in specific contexts. The result is a nuanced picture that tries to capture both the naturalness of default trust and the necessity of epistemic vigilance.

## Jennifer Lackey's Hybrid Account

Jennifer Lackey has developed one of the most carefully argued hybrid positions. She agrees with anti-reductionists that we are prima facie entitled to believe what we are told — not because of general inductive experience but because of the nature of assertion and the epistemic norms that govern sincere communication.

But Lackey criticizes both pure anti-reductionism and reductionism for missing the important role of the *hearer's* active engagement with testimony. Pure anti-reductionism is too passive: it suggests that hearers need only lack defeaters to be justified. Lackey argues that hearers have epistemic responsibilities: to be sensitive to cues about reliability, to adjust credences based on available evidence, and to seek further information when stakes are high.

Pure reductionism is too demanding: it requires positive verification that is often unavailable, and it cannot explain why accepting testimony in the absence of verification is ever appropriate.

Lackey's hybrid: hearers are entitled to a default acceptance but have a positive epistemic responsibility to monitor for defeaters and to calibrate their acceptance based on available contextual evidence. The entitlement is genuine but defeasible, and the hearer has an active role in managing the defeasibility conditions.

## The Speaker's Role in Hybrid Views

One innovative aspect of Lackey's account is its attention to the speaker's epistemic condition. Lackey argues that testimony can produce knowledge in the hearer even when the speaker doesn't know what they assert — as long as certain conditions hold: the speaker has adequate evidence for the assertion (even if not full knowledge), the hearer has no defeaters, and the content is accurate.

This "no-transmission" view (knowledge doesn't require being transmitted from a knower) challenges the transmission model. Knowledge can arise in the hearer through a reliable testimonial process even when the speaker is not a knower. This makes testimonial knowledge more like reliably produced knowledge generally — the key is the process's reliability, not the epistemic status of the particular source.

## The Case of Uninstructed Testimony

Lackey uses the "uninstructed testimony" case to motivate her hybrid view. Consider a cancer patient who, on learning their diagnosis, passes on accurate information about cancer symptoms to another patient. The first patient doesn't believe the diagnosis themselves (they are in denial) but reports it accurately. On the transmission model, no knowledge is transmitted (the speaker doesn't know). But on Lackey's view, the hearer can come to know through this testimony — the testimony was reliable, accurate, and undefeated.

This case suggests that testimonial knowledge is not simply the transfer of knowledge but the reliable generation of true belief through a testimony-based process — a more reliabilist picture.

## The Norm of Testimonial Assertion

Another hybrid approach focuses on the norm governing assertion. Timothy Williamson's knowledge norm of assertion (one must assert only what one knows) connects to testimony epistemology: if speakers are subject to this norm, hearers can expect speakers to assert only what they know, and this expectation provides a basis for prima facie acceptance.

But the norm can be violated, and hearers have some responsibility to track violations. A hybrid view emerges: default acceptance grounded in the knowledge norm of assertion, modulated by contextual monitoring of whether the norm is being followed.

## Stakes and Adjusting Trust

A recurring theme in hybrid views is that the appropriate level of trust should vary with the stakes involved. For low-stakes beliefs (the shop closes at 6pm), minimal monitoring may be sufficient. For high-stakes beliefs (this surgery is safe), more rigorous investigation is required. This is a kind of stakes-sensitive calibration of the epistemic responsibilities associated with testimonial acceptance.

This connects to the broader literature on subject-sensitive invariantism and pragmatic encroachment: whether you know P may depend not just on your evidence but on what is at stake for you. In the testimonial domain, this means that the threshold for responsible acceptance varies with the importance of the belief.
