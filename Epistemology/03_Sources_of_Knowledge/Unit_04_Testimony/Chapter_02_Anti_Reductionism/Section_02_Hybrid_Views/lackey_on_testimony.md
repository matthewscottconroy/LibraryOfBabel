# Lackey on Testimony: Learning from Words and Worlds

Jennifer Lackey's book *Learning from Words* (2008) is the most systematic contemporary treatment of the epistemology of testimony, and it develops her distinctive hybrid position with care and detail. Lackey argues that neither reductionism nor anti-reductionism provides an adequate account of how testimony works epistemically, and she develops a "dualist" theory that assigns irreducible roles to both the speaker and the hearer.

## The Speaker-Based Dimension

Lackey argues that any adequate theory of testimonial knowledge must attend to what she calls the *speaker-based* dimension. Whether a hearer acquires knowledge through testimony depends not only on the hearer's epistemic position but also on the speaker's. Specifically, the speaker's testimony must be reliable: the speaker must have adequate evidence for what they assert, their cognitive faculties must be functioning properly, and the assertion must be produced through processes that reliably generate accurate testimony.

This speaker-based condition is externalist: the hearer need not know anything about the speaker's internal epistemic condition to have justified testimonial belief. But the speaker's condition must be adequate for knowledge to flow through testimony.

This is one of the implications of Lackey's "no-transmission" view: knowledge does not simply pass from a knowing speaker to a hearer. The hearer generates knowledge through a reliable testimonial process. But that process includes the speaker's production of testimony, so the speaker's reliability is a condition on the process.

## The Hearer-Based Dimension

The hearer-based dimension concerns what the hearer brings to the testimonial transaction. Lackey holds that hearers have positive epistemic obligations: they must be appropriately sensitive to available evidence about the speaker's reliability, must not ignore salient defeaters, and must engage with testimony in an epistemically responsible way.

The hearer cannot simply receive testimony passively. Even if the speaker is reliable and the testimony is accurate, if the hearer has clear evidence that the speaker is unreliable and ignores it, the hearer fails to acquire knowledge. The hearer's epistemic agency is irreducibly important.

This is the hybrid element: anti-reductionism is right that a default entitlement exists; reductionism is right that the hearer has responsibilities to monitor and adjust.

## Against the Pure Transmission Model

Lackey's major theoretical contribution is her argument against the pure transmission model: the idea that testimonial knowledge consists in knowledge being transmitted from a knowing speaker to a hearer, so that the hearer knows only if the speaker knows.

She argues against this with cases like the "creationist teacher": a teacher who believes in creationism is required to teach evolution. She sincerely asserts evolutionary propositions that she doesn't believe and does not know. A student who accepts her testimony about evolution does know evolution is true — because the testimony is reliable and accurate, and the student has no defeater.

This case shows that the transmission model is too restrictive. The source of testimonial justification is not the speaker's knowledge but the reliability of the testimonial process — which can hold even when the speaker lacks knowledge.

## Testimonial Non-Transmission of Knowledge

The flip side: if knowledge requires transmission, then a speaker who knows P and asserts P to a hearer should always transmit knowledge. But Lackey argues this fails too. Suppose the hearer has strong independent evidence against P but accepts the speaker's testimony anyway, overriding their own evidence. The hearer may form a true belief through testimony, but the belief doesn't constitute knowledge because it is held irresponsibly (against evidence).

This shows that hearers can block transmission through epistemic irresponsibility: knowledge doesn't automatically flow from knowing speakers to credulous hearers who have evidence to the contrary.

## The Coherence of Lackey's Dualism

Lackey's dualism holds that both the speaker dimension and the hearer dimension are independently necessary and jointly sufficient (with accuracy and no undefeated defeaters) for testimonial knowledge. Neither alone suffices: a reliable speaker whose testimony is accepted by an epistemically irresponsible hearer doesn't produce testimonial knowledge; an epistemically responsible hearer who receives testimony from an unreliable speaker doesn't either.

This dualism mirrors the structural features of other epistemological accounts: virtue epistemology's dual requirement that the cognitive faculty be reliable (external) and that the subject exercise it appropriately (internal), process reliabilism's requirement that both the process and its use be appropriate.

The result is a sophisticated account of testimonial knowledge that takes seriously both the social nature of testimony (the speaker's contribution) and the epistemic agency of the learner (the hearer's contribution).
