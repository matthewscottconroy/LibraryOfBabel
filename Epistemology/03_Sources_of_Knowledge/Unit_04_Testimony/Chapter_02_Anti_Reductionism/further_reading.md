## Primary Sources

- David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, Section X ("Of Miracles"), ed. Tom Beauchamp, Oxford University Press, 1999 (orig. 1748). The canonical reductionist statement: the credibility of testimony must be proportioned to our experiential evidence of speakers' reliability.
- C. A. J. Coady, *Testimony: A Philosophical Study*, Oxford University Press, 1992. The landmark anti-reductionist work arguing that Hume's reductionism is both empirically implausible and logically untenable, and defending the view that testimony is a basic source of evidence.
- Thomas Reid, *Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man*, ed. Derek Brookes, Pennsylvania State University Press, 2002 (orig. 1786). Reid's principle of credulity posits a natural and rational presumption in favor of the truthfulness of testimony as a foundational cognitive principle.
- Tyler Burge, "Content Preservation," *Philosophical Review* 102, 1993. Defends a priori entitlement to accept testimony, arguing that the principle of preservation of content across sincere assertion is constitutive of thought and is justified a priori.
- Tyler Burge, "Interlocution, Perception, and Memory," *Philosophical Studies* 86, 1997. Extends the entitlement view to consider how testimony relates to perception and memory as sources of knowledge.
- Elizabeth Fricker, "Testimony and Epistemic Autonomy," in Jennifer Lackey and Ernest Sosa (eds.), *The Epistemology of Testimony*, Oxford University Press, 2006. Defends local reductionism — requiring positive reasons for accepting individual testimonial assertions — against Burge-style global anti-reductionism.
- Jennifer Lackey, *Learning from Words*, Oxford University Press, 2008. Challenges pure anti-reductionism through the creationist teacher case and develops a hybrid alternative that requires conditions on both speaker and hearer.
- Alvin Goldman, *Knowledge in a Social World*, Oxford University Press, 1999. Reliabilist treatment that grounds testimonial knowledge in the reliability of the relevant processes, a form of externalist anti-reductionism distinct from Burge's a priori approach.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Jennifer Lackey and Ernest Sosa (eds.), *The Epistemology of Testimony*, Oxford University Press, 2006. The essential anthology, with papers directly engaging the reductionism/anti-reductionism debate from multiple angles.
- Peter Graham, "Liberal Fundamentalism and Its Rivals," in Lackey and Sosa (eds.), *The Epistemology of Testimony*, Oxford University Press, 2006. Develops a reliabilist defense of anti-reductionism, arguing that we have basic entitlement to testimony derived from its general reliability.
- Sanford Goldberg, *Relying on Others*, Oxford University Press, 2010. Places anti-reductionism in a broader social-epistemological framework, extending epistemic assessment across chains of testimony.
- Jonathan Adler, *Belief's Own Ethics*, MIT Press, 2002. Examines the epistemic and ethical dimensions of belief, including the specific obligations we have with respect to accepting testimony.
- John Greco, "The Value Problem," in Adrian Haddock et al. (eds.), *Epistemic Value*, Oxford University Press, 2009. Virtue epistemology approach to testimonial knowledge with implications for the anti-reductionism debate.
- Paul Faulkner, *Knowledge on Trust*, Oxford University Press, 2011. Defends the view that testimonial knowledge requires trust between speaker and hearer, with a careful analysis of what trust involves.
- Philip Kitcher, *Science in a Free Society*, Harvard University Press, 1993. Argues that the division of cognitive labor in science creates irreducibly testimonial knowledge that cannot be grounded in individual inductive credentials.
- Miranda Fricker, *Epistemic Injustice*, Oxford University Press, 2007. Extends the analysis of testimony to consider structural credibility deficits — situations in which social power systematically distorts who receives the benefit of the doubt.
