## David Hume (1711–1776)

Hume's reductionist account of testimony in the Enquiry provides the foil against which all anti-reductionist views are defined. Hume argued that our credence in testimony is grounded entirely in inductive evidence: we have learned through experience that human beings are generally honest and that reports tend to correspond to the facts, and it is this accumulated empirical background that justifies individual acts of testimonial acceptance. For Hume, the credibility of testimony is always derivative — borrowed from the track record of speakers and types of testimony — and never primitive or presumptive. His application of this principle to reports of miracles concludes that no testimonial evidence could rationally justify belief in a miracle, since the testimony must overcome our experiential evidence of the regularity of natural laws.

## Thomas Reid (1710–1796)

Reid is the founding father of the anti-reductionist tradition. He argued that the principle of credulity — the natural disposition to believe what we are told absent grounds for suspicion — is as fundamental a feature of our cognitive constitution as the disposition to believe our perceptions. For Reid, Hume's requirement that we ground testimonial acceptance in prior inductive evidence faces an insuperable regress: to learn from testimony that testimony is reliable, we would already need to be accepting testimony. This bootstrapping problem shows, Reid argues, that credulity must be a basic rational disposition rather than a derived one. Reid's view anticipated contemporary anti-reductionism by insisting that testimony's epistemic status is primary, not dependent on other sources.

## C. A. J. Coady (born 1939)

Coady's *Testimony* (1992) revived and systematized Reid's insight against the empiricist tradition. His main argument against Humean reductionism is that individuals cannot acquire inductive evidence for the general reliability of testimony without already relying on testimony — making the reductionist's project necessarily circular. Coady also argues from the pervasiveness of testimonial knowledge: if reductionism were correct and we required positive inductive support for each testimonial acceptance, we would know almost nothing, since the vast majority of our beliefs about the world depend on chains of testimony that we cannot personally verify. Coady concludes that testimony must be a basic source of knowledge, structurally analogous to perception.

## Tyler Burge (born 1946)

Burge's "Content Preservation" (1993) offers the most philosophically sophisticated anti-reductionist account. He argues that we have a priori entitlement — a type of justification that does not require inference or evidence — to accept what we are told. This entitlement is grounded in a constitutive principle: it is part of what it is to be a thinker capable of grasping propositional content that one is entitled to preserve and transmit content across sincere assertion. Burge distinguishes entitlement from justification in the evidential sense: entitlement is a rational warrant that can be defeated by specific evidence but does not require positive support. This makes anti-reductionism compatible with the fallibility of testimony while preserving its presumptive status.

## Elizabeth Fricker (born 1956)

Fricker is the most influential defender of reductionism in the contemporary debate. Her "local reductionism" does not require that hearers accumulate global inductive evidence for the reliability of testimony in general; instead, it requires that they actively monitor individual speakers for signs of unreliability on the specific topic in question. Fricker argues that blind acceptance — accepting testimony without engaging in any monitoring — constitutes epistemic negligence. Her position has been challenged by anti-reductionists who argue that such monitoring is practically impossible across the vast range of topics on which we depend on testimony, but Fricker maintains that the epistemic autonomy ideal requires at least some active engagement from the hearer.

## Jennifer Lackey (born 1971)

Lackey's hybrid view steers between pure reductionism and pure anti-reductionism. Her "creationist teacher" case presents a speaker who sincerely asserts a true proposition (about evolutionary biology) while personally not believing it. Lackey argues that on pure anti-reductionist accounts, the hearer could acquire knowledge from such a speaker even if the speaker's testimony is not knowledge-expressing. She concludes that testimonial knowledge requires conditions on both sides: on the speaker's side, that the relevant portion of the assertion be knowledge-expressing; on the hearer's side, that the hearer have no undefeated reasons to doubt the speaker. This hybrid account is now one of the most widely discussed positions in testimony epistemology.
