## Coady's Language-Learning Child

**The scenario:** A child acquires language and with it the capacity to learn from testimony. At the very beginning of this process, the child has no accumulated track record of experience with which to assess the reliability of speakers. Yet the child does learn from testimony — she accepts what her parents and teachers tell her and builds up a body of knowledge. If reductionism requires prior positive evidence of reliability before testimonial acceptance is rational, the child's earliest testimonial acceptances are irrational. But this seems wrong.

**The philosophical point:** Coady uses this case to argue that the Humean requirement for inductive evidence of reliability cannot be the right account of what grounds testimonial knowledge. The child has no such evidence and cannot yet acquire it independently. If the child's testimony-based beliefs are rational (as they seem to be), then rational testimonial acceptance cannot require prior inductive evidence. The case supports the anti-reductionist view that there is a basic, default entitlement to accept testimony — one that operates before any inductive track record can be established and that is, at least in part, what makes the acquisition of that track record possible in the first place.

## Lackey's Creationist Teacher

**The scenario:** A highly knowledgeable biology teacher accepts the scientific consensus on evolution and teaches it accurately to her students. However, she personally does not believe in evolution — she holds a creationist religious faith that she keeps entirely separate from her professional assertions. She sincerely asserts to her students, in her professional capacity, the truths of evolutionary biology. The students, who have no reason to doubt her, acquire beliefs about evolution on this basis.

**The philosophical point:** Lackey designed this case against pure anti-reductionism. If the anti-reductionist holds that a hearer acquires testimonial knowledge simply by accepting a sincere assertion made by a knowledgeable speaker, then the students in this case should not acquire knowledge — the teacher does not believe what she asserts, so her assertion is not knowledge-expressing in the relevant sense. But pure anti-reductionism has difficulty explaining why the speaker's personal attitude should matter if the content is accurate and reliably produced. The case motivates the hybrid view: both the speaker's side (the assertion should be knowledge-connected) and the hearer's side (no undefeated reasons for doubt) must be satisfied.

## Reid's Regress

**The scenario:** Suppose a philosopher demands inductive evidence for the reliability of testimony before accepting any testimonial belief. To gather this evidence, she must observe cases where testimonial assertions have proven true. But observing that testimony is true requires comparing testimonial reports against facts — and most of our knowledge of those facts is itself testimonially derived. There is no testimony-independent standpoint from which to assess testimony's general reliability.

**The philosophical point:** This regress argument, drawn from Reid, is the most fundamental challenge to Humean reductionism. It suggests that the requirement for inductive validation of testimony is self-undermining: the very evidence that would be needed to validate testimony (knowledge of facts against which testimonial claims could be checked) is itself largely testimonial. The anti-reductionist concludes that some testimonial acceptance must be basic — accepted without prior evidential support — and that reductionism sets an epistemically impossible standard that, if applied consistently, would leave us without any testimonial knowledge at all.

## Burge's Priori Entitlement Scenario

**The scenario:** A philosopher reads a stranger's philosophical paper and forms beliefs about the stranger's claims. She has no independent evidence about whether this particular author is reliable, has a good track record, or has any reason to be truthful. Yet it seems that she is at least prima facie rational in taking the paper's assertions at face value, at least in the absence of specific defeaters.

**The philosophical point:** Burge uses cases like this to motivate the entitlement view. The reader's rational acceptance of the stranger's assertions cannot be traced to any positive inductive evidence she possesses about this author specifically. Something more fundamental is at work: an a priori entitlement grounded in the very nature of comprehending an assertion. Burge's claim is that understanding what it is for someone to assert something already commits one — in a normative sense — to the default credibility of that assertion, because assertion is a norm-governed speech act aimed at conveying truth. This is anti-reductionism grounded not in cognitive psychology (Reid's approach) but in the constitutive norms of linguistic understanding.

## The Global Conspiracy Case

**The scenario:** You live in a world where all your testimonial sources — newspapers, books, teachers, friends, and family — are part of a perfectly coordinated global conspiracy to deceive you about a specific matter. You have no independent means of checking the matter in question. Your beliefs derived from all this testimony are uniformly false, but you have no grounds for suspecting the conspiracy.

**The philosophical point:** This scenario tests the limits of the anti-reductionist's presumptive entitlement to accept testimony. If entitlement is defeated only by specific evidence of unreliability — and you have none — then by the anti-reductionist's lights you remain entitled to your false testimonial beliefs. Critics argue this shows that entitlement cannot be purely presumptive without any truth-tracking connection to the world. Anti-reductionists may respond that this is precisely the structure of all our basic epistemic sources (perception too can be globally deceived) and that this possibility does not undermine the rationality of our default epistemic practices.
