## Primary Sources

- Annette Baier, "Trust and Antitrust," *Ethics* 96, 1986. The foundational philosophical analysis of trust, arguing that trust involves vulnerability and that it differs from mere reliance or confident prediction of another's behavior.
- Paul Faulkner, *Knowledge on Trust*, Oxford University Press, 2011. Develops a trust-based account of testimonial knowledge, arguing that the epistemic relationship between speaker and hearer is grounded in a normative relation of trust rather than merely causal or functional connection.
- Jennifer Lackey, *Learning from Words*, Oxford University Press, 2008. Develops the hybrid account, argues against pure trust-based views with the creationist teacher case, and carefully analyzes what is required of both speakers and hearers for testimonial knowledge.
- Miranda Fricker, *Epistemic Injustice*, Oxford University Press, 2007. Introduces testimonial injustice as a category of wrong done when credibility is withheld due to identity prejudice, connecting the analysis of testimonial trust to social structures of power.
- Onora O'Neill, *A Question of Trust*, Cambridge University Press, 2002. Examines the conditions for rational trust in public life, science, and institutions, with sustained argument that the current "crisis of trust" is partly a misdiagnosis.
- Philip Pettit, "The Cunning of Trust," *Philosophy and Public Affairs* 24, 1995. Analyzes the rationality of trust in social contexts, distinguishing motivational from cognitive trust and examining when trust is epistemically appropriate.
- C. A. J. Coady, *Testimony: A Philosophical Study*, Oxford University Press, 1992. Foundational anti-reductionist work placing trust within a broad account of testimony's epistemic role.
- Richard Holton, "Deciding to Trust, Coming to Believe," *Australasian Journal of Philosophy* 72, 1994. Argues that trust is a practical stance taken toward a person rather than an epistemic assessment of their reliability, with implications for its role in testimonial acceptance.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Katherine Hawley, *Trust: A Very Short Introduction*, Oxford University Press, 2012. Accessible philosophical introduction to trust, covering its structure, conditions, and relevance to knowledge and testimony.
- Philip Kitcher, *Public Knowledge and Its Discontents*, Oxford University Press, 2011. Examines how the social organization of knowledge production affects the rational basis for trusting expert testimony and scientific consensus.
- John Hardwig, "Epistemic Dependence," *Journal of Philosophy* 82, 1985. Argues that trust in expert testimony is epistemically unavoidable and that epistemic dependence is a basic feature of modern knowledge.
- Sanford Goldberg, *Relying on Others*, Oxford University Press, 2010. Extends the analysis of testimonial trust across chains of transmission, arguing that the hearer's epistemic position depends on the standards met throughout the chain.
- Pamela Hieronymi, "The Reasons of Trust," *Australasian Journal of Philosophy* 86, 2008. Examines the reasons that bear on decisions to trust, distinguishing theoretical from practical reasons and their respective roles in epistemic and social trust.
- Karen Jones, "Trust as an Affective Attitude," *Ethics* 107, 1996. Argues that trust has an essentially affective component — not merely a disposition to rely but an optimistic attitude toward the trusted person's goodwill.
- Carolyn McLeod, *Self-Trust and Reproductive Autonomy*, MIT Press, 2002. Develops the concept of self-trust alongside interpersonal trust, with implications for epistemic autonomy and testimonial relationships.
- Miranda Fricker, "Rational Authority and Social Power: Towards a Truly Social Epistemology," *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society* 98, 1998. Early statement of the social dimensions of epistemic trust, anticipating the framework of *Epistemic Injustice*.
