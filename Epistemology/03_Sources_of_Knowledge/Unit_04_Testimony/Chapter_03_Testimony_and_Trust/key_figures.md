## Annette Baier (1929–2012)

Baier's "Trust and Antitrust" (1986) established the philosophical analysis of trust as a relation of vulnerability: to trust someone is not merely to believe they will act reliably but to make oneself vulnerable to them, to rely on their goodwill rather than merely on their competence or predictable behavior. Baier distinguished trust from simple reliance (I can rely on a stone to be immovable without trusting it) and argued that trust involves a normative dimension — a warrant to feel betrayed if the trusted person exploits one's vulnerability. Applied to testimony, this suggests that accepting another's assertion involves a normative relationship, not just a causal or probabilistic one.

## Paul Faulkner (born 1970)

Faulkner's *Knowledge on Trust* (2011) is the most sustained attempt to ground testimonial knowledge in a philosophical account of trust. He distinguishes two kinds of trust-based testimonial knowledge: knowledge grounded in "T-reasons" — reasons that arise from the trust relationship itself, such as the knowledge that one's interlocutor has accepted a responsibility to be truthful — and knowledge grounded in "R-reasons" — reasons about the speaker's reliability and competence. Faulkner argues that trust, properly understood, supplies genuinely epistemic reasons for belief rather than merely practical ones, bridging the gap between the social relationship of trusting and the epistemological question of what justifies testimonial beliefs.

## Miranda Fricker (born 1966)

Fricker's *Epistemic Injustice* (2007) introduced the category of "testimonial injustice" — the wrong done to a speaker when a hearer deflates their credibility due to prejudice based on the speaker's identity (race, gender, class). Fricker argues that this is both an epistemic wrong (the hearer acquires a false belief about the speaker's credibility, and may miss genuine knowledge) and an ethical wrong (the speaker is disrespected in their capacity as a knower). Her work connects the epistemology of testimony directly to questions of social trust, political power, and the conditions under which testimony can function as a genuine source of knowledge.

## Jennifer Lackey (born 1971)

Lackey's hybrid view of testimony has specific implications for the role of trust in testimonial knowledge. She argues against pure trust-based accounts — such as Faulkner's — by maintaining that trust alone is not sufficient for knowledge; the testimony must also be reliable and the hearer must lack defeating evidence. Lackey's attention to cases in which a speaker's sincere testimony fails to express knowledge (the creationist teacher) or in which a speaker lacks the relevant competence highlights the limits of trust as an epistemic foundation. Her work suggests that trust is an important component of testimonial knowledge but must be supplemented by reliability conditions.

## Onora O'Neill (born 1941)

O'Neill's *A Question of Trust* (2002) examines the social epistemology of trust in institutions, science, and public life. She argues that the widely diagnosed "crisis of trust" in modern society is partly a confusion between two distinct questions: whether to trust a person or institution, and whether to take their testimony as true. O'Neill contends that rational trust must be grounded in evidence of trustworthiness — not merely in the absence of specific defeaters — and that the conditions for such evidence have been systematically undermined by opacity and inaccessibility in public institutions. Her work connects the philosophical analysis of testimonial trust to practical questions about institutional design and epistemic accountability.

## Karen Jones (born 1963)

Jones's "Trust as an Affective Attitude" (1996) argues that genuine trust involves an essentially affective component — an "optimistic attitude" toward the goodwill of the trusted person — rather than being a purely cognitive or behavioral disposition. Applied to testimonial contexts, this implies that a hearer who relies on a speaker's assertions without any affective orientation toward the speaker's goodwill is not truly trusting the speaker but merely relying on them. Jones's account has implications for the epistemology of testimony: it suggests that the epistemic relationship between speaker and hearer involves normative and emotional dimensions that purely reliabilist or entitlement-based accounts of testimony may overlook.
