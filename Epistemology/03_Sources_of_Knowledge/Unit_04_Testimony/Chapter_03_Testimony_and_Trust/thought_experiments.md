## Lackey's Creationist Teacher (Trust Version)

**The scenario:** A biology teacher sincerely asserts correct information about evolutionary biology to her students, but does not herself believe it due to religious convictions. The students trust her, believe her to be competent and honest, and form true beliefs about evolution on this basis. Their trust in her is not misplaced in any way they can detect — she is indeed competent and honest in her professional role. But she is not passing on beliefs she holds.

**The philosophical point:** This case, repeated here for its bearing on trust specifically, shows that trust in a speaker's testimony is not sufficient for testimonial knowledge when the speaker's assertions are not belief-expressing. The students genuinely trust the teacher, but the epistemic foundation of that trust — that she believes what she says — is absent. Faulkner's trust-based account must explain how the T-reasons generated by trust can ground knowledge even when the speaker lacks the relevant belief. If trust-based accounts cannot handle this case, they risk implying that knowledgeable trust always suffices for knowledge regardless of whether the speaker is genuinely expressing their own epistemic state.

## Baier's Trusted Confidant

**The scenario:** A patient confides deeply personal medical information to her doctor. She trusts him not merely to be professionally competent but to exercise care and discretion — she relies on his goodwill rather than merely on his professional obligations. The doctor betrays her trust: he shares the information casually with a colleague. The patient learns of this later.

**The philosophical point:** Baier's analysis distinguishes trust from reliance precisely through this kind of case: a stone I rely on to be a doorstop cannot betray me, but a doctor I trust can. The patient's relationship to the doctor involved not just a prediction that he would maintain confidentiality but a normative vulnerability — a warranted expectation that grounds both epistemic reliance and a claim against betrayal. For testimonial epistemology, this shows that the speaker-hearer relationship in genuine testimonial trust is not merely a functional or probabilistic one. It has a normative structure that carries both epistemic and ethical implications, and its disruption constitutes not just a practical loss but an epistemic harm.

## O'Neill's Opacity Problem

**The scenario:** A government agency releases a report claiming that a certain chemical is safe at low exposure levels. The report is accurate, and the scientists who produced it are competent. However, the underlying data and methodology are classified for commercial reasons. Citizens cannot assess the evidence and must either accept or reject the conclusion on the basis of institutional trust. Many distrust the report simply because of the opacity.

**The philosophical point:** O'Neill argues that opacity in institutional testimony systematically undermines the conditions for rational trust, even when the testimony is in fact reliable. A citizen who accepts the report is not being irrational — the testimony is reliable — but she cannot have the kind of trust that is grounded in assessment of trustworthiness. This creates a structural problem for social epistemology: when the conditions for rational trust are absent (because the grounds for trusting are hidden), recipients of testimony must choose between credulity and blanket distrust, neither of which is epistemically ideal. O'Neill concludes that epistemic institutions have an obligation to provide accessible grounds for trust.

## Fricker's Credibility Deficit

**The scenario:** A Black woman reports to her university's disciplinary committee that she has been sexually harassed by a colleague. The committee members — all white, predominantly male — deflate her credibility due to implicit biases associated with her race and gender, even though her account is accurate and her testimony is epistemically impeccable. A different committee member, through the same description given in different circumstances, would be believed without hesitation.

**The philosophical point:** Fricker's analysis of testimonial injustice shows that the social conditions for testimonial trust are not neutral. Structural credibility deficits — systematic under-attribution of credibility to members of marginalized groups — constitute a form of epistemic injustice that is both ethically wrong and epistemically damaging. The committee members are not merely making a practical mistake; they are committing a hermeneutical failure that prevents them from recognizing genuine testimony as such. The case shows that the epistemology of trust cannot be separated from the social conditions of credibility attribution.

## The Trust of the Child and the Conditions of Its Rationality

**The scenario:** A young child trusts her parents and teachers unconditionally, accepting their testimony on all manner of subjects — from the existence of distant countries to the nature of God to the rules of conduct — without any independent capacity to assess their reliability. Most of what she learns is true, but some is false, and she has no way of distinguishing the two.

**The philosophical point:** The child's unconditional trust raises the question of whether trust is rational when the conditions for assessing trustworthiness are absent. Reid and Coady would say the child's trust is rational in a basic, presumptive sense — she has no grounds for suspicion, and default trust is rational. Fricker and O'Neill might argue that the child's epistemic vulnerability imposes obligations on the trusted parties rather than on the child. The case also highlights the developmental character of epistemic trust: the unconditional trust of childhood is not the model for adult rational trust, but neither is it epistemically defective in the way that naive adult trust would be. Trust is contextually rational in ways that vary with the agent's epistemic capacities and social position.
