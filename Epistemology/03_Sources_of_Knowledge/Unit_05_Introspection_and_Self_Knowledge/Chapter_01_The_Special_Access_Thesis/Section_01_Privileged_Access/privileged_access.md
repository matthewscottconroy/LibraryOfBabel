# Privileged Access: The Cartesian Legacy

The idea that we have privileged access to our own mental states — that we know our own minds in a way that is immediate, certain, or more secure than our knowledge of the external world — is one of the most influential and contested ideas in the history of philosophy of mind. It finds its clearest statement in Descartes and its most thoroughgoing critique in contemporary philosophy of mind and cognitive science.

## The Cartesian Picture

Descartes' epistemological project established the subjective, first-person standpoint as the foundation of knowledge. While doubting everything external, the *cogito* showed that the mind's existence as a thinking, experiencing thing could not be doubted. And the contents of the mind — the ideas, thoughts, and experiences that constitute mental life — seemed to be directly and certainly accessible to the thinker.

On the Cartesian picture:

- Mental states are transparent: all of your mental states are available to conscious awareness. If you have a belief, you can know that you have it by introspecting.
- Introspection is infallible: you cannot be wrong about what mental states you have. If it seems to you that you believe P, you believe P; if it seems to you that you desire X, you desire X.
- First-person authority is asymmetric: others can only observe your behavior and infer your mental states, but you have direct, non-inferential access to them.

This picture underlies a great deal of traditional epistemology and philosophy of mind. It makes the subjective domain — the sphere of mental states — a realm of certainty from which the rest of knowledge must be built.

## The Two Theses: Transparency and Infallibility

Two distinct claims are often bundled under "privileged access":

**Transparency**: All your mental states are, in principle, introspectively accessible. There are no mental states you have but cannot know you have. Your mental life is fully open to your inner gaze.

**Infallibility**: When you believe, on the basis of introspection, that you are in a certain mental state, you cannot be mistaken. Your introspective reports accurately track your actual mental states.

These are distinct. Transparency could fail (some mental states are inaccessible) while infallibility holds for the states that are accessible. Or transparency could hold while infallibility fails (you access your states but can mischaracterize them).

Both theses have been attacked:

- Against transparency: Freudian psychology, cognitive science, and ordinary experience all suggest that much of mental life occurs below the threshold of conscious access. We have unconscious beliefs, motivations, and processes that shape behavior without being introspectively available.

- Against infallibility: The fact that you *seem* to introspect a certain mental state doesn't mean you are in that state. Introspective reports can be confabulated, influenced by theoretical expectations, or simply wrong.

## The Self-Intimation Thesis

A related claim is the *self-intimation* thesis: if you are in a mental state, you are in a position to know that you are. This is a weak form of transparency: it doesn't say you always know your mental states, only that they are always available to be known.

The self-intimation thesis is plausible for occurrent mental states — states that are currently active in consciousness — but much less plausible for dispositional states. You have many beliefs right now that you are not consciously entertaining; are you in a position to know each of them by introspection? Probably not — there are too many, and some are very implicit.

Wittgenstein challenged the self-intimation thesis from a different angle: knowing whether you are in pain, or really believe something, is not simply a matter of attending to an inner datum. It involves understanding concepts, applying them to one's situation, and engaging in social practices. There is no purely private inner inspection that settles whether one has a belief.

## The Authority of First-Person Avowals

A weaker claim that survives the critique of infallibility and transparency is that first-person psychological reports have a distinctive kind of *authority* — they are treated as more credible than third-person attributions in normal circumstances. When you say "I'm in pain," we don't typically demand evidence; when someone says "he's in pain," we might.

Sydney Shoemaker has analyzed this authority in terms of the absence of certain kinds of error. First-person psychological reports are not typically based on observation of evidence; they are not typically made after inference; and they are not typically accompanied by doubt about which person is in the relevant state (I don't usually wonder whether *I* am the one in pain). These features make first-person reports epistemically distinctive even if not infallible.

The authority is defeasible: in unusual circumstances — severe depression, trauma, certain neurological conditions — self-reports may be unreliable even by ordinary epistemic standards. But in normal conditions, first-person reports carry a presumptive credibility that third-person reports do not.
