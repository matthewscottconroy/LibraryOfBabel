# Self-Intimation and the Limits of Inner Access

The self-intimation thesis — if you are in a mental state, you are in a position to know you are — represents a moderate version of the Cartesian privileged access doctrine. It does not claim that we always know our mental states, nor that introspection is infallible. It claims only that our mental states are available to us: accessible, in principle, through inner awareness. This modest claim has turned out to be remarkably difficult to defend.

## The Classical Statement

In Cartesian and early modern philosophy, self-intimation was treated as an essential feature of the mental. What distinguishes mental states from physical ones, in Descartes' framework, is that mental states are inherently conscious — they present themselves to the mind. A thought or feeling that I have but cannot be aware of seems, on this view, to be a contradiction in terms: the having of a thought just is the being aware of a thought.

This ties consciousness to the very nature of mentality: every mental state is a conscious state, and conscious states are by definition available to introspection. Mental states that are not accessible to introspection would be, on this view, not genuinely mental.

## The Freudian Challenge

Sigmund Freud's psychoanalytic theory directly challenged self-intimation by positing unconscious mental states — desires, fears, motivations, and beliefs that influence behavior but are not available to ordinary introspective access. These states are "repressed" — blocked from conscious awareness by psychological mechanisms designed to protect the ego from uncomfortable truths.

Freud's evidence included slips of the tongue (Freudian slips), dream content, neurotic symptoms, and transference phenomena — all of which could be explained by positing unconscious states with specific contents that the subject denies or cannot access.

The philosophical implications: if Freud is right, self-intimation is false. You can have mental states — beliefs, desires, motivations — that you are not in a position to know you have. The inner is not transparent to itself.

Many philosophers were resistant: Freud's "unconscious" might be better understood as involving information processing that affects behavior without involving genuinely mental (in the full, conscious sense) states. The battle between eliminativism about the unconscious and acceptance of unconscious mentality remains active.

## Cognitive Science and Implicit Processes

Contemporary cognitive science provides extensive evidence against self-intimation without any need for Freudian theory. The cognitive processes underlying perception, memory retrieval, language production, motor control, and much social cognition operate entirely below the threshold of conscious awareness. You have no introspective access to the computations your visual system performs when you recognize a face, the processes that enable you to speak grammatically, or the mechanisms that underlie your implicit attitudes toward different social groups.

Nisbett and Wilson's influential 1977 paper "Telling More Than We Can Know" documented that when people explain their own cognitive processes, they often give accounts that are demonstrably wrong — confabulated explanations that satisfy the culturally expected form of a reason-giving narrative without tracking the actual processes.

This is not self-intimation but its failure: we are in mental states — states that influence our behavior — that we are not in a position to know we are in.

## The Revised Picture: Privileged Access to Some States

The most defensible contemporary position acknowledges that self-intimation fails as a general thesis while preserving something of the Cartesian insight. There are specific mental states — occurrent phenomenal experiences, certain occurrent thoughts — that are accessible to introspection in a fairly direct way. When you are in pain now, you are in a position to know you are in pain. When you are now consciously thinking about Paris, you are in a position to know this.

But these conscious occurrences are the tip of the iceberg. Dispositional beliefs, background desires, implicit attitudes, unconscious motivations — the vast bulk of what might be called "mental states" — are not self-intimating.

## The Role of Concepts

Wittgenstein's later philosophy adds a different kind of challenge to self-intimation. Knowing whether you are in a particular mental state — believing something, intending to do something, being in pain — requires not just inner access but the correct application of concepts. And concepts are social, public, and learned through practice.

If you don't have the concept of "envy," you cannot know you are envious, even if you are. Self-intimation requires not just inner access but conceptual competence. And conceptual competence is not itself introspectively given — it is acquired through engagement with the public practices of a community. The "inner" is thus not fully self-sufficient: knowing one's own mind requires resources that come from outside it.
