# Shoemaker on Self-Knowledge and Immunity to Error

Sydney Shoemaker's analysis of self-knowledge is one of the most technically precise and philosophically influential in contemporary philosophy of mind. His key contribution is the concept of "immunity to error through misidentification" — a feature of first-person psychological reports that distinguishes them from other kinds of knowledge claims.

## Immunity to Error Through Misidentification

When you see a red chair and form the belief "that chair is red," your belief can fail in two ways: you might be wrong about the color (the chair is not red) or you might be wrong about the object (the thing you're looking at is not a chair, or is not the thing you intended to refer to). The second kind of error is an error of *identification* — you've misidentified the object of your belief.

For first-person psychological reports, Shoemaker argues, a distinctive class of errors is absent. When you say "I am in pain," "I believe it will rain," or "I seem to see a red patch," you cannot make an error of identification: you cannot be wrong about *whose* pain, belief, or experience you are reporting. There is no room to misidentify yourself. This is what Shoemaker means by immunity to error through misidentification.

The immunity is a logical/grammatical feature of first-person reports, not a claim about infallibility. You might be wrong about whether you are in pain (perhaps you are confused about the sensation), but you cannot be wrong that *you* are the one having the sensation — the question of whose sensation it is doesn't arise.

This distinguishes first-person self-knowledge from third-person knowledge about others. When you report on another person's mental states, you can make identification errors. But self-reference through "I" carries a guarantee of proper identification.

## The Source of Immune Self-Reference

Shoemaker argues that the immunity derives from the mechanism of self-reference itself. First-person self-reference does not involve identifying oneself by any criterion — by recognizing one's body, by perceiving one's own face, by matching a description. It is *criterion-free* identification.

Compare "the person who sees P" with "I see P." The first identifies a person by a criterion (being the seer of P) and can be mistaken about whether I satisfy that criterion. "I see P" is criterion-free: the reference to the self does not involve any potentially misapplicable criterion. This is why misidentification errors are impossible for the first-person case.

Elizabeth Anscombe developed a related point: "I" does not refer by describing or picking out a person. It refers without description. This makes first-person reference distinctive and explains why certain kinds of errors — specifically, errors of identification — are impossible.

## The Limits of Immunity

The immunity to error through misidentification is limited in scope. It is immune to *identification* errors but not to *predication* errors: you can still be wrong about what state you are in. "I am in pain" cannot be wrong about who is reporting, but it can be wrong about whether the sensation is pain rather than, say, severe discomfort or pressure.

More importantly, immunity to misidentification does not establish infallibility or privileged access in the strong Cartesian sense. It shows that first-person reports have a distinctive logical structure — they can't fail in a certain way — without showing that they can't fail in other ways.

## Shoemaker's Functionalism and Self-Knowledge

Shoemaker's account of self-knowledge is connected to his functionalist theory of mind. Mental states are functional states — states defined by their causal roles. Self-knowledge is knowledge of one's own functional states. First-person authority derives from the distinctive way we access our own functional states: not by observing their behavioral effects (as a third party would) but through whatever mechanism constitutes having them.

This functionalist account of self-knowledge avoids the Cartesian picture of a "inner eye" that inspects mental objects. Instead, self-knowledge involves the cognitive system's access to its own states — a functional relation within the cognitive architecture.

## The Social Constitution of Self-Knowledge

Shoemaker's later work acknowledges a tension between his formal analysis of self-knowledge (centered on immunity to error) and the social, conceptual preconditions of self-knowledge. Knowing whether you believe something, intend something, or value something requires applying concepts that are socially constituted. The immunity to error through misidentification holds at a level of abstraction that does not engage the full complexity of self-knowledge — the ways in which knowing one's own mind requires understanding the social and conceptual norms that define the mental states one is trying to know.
