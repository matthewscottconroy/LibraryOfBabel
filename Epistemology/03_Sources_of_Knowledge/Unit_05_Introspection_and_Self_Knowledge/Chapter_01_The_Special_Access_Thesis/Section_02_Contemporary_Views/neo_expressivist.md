# Neo-Expressivism and the Grammar of Self-Knowledge

Neo-expressivism is a view about the nature of first-person psychological reports that takes its inspiration from Wittgenstein's later philosophy. Rather than treating "I am in pain" as a description of an inner state — a claim that can be true or false — neo-expressivism treats it as a kind of expression: an avowed manifestation of a psychological condition that functions differently from a report about the external world.

## Wittgenstein's Starting Point

Wittgenstein noticed that there is something grammatically odd about applying the words "know" and "doubt" to our own mental states in the way we apply them to facts about the external world. You can know that the room is cold; you can doubt whether the room is cold. But "I know I am in pain" seems either trivially true (of course you know you're in pain if you are in pain) or confused (as if you might not know whether you're in pain, which only makes sense in very odd circumstances).

Wittgenstein's analysis: "I am in pain" is not used as a report or description of an observed state. It is a replacement for a primitive behavior — the cry of pain — that has been refined by language-learning into a verbal expression. It expresses the pain rather than describing it.

This is the basis of *expressivism* about first-person psychological reports: such reports express psychological states rather than describing them, and this grammatical difference explains why categories like knowledge, certainty, doubt, and error apply differently in the first-person case.

## Contemporary Development: Dorit Bar-On and Neo-Expressivism

Dorit Bar-On's *Speaking My Mind* (2004) develops a sophisticated neo-expressivist account. Bar-On's view is carefully distinguished from Ryle-style behaviorism (which would analyze mental state reports in terms of behavioral dispositions) and from anti-realism about mental states (which would deny that mental states are real).

Bar-On holds that psychological reports express mental states rather than describing them, but that mental states themselves are genuine — they have real psychological profiles, causal powers, and phenomenal characters. "I am in pain" expresses pain; pain is nonetheless real. The expression relation is one of *semantic expression*: the report is directly tied to the psychological state in a way that bypasses the description-making machinery.

The key consequence: because first-person reports express rather than describe, they have a distinctive immunity to certain kinds of epistemic evaluation. When you express pain by saying "I'm in pain," you are not making a claim that needs justification; the expression is authoritative in a grammatical sense — it is an expression of the state, not a representation of it that might fail to represent accurately.

## Avoiding the "Inner Observer" Problem

One attraction of neo-expressivism is that it avoids the problematic "inner observer" picture of introspection. On the Cartesian picture, introspection involves an inner eye that observes inner objects (pain, beliefs, desires) and reports on their presence. This picture generates the "how do we know?" problem: how does the inner observer verify that what it observes is accurate?

Neo-expressivism dissolves this problem by rejecting the observer model. Self-knowledge is not the result of observation of inner objects; it is the exercise of a distinctive expressive capacity tied constitutively to the psychological states expressed. There is no gap between expressing one's pain and knowing one is in pain; the expression just is the first-person manifestation of the knowledge.

## The Reliability Question

Critics press: even if neo-expressivism correctly characterizes the grammar of psychological reports, it still needs to explain their reliability. Why are first-person reports about one's own mental states generally accurate? What explains the connection between the expressed state and the expressed content?

Bar-On's response: the reliability is constitutive of the expressive capacity. The capacity to express one's psychological states linguistically is developed through processes of social learning and correction that produce a reliable connection between states and reports. The reliability is not a mysterious extra; it is built into what it is to have the first-person expressive capacity.

This connects expressivism to social constitution: the reliable first-person voice is a socially conferred competence, not a natural faculty of inner observation. Self-knowledge is partly constituted by one's participation in social practices that calibrate one's expressive capacity.

## Implications for Epistemology

Neo-expressivism has implications for the role of introspection in epistemology. If first-person reports are expressions rather than descriptions, they are not straightforwardly candidates for knowledge in the standard propositional sense. "I am in pain" is not a proposition about which I need justification; it expresses pain directly.

This might seem to undermine the epistemic significance of self-knowledge, but neo-expressivists argue the opposite: it secures a kind of authority for first-person reports that descriptivist accounts cannot provide, by grounding that authority in the constitutive connection between expression and expressed state.
