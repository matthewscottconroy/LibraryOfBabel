## Primary Sources

- René Descartes, *Meditations on First Philosophy*, trans. John Cottingham, Cambridge University Press, 1996 (orig. 1641). The foundational text for Cartesian privileged access: the cogito and subsequent meditations articulate the view that the mind has infallible and immediate access to its own current states.
- Sydney Shoemaker, "Self-Reference and Self-Awareness," *Journal of Philosophy* 65, 1968. Introduces the concept of immunity to error through misidentification (IEM) — the feature of first-person psychological reports that makes a certain class of error impossible.
- Gareth Evans, *The Varieties of Reference*, Oxford University Press, 1982. Develops the concept of immunity to error through misidentification with respect to the first-person pronoun and examines how self-knowledge relates to self-identification.
- Richard Moran, *Authority and Estrangement*, Princeton University Press, 2001. Examines the distinctive authority of first-person avowals, arguing that self-knowledge in the practical, deliberative sense is irreducible to third-person psychological explanation.
- Paul Boghossian, "Content and Self-Knowledge," *Philosophical Topics* 17, 1989. Examines the relationship between the content of mental states and the subject's privileged access to those contents, a central text in the debate over externalism and self-knowledge.
- Crispin Wright, "Wittgenstein's Rule-Following Considerations and the Central Project of Linguistics," in A. George (ed.), *Reflections on Chomsky*, Blackwell, 1989. Develops the concept of "cognitive command" as a condition for genuine knowledge, relevant to assessing the status of self-knowledge.
- Brie Gertler (ed.), *Privileged Access: Philosophical Accounts of Self-Knowledge*, Ashgate, 2003. Essential anthology collecting the key papers in the debate over privileged access, IEM, and the authority of first-person avowals.
- Alvin Goldman, "Interpretation Psychologized," *Mind and Language* 4, 1989. Defends an "inner sense" account of introspection on which we have quasi-perceptual access to our own mental states.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Brie Gertler, *Self-Knowledge*, Routledge, 2011. Comprehensive philosophical introduction to self-knowledge, covering privileged access, IEM, infallibility, and the impact of externalism.
- Tyler Burge, "Our Entitlement to Self-Knowledge," *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society* 96, 1996. Argues that we have a priori entitlement to take our self-ascriptions of mental content at face value, compatible with the externalist account of content.
- Christopher Peacocke, *Being Known*, Oxford University Press, 1999. Systematic epistemological treatment that includes a rationalist account of self-knowledge derived from the theory of concept possession.
- Joseph Levine, "On Leaving Out What It's Like," in M. Davies and G. Humphreys (eds.), *Consciousness*, Blackwell, 1993. Examines the relationship between privileged access to phenomenal states and the explanatory gap problem.
- Declan Smithies and Daniel Stoljar (eds.), *Introspection and Consciousness*, Oxford University Press, 2012. Comprehensive collection on introspection, examining its reliability, methods, and epistemic significance.
- Jonathan Shear (ed.), *Explaining Consciousness: The Hard Problem*, MIT Press, 1997. Contains papers relevant to the relationship between privileged access and the phenomenology of consciousness.
- Eric Schwitzgebel, *Perplexities of Consciousness*, MIT Press, 2011. Detailed and witty survey of the empirical evidence for the unreliability of introspection, challenging the special access thesis from within a philosophical framework.
- John Perry, *The Problem of the Essential Indexical and Other Essays*, CSLI Publications, 1993. Examines the distinctive logical character of first-person thought and the connection between the first-person mode of presentation and privileged access.
