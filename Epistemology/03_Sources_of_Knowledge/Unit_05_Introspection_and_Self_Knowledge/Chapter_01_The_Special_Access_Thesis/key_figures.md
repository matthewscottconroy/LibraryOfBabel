## René Descartes (1596–1650)

Descartes is the original advocate of the strongest form of privileged access — the view that the mind has not merely better but infallible access to its own current states. The cogito ("I think, therefore I am") established that the existence of one's own thinking cannot be doubted even by the most radical skeptic, and the subsequent meditations develop the picture of mental states as self-presenting: to have a thought, sensation, or doubt is automatically to know that one is thinking, sensing, or doubting. Descartes's transparency thesis — the view that nothing is in the mind of which the mind is not aware — became the target of every subsequent challenge to privileged access, from Leibniz's unconscious perceptions to Freud's unconscious to contemporary cognitive science.

## Sydney Shoemaker (born 1931)

Shoemaker introduced one of the most technically important concepts in the self-knowledge literature: immunity to error through misidentification (IEM). In "Self-Reference and Self-Awareness" (1968), he observed that certain first-person psychological reports — "I am in pain," "I see a bright light" — cannot be false in the way that "He is in pain" can be: there is no coherent error of thinking that someone is in pain and then wondering whether it is me. The error of misidentification — being right about the pain but wrong about whose pain it is — is unavailable for such reports. Shoemaker argued that IEM reflects the special logical character of first-person psychological ascription and the distinctively non-observational nature of self-knowledge.

## Gareth Evans (1946–1980)

Evans's *The Varieties of Reference* (1982) extended and refined Shoemaker's notion of IEM, distinguishing cases in which the first-person pronoun is immune to error through misidentification from cases in which it is not. He argued that identification-free use of "I" — as in "I am in pain" — is immune in a way that uses of "I" grounded in perceptual identification of one's body are not. Evans's work placed IEM within a systematic theory of reference, showing that the first person's special relationship to self-knowledge has deep logical roots rather than being a simple empirical fact about human psychology.

## Richard Moran (born 1958)

Moran's *Authority and Estrangement* (2001) provides the most philosophically sophisticated account of the authority of first-person avowals. He argues that self-knowledge in the practical, deliberative sense — knowing what you believe, intend, and value — is not a matter of turning an inner perceptual faculty on your own mental states but is constitutively connected to the exercise of rational agency. When I say "I believe p," I am not reporting an independently existing mental state but acknowledging my commitment to p in a way that has normative implications for my future thought and action. This "agentialist" account of self-knowledge preserves the authority of first-person reports without appealing to a dubious faculty of inner sense.

## Alvin Goldman (born 1938)

Goldman's "inner sense" account of introspection, developed in "Interpretation Psychologized" (1989) and related work, treats introspection as a quasi-perceptual process in which a higher-order monitoring mechanism scans one's current mental states and produces representations of them. This account preserves the special access thesis — introspection is more direct and reliable than third-person access to the same states — while naturalizing it by assimilating it to perception. Goldman's view has been challenged by critics who argue that inner sense models face a dilemma: either the monitoring mechanism can err (in which case introspection is not infallible), or it is stipulated to be reliable (in which case the account is viciously circular).

## Crispin Wright (born 1942)

Wright has developed an influential account of the authority of first-person avowals that is neither infallibilist nor deflationary. He argues that first-person avowals of current mental states have "groundless authority" — we are entitled to make them without needing to cite evidence or reasons, and they have a default presumption in their favor that third-person reports lack. This is not because the first-person perspective is necessarily accurate but because avowal is a norm-governed linguistic act whose correct performance carries a presumption of truth. Wright's account connects the philosophy of self-knowledge to the broader Wittgensteinian tradition's emphasis on rule-following and the social context of language use.
