## The Cogito and the Limits of Doubt

**The scenario:** Descartes employs the method of radical doubt: he resolves to withhold assent from any belief that could possibly be false, including beliefs based on perception, memory, and reasoning. He imagines that an omnipotent evil demon is systematically deceiving him about everything. Yet the act of doubting itself cannot be doubted: to doubt is to think, and to think is, at least in the act of thinking, to exist.

**The philosophical point:** The cogito is the founding thought experiment for privileged access. It establishes that at least one type of first-person knowledge — the knowledge that I am currently thinking — is invulnerable to the skeptic's best challenge. From this Descartes generalizes: if I can know my own thinking with certainty, then the mind has infallible access to its own current states. Critics have challenged whether the cogito establishes this broad conclusion: it may show only that one cannot sincerely doubt one's own doubting, not that the mind is generally transparent to itself. The thought experiment thus marks both the strongest case for privileged access and the limits of arguments from self-evidence.

## Shoemaker's Body-Swapping and IEM

**The scenario:** Imagine a science fiction scenario in which your brain is transplanted into another person's body. The resulting person has your memories and psychological states but another person's body. When this person says "I am in pain," can they be wrong about whose pain it is — thinking "Someone is in pain" and then mistaking whose pain they are tracking? Shoemaker argues no.

**The philosophical point:** Shoemaker's argument is that first-person ascriptions of current mental states are immune to the error of misidentification: one cannot be right that there is a pain and wrong that it is one's own. This contrasts with proprioceptive reports ("My legs are crossed") which, in principle, could involve misidentification if one were perceiving another's legs in a sufficiently unusual situation. The point extends to all current psychological reports: "I am thinking about Vienna," "I feel anxious." In each case, the first-person index cannot be the source of the error, because these reports are not grounded in identification of the subject as the possessor of the state. IEM is a structural feature of first-person psychological language, not a contingent fact about accuracy.

## The Pain in Darkness

**The scenario:** You are in complete darkness, with no proprioceptive or sensory stimulation except an intense pain. You know you are in pain. But you have no information about where the pain is located in your body, what caused it, or what physiological condition it corresponds to. Is your knowledge of your pain in any sense privileged over a doctor's knowledge of the same pain?

**The philosophical point:** This case isolates the core of the special access thesis by separating what is known with certainty (that there is this pain, and that I have it) from what is known with uncertainty (its location, cause, and physiological basis). The Cartesian view says that your first-person access to the pain itself — the qualitative character of what you are experiencing — is privileged and certain in a way that no third-person knowledge can be. Critics argue that this privileged access covers only a very thin and philosophically uninteresting slice of the mental: the bare fact that there is a pain, not its nature, significance, or causes. This thin knowledge is purchased at the price of making introspective self-knowledge almost entirely disconnected from the richer forms of psychological understanding we care about.

## The Transparency of Belief

**The scenario:** Gareth Evans argues that when a subject is asked "Do you believe that p?" the correct introspective procedure is not to look inward at one's mental states but to look outward at the world: to ask whether p is true. If it seems to me that p, then I thereby know that I believe p — my epistemic access to my belief about p goes via my access to p itself.

**The philosophical point:** The transparency of belief — the idea that to know what I believe about the world, I look at the world rather than at my inner states — is a striking thesis about the nature of doxastic self-knowledge. It implies that the "inner sense" model of introspection is misleading for beliefs: I do not scan my mental states and find a belief-state with content p; I assess whether p and thereby form the self-ascription. This model has important consequences for privileged access: it is not infallible (I can be wrong about whether p and hence wrong about what I believe), but it does explain the distinctive character of first-person access to beliefs.

## The Acting Subject and Self-Deception

**The scenario:** A man, Anthony, is convinced that he does not feel any jealousy toward his colleague's success. He provides extensive evidence for this belief — he has helped his colleague, wished him well publicly, and experienced no obvious emotional disturbance. Yet his behavior exhibits classic signs of jealousy: he systematically undervalues his colleague's work, avoids situations in which his colleague is praised, and feels a distinctive unease at his colleague's name.

**The philosophical point:** Self-deception cases challenge the infallibility and self-intimation theses: Anthony's jealousy exists even though he does not know it, and his introspective reports about his own emotional states are systematically false. The case raises a question for every account of privileged access: how far does it extend? Cartesian accounts of infallibility are typically confined to occurrent states — it is more plausible to claim infallible access to a current occurrent pain than to a standing disposition to jealousy. Moran's agentialist account has resources to explain self-deception: Anthony is refusing to acknowledge, in the practical-deliberative sense, his jealousy, and this refusal is itself an act of agency rather than an epistemic failure.
