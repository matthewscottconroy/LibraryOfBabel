# Externalism and Self-Knowledge: A Surprising Conflict

Semantic externalism — the view that the contents of our mental states are partly determined by factors outside the mind, including the physical environment and the social linguistic community — creates a surprising challenge to privileged access. If what you mean and what you think depends partly on external factors you may not know about, then self-knowledge cannot be purely a matter of "looking inward." The conflict between externalism and first-person authority is one of the central problems in contemporary philosophy of mind and epistemology.

## The Setup: Externalism about Mental Content

The externalist revolution in philosophy of mind originated with Hilary Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment (1975) and Tyler Burge's work on linguistic community and content (1979). The key claims:

**Putnam's Twin Earth**: Imagine a planet exactly like Earth, except that what they call "water" is not H₂O but some other liquid (XYZ) with identical surface properties. Your twin on Twin Earth is physically and mentally identical to you in every internal respect. Yet when your twin thinks "water is wet," they are thinking about XYZ, not H₂O. And when you think "water is wet," you are thinking about H₂O. Same internal state; different content. Content depends on what is actually in your environment, not just what is in your head.

**Burge's arthritis case**: Suppose you have always believed "I have arthritis in my thigh" but it turns out that arthritis is by definition a joint disease, so you cannot have it in your thigh. Your concept of arthritis is not quite the community's concept — you would be thinking about what the community calls arthritis. In a counterfactual society where "arthritis" is used to cover the disease you have, you would have different beliefs with the same functional profile.

The upshot of externalism: mental content is not purely "in the head." It depends on environmental and social factors that the subject may not know about.

## The Tension with Privileged Access

Privileged access holds that we have special, direct access to our own mental states. Externalism implies that our mental states have contents that are partly constituted by factors outside our minds. The tension: if you don't know what's in your environment or what your linguistic community means by its words, you don't know the content of your own mental states — even if you have "direct" introspective access to them.

This is not a merely abstract worry. Consider a slow-switching thought experiment (to be discussed in the next section): you are slowly and imperceptibly transported from Earth to Twin Earth, and your mental contents gradually shift from Earth-water thoughts to Twin-Earth-water thoughts. During this transition, do you have privileged access to whether you are thinking about water or twater? Apparently not: the shift happens without any introspective signal.

## Burge's Response: Different Levels of Knowledge

Burge himself does not see externalism as undermining privileged access. He argues that privileged access applies to *cogito-like* self-attributions: "I am thinking that p" is known with authority when it involves the same conceptual capacities as thinking that p. The self-attribution is authoritative because it is guaranteed by the same conditions that make the original thought correct.

On this view, the question "what is my mental content?" and the question "am I thinking about water or twater?" are different. The former is authoritative because self-attribution of thoughts is constitutively connected to the thoughts. The latter is a question about the environmental facts that help constitute the content, which is not directly accessible to privileged access.

## Armchair Knowledge of Content

The most striking implication of externalism for self-knowledge concerns what can be known "from the armchair." Descartes thought that knowledge of one's own mental states was available purely through reflection, without any empirical investigation of the world. Externalism implies this is false: knowing the content of your own beliefs requires knowing empirical facts about your environment and community.

If your concept of gold is the element with atomic number 79 (because that is what your community means by "gold"), then knowing that you are thinking about gold requires knowing that your community uses "gold" for the element with atomic number 79. This empirical fact is not available from the armchair.

The philosophical upshot: self-knowledge is not a purely a priori matter. It has an empirical dimension that requires investigation of the world outside one's head.
