# Slow Switching and the Limits of Introspective Self-Knowledge

The slow-switching thought experiment is designed to press the tension between semantic externalism and privileged access to its sharpest point. It illustrates a case where, by externalist lights, a person's mental contents undergo a systematic change without any corresponding change in their inner states — and without any introspective signal to the person that a change has occurred. This challenges privileged access in a concrete and vivid way.

## The Thought Experiment

Imagine you are gradually and imperceptibly transported between Earth and Twin Earth, spending enough time on each to become "of the relevant community" — so that your thoughts about water are Earth-water thoughts when you've been on Earth long enough and Twin-Earth thoughts when you've been on Twin Earth long enough.

During this process, your physical brain states are essentially the same at any given moment, your subjective experience is continuous and uninterrupted, and you have no awareness that you have been transported or that your mental contents have shifted. Yet, according to externalism, your thoughts have changed their content: what you are thinking about when you use the word "water" shifts as you shift communities.

Now ask: do you know, from the inside, what you are currently thinking about? If you are in a transitional period — shuttled between Earth and Twin Earth rapidly enough — do you know whether your "water" thoughts are about H₂O or XYZ?

Apparently not. The content of your thoughts has changed, or is ambiguous, in ways you have no introspective access to. Slow-switching shows that privileged access does not extend to the content of thoughts in the externalist sense.

## The McKinsey Paradox

Michael McKinsey formulated a sharp version of the problem known as the McKinsey paradox. The argument:

1. If I know a priori (from the armchair) that I am thinking I-water thoughts, and
2. Externalism is true (the content of I-water thoughts depends on whether I'm in an H₂O environment or XYZ environment), then
3. I can know a priori whether I am in an H₂O environment or XYZ environment.

But (3) is clearly wrong — I cannot know from the armchair which watery liquid is in my environment. So either (1) or (2) must be false: either self-knowledge is not a priori, or externalism is false.

McKinsey used this as a *modus tollens* against externalism: since (1) seems right and (3) is absurd, externalism must be false.

## Burge's Response: Denying A Priority

Burge responds by denying (1) or by carefully restricting what kind of a priori self-knowledge is available. There is a kind of self-knowledge available a priori — reflexive self-attribution of the form "I am now thinking I-whatever-water-is-thoughts" — but this does not require knowing the environmental facts that constitute the content. The self-attribution is authoritative relative to whatever content one's thoughts have, without requiring prior knowledge of what that content is.

This is a thin form of self-knowledge: you know you are thinking a thought of a certain type, but you don't know all the details of what that type involves. The privileged access is preserved but is less informative than the Cartesian picture suggested.

## Other Responses

Several responses to the slow-switching problem have been developed:

**The compatibilist response**: Paul Boghossian and others argue that there is no real incompatibility between externalism and privileged access, properly understood. Slow-switching describes an extreme, philosophically constructed scenario; ordinary self-knowledge in ordinary circumstances is not threatened.

**The social externalism response**: Burge's social externalism (content depends on linguistic community norms) differs from Putnam's natural kind externalism (content depends on the natural kind in one's environment). The slow-switching argument may work better against the latter than the former.

**The internalist response**: Some philosophers take slow-switching as evidence against externalism itself — if externalism implies we lack privileged access to our own thoughts, and if privileged access is real, then externalism is wrong.

## Epistemological Significance

The slow-switching discussion highlights a deep tension in the epistemology of mind: between the pull of externalism in the philosophy of content (which seems necessary to account for how thoughts are about a mind-independent world) and the pull of internalism in the epistemology of mind (which seems necessary to preserve the intuitive authority of self-knowledge).

The resolution likely requires a more nuanced picture of self-knowledge — one that distinguishes different kinds of self-knowledge, preserves first-person authority in appropriate domains, and acknowledges that the content of thoughts includes externally-constituted elements that are not introspectively transparent. This is an active area of inquiry at the intersection of epistemology, philosophy of language, and philosophy of mind.
