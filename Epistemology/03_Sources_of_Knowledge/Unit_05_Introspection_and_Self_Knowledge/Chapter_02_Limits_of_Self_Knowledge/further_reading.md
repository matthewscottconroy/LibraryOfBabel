## Primary Sources

- Richard Nisbett and Timothy Wilson, "Telling More Than We Can Know: Verbal Reports on Mental Processes," *Psychological Review* 84, 1977. The landmark study showing that subjects confabulate explanations of their own mental processes, providing evidence that introspective access to the causes of behavior is unreliable.
- Hilary Putnam, "The Meaning of 'Meaning'," in *Mind, Language and Reality: Philosophical Papers*, vol. 2, Cambridge University Press, 1975. Twin Earth thought experiment arguing that mental content is determined by factors external to the individual, with profound implications for the special access thesis.
- Tyler Burge, "Individualism and the Mental," *Midwest Studies in Philosophy* 4, 1979. Extends Putnam's anti-individualism to mental states generally through the arthritis thought experiment, arguing that the content of beliefs is partly constituted by social and environmental factors.
- Tyler Burge, "Our Entitlement to Self-Knowledge," *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society* 96, 1996. Argues that privileged access and externalism are compatible, defending a priori entitlement to self-knowledge even given content externalism.
- Michael McKinsey, "Anti-Individualism and Privileged Access," *Analysis* 51, 1991. Argues that the combination of privileged access and externalism generates a problematic consequence — that we can know a priori what the external world is like — known as the McKinsey paradox.
- Paul Boghossian, "Content and Self-Knowledge," *Philosophical Topics* 17, 1989. Detailed examination of the conflict between the special access thesis and content externalism, with careful analysis of the McKinsey problem.
- Eric Schwitzgebel, *Perplexities of Consciousness*, MIT Press, 2011. Systematic empirical and philosophical case that introspection is far less reliable than the special access thesis supposes, covering color, imagery, emotion, and belief.
- Timothy Wilson, *Strangers to Ourselves: Discovering the Adaptive Unconscious*, Harvard University Press, 2002. Accessible psychological argument that much of our mental life is inaccessible to conscious introspection, with implications for the self-knowledge we take ourselves to have.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Brie Gertler, *Self-Knowledge*, Routledge, 2011. Comprehensive philosophical treatment covering externalism, the McKinsey paradox, and the empirical challenges to privileged access.
- Martin Davies and Tony Stone (eds.), *Mental Simulation: Evaluations and Applications*, Blackwell, 1995. Contains papers on the relationship between simulation, introspection, and self-knowledge.
- Anthony Brueckner, "What an Anti-Individualist Knows A Priori," *Analysis* 52, 1992. Examines what is and is not knowable a priori about mental content given externalism, engaging the McKinsey paradox.
- Peter Ludlow and Norah Martin (eds.), *Externalism and Self-Knowledge*, CSLI Publications, 1998. Major anthology collecting the key papers in the externalism and self-knowledge debate.
- Daniel Dennett, *Consciousness Explained*, Little, Brown, 1991. Provides a deflationary account of introspection and privileged access from a cognitive science perspective, treating introspective reports as theoretically laden rather than directly authoritative.
- Jonathan Haidt, "The Emotional Dog and Its Rational Tail: A Social Intuitionist Model of Moral Judgment," *Psychological Review* 108, 2001. Evidence that moral intuitions are post-hoc rationalizations, relevant to broader questions about confabulation.
- Jerry Fodor, *The Modularity of Mind*, MIT Press, 1983. Argues that perceptual processes are encapsulated and inaccessible to central cognition, with implications for the limits of introspective access.
- Maria Kasmirli, "Epistemic Self-Knowledge and Its Limits," *Philosophical Explorations* 10, 2007. Examines the specific limits of our self-knowledge of epistemic states such as belief and justification.
- Annette Baier, *Postures of the Mind*, Methuen, 1985. Essays on self-knowledge, trust, and moral psychology that illuminate the limits of introspective self-understanding.
