## Richard Nisbett (born 1941) and Timothy Wilson (born 1951)

Nisbett and Wilson's "Telling More Than We Can Know" (1977) is the most influential empirical study challenging the reliability of introspection. They showed across a series of experiments that subjects confidently report reasons for their choices and behaviors that are in fact causally inert — they confabulate plausible-sounding explanations that cannot be verified by any access to the actual causal processes underlying their mental states. Subjects who chose between several identical stockings, for instance, strongly favored the rightmost item due to position effects while reporting reasons of quality and texture. The paper established confabulation as a central concept in the empirical study of self-knowledge and launched decades of research on the limits of introspective access.

## Hilary Putnam (1926–2016)

Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment, introduced in "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" (1975), argues that the content of mental states — what they are "about" — is partly determined by factors external to the individual. If my Twin Earth counterpart and I are atom-for-atom identical in our brains but live on planets with different substances playing the water role, then our beliefs about "water" have different contents even though our internal states are identical. This result — that "meanings ain't in the head" — directly challenges the Cartesian picture of privileged access: if I do not have complete internal access to what fixes the content of my thoughts, then I do not have complete self-knowledge through introspection alone.

## Tyler Burge (born 1946)

Burge's "Individualism and the Mental" (1979) extended Putnam's externalism from natural kind terms to mental states generally through the arthritis thought experiment. Burge argued that a person who misapplies the concept "arthritis" — believing incorrectly that arthritis can affect muscles — is still thinking about arthritis (the disease), because the concept's content is fixed by the social-linguistic community rather than by the individual's dispositions alone. Burge has also argued, against McKinsey, that externalism about content is compatible with privileged access: we have a priori entitlement to take our self-ascriptions of mental content at face value, even though what content we possess is partly fixed by the environment. This position requires careful articulation of what "privileged access" means given externalism.

## Michael McKinsey (born 1944)

McKinsey's 1991 paper "Anti-Individualism and Privileged Access" identified what has become known as the McKinsey paradox: if I have privileged a priori access to the contents of my mental states, and if those contents depend partly on facts about the external world, then I can know a priori — by mere reflection — what the external world is like. This consequence, McKinsey argued, is absurd, and so we must reject either privileged access or content externalism. The paper generated an enormous literature, with Burge, Boghossian, and others arguing that the inference is invalid, and with others exploring what modifications to the special access thesis are needed to block the paradox.

## Eric Schwitzgebel (born 1968)

Schwitzgebel's *Perplexities of Consciousness* (2011) and a series of related papers provide the most systematic empirical and philosophical case for the unreliability of introspection. He examines a range of phenomenal properties — color experience, the visual field's apparent determinacy, the presence or absence of imagery in thought, the phenomenology of emotion — and argues that our introspective reports about each are highly unreliable in ways we do not recognize. Schwitzgebel's work challenges not only the strong Cartesian claim of infallible access but also more modest claims of systematic reliability, suggesting that the epistemological significance of introspection has been deeply overestimated.

## Paul Boghossian (born 1957)

Boghossian's "Content and Self-Knowledge" (1989) examines in careful detail the conflict between the externalist view of mental content and the traditional special access thesis. He identifies several distinct components of the privileged access thesis — infallibility, transparency, special authority — and examines which, if any, are compatible with content externalism. Boghossian's analysis of "slow switching" — the scenario in which a subject gradually moves between environments and whose mental content shifts without their awareness — is particularly important: it seems that the subject would be wrong about their own mental content during the transition, suggesting a direct conflict between externalism and the transparency of self-knowledge that even Burge's response has difficulty fully resolving.
