## Putnam's Twin Earth

**The scenario:** In a distant solar system exists Twin Earth, a planet molecule-for-molecule identical to Earth except that the substance called "water" by Twin Earthers is not H₂O but a compound with the chemical formula XYZ, which happens to be superficially indistinguishable from water in all ordinary circumstances. In 1750, before chemistry had identified the structure of water, Oscar on Earth and Twin Oscar on Twin Earth were in identical mental states — same neural configurations, same dispositional profiles. When Oscar thinks "water is wet," he thinks about H₂O; when Twin Oscar thinks "water is wet," he thinks about XYZ.

**The philosophical point:** Putnam's case establishes that the content of thoughts is not fully determined by what is inside the head: two individuals who are neurologically and psychologically identical can have thoughts with different contents. This directly challenges privileged access in its strongest form: if I cannot determine the content of my own thoughts purely through introspection (because content depends on what is in my environment), then introspection does not give me complete self-knowledge. The subsequent literature has explored whether a weakened form of privileged access — access to "narrow content" or to mode of presentation rather than referent — can be preserved.

## Burge's Arthritis Thought Experiment

**The scenario:** A patient, Arthur, believes he has arthritis in his thigh. His doctor explains that arthritis is a disease only of the joints, and the thigh condition is a different disease. Now consider a counterfactual community in which the term "arthritis" was introduced by physicians who were confused about anatomy and used it to cover both joint inflammation and certain thigh conditions. In that community, Arthur's counterpart would be using "arthritis" to refer to a different disease. Arthur and his counterpart have the same neural and behavioral profile, but different mental content.

**The philosophical point:** Burge extends Putnam's externalism from natural kind terms to ordinary concepts whose content is partly fixed by the expert-governed social norms of the community. Arthur's belief about arthritis has the content it does because of facts about his social-linguistic environment, not purely because of facts about his inner states. This is the "slow switching" scenario applied to conceptual content: if Arthur moved to the counterfactual community and gradually adopted their usage, his concept of arthritis would shift, even if his inner states remained continuous. The implications for self-knowledge are significant: Arthur cannot know purely by introspection what disease he is thinking about.

## The McKinsey Paradox

**The scenario:** Suppose I know a priori (through privileged access) that I am now thinking about water. If externalism is true, then the fact that I am thinking about water — rather than about XYZ — entails that water (H₂O) exists in my environment. But if I can know a priori that I am thinking about water, and this fact entails that H₂O exists, then it seems I can know a priori that H₂O exists in my environment — without any empirical investigation.

**The philosophical point:** McKinsey's paradox is the sharpest version of the conflict between privileged access and content externalism. The absurd conclusion — that mere introspection can deliver knowledge of the external world's composition — seems to show that the two theses cannot both be true as standardly formulated. Burge and others have argued that the inference is invalid: the privileged access we have to our mental states is access to our current thoughts under a certain mode of presentation, and this does not generate the kind of a priori knowledge of environmental facts that McKinsey's argument requires. The debate turns on the precise formulation of both theses.

## Nisbett and Wilson's Stocking Experiment

**The scenario:** Subjects were presented with four identical stockings displayed in a row and asked to choose the best quality pair. They systematically preferred the rightmost pair (due to a position effect well-established in consumer psychology) while sincerely reporting reasons for their choice that cited texture, weave, and sheen rather than position. When experimenters told them that position might have been a factor, they typically rejected the suggestion.

**The philosophical point:** This experiment is a paradigm case of confabulation: the subjects produced confident, detailed introspective reports about the causes of their behavior that were systematically false. They did not merely fail to know why they chose; they actively misrepresented their own cognitive processes, and did so without awareness of error. The philosophical significance extends well beyond consumer behavior: if subjects confabulate in this relatively simple case, there is reason to worry that the same mechanisms produce confabulated explanations in more complex domains — including the rationalizations we offer for our moral judgments, aesthetic preferences, and belief-formation processes. Introspection, these data suggest, is often theory-generation rather than direct access.

## The Slow Switching Case

**The scenario:** Alice lives on Earth, where she has developed normal beliefs about water (H₂O). She is then transported, while asleep and without any awareness of the change, to Twin Earth, where the substance called "water" is XYZ. For years she continues to drink "water," interact with the environment, and form beliefs about "water" — but she is now interacting with XYZ. Her mental content gradually shifts (assuming externalism): she is now thinking about XYZ when she uses the word "water." But she has no awareness of any change in her mental states.

**The philosophical point:** The slow switching case, developed by Boghossian among others, is the clearest illustration of the tension between externalism and the transparency of self-knowledge. Alice's concept has changed in content without her awareness — she cannot tell by introspection that anything is different. If she introspects and asks herself "What am I thinking about when I think about water?", she will get the same answer before and after the switch, even though the correct externalist answer is different. This suggests that introspection gives us access to the phenomenological character or functional role of our thoughts but not to their externally determined content — a significant limitation on the scope of self-knowledge.
