# Essay Questions: Unit 3 — Sources of Knowledge

---

**1. Perception, Disjunctivism, and the Basis of Perceptual Knowledge**

Naïve realism holds that in veridical perception, the perceiver is in direct cognitive contact with mind-independent facts. Representationalism holds that experience has representational content that mediates the perceivers' access to the world. Evaluate disjunctivism — the view that veridical and non-veridical experiences are fundamentally different kinds of states — as a response to the argument from hallucination. Does disjunctivism provide a more epistemologically adequate account of perceptual knowledge than representationalism? What is the most pressing objection to each view?

---

**2. Is Cognitive Penetrability Epistemically Damaging?**

Susanna Siegel argues in *The Rationality of Perception* (2017) that perceptual experiences can be epistemically rational or irrational: an experience produced by prior biased beliefs is itself epistemically compromised and cannot ground fully legitimate perceptual justification. Critically assess this claim. What evidence supports the cognitive penetrability of perception? If Siegel is right, what are the implications for foundationalist accounts of perceptual justification? Is the notion of a "cognitively infiltrated" experience coherent?

---

**3. Testimony: Reductionism versus Anti-Reductionism**

Hume's reductionism holds that testimonial justification reduces to perception- and memory-based evidence for the testifier's reliability. Reid and Coady's anti-reductionism holds that testimony is a basic epistemic source with its own prima facie justificatory force. Critically evaluate each position. Does Coady's argument that reductionism is empirically incoherent succeed? Is Elizabeth Fricker's "local reductionism" — monitoring for defeaters without requiring global prior evidence — a stable middle position, or does it collapse into one of the extreme positions?

---

**4. The Epistemology of A Priori Knowledge**

Is there substantive a priori knowledge — knowledge of truths that is not grounded in sensory experience? Assess the main accounts of how a priori knowledge is possible: rationalism (a faculty of rational intuition), conceptualism (a priori truths are grounded in our mastery of concepts), and reliabilism about rational intuition (a priori faculties are reliable even if not infallible). Which account best meets Quine's holistic challenge? What are the prospects for a moderate empiricism that accepts a priori knowledge without endorsing a sharp analytic/synthetic distinction?

---

**5. Memory as an Epistemic Source**

Memory presents a distinctive epistemological challenge: it purports to justify current beliefs on the basis of past experience. Distinguish the preservative view (memory merely preserves justification originally generated by perception) from the generative view (memory produces new justification). Which view is more defensible? How does the empirical evidence of constructive memory and false memory bear on the epistemological evaluation of memory as a source of justification? Does the reliabilist account of memory (memory processes are usually reliable) adequately address these challenges?

---

**6. Introspection: Privileged Access or Epistemic Vulnerability?**

The Cartesian doctrine of privileged access holds that introspection provides especially secure knowledge of one's own mental states. Eric Schwitzgebel argues, on the basis of psychological evidence and philosophical analysis, that introspection is deeply unreliable. Tyler Burge argues that even if the content of one's mental states is externally determined (anti-individualism), self-knowledge is still possible. Critically assess the debate. Does anti-individualism threaten privileged access? Is there a coherent notion of privileged access that survives both Schwitzgebel's empirical challenge and Burge's philosophical challenge?

---

**7. The Limits of Individual Epistemic Sources**

Contemporary epistemology typically addresses the sources of knowledge one by one: perception, memory, introspection, testimony, a priori reasoning. Assess whether this atomistic approach is adequate. Do epistemic sources interact in ways that cannot be captured by treating each in isolation? For example, does the evidential force of perception depend on memory, does the reliability of testimony depend on perception, and does a priori reasoning depend on conceptual capacities that are themselves shaped by testimony? Defend a view about the appropriate methodology for the epistemology of sources.
