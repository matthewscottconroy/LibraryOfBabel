# Unit 4: Justification and Warrant — Unit Survey

## Central Questions

Justification is the condition in the JTB analysis that does the most philosophical work and that has generated the largest literature in analytic epistemology. If knowledge requires not just true belief but belief held for good reasons, the theory of justification is the theory of what makes reasons good. But there is immediate pressure: the word "justification" is used in at least two ways that pull apart — deontological and evaluative — and the internalism/externalism divide cuts through both.

The central questions are: Is justification fundamentally deontological — a matter of whether the agent has fulfilled epistemic obligations, met standards they are cognitively in a position to recognize? Or is it fundamentally evaluative — a matter of whether the belief has positive epistemic properties, regardless of the agent's access to those properties? If the former, what are the epistemic obligations, and how are they grounded? If the latter, what are the relevant positive properties, and are they accessible to the agent? How does the regress problem for justification — the difficulty that justification of a belief seems to require justification of its support, ad infinitum — motivate foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism as structural solutions? And what is the relationship between justification and warrant (Plantinga's term for the property that distinguishes knowledge from merely true belief)?

This unit also addresses a cluster of debates that cut across the structural solutions: whether epistemic akrasia is possible (believing against one's evidence), whether higher-order evidence can defeat first-order justification, and the relationship between epistemic and practical rationality.

## Map of Major Positions and Their Logical Relations

**The Regress Problem.** If S's belief that p is justified by a further belief that q, and that belief by yet another belief, the chain of justification seems to regress infinitely. Three responses:

*Foundationalism:* some beliefs are basic — justified without requiring justifying beliefs behind them. Basic beliefs provide the foundation from which all other justified beliefs are derived. The question is what makes basic beliefs justified.

*Coherentism:* there are no basic beliefs; justification is a matter of coherence among beliefs. A belief is justified iff it coheres with the rest of the agent's belief system. The question is whether coherence among beliefs can constitute contact with evidence about the world.

*Infinitism:* (Klein) the regress is not vicious. An agent is justified iff for any challenge to p, there is a further justifying belief available. The regress need not be completed; it is sufficient that the appropriate beliefs are available.

**Internalism vs. Externalism.** The most important structural divide in the theory of justification:

*Internalism:* justification depends only on factors internal to the agent's perspective — specifically, on what the agent has cognitive access to. Two varieties:
- *Access internalism:* the justifying factors must be accessible to the agent by reflection.
- *Mentalism:* the justifying factors must supervene on the agent's mental states, whether or not those states are accessible.

*Externalism:* justification can depend on factors that are external to the agent's perspective — factors the agent has no cognitive access to. Process reliabilism (Goldman) is the canonical externalism: a belief is justified iff produced by a reliable belief-forming process, whether or not the agent knows the process is reliable.

**Deontological vs. Consequentialist Justification.** A second structural divide:

*Deontological approaches* (Chisholm, Feldman, Conee) treat epistemic justification on the model of moral obligation. A belief is justified iff the agent is not violating any epistemic duties in holding it. The agent can always ask whether they are doing their epistemic duty.

*Epistemic virtue and consequentialist approaches* treat justification as a matter of epistemic goods — accuracy, understanding, knowledge — rather than duty-fulfillment. This aligns with virtue epistemology and with consequentialist accounts of epistemic normativity.

**Foundationalism: Classical and Contemporary.** Classical foundationalism (Descartes, Chisholm) requires basic beliefs to be infallible or at least certain. Modest foundationalism (Pryor) requires only that basic beliefs be non-inferentially justified — they have prima facie justification by virtue of the kind of experience that produces them, though this justification is defeasible. Dogmatist foundationalism (Pryor) holds that merely having a visual experience as of p provides immediate, non-inferential justification for p, unless that justification is defeated.

**Coherentism.** BonJour's coherentism holds that justification is entirely a matter of the coherence of a belief with the rest of the system. The observation requirement (Lewis): coherentism needs some tie to observational input or it becomes disconnected from the world. BonJour later abandoned coherentism for foundationalism. Quine's epistemological holism is related but not identical to coherentism.

**Proper Functionalism (Plantinga).** Warrant (Plantinga's term, to distinguish from the broader notion of justification) is a matter of proper function: a belief has warrant iff it is produced by cognitive faculties that are functioning properly in an appropriate environment for which they were designed. This is externalist but not reliabilist: the relevant notion is proper function, not statistical reliability.

## Sub-Chapter Contributions

**Unit 1: Structure of Justification.** Chapter 1 presents the regress argument in detail, distinguishing linear and holistic models of justificatory structure. Chapter 2 analyses foundationalism — what makes basic beliefs justified, and why modest rather than classical foundationalism is more defensible. Chapter 3 presents coherentism and the debates about whether coherentism can account for the connection between justified belief and truth.

**Unit 2: Internalism and Externalism.** Chapter 1 formulates the internalism/externalism divide precisely, distinguishing access and mentalist forms of internalism. Chapter 2 presents process reliabilism in detail — the reliability condition, the generality problem, the new evil demon problem — and assesses its prospects. Chapter 3 develops virtue epistemology as a response to both the generality problem and the new evil demon problem.

**Unit 3: Virtue Epistemology.** Chapter 1 distinguishes intellectual virtues as stable dispositions from competences as reliable mechanisms, and maps the resulting positions (Sosa, Greco, Zagzebski). Chapter 2 examines the relationship between virtue epistemology and the analysis of knowledge — does virtue epistemology provide the fourth condition Gettier's cases demand? Chapter 3 extends virtue epistemology to intellectual character — the full range of cognitive dispositions that constitute epistemic excellence — connecting to character ethics and philosophy of education.

**Unit 4: Epistemic Rationality.** Chapter 1 addresses the relationship between epistemic and practical rationality: can practical stakes affect what one is epistemically justified in believing (pragmatic encroachment)? Chapter 2 treats higher-order evidence: what should an agent do when they have evidence that their own first-order reasoning is unreliable? Chapter 3 examines epistemic akrasia: is it possible to believe p while also believing that one's evidence supports not-p?

## Key Philosophers and Arguments

**Roderick Chisholm (1916–1999).** *Theory of Knowledge* (3rd ed., 1989) is the systematic presentation of internalist foundationalism in its most developed analytic form. Chisholm's "directly evident" beliefs — beliefs that are self-presenting, infallible, or indubitable — form the foundation. His careful taxonomy of degrees of epistemic appraisal (*certainly true, evident, beyond reasonable doubt, epistemically in the clear*) is the most precise attempt to specify the normative structure of justification.

**Laurence BonJour (1943–).** *The Structure of Empirical Knowledge* (1985) is the most systematic defence and ultimate rejection of coherentism. BonJour argued that coherentism requires an observation requirement that reintroduces foundationalist elements. His subsequent turn to foundationalism is documented in *In Defense of Pure Reason* (1998), where he defends a priori rational intuition as the foundation of justification. The trajectory from coherentism to foundationalism is philosophically instructive.

**Alvin Goldman (1938–).** "What Is Justified Belief?" (*Justification and Knowledge*, 1979) proposed process reliabilism and identified the generality problem: the reliability of a process depends on how the process type is individuated, and there is no principled way to settle the individuation. Goldman's subsequent *Epistemology and Cognition* (1986) extended reliabilism to a full epistemological theory and engaged with cognitive science.

**Alvin Plantinga (1932–).** The *Warrant* trilogy — *Warrant: The Current Debate* (1993), *Warrant and Proper Function* (1993), *Warranted Christian Belief* (2000) — distinguishes justification (a deontological notion) from warrant (the property that distinguishes knowledge from true belief). Proper functionalism holds that warrant is produced by cognitive faculties functioning properly in environments for which they were designed. The design plan may be the result of evolution, divine creation, or some combination; the theory does not presuppose theism.

**James Pryor (1972–).** "The Skeptic and the Dogmatist" (*Noûs*, 2000) defends dogmatist foundationalism: merely having a visual experience as of p gives the perceiver an immediate, non-inferential justification for p, without any prior justification for ruling out sceptical alternatives. This is "dogmatist" because it does not require the perceiver to first refute the sceptic. Pryor's paper generated an enormous literature on the relationship between perception and justification.

**Peter Klein (1940–).** "Human Knowledge and the Infinite Regress of Reasons" (*Philosophical Perspectives*, 1999) defends infinitism: the regress of justification is not vicious because what is required for justification is not the completion of an infinite chain but the availability of a further reason at each point the chain is challenged. Klein's infinitism is a minority position but has forced the debate about the structure of justification to be more precise.

**Richard Feldman and Earl Conee (1990).** "Internalism Defended" (*Philosophical Perspectives*, 2001) distinguishes access internalism from mentalism and argues that mentalism is the defensible core of internalism: justification supervenes on the mental states of the agent, which are internal in the relevant sense. Their "evidentialism" holds that justified beliefs are those appropriately proportioned to the agent's evidence.

## Essential Readings

1. **Goldman, Alvin, "What Is Justified Belief?", in *Justification and Knowledge***, ed. G. Pappas (1979), pp. 1–23. The founding document of process reliabilism. Clear, precise, and enormously influential. Must be read alongside the generality problem literature.

2. **BonJour, Laurence, *The Structure of Empirical Knowledge*, Chapters 5–8** (1985, Harvard). The most sustained defence of coherentism and its ultimate failure to avoid foundationalist commitments. Chapter 8 is particularly important for the observation requirement.

3. **Chisholm, Roderick, *Theory of Knowledge*, Chapters 1–4**, 3rd ed. (1989). The most careful internalist foundationalist account, with Chisholm's precise taxonomy of epistemic appraisal. Chapters 1–2 on the regress and basic beliefs are essential.

4. **Plantinga, Alvin, *Warrant and Proper Function*, Chapters 1–3** (1993, Oxford). The most important alternative to reliabilism. Chapters 1–2 critique justification concepts; Chapter 3 introduces proper functionalism.

5. **Pryor, James, "The Skeptic and the Dogmatist," *Noûs*, 34:4 (2000), pp. 517–549.** The most important recent defence of dogmatist foundationalism. Clear, philosophically rich, and directly engages scepticism. Read together with Unit 5.

## Open Questions and Live Debates

**The generality problem.** Process reliabilism has never given a principled solution to the problem of type individuation. The same token belief-forming event falls under many type-descriptions with different reliability ratings. Reliabilists have proposed various solutions (the broadest causally relevant type, the finest-grained type under which the process would be recognized), but none commands consensus.

**The new evil demon problem.** If reliabilism is correct, a cognitive duplicate of a reliable believer in an evil demon scenario lacks justification (because their processes are unreliable) even though they are epistemically blameless. Internalists take this to show that reliabilism cannot capture the deontological dimension of justification. Goldman responds that there are distinct concepts of justification (weak and strong).

**Higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia.** If an agent has evidence that their first-order reasoning process is unreliable, are they required to lower their credence in its conclusions? This generates a tension: following the evidence might require disregarding the evidence-following process itself. Christensen and Elga argue that higher-order evidence can defeat first-order justification. Lasonen-Aarnio argues that this generates an unacceptable "epistemic downgrade" with no rational resting point.

**Can virtue epistemology solve the Gettier problem?** Zagzebski argued it could; Greco's agent reliabilism is the most developed version. Critics argue that virtue-theoretic analyses face their own Gettier-like counterexamples (the "environmental virtue" cases, where the agent's competence is reliable but the environment generates accidental truths).

## Connections to Other Units

- **Unit 2 (Nature of Knowledge):** The analysis of justification is continuous with the post-Gettier analysis of knowledge. Process reliabilism is simultaneously a theory of justification and a theory of knowledge.
- **Unit 5 (Scepticism):** Foundationalism's basic beliefs must be secure enough to ground the entire structure of knowledge; this is precisely what the sceptic challenges. The relationship between justification and anti-sceptical strategy is direct.
- **Unit 8 (Formal Epistemology):** Bayesian epistemology provides a formal model of justified belief revision. The relationship between binary belief (justified/unjustified) and graded belief (credences) is a central issue connecting these units.
- **Unit 9 (Social Epistemology):** Social epistemology raises questions about whether justification can be transmitted through testimony or generated by social-epistemic practices such as peer discussion and collective inquiry.
