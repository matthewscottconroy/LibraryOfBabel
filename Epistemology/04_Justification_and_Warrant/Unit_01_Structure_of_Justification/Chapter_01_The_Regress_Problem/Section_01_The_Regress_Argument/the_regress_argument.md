# The Regress Argument and the Structure of Epistemic Justification

The regress argument is one of the oldest and most important arguments in epistemology. It begins with an apparently reasonable requirement — that beliefs should be based on reasons — and derives from it a trilemma that has structured the theory of justification for millennia. Understanding the argument and the responses to it is essential to understanding how epistemologists have thought about the architecture of knowledge.

## The Argument

If you want to know whether a belief B is justified, you might ask: what reasons do you have for B? Suppose you cite some other belief B1. Then B1 must itself be justified — you can't justify a belief by appealing to another that is itself unjustified. So what justifies B1? You cite B2. And B2 requires justification, so you cite B3. And so on.

The argument proceeds by noting that any chain of justification must either:

1. **Continue infinitely** — every belief is justified by another, without end. This seems impossible for finite minds and fails to explain how justification is grounded.

2. **Terminate in a circle** — at some point the chain loops back to a belief that appeared earlier. This is viciously circular: you cannot justify A by B and B by A, since this means the justification of each depends on the other in an obviously problematic way.

3. **Terminate in an unjustified belief** — the chain ends at some belief that is not justified by any further belief. But then that belief is a brute assumption, and anything based on it inherits its arbitrariness.

4. **Terminate in a self-justified belief** — a belief that carries its own justification, not derived from any further belief. This is the foundationalist option.

The trilemma — infinite regress, circularity, or unjustified termination — presents a challenge: how can any belief be justified at all, given these constraints?

## The Historical Background

The regress argument has ancient roots. Aristotle discussed it in the *Posterior Analytics* in the context of scientific demonstration: the regress of demonstrations must terminate in immediate premises that are known non-demonstratively. For Aristotle, this is where *nous* (intellectual intuition) operates: grasping first principles directly.

The Agrippa's trilemma (or Münchhausen trilemma) formulated by ancient skeptics named the three horns: infinite regress, circularity, and brute assumption. For the ancient Pyrrhonists, the trilemma showed that no belief can be adequately justified — they used it to motivate suspension of judgment.

Modern foundationalism is the dominant Western response: identify a class of beliefs that are self-justified, and derive all other justified beliefs from them.

## Why the Regress Matters

The regress matters because it exposes a fundamental structural question about justification: does justification always involve relationships between beliefs (inferential), or can some beliefs have justification that is not derived from relationships to other beliefs (non-inferential)?

The foundationalist says: yes, some beliefs have non-inferential justification. These are the basic beliefs that terminate the regress.

The coherentist says: the regress picture is wrong. Justification doesn't come from linear chains; it comes from the coherence of an entire web of beliefs. No belief is independently justified; all beliefs get their justification from their place in the web.

The infinitist says: maybe infinite chains of justification are possible and not as problematic as assumed.

## The Regress and the Basing Relation

The regress argument concerns inferential justification — the kind where a belief is justified by its connection to other beliefs. But there is another kind of justification: non-inferential, where a belief is justified not by its connection to other beliefs but by its connection to experience, perception, or some other non-doxastic source.

Foundationalists exploit this second kind: basic beliefs are justified by their connection to experience, not by connections to other beliefs. This terminates the regress without the problematic consequences of the other options.

Whether this move succeeds depends on whether experience can genuinely justify belief in the non-inferential way foundationalism requires — or whether, as Sellars argued, experience itself requires conceptual mediation that makes the "given" myth.

## Contemporary Significance

The regress problem has not been solved so much as absorbed into a richer understanding of epistemic architecture. Most epistemologists accept that justification has a complex structure that includes both inferential and non-inferential elements, and that the appropriate model is not a simple linear chain but a more complex network. The regress argument forced this recognition by showing that a purely inferentialist model of justification is untenable.
