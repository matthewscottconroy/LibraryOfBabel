# Infinitism: Embracing the Infinite Regress

Infinitism is the position that the regress of justification is neither vicious nor impossible — that beliefs can be justified by an infinite chain of reasons, and that this is actually the correct account of epistemic justification. Developed systematically by Peter Klein in the 1990s and 2000s, infinitism rejects both foundationalism (which terminates the chain in basic beliefs) and coherentism (which replaces linear chains with a web), and insists that genuine justification requires indefinitely extendable chains of reasons.

## The Core Claim

Klein's infinitism holds that a belief B is justified if and only if there is an infinite, non-repeating chain of reasons supporting it: B is supported by R1, which is supported by R2, which is supported by R3, and so on without end and without circles.

The reasons in the chain must be:
- **Non-repeating**: No reason appears more than once (this avoids circularity)
- **Genuinely supporting**: Each reason actually increases the epistemic status of the reason before it
- **Available**: The reasoner is able to provide each reason if asked

The key element: justification is not a property a belief has independently of the process of reasoning. It is a property a belief acquires as reasoning extends the chain of supporting reasons. The more reasons you can produce, and the more coherent they are, the more justified your belief.

## Why Infinitism?

Klein motivates infinitism by arguing that neither foundationalism nor coherentism escapes the problems of the Agrippa trilemma:

Against foundationalism: supposedly "basic" beliefs — beliefs justified without needing support from further beliefs — are either arbitrary (just assumed without grounds) or self-justifying (circular: the belief justifies itself). Neither option is satisfying. A belief that "justifies itself" is really just a belief held without justification.

Against coherentism: coherentist justification is ultimately circular. If every belief in the web is justified by its coherence with other beliefs in the web, and those beliefs are justified by their coherence with the first, the justification is provided by a big circle rather than a small one. Circular reasoning remains circular regardless of the circle's size.

Infinitism avoids both problems: it neither terminates in unjustified assumptions nor relies on circularity. The regress is genuine but not vicious.

## The Finite Mind Objection

The most immediate objection: humans are finite beings with finite minds. We cannot actually generate an infinite chain of reasons. How can infinite chains of justification be required for something that finite creatures need?

Klein's response distinguishes between justification and the process of reasoning. A belief is justified if the believer *could provide* further reasons at any point — if the chain is, in principle, indefinitely extensible. The believer doesn't need to have actually produced the infinite chain; they need to be in a position to extend it as far as necessary.

This is analogous to how we evaluate mathematical knowledge: we don't say you lack knowledge of addition because you haven't computed every sum. We say you know addition if you could compute any given sum when asked.

## The Availability Requirement

Klein specifies that each reason in the chain must be *available* to the reasoner: the reasoner must be able to supply the next link in the chain when asked. This is an internalist constraint: the chain of reasons must be accessible to the reasoner.

This raises a question: available in what sense? If reasons must be consciously available — if you must be able to articulate them — then the infinitist requirement is quite demanding. If available means merely that the reasoner would endorse the reasons on reflection, the requirement is less demanding but harder to evaluate.

## Infinitism and Contextualism

Klein connects infinitism to contextualism about justification: how many links in the chain are needed varies with the context. In high-stakes contexts (scientific inquiry, legal proceedings), more links are required; in everyday contexts, fewer are needed. The contextual standards determine when a belief counts as adequately justified for practical purposes, even if the chain itself extends infinitely.

## The Status of Infinitism

Infinitism has not attracted many committed defenders, but it has been valuable as a theoretical alternative that forces cleaner articulation of why foundationalism and coherentism are preferable. By insisting that justification requires indefinitely extendable chains, Klein highlights the importance of the *process* of reasoning — of actually being able to provide reasons when asked — rather than just the structure of a belief system at a moment.

Critics maintain that infinitism is psychologically unrealistic and that it mischaracterizes the nature of basic beliefs. But the debate has sharpened the conceptual issues surrounding the regress and clarified what both foundationalists and coherentists are committed to.
