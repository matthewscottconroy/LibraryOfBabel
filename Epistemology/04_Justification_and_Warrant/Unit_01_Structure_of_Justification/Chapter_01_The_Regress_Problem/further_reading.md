# Further Reading: The Regress Problem

## Primary Sources

- Aristotle, *Posterior Analytics*, trans. Jonathan Barnes, Clarendon Press, 1975. The classical locus of the regress argument, where Aristotle argues that demonstrative knowledge requires first principles that are known but not demonstrated.

- Sextus Empiricus, *Outlines of Pyrrhonism*, trans. R.G. Bury, Harvard University Press, 1933. Contains the five modes of Agrippa (including the regress mode), presenting the problem of justification as insoluble and recommending suspension of judgment.

- René Descartes, *Meditations on First Philosophy*, trans. John Cottingham, Cambridge University Press, 1996. Attempts to ground all knowledge in an indubitable foundation, implicitly engaging the regress by seeking beliefs that require no further justification.

- John Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, ed. P.H. Nidditch, Oxford University Press, 1975. Develops an empiricist foundationalism in which simple ideas serve as the termination point for chains of justification.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Peter Klein, "Human Knowledge and the Infinite Regress of Reasons," *Philosophical Perspectives* 13, 1999, pp. 297–325. The landmark defense of infinitism, arguing that justification requires infinite non-repeating chains of reasons rather than termination in foundations or circularity.

- Peter Klein, "Infinitism Is the Solution to the Regress Problem," in *Contemporary Debates in Epistemology*, eds. Matthias Steup and Ernest Sosa, Blackwell, 2005. A compact restatement of the infinitist position engaging its main critics.

- Peter Klein, "Infinitism," in *The Oxford Handbook of Epistemology*, ed. Paul Moser, Oxford University Press, 2002. Surveys the history of the regress problem and argues for infinitism's unique viability.

- John Turri, "On the Regress Argument for Infinitism," *Synthese* 166, 2009, pp. 157–163. A critical examination of Klein's arguments, raising questions about whether potential justifiers can do the required work.

- Scott Aikin, *Epistemology and the Regress Problem*, Routledge, 2011. A book-length treatment defending a version of infinitism while surveying foundationalist and coherentist alternatives in detail.

- Ernest Sosa, "The Raft and the Pyramid: Coherence versus Foundations in the Theory of Knowledge," *Midwest Studies in Philosophy* 5, 1980, pp. 3–25. A widely reprinted paper setting out the problem of choosing between coherentism and foundationalism as responses to the regress.

- Jonathan Dancy, *Introduction to Contemporary Epistemology*, Blackwell, 1985. Chapter 3 provides a clear pedagogical treatment of the regress argument and the trilemma it generates.

- Robert Audi, "The Structure of Justification," Cambridge University Press, 1993. Defends modest foundationalism as the most defensible response to the regress, with careful attention to the varieties of the trilemma.

- Carl Ginet, "Infinitism Is Not the Solution to the Regress Problem," in *Contemporary Debates in Epistemology*, Blackwell, 2005. The principal opposing paper to Klein's infinitism, arguing that merely potential reasons cannot confer actual justification.

- John Post, *The Faces of Existence*, Cornell University Press, 1987. Develops a non-foundationalist response to the regress by distinguishing different senses in which beliefs can be "prior."

- Michael Williams, *Unnatural Doubts*, Blackwell, 1991. Argues that the regress problem is generated by a particular (and questionable) conception of epistemic structure rather than by the nature of justification itself.

- Nicholas Rescher, *The Coherence Theory of Truth*, Oxford University Press, 1973. Defends coherentism partly as a response to the regress; useful background for understanding why coherentism is considered one horn of the trilemma.

- Richard Fumerton, *Metaepistemology and Skepticism*, Rowman & Littlefield, 1995. Examines the regress through the lens of internalism, arguing that foundationalism requires a specific account of non-inferential justification.

- Jeremy Fantl and Matthew McGrath, "Evidence, Pragmatics, and Justification," *Philosophical Review* 111, 2002, pp. 67–94. Relevant for understanding how practical stakes interact with the chain of justification, complicating the regress picture.
