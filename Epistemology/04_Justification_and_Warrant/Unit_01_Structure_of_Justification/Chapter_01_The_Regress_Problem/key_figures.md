# Key Figures: The Regress Problem

## Aristotle (384–322 BCE)

Aristotle's *Posterior Analytics* formulates the epistemic regress problem with enduring clarity. He argues that if every item of knowledge must be demonstrated from prior premises, either the regress continues infinitely, the chain circles back on itself, or it terminates in first principles that are known but not demonstrated. Aristotle himself endorses the third option, positing *nous* — a non-discursive intellectual grasp — as the faculty by which we apprehend foundational truths. His treatment set the framework within which virtually all subsequent responses to the regress have operated.

## Sextus Empiricus (c. 160–210 CE)

Sextus is the primary source for Agrippa's five modes, which generalize the regress from a problem about demonstration to a problem about justification of any kind. The modes include the regress mode (every proof requires a further proof), the circularity mode, and the hypothesis mode (bare assumption), collectively forming what modern epistemologists call the Agrippan trilemma. Unlike Aristotle, Sextus drew the conclusion that justified belief is unattainable, counseling *epoché* (suspension of judgment) as the path to tranquility.

## René Descartes (1596–1650)

Descartes' *Meditations* can be read as a sustained attempt to solve the regress problem through a foundationalist strategy. Seeking beliefs that are immune to doubt, he arrives at the *cogito* as the bedrock of his epistemic architecture, with the clarity-and-distinctness criterion serving to extend justified belief outward from that foundation. The Cartesian project crystallized the foundationalist intuition that rational inquiry requires an Archimedean point — a belief that justifies without needing justification from anything else.

## C.I. Lewis (1883–1964)

Lewis's *Mind and the World Order* (1929) and *An Analysis of Knowledge and Valuation* (1946) develop a classical foundationalist response to the regress grounded in the notion of "the given" — immediate qualitative experience that requires no inferential support. Lewis argued that empirical knowledge rests ultimately on reports of sensory acquaintance that are incorrigible. His position became one of the main targets of Sellars's critique of the myth of the given and thus shaped the debate about whether there can be non-inferentially justified foundational beliefs.

## Peter Klein (b. 1940)

Klein is the principal contemporary defender of infinitism as a solution to the regress problem. In a series of papers beginning in the 1990s, he argues that the Pyrrhonian trilemma is not exhaustive: an infinite, non-repeating chain of reasons can confer justification provided that the believer has the disposition to construct the chain as needed. Klein's key move is distinguishing *propositional* justification (a reason being available) from *doxastic* justification (a believer actually using it), allowing justification to extend through potential reasons that have not been explicitly entertained.

## Robert Audi (b. 1941)

Audi has been a leading defender of moderate foundationalism as the correct response to the regress. He argues that not all justified beliefs need to derive their justification entirely from other beliefs: some beliefs are non-inferentially justified by experience or reason without being incorrigible or self-evident in a strong sense. His careful distinction between different ways a belief can be "basic" — direct, indirect, inferential, dispositional — has refined the foundationalist response to show that it need not be committed to the strong Cartesian form that Sellars and others attacked.

## Ernest Sosa (b. 1940)

Sosa's metaphor of the raft (coherentism) versus the pyramid (foundationalism) in his landmark 1980 paper became the standard framing for the debate. While initially proposing a "supercoherentist" middle position, Sosa later developed a virtue-theoretic foundationalism that grounds basic justification in reliable cognitive faculties. His contribution to the regress debate has been to show that the trilemma's second horn (circularity) can be rehabilitated if it is understood as a global, structural feature of a cognitive system rather than as local, vicious circularity between individual beliefs.
