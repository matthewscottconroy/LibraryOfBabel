# Thought Experiments: The Regress Problem

## The Endless Hallway

**The scenario:** Imagine a vast hallway of doors. You wish to enter the room at the end, but each door will only open if you first retrieve the key from the room behind you. You go back one room, then another, then another — and discover that the hallway has no beginning. There is no first room with a key that requires no prior key. You can never start.

**The philosophical point:** This scenario captures the force of the regress objection to infinitism. If justification always requires further justification, it might seem that no belief can ever be justified, because you could never reach a point from which the chain gets off the ground. Klein's infinitist response is to challenge the analogy: unlike a physical door that must be *actually* opened, propositional justification requires only that the next reason *be available*, not that it be explicitly retrieved. The hallway without a beginning is only problematic if you must travel all the way back before moving forward.

## The Groundless Expert

**The scenario:** A doctor diagnoses a patient on the basis of symptoms, test results, and medical training. If asked why she trusts a particular test, she cites studies; if asked why she trusts those studies, she cites peer-review methodology; if asked why she trusts peer review, she cites its track record of error-correction — and so on. At some point, the doctor can only say: "This is just how good medicine is practiced." Does her inability to provide a further reason undermine her justified belief in the diagnosis?

**The philosophical point:** This case tests the foundationalist intuition that chains of justification must terminate in beliefs that are justified without further support. Foundationalists argue that the doctor's background training constitutes something like basic belief — or at least that the regress bottoms out in reliable perceptual and inferential practices that require no further vindication. The example also raises the question of whether the terminating "this is just how we do it" is a legitimate stopping place or a mere hypothesis — corresponding to the Agrippan trilemma's third horn.

## The Circular Detective

**The scenario:** A detective claims that Smith is guilty because Jones testified against him. When pressed on why he trusts Jones, the detective replies that Jones is credible because he has a reputation for honesty. When asked why he trusts that reputation, he says it is based on past testimony from reliable witnesses — among them, the very testimony that started the inquiry. The justificatory circle is not immediately obvious; it takes six steps to return to the starting point.

**The philosophical point:** This scenario illustrates the coherentist horn of the trilemma: justification via mutual support among beliefs. The case presses the question of whether a sufficiently large and non-trivially structured circle escapes the charge of viciousness. Coherentists argue that coherence across a wide system of beliefs generates genuine justification; critics respond that any circle, however large, remains question-begging. The detective's extended loop raises the intuition that long circles are still circles.

## The Cartesian Pyramid

**The scenario:** Consider a mathematician who has proven a theorem through a long chain of valid deductions from axioms. She knows the theorem because she knows the intermediate steps; she knows those steps because she knows the axioms; she knows the axioms because — she says — they are self-evident. But when asked what makes something self-evident, she cannot point to anything further: self-evidence just is the property of being directly knowable without proof.

**The philosophical point:** This scenario illustrates the foundationalist resolution of the regress: the chain terminates in beliefs that are epistemically self-sustaining. It raises the pressure question that philosophers since Sellars have asked of foundationalism: what exactly is it for a belief to be "self-evident" or "directly known"? If self-evidence is a matter of seeming obvious, then it seems psychological rather than epistemic; if it is a matter of being genuinely unrevisable, then it seems to exclude much of what foundationalists want to include as basic.

## The Infinite Ledger

**The scenario:** An accountant is audited and must justify every entry in her ledger. She can justify entry 1 by citing entry 2, entry 2 by citing entry 3, and so forth — the ledger has entries stretching back indefinitely, each one providing reason for the previous. The auditor asks: "But if the entries never end, what makes the whole ledger trustworthy?"

**The philosophical point:** This thought experiment presents Klein's infinitist position at its most intuitive but also its most vulnerable. Klein would say: the ledger is trustworthy precisely because no entry is arbitrary — every entry has a reason, and no circle is formed. The skeptic's challenge is that "never arbitrary" does not obviously equal "justified," since a well-structured abyss is still an abyss. The case thus brings out the core dispute between infinitism and foundationalism over whether actual termination or the mere absence of circularity is what justification requires.
