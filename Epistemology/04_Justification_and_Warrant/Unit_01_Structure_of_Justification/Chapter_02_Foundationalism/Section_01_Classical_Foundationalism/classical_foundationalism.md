# Classical Foundationalism: Descartes and Certainty

Classical foundationalism is the view that the structure of justified belief is like a building: all the justified beliefs are supported, directly or indirectly, by a set of foundation beliefs that are themselves independently justified. The foundation beliefs are basic — not justified by other beliefs but by something else (experience, reason, self-evidence). All non-basic beliefs are justified by their relationship to these foundations.

Classical foundationalism in its strongest form, associated with Descartes and the early twentieth-century empiricists, demands that the foundations be *certain* — indubitable, infallible, or incorrigible. The justified superstructure inherits its security from the security of the foundations.

## The Cartesian Model

Descartes' method of doubt was designed to identify the indubitable foundations. By systematically doubting everything he could doubt, he aimed to find beliefs that could not be doubted even by the most radical skeptical hypothesis. Only beliefs with this certainty could serve as the foundation for knowledge.

The result: the *cogito* ("I think, therefore I am") and the contents of his own mind as they currently appear to him. These are infallible: even the evil demon cannot deceive him about the fact that he is thinking or about how things currently seem to him. From these incorrigible foundations, Descartes hoped to reconstruct all of human knowledge.

The Cartesian model of foundationalism is demanding:
- **Infallibility**: Basic beliefs cannot be false. If you are in the relevant mental state, the basic belief is guaranteed to be true.
- **Incorrigibility**: You cannot be wrong about whether you are in the relevant state. If you seem to have the experience, you have it.
- **Certainty**: Basic beliefs are known with absolute certainty; they could not be doubted by any rational being.

## Empiricist Versions

The classical empiricist tradition (Locke, Berkeley, Hume, and the twentieth-century positivists) accepted the foundationalist structure while holding that the foundations are experiential rather than rational. The immediate data of experience — sense impressions, sense data, "protocol sentences" — serve as the secure foundation.

The logical positivists' "protocol sentence debate" (Schlick, Carnap, Neurath) concerned what exactly the foundations should be. Schlick held that genuine observation statements ("here now a red patch") report incorrigible immediate experiences. Carnap analyzed them in terms of physical events. Neurath denied incorrigibility altogether and made foundationalism coherentist from the bottom up.

## The Appeal of Classical Foundationalism

Classical foundationalism has powerful motivations:

- It provides a *non-circular* structure for justification: the foundations are not justified by the superstructure.
- It terminates the regress at a *principled point*: basic beliefs are not arbitrary; they are justified by something (experience, self-evidence, intuition) that is not itself a belief.
- It matches the *phenomenology of certainty*: there are some things we seem to know with complete confidence, and foundationalism explains this.

## Why Classical Foundationalism Failed

Classical foundationalism in its strong form has been largely abandoned for several reasons:

**The problem of the foundations**: What exactly are the incorrigible foundations? If they are reports of subjective experience ("I seem to see red"), they are highly limited — they say nothing directly about the external world. Building knowledge of the external world from purely subjective reports requires substantial inference, and the security of the foundations doesn't guarantee the security of what is built on them.

**Sellars's critique**: Even seemingly incorrigible experiential reports are not epistemically innocent. To be epistemically significant, a belief must have conceptual content — it must be the kind of thing that can be right or wrong. But conceptual content requires understanding concepts, which involves being embedded in a practice of correction and evaluation. There is no bedrock of non-conceptual, infallible "given."

**The gap between foundations and superstructure**: Even granting infallible foundations, the inferential connections to ordinary beliefs about the external world are highly uncertain. The security of the foundation doesn't flow upward through uncertain inferences.

**The poverty of the foundations**: If the foundations are limited to subjective experience reports, they are too narrow to support the rich structure of knowledge we actually have.

Classical foundationalism's failure motivated the development of *modest foundationalism* (which abandons the infallibility requirement) and alternative views like coherentism.
