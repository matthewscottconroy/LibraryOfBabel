# Modest Foundationalism: Fallible Foundations

The failure of classical foundationalism to provide certain, incorrigible foundations did not end foundationalism as a research program. Instead, philosophers developed *modest foundationalism* — a version that retains the foundationalist structure (basic beliefs supporting derived beliefs) while abandoning the requirement that basic beliefs be certain or infallible. Modest foundationalism allows basic beliefs to be fallible, revisable, and less than certain, while still playing a structural role in terminating the regress.

## The Core Relaxation

Modest foundationalism retains the two-tier structure: some beliefs are basic (not justified by other beliefs), and non-basic beliefs are justified by their inferential or evidential connections to basic beliefs. What it abandons is the requirement that basic beliefs be infallible.

Basic beliefs, on the modest account:
- Have some degree of prima facie justification — not requiring support from other beliefs for that justification
- Can be revised or overridden by other evidence
- Need not be certain or incorrigible
- Can be wrong

This is a significant departure from the Cartesian model. The foundations are not a bedrock of certainty but a starting point — beliefs that can get the justificatory process started without themselves needing prior support.

## What Justifies Basic Beliefs?

If basic beliefs are not justified by other beliefs, they must be justified by something else. On modest foundationalist accounts, the candidates include:

**Sensory experience**: Perceptual experience provides non-inferential justification for perceptual beliefs. The belief "there is a red apple here" is justified by the perceptual experience of seeing a red apple — not by inference from other beliefs, but by direct evidential connection to experience. The experience is not itself a belief, so there is no regress.

**Phenomenal seemings**: When something seems to be the case to a perceiver, this seeming provides justification for believing it is the case. Michael Huemer's *phenomenal conservatism* holds that if it seems to S that P, then S has prima facie justification for believing P. The seeming can be defeated by other evidence but provides real prima facie justification without inferential grounds.

**Reliable processes**: Reliabilism provides an externalist account of basic beliefs: a perceptual belief is basic and justified if it is produced by a reliable perceptual process. The justification comes from the reliability of the process, not from any relationship to other beliefs.

## Chisholm's Foundationalism

Roderick Chisholm developed a nuanced version of modest foundationalism. Chisholm distinguished between *self-presenting* states — mental states that are directly accessible to the subject — and states about the external world. Self-presenting states (like seeming to see red) are the foundation; their epistemic status is "directly evident" — the subject is directly acquainted with them and cannot be wrong about whether they are in them.

But Chisholm's "directly evident" is weaker than Cartesian infallibility: it means that the self-presenting states are epistemically the most basic things — the starting point — not that they are certain. From this foundation, beliefs about external reality can be rendered probable or reasonable, even if not certain.

## The Role of Defeasibility

Modest foundationalism typically builds in a robust defeasibility structure. Basic beliefs provide prima facie justification — justification that holds unless defeated by other evidence. If my perceptual belief "there is a red apple here" is formed in conditions of unusual lighting, and I learn about the unusual lighting, this can defeat my prima facie justification.

This defeasibility distinguishes modest foundationalism from classical foundationalism, where the foundations were supposed to be indefeasible. On the modest view, even basic beliefs can be revised; they just don't need other beliefs to support them in the first place.

## The Modest Foundationalism / Coherentism Convergence

A notable feature of modest foundationalism is how closely it converges with sophisticated coherentism in practice. Both allow that all beliefs are in principle revisable in light of other beliefs; both recognize that the justification of any belief depends partly on its coherence with the rest of the belief system; both accept a kind of mutual support among beliefs.

The difference is structural: modest foundationalism maintains that some beliefs have prima facie justification independently of coherence, while coherentism holds that all justification derives from coherence relations. In practice, the gap may be narrower than the theoretical disagreement suggests — what matters is that the foundations are not isolated from the rest of the belief system and that revision in either direction (from foundations to superstructure or from superstructure back to foundations) is possible.
