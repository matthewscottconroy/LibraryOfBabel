# Further Reading: Foundationalism

## Primary Sources

- René Descartes, *Meditations on First Philosophy*, trans. John Cottingham, Cambridge University Press, 1996. The paradigm text for classical foundationalism; the method of doubt is deployed to identify the *cogito* as the indubitable foundation from which all other knowledge is reconstructed.

- John Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, ed. P.H. Nidditch, Oxford University Press, 1975. Locke's empiricist version of foundationalism grounds knowledge in simple ideas derived from sensation and reflection, which serve as the inputs that cannot themselves be further analyzed into anything more basic.

- C.I. Lewis, *An Analysis of Knowledge and Valuation*, Open Court, 1946. Defends a rigorous empiricist foundationalism according to which "terminating judgments" — reports of immediate sensory experience — are incorrigible and serve as the ultimate epistemic bedrock.

- Roderick Chisholm, *Theory of Knowledge*, 3rd ed., Prentice-Hall, 1989. A systematic development of foundationalism within an internalist framework, providing careful definitions of epistemic terms and a taxonomy of epistemic statuses from "acceptable" to "certain."

- Wilfrid Sellars, "Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind," in *Science, Perception and Reality*, Routledge & Kegan Paul, 1963. The foundational critique of the "myth of the given," arguing that no experience can both be a non-conceptual given and serve as a reason for belief.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Ernest Sosa, "The Raft and the Pyramid," *Midwest Studies in Philosophy* 5, 1980, pp. 3–25. Frames the choice between foundationalism and coherentism with exceptional clarity and proposes a "supercoherentist" alternative that preserves foundationalist insights.

- Laurence BonJour, *The Structure of Empirical Knowledge*, Harvard University Press, 1985. Chapters 2–4 mount the most sustained coherentist critique of foundationalism, pressing the regress, the myth-of-the-given objection, and the problem of epistemic gap.

- Robert Audi, *The Structure of Justification*, Cambridge University Press, 1993. The most thorough book-length defense of modest foundationalism, distinguishing numerous forms of basicness and replying systematically to coherentist objections.

- William Alston, *Epistemic Justification: Essays in the Theory of Knowledge*, Cornell University Press, 1989. Collects important essays developing and defending a modest foundationalism based on "direct acquaintance" and attacking alternatives.

- Michael Williams, *Groundless Belief*, Yale University Press, 1977. An early and influential critique of classical foundationalism, arguing that the very notion of a privileged epistemic class of beliefs is theoretically unmotivated.

- Paul Moser, *Empirical Justification*, D. Reidel, 1985. A careful defense of foundationalism based on non-doxastic justifiers — arguing that experiential states that are not themselves beliefs can justify beliefs without generating a new regress.

- Jonathan Dancy, *Introduction to Contemporary Epistemology*, Blackwell, 1985. Chapters 3–5 provide an accessible survey of foundationalist positions and their critics, especially useful for understanding the gap between classical and modest versions.

- Susan Haack, *Evidence and Inquiry*, Blackwell, 1993. Proposes "foundherentism" as a synthesis of foundationalism and coherentism, arguing that neither pure theory adequately captures how justification actually works.

- Alvin Goldman, "What Is Justified Belief?" in *Justification and Knowledge*, ed. George Pappas, D. Reidel, 1979. Influential paper proposing reliabilism partly as a way to ground basic beliefs in causal/reliable processes rather than in the problematic notion of the given.

- James Pryor, "The Skeptic and the Dogmatist," *Noûs* 34, 2000, pp. 517–549. Defends "dogmatism" — the view that perceptual seemings provide immediate, non-inferential justification — as a version of modest foundationalism with fresh arguments against skeptical challenges.

- Juan Comesaña, "Unsafe Knowledge," *Synthese* 146, 2005, pp. 395–404. Examines how safety conditions on knowledge interact with foundationalist claims about basic belief.

- Tim Crane and D.H. Mellor, "There Is No Question of Physicalism," *Mind* 99, 1990, pp. 185–206. Relevant background for understanding the relationship between foundationalist epistemology and debates about the mental states that might serve as foundations.

- Richard Fumerton, *Metaepistemology and Skepticism*, Rowman & Littlefield, 1995. Defends "acquaintance" foundationalism — the view that non-inferential justification requires a subject to be acquainted with the truth-making fact — and engages the myth-of-the-given objection in depth.

- John McDowell, *Mind and World*, Harvard University Press, 1994. Offers a nuanced response to Sellars, arguing for a view in which conceptualized experience can be both given and reason-giving, thereby rehabilitating a form of foundationalism without falling into the myth.
