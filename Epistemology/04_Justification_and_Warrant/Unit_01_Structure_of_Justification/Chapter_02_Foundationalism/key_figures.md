# Key Figures: Foundationalism

## René Descartes (1596–1650)

Descartes is the archetype of classical foundationalism. His method of hyperbolic doubt in the *Meditations* is expressly designed to locate beliefs that survive every conceivable challenge to their truth, and the *cogito* ("I think, therefore I am") is his candidate for the indubitable foundation from which the entire edifice of knowledge can be reconstructed. Descartes' foundationalism is characterized by strong constraints: basic beliefs must be *certain* — not merely probably true or resistant to practical doubt, but logically impossible to doubt coherently. This Cartesian demand for certainty is what makes classical foundationalism so demanding and, many argue, so difficult to sustain.

## C.I. Lewis (1883–1964)

Lewis developed the most rigorous empiricist version of classical foundationalism. In *Mind and the World Order* and *An Analysis of Knowledge and Valuation* he argued that empirical knowledge is grounded in "the given" — immediate qualitative sensory content that is available to the subject without inference. Lewis held that reports of one's own present sensory experience (e.g., "I am now appeared to redly") are incorrigible and serve as the epistemic bedrock for all empirical claims. His version ties foundationalism to phenomenalism and to the view that the content of basic beliefs is always about appearances rather than the external world.

## Wilfrid Sellars (1912–1989)

Sellars is foundationalism's most influential critic. In "Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind" he argues that the whole notion of the given — the idea that there is a class of mental states that are both (a) not conceptually structured and (b) capable of serving as reasons — is mythological. Sellars' argument turns on the claim that to serve as a reason, a mental state must be the kind of thing that can stand in inferential relations, which requires it to have conceptual or propositional content; but then it is no longer "merely given" and itself stands in need of justification. This critique forced foundationalists to either retreat to modest versions or articulate a richer theory of non-inferential justification.

## Roderick Chisholm (1916–1999)

Chisholm is the leading defender of foundationalism within classical analytic epistemology. His *Theory of Knowledge* (three editions: 1966, 1977, 1989) develops a systematic framework in which epistemic terms are defined by reference to a scale of epistemic values — from "acceptable" through "justified" to "certain" — and in which basic beliefs occupy the highest position, being epistemically self-presenting. Chisholm's approach is internalist: what makes a belief basic is something entirely accessible to the believer through reflection, specifically the property that the belief's being true entails one's being in a state from which the truth can be known directly.

## Robert Audi (b. 1941)

Audi is the principal contemporary defender of modest foundationalism. He distinguishes between "classical" foundationalism (requiring incorrigibility or infallibility) and "modest" foundationalism (requiring only that basic beliefs have some degree of justification that does not derive entirely from other beliefs). In *The Structure of Justification* (1993), he argues that perceptual, memorial, introspective, and a priori beliefs can all serve as non-inferential justifiers without being certain. Audi also carefully distinguishes *direct* from *indirect* justification and defends the view that basic beliefs can be defeated by further evidence — they are not immune from revision, merely not in need of inferential support to get their initial justification off the ground.

## James Pryor (b. 1973)

Pryor's "dogmatist" version of foundationalism, developed in "The Skeptic and the Dogmatist" (2000), defends the view that perceptual seemings directly and immediately justify beliefs about the external world without the mediation of any inference or argument. His proposal is notable for conceding that this means accepting that perceptual justification is not itself justified by anything else — a feature he embraces rather than apologizes for. Pryor's dogmatism has generated significant debate about whether this "immediacy" counts as foundationalism, and whether it can answer the skeptic's challenge without begging the question.

## Susan Haack (b. 1945)

Haack proposed "foundherentism" as a middle path between foundationalism and coherentism in *Evidence and Inquiry* (1993). She argues that both pure theories are unstable: foundationalism without any coherence-checking leaves basic beliefs dangerously unconstrained, while coherentism without any experiential input is untethered from the world. Her foundherentist picture allows experiential states to provide justificatory "anchoring" for beliefs while also requiring those beliefs to cohere with the broader web of the believer's commitments — making her a significant figure in the contemporary debate about how foundationalism should be reformed.
