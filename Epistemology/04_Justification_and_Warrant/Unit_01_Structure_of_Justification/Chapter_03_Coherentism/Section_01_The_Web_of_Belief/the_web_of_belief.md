# Coherentism and the Web of Belief

Coherentism about justification holds that beliefs are justified not by their relationship to independent foundations but by their coherence with one another within a comprehensive system. The picture is holistic: justification is a property of whole belief systems, and individual beliefs are justified by their place in a coherent whole. No belief is independently justified; all justification derives from coherence relations.

## The Core Idea

The web-of-belief metaphor comes from Quine and Ullian's influential book of that name (1970), but the coherentist idea is much older. The central thought: the beliefs of a rational person form an interconnected system, like a web or net. Some beliefs are more central (deep in the system, highly connected) and some are more peripheral (at the edge, less connected). When a new belief or experience is encountered, it is integrated into the web by adjusting the surrounding beliefs to maximize overall coherence.

Coherentism rejects the foundationalist idea of a two-tier structure. There are no beliefs that are justified independently of their coherence with other beliefs. Every belief's justification depends on its relationship to the whole system.

## What Is Coherence?

Coherence is more than mere consistency (absence of contradiction), though consistency is a necessary component. Coherence is a positive relationship among beliefs that includes:

- **Logical consistency**: No pair of beliefs in the system contradict each other.
- **Inferential connectedness**: Beliefs support one another through valid or reliable inferences.
- **Explanatory coherence**: Beliefs explain one another — they fit into a network of explanatory relationships.
- **Comprehensiveness**: The system covers a wide range of relevant phenomena without requiring many exceptions or unexplained anomalies.
- **Simplicity**: The system achieves its coverage with minimal complexity and few ad hoc additions.

A belief is more or less justified depending on how well it coheres with the rest of the system and how much it contributes to the system's overall coherence.

## Quine's Holism

W.V.O. Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) provided the most influential formulation of a coherentist picture of empirical justification. Quine argued that our beliefs form a "web" that faces experience as a corporate body: no individual belief is directly confirmed or disconfirmed by experience; instead, experience puts pressure on the whole system, and adjustments are made wherever they maximize overall coherence.

When an experience conflicts with the web, something must give. Which beliefs are revised is partly a matter of their centrality: beliefs at the core of the web (logical and mathematical truths, highly general theoretical principles) are rarely revised; beliefs at the periphery (specific empirical claims) are revised more easily. But in principle, any belief can be revised.

Quine's picture is explicitly pragmatic: we adjust the web in whatever way maintains its overall coherence while keeping it in touch with experience. There is no sharp distinction between analytic and synthetic beliefs, between conceptual and empirical revision.

## BonJour's Coherentism

Laurence BonJour's early work (*The Structure of Empirical Knowledge*, 1985) defended coherentism rigorously. BonJour argued that foundationalism cannot work because no belief can be independently justified: even sensory reports require conceptual structure, and anything with conceptual structure is subject to the norms of justification that make it part of a system.

BonJour's coherentism is systematic: a belief is justified if and only if it is a member of the most coherent system available to the believer. The most coherent system is the one that best satisfies the dimensions of coherence (consistency, inferential connections, explanatory power, comprehensiveness) while remaining responsive to experience.

## The Observation Requirement

One challenge for coherentism: how does experience connect to the web? If justification is purely internal (derives only from coherence among beliefs), then experience — which is non-doxastic — seems unable to play a role. But surely experience matters: the reason my belief that there is a red apple here is justified has something to do with my seeing the red apple.

BonJour's response: the web must include "observation beliefs" — perceptual reports about what one is currently experiencing. These reports are justified not by their foundational character but by their coherence with the rest of the web and by the believer's background beliefs about the reliability of their perceptual systems.

But this seems circular: observation beliefs are justified by the coherence of the web, which includes beliefs about the reliability of perception, which are justified by the web, which includes... The coherentist must explain how the web is responsive to experience without making experience a non-doxastic foundation.
