# Versions of Coherentism

Coherentism is not a single view but a family of positions that share the thesis that justification derives from coherence relations among beliefs. The family includes versions that differ in their accounts of what coherence is, which system is relevant, and how experience enters the picture. Understanding these variants is essential to appreciating both the resources and the limitations of the coherentist approach.

## Systematic Coherentism: BonJour's Account

BonJour's systematic coherentism holds that a belief B is justified for a person S if and only if B is a member of the most coherent overall system of beliefs available to S. Coherence is measured along multiple dimensions: logical consistency, inferential connections, explanatory power, simplicity, and comprehensiveness.

The key feature: justification is holistic. You cannot evaluate the justification of a single belief in isolation; you must evaluate the whole system of which it is a part. A belief that is somewhat isolated in the web — that has few inferential connections to other beliefs — is less justified than a belief that is deeply embedded in the web.

BonJour's account includes an "observation requirement": for the web to remain connected to experience, beliefs formed in response to experience must be treated as having initial credibility. The key is that this initial credibility derives from the system's background beliefs about the reliability of perception, not from any non-doxastic "given."

## Explanatory Coherentism: Lycan and Goldman

William Lycan and Alvin Goldman (in some formulations) have connected coherentism to the logic of inference to the best explanation (IBE). A belief is justified if it is part of the best explanation of one's total evidence, including perceptual inputs. Beliefs that explain more of one's experience, explain it more simply, and cohere with other well-supported beliefs are more justified.

This is coherentism with an explanatory flavor: the key dimension of coherence is not just consistency or inferential connection but *explanatory integration*. The most justified system is not just the most consistent but the most explanatorily powerful.

## Doxastic Coherentism vs. Propositional Coherentism

A useful distinction: *doxastic* coherentism concerns the justification of actual beliefs a person holds; *propositional* coherentism concerns the justification of propositions relative to what a person believes.

Doxastic coherentism: S is justified in believing P if P is part of S's most coherent accessible system of beliefs.

Propositional coherentism: P is justified for S if P would cohere well with S's beliefs, whether or not S actually believes P.

This distinction matters for the relationship between justification and rational belief revision: propositional coherentism can explain why some beliefs you don't yet hold would be justified for you, given what you believe.

## Local vs. Global Coherentism

Coherentism can be formulated as a local or global thesis:

*Local coherentism*: A belief B is justified if B coheres with a relevant subset of the believer's beliefs — those relevant to the domain of B.

*Global coherentism*: A belief B is justified if B coheres with the believer's entire belief system.

Global coherentism is the more radical version. It holds that no domain of inquiry is insulated from any other — every belief is potentially relevant to every other. This is closest to Quine's holism.

Local coherentism has more practical plausibility: when evaluating a belief about geology, we consider coherence with geological knowledge, not with the entire belief system including beliefs about poetry and cuisine. But determining which beliefs are "relevant" requires a principled account that local coherentism may not provide.

## The Neurath's Boat Metaphor

Otto Neurath's metaphor captures the coherentist picture vividly: we are like sailors on a ship at sea who must rebuild it plank by plank while remaining afloat. We can't step off the ship to start from scratch (Descartes' strategy); we must work with what we have, replacing planks gradually while keeping the ship seaworthy.

This metaphor emphasizes that justification is always relative to a starting point — the set of beliefs we currently have — and that there is no perspective-independent Archimedean point from which to evaluate the whole system. Revision is always incremental and always coherence-preserving.

The metaphor also highlights a problem: if we can never step outside the ship, how do we know it is headed in the right direction? Neurath's boat might be coherent but systematically false — floating in a sea that is nothing like what the beliefs on board say it is.
