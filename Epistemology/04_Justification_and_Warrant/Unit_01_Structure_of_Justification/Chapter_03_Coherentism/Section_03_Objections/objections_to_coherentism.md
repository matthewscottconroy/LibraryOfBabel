# Objections to Coherentism

Coherentism is an intuitively appealing picture of how beliefs support one another, and it captures important insights about the holistic character of justification. But it faces serious objections that have prevented it from achieving consensus as the correct account of epistemic justification.

## The Isolation Objection

The most fundamental objection: coherentism severs the connection between our beliefs and the external world. A belief system can be internally coherent while being comprehensively false — like an elaborate, internally consistent fiction. If coherence alone determines justification, then a system of beliefs about unicorns could be as justified as a system of beliefs about the physical world, as long as the two systems are equally coherent.

This is the isolation objection: coherentism isolates justification from truth. It gives us no guarantee that a coherent belief system corresponds to reality. But the whole point of justification is to indicate that a belief is likely true — that it "tracks" reality. A purely coherentist justification cannot provide this guarantee.

BonJour's response: a belief system that is appropriately responsive to observation (includes observation beliefs that are triggered by perceptual experience) is not isolated. The connection to experience provides the connection to the world. But critics charge that this response either sneaks in a foundationalist element (the observation beliefs function as basic beliefs) or remains circular (the observation beliefs are justified by the coherence of the system, which is what we're supposed to be establishing).

## The Alternative Coherent Systems Problem

There are, presumably, many mutually inconsistent belief systems, each of which is internally coherent. Coherentism seems to imply that all of them are justified — or that which system is justified is relative to which one you are in. But this seems to make justification relative in a way that undermines its epistemic significance.

Philosophical and cultural traditions often have internally coherent systems of belief that are mutually inconsistent. Coherentism cannot adjudicate between them by appeal to coherence alone. This threatens the objectivity of epistemic justification.

BonJour responds that the most comprehensive and explanatorily powerful system wins. But this introduces criteria that go beyond mere coherence — comprehensiveness and explanatory power connect the system to something beyond itself.

## The Experience Problem

Coherentism must explain how experience gets into the web. If justification is purely doxastic — purely a matter of coherence among beliefs — then sensory experience (which is not a belief) cannot contribute to justification. But this conflicts with the obvious fact that what we see, hear, and touch is fundamental evidence for our beliefs.

The coherentist's standard move is to include *observation beliefs* — beliefs that record what one is experiencing — as part of the system. But observation beliefs are still beliefs; they can cohere with or clash with other beliefs. How do they acquire their initial credibility?

If they acquire credibility just from their coherence with the rest of the system, the connection to experience is mediated by the system — which means experience cannot correct the system from outside. If observation beliefs have some initial credibility independent of coherence, coherentism has smuggled in a foundationalist element.

## The Circularity Problem

As Quine-style coherentism makes explicit, any revision of the web must preserve coherence. But the standards for what counts as coherent are themselves part of the web. The logic we use to evaluate consistency, the inductive principles we use to evaluate inferential connections, the theoretical virtues we use to evaluate explanatory power — all of these are themselves beliefs in the web that are justified by their coherence with the rest.

This means that coherentism's justification standards are self-certifying in a potentially vicious way. The web justifies itself by its own standards. This is not obviously wrong — all epistemic frameworks face some version of this problem — but it is a challenge that coherentism must address.

## The Practical Inaccessibility of "Most Coherent System"

BonJour's coherentism requires that a belief be a member of the most coherent system *available* to the believer. But how does a finite believer identify the most coherent system? The number of possible belief revisions at any point is enormous, and the computation required to determine which revision maximizes coherence is in practice impossible.

Coherentists sometimes respond that this is an idealization — a normative standard, not a description of what actual believers do. But this raises the question of how the idealized standard relates to actual epistemic practice. If the coherentist ideal is unreachable in practice, it is unclear that it gives guidance to actual believers about how to reason.
