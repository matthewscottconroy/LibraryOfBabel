# Key Figures: Coherentism

## Otto Neurath (1882–1945)

Neurath, a member of the Vienna Circle, coined the boat metaphor that became coherentism's most enduring image: we are like sailors who must rebuild their ship plank by plank while at sea, never able to step onto dry land and start from scratch. This captures the coherentist's rejection of the foundationalist idea that inquiry can begin from a set of independently secure beliefs. Neurath's epistemology was inseparable from his anti-metaphysical physicalism; he insisted that all legitimate knowledge claims must be expressed in the language of physical observation and that the test of a claim is always its fit with the total system of accepted claims.

## W.V.O. Quine (1908–2000)

Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" dismantled the analytic-synthetic distinction and thereby undercut the foundationalist picture in which logic and mathematics provide one class of unrevisable truths while empirical observation provides another. Quine's positive contribution was the image of the "web of belief": our total set of beliefs faces the tribunal of experience as a corporate body, not piecemeal. Beliefs at the periphery are most directly sensitive to experience; beliefs at the center (including mathematics and logic) are insulated by the rest of the web and can be retained under almost any empirical pressure if adjustments elsewhere are made. This holism is a form of structural coherentism, though Quine himself was skeptical of normative epistemology as a discipline.

## Laurence BonJour (b. 1943)

BonJour is the most systematic and rigorous defender of coherentism in analytic epistemology. In *The Structure of Empirical Knowledge* (1985) he develops the account of justification as membership in a sufficiently coherent system of beliefs. Coherence for BonJour involves logical consistency, high degrees of inferential interconnection, explanatory breadth, and the absence of unexplained anomalies. A crucial element of his view is the "observation requirement": a coherent system must include a substantial proportion of perceptual beliefs attributed to the believer's own cognitive faculties, which is designed to prevent coherentism from floating entirely free of the world. BonJour later abandoned coherentism in favor of a rationalist foundationalism, a move that itself illuminates the problems he found intractable.

## Keith Lehrer (1936–2024)

Lehrer's *Theory of Knowledge* (1990) develops a distinctive coherentist framework centered on the notion of an "acceptance system" — the set of things a person accepts (in a functional, belief-like attitude) for the purposes of reasoning. Justification is achieved when a belief "beats" every relevant competitor within this system — when the reasons for the belief outweigh the reasons for competing alternatives according to the system's own standards. Lehrer is unusual among coherentists in giving a central role to the concept of *trustworthiness*: a person's beliefs are justified if they arise from cognitive processes that the person correctly regards as reliable within their acceptance system.

## Nicholas Rescher (b. 1928)

Rescher defends coherentism as a theory of both justification and truth in *The Coherence Theory of Truth* (1973) and related works. He argues that the selection among competing hypotheses is made on pragmatic coherentist grounds — we choose the most systematically coherent and practically successful hypothesis-set. Rescher's pragmatic coherentism distinguishes him from purely epistemic coherentists: for him, the coherence that matters is not just logical consistency but system-wide explanatory power and practical fruitfulness, giving coherentism connections to American pragmatism.

## Erik Olsson (b. 1966)

Olsson has mounted the most technically sophisticated contemporary challenge to coherentism in *Against Coherence* (2005). Using formal probabilistic models, he argues that the probability of a coherent set of beliefs being jointly true is not, in general, higher than the probability of an incoherent set — coherence is not truth-conducive in the way coherentists need. While Olsson's result is disputed, his work has forced coherentists to either weaken their claims (coherence improves justification at the margin, not in general) or to develop richer accounts of what epistemic coherence amounts to beyond probabilistic consistency.

## Catherine Elgin (b. 1948)

Elgin defends a coherentist methodology under the label "reflective equilibrium" in *Considered Judgment* (1996), arguing that the goal of inquiry is wide reflective equilibrium among all of one's commitments — beliefs, hypotheses, intuitions, and methodological principles. She extends coherentism beyond propositional belief to include the non-propositional "symbol systems" through which we cognize the world, and she connects coherentist epistemology to Nelson Goodman's constructivist philosophy. Elgin's work is significant for showing how coherentism can be developed as an account of scientific knowledge rather than being confined to the structure of individual belief systems.
