# Thought Experiments: Coherentism

## Neurath's Boat

**The scenario:** You are a sailor at sea, and your boat is in disrepair. You cannot bring the boat to dry dock — you must stay afloat. So you replace planks one at a time, keeping the boat functional throughout, never able to step off onto solid ground and rebuild from scratch. After many repairs, scarcely an original plank remains, yet the boat remains seaworthy throughout the process.

**The philosophical point:** Neurath's boat metaphor is coherentism's central image. The foundationalist picture holds that inquiry proceeds like building a house: you clear the site, lay the foundation, then build upward from a secure base. The coherentist insists that this picture is wrong. We arrive at inquiry already committed to a vast web of beliefs, none of which we can simply set aside to seek an independent foundation. Justification is a matter of revising and adjusting within the ongoing system — keeping the boat afloat — rather than anchoring to bedrock. The thought experiment also raises the question: if the boat has been so thoroughly repaired, is it the same boat? For the epistemologist, the parallel question is whether a belief system so thoroughly revised retains any connection to its original evidential basis.

## The Coherent Conspiracy Theorist

**The scenario:** A devoted conspiracy theorist has an internally consistent, tightly interconnected set of beliefs: the government is concealing the truth about aliens, mainstream scientists are paid to suppress the evidence, the media outlets that report contrary evidence are part of the cover-up, and anyone who questions his view has been compromised. Each element of his belief system is supported by the others; the system achieves a kind of internal coherence.

**The philosophical point:** This scenario presents the "isolation objection" in a vivid form. The coherentist says justification is a matter of belonging to a sufficiently coherent system — but the conspiracy theorist's system is coherent, yet the beliefs are clearly unjustified. The problem is that coherence is a purely internal property of a belief system; it does not guarantee any connection to the external world. BonJour tried to address this with his observation requirement (any adequate system must include beliefs attributed to the believer's own perception), but critics argue this is not enough: a sufficiently creative fiction-writer could construct a coherent system incorporating fictional observation reports.

## The Alternative Coherent World

**The scenario:** Imagine a being — call her "Omega" — who has a belief system just as coherent as yours, equally well-structured, mutually supporting, and internally consistent, but whose beliefs are systematically different: where you believe the sun is a star, Omega believes it is a divine lamp. Where you believe evolution explains the diversity of life, Omega believes a supernatural creator did. Omega's system is, by BonJour's standards, just as coherent as yours.

**The philosophical point:** This is the "alternative coherent systems" objection. If coherence is the criterion of justification, and if there can be equally coherent systems with incompatible contents, then coherentism cannot explain why your beliefs are better justified than Omega's. The objection shows that coherence, even understood richly, may be insufficient to select uniquely among competing world-views. Coherentists typically respond by insisting that true coherence includes explanatory breadth and contact with perceptual data in a way that significantly constrains which systems can count as coherent — but critics find these added constraints suspiciously foundationalist in spirit.

## The Amnesiac's Web

**The scenario:** A philosopher suffers complete amnesia. He retains his logical and inferential competencies, his vocabulary, and his sense of how beliefs ought to fit together — but he has lost all memories and all records of perception. From scratch, he begins constructing a belief system that is internally coherent, building out from purely logical and conceptual starting points. Over time he develops a rich, systematic picture of the world.

**The philosophical point:** This thought experiment tests whether coherentism can provide a starting point. If justification is entirely a matter of fit within a system, then an amnesiac with nothing but inferential skills could in principle construct a justified belief system ex nihilo — which seems wrong. The case illustrates the worry that coherentism can generate self-supporting belief systems without any connection to evidence. It motivates the question of whether coherentism can adequately account for the role that *perceptual input* plays in fixing which coherent system is the right one, or whether any account of that role will smuggle in foundationalist elements.

## The Gradual Scientist

**The scenario:** Dr. Allen develops a scientific theory that coheres well with existing evidence and background theory. Over years she gradually incorporates new data, adjusting her theory iteratively. Each adjustment is locally conservative — changing as little as possible to preserve overall coherence. Eventually her theory has evolved dramatically from its starting point, yet at no single step was there a radical break. Is the current theory justified?

**The philosophical point:** This scenario illustrates both the strength and the challenge of Quinean holism and coherentism. The strength is that it captures how science actually proceeds — not by deducing from fixed foundations but by iterative adjustment of a whole system. The challenge is the "boiling frog" worry: if each small adjustment is justified by local coherence, might the whole system drift away from truth through a series of individually reasonable steps? This raises the question of whether coherentist epistemology can adequately account for radical theory change in science (Kuhnian revolutions) and whether coherence-preserving conservatism is always epistemically virtuous.
