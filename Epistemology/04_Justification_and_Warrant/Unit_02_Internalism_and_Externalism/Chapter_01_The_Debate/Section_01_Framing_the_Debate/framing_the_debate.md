# Internalism vs. Externalism: Framing the Debate

The internalism/externalism debate is one of the central axes of disagreement in contemporary epistemology. At its heart is a question about the nature of epistemic justification: must the factors that make a belief justified be in some sense accessible to or recognizable by the believer, or can they include factors that are entirely external to the believer's perspective?

## The Central Question

Internalism holds that justification is determined by factors that are "internal" to the believer in some sense — typically, factors that are accessible to the believer through introspection or reflection. On this view, what justifies your belief is something you can, at least in principle, become aware of.

Externalism holds that what justifies a belief can include factors that are entirely "external" — beyond the believer's cognitive perspective. A reliable process, a causal connection to the fact believed, or proper function of cognitive faculties can justify a belief without the believer being aware of these facts.

This is not merely a technical dispute. It reflects deep disagreements about the purpose of epistemic justification, the relationship between justification and intellectual responsibility, and what kind of thing an epistemically adequate cognitive life requires.

## Two Conceptions of the Point of Justification

**The deontological conception** (associated with internalism): Justification is about satisfying epistemic obligations — doing one's epistemic duty, reasoning responsibly, living up to norms of inquiry. A belief is justified if you have done everything cognitively you should have done in forming it. This makes justification internal: you can only be held responsible for what is accessible to you. You cannot be faulted for failing to recognize factors you could not have recognized.

**The truth-conduciveness conception** (associated with externalism): Justification is about being in a good position to get to the truth. A belief is justified if it is formed in a way that reliably produces true beliefs. This makes justification external: it depends on the actual reliability of your cognitive processes, not on what you know about them.

On the deontological view, a brain-in-a-vat who follows all the appropriate epistemic procedures is as justified as a normal perceiver — both have done all they could. On the truth-conduciveness view, the vat brain's perceptual beliefs are unjustified because they are reliably false — it doesn't matter that the vat brain is doing everything right.

## Access Internalism and Mentalism

There are two main versions of internalism:

**Access internalism** (Chisholm, Fumerton): The factors that determine justification must be accessible to the believer through reflection or introspection. You can know, by reflecting, what your justification is.

**Mentalism** (Conee and Feldman): The factors that determine justification must be mental states of the believer — perceptions, beliefs, experiences. It's not necessary that the believer can access these states reflectively, only that they are genuinely mental.

The difference: access internalism requires that justification-makers be accessible; mentalism requires only that they be mental. Mentalism is a weaker internalism: a mental state can fail to be introspectively accessible (if the subject lacks the concepts to characterize it) while still being a justification-maker.

## The New Evil Demon Problem

The new evil demon problem is a standard intuition pump for internalism. Imagine a duplicate of you, perceptually and cognitively identical, but systematically deceived by an evil demon. Your duplicate forms beliefs using exactly the same reasoning and epistemic procedures you use. The demon makes their perceptual inputs unreliable.

Externalism implies your duplicate is not justified — their beliefs are produced by an unreliable process. But intuitively, your duplicate is doing everything right epistemically. They are being a good epistemic agent; it is not their fault that they are deceived. Surely they are justified in their beliefs, even if those beliefs are largely false.

This suggests that internalism is right: what matters for justification is the believer's internal epistemic situation, not external factors like reliability.

## The Reliabilism Challenge

The challenge for internalism comes from the opposite direction. Consider a reliable clairvoyant who forms true beliefs through a reliable cognitive process but has no idea how they formed these beliefs and no accessible reasons for them. Or consider animals and young children with reliable perceptual systems but no reflective access to the reliability of those systems.

Externalism says these subjects are justified (their beliefs are formed by reliable processes). Internalism says they are not (they can't recognize their justification through reflection).

Intuitively, the animals and children seem to have perceptual knowledge — to be in good epistemic standing — even without reflective access to the reliability of their faculties. This suggests that externalism captures something important that internalism misses.

## The Guiding Thought Experiment

The tension can be summarized: internalism fits deontological intuitions (about responsibility, doing one's epistemic best) but struggles with reliabilist cases (animals, unreflective perceivers). Externalism fits reliabilist intuitions (about truth-tracking, cognitive success) but struggles with evil-demon cases (epistemically-perfect believers who are nonetheless systematically deceived).

Most contemporary epistemologists hold hybrid positions that try to capture something from both sides.
