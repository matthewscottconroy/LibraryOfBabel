# Motivating Cases for Internalism and Externalism

The internalism/externalism debate has been driven by compelling thought experiments that pull our epistemic intuitions in different directions. Examining the key cases illuminates exactly what is at stake in the debate and why both positions have serious philosophical support.

## Cases Favoring Internalism

**The New Evil Demon**: A perfect epistemic duplicate of you exists who has been systematically deceived by an evil demon from birth. Their sensory experiences are indistinguishable from yours; they form beliefs using the same patterns of reasoning; they are as careful and thoughtful as you. Yet externalism implies their beliefs are unjustified — produced by massively unreliable processes. 

The internalist intuition: this duplicate has done everything right epistemically. They deserve the same epistemic assessment you do. If you are justified in your beliefs, so are they. Justification must depend on the internal situation, not the external facts about reliability.

**The conscientious flat-earther**: Imagine someone who has never encountered reliable sources about the shape of the Earth and has reasoned carefully on the basis of the best available evidence in their environment — all of which, unfortunately, supports the flat-earth conclusion. They believe the Earth is flat and have done their epistemic best.

Externalists imply they are unjustified (unreliable process). But intuitively, they seem epistemically blameless — they have followed all epistemic procedures conscientiously. Internalism preserves this judgment: blameless reasoning constitutes justified belief regardless of external reliability.

## Cases Favoring Externalism

**The reliable clairvoyant**: Norman has a reliable, accurate faculty of clairvoyance that occasionally delivers true beliefs about the locations of specific people. He has no idea how he comes to have these beliefs and no reasons to think clairvoyance is real. He just finds himself with the belief "the President is in Washington."

Externalists say: Norman's belief is justified (the process is reliable). Internalists say: Norman has no accessible grounds for this belief; it is epistemically groundless from his perspective and hence unjustified.

The externalist intuition: if Norman's clairvoyance is genuinely reliable, it seems perverse to say his beliefs are unjustified. If he consistently, reliably gets things right by clairvoyance, he is in a good epistemic position. Internalism's failure to recognize this seems to miss something important about the epistemic value of reliable truth-tracking.

**Animals and children**: A healthy dog reliably tracks its environment through perception. It forms beliefs (or belief-like states) about where the bone is, whether the stranger is threatening, and what is behind the bush. These beliefs are formed by reliable perceptual processes. Externalism says the dog has justified perceptual states. Internalism, with its requirement of reflective accessibility, has trouble accommodating this.

Similarly, young children have reliable perceptual beliefs before they have any capacity for reflective access to justification. It seems right to say they know what they see; internalism struggles to explain how.

## The Swamping Problem

Timothy Williamson and others have raised the "swamping" problem for certain externalist accounts (particularly in the context of the value of knowledge). If knowledge is just reliably-produced true belief, what additional value does knowledge have over mere reliably-produced true belief? Reliability swamps the value of knowledge.

This is a problem for externalism about knowledge (as opposed to justification): if what matters is just reliability, the traditional concept of knowledge may be deflated to mere reliably-produced true belief. Internalists argue that the distinctive value of justified belief — and hence knowledge — derives from its connection to epistemic responsibility and rational assessment.

## The Memory Demon Variant

Suppose an evil demon implants false memories in a population, but the memories are internally coherent and the subjects reason impeccably from them. Externalism says these subjects' memory-based beliefs are unjustified. Internalism says they are justified — they are doing everything right.

The memory demon case shows that internalist and externalist verdicts diverge sharply in systematically deceived populations. Which verdict is correct reveals one's commitments about the nature and purpose of justification.

## Compromise Positions

These motivating cases have led many philosophers to seek positions that honor both sets of intuitions. One approach: distinguish between *epistemic justification* (the internalist concept, concerned with rational responsibility) and *epistemic warrant* (the externalist concept, concerned with truth-conduciveness). Both are genuine and important, but they are different things.

Plantinga's proper function view attempts a hybrid: warrant requires proper function (external) and a design plan aimed at truth (connecting to epistemic goals). BonJour's later work moves from coherentism toward a view that combines internalist and externalist elements.

The proliferation of hybrid views suggests that neither pure internalism nor pure externalism captures the full complexity of epistemic evaluation — a conclusion that has motivated ongoing theoretical development in both camps.
