# Key Figures: The Internalism/Externalism Debate

## Roderick Chisholm (1916–1999)

Chisholm is the paradigmatic access internalist. His systematic epistemology, developed across three editions of *Theory of Knowledge*, holds that all epistemic concepts — justification, evidence, reasonability — are to be defined by reference to what a reflective believer can determine about the epistemic status of her own beliefs. For Chisholm, epistemic justification is a normative concept tied to what a person *ought* to believe given what is epistemically available to her from the inside. This deontological internalism implies that two believers in identical internal states must have the same epistemic status for any given belief, regardless of how their beliefs were formed or what the external world is like.

## Alvin Goldman (b. 1938)

Goldman's "What Is Justified Belief?" (1979) is the founding document of process reliabilism and the most influential statement of epistemic externalism. Goldman argues that what makes a belief justified is not whether the believer has reflective access to its basis, but whether it was produced by a cognitive process that reliably produces true beliefs. Because reliable processes typically operate below the threshold of conscious awareness, Goldman's account explicitly disconnects justification from reflective access. Goldman has also been a pioneering advocate of naturalized epistemology — the project of grounding epistemic norms in empirical psychology and cognitive science.

## Laurence BonJour (1943–2022)

BonJour's career traces a remarkable arc relevant to the internalism/externalism debate. In his 1980 critique of externalism he argued powerfully that externalist accounts fail to capture what matters epistemically — namely, having reasons one can use to guide inquiry. In *The Structure of Empirical Knowledge* (1985) he defended coherentism from an internalist standpoint. Later, in *Epistemic Justification* (2003, with Sosa), he came to accept that his coherentist internalism faced insuperable problems and moved toward a modest rationalist foundationalism. His trajectory illustrates the pressures on sophisticated internalism from both sides.

## Earl Conee (b. 1948) and Richard Feldman (b. 1948)

Conee and Feldman jointly defend "mentalism" — the view that what justifies a belief is solely a function of the believer's mental states. Their mentalism is an internalist position because mental states are constitutively internal to the subject, though they resist the access internalist's additional claim that the believer must always be able to identify which mental states do the justifying. Their paper "Internalism Defended" (2001) is the clearest contemporary statement of why internalism captures intuitions that externalism misses, particularly the new evil demon intuition. Together they also developed "evidentialism," the view that justification is a matter of fitting one's evidence.

## William Alston (1921–2009)

Alston occupies an interesting middle position in the internalism/externalism debate. He distinguishes multiple senses of "internalism" and argues that some are compatible with externalism about justification. His own position — "reliabilist foundationalism" or later "direct perception of God" in religious epistemology — draws on both internalist intuitions (the importance of direct acquaintance) and externalist ones (the importance of reliability). Alston's careful taxonomic work in *Epistemic Justification* (1989) revealed that the debate was partially obscured by terminological confusion and that different internalist and externalist conditions serve different legitimate epistemic functions.

## Michael Bergmann (b. 1964)

Bergmann's *Justification without Awareness* (2006) is the most systematic contemporary defense of externalism against access internalism. He argues that the access internalist's requirement — that to be justified in believing P, one must have access to one's justification for P — generates an infinite regress: access to one's justification would itself require justification, and so on. Bergmann contends that this regress cannot be terminated without either accepting some unjustified access beliefs (which undermines the access internalist's own motivation) or abandoning the access requirement altogether. His work represents the most technically rigorous response to the internalist challenge from the externalist side.

## Richard Fumerton (b. 1949)

Fumerton's "acquaintance" internalism provides a distinctive version of the internalist position. In *Metaepistemology and Skepticism* (1995), he argues that non-inferential justification requires the believer to be directly acquainted with the truth-making fact — a relation that is both epistemically internal (constituted by the believer's own cognitive position) and philosophically robust (it is not merely a matter of having the right phenomenal state). Fumerton's internalism is notable for taking skepticism seriously: he accepts that if internalism is true, our access to the external world is genuinely uncertain, and he treats this as a reason to think carefully about internalism rather than a reductio.
