# Accessibilism: The Case for Internal Access

Accessibilism is the version of internalism that holds that the factors determining epistemic justification must be *accessible* to the believer — available to be recognized or identified through reflection or introspection. On this view, what makes your belief justified is something you can, at least in principle, become aware of by attending to your epistemic situation. If the justification-maker is entirely hidden from you, it cannot properly serve the epistemic role that justification is supposed to play.

## The Core Commitment

The accessibilist's guiding thought: justification is about *guiding belief*. Epistemic reasons are reasons that can govern how you form beliefs. But you can only be guided by reasons you can access. If the factors that make a belief justified are entirely inaccessible — if you could in principle never recognize them — they cannot guide your believing.

This connects accessibilism to the *deontological* conception of justification: justification is the epistemic property a belief has when formed through epistemically responsible reasoning. And responsible reasoning requires the capacity to recognize what you're doing and whether it's appropriate. An agent who forms beliefs on the basis of hidden, inaccessible mechanisms cannot be epistemically evaluated for whether they are meeting their obligations.

## Fumerton's Accessibilism

Richard Fumerton has developed one of the most careful accessibilist positions. Fumerton distinguishes between the *justification-maker* (the fact that makes a belief justified) and the *belief that one has the justification-maker* (the meta-belief). Accessibilism requires that one can form a justified meta-belief — a belief about one's justification — through reflection.

This means justification has a kind of reflective transparency: if you are justified, you can know (or at least be justified in believing) that you are justified. The justification-makers are in the domain of things you can introspect, reflect on, and become aware of.

Fumerton specifically argues that this accessibility condition is met by phenomenal states — the "felt" qualities of experience. These are directly accessible through introspection. What is not directly accessible — the external world, the reliability of processes, the causal origins of beliefs — cannot be a justification-maker in the accessibilist sense.

## Chisholm's Internalism

Roderick Chisholm developed a deontological internalism explicitly connected to the notion of epistemic obligation. For Chisholm, epistemic terms like "justified" are fundamentally normative: to say that a belief is justified is to say that the believer has done their epistemic duty in forming it.

Duties require that the subject be able to recognize whether they are fulfilling them. Hence, the factors determining justification must be recognizable — accessible to the believer through the exercise of their rational faculties. Chisholm worked out a hierarchy of epistemic statuses (certain, obvious, manifest, evident, beyond reasonable doubt, more reasonable than not) in terms of what is accessible through direct consciousness.

## The Metajustification Regress

One objection to accessibilism is the metajustification regress. If justification requires a justified meta-belief (that one has the relevant justification), then the meta-belief also requires a justified meta-meta-belief, and so on. Accessibilism generates a regress of higher-order justifications.

Accessibilists can respond by distinguishing between a belief being justified and a belief requiring explicit justification: the accessibility condition requires that the justification-makers be accessible, not that the believer has actually formed beliefs about them. The access is a potential, not an actuality.

But critics press: if the potential is enough, and if subjects often don't exercise this potential, then accessibilism may not be significantly more demanding than externalism in practice. The distinctive character of accessibilism lies in the normative structure, not the psychological reality.

## Accessibilism and the Value of Self-Knowledge

One appeal of accessibilism is that it connects epistemic justification to epistemic self-knowledge in a philosophically important way. If the factors that make your beliefs justified are accessible to you, then cultivating good epistemic self-knowledge — understanding your evidence, your reasoning processes, your potential biases — is directly relevant to epistemic excellence.

Accessibilism makes epistemic virtue and epistemic reflection substantively important, not merely peripheral to the acquisition of justified beliefs. This is part of why accessibilist positions naturally connect to virtue epistemology and to normative epistemological theory more broadly.
