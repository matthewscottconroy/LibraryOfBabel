# Mentalism: Mental States as the Basis of Justification

Mentalism is an internalist position that identifies the justification-makers with the believer's mental states, rather than requiring that these states be reflectively accessible. On the mentalist account, what makes a belief justified is always something about the believer's mental life — their perceptual experiences, beliefs, and memories — but these states need not be consciously available or introspectively accessible. The position is associated primarily with Earl Conee and Richard Feldman.

## The Core Claim

The mentalist slogan: epistemic justification supervenes on the believer's mental states. Two believers who are in exactly the same mental states — with exactly the same experiences, beliefs, and memories — are equally justified in their beliefs. No purely external factor (the reliability of processes, causal chains to external facts) can affect justification.

This makes mentalism clearly internalist: the justification-makers are internal to the subject's mind. But it is not access internalism: the mental states that determine justification need not be accessible to the believer through reflection.

Consider: you have many beliefs you are not currently entertaining. Your belief that the Earth orbits the Sun, that water is H₂O, that your best friend's name is James — these are all present in your mental economy but may not be consciously accessible to you right now. Mentalism holds that these background beliefs can still contribute to the justification of your other beliefs, even when not accessible.

## Conee and Feldman's Evidentialism

Conee and Feldman develop mentalism through their evidentialist theory: a belief is justified if and only if it fits the believer's evidence, and a believer's evidence consists entirely of their mental states. This is "evidence-based" justification, where the evidence base is mental.

The evidentialist claim: there is always a fact about what evidence a believer has (determined by their mental states), and there is always a fact about what that evidence supports. Justification consists in believing what your evidence supports.

This is internalist because the evidence is entirely mental. It is not access-internalist because you need not currently be able to articulate your evidence to have it.

## Mentalism and the New Evil Demon

Mentalism handles the new evil demon problem cleanly: a deceived duplicate with the same mental states as you has the same evidence you do and hence is equally justified. The demon's intervention changes only external facts, not internal mental states. Since justification supervenes on mental states alone, the victim is as justified as the non-deceived believer.

This is the principal advantage of mentalism over externalism on the internalism/externalism debate's central test case.

## The Generality Problem for Mentalism

Mentalism faces the question: which mental states constitute the evidence relevant to justification of a given belief? Not all of one's beliefs are equally relevant to justifying any given belief. The belief that the sky is blue is relevant to the justification of my belief about the sky's color but not to my belief about mathematical induction.

Conee and Feldman respond that the relevant mental states are those that bear on the truth of the belief being evaluated. But determining which states bear on which beliefs requires a theory of relevance that is not trivially provided by mentalism itself.

## The Problem of Possessed but Unaccessed Evidence

A distinctive feature of mentalism is that it allows unaccessed evidence to contribute to justification. Suppose you believe P, and also believe Q (which strongly supports P), but in forming the belief P you did not consult or use Q as a reason. Is P justified by Q?

Feldman's answer: yes, if Q is part of your evidence and supports P. Justification consists in having evidence that supports the belief, not in actually using that evidence in the process of forming it. This distinguishes justification from the basing relation: a belief can be justified (you have good evidence) without being properly based (you didn't reason from that evidence).

This is controversial. Many epistemologists think that for a belief to be justified, it must be appropriately connected to the believer's evidence — it must be *based on* that evidence, not merely consistent with it. Mentalism's liberal account of justification (evidence supports regardless of basing) is contested.

## Mentalism and Virtue Epistemology

Mentalism connects naturally to certain virtue epistemological positions: intellectual virtues are dispositions of the mind that shape what evidence one acquires and how one reasons from it. A virtuous believer's mental states constitute a better evidential base for their beliefs than a non-virtuous believer's. Mentalism can thus serve as the underlying account of justification within a virtue-epistemological framework that emphasizes the role of character in shaping the quality of one's mental evidence.
