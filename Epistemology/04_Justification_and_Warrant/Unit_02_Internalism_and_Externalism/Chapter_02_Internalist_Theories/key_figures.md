# Key Figures: Internalist Theories

## Roderick Chisholm (1916–1999)

Chisholm is the defining figure of deontological internalism. Throughout his career he maintained that epistemic concepts are irreducibly normative and that their application depends entirely on what the believer can determine through reflection — what he called the "internal" or "autonomous" features of the believer's epistemic situation. His core claim is that the concept of epistemic justification is best understood in terms of epistemic duties or obligations: a belief is justified if holding it does not violate any epistemic obligation the believer has, given what they can determine from the inside about the evidence available to them. Chisholm's "particularist" methodology — beginning with concrete cases of what we know and working backward to criteria — also significantly shaped epistemological method.

## Earl Conee (b. 1948) and Richard Feldman (b. 1948)

Working together, Conee and Feldman developed "evidentialism" as the most influential contemporary internalist theory. Their central claim is that a belief is justified if and only if it fits the believer's evidence, where evidence consists entirely of the believer's mental states. Their mentalist internalism holds that justification supervenes on mental states: any two believers in identical mental states are equally justified (or unjustified) in their beliefs, regardless of how those beliefs were formed or what the external world is like. Their *Evidentialism: Essays in Epistemology* (2004) collects their major papers, including responses to the generality problem and the new evil demon, and constitutes the most thorough development of a mentalist internalism.

## Richard Fumerton (b. 1949)

Fumerton defends "acquaintance" internalism, the view that what makes a belief non-inferentially justified is that the believer is directly acquainted with the fact that makes the belief true, with the belief itself, and with the relation of correspondence between them. This is a demanding version of internalism: it is not enough to have the right phenomenal experience; the believer must stand in an actual cognitive relation to the truth-making fact. Fumerton's willingness to accept the skeptical implications of his view — that we may lack justification for many of our ordinary perceptual beliefs — marks him as an unusually rigorous internalist. He develops this position most fully in *Metaepistemology and Skepticism* (1995) and *Epistemology* (2006).

## Laurence BonJour (1943–2022)

BonJour's contribution to internalist theories spans two phases. In his early coherentist phase, he argued from internalist premises that no externalist account can capture what it is for a believer to *have reasons* — to be able to justify one's beliefs from within. His 1980 paper "Externalist Theories of Empirical Knowledge" is the most incisive early critique of reliabilism from an internalist perspective. In his later phase, having abandoned coherentism, BonJour defended a rationalist foundationalism that is also internalist: the ultimate justifiers are a priori rational intuitions that are accessible to any competent reasoner through reflection. His trajectory illustrates both the power and the pressure points of internalist epistemology.

## William Alston (1921–2009)

Alston's contribution to internalist theories is primarily taxonomic and critical. In *Epistemic Justification* (1989), he distinguishes between "strong" internalism (requiring the believer to be aware of their justifiers) and "weak" internalism (requiring only that justifiers be internal mental states), and he argues that neither version succeeds as a complete account of justification. His critique of deontological internalism — the view that justification is a matter of epistemic duty-fulfillment — is particularly influential: he argues that duties presuppose doxastic voluntarism (the ability to control one's beliefs by will), which is implausible as a general psychological thesis. Alston's critique helped clarify what internalists must commit to in order to hold their position consistently.

## Ralph Wedgwood (b. 1965)

Wedgwood's *The Nature of Normativity* (2007) develops a broadly internalist account of normativity that grounds epistemic norms in the nature of the relevant mental states rather than in external facts. His "internalism about rationality" holds that what it is rational for an agent to believe is determined by what is correct from the agent's own perspective — that is, by the standards implicit in the agent's own mental states and conceptual scheme. Wedgwood's view has been influential in the debate about "higher-order evidence" and epistemic akrasia, connecting internalist considerations to broader questions about the structure of rational agency.

## Stewart Cohen (b. 1954)

Cohen's contribution to internalist theories is primarily the articulation and deployment of the new evil demon intuition as a constraint on acceptable theories of justification. His paper "Justification and Truth" (1984) argued that reliabilism fails to account for the intuition that the victims of an evil demon are epistemically blameless and hence justified in their beliefs. Cohen's framing of this intuition — that justification has a primarily *personal* or *subjective* dimension that tracks how well the agent is managing her epistemic situation from the inside — became the standard way of motivating internalism in subsequent debates.
