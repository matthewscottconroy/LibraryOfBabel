# Thought Experiments: Internalist Theories

## The Conscientious Reasoner in Demon World

**The scenario:** Maria is a careful, thoughtful epistemic agent. She reasons well, weighs her evidence fairly, revises her beliefs in response to new information, and never holds beliefs more strongly than her evidence warrants. Unfortunately, an evil demon has been feeding her systematically false perceptual experiences since birth. All of her beliefs about the external world are false — there are no tables, chairs, or other people; her entire experiential life is an elaborate illusion. Is Maria epistemically blameworthy for her false beliefs?

**The philosophical point:** Evidentialism says no — Maria's beliefs fit her evidence (her phenomenal states), and that is all that matters for justification. Reliabilism says that her beliefs are unjustified, since they are produced by an unreliable process (demonic deception). The internalist argues that this consequence of reliabilism is unacceptable: it makes Maria guilty of epistemic negligence when she has done everything that could possibly be demanded of her from the inside. This thought experiment is the strongest argument that the concept of justification must track something internal — the believer's own epistemic performance — rather than the external reliability of her cognitive processes.

## The Metajustification Regress

**The scenario:** Alex believes P on the basis of her evidence E. An internalist says Alex is justified in believing P because E justifies P. But now ask: is Alex justified in believing that E justifies P? Presumably she needs a reason for that meta-belief too. If she believes that E justifies P on the basis of some further evidence E*, then she must be justified in believing that E* justifies [E justifies P] — and so on, apparently without end.

**The philosophical point:** This is the "metajustification regress" — a regress that operates at the level of the standards for justification rather than the level of first-order beliefs. The regress threatens internalism specifically because internalists typically require that justifiers be accessible to the believer, which seems to imply that the believer must be justified in believing that their justifiers justify their beliefs. Some internalists respond that this meta-level belief can be justified non-inferentially, by a kind of rational insight into evidential relations. Others argue that access internalism should be distinguished from the implausible view that one must explicitly justify one's justificatory standards before being justified in any first-order belief.

## The Sleeping Evidence

**The scenario:** Jordan has amassed excellent evidence for the existence of climate change: she has read dozens of studies, attended scientific conferences, and has a detailed understanding of the methodology. One night she takes a medication that induces a brief amnesiac state. When she wakes, she has completely forgotten all the evidence, though she still believes in climate change. Twenty minutes later, her memories return fully. During those twenty minutes, is she justified in her belief?

**The philosophical point:** This case tests the evidentialist account of justification. For the evidentialist, the answer turns on what Jordan's evidence is at the time of the belief: if her current phenomenal mental states include no relevant evidence, then she is not justified during the amnesiac episode, even though she was justified moments before and will be again moments later. This may seem like too demanding a view — her belief seems, in some sense, to remain rational. The case pushes the question of whether the evidentialist can account for dispositional or "standing" evidence — evidence that a person has but is not currently entertaining — and whether justification must be grounded in occurrently active mental states.

## The Invisible Access Problem

**The scenario:** A philosopher argues that internalism requires that justifiers be "accessible" to the believer. But when she introspects on her belief that 2+2=4, she finds that she cannot identify any specific mental state that justifies it — the justification seems simply to be "there" without any identifiable access route. Similarly, her belief that she is in pain seems immediately justified without her being able to identify a further mental state that does the justifying.

**The philosophical point:** This thought experiment challenges access internalism by pointing to cases where internalists must posit accessible justifiers but cannot coherently describe what they are. Access internalists like Fumerton respond that in cases of basic knowledge, the "access" is the acquaintance relation itself — one is directly acquainted with one's pain or with the mathematical truth, and this acquaintance is the justifier. Critics argue that this move either makes "access" too thin to be informative or collapses into a form of foundationalism that posits non-inferentially justified beliefs without explaining why they are accessible in any epistemically significant sense.

## Chisholm's Trusting Child

**The scenario:** A young child, Alex, forms beliefs about the world entirely on the basis of sensory experiences she cannot yet critically evaluate. She has no concept of evidence, no awareness of what could undermine her beliefs, and no ability to reflect on whether her perceptual faculties are reliable. Yet her beliefs about nearby objects are reliably true and form in response to genuine perceptual information. Is Alex justified in her perceptual beliefs?

**The philosophical point:** This case creates a dilemma for deontological internalism. If justification requires the ability to fulfill epistemic duties — to assess evidence, respond to reasons, reflect on one's epistemic situation — then the child is not justified, since she lacks the reflective capacity. But this seems too harsh: surely the child who accurately perceives the cup in front of her is justified in believing the cup is there. Chisholm tried to handle such cases by distinguishing the "level" of justification available to different kinds of believers, but critics argue that the deontological framing simply does not fit pre-reflective cognition. The case motivates either a move toward reliabilism or a significant revision of what "access" requires.
