# Reliabilism: Process, Truth, and Justification

Process reliabilism, associated primarily with Alvin Goldman, is the most influential externalist theory of epistemic justification. Its central claim: a belief is justified if and only if it is produced by a cognitive process that reliably generates true beliefs. What matters for justification is not the believer's internal perspective but the objective reliability of the process that produced the belief.

## The Core Theory

Goldman's formulation: S's belief in P is justified if P was produced (or sustained) by a cognitive process that is reliable — that is, one that has a tendency to produce true beliefs rather than false ones. Reliability is an objective, statistical property: a process is reliable if, across the range of conditions in which it operates, it produces truths much more often than falsehoods.

Perception is typically reliable: looking carefully at a nearby object in good lighting produces accurate beliefs about the object's properties. Memory is typically reliable: accurately remembering past events is more the rule than the exception. Systematic inference from good evidence is typically reliable. So beliefs produced by these processes are typically justified.

By contrast: wishful thinking, guessing, sloppy inference from insufficient evidence, and beliefs formed under the influence of cognitive biases are unreliable processes. Beliefs produced by these processes are unjustified, regardless of how they feel from the inside.

## Two-Level Reliabilism

Goldman distinguishes between *process* reliabilism (the basic version) and *historical* or *origin* reliabilism, which attends to the entire history of a belief's production. Even if a belief is currently maintained by a reliable process, if it was originally formed by an unreliable one, its justificatory status may be compromised.

Goldman also develops a *two-level* or *meta-level* account: justification requires not only that the belief-forming process be reliable but that the believer not use unreliable meta-level beliefs about their processes. If you falsely believe your clairvoyance is reliable, and use this false belief to license the clairvoyant beliefs, the result is unjustified even if the clairvoyance happens to be reliable.

## Advantages of Reliabilism

**It handles the animals-and-children cases**: Animals and pre-reflective children with reliable perceptual systems have justified perceptual beliefs. Internalism struggles with these cases; reliabilism handles them naturally.

**It connects justification to truth**: Reliably-produced beliefs are likely true. This gives justification its point — it indicates that the belief is truth-tracking. Internalist accounts that focus on subjective rationality can produce justified beliefs that are systematically false (in evil-demon scenarios), which seems to miss the epistemic point.

**It accommodates scientific knowledge**: The methods of science — careful observation, controlled experiment, statistical analysis — are paradigmatically reliable processes. Reliabilism vindicates scientific knowledge as genuinely justified.

## The Generality Problem

The most serious objection to reliabilism is the *generality problem*: the same belief-forming event can be described as an instance of many different processes, and these processes can have different reliability profiles. Seeing a red apple in good lighting can be described as "vision," "vision in good lighting," "vision in good lighting in front of a red object," "this particular act of looking at this apple." Each description picks out a process with a different reliability profile.

Which description — which type — is the relevant one for determining justification? If we choose too narrow a type (this particular act), reliability has no real content. If we choose too broad a type (perception), we ignore relevant variations in reliability.

Goldman has proposed various criteria for selecting the relevant type: natural types, types defined by the believer's cognitive architecture, types relevant to the ecological conditions in which the believer operates. None has achieved consensus.

## The Demonic Scenario

The new evil demon scenario is the main objection from the internalist side. The demon scenario pumps the intuition that the justified believer and their systematically deceived duplicate deserve the same epistemic assessment. Reliabilism says they don't: the deceived duplicate's processes are unreliable.

Goldman's response involves the concept of "normal worlds": justification should be assessed relative to the reliability of processes in the normal world — the world as it typically is, not in extraordinary philosophical scenarios. In this normal world, the processes that produce the deceived duplicate's beliefs are reliable (since they are the same processes that work reliably in ordinary conditions). This narrows the gap between reliabilist and internalist verdicts.

But critics find this response ad hoc: why should justification be relativized to "normal worlds" rather than the actual world?

## The Value Problem

Another challenge: if knowledge is reliably-produced true belief, why is knowledge more valuable than reliably-produced true belief? The reliability already ensures the belief is likely true; adding that the belief is justified (by reliability) seems to add nothing of value.

This "swamping problem" suggests that reliabilist justification cannot explain the special value of knowledge. Some epistemologists take this as evidence against reliabilism; others argue the value problem affects all accounts of knowledge and is not specific to reliabilism.
