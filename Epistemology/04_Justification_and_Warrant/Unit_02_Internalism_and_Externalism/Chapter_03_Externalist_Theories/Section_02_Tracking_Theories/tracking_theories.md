# Tracking Theories of Knowledge and Justification

Tracking theories hold that knowledge requires that your belief track the truth of the proposition believed: if P were false, you would not believe P; and if P is true (in the relevant way), you would believe P. This sensitivity to the truth of P is what makes your belief knowledge rather than mere true belief or Gettiered belief. Robert Nozick developed the most influential tracking theory in *Philosophical Explanations* (1981).

## Nozick's Sensitivity and Adherence

Nozick analyzes knowledge in terms of two conditions:

**Sensitivity** (the subjunctive conditional): If P were false, S would not believe P.

**Adherence**: If P is true (and in a wide range of situations in which P is true), S would believe P.

Sensitivity ensures that your belief is not accidentally true — if the proposition were false, your belief would change with it. Adherence ensures that you would reliably form the belief in the variety of situations where the proposition holds.

The addition of sensitivity and adherence to truth and belief (and some reliability in forming beliefs) is Nozick's attempt to capture what Gettier cases lack: the belief tracks the truth of the proposition, not just coincidentally matching it.

## The Method Clause

Nozick acknowledges that sensitivity needs to be relativized to the *method* by which the belief is formed. If you believe you have hands by seeing them in the mirror, and the close possible world in which you have no hands is one where you have suffered an accident (not one where you are a brain in a vat), then the sensitivity condition seems satisfied for the right method.

The method clause prevents sensitivity from being too easily satisfied: it's not just whether you'd believe P if P were false, but whether you'd believe P *by this method* if P were false. Different methods have different sensitivity profiles.

## Tracking and Skepticism

One of the most striking features of Nozick's tracking account is its approach to skepticism. The brain-in-a-vat hypothesis involves a distant possible world where you have no hands. The sensitivity condition for "I have hands" requires that in the closest possible world where you have no hands, you don't believe you do. The closest such world is not the brain-in-a-vat world (which is very distant) but the ordinary world where you've had an accident.

In that ordinary close-by world, you would not believe you have hands (you'd know about the accident). So the sensitivity condition for "I have hands" is satisfied by normal visual perception. You know you have hands.

But you don't know you're not a brain in a vat. The sensitivity condition for "I am not a brain in a vat" requires that in the closest world where you are a brain in a vat, you don't believe you're not a brain in a vat. But if you were a brain in a vat, you'd still believe you weren't (since the vat would deceive you). So the sensitivity condition fails, and you don't know you're not a brain in a vat.

Nozick accepts this: you can know P (I have hands) without knowing that a skeptical alternative to P is false (I'm not a brain in a vat). This requires *denying closure* — the principle that if you know P and know that P entails Q, you know Q.

## The Closure Denial

Nozick's denial of closure has been one of the most discussed aspects of his view. Closure seems deeply intuitive: how can you know P but fail to know something P logically entails? Nozick's response: knowledge tracks truth, and tracking is not closed under entailment. You can track P (be sensitive to it) without tracking everything P entails.

Critics find closure denial deeply problematic. Even if tracking doesn't transmit via entailment, knowledge seems to. The appearance of knowledge without closure is an artifact of the tracking analysis, not a genuine epistemic phenomenon.

## Dretske's Relevant Alternatives

Fred Dretske independently developed a similar approach through his "relevant alternatives" framework. On this account, you know P if you can rule out the relevant alternatives to P. What counts as "relevant" depends on context — the options that are live or salient given the epistemic situation.

Skeptical scenarios (brain-in-a-vat) are not relevant alternatives in ordinary contexts, so failing to rule them out doesn't undermine knowledge. In skeptical philosophical contexts, they may become relevant, which is why skeptical arguments can seem compelling — they make irrelevant alternatives relevant.

Dretske's account converges with contextualism in its attention to which alternatives must be ruled out, but unlike contextualism, it locates the sensitivity in the objective epistemic situation (what's relevant to rule out) rather than in the semantics of "knows."

## Objections to Tracking Theories

**Lottery cases**: You know you'll lose the lottery (you almost certainly will), but the lottery seems to violate sensitivity: in the close possible world where you win, you believe you'll lose. Tracking theories seem to imply you don't know you'll lose.

**Barn facade cases**: In fake barn country, you point to a real barn and believe "that's a barn." The closest barn-is-not-there world is one where a fake is there and you'd still believe it's a barn. So you fail the sensitivity test, but intuitively you know there's a barn there.

**The method problem**: The method clause for sensitivity is difficult to specify precisely. With appropriate gerrymandering of methods, almost any belief can be made to satisfy or fail the sensitivity condition.
