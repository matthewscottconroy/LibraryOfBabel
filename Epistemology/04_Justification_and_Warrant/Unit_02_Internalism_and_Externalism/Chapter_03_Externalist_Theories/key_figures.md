# Key Figures: Externalist Theories

## Alvin Goldman (b. 1938)

Goldman is the founder of process reliabilism and the most influential externalist in contemporary epistemology. His 1979 paper "What Is Justified Belief?" proposed that a belief is justified if and only if it is the output of a cognitive process that reliably produces true beliefs — making justification a function of external, causal facts about the world rather than of the believer's reflective access to reasons. Goldman has extended this framework in *Epistemology and Cognition* (1986) and through his development of "social epistemology," applying reliabilist standards to testimony, peer disagreement, and institutional knowledge production. His career represents the most sustained and sophisticated development of externalist epistemology.

## Frederick Dretske (1932–2017)

Dretske developed an information-theoretic account of knowledge and an influential "relevant alternatives" framework. In *Knowledge and the Flow of Information* (1981), he argued that to know that P is to have one's belief caused by the information that P — where information is understood in terms of the objective causal-probabilistic structure of the world. His relevant alternatives approach holds that knowledge is always relativized to a set of alternatives that are relevant in a context: you know P if you can rule out the relevant alternatives to P, even if you cannot rule out far-fetched alternatives like evil demons. Dretske's account combines externalist foundations with context-sensitivity, influencing both tracking theories and contextualism.

## Robert Nozick (1938–2002)

Nozick's "tracking" theory of knowledge in *Philosophical Explanations* (1981) is one of the most discussed externalist positions. He proposed two subjunctive conditional conditions: *sensitivity* — if P were false, the agent would not believe P — and *adherence* — if P were true, the agent would believe P. Knowing requires that one's belief track the truth in this modal sense, which is an external condition depending on the counterfactual structure of the world. Nozick also explicitly denied the closure principle (that if one knows P and knows P entails Q, one knows Q), which allowed him to respond to skepticism: we can know ordinary propositions without knowing that we are not brains in vats.

## Alvin Plantinga (b. 1932)

Plantinga's proper functionalism, developed in his *Warrant* trilogy, is the most theologically nuanced version of externalism. He argues that knowledge requires *warrant* — a property over and above true belief — and that warrant consists in a belief being produced by cognitive faculties functioning properly, in an appropriate environment, according to a design plan successfully aimed at truth. Proper function is defined against a "design plan" that may be either theological (God's design) or naturalistic (evolutionary design), though Plantinga argues that only theism can ultimately vindicate the claim that our faculties are genuinely aimed at truth. Plantinga's externalism is notable for its integration of epistemology with philosophy of religion and metaphysics.

## John Greco (b. 1961)

Greco's "agent reliabilism" is a version of externalism that incorporates agential and dispositional elements. He argues that knowledge requires not just that a belief be produced by a reliable process, but that the reliable process be an *ability* or *competence* of the agent — something attributable to the agent as a cognitive achiever rather than merely something that happens to the agent. This emphasis on the agent's stable dispositions allows Greco to handle cases where an individual agent happens to be in circumstances that trigger reliable processes that are not properly the agent's own competencies. His agent reliabilism forms a bridge between simple process reliabilism and virtue epistemology.

## Timothy Williamson (b. 1955)

Williamson's *Knowledge and Its Limits* (2000) defends a "knowledge-first" epistemology that is broadly externalist but differs from standard reliabilism. Rather than analyzing knowledge in terms of justification plus other conditions, Williamson takes knowledge as primitive and treats justification, evidence, and rationality as derived from it. His equation E=K — that one's evidence just is what one knows — is a paradigmatically externalist commitment: evidence is not whatever phenomenally seems to be the case to the believer, but the body of facts one actually knows. Williamson's externalism is also notable for its integration with formal epistemology and his anti-luminosity argument, which challenges the internalist claim that one always knows what mental states one is in.

## Ernest Sosa (b. 1940)

Sosa's virtue epistemology is a sophisticated form of externalism that preserves internalist insights. He distinguishes "animal knowledge" — reliably produced true belief — from "reflective knowledge" — belief that is also rationally integrated into a coherent and self-understood system of beliefs. While animal knowledge is straightforwardly externalist (requiring only reliable competence), reflective knowledge adds an internalist dimension: the believer must understand why her beliefs are reliable and be able to situate them within a reflective account of her own epistemic position. Sosa's distinction allows him to acknowledge the force of internalist intuitions without abandoning the externalist core.
