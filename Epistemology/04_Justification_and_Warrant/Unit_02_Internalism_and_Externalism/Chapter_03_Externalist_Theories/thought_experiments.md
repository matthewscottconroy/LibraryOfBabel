# Thought Experiments: Externalist Theories

## The Reliable Thermometer

**The scenario:** A reliable thermometer displays "72°F." A person reads the display and forms the belief that the room is 72°F. The thermometer is functioning correctly, is well-calibrated, and the reading is accurate. The person has no independent way of checking the thermometer's reliability; she simply reads it and believes. Is her belief justified?

**The philosophical point:** Reliabilists say yes — the belief is produced by a reliable belief-forming process (reading an accurate thermometer), so it is justified. This case illustrates the intuitive appeal of reliabilism: it seems that consulting a known-reliable instrument should produce justified beliefs, and what matters is whether the instrument and the reading process are reliable, not whether the person can independently verify this. Critics note, however, that a thermometer is not an epistemic *agent* — it does not *know* that the temperature is 72°F — and press the question of whether human knowers differ from thermometers in ways that matter for justification.

## The Generality Problem in Action

**The scenario:** Maria forms a belief that there is a red cup on the table by seeing it in good lighting from a normal distance. What cognitive process produced her belief? Candidates include: (a) vision; (b) color vision; (c) vision of medium-sized objects in normal lighting; (d) visual belief-formation in this particular circumstance; (e) vision by human adults in the 21st century. Each of these process-types has a different reliability level — some are more specific and reliable, others broader and less so. Which one determines whether her belief is justified?

**The philosophical point:** This is the generality problem for reliabilism. The reliability of a process depends entirely on which type the process is classified under, and reliabilism provides no principled way to fix the relevant type. Different type specifications yield different verdicts about whether the same belief is justified. The problem is not merely technical: it shows that reliabilism as stated is incomplete and that completing it requires some further account of how process types are to be individuated — which may itself require appeal to internalist considerations about how the believer represents the process.

## Nozick's Barn Country (Sensitivity Revisited)

**The scenario:** Henry drives through an unusual county where most of what look like barns from the road are actually papier-mâché facades, though there are a few genuine barns mixed in. Henry looks at a genuine barn, forms the belief "that is a barn," and his belief is true. However, if the barn he happened to be looking at were not genuine (as most in the area are not), he would still believe it was a barn — he cannot tell the difference from the road.

**The philosophical point:** This is a variant of Goldman's barn case, adapted to test Nozick's sensitivity condition. Henry's belief fails sensitivity: if the object were not a barn (but rather a facade, which is the dominant case in the area), he would still believe it is a barn. Hence, by Nozick's lights, Henry does not know that it is a barn — he is, in the relevant modal sense, lucky. The case illustrates why the tracking theory requires more than reliability: even if Henry's belief-forming process is reliable on average, his specific belief in barn country fails because it does not covary with the truth in the right modal way.

## Plantinga's Warm Days and Proper Function

**The scenario:** A person with a neurological disorder believes, whenever it is warm outside, that the sun is a large sphere of plasma ninety-three million miles away. This belief is true, and it is reliably produced in the sense that it is produced whenever the appropriate stimulus is present. But the neurological disorder means it is produced by a cognitive module that is malfunctioning — it was originally involved in producing beliefs about the person's own body temperature, not about astronomy.

**The philosophical point:** Plantinga uses such cases to argue that reliability is not sufficient for warrant (the property that converts true belief into knowledge). What matters is not merely that a process reliably produces true beliefs, but that it is functioning *properly* — operating as it was designed (or selected) to operate. A malfunctioning process might happen to reliably produce true beliefs without the beliefs thereby having warrant. This thought experiment motivates the shift from process reliabilism to proper functionalism: the right account of externalist warrant must include a normative condition about the design plan of the cognitive system.

## The New Evil Demon Against Reliabilism

**The scenario:** Two epistemic communities, Earth and Twin Earth, are identical in every internal respect. Earth people use reliable perceptual faculties; Twin Earth people live in a systematically deceptive environment created by a powerful evil demon, and their perceptual faculties are therefore unreliable (even though the faculties themselves are intrinsically identical to Earth people's). An Earth person and a Twin Earth person both form the belief "there is a cup on the table" under identical phenomenal conditions. The Earth person is justified (by the reliabilist's lights); the Twin Earth person is not.

**The philosophical point:** Reliabilism says the Twin Earth person is not justified because her belief-forming process is unreliable. But this strikes many as deeply wrong: the Twin Earth person is doing everything epistemically right; she is blameless in every relevant sense. The case shows that reliability, as an external condition, can fail through no fault of the believer — and that when it does fail through no fault of the believer, we are still inclined to say the believer is *epistemically justified*, even if she falls short of knowledge. This thought experiment is the central challenge for externalist theories and the main reason many philosophers seek to preserve an internalist component within their overall theory of epistemic standing.
