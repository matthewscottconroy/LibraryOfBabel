# Sosa's Framework: Virtue Epistemology and the AAA Model

Ernest Sosa is the founder of virtue epistemology in its contemporary form. His work spans several decades and has evolved significantly, but the central idea is consistent: understanding knowledge requires understanding the intellectual virtues — the stable, reliable cognitive dispositions that enable agents to form true beliefs. Sosa's framework connects the evaluation of beliefs to the evaluation of the cognitive agents who form them, in a way that parallels virtue ethics' focus on character over isolated actions.

## The Core Insight

Sosa's fundamental observation: when we evaluate beliefs epistemically, we are not just evaluating propositions or processes — we are evaluating the cognitive achievements of epistemic agents. A belief is not just true or false; it is formed in a way that reflects well or poorly on the cognitive character of the believer. Virtue epistemology centers this evaluative perspective.

An intellectual virtue, on Sosa's account, is a stable cognitive disposition that reliably generates true beliefs in the relevant domain. Vision is a virtue for beliefs about the visible environment; introspection is a virtue for beliefs about one's own mental states; a faculty of mathematical intuition is a virtue for mathematical beliefs. What makes these virtues is their reliability — their tendency to produce true beliefs — and their stability across relevant conditions.

## The AAA Model

Sosa's "AAA" model distinguishes three levels of epistemic evaluation:

**Accurate**: The belief is true.

**Adroit**: The belief is produced by a competent exercise of an intellectual virtue — by a faculty that the agent has in a stable, reliable way.

**Apt**: The belief is accurate *because* it is adroit — the truth of the belief is explained by the exercise of the virtue, not by luck.

The apt belief is the epistemically significant one: it is an intellectual achievement that reflects well on the agent. A belief can be accurate without being adroit (lucky guess) or adroit without being accurate (excellent reasoning, bad luck). The apt belief is the intersection: an accurate belief that is accurate because of the agent's competence.

This models intellectual achievement on the structure of practical achievement. The archer who hits the bullseye accurately and skillfully (the arrow's hitting is explained by the skill) achieves something; the archer who hits by luck achieves nothing significant, even if the arrow lands in the same place.

## Apt Belief and Knowledge

Sosa identifies apt belief with what he calls "animal knowledge" — a modest form of knowledge that animals and unreflective humans can possess. Animal knowledge is apt belief: you know P when you form a true belief through the reliable exercise of a cognitive virtue.

But Sosa distinguishes animal knowledge from "reflective knowledge." Reflective knowledge requires not only that the belief be apt but that the subject has a coherent picture of their epistemic situation — they know something about how and why their belief is reliably formed. This meta-level awareness distinguishes the sophisticated knower from the merely reliable one.

This gives a two-tier account of knowledge: animal knowledge (apt belief) is the foundation; reflective knowledge adds a meta-level awareness that enables the knower to understand their own cognitive situation.

## The Archery Analogy

Sosa frequently uses the archery analogy to illuminate the structure of epistemic assessment. An archer's shot can be evaluated at three levels:
- *Accurate*: did it hit the target?
- *Adroit*: was it a skillful shot, produced by an exercise of the archer's archery competence?
- *Apt*: was its accuracy the result of the archer's adroitness?

In a normal case, all three coincide: the skilled archer hits the target because of her skill. But they can come apart: the arrow can hit the target by a lucky gust of wind (accurate but not apt) or the archer can shoot skillfully and miss due to an unlucky gust (adroit but not accurate). Only the apt shot represents genuine archery achievement.

## Sosa's Response to Gettier

Sosa uses apt belief to respond to Gettier cases. In Gettier cases, the belief is justified and true but not knowledge because its truth is accidental — it is not accurate because of the exercise of a virtue. The barn facade case: you form a true belief that you are looking at a barn, but your belief is not accurate because of your perceptual competence — it would have been equally reliable for a fake. The truth is due to luck, not to the exercise of competence.

This provides a virtue-theoretic diagnosis of Gettier cases: they are cases where the accuracy of the belief is not appropriately explained by the adroitness of its formation. Knowledge requires that accuracy and adroitness coincide in the right way.

## The AAA Model and Epistemic Luck

The apt belief requirement is explicitly designed to exclude epistemic luck. The belief is apt only if its accuracy is explained by the agent's competence — which means the accuracy is not merely coincidental but is reliably connected to the exercise of the virtue. This excludes Gettier cases, lucky guesses, and other cases where the truth of a belief is accidental.

Sosa's framework thus provides a virtue-theoretic account of why luck is incompatible with knowledge: knowledge requires apt belief, and apt belief requires that the accuracy be explained by the virtue, not by luck.
