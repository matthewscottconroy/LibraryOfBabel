# Key Figures: Reliabilist Virtue Epistemology

## Ernest Sosa (b. 1940)

Sosa is the founding figure of reliabilist virtue epistemology. His core innovation was to recast epistemic reliability not as a property of abstract process types (as in Goldman's reliabilism) but as a property of an agent's stable cognitive competencies or "intellectual virtues." In his mature AAA model, a belief is *accurate* if true, *adroit* if produced by an exercise of intellectual virtue (a reliable cognitive competence of the agent), and *apt* if accurate because adroit — that is, if its truth is the manifestation of the agent's competence. Apt belief is animal knowledge. Reflective knowledge adds a further dimension: the agent's belief is apt and the agent believes aptly that her belief is apt, which requires a measure of self-understanding about one's own cognitive faculties. This two-level structure distinguishes Sosa's view from simple reliabilism and accommodates internalist intuitions at the reflective level.

## John Greco (b. 1961)

Greco's version of reliabilist virtue epistemology centers on the concept of *epistemic agency*. His "agent reliabilism" holds that knowledge requires that a true belief be produced by a stable, reliable cognitive disposition that is properly attributable to the agent as a competence — not merely a process that happens to go through the agent. This emphasis on agency allows Greco to handle cases where accidentally reliable processes produce true beliefs: such beliefs do not constitute knowledge because the reliability is not an expression of the agent's own stable competencies. His book *Achieving Knowledge* (2010) develops the view that knowledge is a kind of cognitive achievement — something the agent does well, for which the agent deserves epistemic credit — and uses this to solve Gettier cases and address the value problem.

## Alvin Goldman (b. 1938)

Goldman's process reliabilism is the immediate ancestor of reliabilist virtue epistemology, and Goldman himself has contributed to the virtue-theoretic reformulation. In "Epistemic Folkways and Scientific Epistemology," he identifies epistemic virtues with reliable cognitive faculties as those are recognized in commonsense epistemic practice. Goldman's contribution to the field is foundational: by grounding justification in reliable processes, he opened the door to understanding epistemic norms in terms of the stable traits of cognitive agents, which is the key move that distinguishes virtue reliabilism from bare process reliabilism.

## Linda Zagzebski (b. 1946)

Though Zagzebski is primarily associated with responsibilist virtue epistemology, her *Virtues of the Mind* (1996) provides the clearest systematic comparison of reliabilist and responsibilist approaches, and her critique of reliabilist virtue epistemology has been highly influential. She argues that reliabilist virtue epistemology cannot solve the value problem (why knowledge is better than mere reliable true belief) because reliability — however packaged — adds only more of the same kind of value as true belief itself. Her challenge forced reliabilists like Sosa and Greco to develop richer accounts of the value of knowledge as achievement.

## Wayne Riggs (b. 1963)

Riggs has been one of the most important contributors to the debate about whether virtue epistemology can solve the value problem. His paper "Reliability and the Value of Knowledge" (2002) argues that knowledge has special value because it is a *cognitive achievement* — the believer's own competence is responsible for her arriving at the truth — and this achievement value is distinct from and additional to the value of the true belief itself. Riggs draws the parallel to achievements in practical domains: we value a beautiful dive more than a lucky splash even if both get the swimmer to the water, because the dive manifests skill. His contribution has helped sharpen the virtue-theoretic response to the value problem.

## Duncan Pritchard (b. 1971)

Pritchard's work sits at the intersection of virtue epistemology and the study of epistemic luck. In *Epistemic Luck* (2005), he develops a "safety" condition for knowledge — a belief is safe if the agent could not easily have formed it falsely — and argues that safety and virtue conditions together are needed to handle the full range of Gettier cases and luck cases. His framework distinguishes "environmental" from "competence" luck and argues that while virtue epistemology handles competence luck well, it needs the safety condition to handle environmental luck (as in Goldman's barn-county case). Pritchard's work has become essential reading for anyone seeking to understand how reliabilist virtue epistemology stands up to Gettier cases.

## Jason Baehr (b. 1970)

Baehr's *The Inquiring Mind* (2011) provides a careful analysis of the distinction between reliabilist and responsibilist virtue epistemology and argues that the two approaches are less clearly distinct than often supposed. While ultimately defending a responsibilist position, Baehr's critical engagement with reliabilist virtue epistemology — particularly his analysis of whether reliable dispositions can count as virtues in any robust sense — has helped clarify what is and is not at stake in the reliabilist/responsibilist debate.
