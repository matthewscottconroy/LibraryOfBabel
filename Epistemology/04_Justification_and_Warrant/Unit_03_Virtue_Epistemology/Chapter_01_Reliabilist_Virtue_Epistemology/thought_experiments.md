# Thought Experiments: Reliabilist Virtue Epistemology

## The Archery Analogy

**The scenario:** An archer aims at a target, draws back the string, and releases. The arrow hits the bullseye. Now consider three ways this could happen: (1) The archer is a skilled bowman who executes the shot with practiced precision — it hits because of her skill. (2) The archer is a novice who happens to aim approximately right, and a gust of wind at the last second carries the arrow to the bullseye — it hits, but not because of her skill. (3) The archer is skilled, aims well, and releases cleanly, but a second gust of wind carries the arrow off course and then a third gust corrects the deviation and lands it in the bullseye — her skill was exercised but wasn't what made it hit.

**The philosophical point:** Sosa's AAA model is modeled directly on this archery analogy. Case (1) corresponds to apt belief: accurate (hits the target), adroit (manifests the archer's skill), and apt because accurate in virtue of being adroit. Case (2) is accurate but not apt — the accuracy is not due to the competence. Case (3) is accurate and adroit but not apt — the success is not because of the competence. Only case (1) corresponds to knowledge. The analogy illuminates why Gettier cases are not knowledge: in a Gettier case, the agent's belief-forming competence is exercised, but the truth of the belief does not result from that competence — some intervening luck disconnects accuracy from adroitness.

## Animal Knowledge: The Cat on the Mat

**The scenario:** A cat sees a mouse run behind a flowerpot. The cat reliably perceives medium-sized objects in normal conditions; its perceptual faculties are functioning well, and it forms an accurate "belief" (or belief-analogue) that the mouse is behind the pot. The cat cannot, however, reflect on whether its faculties are reliable. It has no conception of itself as a perceiver, no metacognitive access to its own epistemic condition. Does the cat know where the mouse is?

**The philosophical point:** Sosa's distinction between animal and reflective knowledge is designed to accommodate this case. The cat has animal knowledge — apt belief that is accurate because it is the product of a reliable perceptual competence. What the cat lacks is reflective knowledge: it cannot situate its belief within a self-understood picture of its own cognitive faculties and their reliability. Sosa uses this distinction to acknowledge that reliably produced true belief suffices for a kind of knowledge, while insisting that the distinctively human epistemic achievement — the kind we value for its connection to responsible inquiry — requires the additional reflective dimension.

## The Lucky Gust (Gettier for Virtue Epistemology)

**The scenario:** Sofia is a reliable bird spotter. She is in the field with binoculars, sees what looks exactly like a rare warbler in the tree ahead, and forms the belief "there is a warbler in that tree." Her faculty of avian identification is reliable, her conditions are good, and the bird-like shape she is seeing is indeed a warbler perched behind a cleverly shaped branch that partially obscures it. However, she is actually looking at an unusual shadow of a different bird, which happens to produce the warbler silhouette. The warbler is there — but she is not seeing it, she is seeing the shadow.

**The philosophical point:** This is a Gettier case aimed at virtue epistemology. Sofia's faculty is reliable and she is exercising it; her belief is true; but the truth is not due to the exercise of her competence — there is intervening luck between her competence and the truth. Sosa's APT condition fails: the belief is accurate and adroit, but it is not accurate *because* it is adroit. Pritchard argues that cases like this show that virtue epistemology needs a supplementary safety condition: Sofia's belief is not safe because she could easily have had the same belief on the same basis in a case where it was false (she would have formed the same belief if only the shadow were there and the warbler were absent).

## Reflective Knowledge and the Reliable but Unreflective Savant

**The scenario:** A mathematical savant can calculate prime numbers with astonishing accuracy, producing correct results far beyond what normal humans achieve. But she has no idea how she does it — the calculations happen automatically, pre-reflectively, and she cannot explain her process even to herself. Her faculty of mathematical calculation is highly reliable and properly functions as a cognitive competence. Does she have reflective knowledge of the mathematical truths she computes?

**The philosophical point:** Sosa's view says she has animal knowledge — apt mathematical belief — but lacks reflective knowledge, since she cannot situate her reliable faculty within a coherent, self-understood picture of her own cognitive competencies. This thought experiment is useful for pressing the question of how much reflective access is required for reflective knowledge. A more demanding reading of Sosa's requirements might say she needs to believe *on good grounds* that her faculty is reliable; a weaker reading might allow that a general background trust in one's own competencies suffices. The case also connects to debates about the epistemic value of self-knowledge and whether unreflective but reliably produced true belief is genuinely deficient compared to reflective knowledge.

## The Biased but Reliable Historian

**The scenario:** A historian is deeply biased: she believes that a certain historical narrative is true and selectively attends to evidence that confirms it. Her belief-formation is, in one sense, unreliable — she ignores counterevidence. But as it happens, her narrative is correct: the biased selection strategy has not led her away from truth in this case, and her belief is therefore true. Does she know the historical truth?

**The philosophical point:** This case tests the virtue-theoretic claim that knowledge must be the product of an intellectual virtue — a reliable, truth-aimed competence. The historian's belief is true, but it was produced by a process (confirmation bias) that is not a reliable truth-aimed competence; it just happens to have produced truth this time. Virtue reliabilism says she does not know: her belief is accurate but not adroit in the relevant sense, since confirmation bias is not an intellectual virtue but a cognitive failing. This explains the common intuition that the biased historian does not know — she has not achieved truth through epistemic excellence — and illustrates the added explanatory power of virtue-theoretic accounts over bare reliabilism.
