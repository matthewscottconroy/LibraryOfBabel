# Responsibilist Virtue Epistemology: Intellectual Character and Excellence

Responsibilist virtue epistemology, associated with Lorraine Code, Linda Zagzebski, and James Montmarquet, differs from Sosa's reliabilist approach in emphasizing the *character* of the epistemic agent rather than the *reliability* of cognitive processes. On this view, intellectual virtues are genuine character traits — stable, deep dispositions that reflect who the agent is as a thinking being — not merely reliable belief-forming mechanisms.

## The Analogy to Moral Virtue

Responsibilism draws an explicit analogy to moral virtue theory. Just as the morally virtuous person is not merely one who reliably does good deeds but one who has a good character — whose actions flow from genuine concern for others, justice, and practical wisdom — the intellectually virtuous person is not merely one who reliably forms true beliefs but one who has a good intellectual character.

Intellectual character involves:
- **Love of truth**: Genuine motivation to believe only what is true and not to believe what is false
- **Open-mindedness**: Willingness to consider new evidence and revise beliefs
- **Epistemic humility**: Appropriate awareness of one's own fallibility
- **Intellectual courage**: Willingness to maintain unpopular conclusions when evidence supports them
- **Thoroughness**: Pursuing evidence carefully and extensively before forming conclusions
- **Intellectual honesty**: Accurately representing one's evidence and conclusions

These virtues are not just reliably good processes; they are features of character that define what kind of epistemic agent one is.

## Zagzebski's Virtue Epistemology

Linda Zagzebski's *Virtues of the Mind* (1996) develops the most systematic responsibilist virtue epistemology. Zagzebski defines intellectual virtues as "deep acquired excellence of a person, involving a characteristic motivation to achieve the goal of intellectual activity (cognitive contact with reality) together with reliable success in achieving that goal."

The definition has two components:
1. **Motivational component**: The virtue involves being motivated by the characteristic goal of intellectual activity — truth, understanding, knowledge.
2. **Reliabilist component**: The virtue involves reliable success in achieving that goal.

This is a hybrid: it includes the motivational and character elements emphasized by responsibilism *and* the reliability element emphasized by Sosa. The virtues are neither mere reliable processes (they require appropriate motivation) nor mere character excellences (they require reliable success).

On Zagzebski's account, knowledge is a "state of cognitive contact with reality arising out of acts of intellectual virtue." This gives a virtue-based account of knowledge that doesn't analyze it reductively in terms of justification or other prior epistemic concepts.

## Intellectual Character and Education

One attraction of responsibilist virtue epistemology is its implications for education and epistemic development. If intellectual virtues are character traits that can be cultivated, then education is not just about transmitting information or imparting reliable belief-forming processes — it is about shaping epistemic character.

Cultivating intellectual virtues involves:
- Modeling virtuous intellectual behavior (teachers who exhibit intellectual humility, open-mindedness, and love of truth)
- Creating environments that reward careful thinking, appropriate revision, and intellectual courage
- Providing practice in epistemic activities (inquiry, debate, research) that exercise the virtues
- Developing awareness of intellectual vices (dogmatism, intellectual cowardice, epistemic laziness) so they can be recognized and corrected

This connects epistemology to educational philosophy in a deep way: the goals of education are partly epistemic, and achieving them requires developing intellectual character, not just providing information.

## The Intellectual Community

Responsibilism's focus on character extends naturally to questions about epistemic communities. Intellectual virtues are exercised in social settings — inquiry is typically collaborative, information is shared through testimony, and epistemic norms are enforced by communities. The intellectual virtues that matter include social virtues: epistemic trust, willingness to share evidence, fair assessment of others' contributions, intellectual generosity.

This connects responsibilist virtue epistemology to social epistemology: the quality of epistemic life in a community depends not just on the aggregate reliability of individual cognitive processes but on the intellectual character of individuals and the health of their epistemic institutions.

## Objections to Responsibilism

**The credit objection**: If intellectual virtues require motivation to believe the truth, can we credit reflectively unreflective but reliably accurate cognition (as in animals or unreflective humans)? Zagzebski accepts that animals and children may lack knowledge in the full sense, possessing only a weaker epistemic status.

**The measurement problem**: How do we know when someone has intellectual virtue rather than merely good epistemic habits? The distinction between a genuine intellectual virtue and a socially-maintained epistemic habit is hard to draw empirically.

**The value of reliability**: If motivation to believe the truth is necessary, then a motivated but unreliable thinker has more intellectual virtue than a reliable but unmotivated one. But surely reliable truth-tracking is epistemically important, and the reliable but unmotivated thinker has something valuable that the motivated-but-unreliable thinker lacks.
