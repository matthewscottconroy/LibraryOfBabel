# Vice Epistemology: The Study of Intellectual Failure

If intellectual virtues are the character traits that make for excellent epistemic agency, intellectual vices are the corresponding character traits that make for epistemic failure. Vice epistemology — the study of intellectual vices — has emerged as a significant sub-field of virtue epistemology, motivated by the observation that understanding what goes wrong epistemically is as important as understanding what goes right.

## The Case for Vice Epistemology

Epistemology has traditionally focused on conditions for knowledge and justification — the positive conditions a belief must meet to count as knowledge. But understanding epistemic failure is equally important:

- Much of what matters epistemically in practical life involves avoiding mistakes: recognizing when we are reasoning poorly, when we are susceptible to bias, when our motivations are distorting our beliefs.
- Intellectual vices are richer and more varied than just the negation of intellectual virtues. Studying them illuminates forms of epistemic failure that positive theories of knowledge don't fully capture.
- Social and political contexts make vice epistemology urgent: intellectual cowardice, dogmatism, and epistemic arrogance can sustain injustice and error in ways that virtue epistemology's focus on individual excellence misses.

## The Catalog of Intellectual Vices

Quassim Cassam has catalogued intellectual vices in *Vices of the Mind* (2019). Major intellectual vices include:

**Closed-mindedness**: Resistance to entertaining new evidence or revising beliefs in response to it. The closed-minded person has entrenched beliefs defended by motivated reasoning, confirmation bias, and dismissal of counterevidence.

**Epistemic cowardice**: Failing to assert or maintain well-supported beliefs because of social pressure, desire for approval, or fear of conflict. The epistemically cowardly person hedges, avoids controversy, and says what people want to hear rather than what they believe.

**Intellectual arrogance**: Overestimating one's own epistemic capacities, dismissing others' perspectives, and refusing to engage seriously with counterarguments. The intellectually arrogant person is not open to being wrong.

**Epistemic laziness**: Failure to acquire evidence, failure to reason carefully, failure to check one's work. The epistemically lazy person settles for easy answers rather than pursuing truth with appropriate thoroughness.

**Dogmatism**: Commitment to beliefs beyond what evidence supports, impervious to revision. The dogmatist's commitments are not responsive to evidence or argument.

**Wishful thinking**: Forming beliefs because they are pleasant rather than because they are evidentially supported. The wishful thinker lets desires distort their epistemic judgment.

## The Epistemically Vicious and Their Beliefs

One question for vice epistemology: do intellectually vicious people fail to *know* things, or do they fail to be *epistemically responsible* in ways that are separate from the question of knowledge? The two may come apart:

A closed-minded person might, despite their vice, have a reliably accurate perceptual system and form many true perceptual beliefs. Do they know what they perceive? Virtue epistemologists often say yes — the vice doesn't undermine the faculty of perception, which operates independently of character. The vice matters most for beliefs about complex, contested matters where evidence must be carefully gathered and weighed.

Cassam argues that intellectual vices are *obstacles to knowledge*: they interfere with the epistemic processes that produce knowledge, making the vicious person less likely to know things in domains where their vices are operative. The closed-minded person may miss evidence; the epistemically cowardly person may not revise in response to it; the dogmatist will resist conclusions that challenge entrenched commitments.

## Systemic and Social Vices

A significant extension of vice epistemology concerns systemic intellectual vices — vices that characterize institutions, communities, or cultures rather than just individuals. An institution can be epistemically arrogant (resistant to external criticism), epistemically lazy (insufficient investment in evidence-gathering), or epistemically cowardly (failing to communicate unwelcome findings).

Social media platforms, political parties, academic disciplines, and news organizations can all exhibit institutional epistemic vices that have consequences for the quality of public knowledge. Vice epistemology applied to institutions connects to social epistemology and political philosophy in important ways.

## Epistemic Injustice and Vice

Miranda Fricker's work on epistemic injustice describes how certain epistemic vices — specifically, prejudice that leads to improper credibility assessments — can be components of systematic injustice. The vice of *identity prejudice* leads to testimonial injustice: undervaluing the testimony of members of marginalized groups because of their group membership.

This connects vice epistemology to the ethics of epistemic practices: intellectual vices are not just personally bad; they can cause harm to others by distorting how their testimony and perspectives are received.
