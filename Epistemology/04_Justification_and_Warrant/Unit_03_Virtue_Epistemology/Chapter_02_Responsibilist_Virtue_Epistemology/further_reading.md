# Further Reading: Responsibilist Virtue Epistemology

## Primary Sources

- Linda Zagzebski, *Virtues of the Mind: An Inquiry into the Nature of Virtue and the Ethical Foundations of Knowledge*, Cambridge University Press, 1996. The foundational text of responsibilist virtue epistemology, proposing a hybrid definition of knowledge as "a state of belief arising out of acts of intellectual virtue" and providing the most sustained treatment of intellectual character virtues.

- Lorraine Code, *Epistemic Responsibility*, Brown University Press, 1987. An early and influential defense of the view that epistemic evaluation should be directed at epistemic agents and their character rather than at individual beliefs or processes in isolation.

- James Montmarquet, *Epistemic Virtue and Doxastic Responsibility*, Rowman & Littlefield, 1993. Develops a responsibilist account centered on the concept of epistemic conscientiousness — the desire to hold only true beliefs — as the core intellectual virtue.

- Quassim Cassam, *Vices of the Mind: From the Intellectual to the Political*, Oxford University Press, 2019. The most comprehensive contemporary treatment of intellectual vices, analyzing epistemic cowardice, closed-mindedness, and epistemic malice, and connecting vice epistemology to social and political epistemology.

- Miranda Fricker, *Epistemic Injustice: Power and the Ethics of Knowing*, Oxford University Press, 2007. Analyzes the ways that epistemic injustice — particularly testimonial injustice (deflating a speaker's credibility due to prejudice) and hermeneutical injustice — constitute distinctive intellectual vices and social wrongs.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Jason Baehr, *The Inquiring Mind: On Intellectual Virtues and Virtue Epistemology*, Oxford University Press, 2011. A rigorous defense of responsibilist virtue epistemology that distinguishes intellectual virtues from mere reliable faculties and argues for their independent epistemic value.

- Jason Baehr, ed., *Intellectual Virtues and Education: Essays in Applied Virtue Epistemology*, Routledge, 2016. Applies responsibilist virtue epistemology to educational theory, examining how intellectual virtues can be cultivated and what role they should play in educational aims.

- Michael DePaul and Linda Zagzebski, eds., *Intellectual Virtue: Perspectives from Ethics and Epistemology*, Oxford University Press, 2003. A key anthology bringing together ethicists and epistemologists to examine the nature of intellectual virtue, with papers by Sosa, Greco, Zagzebski, and others.

- Heather Battaly, *Virtue*, Polity Press, 2015. A clear survey of virtue theory in both ethics and epistemology, accessible to upper-level undergraduates and useful for situating the epistemological debate within the broader virtue-theoretic tradition.

- Heather Battaly, "Epistemic Self-Indulgence," *Metaphilosophy* 41, 2010, pp. 214–234. Analyzes epistemic self-indulgence — forming beliefs for ego-gratifying rather than truth-aimed reasons — as an intellectual vice, extending the vice-epistemological program.

- Quassim Cassam, "Stealthy Vices," *Social Epistemology Review and Reply Collective* 5, 2016, pp. 19–25. A key paper on vices that are "stealthy" in the sense that the agent is unaware of them, raising questions about the relationship between intellectual vices and self-knowledge.

- Ian James Kidd, José Medina, and Gaile Pohlhaus, eds., *The Routledge Handbook of Epistemic Injustice*, Routledge, 2017. A comprehensive reference work on epistemic injustice, covering testimonial and hermeneutical injustice and their connection to intellectual vices.

- Kristoffer Ahlstrom-Vij and Jeff Milburn, eds., *Epistemic Virtues and the Theory of Knowledge*, Routledge, 2019. Essays examining how virtue epistemology bears on the analysis of knowledge and on broader questions in social epistemology.

- Robert Roberts and W. Jay Wood, *Intellectual Virtues: An Essay in Regulative Epistemology*, Oxford University Press, 2007. A detailed phenomenological account of individual intellectual virtues (love of knowledge, intellectual humility, practical wisdom) that enriches the responsibilist picture.

- Abrol Fairweather and Linda Zagzebski, eds., *Virtue Epistemology: Essays on Epistemic Virtue and Responsibility*, Oxford University Press, 2001. An early anthology that helped establish the field, with important papers on the relationship between intellectual virtue and epistemic justification.

- Nathan Ballantyne, *Knowing Our Limits*, Oxford University Press, 2019. Examines epistemic limitations from a virtue-theoretic perspective, arguing for epistemic humility grounded in a realistic assessment of how our intellectual character traits operate.

- Amanda Askell et al., "Epistemic Cowardice and Epistemic Courage," in *Epistemic Virtues and the Good Life*, Cambridge, 2020. A recent treatment of the intellectual virtues and vices most directly related to assertion and intellectual courage.

- Miranda Fricker and Michael Brady, eds., *The Epistemic Life of Groups*, Oxford University Press, 2016. Extends virtue-epistemological concerns to groups and collective agents, raising questions about whether groups can have intellectual virtues and vices.
