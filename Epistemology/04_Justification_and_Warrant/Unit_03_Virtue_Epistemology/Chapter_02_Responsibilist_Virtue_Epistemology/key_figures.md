# Key Figures: Responsibilist Virtue Epistemology

## Linda Zagzebski (b. 1946)

Zagzebski is the founding figure of responsibilist virtue epistemology. Her *Virtues of the Mind* (1996) offers both a critique of reliability-based approaches and a positive account of knowledge as "a state of belief arising out of acts of intellectual virtue." For Zagzebski, intellectual virtues are character traits — stable dispositions that motivate and guide inquiry — that are acquired through habituation and reflective practice, much like Aristotelian moral virtues. She insists on a "hybrid" definition: intellectual virtues involve both a motivational component (the love of truth, the desire to understand) and a reliable success component (virtuous inquiry tends to produce true beliefs). Her value-theoretic argument against reliabilism — that mere reliability cannot account for the distinctive value of knowledge over reliable true belief — has become one of the most discussed challenges in contemporary epistemology.

## Lorraine Code (b. 1937)

Code's *Epistemic Responsibility* (1987) is one of the earliest and most influential responsibilist texts, arguing that epistemology has been too focused on individual propositions and their logical relations and too little focused on the characteristics of epistemic agents. Code insists that being a good knower is a matter of intellectual character: epistemic responsibility includes the virtues of intellectual honesty, the willingness to revise one's views, careful attention to evidence, and responsiveness to the perspectives of other knowers. Her work also connects the responsibilist program to feminist epistemology, raising questions about how social position and identity affect epistemic responsibility and who gets to count as a credible epistemic agent.

## Jason Baehr (b. 1970)

Baehr's *The Inquiring Mind* (2011) provides the most rigorous contemporary defense of responsibilist virtue epistemology. He argues that intellectual virtues in the responsibilist sense — character virtues like intellectual courage, open-mindedness, and thoroughness — are constitutively tied to the agent's motivational states and practical agency in ways that make them irreducible to reliable dispositions. Baehr also develops a "personal worth" conception of intellectual virtues: what makes a trait an intellectual virtue is that it contributes to a person's excellence as an inquirer, not merely to the reliable production of true beliefs. His critique of reliabilist virtue epistemology on these grounds is the most sustained available.

## Quassim Cassam (b. 1961)

Cassam's *Vices of the Mind* (2019) inaugurated a systematic program of vice epistemology. He argues that intellectual vices — epistemic cowardice, closed-mindedness, intellectual arrogance, epistemic malice — are not merely the absence of virtues but are constitutive epistemic failings that impede knowledge and good inquiry. Cassam distinguishes "stealthy" vices (those the agent is unaware of) from transparent vices, and he analyzes the social and political conditions that cultivate or reinforce epistemic vices. His work has connected responsibilist virtue epistemology to political epistemology, showing how intellectual vices operate at collective and institutional levels, not only in individual agents.

## Miranda Fricker (b. 1966)

Fricker's *Epistemic Injustice* (2007) analyzes how identity prejudice — particularly regarding race, gender, and social class — generates systematic epistemic wrongs. Her concept of "testimonial injustice" identifies cases where a speaker's credibility is deflated because of identity prejudice, and "hermeneutical injustice" identifies cases where a group lacks the conceptual resources to understand or articulate its own significant social experience. Fricker connects these wrongs to intellectual vices in those who perpetrate them: testimonial injustice is partly caused by the vice of identity-based prejudice; correcting it requires the virtue of testimonial justice, which involves calibrating one's credibility assessments to evidence rather than to social bias. Her work has been enormously influential in connecting epistemology to social and political philosophy.

## Heather Battaly (b. 1965)

Battaly has contributed to both the theoretical foundations of virtue epistemology and its applied dimensions. Her *Virtue* (2015) provides one of the clearest introductions to the field, carefully distinguishing reliabilist, responsibilist, and hybrid positions. Her research on epistemic vices — particularly "epistemic self-indulgence" and "epistemic laziness" — has helped develop the vice-epistemology program beyond Cassam's original framework. Battaly has also contributed to debates about whether intellectual virtues can be entirely agent-focused or must be assessed in social context, connecting the responsibilist tradition to social epistemology.

## James Montmarquet (b. 1945)

Montmarquet's *Epistemic Virtue and Doxastic Responsibility* (1993) offers an early systematic responsibilist account centered on the notion of "epistemic conscientiousness" — the stable desire to hold only true beliefs and to go about forming beliefs in ways that are conducive to truth. His account is useful for understanding the relationship between intellectual virtue and epistemic responsibility: Montmarquet argues that agents are epistemically responsible for their beliefs to the degree that their doxastic states flow from epistemically conscientious character, regardless of whether those states are reliably true.
