# Credit, Luck, and the Value of Knowledge

Virtue epistemology's central contribution to the analysis of knowledge is its treatment of epistemic luck and the role of credit in distinguishing knowledge from mere true belief. The basic intuition: when you know something, your getting it right is attributable to you — to your cognitive competence — in a way that mere true belief is not. This credit-based account of knowledge gives virtue epistemology a distinctive explanation of why knowledge is more valuable than true belief and why Gettier cases fail to be knowledge.

## The Credit Intuition

John Greco and Ernest Sosa have developed the credit-based approach most fully. The core thought: when S knows that P, S's belief is true at least in part because of S's exercise of cognitive competence. S deserves credit for the true belief. In Gettier cases, and in cases of mere true belief formed by luck, S does not deserve this credit.

The credit intuition explains our responses to cases:

- **Lucky guess**: You guess that you will win the lottery, and you do. You have a true belief but not knowledge. You don't deserve credit for being right — you just got lucky.

- **Gettier case**: You form a justified belief "there's a barn in the field" through careful visual inspection, but you're in fake barn country and happen to be looking at the one real barn. You have justified true belief but not knowledge. The truth of your belief is due to luck, not to your cognitive competence — your competence would have equally endorsed a false belief in the adjacent cases.

- **Genuine knowledge**: You look carefully at a nearby barn in ordinary conditions and form the belief "there's a barn." You are right because of your reliable vision. You deserve credit for the true belief.

The difference between knowledge and Gettier cases is whether the subject's competence is "manifested" in the true belief — whether the belief's being true flows from the subject's reliable cognitive performance.

## Greco's Epistemic Agency

John Greco develops the credit account with particular attention to the *agency* involved in epistemic credit. For a person to deserve credit for a true belief, the true belief must be the result of their *competent action* — they must be doing something cognitive, not just happening to form a belief that turns out to be true.

This is analogous to how agency works in moral contexts. A person deserves credit for an action when the action flows from their character and they are responsible for it. Similarly, a believer deserves epistemic credit when the belief flows from their cognitive competence and they are in a sense "responsible" for exercising it.

Greco's account: S knows P iff S's believing P correctly (truly) is sufficiently attributable to S's reliable cognitive powers. "Sufficiently attributable" is analyzed in terms of the degree to which the belief is a manifestation of the agent's cognitive powers rather than a product of luck or interference.

## Epistemic Luck Taxonomy

Virtue epistemology's treatment of epistemic luck has driven a more careful taxonomy of kinds of luck. Not all luck is incompatible with knowledge:

**Environmentally lucky**: You are in fake barn country, but you look at a real barn. The luck involves the environment but is compatible with knowledge if your competence would be equally reliable in normal environments.

**Veritic luck**: A belief could easily have been false. This kind of luck undermines knowledge: if you could easily have been wrong, being right is not a manifestation of your competence.

**Benign luck**: You happen to look in the right direction at the right moment to see the burglar. This kind of luck doesn't undermine knowledge — it brought you into a situation where your competence could operate.

The distinction between benign and malign (veritic) luck is crucial: knowledge is compatible with benign luck (fortunate circumstances) but incompatible with veritic luck (accidental truth).

## Duncan Pritchard's Safety Condition

Duncan Pritchard has developed a modal account of luck that complements virtue epistemology. A belief is luckily true if it could easily have been false — if in close possible worlds, the belief is false. A safe belief is one that could not easily have been false.

Pritchard's safety condition: S knows P only if S's belief in P could not easily have been false. This is a necessary but not sufficient condition: safe beliefs can still fail to be knowledge if they are not appropriately produced by cognitive competence.

Combining safety with credit gives a more comprehensive account: knowledge requires both that the belief be modally safe (couldn't easily be false) and that the agent deserve credit for its truth (the truth flows from their competence).

## The Value of Knowledge

The credit account gives a principled answer to the value question: why is knowledge more valuable than true belief? Because knowledge involves an intellectual achievement that reflects well on the agent. True belief that is merely lucky doesn't reflect on the agent; it could have been equally a false belief. Knowledge that is the result of competence is something the agent can take a certain (not excessive) pride in — it is genuinely theirs.

This parallels how we value achievements in practical life: the athlete who wins by skill has something that the athlete who wins by luck lacks, even if the outcome is the same. Epistemic achievement is analogous.
