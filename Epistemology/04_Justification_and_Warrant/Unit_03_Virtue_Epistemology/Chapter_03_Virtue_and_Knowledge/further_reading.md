# Further Reading: Virtue, Credit, and Knowledge

## Primary Sources

- Ernest Sosa, *A Virtue Epistemology*, Oxford University Press, 2007. Develops the credit-for-true-belief thesis and the APT model; knowledge is treated as a kind of cognitive achievement for which the agent deserves epistemic credit.

- John Greco, *Achieving Knowledge*, Cambridge University Press, 2010. The most sustained defense of the view that knowledge is a cognitive achievement — true belief for which the agent deserves credit — and that this analysis handles Gettier cases and the value problem.

- Duncan Pritchard, *Epistemic Luck*, Oxford University Press, 2005. Develops a taxonomy of epistemic luck (environmental, veritic, benign) and argues that knowledge requires the safety condition alongside virtue conditions to rule out veritic luck.

- Linda Zagzebski, *Virtues of the Mind*, Cambridge University Press, 1996. The original sustained argument that knowledge requires more than reliable true belief because mere reliability cannot account for the distinctive value of knowledge.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Wayne Riggs, "Reliability and the Value of Knowledge," *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* 64, 2002, pp. 79–96. Argues that knowledge is valuable as an achievement, and that this explains why knowledge is more valuable than lucky true belief or reliably produced true belief.

- Jennifer Lackey, "Why We Don't Deserve Credit for Everything We Know," *Synthese* 158, 2007, pp. 345–361. Argues that testimonial knowledge poses a serious problem for credit theories: when we learn from testimony, the speaker not only produces but also accounts for the truth of our belief — casting doubt on whether the hearer deserves full credit.

- Jonathan Kvanvig, *The Value of Knowledge and the Pursuit of Understanding*, Cambridge University Press, 2003. Argues that understanding rather than knowledge is the highest epistemic value and that virtue-theoretic accounts should be reoriented toward understanding as their primary target.

- Duncan Pritchard, "Anti-Luck Virtue Epistemology," *Journal of Philosophy* 109, 2012, pp. 247–279. Proposes combining virtue epistemology with the safety condition — the anti-luck condition — to produce an account that handles both competence luck and environmental luck.

- Duncan Pritchard, Alan Millar, and Adrian Haddock, *The Nature and Value of Knowledge*, Oxford University Press, 2010. Three distinct epistemological frameworks — anti-luck, virtue, and ability — brought into dialogue, with careful attention to their respective strengths in handling luck cases and the value problem.

- John Greco, "Knowledge as Credit for True Belief," in *Intellectual Virtue*, eds. DePaul and Zagzebski, Oxford University Press, 2003. The clearest statement of the credit theory: S knows p if and only if S's true belief that p is creditable to S's intellectual virtues.

- Stephen Hetherington, *How to Know: A Practicalist Conception of Knowledge*, Wiley-Blackwell, 2011. Argues for a "degrees of knowledge" view that accommodates the insight of virtue epistemology while avoiding problems about luck.

- Matthias Steup and John Turri, "The Analysis of Knowledge," *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, 2021 ed. A comprehensive survey that situates virtue-theoretic and safety-based analyses within the broader tradition.

- John Turri, "Veritism for Reliabilists," *Philosophical Psychology* 25, 2012, pp. 829–842. Argues that reliabilist virtue epistemology can accommodate the value problem by appealing to the objective relationship between epistemic achievement and the value of truth.

- Jennifer Lackey and Ernest Sosa, eds., *The Epistemology of Testimony*, Oxford University Press, 2006. Several essays engage with whether testimonial knowledge fits the credit model of knowledge or requires a modified account.

- Carl Ginet, "Remembering That and Knowing That," in *Reasoning*, ed. Stephen Toulmin, Macmillan, 1986. Relevant for understanding how the credit model handles memorial knowledge, where the causal story behind a belief complicates the attribution of credit.

- Trent Dougherty, "Knowledge, Practical Interests, and the Value Problem," *Episteme* 8, 2011, pp. 85–98. Examines interactions between knowledge's value, practical interests, and the virtue-theoretic account.

- Peter Baumann, "Epistemic Luck," *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* 73, 2006, pp. 497–519. An examination of whether the various species of luck distinguished by Pritchard all have the same epistemic significance and whether a unified treatment is possible.

- Christopher Kelp, *Good Thinking: A Knowledge First Virtue Epistemology*, Routledge, 2018. Develops a knowledge-first virtue epistemology that treats knowledge as primitive and defines the virtues in terms of their contribution to knowledge rather than the reverse.
