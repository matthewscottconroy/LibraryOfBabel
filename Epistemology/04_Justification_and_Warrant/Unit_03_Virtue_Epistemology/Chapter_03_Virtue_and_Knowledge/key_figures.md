# Key Figures: Virtue, Credit, and Knowledge

## Ernest Sosa (b. 1940)

Sosa's treatment of the credit intuition is embedded in his AAA model: knowledge is apt belief — belief that is accurate because adroit — and the "because" relation is what generates the credit condition. When a belief is accurate because of the agent's competence, the agent deserves credit for that success in the way that a skilled performer deserves credit for an achievement. Sosa distinguishes environmental luck (the type in Goldman's barn county) from luck that undermines the competence-to-success link, arguing that knowledge requires the latter be absent. His work on the value of knowledge connects to this credit thesis: knowledge is valuable not merely as a state of accurate information but as a manifestation of cognitive achievement.

## John Greco (b. 1961)

Greco's credit theory of knowledge is the most fully developed in the literature. He argues that S knows p if and only if S's true belief that p is creditworthy — that is, if the truth of the belief is appropriately attributable to S's intellectual virtues rather than to luck. His account handles Gettier cases by explaining why they are not knowledge: in a Gettier case, the belief's truth results from some lucky factor independent of the believer's competence, and hence cannot be credited to the agent. Greco also addresses the value of knowledge: knowledge is valuable as a kind of achievement — analogous to winning a race through athletic excellence rather than stumbling across the finish line — and this achievement value is distinct from and additional to the value of the true belief itself.

## Duncan Pritchard (b. 1971)

Pritchard has been the leading analyst of epistemic luck and its relationship to knowledge. His taxonomy distinguishes environmental luck (being in a situation where one could easily have formed a false belief through the same method), veritic luck (the belief itself being only luckily true), and benign luck (true belief that involves no knowledge-undermining randomness). He argues that knowledge is incompatible with veritic luck but not necessarily with benign luck, and that a "safety" condition — the agent could not easily have held the same belief in the same way and been wrong — is the correct anti-luck condition for knowledge. Pritchard's "anti-luck virtue epistemology" combines this safety condition with virtue conditions, arguing that neither alone suffices.

## Jennifer Lackey (b. 1970)

Lackey's challenge to the credit theory focuses on testimonial knowledge. She argues that when we learn from testimony, the credit for the true belief belongs substantially to the speaker who provided the information — not (or not solely) to the hearer who received it. If knowledge requires that its truth be credited to the knower's own intellectual virtues, then a hearer who learns something entirely from testimony seems to lack a necessary condition for knowledge. Lackey's challenge forces virtue epistemologists either to develop a more liberal account of what counts as "one's own" competence (including competent reception of testimony) or to acknowledge that credit theories need significant revision to accommodate the social dimensions of knowledge.

## Linda Zagzebski (b. 1946)

Zagzebski's contribution to the virtue-credit-knowledge nexus is primarily through her analysis of the value problem. She argues that any theory that defines knowledge as true belief plus some further condition (reliability, safety, virtue as a reliable faculty) cannot explain why knowledge is distinctively valuable, because the further condition merely adds more of the same kind of value that true belief already has. Only by treating knowledge as the product of acts of *intellectual virtue* — in the rich responsibilist sense — can one explain why knowledge matters: virtuous inquiry is intrinsically valuable, not merely instrumentally valuable for producing true beliefs, and this explains the special value of knowledge as its product.

## Wayne Riggs (b. 1963)

Riggs's defense of the achievement model of epistemic value argues that knowledge has a distinctive value precisely because it is a *cognitive achievement* — the successful exercise of cognitive skill in arriving at truth. He draws explicitly on parallels with athletic, artistic, and practical achievements: we value not just the product (the winning performance, the beautiful work, the true belief) but the fact that the product resulted from the exercise of excellence. Riggs argues that this "achievement value" is irreducible to the value of true belief and provides an independent rationale for the virtue-theoretic account of knowledge that does not depend on reliabilist assumptions about what makes competences valuable.

## Jonathan Kvanvig (b. 1954)

Kvanvig challenges the consensus that knowledge is the highest epistemic value, arguing in *The Value of Knowledge and the Pursuit of Understanding* (2003) that *understanding* — grasping the explanatory relations among a body of facts — is the genuinely supreme epistemic achievement. His argument has implications for virtue epistemology: if understanding, rather than knowledge, is the goal of good inquiry, then the intellectual virtues should be assessed by their contribution to understanding rather than to true belief. Kvanvig's challenge to knowledge-centrism has reshaped debates about the value of knowledge and the appropriate targets of virtue epistemological analysis.
