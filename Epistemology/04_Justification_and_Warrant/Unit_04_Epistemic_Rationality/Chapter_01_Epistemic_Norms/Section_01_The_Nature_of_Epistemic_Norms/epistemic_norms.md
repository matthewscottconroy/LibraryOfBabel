# The Nature of Epistemic Norms

Epistemology is fundamentally normative: it is concerned with how we *should* form beliefs, not merely with how we do form them. Epistemic norms are the standards that govern the rational formation, maintenance, and revision of beliefs. Understanding the nature of these norms — where they come from, what grounds them, and how demanding they are — is central to epistemological theory.

## What Are Epistemic Norms?

Epistemic norms are standards that govern the epistemic evaluation of cognitive agents and their beliefs. They include:

- **Norms of evidence**: Believe in proportion to your evidence; don't believe against your evidence.
- **Norms of consistency**: Don't hold contradictory beliefs.
- **Norms of inquiry**: Seek evidence relevant to beliefs that matter; update appropriately when evidence comes in.
- **Norms of assertion**: Assert only what you know (Williamson); or: assert only what you believe; or: assert only what you are justified in believing.

These norms are normative in the sense that they prescribe how cognition should proceed, not merely describe how it does proceed. A believer who violates epistemic norms is not just causally deviant; they are doing something they should not do.

## The Deontological vs. Evaluative Distinction

Epistemic norms can be understood in two main ways:

**Deontologically**: Epistemic norms are like moral obligations — they generate duties. You have a duty to believe in accordance with your evidence, a duty not to believe contradictions, and so on. Violating these duties makes you epistemically blameworthy.

**Evaluatively**: Epistemic norms are more like evaluations of excellence — they characterize the ideal epistemic agent rather than obligating all agents. The perfectly rational agent believes in perfect proportion to evidence; actual agents fall short to varying degrees.

The deontological conception connects epistemology to ethics: epistemic obligations are genuine obligations, and violating them is genuinely wrong. The evaluative conception is more modest: it describes the ideal without necessarily entailing blame for failure.

## Are Epistemic Norms Sui Generis?

Are epistemic norms a distinct species of norms, or are they ultimately derived from more general norms of rationality or morality?

**Epistemic norms as rational norms**: On this view, epistemic norms are just the special case of rationality norms applied to belief. Rational consistency, rational proportionality to evidence, rational responsiveness to argument — these are the general norms of rationality manifested in the doxastic domain.

**Epistemic norms as moral norms**: W.K. Clifford's famous dictum ("It is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence") treats epistemic norms as moral norms. William James criticized this: Clifford's norm is too demanding and itself reflects a particular, contestable moral stance toward risk in belief.

**Epistemic norms as sui generis**: On this view, epistemic norms are irreducible to moral or general rational norms. There is a distinctively *epistemic* kind of value — truth, knowledge, understanding — and epistemic norms are norms for achieving and maintaining this value. These are not derived from moral considerations; they are independent epistemic values.

## The Norm of Assertion

Timothy Williamson's *Knowledge and Its Limits* (2000) defends a particular epistemic norm that has attracted enormous discussion: the *knowledge norm of assertion*, which states that one should assert only what one knows (or: the only proper reason to assert P is that you know P).

The knowledge norm of assertion is a specific epistemic norm governing a specific kind of speech act. It makes knowledge — not mere belief, not mere justified belief — the norm. This has implications for the analysis of knowledge (knowledge must be the right kind of thing to norm assertion), for testimonial epistemology (testimony is only proper when backed by knowledge), and for practical action (acting on a belief should require knowledge, not mere justified belief).

## Teleological vs. Deontological Grounding

How are epistemic norms grounded?

**Truth-teleological grounding**: Epistemic norms are grounded in the goal of truth. Norms of evidence, consistency, and inquiry are all means to the end of true belief and the avoidance of false belief. This is the account implicit in reliabilism: what matters is getting to the truth, and epistemic norms are norms that reliably facilitate this.

**Rational deontological grounding**: Epistemic norms are grounded in the requirements of rational agency itself. To be rational is to be responsive to reasons; epistemic norms articulate what responsiveness to reasons requires in the domain of belief.

**Virtue-based grounding**: Epistemic norms express what intellectual virtues require. The virtuous epistemic agent is the model, and epistemic norms are derived from what the virtuous agent does.

## The Demanding and the Achievable

A perennial tension in the theory of epistemic norms is between ideal norms and achievable norms. Ideal norms describe perfect epistemic performance; achievable norms describe what can reasonably be demanded of actual cognitive agents with limited time, attention, and resources.

The distinction between *objective* epistemic obligations (what a perfectly rational agent would do) and *subjective* epistemic obligations (what this agent, given their limitations and circumstances, should do) mirrors the distinction in moral philosophy between perfect-rational standards and what can reasonably be demanded of actual humans.
