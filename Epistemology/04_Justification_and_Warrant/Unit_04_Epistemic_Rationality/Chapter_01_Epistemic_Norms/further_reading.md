# Further Reading: Epistemic Norms

## Primary Sources

- W.K. Clifford, "The Ethics of Belief," in *Lectures and Essays*, Macmillan, 1879. The classic deontological statement: "It is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence" — a foundational text for the view that epistemic obligations are genuine and stringent.

- William James, "The Will to Believe," in *The Will to Believe and Other Essays in Popular Philosophy*, Longmans Green, 1897. James's response to Clifford, arguing that in certain cases (live, forced, and momentous choices) it is rational to believe beyond the evidence — the most influential defense of doxastic pragmatism.

- Timothy Williamson, *Knowledge and Its Limits*, Oxford University Press, 2000. Chapter 11 develops the "knowledge norm of assertion" — that one should assert only what one knows — and argues for a knowledge norm of belief as well, making knowledge (not mere justification) the standard for correct believing.

- Roderick Chisholm, *Theory of Knowledge*, 3rd ed., Prentice-Hall, 1989. Develops epistemic norms as genuine deontological obligations, grounded in the fundamental epistemic aim of having more true beliefs and fewer false ones.

## Key Secondary Sources

- William Alston, "The Deontological Conception of Epistemic Justification," *Philosophical Perspectives* 2, 1988, pp. 257–299. A critical examination of deontological approaches to epistemic norms, arguing that if we cannot choose our beliefs voluntarily, deontological epistemic obligations are incoherent.

- Richard Feldman, "The Ethics of Belief," *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* 60, 2000, pp. 667–695. Defends a strong evidentialist ethics of belief against pragmatist alternatives, arguing that epistemic and practical norms are distinct and must not be conflated.

- John Heil, "Doxastic Agency," *Philosophical Studies* 43, 1983, pp. 355–364. Examines the question of doxastic voluntarism — whether we can choose our beliefs — and its implications for the coherence of epistemic deontology.

- Pascal Engel, "In Defense of Normativism about the Aim of Belief," in *Epistemic Value*, eds. Haddock, Millar, and Pritchard, Oxford University Press, 2009. Defends the view that beliefs are constitutively governed by the norm of truth, against pragmatist and relativist alternatives.

- Jonathan Adler, *Belief's Own Ethics*, MIT Press, 2002. A wide-ranging treatment of the ethics of belief that engages with Clifford, James, and the deontological/evaluative debate, arguing for a "constitutive" relationship between belief and evidence.

- Nishi Shah, "How Truth Governs Belief," *Philosophical Review* 112, 2003, pp. 447–482. Argues that the truth-norm is constitutive of the concept of belief itself, not merely a norm externally imposed on it — a crucial contribution to debates about the nature of epistemic norms.

- Peter Graham, "Ought and Blameworthiness," *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* 91, 2015, pp. 665–673. Examines the relationship between epistemic oughts and blameworthiness, arguing for a distinction between ideal epistemic standards and standards for epistemic blame.

- Clayton Littlejohn, *Justification and the Truth-Connection*, Cambridge University Press, 2012. Develops a "factive" account of epistemic norms, arguing that epistemic justification essentially involves a connection to truth — a strong externalist position on epistemic norms.

- Anthony Booth and Rik Peels, eds., *The Epistemic Duty to Believe*, Routledge, 2010. An anthology examining whether there are genuine epistemic duties to believe, with essays on voluntarism, responsibility, and the grounds of epistemic obligation.

- Ralph Wedgwood, "The Aim of Belief," *Philosophical Perspectives* 16, 2002, pp. 267–297. Defends the view that belief is constitutively governed by the aim of truth, developing this into a normativity account that grounds epistemic obligations in the nature of the mental state of belief.

- Berker, Selim, "Epistemic Teleology and the Separateness of Propositions," *Philosophical Review* 122, 2013, pp. 337–393. A critique of epistemic consequentialism (the view that epistemic norms are determined by their consequences for the total ratio of true to false beliefs) with implications for the structure of epistemic obligation.

- Miranda Fricker, "Epistemic Supererogation," *Synthese* 190, 2013, pp. 3079–3096. Explores whether there is an analogue of supererogation in epistemic contexts — acts of epistemic excellence that go beyond what is required — and what this implies for the structure of epistemic norms.

- Stewart Cohen, "Basic Knowledge and the Problem of Easy Knowledge," *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* 65, 2002, pp. 309–329. Relevant to norms on belief formation and whether certain methods of belief-formation violate standards of epistemic responsibility even when they are reliable.

- Blake Roeber, "Evidence, Judgment, and Belief at Will," *Mind* 127, 2018, pp. 1–29. A recent treatment of doxastic voluntarism and its relationship to epistemic norms, arguing that even if we cannot believe at will, this does not undermine deontological epistemology.
