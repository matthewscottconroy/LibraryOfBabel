# Key Figures: Epistemic Norms

## W.K. Clifford (1845–1879)

Clifford's essay "The Ethics of Belief" (1877) is the canonical statement of strict epistemic deontology. His central claim — that it is always wrong, everywhere and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence — makes epistemic obligations categorical in the same sense as moral obligations. Clifford illustrates his thesis with the parable of a ship-owner who convinces himself, against the evidence, that his old ship is seaworthy and sends passengers to their deaths. Clifford's argument connects epistemic irresponsibility to practical harm, but he also maintains that believing without evidence is wrong independently of its consequences, because it corrupts the epistemic practices on which society depends.

## William James (1842–1910)

James's "The Will to Believe" (1897) is the principal pragmatist challenge to Clifford. He argues that Clifford's demand that we always proportion belief to evidence is itself a substantive epistemic policy — one that James calls the "empiricist" rule — and that this policy is not the only rational one. In certain cases (where the choice between hypotheses is live, forced, and momentous), waiting for sufficient evidence means forfeiting a good that could only be gained by acting on the tentative belief. James argues that in religious belief in particular, the pragmatic benefits of belief are epistemically relevant, and that Clifford's sweeping prohibition against believing without sufficient evidence is self-defeating and excessively restrictive.

## Timothy Williamson (b. 1955)

Williamson's *Knowledge and Its Limits* (2000) proposes the most influential recent account of epistemic norms in terms of knowledge rather than justification. His knowledge norm of assertion — "only assert what you know" — is paired with a corresponding knowledge norm of belief: knowledge, not mere true justified belief, is the epistemic ideal. Williamson's account is notable for being externalist about epistemic norms: whether you are conforming to the norm depends on facts about the world (whether your belief is knowledge), not merely on your internal epistemic position. This generates interesting asymmetries between a subject who violates the norm unwittingly (due to a Gettier situation) and one who violates it through negligence.

## William Alston (1921–2009)

Alston's critique of deontological epistemology is the most systematic in the literature. In "The Deontological Conception of Epistemic Justification" (1988), he argues that if epistemic obligations require the ability to comply with them — a standard presupposition of deontological thinking — then since believers typically cannot control their beliefs by direct act of will (doxastic involuntarism), there can be no genuine deontological obligations to believe. Alston's alternative is an evaluative conception of epistemic justification: rather than asking whether a belief fulfills an obligation, we should ask whether it is "objectively good" from an epistemic point of view, which does not require voluntary control.

## Nishi Shah (b. c. 1970)

Shah's paper "How Truth Governs Belief" (2003) makes a distinctive contribution to the debate about the nature of epistemic norms by arguing that the truth-norm is *constitutive* of the concept of belief rather than an external standard applied to it. His central observation is that when deliberating about whether to believe p, the question "should I believe p?" immediately transforms into "is p true?" — a phenomenon he calls "transparency." This transparency cannot be explained if the truth-norm is merely an external epistemic norm; it can only be explained if the norm is part of what makes a mental state a belief at all. Shah's argument has become a touchstone in debates about the nature of epistemic normativity and the relationship between belief and truth.

## Richard Feldman (b. 1948)

Feldman's defense of the ethics of belief from an evidentialist standpoint represents the dominant contemporary position within internalist epistemology. In "The Ethics of Belief" (2000), he argues that evidence is the only legitimate epistemic ground for belief, and that the Clifford-type demand to proportion belief to evidence is not merely one epistemic policy among others but a requirement of epistemic rationality itself. Feldman also argues for a form of uniqueness thesis — given a body of evidence, there is a unique rational doxastic response — which makes epistemic norms less permissive than pluralist accounts suggest. His evidentialism connects the ethics of belief to his broader defense of mentalist internalism developed jointly with Conee.

## Pascal Engel (b. 1952)

Engel's defense of normativism about belief — the view that belief is constitutively governed by the norm of truth — engages with the most sophisticated challenges from pragmatism and relativism. He argues that the truth-norm is not merely a contingent norm that happens to govern the practice of believing but is analytically tied to the concept of belief itself. This "normativism" about the aim of belief connects to debates about epistemic norms by grounding those norms in the nature of the mental state of belief rather than in external standards of epistemic responsibility. Engel's work has been particularly valuable for clarifying what it would mean for beliefs to be governed by norms at all.
