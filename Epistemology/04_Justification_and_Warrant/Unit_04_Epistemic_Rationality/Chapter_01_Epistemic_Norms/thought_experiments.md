# Thought Experiments: Epistemic Norms

## Clifford's Ship-Owner

**The scenario:** A ship-owner is about to send his aging, not-quite-seaworthy ship across the ocean with a cargo of emigrants. He has doubts about its condition, but he suppresses these doubts by convincing himself that it has made many previous voyages safely and that it will be watched over by Providence. He sends it; it sinks; all passengers die. Now ask: was the ship-owner wrong to believe the ship was seaworthy?

**The philosophical point:** Clifford uses this case to argue that beliefs formed through inadequate inquiry are morally wrong — not merely epistemically inadequate — even when they happen to be true. His claim is that the ship-owner's epistemic crime consists in acquiring belief through "stolen" evidence-circumventing methods. The case raises the question of whether there is a genuinely *epistemic* wrong at issue (believing without sufficient evidence) that is distinct from the *moral* wrong (endangering passengers). Clifford's view is that these are connected: the epistemic wrong corrupts the habits of inquiry that a trustworthy society requires, making every instance of under-evidenced belief a wrong to the community.

## James's Mountain Climber

**The scenario:** A mountain climber comes to a gap she cannot jump without full confidence. If she hesitates and fails to believe she can make the jump, she will not jump with sufficient force and will fall. If she believes she can make it — even though the evidence is genuinely equivocal — her confidence enables a successful leap. The belief must come before the evidence, because the evidence is partly constituted by how she performs.

**The philosophical point:** James's pragmatist response to Clifford is most vivid in cases where the truth of a belief partly depends on acting as if it is true. The climber's case is one of a class of "self-fulfilling" or "performatively determined" beliefs: the belief that she can leap enables the very performance that would confirm the belief. James argues that in such cases Clifford's evidentialist norm is self-undermining — insisting on evidence before belief would guarantee the worst outcome. The case raises the question of whether epistemic norms should be entirely divorced from practical consequences and whether doxastic pragmatism has a legitimate domain.

## Williamson's Assertion and Knowledge

**The scenario:** A friend asks whether the train leaves at 8:00 or 8:30. You believe, with high justification but without certainty, that it leaves at 8:00 — your evidence is a schedule you read this morning. You assert "The train leaves at 8:00." Your belief is justified; it is also, as it happens, true. But as you learn later, you do not *know* it — there were maintenance changes that cancelled that train and you missed the update. According to Williamson's knowledge norm of assertion, did you violate a norm when you asserted?

**The philosophical point:** Williamson's knowledge norm of assertion (KNA) says yes — you violated the norm, even though your assertion was justified and true. The norm requires knowledge, not merely justified true belief. This is counterintuitive because you were epistemically blameless: you did everything right from the inside. Williamson's response is that there is a gap between violating a norm and being blameworthy for violating it; you can violate a norm through no fault of your own (as when one unknowingly promises to do something impossible). The thought experiment raises deep questions about whether epistemic norms are best understood as subjective standards (tied to one's internal epistemic position) or objective standards (tied to one's actual epistemic achievements).

## The Voluntarist's Dilemma

**The scenario:** A student is told that she has an epistemic duty to believe on sufficient evidence. She tries to comply: she carefully reviews the evidence for and against a proposition and determines that it is inconclusive. Yet she finds herself believing it anyway — the belief just persists despite her assessment of its inadequate evidential basis. She cannot seem to stop believing simply by deciding to stop. She tries to take on the belief's negation; she cannot. Believing seems to happen to her, not something she does.

**The philosophical point:** This case presents the problem of doxastic voluntarism — the view that we can choose our beliefs — in its most vivid form. Alston's critique of epistemic deontology depends on the fact that if we cannot control our beliefs by direct act of will, then talk of epistemic *duties* to believe or not believe is incoherent: one can only have a duty to do something one can do. The student's predicament is that she wants to comply with Clifford's norm but finds she lacks the relevant control. Defenders of epistemic deontology must either argue that indirect doxastic control (through inquiry, attention, and evidence-seeking) is sufficient for obligation, or modify the content of the epistemic norm so that it governs inquiry-practices rather than individual beliefs.

## The Epistemic Supererogant

**The scenario:** A scientist goes far beyond what her epistemic duties require: she not only proportions her beliefs to evidence but engages in extraordinary investigative effort, cross-checking every source, consulting experts in adjacent fields, running repeated experiments. Her epistemic performance far exceeds the minimum required for justified belief. Are there acts of epistemic supererogation — going beyond the call of epistemic duty — that deserve distinctive recognition?

**The philosophical point:** Fricker's notion of epistemic supererogation and the broader question of whether epistemic norms have a parallel to the moral supererogation debate raises important questions about the structure of epistemic obligation. If epistemic norms are purely consequentialist (maximize true beliefs), then all extra investigation is required whenever it would improve the expected ratio of true to false beliefs — leaving no room for supererogation. If epistemic norms are deontological (do what a responsible epistemic agent would do), then there may be a threshold above which further effort is praiseworthy but not required. The case illuminates whether epistemic obligations exhaust our epistemic ideals or whether there is room for epistemic excellence beyond the obligatory.
