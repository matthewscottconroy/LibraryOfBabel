# Higher-Order Evidence and Epistemic Akrasia

Higher-order evidence is evidence about your own epistemic situation — about whether your reasoning processes are reliable, whether you are biased, whether your seemings and intuitions are trustworthy in the current circumstances. This kind of meta-level evidence creates distinctive puzzles about rational belief: how should you respond when you have evidence that your own reasoning may be unreliable?

## First-Order and Higher-Order Evidence

*First-order evidence* is evidence directly about the proposition you are evaluating: evidence for or against the claim itself. When I gather data about whether a drug is effective, I am collecting first-order evidence.

*Higher-order evidence* is evidence about my epistemic situation with respect to the proposition: evidence that I am biased in forming beliefs about drug effectiveness, that I have been given a reasoning-distorting drug, that experts in the field disagree with my conclusion and they are more reliable reasoners than I am in this domain. This is meta-level evidence that bears on how much to trust my first-order reasoning.

The puzzle: how should higher-order evidence affect my belief? If I have strong first-order evidence for P and also evidence that my reasoning about P-type questions is unreliable, what should I believe?

## The Case for Full Updating on Higher-Order Evidence

One approach: rational agents should fully incorporate higher-order evidence. If I learn that I have been given a reasoning-impairing drug, this evidence undermines the reliability of my reasoning, and I should reduce my credence in any conclusion I've reasoned to while impaired.

This is the "conciliationist" or "Steadfast-opponent" position, associated with Richard Feldman and David Christensen. It holds that higher-order evidence is just more evidence, and rational agents should update on all their evidence, including evidence about their own reliability.

On this view, if an expert I regard as my epistemic peer disagrees with my conclusion, this is higher-order evidence that I might be wrong (since one of us has made an error, and I can't claim to be systematically more reliable than my peer). I should move toward their position — not all the way, but significantly.

## Epistemic Akrasia

*Epistemic akrasia* is the state of believing P while also believing that you are not justified in believing P — or more precisely, maintaining a belief while believing that the totality of your evidence doesn't support it. This is the epistemic analog of akrasia (weakness of will) in practical reasoning.

The puzzle: is epistemic akrasia possible? Is it coherent to believe P while believing that your evidence makes P unlikely?

**Rational epistemic akrasia**: David Christensen has argued that there are cases where epistemic akrasia is rationally appropriate. If you calculate a logical argument and reach the conclusion, but also have good evidence that you are prone to reasoning errors in formal logic, it might be rational to maintain the conclusion (based on your first-order evidence) while also believing that your evidence doesn't fully support it (based on higher-order evidence about your reliability). The two levels of evidence are in tension, and both must be accommodated.

**Against epistemic akrasia**: Others argue that epistemic akrasia is never rationally appropriate. If you believe your belief in P is unjustified, you should stop believing P — your meta-belief about your own justification should constrain your first-order belief. This is the *enkratic principle*: if you believe you should not believe P, you should not believe P.

## The Debasing Evidence Problem

A particularly acute form of higher-order evidence is "debasing evidence" — evidence that your belief-forming process in this domain has been compromised. Suppose you realize that your credence in a political proposition has been influenced by motivated reasoning, tribal loyalty, or wishful thinking rather than evidence. Should you revise your credence?

The answer seems clearly yes: you should not maintain beliefs you recognize as products of bias. This is one of the most practically significant applications of higher-order evidence — it provides a rationale for epistemic humility about beliefs formed in domains where bias is likely.

## The Independence Principle

David Christensen argues for the *independence principle*: in evaluating the significance of others' disagreement or convergence, you should set aside your own first-order reasoning and evaluate the epistemic situation "independently." Otherwise, your assessment of how reliable you and your peer are will be circular (you'll rely on the first-order reasoning you're supposed to be bracketing).

The independence principle has implications for how to handle peer disagreement (discussed in Topic 9) and for how to respond to experts who challenge your conclusions. The principle pushes toward significant conciliation: you should take seriously the evidence that others' reasoning provides, rather than simply assuming your reasoning is reliable.

## Disagreement and Self-Doubt

Higher-order evidence connects directly to the epistemology of disagreement. When a respected expert disagrees with you, this is both evidence about the disputed proposition (they have evidence you don't) and higher-order evidence about your reliability (you and an equally good reasoner have reached different conclusions, which suggests error somewhere).

How much to revise depends on fine-grained assessments of the epistemic situation: how reliable is the dissenting reasoner? What is their track record in this domain? Is the disagreement due to different evidence or different inference from the same evidence? These questions require a nuanced account of the evidential significance of disagreement that is currently an active research area.
