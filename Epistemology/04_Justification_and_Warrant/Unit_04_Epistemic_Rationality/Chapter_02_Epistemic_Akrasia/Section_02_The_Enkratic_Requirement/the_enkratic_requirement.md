# The Enkratic Requirement

The enkratic principle in epistemology states that rational agents should bring their first-order beliefs into line with their higher-order beliefs about what they are justified in believing. If you believe that you are not justified in believing P, you should not believe P. The term comes from the Greek *enkrateia* — self-mastery or self-control — and the enkratic requirement captures the idea that a rational agent has a kind of reflective control over their own doxastic states.

## The Principle Stated

The enkratic requirement can be formulated in several ways:

**Strong form**: If you believe that you ought not believe P, then you ought not believe P.

**Weak form**: If you believe that you are not justified in believing P, this provides you with a reason to revise or suspend your belief in P.

**Conditional form**: Necessarily, if you believe you ought to believe P, you do believe P (a transparency-like requirement).

The strongest forms make epistemic akrasia — maintaining a belief while believing you are not justified in it — irrational in all circumstances. The weaker forms treat the mismatch as generating a rational pressure toward revision without being absolutely prohibited.

## The Case for the Enkratic Requirement

Why should we accept the enkratic requirement? Several considerations support it:

**The agency argument**: Rational belief is not mere passive reception of inputs; it involves something like cognitive agency. An agent who recognizes that their evidence does not support P but continues to believe P is not exercising appropriate cognitive self-governance. Just as practically rational agents act in accordance with their considered judgments about what to do, epistemically rational agents should believe in accordance with their considered judgments about what to believe.

**The unity of reason**: There is something deeply incoherent about a cognitive state that simultaneously represents the world as containing P and represents one's own epistemic situation as not warranting belief in P. Rational agents aim for coherence in their belief systems, and this includes coherence between first-order beliefs and beliefs about the justificatory status of those first-order beliefs.

**The normative authority of reflection**: Deliberate reflection on one's epistemic situation has a kind of authority over automatic, unreflective belief formation. When careful consideration reveals that a belief is unjustified, that verdict should govern. Allowing unreflective beliefs to persist in the face of contrary verdicts from reflection would be to privilege the automatic over the reflective, which inverts the proper hierarchy of epistemic evaluation.

## Challenges to the Enkratic Requirement

Despite these considerations, several challenges make the enkratic requirement difficult to maintain in full generality.

**The higher-order evidence problem**: David Christensen's work on higher-order evidence has raised the most sustained challenge. Suppose you have strong first-order evidence for P, but also higher-order evidence that your reasoning in this domain is unreliable. The enkratic requirement would say: since you believe your first-order reasoning is unreliable, revise your first-order belief. But this seems to give too much power to higher-order evidence — it allows global skeptical worries about reliability to undermine perfectly good first-order reasoning.

**The bootstrapping objection**: If you update your first-order belief to match your higher-order beliefs about justification, and your higher-order beliefs are themselves formed by the same potentially unreliable process, you may be using your higher-order beliefs to bootstrap inappropriate confidence in your epistemic situation. This can lead to spirals of self-undermining higher-order doubt.

**The akrasia defense**: Some philosophers, including Christensen and Worsnip, have argued that there are genuinely akratic states that are rationally obligatory — where the demands of first-order rationality (respond to your first-order evidence) and higher-order rationality (revise in light of meta-evidence about your reliability) genuinely conflict. In such cases, satisfying one rational requirement requires violating another. The enkratic requirement ignores this possibility.

## Worsnip's Resolution

Alex Worsnip has proposed a sophisticated response: the enkratic requirement is a genuine rational requirement, but it governs a specific type of rationality — *coherence* rationality rather than *responsiveness-to-reasons* rationality. The enkratic requirement says that believing P while believing you are unjustified in P is *incoherent*, and incoherence is always a rational defect. But this doesn't settle which belief you should revise: you should revise whichever is less well-supported.

This means epistemic akrasia is always rationally criticizable in one dimension (coherence), but it may be that no available alternative is better overall — you might be stuck between incoherence and worse overall justification. The enkratic requirement correctly identifies a defect without incorrectly implying that the defect is always avoidable.

## Practical Implications

The enkratic requirement, even in weakened form, has important practical implications. It supports:

- **Epistemic humility**: When you recognize your own potential biases, the enkratic requirement demands that this recognition have some effect on your confidence, not merely be an idle acknowledgment.

- **Intellectual integrity**: The person who says "I know I'm probably wrong about this, but I believe it anyway" is exhibiting a form of epistemic bad faith — failing to live up to their own epistemic standards.

- **Reflective equilibrium**: The method of reflective equilibrium in philosophy and ethics is implicitly enkratic: it requires bringing one's considered judgments and theoretical commitments into coherence with each other.

The tension between the enkratic requirement and the full force of first-order evidence remains one of the most productive ongoing debates in the epistemology of rationality.
