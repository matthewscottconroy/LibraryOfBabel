# Further Reading: Epistemic Akrasia and Higher-Order Evidence

## Primary Sources

- David Christensen, "Epistemology of Disagreement: The Good News," *Philosophical Review* 116, 2007, pp. 187–217. Develops the independence principle — the requirement that when assessing peer disagreement, one's higher-order evidence about one's own reliability must be treated independently of one's first-order evidence — which is central to the epistemic akrasia debate.

- David Christensen, "Higher-Order Evidence," *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* 81, 2010, pp. 185–215. The most systematic development of the view that higher-order evidence (evidence about one's own reliability) can rationally require revising beliefs formed on excellent first-order evidence.

- Alex Worsnip, "The Conflict of Evidence and Coherence," *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* 97, 2018, pp. 2–26. Argues that there is a genuine tension between the requirement to respond to first-order evidence and the requirement to maintain coherence, and that resolving it requires recognizing epistemic akrasia as a real phenomenon.

- Richard Feldman, "Epistemological Puzzles about Disagreement," in *Epistemology Futures*, ed. Hetherington, Oxford University Press, 2006. An early examination of how higher-order evidence about disagreement affects first-order justified belief, approaching the enkratic requirement from an evidentialist perspective.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Thomas Kelly, "The Epistemic Significance of Disagreement," in *Oxford Studies in Epistemology*, vol. 1, eds. Hawthorne and Gendler, Oxford University Press, 2005. Defends the "total evidence" view that higher-order evidence should be weighed against first-order evidence in a unified manner, rather than being given independent standing.

- Adam Elga, "Reflection and Disagreement," *Noûs* 41, 2007, pp. 478–502. Defends "Equal Weight" — when peers disagree, one should split the difference equally — based on considerations about the equal epistemic standing of self and others, connecting to the higher-order evidence debate.

- Ernest Sosa, "The Epistemology of Disagreement," in *Social Epistemology*, eds. Haddock, Millar, and Pritchard, Oxford University Press, 2010. A nuanced treatment of how disagreement bears on one's first-order evidence, arguing against simple conciliationist accounts from a virtue-epistemological standpoint.

- Ryan Doody, "Epistemic Akrasia: Susanna Rinard's New Argument," *Philosophical Studies* 177, 2020. A recent examination of whether epistemic akrasia is possible, engaging with arguments from Rinard's pragmatist epistemology.

- Susanna Rinard, "Against the New Evidentialists," *Philosophical Issues* 25, 2015, pp. 208–223. Argues against the view that evidence is the exclusive determinant of rational belief, with implications for what higher-order evidence should require of us.

- Maria Lasonen-Aarnio, "Higher-Order Evidence and the Limits of Defeat," *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* 88, 2014, pp. 314–345. Argues that higher-order evidence has limits — it cannot always defeat first-order evidence — and develops an account of when higher-order evidence is and is not epistemically efficacious.

- Hilary Kornblith, "Is Epistemic Akrasia Possible?" in *Rationality and the Good*, eds. Shafer-Landau, Oxford University Press, 2007. Examines whether genuine epistemic akrasia (believing p while believing you should not believe p) is psychologically and epistemically coherent.

- Alex Worsnip, *Fitting Things Together: Coherence and the Demands of Structural Rationality*, Oxford University Press, 2021. Develops the theory of structural rationality — the requirement to maintain coherence among one's attitudes — and examines how it interacts with the substantive rationality requirement to respond to evidence.

- Jonathan Way, "Transmission and the Wrong Kind of Reasons," *Ethics* 122, 2012, pp. 489–515. Examines what it means for reasons to be transmitted from lower to higher order, with implications for the structure of higher-order evidence and epistemic rationality.

- Nicholas Silins, "Deception and Evidence," *Philosophical Perspectives* 26, 2012, pp. 375–404. Examines what agents should believe when they have evidence that their evidence is misleading, connecting to akrasia and higher-order evidence.

- Christensen, David, "Disagreement as Evidence: The Epistemology of Controversy," *Philosophy Compass* 4, 2009, pp. 756–767. A survey article on disagreement and higher-order evidence suitable as introductory reading.

- Miriam Schoenfield, "An Accuracy Based Approach to Higher-Order Evidence," *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* 96, 2018, pp. 690–715. Uses accuracy-first epistemology to assess when deferring to higher-order evidence is rational, contributing to the formal epistemology of the topic.

- Earl Conee, "First Things First," in *Disagreement*, eds. Feldman and Warfield, Oxford University Press, 2010. Defends a "first-order" priority view: one's first-order evidence always takes precedence over higher-order evidence about peer disagreement, resisting the conciliationist pressure.
