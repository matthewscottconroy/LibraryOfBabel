# Key Figures: Epistemic Akrasia and Higher-Order Evidence

## David Christensen (b. 1956)

Christensen is the central figure in the contemporary debate about higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia. His independence principle holds that when evaluating what to believe in light of higher-order evidence about one's own reliability, one must assess that higher-order evidence independently of the first-order reasoning it potentially undermines. This is designed to prevent "bootstrapping" — using one's first-order conclusions to dismiss evidence that one reached those conclusions unreliably. Christensen's work on disagreement and higher-order evidence has generated the most discussed set of cases in the literature on epistemic rationality, and his papers "Higher-Order Evidence" (2010) and "Disagreement and Public Controversy" have become required reading for anyone working in this area.

## Alex Worsnip (b. c. 1988)

Worsnip's contribution centers on the relationship between epistemic coherence and evidential rationality. In "The Conflict of Evidence and Coherence" (2018) and his book *Fitting Things Together* (2021), he argues for a distinction between *substantive rationality* (responding correctly to evidence) and *structural rationality* (maintaining coherence among one's attitudes). He argues that epistemic akrasia — believing p while believing one ought not believe p — is a genuine violation of structural rationality even when it results from appropriate responses to first-order and higher-order evidence individually considered. This "coherence as a distinct rational requirement" view positions Worsnip as a major defender of the claim that rationality has both evidential and coherence components that can genuinely conflict.

## Maria Lasonen-Aarnio (b. c. 1978)

Lasonen-Aarnio has developed a sophisticated position on the limits of higher-order defeat. In "Higher-Order Evidence and the Limits of Defeat" (2014), she argues that it is sometimes epistemically permissible — even required — to maintain a belief in the face of higher-order evidence that one's first-order reasoning was unreliable, provided the first-order reasoning was in fact epistemically excellent. Her position challenges the dominant conciliationist tendency in the literature by insisting that ideal epistemic agents will sometimes "look akratic" from the outside — maintaining beliefs that they have higher-order evidence to doubt — precisely because they are responding appropriately to all the evidence taken together.

## Thomas Kelly (b. 1971)

Kelly defends a "total evidence" approach to higher-order evidence that contrasts with Christensen's independence principle. On Kelly's view, higher-order evidence (e.g., that a peer disagrees with you) should simply be added to one's total body of evidence and weighed accordingly — it has no special standing that requires it to be evaluated independently of first-order evidence. Kelly's position is thus more tolerant of "staying put" in the face of peer disagreement and less demanding of revision than Christensen's conciliationism. His papers on disagreement and higher-order evidence, particularly "The Epistemic Significance of Disagreement" (2005), provide the most developed alternative to the Christensen line.

## Adam Elga (b. 1975)

Elga's defense of the "Equal Weight" view of disagreement — the position that when two epistemic peers disagree, one should give equal weight to their respective views, typically resulting in splitting the difference — is directly connected to the higher-order evidence and akrasia debates. Equal Weight is motivated by the thought that if two people are equally reliable reasoners, then the fact of disagreement gives each of them equal reason to doubt their own conclusion: this is itself a form of higher-order evidence. Elga's "Reflection and Disagreement" (2007) is one of the most cited papers in the literature and has framed much of the subsequent debate about when and how much to revise one's beliefs in response to learning of disagreement.

## Richard Feldman (b. 1948)

Feldman's contribution to the epistemic akrasia debate comes primarily through his evidentialist framework. He defends a "uniqueness thesis" — that a body of evidence determines a unique rational doxastic response — and applies this to higher-order evidence, arguing that if the evidence supports a belief, one is required to hold it, and if higher-order evidence shows that one's first-order reasoning was flawed, one is required to revise. Feldman's evidentialist approach makes epistemic akrasia (believing p while believing one shouldn't) irrational by definition: there is no room for rational belief that violates one's assessed epistemic obligations, since the obligation tracks what the evidence dictates.

## Susanna Rinard (b. c. 1982)

Rinard's pragmatist challenge to evidentialist accounts of higher-order evidence and epistemic norms is one of the most provocative recent positions in the field. She argues that the demand to proportion belief entirely to evidence is itself a normative assumption that can be challenged on pragmatic grounds, and that there are cases where the pragmatic case for maintaining a belief despite higher-order evidence against it is overwhelming. Her challenge to the "new evidentialists" (Christensen, Elga, Feldman) has sharpened the debate about whether epistemic norms are entirely distinct from practical norms and whether the ideal of evidence-responsiveness can be justified on purely epistemic grounds.
