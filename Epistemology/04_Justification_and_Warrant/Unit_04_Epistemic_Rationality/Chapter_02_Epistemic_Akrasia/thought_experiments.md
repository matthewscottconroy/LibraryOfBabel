# Thought Experiments: Epistemic Akrasia and Higher-Order Evidence

## The Exhausted Statistician

**The scenario:** A statistician, Dr. Park, has worked through a complex multi-step analysis and arrived at the conclusion that a drug is effective. She is highly confident in her conclusion because her reasoning seemed rigorous. She then realizes she worked through the night with only two hours of sleep, and she knows from research on cognitive performance that sleep-deprived statisticians make systematic errors roughly 40% of the time on analyses of this complexity. This is higher-order evidence that her first-order reasoning may be flawed. Should she revise her confidence in the drug's effectiveness, even if she cannot identify any actual error in her analysis?

**The philosophical point:** This is Christensen's core case type for the force of higher-order evidence. The statistician has good first-order evidence (the analysis itself) and now also has higher-order evidence (about her reliability under the conditions in which she reasoned). Christensen's independence principle requires she assess the latter independently of the former: she should not simply say "but my reasoning seemed fine to me" because this uses the first-order product to evaluate the higher-order concern about the process. The case motivates revising confidence in her conclusion even without identifying a specific error — which may seem demanding but follows from taking the higher-order evidence seriously.

## The Peer Disagreement Problem

**The scenario:** Two philosophers, Sam and Alex, have independently reached opposite conclusions about whether a key argument for moral realism is sound. They are recognized as equally competent and careful reasoners in this area; they have access to the same arguments and literature; and neither can identify a flaw in the other's reasoning. Sam is confident that the argument is sound; Alex is confident it is not. Should Sam revise his confidence in light of Alex's disagreement?

**The philosophical point:** This is the canonical peer disagreement case. Conciliationists (Christensen, Elga) argue that Sam should give significant weight to Alex's contrary view, treating it as higher-order evidence that the argument may not be as compelling as it seemed to Sam. Non-conciliationists (Kelly, van Inwagen) argue that Sam is entitled to maintain his view, since his first-order engagement with the argument is his best guide and Alex's disagreement merely reflects that even competent reasoners reach different conclusions on hard questions. The case exposes a deep tension in our epistemic norms: if we should always revise toward peers, disagreement produces perpetual agnosticism; if we should never revise, peer competence seems epistemically irrelevant.

## Moore's Paradox Applied: The Epistemic Akratic Believer

**The scenario:** A graduate student in epistemology forms the belief p (that a certain argument is valid) while simultaneously believing: "I ought not believe p — the evidence does not support it." She is aware of both mental states and cannot resolve the tension, but both persist. Is this a coherent psychological state? Is she rational?

**The philosophical point:** This case presents epistemic akrasia directly — holding a belief while believing one ought not hold it, the doxastic analogue of practical akrasia (doing what one believes one should not do). The logical structure parallels Moore's paradox ("p, but I don't believe p"). Worsnip argues that epistemic akrasia is a genuine violation of structural rationality — the requirement to maintain coherence among one's doxastic and normative attitudes — even if each individual attitude can be explained by appropriate responses to evidence. Lasonen-Aarnio's counter-view is that an ideally rational agent might sometimes find herself in an akratic-looking state precisely because she is responding correctly to conflicting first-order and higher-order evidence.

## The Enkratic Requirement

**The scenario:** Elena believes that the evidence clearly supports p. She also believes that the epistemically correct response to her evidence is to believe p. Yet Elena does not believe p — she suspends judgment, because she is anxious about committing to a conclusion. Is Elena's suspension of judgment rational?

**The philosophical point:** The enkratic requirement in epistemology holds that if you believe you ought to believe p, you are rationally required to believe p — failing to believe what you judge you should believe is a form of incoherence parallel to weakness of epistemic will. Elena's case violates the enkratic requirement: she judges that she ought to believe p but fails to do so. This is structurally different from the akrasia case above, where one believes p while judging one ought not: here one fails to believe p while judging one ought. The case raises the question of whether the enkratic requirement is genuinely binding or whether there is legitimate epistemic "suspension" that can persist even in the face of a judgment that belief is warranted.

## Calibration and the Modest Expert

**The scenario:** An expert meteorologist, fully calibrated, believes the probability of rain tomorrow is 70%. She also knows that her probabilistic forecasts are correct at exactly the frequency she predicts. She is asked: "Do you *know* it will rain tomorrow?" She says: "I believe it with 70% confidence — it is more likely than not." Her colleague, equally calibrated at 70% confidence, says: "Yes, it will rain" — asserting it flatly. Who is conforming to the norms of assertion and belief?

**The philosophical point:** This case connects the higher-order evidence debate to norms on assertion and credence. Williamson's knowledge norm of assertion would say neither is asserting that it will rain unless they know it will — but the meteorologist who asserts at 70% does not know. Christensen's higher-order evidence framework is relevant here because the meteorologist's calibration data (her track record) functions as higher-order evidence about her reliability, and this higher-order evidence supports having exactly 70% credence rather than flat belief. The case raises the question of whether epistemic norms for belief are best understood in terms of binary belief or graded credence, and whether knowledge norms can apply to probabilistic assertions.
