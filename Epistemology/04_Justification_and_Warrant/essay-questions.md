# Essay Questions: Unit 4 — Justification and Warrant

---

**1. The Structure of Justification: Foundationalism, Coherentism, Infinitism**

Foundationalism holds that justified belief has a hierarchical structure with basic beliefs at the base; coherentism holds that justification is entirely a matter of coherence among beliefs; infinitism (Klein) holds that the regress of justification is not vicious. Critically evaluate each position against the regress argument. Does modest foundationalism — basic beliefs are non-inferentially but defeasibly justified — provide a more plausible position than classical foundationalism? Is coherentism's "observation requirement" (the need for some connection to experience) a fatal concession, or can coherentism accommodate it?

---

**2. Internalism and Externalism about Justification**

Internalists hold that justification depends only on factors accessible to the agent by reflection; externalists hold that justification can depend on factors the agent has no access to. Carefully reconstruct the distinction, noting that "internalism" and "externalism" are each families of positions. What is the strongest argument for internalism (deontological considerations, the "new evil demon" objection to reliabilism)? What is the strongest argument for externalism (the anti-regress argument, the naturalization of epistemology)? Is there a principled intermediate position?

---

**3. Process Reliabilism: Statement, Objections, Responses**

Goldman's process reliabilism holds that a belief is justified iff it is produced by a reliable belief-forming process. Formulate the view precisely and assess the main objections: (a) the generality problem — how to individuate the type of process whose reliability is relevant; (b) the new evil demon problem — a deceived agent whose processes are unreliable seems epistemically blameless, yet reliabilism implies their beliefs are unjustified; (c) the meta-incoherence problem — agents who do not believe their processes are reliable seem to lack justification even when the processes are reliable. Assess Goldman's responses and conclude with your overall evaluation of reliabilism's prospects.

---

**4. Plantinga's Proper Functionalism and its Alternatives**

Alvin Plantinga distinguishes *justification* (a deontological notion) from *warrant* (what distinguishes knowledge from merely true belief) and argues that warrant is a matter of proper function: cognitive faculties functioning properly in the environment for which they were designed, with a truth-aimed module. Critically assess proper functionalism. Does proper functionalism avoid the generality problem that besets reliabilism? Is the notion of "proper function" adequately specified in terms that do not presuppose a design plan? Does the theory have theistic presuppositions that disqualify it as a neutral epistemological account?

---

**5. Virtue Epistemology and the Analysis of Knowledge**

Virtue epistemologists hold that justified belief (and perhaps knowledge) is grounded in the exercise of intellectual virtues. Distinguish at least two versions: agent reliabilism (Greco), where virtues are reliable cognitive faculties, and responsibilist virtue epistemology (Zagzebski), where virtues are character traits. Does virtue epistemology provide a solution to the Gettier problem? Zagzebski argues that Gettier problems are inescapable for any JTB-plus-condition analysis but that virtue epistemology escapes this by treating knowledge as a unified achievement. Is this argument successful?

---

**6. Higher-Order Evidence and Epistemic Akrasia**

An agent has higher-order evidence when they have evidence about the reliability of their own epistemic processes. David Christensen argues that higher-order evidence can defeat first-order justification: if I have evidence that my reasoning is biased, I should revise my first-order credences accordingly. Raine Lasonen-Aarnio argues that this generates an untenable "epistemic downgrade" with no rational resting point. Critically assess the debate. Is epistemic akrasia — believing p while also believing that one's evidence supports not-p — possible for a fully rational agent? Does the answer depend on one's account of the relationship between first-person and third-person epistemic evaluation?

---

**7. The Deontological Conception of Justification and its Critics**

The deontological conception of epistemic justification (Chisholm, Feldman) treats epistemic norms as obligations: a belief is justified iff the agent is not violating any epistemic duty in holding it. Critics (Goldman, Sosa, Alston) argue that this conception is undermined by the involuntariness of belief: since belief is not typically subject to direct voluntary control, it cannot be subject to obligation in the relevant sense. William Alston's paper "The Deontological Conception of Epistemic Justification" (1988) is the canonical statement of this criticism. Is the deontological conception defensible against the involuntariness objection?
