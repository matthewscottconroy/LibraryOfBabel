# Unit 5: Skepticism — Unit Survey

## Central Questions

Scepticism is simultaneously the oldest and the most technically active problem in epistemology. Its basic structure — the argument that our epistemic position falls short of knowledge, because we cannot rule out possibilities of systematic deception — was recognized by the Pyrrhonists and given canonical modern form by Descartes. Contemporary analytic epistemology has refined the argument to such a degree of precision that even sophisticated defences require engagement with modal semantics, epistemic logic, and decision theory.

The central question is whether the sceptical argument should be accepted, refuted, or deflected, and by what means. The sceptical argument takes its sharpest form as follows:

1. If S knows that p, then S knows that not-SH (where SH is a sceptical hypothesis incompatible with p).
2. S does not know that not-SH.
3. Therefore, S does not know that p.

Premise 1 relies on the principle of epistemic closure: if S knows that p, and S knows that p entails q, then S knows (or is in a position to know) that q. Premise 2 seems intuitively compelling: surely we cannot rule out brain-in-vat scenarios or Cartesian demons. The conclusion threatens all ordinary knowledge.

Responses to the sceptical argument fall into three families: (a) accept closure but reject premise 2 by arguing that we do know we are not in a sceptical scenario (neo-Moorean responses); (b) reject closure, arguing that knowledge does not transmit across known entailment (Nozick's tracking account); and (c) defuse the argument by showing that its force depends on a context-sensitive element, so that it is valid within a high-standards context but does not undermine ordinary-standards attributions (contextualism). More recently, pragmatic encroachment and fallibilism provide further options.

## Map of Major Positions and Their Logical Relations

**Cartesian Scepticism and the Evil Demon.** Descartes's first Meditation considers the possibility that all one's experiences are produced by a maximally deceptive evil demon. The evil demon hypothesis is designed to be epistemically indistinguishable from normal experience. If indistinguishability prevents knowledge, and if we cannot rule out the demon, we cannot know anything about the external world.

**Brain-in-a-Vat.** Hilary Putnam's brain-in-a-vat hypothesis is the contemporary form of the evil demon. A brain is removed from its body, placed in a vat of nutrients, and connected to a computer that feeds it exactly the experiences of normal embodied life. The question is whether the envatted brain has the same epistemic position as a normal perceiver or a worse one.

**Closure Principle.** The epistemological closure principle: if S knows that p, and S competently deduces q from p, then S knows (or comes to know) that q. Closure is used in the sceptical argument to move from ordinary knowledge claims to knowledge of the denial of sceptical hypotheses. Nozick's denial of closure and Dretske's relevant alternatives framework both reject closure to block the sceptical argument.

**Neo-Moorean Responses.** G.E. Moore's "Proof of an External World" (1939) argued that he knew he had hands (he held them up) and hence knew he was not a brain in a vat. The argument has the form: since I know I have hands, and having hands entails not being a brain in a vat, I know I am not a brain in a vat. Epistemologists who accept closure and accept Moore's argument — accepting that we have ordinary knowledge and hence know the denial of sceptical hypotheses — are neo-Mooreans. The question is whether this constitutes a refutation of scepticism or merely begs the question against it (Davies, Wright).

**Relevant Alternatives.** Dretske proposes that knowledge requires only the ability to eliminate *relevant* alternatives, not all alternatives. Brain-in-a-vat is not a relevant alternative in ordinary contexts, so ordinary knowledge claims are unaffected. The sceptical argument fails because it demands ruling out irrelevant alternatives. The notion of relevance is doing enormous work and must be made precise.

**Contextualism.** DeRose and Lewis hold that "S knows that p" is context-sensitive: its truth conditions vary with the epistemic standards operative in the context of attribution. In ordinary contexts, low standards apply and ordinary knowledge claims are true. In sceptical contexts (philosophical discussion), high standards apply and ordinary knowledge claims may be false. The sceptical argument is valid — it shows that in the high-standards context generated by raising sceptical hypotheses, we do not know things — but this does not impugn ordinary-context knowledge attributions.

**Subject-Sensitive Invariantism / Pragmatic Encroachment.** Hawthorne and Stanley hold that knowledge attributions are context-sensitive, but the relevant context is the *subject's* practical situation, not the *attributor's* conversational context. An agent who faces high practical stakes in acting on a belief does not know it unless they meet higher epistemic standards than an agent for whom the stakes are low.

**Fallibilism.** Most epistemologists accept some form of fallibilism: knowledge does not require certainty or infallibility. The question for fallibilism is where it draws the line — what degree of fallibility is compatible with knowledge? And does fallibilism have the resources to respond to scepticism, or does it merely relocate the problem?

**Epistemic Externalism as Anti-Sceptical Strategy.** If knowledge does not require the agent to have access to the justifying factors — as reliabilism and proper functionalism hold — then the sceptical argument fails because it assumes the internalist picture that knowledge requires the agent to be able to rule out alternatives from within their perspective.

**Unit 1: Forms of Skepticism.** Chapter 1 presents Cartesian scepticism in its classical and contemporary forms: the dream argument, the evil demon, the brain-in-vat. Chapter 2 analyses the logical structure of the sceptical argument, distinguishing the closure-based form from the underdetermination form. **Unit 3: Fallibilism and Uncertainty.** Chapter 1 analyses fallibilism and the spectrum of epistemic positions between certainty and complete ignorance. **Unit 4: Living with Scepticism.** Chapter 1 examines Pyrrhonist and quietist responses that accept scepticism but deny its destructive consequences. Chapter 2 treats Hinge epistemology: Wittgenstein's *On Certainty* argued that certain "hinge propositions" (I have hands; the world has existed for more than five minutes) function as the framework within which inquiry takes place and are not themselves subject to justification or doubt. Chapters 3 examines the ethics of belief under radical uncertainty.

## Key Philosophers and Arguments

**Descartes (1596–1650).** First Meditation: the dream argument shows that any experience we can have is compatible with dreaming; the evil demon argument shows that any reasoning we can perform is compatible with systematic deception by an omnipotent being. The evil demon is the modern origin of brain-in-vat scepticism and all its descendants.

**G.E. Moore (1873–1958).** "Proof of an External World" (1939) and "Certainty" (1959) defend the commonsense view that we know ordinary empirical propositions with certainty. Moore's proof — I know I have hands; therefore an external world exists — is derided by sceptics as question-begging and celebrated by neo-Mooreans as correctly identifying that the price of scepticism is denial of obvious truths.

**Fred Dretske (1932–2013).** "Epistemic Operators" (*Journal of Philosophy*, 1970) introduced the relevant alternatives framework and the denial of closure. Dretske argued that "knows" is not a fully penetrating operator: it does not transmit from p to all consequences of p. This is counterintuitive but provides a principled response to the sceptical argument.

**Robert Nozick (1938–2002).** *Philosophical Explanations* (1981) developed the tracking account as an anti-sceptical strategy: S knows p iff S's belief tracks the truth of p in nearby possible worlds. Because the sceptical hypothesis is not a nearby possible world (relative to the actual world), the tracking account explains why we know ordinary propositions without knowing the denial of distant sceptical hypotheses. The price is the failure of closure.

**Keith DeRose (1962–).** "Solving the Skeptical Problem" (*Philosophical Review*, 1995) and *The Case for Contextualism* (2009) provide the most systematic defence of contextualism. DeRose's "bank cases" — a subject who faces either low or high financial stakes in knowing their bank is open — are the canonical contextualist thought experiments. DeRose argues that the truth conditions of "S knows p" shift with the alternatives salient in the attributor's conversational context.

**David Lewis (1941–2001).** "Elusive Knowledge" (*Australasian Journal of Philosophy*, 1996) develops a contextualism based on possible-world semantics: S knows p iff p is true in all possible worlds that S cannot eliminate, where "cannot eliminate" is subject to a Rule of Attention that makes any world salient by mentioning it. The Rule of Attention explains why raising the brain-in-vat hypothesis destroys knowledge: the sceptical scenario becomes a relevant alternative simply by being mentioned.

**Crispin Wright (1942–).** Developed a sophisticated version of the neo-Moorean response using the concept of "entitlement": we are entitled to accept certain hinge propositions (the external world exists, my senses are generally reliable) not because we have evidence for them but because they are the presupposition of any inquiry. This is related to Wittgenstein's hinge epistemology and to the concept of epistemic risk discussed by Wright in "Facts and Certainty" (1985).

**Hilary Putnam (1926–2016).** "Brains in a Vat" (*Reason, Truth and History*, 1981) argued that if we were brains in vats, our word "brain" would not refer to real brains (because reference is determined by causal connections to the environment). Hence the sentence "We are brains in a vat" would be self-refuting if we were in fact envatted. This semantic anti-sceptical argument generates a large literature on whether it establishes ordinary knowledge or merely establishes a kind of semantic externalism incompatible with scepticism.

## Essential Readings

1. **Descartes, *Meditations on First Philosophy*, Meditations I and II** (1641). First Meditation is the locus classicus of modern scepticism. Second Meditation introduces the Cogito and begins the reconstruction of knowledge. Essential background for everything in this unit.

2. **DeRose, Keith, "Solving the Skeptical Problem," *Philosophical Review*, 104:1 (1995), pp. 1–52.** The canonical contextualist response to scepticism. Long but essential. Shows how contextualism resolves the tension between sceptical arguments and ordinary knowledge attributions.

3. **Lewis, David, "Elusive Knowledge," *Australasian Journal of Philosophy*, 74:4 (1996), pp. 549–567.** The most elegant version of contextualism. Lewis's possible-worlds analysis of knowledge and the list of rules for relevant alternatives make this one of the most formally precise papers in the literature.

4. **Nozick, Robert, *Philosophical Explanations*, Chapter 3, §§ I–III** (1981). The tracking account and its application to scepticism. The most important denial of closure in the contemporary literature.

5. **Wright, Crispin, "Warrant for Nothing (and Foundations for Free)?", *Aristotelian Society Supplementary Volume*, 78:1 (2004), pp. 167–212.** Develops the concept of epistemic entitlement as the foundation for a neo-Moorean anti-sceptical strategy. The most sophisticated contemporary development of hinge epistemology.

## Open Questions and Live Debates

**Is the closure principle true?** Nozick and Dretske deny it; most epistemologists accept it. The debate turns on whether knowledge can be local (failing to transmit to remote consequences) or must be global. Hawthorne's *Knowledge and Lotteries* (2004) defends closure and argues that its denial has unacceptable consequences for ordinary epistemic practice.

**Does contextualism trivialize the problem?** Critics of contextualism (Hawthorne, Stanley) argue that it provides only a semantic response to what should be a substantive epistemological problem. If ordinary knowledge attributions are true, that shows ordinary knowledge is possible; the contextualist cannot explain *why* knowledge is possible but only that "knows" has different truth conditions in different contexts.

**Is pragmatic encroachment compatible with the idea that knowledge is a purely epistemic notion?** Critics argue that making knowledge sensitive to practical stakes corrupts the concept of knowledge, making it sensitive to factors irrelevant to the epistemic relation between believer and fact. Defenders (Fantl and McGrath) argue that this is a feature, not a bug: knowledge is the epistemic status that matters for rational action.

**Can Wittgenstein's hinge epistemology be systematized?** Wittgenstein's *On Certainty* is a set of notebooks, not a systematic treatise. Duncan Pritchard and Annalisa Coliva have developed systematic accounts of hinge propositions, but there is ongoing debate about whether the concept is clear enough to do the anti-sceptical work claimed for it.
