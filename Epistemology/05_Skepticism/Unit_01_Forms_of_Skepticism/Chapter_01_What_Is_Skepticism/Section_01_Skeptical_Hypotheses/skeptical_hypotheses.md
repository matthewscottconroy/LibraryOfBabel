# Skeptical Hypotheses

Skeptical hypotheses are the engine of philosophical skepticism. They are scenarios — carefully constructed thought experiments — that are stipulated to be evidentially indistinguishable from our ordinary situation while being scenarios in which our ordinary beliefs are massively false. The power of skeptical arguments derives entirely from the power of these hypotheses, and much of the response to skepticism consists in challenging how they work.

## The Dream Hypothesis

Descartes' first skeptical scenario in the *Meditations* is the dream hypothesis: perhaps you are now dreaming. Dreams can be vivid and coherent; in a dream, you might believe you are reading a book, that the world is as it appears, that your memories are accurate. And the crucial point: while you are in the dream, nothing in your experience marks it as a dream rather than waking life.

The dream hypothesis is limited in scope. Descartes acknowledges that even in dreams, there are elements — shapes, colors, general features — that reflect genuine features of reality. The dream hypothesis casts doubt on particular claims about what is before you, not on the entire fabric of experience.

## The Evil Demon

The evil demon (or *malin génie*) hypothesis is far more radical. Suppose an omnipotent being of supreme malice has dedicated itself to deceiving you. All your sensory experiences are fabrications. All your apparent reasoning is corrupted. The external world might not exist at all, or it might be entirely unlike how it appears.

This hypothesis is designed to be maximally powerful: unlike the dream hypothesis, it undermines even basic logical reasoning (if the demon corrupts your reasoning faculties) and mathematical certainty (if "2 + 2 = 4" only seems certain because the demon has arranged your cognition that way). Descartes exempts the *cogito* from this because even the demon's deceiving you guarantees that you exist to be deceived.

## The Brain in a Vat

Hilary Putnam framed a contemporary version of the skeptical hypothesis that has dominated recent discussion: suppose you are not a person with a body interacting with the world, but rather a brain in a vat of nutrients, connected to a supercomputer that generates all your sensory experience. Your experiences are computationally induced; there is no external world that they track.

The brain-in-a-vat (BIV) hypothesis is functionall equivalent to the evil demon for most purposes: it generates an evidentially indistinguishable scenario in which your beliefs about the external world are systematically false. But the BIV scenario is more naturalistically specified — it makes no appeal to magic or omnipotence, only to the possibility of perfect simulation.

Putnam famously argued that the BIV hypothesis is self-refuting: if you were a brain in a vat, your word "brain" would not refer to real brains (since you have never interacted with them) but to your computational analog of brains. So "I am a brain in a vat" would be false if true — a brain in a vat couldn't truly say it. This clever semantic argument has generated enormous discussion.

## The Matrix and Simulation Scenarios

Contemporary popular culture has given us the simulation hypothesis: perhaps the entire universe is a computer simulation run by an advanced civilization. This is philosophically similar to the BIV scenario but framed at a larger scale — not just your perceptions, but the entire physical world might be simulated.

Nick Bostrom's simulation argument suggests this possibility deserves genuine credence: given the likely capabilities of civilizations far in advance of ours, the number of simulations they might run could far exceed the number of "base reality" civilizations. This doesn't establish that we are simulated, but it raises the question of whether we should assign significant credence to the possibility.

## Common Features

All these hypotheses share structural features:

**Evidential indistinguishability**: By stipulation, from the inside, the hypothesis is indistinguishable from ordinary circumstances. This is what gives the hypothesis its bite — you cannot detect that you are in the skeptical scenario.

**Massive falsity**: In the skeptical scenario, your ordinary beliefs are false — perhaps not all of them, but the ones about the external world, other minds, or whatever the hypothesis targets.

**Possibility**: The hypothesis must be genuinely possible, not merely logically coherent but also epistemically possible — compatible with everything you know.

The combination generates the skeptical argument: since you cannot rule out H (the hypothesis), and since H's obtaining would make your beliefs false, you cannot claim to know those beliefs. Responses to skepticism must challenge one of these steps.
