# The Closure Principle and Skeptical Arguments

The skeptical argument from undeniable possibilities gains much of its force from the *closure principle* — a seemingly obvious claim about how knowledge transmits through known logical entailments. Understanding closure, and the role it plays in skeptical arguments, is essential to evaluating the most powerful contemporary formulations of skepticism.

## The Closure Principle

The closure principle (in one standard formulation): if you know that P, and you know that P entails Q, then you know (or are in a position to know) Q.

This seems almost trivially true. If I know that I have two hands, and I know that having two hands entails that I am not a handless brain in a vat, then I know (or can easily come to know) that I am not a handless brain in a vat.

More generally: knowledge is closed under known entailment. If you know the premise and you know the logical connection, you should be able to extend your knowledge to the conclusion.

## The Skeptical Argument Using Closure

Peter Unger and others have used closure to construct a devastating skeptical argument:

1. If I know that I have hands, then I know that I am not a handless brain in a vat.
2. I do not know that I am not a handless brain in a vat.
3. Therefore, I do not know that I have hands.

The argument is formally valid (modus tollens on the closure principle). Premise 1 follows from closure and the obvious entailment between "I have hands" and "I am not a handless brain in a vat." Premise 2 seems compelling: I cannot rule out the BIV hypothesis, so I don't know I'm not a BIV. Therefore, the conclusion follows.

This argument generalizes: for any ordinary knowledge claim K that entails not-H (where H is a skeptical hypothesis), if I don't know not-H, I don't know K.

## Responses to the Closure-Based Argument

Responses fall into four main strategies:

**Accept the conclusion (hard skepticism)**: Perhaps we genuinely don't know the things we ordinarily claim to know. Our epistemic situation is worse than we thought. This response is intellectually honest but practically devastating.

**Reject premise 2 (anti-skeptical externalism)**: Perhaps I do know that I am not a brain in a vat. If my belief that I have hands is formed by a reliable process, then my perceptual beliefs are genuine knowledge, and since I know I have hands and know this entails I'm not a BIV, I know I'm not a BIV — even if I can't prove it from the inside. This is the Moorean/externalist response.

**Reject premise 1 (deny closure)**: Perhaps knowledge does not close under known entailment. This is Robert Nozick's strategy. On his tracking theory, knowledge requires that your belief be sensitive to the truth: if P were false, you wouldn't believe P. Your belief that you have hands is sensitive (if you lacked hands, your perceptual experience would be different and you wouldn't believe you have hands). But your belief that you're not a BIV is not sensitive: even if you were a BIV, you'd still believe you're not. So you can know you have hands without knowing you're not a BIV, because knowledge doesn't close under entailment.

**Accept context-sensitivity (contextualism)**: In ordinary contexts, "know" is governed by low standards, and in ordinary contexts you do know you have hands and you do know you're not a BIV (that hypothesis isn't relevant). When philosophers invoke BIV scenarios, they raise the epistemic standards, and in that context you don't know either. But this doesn't undermine ordinary knowledge — it just reveals that "know" is context-sensitive.

## Closure and Relevant Alternatives

Fred Dretske defended a weaker closure principle and attacked the stronger form. His relevant alternatives theory holds that knowledge is closed under *relevant* alternatives but not all alternatives. The BIV hypothesis is not a relevant alternative in ordinary contexts — it's too far-fetched to count. So I can know I have hands without knowing that the BIV hypothesis is false, because ruling out the BIV hypothesis isn't required for knowing I have hands.

## Why Closure Matters

The debate over closure is not just a technical dispute. It illuminates the structure of epistemic justification: does justification (or knowledge) "transmit" through entailments the way deductive proof transmits truth? The answer shapes how we understand the relationship between ordinary knowledge and philosophical skepticism, and whether the two can coexist.
