# Further Reading: What Is Skepticism?

## Primary Sources

- René Descartes, *Meditations on First Philosophy*, trans. John Cottingham, Cambridge University Press, 1996. The first Meditation deploys global skepticism about the external world as a methodological tool; the subsequent Meditations attempt to respond to it.

- G.E. Moore, "Proof of an External World," in *Philosophical Papers*, Allen & Unwin, 1959. The classic common-sense response to global skepticism, offering a direct argument for the existence of external objects against the skeptic's challenge.

- Sextus Empiricus, *Outlines of Pyrrhonism*, trans. R.G. Bury, Harvard University Press, 1933. The primary source for ancient Pyrrhonism; contains the ten modes of Aenesidemus and five modes of Agrippa as tools for generating skeptical suspension of judgment.

- David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, ed. L.A. Selby-Bigge and P.H. Nidditch, Oxford University Press, 1975. Sections IV and V present Hume's skepticism about induction; Section XII distinguishes different varieties of skepticism and their philosophical uses.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Peter Klein, "Skepticism," *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, 2015 ed. The most reliable and comprehensive survey of skeptical arguments and responses, covering ancient, Cartesian, and contemporary forms.

- Barry Stroud, *The Significance of Philosophical Scepticism*, Oxford University Press, 1984. A sustained examination of why skeptical arguments are so philosophically powerful and so difficult to refute, arguing that they reveal something deep about our epistemic situation rather than being merely logical puzzles.

- Michael Williams, *Unnatural Doubts: Epistemological Realism and the Basis of Scepticism*, Blackwell, 1991. Argues that skepticism is not forced on us by the nature of our epistemic situation but is generated by a particular (and questionable) theoretical framework — "epistemological realism."

- Anthony Kenny, *Descartes: A Study of His Philosophy*, Random House, 1968. An accessible study of Descartes' epistemological project and the skeptical arguments that structure the Meditations.

- Jonathan Dancy, *Introduction to Contemporary Epistemology*, Blackwell, 1985. Chapters 1–2 provide a clear introduction to the structure of skeptical arguments and the different varieties of skeptical challenge.

- Keith DeRose and Ted Warfield, eds., *Skepticism: A Contemporary Reader*, Oxford University Press, 1999. An excellent anthology collecting the most important contemporary papers on skepticism and its responses, ideal as a companion to this chapter.

- Timothy Williamson, *Knowledge and Its Limits*, Oxford University Press, 2000. Chapter 1 examines the nature of knowledge and its relationship to skeptical challenges, developing the knowledge-first framework that shapes Williamson's response to skepticism.

- Fred Dretske, "Epistemic Operators," *Journal of Philosophy* 67, 1970, pp. 1007–1023. Introduces the relevant alternatives approach, which denies the closure principle and thereby challenges the standard structure of skeptical arguments.

- Ernest Sosa, "How to Defeat Opposition to Moore," *Philosophical Perspectives* 13, 1999, pp. 141–153. Develops a virtue-epistemological response to skepticism that does not rely on direct confrontation with the skeptic's argument.

- John Greco, "External World Skepticism," *Philosophy Compass* 2, 2007, pp. 625–649. A clear survey of contemporary responses to external world skepticism, including reliabilist, contextualist, and Moorean approaches.

- Richard Fumerton, *Metaepistemology and Skepticism*, Rowman & Littlefield, 1995. Argues from an internalist perspective that skepticism is a serious threat and that most externalist responses fail to engage its philosophical force.

- Duncan Pritchard, *Epistemic Angst: Radical Scepticism and the Groundlessness of Our Believing*, Princeton University Press, 2016. A recent treatment of radical skepticism, arguing that a satisfying response requires an anti-luck condition on knowledge combined with a Wittgensteinian account of hinge commitments.

- Anthony Brueckner, "The Structure of the Skeptical Argument," *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* 54, 1994, pp. 827–835. A careful logical analysis of the closure-based structure of the standard skeptical argument.

- Stewart Cohen, "How to Be a Fallibilist," *Philosophical Perspectives* 2, 1988, pp. 581–605. Argues that fallibilism — the view that knowledge does not require certainty — is the correct response to skeptical pressure, while acknowledging the difficulty of maintaining fallibilism in the face of the skeptic's challenges.
