# Key Figures: What Is Skepticism?

## Pyrrho of Elis (c. 360–270 BCE)

Pyrrho is the founder of the Western skeptical tradition. Though he wrote nothing, the tradition reports that he advocated complete suspension of judgment (*epoché*) on all matters, on the grounds that for any appearance or argument, an equal and opposing appearance or argument could be produced. His life as described by later writers embodies the skeptical ideal: he cultivated imperturbability (*ataraxia*) by detaching himself from all belief about the nature of things. Pyrrho is important as the figure who first articulated that withholding judgment, rather than seeking epistemic security, might be the philosophically appropriate response to the impossibility of knowledge.

## Sextus Empiricus (c. 160–210 CE)

Sextus is the principal surviving source for ancient Pyrrhonism, and his *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* is the most systematic presentation of skeptical arguments in the Western tradition. He meticulously records the modes of Aenesidemus (ten modes for generating suspension of judgment about appearances) and the modes of Agrippa (five modes targeting any attempt to provide justification). Sextus's skepticism is presented as a philosophical *practice* — a way of life — rather than a doctrine: the Pyrrhonist does not assert that knowledge is impossible (which would itself be a dogmatic claim) but simply continues to find equal reason for and against every proposition, thereby suspending judgment.

## René Descartes (1596–1650)

Descartes deployed skepticism as a methodological tool rather than a philosophical conclusion. His hyperbolic doubt in the *Meditations* — culminating in the evil demon hypothesis — is designed not to embrace skepticism but to identify what can survive it, thereby establishing an indubitable foundation for knowledge. Nonetheless, Descartes gave skepticism its most vivid modern formulations: the dream argument (how do you know you are not dreaming?) and the evil demon (what if an all-powerful deceiver is causing all your experiences to be false?). These scenarios have structured the epistemological debate ever since, and Descartes' method of doubt has been the template for understanding the skeptical challenge in analytic epistemology.

## Barry Stroud (1935–2019)

Stroud's *The Significance of Philosophical Scepticism* (1984) is the most searching analysis of why skeptical arguments are both compelling and difficult to defeat. He argues that attempts to respond to skepticism — including ordinary language approaches, contextualism, and naturalized epistemology — consistently fail to engage the skeptic on the right terms. Stroud's key insight is that the skeptic does not deny our ordinary knowledge claims; rather, the skeptic reveals that we cannot explain what ordinary knowledge *is* without presupposing what the skeptic questions. This makes skepticism philosophically significant beyond a mere logical puzzle, suggesting it reveals something deep about the concepts of knowledge and justification.

## Michael Williams (b. 1947)

Williams's *Unnatural Doubts* (1991) argues for a "diagnostic" approach to skepticism: rather than attempting to refute the skeptic directly, we should explain why the skeptic's arguments seem compelling and show that the appearance of compellingness is an artifact of a particular theoretical framework — "epistemological realism" — that we are not forced to accept. Williams argues that once we abandon the view that all knowledge claims are related to each other within a single unified "epistemic system" subject to global evaluation, the skeptical argument loses its purchase. His approach connects the debate about skepticism to broader questions about the presuppositions of epistemological theorizing.

## Fred Dretske (1932–2017)

Dretske's introduction of the "relevant alternatives" framework has significantly shaped how philosophers understand the structure of skeptical arguments. He argues that the skeptic's argument depends on the closure principle — if you know P and know P entails Q, you know Q — and that this principle is false: knowledge is always relative to a set of relevant alternatives, and skeptical hypotheses are typically not among the relevant alternatives for ordinary knowledge claims. Dretske's framework allows us to say that we know ordinary propositions (there is a hand, this is a barn) while denying that we know we are not brains in vats, because the latter is not a relevant alternative in ordinary epistemic contexts.

## Keith DeRose (b. 1962)

DeRose's contextualism about knowledge attributions — developed in "Solving the Skeptical Problem" (1995) — holds that "knows" is a context-sensitive term and that the skeptical argument is valid in the philosophical context in which it is raised without being valid in ordinary contexts. When a philosopher raises the brain-in-a-vat possibility, this elevates the standards for "knows" so high that ordinary beliefs fail to qualify; but in everyday contexts, where no skeptical alternative is salient, our ordinary knowledge claims are true. DeRose's account attempts to explain both why the skeptic's argument seems compelling (it is compelling, in its context) and why we should not abandon our ordinary knowledge claims (they are true, in their context).
