# Thought Experiments: What Is Skepticism?

## The Dream Argument

**The scenario:** You are convinced you are reading a textbook in a well-lit room. But recall how vivid and convincing your dreams are while you are having them — you believe they are real, you experience clear perceptions and sensations, and only upon waking do you realize they were dreams. Now ask: what criterion do you have that distinguishes your present waking experience from a very vivid dream? If you were dreaming right now, everything would seem exactly as it does.

**The philosophical point:** Descartes' dream argument is the most accessible form of the skeptical challenge to knowledge of the external world. It works by undermining the reliability of the very faculty — sensory experience — on which knowledge of the external world depends. The skeptic does not need to claim you are dreaming; it is enough that you cannot rule out that you are. This generates a closure-based skeptical argument: you know that if you are dreaming, you don't know (on the basis of sensory experience) that there is a book in front of you; you cannot know you are not dreaming; therefore you do not know there is a book in front of you. Responses must either challenge closure, challenge the claimed impossibility of distinguishing dream from waking, or deny that knowledge requires ruling out all alternatives.

## Global vs. Local Skepticism: The Colorblind Expert

**The scenario:** An art expert has outstanding judgment about the compositional structure, historical provenance, and authenticity of paintings, but is completely colorblind and has no knowledge of the color qualities of any painting. Her local ignorance (about color) does not undermine her global competence (about structure, provenance, and authenticity). Now imagine a parallel case: a scientist has outstanding knowledge of physics but cannot be certain that the external world exists at all. Does his local uncertainty (about the existence of the external world) undermine his knowledge of physics?

**The philosophical point:** This case illuminates the distinction between local and global skepticism. Local skepticism (skepticism about a particular domain — color, other minds, the future) does not obviously undermine knowledge in other domains. Global skepticism (skepticism about all knowledge of the external world) threatens to undermine all empirical knowledge at once, since all empirical knowledge depends on there being an external world to know about. The case invites the question of whether global skepticism is qualitatively different from local skepticism, and whether the closure-based argument that generates global skepticism is actually the all-or-nothing challenge it appears to be.

## The Skeptic's Challenge and the Closure Principle

**The scenario:** Suppose you know that you have hands. You also know (by logic) that if you have hands, you are not a handless brain in a vat. Standard logic then says you know that you are not a handless brain in a vat — by closure under known entailment. But surely you do not know that you are not a brain in a vat: no evidence you have rules out that possibility. Therefore — by running the argument backward — you do not know that you have hands.

**The philosophical point:** This is the canonical logical structure of the closure-based skeptical argument. It has three possible responses: (1) deny closure — you can know ordinary propositions without knowing you are not in skeptical scenarios (Dretske, Nozick); (2) accept closure and deny the skeptical premise — you do know you are not a brain in a vat (Moore, contextualists, some reliabilists); (3) accept closure and the skeptical premise and conclude that we don't know ordinary propositions (the skeptic's conclusion). The argument illuminates exactly what is at stake in debates about closure and the structure of epistemic norms.

## Skepticism as a Philosophical Tool

**The scenario:** A methodologist wants to identify which of her beliefs are truly foundational — immune to any possible doubt. She systematically raises skeptical scenarios against each of her beliefs: she doubts her perceptual beliefs (dream argument), her memory beliefs (she might have been created five minutes ago with false memories), her mathematical beliefs (an evil deceiver might make her go wrong even in calculation). She hopes that whatever survives this process will constitute the bedrock of her inquiry.

**The philosophical point:** This use of skeptical scenarios as a methodological tool — Descartes' distinctive contribution — illustrates that skepticism need not be a conclusion but can be a procedure. The point is not to end up without knowledge but to identify the structure of one's epistemic commitments by stress-testing them. Modern philosophers have questioned whether this methodology is coherent: Wittgenstein argued that trying to doubt everything at once is not genuine doubting but a kind of play-acting. Williams argued that the methodological use of skepticism presupposes a theoretical framework that we can and should reject. But the approach remains enormously influential as a model for understanding what distinguishes the most secure from the least secure epistemic commitments.

## Moral Skepticism as a Parallel

**The scenario:** A student in ethics argues: we have no perceptual access to moral facts, no faculty analogous to vision that detects right and wrong, and our moral intuitions vary dramatically across cultures and individuals. Even if we assume the external world exists, there is an additional question about whether there are any moral facts in it. Is moral skepticism a form of skepticism parallel to external world skepticism?

**The philosophical point:** This scenario illustrates the distinction between external world skepticism and other forms of local skepticism, particularly moral skepticism. It raises the question of whether skeptical arguments are domain-specific or whether they follow a common logical structure that applies wherever there is a claim to knowledge. Philosophers disagree about whether moral skepticism is more like external world skepticism (a global threat to a whole domain) or more like local perceptual skepticism about specific facts. The comparison also illuminates the resources that are and are not available for responses: reliabilist and tracking-theory responses to external world skepticism may have no obvious analogue for moral knowledge.
