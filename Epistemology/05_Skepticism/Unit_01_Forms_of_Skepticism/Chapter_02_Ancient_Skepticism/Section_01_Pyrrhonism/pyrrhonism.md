# Pyrrhonism: Suspending Judgment

Pyrrhonism is the oldest and in many ways the most sophisticated form of philosophical skepticism. Named after Pyrrho of Elis (c. 360–270 BCE), and preserved primarily in the writings of Sextus Empiricus (c. 160–210 CE), Pyrrhonism is distinctive in being not merely a philosophical thesis but a philosophical practice — a way of living without dogmatic commitment to any view.

## Pyrrho and the Origins

Pyrrho left no writings; we know his views through later accounts. He reportedly accompanied Alexander the Great's expedition to India and was influenced by Indian philosophical traditions, particularly those emphasizing detachment from judgment and desire. His student Timon of Phlius described Pyrrho as holding that things are equally indifferent, unstable, and indeterminate — and that our senses and our reasoning give us no grounds for preferring one judgment over another.

The Pyrrhonist response to this situation was not despair but *ataraxia* — tranquility, freedom from disturbance. The Pyrrhonist discovered, perhaps surprisingly, that suspending judgment on all matters leads not to paralysis but to peace: without the struggle to maintain dogmatic commitments, without the anxiety of being wrong, the Pyrrhonist achieves a kind of philosophical calm.

## Sextus Empiricus and the Modes

Sextus Empiricus systematized Pyrrhonism in *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* (PH) and *Against the Mathematicians* (M). His method relies on the *modes* — patterns of argument showing that for any apparent fact, an equally compelling opposite can be constructed.

The **Ten Modes of Aenesidemus** target the unreliability of perception:
- Different animals perceive the same things differently
- Different humans perceive the same things differently
- The same sense can give different impressions of the same object
- Different circumstances (distance, health, motion) affect perception
- Mixture with other perceptible qualities alters perception
- Everything is perceived in context; pure objects are not accessible

If perception is inherently variable in these ways, we have no grounds for preferring one perceptual report over another.

The **Five Modes of Agrippa** target rational inquiry more broadly:
1. **Disagreement**: For any claim, there are equally compelling opposing views
2. **Infinite regress**: Any justification requires further justification, without end
3. **Relativity**: All appearances are relative to the perceiver and conditions
4. **Hypothesis**: If we stop the regress by positing a basic claim, this is an arbitrary assumption
5. **Circularity**: Any attempt to justify a claim using the same claim is circular

These five modes form a powerful net: any attempt to establish knowledge ends in disagreement, requires an infinite regress, is relative, rests on ungrounded hypothesis, or is circular.

## Suspension of Judgment (Epoché)

The Pyrrhonist response to these modes is *epoché* — suspension of judgment. The Pyrrhonist does not assert that knowledge is impossible (that would be a dogmatic claim), nor that skepticism is true (another dogmatic claim). They simply note that the appearances and arguments on both sides are equally compelling, and so suspend judgment.

This is subtle: the Pyrrhonist is not asserting P, not asserting not-P, and not asserting "P and not-P are equally true." They are withdrawing from the game of assertion altogether regarding theoretical claims. They continue to function in daily life according to appearances — the honey looks sweet, so they eat it — without committing to judgments about how things really are.

## The Apraxia Objection

Critics raised the *apraxia* objection: a person who suspends judgment on everything could not act, since action requires belief (you act to achieve an outcome, and to do so you must believe you are acting and that the outcome is achievable). Without belief, Pyrrhonism leads to inaction — to *apraxia*.

Sextus responded by distinguishing between *dogmatic* belief (committed assertion about how things really are) and following *appearances*. The Pyrrhonist follows appearances: the road appears to be there, so they walk on it; the food appears edible, so they eat it. They need not commit to the claim that the road really is there or that the food really is edible. Appearances guide action without requiring dogmatic commitment.

## Legacy

Pyrrhonism was rediscovered in the Renaissance (Sextus's writings were published in 1562) and profoundly influenced early modern philosophy. Montaigne, Bayle, and arguably Hume were all influenced by Pyrrhonian themes. The Pyrrhonian modes anticipate many modern epistemological problems — the problem of the criterion, the regress argument, the underdetermination of theory by evidence — and remain philosophically vital today.
