# Academic Skepticism

Academic skepticism was the position of the Middle and New Academies — the Platonic schools under the leadership of Arcesilaus (c. 316–241 BCE) and Carneades (c. 214–129 BCE). Unlike the Pyrrhonists, who suspended judgment on all questions including the question of whether knowledge is possible, the Academics made a positive claim: knowledge, in the Stoic sense of certain, infallible apprehension, is impossible for human beings.

## The Stoic Target

Academic skepticism makes sense only against the backdrop of Stoic epistemology. The Stoics held that knowledge (*episteme*) required *kataleptic impressions* — impressions of such clarity and distinctness that they could not be false and that anyone who received them would necessarily assent to them. A kataleptic impression "grasps" its object directly; it is the mind's equivalent of a firm grip on something real.

Arcesilaus attacked this criterion: there are no impressions of such a character that they cannot be false. For any impression that appears compelling, we can imagine an equally compelling impression that is false (a vivid dream, a perfect hallucination). If the Stoic criterion cannot be met, knowledge in the Stoic sense is impossible.

## Arcesilaus and Suspension of Judgment

Arcesilaus, like the Pyrrhonists, advocated *epoché* (suspension of judgment). But his grounds were different: he argued that the appropriate response to the non-existence of kataleptic impressions is to withhold assent from all impressions, since assent to any impression is assent to something that might be false.

This led to an exchange with the Stoics similar to the *apraxia* objection. If we withhold assent from all impressions, how do we act? Arcesilaus responded that we can act on the basis of *eulogon* — what is "reasonable" or "plausible" in the circumstances — without committing to the claim that our impressions are true. Reasonable action doesn't require certain knowledge.

## Carneades and the Probable

Carneades developed a more sophisticated position. He distinguished degrees of credibility (*pithanon* — often translated as "probable" or "persuasive"):

- Some impressions are simply *pithanon* (credible on their face)
- Some are *pithanon* and *aperispastos* (credible and not undermined by surrounding impressions)
- Some are *pithanon*, *aperispastos*, and *periodeumenon* (credible, not undermined, and confirmed by examination from multiple angles)

The third class — what we might call thoroughly tested credibility — is the guide for life in the absence of knowledge. Carneades was not claiming these impressions are true; only that they are the best guide available to us.

This is a significant move: it acknowledges that skepticism about knowledge doesn't collapse into complete nihilism about rational practice. We can distinguish better and worse grounds for action even without certainty.

## Academic Skepticism and Cicero

Our most detailed account of Academic skepticism comes from Cicero, who was trained in the Academic tradition. In *Academica*, Cicero defends the probabilist position: the wise person does not claim to know but can follow the probable. This is combined with intellectual humility — the Academic gives reasons, considers objections, and holds views tentatively, ready to revise.

Cicero's Academic skepticism is less radical than Pyrrhonism: it allows that some things are more credible than others, that inquiry is rational, and that probabilistic beliefs are a legitimate guide to life. What it denies is that this probabilistic guidance ever rises to the level of *knowledge*.

## The Problem of the Criterion

Both Pyrrhonian and Academic skepticism connect to what Roderick Chisholm called the problem of the criterion: to identify instances of knowledge, we need a criterion of knowledge; but to justify a criterion of knowledge, we need to identify instances first. This circularity seems to doom any attempt to establish either specific knowledge claims or a general theory of knowledge.

The problem of the criterion has ancient roots in the Academic tradition and was revived in early modern philosophy. It remains an important challenge to epistemology: any theory of knowledge seems to presuppose some starting point that cannot itself be justified by that theory.
