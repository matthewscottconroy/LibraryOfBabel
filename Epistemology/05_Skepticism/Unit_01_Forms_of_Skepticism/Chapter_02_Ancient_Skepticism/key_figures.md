# Key Figures: Ancient Skepticism

## Pyrrho of Elis (c. 360–270 BCE)

Pyrrho is the historical founder of ancient skepticism, though he left no writings. The tradition — preserved primarily through Sextus Empiricus and Diogenes Laertius — presents him as having concluded that things are "no more" one way than another (neither beautiful nor ugly, neither just nor unjust), and that the appropriate response is suspension of judgment. This suspension was said to produce *ataraxia* (tranquility), since one's emotional disturbances are caused by dogmatic beliefs about good and bad. Pyrrho may have been influenced by Indian gymnosophists he encountered on Alexander's campaign. Later scholars debate whether Pyrrho denied all appearances or merely the metaphysical claims grounded in them, making him either a pure Pyrrhonist or a kind of ethical skeptic.

## Timon of Phlius (c. 320–230 BCE)

Timon was Pyrrho's principal student and the most important early exponent of his philosophy. His *Silloi* — satirical hexameters attacking dogmatic philosophers — is one of the main sources for early Pyrrhonism. Timon formulated the core Pyrrhonist question: what are the things that we can know, and what attitude should we take toward them? He articulated Pyrrho's answer as the claim that things are "indifferent, unstable, and indeterminate" and that the wise person's response is to make no assertions and to follow appearances (*phainomena*) for practical guidance without forming beliefs about the underlying nature of things.

## Arcesilaus (c. 315–240 BCE)

Arcesilaus transformed the Platonic Academy into a skeptical institution. Taking inspiration from Socrates' profession of ignorance, he argued that it was consistent with following Socrates to suspend judgment on all matters, including whether suspension of judgment is correct. Arcesilaus targeted specifically the Stoic doctrine of the "kataleptic impression" — the idea that some impressions are self-certifying and immediately guarantee their own truth — arguing that no impression has a feature that guarantees its reliability in a way that distinguishes it from a false impression. He is credited with introducing the concept of *epoché* as a systematic philosophical procedure rather than merely a psychological disposition.

## Carneades (c. 214–129 BCE)

Carneades is the most philosophically sophisticated of the Academic skeptics. He extended Arcesilaus's critique of the kataleptic impression and responded to the *apraxia* objection (that a person without beliefs cannot act) by developing the concept of the "persuasive" (*pithanon*) impression: one can act on the basis of impressions that appear compelling or convincing without thereby committing to their truth. This is sometimes read as a form of probabilism — acting on the most probable-seeming option — though Carneades himself may have been making a merely dialectical point. His debate with Clitomachus on whether the Academics had positive doctrines has never been fully resolved and remains a key scholarly controversy.

## Aenesidemus (c. 1st century BCE)

Aenesidemus was a later Academic who revived Pyrrhonism as a reaction against what he saw as the dogmatic drift of the New Academy. He formulated the famous "ten modes" (*tropes*) — ten classes of cases in which appearances differ and neither can be adjudicated — including differences due to different perceivers, different conditions of the perceiver, different positions and distances, different admixtures, and different relations between objects. The ten modes were designed as a practical handbook for generating suspension of judgment on any specific question. Aenesidemus also developed a critique of causal reasoning along Humean lines, anticipating Hume's skepticism about causation by nearly two millennia.

## Sextus Empiricus (c. 160–210 CE)

Sextus is the most important surviving source for ancient skepticism and the only one whose works have come down to us substantially intact. His *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* is both a historical record and a philosophical defense of the Pyrrhonist way of life. Sextus systematized the Pyrrhonist philosophical apparatus: the five modes of Agrippa (regress, circularity, relativity, hypothesis, infinite regress) provide a formal battery of challenges to any attempt at justification, and the ten modes of Aenesidemus provide a more phenomenological set of challenges. Sextus's contribution is to show that Pyrrhonism can be a coherent philosophical practice — not a doctrine but a continuing inquiry — by carefully distinguishing appearances (which the Pyrrhonist follows for practical guidance) from beliefs (which the Pyrrhonist withholds).

## Cicero (106–43 BCE)

Though not a professional philosopher, Cicero's *Academica* is the primary source for the arguments and positions of the Middle and New Academy. Cicero himself adopted a form of Academic probabilism — acting on the more persuasive view without committing to its truth — and defended this as a practical and intellectually honest response to the impossibility of certain knowledge. His elegant Latin exposition of the debate between Stoic dogmatism and Academic skepticism made ancient skepticism available to Renaissance and early modern philosophers, helping to transmit the Pyrrhonian and Academic traditions to thinkers like Montaigne and Descartes.
