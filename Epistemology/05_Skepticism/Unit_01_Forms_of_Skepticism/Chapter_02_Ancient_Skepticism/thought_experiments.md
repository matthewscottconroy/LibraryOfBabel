# Thought Experiments: Ancient Skepticism

## The Tower Seen from a Distance

**The scenario:** A square tower looks round from a great distance. From nearby it looks square. From far away the surfaces appear smooth; from close up you can see the rough individual stones. Which appearance is correct? Neither perceiver is malfunctioning; both are reporting genuine appearances. But the appearances contradict each other. If you try to adjudicate between them by reasoning, you must use further appearances or principles — and these will be equally subject to challenge.

**The philosophical point:** This case exemplifies one of Aenesidemus's ten modes — the mode from distance and position. The point is not merely that appearances change with circumstances, but that there is no neutral standpoint from which to adjudicate among appearances and determine which corresponds to the true nature of the tower. Any standard we invoke is itself dependent on appearances from some perspective. The mode generalizes: for any appearance-conflict, there is a further appearance-conflict about how to resolve it, regressing without a neutral stopping point. The Pyrrhonist concludes that we should suspend judgment about what the tower is *really* like and simply follow the appearance most useful for practical navigation.

## The Honey That Is Sweet and Bitter

**The scenario:** A healthy person tastes honey and reports it as sweet. A jaundiced person tastes the same honey and reports it as bitter. Both are reporting genuine experiences; neither is lying or hallucinating in any pathological sense. The honey cannot be both sweet and bitter in the same respect. Yet we have no way to determine which percipient is "correctly" perceiving the honey's real quality — any standard we use will be the report of some percipient under some condition.

**The philosophical point:** This is one of Sextus's most vivid illustrations of the mode from conditions of the perceiver (the fourth of the ten modes). The example shows that even perceptual qualities — traditionally thought of as the most certain deliverances of experience — are relative to the condition of the perceiver. The mode invites suspension of judgment not only about theoretical claims but about the most basic qualities of everyday objects. The philosophical point extends: if we cannot establish even that honey is sweet *simpliciter*, what can we establish about the nature of things? The ancient skeptic concludes: nothing about the hidden nature of things, though appearances can still guide practical life.

## The Kataleptic Impression Test

**The scenario:** You are looking at your best friend in good lighting and forming what feels like a maximally vivid and compelling perceptual impression of their face. Now imagine an identical twin who looks exactly like your friend stands before you in the same lighting — you form an equally vivid impression of this other person's face, yet your belief that it is your friend is false. Both impressions feel equally compelling and internally indistinguishable. Is there any feature of the first impression that marks it as kataleptic — as truth-guaranteeing?

**The philosophical point:** This is Arcesilaus's and Carneades' attack on the Stoic doctrine of the kataleptic impression — the impression that "pulls along" acceptance and guarantees its own truth by some intrinsic feature. The twin case shows that for any vivid and compelling impression, there is an intrinsically identical impression that is false. There is no intrinsic feature of the impression that distinguishes veridical from non-veridical cases. The Academics concluded that no impression is kataleptic in the Stoic sense, and that the Stoic criterion of truth — the feel of irresistibility — fails. This is an early version of the argument that introspective features of mental states cannot guarantee their accuracy about the external world.

## Agrippa's Modes Applied: The Expert Witness

**The scenario:** A court must decide whether a painting is authentic. It calls in an art expert who declares it authentic. The opposing counsel asks: how do you know? The expert appeals to stylistic analysis. How do you know stylistic analysis is reliable? The expert appeals to her training and track record. How do you know your track record is a reliable guide to reliability? She appeals to the general reliability of expertise in authenticated examples. How do you know those examples were correctly authenticated? — and so on. Alternatively, the expert declares the method self-certifying; or she gives reasons that eventually circle back to the original claim.

**The philosophical point:** This case illustrates Agrippa's five modes in operation. Any attempt at justification leads to one of three outcomes: an infinite regress of justifications (the regress mode), a circular justification (the circularity mode), or a bare assertion without support (the hypothesis mode). The relativity mode adds that even established criteria are relative to particular frameworks or standards, and the disagreement mode notes that experts routinely disagree. The case shows the power of Agrippa's modes as an all-purpose epistemic challenge: they apply not just to philosophical skepticism but to any context in which we try to justify a claim systematically.

## The Practical Pyrrhonist

**The scenario:** A Pyrrhonist philosopher is walking and comes to a fork in the road. If, as the Pyrrhonist claims, she suspends judgment about everything — including which road leads to the city — how does she proceed? If she acts entirely at random, she will be paralyzed or led into danger. If she acts on some "practical" belief about which road to take, does this show she does retain beliefs and that Pyrrhonism is self-undermining?

**The philosophical point:** This is the *apraxia* objection to ancient Pyrrhonism. Opponents argued that suspension of judgment leads to practical paralysis: life requires choices, and choices require preferences grounded in beliefs. Sextus's response was that the Pyrrhonist follows "appearances" (*phainomena*) — the way things present themselves — without committing to beliefs about underlying reality. The road that appears to lead to the city is followed; this is a matter of following appearances, not holding beliefs. Critics argue this is a distinction without a difference — following appearances just is having beliefs about how things appear. The case remains central to debates about whether Pyrrhonism is a coherent way of life or a philosophical position that destroys the practical conditions of its own existence.
