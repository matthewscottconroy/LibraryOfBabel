# Further Reading: Cartesian Skepticism

## Primary Sources

- René Descartes, *Meditations on First Philosophy*, trans. John Cottingham, Cambridge University Press, 1996. The essential text; the first two Meditations develop the method of doubt, the dream argument, the evil demon, and the *cogito*; subsequent Meditations attempt to escape skepticism through God's veracity.

- René Descartes, *Discourse on the Method*, trans. Robert Stoothoff, in *The Philosophical Writings of Descartes*, Vol. I, Cambridge University Press, 1985. The more accessible earlier version of Descartes' project; Part IV contains a compressed version of the *Meditations* argument.

- René Descartes, *Objections and Replies*, in *The Philosophical Writings of Descartes*, Vol. II, Cambridge University Press, 1984. Descartes' responses to critics including Mersenne, Gassendi, and Arnauld; Arnauld's Fourth Objection raises the Cartesian circle, and Descartes' response is a key text in the circle debate.

- Baruch Spinoza, *Treatise on the Emendation of the Intellect*, in *Complete Works*, trans. Samuel Shirley, Hackett, 2002. Spinoza's response to Cartesian doubt, arguing that adequate ideas carry their own guarantee of truth and that the problem of the criterion does not arise for genuinely clear and distinct ideas.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Harry Frankfurt, *Demons, Dreamers, and Madmen: The Defense of Reason in Descartes' Meditations*, Bobbs-Merrill, 1970. A philosophical defense of Descartes' argumentative strategy, arguing that the evil demon thought experiment is coherent and that Descartes' attempted escape from it is more sophisticated than critics acknowledge.

- Bernard Williams, *Descartes: The Project of Pure Enquiry*, Penguin, 1978. The best philosophical analysis of the *Meditations* as a unified systematic project, with detailed treatment of the *cogito*, the Cartesian circle, and the role of God in Descartes' epistemology.

- Margaret Wilson, *Descartes*, Routledge, 1978. A classic scholarly study of Descartes' epistemology and metaphysics, with careful attention to the relationship between the method of doubt and the doctrines Descartes seeks to establish.

- Anthony Kenny, *Descartes: A Study of His Philosophy*, Random House, 1968. An accessible introduction to Descartes' philosophy covering the method of doubt and the *cogito* clearly and in historical context.

- Hiram Caton, *The Origin of Subjectivity: An Essay on Descartes*, Yale University Press, 1973. Situates Descartes' skeptical method within the broader context of early modern intellectual history, connecting the project of pure enquiry to Renaissance changes in the conception of the self.

- Jaakko Hintikka, "Cogito, Ergo Sum: Inference or Performance?" *Philosophical Review* 71, 1962, pp. 3–32. A famous analysis of the *cogito* arguing that it is better understood as a "performative" act than as an inference, with significant implications for its role in Descartes' response to skepticism.

- Alvin Plantinga, "Reason and Belief in God," in *Faith and Rationality*, eds. Plantinga and Wolterstorff, University of Notre Dame Press, 1983. Relevant for the Cartesian circle: examines whether the structure of Descartes' argument (using reason to validate reason) is viciously circular.

- Barry Stroud, *The Significance of Philosophical Scepticism*, Oxford University Press, 1984. Chapters 1–3 analyze the Cartesian skeptical argument in its classic form, arguing that it reveals something deep and unsettling about our epistemic situation rather than merely raising a logical puzzle.

- Keith DeRose, "Descartes, Epistemic Principles, Epistemic Circularity, and Scientia," *Pacific Philosophical Quarterly* 73, 1992, pp. 220–238. A detailed analysis of the Cartesian circle, distinguishing different interpretations and assessing which constitute genuine circularities.

- E.M. Curley, *Descartes Against the Skeptics*, Harvard University Press, 1978. Argues that Descartes' response to skepticism is more successful than usually supposed, and that the Cartesian circle is less damaging when properly interpreted.

- Peter Markie, "The Cogito and Its Importance," in *The Cambridge Companion to Descartes*, ed. John Cottingham, Cambridge University Press, 1992. A clear and thorough examination of different interpretations of the *cogito* and its role in Descartes' anti-skeptical strategy.

- Gary Hatfield, *Descartes and the Meditations*, Routledge, 2003. A study guide and analysis of the *Meditations* that combines close reading with philosophical commentary; ideal for advanced students.

- Lilli Alanen, *Descartes's Concept of Mind*, Harvard University Press, 2003. Examines the relationship between Descartes' dualism and his epistemology, with implications for how the evil demon argument and the *cogito* fit within his overall philosophical project.

- Catherine Wilson, "Descartes and the Corporeal Mind," in *A Companion to Descartes*, eds. Broughton and Carriero, Blackwell, 2008. Provides context for understanding the evil demon argument within Descartes' broader concerns about the relationship between mind and body.
