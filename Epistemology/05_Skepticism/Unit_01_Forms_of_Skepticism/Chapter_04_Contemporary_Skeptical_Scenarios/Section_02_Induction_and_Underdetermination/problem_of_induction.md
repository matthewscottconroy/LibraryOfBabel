# The Problem of Induction

The problem of induction, identified by David Hume and sharpened by subsequent philosophers, is in some ways the most practically significant form of skepticism. Unlike the brain-in-a-vat scenario, inductive skepticism targets not exotic possibilities but the very inferential practices that underlie all empirical science and ordinary learning from experience.

## Hume's Problem

Hume's formulation in the *Treatise of Human Nature* (1739) and *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748) is remarkably clear. We constantly make inferences from past regularities to future expectations: the sun has risen every day so far, so it will rise tomorrow; bread has nourished me before, so it will nourish me again. These are *inductive* inferences — from observed cases to unobserved cases.

What justifies these inferences? Two options:

**Rational demonstration**: Is there a demonstrative (deductive) argument that past regularities must continue? No — it is logically possible that the sun will not rise tomorrow, that bread will suddenly become poisonous. The contrary is conceivable without contradiction. So induction is not logically necessary.

**Probable reasoning**: Perhaps induction is justified by experience — we have found in the past that past regularities continued into the future, so they probably will continue. But this justification is itself inductive: it uses induction (past success of induction) to justify induction. The argument is circular.

Hume concluded that induction has no rational justification; it is a matter of habit and psychological necessity, not reason. We cannot help forming inductive expectations, but we cannot rationally ground them.

## The New Riddle of Induction

Nelson Goodman's "new riddle of induction" (1954) sharpened the problem. Suppose all the emeralds we have ever examined have been green. We infer: all emeralds are green. Now define a new predicate: *grue* means "examined before time T and green, or not examined before time T and blue." All the emeralds we have examined are also grue (since they are green and examined before T).

By the same inductive logic that gives us "all emeralds are green," we should also conclude "all emeralds are grue." But these conclusions conflict: if all emeralds are grue, then unexamined emeralds (after T) are blue, not green.

Goodman's point: our evidence equally confirms "all emeralds are green" and "all emeralds are grue," yet we strongly prefer the first. This preference is not itself grounded in the evidence. We project natural predicates ("green") rather than artificial ones ("grue") — but what makes a predicate natural? Goodman's answer: predicates that have been successfully *entrenched* in past inductive practice. But this seems to be an appeal to tradition rather than reason.

## Responses to Inductive Skepticism

**Pragmatic vindication** (Reichenbach): If there is any regularity in nature to be found, induction will find it. If there is no regularity, no method can succeed. So we have nothing to lose by using induction. This is not a justification but a defense of induction as the pragmatically best bet.

**Dissolution** (Strawson): The demand for a justification of induction is confused. Induction just *is* one of the standards of rational inference. To ask whether induction is rational is like asking whether all bachelors are unmarried — the answer is analytic. There is no external standard against which induction needs to be validated.

**Reliabilism**: If inductive inference is, as a matter of fact, reliable — if it produces true beliefs at a high rate — then inductively formed beliefs are justified. The challenge is that we cannot establish reliability without using induction.

**Bayesianism**: Bayesians argue that induction can be grounded in probabilistic updating. Given a prior probability distribution over hypotheses, updating on evidence by conditionalization produces more accurate posterior beliefs. But skeptics ask: what grounds the prior?

## Underdetermination of Theory by Evidence

A related skeptical challenge is the underdetermination thesis: any finite body of evidence is compatible with multiple, incompatible theories. Whatever evidence we have for our current scientific theories, there are infinitely many alternative theories that are equally well supported by the same evidence.

Pierre Duhem and Willard Quine developed this point. For Quine, our beliefs form a "web" that meets experience at its periphery; any statement can be preserved in the face of any evidence by adjusting other parts of the web. This means evidence never uniquely determines theory — there is always a choice of which beliefs to revise.

Underdetermination connects to inductive skepticism: both challenge the idea that experience can justify our theoretical commitments. They are among the deepest challenges to scientific realism and to the ordinary practice of learning from experience.
