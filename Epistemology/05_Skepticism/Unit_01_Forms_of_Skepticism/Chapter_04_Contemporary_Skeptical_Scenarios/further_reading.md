# Further Reading: Contemporary Skeptical Scenarios

## Primary Sources

- Hilary Putnam, *Reason, Truth and History*, Cambridge University Press, 1981. Chapter 1 presents the brain-in-a-vat scenario and the semantic argument against it: if we were brains in vats, our terms would refer to vat-images rather than real objects, so our utterance "I am a brain in a vat" would not mean what it appears to mean and therefore cannot be truly asserted.

- Nick Bostrom, "Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?" *Philosophical Quarterly* 53, 2003, pp. 243–255. The classic statement of the simulation argument: if advanced civilizations run ancestor simulations, and if simulated minds are possible, then the number of simulated persons vastly exceeds the number of non-simulated persons, making simulation the most probable hypothesis about our situation.

- David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, ed. Selby-Bigge and Nidditch, Oxford University Press, 1975. Sections IV–V present the problem of induction: inductive reasoning is circular (we justify induction by past inductive success) and cannot be justified by reason alone.

- Nelson Goodman, *Fact, Fiction, and Forecast*, Harvard University Press, 1955. Chapter III introduces the "new riddle of induction" (the grue paradox): any set of observations is equally confirming of infinitely many incompatible hypotheses, showing that the problem of induction is more radical than Hume supposed.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Anthony Brueckner, "Brains in a Vat," *Journal of Philosophy* 89, 1992, pp. 148–167. A careful examination of Putnam's semantic argument, identifying its premises and assessing which are vulnerable to objection.

- Simon Blackburn, "Enchanting Views," in *Reading Putnam*, eds. Clark and Hale, Blackwell, 1994. A critical examination of Putnam's semantic response to the brain-in-a-vat scenario, arguing that it does not conclusively refute the skeptical challenge.

- David Chalmers, "The Matrix as Metaphysics," in *Philosophers Explore the Matrix*, ed. Christopher Grau, Oxford University Press, 2005. Argues that even if we are in a simulation, this does not imply that our beliefs are false — the simulated world would be our real world — offering a distinctive response to the simulation-based skeptical challenge.

- Brian Weatherson, "Are You a Sim?" *Philosophical Quarterly* 53, 2003, pp. 425–431. A critical response to Bostrom's simulation argument, questioning the probability calculations and the assumptions about the number of simulated minds.

- Wesley Salmon, *The Foundations of Scientific Inference*, University of Pittsburgh Press, 1967. A classic treatment of the problem of induction, surveying different responses (pragmatic vindication, inductive logic, Bayesianism) and assessing their adequacy.

- Gilbert Harman, *Thought*, Princeton University Press, 1973. Chapter 8 examines the underdetermination of theory by evidence in the context of inference to the best explanation, connecting the underdetermination problem to skeptical challenges.

- Larry Laudan, "Demystifying Underdetermination," in *Scientific Theories*, ed. Savage, University of Minnesota Press, 1990. Distinguishes multiple versions of the underdetermination thesis and argues that the strongest versions are not well supported, providing important background for evaluating underdetermination-based skepticism.

- Bas van Fraassen, *The Scientific Image*, Oxford University Press, 1980. Develops constructive empiricism partly on the basis of underdetermination — the empirically equivalent alternatives to any theory mean that we cannot have knowledge of unobservable entities — a sophisticated form of local scientific skepticism.

- Catherine Elgin, "Quine and the Dissolution of the Demarcation Problem," in *Reflective Equilibrium*, Kluwer, 1996. Relevant for understanding Goodmanian and Quinean approaches to underdetermination and the new riddle.

- Jonathan Vogel, "Cartesian Skepticism and Inference to the Best Explanation," *Journal of Philosophy* 87, 1990, pp. 658–666. Argues that the brain-in-a-vat hypothesis is not the best explanation of our experience, connecting the contemporary skeptical scenario to inference-to-the-best-explanation responses.

- Keith DeRose, "Solving the Skeptical Problem," *Philosophical Review* 104, 1995, pp. 1–52. Contextualizes the brain-in-a-vat scenario within the contextualist response to skepticism, arguing that its intuitive force is a function of the elevated epistemic standards it evokes.

- David Papineau, *The Roots of Reason*, Oxford University Press, 2003. Examines the relationship between evolution, cognitive reliability, and inductive skepticism, arguing that evolutionary considerations can help vindicate rather than undermine inductive practices.

- P.F. Strawson, *Introduction to Logical Theory*, Methuen, 1952. Chapter 9 offers a classic dissolution of the problem of induction, arguing that it is conceptually confused to demand a justification of induction of the same kind as a justification of a particular inductive argument.

- Timothy Williamson, "Scepticism and Evidence," *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* 60, 2000, pp. 613–628. Examines whether brain-in-a-vat scenarios genuinely undermine all evidence, arguing from his knowledge-first framework that they may not be as epistemically devastating as they appear.
