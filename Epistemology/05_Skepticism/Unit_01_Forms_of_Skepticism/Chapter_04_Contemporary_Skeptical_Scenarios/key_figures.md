# Key Figures: Contemporary Skeptical Scenarios

## Hilary Putnam (1926–2016)

Putnam introduced the brain-in-a-vat (BIV) scenario as a contemporary successor to Descartes' evil demon in *Reason, Truth and History* (1981). His distinctive contribution was not to endorse the scenario but to argue against it using a semantic externalist argument: if we were brains in vats, the causal/contextual connections that determine what our words refer to would be entirely internal to the vat environment. Our word "tree" would refer to vat-images of trees, not to actual trees. Hence, if we utter "We are brains in a vat," this utterance does not mean the same thing as it would mean outside the vat — it means something about vat-images, not about actual brains and vats. So the skeptical hypothesis cannot be coherently stated by the very subjects it purports to describe. This semantic response to the BIV is the most discussed externalist move against contemporary skepticism.

## Nick Bostrom (b. 1973)

Bostrom's simulation argument (*Philosophical Quarterly*, 2003) presents a probabilistic case for the hypothesis that we are living in a computer simulation. His argument is tripartite: at least one of the following is true — (1) virtually no civilization reaches the computational sophistication to run ancestor simulations, (2) virtually no civilization that reaches that sophistication chooses to run them, or (3) we are almost certainly living in a simulation. If (1) and (2) are both false, then the number of simulated minds vastly exceeds the number of "base reality" minds, making simulation overwhelmingly probable by a straightforward anthropic argument. Bostrom's contribution is to update the BIV scenario with considerations from computational theory and anthropic probability, making the skeptical hypothesis seem not merely possible but statistically likely.

## David Hume (1711–1776)

Hume's problem of induction — formulated in the *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* and the *Treatise of Human Nature* — is the canonical form of non-Cartesian skepticism. Hume argues that all inference from observed to unobserved cases relies on the assumption that nature is uniform — that the future will resemble the past. But this assumption cannot be justified by reason alone (there is no logical contradiction in the idea that nature might change radically), nor by induction (that would be circular). Hume concludes that inductive inference is based on custom and habit rather than reason, and that strictly speaking we cannot rationally justify it. This is the deepest challenge to scientific knowledge and practical reasoning that has been formulated in Western philosophy.

## Nelson Goodman (1906–1998)

Goodman's "new riddle of induction," introduced in *Fact, Fiction, and Forecast* (1955), shows that the problem of induction is more radical than Hume's formulation suggests. Define "grue" as: green if examined before a certain date, blue otherwise. The same set of observations of green emeralds that confirms "All emeralds are green" equally confirms "All emeralds are grue." Any set of observational evidence is equally consistent with infinitely many incompatible hypotheses. The new riddle shows that even if we could solve Hume's problem and justify the use of induction, we would still need a theory of which predicates are "projectible" — which generalizations can be legitimately inducted upon. Goodman argues that projectibility is determined by entrenchment (how long a predicate has been used in successful predictions), a solution that itself raises further problems about the objectivity of science.

## David Chalmers (b. 1966)

Chalmers offers a distinctive response to simulation-based skepticism that does not attempt to refute the simulation hypothesis but rather to defuse its skeptical bite. In "The Matrix as Metaphysics" (2005), he argues that if we are in a simulation, the simulated objects — tables, chairs, other people — are our real objects, in the only sense relevant to us. Our beliefs about them would be largely true, on a correct understanding of what those beliefs are about. For Chalmers, being in a simulation is not like being deceived about an external reality; it is like occupying a different metaphysical level of reality. His response is important for showing that BIV and simulation skepticism may not be equivalent to Cartesian evil-demon skepticism, and that the strength of the skeptical challenge depends on contested questions about reference and metaphysics.

## Pierre Duhem (1861–1916) and W.V.O. Quine (1908–2000)

The Duhem-Quine thesis — that any hypothesis can be maintained in the face of any observation by making suitable adjustments elsewhere in one's system of beliefs — is the basis for underdetermination-based skepticism. Duhem, in *The Aim and Structure of Physical Theory* (1906), argued that scientific hypotheses are tested as groups, not individually, so falsifying observations never conclusively refute a single hypothesis. Quine extended this in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) to all knowledge. Together, they show that empirical evidence always underdetermines the choice among competing theories, generating a form of skepticism about theory-choice that is internal to scientific practice rather than based on exotic philosophical scenarios.

## Bas van Fraassen (b. 1941)

Van Fraassen's constructive empiricism in *The Scientific Image* (1980) exploits the underdetermination of theory by evidence to argue for agnosticism about unobservable entities. He holds that science aims at "empirical adequacy" — agreement with all observable phenomena — rather than truth, because we can never determine which of the empirically adequate theories corresponds to the truth about unobservables. While van Fraassen does not endorse global skepticism, his work shows how underdetermination-based local skepticism can be developed into a sophisticated and defensible philosophical position about the limits of scientific knowledge.
