# Thought Experiments: Contemporary Skeptical Scenarios

## The Brain in a Vat

**The scenario:** Imagine that a malicious neuroscientist has removed your brain from your body and placed it in a vat of nutrients. Electrodes connect your brain to a supercomputer that feeds you perfectly coherent, convincing sensory experiences. You seem to be living a normal life — reading, walking, talking with friends — but every experience is entirely computer-generated. No perceptual evidence available to you can distinguish this scenario from ordinary embodied life. Is there any way you could discover you were in the vat? If not, how do you know you are not in one?

**The philosophical point:** The BIV scenario is the contemporary successor to Descartes' evil demon, updated to feel more scientifically plausible. It challenges the reliability of all sensory experience by providing an alternative explanation of that experience that is indistinguishable from the inside. Putnam's semantic response argues that if you *were* a BIV, your terms would not refer to the real brains and vats they would refer to in normal use — you couldn't coherently articulate the hypothesis. Critics of Putnam argue this response fails because the skeptic's point is not that you should believe you're a BIV, but that you cannot *know* you aren't one.

## Bostrom's Simulation Argument

**The scenario:** Suppose that in the distant future, civilization develops computers powerful enough to simulate entire civilizations, including all the minds in them. If even a small fraction of future civilizations choose to run such "ancestor simulations," the total number of simulated minds would be astronomical — vastly outnumbering the minds of "base-level" biological beings. Given that there would be far more simulated minds than non-simulated minds, and given that simulated and non-simulated minds would be indistinguishable from the inside, shouldn't you assign a high probability to the hypothesis that you are right now a simulated mind?

**The philosophical point:** Unlike the BIV scenario, the simulation argument is probabilistic: it does not merely claim that simulation is possible but that it is probable, on the assumption that advanced simulation technology exists somewhere in the universe. This raises new questions for epistemology: can a skeptical hypothesis be defeated by showing it is improbable, and if so, what probability distribution should we use? Chalmers' response — that simulated objects would be our real objects — attempts to defuse the skeptical bite without challenging the probability calculations. The thought experiment forces a consideration of whether epistemic threats require only possibility or whether probability is also relevant.

## The Grue Paradox

**The scenario:** All the emeralds examined so far have been green. You infer by induction that the next emerald will be green. Now define the predicate "grue" as follows: an object is grue if it has been examined before a certain future date T and is green, or if it has not been examined before T and is blue. All the emeralds examined so far have also been grue (since they are green and examined before T). Why should your observations confirm "All emeralds are green" rather than "All emeralds are grue"?

**The philosophical point:** Goodman's new riddle shows that induction cannot be the epistemically neutral procedure it appears to be. The same evidence confirms incompatible hypotheses equally well, from a purely logical point of view. Goodman's solution — that "green" is projectible because it is "entrenched" (established in scientific and everyday practice) while "grue" is not — raises the question of whether this pragmatic answer satisfies the epistemological demand. The thought experiment shows that solving Hume's problem of induction (justifying inductive inference in general) would not solve the deeper problem: we need a theory of which predicates and generalizations to project, and no purely logical theory seems adequate.

## The Underdetermination of Theory

**The scenario:** Two astronomers propose competing theories about the structure of the solar system. Both theories agree on all current and past observations; they predict the same observable positions of planets, the same eclipses, the same behavior of comets. The theories differ, however, in their claims about unobservable features — the existence of a hypothetical hidden planet, the curvature of spacetime in remote regions. No possible observation could distinguish the theories within any foreseeable time horizon. Are either of the astronomers justified in claiming to *know* which theory is correct?

**The philosophical point:** The Duhem-Quine underdetermination thesis applied to this case generates a form of scientific skepticism that is internal to good scientific practice rather than dependent on exotic philosophical scenarios. The case shows that empirical evidence, however extensive, always leaves the choice among empirically equivalent theories underdetermined. Van Fraassen's constructive empiricism draws the modest conclusion that science should aim for empirical adequacy rather than truth about unobservables. Realists respond that inference to the best explanation — asking which theory is simpler, more unified, and more explanatorily powerful — can rationally adjudicate between empirically equivalent theories even without further observational evidence.

## Hume's Turkey Problem

**The scenario:** A turkey is fed by the farmer every morning at 9 AM without fail. The turkey observes this pattern for months and forms the inductive belief that it will be fed every morning at 9 AM. On the day before Thanksgiving, the farmer arrives at 9 AM and wrings the turkey's neck. The turkey's past-to-future inference was perfect by every inductive standard available — and it was catastrophically wrong.

**The philosophical point:** This vivid illustration of Hume's problem (adapted from Bertrand Russell's version in *The Problems of Philosophy*) shows that inductive inference can fail not through any error in the inductive procedure but because the world can change in ways that no past evidence reveals. The turkey case illustrates what statisticians call "model misspecification" and what philosophers call the "principle of the uniformity of nature": there is no guarantee that nature will continue to be uniform in the ways it has been so far. The epistemological point is that inductive knowledge, if we have it at all, must be grounded in something beyond the bare pattern of past regularities — perhaps in structural features of the world, or in inference to the best explanation, or in pragmatic conventions — and identifying what that something is remains one of the deepest problems in epistemology.
