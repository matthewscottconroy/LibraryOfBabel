# Moore and Common Sense

G.E. Moore's response to skepticism is one of the most famous and most discussed in the history of philosophy. In papers like "Proof of an External World" (1939) and "A Defence of Common Sense" (1925), Moore argued that the skeptic's conclusions are less credible than the common-sense beliefs they purport to undermine. His approach is often called "Moorean" and has been enormously influential, even when philosophers disagree about whether it succeeds.

## The Proof of an External World

Moore's "Proof" runs:

1. Here is one hand.
2. Here is another hand.
3. Therefore, there are at least two external objects.

Moore held that this proof is perfectly valid. The conclusion follows from the premises; the premises are true; and he knows them to be true. Therefore, the conclusion is established.

The target is the skeptic who argues that we cannot know that the external world exists. Moore's response: of course we can. I know I have two hands; I can see and feel them. If knowing I have two hands doesn't count as knowing something about the external world, then the skeptic's standards for knowledge are simply wrong.

## The Certainty Claim

Moore was explicit that he was claiming *knowledge*, not mere belief. He knew he had hands with certainty. When he compared his certainty about having hands with his uncertainty about his philosophical premises (the premises used in skeptical arguments), he was confident that the hands were better supported.

This is the core of Moore's move: the skeptical argument is itself supposed to be known or at least rationally believed. But if the conclusion of the argument — "you don't know you have hands" — conflicts with what you know most firmly (that you do have hands), then the premises must be wrong, even if you can't identify which one or why.

## The Moorean Modus Tollens

Philosophers have analyzed Moore's move as a *modus tollens* response to skeptical arguments. The skeptic argues:

(P1) If you know you have hands, you know you're not a BIV.
(P2) You don't know you're not a BIV.
(C) Therefore, you don't know you have hands.

Moore's response: since I know I have hands (this is more certain than the premises), I can run the argument backwards:

(P1) If you know you have hands, you know you're not a BIV.
(not-C) I do know I have hands.
(not-P2) Therefore, I know I'm not a BIV.

Both the skeptical argument (from P1 and P2 to C) and Moore's response (from P1 and not-C to not-P2) are valid. The question is which direction to run the argument — which is more credible, the skeptic's P2 or Moore's not-C.

Moore's answer: my ordinary knowledge of having hands is more credible than the philosophical claim that I cannot rule out BIV scenarios. So I should reject the skeptic's P2 and conclude that I know I'm not a BIV.

## Wittgenstein's "On Certainty"

Wittgenstein's final notes, published as *On Certainty* (1969), can be read as a sophisticated development of Moore's response. Wittgenstein argues that the claims Moore identifies — "I have two hands," "the earth has existed for a long time before my birth" — are not ordinary empirical beliefs that could be mistaken. They are part of the framework within which empirical beliefs get their sense.

These "hinge propositions" are not known in the ordinary sense — we don't verify them or test them against evidence. They are the stable background against which verification and testing take place. Doubting them would not be a refined epistemic achievement but a failure to participate in our ordinary practices of inquiry.

For Wittgenstein, the skeptic's demand that we justify these background certainties is confused: they are not the kind of thing that admits of justification or skeptical challenge. The skeptic has misidentified the epistemic status of these propositions.

## Criticisms of Moorean Responses

The main criticism: Moore's response seems question-begging. He simply asserts that he knows he has hands and uses this to defeat the skeptical argument. But the skeptic's whole point is to challenge whether such ordinary claims amount to knowledge. Moore seems to be refusing to engage with the argument rather than answering it.

Proponents respond that this criticism misunderstands the dialectical situation. Both the skeptic and Moore have premises and conclusions. The question is which set is more credible. Moore's premises (I have hands) may have more going for them than the skeptic's premises (you can't rule out BIV scenarios). The skeptic needs to show that their premises are more credible than Moore's — not just assert it.
