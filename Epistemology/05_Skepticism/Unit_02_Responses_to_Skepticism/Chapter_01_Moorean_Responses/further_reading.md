# Further Reading: Moorean Responses to Skepticism

## Primary Sources

- G.E. Moore, "Proof of an External World," in *Philosophical Papers*, Allen & Unwin, 1959. The classic text: Moore holds up his hands, argues "Here is one hand, here is another, therefore at least two external objects exist," and claims this constitutes a rigorous proof that external objects exist.

- G.E. Moore, "Certainty," in *Philosophical Papers*, Allen & Unwin, 1959. Develops the view that certain common-sense propositions — "I have a body," "the earth has existed for many years" — are known with certainty, and examines what this certainty amounts to.

- G.E. Moore, "A Defence of Common Sense," in *Philosophical Papers*, Allen & Unwin, 1959. Presents Moore's list of common-sense truisms — propositions that every sane adult knows to be true — and argues that any philosophical theory that contradicts them must be false.

- Ludwig Wittgenstein, *On Certainty*, trans. G.E.M. Anscombe and G.H. von Wright, Blackwell, 1969. Wittgenstein's late reflections on Moore's proof and the nature of certainty; introduces the concept of "hinge propositions" — beliefs that form the bedrock of epistemic practices but are themselves neither known nor believed in the ordinary sense.

## Key Secondary Sources

- James Pryor, "The Skeptic and the Dogmatist," *Noûs* 34, 2000, pp. 517–549. Defends a neo-Moorean "dogmatist" position: perceptual experience immediately justifies perceptual beliefs without inferential mediation, and this is not question-begging because the justification does not presuppose the truth of what it justifies.

- Ernest Sosa, "How to Defeat Opposition to Moore," *Philosophical Perspectives* 13, 1999, pp. 141–153. Argues that the Moorean response to skepticism succeeds when supplemented with virtue-epistemological considerations about why our perceptual faculties are reliable.

- Barry Stroud, "Understanding Human Knowledge in General," in *Knowledge and Mind*, eds. Clay and Lehrer, Oxford University Press, 1989. Argues that Moore's response, while grammatically correct, fails to *explain* how we have knowledge of the external world — which is what the skeptic really challenges.

- Crispin Wright, "Wittgenstein's Certitude," in *Wittgenstein in America*, ed. McCarthy and Stidd, Oxford University Press, 2001. An examination of whether Wittgenstein's hinge propositions constitute a genuine response to the skeptical challenge or merely describe the structure of our epistemic practices without explaining their legitimacy.

- Annalisa Coliva, *Moore and Wittgenstein: Scepticism, Certainty and Common Sense*, Palgrave Macmillan, 2010. A scholarly comparison of Moore's and Wittgenstein's responses to skepticism, with careful attention to where they agree and where they diverge.

- Duncan Pritchard, *Epistemic Angst*, Princeton University Press, 2016. Argues that a satisfying response to radical skepticism requires combining Wittgensteinian insights about hinge commitments with an anti-luck condition on knowledge.

- Michael Williams, "Mooreanism Revisited," *Synthese* 165, 2008, pp. 263–279. Examines contemporary neo-Moorean responses and assesses whether they succeed in engaging the skeptic's actual challenge.

- Thomas Kelly, "Moorean Facts and Bayesian Epistemology," *Philosophical Studies* 128, 2006, pp. 333–352. Examines whether Moorean methodology can be vindicated from within a Bayesian probabilistic framework.

- Annalisa Coliva, *Extended Rationality: A Hinge Epistemology*, Palgrave Macmillan, 2015. Develops a "hinge epistemology" that takes Wittgenstein's framework as its basis and uses it to explain both the force of the skeptical challenge and why it does not undermine knowledge.

- Avner Baz, *When Words Are Called For*, Harvard University Press, 2012. Examines the ordinary language tradition in analytic philosophy, including Moore's common-sense philosophy, from an Austinian perspective.

- Crispin Wright, "Cogency and Question-Begging: Some Reflections on McKinsey's Paradox and Putnam's Proof," *Philosophical Issues* 10, 2000, pp. 140–163. Relevant to the question of whether Moorean arguments beg the question against the skeptic.

- John McDowell, "Criteria, Defeasibility, and Knowledge," *Proceedings of the British Academy* 68, 1982, pp. 455–479. Defends a disjunctivist response to skepticism that preserves Moorean directness while avoiding the "highest common factor" model of perception.

- Stewart Cohen, "How to Be a Fallibilist," *Philosophical Perspectives* 2, 1988, pp. 581–605. Defends a Moorean-compatible fallibilism that acknowledges the conceivability of skeptical scenarios while denying they undermine ordinary knowledge.

- P.F. Strawson, *Skepticism and Naturalism*, Columbia University Press, 1985. Defends a naturalistic response to skepticism that draws on both Humean and Wittgensteinian themes, arguing that skeptical doubts cannot be genuinely sustained in practice.
