# Key Figures: Moorean Responses to Skepticism

## G.E. Moore (1873–1958)

Moore's response to skepticism is the paradigm of the common-sense approach. In "Proof of an External World" (1939), he argued that holding up his hands and saying "Here is one hand, here is another" constitutes a rigorous proof that at least two external objects exist. Moore was fully aware of the apparent philosophical weakness of this move — he acknowledged that he could not prove the premises of his proof — but he insisted that the proof was valid and that the premise ("here is a hand") was *known* with at least as much certainty as any philosophical argument for skepticism. Moore's implicit point is that the skeptic's premises (such as "one cannot know one is not dreaming") should be *less* credible to us than the common-sense claim that we have hands. His methodology — beginning with what we know rather than with what we can prove — is the defining characteristic of the common-sense philosophical tradition.

## Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889–1951)

Wittgenstein's late notebooks, published as *On Certainty* (1969), represent a deep engagement with Moore's response to skepticism. Wittgenstein agrees with Moore that the skeptic is making a philosophical mistake, but disagrees about what the mistake is. For Wittgenstein, the common-sense propositions Moore lists (there is an external world, I have a body, the earth existed before I was born) are not "known" in the ordinary sense — they are "hinge propositions" that serve as the unquestioned backdrop against which ordinary knowing and doubting take place. They are not at the center of a web of beliefs; they are the hinges on which the web turns. To doubt them is not to exercise sophisticated philosophical criticism but to step outside the language game of normal inquiry altogether. Wittgenstein's "hinge epistemology" has generated a rich contemporary literature.

## James Pryor (b. 1973)

Pryor's "dogmatist" epistemology defends a neo-Moorean position. He argues that when you have a perceptual experience as of P, this experience immediately and non-inferentially justifies your belief that P — you do not need to first establish that you are not in a skeptical scenario. This "immediate" or "direct" justification is not question-begging because it does not *argue from* any premise that entails that you are not in a skeptical scenario; it simply has perceptual experience as its ground. Pryor's dogmatism is a version of the Moorean insight that our perceptual beliefs have initial epistemic standing that is not hostage to the skeptic's challenge, and that we are entitled to them unless we have specific reasons to doubt.

## Annalisa Coliva (b. 1972)

Coliva's hinge epistemology, developed in *Moore and Wittgenstein* (2010) and *Extended Rationality* (2015), has become the most systematic contemporary development of the Wittgensteinian response to skepticism. She argues that hinge propositions — propositions like "I have a body" or "the external world exists" — are not beliefs at all (pace Moore) but framework commitments that make ordinary epistemic practices possible. Their status is not epistemic but practical: they are what it is rational to take for granted in order to engage in inquiry at all. Coliva distinguishes this from relativism by arguing that the hinges are not merely relative to a community but are constitutively presupposed by any coherent cognitive practice.

## Duncan Pritchard (b. 1971)

Pritchard's response to skepticism combines Wittgensteinian insights about hinge propositions with his anti-luck epistemology. In *Epistemic Angst* (2016), he argues that the Wittgensteinian approach correctly identifies that hinge commitments like "I am not a brain in a vat" are not subject to ordinary epistemic evaluation — they are not the kind of thing that can be known or doubted. But he also insists that this alone is not enough: a satisfying response must also include a safety condition on knowledge that explains why, in ordinary (non-skeptical) situations, our perceptual beliefs are safe and hence constitute knowledge. His "neo-Wittgensteinian" response thus combines the insight that certain commitments are foundational with the externalist insight that ordinary knowledge is modally robust.

## Ernest Sosa (b. 1940)

Sosa's virtue-epistemological response to skepticism supports a neo-Moorean position: our perceptual and cognitive faculties are reliable, and beliefs formed through their exercise constitute knowledge. His argument against the skeptic is not that we can *prove* our faculties are reliable from some neutral standpoint, but that we can know our faculties are reliable by exercising those very faculties — and this is not viciously circular because the circularity is at the level of a whole system of knowledge rather than a local, premise-to-conclusion circularity. Sosa's response is "coherentist" in the structural sense: the justification of individual beliefs comes from their being part of an overall well-functioning cognitive system, not from a linear chain of argument that terminates in some independently established foundation.

## Barry Stroud (1935–2019)

Stroud's role in the Moorean debate is primarily critical: he argues that Moore's response fails to engage with the skeptical challenge at the right level. In *The Significance of Philosophical Scepticism* (1984), he maintains that the skeptic is not challenging particular beliefs but is questioning our ability to *explain* how knowledge of the external world is possible at all. Moore's proof tells us that he knows he has hands, but it does not explain how this knowledge is possible — what makes it the case that perceptual experience tracks the external world. Stroud's critique has been enormously influential in shaping neo-Moorean responses: any adequate Moorean position must show not just that we have the knowledge Moore claims but that we can provide some account of how we have it.
