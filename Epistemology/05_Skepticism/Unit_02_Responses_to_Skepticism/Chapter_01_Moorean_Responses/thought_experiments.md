# Thought Experiments: Moorean Responses to Skepticism

## Moore's Proof of Hands

**The scenario:** A philosopher stands before an audience and holds up his right hand, saying "Here is one hand." He then holds up his left hand, saying "And here is another." He proceeds: "I knew that there was one hand here; I knew there was another; therefore I knew there were at least two hands here; therefore there is an external world." He acknowledges that he cannot prove the premises of the argument — he cannot prove he is not dreaming, for instance — but insists that nonetheless he knew them with certainty.

**The philosophical point:** Moore's proof is intentionally direct and apparently unsophisticated. Its philosophical point is not to provide an argument that the skeptic hasn't heard before, but to assert that the premises of the proof (there is a hand here) are *more* certain than the premises of any skeptical argument. The "Moorean modus tollens" exploits this: if the skeptic argues "If you cannot rule out dreaming, you don't know there is a hand" and the conclusion "you don't know there is a hand" seems less credible than the premise "there is a hand," the rational response is to reject the skeptic's conditional rather than the common-sense claim. The thought experiment forces a confrontation with the question of how to choose between equally valid-seeming arguments with conflicting conclusions.

## Wittgenstein's Hinge

**The scenario:** A philosopher asks a child: "Do you know that you have two hands?" The child holds up her hands and says "Yes!" The philosopher presses: "But how do you know you are not dreaming right now?" The child is puzzled — not because she cannot answer, but because the question seems somehow out of place. She has always had hands; she can see them; she cannot even understand what it would be to doubt this. What is the child's epistemic situation?

**The philosophical point:** Wittgenstein's concept of hinge propositions is illuminated by this case. The child's certainty is not the product of inference or evidence; it is not a belief she has formed on the basis of proof. It is a certainty that functions as the background against which ordinary believing, doubting, and knowing take place. Wittgenstein's point is not that the child is justified (in any ordinary inferential sense) in being certain she has hands, but that this certainty is constitutive of her participation in the language game of inquiry. The skeptical scenario "you might be dreaming" does not succeed in generating doubt here — it merely describes a scenario that does not engage with the bedrock certainty at stake.

## The Moorean Modus Tollens

**The scenario:** A philosopher presents the skeptical argument: "Premise 1: If you cannot tell the difference between waking and dreaming, you cannot know your perceptual beliefs are true. Premise 2: You cannot tell the difference. Conclusion: You cannot know your perceptual beliefs are true." A Moorean responds: "I know I have hands. Your conclusion conflicts with this. Therefore, at least one of your premises must be wrong. I will take P1 as the culprit — being unable to distinguish waking from dreaming does not entail I cannot know my perceptual beliefs are true."

**The philosophical point:** The Moorean modus tollens is the logical structure of Moore's common-sense response to skepticism. Rather than accepting the skeptic's argument and drawing the skeptical conclusion, the Moorean runs the argument backward: since the skeptical conclusion is known to be false (because we know ordinary things), one of the premises must be false. The key philosophical question is: which side of the argument is more credible — the skeptic's premises or the Moorean's ordinary knowledge claims? Moore's implicit answer is that our ordinary knowledge claims are more credible than any philosophical argument whose premises entail their negation, making common sense the tribunal before which philosophical arguments must stand.

## The Bedrock of Practice

**The scenario:** An anthropologist from an isolated culture attempts to doubt the proposition that the earth has existed for more than five minutes — that everything was not created five minutes ago complete with false memories. She finds that she literally cannot conduct her work — cannot interview subjects, cannot trust her field notes, cannot make sense of the culture she studies — while entertaining this doubt. When she stops entertaining it, she works perfectly well. Is this a reason to believe the earth has existed for more than five minutes?

**The philosophical point:** This case illustrates Wittgenstein's point that some doubts cannot be practically sustained — that they are not so much refuted as rendered idle by the practical demands of inquiry. The anthropologist's situation shows that "the earth has existed for more than five minutes" is not merely a belief she holds but a practice-constituting commitment — it is part of what makes sense to say she is conducting research at all. Wittgenstein's anti-skeptical move is not to prove that such hinge propositions are true but to show that doubting them is not a coherent epistemic alternative: it is not so much false doubt as outside doubt. The thought experiment connects to the question of whether philosophy should or can demand more than the conditions of any possible inquiry allow.

## The Question-Begging Objection

**The scenario:** A student in a philosophy class is impressed by Moore's proof and offers it in response to the skeptic. The skeptic replies: "But you have simply *assumed* that you are not dreaming — that is precisely what is in question. Your proof uses as a premise exactly what the skeptic challenges. That's question-begging." The student is unsure how to respond.

**The philosophical point:** The question-begging objection is the standard challenge to Moorean responses. If the skeptic's worry is precisely that we cannot know our perceptual beliefs are true, and if Moore's proof takes such beliefs as premises, then the proof seems to assume what it aims to prove. Neo-Mooreans (Pryor, Sosa) respond that the objection itself presupposes an internalist picture of justification: Moore's premise ("here is a hand") is not assumed without justification — it is immediately justified by perceptual experience, and this immediate justification does not require prior proof that one is not dreaming. The question-begging objection, on this view, is itself question-begging: it assumes that the only legitimate justification is inferential, ruling out immediate perceptual justification by fiat.
