# Reliabilism and the Externalist Response to Skepticism

Externalist theories of knowledge offer a distinctive response to skepticism: skeptical arguments fail because they impose excessively demanding internalist constraints on knowledge. If knowledge only requires that beliefs be formed by reliable processes — and not that the believer be able to prove their reliability from the inside — then ordinary perceptual beliefs can constitute knowledge even in the face of undeniable skeptical possibilities.

## The Internalist Assumption Behind Skepticism

Skeptical arguments typically assume something like the following:

*If S knows that P, then S can show or demonstrate that P is true (or at least that P is probable given S's evidence).*

This assumption is internalist: it requires that the justification for P be accessible from the subject's first-person perspective. The skeptic then argues that since you cannot demonstrate that you're not a BIV, and since your ordinary empirical beliefs depend on your not being a BIV, you cannot demonstrate your ordinary beliefs — and therefore you don't know them.

Externalists challenge this assumption at its root. Knowledge requires reliability, not demonstrated reliability. If your perceptual system reliably produces true beliefs, your perceptual beliefs are knowledge — regardless of whether you can prove this reliability from the inside.

## Goldman's Reliabilist Response

Alvin Goldman's process reliabilism generates a simple response to the BIV scenario: you are not a BIV. Your perceptual processes are reliable — they actually track features of the actual world. Therefore, your perceptually formed beliefs are produced by reliable processes, and they count as knowledge.

The BIV scenario is irrelevant to your actual epistemic situation, because you're not actually in that scenario. The fact that if you *were* a BIV your processes would be unreliable doesn't undermine your actual knowledge, any more than the fact that if you were constitutionally incapable of perceiving red things your beliefs about red things would be unreliable.

This might seem like a sleight of hand, but the underlying point is serious: skeptical scenarios need to be actual to undermine knowledge. Merely possible unreliability doesn't undermine actual reliability.

## The New Evil Demon Objection

The most powerful objection to the reliabilist response is the new evil demon thought experiment. Imagine a population of people who are deceived by an evil demon: their experiences are exactly like ours, but they systematically misrepresent reality. Their perceptual processes are, as a matter of fact, unreliable.

Intuitively, these demon-world people are just as epistemically virtuous as we are. They reason carefully, respond appropriately to their evidence, and are in no way negligent. Yet on the reliabilist account, they lack justified beliefs (since their processes are unreliable). This seems wrong: epistemic blame for false beliefs should depend on factors within the agent's control, not on cosmic accident.

Goldman responded by distinguishing two senses of justified belief: one sensitive to actual reliability (appropriate for assessing epistemic success), one sensitive to what a normal, well-functioning cognitive system would do in the circumstances (appropriate for assessing epistemic blame). The demon-world people are not blameworthy, but their beliefs are not the right kind of thing to count as knowledge.

## Tracking Theories

Robert Nozick's tracking theory offers a different externalist response. On Nozick's account, knowledge requires:
- If P were false, you wouldn't believe P (sensitivity)
- If P were true, you would believe P (adherence)

For ordinary beliefs — about hands, about medium-sized objects — the sensitivity condition is met: if you didn't have hands, you'd have different experiences and wouldn't believe you did. The sensitivity condition links knowledge to counterfactual reliability in a way that ordinary beliefs satisfy.

The BIV hypothesis involves: if you were a BIV, you'd still believe you weren't. So your belief that you're not a BIV fails sensitivity. But your belief that you have hands is sensitive: in the closest possible world where you don't have hands, your experience would be different and you wouldn't believe you did.

This allows Nozick to deny closure: you know you have hands (sensitivity met), but you don't know you're not a BIV (sensitivity fails), even though "I have hands" entails "I'm not a handless BIV." Knowledge doesn't close under entailment.

## The Costs of Externalism

Externalist responses to skepticism face a cost: they make epistemic evaluation partially blind to the agent's perspective. A reliably-functioning agent who, through no fault of their own, cannot see that their faculties are reliable still has knowledge. This can seem to trivialize the epistemological project — reducing knowledge to mere reliable production of true beliefs, severing the connection between knowledge and rational agency.

The debate between internalists and externalists about the response to skepticism is in part a debate about what knowledge is *for* and why it matters: is it fundamentally about reliability (externalist), or about rational accountability (internalist)?
