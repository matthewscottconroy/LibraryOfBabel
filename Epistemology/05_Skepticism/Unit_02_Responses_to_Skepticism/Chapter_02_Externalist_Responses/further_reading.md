# Further Reading: Externalist Responses to Skepticism

## Primary Sources

- Alvin Goldman, *Epistemology and Cognition*, Harvard University Press, 1986. Chapters 1–4 develop the reliabilist response to skepticism: if our cognitive processes are in fact reliable, we have justified beliefs about the external world, regardless of whether we can prove this from the inside.

- Robert Nozick, *Philosophical Explanations*, Harvard University Press, 1981. Chapter 3 uses the sensitivity condition to respond to skepticism: we can know ordinary propositions (there is a hand) without knowing we are not in a skeptical scenario, because "knows" is not a penetrating operator and the closure principle fails.

- Fred Dretske, "Epistemic Operators," *Journal of Philosophy* 67, 1970, pp. 1007–1023. Introduces the relevant alternatives framework and denies closure: you know there is a zebra in the pen even though you cannot rule out that it is a cleverly disguised mule, because cleverly disguised mules are not a relevant alternative in the normal context.

- Fred Dretske, *Knowledge and the Flow of Information*, MIT Press, 1981. Develops the information-theoretic account of knowledge as a response to skepticism, arguing that knowledge is a matter of information causally transmitted rather than of defeating all possible skeptical scenarios.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Laurence BonJour, "Externalist Theories of Empirical Knowledge," *Midwest Studies in Philosophy* 5, 1980, pp. 53–73. The most influential early critique of reliabilist responses to skepticism, arguing that they fail because the skeptic can always ask: but how do you know your faculties are reliable?

- Richard Fumerton, *Metaepistemology and Skepticism*, Rowman & Littlefield, 1995. From an internalist perspective, argues that externalist responses to skepticism do not engage the skeptic's real challenge, which is internal: how can we *from the inside* know our faculties track the truth?

- Keith DeRose, "Solving the Skeptical Problem," *Philosophical Review* 104, 1995, pp. 1–52. Uses contextualism about "knows" to accommodate both the externalist insight (we do have ordinary knowledge) and the internalist intuition (the skeptic's argument has real force), by relativizing both to different epistemic contexts.

- Stewart Cohen, "Skepticism, Relevance, and Relativity," in *Dretske and His Critics*, ed. McLaughlin, Blackwell, 1991. Examines whether Dretske's relevant alternatives approach successfully responds to skepticism, arguing that it faces difficulties in specifying which alternatives are relevant.

- Jonathan Vogel, "Reliabilism Leveled," *Journal of Philosophy* 97, 2000, pp. 602–623. Presses bootstrapping objections against reliabilism that are particularly relevant to whether reliabilism can provide a satisfying response to skepticism.

- John Greco, "External World Skepticism," *Philosophy Compass* 2, 2007, pp. 625–649. A clear survey of externalist responses to skepticism and the objections they face.

- Barry Stroud, *The Significance of Philosophical Scepticism*, Oxford University Press, 1984. Chapter 4 criticizes externalist responses, arguing that reliabilism offers the right answer to the wrong question: the skeptic is not asking whether our faculties are reliable but how we can know that they are.

- Ernest Sosa, *A Virtue Epistemology*, Oxford University Press, 2007. Chapter 7 develops the virtue-epistemological response to skepticism, arguing that our faculties count as intellectual virtues and that knowledge produced by them is genuine even without independent proof of their reliability.

- Michael Williams, *Unnatural Doubts*, Blackwell, 1991. Argues that the externalist response to skepticism only works if we accept a diagnostic approach — showing that the skeptical problem is generated by an unjustified theoretical framework — rather than meeting it head-on.

- Peter Klein, "Skepticism," *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, 2015 ed. Contains a clear survey of externalist responses to skepticism and the debate about whether they succeed.

- Hilary Kornblith, *Knowledge and Its Place in Nature*, Oxford University Press, 2002. Defends a naturalized reliabilism and argues that this provides a satisfying response to skeptical challenges by embedding epistemology within cognitive science.

- Thomas Greco, *Achieving Knowledge*, Cambridge University Press, 2010. Chapter 8 develops the virtue-theoretic response to skepticism, connecting the credit theory of knowledge to the anti-skeptical argument.

- Anthony Brueckner, "The Scope of Skepticism," *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* 52, 1992, pp. 59–78. Examines which forms of skepticism externalist responses can and cannot handle.

- Crispin Wright, "Warrant for Nothing (and Foundations for Free)?" *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society* 78, 2004, pp. 167–212. Examines whether externalist responses to skepticism can provide *entitlement* — rational permission to accept basic propositions — even without positive evidence for them.
