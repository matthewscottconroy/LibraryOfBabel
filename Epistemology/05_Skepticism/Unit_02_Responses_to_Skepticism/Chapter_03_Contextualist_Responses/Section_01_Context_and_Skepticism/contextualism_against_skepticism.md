# Contextualism as a Response to Skepticism

Epistemic contextualism, discussed earlier in the context of knowledge attribution, has a particularly elegant application to skepticism. The contextualist response holds that skeptical arguments are valid in the philosophical context in which they are raised but do not undermine ordinary knowledge — because "know" means different things in those different contexts.

## The Contextualist Diagnosis

Why do skeptical arguments seem so compelling in philosophy seminars but leave no trace on our practical reasoning? The contextualist's answer: because they are compelling in philosophy seminars — the standards operative there are very high — but those high standards don't govern ordinary attributions of knowledge.

When Descartes in his study raises the evil demon hypothesis, he raises the stakes for knowledge claims. The relevant alternatives now include demon-induced beliefs, BIV scenarios, dream hypotheses. Against this background, no ordinary empirical belief can be shown to satisfy the requirement of ruling out all relevant alternatives. So "I know I have hands" is false in the context where Descartes asks the question.

But in ordinary contexts — planning a shopping trip, warning someone that the road is icy — the relevant alternatives don't include skeptical hypotheses. Against the background of ordinary practical reasoning, my perceptual beliefs do rule out all the relevant alternatives. In those contexts, "I know I have hands" is true.

## Stewart Cohen's Version

Stewart Cohen argues that contextualism resolves what he calls the skeptical paradox: three individually plausible claims that are jointly inconsistent:

1. I know that I have hands.
2. I don't know that I'm not a BIV.
3. If I know that I have hands, then I know I'm not a BIV (closure).

The contextualist dissolves the paradox: (1) is true in ordinary contexts, (2) is true in philosophical contexts (where skeptical hypotheses are relevant alternatives), and (3) is true in any context. But these three claims are made in different contexts, so there is no inconsistency — "know" means something different in each.

When the skeptic presents the argument and raises the BIV hypothesis, they shift us to a high-standards context. In that context, (1) becomes false — we don't know we have hands by the now-operative standards. But this doesn't show that ordinary knowledge claims are wrong; it just shows that our ordinary word "know" expresses something less demanding than what philosophy requires.

## DeRose's Contextualism

Keith DeRose develops contextualism more carefully. When someone raises a skeptical hypothesis — even hypothetically — this raises the epistemic standards in the conversational context. The hypothesis makes alternative possibilities relevant that were not relevant before, and the relevant alternatives framework requires ruling out all relevant alternatives.

This explains the intuitive pull of skeptical arguments: by raising the BIV scenario, the skeptic makes it relevant, which means that ruling it out is required for knowledge in this context. We cannot rule it out. So in the context the skeptic creates, we don't know. But we don't always need to rule out BIV scenarios; in ordinary contexts, they're not relevant.

## Virtues of the Contextualist Response

Contextualism preserves both ordinary knowledge and the compelling force of skeptical arguments:

- Ordinary knowledge attributions (we know many things) are true in their context.
- Skeptical conclusions (we don't know things by demanding standards) are true in the philosophical context.
- There is no contradiction because "know" is context-sensitive.

The response vindicates our ordinary epistemic practices while explaining why skeptical arguments are so difficult to dismiss.

## Criticisms

The main objections to contextualism as a response to skepticism:

**The "just" objection**: Even if contextualism is semantically correct, the skeptic can reformulate: "I don't care about the ordinary use of 'know.' Even if in ordinary contexts you count as knowing, you don't have what really matters — genuine epistemic contact with the truth, belief that can't easily be false, certainty. Contextualism just shows that 'know' is context-sensitive; it doesn't show that we have what matters."

**The practical implications problem**: If in ordinary contexts we know, but in philosophy seminars we don't, what guides our actions? If I'm deciding whether to risk my life on the belief that the road is clear, do I need philosophical-context certainty, or ordinary-context knowledge?

**The error theory problem**: Contextualism implies that when ordinary people make knowledge claims and philosophers deny them, both are speaking truly (in their respective contexts). But this seems to change the subject rather than resolve a genuine disagreement.
