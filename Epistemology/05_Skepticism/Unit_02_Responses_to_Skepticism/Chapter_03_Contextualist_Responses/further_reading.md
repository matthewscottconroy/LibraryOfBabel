# Further Reading: Contextualist Responses to Skepticism

## Primary Sources

- Stewart Cohen, "How to Be a Fallibilist," *Philosophical Perspectives* 2, 1988, pp. 581–605. An early development of contextualism as a response to the lottery problem and skepticism, arguing that the standards for "knows" vary with context.

- Stewart Cohen, "Knowledge and Context," *Journal of Philosophy* 84, 1987, pp. 574–583. Develops the contextualist framework with attention to how epistemic standards shift when skeptical scenarios are raised.

- Keith DeRose, "Solving the Skeptical Problem," *Philosophical Review* 104, 1995, pp. 1–52. The landmark paper developing DeRose's contextualist response to skepticism, arguing that the skeptic's argument is valid *in the skeptical context* while ordinary knowledge claims are true *in ordinary contexts*.

- David Lewis, "Elusive Knowledge," *Australasian Journal of Philosophy* 74, 1996, pp. 549–567. Lewis's contextualist account of knowledge: S knows p iff S's evidence eliminates all relevant alternatives to p, where relevance is context-relative and follows rules for what can and cannot be "properly ignored."

## Key Secondary Sources

- John Hawthorne, *Knowledge and Lotteries*, Oxford University Press, 2004. A sustained examination of lottery cases, contextualism, and the relationship between knowledge and practical interests, including significant criticism of contextualist responses to skepticism.

- Jeremy Fantl and Matthew McGrath, *Knowledge in an Uncertain World*, Oxford University Press, 2009. Defends a "moderate" pragmatic contextualism, arguing that the relationship between knowledge and practical stakes is internal to the concept of knowledge.

- Timothy Williamson, *Knowledge and Its Limits*, Oxford University Press, 2000. Chapter 8 criticizes contextualism, arguing that it cannot explain why the skeptic's argument seems compelling in the philosophical context and why knowledge is binary rather than context-relative.

- Keith DeRose, *The Case for Contextualism*, Oxford University Press, 2009. The most comprehensive defense of contextualism, developing the account through responses to lottery cases, Moorean cases, and skeptical arguments, with detailed replies to objections.

- John Hawthorne and Jason Stanley, "Knowledge and Action," *Journal of Philosophy* 105, 2008, pp. 571–590. Connects knowledge attributions to practical interests (interest-relative invariantism), developing a rival to contextualism that also accommodates the practical stakes intuitions.

- Michael Williams, *Unnatural Doubts*, Blackwell, 1991. Offers a different kind of context-sensitive response to skepticism — not semantic contextualism but methodological contextualism — arguing that skeptical doubts are not genuine because they presuppose an unjustified theoretical framework.

- Anthony Brueckner, "The Elusive Virtues of Contextualism," *Philosophical Studies* 118, 2004, pp. 401–405. A critical examination of Lewis's contextualist account, pressing the question of which propositions can and cannot be "properly ignored."

- Gillian Russell and John Doris, "Knowledge by Indirection," *Australasian Journal of Philosophy* 86, 2008. Examines the relationship between contextualism and knowledge-by-testimony, raising questions about whether contextualism applies consistently across epistemic contexts.

- Jonathan Schaffer, "The Contrast Class and the Skeptic," *Philosophical Studies* 119, 2004, pp. 73–103. Defends a contrastivist view of knowledge — one knows P rather than Q — that accommodates contextualist intuitions while avoiding some of the objections to standard contextualism.

- Peter Unger, *Ignorance: A Case for Scepticism*, Oxford University Press, 1975. The classic argument for an "absolute" conception of knowledge that denies contextualism; useful background for understanding what contextualism is responding to.

- Jason Stanley, *Knowledge and Practical Interests*, Oxford University Press, 2005. Develops interest-relative invariantism as an alternative to contextualism, arguing that whether a true belief counts as knowledge depends on the practical stakes for the believer.

- Patrick Rysiew, "The Context-Sensitivity of Knowledge Attributions," *Noûs* 35, 2001, pp. 477–514. An important examination of whether contextualism follows from the ordinary language evidence it typically cites, arguing that conversational implicature can explain the data without semantic context-sensitivity.

- Earl Conee, "Contextualism Contested," in *Contemporary Debates in Epistemology*, eds. Steup and Sosa, Blackwell, 2005. A concise critical examination of contextualism as a response to skepticism.

- Mark Richard, "Contextualism and Relativism," *Philosophical Studies* 119, 2004, pp. 215–242. Examines the relationship between contextualism and relativism about knowledge, with implications for how the contextualist response to skepticism should be understood.
