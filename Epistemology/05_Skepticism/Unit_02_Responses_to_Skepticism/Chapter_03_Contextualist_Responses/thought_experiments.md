# Thought Experiments: Contextualist Responses to Skepticism

## The Airport Case

**The scenario:** Hannah is planning to fly and wants to confirm her flight is departing from terminal B. She checks the departures board and it says terminal B. She forms the belief "My flight departs from terminal B" and plans accordingly. Her husband, who is reading a philosophy book, asks: "But how do you know it hasn't changed? Could you be wrong?" Hannah considers: well, yes, theoretically, schedules do change. In the ordinary context — busy airport, time pressure — Hannah's "I know it departs from B" seems clearly true. In the philosophical context of the worried husband's questioning, she seems less certain.

**The philosophical point:** DeRose uses airport-like cases to motivate contextualism. The suggestion is that the standards for "knows" in the busy-airport practical context are lower than in the husband's philosophically reflective context. Both Hannah's first utterance ("I know it departs from B") and her husband's subsequent doubt can be true, relativized to their different contexts. The contextualist resolution explains why the skeptic's argument seems compelling in the philosophical seminar (where high standards are invoked) without requiring us to revise our ordinary knowledge claims (which are made at lower standards). The case also illustrates the connection between contextualism and the practical stakes literature.

## Lewis's Ignored Possibility

**The scenario:** You are sitting in a room and you believe there is no lion in the room. You know this — there is no lion. But technically, it is *possible* (in some far-fetched sense) that a lion escaped from a zoo, wandered into the building, and is hiding behind the sofa. You are not attending to this possibility; the possibility has not been raised in your conversation; and there is no specific evidence for it. Does the mere possibility undermine your knowledge that there is no lion?

**The philosophical point:** Lewis's Rule of Proper Ignoring says no — possibilities that are not actual, not attended to in the conversation, not made salient by evidence, can be properly ignored for the purposes of knowledge attribution. Your knowledge claim is true because the lion-behind-the-sofa possibility is properly ignored. But Lewis also holds that "elusive" knowledge can be destroyed by attending to it: if someone in your conversation says "but what about the possibility of a hidden lion?", that possibility suddenly becomes a relevant alternative and your claim to "know" there is no lion is undermined. The thought experiment illustrates the instability of knowledge on Lewis's account and raises the question of whether it is epistemically rational to refuse to think about disturbing possibilities in order to preserve one's knowledge.

## The Skeptical Seminar

**The scenario:** Two students have identical beliefs about the external world — both believe they have hands, both have good perceptual evidence. Student A is sitting in a philosophy seminar where the professor is pressing the BIV scenario; the professor has just made the BIV possibility vivid and has argued that it cannot be ruled out. Student B is sitting in a chemistry lab, focused on an experiment. Both utter "I know I have hands." Does one of them speak truly and the other falsely?

**The philosophical point:** The contextualist says yes — Student B speaks truly (at the low standards of the chemistry lab context) while Student A speaks falsely (because the BIV possibility has been made relevant in the seminar context). Critics of contextualism find this hard to accept: the two students are in identical epistemic positions, their knowledge cannot depend on what their conversation partners are talking about. The "just practical implications" objection holds that contextualism confuses the pragmatic implications of "I know" in different contexts with the truth conditions of the knowledge claim itself. The thought experiment thus forces a decision between semantic and pragmatic accounts of the apparent context-sensitivity of knowledge attributions.

## The Error Theorist's Challenge

**The scenario:** A philosophy professor is puzzled. She argues: "When I say 'I know there is a table here' in ordinary life, I seem to be saying something true. When the skeptic raises the BIV scenario and I say 'I don't know there is a table here', I also seem to be saying something true. But these two statements are about the same thing — my epistemic relation to the proposition that there is a table. If contextualism is right, these are both true but neither is about *the same* property. What is left of the concept of knowledge if it is different things in different contexts?"

**The philosophical point:** The "error theory" objection to contextualism holds that ordinary speakers are *wrong* about their own knowledge claims: when I say "I know there is a table here," I am implicitly claiming to meet all relevant epistemic standards, including the skeptical ones — and since I don't meet those standards, my claim is simply false. Contextualists deny this: when ordinary speakers say "I know," they are not (silently) claiming to rule out far-fetched alternatives; they are claiming to meet the standards appropriate for their context, which are the only standards relevant to their claim. The thought experiment forces examination of what we are actually claiming when we say we know something and whether contextualism mischaracterizes those claims.

## Lewis's Rule of Attention Applied to Therapy

**The scenario:** A therapist advises a patient with an anxiety disorder: "Stop thinking about the possibility that you might be dying of cancer — you have no symptoms and no reason to think so, and attending to that possibility is making you miserable." The patient follows the advice; she stops attending to the possibility and feels better. Has she changed her *epistemic* situation — does she now *know* she is not dying of cancer in a way she did not before?

**The philosophical point:** Lewis's Rule of Attention says that possibilities currently being attended to in a conversational or deliberative context cannot be properly ignored for purposes of knowledge attribution. Applied to the therapeutic case, before the therapist's intervention (when the patient was attending to the cancer possibility), she arguably didn't know she was cancer-free; after (when she is properly ignoring the possibility per the therapist's guidance), she does know. This has counterintuitive practical implications: one can apparently increase one's knowledge by strategically not attending to relevant possibilities. The case probes whether epistemic context-sensitivity really has the practical and psychological dimensions Lewis's account seems to imply, and whether this makes contextualism a satisfying epistemological position or a potentially epistemically dangerous one.
