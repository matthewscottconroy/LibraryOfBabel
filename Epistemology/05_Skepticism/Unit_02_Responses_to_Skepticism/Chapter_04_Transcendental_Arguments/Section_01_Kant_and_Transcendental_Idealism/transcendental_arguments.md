# Transcendental Arguments Against Skepticism

Transcendental arguments are arguments that proceed by identifying necessary preconditions of experience or thought. Against skepticism, they take a distinctive form: the skeptic uses thought and experience in formulating the skeptical challenge; but the very conditions that make thought and experience possible rule out the skeptical scenario. Therefore, the skeptic's challenge is self-undermining.

## Kant's Original Transcendental Argument

Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781/1787) is the fountainhead of transcendental argumentation. The problem Kant addresses is not exactly the Cartesian problem (he takes the existence of outer experience as a starting point) but the Humean problem: what grounds our judgments of causal necessity, substance, and other a priori concepts?

Kant's strategy: identify the *a priori* conditions that must hold if experience is possible at all. Experience requires:

- **Unity of apperception**: For experience to be mine, all its elements must be capable of being united in a single self-consciousness. The "I think" must be able to accompany all my representations.

- **Categories of the understanding**: For perceptions to constitute experience (rather than mere sensation), they must be structured by the categories — cause and effect, substance, plurality, etc. Experience of objects requires that these categories apply.

- **Transcendental unity of space and time**: Objects of experience must be located in a unified spatiotemporal framework.

The skeptic cannot coherently doubt the application of these conditions: they are required for there to be experience at all. If I doubt the existence of the external world, I am having an experience of doubt — but that experience is already structured by the conditions that guarantee contact with a world of objects.

## Strawson's Transcendental Arguments

P.F. Strawson revived transcendental argumentation in *Individuals* (1959) and "Skepticism and Naturalism" (1985). Strawson argues that identifying necessary conceptual connections can defeat skepticism.

For example: the skeptic questions whether we can know other minds exist. But our own psychological concepts (pain, belief, desire) require a contrast case — we understand what it is for others to be in pain because we apply the same concept to ourselves and others, using observable criteria. If there were no other minds, there would be no contrast case, and our psychological self-ascriptions would lose their meaning. So the possibility of psychological self-knowledge requires the existence of other minds.

This doesn't prove that specific other humans are conscious, but it establishes that the existence of *some* other minded beings is a necessary condition of one's own psychological self-knowledge.

## The Refutation of Idealism

Kant's "Refutation of Idealism" (added in the second edition of the *Critique*) is a more specific transcendental argument against the Cartesian "problematic idealism" — the view that the existence of external objects is doubtful. Kant argues:

1. I am conscious of my own existence as determined in time.
2. The determination of my existence in time requires something permanent outside me.
3. Therefore, something permanent outside me exists — the external world.

The argument: my inner experience involves temporal succession — thoughts and experiences follow one another. But to perceive temporal succession, I need a fixed point against which to measure it. No internal mental state can provide this fixed point (it too would be in flux). So temporal experience requires something permanent in outer experience. Consciousness of my own existence in time therefore presupposes the existence of the external world.

## The Limits of Transcendental Arguments

Barry Stroud's influential critique of transcendental arguments (1968) identified a persistent problem: transcendental arguments at best establish that we must *believe* or *conceive* something to be the case, not that it actually is the case.

Even if Strawson is right that we must believe in other minds as a precondition of self-knowledge, this only shows that we cannot coherently doubt other minds — not that other minds exist. The verification principle connects belief-necessity to fact-necessity only on anti-realist assumptions: if what it is for other minds to exist just is for us to have justified beliefs in them, then establishing the necessary belief establishes the fact. But the skeptic rejects this anti-realist move.

Transcendental arguments thus seem to require either anti-realism (which the skeptic may reject) or a substantive epistemological claim (that we must believe X) that doesn't establish the metaphysical claim (that X is true). Stroud's critique remains the central challenge for this approach.
