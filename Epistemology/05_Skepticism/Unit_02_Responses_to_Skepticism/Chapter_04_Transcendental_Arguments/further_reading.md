# Further Reading: Transcendental Arguments

## Primary Sources

- Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, trans. Norman Kemp Smith, Macmillan, 1929. The source of transcendental argumentation: the "Transcendental Deduction" and "Refutation of Idealism" argue that the conditions for the possibility of experience entail the existence of an objective external world and the permanence of substance.

- P.F. Strawson, *Individuals: An Essay in Descriptive Metaphysics*, Methuen, 1959. Contains Strawson's transcendental argument against private languages and for the necessity of a framework of objective physical objects and persons, influencing the interpretation of Kant and the development of transcendental methodology.

- P.F. Strawson, *The Bounds of Sense: An Essay on Kant's Critique of Pure Reason*, Methuen, 1966. An influential reconstruction of Kant's transcendental arguments, stripping them of idealist commitments and extracting a core argument about the conditions of possible experience.

- Wittgenstein, Ludwig, *On Certainty*, trans. Anscombe and von Wright, Blackwell, 1969. Contains what has been called a Wittgensteinian transcendental argument: the conditions for the possibility of doubt presuppose a framework of accepted certainties (hinge propositions), so radical doubt is incoherent.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Barry Stroud, "Transcendental Arguments," *Journal of Philosophy* 65, 1968, pp. 241–256. The classic critical examination of transcendental arguments, arguing that they typically require a "verificationist premise" (that what must be thought of as existing must exist) that is not independently established.

- Ralph Walker, *Kant: The Arguments of the Philosophers*, Routledge, 1978. A clear philosophical analysis of Kant's transcendental arguments, situating them within his critical philosophy and assessing their relationship to anti-skeptical aims.

- Robert Stern, ed., *Transcendental Arguments: Problems and Prospects*, Oxford University Press, 1999. An anthology of contemporary papers on transcendental arguments, including contributions on their logical structure, scope, and philosophical legitimacy.

- Robert Stern, *Transcendental Arguments and Scepticism*, Oxford University Press, 2000. A comprehensive book-length treatment arguing that transcendental arguments cannot by themselves refute skepticism but have a more modest but legitimate diagnostic role.

- Beatrice Han-Pile, "Transcendental Arguments and Constitutivism," *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society* 109, 2009, pp. 109–132. A recent examination of the relationship between transcendental arguments and constitutivism about the conditions of rational agency.

- Charles Taylor, "The Validity of Transcendental Arguments," *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society* 79, 1978, pp. 151–165. Defends transcendental arguments against Stroud's critique, arguing that the verificationist premise can be replaced by a weaker premise about the conditions of self-understanding.

- Quassim Cassam, *Self and World*, Oxford University Press, 1997. Develops transcendental arguments for the existence of physical objects, connecting Kantian and Strawsonian concerns with contemporary debates about self-consciousness and its conditions.

- Crispin Wright, "Wittgenstein's Later Philosophy of Mind: Sensation, Privacy and Intention," *Journal of Philosophy* 86, 1989, pp. 622–634. Examines Wittgensteinian arguments against privacy in mental states — a form of transcendental argument about the conditions for meaningful thought.

- Adrian Moore, *The Evolution of Modern Metaphysics*, Cambridge University Press, 2012. Chapter 6 provides an excellent account of Kant's transcendental project and how it has been developed by subsequent philosophers.

- Paul Franks, *All or Nothing: Systematicity, Transcendental Arguments, and Skepticism in German Idealism*, Harvard University Press, 2005. Traces the development of transcendental arguments from Kant through Fichte and Hegel, with attention to their anti-skeptical purposes.

- Patricia Kitcher, *Kant's Transcendental Psychology*, Oxford University Press, 1990. Examines the psychological dimension of Kant's transcendental arguments, arguing that they are best understood as claims about the structure of rational cognition.

- Jim Conant, "The Method of the Tractatus," in *From Frege to Wittgenstein*, ed. Reck, Oxford University Press, 2002. Relevant for understanding the Wittgensteinian form of transcendental argument and how it differs from Kantian versions.

- Stroud, Barry, "The Goal of Transcendental Arguments," in *Transcendental Arguments*, eds. Stern, 1999. Revisits and refines Stroud's 1968 critique with attention to subsequent defenses of transcendental methodology.

- Moltke Gram, "Things in Themselves: The Historical Lessons," *Journal of the History of Philosophy* 18, 1980, pp. 407–431. Background on Kant's noumenal/phenomenal distinction and its relationship to the anti-skeptical aims of the transcendental project.
