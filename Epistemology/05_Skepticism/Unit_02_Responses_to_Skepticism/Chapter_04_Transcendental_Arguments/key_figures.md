# Key Figures: Transcendental Arguments

## Immanuel Kant (1724–1804)

Kant is the originator of transcendental argumentation. His "Refutation of Idealism" in the *Critique of Pure Reason* argues that the existence of an external world is a condition of the possibility of our having determinate temporal experience: in order to locate my inner states in time, I must be able to compare them to outer, persistent substances. Hence the persistent external world is not an inference from inner experience but a presupposition of that experience having the structure it does. More broadly, Kant's "Transcendental Deduction" argues that the categories of pure understanding (causality, substance, etc.) are necessary conditions for experience in general — not inductively derived from experience but constitutively prior to it. These transcendental arguments are meant to refute Humean skepticism about causality and idealist skepticism about the external world simultaneously.

## P.F. Strawson (1919–2006)

Strawson developed transcendental arguments as a central tool of analytic philosophy. In *Individuals* (1959) he argues that a framework of spatiotemporally continuous physical objects and persons is a necessary condition for objective thought and experience — any thinking being that represents a world at all must employ this framework. In *The Bounds of Sense* (1966), he reconstructs Kant's transcendental arguments while trying to extract them from their idealist presuppositions, arguing that the core insight — that certain structures are necessary conditions for experience — survives even without Kant's commitment to transcendental idealism. Strawson's work made transcendental methodology central to analytic metaphysics and epistemology.

## Barry Stroud (1935–2019)

Stroud is the most important critic of transcendental arguments as a response to skepticism. In his 1968 paper "Transcendental Arguments," he argues that transcendental arguments typically reach their anti-skeptical conclusions only by presupposing a verificationist principle — roughly, that if we must *think of* something as existing, it must exist. Without this premise, the argument shows only that we must *conceptualize* the world as containing external objects, not that external objects actually exist. This is a form of "idealist" rather than "realist" transcendental argument, and it would leave the skeptic's challenge intact at the level of realism. Stroud's critique has defined the agenda for subsequent defenses of transcendental methodology.

## Charles Taylor (b. 1931)

Taylor's defense of transcendental arguments against Stroud's critique argues that the verificationist premise can be replaced by a weaker premise about the conditions of self-understanding. In "The Validity of Transcendental Arguments" (1978), Taylor contends that transcendental arguments show not just that we must *think* of ourselves as in a world of objects, but that this self-understanding is constitutive of what we are — and that to deny it is not just incorrect but self-undermining. This move attempts to give transcendental arguments real force against skepticism without presupposing verificationism. Taylor's approach has been particularly influential in connecting transcendental methodology to debates about self-consciousness and personal identity.

## Quassim Cassam (b. 1961)

Cassam's *Self and World* (1997) develops a neo-Kantian transcendental argument for the existence of physical objects from the conditions of self-consciousness. He argues that any being that represents itself as a subject of experience must represent itself as located in a world of physical objects — that genuine self-consciousness requires perception of oneself as a physical object in a world of physical objects. This connects Strawsonian arguments about objective reference with contemporary debates about self-consciousness and bodily self-awareness. Cassam's transcendental argument is notable for being more specific and empirically tractable than Kantian predecessors, grounding the transcendental claim in facts about the structure of self-consciousness rather than in claims about the conditions of experience in general.

## Crispin Wright (b. 1942)

Wright's contribution to the transcendental argument tradition concerns the concept of "entitlement" — a kind of rational permission to accept certain foundational propositions without positive justification. In "Warrant for Nothing (and Foundations for Free)?" (2004) and related work, he argues that certain presuppositions of inquiry — including that we are not radically deceived — are such that doubting them is a condition of the impossibility of inquiry. We are *entitled* to accept them because rejecting them would undermine the very practices through which justification is sought. Wright's account is a form of transcendental argument in the broad sense: it argues from the conditions of the possibility of rational inquiry to the rational acceptability of certain foundational propositions.

## Robert Stern (b. 1962)

Stern's *Transcendental Arguments and Scepticism* (2000) is the most comprehensive recent treatment of transcendental arguments in the epistemological literature. He argues, against optimistic interpretations, that transcendental arguments cannot refute skepticism because they can show at most that we must *believe* or *conceptualize* certain things, not that those things are actually the case. His more modest assessment is that transcendental arguments have a legitimate diagnostic role: they can show that certain commitments are presupposed by our epistemic practices, making visible the structure of what we are committed to in inquiry. This is valuable even if it does not refute the skeptic.
