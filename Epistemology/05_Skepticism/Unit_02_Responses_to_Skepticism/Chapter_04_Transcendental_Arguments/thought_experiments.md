# Thought Experiments: Transcendental Arguments

## Kant's Time-Determiner

**The scenario:** Imagine trying to locate a sensation — a particular feeling of warmth — in time. You remember feeling warm yesterday and feeling cool this morning. The sensation of warmth is now past. To locate it in time (yesterday, not this morning), you must be able to compare it to other experiences and to something that *persists* through all these experiences. What persists? Not your inner states — those change. Not another inner state — that changes too. For temporal determination of inner states to be possible, there must be something *outer* and *permanent* against which inner states can be located.

**The philosophical point:** This is Kant's "Refutation of Idealism" in compressed form. Kant argues that in order to have *determinate temporal experience* — to locate mental events in an ordered time-series — one must have access to persisting outer objects, since inner states alone have no fixed temporal landmarks. The argument's form is transcendental: from the fact that we do have temporally ordered experience (a fact the skeptic does not deny), we can infer as a condition of its possibility that there is a persisting external world. If the argument succeeds, it turns the skeptic's own premise (we have experience) into evidence against the skeptical conclusion (there is no external world), making skepticism self-undermining.

## Strawson's Objective World

**The scenario:** Imagine a being that has only sounds — a stream of auditory experience with no persistent objects, no spatial framework, no re-identification of particular things. Can this being represent an *objective* world — one that exists independently of its experiences? It seems not: without the concept of persistence and re-identifiability across time, there is no room for a distinction between "how things seem to me" and "how things are." Now imagine giving this being a framework of persistent physical objects — suddenly the distinction between appearance and reality becomes available.

**The philosophical point:** Strawson's transcendental argument from *Individuals* argues that a framework of re-identifiable physical objects is a necessary condition for objective thought. If you lack such a framework, you cannot represent a world distinct from your own experiences — which means you cannot have the concept of experience *as experience* (as distinct from reality). The thought experiment suggests that the skeptic who doubts the external world is in a performative contradiction: the very capacity to doubt presupposes a conceptual framework of objective physical things. Stroud's objection is that this shows only that we must *think* in terms of physical objects, not that physical objects actually exist.

## The Other Minds Transcendental Argument

**The scenario:** Consider a philosophically minded being who has been isolated since infancy — raised in a closed room with no exposure to other human beings. Could this being develop genuine first-person self-awareness — the concept of itself as a *subject* — without any exposure to other minds? Intuitively, something seems missing: the very concept of oneself as a subject, as opposed to merely a stream of experiences, seems to require a contrast with other subjects. Without encountering other persons, the isolated being would have experiences but might never constitute itself as *the subject* of those experiences.

**The philosophical point:** This case is the basis for a transcendental argument against skepticism about other minds. If self-consciousness — which the skeptic cannot deny having — is a condition of the possibility of genuine thought, and if self-consciousness requires the concept of other selves (a contrast class), then the existence of other minds is a presupposition of the very capacity to entertain skeptical thoughts. This is a form of Strawsonian transcendental argument: from the conditions of what is not doubted (rational self-consciousness) to the conditions of what is doubted (other minds). The argument's force depends on whether the concept of other selves must be acquired by genuinely encountering other selves, or whether some more minimal conceptual exposure would suffice.

## Stroud's Challenge: What Is the Gap?

**The scenario:** A philosopher runs the following transcendental argument: "I must think of myself as located in a world of persisting physical objects; therefore there are persisting physical objects." The skeptic responds: "You have shown that I *must conceptualize* the world as containing physical objects. But you have not shown that those objects exist. I am a brain in a vat — I also must conceptualize my experiences as representing physical objects, but they don't. Your argument only shows something about the structure of my thought, not about the world."

**The philosophical point:** Stroud's challenge identifies the gap in transcendental arguments: they bridge from claims about the necessary structure of thought to claims about what must exist, and this bridge requires a verificationist or idealist premise — that what must be thought of as existing *is* existing. This premise is precisely what the skeptic denies. The thought experiment shows that transcendental arguments, as typically formulated, face the same general problem as other a priori responses to skepticism: they can show that our conceptual scheme is structured in a certain way without thereby showing that the world must conform to that scheme. Defenders of transcendental arguments (Taylor, Wright) respond by arguing that the conditions of *meaningful thought* are stronger than mere conditions of *conceptual structure* — but articulating this difference precisely is the central task of transcendental methodology.

## Wittgenstein's Doubting Doubter

**The scenario:** A philosopher attempts to doubt everything, including the presuppositions of inquiry. She tries to doubt that inference is reliable, that memory is broadly trustworthy, that the words she uses have the meanings she thinks they have. She finds that the very act of formulating a doubt — saying to herself "perhaps inference is unreliable" — presupposes that her inference from evidence to doubt is reliable, that her memory of the relevant considerations is correct, and that the words she uses have their intended meanings. The attempt to doubt the presuppositions of inquiry uses those very presuppositions.

**The philosophical point:** This Wittgensteinian-style transcendental argument shows that radical doubt is self-undermining: it presupposes the very conditions it attempts to call into question. The argument does not positively prove that inference is reliable or that memory is trustworthy; it shows that doubting them is incoherent in a performative sense. Wittgenstein's point in *On Certainty* is not that we *know* hinge propositions to be true, but that doubting them falls outside the bounds of intelligible epistemic practice. The thought experiment connects to the question of whether the "conditions of possibility of doubt" can serve as a response to skepticism or whether, as Stroud worries, they merely reveal that skepticism is idle rather than proving it is false.
