# Inference to the Best Explanation Against Skepticism

One of the most natural responses to skepticism appeals to *inference to the best explanation* (IBE): the hypothesis that there is an external world of physical objects explains our experience far better than skeptical alternatives (evil demon, BIV, dream), and we are entitled to believe the best explanation of our evidence. This response has considerable intuitive appeal and connects to broader debates about scientific realism.

## IBE as an Epistemic Principle

Inference to the best explanation (sometimes called *abduction*, after Peirce) is the principle that when one hypothesis explains some body of evidence better than available alternatives, we have reason to believe that hypothesis. The considerations that make an explanation better include: simplicity (fewer unexplained posits), coherence with background knowledge, explanatory power (accounting for more data), predictive success (enabling accurate predictions), and non-ad-hoc character (not specifically designed to fit the data without independent motivation).

IBE is pervasive in both ordinary and scientific reasoning. We infer that the footprints in the snow were made by a person (not teleported there) because that explanation is simpler and more coherent. Scientists infer that viruses are real because the viral theory explains disease patterns far better than alternatives.

## The Application to Skepticism

The application to skepticism runs as follows: Our experience is extensive, interconnected, and exhibits remarkable regularities. Things behave predictably; expectations are met; communication works; actions have foreseeable consequences. The hypothesis that there is an external physical world of objects causally interacting with our sensory systems explains all of this beautifully.

The skeptical alternatives — evil demon, BIV, dream — explain the evidence far worse:

**The evil demon**: The demon must be stipulated to systematically produce all the regularities and interconnections of our experience. This is an ad-hoc, maximally complex hypothesis: the demon needs to simulate not just our experiences but the entire coherent world. By contrast, the external world hypothesis explains the coherence and regularity of experience naturally.

**The BIV**: Similar complexity: the computer simulation must be stipulated to generate exactly the kind of coherent, law-governed experience we have. This is a less parsimonious hypothesis than just positing the world our experience seems to be about.

**The dream**: Dreams are typically incoherent, fail to obey natural laws, and don't exhibit the predictive structure of waking experience. The hypothesis that current experience is a dream explains the data worse, not better.

On these grounds, the external world hypothesis wins out: it's simpler, more coherent, and better supported by standard canons of explanatory goodness.

## Jonathan Vogel's Defense

Jonathan Vogel has developed this response most carefully. He argues that IBE gives us a path to knowledge of the external world that bypasses the Cartesian circle problem. We don't need to first validate our cognitive faculties and then use them; we just need to identify the best explanation of our evidence. The best explanation is the external world hypothesis, and so we are entitled to believe it.

Vogel also addresses the coherent-dream and coherent-BIV objections: of course, we can stipulate that a dream or simulation is maximally coherent. But stipulated coherence is different from natural coherence. The external world hypothesis predicts coherence as a consequence of natural law; the dream/BIV hypotheses must add coherence as an extra stipulation.

## Objections

**The problem of meta-coherence**: The skeptic can insist that IBE itself is not validated — why should we trust IBE? If our faculties are unreliable, IBE reasoning is just another faculty that might be misleading us. The BIV or demon could be making IBE reasoning seem compelling when it isn't.

**Begging the question**: Critics argue that appealing to simplicity, naturalness, and explanatory power already presupposes realist background assumptions. What makes external-world hypotheses simpler is only apparent against a background where physical objects exist. The skeptic can deny this background.

**Coherent skeptical hypotheses**: Sophisticated skeptical hypotheses (like a simulation run by an advanced civilization) may be designed to be maximally similar to the external world hypothesis in explanatory power. If the simulation is computationally equivalent to the physical world, what does IBE recommend?

**The pragmatic turn**: Even if IBE supports the external world hypothesis over skeptical alternatives, this might be because IBE is calibrated to generate useful beliefs for creatures like us, not because it tracks truth. The skeptic can challenge whether IBE-justified beliefs are true, not just useful.
