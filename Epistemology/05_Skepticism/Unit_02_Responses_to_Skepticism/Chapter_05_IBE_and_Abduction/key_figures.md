# Key Figures: IBE and Abduction Against Skepticism

## C.S. Peirce (1839–1914)

Peirce coined the term "abduction" for the distinctive form of reasoning that infers to the best explanation of surprising facts. He distinguished abduction from both deduction (which proves conclusions from premises) and induction (which generalizes from cases) as the creative, hypothesis-generating mode of inference. Abduction has the form: "The surprising fact C is observed; but if H were true, C would be a matter of course; hence there is reason to suspect H is true." Peirce connected abduction to scientific discovery — it is the logic of hypothesis formation — and insisted that while abduction does not prove its conclusions, it identifies the most economical hypothesis worth pursuing. His account is the philosophical ancestor of all subsequent discussions of inference to the best explanation.

## Gilbert Harman (b. 1938)

Harman introduced the phrase "inference to the best explanation" into contemporary philosophy in his 1965 paper. He argued that IBE is a fundamental and irreducible form of rational inference — irreducible to inductive generalization or to Bayesian conditionalization — and that it underlies a wide range of ordinary and scientific reasoning. Harman connected IBE to the epistemology of perception (we infer to the best explanation of our sensory experience), memory, and testimony. His *Thought* (1973) developed these ideas further, arguing that our best account of how humans actually reason vindicates IBE as a basic epistemic norm, which makes it a legitimate tool in responding to skeptical challenges.

## Jonathan Vogel (b. 1954)

Vogel is the philosopher who most explicitly and systematically developed IBE as a response to Cartesian skepticism. In "Cartesian Skepticism and Inference to the Best Explanation" (1990), he argues that the external world hypothesis — that there are mind-independent physical objects that causally produce our experience — is a better explanation of our sensory experience than the evil demon or BIV hypothesis. It is simpler, more unified, more parsimonious, and has greater predictive power. Since IBE is a legitimate epistemic procedure, and since the external world hypothesis is the best explanation, we are justified in accepting it. Vogel's defense has become the standard statement of the IBE response to skepticism, and the objections to it have defined subsequent debate.

## Peter Lipton (1954–2007)

Lipton's *Inference to the Best Explanation* (2004) is the most thorough philosophical analysis of IBE. He distinguishes the "likeliest" explanation from the "loveliest" explanation and argues that IBE is better understood as inference to the loveliest (most explanatorily satisfying) rather than the likeliest explanation — but he also argues for a connection between loveliness and truth-conduciveness. Lipton's treatment of objections to IBE — particularly van Fraassen's challenge that "loveliness" is not a guide to truth — is the most sophisticated available. His work is essential for understanding what the IBE response to skepticism is committed to and where it is most vulnerable.

## Bas van Fraassen (b. 1941)

Van Fraassen is the most influential critic of IBE. In *Laws and Symmetry* (1989), he argues that there is no good reason to think that explanatory virtues — simplicity, unity, predictive power — are reliable guides to truth. He develops a probabilistic Dutch book argument against IBE, showing that an agent who uses IBE as a rule of inference can be made to accept a set of bets they are guaranteed to lose. Van Fraassen's challenge forces defenders of IBE to articulate precisely what the connection between explanatory goodness and truth is. His constructive empiricism — the view that science should aim at empirical adequacy rather than truth — is a sophisticated alternative to the realist IBE response to scientific underdetermination.

## Michael Huemer (b. 1969)

Huemer's "phenomenal conservatism" offers an alternative to IBE as a response to skepticism. He argues that if something *seems* true to you, that seeming itself provides prima facie justification for believing it, without any need for inference to the best explanation. Huemer's direct realism is presented partly as a response to the worry that IBE-based anti-skeptical arguments are question-begging: if the justification for the external world hypothesis depends on IBE, and if IBE is itself an inference from experience, the justification runs in a circle. Phenomenal conservatism short-circuits this by granting immediate justification to whatever seems to be the case — including that there is an external world — without requiring any inferential step.

## Ted Poston (b. c. 1974)

Poston's *Reason and Explanation* (2014) develops "explanatory coherentism" — the view that justification is fundamentally a matter of a belief's fitting within the most explanatorily coherent total system of beliefs. This view incorporates IBE as a core epistemic norm within a coherentist framework, arguing that what makes a belief system justified is not merely logical consistency but its explanatory integration. Poston applies this framework to skepticism, arguing that our total belief system (including beliefs about the external world) is more explanatorily coherent than any skeptical alternative, making the skeptic's hypothesis not merely less probable but epistemically less well-supported in the sense that matters for justification.
