# Thought Experiments: IBE and Abduction Against Skepticism

## The Fossil Record

**The scenario:** A paleontologist excavates a site and discovers a stratum of rock containing fossilized bones arranged in a pattern consistent with a predator-prey interaction. She infers that two dinosaurs of specific species interacted at this location approximately 75 million years ago. She was not there; she cannot directly observe the past event; there are alternative explanations (the bones were deposited by water flow, for example). But the predator-prey interaction hypothesis is the most parsimonious, most detailed, and most coherent with background knowledge. Is she justified in her inference?

**The philosophical point:** This case illustrates IBE in its most natural scientific context. The paleontologist's inference is clearly rational, and its rationality cannot be reduced to deduction or simple induction: it depends on selecting the hypothesis that best explains the total evidence pattern. If IBE is legitimate here — as it clearly seems to be — it should be legitimate when applied to the external world hypothesis as an explanation of our sensory experience. Vogel's anti-skeptical argument proceeds by analogy: just as the predator-prey hypothesis is the best explanation of the fossil evidence, the external world hypothesis is the best explanation of our coordinated, coherent sensory experience. The case motivates the view that IBE is a legitimate and foundational form of rational inference.

## Vogel's External World as Best Explanation

**The scenario:** Consider two competing hypotheses about the source of your current sensory experience. Hypothesis A: There is an external world of physical objects and events that causally produce your experiences through reliable sensory processes. Hypothesis B: An evil demon (or sophisticated computer) produces in your brain exactly the experiences you would have if Hypothesis A were true, while the underlying reality is entirely different. Both hypotheses explain all your experiences equally well, in the sense that neither predicts experiential events the other cannot accommodate. Which is the better explanation?

**The philosophical point:** Vogel argues that Hypothesis A is the better explanation on multiple grounds: it is simpler (it does not posit an additional explanatory entity — the demon — beyond what is needed), it is more unified (it explains sensory experiences as products of a single coherent causal structure rather than as outputs of an additional manipulating agent), and it has independent support (whereas the demon hypothesis is postulated solely to undermine our beliefs). IBE licenses belief in the better hypothesis, so we are justified in believing the external world exists. Van Fraassen's objection is that "simpler" and "more unified" are pragmatic virtues, not truth-indicators; the demon hypothesis, while less elegant, may be just as likely to be true.

## The Question-Begging Challenge

**The scenario:** A philosopher argues that the IBE response to skepticism is question-begging: it assumes that IBE is a reliable form of inference, but a skeptic who doubts that our faculties reliably track the world will also doubt that our capacity for explanatory evaluation reliably tracks which explanations are true. If all our cognitive faculties are unreliable (demon world), then so is our sense of which explanations are "better." Therefore, the IBE response presupposes the reliability of a faculty the skeptic is challenging.

**The philosophical point:** This is the strongest objection to the IBE response to skepticism. It parallels the skeptical challenge to reliabilism: any response that appeals to our cognitive faculties to vindicate those faculties runs in a circle. The IBE defender has several responses. First, the circularity may be virtuous (system-level) rather than vicious (local): we are using coherent reasoning, not a single premise, to support our epistemic practices. Second, any response to skepticism must begin somewhere, and IBE begins at a more basic epistemic level than most alternatives. Third, the skeptic's challenge is itself constructed using the inferential norms it challenges — making the skeptical argument, if anything, more self-undermining than the IBE response.

## The Coherent Skeptical Hypothesis

**The scenario:** A determined anti-IBE philosopher constructs a skeptical hypothesis that is, by every measure of explanatory virtue, as good as the external world hypothesis. Suppose the demon not only produces coherent experiences but has a rich theoretical structure — a complete "demon physics" with its own laws, predictive power, and unifying principles. This demon hypothesis is just as simple, just as unified, and just as powerful as the external world hypothesis. If such a coherent skeptical alternative exists, does IBE fail to rule it out?

**The philosophical point:** This is the "coherent skeptical hypothesis" objection to Vogel's argument. If a well-constructed skeptical hypothesis can match the external world hypothesis on every dimension of explanatory virtue, IBE cannot adjudicate between them — and we are back to an underdetermination situation. Vogel's response is to argue that the demon hypothesis is not genuinely simpler or more unified: it posits an additional entity (the demon) with miraculous properties (the ability to generate perfectly coherent experiences indistinguishable from perception of a physical world) and lacks any independent theoretical support. But critics argue that this response implicitly appeals to background theoretical preferences that are not themselves explicable by IBE, revealing a meta-coherence assumption that IBE cannot itself justify.

## Harman's Perceptual IBE

**The scenario:** You see what appears to be a red tomato on the kitchen counter. You are having a vivid, round, red sensory experience. You believe there is a red tomato on the counter. Is this belief the product of IBE — an inference from the sensory experience to the best explanation of that experience (there is a tomato causing it)? Or is it a direct perceptual response, without any inferential mediation?

**The philosophical point:** Harman argued that ordinary perceptual belief involves implicit IBE: the belief that there is a tomato is the conclusion of a (typically unconscious) inference to the best explanation of the sensory experience. This view makes IBE fundamental to ordinary cognition, not just to sophisticated theoretical reasoning. Critics (including direct realists like Huemer) argue that this intellectualizes perception: what happens when you see a tomato is not inference but direct perceptual contact with the object. The disagreement connects to debates about the structure of perceptual justification and whether foundationalism or IBE-based coherentism provides the correct account. If Harman is right, the IBE response to skepticism is not a special philosophical move but a description of how ordinary perception works — making it more natural but also more vulnerable to the question-begging objection.
