# Fallibilism About Knowledge

Fallibilism is the view that knowledge does not require certainty. A person can know that P even if it is possible, in some sense, that P is false — even if their grounds for P don't guarantee P's truth. Fallibilism is now the dominant position in mainstream epistemology, largely because infallibilism (the view that knowledge requires certainty) seems to leave us with almost no knowledge at all.

## The Infallibilist Standard

The history of epistemology, especially in the Cartesian tradition, has often been hospitable to infallibilism. If knowledge requires certainty — if to know P you must have grounds that make error impossible — then the scope of human knowledge is very limited. Mathematical truths, basic logical tautologies, and perhaps immediate present experience are the obvious candidates. Everything else — empirical science, the past, the future, other minds, most of what we ordinarily claim to know — falls short of certainty.

Descartes's method of doubt is implicitly infallibilist: he sets aside everything that admits of any possible doubt, in search of something genuinely certain. The skeptical scenarios he deploys are designed to show that ordinary belief doesn't meet the infallibilist standard.

## Fallibilism Stated

Fallibilism holds that the connection between justified grounds and truth can be "fallible" — a person can have good grounds for P, grounds that make P very likely, without those grounds guaranteeing P. Knowledge is compatible with the epistemic possibility of error, as long as the actual grounds are appropriately strong.

This captures how we actually think and talk about knowledge. A doctor who carefully examines a patient, considers all the symptoms, and diagnoses correctly *knows* the diagnosis — even though it is possible, in some abstract sense, that the diagnosis is wrong. A historian who carefully studies the evidence *knows* what happened at a battle — even though it is conceivable (though unlikely) that all the evidence is misleading.

## The Lottery Problem

Fallibilism faces a challenging puzzle from the lottery. Suppose you hold a ticket in a fair lottery with a million tickets. You know that the probability of your ticket winning is one in a million. Can you know, before the draw, that your ticket will not win?

The case for knowledge: your grounds (a one-in-a-million chance) strongly support the belief that you won't win. On any reasonable probabilistic account, a credence of 999,999/1,000,000 counts as very strong justification.

The case against: knowing that your ticket will not win seems wrong. If someone tells you "don't worry, you know your ticket won't win" before the draw, something seems off.

The lottery problem suggests that knowledge cannot be grounded solely in high probability. Some additional ingredient seems required — perhaps safety (your belief couldn't easily have been false), or proper basing (your belief is connected to your evidence in the right way), or ruling out relevant alternatives.

## Fallibilism and Skepticism

Fallibilism is partly motivated by its handling of skepticism. If knowledge doesn't require certainty, then the fact that you can't rule out BIV scenarios with certainty doesn't show that you lack knowledge. Your grounds for ordinary beliefs — perception, memory, inference — give you very strong (though fallible) support. That's enough for knowledge.

But the skeptic can reformulate: even fallible knowledge requires that the belief be *properly based* on evidence, that the relevant alternatives be *ruled out* (in a relevant-alternatives sense), that the belief be *safe* (couldn't easily be false). These requirements can be challenged by skeptical scenarios even on a fallibilist framework.

## The Problem of Easy Knowledge

A challenge for fallibilism identified by Stewart Cohen is the *problem of easy knowledge*. If fallibilism allows that perceptual beliefs can constitute knowledge despite the possibility of perceptual error, it seems to allow very easy knowledge — knowledge that can be bootstrapped without additional effort.

For instance: on a fallibilist account, I can know (fallibly) that the table is red by looking at it. I can then know that my color perception is reliable by observing that it consistently produces true beliefs about the table's color (I believe it's red, and it is red). But this seems to generate knowledge of perceptual reliability without any independent check — just by using the very faculty in question. Easy knowledge of the reliability of one's faculties seems wrong.

The problem of easy knowledge suggests that fallibilism needs to be carefully calibrated: it must avoid both the infallibilist excess (too demanding) and the fallibilist excess (too permissive).
