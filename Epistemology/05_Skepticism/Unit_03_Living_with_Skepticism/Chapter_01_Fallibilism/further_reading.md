# Further Reading: Fallibilism

## Primary Sources

- C.S. Peirce, "The Fixation of Belief," *Popular Science Monthly* 12, 1877, pp. 1–15. The locus classicus of pragmatist fallibilism: inquiry aims at stable belief, and fallibilism is essential to the self-correcting character of scientific inquiry.

- John Dewey, *The Quest for Certainty*, Minton, Balch, 1929. A sustained critique of the "quest for certainty" as a philosophical illusion and a defense of fallibilism as the appropriate epistemic attitude for all inquiry.

- Willard Quine, "Two Dogmas of Empiricism," *Philosophical Review* 60, 1951, pp. 20–43. Defends a web-of-belief fallibilism in which no belief (not even logic) is absolutely certain and revision of any part of the web is in principle possible.

- Stewart Cohen, "How to Be a Fallibilist," *Philosophical Perspectives* 2, 1988, pp. 581–605. Defends fallibilism against the charge that it is epistemically self-undermining and develops a fallibilist account that accommodates the lottery problem.

## Key Secondary Sources

- John Hawthorne, *Knowledge and Lotteries*, Oxford University Press, 2004. A wide-ranging examination of the lottery problem, its implications for fallibilism, and the connections between knowledge, assertion, and practical reasoning.

- Keith DeRose, "The Case for Contextualism," in *The Case for Contextualism*, Oxford University Press, 2009. Relevant for the connection between fallibilism and contextualism, examining how varying standards for knowledge relate to fallibilist commitments.

- Timothy Williamson, *Knowledge and Its Limits*, Oxford University Press, 2000. Chapters 1–2 examine the relationship between fallibilism and the concept of knowledge, arguing that knowledge is not to be analyzed in terms of certainty even while being treated as the norm of assertion.

- Peter Unger, *Ignorance: A Case for Scepticism*, Oxford University Press, 1975. The case for infallibilism as leading to skepticism: if knowledge requires certainty, then almost nothing is known — making infallibilism a powerful support for the skeptic.

- Jonathan Sutton, *Without Justification*, MIT Press, 2007. Defends an infallibilist knowledge norm (knowledge requires truth) while arguing that justification is not a necessary condition for knowledge, complicating the fallibilism/infallibilism debate.

- Clayton Littlejohn, *Justification and the Truth-Connection*, Cambridge University Press, 2012. Develops a factive account of justification that raises questions about whether standard fallibilism can accommodate the connection between justification and truth.

- Jeremy Fantl and Matthew McGrath, *Knowledge in an Uncertain World*, Oxford University Press, 2009. Argues for "fallibilism about knowledge" combined with "pragmatic encroachment" — the view that practical stakes affect whether one knows — a position that mediates between pure fallibilism and infallibilism.

- Duncan Pritchard, *Epistemic Luck*, Oxford University Press, 2005. Chapters 6–7 examine how fallibilism relates to epistemic luck and whether the safety condition on knowledge is compatible with fallibilism.

- Jonathan Ichikawa and Matthias Steup, "The Analysis of Knowledge," *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, 2018 ed. Surveys the relationship between fallibilism, the analysis of knowledge, and the Gettier problem.

- Richard Foley, *Working Without a Net: A Study of Egocentric Epistemology*, Oxford University Press, 1993. Develops a fallibilist epistemology grounded in the first-person perspective, arguing that self-critical rationality is the appropriate response to fallibility.

- Brian Weatherson, "Knowledge, Bets, and Interests," in *Epistemic Justification and the Skeptical Threat*, ed. Brown and Gerken, Oxford University Press, 2012. Examines how practical stakes interact with knowledge ascription in a fallibilist framework.

- Roger White, "Problems for Dogmatism," *Philosophical Studies* 131, 2006, pp. 525–557. Examines Pryor's dogmatist fallibilism and presses objections about the relationship between certainty, perceptual justification, and the lottery paradox.

- Mark Kaplan, "It's Not What You Know That Counts," *Journal of Philosophy* 82, 1985, pp. 350–363. A defense of fallibilism against the view that knowledge requires certainty, arguing that probability-relative justification is sufficient for knowledge.

- Jonathan Vogel, "The Lottery Paradox Revisited," *Philosophical Issues* 5, 1994, pp. 33–40. A careful reexamination of the lottery problem and its implications for fallibilism, with attention to the role of safety and sensitivity conditions.
