# Key Figures: Fallibilism

## C.S. Peirce (1839–1914)

Peirce is the founder of the fallibilist tradition in epistemology. His "Fixation of Belief" (1877) and "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" (1878) argue that inquiry is a self-correcting process that aims at stable belief, and that acknowledging the fallibility of any particular belief is essential to the health of that process. Peirce distinguished between the working certainty that allows us to get on with inquiry and the absolute, revisability-proof certainty that no inquiry can actually achieve. His fallibilism extends to all domains including mathematics and logic: even the most certain-seeming results of formal reasoning are, in principle, open to revision in light of new insight. Peirce connected fallibilism to his broader pragmatism — the meaning of a belief is its practical consequences, and beliefs that we treat as practically certain may nonetheless be theoretically revisable.

## John Dewey (1859–1952)

Dewey's *The Quest for Certainty* (1929) is the sustained pragmatist critique of the philosophical tradition's obsession with absolute certainty. Dewey argues that the quest for certainty is a response to anxiety about an uncertain world and that it has systematically distorted philosophical inquiry by setting an impossible standard. His positive account — "experimental inquiry" — takes fallibilism as its methodological foundation: we begin from problematic situations, form hypotheses, test them, and revise them in light of outcomes. Knowledge for Dewey is not the achievement of certainty but the development of increasingly reliable and fruitful habits of inquiry. Dewey's fallibilism connects to his broader philosophical naturalism: epistemology should be continuous with empirical science rather than standing above it.

## Stewart Cohen (b. 1954)

Cohen's "How to Be a Fallibilist" (1988) is the most systematic contemporary defense of fallibilism as a response to skepticism. He argues that fallibilism — the view that knowledge does not require certainty or the ability to rule out all possible alternatives — is the correct response to the skeptic's challenge. The skeptic exploits the fact that we cannot eliminate all error possibilities to argue that we don't know; the fallibilist denies that eliminating all possibilities is required for knowledge. Cohen also examines the lottery paradox in this context: if I know I won't win the lottery (because the probability is astronomically low), it seems I should be able to conclude I know I am not a lottery winner, which conflicts with other intuitions. His contextualist resolution of this problem connects fallibilism to his broader contextualist framework.

## John Hawthorne (b. 1967)

Hawthorne's *Knowledge and Lotteries* (2004) examines the lottery problem — if fallibilism is true and knowledge does not require certainty, why don't we know we will lose the lottery? — as the central test case for fallibilist epistemology. He argues that several prominent fallibilist positions (contextualism, relevant alternatives) face serious difficulties with lottery cases and that this calls for a thorough reconsideration of the relationship between knowledge, certainty, and practical reasoning. His interest-relative invariantism — the view that whether a belief counts as knowledge depends on the practical stakes for the believer — is partly motivated by the insight that fallibilism needs to be constrained by practical-epistemic considerations that ordinary fallibilism does not capture.

## Peter Unger (b. 1942)

Unger's *Ignorance: A Case for Scepticism* (1975) defends infallibilism — the view that knowledge requires certainty — as a premise for the skeptical conclusion that we know almost nothing. His argument is instructive for fallibilists: if we accept that ordinary knowledge claims are true, we must accept fallibilism; but if we accept infallibilism, we must accept skepticism. Unger argues that "knows" is an absolute term (like "flat" or "empty") and that absolute terms allow no degrees — you either know (with certainty) or you don't know. Fallibilists respond that "knows" is not an absolute term and that the ordinary use of "knows" is compatible with fallibility. Unger's challenge has forced fallibilists to provide a more precise account of what the "certainty" that knowledge does not require actually amounts to.

## Timothy Williamson (b. 1955)

Williamson's contribution to the fallibilism debate is through his knowledge-first epistemology and his claim that knowledge is the norm of assertion. He defends a fallibilist view of knowledge in the sense that knowledge does not require certainty — one can know without being absolutely certain — but he also argues that knowledge is a factive state (one can know only what is true) and that it is the highest epistemic achievement, not merely justified true belief. His "luminosity" argument — that one is not always in a position to know whether one is in a given mental state — challenges the internalist assumption underlying much fallibilist epistemology and connects the fallibilism debate to broader questions about self-knowledge and the reliability of introspection.

## Jeremy Fantl (b. c. 1971) and Matthew McGrath (b. c. 1968)

Fantl and McGrath's "Knowledge Enough" (2002) and *Knowledge in an Uncertain World* (2009) defend a fallibilist position that incorporates pragmatic encroachment: the standards for knowledge are sensitive to the practical stakes for the believer. Their central claim — "If you know that p, you are rational to act on p" — is connected to fallibilism by the observation that in high-stakes situations, mere probabilistic justification is not sufficient for the kind of knowledge that rationalizes action; but in low-stakes situations, lower standards apply. This pragmatic modulation of epistemic standards is a form of fallibilism that takes practical context seriously, making the connection between epistemology and practical rationality central to the analysis of knowledge.
