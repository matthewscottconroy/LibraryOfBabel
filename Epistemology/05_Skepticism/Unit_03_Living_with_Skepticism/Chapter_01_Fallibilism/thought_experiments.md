# Thought Experiments: Fallibilism

## The Lottery Paradox

**The scenario:** A lottery has one million tickets. You hold ticket number 573,812. You believe you will lose the lottery (the probability is overwhelmingly high: 999,999/1,000,000). You also believe the same of ticket 573,813 — and of every other individual ticket. If you know that each ticket will lose, you can derive by conjunction introduction that all tickets will lose — but you know one ticket must win. Contradiction. So either you don't know any individual ticket will lose, or knowledge does not close under conjunction.

**The philosophical point:** The lottery paradox is the central challenge for fallibilism. If knowledge requires certainty, you clearly don't know your ticket will lose (there is a small chance it won't). If knowledge is compatible with high probability (fallibilism), then you seem to know your ticket will lose — but the paradox follows. Fallibilists must explain why near-certainty (high probability) is not sufficient for knowledge in the lottery case even though it seems sufficient in ordinary cases. Cohen's contextualism holds that in lottery contexts the standards for knowledge are higher than in ordinary life; Hawthorne's interest-relative invariantism holds that knowledge depends on practical stakes; others argue that the safety condition (you could easily have lost by a different mechanism) rules out lottery knowledge. The paradox has no consensus resolution.

## The Easy Knowledge Problem

**The scenario:** Using a reliable thermometer, you determine that the room is 72°F. You thereby come to know, on a fallibilist account, that the room is 72°F. You also know (a priori) that if the thermometer says 72°F and the room is 72°F, then the thermometer is reliable in this instance. So you now know (by closure) that the thermometer is reliable — which was your prior uncertainty that led you to use it in the first place. Have you now "bootstrapped" yourself into knowledge of the thermometer's reliability?

**The philosophical point:** This is the "easy knowledge" problem for fallibilism. If fallibilism allows that using a reliable instrument counts as knowledge even without prior verification of its reliability, then one can apparently come to know the instrument's reliability through a trivially easy inferential step — using the instrument to verify itself. Cohen introduced this problem specifically to show that fallibilism needs constraints beyond mere reliability. The thought experiment forces fallibilists to explain what additional condition on knowledge (sensitivity? safety? no-method circularity?) prevents this bootstrapping inference from being legitimate. The easy knowledge problem connects to broader debates about the structure of self-supporting belief systems and the regress of justification.

## The Safety Condition Applied

**The scenario:** A student guesses at random that the capital of France is Paris. She has no evidence for this; it is a pure guess. As it happens, she guesses correctly. A second student has studied French geography carefully and knows that Paris is the capital of France. Both believe the same true proposition. Does either, or both, know that Paris is the capital of France?

**The philosophical point:** The safety condition on knowledge — one's belief is safe if one could not easily have believed it falsely in similar circumstances — handles this case nicely. The guessing student could easily have guessed wrong: guessing at random is, by definition, a method that produces false beliefs in nearby possible worlds (where she guesses Lyon or Marseille). The knowledgeable student could not easily have believed falsely: her carefully formed belief would not change in nearby worlds where Paris is still the capital. Fallibilism is compatible with the safety condition: the knowledgeable student knows even though she is not absolutely certain. The case illustrates how safety provides the right anti-luck condition that distinguishes genuine knowledge from mere lucky true belief, even within a fallibilist framework.

## The Fallibilist's Assertion

**The scenario:** Dr. Reyes is a fallibilist philosopher who sincerely believes that fallibilism is true — that knowledge does not require certainty. She is giving a lecture and asserts: "Paris is the capital of France." A student asks: "But could you be wrong?" Dr. Reyes must reply yes — fallibilism implies she could always in principle be wrong. "Then how can you assert it?" says the student. "Asserting implies you know, and knowing implies you can't be wrong."

**The philosophical point:** This thought experiment probes the relationship between fallibilism and the norm of assertion. If Williamson's knowledge norm of assertion is correct (assert only what you know), and if fallibilism is true (knowledge is compatible with the possibility of error), then asserting P is compatible with the possibility that P is false. The student's objection assumes an infallibilist norm for assertion — that assertion implies certainty — which fallibilists reject. Fallibilists hold that assertion is governed by a knowledge norm but that knowledge itself is fallible: to assert that Paris is the capital is to claim knowledge (not certainty), and knowledge is compatible with the bare possibility of error. The case illuminates the connection between theories of knowledge and the pragmatics of assertion.

## Sensitivity and the External World

**The scenario:** You believe there is a tree outside your window because you can see it clearly. If the tree were not there, would you still believe it was? In the actual world, no — if the tree were not there, you would see a different scene, and you would not believe there was a tree. Your belief is sensitive: it would not be held falsely in the nearest possible world where it is false. Now suppose you believe you are not a brain in a vat. If you were a brain in a vat, would you still believe you were not? Yes — a BIV would have exactly the same experiences and would believe exactly what you believe. Your belief is not sensitive to the BIV possibility.

**The philosophical point:** Nozick used this contrast to explain, within a fallibilist framework, why you can know there is a tree (your belief is sensitive) but cannot know you are not a BIV (your belief is not sensitive). The thought experiment shows how the sensitivity condition — which is a modal, fallibilist condition — allows us to "solve" the skeptical problem without abandoning either fallibilism or the closure principle (which Nozick does deny). Critics argue that the denial of closure is too high a price. Defenders of sensitivity argue it captures something important about why ordinary knowledge is secure (being wrong about the tree would mean *not seeing it*) while knowledge of not-being-a-BIV is unavailable (being wrong about that wouldn't change any of one's experiences).
