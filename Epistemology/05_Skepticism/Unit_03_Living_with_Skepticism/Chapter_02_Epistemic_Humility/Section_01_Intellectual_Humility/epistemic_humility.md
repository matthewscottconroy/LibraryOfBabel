# Epistemic Humility

Epistemic humility is the recognition that one's knowledge and justified beliefs are limited, fallible, and often subject to revision — and the disposition to hold one's beliefs accordingly, without excessive confidence. It is both a theoretical position about human epistemic limitations and a practical virtue connected to intellectual character.

## Humility as a Response to Skepticism

One way to live with the lessons of skepticism is through epistemic humility. Rather than claiming that the skeptical challenges are decisively refuted, the epistemically humble thinker accepts that there are genuine limits to human knowledge, genuine possibilities of systematic error, and genuine reasons for tentativeness about many beliefs.

This is not the same as endorsing skepticism. The humble thinker does not claim that we know nothing; they claim that we know less than we typically think, that our confidence levels are often miscalibrated, and that our epistemic situation calls for tentativeness rather than dogmatic certainty. This is a kind of *partial* or *local* acceptance of skeptical lessons without global collapse.

## The Two Dimensions of Epistemic Humility

Epistemic humility has two distinct dimensions that are sometimes conflated:

**First-order humility**: Uncertainty about the truth of particular beliefs. An agent with first-order humility about their scientific beliefs acknowledges that current scientific consensus may be wrong about many questions, that future evidence may require revision, and that our current best theories are probably not the final word.

**Reflective humility**: Uncertainty about one's own epistemic faculties and processes. An agent with reflective humility recognizes that their reasoning may be biased, that their intuitions may be unreliable in certain domains, that their assessments of evidence may be distorted by wishful thinking or motivated reasoning.

Reflective humility is connected to the discussions of higher-order evidence: learning about the ways human cognition systematically goes wrong — confirmation bias, the planning fallacy, the Dunning-Kruger effect — is evidence that one's own cognition may be affected by these failures.

## Calibration as Epistemic Ideal

A technical concept from formal epistemology captures one dimension of humility: *calibration*. A thinker is well-calibrated if, for all the propositions they believe with probability p, roughly p-proportion of them are true. A person who is 90% confident in things that turn out to be true only 60% of the time is systematically overconfident — miscalibrated.

Epistemic humility, on this account, involves striving for good calibration: having high confidence only in things that are actually very likely, and moderate confidence in things that are moderately supported. The well-calibrated thinker neither overclaims nor underclaims.

Research in cognitive psychology suggests that most people are systematically overconfident. People are right 70-80% of the time in situations where they report being 90-100% certain. One practical application of epistemic humility is to consciously reduce confidence levels — to assume that your high-confidence beliefs are less reliable than they feel.

## The Social Dimensions of Epistemic Humility

Epistemic humility has important social dimensions:

**Deference to experts**: One application of reflective humility is appropriate deference to people with greater expertise. If I recognize that my judgment in a domain is less reliable than an expert's, humility requires updating toward their judgment. This is not self-effacement but a rational response to higher-order evidence about differential reliability.

**Openness to revision**: A humble thinker is genuinely open to revising their views in light of new evidence or arguments — not just paying lip service to openness while being practically unmovable. This is connected to intellectual open-mindedness as a virtue.

**Epistemic courage and humility**: Epistemic humility is sometimes confused with epistemic cowardice — failing to commit to views because of social pressure, or hedging as a strategy for avoiding criticism. But genuine humility involves committing to views proportional to one's evidence while acknowledging the possibility of error. This can require courage: asserting a view in the face of disagreement while being genuinely open to being wrong.

## Humility and Moral Epistemology

Epistemic humility has distinctive importance in the moral domain. History is replete with cases where confident moral views turned out to be gravely wrong — about slavery, about who counts as a person, about the acceptable treatment of outgroups. This track record is strong evidence for humility about current confident moral views: we may be wrong about moral questions in ways we currently cannot see.

The lesson is not moral relativism but moral fallibilism — the view that moral beliefs are genuine, can be better or worse supported, but can be revised. Epistemic humility supports maintaining moral views while holding them with appropriate tentativeness and genuine openness to argument.
