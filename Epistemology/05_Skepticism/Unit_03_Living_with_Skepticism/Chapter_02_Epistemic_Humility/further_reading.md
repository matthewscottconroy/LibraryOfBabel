# Further Reading: Epistemic Humility

## Primary Sources

- John Stuart Mill, *On Liberty*, ed. John Gray, Oxford University Press, 1991. Chapter II develops the case for freedom of expression partly on epistemically humble grounds: because we cannot be certain our current beliefs are true, silencing dissent destroys the best mechanism for error-correction.

- Charles Sanders Peirce, "The Fixation of Belief," *Popular Science Monthly* 12, 1877. Argues that genuine inquiry requires fallibilism and that epistemic humility — readiness to revise beliefs in light of inquiry — is a precondition for the advancement of knowledge.

- W.K. Clifford, "The Ethics of Belief," in *Lectures and Essays*, Macmillan, 1879. Although an argument for evidentialist rigor, Clifford's ethics of belief implies epistemic humility: we should not hold beliefs more firmly than our evidence warrants.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Nathan Ballantyne, *Knowing Our Limits*, Oxford University Press, 2019. A comprehensive treatment of intellectual limitations and their epistemic implications, defending epistemic humility as both a virtue and a rational response to reliable evidence of human cognitive fallibility.

- Jason Baehr, *The Inquiring Mind*, Oxford University Press, 2011. Contains an important treatment of intellectual humility as an epistemic virtue, distinguishing it from epistemic cowardice and analyzing its relationship to open-mindedness and intellectual courage.

- Robert Roberts and W. Jay Wood, *Intellectual Virtues: An Essay in Regulative Epistemology*, Oxford University Press, 2007. Provides a phenomenologically rich account of intellectual humility as a character virtue, distinguishing proper epistemic self-assessment from false modesty.

- David Dunning, *Self-Insight: Roadblocks and Detours on the Path to Knowing Thyself*, Psychology Press, 2005. Discusses the empirical psychology of self-assessment, including the Dunning-Kruger effect, providing the scientific background for epistemically humble attitudes.

- Quassim Cassam, *Vices of the Mind*, Oxford University Press, 2019. Examines intellectual arrogance — the epistemic vice that epistemic humility directly opposes — with attention to its social and political dimensions.

- Miranda Fricker, *Epistemic Injustice*, Oxford University Press, 2007. Argues that testimonial injustice involves a failure of epistemic humility (inappropriate overcrediting of one's own judgments relative to marginalized speakers) — connecting humility to questions of social justice.

- Holly Sondergaard and Heather Battaly, "Epistemic Virtue and Vice: Reliabilism, Responsibilism, and Personalism," in *Epistemic Virtues and the Good Life*, Cambridge, 2020. Discusses intellectual humility within the virtue-epistemological tradition, examining whether it is a reliabilist or responsibilist virtue.

- Lani Watson, "Intellectual Humility and Epistemic Agency," *Australasian Philosophical Review* 2, 2018, pp. 43–57. Defends intellectual humility as involving an accurate self-assessment of one's intellectual limitations, and connects this to the concept of epistemic agency.

- Michael Lynch, *In Praise of Reason: Why Rationality Matters*, MIT Press, 2012. Defends epistemic humility as essential to democratic self-governance, arguing that public discourse requires participants to model the fallibility of their own commitments.

- Philip Kitcher, *Science in a Democratic Society*, Columbia University Press, 2011. Examines how epistemic humility relates to the appropriate deference to scientific expertise in democratic decision-making, with attention to the social conditions for good epistemic practices.

- Miranda Fricker and Michael Brady, eds., *The Epistemic Life of Groups*, Oxford University Press, 2016. Examines collective epistemic humility — how groups should model their own fallibility — with implications for institutional epistemology.

- Alvin Goldman and Matthew McGrath, *Epistemology: A Contemporary Introduction*, Oxford University Press, 2015. Chapter 12 provides an accessible treatment of epistemic humility and its relationship to skepticism, fallibilism, and peer disagreement.

- David Christensen, "Disagreement as Evidence: The Epistemology of Controversy," *Philosophy Compass* 4, 2009, pp. 756–767. Examines how knowledge of peer disagreement should induce epistemic humility, and whether conciliationism requires unacceptable degrees of self-doubt.

- Kathryn Abramson, "Dissenting in Public Life," in *The Oxford Handbook of Social Epistemology*, ed. Fricker et al., Oxford University Press, 2019. Examines the relationship between intellectual courage, epistemic humility, and the ethics of public disagreement.
