# Essay Questions: Unit 5 — Skepticism

---

**1. The Structure of the Sceptical Argument and Epistemic Closure**

Reconstruct the Cartesian sceptical argument in its most rigorous contemporary form, distinguishing the closure-based form from the underdetermination form. Is the principle of epistemic closure plausible? Assess Nozick's and Dretske's denials of closure. Does the denial of closure provide a satisfying response to scepticism, or does it save knowledge at too high a cost to the structure of epistemic reasoning? What would it mean for ordinary epistemic practice if closure failed?

---

**2. Contextualism and the Sceptical Paradox**

Contextualism (DeRose, Lewis) holds that "S knows that p" is context-sensitive: its truth conditions shift with the epistemic standards operative in the attributor's context. Critically assess whether contextualism provides a genuine solution to the sceptical paradox or merely a semantic deflection. Lewis's "Elusive Knowledge" argues that the Rule of Attention makes sceptical hypotheses relevant the moment they are mentioned, thereby temporarily destroying ordinary knowledge. Is this account of knowledge's elusiveness epistemologically satisfying? What are the most serious objections to contextualism?

---

**3. Neo-Moorean Responses to Scepticism**

G.E. Moore's "Proof of an External World" argues that he knows he has hands and hence knows he is not a brain in a vat. Contemporary neo-Mooreans (Pryor, Huemer, Wright) have developed this strategy in sophisticated ways. Critically assess the neo-Moorean response. Is Moore's proof question-begging against the sceptic, as some critics claim, or does it correctly identify that the price of scepticism is denial of obvious truths? Evaluate Crispin Wright's concept of "epistemic entitlement" — the idea that certain "hinge propositions" can be accepted without evidence as the preconditions of inquiry.

---

**4. Externalism and Anti-Sceptical Strategy**

Process reliabilism and proper functionalism are externalist theories of knowledge: justification depends on factors the agent may have no access to. If reliabilism is correct, a normal perceiver whose cognitive processes are reliable has justified beliefs even if they cannot rule out brain-in-vat scenarios by their own lights. Assess whether externalism provides a genuine response to scepticism or merely changes the subject. Does the externalist response to scepticism leave an important residual question — why should I trust my own faculties? — unanswered? Is the internalist/externalist debate relevant to whether scepticism is a genuine threat?

---

**5. Pragmatic Encroachment and Sceptical Pressure**

Hawthorne and Stanley argue that knowledge depends on the agent's practical stakes: an agent who faces high stakes in acting on a belief must meet higher epistemic standards to know it. This implies that sceptical pressure may be partly pragmatic rather than purely epistemic. Critically assess the implications of pragmatic encroachment for scepticism. If knowledge is stakes-sensitive, does the sceptic raise the stakes by posing the brain-in-vat hypothesis, or does the sceptic identify a fact about the agent's epistemic position that is independent of stakes? What does the answer imply about the relationship between knowledge and action?

---

**6. Wittgenstein's Hinge Epistemology**

In *On Certainty*, Wittgenstein argues that certain "hinge propositions" (I have two hands; the world has existed for more than five minutes; I am a human being) function as the framework within which inquiry takes place and are not themselves subject to doubt or justification within that framework. Duncan Pritchard has systematized this into "hinge epistemology." Critically assess whether hinge epistemology provides a satisfying response to Cartesian scepticism or whether it merely articulates the psychological impossibility of global doubt. Is Wittgenstein's position a form of contextualism, a form of neo-Mooreanism, or something different?

---

**7. Is Global Scepticism Coherent?**

Putnam's brain-in-vat argument claims that the hypothesis that we are brains in vats is self-refuting: if we were envatted, our words would refer to vat-images, not real objects, so the sentence "We are brains in a vat" would be false in the mouth of an envatted brain. Critically assess Putnam's argument. Does it establish that we know we are not brains in vats, or only that the hypothesis is self-refuting in a way that requires the semantic externalism that itself needs to be established? More broadly, evaluate whether global scepticism is coherent: can there be a meaningful worry that all our beliefs are false?
