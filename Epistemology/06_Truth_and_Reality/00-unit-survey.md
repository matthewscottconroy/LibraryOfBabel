# Unit 6: Truth and Reality — Unit Survey

## Central Questions

Truth is the first condition in the JTB analysis of knowledge, and in that sense all of epistemology presupposes a theory of truth. But the theory of truth is also a topic in its own right, and one that connects epistemology to metaphysics, philosophy of language, and philosophy of science. The central question is what it means to say that a proposition or sentence is true.

This is not a trivial question. Deflationism holds that there is nothing substantive to say about truth — that "it is true that snow is white" says exactly as much as "snow is white," and that the truth predicate serves only a logical function (generalizing and endorsing). Correspondence theory holds that truth consists in a relation between propositions (or sentences, or beliefs) and facts (or the world). Coherence theory holds that truth consists in membership in a maximally coherent system of propositions. Epistemic theories (Dummett, anti-realism) hold that truth is idealized assertibility or what a sufficiently informed inquirer would believe. Each theory faces characteristic objections.

The debate about truth connects to debates about realism and anti-realism. The realist holds that the world exists independently of mind and language, and that our claims about it can be determinately true or false whether or not we have access to the relevant facts. The anti-realist (in Dummett's sense) holds that truth must be grounded in verification conditions: a proposition is true only if we can (in principle) verify it. This generates debates about semantic bivalence, about the limits of knowledge, and about the relationship between truth and what we are in a position to assert.

## Map of Major Positions and Their Logical Relations

**Deflationism.** Truth is a logical device for generalization and endorsement. "What Alice says is true" is a convenient way of affirming all of Alice's assertions without listing them. The truth predicate does not describe a property of propositions — there is no "deep" metaphysical relation of correspondence to be theorized. Proponents: Frege (proto-deflationist), Quine (disquotationalism), Horwich (minimalism), Field (deflationary correspondence).

*Disquotationalism* (Quine, Leeds): "'Snow is white' is true" is equivalent to "snow is white." The truth predicate serves to disquote. There is no further relational property being attributed.

*Minimalism* (Horwich): Every instance of the schema "⟨p⟩ is true iff p" is true, and this exhausts the concept of truth. The schema generates all the truths about truth; no deeper explanation is possible or needed.

**Correspondence Theory.** Truth is a relation between truth-bearers (propositions, beliefs, sentences) and truth-makers (facts, states of affairs, the world). The main challenges: (a) specifying what facts are (do negative facts, general facts, and modal facts exist?); (b) specifying the correspondence relation (is it a structural isomorphism, a reference-based semantic relation, or something else?). Proponents: early Russell, early Wittgenstein (*Tractatus*), Armstrong (truth-maker theory).

*Truth-maker theory* (Armstrong, Restall): for every truth, there is an entity whose existence necessitates the truth. This version of correspondence avoids some of the problems with classical correspondence by making the relatum the truth-maker rather than the fact.

**Coherence Theory.** Truth is membership in a maximally coherent and comprehensive system of propositions. A proposition is true iff it coheres with the system. The main challenges: (a) multiple coherent systems may be possible, each defining its own "truth"; (b) coherence seems to be a mark of truth rather than constitutive of it. Historical proponents: British idealists (Bradley, Bosanquet); contemporary successor: Rescher.

**Epistemic Theories.** Truth is identified with what would be believed under idealized epistemic conditions.

*Pragmatist truth* (Peirce): truth is the limit of successful inquiry — what the community of investigators would converge on at the end of inquiry. This identifies truth with a regulative ideal rather than with current justified belief.

*Anti-realist truth* (Dummett): truth is warranted assertibility under idealized conditions. A statement is true iff it would be verifiable by an idealized inquirer with access to all available evidence. This leads to a rejection of the law of excluded middle for undecidable propositions.

*Constructivist truth*: truth is constructed by cognitive activity. This is distinct from epistemic theories but often grouped with them in debates about realism.

**Tarski's Semantic Theory.** Tarski's material adequacy condition (the T-schema: "S" is true iff S) and formal definition of truth for formalized languages provides the technical foundation for formal semantics. Tarski did not identify truth with any metaphysical property; his definition is immanent to a language, not a correspondence theory in the philosophical sense. But it has been used by both deflationists and correspondence theorists to support their positions.

## Sub-Chapter Contributions

**Unit 1: Theories of Truth.** Chapter 1 presents correspondence, coherence, and pragmatist theories systematically, with their historical roots and canonical objections. Chapter 2 examines deflationism in detail — Quine's disquotationalism, Horwich's minimalism, Field's nominalist correspondence — and assesses its prospects for capturing all the platitudes about truth. Chapter 3 develops Tarski's semantic theory and its relationship to the philosophical debate.

**Unit 2: Realism and Anti-Realism.** Chapter 1 presents Dummett's anti-realism — the argument that bivalence fails for undecidable statements, so that some statements are neither true nor false — and the metaphysical conclusions Dummett draws. Chapter 2 examines realist responses: semantic realism (Devitt), scientific realism, and the relationship between metaphysical realism and truth-aptness. Chapter 3 examines constructivism and relativism about truth.

**Unit 3: Reference and Meaning.** Examines the epistemological implications of theories of reference. Kripke's causal theory of reference — names refer by causal-historical chains, not by descriptions — has implications for what we know when we use proper names and natural kind terms. Putnam's semantic externalism — "meanings ain't in the head" — connects to anti-individualism in epistemology and to debates about testimony and self-knowledge.

## Key Philosophers and Arguments

**Alfred Tarski (1901–1983).** "The Concept of Truth in Formalized Languages" (1933) and "The Semantic Conception of Truth and the Foundations of Semantics" (1944) established the formal theory of truth for first-order languages and provided the material adequacy condition (T-schema). Tarski's criterion — a satisfactory theory of truth must entail all instances of "S is true iff S" for sentences S of the object language — constrains all subsequent proposals.

**Michael Dummett (1925–2011).** *Truth and Other Enigmas* (1978) and *The Logical Basis of Metaphysics* (1991) develop the anti-realist argument that semantic realism (the view that statements have truth values independently of our capacity to recognize them) is incoherent. Dummett's anti-realism is the most sustained and technically sophisticated challenge to correspondence theories and to classical logic.

**Paul Horwich (1947–).** *Truth* (2nd ed., 1998) defends minimalism: all there is to say about truth is captured by the schema "⟨p⟩ is true iff p." Horwich argues that this schema, together with the non-semantic facts about the world, explains all the features of truth without positing a metaphysical correspondence relation.

**Hilary Putnam (1926–2016) on truth and internal realism.** *Reason, Truth and History* (1981) develops "internal realism": truth is idealized rational acceptability within a conceptual scheme. This is a moderate epistemic theory of truth that avoids both metaphysical realism and radical relativism. Putnam later largely abandoned internal realism, but it remains important as a position between Dummett's anti-realism and correspondence theory.

**Saul Kripke (1940–).** *Naming and Necessity* (1980) introduces the causal theory of reference and distinguishes it from descriptivist theories. Kripke argues that proper names are "rigid designators" — they designate the same individual in all possible worlds. The epistemological implications: we can have a priori knowledge of analytic truths about names (e.g., "Hesperus = Phosphorus" is necessarily true) while a posteriori discovering what the referent is.

**Bertrand Russell (1872–1970) on facts and correspondence.** In "The Philosophy of Logical Atomism" (1918) and related works, Russell defends a correspondence theory where the truth-makers are atomic facts — complexes of particulars and universals. Russell's multiple-relation theory of judgment — a judgment is a relation between a mind and a number of objects, not between a mind and a proposition — is an attempt to explain correspondence without positing abstract propositions.

## Essential Readings

1. **Tarski, Alfred, "The Semantic Conception of Truth and the Foundations of Semantics," *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research*, 4:3 (1944), pp. 341–376.** The accessible version of Tarski's technical work. Essential for understanding what formal semantics tells us about truth and what remains to be said philosophically.

2. **Horwich, Paul, *Truth*, Chapter 1** (2nd ed., 1998, Blackwell). The clearest statement of minimalism. Shows what deflationism is committed to and what it is not.

3. **Dummett, Michael, "Truth," *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society*, 59 (1959), pp. 141–162.** The original paper developing Dummett's anti-realist position. Still the clearest entry point into Dummett's thought on truth and bivalence.

4. **Lewis, David, "Truth in Fiction," *American Philosophical Quarterly*, 15:1 (1978), pp. 37–46.** Examines a test case for correspondence and deflationism: what makes fictional statements true? Illustrates the connections between the metaphysics of truth and specific semantic questions.

5. **Kripke, Saul, *Naming and Necessity*, Lectures I–II** (1980, Harvard). The causal theory of reference and its epistemological implications. Essential for understanding the relationship between truth, reference, and knowledge of necessity.

## Open Questions and Live Debates

**Is deflationism coherent?** Deflationists must explain what the truth predicate does in generalizations ("Everything the Pope says is true") and in logical laws ("Every sentence of the form 'p or not-p' is true") without invoking a substantial truth property. Critics argue that deflationists cannot give a satisfactory account of these uses.

**Does the T-schema collapse with Liar paradoxes?** The Liar sentence ("This sentence is false") generates a contradiction when combined with the T-schema. This is not just a technical puzzle: it suggests that the T-schema cannot be the whole story about truth. Solutions (Tarski's hierarchy of languages, Kripke's partial truth, revision theory) all make significant theoretical commitments.

**Can there be truth without a truthmaker?** Truthmaker maximalism (Armstrong) holds that every truth requires a truthmaker. But negative truths ("there are no unicorns"), general truths ("all ravens are black"), and modal truths ("it is possible that p") pose difficulties. Some deflationists deny that truthmakers are needed.

**Is semantic anti-realism self-refuting?** If truth requires verifiability, is the anti-realist's own claim that "truth requires verifiability" itself verifiable? This is the most common objection to Dummett. Dummett responds that the anti-realist can use the concept of truth as internal to inquiry without claiming that the meta-claim is itself verified.
