# The Correspondence Theory of Truth

The correspondence theory of truth is the most intuitive and historically dominant account: a belief or statement is true if and only if it corresponds to how things are in reality. When I believe that snow is white, my belief is true if and only if snow is, in fact, white — if reality contains the fact that snow is white. Truth is a relation between a mind-dependent representation and a mind-independent reality.

## The Basic Idea

The correspondence theory captures what most people mean when they first try to articulate what truth is. To say "the cat is on the mat" is true just when there is a cat, there is a mat, and the cat is on it. The sentence accurately describes a state of affairs in the world. When reality does not cooperate — when there is no cat on any mat — the sentence is false.

This account has obvious ancestors in Aristotle's formulation: "To say of what is that it is not, or of what is not that it is, is false, while to say of what is that it is, and of what is not that it is not, is true." The idea is simple: match the world, be true; fail to match, be false.

## Making Correspondence Precise

The difficulty lies in articulating the correspondence relation precisely. What makes a belief or sentence correspond to a fact?

**The classical account** holds that beliefs correspond to facts when they "picture" reality — when the structure of the belief mirrors the structure of the facts. Bertrand Russell and the early Wittgenstein (in the *Tractatus*) developed versions of this picture theory. A belief that aRb (a stands in relation R to b) is true if the objects a and b stand in the relation R in reality.

**The truthmaker approach**: A more contemporary development holds that for every true proposition, there is something in the world that "makes" it true — a truthmaker. The truthmaker for "snow is white" is the fact that snow is white. Truthmaker theory investigates what kinds of entities serve as truthmakers (facts, states of affairs, tropes?) and what the relation between a proposition and its truthmaker consists in.

**The deflationary challenge**: Deflationists point out that the correspondence idiom ("true if and only if it corresponds to the facts") adds no content over simply saying when the proposition is true: "snow is white" is true iff snow is white. The "correspondence" is just the biconditional; there is no deep relation of correspondence to analyze.

## The Problem of Facts

The correspondence theory seems committed to facts — entities in the world that truth-bearers correspond to. But facts are philosophically problematic:

**The slingshot argument**: Church, Gödel, and Quine each developed versions of an argument that if there are facts, all true sentences correspond to the same fact. The argument uses standard logical substitutions to collapse all facts into a single "the True." If the slingshot succeeds, correspondence to different facts cannot differentiate truth conditions.

**Negative and general facts**: What is the fact that corresponds to "there are no unicorns"? What about "all swans are white"? These seem to require negative facts and general facts, which are metaphysically strange entities. Does the world contain the fact of something's non-existence?

**Structural matching**: The picture theory requires a structural match between belief/sentence and fact. But what determines the structure of a fact? Facts don't come pre-structured; how they are carved up depends on the concepts and categories we bring to experience.

## Arguments for Correspondence

Despite these difficulties, correspondence intuitions are compelling:

**Realist motivation**: The correspondence theory naturally captures the thought that truth is mind-independent — that whether snow is white doesn't depend on what anyone thinks about it. Truth is objective, in the sense that it is determined by how the world is.

**Truth and assertion**: What we do when we assert something is represent the world as being a certain way. If the world is that way, we've succeeded; if not, we've failed. This success-failure structure is naturally captured by correspondence.

**The role of causation**: Our beliefs are caused by how the world is (via perception and inference), and this causal connection is what grounds their truth. A purely internal account of truth can't capture this connection to reality.

The correspondence theory remains the default view in analytic philosophy of language and metaphysics, even if the detailed account of what correspondence consists in remains contested.
