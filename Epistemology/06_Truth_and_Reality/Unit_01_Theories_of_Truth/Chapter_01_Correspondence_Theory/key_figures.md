## Aristotle (384–322 BCE)

The earliest systematic statement of the correspondence intuition appears in Aristotle's *Metaphysics*: truth consists in saying of what is that it is, and of what is not that it is not. Although Aristotle did not develop a full theory of truth-makers or of the correspondence relation itself, his formula set the agenda for two millennia of debate. His insight that truth is tied to how things stand in the world, independently of what we believe, anchors the realist tradition.

## Bertrand Russell (1872–1970)

Russell developed the most technically precise early correspondence theory in his multiple-relation theory of judgment and his logical atomism lectures. He argued that the world is composed of facts—combinations of particulars and universals—and that propositions correspond to facts when true. Russell's work also generated the problem of negative facts: if "the cat is not on the mat" is true, what fact makes it so? His honest confrontation with this problem shaped subsequent debates about truth-makers.

## Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889–1951)

In the *Tractatus*, Wittgenstein proposed the picture theory: propositions share logical form with the facts they depict. A proposition is true when the arrangement of its elements mirrors an actual arrangement in the world. Though Wittgenstein later repudiated this view, the picture theory remains the most elegant correspondence account and its problems—especially with logical and modal propositions—illuminate the limits of any purely structural correspondence.

## J.L. Austin (1911–1960)

Austin's 1950 paper "Truth" proposed a refined correspondence that avoids commitments to problematic metaphysical facts by distinguishing descriptive conventions (linking sentences to types of situation) from demonstrative conventions (indicating what situation obtains). Austin insisted that truth is a relation between a statement and a historical state of affairs, not a property of propositions or sentences in the abstract. His pragmatically oriented correspondence remains influential in ordinary-language philosophy.

## D.M. Armstrong (1926–2014)

Armstrong's late work systematized truth-maker theory, arguing that every truth requires an entity whose existence necessitates that truth. He distinguished states of affairs (his preferred truth-makers) from mere sets or properties, and grappled seriously with the problem of negative truths—ultimately entertaining totality facts to handle them. His *Truth and Truthmakers* (2004) is the indispensable statement of the contemporary truth-maker program.

## Stephen Neale (b. 1958)

Neale's philosophical logic work clarified the slingshot argument originally associated with Frege, Church, Quine, and Davidson. The slingshot purports to show that if sentences have facts as referents, and if co-referential expressions are substitutable, then all true sentences refer to the same fact—trivializing the correspondence relation. Neale's careful reconstruction of the argument made it a decisive challenge that any fact-based correspondence theorist must answer.
