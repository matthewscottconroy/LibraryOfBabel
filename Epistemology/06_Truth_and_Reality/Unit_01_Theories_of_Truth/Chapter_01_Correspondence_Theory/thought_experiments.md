## The Negative Fact Problem

**The scenario**: Consider the true statement "There are no unicorns." If correspondence theory is correct, this statement is made true by some fact in the world. But what fact? Not any positive combination of objects and properties—unicorns simply do not exist. One might posit a negative fact—the fact that unicorns do not exist—but Bertrand Russell famously felt uneasy about this: negative facts seem ontologically suspicious, as if reality contained absences as genuine constituents alongside tables and electrons.

**The philosophical point**: This thought experiment reveals a tension at the heart of correspondence theory. The theory seems most plausible for positive, atomic sentences ("The cat is on the mat"), but truth extends far beyond these. Any adequate correspondence account must either embrace negative facts (or their equivalents), find some reduction of negative truths to positive ones, or retreat to a more deflationary position. The discomfort with negative facts has driven some philosophers toward truth-maker maximalism (Armstrong's totality facts) and others toward abandoning correspondence altogether.

## The Slingshot Trap

**The scenario**: Suppose the correspondence theorist says "Snow is white" corresponds to the fact that snow is white, while "Grass is green" corresponds to the distinct fact that grass is green. Now consider a logical transformation: "Snow is white" is logically equivalent to "The unique x such that x = snow-being-white = the unique x such that x = snow-being-white, and snow is white." Substituting co-referential definite descriptions step by step, one can apparently maneuver from any true sentence to any other, suggesting they all correspond to the same "Great Fact."

**The philosophical point**: The slingshot argument, reconstructed by Neale from Frege, Church, Quine, and Davidson, threatens to collapse the correspondence theory by showing that fine-grained facts cannot be the relata of the correspondence relation without generating paradox. The theorist must either reject the substitutability of co-referential expressions in fact-specifying contexts (embracing structured propositions), deny that logical equivalents are intersubstitutable, or abandon the idea that facts are the relevant truth-makers. The argument forces a choice between different logical frameworks.

## Russell's Multiple Relations

**The scenario**: Imagine Othello believes that Desdemona loves Cassio. In Russell's multiple-relation theory, this belief is not a two-place relation between Othello and a proposition (which would be an abstract object). Instead, it is a four-place relation among Othello, the relation of loving, Desdemona, and Cassio. The belief is true if Desdemona actually stands in the loving relation to Cassio—i.e., if the relevant fact obtains.

**The philosophical point**: This thought experiment illustrates how correspondence can be made precise without invoking propositions as mysterious intermediaries. But it raises the order problem: what distinguishes Othello's belief that Desdemona loves Cassio from his belief that Cassio loves Desdemona? The terms and relation are the same; only their order differs. Unless the multiple-relation theory can account for this ordering, it cannot distinguish beliefs with the same constituents but opposite truth values—a serious structural problem that Wittgenstein pressed against Russell.

## The Truthmaker for Mathematical Truths

**The scenario**: "7 + 5 = 12" is necessarily true. If correspondence theory requires truth-makers—real-world entities whose existence necessitates truth—what is the truth-maker for this arithmetic fact? One option: abstract mathematical objects (numbers, functions) exist necessarily and constitute the truth-makers. Another: the truth-maker is a fact about how finite collections of concrete objects combine. A third option: there is no discrete truth-maker because mathematical truths are true by convention or logic alone.

**The philosophical point**: Mathematical truth is a stress test for correspondence theories. If truth-makers must be concrete, empirical states of affairs, then mathematics is either not genuinely true or its truths are oddly dependent on contingent arrangements of physical objects. If truth-makers can include abstract entities, then the ontological parsimony often attributed to correspondence realism evaporates. The example shows that the scope of correspondence—whether it covers all truths or only empirical ones—is itself a substantive philosophical commitment.

## Austin's Descriptive and Demonstrative Conventions

**The scenario**: J.L. Austin asks us to consider what makes an utterance true. He suggests that language is governed by two types of conventions: descriptive conventions, which correlate sentence-types with types of situation in the world, and demonstrative conventions, which relate token utterances to particular historical situations. A statement is true when the historical situation it refers to is of the type the descriptive conventions associate with the sentence. Imagine two utterances of "The cat is on the mat"—one true, one false. They use the same words but refer to different historical situations.

**The philosophical point**: Austin's framework shows that correspondence need not invoke an abstract "fact" as a metaphysical entity. Instead, the world consists of situations, and sentences describe types of situation. Truth is a two-layer affair: type-match (descriptive) and token-reference (demonstrative). This defuses some metaphysical puzzles about facts while preserving the core correspondence insight that truth is responsive to how the world is. It also anticipates later situation semantics (Barwise and Perry) and grounds a robust realism about truth without a heavy ontology of structured facts.
