# The Coherence Theory of Truth

The coherence theory of truth holds that truth consists in coherence with a system of beliefs, rather than correspondence to mind-independent facts. A belief is true if and only if it coheres with — fits harmoniously with — a sufficiently comprehensive and systematic body of beliefs. This theory is associated historically with the British Idealists (F.H. Bradley, Bernard Bosanquet) and has been developed more recently in various forms.

## The Motivation

The correspondence theory seems to require that we have access to mind-independent facts against which to check our beliefs. But we only ever have access to our own experience and beliefs — we cannot step outside our cognitive perspective to compare beliefs with unmediated reality. This suggests that truth must be analyzed in terms of relations among beliefs rather than between beliefs and something entirely outside them.

F.H. Bradley argued that the coherence theory follows from a general metaphysical idealism: reality just *is* a coherent system of experience (the Absolute). Truth is membership in this coherent system. Bradley rejected the correspondence theory because it commits us to something unintelligible — an unmediated "fact" that stands outside all thought and experience.

## What Is Coherence?

The coherence relation must be more than mere consistency (non-contradiction). A set of beliefs can be consistent without being coherent — they might be disconnected, unrelated, or simply not mutually contradictory. Coherence typically involves:

- **Logical consistency**: No contradictions among the beliefs
- **Mutual support**: Beliefs positively support each other; each belief is made more probable by the others
- **Explanatory connections**: Beliefs stand in explanatory relations — some explain others
- **Comprehensiveness**: The coherent system covers a wide range of domains without gaps

The ideal coherent system is one in which every member is supported by every other member and none could be removed without disrupting the whole. Bradley's Absolute is supposed to be such a system.

## Difficulties for Coherence Theories of Truth

**The alternative systems problem**: It seems possible to construct multiple, mutually incompatible systems of beliefs that each have high internal coherence. If truth is coherence with a system, which system? If we say "the right system," we need an independent criterion of rightness — which threatens to appeal to correspondence.

**The input problem**: Coherence theories struggle to explain why experience should matter to truth. My beliefs about the external world are partly caused by how the external world is; they have experiential grounding. But if truth is coherence, I could have maximally coherent beliefs about the external world that are entirely disconnected from experience — and they would be true. This seems wrong.

**Regress**: The coherence theory defines truth in terms of coherence. But what makes the claim that "belief B coheres with system S" true? If the answer is further coherence, we have an infinite regress.

## Coherence Theory of Justification vs. Truth

Many philosophers who find coherence theories of *justification* attractive are careful to distinguish this from coherence theories of *truth*. The coherentist theory of justification holds that what justifies a belief is its coherence with other beliefs — this is a view about the structure of epistemic support. It does not commit one to the view that truth consists in coherence.

Most contemporary coherentists about justification (BonJour, Lehrer) are *not* coherentists about truth; they accept a correspondence or at least realist account of truth. The coherence of a belief system is what makes the system epistemically good, but what makes beliefs *true* is how the world is.

## The Pragmatist Connection

William James's pragmatist account of truth has connections to coherence theory but is importantly different. For James, a belief is true if it "works" — if acting on it leads to success, satisfies our cognitive needs, and coheres with our other beliefs and practices. Truth is not static membership in a fixed system but a dynamic property that beliefs acquire through their successful operation in inquiry.

James's account is not a pure coherence theory because it appeals to practical success and verification, not just internal coherence. But it shares the idealist rejection of mind-independent correspondence and the emphasis on systematic fit.
