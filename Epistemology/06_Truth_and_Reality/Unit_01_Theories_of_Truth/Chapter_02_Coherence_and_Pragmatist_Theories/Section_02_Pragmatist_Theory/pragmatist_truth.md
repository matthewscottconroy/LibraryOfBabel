# Pragmatist Theories of Truth

Pragmatism about truth holds that truth is not a static property of beliefs in virtue of their correspondence to mind-independent facts, but rather something that beliefs acquire through their role in inquiry, action, and successful practice. The pragmatist tradition — developed by C.S. Peirce, William James, and John Dewey, and revived in neo-pragmatist forms by Hilary Putnam, Richard Rorty, and Robert Brandom — offers a family of views that agree in rejecting the correspondence theory while disagreeing significantly among themselves.

## Peirce: Truth as the Ideal Limit of Inquiry

C.S. Peirce developed the first systematic pragmatist account of truth. For Peirce, truth is what a community of inquirers would agree on in the long run, following the methods of scientific inquiry. A true belief is one that would survive indefinitely sustained, rational inquiry — that would not be revised no matter how much evidence was gathered.

This is not a subjective account: Peirce was a scientific realist who believed that inquiry converges on truth. The long-run convergence is objective — it is not a matter of what we would agree on given our biases, but what the evidence, when fully processed by rational inquiry, would support. Truth is the ideal limit toward which inquiry is directed.

Peirce's account avoids the problem of mind-independence without the metaphysical commitment to correspondence: truth is objective in the sense that it is determined by reality (inquiry is responsive to how things are), but it is analyzed in terms of the ideal outcome of inquiry rather than a metaphysical correspondence relation.

## James: Truth as Successful Working

William James's account is more pragmatist in the everyday sense: truth is what *works*. True beliefs are those that, when acted upon, lead to successful outcomes, satisfy our cognitive needs, and prove their worth through experience.

James extended truth beyond empirical beliefs to religious beliefs, moral beliefs, and metaphysical beliefs. If believing in God "works" for you — produces a more satisfying life, makes your experience more coherent, motivates beneficial action — then for you the belief is true, or at least has a right to be believed.

This aspect of James's account was widely criticized. Bertrand Russell argued that a falsehood believed might have better consequences than a truth, and this would make the falsehood true on James's view. James's equation of truth with workability seems to confuse truth with justified belief or with belief that is beneficial to hold.

James's deeper point, however, was that there is no "God's eye view" from which to assess correspondence. What we mean by "true" is always cashed out in terms of the standards and practices of human inquiry. "Truth happens to an idea. It becomes true, is made true by events."

## Dewey: Warranted Assertibility

John Dewey avoided the term "truth" in favor of *warranted assertibility*: beliefs are properly held when they are "warranted" — when they arise from responsible inquiry, are responsive to evidence, and would survive critical scrutiny. Dewey was skeptical of the entire project of finding a metaphysical account of truth; what matters is the quality of inquiry and the standards of responsible assertion.

Dewey's naturalism connects epistemology to biology and social practice: what counts as good inquiry is determined by the practices of the relevant community of inquirers, shaped by evolutionary and social history. There is no standpoint outside these practices from which to validate them.

## Neo-Pragmatism: Rorty and Brandom

Richard Rorty radicalized the pragmatist tradition in *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature* (1979). Rorty rejected the very idea that our concepts "mirror" or represent reality, and with it the entire problematic of correspondence and epistemological justification. Philosophy should give up the goal of providing a foundation for knowledge and instead engage in the "edifying" project of expanding our vocabulary for self-description and social coordination.

For Rorty, "truth" has no interesting metaphysical analysis; we should say "true" about things we are prepared to assert and defend, and that's all.

Robert Brandom's inferentialist pragmatism develops a more technical account: the content of beliefs and assertions is determined by their inferential role — by what follows from them and what they follow from. Truth is not correspondence to facts but a normative property conferred by the practices of assertion, inference, and mutual accountability.

## The Minimal Insight of Pragmatism

Even philosophers who reject pragmatist accounts of truth often accept a minimal pragmatist point: what we *do* with truth — how truth functions in inquiry, how we use the concept in practice — is philosophically important. A metaphysically adequate theory of truth must connect to the practical role of truth in our cognitive and social lives.
