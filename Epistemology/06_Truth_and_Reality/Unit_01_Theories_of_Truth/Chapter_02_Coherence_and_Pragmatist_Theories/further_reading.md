## Primary Sources

- F.H. Bradley, *Appearance and Reality*, Swan Sonnenschein, 1893. The founding text of British absolute idealism; argues that reality is a single coherent whole and that partial, relational truths are ultimately incoherent.

- F.H. Bradley, "On Truth and Coherence," in *Essays on Truth and Reality*, Oxford University Press, 1914. Bradley's clearest statement of the coherence theory of truth as the test—and ultimately the nature—of truth.

- Harold Joachim, *The Nature of Truth*, Oxford University Press, 1906. A systematic defense of coherence as the essential character of truth; important precursor to later idealist coherentism.

- William James, *Pragmatism: A New Name for Some Old Ways of Thinking*, Longmans, Green, 1907. Defines truth as what "works" or is "expedient in the way of our thinking"—the most accessible entry point to pragmatist theories of truth.

- Charles Sanders Peirce, "How to Make Our Ideas Clear," *Popular Science Monthly*, 1878. Introduces the pragmatic maxim and the ideal-limit theory of truth as what inquiry converges on in the long run.

- John Dewey, *Logic: The Theory of Inquiry*, Henry Holt, 1938. Develops warranted assertibility as the preferred epistemic norm, replacing truth-talk with the outcome of controlled, successful inquiry.

- John Dewey, "Propositions, Warranted Assertibility, and Truth," *Journal of Philosophy* 38, 1941. Dewey's direct reply to Russell's criticism; clarifies the relationship between warranted assertibility and correspondence.

- Richard Rorty, *Consequences of Pragmatism*, University of Minnesota Press, 1982. Neo-pragmatist essays arguing that truth is not a goal of inquiry but a compliment we pay to beliefs that survive scrutiny.

- Richard Rorty, *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature*, Princeton University Press, 1979. Influential critique of representationalism; argues for solidarity over objectivity as the proper aim of inquiry.

- Robert Brandom, *Making It Explicit*, Harvard University Press, 1994. Inferentialist semantics that grounds meaning and truth in normative practices of giving and asking for reasons.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Ralph Walker, *The Coherence Theory of Truth*, Routledge, 1989. The best book-length examination of coherence theories; covers idealist and epistemic variants and the most serious objections.

- Susan Haack, *Evidence and Inquiry*, Blackwell, 1993. Develops "foundherentism" as a middle path; essential for understanding why pure coherentism is vulnerable to input-blocking objections.

- Christopher Hookway, *Truth, Rationality, and Pragmatism*, Oxford University Press, 2000. Authoritative study of Peirce's theory of truth and its relationship to his broader philosophy of science.

- Cheryl Misak, *Truth and the End of Inquiry*, Oxford University Press, 1991. Defends a Peircean convergence theory while clarifying how it differs from idealist coherentism.

- Cheryl Misak (ed.), *New Pragmatists*, Oxford University Press, 2007. Essays by Misak, Brandom, and others developing contemporary pragmatist views of truth.

- Crispin Wright, *Truth and Objectivity*, Harvard University Press, 1992. Though primarily about pluralism, contains the best critical analysis of the connections between coherence, superassertibility, and truth.
