## F.H. Bradley (1846–1924)

Bradley is the central figure in the British idealist tradition and the philosopher most associated with a thoroughgoing coherence theory of truth. His *Appearance and Reality* argues that the world of ordinary experience—including relations, space, time, and individual things—is shot through with contradiction and can only be redeemed by absorption into a coherent Absolute. For Bradley, the coherence of a judgment with the entire system of experience is both the criterion and ultimately the constitutive nature of truth. His relentless criticism of relational ontology forced subsequent realists (especially Russell and Moore) to articulate their positions more precisely.

## Charles Sanders Peirce (1839–1914)

Peirce founded pragmatism and formulated its most epistemically rigorous theory of truth: truth is what inquiry would converge on in the long run under ideal conditions. This "ideal-limit" theory avoids equating truth with current belief while still tying truth to the practice of inquiry. Peirce distinguished his view sharply from James's more permissive pragmatism, insisting that truth is intersubjectively constrained by the community of inquirers and the world's resistance to incorrect belief. His account deeply influenced twentieth-century philosophy of science.

## William James (1842–1910)

James popularized pragmatism and offered the most provocative pragmatist theory of truth: true ideas are those that "work" in the sense of being satisfying, useful, and capable of being assimilated into experience. James intended this as a genetic account—what makes something true is its having good practical consequences for belief—though critics (including Peirce) worried it collapsed into relativism. His debate with Bradley and Russell over "the will to believe" and the cash value of abstract truths remains philosophically instructive.

## John Dewey (1859–1952)

Dewey replaced truth-talk with "warranted assertibility," arguing that calling a belief "true" is a misleading way of saying it is the product of successful inquiry—a process of transforming a problematic situation into a settled one. His instrumentalist epistemology ties the value of beliefs to their role in guiding action and resolving genuine problems. Dewey's rejection of the correspondence picture influenced American pragmatism throughout the twentieth century and anticipated later naturalist and anti-representationalist positions.

## Richard Rorty (1931–2007)

Rorty's neo-pragmatism revived anti-representationalist themes by arguing that the notion of truth as correspondence to mind-independent reality is a relic of the Cartesian tradition that should be abandoned. Instead of seeking truth, Rorty proposed, we should aim at solidarity—agreement among conversational partners—and treat "true" as simply a term of endorsement. His *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature* catalyzed the "pragmatic turn" in analytic philosophy, though critics (including Misak and Brandom) argued that Rorty's deflationism was too deflationary.

## Robert Brandom (b. 1950)

Brandom's inferentialism holds that the content of any claim is constituted by its inferential role—what follows from it and what it follows from—within a normative practice of reasoning. Truth, on this view, is not a representational relation but a device for facilitating inference and endorsing claims across contexts. Brandom's complex and systematic framework, developed in *Making It Explicit* and *Between Saying and Doing*, represents the most technically sophisticated contemporary descendant of the pragmatist tradition.
