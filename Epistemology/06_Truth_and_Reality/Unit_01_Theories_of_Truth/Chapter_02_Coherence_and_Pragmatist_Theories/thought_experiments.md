## The Alternative Coherent System

**The scenario**: Imagine a complete and fully coherent set of beliefs—call it System A—held by a person or community. Now imagine a second set of beliefs, System B, which is equally coherent internally but systematically disagrees with System A: what A says is true, B says is false, and vice versa. Both systems are maximally coherent. The coherence theory of truth says that truth is a matter of coherence with the right system—but which system is the right one?

**The philosophical point**: This is the classic "alternative coherent systems" objection to coherentism (associated with Russell and Blanshard's critics). If coherence alone determines truth, there is no principled way to choose between two maximally coherent but mutually inconsistent systems. The objection forces coherentists to either add further constraints—such as coherence with sense experience, or with a single absolute system (Bradley's move)—or to bite the bullet and accept that truth is system-relative. Neither option is fully comfortable: the first smuggles in a correspondence-like anchor; the second risks relativism.

## Peirce's Long-Run Convergence

**The scenario**: Consider a factual question—say, the exact distance from the Earth to the Sun. Different scientific communities in 1700, 1800, and 1900 had different, progressively more accurate estimates. Peirce imagines this process of inquiry extended indefinitely: over an infinite long run, all genuine inquirers following the scientific method would converge on a single answer. That answer is, by definition, the truth. Now consider: what if inquiry simply ended—humanity perished before convergence? Would that mean there was no truth about the Earth-Sun distance?

**The philosophical point**: Peirce's ideal-limit theory ties truth to an idealized endpoint of inquiry, but this creates a puzzle about unfulfilled inquiry. If truth requires actual convergence, then truths about the past (before inquiry) or truths inquiry will never reach seem threatened. Peirce's response—that truth is what inquiry *would* converge on under ideal conditions, not what it *does* converge on—shifts the view toward a counterfactual, which introduces its own complexities. The thought experiment probes the border between epistemology and metaphysics: is truth constituted by epistemic processes, or merely tracked by them?

## James's Sick Friend and the "Will to Believe"

**The scenario**: You believe, with good but not overwhelming evidence, that your gravely ill friend will recover. This belief, James suggests, may itself contribute causally to the friend's recovery by sustaining your caregiving and your friend's morale. Suppose the belief is in this way instrumental in bringing about its own verification. Does this mean the belief was true before the friend recovered, or only after? And is a belief that helps bring about its truth-conditions genuinely "true" in the ordinary sense?

**The philosophical point**: James's pragmatism sometimes seems to say that a belief's truth consists in its having good practical consequences—but this thought experiment reveals an ambiguity: are the consequences evidence for independent truth, or do they constitute it? If the latter, we have a strange bootstrapping where wishing hard enough can make things true. Critics like Moore and Russell used such cases to show that pragmatism collapses the distinction between truth and wishful thinking. Defenders reply that James meant only that truth-assessment is inseparable from the interests of actual inquirers—a point that need not collapse into mere wish-fulfillment.

## Dewey's Problematic Situation

**The scenario**: A scientist faces a recalcitrant experimental result—the data don't fit any existing theory. On Dewey's instrumentalist picture, this is a "problematic situation": the existing conceptual tools fail to organize experience satisfactorily. Inquiry then transforms the situation by producing new concepts and theories that restore coherence and allow successful action. When inquiry succeeds, the resulting beliefs are "warranted." But suppose two different inquiry paths both resolve the problematic situation: do both produce "warranted" beliefs? Are both true?

**The philosophical point**: Dewey's replacement of "true" with "warranted assertible" raises the question of whether warrant is convergent (will different successful inquiries produce the same warrants?) or pluralist (different situations may license different but equally warranted beliefs). If convergence is built in, Dewey approximates Peirce. If not, warranted assertibility may be relative to a community or practice, raising questions about the objectivity of inquiry. The thought experiment highlights the tension between Dewey's anti-foundationalism and the need for some constraint that prevents warranted assertibility from collapsing into "whatever the community approves."

## Rorty's Last Pragmatist

**The scenario**: Rorty imagines a philosopher who has thoroughly abandoned the vocabulary of "correspondence to reality" and "objective truth" in favor of solidarity—seeking agreement with one's community and expanding the circle of those included in conversation. This pragmatist cannot say "my view is true because it corresponds to reality"—they can only say "my view is justified by the standards of my community and I recommend those standards." Now a critic charges: "But surely the Holocaust really happened, and it would be wrong to say otherwise even if a community cohered around denial." Rorty's pragmatist must respond without invoking mind-independent truth.

**The philosophical point**: This scenario tests the limits of Rorty's neo-pragmatism. Rorty's response is that we can still say Holocaust denial is wrong—it conflicts with the best standards of historical inquiry and with the moral practices we rightly hold. But critics (especially Cheryl Misak) argue that this response presupposes some notion of truth that goes beyond mere communal solidarity. The thought experiment isolates whether the pragmatist reduction of truth to solidarity can handle cases where moral and historical urgency seems to demand a stronger, non-relativist notion of truth.
