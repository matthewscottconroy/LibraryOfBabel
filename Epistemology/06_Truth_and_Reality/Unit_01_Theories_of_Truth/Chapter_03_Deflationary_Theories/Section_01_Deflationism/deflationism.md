# Deflationary Theories of Truth

Deflationary theories of truth hold that "is true" does not express a substantive property of beliefs, sentences, or propositions — that there is no deep metaphysical analysis of what truth consists in, because truth is not that kind of thing. The truth predicate has an important logical and expressive function, but it doesn't pick out a real property that requires investigation. Deflationism comes in several versions: disquotationalism, minimalism, and prosentential theories.

## The Core Deflationary Insight

The central observation motivating deflationism is the equivalence schema (also called the T-schema or Tarski's biconditional):

"Snow is white" is true if and only if snow is white.

This biconditional seems to tell us everything there is to know about what it means for "snow is white" to be true. There's no further fact beyond snow's being white that makes the sentence true. The word "true" is just a device for recovering content — "it's true that snow is white" just means the same as "snow is white."

If this is right, then asking "what is truth?" and expecting a deep metaphysical answer is confused. Truth is no more a substantive property than "is the case" or "obtains." The truth predicate plays a grammatical and logical role; it does not name a metaphysically interesting property.

## Disquotationalism

W.V.O. Quine developed disquotationalism: "is true" is a device of disquotation. To say "snow is white" is true is to remove the quotation marks from "'snow is white'" — to assert the sentence rather than mention it. The predicate "is true" allows us to generalize over sentences without actually stating each one: "everything the professor said is true" means (roughly) "for every sentence S, if the professor asserted S, then S."

Without the truth predicate, we'd have to enumerate: "Snow is white is true, and water is wet is true, and..." The truth predicate enables infinitary generalization that we couldn't otherwise express. This is its entire function; there is no further property it attributes.

## Minimalism

Paul Horwich's minimalism (in *Truth*, 1990) holds that the "minimal theory" of truth — the set of all instances of the equivalence schema — exhausts the nature of truth. Every instance of:

The proposition that P is true iff P.

is a basic axiom. There is no further explanatory claim about what makes these biconditionals true. The minimalist theory does not specify a property; it specifies a pattern of entailment.

Minimalism is carefully distinguished from Quine's disquotationalism: Horwich takes the bearers of truth to be propositions (abstract entities), not sentences, and he explicitly holds that there is a property of truth — it's just minimal, not substantive.

## The Prosentential Theory

Grover, Camp, and Belnap developed the prosentential theory: "that's true" and "it is true" are prosentences — analogous to pronouns. Just as "it" stands in for a noun phrase in discourse ("Mary walked in. She was tired" — "she" refers back to "Mary"), "that's true" or "it's true" stands in for a sentence in discourse. "You said it's raining, and that's true" uses "that's true" as a prosentence anaphoric on "it's raining."

On this view, truth-talk is primarily a device for tracking and endorsing content across discourse, not for attributing a property.

## Objections to Deflationism

**The explanatory role of truth**: Truth seems to explain why beliefs that are true tend to have certain properties — true beliefs, when acted on, tend to lead to successful outcomes. This explanatory role seems to require a substantive property. The deflationists responds: the explanation can be handled without invoking a metaphysical property; the generalizations can be spelled out in terms of individual T-schema instances.

**Truth in logic and mathematics**: Truth plays a central role in semantics, model theory, and logic. Model-theoretic semantics defines logical validity in terms of truth-in-all-models. The deflationists need to explain how a "mere device of disquotation" can play this theoretical role.

**Semantic paradoxes**: The Liar paradox ("This sentence is false") creates difficulties. Any theory that accepts all instances of the T-schema faces paradox: "This sentence is not true" appears to generate contradiction. Deflationists need a principled restriction of the schema.

**Cross-language truth**: "Snow is white" is true in all languages that translate it correctly. But the disquotational schema only gives truth-in-English. A more substantive account may be needed for cross-linguistic truth attributions.
