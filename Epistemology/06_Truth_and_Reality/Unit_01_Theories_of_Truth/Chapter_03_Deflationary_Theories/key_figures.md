## Frank Ramsey (1903–1930)

Ramsey's brief observation that "It is true that p" is equivalent to "p"—made in passing in his 1927 paper—launched the deflationary tradition. He concluded that "true" and "false" are not genuine predicates that ascribe properties but are merely redundant devices. Ramsey's insight has been refined and challenged in a hundred ways since, but the redundancy observation remains the core deflationary datum that every substantive theory of truth must explain away.

## Alfred Tarski (1902–1983)

Tarski provided the formal machinery without which contemporary deflationary and inflationary theories alike could not be stated. His Convention T—requiring that an adequate truth definition generate all instances of the schema "'p' is true if and only if p"—and his recursive definition of truth for formal languages set the standard of rigor. Deflationists read Tarski as showing that truth is a logical device for disquotation; inflationists read him as providing the skeleton of a substantive semantic theory. Either way, his work is unavoidable.

## W.V.O. Quine (1908–2000)

Quine's disquotationalism treats the truth predicate as a device of "semantic ascent": it allows us to say things like "Everything Einstein said about relativity is true" without listing all the sentences Einstein uttered. The truth predicate enables generalizations over sentences, but it has no further metaphysical significance. Quine's nominalist and naturalistically austere framework made deflation attractive to philosophers who wanted to minimize ontological commitments while preserving the logical behavior of "true."

## Paul Horwich (b. 1947)

Horwich's minimalism, developed in his 1990 book *Truth*, is the most carefully worked-out contemporary deflationary position. Horwich holds that the entire meaning and use of "true" is captured by our acceptance of all instances of the equivalence schema: <p> is true iff p. He explicitly rejects the claim that these instances need to be unified by any deeper property or nature. Horwich's minimalism has been particularly influential because of its precision: it makes specific commitments that critics can test, and it has generated a sustained literature of objection and response.

## Dorothy Grover (b. 1936)

Grover, together with Camp and Belnap, developed the prosentential theory of truth, which holds that "true" is not a predicate at all but rather a proform—an expression that inherits its content from context, analogously to how pronouns inherit their reference. "That's true," on this view, is a prosentence meaning roughly "It is so." The prosentential theory explains why "true" seems redundant in many contexts while also accounting for its grammatical behavior as a noun phrase, offering an alternative to both minimalism and redundancy theories.

## Hartry Field (b. 1946)

Field's 1972 paper arguing that Tarski's definition is physicalistically inadequate—because it reduces truth to reference and satisfaction without explaining what makes those relations physical—challenged deflationists to say whether they are committed to a substantive theory of reference or not. Field later shifted to developing his own deflationary view in which truth is understood in terms of a property that is useful for expressing generalizations and facilitating inference, without any deeper metaphysical nature. His work bridges the philosophy of language and formal logic.
