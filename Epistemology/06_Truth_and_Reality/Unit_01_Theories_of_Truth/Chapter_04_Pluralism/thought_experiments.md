## The Mixed Conjunction (The Mixing Problem)

**The scenario**: Consider the sentence: "Torturing innocents is wrong and water is H₂O." This is a conjunction of a moral claim and an empirical claim. A pluralist who holds that moral truths are governed by one truth property (say, superassertibility) and physical truths by another (correspondence) must say what truth property governs this conjunction. If the conjunction is true, which truth property is it? It cannot simply be superassertibility (since the physical conjunct requires correspondence) nor simply correspondence (since the moral conjunct, on many pluralist views, doesn't correspond to physical facts).

**The philosophical point**: The mixing problem, pressed by philosophers including Tapani Salminen and later developed extensively in the literature, is the central challenge for pluralism. Lynch's functionalist response is that the conjunction is true in the generic functional sense—it plays the truth role—even though each conjunct is true in a domain-specific sense. Critics argue this collapses into monism about truth at the conjunctive level, undermining the distinctiveness of pluralism. The problem is acute for any pluralism that wants both genuine domain specificity and a unified logical framework.

## Superassertibility vs. Correspondence

**The scenario**: Is the moral claim "gratuitous cruelty is wrong" true in the same sense as "lead is denser than aluminum"? A physical realist says the density claim corresponds to mind-independent physical facts; a non-cognitivist says the moral claim merely expresses an attitude. Wright's pluralism proposes a middle path: "gratuitous cruelty is wrong" is true in the sense of being superassertible—any sufficiently informed and reflective inquirer would be warranted in asserting it, and this warrant could not be defeated by additional information. But the density claim is true in a more robustly correspondence-like sense.

**The philosophical point**: This contrast illustrates Wright's core pluralist claim that different discourse areas warrant different notions of truth without either dismissing moral discourse as non-cognitive or making moral truths metaphysically equivalent to physical truths. The philosophical question is whether superassertibility is genuinely a truth property or merely an epistemic standard dressed up as truth. Correspondence realists about morality (like Russ Shafer-Landau) argue that robust moral truth requires more than superassertibility; error theorists (like Mackie) argue that superassertibility is not enough to save moral truth.

## Lynch's Functionalist Vision

**The scenario**: Imagine a versatile tool—say, a multi-function Swiss army knife—that can perform different physical operations (cutting, filing, uncorking) through different blades, yet there is a single property common to all these operations: performing the designated tool-function. Lynch proposes that truth is like this: there is a single truth-role (being a norm of assertion, being transparent: p is true iff p, etc.) that can be realized by different properties in different domains. In mathematics, a Platonist truth-maker realizes the role; in everyday perception, correspondence does so; in ethics, superassertibility might.

**The philosophical point**: Lynch's functionalism is designed to give pluralism a unified foundation: truth is one thing (one role), realized by many things (different properties). But critics press the question of whether the truth-role itself is characterized in a domain-neutral way—if the role is defined by conditions that include "corresponds to reality," then mathematical or ethical truth either satisfies that condition or fails to qualify as truth at all. The functionalist vision requires a truth-role specification that is genuinely neutral between domains, and it is not obvious that any such specification is available.

## The Logical Reasoning Problem

**The scenario**: A detective reasons: "Either the suspect was in London or the suspect was in Paris. If he was in London, then his alibi is false (a physical, correspondence-governed truth). If he was in Paris, then he has committed perjury (a legal/normative truth, perhaps governed by a different truth property). He was in one place or the other. Therefore, either his alibi is false or he has committed perjury." This reasoning is valid. But the conclusion mixes truth domains. Can a pluralist validate such reasoning across domains?

**The philosophical point**: This is a version of the logical reasoning challenge to pluralism, related to but distinct from the mixing problem. Valid logical inference—modus ponens, disjunctive syllogism, etc.—seems to require that the truth predicate behave uniformly across domains: if p is true (in any sense) and p implies q, then q is true. But if "true" means different things in different domains, it is unclear that standard inference rules are valid across the resulting heterogeneous truths. Pluralists must either argue that logic is domain-neutral (using the functional notion of truth) or accept some revisionary logic for cross-domain reasoning.
