# Metaphysical Realism

Metaphysical realism is the view that there is a mind-independent reality — a world that exists and has determinate features independently of what any mind thinks about it. This is not a single theory but a family of commitments that form the background assumption of much of science, common sense, and a large portion of philosophy. Its denial generates the various forms of idealism and anti-realism that have been philosophically important from Berkeley to the present.

## The Core Commitments

Hilary Putnam, in his critical discussions of metaphysical realism, characterized it as involving three claims:

1. **The existence thesis**: The world consists of some fixed totality of mind-independent objects.
2. **The uniqueness thesis**: There is exactly one true and complete description of the world.
3. **The truth thesis**: Truth involves some form of correspondence or representation-to-fact, not just epistemic utility or coherence.

This formulation captures what makes realism distinctive: not just that there is something out there, but that there is a *determinate* structure to what is out there, independent of how we describe or conceptualize it.

## Scientific Realism

Scientific realism is a specific form of realism about the entities posited by our best scientific theories. The scientific realist holds that electrons, quarks, black holes, and other theoretical entities really exist — they are not just useful fictions for organizing observations. The success of science at predicting and manipulating the natural world is best explained by supposing that scientific theories are, at least approximately, true of a mind-independent reality.

The standard argument for scientific realism is the *no-miracles argument* (Putnam): the empirical success of science would be miraculous if our best theories were not at least approximately true and if the entities they posit did not really exist. The best explanation of scientific success is realism.

Arguments against scientific realism include the *pessimistic meta-induction* (Laudan): the history of science shows that even highly successful theories have been replaced by incompatible successors (Newtonian mechanics replaced by relativity and quantum mechanics). If past successful theories were not approximately true, why should we believe current ones are?

## Realism About Specific Domains

Metaphysical realism is typically applied domain by domain: one can be a realist about physics while being an anti-realist about ethics, a realist about the past while being anti-realist about possible worlds, and so on. Each domain raises specific questions:

**Mathematical realism (Platonism)**: Numbers, sets, and other mathematical objects exist mind-independently. The success of mathematics in describing the physical world and the apparent objectivity of mathematical truth support this view. The challenge: how do we know about abstract, non-spatiotemporal entities?

**Moral realism**: Moral facts exist mind-independently; moral statements are genuinely true or false. The challenge: what is the metaphysical nature of moral facts, and how do we know them?

**Modal realism**: There are real possible worlds, not just actual things that could be otherwise. David Lewis's extreme modal realism holds that all possible worlds are equally real as our actual world.

## The Nature of Mind-Independence

"Mind-independence" requires careful formulation. The obvious intended meaning: whether there is a cat on the mat doesn't depend on whether anyone believes there is, or on whether anyone observes it, or on the concepts people use to describe the world. Reality is not constituted by mental activity.

But the realist must be careful not to claim too much. Some things are clearly mind-dependent in various senses — currencies, marriages, laws — without this making anti-realism true. The relevant independence is: the fundamental facts about the physical world do not depend on minds for their existence or character.

## Kant's Distinction

Kant's distinction between *phenomena* (things as they appear to us, structured by our categories) and *noumena* (things in themselves, independent of our cognition) sets up a version of this debate. For Kant, we can only know phenomena; noumena are unknowable. This is a form of anti-realism about knowledge of things-in-themselves while being realist about the empirical world (which is, however, structured by our cognitive apparatus).

Post-Kantian idealists (Hegel, Fichte) denied the coherence of noumena altogether, pushing toward a more thoroughgoing idealism. Neo-Kantian attempts to recover a viable realism constitute much of the history of 19th and 20th century metaphysics.
