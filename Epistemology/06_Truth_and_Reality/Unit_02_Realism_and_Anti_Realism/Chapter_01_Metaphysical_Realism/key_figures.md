## Immanuel Kant (1724–1804)

Kant's transcendental idealism transformed the realism debate by distinguishing phenomena (the world as it appears to us, structured by our cognitive faculties) from noumena (things as they are in themselves, independent of our cognition). Kant was a realist about the empirical world of phenomena but agnostic or deflationary about the noumenal world. This split—between the world-as-accessible and the world-as-independent—defines the terrain on which modern realism debates are fought. Metaphysical realists want to reclaim the noumenal; anti-realists typically embrace some version of the phenomenal.

## Hilary Putnam (1926–2016)

Putnam's career traverses the realism debate in a uniquely illuminating way. His early work on semantic externalism supported realism; his model-theoretic argument against "metaphysical realism" (the view that truth is a radically non-epistemic correspondence to the world) turned anti-realist; his "internal realism" offered a middle path where truth is idealized rational acceptability within a conceptual scheme. Putnam's persistent refinement of his views—culminating in "natural realism"—shows how difficult it is to hold a coherent metaphysical realist position.

## Michael Devitt (b. 1938)

Devitt offers the most straightforward contemporary defense of metaphysical realism, distinguishing it sharply from semantic debates about truth. His realism has two components: existence (entities of the relevant kinds exist) and independence (those entities are independent of minds and language). Devitt insists that realism is an empirical thesis, not a priori, and that it is best supported by the overall success of scientific inquiry. His critique of anti-realist alternatives—including Putnam's internal realism and Dummett's anti-realism—is precise and sustained.

## Stathis Psillos (b. 1965)

Psillos is the most systematic contemporary defender of scientific realism. His *Scientific Realism: How Science Tracks Truth* defends the no-miracles argument—the argument that the predictive success of mature scientific theories is best explained by their approximate truth—while answering the pessimistic meta-induction. Psillos draws an important distinction between the "working posits" of successful theories (whose reality is supported by the no-miracles argument) and their "idle posits" (whose reality is not so supported), allowing a selective defense of realism.

## Penelope Maddy (b. 1950)

Maddy's work on mathematical realism navigates between Gödel's pure Platonism and naturalistic constraints. Her early defense of mathematical realism argued that mathematical objects are perceivable sets; her later "naturalism" retreated from this to focus on the mathematical methods and practices that scientists actually use. Maddy's work shows that realism debates about abstract objects differ importantly from realism debates about physical unobservables, and that mathematics presents distinctive challenges for any uniform realism.

## Larry Laudan (1941–2022)

Laudan developed the pessimistic meta-induction—the anti-realist argument that the history of science is littered with successful but ultimately false theories (phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous ether), and that we should therefore not conclude from current scientific success that current theories are approximately true. His careful historical case-studies forced scientific realists to develop more nuanced positions (like Psillos's selective realism) rather than relying on the blanket no-miracles argument.
