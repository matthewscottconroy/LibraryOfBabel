## The No-Miracles Argument

**The scenario**: Consider the astonishing predictive success of mature scientific theories—Maxwell's electromagnetism predicting radio waves, general relativity predicting gravitational lensing, quantum electrodynamics making accurate predictions to twelve decimal places. Now ask: what is the best explanation for this success? One answer: the theories are approximately true—they accurately describe the structure of the world—and so of course they make good predictions. The alternative—that the theories are radically false yet somehow generate accurate predictions—would be a miraculous coincidence.

**The philosophical point**: The no-miracles argument (associated with Putnam) is the central positive argument for scientific realism. Its force depends on inference to the best explanation: if approximate truth is the best explanation of predictive success, and if IBE is a reliable form of reasoning, then we have good grounds for scientific realism. Critics (anti-realists like van Fraassen) argue that this reasoning is circular—it uses IBE to justify IBE—and that all we can establish is that theories are "empirically adequate," not that their theoretical claims about unobservables are true.

## The Pessimistic Meta-Induction

**The scenario**: Phlogiston theory was enormously successful at organizing chemistry in the eighteenth century; caloric theory successfully explained heat transfer; the luminiferous ether was posited to explain wave propagation of light. All three were subsequently abandoned as straightforwardly false—not even approximately true. The same pattern appears throughout the history of science. Now consider: our current best theories will likely appear to future scientists as we regard phlogiston. Should we therefore withhold realist commitment to current theoretical entities?

**The philosophical point**: Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction is the strongest argument against scientific realism. If past success was no indicator of truth, the no-miracles argument cannot establish that current success indicates truth. Realists respond in several ways: by arguing that the abandoned theories were not as successful as supposed; by distinguishing the "working posits" (electrons, DNA) that were retained through theory change from "idle posits" (ether) that were abandoned; and by arguing for "structural realism"—the view that science tracks the structure of reality even when it fails about the nature of its posits.

## Putnam's Model-Theoretic Argument

**The scenario**: Suppose the metaphysical realist holds that the world consists of a fixed set of objects and that our theories are true or false depending on whether their terms refer to the right objects. Now consider all possible ways of mapping our theoretical terms onto real-world objects—all possible "models" of our theory. If our theory is consistent, there will be many such models, including models that make the theory trivially true by mapping all its terms to arbitrary objects. What fixes the "intended" interpretation—the one that captures what our terms really refer to?

**The philosophical point**: Putnam's model-theoretic argument challenges the coherence of metaphysical realism's notion of "objective reference." If reference is determined entirely by the formal structure of the theory, then any consistent theory has a model—and so is trivially "true" in some model. The metaphysical realist needs some further constraint (causal, epistemic, or otherwise) to single out the intended model. But if such constraints are available, they must come from within our epistemic practices—suggesting that truth is internally related to rational acceptability after all. The argument drives Putnam toward internal realism.

## The Benign Neglect of Mathematical Objects

**The scenario**: A physicist writes down equations using real numbers, Hilbert spaces, differential geometry. She makes startlingly accurate predictions. Does her success depend on real numbers existing as abstract objects? A mathematical fictionalist says no: the physicist uses mathematical "fictions" as convenient tools; her predictions succeed because the physical world has certain structural properties, not because abstract numbers exist. A mathematical Platonist says yes: the uncanny applicability of mathematics to physics is best explained by the reality of mathematical objects.

**The philosophical point**: This thought experiment (related to the "unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics" puzzle) probes what mathematical realism requires. If mathematical entities are genuinely real, we need an account of how a physicist epistemically accesses them—and abstract entities seem causally inert. If mathematical entities are fictions, we need an account of why fictions are so precisely predictive. The case tests whether the no-miracles argument applies in the mathematical domain and whether the pessimistic meta-induction has a mathematical analogue.

## Things in Themselves

**The scenario**: Kant asks us to consider what the world would be like if there were no minds to perceive it. Would space and time exist? Kant's answer is no: space and time are forms of our intuition, not features of things in themselves. Now consider: science describes the world using spatial and temporal concepts. If space and time are contributions of the perceiving mind, what do scientific theories—framed in terms of space and time—actually tell us about mind-independent reality?

**The philosophical point**: Kant's phenomenon/noumenon distinction raises a persistent question for realism: is there a coherent notion of "the world as it is independently of any cognitive contribution"? Metaphysical realists insist there is, and that science approximates this. But Kant's argument—that our very capacity to describe the world requires imposing cognitive structures—threatens to make every description scheme-relative. Neo-Kantian philosophers and constructivists extend this point; anti-realists argue it establishes that the "things in themselves" concept is empty and should be abandoned.
