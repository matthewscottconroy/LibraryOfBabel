# Dummett and Anti-Realism

Michael Dummett's anti-realism is the most systematic and philosophically sophisticated form of contemporary anti-realism. Dummett argues that realism — the view that statements have determinate truth values independent of our capacity to verify them — is undermined by attention to the conditions for understanding language. The key move: understanding a statement consists in knowing its conditions of correct assertion, not its verification-transcendent truth conditions.

## The Manifestation Challenge

Dummett's central argument against realism is the *manifestation challenge*. Understanding a sentence consists in knowing what would verify or falsify it — knowing what would count as evidence for or against it. If the meaning of a sentence were given by its verification-transcendent truth conditions, then competent speakers would need to know something that cannot be manifested in their behavior or inferred from their linguistic practice.

Suppose a realist says: "There is a mountain higher than Everest on a planet in a distant galaxy that will never be visited." The realist claims this statement is either true or false, even though we can never verify it. But what does understanding this statement consist in? If it consists in knowing when to assert it (when it would be verified), we grasp its meaning. If it consists in knowing some further, verification-transcendent truth condition, we cannot specify what that knowledge amounts to — it cannot be manifested in anything we do or say.

This is the manifestation challenge: a verificationist account of meaning (understanding = knowing assertibility conditions) threatens the realist's claim that statements can have determinate truth values beyond our capacity to recognize them.

## The Acquisition Argument

A related argument concerns the acquisition of meaning. We learn language through contact with other speakers and with the world. What we can acquire from this process is, at most, conditional dispositions to assert and deny — patterns of linguistic behavior in response to evidence. We cannot acquire from this process the grasp of verification-transcendent truth conditions. If meaning cannot be acquired in this way, then meaning cannot consist in such conditions.

## The Semantic Basis of the Debate

Dummett connects the realism/anti-realism debate to the logic one endorses. Classical logic includes the *law of excluded middle* (for any P, either P or not-P) and its consequences. If we reject verification-transcendent truth values, we have no basis for excluded middle for unverifiable sentences. Anti-realism supports *intuitionistic logic*, which rejects excluded middle and double-negation elimination.

This is a significant revisionary proposal: intuitionistic logic is weaker than classical logic. Many classical theorems have no intuitionistic proof. Dummett's anti-realism, if correct, requires revising not just metaphysics but logic.

## Realism and the Principle of Bivalence

The realism/anti-realism debate can be precisely formulated around *bivalence*: every statement is either true or false. A realist about a domain holds bivalence for that domain; an anti-realist denies it.

For example:
- **Moral realism vs. expressivism**: Moral realism holds that moral statements have determinate truth values; expressivism/non-cognitivism holds that they are not truth-apt (or, in some formulations, that they lack verification-transcendent truth values).
- **Mathematical Platonism vs. intuitionism**: Platonism holds bivalence for mathematical statements (e.g., the Continuum Hypothesis is either true or false); intuitionism holds that mathematical statements are true only when we can prove them and false only when we can refute them — unproved and unrefuted statements may be neither.

## Constructive Empiricism

Bas van Fraassen's *constructive empiricism* (not exactly Dummett's position but related) holds that we should only believe the observable consequences of our scientific theories, not their claims about unobservable entities. This is anti-realism specifically about unobservables: electrons and quarks are useful theoretical posits, but we have no reason to believe they actually exist beyond their role in predicting observations.

Van Fraassen distinguishes *accepting* a theory (believing it is empirically adequate — that it correctly predicts observations) from *believing* it (believing it is true). Scientists ought to accept theories but need not believe them.

## The Legacy

Dummett's anti-realism has been deeply influential in analytic philosophy, particularly in the philosophy of mathematics and logic. The debate it generated — about meaning, understanding, and the metaphysical underpinnings of logic — is one of the central debates of 20th-century analytic philosophy. Even philosophers who reject Dummett's conclusions often accept that the connection between meaning and use, and the manifestation constraint on content, are important considerations.
