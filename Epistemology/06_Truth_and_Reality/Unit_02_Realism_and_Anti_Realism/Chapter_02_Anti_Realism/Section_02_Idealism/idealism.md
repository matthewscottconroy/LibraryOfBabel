# Idealism: Mind and Reality

Idealism is the metaphysical view that reality is fundamentally mental or mind-dependent. In its strongest forms, idealism holds that there is no mind-independent material world — what we call "physical objects" are really ideas, experiences, or mental constructs. Idealism has a distinguished philosophical history, from Berkeley's immaterialism through Kant's transcendental idealism to Hegel's absolute idealism, and continues to find defenders in contemporary metaphysics.

## Berkeley's Immaterialism

George Berkeley's *A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge* (1710) makes the central idealist argument. Berkeley denies the existence of matter — of any mind-independent material substance. Everything that exists is either a mind or an idea in a mind.

Berkeley's argument: matter is by definition something whose existence consists in being a support for sensible qualities. But sensible qualities (color, shape, texture) are ideas — they exist only when perceived. "Esse est percipi" — to be is to be perceived. A material substance that bears qualities while being itself unperceived is a contradiction.

The result: ordinary objects (tables, trees, other people) exist — they are real. But they exist as ideas in minds. A tree exists when perceived by human minds; when no human mind perceives it, it exists because God continuously perceives it. God's constant perception sustains the existence of the "physical" world.

Berkeley's idealism was partly a response to the skepticism generated by Locke's distinction between primary and secondary qualities and his indirect realism. If physical objects are directly given as ideas, there is no "veil of perception" to worry about — we directly perceive tables and trees without any mediating representation.

## Kant's Transcendental Idealism

Kant's transcendental idealism is more subtle. Kant does not deny the existence of a mind-independent reality; he holds that we cannot know things-in-themselves (*Dinge an sich*) — reality as it is independent of our cognitive apparatus. We only know *phenomena* — reality as structured by the forms of intuition (space and time) and the categories of understanding (causality, substance, etc.).

This is a form of idealism because the *form* of our experience — spatiotemporality, causal structure — is contributed by our minds, not read off from mind-independent reality. The world as we know it is not the world as it is in itself.

Kant's transcendental idealism is combined with *empirical realism*: within the framework of our cognitive constitution, objects are real, genuinely there to be known, not merely figments of individual imagination. The distinction is between transcendental idealism (the conditions of knowledge are mind-contributed) and empirical idealism (each person's objects are merely their private ideas).

## Hegel's Absolute Idealism

Hegel radicalized Kant by rejecting the thing-in-itself. The very idea of something beyond all possible experience is, for Hegel, incoherent — we cannot even coherently *refer* to something entirely beyond thought. Therefore, reality cannot be divided into knowable appearances and unknowable things-in-themselves.

Hegel's absolute idealism holds that reality is the self-development of Absolute Spirit — a single, all-encompassing mental whole that comes to know itself through the history of human consciousness, culture, and philosophy. The apparent dualisms (subject/object, mind/nature, freedom/necessity) are moments in the dialectical self-development of the Absolute, ultimately reconciled in the comprehensive system.

## Contemporary Idealism

Contemporary analytic metaphysics has seen renewed interest in idealism. John Foster's *The Case for Idealism* (1982) defends a neo-Berkeleyan view. More recently, Philip Goff and others have defended *panpsychism* — the view that mentality is a fundamental feature of reality — which, in some forms, approaches idealism.

Idealism also receives support from interpretations of quantum mechanics. On some interpretations (Copenhagen, relational quantum mechanics), physical properties are not determinate prior to observation — they are, in some sense, constituted by or relative to measurement contexts. While not straightforwardly idealist, these interpretations challenge the realist picture of a mind-independent, determinate physical world.

## The Objection from Intersubjectivity

The most common objection to idealism (especially Berkeleyan varieties) is: if reality consists of ideas in minds, what explains the intersubjective agreement between different minds? Why do different people's experiences of the "same" table agree? Berkeley's answer (God coordinates experience) may be thought too ad hoc. The physical world hypothesis — that there is a mind-independent world that causes all our experiences — explains intersubjectivity more naturally.
