## George Berkeley (1685–1753)

Berkeley's immaterialism is the most radical anti-realist position in the early modern tradition. His central thesis—esse est percipi (to be is to be perceived)—holds that material objects exist only as ideas in minds. Berkeley argued that the concept of a mind-independent material substance is incoherent (since we could only ever know ideas, not their supposed external causes) and unnecessary (since God's continuous perception guarantees the stability of the world). Though contemporary philosophers rarely accept Berkeleyan idealism, his arguments sharply isolate the epistemological challenge facing any realism that posits objects beyond possible experience.

## Immanuel Kant (1724–1804)

Kant's transcendental idealism holds that the spatial and temporal structure of the world we experience is contributed by the cognitive faculties of the perceiver, not by things as they are in themselves. Empirical objects—the objects of science and ordinary experience—are phenomena, constituted by the forms of intuition (space and time) and the categories of the understanding. Kant believed this was the only way to account for the necessity and universality of mathematical and natural-scientific knowledge. His position remains the most sophisticated alternative to both naive realism and Berkeleyan immaterialism.

## G.W.F. Hegel (1770–1831)

Hegel transformed Kant's transcendental idealism into absolute idealism: reality is not a fixed domain of things-in-themselves but the progressive self-articulation of absolute Spirit (Geist). For Hegel, the separation of mind and world is itself a deficient stage of understanding that is overcome when thought recognizes itself in its objects. Hegel's philosophy of nature and mind treats the natural world as externalized Spirit; the task of philosophy is to show how finite things are internally related to the Absolute. Hegel's influence reaches contemporary philosophers through Brandom's inferentialism and McDowell's neo-Hegelian epistemology.

## Michael Dummett (1925–2011)

Dummett is the most important twentieth-century philosopher of anti-realism about semantics and logic. His central argument—the "manifestation challenge"—holds that if meaning is a mental state that could be entirely private and unmanifested, it cannot be communicated. Meaning must instead be publicly manifestable in conditions of use. For undecidable propositions (those whose truth value cannot in principle be verified), the meanings of "true" and "false" cannot be manifested, and so classical bivalence—the claim that every proposition is either true or false—must be abandoned in favor of intuitionist logic. Dummett's rigor forced realists to articulate their positions with unprecedented precision.

## Bas van Fraassen (b. 1941)

Van Fraassen's constructive empiricism offers an anti-realist philosophy of science that does not require full-blown idealism. He argues that the aim of science is empirical adequacy—saving the observable phenomena—not truth about unobservable entities. We can accept a theory for practical purposes (use it, act on it) without believing its claims about electrons and quarks. Van Fraassen's position is anti-realist about unobservables while remaining broadly realist about observables. His rigorous philosophy of physics has shaped debates about quantum mechanics, modality, and scientific inference.

## L.E.J. Brouwer (1881–1966)

Brouwer founded intuitionism in mathematics, arguing that mathematical objects are mental constructions and that a proof-independent notion of mathematical truth is incoherent. His rejection of the law of excluded middle for mathematical propositions—grounded in the claim that we cannot assert a disjunction unless we have a proof of one disjunct—parallels Dummett's anti-realist semantics for empirical discourse. Brouwer's intuitionism remains the most developed example of an anti-realist position in the philosophy of mathematics, though it comes at the cost of rejecting significant portions of classical mathematics.
