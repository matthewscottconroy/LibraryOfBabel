## The Manifestation Challenge

**The scenario**: A child learning the word "red" is trained in its use through public, observable cases: red apples, red fire engines, red stoplights. She comes to use the word correctly in observable circumstances. Now consider a realist who says the child has learned to associate "red" with a mind-independent property of surfaces—a property that would obtain even if no one perceived it. Dummett asks: in what does the child's grasp of this mind-independent meaning consist? She cannot manifest any grasp that goes beyond her dispositions to apply the word in observable cases.

**The philosophical point**: The manifestation challenge targets the realist's claim that we understand statements that are potentially verification-transcendent. If meaning is fully captured by conditions of use—by dispositions to assert and deny—then the realist's claimed surplus meaning (the mind-independent property the word "really" refers to) is empty. The argument has implications for logic: if we cannot manifest understanding of verification-transcendent truth, we cannot make sense of classical bivalence for undecidable sentences. This is Dummett's route from semantics to intuitionism.

## Berkeley's Unperceived Tree

**The scenario**: Does a tree in a forest make a sound when it falls, if no one is there to hear it? More fundamentally, does the tree exist when no one is perceiving it? Berkeley's answer, in the *Three Dialogues*, is that the tree continues to exist because God is always perceiving it. But his questioner asks: can we form a coherent conception of an object that exists unperceived? When we try to imagine the unperceived tree, we find we are simply imagining ourselves perceiving it—which is not the same as imagining it unperceived.

**The philosophical point**: Berkeley's argument—that conceiving of an unperceived object is self-defeating—is an early form of what later philosophers call the anti-realist argument from conceivability. If every attempt to conceive of mind-independent existence involves the conceiving mind, then the concept of mind-independent existence may be empty or incoherent. Realists respond that conceiving of something does not require imagining oneself perceiving it; one can conceptually represent objects whose existence is not tied to any perceiver. The exchange crystallizes the most basic question in realism debates.

## Van Fraassen's Observability Boundary

**The scenario**: You observe bacteria through a microscope. Are you observing the bacteria, or only observing an image produced by the instrument? Van Fraassen draws a line between what is observable (in principle visible to the naked eye, given ideal conditions) and what is merely detectable (accessible only through instruments). Electrons are merely detectable—never observable. He argues that we are rationally entitled to believe only in the existence of observables; our attitude toward unobservables should be acceptance for practical purposes, not belief.

**The philosophical point**: Van Fraassen's observable/unobservable distinction is the hinge of constructive empiricism. Critics (like Churchland and Maxwell) argue the distinction is untenable: there is no principled boundary between extending human perception through glasses, telescopes, and electron microscopes—all extend our epistemic reach by physical means. If the realist can respond that glasses and telescopes are continuous with unaided perception, van Fraassen's anti-realism seems arbitrary. Van Fraassen responds that the relevant point is anthropocentric: we could in principle observe some entities unaided; others we could never observe regardless of our physical location and condition.

## The Intuitionist's Excluded Middle

**The scenario**: Consider Goldbach's Conjecture: every even number greater than 2 is the sum of two primes. As of now, this has neither been proved nor disproved, and no one knows whether it is true or false. The classical logician says: it is either true or false; we just don't know which. The intuitionist (following Dummett) says: "Goldbach's Conjecture is either true or false" is itself a claim we are not entitled to make unless we have a proof of it or a proof of its negation. Since we have neither, we should not assert bivalence here.

**The philosophical point**: This thought experiment makes concrete what is at stake in the dispute between classical and intuitionist logic. The anti-realist rejects classical bivalence for undecidable propositions on the grounds that there is nothing for the truth-value to consist in if it outstrips all possible evidence. The realist insists that mathematical truth is perfectly determinate even where proofs are lacking. This is not merely a technical logical dispute: it reflects deep disagreement about whether mathematical truth is constituted by our practices of proof-construction or whether it is a mind-independent domain we aim to access.

## The Empirically Equivalent Rivals

**The scenario**: Van Fraassen asks us to consider two theories that are empirically equivalent—they make exactly the same observable predictions—but differ in their claims about unobservables. Theory A says neutrinos have a specific mass; Theory B says there are no neutrinos but introduces different unobservable fields that produce identical observable effects. If the theories are genuinely empirically equivalent, the realist has no empirical grounds to prefer one over the other. But the realist claims they may differ in truth value.

**The philosophical point**: This thought experiment is the constructive empiricist's strongest move. If empirical evidence can never distinguish between empirically equivalent rivals, then the realist's claim that one of them is (mind-independently) true adds nothing epistemically useful. The realist typically responds that empirical equivalence is rarely permanent—future observations may distinguish them—or by invoking theoretical virtues (simplicity, explanatory power) as truth-indicators beyond empirical adequacy. Van Fraassen accepts the latter as pragmatic virtues but denies they track truth about unobservables.
