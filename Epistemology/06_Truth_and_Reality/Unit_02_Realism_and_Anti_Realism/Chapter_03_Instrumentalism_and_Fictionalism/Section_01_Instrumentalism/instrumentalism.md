# Instrumentalism and Fictionalism

Instrumentalism and fictionalism are anti-realist positions that accept the *utility* of theories, concepts, or statements while denying that they are *true*. The instrumentalist treats theoretical claims as tools for organizing experience and generating predictions, not as genuine descriptions of reality. The fictionalist holds that we speak *as if* certain things exist or are true, without being committed to their reality.

## Instrumentalism in the Philosophy of Science

Scientific instrumentalism holds that scientific theories are not literally true descriptions of unobservable entities and processes; they are instruments for organizing observations and generating predictions. Electrons, quarks, fields, and curved spacetime are not real entities that we theorize about; they are computational tools in a system for predicting observational outcomes.

The instrumentalist position was historically associated with Ernst Mach and later with some versions of logical empiricism. Mach denied the existence of atoms (before their indirect observation became compelling), arguing that atomic theory was a useful fiction for organizing chemical and physical observations.

The motivation for instrumentalism:
- **Epistemic humility**: We only have direct access to observations; claims about unobservables go beyond what we can know.
- **History of science**: Successful theories have been repeatedly replaced; their theoretical entities have been "eliminated." Perhaps we shouldn't believe in current theoretical entities either.
- **Underdetermination**: Multiple incompatible theories can be observationally equivalent; no evidence can adjudicate between them if both are empirically adequate.

Van Fraassen's *constructive empiricism* is the most carefully developed contemporary instrumentalism. He distinguishes between what a theory *says* (literal content) and what we should *believe* (only the empirical adequacy of the theory — that it correctly describes observable phenomena). We need not believe claims about unobservables.

## Fictionalism

Fictionalism holds that a class of statements should be understood as a useful fiction: we engage with them as if they were true, gaining the cognitive and practical benefits of this engagement, while knowing or accepting that they aren't literally true.

**Mathematical fictionalism** (Hartry Field): Mathematical statements are false — there are no numbers, sets, or functions. But mathematical theories are useful fictions that can be used as inference licenses without being true. Field's program attempts to "nominalize" physics — to show that the mathematical machinery used in physics is eliminable, that all physical truths can be stated without quantifying over abstract objects.

**Moral fictionalism**: Moral statements are false (there are no moral facts), but we are better off engaging with them as if they were true (quasi-asserting them rather than asserting them). Richard Joyce argues for moral fictionalism: our moral practices are evolutionary adaptations that generate useful commitments, but they don't involve genuine beliefs about mind-independent moral facts.

**Modal fictionalism** (Gideon Rosen): We speak as if possible worlds exist, gaining the benefits of possible-world semantics, without being ontologically committed to the existence of possible worlds. Modal claims are true only in the fiction of modal realism.

## The Meta-Fictional Stance

A crucial distinction for fictionalism: the difference between speaking *within* a fiction and speaking *about* it. When engaged in fiction, we say things like "Sherlock Holmes lived at 221B Baker Street." When speaking about the fiction, we say "According to Doyle's stories, Sherlock Holmes lived at 221B Baker Street."

The fictionalist proposes that our engagement with mathematics, modality, or morality is more like the first: we speak within the fiction, using the fiction's vocabulary, without the metalinguistic "according to the fiction" qualifier. Whether this is phenomenologically accurate — whether this is really how we engage with these domains — is deeply debated.

## Instrumentalism, Fictionalism, and Cognitive Values

Both instrumentalism and fictionalism raise questions about the value of truth in inquiry. If we are rational in using theories or stories we believe to be false, what role does truth play? One answer: truth plays a role, but it is truth-within-the-fiction or empirical adequacy, not literal truth about unobservables. Another answer: truth is instrumentally valuable, and instruments need not themselves be true.

The deeper issue is whether the theoretical virtues — explanatory power, predictive success, unification — give us reason to believe theories or merely to use them. Realists argue that they give us reason to believe; instrumentalists and fictionalists that they give us reason to use.
