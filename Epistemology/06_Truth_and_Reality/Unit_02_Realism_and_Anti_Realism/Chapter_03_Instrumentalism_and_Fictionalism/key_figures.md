## Ernst Mach (1838–1916)

Mach was the most influential scientific instrumentalist of the nineteenth century. He held that science aims at the economical description and prediction of experience, not the discovery of hidden entities; atoms, for instance, were for Mach merely convenient fictions for calculation. His influence on the Vienna Circle and logical positivism was substantial, and his critique of Newtonian absolute space shaped both Einstein's development of relativity and later debates about scientific ontology. Mach's legacy is the idea that theoretical entities earn their keep by their predictive utility, not by being discovered to exist.

## Pierre Duhem (1861–1916)

Duhem's philosophy of physics argued that theoretical hypotheses are not individually testable but face experience only as whole systems (the "Duhem-Quine thesis"). He held that the theoretical part of physics is a system of mathematical propositions whose value lies in the classification and prediction of observations, not in revealing the nature of underlying reality. For Duhem, scientific theories are logically prior to experiment in a way that prevents any simple falsification—a position that both instrumentalists and holists have exploited.

## Hartry Field (b. 1946)

Field is the most important contemporary mathematical fictionalist. His *Science Without Numbers* (1980) argued that the indispensability argument for mathematical Platonism fails because he can nominalize scientific theories—restate them without quantification over abstract objects—while preserving their physical content. Mathematics, on Field's view, is a useful fiction: it is "conservative" with respect to physical theories (adds no new physical consequences) but not true, because there are no mathematical objects. Field's program set the agenda for two decades of debate between Platonists and nominalists.

## Richard Joyce (b. 1966)

Joyce is the leading contemporary moral fictionalist. His *The Myth of Morality* (2001) argues that ordinary moral discourse makes claims that presuppose the existence of categorically binding moral facts—facts that exist independently of human desires and agreements. Since no such facts exist (his error theory), moral discourse is systematically in error. But rather than abandoning moral discourse, Joyce recommends a fictionalist stance: we engage in moral discourse as a useful fiction, knowing our claims are not literally true but valuing them for their social and motivational benefits.

## Gideon Rosen (b. 1962)

Rosen introduced modal fictionalism—the view that claims about possible worlds are useful for analyzing modal truths without ontological commitment to Lewis's concrete possible worlds. On modal fictionalism, "Necessarily, p" is analyzed as: "According to the fiction of possible worlds, p is true in all worlds." Rosen's paper opened a debate about whether fictionalism can coherently account for modal discourse: the challenge is that the fictionalist must use modal language to characterize the fiction itself, risking circularity.

## Bas van Fraassen (b. 1941)

Van Fraassen's constructive empiricism is instrumentalism refined to its contemporary form. He accepts that we can use theoretical claims about electrons and quarks as tools for organizing experience and making predictions; his anti-realism is specifically targeted at the demand that we believe in the existence of unobservables. Van Fraassen draws a careful distinction between accepting a theory (using it) and believing it (taking its theoretical claims to be true)—a distinction that runs through all sophisticated instrumentalist and fictionalist positions.
