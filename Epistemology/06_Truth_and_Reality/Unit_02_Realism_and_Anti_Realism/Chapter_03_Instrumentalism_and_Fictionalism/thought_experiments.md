## Mach's Economy of Thought

**The scenario**: A nineteenth-century physicist uses the kinetic theory of gases—treating gases as composed of tiny molecules in rapid motion—to derive the ideal gas law PV = nRT. The derivation is elegant and the predictions accurate. Mach asks: does this theoretical success give us reason to believe molecules exist? Or is the molecular theory simply an economical cognitive instrument—a mathematical scaffold that organizes our observations without corresponding to any hidden reality?

**The philosophical point**: Mach's instrumentalism takes predictive success as the only proper measure of a theory's value. The molecules are "fictions" in Mach's sense: useful conceptual entities that facilitate calculation without requiring ontological commitment. The challenge for Mach's view came when Jean Perrin's Brownian motion experiments (1908) provided what seemed like direct evidence for atoms—at which point the instrumentalist must say either that the evidence supports a true theory or that "direct" evidence can never cross the observability threshold. The case shows how empirical progress can strain instrumentalist constraints.

## Field's Nominalization Program

**The scenario**: Field asks us to imagine a physicist who needs to calculate the gravitational force between two bodies using Newton's formula F = Gm₁m₂/r². Standard presentations quantify over real numbers. Field claims he can reformulate Newtonian mechanics using only quantification over concrete physical regions of space-time and comparative relations among them—avoiding all reference to abstract numbers. If he succeeds, the physics is content-equivalent to the standard formulation, but without ontological commitment to abstract objects.

**The philosophical point**: Field's nominalization program, if successful, defeats the indispensability argument for mathematical Platonism. The argument runs: we are committed to the existence of whatever our best scientific theories quantify over, and our best theories quantify over numbers; therefore, numbers exist. Field attacks the first premise: we can do the physics without the numbers, so quantification over numbers is not indispensable. Critics (Malament, Shapiro, Resnik) argue that the nominalization is only feasible for classical mechanics and fails for quantum theories; others argue that Field's space-time points are just as abstract as numbers.

## Joyce's Moral Fictionalist in Practice

**The scenario**: A moral fictionalist, convinced that no categorical moral facts exist, nonetheless raises her children to be honest, kind, and fair. She tells them "it is wrong to lie" and "you ought to share." Does this make her a hypocrite? On Joyce's view, she is not asserting moral propositions with full literal sincerity—she knows the categorical moral authority those propositions presuppose is not real. But she uses moral language as a useful fiction: it is evolutionarily and socially adaptive, it coordinates behavior, and it motivates in ways that baldly self-interested language cannot.

**The philosophical point**: Joyce's fictionalism distinguishes between sincere assertion (claiming a proposition is literally true) and performance within a fiction (treating a proposition as if true for practical purposes). The challenge is whether this distinction is psychologically stable: can a person consistently hold moral beliefs as fictions while relying on them to motivate action? Critics (including Mackie-defenders and error theorists who favor abolitionism rather than fictionalism) argue that once you know beliefs are fictions, their motivational force erodes. Fictionalists reply that awareness of a fiction's status does not necessarily undermine it—we know novels are not true but they still move us.

## The Meta-Fictional Stance

**The scenario**: Consider someone who adopts fictionalism about possible worlds: "There are infinitely many possible worlds" is true, she says, "according to the modal fiction." Now she needs to express the modal fictionalist thesis itself: "Modal fictionalism is possibly true"—meaning that there is a possible world in which modal fictionalism is true. But to say this, she invokes possible worlds again. Is she speaking within the fiction or outside it? If inside, she cannot use the modal claim to evaluate the fiction from the outside; if outside, she has made a non-fictional modal claim.

**The philosophical point**: This self-referential puzzle is the "meta-fictional problem" for modal fictionalism, pressed by Rosen himself. The fictionalist uses modal language to characterize the fiction, but modal language is what the fiction is supposed to ground. The problem generalizes: any fictionalism that needs to characterize the fiction using the very discourse that is being fictioned may face a circularity or regress. Solutions include adopting a non-modal formulation of the fiction or accepting some primitive modal truths that are not grounded in the fiction.

## The Useful Lie

**The scenario**: A navigation system uses Newtonian mechanics rather than relativistic mechanics to compute GPS positions, knowing that Newtonian mechanics is strictly speaking false—the corrections needed for relativistic effects are negligible at the speeds and scales involved. The engineers know they are using a false theory. Is their use of Newtonian mechanics an example of instrumentalism in practice? And does this show that instrumentalism is ultimately committed to the view that false theories can be epistemically appropriate tools?

**The philosophical point**: This case tests whether instrumentalism entails a problematic pragmatism about truth. The GPS engineers are sophisticated users who know their theory is false and use it anyway. Van Fraassen would say this is perfectly rational: they accept the Newtonian theory for the domain—it is empirically adequate for the relevant range of phenomena—without believing it to be strictly true. This illustrates the distinction between acceptance and belief that is central to constructive empiricism. But it also raises the question of whether "empirically adequate in a domain" is itself a covert truth claim about observables.
