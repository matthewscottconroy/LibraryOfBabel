# Semantic Externalism: Meaning Ain't in the Head

Semantic externalism is the view that the meanings of at least some of our words and thoughts are determined not solely by what is in our minds — our mental representations, descriptions, or intentions — but also by factors in our environment and social context. Hilary Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment is the classic argument for this position, and it has had profound implications for philosophy of language, mind, and epistemology.

## Putnam's Twin Earth

Hilary Putnam's *The Meaning of "Meaning"* (1975) introduces the Twin Earth thought experiment. Imagine a planet exactly like Earth in every detail except one: what fills the lakes and oceans, what falls as rain, what people call "water" is not H₂O but some other compound, XYZ, which is phenomenologically identical to water (same appearance, taste, behavior) but chemically different.

On Twin Earth, the English-speaking inhabitants call XYZ "water." Before the discovery of molecular chemistry, an Earthling and their Twin Earth counterpart used the word "water" in identical ways: they looked the same, had the same functional roles, were associated with the same phenomenal properties.

Yet, Putnam argues, when the Earthling said "water" they meant H₂O, and when their Twin Earth counterpart said "water" they meant XYZ. The two words have different meanings even though the speakers' internal states are identical.

This is the slogan: "meanings just ain't in the head." What fixes the extension (reference) of "water" is not any internal representation but the actual chemical nature of the substance being referred to.

## The Indexical Picture of Natural Kind Terms

Putnam's explanation relies on natural kind terms functioning like indexicals. When you use a term for a natural kind — water, gold, tiger — you are pointing (implicitly) at the real kind that your environment exemplifies. The term picks out the kind that is "the same kind as this stuff" — where "this stuff" refers to the actual substance in your environment.

What determines the meaning of "water" in my mouth is what substance plays the role of water here — which is H₂O. What determines it in my Twin Earth counterpart's mouth is what substance plays the role there — XYZ. Same functional role; different substances; different meanings.

This introduces a division between what Putnam calls the *stereotype* (associated descriptions, typical properties) and the *extension* (actual reference). The stereotype can be the same while the extension differs.

## The Linguistic Division of Labor

Putnam adds a social dimension: ordinary speakers rely on experts to fix the extensions of natural kind terms. When I use "gold," I rely on chemists to have determined what gold really is (element with atomic number 79). I can defer to experts for the precise extension of terms I use. This is the *linguistic division of labor*: the meaning-determining work is distributed across the linguistic community.

This has important consequences: not every individual speaker needs to know what determines the extension of the terms they use. The content of their terms is determined by the community's experts and by the world they are embedded in.

## Burge's Social Externalism

Tyler Burge developed a complementary form of externalism in "Individualism and the Self" (1979). Burge's externalism is social rather than physical: the contents of one's mental states are partly determined by the social and linguistic community one belongs to, not just by the physical environment.

In Burge's classic case, Oscar uses the word "arthritis" and has beliefs about arthritis, but has a slightly incorrect understanding — he believes arthritis can occur in muscles (it can't; it's restricted to joints). Oscar's belief that he has arthritis in his thigh is nonetheless a belief *about arthritis* because his community's use determines the extension.

Now imagine a counterfactual Oscar who grew up in a community where "arthritis" is used for muscle inflammations as well. Counterfactual Oscar has the same internal states as Oscar but different beliefs — beliefs about "tharthritis" (the community's condition), not arthritis. Same internal states; different content.

## Epistemological Implications

Semantic externalism creates puzzles for epistemology, particularly for self-knowledge. If the contents of my thoughts are partly determined by my environment, then I may not have privileged access to what I am thinking. I might think I am thinking about water, but if my environment contains XYZ rather than H₂O, I am thinking about XYZ. And I don't know by introspection which substance my environment contains.

This tension between semantic externalism and the traditional doctrine of privileged access is the *McKinsey paradox* and has generated extensive discussion. How can it be that I know a priori what I am thinking (the traditional picture) while the content of my thought is determined by factors in my environment that I don't know a priori (Putnam/Burge)?
