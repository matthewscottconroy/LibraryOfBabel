## Hilary Putnam (1926–2016)

Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment is the most influential argument for semantic externalism. His thesis—that "meanings just ain't in the head"—argues that the extension of natural kind terms like "water" is determined not by mental representations alone but by the actual chemical constitution of the substance in the environment. Even if Oscar on Earth and Twin Oscar on Twin Earth are molecularly identical, their words "water" refer to different substances (H₂O and XYZ respectively), because reference is fixed by the actual kind in their environments and by the linguistic division of labor with experts. This severed the traditional Fregean link between sense (in the head) and reference (in the world).

## Tyler Burge (b. 1946)

Burge extended semantic externalism from natural kind terms to all intentional mental states through his arthritis thought experiment. If Bert mistakenly believes he has arthritis in his thigh (arthritis is only a joint disease), and a counterfactual Bert lives in a community where "arthritis" means something that can occur in thighs, then the two Burts have different mental contents despite being internally identical. Burge concluded that mental content is individuated partly by social facts about how words are used in the community, not just by what is "in the head." This "social externalism" is broader and more challenging than Putnam's natural kind externalism.

## Saul Kripke (1940–2022)

Kripke's arguments in *Naming and Necessity* provide the background framework for Putnam's externalism. Kripke argued that proper names and natural kind terms are rigid designators—they refer to the same individual or kind in all possible worlds in which that individual or kind exists. Natural kind terms like "gold" and "water" have their reference fixed by an initial baptism and causal-historical chains, not by descriptions. This picture supports Putnam's claim that the reference of "water" is fixed externally by the actual substance, not internally by speakers' mental representations.

## Jerry Fodor (1935–2017)

Fodor was semantic externalism's most important critic. He defended methodological solipsism—the view that psychological laws and explanations must be framed in terms of narrow content, content that supervenes on the internal states of the individual. If mental states have wide content (as Putnam and Burge argue), they cannot figure in causal-explanatory psychological laws, since the same internal state can have different wide content in different environments. Fodor's work forced externalists to explain how wide content can be epistemically and scientifically relevant.

## David Kaplan (1933–2022)

Kaplan's semantics for indexicals—expressions like "I," "here," "now"—provides the logical framework within which Putnam's externalism can be precisely stated. The idea that natural kind terms function as indexicals (their reference depending on the context of use, including the actual environment) derives from the Kripke-Putnam picture. Kaplan showed how character (the rule for determining content) can be distinguished from content (what is actually expressed in a context), allowing a precise statement of how the same "conceptual role" can yield different referents in different environments.

## Jessica Brown (b. 1970)

Brown has done the most sustained work defending externalism against epistemological objections, particularly the McKinsey paradox (the claim that combining externalism with privileged access to mental states allows a priori knowledge of contingent environmental facts). Her *Anti-Individualism and Knowledge* (2004) argues that the paradox rests on a mischaracterization of what privileged access amounts to and that externalism is compatible with the kind of first-person authority we actually have over our mental states.
