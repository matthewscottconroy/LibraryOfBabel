## Twin Earth

**The scenario**: Hilary Putnam asks us to imagine a planet—Twin Earth—that is qualitatively identical to Earth in every respect except that the substance the Twin Earthlings call "water" is not H₂O but a different compound, XYZ, which has the same superficial properties (clear, drinkable, found in rivers and seas) but a different chemical structure. In 1750, before chemistry had developed, an English speaker on Earth and their molecule-for-molecule duplicate on Twin Earth would have been in exactly the same psychological state when using the word "water." But Putnam argues that "water" means H₂O on Earth and XYZ on Twin Earth.

**The philosophical point**: Twin Earth establishes that meaning—in the sense of extension or reference—is not determined by anything "in the head." Two speakers can be psychologically identical yet use a word that refers to different things because the actual substances in their environments differ. This severs the traditional internalist assumption that meaning is a kind of mental content fully determined by the speaker's mental states. The philosophical upshot is that semantic facts depend partly on environmental facts, making meaning at least partially a matter of social and natural embedding.

## Burge's Arthritis Case

**The scenario**: Bert has a painful condition in his thigh and sincerely believes he has arthritis there. In fact, arthritis is defined as a disease of the joints, so his belief is false—he cannot have arthritis in his thigh. Now imagine a counterfactual Bert (call him Bert*) who is internally identical to Bert—same neural states, same functional organization—but lives in a community where "arthritis" is used for a broader range of rheumatoid conditions, including conditions that can occur in muscles. In Bert*'s community, his belief that he has "arthritis" in his thigh would be true, and the belief would have different content.

**The philosophical point**: Burge's case extends externalism to socially established meanings rather than natural kinds. Since Bert and Bert* are internally identical but have beliefs with different contents, content is not solely determined by what is inside the individual—it also depends on the social-linguistic community. This social externalism is more radical than Putnam's, because it applies to terms whose meanings are not fixed by experts with scientific knowledge but simply by conventional linguistic usage. Burge concludes that intentional mental states themselves—not just their linguistic expressions—have wide content.

## The Linguistic Division of Labor

**The scenario**: Most of us use the word "gold" confidently but cannot distinguish gold from fool's gold, or from yellow alloys, by inspection. We rely on metallurgists, jewelers, and chemists to determine what gold actually is. Putnam calls this the linguistic division of labor: ordinary speakers can use natural kind terms reliably without possessing expert knowledge, because the community contains experts who can determine the extension of the term. The extension of "gold" is fixed by what the experts call "gold," not by the average speaker's partial knowledge.

**The philosophical point**: The division of labor shows that semantic competence is a social phenomenon: it is distributed across the community, not possessed entirely by any individual speaker. This supports externalism (meaning depends partly on social facts about expert usage) and undermines the idea that every competent user of a term must have in their head a complete specification of its extension. It also raises questions about semantic authority: who counts as an expert, and what happens when experts disagree?

## McKinsey's Paradox

**The scenario**: Suppose externalism is true: Bert knows that his mental state M has the content "arthritis." And suppose he has privileged first-person access to his mental states. Then Bert can know a priori—without any further empirical investigation—that he has the mental state M. But by externalism, the content of M depends on social facts about how "arthritis" is used in his community. So Bert can know a priori what "arthritis" means in his community. But surely that is empirical knowledge, not a priori knowledge.

**The philosophical point**: McKinsey's paradox is the epistemological objection to combining externalism with first-person authority. It purports to show that if we accept both (1) first-person access to mental content and (2) externalism about that content, we can derive a priori knowledge of contingent environmental facts—an absurd result. Responses include denying that privileged access gives a priori knowledge of content (one can know one has M without knowing its content), or accepting that the combination shows privileged access must itself be "thin" (access to mental states under a description that does not settle their content), or arguing that the inference to environmental facts is not actually a priori because the premise about mental content already encodes empirical knowledge.
