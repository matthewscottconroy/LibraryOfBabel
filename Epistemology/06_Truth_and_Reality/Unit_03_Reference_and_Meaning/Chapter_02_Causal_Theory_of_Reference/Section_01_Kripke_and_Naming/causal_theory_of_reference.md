# The Causal Theory of Reference

The causal theory of reference holds that names and natural kind terms refer to objects or kinds by virtue of standing in the right kind of causal-historical relationship to them, not by virtue of being associated with identifying descriptions. Saul Kripke's *Naming and Necessity* (1972/1980) is the foundational text, supplemented by work from Putnam and Donnellan.

## The Descriptivist Picture

The traditional account of how names refer is descriptivism. On Frege's and Russell's views, a name like "Aristotle" is associated with a cluster of descriptions — "the pupil of Plato," "the teacher of Alexander," "the author of the Nicomachean Ethics." The name refers to whatever uniquely satisfies these descriptions (or enough of them to fix a unique referent).

Descriptivism has intuitive support: how else would we identify Aristotle? We associate him with a cluster of properties, and these properties pick him out. The meaning of "Aristotle" just is something like "the philosopher who tutored Alexander the Great."

## Kripke's Critique of Descriptivism

Kripke argues that descriptivism is wrong in two respects: it misdescribes our epistemic relationship to the referent, and it gets the semantics wrong.

**The modal argument**: Descriptions express contingent truths. It is possible that Aristotle never tutored Alexander — perhaps he died young, or Alexander had different teachers. But "Aristotle is the teacher of Alexander" would then be false. Yet we would still be talking about Aristotle — we'd just be saying something false about him. This suggests the name doesn't mean "the teacher of Alexander": if it did, the sentence would be necessarily true (Aristotle = the teacher of Alexander, by definition) or necessarily false (if no one satisfying the description exists). But it's contingently true or false.

**The epistemological argument**: Suppose we discover that our beliefs about Aristotle are all false — he didn't tutor Alexander, he didn't write the Nicomachean Ethics, etc. We don't conclude that "Aristotle" fails to refer; we conclude that we were wrong about Aristotle. This suggests reference is not determined by descriptions.

**Gödel/Schmidt case**: Suppose the only thing you know about Gödel is that he proved the incompleteness theorems. But suppose Schmidt actually proved them, and there was a cover-up. On descriptivism, "Gödel" in your mouth would refer to Schmidt (since Schmidt satisfies the description). But this seems wrong: you're still talking about Gödel, even if you're wrong about what he did.

## The Causal-Historical Account

Kripke proposes an alternative: reference is fixed through a causal-historical chain. A name is initially introduced — perhaps by ostension ("let's call this man 'Aristotle'"), perhaps by description used to fix the reference. The name then spreads through the community via a chain of communication: people use the name to refer to the same object as the people from whom they learned it.

The reference-fixing initial use, and the subsequent transmission of the name through the community, constitute a causal chain that connects current uses of the name to the object. This is what makes "Aristotle" refer to Aristotle: there is a causal-historical chain connecting uses of the name today to the man himself.

Crucially, speakers need not know the descriptions that originally fixed the reference. I can use "Aristotle" to refer to Aristotle even if my only belief about him is that he was a Greek philosopher — provided my use is historically connected to the original introduction.

## Rigid Designation

A central concept in Kripke's account is *rigid designation*. A designator is rigid if it refers to the same object in all possible worlds where that object exists. Proper names are rigid designators: "Aristotle" refers to Aristotle in every possible world where Aristotle exists.

Definite descriptions are typically non-rigid: "the teacher of Alexander" refers to whoever happened to teach Alexander in the actual world, but in a possible world where someone else taught Alexander, the description refers to a different person.

This explains the modal arguments: "Aristotle could have never taught Alexander" is true because the name rigidly designates the man, while the description "teacher of Alexander" might apply to someone else in that counterfactual situation.

## Implications for Epistemology

The causal theory separates reference from epistemic access. I refer to Aristotle not because I have accurate beliefs about him but because I am historically connected to him. This has implications for the epistemology of historical and scientific knowledge: my theories can be wrong, my descriptions inaccurate, and I can still be talking about and investigating the same entities that past thinkers discussed.

It also connects to the necessary a posteriori: the statement "Aristotle is the individual historically responsible for founding the Peripatetic school" may be true necessarily (if it is true) but is known a posteriori — we had to discover it historically, not derive it from the meaning of the name.
