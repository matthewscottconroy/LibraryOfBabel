## Gottlob Frege (1848–1925)

Frege's distinction between Sinn (sense) and Bedeutung (reference) is the primary target of the causal theory. Frege held that names refer in virtue of their sense—the descriptive "mode of presentation" associated with the name—and that the sense is shared by competent speakers and determines the reference. The causal theorist's central objection is that the descriptions we associate with names are often false of the actual referents, incomplete, or merely contingent features, yet the names still refer successfully. Frege's framework remains the indispensable foil for understanding what Kripke's revolution achieved.

## Saul Kripke (1940–2022)

Kripke's *Naming and Necessity* is the most important work in the philosophy of language of the twentieth century after Frege. Through the modal argument (names designate rigidly—they pick out the same individual in all possible worlds), the epistemic argument (the descriptions we associate with names are not conceptually tied to them), and the celebrated Gödel/Schmidt case, Kripke demolished the description theory and replaced it with a picture in which names are fixed by initial baptisms and passed down through causal-historical chains of use. Kripke also established the existence of necessary a posteriori truths (e.g., "water is H₂O") and contingent a priori truths.

## Gareth Evans (1946–1980)

Evans was the most philosophically subtle early critic and refiner of the causal theory. His "Madagascar" case—in which the name "Madagascar" originally referred to part of the African mainland but now refers to the island, due to a misunderstanding that became entrenched in the chain—shows that causal history alone cannot determine reference without some account of which uses in the chain are "grounding" and which are "deviant." Evans proposed that reference is determined by which object is the dominant source of information in the community's use of the name, a position that incorporates both causal and descriptive elements.

## Michael Devitt (b. 1938)

Devitt developed the most sustained and systematic causal theory in *Designation* (1981) and *Language and Reality* (with Kim Sterelny). He argues that the reference of a name is determined by "groundings"—causal connections between uses of the name and the object—rather than by descriptions. Devitt addresses the "qua problem": when we baptize something, we use it as a token of some type, and the type must be specified for reference to be determinate. His work integrates the causal theory with a broader naturalistic semantics.

## Keith Donnellan (1931–2015)

Donnellan's 1966 paper distinguishing referential and attributive uses of descriptions influenced Kripke's development of the causal theory. On a referential use, a speaker uses a description to pick out a particular individual they have in mind, even if the description is false of that individual. This suggests that reference in some contexts is contextually determined by the speaker's causal contact with the referent, not by the descriptive content used. Donnellan's distinction anticipates the idea that proper names function referentially in a way that goes beyond description.

## Nathan Salmon (b. 1951)

Salmon developed the direct reference theory—the view that the semantic content of a proper name is simply its referent (a Millian view)—and used it to generate a puzzle about cognitive significance (Frege's puzzle). If "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" have the same semantic content (the planet Venus), how can "Hesperus is Hesperus" be trivially known while "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is an astronomical discovery? Salmon's solution—that cognitive significance involves pragmatic "ways of grasping" propositions, not semantic content—is a major contribution to debates about the relationship between meaning, reference, and cognition.
