## The Modal Argument Against Descriptivism

**The scenario**: Suppose the name "Aristotle" refers in virtue of the description "the greatest philosopher of antiquity." If this were so, then "Aristotle was the greatest philosopher of antiquity" would be necessarily true—it would be true in every possible world in which Aristotle exists, since "Aristotle" just means the greatest philosopher. But intuitively, Aristotle could have been a mediocre thinker who gave up philosophy for fishing; in that world, Aristotle would exist but would not be the greatest philosopher. So the sentence is only contingently true.

**The philosophical point**: Kripke's modal argument shows that whatever descriptions are associated with a name, those descriptions express only contingent facts about the bearer and cannot constitute the meaning of the name. If names were abbreviations for descriptions, the truths expressed by identifications involving those names would be necessary—but they are not. Names are rigid designators: they pick out the same individual in all possible worlds, whereas definite descriptions pick out whoever satisfies the description in a given world, which may vary. This establishes a fundamental semantic difference between names and descriptions.

## The Gödel/Schmidt Case

**The scenario**: Kripke asks us to suppose that most people who use the name "Gödel" associate it with the description "the man who proved the incompleteness theorems." Now suppose (counterfactually) that the incompleteness theorems were actually proved by a man named Schmidt, and that Gödel stole the proof and published it under his own name. In that case, if "Gödel" referred to whoever proved the incompleteness theorems, it would refer to Schmidt. But intuitively, "Gödel" still refers to Gödel—to the man who was called "Gödel" in the causal chain leading to our use.

**The philosophical point**: The Gödel/Schmidt case is Kripke's decisive epistemic argument against descriptivism. It shows that we can use a name to refer successfully to an individual even when our associated descriptions are false of that individual—provided the name reaches us through a causal chain connected to that individual. This establishes that reference is not determined by what speakers believe or know about the referent, but by causal-historical connections. The case motivates the causal-historical theory of how names acquire and preserve their reference through a community of users.

## Necessary A Posteriori: Water Is H₂O

**The scenario**: The discovery that water is H₂O is an empirical discovery—we had to do chemistry to find it out. It is knowable only a posteriori. Yet Kripke argues that if it is true at all, it is necessarily true: in every possible world in which water exists, water is H₂O, because "water" rigidly designates the very substance we actually have, and that substance is constituted by H₂O. There is no possible world in which water exists but is not H₂O (though there could be a world where XYZ plays the same role as water does here).

**The philosophical point**: This thought experiment establishes one of Kripke's most influential claims: that the necessary/a priori and contingent/a posteriori pairings, which Kant seemed to show were exhaustive, do not in fact exhaust the possibilities. The discovery of "water is H₂O" is a posteriori (requires empirical investigation) but the truth it expresses is necessary (could not have been otherwise, given the identity of the substance). This separates the epistemological category (how we know it) from the metaphysical category (what the truth is). The necessary a posteriori vindicates scientific essentialism: natural kind terms rigidly designate kinds whose essential properties are discovered, not stipulated.

## Evans's Madagascar

**The scenario**: Gareth Evans notes that according to historical evidence, when Marco Polo referred to "Madagascar," he was actually using a name that originally applied to a portion of the African mainland. Through a miscommunication, European cartographers applied the name to the large island off the east coast of Africa. The causal chain of uses of "Madagascar" traces back to a use that was causally connected to the mainland. Yet "Madagascar" now refers to the island. The current referent is not what the causal chain ultimately leads back to.

**The philosophical point**: Evans's case challenges the simple causal-historical theory by showing that causal chains can "bend"—a name can shift its reference through a series of uses in which later users take themselves to be using the name for a different thing than earlier users. The case motivates Evans's refinement: reference is determined not merely by causal history but by which object is the dominant source of information that regulates the community's use of the name. This shows that the causal theory must be supplemented with a normative or intentional element specifying which uses in the chain are "genuine" transmissions and which are misappropriations.

## Rigid Designation and Modal Contexts

**The scenario**: Consider two sentences: (1) "The morning star is the evening star" and (2) "Hesperus is Phosphorus." The first sentence uses definite descriptions, which are not rigid; in a world where Venus did not rise in the morning, "the morning star" would refer to a different object. The second sentence uses names, which are rigid: "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" both refer to Venus in all worlds. Thus (1) expresses a contingent identity, while (2) expresses a necessary identity—even though both were discovered empirically.

**The philosophical point**: This case makes vivid the semantic difference between rigid designators (names, which refer to the same thing in all possible worlds) and non-rigid designators (definite descriptions, which refer to whatever satisfies them in a given world). It also shows why identity statements involving names are necessarily true when true: if "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" both rigidly designate Venus, their identity holds in every world where Venus exists. The case thus isolates how the modal status of a truth depends on the semantic type of its expressions—a lesson with far-reaching implications for the philosophy of mind, where debates about mind-brain identity often turn on whether mental and physical terms are rigid designators.
