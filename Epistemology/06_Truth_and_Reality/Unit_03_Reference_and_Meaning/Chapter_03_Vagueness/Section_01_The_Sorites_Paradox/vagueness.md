# Vagueness and the Sorites Paradox

Vagueness is a pervasive feature of natural language. Many predicates — "tall," "bald," "heap," "red," "old," "rich" — admit of borderline cases: cases in which it is neither clearly true nor clearly false that the predicate applies. The philosophical problem of vagueness concerns how to understand this feature of language and thought, and the sorites paradox shows that vagueness generates genuine logical difficulties.

## The Sorites Paradox

"Sorites" comes from the Greek *soros* (heap). The paradox takes its classic form from the question: what is a heap?

If you have a heap of sand, removing one grain cannot turn it into a non-heap. A single grain can't make the difference between a heap and a non-heap. So: if n grains make a heap, then n-1 grains make a heap. And if 1,000,000 grains make a heap, so do 999,999, and so do 999,998, all the way down to 1 grain — which is absurd.

More formally:
- P1: 1,000,000 grains of sand constitute a heap.
- P2: If n grains constitute a heap, then n-1 grains constitute a heap.
- C: 1 grain of sand constitutes a heap. (Absurd!)

Both premises are plausible. P1 is clearly true. P2 captures the intuition that vague predicates don't have sharp cutoffs — single grains can't make the relevant difference. But the conclusion is clearly false. Something must give.

The same structure applies to any sorites-susceptible predicate: tall, red, bald, old, etc.

## Theories of Vagueness

**Epistemicism** (Timothy Williamson, Roy Sorensen): There are sharp cutoffs, but we don't know where they are. There is some precise number of grains that makes the difference between heap and non-heap; some precise number of hairs that makes the difference between bald and not-bald. We are ignorant of these cutoffs due to the limits of introspection and the indeterminate social facts that fix the meanings of vague predicates.

Epistemicism preserves classical logic. The sorites paradox is resolved by denying P2: there is some n such that n grains are a heap and n-1 are not. We just can't know which n.

**Degree theories**: Truth admits of degrees between 0 and 1. "Harry is tall" might be true to degree 0.7 — more true than false, but not definitely true. The sorites is handled by denying that "if P is true to degree 0.7, then P-minus-one-grain is also true to degree 0.7." Each removal of a grain slightly reduces the degree of truth.

Degree theories require a non-classical logic where the law of excluded middle fails in sharp form and truth is continuous rather than binary.

**Supervaluationism**: A sentence is "supertrue" if it is true on all admissible sharpenings of the predicate; "superfalse" if false on all sharpenings; neither if it varies across sharpenings.

For "Harry is tall": there are many precise meanings for "tall" that are all consistent with how we actually use the word. On some of these, Harry counts as tall; on others, he doesn't. So "Harry is tall" is neither supertrue nor superfalse — it lacks a definite truth value.

The sorites P2 is superfalse: on every admissible sharpening, there is some n that makes the difference. P2 is false on every sharpening. But we can't say *which* n — that depends on the sharpening.

**Contextualism about vagueness**: The extension of "tall" varies with context. In different contexts, different cutoffs are operative. What counts as tall changes as the conversation shifts. The sorites paradox arises from treating "heap" as if it has a fixed extension when actually its extension is fixed anew in each context.

## Metaphysical Vagueness

A more radical view holds that vagueness is not just linguistic or epistemic but *metaphysical*: the world itself is vague. There are objects whose existence is indeterminate — borderline cases of existence — and properties that are indeterminately instantiated.

Metaphysical vagueness is controversial. David Lewis argued that it is impossible: vagueness is always in the representation, not in the world. Others (Barnes, Williams) have defended the coherence of metaphysical vagueness.

## The Significance of Vagueness

Vagueness is not merely a philosophical puzzle. It has implications for law (does this action count as assault?), ethics (at what stage does a fetus become a person?), personal identity (am I still the same person after gradual changes?), and scientific classification (where do species boundaries lie?). Understanding vagueness is practically important as well as philosophically fascinating.
