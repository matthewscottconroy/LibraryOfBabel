## Timothy Williamson (b. 1955)

Williamson is the primary defender of epistemicism about vagueness—the view that vague predicates have perfectly sharp extensions, and that our ignorance of the cut-off points is an irreducible epistemic fact. In *Vagueness* (1994), he argues that there is a determinate fact about whether a given person is tall, a given heap is a heap, and so on—we simply cannot know what it is, because knowing requires a reliable cognitive margin that vague predicates systematically preclude. Williamson's epistemicism is philosophically conservative (it preserves classical logic and bivalence) but requires accepting systematic, unknowable facts.

## Kit Fine (b. 1946)

Fine's 1975 paper introduced supervaluationism as a systematic response to vagueness. On this view, vague sentences lack a determinate truth value because they can be "completed" (made precise) in multiple ways; a sentence is "super-true" if it is true on every completion and "super-false" if false on every completion. Sentences in the borderline zone are neither super-true nor super-false, but classical logical tautologies remain super-true. Fine's framework preserves the classical logic of logical truths while allowing for truth-value gaps at the level of object-language sentences about borderline cases.

## Rosanna Keefe (b. 1971)

Keefe's *Theories of Vagueness* (2000) is the most systematic comparative study of vagueness theories and concludes in favor of supervaluationism. She argues that epistemicism fails because it posits unknowable semantic facts with no explanation; degree theories fail because numerical truth values are too precise to capture the phenomenology of vagueness; and contextualism fails because it relocates rather than resolves the vagueness. Supervaluationism, Keefe argues, best captures both the logical behavior of vague language and the intuitions about borderline cases.

## Roy Sorensen (b. 1951)

Sorensen developed epistemicism independently of Williamson in *Blindspots* (1988). He introduced the concept of "blindspots"—truths that a given agent cannot know. Sorensen argues that the cut-off point for a vague predicate is a blindspot for language users: there is a fact about where "bald" cuts off, but the way we use the predicate makes it impossible for us to know where that point is. His approach is more psychologistic than Williamson's, grounding the unknowability in features of human cognition rather than purely in semantic margin conditions.

## Crispin Wright (b. 1942)

Wright's work on vagueness, developed over several papers, focuses on the "tolerance" intuition: vague predicates seem tolerant to small changes (if one grain of sand makes a heap, removing one grain should still leave a heap). Wright argues that this intuition, though seemingly compelling, leads directly to the sorites paradox, and that we need a logic that can manage the resulting "quandary"—a situation where neither p nor not-p can be assertible. His "third-possibility" approach to vagueness challenges both epistemicism (which denies there is a quandary) and degree theory (which uses numerical precision to dissolve it).

## Diana Raffman (b. 1954)

Raffman defends a psychological and contextualist approach to vagueness, developed most fully in *Unruly Words* (2014). She argues that the extension of vague predicates shifts with context—specifically, with the conversational comparison class and with the perceiver's recent perceptual history. On a "forced march" through a sorites series, a subject's judgment about when "green" becomes "blue" depends on where they are in the series, not on any sharp semantic fact. Raffman's account is empirically grounded in color perception research and connects philosophy of language to cognitive science.
