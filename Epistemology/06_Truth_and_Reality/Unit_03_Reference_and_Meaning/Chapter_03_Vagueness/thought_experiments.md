## The Original Sorites Paradox

**The scenario**: Begin with 1,000,000 grains of sand arranged in a heap. Remove one grain: surely a heap minus one grain is still a heap. Remove another: still a heap. Repeat this reasoning one grain at a time. By repeated application of the principle that one grain cannot make the difference between a heap and a non-heap, you conclude that a single grain of sand is a heap—which is clearly false. The argument can be run in reverse: a single grain is not a heap; adding one grain cannot turn a non-heap into a heap; therefore 1,000,000 grains is not a heap.

**The philosophical point**: The sorites paradox (from the Greek "soros," heap) reveals a structural feature of all vague predicates: they seem to satisfy a tolerance principle (small changes cannot affect their application), but tolerance principles generate contradictions when iterated. The paradox cannot be evaded by denial of any single step—every step seems individually valid. Solutions divide into those that find an error in the argument (epistemicists, supervaluationists) and those that revise the logic to tolerate the contradiction (dialetheists) or accept truth-value gaps (degree theorists).

## The Forced March

**The scenario**: A subject is presented with a sorites series: 1000 color patches ranging continuously from clearly red (patch 1) to clearly orange (patch 1000). The subject is asked, for each patch, "Is this red?" At first they say yes confidently; in the middle they become uncertain; eventually they say no. But if the experimenter now walks them back from patch 1000 toward patch 1, asking the same question, the subject will typically report a different crossover point on the way back. No single patch stands out as the boundary. The subject's judgments are sensitive to their position in the series.

**The philosophical point**: The forced march reveals that borderline judgments are not stable and that there is no single experienced boundary. Epistemicists say this shows our ignorance of the true boundary—our judgments vary because they are unreliable near the unknown cut-off. Contextualists (like Raffman) say there is no single boundary because the extension of "red" shifts with context; what matters is where the subject is in the series at the moment of judgment. The forced march is philosophically important because it tests the phenomenology of vagueness: does it feel like ignorance (supporting epistemicism) or like a genuine semantic indeterminacy?

## Williamson's Sharp but Unknowable Boundary

**The scenario**: Williamson asks us to consider a man, Tek, who is losing his hair gradually—one hair per day. According to epistemicism, there is a precise day on which Tek becomes bald: on day n he is not bald, on day n+1 he is bald. We cannot know which day this is, but the sharp boundary is there. Williamson argues that this consequence, however counterintuitive, is less problematic than the alternatives. Rejecting bivalence leads to logical complications; embracing degree truth requires unjustified precision; supervaluationism has trouble with higher-order vagueness.

**The philosophical point**: The sharp-boundary scenario makes epistemicism vivid. It seems absurd that one hair could make the difference between baldness and non-baldness, but Williamson argues that this intuition is itself explained by the epistemicist picture: we are constitutionally unable to know where the boundary falls, which is why it seems not to exist. The philosophical question is whether the unknowability is genuinely explanatory or merely an artifact of refusing to revise classical logic. Critics charge that positing sharp but unknowable semantic facts is metaphysically extravagant and explanatorily idle.

## Ontic Vagueness: The Cloud and Its Boundary

**The scenario**: Look at a cloud in the sky and ask: exactly how many water molecules are in it? There seems to be no sharp boundary between cloud-molecules and non-cloud-molecules; water molecules gradually thin out toward the edges. Some philosophers argue that this is not merely semantic vagueness (our word "cloud" has no sharp application conditions) but ontic vagueness—the cloud itself, as a mind-independent object, has no determinate boundary. The world, on this view, contains genuinely indeterminate states of affairs.

**The philosophical point**: The question of metaphysical (ontic) vagueness is distinct from semantic vagueness. Williamson denies ontic vagueness: any indeterminacy is in our concepts, not in the world. Defenders of ontic vagueness (Barnes and Williams) argue that we should be realists about indeterminacy: just as the world can contain contingency and possibility, it can contain genuine indeterminacy. The ontic vagueness debate connects vagueness theory to metaphysics (are objects sharply individuated?), philosophy of science (do natural kinds have sharp boundaries?), and the theory of identity (can there be indeterminate identity?).

## The Legal Sorites

**The scenario**: A law prohibits "slow" driving on a highway. Is 55 mph slow? Clearly not. Is 54 mph slow? Probably not either. Is 1 mph slow? Obviously yes. Somewhere between 1 and 55 mph, "slow" applies—but no single mph threshold marks the transition. Now a driver charged with the offense of driving slowly at 40 mph argues: since there is no sharp meaning-determined boundary, the charge is indeterminate and legally void for vagueness. Should legal vagueness be resolved by sharp judicial line-drawing, or does the law itself inherit the semantic indeterminacy of its language?

**The philosophical point**: This case illustrates the practical significance of vagueness theory beyond abstract logic. Legal systems face the sorites constantly (what is "reasonable," "excessive," "negligent"?) and must decide whether to accept vague standards or sharpen them by legislative or judicial decision. Epistemicists would say courts are discovering the correct sharp interpretation; contextualists would say courts are establishing a context in which the expression gets a precise extension; supervaluationists would say any sharp threshold lying within the range of reasonable completions is legally valid. The practical stakes of choosing between theories of vagueness are real.
