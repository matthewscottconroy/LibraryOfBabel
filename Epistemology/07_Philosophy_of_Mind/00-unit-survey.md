# Unit 7: Philosophy of Mind and Epistemology — Unit Survey

## Central Questions

Philosophy of mind and epistemology are not separate disciplines that occasionally interact — they address the same questions from different directions. The theory of knowledge requires a theory of the knower: what kind of entity is the agent who perceives, reasons, and forms beliefs? The theory of mind requires a theory of how mental states represent the world: what is it for a belief to be about something, and how can mental processes produce knowledge?

This unit examines the epistemological implications of philosophy of mind. The central questions are: What is the relationship between perceptual experience and perceptual knowledge? Does consciousness play an essential epistemological role, or can knowledge be understood entirely in functional/computational terms? What is the epistemic status of self-knowledge — knowledge of one's own mental states? Is there a form of self-knowledge that is epistemically privileged over our knowledge of other minds or the external world? And what does cognitive science tell us about the reliability of the mental processes through which we acquire knowledge?

Three clusters of questions drive this unit: (1) The epistemology of consciousness — does phenomenal consciousness contribute to knowledge in ways that non-conscious information processing does not? (2) Epistemic akrasia and higher-order thought — is it possible to believe against one's evidence, and what does self-knowledge require about higher-order awareness of one's own mental states? (3) The mind-body problem and epistemological implications — does physicalism about the mind threaten or support epistemic naturalism?

## Map of Major Positions and Their Logical Relations

**The Mind-Body Problem and Epistemology.** Substance dualism (Descartes) holds that mind and body are fundamentally different substances. Epistemological consequence: there is a veil of perception between the perceiver and the physical world; self-knowledge (knowledge of mental states) is epistemically privileged over knowledge of the body. Property dualism holds that mental properties are not reducible to physical properties, even if mental events are physical events. Physicalism/materialism holds that mental states are identical to or realized by physical states. Eliminative materialism (Churchland) holds that folk-psychological notions of belief, desire, and knowledge should be replaced by neuroscientific categories.

**Cognitive Penetrability.** If higher-level cognitive states can alter the content of perceptual experience, then perception is not an independent, incorrigible source of evidence. Susanna Siegel (*The Rationality of Perception*, 2017) argues that experiences can be epistemically rational or irrational — that cognitive penetration of experience by biased beliefs produces experiences that are themselves epistemically compromised. This connects to the debate about the independence of perceptual evidence.

**Epistemic Akrasia.** Can an agent believe p while also believing that their evidence supports not-p? This is the epistemic analog of akrasia (weakness of will). Rationality may require that agents proportion their beliefs to their evidence, but psychological facts about belief formation (motivated reasoning, cognitive bias) suggest that akrasia is common. The philosophical question is whether it is possible in principle — whether a fully rational agent could be akratic — and whether higher-order evidence (evidence about the reliability of one's own reasoning) can generate akrasia.

**Higher-Order Thought (HOT) and Self-Knowledge.** Higher-order thought theories of consciousness (Rosenthal) hold that a mental state is conscious iff there is a higher-order thought representing it. This has epistemological implications: self-knowledge requires having higher-order thoughts about one's first-order states. The question is whether HOT theories can account for the asymmetry between first-person and third-person access to mental states.

**Privileged Access and Transparency.** The Cartesian doctrine of privileged access holds that one has especially secure, perhaps infallible, knowledge of one's own mental states. Contemporary defenders include Richard Moran (*Authority and Estrangement*, 2001), who argues that first-person authority over one's mental states is grounded in the distinctive first-person deliberative perspective. Critics (Schwitzgebel, Nisbett and Wilson's classic psychology work) argue that privileged access is an illusion.

**Intentionality.** The aboutness of mental states — the fact that beliefs and desires are always about something — is the central topic of the philosophy of intentionality. Brentano introduced intentionality as the mark of the mental. The question of how intentional content is fixed has epistemological implications: if content is determined by causal relations to the environment (Dretske, Fodor), then self-knowledge of content is not privileged.

**Personal Identity.** Knowledge of one's own persistence through time requires a theory of personal identity. Psychological continuity theories (Parfit, Shoemaker) connect closely to the epistemology of memory: we know we are the same person over time by remembering our earlier experiences. Narrative theories connect self-knowledge to the stories we tell about ourselves.

## Sub-Chapter Contributions

**Unit 1: The Mind-Body Problem.** Chapter 1 surveys the main positions on the mind-body problem and their epistemological implications. Chapter 2 examines property dualism and its connection to the epistemic gap argument (Jackson's Mary, Chalmers's zombie argument). Chapter 3 addresses the relationship between naturalism about the mind and epistemic naturalism.

**Unit 2: Consciousness.** Chapter 1 examines the phenomenal concept strategy — the claim that phenomenal concepts are sui generis and cannot be analyzed in functional terms — and its epistemological implications for self-knowledge. Chapter 2 addresses the cognitive penetrability debate: can expectations alter what we perceive? Chapter 3 examines the relationship between consciousness and epistemic access to the external world.

**Unit 3: Intentionality.** Chapter 1 presents theories of intentional content: causal-informational theories (Dretske, Fodor), teleosemantic theories (Millikan), and conceptual role theories. Chapter 2 examines the epistemological implications: if content is externally determined, is self-knowledge of content possible? Chapter 3 addresses singular thought and demonstrative reference.

**Unit 4: Mental Causation.** Examines the causal efficacy of mental states and its epistemological relevance. If mental states qua mental are causally inert (epiphenomenalism), do they play a genuine role in producing knowledge? Kim's causal exclusion argument threatens to render mental causation redundant; responses by Baker, Heil, and others.

**Unit 5: Cognitive Science and Mind / Mind and Computation.** Examines the computational theory of mind and its epistemological implications. If cognitive processes are computational, are they truth-tracking in the relevant sense? Connectionism and its relationship to the symbolic processing model. Machine learning and the epistemology of model-based inference.

**Unit 6: Personal Identity and Self.** Chapter 1 presents Parfit's reductionist account of personal identity (Reasons and Persons) and its implications for self-knowledge and prudential concern. Chapter 2 examines narrative theories of personal identity. Chapter 3 addresses the epistemology of memory and its role in sustaining personal identity.

## Key Philosophers and Arguments

**Gilbert Ryle (1900–1976).** *The Concept of Mind* (1949) attacks the "ghost in the machine" picture of the mind as a private inner theatre. Ryle's distinction between knowing how and knowing that is central to debates about the nature of knowledge in Unit 2. His critique of Cartesian privacy challenges the doctrine of privileged access.

**Frank Jackson (1943–).** The Mary thought experiment: Mary is raised in a black-and-white room but knows all physical facts about color perception. When she leaves and sees red for the first time, she learns something new — what it is like to see red. This "knowledge argument" against physicalism has generated enormous literature and connects to debates about the relationship between phenomenal consciousness and propositional knowledge.

**David Chalmers (1966–).** *The Conscious Mind* (1996) argues that phenomenal consciousness — the "hard problem" of why there is something it is like to have certain brain states — cannot be explained in functional or physical terms. Chalmers's zombie argument: it is conceivable that there are physical duplicates of humans with no phenomenal consciousness; if conceivable, it is possible; if possible, consciousness is not identical to any physical state. This bears on epistemic access to consciousness.

**Susanna Siegel (1970–).** *The Rationality of Perception* (2017) argues that perceptual experiences are subject to epistemic evaluation: an experience produced by epistemically compromised processes (biased expectations, motivated reasoning) is itself compromised. This "cognitive penetrability" thesis has direct implications for the independence of perceptual evidence in epistemology.

**Richard Moran (1956–).** *Authority and Estrangement* (2001) develops a non-Cartesian account of first-person authority: my special authority over my own beliefs is not based on inner perception but on the distinctive deliberative relationship I have with my own mental states. To say what I believe is to commit myself in a first-person way, not merely to report an inner fact.

**Tyler Burge (1946–).** "Individualism and the Mental" (*Midwest Studies in Philosophy*, 1979) introduces "slow switching" cases and defends anti-individualism: the content of mental states is not determined solely by the internal states of the agent but by the agent's social and physical environment. This undermines privileged access to content: one can be mistaken about the content of one's own mental states.

**Eric Schwitzgebel (1968–).** "The Unreliability of Naive Introspection" (*Philosophical Review*, 2008) makes the empirical case that introspection is unreliable. Schwitzgebel cites evidence from psychology (Nisbett and Wilson on unconscious mental processes), from philosophy of mind (the complexity of phenomenal consciousness), and from phenomenological disagreements among careful introspectors.

## Essential Readings

1. **Ryle, Gilbert, *The Concept of Mind*, Chapters 1–2** (1949, Hutchinson). The attack on the Cartesian picture. Chapter 2 on the category mistake is essential. Ryle's critique of the "ghost in the machine" remains the starting point for anti-Cartesian philosophy of mind.

2. **Jackson, Frank, "Epiphenomenal Qualia," *Philosophical Quarterly*, 32:127 (1982), pp. 127–136.** The Mary case. Short, clear, and enormously influential. Essential for understanding the "knowledge argument" and the relationship between phenomenal consciousness and propositional knowledge.

3. **Moran, Richard, *Authority and Estrangement*, Chapters 1–3** (2001, Princeton). The most sophisticated contemporary defence of first-person authority that is not Cartesian. Chapter 1 sets up the problem; Chapters 2–3 develop the deliberative account.

4. **Siegel, Susanna, *The Rationality of Perception*, Chapters 1–3** (2017, Oxford). The cognitive penetrability argument and its epistemological implications. Chapter 1 motivates the thesis; Chapters 2–3 develop the account of hijacked experience.

5. **Burge, Tyler, "Individualism and Self-Knowledge," *Journal of Philosophy*, 85:11 (1988), pp. 649–663.** The paradox of self-knowledge under anti-individualism: if content is externally determined, but self-knowledge is privileged, there is a tension. Burge's resolution is important and connects to debates about introspection.

## Open Questions and Live Debates

**Is the knowledge argument sound?** Most physicalists deny premise 1 (that Mary knew all physical facts before leaving the room) or premise 2 (that she learned something new by seeing red). The phenomenal concept strategy — phenomenal concepts pick out physical properties via a distinctive mode of presentation — is the most popular physicalist response. Chalmers's response to this strategy is ongoing.

**Can cognitive penetration undermine the epistemic role of perception?** Siegel's argument requires that experiences have epistemic status independent of the beliefs they cause. Critics argue that the epistemological damage from cognitive penetration is overstated: even if experiences are influenced by beliefs, they can still provide evidential warrant.

**Is self-knowledge possible under anti-individualism?** Burge holds that self-knowledge and anti-individualism are compatible: one can know the content of one's mental states even though that content is partially determined by the environment. McKinsey's anti-individualism argument — self-knowledge + anti-individualism implies a priori knowledge of the external world — is a persistent challenge.

**What is the relationship between higher-order thought and knowledge?** Rosenthal's HOT theory has epistemological implications that are not fully worked out. If consciousness requires a higher-order thought, and knowledge requires consciousness, then knowledge requires two levels of representation. This seems to inflate the cognitive requirements for knowledge.
