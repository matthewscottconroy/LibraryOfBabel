## René Descartes (1596–1650)

Descartes is the philosopher most associated with substance dualism. In the *Meditations*, he argues that mind and body are genuinely distinct substances: the mind (res cogitans, thinking thing) is essentially unextended and indivisible, while the body (res extensa, extended thing) is essentially spatial and mechanically governed. The distinction is established through the method of doubt: Descartes can coherently doubt the existence of his body but not of his thinking self, establishing their conceptual separability; the conceivability of disembodied existence then supports their metaphysical distinctness. Descartes's legacy is both the dualist tradition and the problem he failed to solve: how two radically different substances interact causally.

## Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia (1618–1680)

In her correspondence with Descartes (1643), Elisabeth pressed the interaction problem with devastating clarity: if the mind is unextended and the body is extended, how can the mind move the body? Contact and impulse—the known mechanisms of bodily causation—require extension. Descartes's fumbling replies reveal that his mechanism could not accommodate genuine mind-body causation. Elisabeth's objection is philosophically important because it shows that the problem of interaction is not a mere quibble but a fundamental challenge internal to the dualist framework.

## Nicolas Malebranche (1638–1715)

Malebranche accepted Cartesian dualism but concluded that mind-body interaction is genuinely impossible: neither can cause the other to move, because causal powers belong to God alone. On occasionalism, God causes bodily changes on the "occasion" of mental events, and mental changes on the occasion of bodily events. While this relieves the interaction problem, it introduces a theological dependence and the further puzzle of why God would institute such a scheme. Malebranche's occasionalism is the most logically consistent response to the interaction problem available to a strict dualist.

## G.W. Leibniz (1646–1716)

Leibniz proposed pre-established harmony as his alternative to both Cartesian interactionism and Malebranchean occasionalism. Mind and body never genuinely interact; instead, God has programmed them like two clocks set at the beginning of creation to run in perfect synchrony. Mental and physical events are causally isolated but correlated because their sequences were coordinated by divine foresight. Pre-established harmony avoids the interaction problem but at the cost of denying genuine downward causation from mental to physical—making every mental-physical correlation miraculous in a different sense.

## David Chalmers (b. 1966)

Chalmers is the most important contemporary defender of property dualism. His *Conscious Mind* (1996) distinguishes phenomenal properties—the intrinsic, subjective qualities of conscious experience—from functional properties, and argues that the former cannot be reduced to or identified with the latter. Chalmers's "naturalistic dualism" holds that consciousness is a fundamental, non-physical feature of the world, like charge or spin in physics, that must be added to the physical ontology. While not a substance dualist, Chalmers's work has revived serious philosophical engagement with dualist alternatives to physicalism.

## Frank Jackson (b. 1943)

Jackson's knowledge argument for property dualism, developed in "Epiphenomenal Qualia" (1982) and "What Mary Didn't Know" (1986), argues that the qualitative character of experience cannot be captured by physical descriptions. Mary the color scientist learns all physical facts about color from a black-and-white room; when she leaves and sees red for the first time, she learns something new—what red looks like. If she learns a new fact, and all physical facts were known, then the new fact is non-physical. This motivates property dualism: there are phenomenal properties over and above physical properties, though they may be instantiated by physical substances.
