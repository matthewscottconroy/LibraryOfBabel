# Type Identity Theory

Type identity theory is the view that mental state types are identical to physical (brain state) types. Pain is not just correlated with some brain state or realized by some brain state — pain just *is* that brain state. The mental and the physical are identical at the type level: every mental kind is a physical kind.

## The Mind-Brain Identity Theory

U.T. Place's "Is Consciousness a Brain Process?" (1956) and J.J.C. Smart's "Sensations and Brain Processes" (1959) established the identity theory in analytic philosophy. Their motivation was partly scientific: the success of neuroscience suggested that mental processes were simply brain processes. There was no need for irreducible mental entities or properties; mental talk was translatable into brain talk.

Smart's key move was distinguishing *topic neutral* descriptions of mental states from their physical realizations. When I say "I have a burning sensation," I am saying something like "there is something going on in me that is like what goes on when I burn my finger." This topic-neutral description is compatible with the burning sensation being a brain state — it doesn't presuppose any particular account of the realization.

The identity claim: pain = C-fiber stimulation (or some brain state type). This is an identity, not a correlation or a causal relation. Just as water = H₂O and lightning = electrical discharge, pain = neural state N.

## Advantages of the Identity Theory

**Causal efficacy**: If mental states are physical states, they can causally interact with the physical world. The problem of mental causation that plagues dualism doesn't arise.

**Theoretical parsimony**: No additional ontological posits beyond the physical. Mental entities are just physical entities, redescribed.

**Scientific compatibility**: The identity theory is continuous with the scientific program of explaining everything in physical terms. It doesn't require any special non-physical science of the mind.

## The Multiple Realizability Objection

The most influential objection to type identity theory is Hilary Putnam's *multiple realizability* argument. Pain, as a mental state type, can in principle be realized by different physical state types in different kinds of creatures:

- In humans, pain might be C-fiber stimulation.
- In octopuses, pain might involve a completely different neural architecture.
- In hypothetical silicon-based aliens, pain might be a very different computational state.

If pain can be realized by C-fiber stimulation in humans, D-fibers in octopuses, and X-circuits in aliens, then pain is not identical to any one physical state type. If the identity held, octopus pain would be C-fiber stimulation — but octopuses don't have C-fibers.

Multiple realizability seems to show that mental properties can't be identified with physical properties, because the same mental property is realized by different physical properties in different systems.

## Token Identity Theory

Token identity theory responds to multiple realizability by holding that individual mental events (tokens) are identical to individual physical events, without requiring that mental state types be identical to physical state types. My particular pain event at 3pm is identical to some particular brain event at 3pm, even though pain-in-general is not identical to any particular brain-state type.

Token identity preserves the anti-dualist insight (no non-physical entities or events) while accommodating multiple realizability (the type "pain" picks out whatever physical state plays the pain role in the relevant system).

Token identity theory is compatible with *anomalous monism* (Davidson): mental events are physical events, but mental descriptions are not reducible to physical descriptions. There are no psychophysical laws — no lawlike connections between mental and physical descriptions — even though every mental event is identical to a physical event.

## Type Physicalism Today

Despite the multiple realizability objection, type identity theory has defenders. Some argue that mental kinds, properly individuated, may turn out to track physical kinds more closely than the multiple realizability argument suggests. If "pain" refers to a functional state that is realized differently in different species, perhaps the functional type is itself a physical type (at a higher level of description).

Others accept that type identity is false for most mental kinds but maintain it for specific mental phenomena where the neural correlates are well-established — color perception, specific cognitive processes, etc.
