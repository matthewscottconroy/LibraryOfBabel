# Eliminative Materialism

Eliminative materialism holds that our ordinary "folk psychological" framework — our common-sense understanding of belief, desire, pain, intention, and other mental states — is a radically false theory that will ultimately be replaced by a mature neuroscience. The mental states posited by folk psychology don't exist; they will be eliminated, as phlogiston and caloric were eliminated by modern chemistry.

## The Folk Psychology Target

Eliminativists argue that folk psychology is a theory — the "theory-theory" account of commonsense psychology. Folk psychology explains behavior by citing mental states: "She raised her hand because she believed that was how to get attention and desired to be called on." This explanatory practice employs a folk theory with implicit laws and generalizations.

Patricia and Paul Churchland, the leading eliminativists, contend that folk psychology has a very poor track record. It has made essentially no progress in understanding mental disorders, creativity, memory, sleep, learning, or the mechanisms of emotion. No other successful science has been as stagnant as folk psychology. And as neuroscience develops, there is reason to expect that folk psychological categories (belief, desire) will not map onto natural neural categories — just as "vital spirits" didn't map onto biochemistry.

## The Argument from Neuroscience

Mature neuroscience will, the eliminativists predict, explain behavior in terms of neural states, processes, and computational architectures that bear no resemblance to the folk psychological taxonomy. "Beliefs" and "desires" are not natural kinds — they don't correspond to any unified neural type that neuroscience will want to cite in its laws.

This is the key move: not just that folk psychology is incomplete or needs refinement (what a revisionist might say), but that the folk psychological kinds don't carve reality at its joints. They are as far from the truth as "witches" or "phlogiston" — they name nothing real.

## Paul Churchland's Connectionism

Paul Churchland argues that connectionist neural networks provide a better model of cognition than the belief-desire model. In a connectionist system, processing involves patterns of activation across many units, not the storage and retrieval of discrete propositional states. There are no "beliefs" stored in specific locations; there are activation patterns that encode information in a way that is fundamentally non-propositional.

If this is right, the very structure of folk psychology — propositional attitudes, sentences in a language of thought — misrepresents the nature of cognitive processing. Neural networks don't manipulate sentences; they transform activation vectors. Cognitive science should describe behavior in these terms, not in folk-psychological terms.

## The Self-Refutation Objection

The most common objection to eliminativism: if eliminativism is true, there are no beliefs, including the eliminativist's belief that eliminativism is true. The eliminativist can't assert their view without self-refutation.

The Churchlands respond: this objection presupposes that "belief" is indispensable for cognition, which is precisely what they are questioning. Maybe there is something that plays the functional role of commitment to a proposition — call it whatever you like — but this something may not have the properties folk psychology attributes to beliefs. The eliminativist isn't asserting beliefs (as folk psychology understands them); they are in some state that disposes them toward certain behaviors, including assertion of eliminativist sentences.

## Eliminativism About Consciousness

A more radical eliminativism extends to consciousness itself. Daniel Dennett, while not a straightforward eliminativist, argues that the folk concept of phenomenal consciousness — of "what it is like" to be in a mental state, of genuine "qualia" — is confused and should be abandoned. There is no such thing as qualia as commonly described; what exists is complex information processing that gives rise to the *illusion* of phenomenal properties.

If this is right, the "hard problem" of consciousness (how to explain qualia) is a pseudo-problem — there are no qualia to explain. The problem dissolves once we abandon the confused folk concept.

## The Legacy of Eliminativism

Even for philosophers who reject eliminativism, the challenge has been productive. It forces a careful examination of what folk psychology is — a theory? a practice? an interpretive scheme? — and what standards it should be held to. The eliminativist challenge has motivated more sophisticated defenses of the reality of propositional attitudes and has sharpened the debate about the relationship between folk psychology and neuroscience.
