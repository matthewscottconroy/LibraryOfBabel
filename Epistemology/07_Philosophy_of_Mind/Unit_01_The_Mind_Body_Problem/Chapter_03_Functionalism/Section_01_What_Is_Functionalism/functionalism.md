# Functionalism

Functionalism is the view that mental states are defined by their functional roles — by their causal relations to sensory inputs, behavioral outputs, and other mental states — rather than by their intrinsic physical or phenomenal properties. What makes something a pain is not what it is made of (neurons, silicon, or anything else) but what it *does*: it is caused by tissue damage, it causes avoidance behavior, it causes the belief that something is wrong, and it interacts with desires and other beliefs in characteristic ways.

## The Motivation

Functionalism emerged as a response to two inadequacies: the multiple realizability problem for type identity theory, and the behaviorist's failure to distinguish states that have the same behavioral manifestations but different internal character.

Against type identity theory: if pain is defined by its functional role rather than its physical realization, pain can be multiply realized — in humans, animals, aliens, or machines — without this being a problem. Different physical states play the pain role in different systems; they are all pain.

Against behaviorism: behaviorism defined mental states in terms of dispositions to behavior, but the same disposition to behavior can stem from different mental states. Functionalism adds internal structure: mental states are defined not just by their behavioral outputs but by their causal relations to other mental states, inputs, and outputs.

## Machine Functionalism

Hilary Putnam's early development of functionalism drew on the theory of Turing machines. A Turing machine is defined by its functional organization — its table of states and transitions — not by its physical implementation. Any physical system that realizes the same functional organization implements the same Turing machine.

Putnam's suggestion: mental states are like Turing machine states. What makes a state a belief that p is that it plays the belief-that-p functional role in the overall system. The physical realization is irrelevant; what matters is the pattern of functional relations.

This is a liberating idea: it means that questions about whether computers or aliens have mental states are questions about functional organization, not about whether they are made of neurons.

## Causal Role Functionalism

A more sophisticated version defines mental states in terms of their causal roles in the whole system. Pain is the state that is typically caused by tissue damage, that causes the belief that something is damaged, that causes desire to avoid the cause, that causes wincing, that causes reports of pain, and so on. The functional role is specified by a Ramsey sentence that identifies mental state types with whatever states play the relevant causal roles.

Lewis's functionalism uses the Ramsey-Lewis technique: take all the platitudes of folk psychology (the claims that everyone would assent to about how mental states are related), replace the mental state terms with variables, and the mental state types are the unique realizers of those platitudinal roles.

## Block's Challenge: The China Brain

Ned Block raised a challenge to functionalism with the *China brain* thought experiment. Suppose the entire population of China was organized to simulate a brain, with each person playing the role of a neuron and communicating in ways that mirror neural communication. This system would implement the same functional organization as a human brain.

Functionalism implies that this system would have mental states — including phenomenal consciousness (what it feels like to be in a mental state). But this seems wrong. The population of China implementing a brain simulation doesn't have experiences; there is no "what it is like" to be this system.

Block distinguishes *access consciousness* (information being available for reasoning and reporting) from *phenomenal consciousness* (there being something it is like). Functionalism can capture access consciousness but seems to fail for phenomenal consciousness.

## Objections and Responses

**The absent qualia objection**: Functional duplicates might lack phenomenal consciousness entirely. A zombie (physically and functionally identical to a normal human, but with no inner experience) is conceivable; if conceivable, it is possible; if possible, functionalism is wrong about consciousness.

**The inverted qualia objection**: Two systems might implement the same functional organization but have systematically different phenomenal experiences (your "red" experience might be like my "green" experience functionally, but phenomenally different).

Functionalists have several responses: deny the conceivability of zombies, argue that phenomenal properties are functional properties at the right level of description, or bite the bullet and accept that the absent-qualia objection shows only that phenomenal consciousness requires something more than functional organization.

Functionalism remains the dominant view in philosophy of mind, even as the debate about phenomenal consciousness continues to challenge it.
