## Hilary Putnam (1926–2016)

Putnam's machine functionalism, developed in a series of papers in the 1960s, defined mental states in terms of their causal-computational roles relative to inputs, outputs, and other states—analogous to the states of a Turing machine. This allowed mental states to be multiply realizable (instantiated in different physical substrates) while preserving their type-identity through their functional role. Putnam's functionalism was the dominant philosophy-of-mind position for two decades, though Putnam himself later repudiated it, arguing in *Representation and Reality* (1988) that mental states cannot be captured by any single functional description.

## Ned Block (b. 1942)

Block is functionalism's most careful and persistent internal critic. His "Troubles with Functionalism" (1978) introduced the China brain objection (a billion people organized to simulate the functional organization of the human brain would be attributed mental states on functionalism, but intuitively lacks them) and the inverted qualia problem (two beings with the same functional organization might have systematically different qualitative experiences). Block also distinguished "access consciousness" (functional availability of content for reasoning and report) from "phenomenal consciousness" (subjective character), arguing that functionalism can account for the former but not the latter.

## David Armstrong (1926–2014)

Armstrong's causal functionalism, developed in *A Materialist Theory of the Mind* (1968), defines mental states as states apt to cause certain behaviors and be caused by certain stimuli. Unlike computational functionalism, Armstrong's version is explicitly physicalist: the causally defined states are identified with physical (neural) states. Armstrong's functionalism allows for genuine empirical identification of mental with physical kinds—it is a reductionist form of functionalism in contrast to Putnam's anti-reductionist version.

## John Searle (b. 1932)

Searle developed the Chinese Room argument in 1980 as a critique of computational functionalism. His thought experiment argues that even a perfect simulation of understanding—a system that passes any behavioral test—need not involve genuine understanding, because syntax (the manipulation of formal symbols) does not suffice for semantics (genuine meaning and intentionality). Searle's argument targeted "strong AI" (the view that running the right program is sufficient for mentality) and challenged the functionalist identification of minds with computational processes.

## Jerry Fodor (1935–2017)

Fodor developed the most scientifically serious version of computational functionalism in the language of thought hypothesis. Mental states are functional states involving symbol-like representations in a proprietary cognitive language ("Mentalese"); mental processes are computation over these representations. Fodor's functionalism was explicitly designed to be compatible with cognitive science while resisting reduction to neuroscience. His later work, however, increasingly acknowledged that functionalism faces severe problems—especially the frame problem and the challenge of accounting for cognition beyond modular systems.

## David Chalmers (b. 1966)

Chalmers developed a systematic critique of functionalism's treatment of consciousness, arguing that even a perfect functional duplicate of a conscious being (a "philosophical zombie") might lack phenomenal consciousness. His "fading qualia" and "dancing qualia" arguments probe whether gradual functional replacement of neurons could preserve consciousness or would inevitably drain it. These thought experiments—published in *The Conscious Mind* (1996)—represent the most technically developed challenges to functionalism and have driven ongoing debate about whether consciousness is a functional property.
