## The China Brain (Nation of China)

**The scenario**: Ned Block asks us to imagine the entire population of China—over a billion people—organized so that each person simulates one neuron in the human brain. They communicate by radio, following a detailed program that replicates the functional organization of a human nervous system. This "China Brain" has all the same functional relations as a human brain: the right inputs cause the right internal state transitions, which produce the right outputs. According to functionalism, it should be in mental states—including perhaps being in pain or having perceptual experiences.

**The philosophical point**: Block intends the China Brain to generate a reductio of functionalism by exploiting our intuition that this country-sized system is not conscious—it has no subjective experience despite having the right functional organization. This tests whether functional organization is sufficient for mentality. Functionalists can respond by biting the bullet (the China Brain is indeed conscious—our intuition is simply unreliable) or by adding constraints (genuine mentality requires that the functional organization be realized in the right physical medium, at the right speed, or in the right biological substrate). Each response has consequences for what functionalism actually claims.

## Inverted Qualia

**The scenario**: Imagine two people, Anna and Beth, who are functionally identical—they respond identically to red and green lights, use the words "red" and "green" correctly, and have the same dispositions regarding traffic signals. But suppose Anna's subjective experience of red—the qualitative feel she has when seeing a red light—is exactly what Beth experiences when she sees green. Their experiences are "inverted" in the subjective dimension, though perfectly synchronized in the functional dimension.

**The philosophical point**: If inverted qualia are possible, then phenomenal properties are not identical to functional properties: two systems can be functionally identical but qualitatively different. This shows that functionalism cannot fully account for the qualitative character of experience. Functionalists respond by either denying that inverted qualia are genuinely conceivable (once all functional relations are fixed, qualia are fixed), or by accepting the possibility but arguing it does not undermine functionalism about psychological states (only about phenomenal states, which may be a different matter). The debate hinges on whether qualitative character is exhausted by functional role.

## Absent Qualia: The Functional but Dark System

**The scenario**: Imagine a system that has exactly the functional organization of a conscious human being—the right input-output relations, the right internal causal structure—but in which there is "no one home." The lights are on from the outside (functionally), but there is no subjective experience, no qualitative character, no phenomenal "what it's like." According to functionalism, this system should be conscious. But the system, by stipulation, has no phenomenal states.

**The philosophical point**: The absent qualia thought experiment, like the inverted qualia case, challenges the functionalist claim that functional organization is sufficient for phenomenal consciousness. The force of the case depends on whether we can coherently conceive of a functional duplicate with no phenomenal states. Functionalists like Shoemaker argue this is not conceivable: if we fully understand what it means for states to play the right functional role—including their role in generating reports, affecting attention, guiding behavior—we will see that absent qualia cannot be functionally indistinguishable from present ones. Chalmers disputes this, arguing for a principled gap between function and phenomenal character.

## Searle's Chinese Room

**The scenario**: John Searle imagines himself locked in a room, receiving slips of paper with Chinese symbols. He has a rulebook (in English) for responding to each combination of symbols with other symbols. To outside observers, his responses look like those of a Chinese speaker: he appears to understand Chinese. But Searle understands no Chinese—he is merely manipulating symbols according to formal rules. If a computer program simulates Chinese comprehension, the situation is analogous: the program manipulates symbols without understanding them.

**The philosophical point**: Searle's Chinese Room is the most discussed argument against computational functionalism. It targets the claim that syntax is sufficient for semantics—that running the right program is sufficient for genuine understanding. Responses include the "systems reply" (Searle-in-the-room does not understand Chinese, but the whole system—room, rulebook, Searle—does), the "robot reply" (if the system were embodied and causally connected to the world, it would have genuine semantics), and the "other minds reply" (why should the functional behavior of neurons be more convincing than the functional behavior of a program?). The debate remains unresolved and central to philosophy of AI.

## Fading Qualia

**The scenario**: Chalmers asks us to imagine gradually replacing your neurons, one by one, with silicon chips that perform exactly the same functional role. After the replacement of the first neuron, your behavior is unchanged and your functional organization is preserved. After two neurons, the same. Gradually, your brain becomes entirely silicon while remaining functionally identical. At what point, if any, do your phenomenal experiences fade? Chalmers argues that any principled answer commits the functionalist to an uncomfortable position: either experiences fade suddenly (at some arbitrary point) or they never fade (in which case silicon is conscious just like you).

**The philosophical point**: The fading qualia argument is designed to show that functionalism either accepts the gradual elimination of phenomenal consciousness through functional preservation (which seems absurd) or must accept that silicon systems with the right functional organization are conscious (which is what functionalists want but which seems to require an argument). The thought experiment is a sophisticated pressure test: it reveals that the functionalist must either commit to a sharp threshold for consciousness (at odds with the multiple realizability spirit) or accept a counterintuitive claim about the consciousness of silicon systems.
