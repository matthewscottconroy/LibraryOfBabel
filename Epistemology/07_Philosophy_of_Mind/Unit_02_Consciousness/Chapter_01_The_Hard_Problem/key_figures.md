## David Chalmers (b. 1966)

Chalmers coined the phrase "the hard problem of consciousness" and has given it its most precise formulation. The easy problems—explaining attention, memory, introspective access, verbal report, integration of information—are scientifically tractable because they involve explaining cognitive function. The hard problem is explaining why any cognitive function is accompanied by subjective experience at all: why there is "something it is like" to be in these functional states. Chalmers argues that the hard problem is not just practically difficult but presents a principled explanatory gap: functional and physical facts do not entail phenomenal facts, making consciousness irreducible to any physical or functional description.

## Thomas Nagel (b. 1937)

Nagel's 1974 paper "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" is the most influential pre-Chalmers statement of the hard problem. Nagel argued that consciousness has an essentially subjective character—it is always "from the inside"—and that objective, third-person science cannot capture this. A bat's echolocation experience has a subjective character that we cannot know just by knowing the physical facts about bat neurology, because we would need to be a bat to know what it is like. Nagel concluded that current scientific concepts are inadequate for a theory of consciousness—not because consciousness is supernatural, but because science needs new concepts.

## Joseph Levine (b. 1952)

Levine introduced the "explanatory gap" as a distinct philosophical problem. Even if mind-brain type identity is true—even if pain just is C-fiber firing—we lack an explanation of why C-fiber firing feels painful rather than feeling like nothing at all. The gap is explanatory rather than ontological: there may be no gap in nature, but our concepts are organized in a way that leaves us unable to see why the physical story necessitates the phenomenal story. Levine's distinction between an ontological hard problem (Chalmers) and an explanatory gap (his own view) is important for evaluating different kinds of physical responses.

## Daniel Dennett (b. 1942)

Dennett is the most prominent and systematic critic of the hard problem as traditionally conceived. His "heterophenomenology" and multiple drafts model of consciousness argue that the intuitions driving the hard problem—especially the intuition that there is a special "phenomenal" quality to experience that transcends its functional role—are cognitive illusions created by how we represent our own mental states. On Dennett's view, there is no explanatory gap because phenomenal consciousness as traditionally conceived does not exist: what exists is a complex of functional dispositions and cognitive representations. His work represents the most serious challenge to treating the hard problem as a genuine metaphysical puzzle.

## Colin McGinn (b. 1950)

McGinn's "mysterianism" proposes an unusual resolution to the hard problem: it is a genuine problem, but one that human minds are cognitively closed to solving. Our cognitive faculties evolved to handle physical and social environments, not to grasp the constitutive relationship between brain processes and consciousness. McGinn argues that there is a physical explanation of consciousness—consciousness is fully determined by neural facts—but that our minds are constitutionally incapable of understanding what that explanation looks like. Mysterianism takes the hard problem seriously without embracing dualism.

## Ned Block (b. 1942)

Block's distinction between phenomenal consciousness (what it is like) and access consciousness (information being globally available for reasoning, report, and behavioral control) clarified what the hard problem is specifically a problem for. The "easy" problems—which Chalmers contrasts with the hard problem—are actually explanatory challenges for access consciousness; the hard problem concerns phenomenal consciousness specifically. Block's conceptual work has been essential for avoiding conflation and for evaluating empirical theories of consciousness that may explain access without touching the hard problem.
