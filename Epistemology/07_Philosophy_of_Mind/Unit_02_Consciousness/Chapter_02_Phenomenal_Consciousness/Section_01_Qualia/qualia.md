# Qualia and Phenomenal Properties

Qualia (singular: quale) are the subjective, phenomenal properties of conscious experience — the "what it is like" aspects of perception, emotion, and thought. The redness of red as seen, the painfulness of pain as felt, the sweet taste of sugar as tasted — these are qualia. They are the raw qualitative feel of experience, often described as the most immediately certain things about our mental lives.

## What Qualia Are Supposed to Be

Frank Jackson's "Epiphenomenal Qualia" (1982) gives a classic characterization. Qualia have the following features:

**Private**: Only I can directly know my own qualia. You can infer facts about my phenomenal states from my behavior and reports, but you have no direct access to my experiences.

**Intrinsic**: Qualia are not relational properties — they are features of experience in itself, not relations to external objects or other mental states.

**Ineffable**: Qualia resist capture in functional terms. No description of the functional role of an experience fully captures what the experience is like.

**Directly apprehensible**: We know our qualia directly, without inference. There is no way to be wrong about whether you are currently in pain (though there can be ways to be wrong about the cause).

## Frank Jackson's Knowledge Argument

Jackson's "Mary's Room" thought experiment is the most famous argument for the reality of qualia. Mary is a brilliant color scientist who has spent her entire life in a black-and-white room. She knows everything physical science will ever tell us about color vision — every neurological fact, every functional fact, every physical fact about light and perception.

Now suppose Mary leaves her black-and-white room and sees a red tomato for the first time. Does she learn something new?

Jackson says yes: she learns what it is *like* to see red. This is new knowledge, not already contained in her complete physical knowledge. If she learns something new, this something — the phenomenal property of seeing red — is not captured by physical knowledge. Therefore, there are non-physical properties (qualia).

## Responses to the Knowledge Argument

**The ability hypothesis** (Lewis, Nemirow): Mary doesn't gain new propositional knowledge; she gains new abilities — the ability to recognize, remember, and imagine red experiences. This is know-how, not know-that. The argument illicitly treats an ability as a piece of propositional knowledge.

**The phenomenal concepts strategy**: Mary does gain new knowledge, but this is compatible with physicalism. Mary knows a new concept — a *phenomenal concept* of redness — that she couldn't form from inside the black-and-white room. But the property this concept picks out is a physical property. The same property is known under two different conceptual guises: functional/physical and phenomenal.

**The old fact, new acquaintance view**: What Mary gains is not new knowledge of a new fact but a new *mode of acquaintance* with an old fact. She already knew about red experience functionally; she now acquaints herself with it from the inside.

**The anti-physicalist conclusion**: Mary does learn a new fact about a non-physical property. Qualia are real, non-physical properties. This is Jackson's original conclusion, though he later retracted it.

## Inverted Qualia

Could two people have functionally identical color vision but systematically inverted experiences — your "red" looks to you the way my "green" looks to me? If they could, this shows that qualia are not captured by functional organization.

The argument for inverted qualia: there seems to be no functional difference between the two cases (both call fire hydrants "red," both stop at red lights). But the phenomenal difference (inverted experiences) would be real.

The argument is controversial because it is unclear whether inverted qualia are genuinely conceivable or whether the conceivability is due to confusion. If our color concepts are entirely functional, there is nothing "extra" that could be inverted.

## Absent Qualia and Dennett's Challenge

Dennett's challenge to qualia begins with the observation that our intuitions about qualia are not reliable. We think we have rich, detailed phenomenal experiences, but this may be an illusion. Experiments on change blindness and inattentional blindness show that we miss huge amounts of information in our visual field; the "richness" of experience may be a post-hoc construction.

Dennett's "heterophenomenology" proposes to study consciousness by taking subjects' reports seriously as data about their *beliefs* about their experiences, without committing to the existence of phenomenal properties that are reported. This method avoids assuming that phenomenal reports accurately describe inner experiences.
