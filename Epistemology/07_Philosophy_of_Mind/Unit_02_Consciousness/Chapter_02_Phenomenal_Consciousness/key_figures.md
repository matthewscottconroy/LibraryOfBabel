## Frank Jackson (b. 1943)

Jackson's knowledge argument, presented through the thought experiment of Mary the color scientist, is the most widely discussed argument in contemporary philosophy of mind. In his original papers (1982, 1986), Jackson used Mary to argue that physical knowledge is incomplete—there are phenomenal facts over and above physical facts, supporting property dualism. Jackson later (2003) recanted, arguing that Mary merely gains new abilities (to recognize, imagine, and remember red) rather than new factual knowledge. His recantation remains controversial: many philosophers find it philosophically unmotivated and continue to defend the original knowledge argument.

## Thomas Nagel (b. 1937)

Nagel's analysis of the "what it's like" character of consciousness in "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974) established the concept of phenomenal consciousness as philosophically central. His argument that objective science cannot capture the subjective character of experience anticipated the qualia debates of the 1980s and 1990s. Nagel's *The View from Nowhere* (1986) extended this to a general account of the tension between objective and subjective perspectives, arguing that the mind-body problem reflects a deep structural feature of the relationship between first-person and third-person description.

## Daniel Dennett (b. 1942)

Dennett has persistently argued that the concept of qualia—as traditionally characterized—is incoherent, and that the apparent hard problem of consciousness is a cognitive illusion generated by our tendency to misrepresent our own functional states as having a special, non-functional phenomenal character. His heterophenomenology (treating subjects' reports as data to be interpreted, not as direct windows onto phenomenal reality) and his multiple drafts model of consciousness (denying a single "Cartesian theater" where experiences become conscious) constitute an ambitious alternative to the received picture of phenomenal consciousness.

## Brian Loar (1939–2014)

Loar developed the phenomenal concepts strategy—the most sophisticated physicalist response to the knowledge argument—in his 1990 paper "Phenomenal States." He argued that Mary, on leaving the room, acquires a new concept of a physical property she already knew under a different (physical-functional) concept. Phenomenal concepts are distinct in type from physical-functional concepts: they are recognitional concepts formed through direct acquaintance with the experience itself. The two concepts refer to the same property, but because they are conceptually independent, no a priori inference between them is available—which explains the knowledge argument's apparent force without conceding dualism.

## David Papineau (b. 1947)

Papineau's *Thinking About Consciousness* (2002) provides a systematic defense of physicalism using the phenomenal concepts strategy. He argues that the characteristic features of phenomenal concepts—their use in thinking about experience by deploying experience itself as a constituent of the concept—explain why we cannot a priori identify phenomenal properties with physical ones, even if they are identical. The intuitive force of the knowledge argument and the zombie argument is thus explained by the peculiar structure of phenomenal concepts, not by any genuine non-physical fact about experience.

## Michael Tye (b. 1950)

Tye's representationalism holds that phenomenal properties are wide representational properties: what makes an experience phenomenally the way it is is its representing certain properties of objects in the world. On Tye's PANIC theory (Poised, Abstract, Nonconceptual, Intentional Content), phenomenal states are functional-representational states. Tye argues that this account can capture the phenomenology of experience without positing irreducible phenomenal properties, and defends it against the spectrum inversion and absent qualia objections by arguing that these scenarios are impossible if representation is wide enough.
