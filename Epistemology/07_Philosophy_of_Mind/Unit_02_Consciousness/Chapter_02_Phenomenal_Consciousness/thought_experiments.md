## Mary's Room

**The scenario**: Mary is the world's greatest color scientist. She has spent her entire life in a black-and-white room, learning everything there is to know about the physics of color, the neuroscience of color vision, the behavioral dispositions of people who see red. She knows which wavelengths correspond to which colors, which neural responses they trigger, which emotional and behavioral responses follow. One day she leaves the room and sees red for the first time. Does she learn something new—what red looks like?

**The philosophical point**: Frank Jackson argues that Mary does learn something new: the qualitative character of red experience—what it is like to see red. Since she knew all the physical facts before leaving and still learns something, that something must be a non-physical fact—a phenomenal fact about what red experience is like. The argument supports property dualism: phenomenal properties are not captured by physical or functional descriptions. Responses include: (1) the ability hypothesis (she gains a new ability to recognize, imagine, and remember red—not new propositional knowledge); (2) the phenomenal concepts strategy (she gains a new concept of an old physical fact); (3) denial (if physicalism is true, she already knew all facts, including phenomenal ones, under some description).

## The Ability Hypothesis

**The scenario**: Lewis and Nemirow propose that when Mary leaves the room, she does not gain new knowledge of a fact she did not previously know. Rather, she gains new abilities: the ability to recognize red things by their visual appearance, the ability to imagine red in the relevant phenomenal way, the ability to remember what red looks like. These abilities did not require any new propositional knowledge—they required direct acquaintance with the experience.

**The philosophical point**: The ability hypothesis is the most widely accepted physicalist response to the knowledge argument. If Mary's gain is merely an ability gain, not a factual discovery, then the knowledge argument fails: there is no new phenomenal fact, only new know-how. Critics argue that the distinction between knowing how and knowing that is not sharp—when Mary recognizes red by its feel, she seems to know something, namely what red looks like—and that the ability hypothesis fails to capture this propositional dimension of phenomenal knowledge. The debate connects to broader issues about the relationship between propositional and non-propositional knowledge.

## Spectrum Inversion at Birth

**The scenario**: Suppose two people are born with inverted color experiences: what one experiences as red, the other experiences as green, and vice versa—but because they are trained using the same objects and words from birth, they never detect any difference. Both correctly learn to call grass "green" and fire engines "red." Their behavior, linguistic dispositions, and all functional relations are identical. Yet their phenomenal experiences are, by hypothesis, systematically different.

**The philosophical point**: The spectrum inversion case tests whether phenomenal properties can vary independently of functional properties. If two functionally identical people can have different qualia, then qualia are not identical with or reducible to functional states—which challenges both functionalism and representationalism. Defenders of these views respond by arguing that inversion scenarios are less coherent than they seem: if the full functional organization is identical (including all comparative judgments, emotional responses, and associative memories), then the experiences must be identical too. The debate hinges on whether qualia have a "self-intimating" character that makes them knowable independently of their functional role.

## Dennett's Heterophenomenology

**The scenario**: Dennett proposes that we study consciousness by treating subjects' reports about their experiences as data to be interpreted in the best way—just as we interpret the beliefs and desires of a person whose language we are learning. We do not simply take subjects at their word when they say "I see red" or "I feel pain"—we take this as evidence about their internal states, to be explained by the best theory of mind. The question "what is it really like to be this subject?" is replaced by "what does the best theoretical interpretation of this subject's reports reveal?"

**The philosophical point**: Heterophenomenology is Dennett's methodological alternative to introspective phenomenology. By bracketing first-person authority and treating reports as evidence rather than direct disclosure, Dennett aims to study consciousness scientifically without presupposing that phenomenal properties exist beyond functional-representational states. Critics argue that heterophenomenology simply ignores the hard problem: it describes functional facts but never engages with the question of why those functional facts are accompanied by experience. Dennett's response is that this question is empty once we correctly understand what experience is.

## Nagel's Color Concepts Without Color Experience

**The scenario**: Suppose a being exists that has complete neurological and physical knowledge of human color vision but has never experienced color itself—perhaps it experiences the world in an entirely different way. This being can predict with perfect accuracy which colors humans will report seeing in any situation, can describe the functional role of color experience in human cognition, but has never had the relevant experience. Does it know what red looks like?

**The philosophical point**: This variant of the knowledge argument, closely related to Mary's Room, tests whether functional and physical knowledge can substitute for experiential knowledge. Nagel's original intuition—that the subjective character of experience is irreducibly first-personal—suggests not. Even complete third-person knowledge leaves a first-person residue. This case is particularly useful for discussions of AI and consciousness: could an AI system trained on all available data about human experience thereby know what human experience is like? The thought experiment suggests not—which raises deep questions about the limits of objective knowledge of the mental.
