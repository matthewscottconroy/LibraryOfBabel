# Higher-Order Theories of Consciousness

Higher-order theories hold that a mental state is conscious when it is accompanied by, or is the object of, another mental state that represents it — a higher-order state. On these theories, what makes a state conscious is not anything intrinsic to it but its being represented by (or accompanied by) another mental state.

## The Core Idea

An ordinary (first-order) mental state — a perception of red, a pain, a desire for coffee — may or may not be conscious. What makes it conscious is the presence of a higher-order state that is "about" the first-order state: a representation of that state, or a thought about it.

The intuitive motivation: introspection seems to be involved in conscious experience. When I'm conscious of being in pain, there seems to be both the pain and an awareness of being in pain. Higher-order theories build this intuition into the definition of consciousness.

## Higher-Order Thought Theories

David Rosenthal's Higher-Order Thought (HOT) theory is the leading version. A mental state is conscious iff the subject has a higher-order thought about it — a thought to the effect that one is in that state. The HOT must be about the first-order state in a non-inferential way (it shouldn't be a conclusion drawn from other evidence; it should be immediate and direct).

On Rosenthal's view, it is possible to be in a mental state — even a painful one — without that state being conscious. The state is not accompanied by a HOT, so it's "in the dark." This is not contradictory: unconscious mental states exist and influence behavior. Consciousness is a particular property some mental states have — being accompanied by appropriate higher-order representation.

The HOT theory has a naturalistic advantage: if consciousness is explained by higher-order thought, then consciousness can be explained by cognitive mechanisms — memory, attention, representation — that are themselves amenable to neuroscientific study.

## Higher-Order Perception Theories

David Armstrong and William Lycan developed higher-order *perception* theories: a mental state is conscious when it is the object of an inner monitoring faculty — an inner scanner or inner eye that perceives mental states. The higher-order state is a perceptual state, not a thought.

This makes consciousness more like inner perception than inner thought. The inner monitoring faculty operates below the level of conceptual thought; it is possible to be conscious of states without having concepts that fully articulate them.

## Self-Representationalism

A variant position holds that conscious states are self-representing: instead of being represented by a distinct higher-order state, conscious mental states have the property of representing themselves as well as their typical object. The pain represents my damaged tissue (first-order content) and also represents itself (self-representation).

Uriah Kriegel has developed this view. It avoids a regress problem that threatens higher-order theories: if consciousness requires a HOT, does the HOT need to be conscious to do its work? If so, it needs its own HOT, and we have a regress. Self-representationalism avoids this: the conscious state represents itself without needing a further state to make that self-representation conscious.

## Objections

**The absent-minded driver**: Consider a case where you drive competently, attending to traffic, without being aware of the mental states involved in your driving. Your driving is controlled by first-order states that are not accompanied by HOTs. Rosenthal's theory predicts these states are unconscious. But sometimes it seems like they were conscious — we can recall them.

**The targeted HOT problem**: A HOT represents you as being in a first-order state with some property. If the HOT misrepresents the first-order state (you think you're in a state of pain when you're actually in discomfort), the higher-order theory implies you are conscious of pain — not discomfort. But this seems to get the phenomenology wrong.

**The correlation problem**: Do HOTs really make states conscious, or are they just typically correlated with conscious states? Maybe both the first-order conscious state and the HOT are effects of a common cause (the neural correlate of consciousness), rather than the HOT causing the consciousness.

Higher-order theories represent a major approach to consciousness that takes seriously the role of self-representation and introspection in conscious experience, while maintaining a naturalistic framework.
