## David Rosenthal (b. 1939)

Rosenthal is the primary architect of higher-order thought (HOT) theory. On his view, a mental state is conscious if and only if the subject has a higher-order thought that they are in that state. A perception of red becomes a conscious experience when accompanied by a (roughly simultaneous) thought: "I am seeing red." States that occur without any such accompanying higher-order thought remain unconscious. Rosenthal's theory makes consciousness a relational property—a state's being represented by another state—rather than an intrinsic property. This allows a naturalistic, functional account while explaining why introspection is both informative and fallible.

## David Armstrong (1926–2014)

Armstrong's higher-order perception (HOP) theory treats consciousness as the inner perception of one's own mental states. In *A Materialist Theory of the Mind* (1968), he argued that introspective consciousness is literally a form of perception—a second-order mental state that monitors first-order states. This view was developed independently of but in parallel with Rosenthal's HOT theory. The perception-based account differs from the thought-based account in emphasizing the phenomenological immediacy of introspective awareness and in allowing for nonconceptual higher-order states.

## Bernard Baars (b. 1946)

Baars developed global workspace theory (GWT) in *A Cognitive Theory of Consciousness* (1988). The central idea is that the brain contains many specialized, relatively independent processors (visual, auditory, motor, etc.) that normally operate unconsciously. Consciousness arises when information is selected for broadcast into a "global workspace"—a shared resource that makes information widely available across processors. Baars modeled this on the metaphor of a theater with a spotlight: consciousness is the spotlight, making certain contents visible to the whole "audience" of cognitive systems. GWT has been enormously influential in cognitive neuroscience.

## Stanislas Dehaene (b. 1965)

Dehaene developed the neural correlates of global workspace theory, providing neuroscientific evidence for a system of long-distance cortical connections that broadcasts information globally when a stimulus becomes consciously perceived. His work on "the ignition" of consciousness—the sudden, nonlinear spread of neural activity that accompanies conscious access—has been among the most empirically productive in consciousness research. Dehaene's framework distinguishes subliminal, preconscious, and conscious processing in terms of their global workspace dynamics.

## Giulio Tononi (b. 1960)

Tononi's integrated information theory (IIT) proposes that consciousness is identical with integrated information, measured by a quantity called Phi (Φ). The more a system integrates information—the more its whole is greater than the sum of its parts in terms of information—the more conscious it is. IIT has the radical implication that any system with high Phi is conscious, including simple physical systems, and that some common computers may be nearly unconscious despite their functional sophistication. IIT is notable for being both mathematically precise and philosophically contentious, attracting criticism from both scientists and philosophers.

## William Lycan (b. 1945)

Lycan's higher-order representation (HOR) theory extends Armstrong's and Rosenthal's frameworks by grounding consciousness in the representational function of higher-order states. On Lycan's view, phenomenal states are inner representations of the world (first-order) that are in turn represented by inner monitoring systems (higher-order). Lycan emphasizes that his view is explicitly functionalist and materialist—the higher-order monitors are physical systems—and that it can account for the qualitative character of experience through the representational content of first-order states.
