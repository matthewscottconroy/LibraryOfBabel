## Bertrand Russell (1872–1970)

Russell's structural realism about physics—developed in *The Analysis of Matter* (1927)—provides the philosophical basis for Russellian monism. Russell argued that physics describes only the relational structure of matter, not the intrinsic categorical properties of the physical. Since the intrinsic nature of physical reality is left open by physics, it can coherently be identified with—or grounded in—phenomenal or proto-phenomenal properties. Russell himself did not explicitly endorse panpsychism, but his framework became the foundation for contemporary Russellian monism, which holds that the intrinsic nature of physical reality is experiential or proto-experiential.

## Galen Strawson (b. 1952)

Strawson's 2006 paper "Realistic Monism" is the founding text of the contemporary panpsychist revival. His argument runs: (1) phenomenal consciousness genuinely exists; (2) physicalism is true (everything is physical); (3) therefore, phenomenal consciousness is physical; (4) the only coherent way to understand consciousness as physical is to see phenomenal properties as fundamental features of physical reality—i.e., panpsychism. Strawson argues that emergence of consciousness from wholly non-conscious matter is less credible than panpsychism; if anything is real, consciousness is.

## Philip Goff (b. 1975)

Goff is the most prolific and publicly engaged contemporary defender of panpsychism. His technical work in *Consciousness and Fundamental Reality* (2017) develops panpsychism through Russellian monism and confronts the combination problem directly; his popular *Galileo's Error* (2019) argues that the exclusion of consciousness from the mathematical description of nature—inaugurated by Galileo—was a foundational mistake that has trapped us in a framework unable to explain consciousness. Goff's work has significantly raised panpsychism's profile in both academic philosophy and public intellectual discourse.

## David Chalmers (b. 1966)

Chalmers has sympathetically explored panpsychism and Russellian monism as alternatives to both standard physicalism and strong dualism. In his 2015 paper on panpsychism and panprotopsychism, he distinguishes pure panpsychism (all matter has phenomenal properties) from panprotopsychism (all matter has proto-phenomenal properties that combine to produce phenomenal consciousness). Chalmers argues that both are serious options for a naturalist who takes the hard problem seriously, and that Russellian monism has the advantage of explaining how consciousness is physically grounded without reducing it to functional properties.

## Thomas Nagel (b. 1937)

Nagel has been sympathetic to panpsychism as a response to the hard problem, arguing that neither standard physicalism nor interactionist dualism is satisfactory. In *Mind and Cosmos* (2012), he argued that the natural world must be understood as containing the potential for consciousness from the beginning—not as an emergent accident but as a fundamental feature of reality. Nagel does not commit to a fully worked-out panpsychism but insists that any adequate account of nature must include teleological or proto-mental properties that allow for the explanation of consciousness, life, and rationality.

## Luke Roelofs (b. 1988)

Roelofs has developed the most technically sophisticated treatment of the combination problem—the central challenge for panpsychism. His *Combining Minds* (2019) examines how micro-level experiential properties could constitute or combine to produce macro-level conscious experiences, and he defends a constitution-based approach: macro-experiences are literally constituted by, rather than merely caused by, micro-experiences. Roelofs's work shows that the combination problem, while genuine, is not obviously more difficult than the corresponding problems in other areas of metaphysics (such as how micro-physical properties constitute macro-physical ones).
