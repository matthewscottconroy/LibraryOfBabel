## The Intrinsic Nature of the Physical

**The scenario**: Physics tells us that an electron has charge, mass, and spin—but what do these properties consist in? Physics tells us how they behave: electrons repel each other, attract positrons, deflect in magnetic fields. These are all relational, dispositional properties. But what are the electrons themselves, in their intrinsic categorical nature? Physics is silent: it gives us only the mathematical structure of the relations among physical entities, not what those entities are in themselves. Russell observed that we have complete ignorance of the intrinsic nature of matter.

**The philosophical point**: Russellian monism exploits this point to argue that the intrinsic nature of matter could be phenomenal or proto-phenomenal without conflicting with anything physics tells us. If consciousness is the "inside" of what physics describes from the "outside," panpsychism becomes not just possible but potentially explanatorily attractive. The thought experiment challenges the assumption that physics gives us a complete picture of material reality, and shows that there is genuine theoretical space for consciousness at the fundamental level without positing anything supernatural.

## Strawson's Emergence Problem

**The scenario**: Suppose panpsychism is false: matter at the fundamental level is entirely devoid of any phenomenal properties. Then consider how consciousness could arise from wholly non-conscious matter. As neurons become more complex, organized in the right ways, suddenly—at some threshold of complexity—consciousness appears. This emergence of something (phenomenal experience) from nothing (purely non-experiential matter) seems mysterious. Strawson calls this "radical emergence"—the emergence of something categorically new from the combination of things that have none of the relevant properties.

**The philosophical point**: Strawson argues that radical emergence of consciousness is less credible than panpsychism. In all other cases where we speak of emergence in science—temperature emerging from molecular motion, wetness from water molecules—the emergent property is explicable in terms of the properties of the constituents. But phenomenal consciousness has no such explanation from wholly non-phenomenal matter. Panpsychism avoids radical emergence: consciousness at the macro-level is grounded in consciousness (or proto-consciousness) at the micro-level, making the emergence intelligible. Critics argue that the "combination problem" facing panpsychism is no easier than the emergence problem it was meant to dissolve.

## The Combination Problem

**The scenario**: Suppose that every electron and quark has some minimal form of experience—some tiny proto-phenomenal property. When billions of electrons and quarks combine to form a brain, why should their experiences combine into a unified human experience? James, who first noticed this problem, called it the "compounding of consciousness." How do millions of micro-experiences combine to produce the single, unified stream of experience we have? The experiences of individual neurons (if any) do not obviously sum to a unified experience of the whole brain.

**The philosophical point**: The combination problem is the most serious internal challenge for panpsychism. It has three sub-problems: (1) how do micro-subjects combine to produce a macro-subject? (2) how do micro-qualities combine to produce macro-qualities? (3) how does the structural combination of micro-experiences produce the structural features of macro-experience? Responses include: cosmopsychism (the whole universe is the fundamental subject, and individuals are derived by "decombination"); constitutive accounts (macro-experience is literally constituted by micro-experiences without additional "combination"); and panprotopsychism (fundamental properties are proto-experiential, with consciousness emerging at higher levels without radical emergence).

## Cosmopsychism: The Universe Experiences Itself

**The scenario**: Instead of building consciousness up from micro-level experiences (panpsychism), consider building it down from a single cosmic consciousness. The universe as a whole is the fundamental subject of experience; individual human (and animal, and possibly electron) experiences are aspects or limitations of this cosmic experience—not combinations of separate micro-experiences but derivations from a single whole. This view, cosmopsychism, reverses the direction of explanation: rather than explaining macro-experience by combining micro-experiences, it explains micro-experience by deriving it from the cosmic whole.

**The philosophical point**: Cosmopsychism avoids the combination problem entirely (there is nothing to combine) but faces a different challenge: the derivation problem. How do individual, bounded, perspectival human experiences derive from a single, unbounded cosmic experience? It is not obvious that decomposition is any easier to explain than combination. The view also raises questions about the nature of the cosmic experience—is it something like total awareness of everything simultaneously?—and about how distinct subjects of experience can be carved out of a single cosmic whole without residue. Cosmopsychism connects panpsychism to certain traditions in Eastern philosophy and to monist metaphysics more generally.

## Goff's Galileo's Error Revisited

**The scenario**: Goff asks us to consider why physics has been so successful and yet has failed to explain consciousness. His answer: Galileo, in founding the mathematical science of nature, deliberately excluded from the physical world everything qualitative—colors, sounds, tastes, feels—treating them as "secondary qualities" existing only in minds. This was a productive methodological strategy but also a foundational error: consciousness was simply excluded from the physical world by stipulation, not by any empirical discovery. Physics then became constitutively incapable of accounting for consciousness.

**The philosophical point**: Goff's historical argument shows that the difficulty of explaining consciousness in physical terms may be partly an artifact of how we defined "physical" at the founding of modern science. If the definition of the physical is expanded to include experiential or proto-experiential properties—as Russellian monism does—the explanatory gap narrows. This is a methodological and conceptual point: the hard problem may be as much a problem about the concepts of "physical" and "mental" as it is a problem about the nature of the world. Panpsychism, on Goff's view, is not a return to mysticism but a revision of the conceptual framework that created the problem.
