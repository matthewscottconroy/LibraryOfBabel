# Intentionality: The Directedness of Mind

Intentionality is the property of mental states of being *about* or *directed at* objects, properties, states of affairs, or possibilities. When I think about Paris, I am in a mental state that is directed at Paris; when I desire a cup of coffee, my desire is directed at the coffee; when I fear the approaching storm, my fear is directed at the storm. This directedness — aboutness — is what Brentano called intentionality, and it is one of the most fundamental features distinguishing mental states from non-mental physical states.

## Brentano's Discovery

Franz Brentano's *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* (1874) reintroduced the scholastic concept of intentionality into modern philosophy. Brentano held that intentionality is the mark of the mental — what distinguishes mental phenomena from physical phenomena:

"Every mental phenomenon is characterized by what the Scholastics of the Middle Ages called the intentional (or mental) inexistence of an object, and what we might call, though not wholly unambiguously, reference to a content, direction towards an object."

Mental states contain their objects "intentionally within them" — the object is present to the mind in a distinctive way. The intentional object need not actually exist: you can think about Santa Claus, fear nonexistent threats, desire things that don't yet exist.

Brentano's claim: intentionality is the *mark of the mental*. Physical phenomena (rocks, tables, physical events) are not about anything; they simply are what they are. Mental phenomena are intrinsically about something else.

## Intentional Inexistence

A crucial feature of intentionality: the intentional object may not exist, and the properties the object is represented as having may not be its real properties.

- I can think about Sherlock Holmes (who doesn't exist)
- I can desire a round square (which is impossible)
- I can fear what I falsely believe is a monster (which is actually a shadow)
- I can love someone I don't fully understand (and represent them as having properties they lack)

The objects of mental states have "intentional inexistence" — they exist *in the mind* as intentional objects, independently of whether they exist in reality. This is philosophically puzzling: if the object doesn't exist, what is the mental state directed *at*?

Philosophers have proposed several accounts: intentional objects are abstract entities (Meinong), they are descriptions (Frege's sense/reference distinction), they are representations with certain contents, or the intentional object is the content itself (the thought is about whatever satisfies its descriptive content).

## The Puzzle of Non-Existent Objects

The problem of non-existent intentional objects is particularly sharp. When I think about Pegasus, my thought is genuinely directed at Pegasus — it's not about any real horse. But Pegasus doesn't exist. What am I thinking about?

**Meinong's proposal**: There are non-existent objects — things that "don't exist" but can nonetheless be "thought about." These are abstract, mind-independent, though they lack existence in the full sense. Meinong's "jungle" of non-existent objects is ontologically profligate but captures the directedness of thought.

**Russell's response**: Proper names and apparent referring terms in thought actually express disguised descriptions. "I think about Pegasus" means something like "I think about the winged horse of Greek mythology." If no such thing exists, the description is empty, but the description is what the thought is about.

**Representationalist accounts**: Mental states have representational content — they represent objects as being certain ways. The content is the immediate object of thought; whether any real object satisfies the content is a further question.

## Intentionality and Phenomenality

A central debate in philosophy of mind concerns the relationship between intentionality and phenomenal consciousness. Two questions:

**Is all intentionality phenomenally conscious?** Many states seem intentional without being phenomenally conscious: unconscious desires, automatic beliefs, subliminal perceptions. So intentionality seems wider than phenomenal consciousness.

**Is all phenomenal consciousness intentional?** Some philosophers (Crane, Tye) hold that all phenomenal experience is intentional — even apparently "pure" sensations like bodily feelings are directed at properties. Others (Block) hold that some phenomenal experience (certain moods, undirected anxiety) lacks intentional content.

The relationship between the two fundamental features of mind — intentionality and phenomenality — is one of the central open questions in philosophy of mind.
