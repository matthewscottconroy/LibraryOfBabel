## Franz Brentano (1838–1917)

Brentano introduced the concept of intentionality as the defining mark of the mental in *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* (1874). Every mental act, he argued, is directed toward an object—an intentional object that "exists inexistently" within the act. This concept of "intentional inexistence" distinguishes mental states from physical ones: a stone does not directionally refer to or represent anything, while a belief, desire, or perception is always about or of something. Brentano's thesis generated a tradition of philosophical analysis and set the central problem for theories of mental content.

## Alexius Meinong (1853–1920)

Meinong took Brentano's notion of intentional objects seriously to its logical extreme, developing a formal "theory of objects" that includes non-existent objects as genuine objects of thought. When we think of the golden mountain or the round square, Meinong held that these objects genuinely "subsist" even though they do not exist. His Gegenstandstheorie (object theory) attempted to give a systematic account of the different modes of being—existence, subsistence, and "sosein" (having properties)—applicable to both real and non-real objects. While widely criticized, Meinong's framework remains the most systematic attempt to take the content of intentional acts at face value.

## Bertrand Russell (1872–1970)

Russell famously attacked Meinong's "jungle" of non-existent objects, arguing that apparent reference to non-existents is an artifact of grammatical surface form. His theory of descriptions paraphrases "The present King of France is bald" as "There is an x such that x is presently King of France, and nothing else is, and x is bald"—an existential claim that is simply false, rather than a claim about a non-existent king. Russell's framework eliminates non-existent objects in favor of false existential claims, but critics argue it cannot handle all cases—particularly those involving propositional attitudes toward non-existents.

## Edmund Husserl (1859–1938)

Husserl developed the most detailed and rigorous phenomenological theory of intentionality. His distinction between the intentional act (noesis) and its content or meaning (noema), and his analysis of how noematic content determines the intentional object, provided tools for analyzing the structure of experience with unprecedented precision. Husserl's concept of the "intentional correlate"—the object as meant—allowed him to account for intentionality without committing to Meinongian non-existent objects or to a purely causal, externalist theory. His work influenced both Continental phenomenology and analytic philosophy of mind.

## John Searle (b. 1932)

Searle developed a theory of intentionality that grounds it in biological facts about the brain and in "conditions of satisfaction"—the conditions that must obtain for an intentional state to be satisfied. A belief is satisfied (true) if the world is as it represents; a desire is satisfied if the represented state of affairs comes to pass. Searle distinguishes intrinsic intentionality (genuine intentionality in minds) from "derived" intentionality (intentionality attributed by convention to linguistic symbols and artifacts). His insistence that genuine intentionality requires the right kind of causal powers—specifically biological ones—is part of his critique of computational functionalism.

## Tim Crane (b. 1962)

Crane has defended intentionalism—the thesis that phenomenal properties of experience are identical with or supervene on intentional properties—against its critics. In *The Objects of Thought* (2013), he defends a neo-Meinongian approach to intentionality, arguing that intentional objects need not exist for intentional acts to be genuinely directed at them. Crane's work draws on both the phenomenological tradition and analytic philosophy to develop an account of intentionality that preserves the phenomenon of intentionality without reducing it to either causal relations or abstract representational contents.
