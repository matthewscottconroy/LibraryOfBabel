## Brentano's Mark of the Mental

**The scenario**: Compare two situations: (1) A stone sits on a surface. The stone is not "about" anything—it just occupies space and interacts with its environment causally. (2) A person thinks about the Eiffel Tower. Their thought is directed toward the tower—it is "about" the tower, even though the tower is not literally inside the person's head. The person's mental state has what Brentano calls "intentional inexistence"—the tower is immanent in the act as its object, even though no physical Eiffel Tower is in the mind. Brentano claims that this feature—directedness toward an object—distinguishes every mental phenomenon from every physical phenomenon.

**The philosophical point**: Brentano's mark of the mental is the claim that intentionality is both necessary and sufficient for mentality. This has been challenged on both ends: some mental states (pains, undirected moods, emotions without objects) seem to lack intentionality; and some non-mental systems (thermostats, guided missiles) seem to have a functional analog of directedness. The thought experiment sets up the challenge of giving a principled account of what makes mental directedness genuinely different from the functional "targeting" behavior of physical systems—a challenge that motivates the move from Brentano to causal and teleological theories of content.

## The Golden Mountain

**The scenario**: The sentence "Ponce de León searched for the Fountain of Youth" is true, even though the Fountain of Youth does not exist. If intentional states (like searching) require an object, and the Fountain of Youth does not exist, what is Ponce de León's search directed toward? Meinong says: toward a non-existent object—the Fountain of Youth subsists (has properties) even though it does not exist. Russell says: there is no such object; the sentence should be paraphrased as a false existential claim about what Ponce de León was trying to find.

**The philosophical point**: The problem of intentionality toward non-existent objects reveals a deep tension. A naive theory of intentionality—that for every thought there is an object of that thought—generates an ontology of shadowy non-existents. Russell's alternative—eliminating non-existent objects via the theory of descriptions—is ontologically parsimonious but seems to miss something: there is a genuine difference between thinking about something that doesn't exist and thinking about something that does, and this difference seems to involve the thought's "presenting" an object in some way. Modern treatments often appeal to "intentional content" or "mental files" to preserve the directedness without the Meinongian ontology.

## Hallucination and the Intentional Object

**The scenario**: Macbeth sees a dagger before him but reaches out and grasps nothing. His perceptual state is directed toward a dagger—it represents a dagger as being there—but there is no dagger. Is there an intentional object of his hallucination? A naive view says yes: there is an "apparent dagger" present to his mind. A disjunctivist says no: when Macbeth hallucinates, he is not in a genuine perceptual relation with any object; he is in a mentally distinct state that merely resembles genuine perception. An intentionalist says: the hallucination has intentional content (the content that represents a dagger) but no intentional object in the ordinary sense.

**The philosophical point**: The case of hallucination is the hardest for theories of intentionality. It shows that intentional directedness does not always require an existing object—mental states can be "about" things that are not there. This creates pressure toward either (a) accepting non-existent objects as the targets of hallucination (Meinong's route), (b) treating the intentional object as the intentional content itself (a kind of internal representationalism), or (c) accepting that some apparent cases of intentionality are not genuine intentional relations at all (disjunctivism). The choice between these options has implications for the philosophy of perception and for the causal theory of reference.

## Intentionality and Phenomenality: Are They the Same?

**The scenario**: Consider two positions: (1) Intentionalism: all phenomenal properties are intentional properties—what it is like to see red just is what it is to represent something as red; phenomenal states are exhausted by their intentional content. (2) Phenomenal primitivism: phenomenal states have an intrinsic "feel" that is not captured by any intentional relation—the "redness" of seeing red is not just a matter of representing red things; it has an intrinsic qualitative character. Imagine a subject who correctly represents all color properties without any phenomenal experience—a super-functioning zombie. Which theory predicts this is possible?

**The philosophical point**: The relationship between intentionality and phenomenal consciousness is one of the deepest questions in philosophy of mind. Intentionalists hold that phenomenal consciousness is a species of intentionality, making the question of phenomenal consciousness continuous with the question of mental content. Non-intentionalists (Block, Levine) argue that phenomenal properties are irreducibly qualitative—that there is something it is like to have them that goes beyond any representational relation. The zombie thought experiment is a pressure test: if a perfect representer without phenomenal states is conceivable, intentionalism is false.
