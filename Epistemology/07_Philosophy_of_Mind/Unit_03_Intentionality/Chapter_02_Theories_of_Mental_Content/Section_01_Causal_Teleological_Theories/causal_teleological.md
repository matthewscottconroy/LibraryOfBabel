# Causal and Teleological Theories of Mental Content

How do mental representations get their content? What makes my thought a thought *about* water, rather than a thought about XYZ, or about the micro-structure of liquids? Causal and teleological theories attempt to naturalize intentionality — to explain the content of mental states in terms of natural, non-intentional facts.

## Causal Theories

Causal theories hold that a mental state has a given content because it stands in the right kind of causal relation to the things in the world that are its content. My "water" thoughts have water as their content because they are causally connected to water — water causes them (via perception, testimony, etc.), and they cause me to act in ways directed toward water.

Dretske's early account: a mental state S *indicates* property P if there is a reliable informational connection — the state is causally nomically connected to P, so that S occurs only when P is instantiated. The content of S is P.

The *pure* causal theory faces the disjunction problem. Suppose I form a "frog" thought in response to seeing both frogs and frog-shaped black patches. Both frogs and black patches cause my "frog" thoughts. What is the content of my "frog" thought? If causation determines content, the content seems to be "frog or black patch" — but this isn't my intention; I think I'm representing frogs.

## Teleological Theories

Teleological theories explain mental content in terms of the *function* of mental representations — specifically, their proper biological function. A mental state has content P if it is the function of that state (what it was designed by evolution to track) to be caused by P.

Fred Dretske's mature account and Ruth Millikan's biosemantics are the leading teleological theories.

**Millikan's Biosemantics**: A mental representation has content P if P is what the representation was "selected for" — what its biological function is to covary with. The function is determined by evolutionary history: the content is what the representation tracked in the ancestral environments in which it was selected.

For the disjunction problem: if my frog-detector was selected because it detected frogs (which frogs provided the evolutionary payoff), then the content is "frog" even if it is also caused by black patches in the current environment. Black patches were not the historical explanation for the selection of the mechanism; frogs were.

Millikan extends this to propositional content: a belief that P has the function of guiding behavior as if P were the case. When the belief is satisfied (P is the case), the behavior succeeds; when not (P is not the case), the behavior fails. Content is what the representation has the function of tracking.

## The Teleological Framework

Teleological theories connect intentionality to the broader biological notion of purpose. Mental states, on this view, are biological entities with natural purposes — they are for detecting certain things, for guiding behavior in certain ways. Intentionality is not a mysterious feature of a non-natural mind but a natural feature of biological information-processing systems.

This connects philosophy of mind to evolutionary biology and philosophy of biology: the same notion of "function" that applies to the heart (function: pump blood) applies to mental representations (function: track environmental features).

## Problems for Teleological Theories

**The new creature problem**: A creature newly created with a frog-detector mechanism (by direct design, not evolution) has a detector with no evolutionary history. Does it have any intentional states? Teleological theories seem to imply it doesn't — but this seems wrong.

**The Swampman case**: Suppose a lightning strike in a swamp creates a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of you — a "Swampman." The Swampman has no evolutionary history. Do their mental states have content? Teleological theories imply not (no proper functions without history), but intuition may resist.

**The specificity of function**: For any behavioral mechanism, there are many functions we could assign at different levels of description. What determines which level specifies the content?

**Holism about content**: The content of a mental state may depend on its inferential connections to other mental states (what follows from it, what it follows from). Causal and teleological theories abstract from these inferential connections, which may leave out something essential to content.

Both causal and teleological theories have been widely discussed and refined, and they remain the dominant naturalistic approach to the problem of mental content.
