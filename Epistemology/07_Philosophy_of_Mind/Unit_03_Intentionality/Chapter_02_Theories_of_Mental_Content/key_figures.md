## Fred Dretske (1932–2013)

Dretske pioneered the causal-informational theory of mental content. In *Knowledge and the Flow of Information* (1981), he argued that a state S represents F iff S carries the information that F is present—i.e., iff S would not occur unless F were present (under the right conditions). This naturalistic account connects content to the causal-nomological structure of the world. Dretske's later work introduced a teleological element: S represents F iff S has the function (in a biological/learning sense) of indicating F. The combination of informational and functional analysis became the basis for most naturalistic theories of content.

## Ruth Millikan (b. 1933)

Millikan's biosemantic theory is the most systematically developed teleological account of mental content. A mental representation has a certain content iff it evolved (or was selected for) to represent that content, and its proper function is fulfilled when it correctly represents the world. Millikan introduces important distinctions: between "producer devices" (mechanisms that produce representations) and "consumer devices" (mechanisms that use them), and between "proper function" (what something evolved to do) and what it actually does in any given case. Millikan's theory handles misrepresentation naturally: a representation misfires when it fails to fulfill its proper function.

## Jerry Fodor (1935–2017)

Fodor's asymmetric dependence theory was his attempt to solve the disjunction problem within a broadly causal framework. He argued that the content of a mental state S is F (not F-or-G) iff: (1) S is caused by both Fs and Gs; but (2) S's being caused by Gs depends asymmetrically on S's being caused by Fs (because S evolved or was designed to track Fs). The theory is precise but faces challenges: specifying the right asymmetric dependence relation without circularity, and explaining why biological selection history determines content rather than just recent causal history.

## Daniel Dennett (b. 1942)

Dennett's interpretationism holds that mental content is not a natural kind discovered by scientific investigation but an attribution made by an observer who adopts the "intentional stance"—predicting behavior by treating a system as rational and belief-desire-driven. Content is whatever we need to attribute to make best sense of a system's behavior; there is no "fact of the matter" about content beyond what the best interpretation says. Dennett's view dissolves the metaphysical problem of content naturalization at the cost of treating content as observer-relative rather than intrinsic.

## Karen Neander (b. 1949)

Neander's *A Mark of the Mental* (2017) develops a rigorous teleosemantic theory grounded in neuroscience. Her account uses the notion of normal conditions—conditions under which a neural system has its proper function fulfilled—to specify the content of perceptual states. Neander argues that content is determined by the function of neural mechanisms as established by natural selection, and she addresses the problem of underdetermination (multiple contents that are always co-instantiated in evolutionary history) through careful attention to the structure of neural systems and their evolutionary history.

## Tyler Burge (b. 1946)

Burge's *Origins of Objectivity* (2010) develops a perceptual-causal account of content that emphasizes the objective character of perceptual representation. Perceptual states represent objective features of the environment—not because they carry information in Dretske's sense, but because they evolved to track distal environmental properties for the organism's purposes. Burge argues that representational content is grounded in the objective character of perceptual relations between organisms and environments, without reducing content to either causal-informational or teleofunctional properties as such.
