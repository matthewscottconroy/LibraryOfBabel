## The Disjunction Problem

**The scenario**: A frog's visual system fires when a fly is present—this is its normal function, and it is the state we want to say represents "fly." But the frog's visual system also fires for any small, dark, moving thing—including BBs, pebbles, and pieces of black confetti. If the state is caused by both flies and non-fly fly-like things, why doesn't it represent "fly or small dark moving thing"? Dretske's informational theory says: the state carries information about flies (under the right, normal conditions), not about non-flies. But what makes some causal connection "normal"?

**The philosophical point**: The disjunction problem is the central challenge for causal theories of mental content. Pure causal theories face it directly: if content were just what causally triggers a state, then the content would be the highly disjunctive "fly or BB or pebble or confetti." Teleosemantic theories (Millikan, Dretske's later work) respond by appealing to biological function: the state's proper function is to indicate flies, and only this makes it a misrepresentation when a BB triggers it. But critics ask how natural selection could have selected for "representing flies" rather than "representing small dark moving things," since both have the same evolutionary history up to a point.

## Swampman

**The scenario**: Donald Davidson imagines a cosmic coincidence: a lightning bolt strikes a swamp, and by pure chance, rearranges the carbon atoms into a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of Donald Davidson, complete with all his neural organization, behavioral dispositions, and functional states. This "Swampman" walks out of the swamp and behaves exactly like Davidson. But Swampman has no causal history—no evolutionary past, no learning history, no prior causal connections to the world. Does Swampman have genuine mental content? And is he thinking genuine thoughts?

**The philosophical point**: Davidson introduced the Swampman case to test the implications of externalist and teleosemantic theories of content. Teleosemantic theories say Swampman has no genuine content: his states have no proper biological functions, since he has no evolutionary history. Causal theories may also deny him content: his states have no causal history with the relevant objects. Yet intuitively, Swampman seems to think, believe, and understand—he is, after all, functionally identical to a competent human. The case forces a choice: accept the counterintuitive verdict (no content for Swampman) or revise the theory to include functional duplicates.

## Narrow vs. Wide Content: The Parallelism

**The scenario**: Oscar on Earth and Twin Oscar on Twin Earth are internally identical. When Oscar thinks about water, his thought has wide content about H₂O. When Twin Oscar thinks about what he calls "water," his thought has wide content about XYZ. But consider: when each of them reasons about their respective substance, they reason identically—the inferences they make, the conclusions they reach, the ways they are moved by their thoughts are the same. This parallelism suggests that their thoughts share something—call it narrow content—that is psychologically explanatory even if it is not environmentally specific.

**The philosophical point**: The narrow/wide content distinction, pressed by Fodor and others, argues that psychological explanation requires content that supervenes on the internal states of the organism—narrow content—rather than on the external environment. If psychological laws and explanations must be the same for Oscar and Twin Oscar (since their behavior patterns are identical), then psychology needs a notion of content that is shared across environmental duplicates. Critics (including Burge and Putnam) deny that there is a coherent notion of narrow content that is both psychologically relevant and genuinely contentful—they argue that content is essentially environmental.

## Millikan's Bee Dance

**The scenario**: A bee performs a waggle dance to indicate the direction and distance of a food source to other bees. The bee is not consciously representing or communicating information; the dance is an evolved mechanism whose "proper function" is to indicate food location. When the dance is performed correctly, other bees successfully navigate to the food. When environmental conditions change (e.g., the dance is performed on a vertical surface), the other bees fail to navigate correctly—the dance misfires. Millikan argues this is a case of genuine biological representation with determinate content.

**The philosophical point**: Millikan uses the bee dance to motivate teleosemantics by showing that biological representation—genuine misrepresentation—is possible without any deliberate cognitive intention. The dance has content (food is in direction D at distance X) even though no bee "intends" to communicate this, because that is its biological proper function. This naturalistic account extends to mental states: neural representations have content because of their proper biological functions, just as the bee dance does. Critics argue that proper-function content is under-determined (the dance could equally be said to represent angle-of-flight and duration) and that evolutionary considerations underdetermine specific semantic content.
