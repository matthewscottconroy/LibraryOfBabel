# The Language of Thought Hypothesis

Jerry Fodor's Language of Thought Hypothesis (LOT), developed in *The Language of Thought* (1975) and subsequent works, holds that thinking consists in the manipulation of mental representations that have the structure of sentences in a language — a "Mentalese" or Language of Thought. These representations are syntactically structured (they have compositional structure) and are processed by computational mechanisms that are sensitive to their syntax.

## The Computational/Representational Theory of Mind

LOT is the flagship version of the Computational/Representational Theory of Mind (CRTM), which holds:

1. Mental processes are computational processes — they operate over formal, syntactic properties of representations.
2. Mental representations are structured symbols with semantic content — they stand for things in the world.
3. The computational processes preserve semantic properties because they operate on syntactic properties that "mirror" semantic ones.

This is essentially the view that the mind is something like a computer, and thinking is something like running programs. The representations are the data structures; the cognitive processes are the programs.

## The Systematicity and Productivity Arguments

Fodor's primary argument for LOT is from the systematicity and productivity of thought.

**Productivity**: Human thought is potentially infinite in extent. We can entertain indefinitely many thoughts — there is no upper bound on the number of propositions we can think. This productivity suggests that thoughts are compositionally structured: complex thoughts are built from simpler components in ways that generate unbounded complexity, just as sentences in natural language are composed from words and phrases.

**Systematicity**: If you can think "John loves Mary," you can also think "Mary loves John." The ability to entertain one thought seems to imply the ability to entertain systematically related thoughts. This systematicity is explained if the representations involved are structured: the same parts (JOHN, MARY, LOVES) can be assembled in different ways.

Connectionists have attempted to implement systematicity in networks without explicit compositional structure, but Fodor and Pylyshyn argued that such implementations are either accidental (not principled) or smuggle in compositional structure implicitly.

## The Mentalese Vocabulary

LOT postulates a language of thought — Mentalese — in which cognitive processing takes place. Unlike natural language, Mentalese is:

**Innate**: The basic vocabulary of Mentalese must be innate — we cannot learn all our concepts from experience, since learning requires already having the representational resources to formulate hypotheses. Fodor pushed this argument into the radical conclusion that most concepts are innate — including "CARBURETOR" and other apparently complex, culturally specific concepts.

**Non-linguistic**: Mentalese underlies natural language; it is not itself a natural language. Animals and pre-linguistic infants have cognitive processes in Mentalese, even though they don't speak.

**Syntactically structured**: Mentalese has combinatorial syntax: complex representations are formed from simpler ones by concatenation and other structural operations.

## The Frame Problem

One challenge for LOT concerns the *frame problem* in AI: when you update one belief (the block moved), which other beliefs do you update? A reasoning system must determine, for any change, which aspects of the world it doesn't change. For humans, this seems effortless; for LOT systems, it is technically challenging.

Fodor acknowledged the frame problem as a major difficulty, arguing that it shows that much of cognition is *unencapsulated* — it requires access to the entire cognitive system in a way that current LOT theories don't explain.

## Challenges from Connectionism

Connectionist networks process information differently from LOT: they use distributed representations over activation patterns, not discrete symbolic representations. Connectionists argue that the mind is more like a neural network than a symbol processor.

Fodor and Pylyshyn's response: connectionist networks either implement a symbolic architecture (in which case LOT is vindicated at the implementation level) or they exhibit systematicity accidentally (in which case they are cognitively inadequate). The debate between LOT and connectionism remains active, particularly as large neural networks challenge traditional cognitive architectures.

## Legacy

LOT remains influential in cognitive science, linguistics, and philosophy of mind. The hypothesis connects to generative linguistics (Chomsky's Universal Grammar), cognitive science (representational and computational models of mind), and philosophy of language (the hypothesis that thought precedes language). Even for those who reject the specific details, Fodor's arguments for productivity and systematicity set the terms for ongoing debates.
