## Jerry Fodor (1935–2017)

Fodor is the originator and principal defender of the Language of Thought (LOT) hypothesis. His central argument is that the productivity, systematicity, and inferential coherence of human thought are best explained by positing a system of mental representations with a combinatorial syntax analogous to a natural language. Just as the infinity of grammatical English sentences is generated by a finite set of rules and a finite vocabulary, the infinity of thinkable thoughts is generated by combinatorial operations on a finite set of primitive mental symbols. Fodor also argued for the modularity of the mind (specialized input systems) and for the reality of propositional attitudes as the proper objects of intentional psychology.

## Alan Newell (1927–1992) and Herbert Simon (1916–2001)

Newell and Simon developed the "physical symbol system hypothesis"—the claim that an intelligent system must be capable of manipulating symbol structures according to formal rules, and that this is both necessary and sufficient for general intelligence. Their work on GOFAI (Good Old-Fashioned Artificial Intelligence), including General Problem Solver and later chess-playing programs, represented the most developed computational implementation of LOT-like principles. Their hypothesis is the scientific counterpart of Fodor's philosophical thesis and faced many of the same challenges.

## Paul Smolensky (b. 1955)

Smolensky developed the most philosophically sophisticated connectionist alternative to LOT. His "tensor product" framework attempts to give connectionist systems a compositional structure that can, in principle, implement LOT-like representations distributedly. He argued that the evidence for systematicity is better explained by his integrated connectionist/symbolic (ICS) architecture than by classical LOT, since connectionist networks can exhibit systematicity as a soft constraint rather than a hard architectural requirement. The Fodor-Smolensky debate shaped the philosophy of cognitive architecture for decades.

## John Searle (b. 1932)

Searle's Chinese Room argument targets the computational conception of mind that underlies LOT. He argues that even if a system manipulates formal symbols exactly as LOT describes, this gives it only syntactic properties—formal relationships among symbols—not semantic content. Genuinely intentional states require "original" intentionality (not derived from conventions), which requires the right kind of causal powers that biological systems but not silicon systems possess. Searle's critique challenges the assumption that LOT provides an account of genuine mental content rather than mere symbol-manipulation.

## Andy Clark (b. 1957)

Clark's work on embodied and extended cognition challenges the LOT framework's assumption that cognition is a matter of internal symbolic computation. In *Being There* (1997) and later work, Clark argues that cognitive processes are not confined to the brain but are distributed across brain, body, and environment. This challenges the LOT model's assumption that the relevant computations are internal and over discrete, context-independent representations. Clark's connectionism-friendly position also draws on Smolensky's framework to argue that distributed representations can exhibit the relevant cognitive properties without classical symbolic architecture.

## Susan Schneider (b. 1972)

Schneider's *The Language of Thought* (2011) is the most comprehensive recent defense of the LOT hypothesis. She updates Fodor's arguments to address subsequent challenges—including from connectionism, dynamical systems theory, and embodied cognition—and argues that the case for LOT is actually stronger than Fodor himself came to believe. She defends the view that intentional psychology requires a symbolic substrate, and that the productivity and systematicity arguments remain compelling even when the neurological details of implementation are taken seriously.
