## Jaegwon Kim (1934–2019)

Kim is the philosopher most associated with developing and pressing the causal exclusion argument. His central claim: if every physical event has a sufficient physical cause (causal closure), and if mental events are distinct from physical events (as non-reductive physicalism maintains), then mental events are causally excluded from producing physical effects—they are redundant at best, epiphenomenal at worst. Kim argued that non-reductive physicalism cannot secure genuine mental causation, and that the only satisfactory options are type-type reduction (which many find too strong) or acceptance that consciousness is epiphenomenal. His work dominated the mental causation literature for thirty years.

## Donald Davidson (1917–2003)

Davidson's anomalous monism attempted to reconcile the causal efficacy of the mental with the irreducibility of mental descriptions. Token mental events are identical with physical events and are thus causally efficacious via their physical descriptions. But there are no strict psychophysical laws—mental descriptions are governed by holistic rationality constraints that cannot be captured by the nomological vocabulary of physics. Davidson thought this allowed mental causation without reduction, but Kim pressed the objection that on this view, mental events cause effects only qua physical—their mental properties are causally irrelevant.

## Stephen Yablo (b. 1957)

Yablo proposed the proportionality approach to mental causation as an alternative to both identity theory and non-reductive physicalism. A cause C is proportional to an effect E if C is the most specific description of the causal antecedent that is sufficient for E. When a pigeon pecks a red button, the color red is the proportional cause—not the specific shade of scarlet, which is over-determinate. Yablo argues that mental properties are often proportional causes of behavior, while specific neural properties are over-determinate—providing a principled way to preserve mental causation without identification.

## Karen Bennett (b. 1970)

Bennett's philosophical work on the exclusion argument carefully distinguishes different formulations of the causal closure principle and different versions of the exclusion argument, showing that many versions are not compelled by non-reductive physicalists. She argues that the exclusion argument depends on contested assumptions—about the transitivity of causation, the nature of the closure principle, and the criteria for causal relevance—and that a sophisticated non-reductive physicalist can reject one or more of these assumptions without ad hoc maneuvering.

## Ernest Sosa (b. 1940)

Sosa argued early for the compatibility of supervenient causation with the causal closure of the physical. His "aspects" framework holds that mental and physical descriptions pick out different aspects of the same events; these different aspects can be causally relevant in different contexts, without generating genuine overdetermination. Sosa's approach anticipates later "causal pluralism" views that allow different causal descriptions to be appropriate in different contexts without conflict.

## Sydney Shoemaker (1931–2022)

Shoemaker's approach to mental causation holds that mental properties are causally efficacious in virtue of the physical properties that realize them: a mental property M causes effect E because M is realized by a physical property P, and P has the causal power to produce E. On Shoemaker's view, this is not a case of overdetermination (two independent causes of E) but a single causal process with a physical implementation—where the mental property is causally relevant because it determines which physical powers are at work.
