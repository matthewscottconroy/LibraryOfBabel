# Computationalism and the Turing Test

Computationalism is the view that the mind is, in a fundamental sense, a computing machine — that mental processes are computational processes, and that the appropriate level of description for mental phenomena is computational. This view, which drew initially on the development of digital computing, has been enormously influential in philosophy of mind, cognitive science, and artificial intelligence.

## Turing's Contribution

Alan Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" framed the question "Can machines think?" and proposed a behavioral criterion for answering it: the *imitation game*, now called the Turing Test. A human interrogator communicates via text with two entities — one a human, one a computer — and tries to determine which is which. If the computer can fool the interrogator, it has passed the test and should, Turing argued, be regarded as thinking.

The Turing Test is important for two reasons. First, it redirects the question "can machines think?" from a metaphysical question (do machines have inner experience?) to a behavioral one (can machines behave indistinguishably from humans?). Second, it implicitly endorses a functionalist picture: what matters is the capacity to perform the intellectual functions that we associate with thinking.

Turing anticipated objections: the "theological objection" (only God can create thinking beings), the "consciousness objection" (machines aren't conscious), the "mathematical objection" (Gödel's theorems show machines have limitations humans don't). His responses remain instructive.

## Classical (GOFAI) Computationalism

The classical AI paradigm — Good Old-Fashioned AI (GOFAI, Haugeland's term) — holds that intelligence consists in the manipulation of discrete symbolic representations according to explicit rules. Intelligence is symbol manipulation: the right symbols, organized in the right structures, manipulated by the right rules.

Newell and Simon's Physical Symbol System Hypothesis: a physical symbol system has the necessary and sufficient means for general intelligent action. Human intelligence and artificial intelligence are both instances of physical symbol systems.

This view connects directly to Fodor's Language of Thought: thought is symbol manipulation in Mentalese. Classical AI attempts to implement this in machines.

## Searle's Chinese Room

John Searle's Chinese Room thought experiment (1980) attacks computationalism directly. Suppose Searle is locked in a room with a large rule book that tells him what Chinese symbols to output in response to Chinese symbol inputs. From outside the room, the outputs look like competent Chinese language use. But Searle inside the room doesn't understand Chinese — he's just following rules without any semantic comprehension.

The argument: the room implements a program that produces outputs indistinguishable from a Chinese speaker. But the room doesn't understand Chinese. Therefore, implementing a program is not sufficient for understanding. Syntax is not sufficient for semantics. Computation is not sufficient for intentionality or consciousness.

Responses:
- **The systems reply**: The room-plus-rules understands Chinese even if Searle-inside doesn't. Searle responds: even if Searle memorizes the rules, he still doesn't understand.
- **The robot reply**: If the symbol manipulator is connected to sensors and motors (is embodied), it might develop genuine understanding.
- **The brain simulator reply**: If the program simulates every neuron in a Chinese speaker's brain, would the simulation understand? Searle doubts this but many find it more compelling.

## The Significance of the Chinese Room

The Chinese Room is one of the most discussed thought experiments in contemporary philosophy. Its lesson — if correct — is that computation (formal, syntactic manipulation of symbols) cannot by itself generate intentionality (genuine aboutness, semantic content, understanding). This would mean that computationalism as a theory of mind is fundamentally mistaken.

Defenders of computationalism typically attack the premise that syntactic manipulation without semantic understanding is possible. If a system implements the right computations, they argue, it necessarily has the semantic properties.

## Beyond Classical Computationalism

Contemporary philosophy of mind extends beyond classical GOFAI to include connectionism (neural networks), embodied cognition, dynamical systems theory, and predictive processing (discussed in subsequent sections). These approaches challenge the classical picture of computation while retaining something like a computational/information-processing perspective on mind.
