## Primary Sources

- Alan Turing, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," *Mind* 59, 1950. Introduces the imitation game (Turing test) and argues that machines can think.

- Allen Newell and Herbert Simon, "Computer Science as Empirical Inquiry: Symbols and Search," *Communications of the ACM* 19, 1976. The Physical Symbol System Hypothesis; argues that symbol manipulation is necessary and sufficient for intelligence.

- John Searle, "Minds, Brains and Programs," *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 3, 1980. The Chinese Room argument against strong AI and computational functionalism.

- Jerry Fodor, *The Language of Thought*, Crowell, 1975. The most philosophically developed version of computationalism; thinking as computation over Mentalese.

- Hubert Dreyfus, *What Computers Can't Do*, Harper & Row, 1972. Phenomenological critique of classical AI; argues that intelligence requires embodied coping, not symbol manipulation.

- Roger Penrose, *The Emperor's New Mind*, Oxford University Press, 1989. Argues that human mathematical insight cannot be captured by any algorithm; uses Gödel's theorem against computationalism.

- David Chalmers, "Does a Rock Implement Every Finite-State Automaton?" *Synthese* 108, 1996. Examines the trivialization objection to computationalism and proposes a response.

## Key Secondary Sources

- Jack Copeland, *Artificial Intelligence: A Philosophical Introduction*, Blackwell, 1993. Accessible philosophical survey; good on the Turing test and its implications.

- Margaret Boden, *The Philosophy of Artificial Intelligence*, Oxford University Press, 1990. Anthology; contains key papers including Turing, Searle, and early responses.

- Stevan Harnad, "Other Bodies, Other Minds," *Minds and Machines* 1, 1991. Develops the "symbol grounding problem"—the problem of connecting computation to meaning.

- Susan Schneider, *Artificial You*, Princeton University Press, 2019. Explores computationalism and its implications for personal identity and consciousness.

- Zenon Pylyshyn, *Computation and Cognition*, MIT Press, 1984. Defends computationalism on scientific grounds; addresses cognitive science methodology.

- Andy Clark, *Natural-Born Cyborgs*, Oxford University Press, 2003. Argues for a broadly computationalist picture that is distributed across brain, body, and technology.

- Scott Aaronson, *Quantum Computing Since Democritus*, Cambridge University Press, 2013. Technical but accessible; relevant to questions about the physical limits of computation and their implications for mind.
