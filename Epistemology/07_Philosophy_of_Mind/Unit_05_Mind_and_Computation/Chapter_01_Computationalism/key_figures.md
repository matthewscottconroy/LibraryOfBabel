## Alan Turing (1912–1954)

Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" is the founding text of computational theories of mind. Turing proposed the imitation game (now called the Turing test) as an operational criterion for machine intelligence: a machine is intelligent if it can converse in text so that a human interrogator cannot reliably distinguish it from a human. Turing deflected philosophical concerns about whether machines can "really" think by arguing that the question is operationally empty—what matters is behavioral indistinguishability. His paper anticipated and addressed many objections to machine intelligence, from Gödel's theorem to consciousness.

## Allen Newell (1927–1992) and Herbert Simon (1916–2001)

Newell and Simon developed GOFAI (Good Old-Fashioned Artificial Intelligence) and articulated its philosophical foundation in the Physical Symbol System Hypothesis: intelligence requires a physical system capable of manipulating symbol structures according to formal rules, and this is sufficient for general intelligent action. Their work on General Problem Solver, SOAR, and other architectures represented the most ambitious implementation of the computationalist thesis. Their hypothesis was both an empirical prediction and a philosophical claim about the nature of intelligence.

## John Searle (b. 1932)

Searle is GOFAI's most influential critic. The Chinese Room argument (1980) argues that a system running any program whatsoever has only syntactic properties—formal relations among symbols—but not semantic properties, since syntax does not suffice for semantics. A computationalist system processes symbols without understanding what they mean; understanding requires "intrinsic intentionality," which for Searle requires the right kind of biological causal powers. Searle's argument has generated enormous philosophical debate and remains the central challenge for computationalism to answer.

## Hubert Dreyfus (1929–2017)

Dreyfus's phenomenologically grounded critique of classical AI, developed in *What Computers Can't Do* (1972) and subsequent works, argued that human intelligence is not best characterized as symbol manipulation. Drawing on Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty, Dreyfus argued that human coping with the world is grounded in embodied, practical engagement—a form of "knowing how" that is not articulable as a set of explicit rules. His criticisms proved prescient: early AI systems struggled exactly with the common-sense reasoning and contextual flexibility that Dreyfus predicted would be difficult.

## Roger Penrose (b. 1931)

Penrose's *The Emperor's New Mind* (1989) and *Shadows of the Mind* (1994) argue that human mathematical understanding transcends what any algorithm can compute. His argument draws on Gödel's incompleteness theorems: any consistent formal system capable of arithmetic is incomplete (it cannot prove its own consistency), but human mathematicians can see that such systems are consistent. This suggests human mathematical insight is non-algorithmic, requiring quantum mechanical processes in the brain (specifically, in microtubules). Penrose's argument is controversial among both mathematicians and philosophers of mind.

## Stevan Harnad (b. 1945)

Harnad developed the symbol grounding problem—the challenge of explaining how computational symbols acquire genuine meaning when they are defined purely in terms of relations to other symbols (not to the things they are supposed to represent). A dictionary defines words in terms of other words; if we know no language, we cannot ground the definitions in genuine understanding. Harnad argued that grounding requires sensorimotor contact with the world and that GOFAI, which operates over abstract symbols without sensorimotor connection, cannot ground meaning—providing an independent route to Searle's conclusion.
