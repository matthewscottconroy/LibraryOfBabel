## Maurice Merleau-Ponty (1908–1961)

Merleau-Ponty's *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945) is the philosophical foundation of embodied cognition. He argued that the body is not merely a physical object that the mind inhabits but the fundamental medium through which we encounter the world—a "lived body" that shapes perception, action, and understanding. His analysis of the blind man's cane as an extension of the body schema—where the cane becomes a perceptual organ rather than a mere tool—directly anticipates the extended mind thesis. Merleau-Ponty's critique of the intellectualist tradition (which analyzes cognition in terms of representation) prefigures contemporary challenges to GOFAI.

## Andy Clark (b. 1957)

Clark is the most influential philosopher of embodied and extended mind. His *Being There* (1997) argued against the representationalist orthodoxy of GOFAI, proposing instead that intelligent behavior often involves tight coupling between brain, body, and environment rather than internal computation over rich world-models. His 1998 paper with Chalmers introduced the extended mind thesis with the parity principle: if an external resource plays a role functionally equivalent to an internal process we would call cognitive, then the external resource is part of the cognitive system. Clark's subsequent *Supersizing the Mind* (2008) responded to objections and extended the framework.

## David Chalmers (b. 1966)

Chalmers co-developed the extended mind thesis in the famous 1998 paper with Clark. His contribution was partly philosophical groundwork: the argument draws on his earlier functionalism about mental states and the thought experiment of Otto's notebook. Chalmers has maintained that the extended mind thesis follows naturally from functionalism: if mental states are individuated by their functional roles, and if external resources can play those roles, then mental states can extend beyond the brain. However, Chalmers himself has also explored the limits of the view in relation to phenomenal consciousness.

## Francisco Varela (1946–2001)

Varela, together with Thompson and Rosch, developed enactivism in *The Embodied Mind* (1991). Enactivism holds that cognition is not internal computation but the ongoing process by which an organism enacts or brings forth a world through its structural coupling with the environment. Perception is not passive reception of information but an active process in which the organism and environment co-constitute each other. Varela's work drew on phenomenology (Husserl, Merleau-Ponty), dynamical systems theory, and Buddhist philosophy of mind to develop an alternative to representationalist cognitive science.

## Lawrence Barsalou (b. 1952)

Barsalou's grounded cognition program develops the empirical case for embodied cognition. His "perceptual symbol systems" theory argues that cognition does not operate over amodal abstract symbols but over partial simulations of sensorimotor states—perceptual symbols that are grounded in the body's interaction with the world. Empirical evidence (from studies of language processing, conceptual representation, and motor planning) supports the view that understanding a word for a physical object activates sensorimotor areas of the brain. Barsalou's work bridges the philosophy of embodied cognition and experimental psychology.

## Alva Noë (b. 1964)

Noë's *Action in Perception* (2004) develops an enactivist theory of visual perception. He argues that perceiving is a kind of action: it involves the exercise of sensorimotor skills—knowledge of how one's movements affect one's perceptual experience. Perception is not a process that occurs in the brain alone but an activity that extends across brain, body, and environment. Noë uses this framework to explain perceptual phenomena (shape constancy, the perceived richness of visual experience) that he argues cannot be explained by purely internal, representational theories.
