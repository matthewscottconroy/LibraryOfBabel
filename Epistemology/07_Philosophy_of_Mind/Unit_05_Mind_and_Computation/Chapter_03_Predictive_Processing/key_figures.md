## Hermann von Helmholtz (1821–1894)

Helmholtz proposed the idea that perception is "unconscious inference"—the brain actively constructs perceptual experience by inferring the most probable cause of sensory signals, rather than passively receiving information from the world. His observation that we unconsciously compensate for eye movements, light sources, and other confounding factors in perception laid the groundwork for predictive processing. Helmholtz's insight that the brain is a hypothesis-generating machine rather than a passive recorder of stimuli anticipates the core claim of contemporary predictive processing frameworks.

## Karl Friston (b. 1959)

Friston is the primary architect of the free energy principle and active inference. His mathematically rigorous framework, developed over two decades, proposes that all biological self-organizing systems minimize "free energy"—a quantity related to the difference between the brain's predictions and its sensory signals (prediction error). Friston's framework unifies perception, action, attention, learning, and cognition under a single mathematical principle, and has been applied to psychiatry (modeling hallucination and delusion), development, and evolutionary biology. His work is among the most cited in theoretical neuroscience.

## Andy Clark (b. 1957)

Clark's *Surfing Uncertainty* (2016) provides the most philosophically sophisticated treatment of predictive processing. He argues that the predictive brain framework transforms our understanding of perception (which becomes active prediction rather than passive reception), action (which becomes prediction of proprioceptive states), attention (which becomes precision-weighting of prediction errors), and the relationship between mind and world. Clark develops the framework's implications for embodied cognition, the extended mind, and consciousness, arguing that predictive processing is naturally compatible with—and indeed supports—his earlier embodied and extended mind views.

## Jakob Hohwy (b. 1972)

Hohwy's *The Predictive Mind* (2013) develops predictive processing as a philosophical framework and draws conclusions that are, in some respects, the inverse of Clark's. While Clark sees predictive processing as supporting embodied, extended cognition, Hohwy argues that the framework implies a broadly Helmholtzian, internalist picture: the brain is fundamentally hidden from the world, receiving only impoverished sensory signals and constructing its best hypothesis about the external causes of those signals. Hohwy's reading emphasizes that perception is always mediated by prior beliefs and that there is a fundamental barrier between the brain and the world.

## Anil Seth (b. 1972)

Seth's work develops predictive processing into a theory of consciousness. His central claim—that perception is a "controlled hallucination"—holds that what we experience as the external world and as our own body and self is always the brain's best prediction, constrained but not determined by sensory signals. Seth extends this to interoception: the sense of having a body and being an embodied self is a predictive model of bodily signals. His widely read *Being You* (2021) makes these ideas accessible and connects them to empirical consciousness research, psychedelics, and the nature of the self.

## Lisa Feldman Barrett (b. 1963)

Barrett's "theory of constructed emotion" draws on predictive processing to argue that emotions are not hard-wired responses triggered by external events but are constructions: the brain uses prior emotional concepts to predict and explain interoceptive and exteroceptive signals. This challenges the received view that there are universal "basic emotions" with fixed biological substrates, arguing instead that emotion concepts shape emotional experience. Barrett's work exemplifies how predictive processing can be applied to develop a naturalistic, constructivist account of psychological experience.
