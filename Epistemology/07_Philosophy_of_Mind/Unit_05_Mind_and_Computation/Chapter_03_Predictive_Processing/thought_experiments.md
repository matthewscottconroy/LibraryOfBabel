## Perception as Controlled Hallucination

**The scenario**: Consider the hollow mask illusion: a hollow face seen from inside (the concave side) is perceived as a normal convex face. The brain's prior model of faces—always convex, always protruding—overrides the actual sensory signal (which, if processed without priors, would indicate a concave surface). Even when observers know the mask is hollow, they continue to see it as convex. The brain's top-down prediction wins over the bottom-up sensory evidence.

**The philosophical point**: Seth uses the hollow mask case to illustrate what he means by perception as "controlled hallucination." All perception involves the brain imposing its best predictive model on sensory data; in most circumstances this model is constrained enough by sensory evidence to track the world accurately. But in the hollow mask case, the prior is so strong that it overrides accurate sensory processing. The "normal" case is not so different: we always perceive a construction, not the world directly. This has implications for epistemology (direct realism is false; perception is always inference), for consciousness (what we experience is a model, not the world), and for understanding conditions like schizophrenia (where the balance between top-down and bottom-up may be disrupted).

## Friston's Free Energy and the Self

**The scenario**: A bacterium navigates toward glucose and away from toxins. On Friston's account, this can be understood in terms of free energy minimization: the bacterium has a model of its expected sensory states (glucose-rich environments) and acts to minimize the divergence between expected and actual sensory states. It is not "computing" or "representing" in any rich sense—it is simply a self-organizing system that resists dissolution by maintaining itself in its expected states. Now extend this upward: human beings are doing the same thing at a dramatically higher level of complexity.

**The philosophical point**: Friston's free energy principle applies across all scales of biological organization, from single cells to social systems. For philosophy, the striking claim is that agency, goal-directedness, and even selfhood are all manifestations of free energy minimization—of organisms maintaining themselves in their expected states by modeling their environment and acting on those models. This offers a naturalistic account of intentionality and agency without requiring explicit representations or deliberate planning. Critics worry that the framework is too general—that it applies to everything that persists (thermostats, stars) and thus fails to distinguish genuine cognitive systems from mere physical regularities.

## Active Inference: Changing the World vs. Changing the Prediction

**The scenario**: You feel cold (your skin temperature receptors report below-expected temperature). A purely passive Bayesian system would update its model of the environment to reflect the cold. But active inference adds a second option: the organism can act to change the sensory input—by putting on a coat, moving to a warmer location, generating body heat—thus making the sensory signal match the prediction rather than the other way around. On Friston's account, this is the fundamental structure of purposive action: acting to fulfill one's predictions.

**The philosophical point**: Active inference provides a unified account of perception and action that dissolves the traditional distinction between them. In perception, the organism updates its model to explain sensory signals. In action, the organism changes the sensory signals to match its model. Both are prediction-error minimization; they differ only in which side of the prediction-signal gap is adjusted. Philosophically, this is important because it suggests that agency is not a fundamentally different kind of process from cognition—it is cognition oriented toward the world rather than toward internal model-updating. This also raises questions about free will: if action is prediction fulfillment, in what sense is it free?

## The Bayesian Brain in Dreaming

**The scenario**: During dreaming, the brain continues to generate predictive models of the world, but without the normal bottom-up constraints of sensory input. Dreams are often vivid, detailed, and—from within the dream—compelling: the dreamer does not normally know they are dreaming. From the predictive processing perspective, dreams are a case where the brain's generative model runs unconstrained by actual sensory evidence, producing a constructed reality that is entirely top-down.

**The philosophical point**: The dream case illuminates the constructive character of all perception: if the machinery that generates perceptual experience can run without sensory input—producing full-blown, convincing experiences—then ordinary waking perception involves the same machinery, just with the addition of sensory constraints. This supports the "controlled hallucination" picture and raises deep epistemological questions (how do we know our waking experience is more constrained than dreaming?). It also connects to philosophical traditions—Descartes's dream argument, Hindu philosophy of maya—while grounding them in empirical neuroscience.

## Predictive Processing and Hallucination

**The scenario**: A patient with schizophrenia hears voices—auditory verbal hallucinations. On the predictive processing account, these are cases where the brain's top-down predictions are so strong (or the precision-weighting of prediction errors is so reduced) that the predicted auditory signal is experienced as an actual sensory event. The patient's brain predicts speech and, because the bottom-up error signal is suppressed or ignored, experiences that prediction as a genuine perception.

**The philosophical point**: The predictive processing account of hallucination provides a specific, testable model that connects philosophical questions about the relationship between perception and hallucination to empirical psychiatry. It also raises philosophical questions about the distinction between perception and imagination: if both involve generative models and differ only in their relationship to sensory input, is the distinction between perceiving and imagining merely quantitative (degree of sensory constraint) rather than qualitative? And what implications does this have for the epistemological notion of the "given"—the idea that perception provides a direct, unmediated contact with reality?
