# Personal Identity: What Makes You the Same Person Over Time?

Personal identity over time is the question of what makes a person at one time the same person as a person at another time. You were once a child; you are now an adult. Many things have changed — your body, your beliefs, your values, your memories. What makes you the same person as that child? This question intersects with deep issues about the self, survival, moral responsibility, and the nature of persons.

## Why Personal Identity Matters

Personal identity matters for several practical and philosophical reasons:

**Moral responsibility**: We hold people responsible for past actions. For this to be fair, the person held responsible must be the same person who performed the action. If personal identity breaks down, moral responsibility becomes problematic.

**Survival and death**: If personal identity requires continuous biological life, then death is clearly the end. But if personal identity can persist through radical change, or be reestablished after interruption, questions about death, resurrection, teleportation, and brain transplants become complicated.

**Rational planning**: We make plans that require us to be the same person in the future as we are now. Investing in education, making long-term commitments — these presuppose a robust sense of personal identity over time.

## The Psychological Continuity View

John Locke's account in the *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1689) is the classic statement of psychological continuity. For Locke, personal identity consists in *memory continuity*: you are the same person as the person who performed an action if you can remember performing that action (or are connected to it by a chain of overlapping memories).

Sydney Shoemaker, Derek Parfit, and others developed sophisticated versions of psychological continuity. On Parfit's account in *Reasons and Persons* (1984), personal identity consists in overlapping chains of psychological connectedness: memory connections, continuity of character and values, continuity of intentions and their fulfillment, and so on. The person now and the person then are the same if there is sufficient psychological continuity and connectedness.

## Parfit's Reductionism

Parfit's most influential contribution is his *reductionism* about personal identity. He argues that personal identity is not a further fact over and above physical and psychological facts. To know everything about a person's psychology and biology is to know everything about their identity; there is no additional question about whether they are "really" the same person.

This contrasts with *non-reductionism* (associated with Chisholm and some versions of soul theory): there is a further fact about personal identity — whether the same *self* or *person* persists — that is not captured by physical or psychological continuity.

Parfit argued that reductionism, when accepted, transforms our attitudes toward survival and death. If personal identity is just psychological continuity, then what matters in survival is not strict numerical identity but the preservation of what is psychologically continuous. This has radical implications: perhaps personal identity matters less than we think; what matters is the continuation of our psychological life, which can be a matter of degree.

## The Fission Problem

Fission thought experiments are the classic challenge for personal identity theories. Suppose your brain is divided and each hemisphere transplanted into a different body. Both resulting people remember your life, have your psychological character, and are continuous with you. Which one is you?

On any natural view, both resulting people have the same claim to be you. But they can't both be you (identity is transitive; if A is you and B is you, then A is B, but the two resulting people are clearly distinct). And neither is obviously more "you" than the other.

Parfit's response: this shows that personal identity is not what matters. What matters — psychological continuity — is had by both resulting people. The question "which one is really me?" is an empty question, the kind that only arises if we assume there is always a determinate fact about personal identity. Reductionism lets us say: both have what matters; the question of which is "really" me has no further answer.

## Bodily Continuity and Animalism

Eric Olson defends *animalism*: personal identity consists in biological continuity — you are a biological organism, and you persist exactly as long as that organism persists. This view has the advantage of simplicity and alignment with common sense about animals: a dog is the same dog over time because it is the same organism.

But animalism struggles with cases where psychology and biology come apart: if your brain were transplanted into another body, would you be the same biological organism (in the new body) or the same organism as the old body (now headless)? Most people intuit that the person goes with the brain, suggesting that psychological factors are relevant after all.
