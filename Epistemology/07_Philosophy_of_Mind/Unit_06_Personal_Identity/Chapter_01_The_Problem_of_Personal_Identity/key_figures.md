## John Locke (1632–1704)

Locke formulated the memory theory of personal identity in *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1689). For Locke, what makes a person the same person over time is the psychological relation of memory: a person at time t₂ is the same as a person at t₁ iff the t₂-person can remember the experiences of the t₁-person. Locke distinguished personal identity (psychological) from the identity of a man (biological organism) and the identity of a substance (metaphysical). His theory was the first systematic attempt to ground personal identity in psychological rather than metaphysical or biological facts, establishing the framework for subsequent debate.

## David Hume (1711–1776)

Hume's bundle theory holds that there is no persistent self—only a bundle of perceptions. When he introspects, Hume reports finding only particular impressions and ideas, never a self that has them. The self is a fiction: we project a sense of unity and continuity onto a succession of experiences that are in fact distinct and momentary. Hume's bundle theory challenges any substantial account of personal identity and connects the question of personal identity to broader skeptical concerns about substance and causation. His account was enormously influential on Buddhist no-self views and on Parfit's reductionism.

## Derek Parfit (1942–2017)

Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* (1984) is the most important work on personal identity of the twentieth century. Parfit argued for psychological continuity as the criterion of personal identity, but more importantly, he argued that personal identity is not what matters in survival. Through fission cases and other thought experiments, he showed that what we care about—psychological continuity—can hold without strict identity and that identity can hold without what we care about. Parfit's reductionism holds that facts about personal identity are not further facts over and above facts about physical and psychological continuity—there is no separately existing self.

## Eric Olson (b. 1962)

Olson defends animalism—the view that we are human animals, not Cartesian egos or psychological subjects. On animalism, the criterion of personal identity is biological: we persist as long as our biological organism persists. The main objection—that animalists must say we survive persistent vegetative states—is accepted by Olson as a consequence of the view's coherence. Animalism challenges psychological continuity theories by asking about cases where psychology and biology come apart: if a person's brain is transplanted into another body, does the person go with the brain (psychology) or stay with the body (biology)?

## Marya Schechtman (b. 1960)

Schechtman's narrative theory holds that personal identity is constituted by the self-narrative a person constructs—the story they tell about who they are, where they came from, and where they are going. This narrative theory grounds personal identity in something richer than mere psychological continuity (memory): it requires that memories be integrated into a coherent self-narrative that connects past and present into an intelligible story. Schechtman's view connects personal identity to practical concerns—moral responsibility, self-knowledge, relationships—in ways that purely psychological or biological accounts miss.

## Sydney Shoemaker (1931–2022)

Shoemaker defended a version of the psychological continuity theory that emphasizes the role of causal connections among psychological states. Psychological continuity, for Shoemaker, is not just memory overlap but a web of causal relations among beliefs, desires, memories, and character traits—relations that must be "of the right kind" (appropriately caused) to ground identity. Shoemaker's debate with Swinburne in *Personal Identity* (1984) clarified the distinction between the psychological continuity view (no simple fact of the matter about identity beyond physical and psychological facts) and the "simple view" (personal identity is an irreducible fact).
