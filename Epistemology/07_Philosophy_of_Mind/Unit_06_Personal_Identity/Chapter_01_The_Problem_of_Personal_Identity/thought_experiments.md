## The Fission Case

**The scenario**: Parfit asks us to imagine a medical procedure in which your brain is bisected and each hemisphere is transplanted into a different body. Both resulting people wake up with memories of being you, with your personality, your beliefs, and your psychological character. Which one is you? If Person A is you (because they have the relevant psychological continuity), what about Person B, who has the same continuity? Person A cannot be identical with Person B (they are distinct people). So it seems neither can be identical with you—since identity is transitive and one-one.

**The philosophical point**: The fission case shows that psychological continuity—which seems like the right criterion for personal identity—does not determine a unique answer in branching cases. If both branches are equally psychologically continuous with the original, strict personal identity cannot hold (it would violate transitivity and one-one-ness). Parfit concludes that psychological continuity is what matters in survival—it is what we actually care about when we care about our future—but that strict personal identity is not what matters. The case supports his reductionism: there is no further fact about whether you are one of the branches or neither.

## Reid's Brave Officer

**The scenario**: Thomas Reid imagines three stages of a man's life: (1) as a young boy, he is flogged for stealing from an orchard; (2) as a young officer, he captures a standard in battle and can remember the flogging; (3) as a general, he can remember capturing the standard but has completely forgotten the flogging. On Locke's memory theory: the young officer is the same person as the boy (he remembers the flogging), and the general is the same person as the officer (he remembers capturing the standard). By transitivity, the general should be the same person as the boy. But the general cannot remember the flogging—which, on Locke's theory, means he is not the same person as the boy.

**The philosophical point**: Reid's case shows that the memory theory of personal identity, as Locke stated it, violates the transitivity of identity. Defenders of psychological continuity respond by revising the account: what matters is not direct memory but overlapping chains of memory—a person at t₃ is the same as a person at t₁ iff there is a chain of overlapping direct-memory connections from t₃ back to t₁. This revision handles the brave officer case but raises further questions about how much overlap is required and what counts as the right kind of psychological connection.

## Parfit's Teleporter

**The scenario**: You step into a teleporter that records your exact physical and psychological state, destroys your body, and reconstructs you from new matter on Mars. You wake up on Mars with all your memories, personality, and physical characteristics intact. Is the person on Mars you? Now modify the case: the original body on Earth is not destroyed—it remains alive, but the duplicate on Mars also exists. Now which one is you?

**The philosophical point**: The teleporter case tests our intuitions about what constitutes personal identity and survival. In the standard case (original destroyed), most people feel the Mars person is them—psychological continuity suffices. But in the no-destruction case (both survive), it becomes clear that psychological continuity can hold between distinct persons—you cannot be identical with both. This motivates Parfit's view that psychological continuity is not sufficient for personal identity and that identity is not what we really care about in survival. What we care about is the continuation of our psychology—which can be achieved by the Mars person even if it is not strictly identical with us.

## Olson's Brain Transplant

**The scenario**: Suppose your brain is extracted from your skull and transplanted into the skull of a braindead body. The resulting person has all your memories, personality, and psychological characteristics. Where are you—with the brain/body (you can think and remember), or with the original body (now braindead)? Animalists say: you went with the original body, which is now braindead; the brain-recipient is a different person. Psychological continuity theorists say: you went with the brain, since all your psychological properties went with it.

**The philosophical point**: The brain transplant case forces the clash between animalism and psychological continuity theory. Animalists accept the counterintuitive verdict that you would survive the loss of your brain (since your organism is still alive) but not survive its transplant into another body (since your organism would then be a different animal). Psychological continuity theorists accept the counterintuitive verdict that the braindead body is you—which seems deeply wrong. The case shows that both views have uncomfortable implications and that our intuitions about personal identity are inconsistent.

## The Ship of Theseus Applied to Persons

**The scenario**: Imagine that over the course of many years, every cell in your body is replaced—as indeed happens in reality. Every neuron is gradually replaced, every bone cell renewed, every skin cell shed. The person who exists at the end of thirty years shares no matter with the person at the beginning, though there was a continuous gradual replacement throughout. Are you the same person at thirty as at birth? And if you are, could we replace neurons faster—one per day—and still preserve identity?

**The philosophical point**: The Ship of Theseus applied to personal identity raises questions about the role of physical continuity in personal identity. If we accept that gradual replacement preserves identity (because there is spatio-temporal continuity and causal connection throughout the process), we seem to commit to psychological or organizational continuity rather than material continuity as the criterion. But if we imagine speeding up the replacement, the intuition becomes strained. The case is important for animalism (biological continuity may be what matters, not material identity) and for four-dimensionalism (the person may be a temporal stage-series, with the later stages related to earlier ones by appropriate causal connections).
