# Essay Questions: Unit 7 — Philosophy of Mind and Epistemology

---

**1. The Knowledge Argument and its Epistemological Implications**

Frank Jackson's Mary thought experiment: Mary knows all physical facts about color perception but, raised in a black-and-white room, has never seen red. When she leaves, does she learn something new? Critically assess the knowledge argument against physicalism. Does the argument establish that phenomenal consciousness cannot be identified with any physical state, or only that phenomenal concepts cannot be identified with any physical concept (the phenomenal concept strategy)? What does the answer imply for the relationship between phenomenal consciousness and propositional knowledge?

---

**2. Privileged Access, Anti-Individualism, and Self-Knowledge**

Burge's anti-individualism implies that the content of mental states is partly determined by the external environment. McKinsey's argument: if one has privileged access to one's own mental states (and hence knows a priori what one believes), and if anti-individualism is true (mental content is partly determined by the environment), then one can know a priori what the external world is like. This seems absurd. Critically assess the debate. Does Burge's response — distinguishing between the fact of having a belief and the nature of its content — succeed? Is privileged access compatible with anti-individualism, or must one of them go?

---

**3. Cognitive Penetrability and the Epistemic Independence of Perception**

Susanna Siegel argues that perceptual experiences can be epistemically rational or irrational: when prior biased beliefs cause an agent to have a distorted experience, that experience is itself compromised and cannot ground fully legitimate perceptual justification. Critically assess this argument. What is the evidence that perception is cognitively penetrable? If Siegel is right, what are the implications for foundationalist accounts of perceptual justification and for internalism about justification? Is the notion of an "epistemically compromised" experience coherent, and does it require a view about the intentional content of experience?

---

**4. Higher-Order Thought and the Epistemology of Consciousness**

Higher-order thought (HOT) theories (Rosenthal) hold that a mental state is conscious iff there is a higher-order thought representing it. This has epistemological implications: self-knowledge requires having a higher-order thought about one's mental states. Critically assess HOT theories. Does the requirement of a higher-order thought set the bar for consciousness too high (excluding some mental states that seem clearly conscious)? Does it provide an adequate account of the asymmetry between first-person and third-person access? Is the relationship between HOT theories and the epistemology of first-person authority philosophically illuminating?

---

**5. The Extended Mind Thesis and Social Self-Knowledge**

Clark and Chalmers's extended mind thesis holds that cognitive processes can extend beyond the skull into the environment: Otto's notebook is part of his cognitive process in the relevant sense, and he "knows" the museum hours in the same sense that Inga knows them. Critically evaluate the thesis. What are the conditions for a process to count as part of the mind? If cognitive processes extend into the social environment (Sandy Goldberg's extended epistemic agency), what are the implications for self-knowledge and for the social dimensions of epistemological agency?

---

**6. Introspective Reliability: Philosophical and Empirical Perspectives**

Eric Schwitzgebel argues, drawing on psychological evidence and phenomenological disagreements, that naive introspection is deeply and systematically unreliable. Critically assess Schwitzgebel's case. Does the evidence from Nisbett and Wilson (1977) on the unreliability of reports about mental processes establish that introspection is unreliable about phenomenal states, or only about the causal determinants of behavior? Is there a distinction between trained introspection (phenomenological method) and naive introspection that Schwitzgebel's argument does not adequately address?

---

**7. Epistemic Akrasia and Higher-Order Evidence in Philosophy of Mind**

Epistemic akrasia is the phenomenon of believing p while judging that one's evidence supports not-p. David Christensen argues that higher-order evidence — evidence about the reliability of one's own reasoning — can require revisions to first-order credences. Assess the phenomenon of epistemic akrasia from the perspective of philosophy of mind. What account of the architecture of belief formation is required to make akrasia possible? Is Williamson's anti-luminosity argument — no condition is such that whenever one is in it, one is in a position to know one is — relevant to whether epistemic akrasia is possible for a rational agent?
