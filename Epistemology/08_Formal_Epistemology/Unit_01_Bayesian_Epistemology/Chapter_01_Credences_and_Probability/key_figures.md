## Frank P. Ramsey (1903–1930)

Ramsey was a Cambridge philosopher and mathematician who, in his 1926 paper "Truth and Probability," first gave a rigorous account of degrees of belief as dispositions measurable through betting behavior. He showed that an agent's credences are coherent if and only if they satisfy the probability calculus, founding the subjective interpretation of probability. His work, though published posthumously, set the agenda for all subsequent Bayesian epistemology.

## Bruno de Finetti (1906–1985)

An Italian probabilist, de Finetti independently developed the subjective Bayesian framework and gave it its most thoroughgoing defense in his magnum opus *Theory of Probability*. He is particularly known for the exchangeability theorem, which shows that subjective probabilists can recover much of the practical content of frequentist probability from coherence constraints alone. De Finetti's insistence that "probability does not exist" objectively was deliberately provocative and captured the core subjectivist commitment.

## Leonard J. Savage (1917–1971)

Savage unified Bayesian probability with decision theory in *The Foundations of Statistics*, deriving both credences and utilities simultaneously from axioms on rational preference orderings. His "sure-thing principle" became a touchstone for debates in decision theory, and his framework remains the canonical formulation of subjective expected utility maximization. Savage's work grounded credences within a broader theory of rational agency rather than treating them as isolated psychological states.

## Rudolf Carnap (1891–1970)

Carnap sought to construct an objective, purely logical concept of degree of confirmation, developing inductive logic as an extension of deductive logic. His two-volume *Logical Foundations of Probability* and subsequent work on the continuum of inductive methods aimed to show that rational initial credences are fixed by logical form and structure. Though his specific systems faced technical difficulties, Carnap's project established the objective Bayesian tradition and influenced the subsequent debate between subjectivists and objectivists.

## David Lewis (1941–2001)

Lewis's "Principal Principle" articulated the normative constraint that rational credences must defer to known objective chances: if you know the objective chance of an event is p, your credence in it ought to be p in the absence of inadmissible evidence. This principle bridges the subjective and objective interpretations of probability and has generated an enormous literature on how to understand "admissibility." Lewis's broader modal realism also informed discussions of the possible-worlds semantics for probability.

## James M. Joyce (1960–)

Joyce developed an influential accuracy-based argument for probabilism, arguing that credences violating the probability axioms are epistemically defective because they are accuracy-dominated — there exists an alternative credence function that is closer to the truth at every possible world. This "scoring rule" approach bypasses the Dutch book argument's pragmatic assumptions and attempts to give credences a purely epistemic justification. His work has stimulated a growing research program in accuracy-first epistemology.
