## The Dutch Book

**The scenario**: A bookie offers you a series of bets on different propositions. If your credences violate the probability axioms — for instance, if your credence in P plus your credence in not-P sums to more than 1 — then the bookie can construct a set of individually fair-seeming bets (fair given your own credences) that guarantee you a net loss no matter what happens. You have been "Dutch booked": your betting dispositions expose you to certain loss.

**The philosophical point**: The Dutch book argument purports to show that having incoherent credences is rationally impermissible. If your degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, they are exploitable in a way that a rational agent would want to avoid. The argument has been controversial — critics note that epistemic rationality should not be reduced to avoiding monetary loss — but it remains one of the most influential arguments for thinking that the probability calculus constitutes the logic of degrees of belief.

## The Principal Principle Scenario

**The scenario**: You know that a fair coin is about to be flipped, and you know nothing else relevant about the outcome. A friend asks what credence you assign to heads. Now suppose you learn that the objective (physical) chance of heads is exactly 0.5. Most people feel compelled to set their credence at 0.5. But suppose you also have a reliable crystal ball telling you the coin will land heads — does that change things? Lewis's Principal Principle says it should: when "inadmissible" information is available, it can override deference to chances.

**The philosophical point**: The scenario illustrates the normative relationship between subjective credences and objective chances. It shows that credences are not merely psychological states but are answerable to facts about the world, and that the interface between the two requires careful philosophical articulation. The notion of admissibility — what counts as information that overrides deference to chances — remains deeply contested.

## The Incoherent Weather Forecaster

**The scenario**: A weather forecaster publicly reports a 70% chance of rain tomorrow and a 50% chance of no rain tomorrow. An alert listener notices that 70% + 50% = 120%, which exceeds 1. The forecaster's stated probabilities are incoherent.

**The philosophical point**: This simple scenario illustrates why the probability axioms are not merely conventions but express something like internal consistency for degrees of belief. Just as contradictory binary beliefs are defective because they cannot both be true, incoherent credences are defective because no possible state of the world could vindicate them simultaneously. The thought experiment helps motivate why coherence — not just truth — is a rational norm on partial belief.

## Betting at Long Odds on a Necessary Truth

**The scenario**: You are offered a bet at 99:1 odds against a mathematical theorem you haven't yet verified. You refuse the bet because you are nearly certain the theorem is true. But if asked your credence in "2 + 2 = 4," you might say 1. Should you have credence 1 in any contingent or unverified claim?

**The philosophical point**: The scenario raises the question of whether credence 1 (or 0) can ever be rational for anything short of a logical truth. Ramsey and subsequent Bayesians debate whether "certainty" should be achievable and whether credences of exactly 0 or 1 should be permitted (since conditionalization on a probability-0 event is undefined). This connects to broader issues about ideal versus bounded rationality and the idealizations embedded in Bayesian models of belief.

## The Independence Puzzle: Urns and Dependence

**The scenario**: An urn contains 60 red balls and 40 blue balls. You draw two balls with replacement. Most people correctly judge that the two draws are independent — knowing the color of the first tells you nothing new about the second. Now vary the case: the urn contains a mixture of balls from two factories, one producing predominantly red and one predominantly blue. Now knowing the first draw is red makes red on the second draw more likely, because the first draw is evidence about which factory supplied the urn.

**The philosophical point**: This scenario clarifies the epistemological content of probabilistic independence, showing that independence is not a metaphysical or physical feature but an epistemic one: whether events are independent depends on what background information an agent has. It illustrates why the concept of conditional credence — credence given certain information — is the fundamental notion in Bayesian epistemology, with unconditional credence being a limiting case.
