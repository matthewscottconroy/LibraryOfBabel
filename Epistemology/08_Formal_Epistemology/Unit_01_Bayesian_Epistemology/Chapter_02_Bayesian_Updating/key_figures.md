## Thomas Bayes (1701–1761)

A nonconformist minister and amateur mathematician, Bayes derived the theorem bearing his name in a paper read to the Royal Society and published posthumously in 1763. He used it to address an inverse probability problem: given observed data, what can we infer about the probability parameter that generated them? Though Bayes himself was cautious about broader philosophical applications, the theorem he proved became the engine of an entire epistemological tradition and remains the most widely applied result in the philosophy of scientific inference.

## Richard Jeffrey (1926–2002)

Jeffrey recognized a fundamental limitation of standard Bayesian conditionalization: it requires that new evidence be received as a proposition known with certainty. In *The Logic of Decision* he introduced "probability kinematics" (later known as Jeffrey conditionalization), which allows updating when experience shifts probabilities over a partition without making any element certain. This extension dramatically broadens the applicability of Bayesian epistemology to cases of uncertain perception and ambiguous evidence, and initiated a large literature on the conditions under which different updating rules are appropriate.

## Clark Glymour (1942–)

Glymour's *Theory and Evidence* advanced "bootstrapping" as an alternative to Bayesian confirmation theory and formulated the problem of old evidence: if Bayesian confirmation requires that evidence raise the probability of a hypothesis, then evidence known before the hypothesis was formulated cannot confirm it — yet scientists regularly take such evidence to confirm theories. The problem has driven extensive work on how to handle historical confirmation within a Bayesian framework. Glymour's sustained critique of Bayesianism sharpened its defenders' understanding of the framework's commitments.

## Amos Tversky (1937–1996) and Daniel Kahneman (1934–2024)

Working together from the early 1970s, Tversky and Kahneman documented systematic deviations of human probability judgment from Bayesian norms, including the representativeness heuristic, anchoring, and — most relevant to this chapter — base rate neglect. Their studies showed that people systematically underweight prior probabilities when given vivid case descriptions, an empirical finding that raises normative questions about the relationship between ideal Bayesian rationality and achievable human rationality. Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow* synthesized these decades of findings for a broad audience.

## Brian Skyrms (1938–)

Skyrms produced one of the most rigorous defenses of conditionalization as the uniquely rational updating rule, grounding it in diachronic Dutch book arguments that show an agent who plans to update otherwise is exploitable across time. His work on "dynamic coherence" extended the Dutch book approach from synchronic to diachronic rationality, and his book *The Dynamics of Rational Belief* placed conditionalization within a broader account of how rational belief systems evolve. Skyrms also contributed importantly to evolutionary game theory and the social dynamics of epistemic norms.

## Colin Howson (1945–) and Peter Urbach (1944–)

Howson and Urbach's *Scientific Reasoning: The Bayesian Approach* remains the definitive book-length defense of Bayesian confirmation theory as a philosophy of science. They argue systematically that conditionalization provides the correct normative model of how scientists learn from evidence, addressing the Duhem problem, the raven paradox, and the problem of old evidence in Bayesian terms. Their detailed engagement with scientific examples made Bayesian epistemology concrete and influential beyond pure philosophy.
