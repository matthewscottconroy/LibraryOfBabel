## The Medical Test (Base Rate Neglect)

**The scenario**: A disease affects 1 in 1,000 people in the general population. A diagnostic test for the disease has a 99% sensitivity (true positive rate) and a 99% specificity (true negative rate). A randomly selected person tests positive. What is the probability that they actually have the disease? Most people, including medical professionals, answer "about 99%." The correct Bayesian answer is approximately 9%: because the prior probability is so low (0.1%), even a highly accurate test produces mostly false positives in a random population.

**The philosophical point**: This scenario is a canonical illustration of base rate neglect, the systematic human tendency to underweight prior probabilities when updating on vivid new evidence. It shows that Bayesian conditionalization is not psychologically natural — our intuitive updating ignores priors and focuses on the likelihood ratio alone. The medical test example motivates both the normative importance of Bayesian updating and the empirical study of how human cognition systematically departs from it.

## The Diachronic Dutch Book

**The scenario**: On Monday you form the plan that if you learn E on Tuesday, you will update by something other than conditionalization — say, you will arbitrarily raise your credence in H by more than conditionalization dictates. A clever bookmaker who knows your updating plan can offer you a sequence of bets spanning Monday and Tuesday that are each individually fair by your own lights at the time, but that guarantee you a net loss whatever happens.

**The philosophical point**: This thought experiment extends the synchronic Dutch book argument to diachronic settings. It purports to show that rational agents must not only have coherent credences at any time but must plan to update by conditionalization. Critics have contested the argument: an agent who is certain she will not be Dutch-booked need not fear exploitation, and the pragmatic framing may again be the wrong basis for epistemic norms. But the diachronic Dutch book remains influential as a constraint on rational updating strategies.

## Jeffrey's Candlelight Reading

**The scenario**: You read a text in poor candlelight and come away with uncertain impressions — you think the word is probably "raven" but might be "haven." Your experience shifts your credence in "the word is raven" from 0.3 to 0.7, but it does not make you certain of it. Standard conditionalization requires that evidence be received with certainty — you condition on the proposition learned. But here no proposition is learned with certainty. How should you update?

**The philosophical point**: Jeffrey's scenario motivates the extension of conditionalization to "probability kinematics." Jeffrey conditionalization allows updating over a partition without fixing any element with certainty, modeling the way perception often provides graded rather than all-or-nothing evidence. The scenario raises deep questions about what distinguishes a good updating rule: should we preserve the new probabilities over the partition exactly (as Jeffrey requires) or allow more complex interactions between the new evidence and background beliefs?

## The Problem of Old Evidence

**The scenario**: Einstein formulated general relativity partly in awareness that Newtonian mechanics had a persistent unexplained anomaly: the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion. When GTR predicted and explained this precession exactly, scientists treated this as confirmation. But on a standard Bayesian account, evidence E confirms hypothesis H only if P(H|E) > P(H) — and if P(E) = 1 (because E was already known), then E cannot raise the probability of H, and so cannot confirm it.

**The philosophical point**: This scenario isolates a structural problem for Bayesian confirmation theory: genuinely new evidence updates our credences, but evidence we already possess cannot, by the mathematics of conditionalization, do any further work. Yet our scientific practice treats old evidence as capable of confirming new theories. Proposed solutions include counterfactual Bayesianism (what would the probability of E have been if we had not known it?), but each solution faces its own difficulties — making this one of the central challenges in the formal epistemology of science.

## Diachronic Incoherence Without Conditionalization

**The scenario**: An agent, Alice, has credence 0.6 in proposition P before receiving any evidence. She receives compelling testimony and decides to adopt credence 0.9 in P — but she does this not by conditionalization but by simply choosing credence 0.9 because she "feels" it is right. A second agent, Bob, updates from 0.6 to 0.9 by strict conditionalization on the testimony received. From the outside, their final credences are identical.

**The philosophical point**: Can we distinguish rational from irrational updating by looking only at the resulting credences? This scenario suggests not — we must look at the process. It raises the question of whether the norms of rationality are procedural (concerning how one updated) or purely state-based (concerning only the end state). Internalists about rationality tend to care about process; externalists and reliabilists may focus on the reliability of the method. The scenario thus connects Bayesian updating to the broader internalism/externalism debate.
