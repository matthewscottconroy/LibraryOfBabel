## Pierre-Simon Laplace (1749–1827)

Laplace was the first to give the principle of indifference systematic application in probability theory, using it to argue that in the absence of any reason to prefer one outcome over another, equal probabilities should be assigned to all. His work in *Théorie analytique des probabilités* made probability the primary tool of scientific inference and established the classical conception of probability that dominated until the twentieth century. Though his specific applications were later criticized for their dependence on arbitrary choices of description, his insight that prior probabilities must somehow reflect a background of epistemic neutrality remains the starting point for all work on non-informative priors.

## Harold Jeffreys (1891–1989)

Jeffreys was a geophysicist who became one of the most important figures in the development of objective Bayesian statistics. He showed that the principle of indifference is not invariant under changes of parametrization — assigning equal probability to the parameter θ does not assign equal probability to θ², which represents a different but equivalent way of parametrizing the same model. His solution, the "Jeffreys prior," is derived from the Fisher information matrix and is invariant under smooth reparametrization, providing a principled non-informative prior for statistical inference.

## Edwin T. Jaynes (1922–1998)

Jaynes was a physicist who became a leading advocate for the use of maximum entropy as the fundamental principle for selecting prior distributions in Bayesian inference. In his posthumously completed *Probability Theory: The Logic of Science* he argued that probability theory, properly understood, is an extension of deductive logic to situations of incomplete information, and that the maximum entropy principle uniquely determines rational priors given known constraints. His work has been enormously influential in physics, information theory, and machine learning, and provides the most systematic contemporary defense of objective Bayesianism.

## Rudolf Carnap (1891–1970)

Carnap devoted the second half of his career to developing inductive logic as a rigorous framework for determining rational prior credences from purely structural features of languages and evidence. His *Continuum of Inductive Methods* showed that infinitely many probability functions satisfy coherence constraints, all varying in a single parameter that governs the balance between a priori symmetry and observed frequencies. Though Carnap never achieved his goal of deriving a unique rational prior function, his work clarified the nature of the problem and established that objective constraints on priors, if they exist, must come from more than the probability calculus alone.

## Adam Elga (1974–)

Elga introduced the Sleeping Beauty problem into the contemporary philosophical literature and used it to challenge existing frameworks for rational prior credences involving self-locating beliefs. His argument for the "thirder" position — that Beauty should assign probability 1/3 to Heads on waking — relies on a subtle application of the principle of indifference across "centered worlds" (worlds described from a particular agent-time perspective). The Sleeping Beauty debate has become the central test case for competing theories of how priors over self-locating propositions should be assigned and updated.

## Jon Williamson (1969–)

Williamson has developed a systematic and technically sophisticated version of objective Bayesianism, arguing that rational credences must satisfy not only coherence but also a calibration norm derived from maximum entropy. His book *In Defence of Objective Bayesianism* argues that this framework avoids the key objections to earlier objective Bayesian approaches while providing stronger normative guidance than subjective Bayesianism, and he has applied it extensively to problems in philosophy of science, artificial intelligence, and medicine.
