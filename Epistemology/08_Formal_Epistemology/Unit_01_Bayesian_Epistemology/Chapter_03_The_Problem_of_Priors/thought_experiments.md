## The Sleeping Beauty Problem

**The scenario**: Sleeping Beauty is put to sleep on Sunday. A fair coin is flipped. If it lands Heads, she will be woken once on Monday and the experiment ends. If it lands Tails, she will be woken on Monday, put back to sleep with a memory-erasing drug, and woken again on Tuesday. In all cases, when she wakes she has no memory of any previous waking. On waking, what credence should she assign to the proposition that the coin landed Heads?

**The philosophical point**: "Halfers" argue she should say 1/2, because the coin is fair and she learns nothing new on waking. "Thirders" argue she should say 1/3, because there are three equally likely waking situations (Heads-Monday, Tails-Monday, Tails-Tuesday) and only one involves Heads. The dispute turns on how to extend Bayesian updating to self-locating beliefs — how to assign probabilities to "centered worlds" that specify not just objective facts but also who and when you are. No consensus has emerged, making Sleeping Beauty the most debated thought experiment in formal epistemology.

## Bertrand's Chord Paradox

**The scenario**: A chord of a circle is drawn at random. What is the probability that its length exceeds the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle? The principle of indifference says: assign equal probability to all equally described alternatives. But there are at least three natural ways to specify the random chord (by its midpoint, by its endpoint angles, by its distance from the center), and each gives a different answer: 1/4, 1/3, or 1/2.

**The philosophical point**: Bertrand's paradox shows that the principle of indifference is not well-defined without a prior choice of description or parametrization. Two agents who both claim to be "equally ignorant" can assign different probabilities, depending on which natural parameter they use. This is the reference class problem for priors in its purest form: without principled grounds for choosing one description over another, objective Bayesianism cannot uniquely determine prior credences, and the demand for a "neutral" prior seems to presuppose exactly what it claims to derive.

## The Doomsday Argument

**The scenario**: The philosopher John Leslie (adapting work by Brandon Carter) argues as follows: assume all humans who will ever live are equally likely to be you. You know approximately where in the sequence of humans you fall — somewhere around the 60–100 billionth human to have lived. This prior, together with demographic projections, gives you evidence about the total number of humans who will ever live. If the human species persists for a million more years, there will be vastly more future humans than past ones, making your position near the beginning extremely improbable. Therefore, you should update significantly toward humanity's early extinction.

**The philosophical point**: The Doomsday Argument applies indifference over a reference class (all humans) in a self-locating context. It is controversial because the conclusion seems to follow from almost no empirical input — just from where you happen to be in the human sequence. Critics argue the argument commits a reference class fallacy: why should "humans" be the reference class rather than "mammals," "conscious beings," or "people alive in 2000"? The argument vividly illustrates how different prior assignments over reference classes can generate radically different — and alarming — posterior conclusions.

## The Two-Envelope Problem

**The scenario**: Two envelopes each contain money, with one containing twice the amount of the other. You pick one at random and see it contains £100. You reason: the other envelope contains either £50 or £200. The expected value of switching is 0.5 × £50 + 0.5 × £200 = £125 > £100, so you should switch. But by symmetry, you would have reached the same conclusion had you seen any amount. So you should always switch — but this is absurd, since the envelopes are symmetric.

**The philosophical point**: The two-envelope problem reveals that the principle of indifference can generate paradoxical results when applied to continuous or unbounded distributions. The "reasoning to always switch" smuggles in an improper prior over the amounts in the envelopes — a distribution that cannot be normalized. This illustrates a general problem for objective Bayesianism: attempts to represent "total ignorance" by improper (non-normalizable) priors can generate incoherent recommendations. Resolving the paradox requires a prior theory of which distributions represent genuine epistemic states.

## Prior Probabilities and the Higgs Boson

**The scenario**: Before the Large Hadron Collider detected the Higgs boson in 2012, physicists using Bayesian statistics disagreed about what prior probability to assign to the Higgs boson's existence and to the specific parameter values the Standard Model predicted. Subjective Bayesians said individuals could legitimately use different priors reflecting their theoretical commitments; objective Bayesians insisted on a principled non-informative prior; frequentists refused to assign priors at all and reported p-values instead.

**The philosophical point**: This real-world case illustrates how the choice of prior is not merely a technical detail but reflects fundamental commitments about the nature of scientific reasoning. The different approaches yield different pronouncements about the strength of evidence, and none is obviously correct. The scenario motivates the question whether there is a fact of the matter about rational prior credences or whether, as subjective Bayesians claim, priors are legitimately personal as long as they are coherent.
