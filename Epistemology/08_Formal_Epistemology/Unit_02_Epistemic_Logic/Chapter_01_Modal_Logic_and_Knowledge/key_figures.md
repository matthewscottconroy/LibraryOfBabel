## Jaakko Hintikka (1929–2015)

Hintikka was a Finnish philosopher-logician who founded modern epistemic logic in his 1962 *Knowledge and Belief*. He proposed treating knowledge as a modal operator "Ka" (agent a knows that) governed by possible-worlds semantics: Ka(φ) is true at world w if φ is true at all worlds epistemically accessible to a from w. He derived the characteristic axiom of the S4 system (knowing implies knowing that one knows) and initiated the formal study of the logic of belief using the weaker B operator. Hintikka's framework gave philosophers and computer scientists a rigorous language for reasoning about knowledge attributions.

## Saul Kripke (1940–2022)

Kripke's relational semantics for modal logic, published when he was nineteen, provided the mathematical foundations that made epistemic logic precise. By interpreting modal operators as quantifiers over possible worlds connected by accessibility relations, Kripke enabled rigorous completeness proofs for different systems (K, T, S4, S5) and clarified which properties of the accessibility relation correspond to which axioms. His later philosophical work on names and necessity transformed analytic philosophy's engagement with modality, reinforcing the centrality of the possible-worlds framework to both logic and epistemology.

## David Lewis (1941–2001)

Lewis developed the notion of common knowledge in the context of his analysis of convention in *Convention* (1969), showing that for a regularity to constitute a convention it must be not merely known but common knowledge — each agent knows it, knows that others know it, and so on ad infinitum. Lewis proved that this infinite iteration has a fixed-point characterization distinct from any finite level of mutual knowledge, a result that transformed subsequent game theory and social philosophy. His analysis of common knowledge became the standard framework in economics, computer science, and philosophy of language.

## Robert Aumann (1930–)

Aumann's "Agreeing to Disagree" result (1976) showed that if two agents share a prior probability, update by conditionalization, and have common knowledge of each other's posteriors, then their posteriors must agree. This result uses epistemic logic — specifically the fixed-point characterization of common knowledge — to derive a substantive constraint on rational disagreement. Aumann's work bridged epistemic logic and game theory, spawning the field of interactive epistemology and demonstrating that formal epistemic concepts have rigorous implications for economics and the theory of rational agency.

## Johan van Benthem (1949–)

Van Benthem has been the leading figure in dynamic epistemic logic (DEL), extending standard epistemic logic to model how agents' epistemic states change in response to communicative actions and learning events. His framework of "logical dynamics" uses action models to represent the epistemic effects of public announcements, private communications, and strategic disclosure. He has also argued that epistemic logic should be understood as part of a general logic of rational agency, connecting it to game theory, computer science, and the philosophy of information. His sustained work has made DEL the dominant framework for formal epistemology in computer science and AI.

## Timothy Williamson (1955–)

Williamson's *Knowledge and Its Limits* contains an important critique of standard epistemic logic axioms, particularly the KK thesis (Ka(φ) → Ka(Ka(φ))). He argues on anti-luminosity grounds that knowing does not entail knowing that one knows — a claim that puts him at odds with the S4 axiom standardly assumed in epistemic logic. Williamson's critique has stimulated formal responses that weaken the system while preserving its core applications, and his work on margin-for-error reasoning connects modal epistemology with vagueness theory and the logic of approximate knowledge.
