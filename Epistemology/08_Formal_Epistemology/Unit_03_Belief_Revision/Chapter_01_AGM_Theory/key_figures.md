## Carlos Alchourrón (1931–1996)

Alchourrón was an Argentine legal philosopher whose interests in normative systems and the logic of legal change led him to collaborate with Gärdenfors and Makinson on the belief revision framework bearing their initials. His contribution brought a distinctive concern for how rational systems of norms are revised when new obligations conflict with old ones, giving the AGM project its initial impetus from jurisprudence and deontic logic. The contraction operation — the hardest of the three change types to characterize — was largely shaped by his legal intuitions about the minimal abandonment of commitments.

## Peter Gärdenfors (1949–)

Gärdenfors is the philosopher of the trio who gave AGM theory its broadest epistemological development. His book *Knowledge in Flux* situated belief revision within a theory of rational epistemic agents, arguing that the three operations of expansion, contraction, and revision exhaust the ways a theory can coherently change. He also developed the Ramsey test connection between belief revision and conditionals, which proved provocative: he later showed that accepting the Ramsey test, the Preservation condition, and the logic of conditionals leads to triviality, a result known as the Gärdenfors impossibility theorem.

## David Makinson (1941–)

Makinson contributed the technical rigor that turned intuitive rationality postulates into a provably complete and decidable formal system. His work established the equivalence of the postulate-based and construction-based (partial meet) characterizations of revision and contraction, and he has continued to develop connections between AGM theory and nonmonotonic reasoning. The Makinson-Gärdenfors work on safe contraction provided an alternative construction to partial meet that has independent philosophical interest, representing contraction in terms of the relative epistemic "safety" of beliefs.

## Adam Grove

Grove's 1988 paper introduced the sphere semantics for belief revision, demonstrating that the AGM postulates are exactly the constraints satisfied by revision functions definable in terms of a system of nested spheres around the current belief set (representing the agent's entrenchment ordering over worlds). This possible-worlds representation was philosophically important because it made the connection between belief revision and Lewis-style conditional logic explicit, and it provided an intuitive picture — concentric spheres of decreasing plausibility — that has guided subsequent work in dynamic epistemic logic and formal epistemology.

## Wolfgang Spohn (1950–)

Spohn developed ranking theory as a systematic alternative to both Bayesian probability and AGM belief revision, representing epistemic states as functions assigning natural numbers (ranks) to worlds, where higher ranks mean greater implausibility. Ranking theory handles iterated belief revision naturally — unlike AGM, it does not stop with a single revision but carries information about the "firmness" of beliefs through any sequence of changes. His magnum opus *The Laws of Belief* argues that ranking theory is not just a formal tool but a philosophically adequate theory of the functional role of belief in rational agency.

## Adnan Darwiche and Judea Pearl

Darwiche and Pearl's influential 1997 paper identified a crucial gap in the AGM framework: it is defined for single revisions, but provides insufficient constraints on how a belief state should evolve under sequences of revisions. They proposed four additional postulates (the DP postulates) for iterated revision, sparking an ongoing research program in iterated belief revision. Pearl's broader work on causality and probabilistic reasoning also contributed to the integration of formal epistemology with AI, making belief revision research directly relevant to machine learning and knowledge representation.
