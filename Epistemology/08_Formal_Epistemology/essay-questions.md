# Essay Questions: Unit 8 — Formal Epistemology

---

**1. The Dutch Book Argument and its Limits**

The Dutch book argument holds that an agent whose credences violate the probability axioms is vulnerable to a set of bets guaranteeing a net loss. Carefully reconstruct the argument. Is the Dutch book argument pragmatic (it shows only that incoherent credences are practically defective) or epistemic (it shows that incoherent credences are rationally defective)? Assess Joyce's accuracy-dominance argument as an alternative justification: do credences violating the probability axioms fail in purely epistemic terms because they are accuracy-dominated? Which justification for probabilism is more satisfying, and why?

---

**2. Is Bayesian Epistemology the Right Framework for Rational Belief Revision?**

Bayesian epistemology holds that rational agents have credences satisfying the probability axioms and update by conditionalization. Assess this framework against two major challenges: (a) the problem of old evidence — classical Bayesian conditioning cannot increase credence in a hypothesis on evidence already incorporated, yet new understanding of old evidence can confirm a theory; (b) the problem of priors — if any coherent prior is permissible (subjective Bayesianism), does Bayesian epistemology provide a genuine normative theory, or merely a consistency requirement? How does Jeffrey conditionalization address situations where the evidence is uncertain?

---

**3. The Preface Paradox and the Relationship between Credence and Belief**

An author who has written a well-researched book may rationally believe each claim in the book while also rationally asserting in the preface that at least one claim is false. This generates a contradiction if rational binary belief is closed under conjunction. Analyse the preface paradox carefully. What are the main solutions: abandoning classical belief logic, adopting a threshold account (believe p iff credence in p exceeds threshold t), or Leitgeb's stability account? Does the paradox show that binary belief should be replaced by credence, or only that binary belief is subject to different rationality norms than credence?

---

**4. The Lottery Paradox and Knowledge Norms**

Kyburg's lottery paradox: if a fair lottery has 1,000,000 tickets, one can have very high (> 0.999999) credence that any given ticket will lose, yet it is known that exactly one ticket will win. If belief requires only high credence, this generates a contradiction. Reconstruct the paradox precisely. Does the paradox show that knowledge cannot be grounded in mere probabilistic support? Assess Hawthorne's claim (*Knowledge and Lotteries*, 2004) that the paradox reveals a fundamental tension between the closure of knowledge and the ticket-based belief that "Ticket #n will lose." Does the knowledge norm of assertion resolve the paradox?

---

**5. The Sleeping Beauty Problem and Self-Locating Belief**

Sleeping Beauty is told she will be awakened once if a coin lands heads, twice (with amnesia) if it lands tails. When she wakes, she is asked her credence that heads landed. Halfers say 1/2; thirders say 1/3. Reconstruct both arguments precisely. What is the key dispute — whether Beauty receives new information upon waking, whether the principle of indifference applies to centred worlds, or something else? Assess whether the problem has a correct solution or whether it reveals genuine indeterminacy in the notion of rational credence under self-locating uncertainty.

---

**6. Accuracy-First Epistemology and Epistemic Utility**

Joyce's accuracy-dominance argument and subsequent work in epistemic utility theory (Pettigrew, Greaves) hold that epistemic norms (probabilism, conditionalization, calibration) can be derived from the requirement that agents maximize expected epistemic utility — where epistemic utility is accuracy, measured by a proper scoring rule. Critically assess this research program. Does the choice of scoring rule introduce an arbitrary element that undermines the derivation? Is the notion of epistemic utility coherently distinct from practical utility? Does accuracy-first epistemology provide a genuinely epistemic foundation for Bayesian norms?

---

**7. AGM Belief Revision and its Relationship to Bayesian Epistemology**

The AGM framework (Alchourrón, Gärdenfors, Makinson) specifies rationality postulates for belief revision: consistency, minimal change, and coherence. These govern how agents revise binary beliefs in the face of new information, including information inconsistent with current beliefs. Assess the relationship between AGM belief revision and Bayesian conditionalization. Under what conditions does Bayesian updating correspond to AGM-style belief revision? Does the AGM framework provide a complete theory of rational belief revision, or does it require supplementation by probability theory? Is the "minimal change" postulate epistemologically motivated?
