# Unit 9: Social and Political Epistemology — Unit Survey

## Central Questions

Classical epistemology focused almost exclusively on the individual knower. Social epistemology broadens the inquiry: how are epistemic practices, norms, and outcomes shaped by social structures, institutional arrangements, and relations of power? Can groups, institutions, and communities be knowers? How is knowledge transmitted, generated, and distributed through social interaction?

The central questions of this unit are: (1) Testimony — is testimonial knowledge a basic form that cannot be reduced to individual perception and memory, or is it always transmitted from individual epistemic sources? (2) Peer disagreement — when epistemic peers (agents with equal evidence and equal epistemic reliability) disagree, should they converge their credences, retain their original views, or do something more complex? (3) Epistemic injustice — are there distinctively epistemic wrongs, wrongs inflicted on people in their capacity as epistemic agents? (4) Group epistemology — can groups have beliefs, and if so, do they have the same epistemic properties as individual beliefs? (5) Feminist epistemology — do gender, race, and standpoint affect what one can know?

Social epistemology has two main branches: *analytic* social epistemology (Goldman's project of identifying social institutions that promote reliable belief formation) and *political* or *critical* social epistemology (the concern with power relations, marginalization, and the social conditions of epistemic agency). Miranda Fricker's work on epistemic injustice is the most important point of contact between these traditions.

## Map of Major Positions and Their Logical Relations

**Testimony: Reductionism vs. Anti-Reductionism.** (Covered also in Unit 3, from the perspective of sources of knowledge; here we focus on the social dimensions.)

*Reductionism* (Hume, Elizabeth Fricker): testimonial justification reduces to perception- and memory-based justification of the testifier's reliability. Each testimonial belief must be individually or globally reducible to non-testimonial evidence.

*Anti-reductionism* (Reid, Coady): testimony is a basic epistemic source with its own positive justificatory force. The natural default is to trust testimony; defeasors defeat that trust. Anti-reductionism must address the question of transmission: does testimony transmit knowledge from speaker to hearer, or does it generate new knowledge in the hearer?

*Transmission vs. generation*: Reductionists tend to hold that testimony merely generates justification in the hearer based on the hearer's evidence for the testifier's reliability. Anti-reductionists disagree about whether testimony transmits knowledge (Burge) or generates it independently (Lackey).

**Peer Disagreement.** When two epistemic peers discover they hold incompatible credences based on the same evidence, what should they do?

*Conciliationism* (Elga, Christensen): peers should split the difference — converge toward a midpoint credence. The evidential force of the peer's testimony that they believe otherwise is itself evidence to be incorporated.

*Steadfastness / "Right Reasons" view* (Kelly, Williamson): if one's own first-order evidence is strong enough, one can rationally maintain one's view even in the face of peer disagreement. The peer's disagreement provides some reason for doubt, but it need not lead to convergence.

*Genuine disagreement is rare*: (Sosa) when apparent peers disagree, this is usually evidence that they are not genuine epistemic peers — one of them is making an error that the other can diagnose.

**Epistemic Injustice.** Fricker's *Epistemic Injustice* (2007) identifies two forms:

*Testimonial injustice*: a speaker is deflated in credibility because of identity prejudice (e.g., racial stereotyping, sexism). The hearer gives the speaker less epistemic credit than they deserve. The wrong is distinctively epistemic: it wrongs the speaker in their capacity as a knower.

*Hermeneutical injustice*: a speaker is unable to make sense of a significant experience because the shared hermeneutical resources of the community do not include the relevant concept. Example: sexual harassment did not have a name in legal and social discourse until relatively recently; women who experienced it lacked the conceptual resources to identify their experience adequately.

*Miranda Fricker's extension*: epistemic injustice is not limited to testimony — it affects the distribution of epistemic resources and opportunities more broadly.

*Extensions by other theorists*: José Medina's *The Epistemology of Resistance* (2012) connects epistemic injustice to resistance and epistemic activism. Kristie Dotson's "Tracking Epistemic Violence" identifies *epistemic violence* — the broader practice of denying the epistemic status of marginalized groups.

**Group Epistemology.** Can groups have beliefs and knowledge? Two positions:

*Summativism*: group belief is simply the aggregate of individual beliefs. A group believes p iff most (or all, or some relevant majority of) its members believe p. Simple but faces counterexamples: a group can collectively affirm a belief no individual member holds (joint acceptance).

*Non-summativism* (Gilbert, Bratman, Pettit): group belief is a distinct phenomenon — an act of joint acceptance or group commitment that is not reducible to individual beliefs. Pettit's discursive dilemma shows that a group can reach a conclusion by majority voting on each premise while no individual accepts all the premises.

**Feminist Epistemology.** Standpoint epistemology (Hartsock, Harding) holds that one's social position affects what one can know. Marginalized standpoints may have epistemic advantages — insights unavailable from privileged positions. This is not mere perspectivism or relativism: the claim is not that all standpoints are equally valid but that some standpoints provide better access to certain truths.

## Key Philosophers and Arguments

**Alvin Goldman (1938–).** *Knowledge in a Social World* (1999) introduces veritistic social epistemology: the epistemic evaluation of social practices by their contribution to true belief formation. Goldman's framework is explicitly consequentialist about epistemic value and provides a basis for evaluating institutions (scientific peer review, journalistic practice, democratic deliberation).

**C.A.J. Coady (1939–).** *Testimony* (1992) is the foundational anti-reductionist text. Coady's argument that anti-reductionism is empirically necessary — human beings cannot form the body of individual evidence needed to ground all their testimonial beliefs — is the central positive case for treating testimony as a basic epistemic source.

**Miranda Fricker (1966–).** *Epistemic Injustice* (2007) is the most important contribution to political social epistemology. Fricker's identification of testimonial and hermeneutical injustice as distinctively epistemic wrongs — wrongs inflicted on persons in their capacity as epistemic agents — is both philosophically precise and politically significant.

**Philip Pettit (1945–).** The discursive dilemma (with Christian List, 2002): a group that makes decisions by majority vote on each premise can reach a collective judgment that contradicts each individual member's overall judgment. This shows that group agency is a distinct level of description that cannot be reduced to individual agency.

**David Christensen (1956–) and Adam Elga (1971–).** The conciliationist position on peer disagreement: when two epistemic peers disagree, they should give equal weight to each other's credences and converge toward the midpoint. The formal argument uses the idea that the peer's disagreement is itself evidence to be incorporated by Bayesian conditionalization.

**Thomas Kelly (1968–).** "The Epistemic Significance of Disagreement" (*Oxford Studies in Epistemology*, 2005) defends steadfastness: peer disagreement provides some evidence that one is in error, but it does not require giving equal weight to the peer's view. If one's first-order evidence is good, one may rationally retain one's view.

**José Medina (1967–).** *The Epistemology of Resistance* (2013) extends Fricker's framework, arguing that testimonial injustice generates epistemic resistance — the development of alternative epistemic practices and communities that challenge dominant credibility economies. Medina's polyphonic contextualism holds that epistemic norms are always contested and that resistance is epistemically valuable.

**Sandy Goldberg (1965–).** *Relying on Others* (2010) examines extended epistemic agency — the claim that our cognitive processes can extend into the social environment, so that what one knows depends partly on the epistemic states of one's social cognitive partners. This connects to Clark and Chalmers's extended mind thesis and to debates about distributed cognition.

## Essential Readings

1. **Goldman, Alvin, *Knowledge in a Social World*, Chapters 1–4** (1999, Oxford). The systematic foundation for analytic social epistemology. Chapters 1–4 develop the veritistic framework and apply it to interpersonal testimony.

2. **Fricker, Miranda, *Epistemic Injustice*, Chapters 1–4** (2007, Oxford). The most important book in political social epistemology. Chapters 1–2 on testimonial injustice and Chapters 3–4 on hermeneutical injustice are essential.

3. **Elga, Adam, "Reflection and Disagreement," *Noûs*, 41:3 (2007), pp. 478–502.** The canonical conciliationist paper. Precise, formal, and philosophically important.

4. **Kelly, Thomas, "The Epistemic Significance of Disagreement," in *Oxford Studies in Epistemology*, Vol. 1** (2005), pp. 167–196. The most careful steadfastness argument. Should be read alongside Elga.

5. **Pettit, Philip and List, Christian, "Aggregating Sets of Judgments: An Impossibility Result," *Economics and Philosophy*, 18:1 (2002), pp. 89–110.** The discursive dilemma and its implications for group rationality. Short and technically precise.

## Open Questions and Live Debates

**Is conciliationism self-undermining?** Christensen and Elga's conciliationism has been criticized as self-undermining: if conciliationists disagree with steadfasters about peer disagreement, should they conciliate? And if they should, they seem required to give up their conciliationism. Thomas Kelly and Jennifer Lackey have pressed this objection.

**Can epistemic injustice be fully separated from moral injustice?** Fricker holds that testimonial injustice is primarily epistemic — the wrong is done to the person as a knower, not merely as a moral patient. Critics argue that the epistemic and the moral cannot be cleanly separated. Medina and Dotson argue that epistemic injustice is thoroughly political.

**Can groups have knowledge in a non-derivative sense?** Pettit's non-summativism explains how groups can have beliefs not held by any individual, but the epistemological question is whether groups can know in a sense that is not merely metaphorical. If groups are genuine knowers, they are subject to epistemic evaluation — they can have justified or unjustified group beliefs, safe or unsafe group beliefs.

**Does feminist standpoint epistemology commit to a form of relativism?** Standpoint epistemologists uniformly deny that they are relativists, but the claim that standpoint affects what one can know raises the question of whether epistemic privilege from marginalized standpoints is universal, local, or domain-specific.
