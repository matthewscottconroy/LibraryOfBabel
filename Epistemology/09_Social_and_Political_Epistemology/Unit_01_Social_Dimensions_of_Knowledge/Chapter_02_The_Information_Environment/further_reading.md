## Primary Sources

- C. Thi Nguyen, "Echo Chambers and Epistemic Bubbles," *Episteme* 17(2), 2020. Draws a crucial distinction between epistemic bubbles (passive exclusion of contrary evidence) and echo chambers (active inoculation against outside sources), arguing that the latter are far more epistemically dangerous.
- Eli Pariser, *The Filter Bubble: What the Internet Is Hiding from You*, Penguin Press, 2011. Introduces the concept of the filter bubble — personalized algorithmic curation that limits users to information aligned with their existing preferences — and argues for its epistemically corrosive effects.
- Cass Sunstein, *Republic.com 2.0*, Princeton University Press, 2007. An expanded examination of how internet architecture enables selective exposure to information, contributing to polarization and the erosion of the shared epistemic commons required for democratic deliberation.
- Sander van der Linden, *Foolproof: Why Misinformation Infects Our Minds and How to Build Immunity*, W. W. Norton, 2023. Synthesizes psychological research on misinformation and presents the prebunking/inoculation approach as a more effective counter-strategy than after-the-fact debunking.
- Stephan Lewandowsky, Ullrich K.H. Ecker, and John Cook, "Beyond Misinformation: Understanding and Coping with the 'Post-Truth' Era," *Journal of Applied Research in Memory and Cognition* 6(4), 2017. Reviews the psychological mechanisms that make misinformation persistent and evaluates both debunking and prebunking strategies from a cognitive science perspective.
- Walter Quattrociocchi, Antonio Scala, and Cass Sunstein, "Echo Chambers on Facebook," SSRN Working Paper, 2016. An empirical study of information clustering on social media, showing that users in like-minded communities are systematically less exposed to challenging information.
- Regina Rini, "Fake News and Partisan Epistemology," *Kennedy Institute of Ethics Journal* 27(2), 2017. Analyzes the epistemological dimensions of the fake news phenomenon, arguing that partisan information environments distort the testimonial norms on which knowledge-sharing depends.

## Secondary Sources

- Alvin Goldman, "Epistemics: The Regulative Theory of Cognition," *Journal of Philosophy* 75(10), 1978. An early contribution to the regulation of information environments from an epistemic standpoint, applying reliabilist epistemology to social information systems.
- Kate Starbird, "Examining the Alternative Media Ecosystem through the Production of Alternative Narratives of Mass Shooting Events," *Proceedings of the 11th International AAAI Conference on Web and Social Media*, 2017. An empirical study of how alternative media ecosystems produce and amplify misinformation following high-profile events.
- Floridi, Luciano, *The Ethics of Artificial Intelligence*, MIT Press, 2023. Examines how AI-mediated information environments alter the epistemic landscape, raising questions about autonomy, manipulation, and the right to epistemic self-determination.
- Harry Frankfurt, *On Bullshit*, Princeton University Press, 2005. A short but influential analysis distinguishing lying (which respects the truth-telling norm while violating it) from bullshitting (which is indifferent to truth), providing a conceptual tool for analyzing disinformation and post-truth discourse.
- Naomi Oreskes and Erik Conway, *Merchants of Doubt*, Bloomsbury Press, 2010. A historical account of deliberate campaigns to manufacture scientific controversy and public confusion, illustrating how disinformation functions at the level of epistemic ecosystems rather than individual cognition.
- Adrienne Russell, *Networked: A Contemporary History of News in Transition*, Polity Press, 2011. Examines the structural transformation of news production and consumption in digital network environments and its consequences for the epistemic quality of public information.
- Renée DiResta, "The Supply of Disinformation Will Soon Be Infinite," *The Atlantic*, September 20, 2020. An accessible but analytically sharp account of AI-generated disinformation and its implications for epistemic ecosystems.
- John Cook, Naomi Oreskes, and colleagues, "Consensus on Consensus," *Environmental Research Letters* 11(4), 2016. Examines the epistemic significance of scientific consensus and the mechanisms by which consensus claims are contested in public information environments.
