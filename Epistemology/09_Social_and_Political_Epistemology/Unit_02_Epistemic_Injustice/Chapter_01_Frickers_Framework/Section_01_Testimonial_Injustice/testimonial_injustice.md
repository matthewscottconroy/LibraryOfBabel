# Testimonial Injustice

Miranda Fricker's *Epistemic Injustice: Power and the Ethics of Knowing* (2007) introduced the concept of *testimonial injustice* — a distinctive wrong done to someone in their capacity as a knower. Testimonial injustice occurs when a speaker receives less credibility than they deserve from a hearer, due to identity prejudice. This is not merely a mistake about the evidence; it is a harm that targets the speaker's epistemic standing.

## The Core Concept

When a hearer assesses the credibility of a speaker's testimony, they draw on various cues: the speaker's evidence, their track record, their apparent expertise. But they also — often unconsciously — draw on the speaker's social identity: their race, gender, class, age, disability status. If the hearer systematically underestimates the credibility of speakers from certain social groups due to prejudice (stereotypes, biases), those speakers suffer testimonial injustice.

Fricker's paradigm cases:
- A woman reports being assaulted; the police officer gives her lower credibility because she is a woman (and women are stereotyped as emotionally unreliable).
- A Black man's testimony in court is systematically undervalued because of racial stereotypes about Black men's honesty.
- An elderly person's report of pain is dismissed because of stereotypes about elderly people exaggerating symptoms.

In each case, the speaker is wronged not just epistemically (they fail to communicate their knowledge) but ethically (they are treated as epistemically lesser than they are, in virtue of their identity).

## Identity Prejudice and Credibility Deficit

Fricker distinguishes two directions of credibility distortion:
- **Credibility deficit**: The speaker receives less credibility than warranted. This is testimonial injustice when caused by identity prejudice.
- **Credibility excess**: The speaker receives more credibility than warranted — also a distortion, but typically benefiting rather than harming.

The harm of testimonial injustice operates at multiple levels:

**Epistemic harm**: The speaker fails to transmit knowledge. True, important information is not received. The epistemic commons suffers.

**Psychological harm**: The speaker may internalize the credibility deficit, doubting their own knowledge, becoming less willing to share information, developing what Fricker calls "epistemic self-doubt."

**Ethical harm**: The speaker is wronged in their fundamental capacity as a rational, knowing agent. This is a form of objectification — treating the speaker as something less than a full epistemic subject.

## Structural and Individual Testimonial Injustice

Testimonial injustice can be individual (a single hearer's prejudice) or structural (a pattern embedded in institutions, norms, and practices). Structural testimonial injustice is harder to address because it does not require any individual to be consciously prejudiced; the structural pattern of credibility attribution has a life of its own.

Examples of structural testimonial injustice:
- Medical institutions historically dismissing women's pain reports more readily than men's
- Legal systems in which minority testimony was formally discounted
- Academic fields in which work by women or non-Western scholars received less attention despite equal quality

## The Virtuous Epistemic Agent

Fricker argues that the remedy for testimonial injustice requires epistemic virtue on the part of hearers: specifically, *testimonial justice* — the virtuous disposition to assign credibility based on evidence about the speaker's reliability and knowledge, not on their social identity. The virtuous hearer critically monitors their own credibility assessments for identity prejudice.

This requires both reflective self-awareness (noticing when identity prejudice may be affecting credibility assessments) and a corrective calibration (adjusting credibility assignments when prejudice is detected).

## Connections to Social Epistemology

Testimonial injustice connects to broader social epistemological concerns. Silenced or undervalued testimony impoverishes the epistemic commons: if certain speakers are systematically silenced, entire categories of knowledge and perspective are excluded from collective inquiry. The social conditions for reliable knowledge production include fair credibility attribution.

This connects Fricker's work to feminist standpoint epistemology, critical race theory, and social philosophy: questions about whose voices count are simultaneously questions about epistemic and political justice.
