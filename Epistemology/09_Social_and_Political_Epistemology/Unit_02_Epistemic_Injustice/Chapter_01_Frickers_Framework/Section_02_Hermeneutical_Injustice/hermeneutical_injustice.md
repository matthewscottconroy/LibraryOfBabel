# Hermeneutical Injustice

Hermeneutical injustice is the second form of epistemic injustice identified by Miranda Fricker. While testimonial injustice occurs at the moment of testimony (the speaker is not believed as they deserve), hermeneutical injustice operates at an earlier and deeper level: it occurs when there are gaps in the collective interpretive resources — the shared concepts, vocabulary, and frameworks — through which people make sense of their experience. When some groups' experiences are not captured by the available interpretive resources, they are left unable to understand and communicate aspects of their own lives.

## The Concept

Every community of knowers operates with a shared hermeneutical resource — a stock of concepts, vocabulary, and frameworks for making sense of experience. This resource is a social product, developed over time through collective inquiry and communication. But the development of this resource is not politically neutral: those with less social power have historically had less access to the institutions through which collective hermeneutical resources are developed and refined.

When a social group's distinctive experiences are not captured in the existing hermeneutical resource — because the group's experiences have been excluded from the process of resource development — members of that group face *hermeneutical lacunae*: gaps that prevent them from adequately understanding and communicating their own experience.

## Fricker's Example: Sexual Harassment

Fricker's canonical example is sexual harassment before the concept existed (roughly, before the 1970s in the United States). Women who were subject to unwanted sexual attention, propositions, and harassment in the workplace had the experience, but lacked the concept "sexual harassment" to understand it. They might know something was wrong but lack the vocabulary to articulate it precisely — either to themselves or to others.

This conceptual gap was not accidental: the people who had the most interest in developing the concept (women who experienced it) had less access to the institutions through which concepts are socially ratified (law, medicine, journalism, philosophy). The concept's absence served the interests of those who benefited from the behavior going unnamed and unchallenged.

When the concept of "sexual harassment" was developed and disseminated, it enabled women to make sense of their experience, articulate the wrong done to them, seek redress, and connect their individual experiences to a social pattern.

## Structural Character

Hermeneutical injustice is paradigmatically structural rather than individual. No individual needs to be consciously culpable; the injustice lies in the social-structural distribution of interpretive power. Groups with less power in the production of shared concepts are more likely to find their experiences under-conceptualized.

This makes hermeneutical injustice different from testimonial injustice: testimonial injustice can be an individual wrong (this hearer unjustly discounts this speaker's testimony), while hermeneutical injustice is a systemic pattern that is not the fault of any individual.

## The Role of Power in Hermeneutical Resources

Fricker argues that the social production of hermeneutical resources is shaped by *hermeneutical power* — the power to shape the conceptual vocabulary through which experience is understood. Groups with more social power (in terms of access to education, media, professional institutions) have more hermeneutical power. Their experiences are more likely to be articulately represented in the shared resource.

Marginalized groups, by contrast, may find that their distinctive experiences — forms of discrimination, particular social vulnerabilities, specific kinds of suffering — lack adequate conceptualization. This is not because their experiences are less real or less significant, but because the process of concept development has been less attentive to them.

## Connections to Knowledge

Hermeneutical injustice impairs knowledge production in multiple ways:
- Individuals cannot adequately report their own experiences
- Medical, legal, and therapeutic institutions cannot recognize patterns in experiences that lack names
- Collective understanding of social reality is impoverished by the underrepresentation of certain experiences

This connects to standpoint epistemology: marginalized groups may have epistemic insights — forms of knowledge derived from their distinctive social position — that are not accessible to those in positions of privilege. Hermeneutical injustice can result in these insights being lost or undervalued.

## Extensions of Fricker's Framework

Subsequent work has extended Fricker's analysis. Jose Medina's *The Epistemology of Resistance* argues for a more active notion of resistance: marginalized groups can develop "outlaw discourses" and "coalitional epistemologies" that challenge dominant hermeneutical frameworks. Kristie Dotson distinguishes hermeneutical injustice from *contributory injustice* — the case where marginalized knowers do have the conceptual resources but are systematically excluded from contributing to the social development of shared understanding.
