## Primary Sources

- Miranda Fricker, *Epistemic Injustice: Power and the Ethics of Knowing*, Oxford University Press, 2007. The foundational text, introducing and defending the concepts of testimonial injustice, hermeneutical injustice, and the epistemic virtue of testimonial justice, with rich literary examples.
- Kristie Dotson, "Tracking Epistemic Violence, Tracking Practices of Silencing," *Hypatia* 26(2), 2011. Extends Fricker's framework by identifying additional forms of epistemic silencing, including "testimonial smothering" (where speakers preemptively restrict their testimony anticipating incredulity).
- José Medina, *The Epistemology of Resistance: Gender and Racial Oppression, Epistemic Injustice, and the Social Imagination*, Oxford University Press, 2013. Develops the concept of "epistemic insensitivity" and argues for an agonistic pluralism in which epistemic resistance by marginalized groups plays a positive role in correcting collective epistemic failures.
- Gaile Pohlhaus Jr., "Relational Knowing and Epistemic Injustice: Toward a Theory of Willful Hermeneutical Ignorance," *Hypatia* 27(4), 2012. Extends Fricker's analysis of hermeneutical injustice to include cases of "willful ignorance" — active maintenance of deficient hermeneutical resources by those who benefit from their persistence.
- Charles Mills, *The Racial Contract*, Cornell University Press, 1997. Argues that the social epistemology of white-dominated societies is structured by systematic epistemic distortions that serve the interests of racial domination, anticipating key themes of epistemic injustice theory.
- Angela Davis, *Women, Race, and Class*, Vintage Books, 1981. A foundational text documenting the intersecting epistemic and social silencing of Black women in American history, providing the historical grounding for later philosophical analyses of epistemic oppression.
- W.E.B. Du Bois, *The Souls of Black Folk*, Penguin Classics, 1903/1996. Develops the concept of double consciousness — the experience of seeing oneself through the eyes of a hostile other — which anticipates many themes in the later philosophy of epistemic injustice.

## Secondary Sources

- Jennifer Saul, *Lying, Misleading, and What Is Said*, Oxford University Press, 2012. Examines the norms governing assertion and the ways that technically truthful speech can be epistemically oppressive, extending Fricker's concerns into the philosophy of language.
- Ian James Kidd, José Medina, and Gaile Pohlhaus Jr., eds., *The Routledge Handbook of Epistemic Injustice*, Routledge, 2017. An essential collection covering the core debates, applications to medicine, law, education, and various extensions of Fricker's framework.
- Naomi Zack, *White Privilege and Black Rights*, Rowman and Littlefield, 2015. Examines the epistemic dimensions of racial privilege, arguing that structural inequalities in credibility assignment reproduce and reinforce social hierarchies.
- Rebecca Kukla, "Whose Voice? Epistemic Injustice, Scientism, and the Ethics of Testimony," *Synthese* 190(7), 2014. Examines how scientific authority structures can themselves produce testimonial injustice, including within the practice of science.
- Nora Berenstain, "Epistemic Exploitation," *Ergo* 3(22), 2016. Introduces the concept of epistemic exploitation — requiring marginalized people to educate dominant-group members about their own oppression — as an undertheorized form of epistemic injustice.
- Elizabeth Anderson, "Epistemic Justice as a Political Problem," in *Epistemic Injustice and the Philosophy of Recognition*, Ashgate, 2012. Connects Fricker's individual-level analysis to structural and political dimensions, arguing that epistemic injustice requires institutional remedies, not just individual virtue.
- Axel Gelfert, *A Critical Introduction to Testimony*, Bloomsbury, 2014. A pedagogically careful introduction to the epistemology of testimony that situates Fricker's work within the broader debate on testimonial norms and credibility assessment.
