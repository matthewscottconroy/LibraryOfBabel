# Can Groups Know? Group Epistemology

Group epistemology asks whether groups — committees, institutions, scientific communities, nations — can be epistemic agents in a robust sense: can they have beliefs, justify them, and know things? Or is knowledge always, ultimately, an individual achievement?

## The Question

Ordinary language supports attributing epistemic states to groups: "the committee decided that the proposal was sound," "NASA knew the O-ring was a risk," "the Catholic Church believed the Earth was the center of the universe." These attributions seem natural and practically indispensable.

But are they genuine attributions of group belief and knowledge, or are they shorthand for claims about the beliefs of individual members? Is there group-level cognition, or only individual cognition attributed to groups for convenience?

## Summativism

The simplest view: a group believes P if and only if the majority (or a sufficient proportion) of its members believe P. Group knowledge is just the aggregate or summary of individual members' knowledge.

Summativism has the advantage of being deflationary: group-level beliefs are just convenient shorthand for facts about individual beliefs. There is no ontological addition.

But summativism faces the *discursive dilemma* (Philip Pettit, List and Pettit). Consider a committee evaluating three conditions:
- Condition A: satisfied (majority believes this)
- Condition B: satisfied (majority believes this)
- Condition C: satisfied (majority believes this)
- Conclusion (A ∧ B ∧ C): satisfied (majority believes this? — NO)

It is possible that a majority believes each of A, B, and C individually, but no member believes all three, and therefore no majority believes A ∧ B ∧ C. The group's "belief" in A, B, and C is individually consistent but jointly inconsistent when aggregated. This shows that simply aggregating individual beliefs can produce incoherence.

## Non-Summativism

Non-summativist views hold that group belief is not reducible to individual members' beliefs. A group can believe something that no individual member believes, or fail to believe something that all members believe, depending on the group's decision procedures and institutional structures.

Margaret Gilbert's *joint commitment* account: a group believes P when its members are jointly committed to believe P as a body. This is a performative, practical account: the group "believes" P when its members have undertaken a commitment to act as if P is true and to give voice to P in the relevant contexts. This doesn't require that any individual believes P; it requires only the joint commitment.

## Collective Knowledge

Jennifer Lackey and others have argued for genuine collective knowledge — knowledge that is not simply the sum of individual members' knowledge. A research team can collectively know the results of an experiment even if no individual team member knows all the relevant details; each member's specialized knowledge combines to constitute the team's overall knowledge.

This *distributed knowledge* is not merely the set-theoretic union of individual knowledge states; it requires integration, coordination, and the institutional mechanisms that pool and organize individual contributions.

## Institutional Epistemic Agency

Some institutions — courts, scientific journals, legislative bodies — have deliberate procedures for arriving at collective verdicts, publishing authoritative results, and enacting collective decisions. These procedures give institutions a kind of epistemic agency: they produce authoritative outcomes that reflect the institution's epistemic states.

The court that convicts is not merely expressing the sum of jurors' beliefs; the verdict is the court's finding, arrived at through a procedure designed to produce reliable judgments. The peer-reviewed publication is not merely the editor's belief; it is the journal's endorsement.

## Responsibility and Blame

Group epistemology connects to questions of collective moral and epistemic responsibility. When an organization makes an epistemically bad decision — ignores warning signs, discounts evidence of harm — who is responsible? And what kind of responsibility is it?

If groups can have genuine epistemic states, they can be epistemically blamed: the organization was negligent, closed-minded, or dogmatic. If group epistemic states are just shorthand for individual beliefs, then only individuals can be blamed.

The practical stakes are significant: corporate liability, institutional accountability, and the design of organizations to be epistemically reliable all depend on how we understand group epistemic states.
