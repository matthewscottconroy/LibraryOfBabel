## Primary Sources

- Philip Pettit, "The Discursive Dilemma and Republican Theory," in *Deliberative Democracy and Its Discontents*, 2001; and with Christian List, "Aggregating Sets of Judgments: An Impossibility Result," *Economics and Philosophy* 18, 2002. Introduces the discursive dilemma — the impossibility of aggregating individual consistent judgment sets into a consistent group judgment — and establishes its implications for collective rationality.
- Margaret Gilbert, *On Social Facts*, Princeton University Press, 1989. Defends the "joint commitment" account of collective belief, arguing that a group believes P when its members are jointly committed to believing P as a body — a commitment that is not reducible to the beliefs of individual members.
- Jennifer Lackey, "What Is Justified Group Belief?," *Philosophical Review* 130(1), 2021. Develops an account of group justification that goes beyond summativism, arguing that the epistemic quality of a group's belief depends partly on the proper functioning of its epistemic processes.
- Christian List and Philip Pettit, *Group Agency: The Possibility, Design, and Status of Corporate Agents*, Oxford University Press, 2011. A systematic defense of the view that groups can be genuine rational agents with their own attitudes, including beliefs, that are not reducible to aggregates of individual attitudes.
- Raimo Tuomela, *The Philosophy of Sociality: The Shared Point of View*, Oxford University Press, 2007. Develops a comprehensive theory of collective intentionality, arguing that we-attitudes and joint beliefs are irreducible to I-attitudes and individual beliefs.
- Frederick Schmitt, ed., *Socializing Epistemology: The Social Dimensions of Knowledge*, Rowman and Littlefield, 1994. An anthology that helped establish social epistemology as a philosophical field, containing influential early papers on group knowledge and epistemic responsibility.
- Alvin Goldman, "Group Knowledge versus Group Rationality: Two Approaches to Social Epistemology," *Episteme* 1(1), 2004. Compares the "group knowledge" approach (which asks when groups have knowledge) with the "group rationality" approach (which evaluates group epistemic practices), arguing for complementary rather than competing goals.

## Secondary Sources

- Christian List, "The Discursive Dilemma and Public Reason," *Ethics* 116(2), 2006. Examines the implications of the discursive dilemma for democratic deliberation and public reason, connecting formal results about collective rationality to political philosophy.
- Deborah Tollefsen, *Groups as Agents*, Polity Press, 2015. A clear and accessible philosophical defense of group agency and collective belief, engaging with both analytic philosophy and social theory.
- Michael Bratman, *Shared Agency: A Planning Theory of Acting Together*, Oxford University Press, 2014. Develops a theory of shared intention and shared agency grounded in the interplay of individual planning structures, offering an alternative to Gilbert's joint commitment approach.
- Kay Mathiesen, "We're All in This Together: Responsibility and Collective Action," *Midwest Studies in Philosophy* 30(1), 2006. Examines conditions for collective responsibility in epistemic contexts, asking when groups can be blamed for collective epistemic failures.
- Miriam Solomon, *Social Empiricism*, MIT Press, 2001. Argues for a naturalistic social epistemology in which the proper unit of epistemic evaluation is the research community rather than the individual scientist, applying this to the history of cognitive science.
- Jennifer Lackey, "Group Testimony," *Philosophical Issues* 22(1), 2012. Examines whether and how groups can give testimony and be held to testimonial norms — an extension of the individual testimony literature to collective epistemic agents.
- Anthea Roberts, *Is International Law International?*, Oxford University Press, 2017. A concrete case study of how international legal communities produce and maintain group knowledge through institutional practices, providing empirical grounding for philosophical accounts of institutional epistemic agency.
