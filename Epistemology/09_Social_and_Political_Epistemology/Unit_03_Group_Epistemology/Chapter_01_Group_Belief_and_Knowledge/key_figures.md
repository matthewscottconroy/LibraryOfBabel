## Philip Pettit (1945–)

Pettit, with Christian List, formulated the discursive dilemma — a formal impossibility result showing that if a group attempts to form consistent collective judgments by aggregating the judgment-sets of its individual members, it can end up with collectively inconsistent beliefs even when each member is individually consistent. Pettit argued that avoiding this result requires groups to operate as genuine reasoning agents that track reasons and revise judgments as a corporate body, not merely as aggregators of individual views. This work transformed the philosophy of collective rationality and has been applied to democratic theory, organizational theory, and the law.

## Margaret Gilbert (1942–)

Gilbert developed the "joint commitment" theory of collective intentionality in *On Social Facts* and subsequent work. She argued that what makes a belief genuinely collective — rather than merely shared — is a joint commitment of members to believe something as a body, a commitment that is not reducible to the sum of individual commitments and that generates special obligations: each member is obligated to the others to act in accordance with the joint commitment. Gilbert's account makes collective beliefs genuinely sui generis — irreducible to individual mental states — and has been the target of "summativist" critics who resist positing irreducible collective attitudes.

## Jennifer Lackey (1972–)

Lackey's work on group testimony and group justification has been among the most analytically rigorous contributions to group epistemology. She has argued that groups can give testimony, that group testimony is subject to epistemic norms, and that when groups give false testimony, they can bear collective epistemic responsibility. Her 2021 paper "What Is Justified Group Belief?" develops a non-summativist account of group justification that focuses on whether a group's relevant internal processes are properly functioning — a group-level analog to reliabilist individual epistemology.

## Christian List (1972–)

List's technical contributions to the formal theory of collective judgment have been foundational. With Pettit, he proved impossibility results for judgment aggregation that generalize the well-known Arrow impossibility theorem from preference aggregation to the richer domain of logical judgment sets. His book with Pettit, *Group Agency*, defends a sophisticated non-reductionist view of group agents as possessing their own beliefs, desires, and rationality constraints, and examines the normative implications for how group agents should be designed and held accountable.

## Raimo Tuomela (1940–2020)

Tuomela developed a comprehensive philosophical theory of social ontology and collective intentionality over four decades. He distinguished "we-mode" group attitudes from "I-mode" individual attitudes, arguing that genuine group beliefs are formed from the we-mode — from acting and thinking as a member of a group rather than merely as an individual who happens to coordinate with others. His work on the irreducibility of we-attitudes has been influential in philosophy of mind, social ontology, and group epistemology, and his framework provides the most systematic formal account of the difference between individual and collective mental states.

## Michael Bratman (1945–)

Bratman's planning theory of shared agency provides the most widely discussed alternative to Gilbert's joint commitment account. He argues that shared intention and shared activity can be understood in terms of interlocking individual plans: we are engaged in a shared activity when each participant has intentions that refer to the other's intentions in the right way, without any irreducibly collective mental state being required. While Bratman's account is nominally about action rather than belief, it has been extended to collective epistemic states and provides a more deflationary, individualistically acceptable theory of group agency.
