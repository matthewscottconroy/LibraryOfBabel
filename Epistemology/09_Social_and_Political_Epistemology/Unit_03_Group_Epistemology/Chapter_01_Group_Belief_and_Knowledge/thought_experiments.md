## The Discursive Dilemma

**The scenario**: A three-member committee must decide whether to act on a policy that requires all three premises to be true: (P1) There is a legal basis for action; (P2) The action will be effective; (P3) The action is affordable. Member A believes P1 and P2 but not P3; Member B believes P1 and P3 but not P2; Member C believes P2 and P3 but not P1. Each member is individually consistent and rationally holds that the conclusion (act) does not follow. Yet a majority vote on each premise yields: P1 (A and B vote yes, majority), P2 (A and C vote yes, majority), P3 (B and C vote yes, majority) — and therefore the conclusion (act) also wins a majority. The group "concludes" what no member believes.

**The philosophical point**: The discursive dilemma shows that premise-based majority voting can generate collectively inconsistent conclusions even when all individuals are consistent. It demonstrates that there is no obvious neutral aggregation procedure for collective judgment-sets, and that groups must make a structural choice: either aggregate on conclusions (ignoring the logical connections) or aggregate on premises (following logic, but potentially reaching conclusions no member endorses). The dilemma motivated List and Pettit's formal impossibility results and raises deep questions about whether groups can be rational agents in the same sense that individuals are.

## The Walking Club's Belief

**The scenario**: The members of a walking club jointly commit to planning their trips on the assumption that the weather will be good on Sundays, even though individually most of them privately doubt the weather will be good this particular Sunday. The club, acting as a body, plans a full expedition. When it rains and they must cancel, a member says: "The club believed the weather would be good, and was wrong." Is this attribution of group belief intelligible?

**The philosophical point**: Gilbert uses examples like this to argue that collective belief is not merely the aggregate of individual beliefs — the club's belief is constituted by a joint commitment that can diverge from what any member individually believes. The scenario tests whether we find it natural to attribute beliefs to groups as genuine agents, and whether group beliefs generate genuine normative constraints on members (are they obligated to act as if the weather will be good, given the joint commitment?). It motivates the distinction between summativist and non-summativist accounts of group belief.

## The Tobacco Research Committee

**The scenario**: A research committee is tasked with summarizing the evidence on whether tobacco causes lung cancer. Each member reads different subsets of the literature. No individual member has access to all the evidence. The committee's collective summary reflects evidence that no individual has fully surveyed. The committee's final report says "The evidence strongly supports a causal link." Is the committee's belief in this claim epistemically justified? Is it knowledge?

**The philosophical point**: The scenario tests whether epistemic norms — justification, knowledge — apply to groups as groups. Summativists say the committee's belief is justified only if a sufficient proportion of members are individually justified. Non-summativists say the committee can be collectively justified even if no individual has sufficient evidence alone, because the epistemic work is genuinely distributed. Lackey's account of group justification focuses on whether the committee's processes for assembling and evaluating evidence are epistemically proper — a group-level reliability criterion.

## The Whistleblower's Dilemma

**The scenario**: An organization collectively holds — in its official documents, policies, and public statements — that its product is safe. Individually, several of its engineers privately believe the product has serious safety flaws. One engineer considers whether to report this discrepancy. Does the organization genuinely believe the product is safe? Do the individual engineers bear epistemic responsibility for the group's false belief?

**The philosophical point**: The scenario raises questions about the distribution of epistemic responsibility within groups and the relationship between individual and collective epistemic agency. It tests whether group beliefs can be epistemically defective in ways that generate obligations for individual members to correct them — and under what conditions an individual member bears moral and epistemic responsibility for group-level false beliefs. The case connects group epistemology to questions about institutional integrity, organizational ethics, and the distribution of epistemic blame.
