# Peer Disagreement

The epistemology of disagreement asks: how should you respond when someone you regard as your epistemic equal disagrees with you? This question arises pervasively in intellectual life — between colleagues, between scientists, between political citizens — and its answer has implications for both individual rational belief revision and social epistemic practice.

## The Setup

You and a friend both carefully examine the evidence and reason from it. You conclude P; your friend concludes not-P. Neither of you has made an obvious error. You regard each other as equally careful, intelligent, and well-informed — you are *epistemic peers*.

The question: what should you believe after learning of this disagreement?

If you are right that you are peers — that neither of you is more reliable — then the disagreement provides you with evidence that you might be wrong. Before the disagreement, you had your reasoning as support for P. After, you have your reasoning but also your peer's contrary reasoning. It seems like you should be less confident in P than before.

## Conciliationism

The *conciliationist* view holds that you should revise your belief toward your peer's position — "splitting the difference" to some extent. The key argument:

Before learning of the disagreement, you have your evidence and your peer's agreement (implied). After, your peer's contrary conclusion is itself evidence that your reasoning is flawed. Since you accept your peer's reliability, their disagreement is evidence that your conclusion is wrong. The rational response is to reduce your confidence in P.

David Christensen and Richard Feldman are leading conciliationists. Christensen argues for the *equal weight view*: you should give equal weight to your own conclusion and your peer's conclusion, arriving at a midpoint credence. This is the epistemic analog of splitting the difference.

## Steadfastness

The *steadfast* view holds that you may (or should) maintain your original belief even in the face of peer disagreement. Your carefully formed belief gives you evidence for P; your peer's disagreement doesn't automatically undermine that evidence, even if your peer is equally reliable in general.

Kelly and Elga have defended versions of steadfastness. Kelly's "total evidence" view: the rational response to disagreement depends on the total evidence — including your original reasons for believing P. If your evidence strongly supports P, you can maintain your belief even when your peer disagrees. The peer's disagreement is evidence that one of you is wrong, but you know it's more likely to be them, given your strong evidence.

## The Independence Principle

Christensen's *independence principle* holds that in evaluating the significance of peer disagreement, you should not use the very reasoning that led to your disputed conclusion. If you use your conclusion P to support your reliability relative to your peer ("I'm right about P, so I'm the reliable one"), you are arguing in a circle.

The independence principle pushes toward conciliation: you should evaluate your peer's disagreement from a neutral standpoint, not one that presupposes your own conclusion. This neutral standpoint makes the peer's disagreement more significant.

Critics argue that the independence principle is too demanding: all of our assessments of epistemic position use some of our existing beliefs. There is no perfectly neutral standpoint from which to evaluate disagreement.

## Disagreement, Expertise, and Non-Peers

Most of the peer disagreement literature focuses on genuine epistemic peers — equally reliable reasoners. But in practice, most disagreement is not between peers:

- I disagree with a climate scientist about climate change (they're not my epistemic peer; they have relevant expertise I lack).
- I disagree with a conspiracy theorist about vaccine safety (they're not my epistemic peer in the relevant domain).
- A Nobel laureate in physics disagrees with me about interpretations of quantum mechanics (I should defer significantly).

The epistemology of disagreement with non-peers — the conditions for appropriate deference or appropriate skepticism — is more complex and practically more important than the peer case.

## Disagreement and Democratic Deliberation

Peer disagreement has significant implications for democratic epistemology. Citizens disagree about policy-relevant empirical questions (does this policy reduce crime? does this intervention improve health?). If disagreement among political citizens who are epistemic peers should lead to mutual conciliation, this supports a "civic epistemology" that values mutual recognition and deliberation.

But if citizens who disagree are not epistemic peers — if some citizens are better informed about relevant facts — the implications are different: better-informed citizens might justifiably maintain their views, and worse-informed citizens should defer. This connects to debates about expertise, democracy, and the role of evidence in political discourse.
