## David Christensen (1952–)

Christensen is the leading defender of conciliationism in the peer disagreement debate. His argument begins from the independence principle: when two people who have thought carefully about the same question using similar methods reach different conclusions, each should treat the other's view as significant evidence that their shared reasoning process has gone wrong. He argues that it would be epistemically arrogant to privilege your own conclusion simply because it is yours; the evidential force of a peer's disagreement is symmetric. Christensen has also worked extensively on the higher-order evidence problem — what to do when evidence bears not directly on the world but on your own reliability as a reasoner.

## Richard Feldman (1948–)

Feldman developed the "Equal Weight View" as the strongest conciliationist position: when you discover that an epistemic peer disagrees with you, you should give equal weight to their conclusion and yours, often ending in suspension of judgment. He applied this to religious disagreement, arguing that reasonable, intelligent people who disagree about religion provide each other with strong epistemic reasons to suspend judgment, and that treating the mere familiarity of your own view as a reason to maintain it violates the Equal Weight norm. His work has been particularly influential in the philosophy of religion and in debates about rational religious belief.

## Thomas Kelly (1973–)

Kelly is one of the most prominent defenders of a "steadfast" or "total evidence" position in the peer disagreement debate. He argues that conciliationists underestimate the evidential weight of the original reasons for your view: discovering a peer's disagreement is evidence, but so are all your prior reasons, and in many cases your prior reasons outweigh the evidence provided by the disagreement alone. Kelly also developed the "uniqueness" thesis debate, noting that whether the same evidence requires a unique doxastic response has direct implications for how seriously to take peer disagreement.

## Adam Elga (1974–)

Elga's defense of the Equal Weight View used a thought experiment about an "Epistemic Peer Oracle" — a device that tells you what an epistemic peer would believe about your question — to argue that rationality requires fully conciliating when you receive peer disagreement. His "Reflection and Disagreement" paper argued that the principles governing trust in your own future self (van Fraassen's reflection principle) should extend to epistemic peers, since peers' judgments are as reliable a guide to the truth as your own. Elga has also contributed to debates about self-locating belief, Sleeping Beauty, and formal epistemology.

## Ernest Sosa (1940–)

Sosa defended a more steadfast position by grounding the response to peer disagreement in epistemic virtue. He argued that a virtuoso performer — someone who exercises genuine epistemic competence — is justified in maintaining their view even in the face of peer disagreement, because their virtuous judgment is itself a form of evidence that their conclusion is correct. For Sosa, virtuosity involves not just following evidence but exercising a reliable and well-formed competence, and the mere existence of disagreement among well-functioning competences does not automatically mandate conciliation. His approach connects the peer disagreement debate to the broader project of virtue epistemology.

## Jonathan Matheson (1980–)

Matheson has worked to systematize the peer disagreement literature and extend it to practical applications. His book *The Epistemic Significance of Disagreement* traces the implications of different positions across a range of cases — scientific, political, religious, philosophical — and develops a "justificationist" account that focuses on what your reasons justify rather than on what your peer believes. He has also examined asymmetric disagreement (with non-peers) and expert disagreement, extending the core theoretical debates to politically significant cases like disagreement about climate science and vaccination.
