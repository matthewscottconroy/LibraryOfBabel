## The Restaurant Bill

**The scenario**: Christensen's classic case: you and a friend split a restaurant bill. You both calculate what each person owes. You get $43; your friend gets $45. You know you are both equally good at arithmetic, equally careful, and equally sober. Should you maintain your own calculation, average the two, or suspend judgment?

**The philosophical point**: This simple scenario isolates the peer disagreement question in a domain where there is a clear fact of the matter and roughly equal competence. Most people find it intuitive that you should not simply assume you got it right; you should at least reconsider. Christensen uses this to motivate conciliationism: discovering a peer's disagreement is evidence that your shared process went wrong somewhere, and it would be unreasonably self-trusting to assume the error was theirs. The scenario is deliberately mundane to set aside the special pleading that often attaches to philosophical, religious, or political disagreements.

## The Brilliant Friend Who Disagrees with You about God

**The scenario**: You and your brilliant, thoughtful, philosophically sophisticated friend of many years have spent months discussing the existence of God. You have considered the same arguments, read the same texts, and are comparably intelligent and careful. Your friend is a theist; you are an atheist. Neither of you has a special source of evidence. Learning this about each other, should you both suspend judgment? Does the mere fact that a brilliant peer disagrees give you reason to doubt?

**The philosophical point**: Feldman and Christensen argue yes: the brilliant peer's disagreement is significant evidence that the issue is genuinely harder than your confidence suggests. Critics argue that if this reasoning is accepted universally, philosophy and religion would demand universal agnosticism — since virtually every substantive philosophical view has brilliant peers on both sides. The scenario tests whether the Equal Weight View leads to an unacceptable "spinelessness" result, in which no view can be confidently maintained because disagreement is ubiquitous.

## The Steadfast Expert

**The scenario**: A climate scientist has spent twenty years studying ice cores, atmospheric chemistry, and climate modeling. She is deeply confident that anthropogenic climate change poses serious near-term risks. She discovers that a colleague at a comparable institution, with comparable credentials, disagrees on the magnitude of the risk. Should she significantly revise her assessment? Or is her twenty years of intimate acquaintance with the evidence a sufficient reason to maintain her view, even though her colleague — a genuine peer by external criteria — disagrees?

**The philosophical point**: Kelly's "total evidence" view suggests she may be entitled to maintain her view: her prior reasons — twenty years of first-hand engagement with the data — constitute powerful evidence that may outweigh the evidential force of her colleague's disagreement. The conciliationist might respond that her confidence that her twenty years have been well-spent is itself subject to peer evaluation. The scenario raises the question of whether the intuition supporting steadfastness in this case is principled or merely self-serving, and whether there are features of expert knowledge that justify asymmetric weight-assignment.

## The Independence Principle Under Strain

**The scenario**: You and a peer are evaluating the same evidence for a conclusion. Unknown to you, your peer has used the same reasoning process as you, following the same steps, and has reached the same conclusion by the same chain of reasoning. From the outside, you look like two independent thinkers who have converged. But the convergence is not really independent — it merely reflects that you were both taught by the same teacher, using the same textbook. Does the agreement between you give you additional confirmation?

**The philosophical point**: The independence principle — that the evidential force of peer agreement or disagreement depends on the independence of the judgments — is tested here. If peer agreement is evidentially valuable because it represents an independent check, then merely apparent independence (caused by common intellectual ancestry) does not provide the boost it seems to. The scenario motivates attention to the conditions under which peer judgments are genuinely independent evidence, and raises concerns about the epistemic consequences of intellectual monocultures in academia and other knowledge-producing institutions.

## Disagreeing with Your Past Self

**The scenario**: You held a confident philosophical position for ten years. You now, after more study, hold the opposite view with equal confidence. How should you regard your past self? Was your past self wrong, or were both past-you and present-you equally rational given your respective evidence sets? And what should you conclude about your current confidence: if you know you have been epistemically reliable and you changed your mind dramatically, shouldn't that undermine your current confidence?

**The philosophical point**: Intra-personal disagreement across time tests whether the norms governing peer disagreement extend to one's own past views. If conciliationism is correct, discovering that your earlier, carefully considered self held the opposite view should count as evidence against your current view — producing a ratchet effect of diminishing confidence. Defenders of steadfastness point out that you now have more evidence than your earlier self did, breaking the symmetry of "peer" status. The scenario illuminates the role of epistemic asymmetry — in evidence, in competence, in temporal position — in determining how much weight to give disagreeing views.
