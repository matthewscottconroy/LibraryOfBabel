## Primary Sources

- Sandra Harding, *The Science Question in Feminism*, Cornell University Press, 1986. The foundational text for feminist standpoint epistemology as a developed philosophical position, arguing that women's standpoint provides a less distorted basis for inquiry into social relations than men's, and introducing the concept of "strong objectivity."
- Dorothy E. Smith, *The Everyday World as Problematic: A Feminist Sociology*, Northeastern University Press, 1987. Develops standpoint epistemology from a sociological direction, arguing that starting inquiry from women's everyday experience reveals structures of ruling relations invisible from the standpoint of the dominant.
- Patricia Hill Collins, *Black Feminist Thought: Knowledge, Consciousness, and the Politics of Empowerment*, Routledge, 1990. Articulates a Black feminist standpoint epistemology, arguing that the intersection of race, gender, and class creates distinctive epistemic resources and that Black women's intellectual tradition constitutes an alternative knowledge project.
- Donna Haraway, "Situated Knowledges: The Science Question in Feminism and the Privilege of Partial Perspective," *Feminist Studies* 14(3), 1988. A highly influential paper arguing that all knowledge is situated — produced from a particular embodied location — and that "the view from nowhere" is an illusion; objectivity should be reconceived as accountability for our situated perspectives.
- Alison Wylie, "Why Standpoint Matters," in *Science and Other Cultures*, Routledge, 2003. A measured philosophical defense of standpoint epistemology that distinguishes its defensible core from overstatements, arguing that structural social position can confer genuine epistemic advantages.
- Nancy Hartsock, "The Feminist Standpoint: Developing the Ground for a Specifically Feminist Historical Materialism," in *Discovering Reality*, Reidel, 1983. Develops standpoint epistemology from a Marxist materialist base, arguing that women's labor in reproduction and domestic work provides the grounding for a distinctive feminist standpoint.
- Georg Lukács, *History and Class Consciousness*, MIT Press, 1971. The Marxist antecedent of standpoint theory, arguing that the proletariat's standpoint provides a privileged vantage on the social totality — a model Harding and others explicitly adopted and transformed.

## Secondary Sources

- Uma Narayan, "The Project of Feminist Epistemology: Perspectives from a Nonwestern Feminist," in *Gender/Body/Knowledge*, Rutgers University Press, 1990. Raises concerns about standpoint theory from a postcolonial perspective, arguing that the concept of a unified women's standpoint obscures important differences among women globally.
- Elizabeth Anderson, "Feminist Epistemology and Philosophy of Science," *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, 2015. A comprehensive and carefully reasoned survey of feminist standpoint epistemology and related positions, assessing the main objections of relativism and essentialism.
- Anita Superson and Ann Cudd, eds., *Theorizing Backlash: Philosophical Reflections on the Resistance to Feminism*, Rowman and Littlefield, 2002. Examines epistemic and political resistance to feminist standpoint claims, analyzing the social and psychological mechanisms of backlash.
- Kristina Rolin, "Why Feminist Standpoint Epistemology?," *Canadian Journal of Philosophy* 36(2), 2006. Offers a defense of standpoint epistemology against the charge of essentialism, arguing that the standpoint is acquired through political practice rather than given by biological or social identity.
- Lorraine Code, *Epistemic Responsibility*, Brown University Press, 1987. A feminist epistemology that, while not strictly standpoint theory, develops the claim that responsible knowing requires attention to the social position and context of the knower.
- Sally Haslanger, "Ontology and Social Construction," *Philosophical Topics* 23(2), 1995. Connects feminist standpoint concerns to questions in social ontology about the constructed character of gender categories and their epistemic implications.
- Helen Longino, *Science as Social Knowledge*, Princeton University Press, 1990. Develops a social account of scientific objectivity through the norms of critical interaction in scientific communities, complementing standpoint approaches with a focus on the social processes of inquiry rather than the standpoint of individual inquirers.
