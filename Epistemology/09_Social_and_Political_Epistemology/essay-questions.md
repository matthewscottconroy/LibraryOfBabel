# Essay Questions: Unit 9 — Social and Political Epistemology

---

**1. The Epistemology of Peer Disagreement**

When two epistemic peers — agents with equal evidence and equal epistemic reliability — disagree, what should they do? Critically compare the conciliationist answer (Elga, Christensen: split the difference) and the steadfastness answer (Kelly, Williamson: retain one's view if the first-order evidence warrants it). Does the conciliationist position suffer from a bootstrapping problem — is it self-undermining when applied to the dispute between conciliationists and steadfasters? What is the significance of the peer disagreement debate for philosophy itself, given the prevalence of persistent philosophical disagreement?

---

**2. Testimonial Injustice and the Ethics of Credibility**

Miranda Fricker's concept of testimonial injustice identifies a distinctive epistemic wrong: the unjust deflation of a speaker's credibility because of identity prejudice. Critically assess Fricker's account. Is testimonial injustice primarily a moral wrong, a epistemic wrong, or both — and can the two be separated? Does Fricker's account adequately address cases where unjust credibility excess (overcrediting) occurs? How does José Medina's "epistemology of resistance" extend or challenge Fricker's framework?

---

**3. Group Epistemology: The Discursive Dilemma and Collective Agency**

Pettit and List's discursive dilemma shows that a group that reasons by aggregating judgments on individual propositions can reach a collective conclusion inconsistent with each member's overall view. Critically assess the epistemological significance of this result. Does it establish that groups have beliefs and make judgments that are genuinely irreducible to individual beliefs and judgments? What conditions on group structure are required for groups to function as responsible epistemic agents? Is the epistemology of groups best understood within a non-summativist or summativist framework?

---

**4. Social Epistemology of Science: Veritistic vs. Political Models**

Goldman's veritistic social epistemology evaluates social epistemic practices by their contribution to true belief formation. Helen Longino's contextual empiricism holds that scientific knowledge is constitutively social and that the legitimacy of social values entering inquiry is determined by the quality of the social-epistemic processes involved. Critically compare these models. Is veritistic social epistemology too individualistic — does it fail to account for the genuinely social character of scientific knowledge? Is Longino's framework too permissive — does it allow too many value influences on inquiry?

---

**5. Hermeneutical Injustice and Conceptual Resources**

Fricker's hermeneutical injustice occurs when a speaker lacks the conceptual resources to make sense of a significant experience because those resources are absent from the shared hermeneutical stock. Critically assess whether hermeneutical injustice is genuinely a form of epistemic injustice — a wrong done to the speaker in their epistemic capacity — or whether it is better understood as a social or political wrong with epistemic dimensions. What are the conditions for a hermeneutical lacuna to constitute an injustice rather than merely an epistemic deficit? How can epistemic communities address hermeneutical injustice?

---

**6. The Epistemology of Expertise and Rational Deference**

When a layperson cannot independently evaluate an expert's claims, what is the rational response? John Hardwig argued ("Epistemic Dependence," 1985) that rational epistemic dependence on experts is legitimate and often necessary. But when experts disagree, or when expertise is corrupted by institutional pressures, rational deference becomes problematic. Critically assess the conditions for rational deference to experts. What "meta-level" indicators of expertise — track record, institutional affiliations, peer endorsement, conflict of interest — should a non-expert use? Is there an ineliminable epistemological vulnerability in the layperson's epistemic situation?

---

**7. Epistemic Bubbles, Filter Bubbles, and the Social Distribution of Knowledge**

Social media and algorithmic curation create "filter bubbles" that reinforce existing beliefs and reduce exposure to defeaters. Critically assess the epistemological consequences of filter bubbles. Do filter bubbles constitute a form of epistemic harm, and if so, to whom? Is the concept of an "epistemic bubble" (Nguyen) — where one fails to encounter opposing views — epistemologically distinct from an "echo chamber" (where one is actively inoculated against opposing views)? What epistemic responsibilities do individuals and social media platforms have to prevent epistemically harmful filter effects?

---

**8. Feminist Standpoint Epistemology and its Prospects**

Standpoint epistemology (Hartsock, Harding) holds that one's social position — particularly membership in a marginalized group — can provide epistemic advantages: insights unavailable from privileged positions. Critically assess this claim. Does standpoint epistemology commit to a form of relativism (all standpoints are equally valid) or to a more selective view (marginalized standpoints provide advantages in specific domains)? How should standpoint considerations be integrated with the universalist aspirations of classical epistemology? Is standpoint epistemology best understood as a claim about individual agents, social groups, or epistemic communities?
