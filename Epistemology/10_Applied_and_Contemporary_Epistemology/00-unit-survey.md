# Unit 10: Applied and Contemporary Epistemology — Unit Survey

## Central Questions

Applied epistemology extends the theoretical work of Units 1–9 to domains where knowledge claims have practical stakes: science, religion, morality, artificial intelligence, digital media, and political deliberation. The unit asks not merely what knowledge is but what our epistemic responsibilities are in domains where the methods, institutions, and technologies of knowledge acquisition are contested, unreliable, or under attack.

The central questions of this unit are: (1) What is the epistemic status of scientific knowledge, and how does consensus relate to truth? (2) How should we relate to experts when we lack the ability to independently evaluate their claims? (3) Is belief in God epistemically rational, and what relationship does faith bear to evidence? (4) Can AI systems have knowledge, and what epistemic challenges does AI-mediated information raise? (5) What are the epistemic effects of filter bubbles, fake news, and social media? (6) Is there a distinctive form of moral knowledge, and how is it acquired? (7) What does the "knowledge-first" program achieve when applied to these domains?

This unit is unified by a shared concern: that epistemic agency — the ability to form, revise, and act on well-grounded beliefs — can be enhanced or undermined by institutional, technological, and social conditions. Applied epistemology is not merely the "application" of theory to practice; it generates new theoretical insights and often reveals hidden presuppositions in the theoretical literature.

## Map of Major Positions and Their Logical Relations

**Philosophy of Science and Scientific Knowledge.** Scientific knowledge is paradigmatically well-grounded — it is the product of systematic inquiry, peer review, and methodological discipline. But the epistemology of science raises genuine questions: Is scientific theory (beyond observation) genuinely known, or merely empirically adequate (van Fraassen's constructive empiricism)? How does scientific consensus relate to truth? Can the replication crisis in psychology and social science be accommodated within a reliabilist account of scientific knowledge?

*Scientific realism*: successful scientific theories are approximately true, and their theoretical entities are real. The "no miracles" argument: the predictive success of science would be a miracle unless the theories are at least approximately true.

*Constructive empiricism* (van Fraassen): scientific theories need only be empirically adequate — correct about observable phenomena. We need not believe in unobservable entities.

*Social epistemology of science* (Longino, Solomon): scientific knowledge is produced by social processes (negotiation, criticism, collaborative research), and its epistemic properties depend on the quality of those processes.

**Moral Epistemology.** How is moral knowledge possible? Is there such a thing as moral perception — direct acquaintance with moral facts? Or must moral knowledge be inferential, derived from moral principles and empirical facts about consequences?

*Moral intuitionism* (Ross, Prichard, Audi): moral intuitions provide direct, non-inferential justification for moral beliefs. Moral knowledge has the same structure as a priori knowledge — it is grounded in rational insight into moral facts.

*Coherentism about moral knowledge*: moral beliefs are justified by their coherence with other moral and non-moral beliefs. Reflective equilibrium (Rawls) is the canonical method.

*Quasi-realism* (Blackburn): starts from an expressivist account of moral discourse but earns the right to use realist-sounding language. Moral claims express attitudes but can be qualified as true or false in the ordinary sense.

*Moral skepticism*: there is no moral knowledge because there are no moral facts, or moral facts are not accessible to human cognitive faculties.

**Religious Epistemology.** Reformed epistemology (Plantinga, Wolterstorff) holds that belief in God can be properly basic — justified without requiring inference from natural theology. Evidentialist critiques (Clifford: "it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence") require positive evidence for religious belief. Plantinga's proper functionalism implies that if God exists and has designed human cognitive faculties, religious belief can satisfy warrant conditions.

**Epistemology of Expertise.** When a layperson cannot independently evaluate an expert's claims, what is the rational response? Options: defer to the expert consensus; seek multiple experts and evaluate meta-level indicators of expertise; apply reliabilist considerations to the expert's track record. The epistemology of expertise connects to the vaccination debate, climate science denialism, and the sociology of expert knowledge.

**AI and Epistemic Agency.** Can AI systems have knowledge? The machine learning revolution raises new epistemological questions: AI systems can make accurate predictions without anything like understanding or justification in the traditional sense. Large language models (LLMs) are trained to produce plausible text, not to track truth. The epistemological question is whether LLM outputs constitute testimony, evidence, or neither.

*AI-generated misinformation*: LLMs can produce plausible falsehoods ("hallucinations"). The epistemological challenge for users is to identify which outputs are reliable without independently verifiable grounds.

**Epistemic Bubbles and Filter Flows.** Eli Pariser's concept of the "filter bubble" — algorithmic curation of information that reinforces existing beliefs — has epistemological implications. An agent in a filter bubble has reduced access to defeaters for their beliefs. This is an applied version of the coherentism objection: a system that only reinforces existing beliefs is not epistemically tracking truth.

*Fake news*: intentionally false information disseminated with the appearance of journalistic credibility. The epistemological challenge is distinguishing genuine from fake news without independent verification of each claim.

**Knowledge-First Applied Epistemology.** Williamson's knowledge norm of assertion (assert only what you know) and knowledge norm of action (act only on what you know) apply directly to applied epistemology: experts should only assert things they know; policy decisions should be made only on the basis of what is known. This normative framework has direct implications for scientific communication, journalism, and public discourse.

## Sub-Chapter Contributions

**Unit 1: Philosophy of Science.** Chapter 1 examines the epistemological foundations of scientific method — induction, falsification, inference to the best explanation. Chapter 2 examines the realism/anti-realism debate and the status of theoretical entities. Chapter 3 addresses the social epistemology of science: peer review, replication, and the epistemological effects of funding structures.

**Unit 2: Moral Epistemology.** Chapter 1 examines moral intuitionism and the epistemological role of moral intuitions. Chapter 2 develops coherentism and reflective equilibrium as an alternative. Chapter 3 examines moral relativism and its epistemological basis.

**Unit 3: Religious Epistemology.** Chapter 1 examines the evidentialist challenge (Clifford) and reformed epistemology (Plantinga, Wolterstorff). Chapter 2 examines natural theology — the attempt to establish religious beliefs on natural evidence. Chapter 3 addresses the relationship between faith and evidence.

**Unit 4: Epistemology, Technology, and AI.** Chapter 1 examines the epistemology of AI systems — can they know, and what are the implications of AI-generated testimony? Chapter 2 examines filter bubbles and algorithmic curation. Chapter 3 addresses the epistemological ethics of AI — what epistemic responsibilities do AI developers have?

**Unit 5: Knowledge-First and Contemporary.** Chapter 1 presents Williamson's knowledge-first program and its applications. Chapter 2 examines pragmatic encroachment in applied contexts. Chapter 3 surveys the current state of analytic epistemology and its relationship to cognitive science and formal methods.

## Key Philosophers and Arguments

**W.K. Clifford (1845–1879).** "The Ethics of Belief" (1877) asserts the evidentialist requirement in its strongest form: it is morally wrong to believe anything without sufficient evidence, because we are members of an epistemic community whose beliefs affect others. The railway engineer who believes his ship is seaworthy without proper investigation is culpable even if the ship happens to be safe.

**William James (1842–1910).** "The Will to Believe" (1897) responds to Clifford: in "forced, live, and momentous" decisions where evidence is insufficient, it can be rational to choose a belief that facilitates practical engagement. James's pragmatist epistemology allows that practical consequences can justify belief in a weak sense. The religious belief case is James's primary example.

**Alvin Plantinga (1932–).** *Warranted Christian Belief* (2000) completes the *Warrant* trilogy with an application to religious epistemology. Plantinga argues that if Christian theism is true, then the cognitive faculties through which humans come to believe in God are functioning properly, so Christian belief has warrant. If Christian theism is false, it may lack warrant. The epistemological argument does not settle the theological question but shows that Christian belief is not epistemically irrational.

**Helen Longino (1944–).** *Science as Social Knowledge* (1990) argues that scientific knowledge is constitutively social: it is produced by communities of inquiry whose practices meet certain social-epistemological standards (public criticism, uptake of criticism, public standards of evaluation, and tempered equality of intellectual authority). Longino's "contextual empiricism" shows how social values can legitimately enter scientific inquiry without undermining objectivity.

**Philip Kitcher (1947–).** *Science in a Democratic Society* (2011) addresses the social epistemology of science in democratic contexts. Kitcher argues that scientific inquiry should be organized to maximize well-ordered inquiry — inquiry that serves the needs of a democratically organized society. This generates a normative theory of science policy with epistemological foundations.

**Bas van Fraassen (1941–).** *The Scientific Image* (1980) defends constructive empiricism: scientific theories need only be empirically adequate (correct about observables), not true about unobservable entities. Acceptance of a theory is acceptance of its empirical adequacy; belief in its truth is unnecessary and goes beyond evidence.

**Cailin O'Connor and James Owen Weatherall (contemporary).** *The Misinformation Age* (2019) provides a social-epistemological analysis of misinformation campaigns, drawing on network theory and Bayesian models of belief updating. The analysis shows how doubt can be strategically manufactured in epistemic communities, with applications to climate science denialism and tobacco industry practices.

**Miranda Fricker (1966–).** *Epistemic Injustice* (2007) bears directly on applied epistemology: testimonial injustice affects the credibility attributed to victims of crime, patients in medical settings, and members of marginalized communities. The applied implications of epistemic injustice are as important as the theoretical ones.

## Essential Readings

1. **Clifford, W.K., "The Ethics of Belief," in *Lectures and Essays*** (1879, Macmillan). The classic statement of evidentialism. Short and polemical. Should be read with James's "The Will to Believe" as a contrasting response.

2. **Plantinga, Alvin, *Warranted Christian Belief*, Chapters 1–3** (2000, Oxford). The most sophisticated epistemological defence of religious belief. Chapters 1–3 are accessible without the full technical apparatus of the earlier *Warrant* volumes.

3. **van Fraassen, Bas, *The Scientific Image*, Chapters 1–2** (1980, Oxford). The canonical statement of constructive empiricism. Chapters 1–2 introduce empirical adequacy and the distinction between acceptance and belief.

4. **Longino, Helen, *Science as Social Knowledge*, Chapters 1–4** (1990, Princeton). The social epistemological framework for scientific knowledge. Chapters 1–4 develop contextual empiricism.

5. **O'Connor, Cailin and Weatherall, James Owen, *The Misinformation Age*, Chapters 1–3** (2019, Yale). The social-epistemological analysis of misinformation. Chapters 1–3 develop the network model of belief and apply it to manufactured doubt.

## Open Questions and Live Debates

**How should laypersons relate to expert disagreement?** The epistemology of expertise is urgent in the context of climate science, vaccine safety, and COVID-19. John Hardwig argued ("Epistemic Dependence," 1985) that laypeople are inevitably dependent on experts and that this dependence is epistemically legitimate. But when experts disagree, the question of which expert to trust is unanswerable by non-expert means.

**Can AI systems have knowledge, and is AI-generated text testimony?** The epistemological status of LLM outputs is contested. Some argue that LLMs produce testimony in the functional sense (sincere-seeming assertions about the world). Others argue that LLMs have no beliefs and hence cannot testify. The stakes are high for the epistemology of information and for the design of AI systems.

**Does the replication crisis undermine scientific knowledge?** The discovery that many published results in psychology and social science cannot be replicated raises the question of whether the peer-reviewed literature constitutes knowledge. Reliabilist accounts imply that if the relevant processes are unreliable, the resulting beliefs are not justified. The institutional implications — changes in peer review, statistical standards, pre-registration — are under active development.

**Is the knowledge norm of assertion too demanding for public discourse?** Williamson's KNA (assert only what you know) has been applied to journalism, scientific communication, and political speech. Critics argue that it is too demanding: in conditions of genuine uncertainty, we must assert claims we believe to be probably true but do not know. The normative question about the appropriate epistemic standard for public discourse is unresolved.
