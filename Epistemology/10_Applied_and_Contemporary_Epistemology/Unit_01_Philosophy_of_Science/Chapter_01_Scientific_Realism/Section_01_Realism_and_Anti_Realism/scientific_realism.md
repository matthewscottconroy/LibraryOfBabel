# Scientific Realism

Scientific realism is the view that our best scientific theories are (approximately) true and that the entities they posit — electrons, genes, quarks, black holes — actually exist, independently of our theorizing about them. It is the common-sense view of science: science makes progress because it is learning real things about a real world. Yet the history of science and the underdetermination of theory by evidence provide persistent challenges to this commonsensical position.

## The No-Miracles Argument

The primary positive argument for scientific realism is the *no-miracles argument* (Putnam, 1975): the empirical success of science would be a miracle if our theories were not approximately true. Scientific theories make remarkably precise predictions about phenomena far removed from the original observations; they enable the development of technologies that work; they support intervention in the natural world. The best explanation of this success is that the theories are at least approximately true — that they correctly describe the entities and processes they posit.

Realists point to convergence as additional evidence: successive scientific theories in the same domain tend to converge on similar theoretical posits. Relativistic mechanics converges on Newtonian mechanics in low-velocity limits; quantum field theory "contains" quantum mechanics. This convergence suggests that the theories are tracking something real.

## The Pessimistic Meta-Induction

Larry Laudan's *pessimistic meta-induction* (1981) is the central challenge to scientific realism. Historical survey shows that successful scientific theories have repeatedly been replaced by incompatible successors:

- Phlogiston theory was successful by the standards of the time; it was replaced by oxygen-based chemistry.
- Caloric theory of heat was successful; it was replaced by kinetic theory.
- Newtonian mechanics was extraordinarily successful; it was replaced by relativity and quantum mechanics.

If past successful theories were not approximately true (as evidenced by their abandonment), why should we believe current successful theories are approximately true? Induction from scientific history suggests that current theories will also be abandoned and replaced.

## Structural Realism

John Worrall's *structural realism* (1989) attempts to capture what is preserved through theory change while acknowledging what is lost. What persists across scientific revolutions is not the postulation of specific entities (e.g., the luminiferous ether) but the *mathematical structure* of the theories (the equations).

For example, the Fresnel equations for light polarization were preserved in the transition from wave to electromagnetic theory, even though the ontological interpretation changed dramatically. The structure survived; the posited entity (ether) did not.

Structural realism holds: what science successfully tracks is the structure of reality — the relational properties and mathematical organization — not the intrinsic nature of theoretical entities. This preserves the no-miracles argument while accommodating the pessimistic meta-induction.

## Constructive Empiricism

Bas van Fraassen's *constructive empiricism* (*The Scientific Image*, 1980) offers a sophisticated anti-realist alternative. Van Fraassen holds that scientific theories should be accepted as *empirically adequate* — as correctly describing all observable phenomena — without being believed as *true* about unobservable entities.

The distinction between observable and unobservable is crucial. Electrons are unobservable in principle; we only ever see their effects. For van Fraassen, accepting the electron theory means accepting its predictions about observable phenomena, not believing that electrons literally exist.

Constructive empiricism preserves the rationality of science without committing to the truth of claims about unobservables. It explains the success of science (empirically adequate theories make accurate predictions) without the no-miracles argument.

## The Underdetermination Problem

Any finite body of evidence is compatible with multiple, logically incompatible theories. This *underdetermination* of theory by evidence means that the evidence cannot uniquely determine which theory is true. If multiple incompatible theories are equally well-supported by all possible evidence, we have no grounds for preferring one as true.

Realists respond that underdetermination is a theoretical possibility rarely realized in practice: we do have grounds for preferring theories on the basis of simplicity, explanatory depth, and predictive power. But anti-realists maintain that these theoretical virtues don't give us truth; they give us empirically adequate theories.

## Implications for Epistemology

The scientific realism debate illustrates general epistemological themes: the role of inference to the best explanation, the problem of underdetermination, the relationship between confirmation and truth, and the epistemic status of claims about unobservable entities. These connect to debates about perception, justification, and the limits of knowledge.
