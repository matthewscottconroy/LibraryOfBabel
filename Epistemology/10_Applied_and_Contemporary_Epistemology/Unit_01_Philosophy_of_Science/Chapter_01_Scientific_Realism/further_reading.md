## Primary Sources

- Hilary Putnam, "What Is Mathematical Truth?," in *Mathematics, Matter and Method*, Cambridge University Press, 1975. Contains the "no-miracles argument" in its most influential form: the success of science would be miraculous if scientific theories were not at least approximately true.
- Larry Laudan, "A Confutation of Convergent Realism," *Philosophy of Science* 48(1), 1981. The canonical statement of the pessimistic meta-induction: the history of science shows successful theories later proven false, undermining the inference from success to truth.
- Bas van Fraassen, *The Scientific Image*, Oxford University Press, 1980. The definitive statement of constructive empiricism — the anti-realist view that science aims at empirical adequacy rather than truth, and that we should believe theories only to be "empirically adequate."
- John Worrall, "Structural Realism: The Best of Both Worlds?," *Dialectica* 43(1–2), 1989. Introduces structural realism as a position that accommodates the pessimistic meta-induction while preserving the no-miracles argument by claiming science tracks structural rather than intrinsic features of the world.
- Richard Boyd, "Scientific Realism and Naturalistic Epistemology," *PSA: Proceedings of the Biennial Meeting of the Philosophy of Science Association*, vol. 2, 1980. Develops causal-explanatory realism grounded in naturalistic epistemology, arguing that the theoretical posits of mature science are causally responsible for predictive success.
- Kyle Stanford, *Exceeding Our Grasp: Science, History, and the Problem of Unconceived Alternatives*, Oxford University Press, 2006. Argues for a new anti-realist challenge: scientists systematically fail to conceive of alternative theories that would be equally well-confirmed by available evidence, undermining confidence that current theories are approximately true.
- W. H. Newton-Smith, *The Rationality of Science*, Routledge and Kegan Paul, 1981. Defends a moderate scientific realism, arguing that science is a truth-seeking practice and that its progress is best explained by the approximate truth of its mature theories.

## Secondary Sources

- Stathis Psillos, *Scientific Realism: How Science Tracks Truth*, Routledge, 1999. A comprehensive philosophical defense of scientific realism, responding to the main anti-realist challenges including van Fraassen's constructive empiricism and Laudan's meta-induction.
- Anjan Chakravartty, *A Metaphysics for Scientific Realism*, Cambridge University Press, 2007. Develops a "semi-realism" that draws on structural and entity realism, arguing for selective belief in the well-confirmed structural and causal features of scientific theories.
- Alexander Bird, *Philosophy of Science*, Routledge, 1998. A clear and accessible introduction to the scientific realism debate, covering the main arguments for and against realism.
- James Ladyman and Don Ross, *Every Thing Must Go: Metaphysics Naturalized*, Oxford University Press, 2007. Develops "ontic structural realism," arguing that structural realism properly understood eliminates individual objects from ontology in favor of relations.
- Hasok Chang, *Is Water H₂O? Evidence, Realism and Pluralism*, Springer, 2012. Uses the historical chemistry of water to interrogate the relationship between theoretical identification, empirical success, and realist commitment, developing a "scientific pluralism."
- Steven French and Décio Krause, *Identity in Physics: A Historical, Philosophical, and Formal Analysis*, Oxford University Press, 2006. Examines the implications of quantum mechanics for structural realism and the identity of scientific objects, with rigorous attention to the formal foundations.
- Philip Kitcher, *The Advancement of Science*, Oxford University Press, 1993. Defends a naturalistic scientific realism grounded in the distinction between working posits and idle wheels in mature science, offering a selective realist response to the pessimistic meta-induction.
