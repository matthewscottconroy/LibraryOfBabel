## Hilary Putnam (1926–2016)

Putnam articulated the most widely cited argument for scientific realism: the "no-miracles argument." He argued that the extraordinary predictive and technological success of mature scientific theories would be a miracle — a coincidence without explanation — if the theoretical posits of those theories did not at least approximately describe how the world is. Putnam subsequently became more skeptical of realism, developing internal realism and later pragmatic realism, and his own evolution tracked the central tensions in the debate. His work on reference and truth is also foundational for understanding how scientific terms can successfully refer even when descriptions associated with them change.

## Larry Laudan (1941–)

Laudan's "A Confutation of Convergent Realism" mounted the most systematic historical challenge to scientific realism. Through careful examination of the history of science, he produced a list of successful but now-abandoned theories whose central theoretical posits are no longer recognized as real (phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous ether, vital force). The "pessimistic meta-induction" — the inference from the past falsity of successful theories to the probable falsity of current ones — has driven the subsequent debate and forced realists to develop increasingly sophisticated selective and structural responses.

## Bas van Fraassen (1941–)

Van Fraassen's *The Scientific Image* made the most philosophically sophisticated case for anti-realism, developing constructive empiricism: the view that the goal of science is empirical adequacy — saving the phenomena — rather than truth. He argued that instrumentalism is motivated not by skepticism but by a "voluntarist" stance toward science: we choose to accept theories, and acceptance need not involve full belief. His distinction between "observable" and "unobservable" entities drew criticism from realists but defined the terms of the debate for decades. Van Fraassen's subsequent work on symmetry, laws of nature, and quantum mechanics has continued to challenge realist assumptions.

## John Worrall (1946–)

Worrall revived Poincaré's structural realism as a response to the tension between the no-miracles argument and the pessimistic meta-induction. His key insight was that in the history of science, mathematical structure is preserved through theory change even when physical interpretations are abandoned — Fresnel's equations survived the transition from his elastic ether to Maxwell's electromagnetism, preserving the structural relationships. Structural realism holds that science reliably tracks the structure of the world rather than its intrinsic nature, making success explicable without commitment to the reality of theoretical objects.

## Kyle Stanford (1965–)

Stanford developed a new anti-realist challenge that goes beyond Laudan's historical argument: the "problem of unconceived alternatives." He argued that scientists at every historical period have systematically been unable to conceive of alternative theories that would be equally well-confirmed by available evidence — and that current scientists are in no better position. This is not merely a skeptical possibility but a documented historical pattern, giving us positive reason to believe our best current theories are not the only ones consistent with evidence. Stanford's challenge has pushed realists to develop more nuanced accounts of the relationship between predictive success and theoretical truth.

## Anjan Chakravartty (1971–)

Chakravartty developed "semi-realism" as an attempt to identify exactly what scientific realists should be committed to. He distinguished between "detection properties" — causal features of things invoked in tracking and manipulating them — and "auxiliary properties" — features posited to explain but not directly causally relevant to detection. Semi-realism advocates belief in detection properties while suspending judgment on auxiliary ones, providing a principled selective realism that accommodates the history of science while preserving the explanatory force of the no-miracles argument.
