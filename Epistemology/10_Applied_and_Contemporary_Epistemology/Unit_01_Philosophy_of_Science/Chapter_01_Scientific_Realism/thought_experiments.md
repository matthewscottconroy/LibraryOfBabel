## The No-Miracles Argument

**The scenario**: Scientists in the nineteenth century, using Newtonian mechanics and Maxwell's electromagnetic theory, could predict the behavior of spacecraft trajectories, the bending of light near massive objects (later confirmed by Einstein's theory), and the existence of radio waves before their discovery. These predictions were wildly successful, confirmed across decades and continents. A philosopher asks: if the theoretical entities — gravitational fields, electromagnetic waves — postulated by these theories do not exist, how do we explain the success?

**The philosophical point**: The no-miracles argument infers approximate truth from predictive success: the most natural explanation for science's extraordinary predictive power is that its theories accurately describe the unobservable structure of the world. Anti-realists dispute whether success requires truth: van Fraassen argues that empirically adequate theories succeed by construction, not because they are true; and Laudan's meta-induction shows that past successful theories were false. The scenario sets up the central realist/anti-realist debate by making vivid why the success of science seems to demand a realist explanation.

## The Pessimistic Meta-Induction

**The scenario**: Laudan presents a catalogue: phlogiston theory was empirically successful and then abandoned; caloric fluid theory was successful and then abandoned; the luminiferous ether was central to successful electromagnetic theory before special relativity; vital force explained phenomena of life before organic chemistry. In each case, the central theoretical posit of a successful science was later shown to be non-existent. What should we conclude about the central theoretical posits of our current successful science?

**The philosophical point**: The pessimistic meta-induction is an inductive argument from the history of science against realist confidence in current theoretical posits. It challenges not just specific scientific claims but the realist meta-level inference pattern: we cannot reliably infer from predictive success to approximate truth, because we have been wrong about successful theories too many times. Realists typically respond by arguing that the cited cases involve "idle wheels" rather than working posits, that the structure of theories is preserved even when interpretations change, or that modern science has achieved greater maturity and continuity than its predecessors.

## The Constructive Empiricist's Acceptance

**The scenario**: van Fraassen invites us to consider two scientists who accept the same theory — quantum electrodynamics. Scientist A is a scientific realist who believes the theory is true: there really are electrons with the properties QED describes. Scientist B is a constructive empiricist who merely accepts the theory as empirically adequate: it correctly predicts all observable phenomena, but she makes no commitment to unobservable entities. Both scientists make the same experiments, use the same calculations, publish the same papers. What difference does the metaphysical commitment make?

**The philosophical point**: Van Fraassen's scenario asks whether the difference between realism and anti-realism is a difference that makes a practical scientific difference, or merely a metaphysical stance with no empirical consequences. Realists argue that the stance does matter: it affects the choice between empirically equivalent theories, guides abductive inference, and motivates research programs. Anti-realists argue it is a "ladder to be kicked away" — the acceptance stance is epistemically safer without any loss of scientific content. The scenario illuminates the question of whether scientific epistemology can remain agnostic about unobservables.

## The Ether's Success and Disappearance

**The scenario**: In the late nineteenth century, the luminiferous ether was not an idle theoretical posit but a central, indispensable component of Maxwell's electromagnetic theory. Physical properties were attributed to it; calculations based on it yielded successful predictions; experiments were designed around it. Michelson and Morley's experiment was intended to detect the Earth's motion through the ether — not to test its existence. When special relativity replaced it, the ether simply disappeared, without any entity taking its place.

**The philosophical point**: Worrall uses this case to argue for structural realism: what was preserved from Fresnel and Maxwell to Einstein was the mathematical structure — the field equations — while the physical interpretation (the ether) was abandoned. The scenario motivates the realist to retreat to structural claims: science reliably captures the relational structure of the world, even when its ontological stories change radically. Critics of structural realism ask whether pure structure without objects is coherent, and whether the structure preserved is enough to ground scientific realism's explanatory ambitions.
