# Scientific Methodology: Confirmation, Falsification, and Inference

The epistemology of science includes a set of questions about the methods by which science generates knowledge. What distinguishes science from pseudoscience? How does evidence confirm or disconfirm theories? What is the logic of scientific inference? These questions have generated rich philosophical debate, connecting to broader issues about induction, explanation, and rational belief revision.

## Hypothetico-Deductivism

The simplest account of scientific reasoning is *hypothetico-deductivism* (HD): scientists propose hypotheses, deduce observable consequences from them, and test whether those consequences are observed. If the predicted observation is not made, the hypothesis is falsified (by modus tollens). If the prediction is confirmed, the hypothesis is (partially) supported.

HD captures something important about scientific practice but is too simple. The Duhem-Quine thesis shows that hypotheses are not tested in isolation: when a prediction fails, we can always "save" the hypothesis by modifying auxiliary assumptions (calibration of instruments, background conditions, etc.). There is no single hypothesis that is straightforwardly falsified by any single observation.

## Popper's Falsificationism

Karl Popper's philosophy of science (*The Logic of Scientific Discovery*, 1934; *Conjectures and Refutations*, 1963) is the most influential attempt to characterize the logic of science. Popper argued that confirmation does not establish truth: no finite number of confirming instances can verify a universal law. But a single disconfirming instance can falsify it.

The criterion of *demarcation*: a theory is scientific if and only if it is in principle falsifiable — if there is some possible observation that would disconfirm it. Unfalsifiable theories (Freudian psychoanalysis, Marxist historical theory, as Popper applied it) are not scientific but metaphysical.

Science progresses not by accumulating confirmations but by eliminating falsified theories through *bold conjectures* and rigorous testing. The surviving theories are not verified; they are *corroborated* — they have survived many attempts at falsification.

## The Problem of Falsification

Popper's account faces several challenges:

**Duhem-Quine**: As noted, theories are never falsified in isolation. Apparent falsification can always be attributed to auxiliary hypotheses rather than the core theory. This makes falsification more a matter of decision than logic.

**Saving the hypothesis**: Scientists regularly maintain theories in the face of apparent falsifications, on the grounds that the counterevidence is the result of experimental error or poor measurement. This is sometimes rational (the counterevidence is genuinely unreliable) and sometimes not (the theory is being immunized from falsification).

**Qualitative vs. quantitative**: Popper's account works best for qualitative predictions; it struggles with the stochastic predictions of statistical theories (which are never strictly falsified by any finite sample).

## Bayesian Confirmation Theory

Bayesian confirmation theory provides a probabilistic account of how evidence confirms or disconfirms hypotheses. Evidence E confirms hypothesis H if P(H|E) > P(H) — if learning E raises the probability of H.

The Bayesian framework handles:
- **Degrees of confirmation**: Confirmation is not all-or-nothing; evidence can partially confirm.
- **Prior probabilities**: The prior probability of H affects how much confirmation E provides.
- **Competing hypotheses**: Evidence can confirm multiple hypotheses simultaneously; Bayesianism handles the comparative evaluation.

Bayesian confirmation is sensitive to the prior: for a hypothesis with very low prior probability, even strong positive evidence may not raise its probability much. This explains why extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence.

## Kuhn's Scientific Revolutions

Thomas Kuhn's *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962) offered a historical account of science that challenged the standard rationalist picture. Kuhn distinguished *normal science* (puzzle-solving within an accepted paradigm) from *scientific revolutions* (paradigm shifts).

During normal science, anomalies are accommodated rather than treated as falsifications. Scientists are trained not to question the paradigm but to extend it. When anomalies accumulate sufficiently, a crisis occurs and a revolution becomes possible — but the transition from one paradigm to another is not purely rational; it involves sociological, psychological, and historical factors.

Kuhn's concepts of *paradigm incommensurability* — the idea that competing paradigms may be partially untranslatable and incomparable — generated enormous controversy and connected to broader debates about relativism and objectivity in science.

## Inference to the Best Explanation

Scientists routinely infer from evidence to the best explanation of that evidence. The discovery of Neptune (its existence inferred from perturbations in Uranus's orbit), the germ theory of disease, the continental drift theory — all were accepted on the basis of explanatory power.

Inference to the best explanation (IBE) involves judging which hypothesis, if true, would best explain the available evidence, and concluding that this hypothesis is (probably) true. The epistemic issues include: what makes an explanation "best"? Is explanatory goodness epistemically relevant? And can IBE be justified without circularity?
