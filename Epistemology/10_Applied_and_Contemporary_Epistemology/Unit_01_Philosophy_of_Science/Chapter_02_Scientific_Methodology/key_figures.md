## Karl Popper (1902–1994)

Popper's falsificationism transformed the philosophy of scientific method. He argued that scientific theories are distinguished not by their verifiability (a positivist criterion he rejected) but by their falsifiability: the mark of a scientific theory is that it makes risky predictions that could, in principle, be refuted by observation. Scientific progress is the result of bold conjectures subjected to the most rigorous attempts at refutation; theories that survive are not confirmed but merely "corroborated" — not yet refuted. Popper's demarcation criterion has been enormously influential, though philosophers have identified problems with its application to whole scientific research programs.

## Pierre Duhem (1861–1916)

Duhem introduced the thesis that in physics, individual hypotheses cannot be tested in isolation because they always face experience only in conjunction with a large number of auxiliary assumptions. This means that when an experiment gives unexpected results, the fault may lie with any of the auxiliaries rather than the hypothesis under test — there is always a choice about where to place the "blame." Duhem's holism about testing anticipated much of twentieth-century philosophy of science and is the foundation for the Duhem-Quine thesis, which extends this insight to all knowledge.

## W. V. O. Quine (1908–2000)

Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" extended Duhem's thesis about the testing of physical hypotheses into a comprehensive holism about knowledge: our beliefs face the "tribunal of experience" as a corporate body, not individually. This means that any statement can be immunized against refutation by revising other beliefs, and conversely that even logical and mathematical truths are in principle revisable in light of experience. Quine's naturalism — the view that epistemology is continuous with empirical science — reshaped analytic philosophy and grounded the Duhem-Quine thesis in a systematic philosophical framework.

## Thomas Kuhn (1922–1996)

Kuhn's *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* introduced the vocabulary of paradigms, normal science, anomalies, and scientific revolutions, transforming how philosophers and sociologists understand scientific change. He argued that science is conducted within paradigms — shared exemplars, methods, and background assumptions — and that paradigm shifts are not purely rational responses to evidence but involve gestalt-like transformations of the scientist's whole conceptual framework. Kuhn's account challenged the image of science as cumulative and rational, sparking debates about scientific rationality, relativism, and the relationship between history and philosophy of science that continue today.

## Imre Lakatos (1922–1974)

Lakatos developed the methodology of scientific research programs as a sophisticated alternative to both Popper's naive falsificationism and Kuhn's apparently irrational account of scientific change. A research program consists of a "hard core" of theoretical commitments protected by a "protective belt" of auxiliary hypotheses that are adjusted to meet experimental challenges. Programs are evaluated not by individual theory tests but by whether their sequence of theories is progressive (making novel successful predictions) or degenerating (merely accommodating anomalies post hoc). Lakatos attempted to show that major episodes in the history of science are rational, even when individual theories are not immediately falsified.

## Peter Lipton (1954–2007)

Lipton developed the most philosophically rigorous analysis of inference to the best explanation (IBE), arguing that scientists and ordinary reasoners alike infer not merely to the best explanation available but to what would be "loveliest" if true — the explanation that would provide the most understanding. Lipton's careful work on the relationship between explanation and confirmation, and his examination of the relationship between IBE and Bayesian inference, helped establish IBE as a serious and philosophically tractable concept. His untimely death left unfinished a project that would have connected IBE more fully to the virtues of explanatory understanding.
