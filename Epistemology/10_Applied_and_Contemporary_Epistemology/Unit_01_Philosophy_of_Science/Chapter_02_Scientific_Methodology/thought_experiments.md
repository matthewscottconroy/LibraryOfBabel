## The Raven Paradox

**The scenario**: All ravens are black. This hypothesis is logically equivalent to "All non-black things are non-ravens." By the equivalence condition, whatever confirms the second hypothesis confirms the first. Observing a green apple (a non-black non-raven) confirms "All non-black things are non-ravens" — and therefore, apparently, it also confirms "All ravens are black." Surely the green apple in your kitchen tells you nothing about ravens?

**The philosophical point**: Hempel's raven paradox exposes a tension between the equivalence condition (logically equivalent statements are confirmed by the same evidence) and the intuition that observing a green apple does not support the hypothesis about ravens. Bayesians have argued that in fact the apple does give very weak confirmation to the ravens hypothesis — just so weak that it seems like zero. The paradox motivates careful attention to confirmation theory: what counts as evidence for a hypothesis, and how should confirmation be measured? It also illustrates how seemingly innocent logical equivalences can generate counterintuitive epistemological results.

## Falsificationism and Freudian Theory

**The scenario**: A patient exhibits a pattern of behavior that a Freudian analyst explains as expression of unconscious conflict. Another patient exhibits the opposite behavior; the analyst explains this too as expression of unconscious conflict, but of a different type. When pressed, the analyst says that both behaviors confirm Freudian theory, because the theory is rich enough to accommodate any behavioral pattern. Popper contrasts this with a physicist who specifies in advance exactly what observations would refute their theory.

**The philosophical point**: Popper used psychoanalysis (and Adlerian psychology) as examples of unfalsifiable and therefore unscientific theories — not because they are false but because they cannot be refuted by any observation. A theory that can accommodate any outcome of experience is not making risky predictions and therefore not subject to the control of experience that distinguishes science. The example motivates the demarcation criterion of falsifiability, while also raising the question of whether some scientific theories (such as the theory of evolution or quantum mechanics in certain interpretations) are themselves difficult to falsify.

## The Duhem-Quine Problem: Adapting the Auxiliary

**The scenario**: In 1846, Urbain Le Verrier noted anomalies in the orbit of Uranus that were inconsistent with Newtonian mechanics plus the known planets. Rather than abandoning Newtonian mechanics, astronomers adjusted the auxiliary hypotheses — specifically, they posited a new planet, Neptune, whose gravitational influence would account for the anomaly. This was successful. But when a similar anomaly appeared in Mercury's orbit, Vulcan (a hypothetical intra-Mercurial planet) was posited and never found; Einstein's general relativity eventually explained Mercury's precession instead.

**The philosophical point**: The Duhem-Quine thesis predicts that when theory meets anomalous evidence, scientists have a choice of where to revise. The Neptune case shows this can be epistemically productive; the Vulcan case shows it can lead to failed auxiliary hypotheses. The parallel cases illustrate why neither falsificationism (revise the central hypothesis) nor a blanket policy of protecting the core is epistemically adequate. Lakatos's research program methodology attempts to give principled guidance about when protective belt adjustments are scientifically progressive and when they are degenerating.

## Kuhn's Gestalt Switch

**The scenario**: Kuhn describes how scientific training within a paradigm shapes what scientists see when they look at the world. Before the chemical revolution, "dephlogisticated air" and oxygen were literally the same thing observed; after Lavoisier, chemists saw a different world. A pre-revolutionary chemist and a post-revolutionary chemist perform the same experiment and seemingly observe different things. This is not merely a difference in interpretation — Kuhn suggests it is a difference in what is perceived.

**The philosophical point**: Kuhn's claim about theory-ladenness of observation and the gestalt-like character of paradigm shifts raises the question of whether scientific revolutions involve genuine communication breakdown between adherents of different paradigms ("incommensurability"), or whether scientists can ultimately understand each other across paradigms through translation and reconstruction. The scenario challenges the standard picture of evidence as neutral arbiter between theories, suggesting that observation itself is shaped by the theoretical framework that gives it meaning.

## Inference to the Best Explanation: The Doctor's Diagnosis

**The scenario**: A physician examines a patient with fever, swollen lymph nodes, fatigue, and a distinctive rash. She does not observe bacteria or viruses directly. She reasons: if the patient has a specific bacterial infection, then all of these symptoms would be expected. No other diagnosis she can think of would explain all the symptoms as elegantly. She concludes that bacterial infection is the diagnosis — not because she has eliminated all alternatives but because it is the best available explanation.

**The philosophical point**: The scenario illustrates inference to the best explanation as a model of ampliative reasoning that goes beyond what the evidence strictly entails. The physician infers to an unobservable cause from observable effects by appealing to explanatory power. Lipton's analysis asks what makes one explanation "better" than another: the answer involves virtues like simplicity, consilience, and explanatory depth. The case raises the question of whether explanatory virtues are epistemic guides to truth or merely cognitive values — and whether the practice of IBE can be given Bayesian foundations.
