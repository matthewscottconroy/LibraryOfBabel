# Moral Knowledge and Moral Epistemology

Can we know moral facts? And if so, how? Moral epistemology addresses these questions, connecting metaethics (the nature of moral facts and discourse) to general epistemological issues about justification, evidence, and the nature of knowledge. The questions are pressing: moral knowledge underwrites practical ethics, shapes policy, and informs our evaluations of persons and actions.

## The Metaethical Background

Moral epistemology depends partly on metaethics. The main metaethical positions generate different epistemological prospects:

**Moral realism**: There are mind-independent moral facts. Moral epistemology asks: how do we access them? What is our epistemic relationship to the moral domain?

**Expressivism/Non-cognitivism**: Moral statements are not truth-apt; they express attitudes, emotions, or commitments rather than describe facts. If this is right, there are no moral facts to know, and "moral knowledge" is a category mistake.

**Constructivism** (Rawls, Korsgaard): Moral facts are constructed through rational procedures (what would rational agents agree to under ideal conditions?). Moral knowledge is knowledge of these constructions.

**Error theory** (Mackie): Moral statements purport to describe moral facts, but there are no moral facts — all moral statements are false. There is no moral knowledge, only false moral belief.

Most contemporary work in moral epistemology proceeds under realist or constructivist assumptions, examining how moral knowledge is possible if it is.

## The Problem of Moral Perception

Can we perceive moral facts? Moral intuitionists (Moore, Ross) hold that we have a faculty of moral intuition — a non-inferential capacity to grasp moral truths, analogous to perceptual faculty. "Cruelty is wrong" is known non-inferentially, by direct moral insight.

The challenge: what is the faculty of moral intuition, and how does it work? Unlike perceptual knowledge, where we can explain the causal chain from object to perceiver (light reflects from object, hits retina, etc.), no analogous causal story is available for moral intuition. This makes moral intuitionism difficult to square with naturalistic epistemology.

David Enoch's "robust realism" defends moral intuition but concedes that its epistemic status is less clear than perceptual knowledge. He argues that we should accept moral intuitions as defeasible evidence about moral reality — analogous to how we accept perceptual seemings as defeasible evidence about physical reality.

## Reflective Equilibrium

John Rawls's method of *reflective equilibrium* is the most influential approach to moral justification in contemporary ethics. The method proceeds by:

1. Starting with considered moral judgments (moral intuitions about particular cases that survive reflection)
2. Formulating moral principles that organize and explain these judgments
3. Testing the principles against further cases
4. Revising both the principles and the judgments when they conflict, seeking coherence

Wide reflective equilibrium extends this to include background theories — theories of the person, of society, of practical reason — that further constrain both principles and judgments.

The method does not derive moral knowledge from non-moral premises (no naturalistic reduction); it seeks internal coherence among moral beliefs, principles, and theories. This is a form of coherentism applied to moral epistemology.

## The Evolutionary Debunking Argument

A challenging argument against moral knowledge comes from evolutionary biology. Sharon Street's "A Darwinian Dilemma for Realist Theories of Value" argues:

Our moral evaluative attitudes are the product of evolutionary processes. Evolution selected for evaluative attitudes that enhanced reproductive fitness, not for attitudes that tracked mind-independent moral facts (there's no reason to expect evolutionary processes to be sensitive to moral facts).

If our moral intuitions were shaped by evolution rather than moral facts, then the agreement between our intuitions and moral facts would be a coincidence. Moral realism implies that our moral faculty tracks moral facts — but evolution provides no mechanism for this tracking.

The debunking argument threatens the epistemic credentials of moral intuitions. Moral realists have responded with various strategies: questioning whether evolution fully explains moral psychology, arguing that fitness-enhancing moral beliefs might systematically track moral facts, or accepting that some moral beliefs are debunked while others retain their evidential value.

## Disagreement and Moral Knowledge

The persistence of moral disagreement — across cultures, history, and within contemporary societies — challenges moral realism. If moral facts exist and we have access to them, why do we disagree so persistently?

Responses: moral disagreement is overstated (there is substantial cross-cultural convergence on basic moral norms); moral disagreement is explained by epistemic factors (people disagree because of different information, different evaluative frameworks, cognitive biases) rather than the absence of moral facts; moral disagreement is analogous to theoretical disagreement in science, which doesn't show there are no scientific facts.
