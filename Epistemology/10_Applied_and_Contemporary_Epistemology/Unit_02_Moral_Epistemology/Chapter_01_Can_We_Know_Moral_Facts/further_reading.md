## Primary Sources

- John Rawls, *A Theory of Justice*, Harvard University Press, 1971. Introduces reflective equilibrium as the method of moral epistemology: the process of bringing our considered judgments and moral principles into coherent alignment through mutual adjustment.
- G. E. Moore, *Principia Ethica*, Cambridge University Press, 1903. Defends moral realism grounded in non-natural moral properties, argues that "good" is a simple unanalyzable property, and introduces the "naturalistic fallacy" — the alleged error of defining moral terms by natural properties.
- Sharon Street, "A Darwinian Dilemma for Realist Theories of Value," *Philosophical Studies* 127(1), 2006. The canonical evolutionary debunking argument against moral realism: our moral intuitions were shaped by natural selection for reproductive fitness, not truth-tracking, making moral realism explanatorily unstable.
- Simon Blackburn, *Essays in Quasi-Realism*, Oxford University Press, 1993. Defends a sophisticated expressivism (quasi-realism) that earns the right to moral discourse that sounds realistic without positing moral facts as part of the furniture of the world.
- Allan Gibbard, *Wise Choices, Apt Feelings*, Harvard University Press, 1990. Develops a normative expressivism grounded in the analysis of "making sense" — our moral language expresses endorsements of norms for action and feeling.
- Russ Shafer-Landau, *Moral Realism: A Defence*, Oxford University Press, 2003. A sustained systematic defense of non-naturalist moral realism, arguing that moral facts are necessary truths not reducible to natural facts.
- Hilary Putnam, *The Collapse of the Fact/Value Dichotomy and Other Essays*, Harvard University Press, 2002. Argues against the sharp separation of facts and values that underlies much anti-realist moral epistemology, drawing on the entanglement of descriptive and evaluative concepts.

## Secondary Sources

- Michael Huemer, *Ethical Intuitionism*, Palgrave Macmillan, 2005. Defends moral intuitionism — the view that we have direct, non-inferential moral knowledge through intuition — against anti-realist and debunking challenges.
- Justin Clarke-Doane, *Morality and Mathematics*, Oxford University Press, 2020. Compares moral realism and mathematical realism, examining evolutionary debunking arguments in both domains and arguing for a structural parallel that challenges simple anti-realist uses of evolution.
- Derek Parfit, *On What Matters*, Oxford University Press, 2011. A monumental work in normative ethics and metaethics that includes extended discussion of moral epistemology, moral convergence, and the objectivity of ethical reasoning.
- David Enoch, *Taking Morality Seriously: A Defense of Robust Realism*, Oxford University Press, 2011. Defends robust moral realism — including commitment to irreducible moral facts — against debunking arguments, naturalist reduction, and anti-realist alternatives.
- R. M. Hare, *The Language of Morals*, Oxford University Press, 1952. The classic prescriptivist analysis of moral language, providing the linguistic grounding for non-cognitivist positions in moral epistemology.
- Richard Brandt, *A Theory of the Good and the Right*, Oxford University Press, 1979. Develops an ideal observer theory of moral knowledge, arguing that our moral beliefs can be evaluated against the standard of what a fully informed and rational agent would believe.
- T. M. Scanlon, *What We Owe to Each Other*, Harvard University Press, 1998. While primarily a normative theory, develops an account of moral reasoning and justification that has important implications for moral epistemology.
