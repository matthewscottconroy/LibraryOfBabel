## John Rawls (1921–2002)

Rawls introduced reflective equilibrium as the canonical method of moral epistemology: a process of mutual adjustment between our considered moral judgments (intuitions about particular cases we are relatively confident about) and moral principles, in which neither is treated as fixed but both are revised toward coherence. Wide reflective equilibrium extends this to background moral theories and comparisons between competing frameworks. Rawls's method is explicitly anti-foundationalist — it does not seek an Archimedean point outside moral experience — and has become the standard methodological framework in analytic moral philosophy.

## Sharon Street (1975–)

Street's evolutionary debunking argument poses one of the most influential contemporary challenges to moral realism. She argues that our basic evaluative attitudes were shaped by natural selection to promote reproduction, not to track mind-independent moral facts. This creates a dilemma for the realist: either the evolutionary pressures happened to track moral facts (requiring a mysterious pre-established harmony between natural selection and moral truth), or they did not (and our moral intuitions are unreliable guides to moral facts). Street concludes that moral realism is explanatorily untenable and develops a constructivist alternative she calls "Humean constructivism."

## Simon Blackburn (1944–)

Blackburn developed quasi-realism as a way to be an expressivist about moral language — holding that moral statements express attitudes rather than describe facts — while defending the right to use realistic-sounding moral vocabulary. His key insight is that expressivists need not deny that there are truths of morality; they must only explain those truths without positing mind-independent moral facts. Blackburn's "earning the right" to moral truth, objectivity, and knowledge through a series of quasi-realist moves has been influential in both metaethics and epistemology, and his work connects expressivism to the broader project of naturalizing the normative.

## Russ Shafer-Landau (1963–)

Shafer-Landau has been among the most systematic and analytically rigorous defenders of non-naturalist moral realism. His *Moral Realism: A Defence* argues that moral facts are necessary truths about what matters, comparable to mathematical truths, and that they are not reducible to natural facts without loss. He has developed detailed responses to debunking arguments, supervenience challenges, and companions-in-guilt arguments, and his work has helped re-establish moral realism as a philosophically respectable position after the dominance of non-cognitivist alternatives in the mid-twentieth century.

## G. E. Moore (1873–1958)

Moore's *Principia Ethica* shaped twentieth-century moral epistemology in two ways. His "open question argument" purported to show that "good" cannot be defined in natural terms — the question "Is what is natural also good?" always remains open, unlike "Is a bachelor unmarried?" which is trivially closed. This argument established the non-naturalist tradition in moral realism and the charge of the "naturalistic fallacy." Moore also defended moral intuitionism — the view that basic moral truths are self-evident and known directly — though his specific list of intrinsic goods (pleasure, friendship, beauty) has been criticized.

## David Enoch (1971–)

Enoch's *Taking Morality Seriously* provides one of the most philosophically uncompromising defenses of robust moral realism — the view that there are mind-independent moral facts, that our moral discourse purports to be about them, and that this is not a mistake. He argues that the best explanation of the "indispensability" of moral talk and reasoning requires positing moral facts, and that evolutionary debunking arguments fail because they misidentify what needs explaining. Enoch's "companions in guilt" strategy argues that if debunking arguments succeed against moral realism, analogous arguments succeed against other forms of realism we are strongly motivated to preserve.
