## Reflective Equilibrium: The Trolley Problem and Principles

**The scenario**: You have strong intuitions about trolley cases: it seems wrong to push a large man off a bridge to stop a trolley from killing five people, but right to redirect the trolley with a lever. You also hold a utilitarian principle: always act to maximize welfare. The intuitions conflict with the principle: pushing the large man would maximize welfare. Rawls's method of reflective equilibrium requires you to decide whether to revise the principle in light of the intuition, or revise the intuition in light of the principle — or find a more nuanced principle that accommodates both.

**The philosophical point**: Reflective equilibrium treats neither intuitions nor principles as epistemically basic. Both are revisable, and the process of bringing them into coherence is the primary method of moral inquiry. The trolley case shows that this process is not mechanical: there is no algorithm for determining which item to revise when intuitions and principles conflict. Critics argue that reflective equilibrium is too conservative — it preserves culturally local intuitions rather than tracking moral truth — and that it cannot adjudicate between radically different moral frameworks that are each internally coherent.

## Street's Darwinian Dilemma

**The scenario**: Imagine a group of our evolutionary ancestors who possessed a gene variant causing them to feel powerful moral repugnance at harming kin but indifference to strangers. This variant spread because it promoted kin-cooperative behavior, enhancing reproductive fitness. Their descendants (us) now experience strong moral intuitions about the wrongness of harming family members. Question: do these intuitions track moral facts, or merely what was reproductively adaptive?

**The philosophical point**: Street's debunking argument runs as follows: if our evaluative attitudes were shaped by natural selection, and natural selection is indifferent to moral truth, then we have no reason to think our moral intuitions reliably track moral facts. The moral realist must either show that evolutionary pressures happened to track moral facts (a seemingly miraculous coincidence) or accept that the causal story explains away our intuitions rather than validating them. Defenders of moral realism have responded by arguing that some basic moral facts (like "pain is bad") are knowable independently of evolution, or that evolutionary debunking proves too much — applying to all normative domains, not just ethics.

## The Moral Perception Case

**The scenario**: A social worker enters a client's home and immediately "sees" that the child is at risk — she perceives neglect, fear, the pattern of dynamics in the room. She has not explicitly reasoned to this conclusion; it presents itself directly to her trained attention. She later articulates reasons, but the perception preceded the reasoning. Is this moral perception a genuine form of epistemic access to moral facts, or a rapid inference from natural facts?

**The philosophical point**: The concept of moral perception — that moral facts can be directly apprehended in experience by a trained observer — is defended by moral intuitionists and challenged by expressivists. The scenario motivates the question of whether moral knowledge, like perceptual knowledge, can be non-inferential. The challenge is to explain what it is that is perceived: if the social worker perceives that "this child is at risk," is there a moral fact she is detecting, or is she detecting only natural facts (the child's behavior, the parent's tone) that she then evaluates through attitudes formed by training and experience?

## Moral Disagreement Across Cultures

**The scenario**: Anthropological records document societies with moral codes that permit practices — female genital cutting, honor killings, infanticide of twins — that contemporary Western moral philosophy condemns. Practitioners are not ignorant of consequences; they endorse the practices as genuinely right. If moral facts exist and are knowable, why is there such persistent cross-cultural disagreement? A moral realist must explain disagreement; an anti-realist argues it is exactly what we should expect if moral norms are expressions of attitudes shaped by local culture.

**The philosophical point**: Moral disagreement is one of the most discussed challenges for moral realism. Realists typically respond in one of two ways: argue that apparent moral disagreement often reflects disagreement about natural facts (not moral principles), or argue that disagreement about p does not show there is no fact of the matter about p (people disagree about mathematical and historical questions, too). Anti-realists argue that moral disagreement is systematically different from other disagreements — it is more persistent, more globally distributed, and more resistant to convergence through inquiry — making realism implausible.
