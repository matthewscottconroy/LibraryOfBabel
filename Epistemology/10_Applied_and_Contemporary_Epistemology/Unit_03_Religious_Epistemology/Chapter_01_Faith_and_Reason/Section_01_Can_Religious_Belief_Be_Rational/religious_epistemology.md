# Religious Epistemology: Faith, Reason, and the Rationality of Religious Belief

Religious epistemology asks whether religious beliefs can be rationally held, what kinds of evidence or justification are relevant to them, and whether there is any distinctively "religious" way of knowing. These questions connect the general epistemological framework — justification, evidence, rationality, knowledge — to the specific context of religious belief.

## The Evidentialist Challenge

Classical religious epistemology, associated with natural theology, attempts to provide rational evidence for religious beliefs: the cosmological argument, the teleological argument, the ontological argument, and others. These arguments attempt to show that theistic belief is rationally warranted on the basis of publicly available evidence.

The Enlightenment *evidentialist challenge* (Clifford's version: it is wrong always, everywhere, to believe anything on insufficient evidence) threatens religious belief. If religious beliefs cannot be supported by evidence meeting the evidentialist standard, they are epistemically impermissible.

W.K. Clifford's "The Ethics of Belief" (1877) is directed in part at religious belief: believing without evidence is not just intellectually sloppy but morally wrong. Religious belief, on this view, must meet the standards of rational evidence that any belief must meet.

## Reformed Epistemology: Basic Beliefs and Proper Function

Alvin Plantinga's *reformed epistemology* is the most developed challenge to the evidentialist requirement. Plantinga argues that belief in God can be *properly basic* — rationally held without inference from other beliefs.

**Foundationalism and proper basicality**: In classical foundationalism, properly basic beliefs are beliefs that are either self-evident, evident to the senses, or incorrigible. Plantinga argues this criterion is too narrow and self-defeating (it is not itself self-evident, evident to the senses, or incorrigible). A better account of proper basicality appeals to the conditions under which a belief is formed: a belief is properly basic if it is formed by a faculty that is *functioning properly* in an appropriate environment, according to a design plan oriented toward truth.

**Sensus Divinitatis**: Plantinga (following Calvin) proposes that humans have a *sensus divinitatis* — a faculty that, when functioning properly, produces belief in God in response to certain circumstances (the beauty of nature, experiences of guilt, a sense of being called). This faculty, if it exists and functions properly, would make theistic belief properly basic.

**Objection**: Critics argue that the sensus divinitatis is either not a genuine cognitive faculty (there is no evidence for such a faculty from cognitive science) or that its "proper function" is not aimed at truth (it may be adaptive, but adaptation doesn't imply truth-tracking).

## William Alston and Religious Experience

William Alston's *Perceiving God* (1991) defends the epistemic value of religious experience — experience that seems to be direct perception of God. Alston argues that:

1. There is a "mystical perceptual practice" — a doxastic practice of forming beliefs about God based on seemingly direct experience of God.
2. This practice is socially established, self-consistent, and produces beliefs that lead to successful "steering" in the religious life.
3. The grounds for accepting sense perception are analogous to the grounds for accepting mystical perception: both are doxastic practices with a track record of producing beliefs we act on successfully.

Alston doesn't claim to prove that mystical perception is reliable; he claims that we can't prove that sense perception is reliable either (without circularity). If we accept sense perception without such proof, fairness requires we also accept mystical perception.

## Faith, Evidence, and Pascal's Wager

Blaise Pascal's *Wager* approaches religious belief from a decision-theoretic rather than epistemic angle. If God exists and you believe, you gain eternal life; if God exists and you don't believe, you lose it. The expected value calculation supports belief, even if the probability of God's existence is very small.

The wager has been widely criticized: which God to believe in? (The "many gods" objection.) Can you choose to believe? (The voluntarism problem.) Is it morally acceptable to believe for self-interested reasons? (The integrity problem.) Does genuine faith accept this kind of pragmatic calculation?

## Religious Knowledge and Religious Diversity

The fact of religious diversity — the existence of many mutually incompatible religious traditions, each with sincere and sophisticated believers — creates an evidential problem for any religious tradition. If Hindus, Christians, Muslims, and Buddhists all claim religious knowledge based on apparently similar experiences and traditions, they cannot all be right. Religious diversity is a form of peer disagreement applied to religious belief.

John Hick's religious pluralism responds by arguing that all religious traditions are responses to the same ultimate reality, experienced through different cultural lenses. Other responses: one tradition is right and the others are mistaken (exclusive); religious truths are complementary (inclusive); religious experiences are not reliable guides to theological truth.
