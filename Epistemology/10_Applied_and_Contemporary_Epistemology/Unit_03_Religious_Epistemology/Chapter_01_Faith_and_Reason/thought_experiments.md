## Clifford's Ship-Owner

**The scenario**: A ship-owner has doubts about the seaworthiness of an emigrant ship. He fights down his doubts by reminding himself of the ship's long history of voyages and convincing himself that the company's inspectors are trustworthy. He sends the ship to sea. It sinks and all aboard are lost. But suppose instead that the ship makes it safely across the ocean. Clifford argues that the ship-owner acted wrongly in both cases — the ship happens to survive, but the wrongness of the belief lies in the process of its formation.

**The philosophical point**: Clifford's example is designed to show that epistemic irresponsibility is a moral wrong independent of outcomes. The ship-owner did not weigh the evidence; he allowed wishful thinking to suppress doubt. Clifford generalizes: forming beliefs through insufficient evidence is always wrong, because it corrupts the epistemic character of the agent and contributes to a climate of epistemic irresponsibility. The scenario motivates the evidentialist challenge to religious belief and raises the question of whether the ethics of belief is indeed a matter of epistemic virtue and process rather than merely of true/false outcomes.

## The Forced Choice

**The scenario**: James imagines two climbers, one of whom is trapped on a mountain ledge. The only escape requires making a jump the climber is not sure she can make. The evidence does not clearly support the proposition "I can make this jump" — she has no data on her performance in such situations. Yet she must decide: jump or stay. Choosing to stay and do nothing is itself a choice, with its own expected costs. James argues that in this forced situation, allowing confidence to guide the decision is permissible — even required.

**The philosophical point**: James uses cases of forced choice to challenge Clifford's blanket prohibition on belief without sufficient evidence. When action is unavoidable, when the stakes are high, and when evidence is genuinely insufficient, withholding judgment is itself a choice with costs. James argues the same logic applies to religious choices: if the question of God's existence is live for you, forced (you must conduct your life somehow), and momentous, then the Will to Believe permits faith. Critics respond that the analogy is imperfect — the climber has practical evidence about her athletic ability, while the religious believer faces a much harder epistemic situation.

## Plantinga's Sensus Divinitatis

**The scenario**: Calvin describes the *sensus divinitatis* — a faculty by which human beings, when confronted with the majesty of nature, with great personal danger, or with the overwhelming of conscience, spontaneously form beliefs about God. Plantinga develops this into an epistemological claim: just as perceptual beliefs are formed through a faculty calibrated by proper function to track features of the environment, theistic belief can be formed through a faculty (the *sensus divinitatis*) calibrated by its designer to produce warranted beliefs about God. If God exists and designed this faculty, beliefs formed through it are warranted.

**The philosophical point**: Plantinga's scenario inverts the traditional epistemological challenge: the question of whether theistic belief requires evidence dissolves if the belief is properly basic — directly produced by a properly functioning faculty in appropriate circumstances. But the argument is conditional: if theism is false, the sensus divinitatis is either malfunctioning or non-existent, and theistic beliefs are unwarranted. Critics argue this makes the epistemological argument trivially dependent on the theological question, failing to provide independent epistemic support. The scenario focuses the debate on whether the "proper function" account can ground religious knowledge without begging the question.

## The Problem of Religious Diversity

**The scenario**: Three students have each been raised in different religious traditions — one Buddhist, one Christian, one Muslim. Each has a genuine, deeply felt experience of the divine/transcendent that their tradition helps them interpret. Each claims this experience as the basis of their religious knowledge. Their traditions make incompatible claims about the nature of the divine. By Alston's own lights, each is engaged in a "doxastic practice" comparable to perception. Can all three be right? If not, how are they to adjudicate their incompatible experiential claims?

**The philosophical point**: The problem of religious diversity is one of the most pressing for Reformed epistemology and experience-based religious epistemology. If the rationality of religious belief is grounded in the reliability and social establishment of a doxastic practice, then the multiplicity of established religious practices — each internally consistent but mutually inconsistent — seems to undermine the claim to epistemic privilege for any one of them. Plantinga responds that the practices differ in their internal tracks of success; Hick responds with pluralism; others argue that the problem motivates skepticism rather than tolerance.

## Pascal's Wager: The Many Gods Objection

**The scenario**: You accept Pascal's Wager and decide to wager on God's existence. But which God? The same expected-value reasoning that recommends wagering on the Christian God also recommends wagering on the Islamic God, the Hindu gods, and any deity with infinite reward and some positive probability of existence. Many of these deities have incompatible requirements — if the Christian God rewards Christian worship but punishes Islamic worship, and the Islamic God rewards Islamic worship but punishes Christian worship, then wagering on one may cost you the expected benefit from others.

**The philosophical point**: The Many Gods Objection shows that Pascal's Wager cannot single out a specific religious commitment: it recommends wagering on every religion simultaneously, which may be logically impossible if the religions are mutually exclusive. More broadly, the objection shows that decision-theoretic reasoning in conditions of radical uncertainty about the available options is extremely sensitive to how those options are specified. The scenario motivates careful attention to the relationship between decision theory and religious epistemology and raises the question of whether practical reasoning can ever serve as an adequate substitute for evidence in choosing foundational beliefs.
