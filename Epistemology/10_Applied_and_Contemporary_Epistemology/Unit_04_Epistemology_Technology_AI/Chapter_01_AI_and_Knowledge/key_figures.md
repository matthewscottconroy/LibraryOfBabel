## Alan Turing (1912–1954)

Turing's "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" introduced the imitation game (later called the Turing Test) as a pragmatic criterion for machine intelligence, and posed the question "Can machines think?" in a form that has defined debates in philosophy of mind and AI epistemology ever since. By grounding machine intelligence in behavioral indistinguishability from human intelligence, Turing implicitly suggested a functionalist approach to epistemic concepts: if a system behaves as if it knows, reasons, and understands, the question of what is "really" going on inside may be empirically idle. The Turing Test has been criticized on numerous grounds but remains the starting point for all subsequent thinking about machine cognition.

## John Searle (1932–)

Searle's Chinese Room argument challenged the functionalist view of machine intelligence at its foundations. He imagined a person who does not understand Chinese but manipulates Chinese symbols according to rules, passing the Turing Test for Chinese understanding. Searle argued that the person — and by extension any computational system — has syntax but no semantics: they manipulate symbols without grasping their meaning. If understanding requires more than syntactic processing, then no current AI system genuinely knows or understands anything, regardless of its performance. The argument launched a massive philosophical debate about intentionality, consciousness, and the limits of computation.

## Luciano Floridi (1964–)

Floridi has developed the most systematic philosophical framework for thinking about the epistemic dimensions of AI. His "philosophy of information" treats information — rather than justified true belief — as the fundamental concept in epistemology, and he has argued that AI systems can be epistemic agents in a weaker but real sense: they process, produce, and communicate information in ways that have genuine epistemic consequences for the agents who interact with them. His work on the "right to explanation" — that people affected by AI decisions should be able to demand an explanation — has been influential in AI ethics and policy.

## Safiya Umoja Noble (1973–)

Noble's *Algorithms of Oppression* provided a detailed empirical and philosophical analysis of how search engine algorithms encode, amplify, and propagate racial and gender biases. Her work transformed the epistemology of AI from an abstract question about machine cognition to a concrete question about epistemic justice: when AI systems mediate access to information, they are making epistemic choices with distributive consequences. Noble's scholarship connects the philosophy of AI to feminist epistemology, critical race theory, and the empirical sociology of technology, showing that the black box problem is not merely a technical issue but a political one.

## Kate Crawford (1976–)

Crawford's *Atlas of AI* examined the material and political infrastructure of AI systems — the mines, the data centers, the labor practices, the legal frameworks — as the context in which AI epistemology must be understood. Her work argues that AI systems are not neutral cognitive tools but embedded in and shaped by specific power relations that affect what they "know," what they classify, and whose knowledge counts. Crawford's interdisciplinary approach has been influential in moving AI epistemology beyond questions about individual systems to questions about the social and political conditions of AI-mediated knowledge production.

## Shannon Vallor (1969–)

Vallor's *Technology and the Virtues* developed a virtue-theoretic approach to the epistemological dimensions of AI. She argued that the use of AI systems poses distinctive risks to epistemic virtues — curiosity, open-mindedness, intellectual humility — that are essential for good knowing. Epistemic over-reliance on AI systems can atrophy the habits of mind that enable genuine understanding, while appropriate use can enhance them. Vallor's framework provides resources for thinking about epistemic autonomy in a world of increasingly capable AI systems, and for evaluating AI design choices by their consequences for human epistemic character.
