## The Chinese Room

**The scenario**: Searle imagines himself locked in a room, receiving strings of Chinese symbols through a slot. He has a large book of rules specifying how to respond to these symbols with other symbols. He follows the rules meticulously and passes the symbols back through the slot. To people outside the room, the responses look like those of a fluent Chinese speaker. Searle, inside, understands nothing; he merely follows syntactic rules. He argues: if this is a valid model of how any computer processes language, then no computer understands language in the sense of grasping meaning.

**The philosophical point**: The Chinese Room is the most influential challenge to the functionalist view that genuine understanding can be attributed to any system that performs the right input-output transformations. Searle argues that intentionality — the "aboutness" of mental states — requires more than syntactic computation: it requires the kind of causal embedding in the world that biological brains have. The philosophical point bears directly on whether large language models or other AI systems can genuinely know or understand, or whether they are elaborate symbol-manipulation systems that mimic understanding without possessing it.

## The Black Box Medical Diagnosis

**The scenario**: A hospital adopts a deep learning system trained on millions of patient records that predicts diagnoses with very high accuracy — significantly outperforming experienced physicians on the test set. A physician asks the system why it made a particular diagnosis; it cannot explain its reasoning. The physician cannot identify which features of the patient data drove the prediction. The diagnosis turns out to be correct. Does the system know the patient has the condition? Does the physician who follows the recommendation come to know it?

**The philosophical point**: The scenario illustrates the black box problem for AI epistemic agency. Even if the system produces reliably true outputs, the opacity of its reasoning raises questions about whether it satisfies the justification requirement for knowledge. A reliabilist might say the system "knows" in a functional sense if its process is reliably truth-tracking; an internalist would deny this, since neither the system nor the physician can access the reasons for the belief. The case also raises questions about epistemic responsibility: when humans defer to opaque AI systems, who is epistemically responsible for the resulting beliefs?

## The AI-Generated Misinformation

**The scenario**: An AI language model, trained on a vast corpus of text, produces a plausible-sounding description of a historical event that never occurred, complete with fabricated names, dates, and sources. A student uses this description in a research paper. The description is confident in tone and passes a superficial plausibility check. The student forms a false belief on the basis of the AI's output and cites it as a source.

**The philosophical point**: The scenario raises the question of what testimonial norms should govern AI outputs. When a human speaker confidently asserts something, we typically hold them to a norm of sincerity — they assert only what they believe. AI language models assert without believing: they produce outputs that maximize predicted textual plausibility, not truth. This means the standard epistemology of testimony — which grants initial credibility to sincere assertion — does not straightforwardly apply. The scenario motivates the development of distinctively new epistemic norms for AI-mediated information, and raises questions about how AI-generated misinformation differs epistemologically from human lying and human error.

## Epistemic Autonomy Under AI Assistance

**The scenario**: A researcher routinely uses AI tools to identify relevant papers, draft initial summaries of complex topics, and check her reasoning. Over years, she becomes highly productive and her work is well-regarded. But when the AI tools are unavailable for a month, she finds it very difficult to conduct research independently: she has not developed the skills of manual literature search, she struggles to construct initial framings without AI assistance, and she is uncertain about the reliability of her own reasoning without external checking.

**The philosophical point**: The scenario raises questions about what Vallor calls "epistemic autonomy" — the capacity to engage in inquiry and form beliefs through the exercise of one's own cognitive faculties. The researcher's productivity was high, but her epistemic autonomy has been eroded: she can produce good research outputs only in a particular technological environment. This raises the question of whether epistemic virtue requires not just reliable processes but the internalization of reliable capacities that remain available across contexts. The case mirrors classical debates about extended cognition — whether cognitive tools outside the brain can genuinely extend knowledge — while adding distinctive concerns about dependency and atrophy.

## The Justified AI

**The scenario**: An AI system trained to do mathematical proof-checking processes a complex proof and outputs: "This proof is correct." The proof is, in fact, correct. The system's process is reliable and its output is a function of the logical structure of the proof — not of any feature irrelevant to correctness. A philosopher asks: does the system know the proof is correct? It has a true output, produced by a reliable process. But it has no understanding of mathematics, no ability to explain why the proof is correct, and no awareness that it has produced an output.

**The philosophical point**: The scenario tests which account of knowledge is most plausible for AI systems. A simple reliabilist account suggests the system knows, since its process reliably produces true outputs. A more demanding account requiring understanding — the ability to explain, to see why the conclusion follows, to recognize related proofs — suggests it does not know but merely reliably registers. The case motivates the question of whether understanding is a component of knowledge or a separate epistemic achievement, and whether AI systems that reliably produce truths without understanding deserve to be credited with knowledge.
