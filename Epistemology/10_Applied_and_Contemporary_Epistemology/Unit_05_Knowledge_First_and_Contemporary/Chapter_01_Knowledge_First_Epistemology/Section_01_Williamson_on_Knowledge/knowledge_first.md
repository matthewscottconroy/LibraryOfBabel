# Knowledge-First Epistemology

Knowledge-first epistemology, developed most fully by Timothy Williamson in *Knowledge and Its Limits* (2000), inverts the traditional order of epistemological analysis. Instead of analyzing knowledge in terms of more basic concepts (justified true belief, or any refinement thereof), knowledge-first epistemology treats knowledge as the primitive, unanalyzable starting point from which other epistemic concepts — belief, evidence, justification — are to be understood.

## The Traditional Picture

Traditional epistemology takes belief as the basic psychological attitude and knowledge as a complex attitude analyzed in terms of belief plus additional conditions (truth, justification, and whatever is needed to solve Gettier problems). Belief is ontologically and conceptually prior to knowledge: you know only if you first believe, and knowledge is belief that satisfies further conditions.

This order of analysis reflects the assumption that we understand belief independently of knowledge, and can build up an account of knowledge from this simpler foundation.

## Williamson's Reversal

Williamson argues this order of analysis is mistaken. The analysis of knowledge in terms of justified true belief (or any such analysis) has failed: Gettier cases have resisted decades of attempted solutions, and no principled account of what converts justified true belief into knowledge has succeeded.

Williamson's alternative: abandon the project of analyzing knowledge in terms of more basic elements. Knowledge is *sui generis* — a distinct mental state that cannot be analyzed in terms of belief plus conditions.

The alternative analysis goes in the *other* direction. Take knowledge as fundamental and analyze other epistemic concepts in terms of it:

**Evidence**: E is part of S's evidence iff S knows that E. Evidence just is knowledge; the two are co-extensive.

**Justification**: S is justified in believing P iff S knows P (strong version) or knows enough to make believing P reasonable (weak version).

**The norm of assertion**: The knowledge norm of assertion states that you should assert P only if you know P. Assertion is constitutively governed by knowledge, not by justification or truth alone.

## The Knowledge Norm of Assertion

The knowledge norm of assertion is one of the most discussed aspects of Williamson's program. The norm: assert P only if you know P. Evidence for this norm:

- If someone asserts P and it turns out they didn't know P (they only believed it, or were guessing), they are criticizable — even if P is true. "How did you know that?" and "I didn't know it, I just guessed" is a legitimate form of criticism-and-retraction.
- The knowledge norm explains the infelicity of *Moorean paradoxes*: "It's raining, but I don't know it's raining" is infelicitous even though it might be true. Asserting P is presenting yourself as knowing P; asserting "I don't know P" in the same breath is self-contradictory.

Alternative norms (truth norm, belief norm, justified-belief norm) face counterexamples that the knowledge norm handles naturally.

## Safe Belief and Anti-Luck

Williamson's account of knowledge connects to an anti-luck condition. Knowledge requires *safe* belief: S knows P only if S could not easily have believed P falsely — in nearby possible worlds where S uses the same method, S does not believe P falsely.

Safety is weaker than sensitivity: sensitivity requires that if P were false, S wouldn't believe P. Safety requires that in close possible worlds, if S believes P via the same method, P is true. This makes safety more permissive while still excluding cases of epistemic luck.

## Knowledge and Action

A significant implication of knowledge-first epistemology concerns practical reason. Williamson and others (Hawthorne, Stanley) have argued for a *knowledge norm of action*: it is rational to act on the basis of P only if you know P. When you don't know P, acting on P involves a kind of epistemic irresponsibility, even if P is probably true.

This connects to the lottery problem: you are 99.9% confident you won't win the lottery, but you don't know it. The knowledge norm implies you shouldn't act on the assumption that you won't win (you shouldn't spend the money on something that would be ruined if you did win), even though your credence is very high.

The knowledge norm of action has been criticized as too demanding: in many practical situations, we have no choice but to act on probabilities. But it captures something important about the difference between acting on knowledge and acting on high-probability belief.

## Legacy and Influence

Knowledge-first epistemology has transformed the field. It has:
- Redirected debate from the analysis of knowledge to the role of knowledge in action, testimony, and inquiry
- Highlighted the distinctive normative significance of knowledge (versus mere true belief)
- Connected epistemology to philosophy of language through the knowledge norm of assertion
- Revived interest in the relationship between knowledge and practical rationality

The knowledge-first program is now a major research tradition within analytic epistemology, inspiring significant work on assertion, evidence, higher-order knowledge, and the epistemology of action.
