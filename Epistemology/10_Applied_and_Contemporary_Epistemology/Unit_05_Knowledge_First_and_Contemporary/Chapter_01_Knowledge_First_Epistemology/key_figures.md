## Timothy Williamson (1955–)

Williamson's *Knowledge and Its Limits* (2000) is the most important work in epistemology of the past three decades. He proposed reversing the standard order of analysis: rather than analyzing knowledge in terms of justification, truth, and belief, we should take knowledge as primitive and use it to analyze other epistemic concepts. His E=K thesis identifies evidence with knowledge; his knowledge norm of assertion holds that one should assert only what one knows; his concept of safe belief captures the anti-luck intuition. The anti-luminosity argument — that no condition is such that if one is in it, one always knows one is — challenges the KK thesis and internalist conceptions of the mind. Williamson's framework has transformed professional epistemology and continues to generate an enormous literature.

## John Hawthorne (1966–)

Hawthorne developed knowledge-first epistemology in the specific context of the lottery problem in *Knowledge and Lotteries*. He argued that we cannot know lottery propositions (that our ticket will lose) and drew out the implications for contextualism — the view that "knows" is context-sensitive in a way that explains our intuitions — ultimately rejecting contextualism in favor of sensitive invariantism. His *Knowledge and Action* (with Jason Stanley) developed the knowledge norm of practical reasoning, arguing that the role knowledge plays in licensing action is irreducible to any weaker epistemic state. Hawthorne has also contributed importantly to the philosophy of language and formal epistemology.

## Jennifer Nagel (1968–)

Nagel's work occupies an important position at the intersection of knowledge-first epistemology and cognitive science. Her book *Knowledge: A Very Short Introduction* presented knowledge as a natural kind with distinctive functional properties, arguing that human knowledge attributions are tracked by evolved cognitive systems. She has also developed a "metacognitive" account of epistemic concepts, arguing that we attribute knowledge to others by tracking not just their information states but their epistemic situation, including stakes and error possibilities. Her psychological research on knowledge attributions provides empirical grounding for epistemological theories.

## Jeremy Fantl (1974–) and Matthew McGrath (1969–)

Fantl and McGrath developed a position closely related to Williamson's knowledge norms, arguing in *Knowledge in an Uncertain World* that whether you know a proposition depends partly on your practical situation — specifically on what stakes are riding on the question. Their "knowledge-action" principle holds that you know p only if it is appropriate for you to act as if p, and that what counts as appropriate action varies with stakes. This "interest-relative invariantism" shares knowledge-first epistemology's focus on the relationship between knowledge and action while departing from pure invariantism about knowledge attributions.

## John Hawthorne and Jason Stanley (collaborative work on knowledge-action)

The joint paper "Knowledge and Action" by Hawthorne and Stanley is among the most influential recent contributions to knowledge-first epistemology's practical dimension. They argue for the principle that a fact can be cited as a reason for action only if the agent knows it, and draw out implications for debates about contextualism, interest-relative invariantism, and practical reasoning. Stanley's broader work on knowledge-how (*Know How*, Oxford University Press, 2011) argues that knowing how to do something is a species of propositional knowledge, connecting the knowledge-first framework to the analysis of skill and practical competence.

## Duncan Pritchard (1971–)

Pritchard's anti-luck epistemology, developed in *Epistemic Luck* and subsequent work, engages closely with Williamson's knowledge-first framework while defending an independent approach. His "modal" account of the anti-luck condition uses safety (the modal notion Williamson also employs) but argues that knowledge requires a virtue-theoretic component as well — that knowledge must be the result of the agent's own cognitive competence, not merely a safe and true belief. Pritchard's distinction between veritic luck (that one's belief is true) and reflective luck (that one knows one has knowledge) connects to Williamson's anti-luminosity arguments.
