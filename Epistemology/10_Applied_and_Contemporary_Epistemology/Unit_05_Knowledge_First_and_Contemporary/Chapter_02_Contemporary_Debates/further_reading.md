## Primary Sources

- Timothy Williamson, *Knowledge and Its Limits*, Oxford University Press, 2000. Argues that the Gettier program of analyzing knowledge is a research program that should be abandoned, while developing knowledge-first epistemology as an alternative.
- David Christensen, "Higher-Order Evidence," *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* 81(1), 2010. The canonical treatment of higher-order evidence — evidence about the reliability of one's own reasoning — and the epistemic challenges it poses for both synchronic and diachronic rationality.
- Roger White, "Epistemic Permissivism," *Philosophical Perspectives* 19, 2005. Defends epistemic uniqueness — the view that a given body of evidence always uniquely determines a rational doxastic response — against permissivism, with implications for the analysis of peer disagreement.
- Paul Boghossian, *Fear of Knowledge: Against Relativism and Constructivism*, Oxford University Press, 2006. A sustained attack on epistemic relativism — the view that what counts as evidence or justified belief is relative to a conceptual scheme — arguing that relativism is self-refuting.
- Harvey Siegel, *Relativism Refuted: A Critique of Contemporary Epistemological Relativism*, Reidel, 1987. An early and still relevant critique of epistemic relativism, arguing that the relativist cannot consistently assert their position.
- Kenny Easwaran and Branden Fitelson, "An 'Evidentialist' Worry about Accuracy and Coherence," *Dialectica* 69(3), 2015. Examines the relationship between formal and traditional epistemology, asking whether accuracy-first formal frameworks and traditional justification-first frameworks are in tension.
- Maria Lasonen-Aarnio, "Unreasonable Knowledge," *Philosophical Perspectives* 24(1), 2010. Examines the relationship between higher-order evidence and akrasia, arguing that following higher-order evidence can lead to epistemically akratic states that are knowledge despite being unreasonable.

## Secondary Sources

- Jonathan Weinberg, Shaun Nichols, and Stephen Stich, "Normativity and Epistemic Intuitions," *Philosophical Topics* 29, 2001. The founding paper of experimental philosophy of epistemology, arguing that cross-cultural variation in epistemic intuitions undermines the evidential status of intuitions in analytic epistemology.
- Ernest Sosa, *Reflective Knowledge: Apt Belief and Reflective Knowledge*, Oxford University Press, 2009. Develops the two-level structure of animal knowledge and reflective knowledge, offering an alternative to both knowledge-first epistemology and traditional analysis.
- John Greco, *Achieving Knowledge*, Cambridge University Press, 2010. Defends virtue reliabilism as an account of knowledge that explains the Gettier problem without abandoning the analytic project.
- Peter D. Klein, "Infinitism Is the Solution to the Regress Problem," in *Contemporary Debates in Epistemology*, Blackwell, 2005. Defends infinitism — the view that justification involves an infinite regress of reasons — as a solution to the regress problem, challenging both foundationalism and coherentism.
- Richard Fumerton, *Metaepistemology and Skepticism*, Rowman and Littlefield, 1995. Defends internalist epistemology and examines the relationship between first-order epistemological questions and metaepistemological questions about the nature and scope of epistemic norms.
- Alvin Goldman and Matthew McGrath, *Epistemology: A Contemporary Introduction*, Oxford University Press, 2015. A comprehensive and pedagogically effective survey of contemporary epistemology that situates debates about analysis, formal epistemology, and knowledge-first approaches within the broader field.
- Michael Huemer, "Phenomenal Conservatism and the Internalist Intuition," *American Philosophical Quarterly* 43(2), 2006. Defends phenomenal conservatism — that what seems true has prima facie justificatory force — as a foundationalist account that bridges traditional and contemporary debates.
- Sanford Goldberg, ed., *The Brain in a Vat*, Cambridge University Press, 2016. A collection examining the contemporary relevance of Cartesian skepticism and its relationship to externalism, contextualism, and knowledge-first epistemology.
