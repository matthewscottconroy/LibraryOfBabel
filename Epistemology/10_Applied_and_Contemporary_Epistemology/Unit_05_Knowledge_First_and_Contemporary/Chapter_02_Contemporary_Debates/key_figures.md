## David Christensen (1952–)

Christensen's work on higher-order evidence has defined one of the most active contemporary debates in epistemology. Higher-order evidence is evidence about the reliability of your own reasoning — evidence that you might be mistaken not because the object-level evidence points the other way, but because you are impaired, biased, or simply fallible. Christensen argues that rational agents must respond to such evidence by reducing their confidence in the conclusion of the reasoning in question, even when the first-order evidence seems compelling. This creates the possibility of epistemic akrasia — believing p while believing one is not justified in believing p — and connects to the peer disagreement debate, since a peer's disagreement is a form of higher-order evidence.

## Paul Boghossian (1957–)

Boghossian's *Fear of Knowledge* provides the most rigorous contemporary critique of epistemic relativism and constructivism. He distinguishes between content relativism (the view that facts are relative to conceptual schemes) and normative relativism (the view that what counts as evidence is scheme-relative) and argues that both are philosophically incoherent. His critique of the sociology of scientific knowledge movement — which treated the content of scientific knowledge as socially constructed — connects metaepistemology to philosophy of science and to the political philosophy of knowledge. Boghossian defends the objectivity of epistemic norms without committing to Platonism about their source.

## Maria Lasonen-Aarnio (1978–)

Lasonen-Aarnio has developed a sophisticated response to the higher-order evidence problem, arguing that some epistemically akratic states — believing p while having higher-order evidence that one should not believe p — can constitute genuine knowledge. Her concept of "unreasonable knowledge" challenges the assumption that knowledge must always be the result of epistemically blameless reasoning: an agent can know p even if their knowledge is the result of "strong-willed" belief that resists higher-order defeat. This position has important implications for the relationship between knowledge, rationality, and epistemic responsibility, and connects to debates about epistemic akrasia and the normativity of knowledge.

## Roger White (1966–)

White's defense of the "uniqueness thesis" — that a given body of evidence always uniquely determines a rational credence — has been one of the most influential contributions to the debate about epistemic permissivism. If uniqueness is true, then epistemic peers cannot rationally respond differently to the same evidence, making conciliationism in peer disagreement more compelling. If permissivism is true, different rational responses to the same evidence are possible, which would allow steadfastness. White's work has also contributed to debates about priors, the nature of evidence, and the relationship between rationality and truth-tracking.

## Jonathan Weinberg (1970–)

Weinberg's experimental philosophy of epistemology challenged the methodology of analytic epistemology at its foundations. His studies showed that epistemic intuitions — about Gettier cases, about knowledge attributions — vary significantly across cultural groups, and argued that this undermines the evidential status of intuitions in philosophical theorizing. While many epistemologists have disputed his methodological conclusions, his work forced a productive reconsideration of the role of intuitions in epistemology and contributed to the development of "restrictionist" positions that carefully limit which intuitions are epistemologically evidential.

## Kenny Easwaran (1981–)

Easwaran has been among the most productive contributors to the interaction between formal and traditional epistemology. His work on accuracy-first epistemology, on the relationship between Bayesian and non-Bayesian norms of belief, and on the nature of evidence has helped bridge the divide between probabilistic and categorical approaches to belief and justification. His collaborative work on the relationship between formal epistemology's accuracy criteria and traditional epistemology's internalist justification concepts has revealed both complementarities and genuine tensions, contributing to a more integrated picture of the normative epistemology of belief.
