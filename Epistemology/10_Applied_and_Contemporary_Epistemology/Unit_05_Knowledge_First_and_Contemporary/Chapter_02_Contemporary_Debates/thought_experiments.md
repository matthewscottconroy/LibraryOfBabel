## The Epistemic Akrasia Case

**The scenario**: Alice is a careful reasoner who has thought about climate change and formed the justified belief that the evidence strongly supports anthropogenic global warming. She then learns that she took a cognitive-performance-reducing drug this morning without knowing it. She knows that people in her condition systematically make reasoning errors. She still endorses her argument for climate change when she reviews it, but she also believes that her reasoning capacity is currently impaired. Should she maintain her belief in climate change, given that her evidence for it is exactly the same but she now has higher-order evidence that her reasoning is unreliable?

**The philosophical point**: Higher-order evidence cases reveal a tension between first-order and higher-order epistemic norms. If Alice follows higher-order evidence and reduces her confidence in climate change, her reduced confidence does not track the object-level evidence — the evidence about climate change is unchanged. If she ignores the higher-order evidence, she seems epistemically arrogant. Christensen argues she should reduce her confidence in her own conclusions; Lasonen-Aarnio argues she can rationally maintain her belief but it will be "unreasonable." The case raises the question of whether rationality is a univocal concept or whether there is irreducible tension between different rational requirements.

## The Gettier Case Revisited

**The scenario**: Smith has conclusive evidence that Jones will get the job: the company president told him so. Smith also knows that Jones has ten coins in his pocket (he counted them). He infers: the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket. Unknown to Smith, he himself will get the job — and he also has ten coins in his pocket. Smith's belief that "the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket" is true and justified. Does he know it?

**The philosophical point**: Gettier's original case illustrates that justified true belief is not sufficient for knowledge. Sixty years of subsequent analysis have failed to produce an agreed-upon fourth condition that closes the gap without generating new counterexamples. Knowledge-first epistemologists argue this demonstrates that the analysis project is misguided: knowledge is the fundamental concept, and the persistent failure to analyze it in terms of more basic conditions confirms that it cannot be done. Traditional epistemologists continue the analysis project, proposing conditions of safety, sensitivity, no-false-grounds, and others. The case continues to structure contemporary epistemology.

## Epistemic Relativism: The Evidential Standard

**The scenario**: A philosopher argues that what counts as "good evidence" varies with cultural or conceptual framework: a Western scientist counts repeatable experiments as good evidence; an indigenous elder counts consistent ancestral testimony and dream experience as good evidence. There is, on this view, no framework-neutral standpoint from which to adjudicate between them. A critic replies: "Is the claim that evidence is framework-relative itself framework-relative? If so, I need not accept it by my standards. If not, you are making an absolute claim — one that is itself framework-neutral — and you have refuted your own relativism."

**The philosophical point**: Boghossian's self-refutation argument against epistemic relativism is crystallized here. The relativist cannot coherently assert relativism as a universally binding claim while also holding that all claims are framework-relative. The scenario motivates the distinction between first-order epistemic norms (which could, in principle, vary by framework) and metaepistemological claims about the nature of those norms (which must themselves be either absolute or self-undermining). The case illustrates the classical tu quoque challenge to relativism and motivates attention to whether any non-circular justification of epistemic norms is possible.

## The Permissivist's Rational Agents

**The scenario**: Two epistemically impeccable reasoners, equally intelligent, equally careful, with access to exactly the same evidence about a contested proposition P (say, a complex historical counterfactual), reason to different credences: one assigns 0.7 to P, the other 0.4. Neither is guilty of any reasoning error; both are fully rational by their own lights. A uniqueness theorist says this is impossible — the same evidence cannot rationally support different credences. A permissivist says it is possible — rationality permits a range of responses to underdetermining evidence.

**The philosophical point**: The debate between uniqueness and permissivism about rational belief has ramifications for the peer disagreement debate (if permissivism is true, peers can rationally disagree and both be rational, weakening conciliationism), for the epistemology of science (if permissivism is true, theory choice may be underdetermined in ways that allow equally rational scientists to prefer different theories), and for democratic epistemology (if permissivism is true, political disagreement may be rational even between fully informed citizens). The scenario tests intuitions about whether there is a unique rational response to evidence or a family of equally acceptable ones.

## The Future of Epistemology: AI, Memory, and Distributed Knowledge

**The scenario**: In 2035, a researcher's epistemic life is thoroughly distributed across digital systems: she offloads memory to cloud-stored notes, delegates literature searches to AI, uses AI co-pilots to check her reasoning in real time, and participates in research communities where outputs are generated by teams of humans and AI systems working collaboratively. The traditional epistemological framework — developed for individual human knowers with discrete memories and bounded reasoning — seems inadequate to describe this distributed epistemic agent.

**The philosophical point**: The scenario projects contemporary trends in extended cognition, AI-assisted inquiry, and distributed knowledge into the near future to ask whether epistemology's traditional categories need revision. Questions include: What is the unit of epistemic analysis — the individual, the person-plus-tools, the research team? What are the epistemic norms for distributed inquiry? How should knowledge, justification, and understanding be attributed when outputs are jointly produced by human and artificial systems? The scenario motivates reflection on whether the future of epistemology requires conceptual innovation comparable to what Gettier and Williamson represented for the analysis of knowledge — or whether the traditional categories, properly understood, are already adequate.
