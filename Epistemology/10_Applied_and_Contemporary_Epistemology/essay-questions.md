# Essay Questions: Unit 10 — Applied and Contemporary Epistemology

---

**1. Scientific Knowledge and the Replication Crisis**

The replication crisis — the discovery that a substantial proportion of published findings in psychology and social science cannot be replicated — raises questions about the epistemic status of scientific knowledge. Critically assess the epistemological implications. Does the failure of replication show that many published claims were never justified (because the evidence was insufficient or the methodology flawed)? Does it show that the relevant knowledge was only defeasibly justified (and has now been defeated)? What reforms to scientific practice — pre-registration, open data, higher power requirements — are epistemologically motivated?

---

**2. The Epistemology of Expert Testimony in Public Discourse**

The public receives scientific information almost exclusively through testimony from experts and science journalists. Using the resources of the epistemology of testimony (Unit 3) and the epistemology of expertise (Unit 9), assess the epistemic situation of a layperson trying to evaluate claims about climate science, vaccine safety, or pandemic models. Is anti-reductionism about testimony (Reid, Coady) justified in this context? What "meta-level" indicators of expertise should a non-expert use? Does the existence of manufactured doubt (the tobacco and fossil fuel industry models) change the epistemological analysis?

---

**3. Reformed Epistemology and the Rationality of Religious Belief**

Plantinga's reformed epistemology holds that belief in God can be properly basic: justified without requiring inference from natural theology. Critically assess this position. Does proper functionalism — the view that warrant is a matter of cognitive faculties functioning as they were designed to function — provide a coherent account of the conditions under which religious belief has warrant? Is Clifford's evidentialist challenge to religious belief (it is always wrong to believe without sufficient evidence) too demanding? Does the reformed epistemologist have the resources to explain how religious belief can be epistemically responsible given widespread rational disagreement about religious questions?

---

**4. AI-Generated Text and the Epistemology of Testimony**

Large language models (LLMs) produce plausible-seeming text on virtually any topic. Critically assess the epistemological status of LLM outputs. Is an LLM output a form of testimony in the relevant epistemic sense — can it transmit knowledge from the training data to the user? What are the conditions for a speech act to count as testimony (Lackey: assertion-based view; Coady: speaker-based view), and do LLMs satisfy them? If LLMs cannot testify, what epistemic status do their outputs have, and what are the implications for users who take them to be informationally reliable?

---

**5. Fake News, Epistemic Bubbles, and the Duty of Inquiry**

The proliferation of deliberately false news that mimics the appearance of journalistic credibility raises epistemic challenges for ordinary agents. Assess the claim that agents have a distinctive duty of epistemic care in their consumption of information, analogous to Clifford's ethics of belief. What epistemic capacities (media literacy, source evaluation, calibrated trust) should citizens cultivate? Is the individual agent's epistemic situation relevantly different from the pre-internet era, and if so, are there new epistemic obligations that follow?

---

**6. Scientific Realism and the No-Miracles Argument**

The "no miracles" argument for scientific realism holds that the predictive success of mature scientific theories would be a miracle unless those theories are at least approximately true. Critically assess the argument. Does van Fraassen's constructive empiricism — we need only accept that theories are empirically adequate, not that they are true about unobservables — provide a satisfying alternative? How does the "pessimistic meta-induction" (many highly successful historical theories were later found to be false) bear on scientific realism? Which position provides the more adequate epistemological account of the success of science?

---

**7. The Knowledge Norm of Assertion and Public Discourse**

Williamson's knowledge norm of assertion (KNA) holds that one should assert p only if one knows p. Critically assess the implications of the KNA for scientific communication, journalism, and political speech. Does the KNA set too high a standard for public discourse under conditions of genuine uncertainty? Should scientists assert what they merely believe (with appropriate hedges), or only what they know? Assess whether a weaker norm — assert only what you believe with sufficient justification — is more appropriate for the epistemic conditions of public discourse while still capturing the core insight behind the KNA.

---

**8. Moral Epistemology: How Can We Know Moral Facts?**

Assume for the purposes of this question that there are moral facts. How can we know them? Evaluate at least two accounts: (a) moral intuitionism — moral intuitions provide non-inferential justification for moral beliefs, comparable to perceptual justification for empirical beliefs; (b) reflective equilibrium (Rawls) — moral knowledge is achieved by coherentizing moral judgments about particular cases and general principles. Does the existence of persistent moral disagreement provide evidence against moral knowledge, or only evidence that moral inquiry is difficult? What does the answer imply about the relationship between moral epistemology and moral metaphysics?
