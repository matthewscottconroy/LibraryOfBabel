# Recommended Reading Paths through the Epistemology Curriculum

This document provides three structured paths through the ten units of this epistemology curriculum. Each path is designed for a specific background and set of goals. All three paths presuppose the ability to read analytic philosophy at the graduate level, though Path 3 (Applied Track) is accessible to advanced undergraduates.

---

## Path 1: Analytic Epistemology Core
### For: Philosophy Graduate Students and Advanced Undergraduates in Philosophy

This path covers the canonical literature of analytic epistemology — the debates that define the discipline from the 1963 Gettier paper through the contemporary knowledge-first and formal epistemology debates. It is suitable for students preparing for graduate seminars, comprehensive exams, or research in mainstream analytic epistemology.

**Estimated commitment:** 16–20 weeks of sustained study (3–4 units per month)

**Prerequisite:** Familiarity with basic philosophical methodology and argument analysis. No formal logic required beyond elementary propositional logic.

---

### Phase 1: Foundations (Weeks 1–3)

**Unit 1: Foundations and Historical Survey**

Begin with the 00-unit-survey.md for orientation. Then read selectively in the historical material:

Primary readings (in order):
1. Plato, *Meno* — complete. The source of the JTB analysis and the paradox of inquiry.
2. Plato, *Theaetetus* — complete. Three failed definitions of knowledge; essential background for everything that follows.
3. Descartes, *Meditations on First Philosophy* — Meditations I, II, VI. The foundation of modern epistemology: method of doubt, Cogito, external world problem.
4. Chisholm, *Theory of Knowledge*, Chapters 1–3 — the analytic canonization of the JTB analysis.

Key distinctions to master before proceeding:
- A priori / a posteriori
- Analytic / synthetic
- Propositional / ability / acquaintance knowledge
- Deontological vs. evaluative conceptions of justification

---

### Phase 2: The Analysis of Knowledge (Weeks 4–7)

**Unit 2: The Nature of Knowledge**

This is the historical and conceptual center of the course.

Primary readings:
1. Gettier, "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?" (1963) — read slowly and reconstruct each case precisely.
2. Goldman, "A Causal Theory of Knowing" (1967) — the first major response.
3. Goldman, "Discrimination and Perceptual Knowledge" (1976) — the fake barns case; essential.
4. Nozick, *Philosophical Explanations*, Chapter 3, §§I–IV (1981) — tracking theory in full.
5. Zagzebski, "The Inescapability of Gettier Problems" (1994) — why the analysis program may be misconceived.
6. Williamson, *Knowledge and Its Limits*, Chapters 1–3 (2000) — knowledge-first; the most important recent challenge.
7. Sosa, *A Virtue Epistemology*, Chapters 1–3 (2007) — virtue epistemology; connects analysis to Unit 4.

Essay questions to write (see essay-questions.md):
- Questions 1, 3, and 4 address the core issues of this unit.

---

### Phase 3: Justification and Sources (Weeks 8–11)

**Unit 4: Justification and Warrant** (before Unit 3)

This phase is best done in the order 4 → 3 for this path, since the structural questions of justification (internalism/externalism, foundationalism/coherentism) provide the framework for understanding why the debates about sources matter.

Primary readings:
1. Chisholm, *Theory of Knowledge*, Chapters 4–6 — foundationalist internalism.
2. BonJour, *The Structure of Empirical Knowledge*, Chapters 3–5 (1985) — coherentist critique of foundationalism.
3. Goldman, "What Is Justified Belief?" (1979) — process reliabilism.
4. Plantinga, *Warrant and Proper Function*, Chapters 1–4 (1993) — proper functionalism vs. reliabilism.
5. Pryor, "The Skeptic and the Dogmatist" (2000) — dogmatist foundationalism.
6. Feldman and Conee, "Internalism Defended" (2001) — mentalism and evidentialism.

**Unit 3: Sources of Knowledge**

Primary readings:
1. Coady, *Testimony*, Part I (1992) — anti-reductionism about testimony.
2. McDowell, *Mind and World*, Lectures I–II (1994) — perceptual knowledge.
3. Fricker, Miranda, *Epistemic Injustice*, Chapters 1–2 (2007) — testimonial injustice.

---

### Phase 4: Skepticism (Weeks 12–14)

**Unit 5: Skepticism**

Primary readings:
1. Descartes, Meditation I (revisit with new tools).
2. Nozick, *Philosophical Explanations*, Chapter 3, §V — tracking and scepticism.
3. DeRose, "Solving the Skeptical Problem" (1995) — contextualism.
4. Lewis, "Elusive Knowledge" (1996) — contextualism with possible-worlds semantics.
5. Pryor, "The Skeptic and the Dogmatist" (2000) — already read; now re-read for anti-sceptical import.
6. Wright, "Warrant for Nothing (and Foundations for Free)?" (2004) — entitlement and hinge epistemology.

---

### Phase 5: Formal and Social Epistemology (Weeks 15–18)

**Unit 8: Formal Epistemology** (selective)

Primary readings:
1. Joyce, "A Nonpragmatic Vindication of Probabilism" (1998) — accuracy-dominance.
2. Lewis, "A Subjectivist's Guide to Objective Chance" (1980) — Principal Principle.
3. Christensen, *Putting Logic in Its Place*, Chapters 1–3 (2004) — lottery and preface paradoxes.
4. Elga, "Self-Locating Belief and the Sleeping Beauty Problem" (2000) — self-locating belief.

**Unit 9: Social and Political Epistemology** (selective)

Primary readings:
1. Goldman, *Knowledge in a Social World*, Chapters 1–3 (1999).
2. Fricker, *Epistemic Injustice*, Chapters 1–5 (2007) — complete.
3. Elga, "Reflection and Disagreement" (2007) and Kelly, "The Epistemic Significance of Disagreement" (2005).

**Unit 10: Applied Epistemology** (selective)

Read as interest dictates; most directly relevant to this path:
- van Fraassen, *The Scientific Image*, Chapters 1–2 (1980).
- Plantinga, *Warranted Christian Belief*, Chapters 1–3 (2000).
- O'Connor and Weatherall, *The Misinformation Age*, Chapters 1–3 (2019).

---

### Integrative Essay Questions for this Path

After completing all five phases, write full essays on:
- Unit 2, Question 1 (Gettier and the fourth-condition program)
- Unit 4, Question 2 (internalism and externalism)
- Unit 5, Question 2 (contextualism)
- Unit 8, Question 1 (Dutch book argument)
- Unit 9, Question 1 (peer disagreement)

## Path 2: Formal Epistemology
### For: Logicians, Mathematicians, and Philosophers with Formal Logic Background

This path emphasizes the formal dimensions of epistemology: probability theory, decision theory, epistemic logic, and belief revision. It assumes comfort with first-order logic and basic probability theory. It is suitable for students coming from logic, mathematics, computer science, or decision theory who want a rigorous epistemological grounding.

**Estimated commitment:** 12–16 weeks

**Prerequisite:** Familiarity with probability theory (Kolmogorov axioms, conditional probability, Bayes's theorem) and basic modal logic (possible worlds, accessibility relations). Formal logic through first-order logic.

---

### Phase 1: Core Philosophical Grounding (Weeks 1–3)

Read quickly for orientation — this material provides the philosophical problems that formal epistemology addresses:

1. Unit 1 00-unit-survey.md — read for context; focus on the analytic/synthetic distinction and the problem of induction.
2. Gettier, "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?" (1963) — essential background.
3. Hume, *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, §§IV–V — the problem of induction.
4. Unit 8 00-unit-survey.md — the central unit for this path.

---

### Phase 2: Bayesian Epistemology in Depth (Weeks 4–7)

Work through Unit 8 systematically:

1. Ramsey, "Truth and Probability" (1926) — the original subjective Bayesian paper.
2. de Finetti, *Theory of Probability*, Vol. I, Chapter 1 (1970) — subjectivism in full.
3. Savage, *Foundations of Statistics*, Chapters 1–3 (1954) — decision-theoretic foundation.
4. Lewis, "A Subjectivist's Guide to Objective Chance" (1980) — Principal Principle.
5. Joyce, "A Nonpragmatic Vindication of Probabilism" (1998) — accuracy-dominance; read in full and work through the scoring-rule mathematics.
6. Pettigrew, *Accuracy and the Laws of Credence*, Chapters 1–5 (2016) — the book-length development of accuracy-first epistemology; most formally demanding text in the curriculum.
7. van Fraassen, "Belief and the Will" (1984) — reflection principle.
8. Christensen, *Putting Logic in Its Place*, complete (2004).
9. Elga, "Self-Locating Belief and the Sleeping Beauty Problem" (2000) and the halfer response (Lewis, "Sleeping Beauty: Reply to Elga," 2001).

Additional formal tools to study alongside these readings:
- Koopman, "The Bases of Probability" (1940) — qualitative probability.
- Paris, *The Uncertain Reasoner's Companion* (1994) — maximum entropy.
- Walley, *Statistical Reasoning with Imprecise Probabilities* (1991) — imprecise credences.

---

### Phase 3: Epistemic Logic (Weeks 8–10)

1. Halpern, *Reasoning about Uncertainty*, Chapters 1–3 (2003) — the textbook treatment.
2. Fagin, Halpern, Moses, Vardi, *Reasoning about Knowledge*, Chapters 1–3 (1995) — the standard reference for multi-agent epistemic logic.
3. Williamson, *Knowledge and Its Limits*, Chapters 1–2 (2000) — for the philosophical interpretation of S4/S5 epistemic logic.
4. Baltag and Smets, "A Qualitative Theory of Dynamic Interactive Belief Revision" (2008) — dynamic epistemic logic.

Mathematical exercises:
- Prove the completeness theorem for S5 epistemic logic.
- Model the "muddy children" puzzle in epistemic logic and solve it.
- Formalize the Sleeping Beauty problem in a temporal epistemic logic and derive the halfer and thirder positions as theorems under different axioms.

---

### Phase 4: Belief Revision (Weeks 11–13)

1. Gärdenfors, *Knowledge in Flux*, Chapters 1–4 (1988) — the philosophical motivation for AGM.
2. Alchourrón, Gärdenfors, and Makinson, "On the Logic of Theory Change" (1985) — the technical AGM paper; work through the proofs.
3. Darwiche and Pearl, "On the Logic of Iterated Belief Revision" (1997) — iterated revision.
4. Boutilier, "Revision Sequences and Nested Conditionals" (1993) — conditional logic approach.

Comparison project: Prove that for the special case of updating on evidence with probability 1, Bayesian conditionalization and AGM revision coincide. Identify the conditions under which they diverge.

---

### Phase 5: Connections to Main Epistemological Debates (Weeks 14–16)

This phase connects the formal work back to the philosophical debates:

1. Unit 5 (Skepticism): Assess how Bayesian epistemology bears on scepticism. Can the sceptic be modeled as a Dutch-book opponent?
2. Unit 9, Questions 1 and 4 (Peer Disagreement, Social Epistemology of Science): The formal apparatus for peer disagreement (Bayesian updating on peer testimony) is directly relevant.
3. Unit 10 (Applied Epistemology): Read O'Connor and Weatherall, *The Misinformation Age* — it uses Bayesian network models of belief updating to analyze misinformation.

---

### Key Formal Exercises for this Path

Work through these in sequence; they require increasing sophistication:
1. Prove Bayes's theorem from the axioms of probability.
2. Derive the Dutch book theorem (forward and backward directions).
3. Prove that any strictly proper scoring rule satisfies the accuracy-dominance theorem for Bayesian credences.
4. Model multi-agent knowledge in S5 epistemic logic and prove the muddy children puzzle.
5. Prove the AGM representation theorem (belief revision is rational iff it is representable by a total pre-order on possible worlds).
6. Prove convergence to truth under Bayesian conditionalization (given a true hypothesis with positive prior).

## Path 3: Applied Track
### For: Policy Analysts, AI Researchers, Journalists, and Students in Professional Programs

This path focuses on the practical applications of epistemology: the epistemology of expertise, scientific knowledge, AI, digital media, and ethical dimensions of belief. It is accessible to students without a philosophy background. Abstract theory is introduced only where necessary for the applied questions.

**Estimated commitment:** 10–12 weeks

**Prerequisite:** None beyond standard reading ability. Some exposure to social science or journalism is helpful.

---

### Phase 1: What is Knowledge and Why Does It Matter? (Weeks 1–2)

Goal: Establish the conceptual tools needed for the applied questions.

1. Unit 1 00-unit-survey.md — focus on the introduction to epistemology and the early modern debate.
2. Gettier, "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?" (1963) — three pages; shows why knowledge is more than lucky true belief.
3. Unit 10 00-unit-survey.md — overview of the applied questions.
4. Clifford, "The Ethics of Belief" (1877) — the strongest statement of epistemic responsibility.
5. James, "The Will to Believe" (1897) — the pragmatist response; read alongside Clifford.

Central question: What is the difference between knowing something, believing it with good reasons, and merely believing it? Why does this matter for practical decision-making?

---

### Phase 2: Sources and Reliability (Weeks 3–4)

Goal: Understand how knowledge is acquired and transmitted, with special attention to testimony.

1. Unit 3 00-unit-survey.md — focus on testimony and introspection sections.
2. Coady, *Testimony*, Part I, Chapter 2 (1992) — accessible; the case for taking testimony seriously as an epistemic source.
3. Fricker, Miranda, *Epistemic Injustice*, Chapters 1–2 (2007) — testimonial injustice; accessible and practically significant.
4. Schwitzgebel, "The Unreliability of Naive Introspection" (2008) — what the research on introspective unreliability means for first-person reports.

Central question: When should we trust what others tell us? When should we trust our own self-reports?

---

### Phase 3: Expert Knowledge and Public Discourse (Weeks 5–6)

Goal: Develop tools for evaluating expert claims in public contexts.

1. Unit 9 00-unit-survey.md — focus on the epistemology of expertise section.
2. Goldman, *Knowledge in a Social World*, Chapters 1–2 (1999) — accessible introduction to social epistemology.
3. Kitcher, *Science in a Democratic Society*, Chapters 1–3 (2011) — the relationship between scientific inquiry and democratic governance.
4. Hardwig, "Epistemic Dependence" (1985) — the classic statement of why laypersons rationally depend on experts.
5. O'Connor and Weatherall, *The Misinformation Age*, Chapters 1–4 (2019) — how misinformation spreads through social networks; most directly applied text in the curriculum.

Central question: What is the epistemically appropriate relationship between experts, non-experts, and democratic deliberation?

---

### Phase 4: Skepticism, Fake News, and the Digital Epistemic Environment (Weeks 7–8)

Goal: Apply the epistemological tools to digital media and information politics.

1. Unit 5 00-unit-survey.md — focus on contextualism and pragmatic encroachment sections.
2. Unit 10 essay questions 5, 1, and 2 — use these as reading guides for the applied material.
3. Pariser, *The Filter Bubble*, Chapters 1–3 (2011) — accessible introduction to algorithmic curation; not academic philosophy but provides the empirical context.
4. Nguyen, C. Thi, "Echo Chambers and Epistemic Bubbles" (2020) — philosophically precise distinction between filter bubbles and echo chambers; the most important recent applied epistemology paper for this context.
5. Levy, Neil, *Bad Beliefs*, Introduction and Chapters 1–3 (2022) — accessible account of how bad epistemic practices become entrenched.

Central question: How do digital media environments affect our ability to know things, and what responsibilities do individuals and institutions have in response?

---

### Phase 5: AI, Scientific Knowledge, and the Ethics of Belief (Weeks 9–10)

Goal: Apply epistemological analysis to AI-generated information and scientific communication.

1. Unit 10 essay questions 1, 4, and 7 — use as reading guides.
2. van Fraassen, *The Scientific Image*, Chapters 1–2 (1980) — accessible; the question of whether scientific theories need to be believed or only accepted.
3. Longino, *Science as Social Knowledge*, Chapters 1–3 (1990) — how social processes shape scientific knowledge.
4. On AI and testimony: for a current account, read the growing literature on LLMs and epistemic agency (Floridi and Cowls, "A Unified Framework of Five Principles for AI in Society," 2019; Bender et al., "On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots," 2021).
5. Williamson, "The Knowledge Norm of Assertion" — accessible summary in *Knowledge and Its Limits*, Chapter 11 (2000) — what it means for scientists and journalists to assert only what they know.

Central question: What are the epistemic obligations of those who produce and disseminate information — scientists, journalists, AI developers — and how can these be institutionally enforced?

---

### Integrative Project for Applied Track

After completing the five phases, choose one of the following projects:

**Option A: Policy Memo.** Write a 2,000-word policy memo on the epistemic obligations of a social media platform with respect to algorithmic curation. Draw on the epistemological tools acquired in the course (testimonial injustice, filter bubbles, the ethics of belief) to make specific, epistemologically grounded recommendations.

**Option B: Case Study.** Choose a public controversy in which expert testimony played a central role (climate science communication, COVID-19 policy, vaccine safety). Write a 3,000-word case study analysing the epistemological failures and successes in how expert knowledge was transmitted to the public. Use the framework from Phases 3–5.

**Option C: Essay on AI and Knowledge.** Write a 2,500-word philosophical essay addressing the question: Is an AI language model capable of testimony, and what are the epistemological implications for users? Draw on the philosophy of testimony (Coady, Lackey), the epistemology of sources, and the concept of the knowledge norm of assertion.

## How to Use the Unit Surveys and Essay Questions

Each unit contains:
- **00-unit-survey.md**: A comprehensive overview of the unit's central questions, positions, arguments, and reading recommendations. Read this before beginning any unit.
- **essay-questions.md**: 5–8 essay questions at graduate level. Use these to test comprehension and to structure independent research.

The essay questions are not merely comprehension checks: they are designed to identify the live debates in each unit and to require the student to take a position and defend it. Students in Path 1 should aim to write at least one full essay per unit; students in Path 2 should focus on the formal questions; students in Path 3 should write one of the integrative projects above.
