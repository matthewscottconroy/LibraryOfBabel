# Unit I: Logic, Proof, and the Language of Mathematics

---

## Unit Introduction

Every scientific theory rests on a foundation. For general relativity, the physical foundation is the equivalence principle — the observation that gravity and acceleration are locally indistinguishable. But the *mathematical* foundation, the language in which the theory is expressed, goes much deeper. It rests on logic, proof, and the structures of pure mathematics.

This might seem like an unnecessary detour. You want to learn about black holes and curved spacetime, and here we are talking about propositions and quantifiers. But consider this: when Einstein wrote down his field equations in November 1915, he was making precise claims about an object called the Riemann curvature tensor, which lives on something called a pseudo-Riemannian manifold, which is built from smooth charts and atlases, which are defined using continuous functions, which require a notion of limits, which requires a precise definition of distance, which lives in a real number system constructed from equivalence classes of rational Cauchy sequences. Every link in this chain depends on the one before it, and every link is a mathematical object defined with the precision that only formal logic makes possible.

You are not learning logic as a prerequisite to be endured. You are learning the grammar of the language that makes physics possible.

## What Mathematics Is

There is a question that mathematicians rarely ask but philosophers ask constantly: what is mathematics *about*? There are three main answers.

The **Platonist** says mathematics is the study of abstract objects — numbers, sets, functions — that exist independently of human minds, as real as electrons. We *discover* mathematical truths; we do not invent them. This is the view most mathematicians implicitly hold when they work.

The **Formalist** says mathematics is a game played with symbols according to rules. A mathematical theorem is simply a string of symbols derivable from the axioms by the rules of inference. Truth is relative to a system of axioms. This is Hilbert's view.

The **Constructivist** says a mathematical object exists only if it can be explicitly constructed in finitely many steps. A proof by contradiction that something *must* exist does not suffice — you have to build it. This is the view of Brouwer and his school.

Each view has difficulties. Platonism struggles to explain how minds that evolved to find food can access abstract mathematical objects. Formalism was dealt a severe blow by Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931), which showed that any sufficiently powerful formal system contains true statements it cannot prove. Constructivism requires abandoning large parts of classical mathematics.

For our purposes, we need not resolve this debate. We will use mathematics the way a physicist uses it: as a set of reliable tools for making precise statements and checking that our reasoning is sound. But it is worth knowing the debate exists, because the foundational questions of mathematics are not entirely separate from the foundational questions of physics.

## A Note on Style

The chapters that follow are written to be read, not merely consulted. Mathematics textbooks often strip away all motivation and narrative, presenting definitions and theorems in stark succession. This efficiency has costs: the reader has no idea why any definition was made, and no sense of the intellectual adventure that led someone to make it.

We will tell you where definitions come from. We will show you the false starts. We will point out when a result is surprising. When a proof is long, we will tell you the key idea before giving the details. When a concept is subtle, we will sit with it.

This unit covers three chapters:

- **Chapter 1** develops propositional and predicate logic and the major methods of proof.
- **Chapter 2** builds set theory — the foundational language of all modern mathematics.
- **Chapter 3** constructs the number systems: natural numbers, integers, rationals, reals, and complex numbers.

By the end of this unit, you will have the logical and algebraic vocabulary needed to engage seriously with calculus and linear algebra — and, much later, with differential geometry and the field equations of general relativity.
