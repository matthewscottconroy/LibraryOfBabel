# Chapter 1: Logic and Proof

---

## Chapter Introduction

There is something strange about proof. We use it to establish certainty — to move from "I think this is true" to "this *is* true, and here is why." Yet proof itself rests on assumptions, the axioms, which we accept without proof. Mathematics is not built on bedrock; it is built on carefully chosen starting points, and the whole edifice depends on those choices being reasonable.

This might seem to undermine everything. If the axioms are assumed without proof, how can we trust what follows from them? The answer is that we are not claiming the axioms are *necessarily* true in some metaphysical sense. We are claiming that if the axioms hold, then the theorems follow with complete logical necessity. The certainty of mathematics is conditional: given these assumptions, these conclusions cannot fail to hold. That is a powerful thing to say, even if it falls short of absolute certainty.

Aristotle first systematized logic roughly 2,400 years ago in his *Organon*, particularly in the *Prior Analytics*, where he developed syllogistic reasoning — the formal study of arguments of the form "All A are B; all B are C; therefore all A are C" (Aristotle, ca. 350 BCE). For two millennia, Aristotelian logic was essentially all the formal logic there was. Then, in the mid-nineteenth century, George Boole published *The Laws of Thought* (1854), reducing logic to algebra and opening the door to the mathematical study of reasoning itself. Gottlob Frege's *Begriffsschrift* (1879) introduced predicate logic — quantifiers, variables, the full machinery of modern mathematical logic. Russell and Whitehead's monumental *Principia Mathematica* (1910–1913) attempted to derive all of mathematics from pure logic.

We inherit this tradition. In this chapter we will develop both propositional and predicate logic, then spend the bulk of our time on the methods of proof that every mathematician — and every careful theoretical physicist — needs to master.

## Why Proof Matters for Physics

A physicist might ask: why bother with formal proof? Physics is about the real world, not abstract symbol manipulation. Experiments decide what is true, not arguments.

This objection has real force, but it misses something. Consider the following situation, which arises routinely: you have a physical intuition that some quantity is always positive, or that two solutions must be identical, or that a series converges. How do you *know* this is true? In some cases, numerical experiments can check your intuition. But numerical experiments cannot rule out exceptions that only appear for large parameters, or in degenerate cases, or in higher dimensions.

The history of physics is littered with "obvious" results that turned out to be false. It was "obvious" that simultaneously defining position and momentum was unproblematic — then quantum mechanics appeared. It was "obvious" that time is absolute — then special relativity appeared. It was "obvious" that the presence of matter and the geometry of space are independent — then general relativity appeared.

Proof is the discipline that forces you to identify every assumption you are making, and to verify that your conclusion genuinely follows from those assumptions without any hidden steps. This habit of mind is as valuable in theoretical physics as it is in pure mathematics.
