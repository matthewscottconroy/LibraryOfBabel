# 1.1.1 Propositions and Truth Values

---

## The Basic Building Block

A **proposition** (sometimes called a *statement* or *sentence* in logic) is a declarative sentence that is either true or false, but not both and not neither.

This is a definition, so let us examine it carefully. The word "declarative" excludes questions ("Is the sun a star?"), commands ("Measure the perihelion advance of Mercury"), and expressions of feeling ("How beautiful spacetime is!"). The requirement that a proposition be *either true or false* — the **law of the excluded middle** — is what gives classical logic its binary character. And the requirement that it not be *both* true and false — the **law of non-contradiction** — is what makes logical reasoning reliable.

**Examples of propositions:**
- "The integer 17 is prime." (True)
- "The sum of the angles of a Euclidean triangle is 360°." (False; it is 180°.)
- "The speed of light in vacuum is approximately 3 × 10⁸ meters per second." (True)
- "General relativity was published by Einstein in 1915." (True)
- "Every even integer greater than 2 is the sum of two primes." (This is the Goldbach Conjecture, unproven as of 2026, but it is still a proposition — it has a definite truth value even though we do not know what it is.)

**Examples of non-propositions:**
- "What is the Riemann curvature tensor?" (A question)
- "Solve the Einstein field equations." (A command)
- "This statement is false." (Self-referential; paradoxical — the Liar's Paradox)
- "x + 2 = 5." (A *propositional function* or *predicate*; its truth depends on the value of x)

The last example is particularly important. The sentence "x + 2 = 5" is not a proposition because x is a free variable — it has no assigned value. Once we specify x (or *quantify* over it, with phrases like "for all x" or "there exists x"), it becomes a proposition. We will formalize this in Section 1.2.

## Truth Values

Every proposition has exactly one **truth value**: either **True** (abbreviated T or 1) or **False** (abbreviated F or 0). We use capital letters such as P, Q, R, S to denote propositions when we want to reason about them abstractly, without specifying their content.

For example:
- Let P = "The Schwarzschild radius of the Sun is approximately 3 kilometers."
- Let Q = "The Lorentz group is a compact Lie group."

Then P is True (Schwarzschild, 1916; the value is r_s = 2GM_☉/c² ≈ 2.95 km) and Q is False (the Lorentz group is non-compact; see Carroll, 2004, Ch. 1).

---

## The Law of the Excluded Middle and Its Discontents

The claim that every proposition is either true or false — with no third option — is called the **law of the excluded middle** (*tertium non datur* in Latin: "no third is given"). It is one of the three classical laws of thought attributed to Aristotle:

1. **Law of identity**: A is A.
2. **Law of non-contradiction**: Nothing can be both A and not-A at the same time.
3. **Law of the excluded middle**: Everything is either A or not-A.

These feel obviously correct, and for the most part they are. But they have been challenged.

**Constructivists and intuitionists**, led by L.E.J. Brouwer in the early twentieth century, rejected the law of the excluded middle (Brouwer, 1908). Their argument: to say a proposition is true, you must have a *construction* — an explicit procedure that establishes it. To say it is false, you must have a *refutation*. If you have neither, you cannot say the proposition is either true or false. For a constructivist, the Goldbach Conjecture is neither true nor false until someone produces a proof or a counterexample.

**Quantum mechanics** introduces a different kind of complication. Before a measurement, a quantum system can exist in a superposition of states — neither spin-up nor spin-down, but a combination of both. Does this violate the law of the excluded middle? No — the proposition "the spin is up" simply has no definite truth value before measurement, not because of a logical failure, but because the physical state is genuinely indefinite. The law of the excluded middle applies to *propositions with definite truth values*, and in quantum mechanics, not all propositions have them prior to measurement (see the discussion in Isham, 1995, Ch. 2).

For the purposes of this book, we will use classical logic throughout, accepting the law of the excluded middle. This is standard practice in mathematics and physics.

## Atomic and Compound Propositions

A proposition that cannot be broken down into simpler propositions is called **atomic** or **primitive**. "17 is prime" is atomic. "17 is prime and 13 is prime" is **compound** — it is built from two atomic propositions connected by "and."

We form compound propositions using **logical connectives**, which we study in the next subsection. The power of propositional logic lies in the systematic way it handles these compounds: the truth value of a compound proposition is completely determined by the truth values of its atomic components, together with the definitions of the connectives. This is the **truth-functional** character of propositional logic.

This truth-functionality is what distinguishes formal logic from ordinary language. In ordinary speech, the sentence "It is raining and I am happy" has a different feel from "It is raining," even if both are true — because ordinary language carries connotations, context, emotional color. Formal logic strips all of this away and asks only: given the truth values of the parts, what is the truth value of the whole?

## Historical Note: The Formalization of Logic

For most of history, logic was studied qualitatively — Aristotle's syllogisms, for instance, are valid argument forms but not a symbolic calculus. The decisive step toward symbolic logic was taken by George Boole (1815–1864) in *The Mathematical Analysis of Logic* (1847) and *An Investigation of the Laws of Thought* (1854). Boole showed that logical operations could be represented algebraically, with 0 and 1 representing False and True, and with "AND," "OR," and "NOT" playing roles analogous to multiplication, addition (modulo 2), and complementation.

Augustus De Morgan (1806–1871), working in parallel, established the duality laws that bear his name. Gottlob Frege (1848–1925) went further still: his *Begriffsschrift* ("Concept Script") of 1879 introduced a formal language rich enough to express all of mathematical reasoning, including quantification over individuals. Frege's work was largely ignored in his lifetime — partly because of the idiosyncratic notation — but was recovered and developed by Bertrand Russell, Giuseppe Peano, and others. Russell and Alfred North Whitehead's *Principia Mathematica* (1910–1913) is the monument of this tradition: a massive attempt to derive all of mathematics from pure logic alone, using precisely the kind of formal system we are developing here.

The program ran into trouble in 1931, when Kurt Gödel proved his incompleteness theorems: any consistent formal system powerful enough to describe arithmetic contains true statements that cannot be proved within the system, and it cannot prove its own consistency. This was a profound shock to the Hilbert program of establishing mathematics on a purely formal foundation. But it did not undermine the practical utility of formal logic — it only showed that formal systems have inherent limitations, which is itself a deep and precise result.

---

## Summary

- A **proposition** is a declarative sentence with a definite truth value: True or False.
- The **law of the excluded middle** says every proposition is one or the other.
- The **law of non-contradiction** says no proposition is both.
- Propositions are either **atomic** (irreducible) or **compound** (built from atomic propositions via connectives).
- The truth value of a compound proposition is determined *truth-functionally* — entirely by the truth values of its parts and the connectives used.
