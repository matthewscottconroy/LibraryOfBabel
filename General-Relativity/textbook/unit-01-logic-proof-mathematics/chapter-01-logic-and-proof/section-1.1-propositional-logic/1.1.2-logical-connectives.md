# 1.1.2 Logical Connectives

---

## Building Compound Propositions

Propositions on their own are statements. The real power of logic comes from combining them. A **logical connective** is an operation that combines one or more propositions to form a new proposition. The standard connectives of classical propositional logic are:

| Connective | Symbol | Name | Pronunciation |
|------------|--------|------|---------------|
| Negation | ¬P (or ~P) | NOT | "not P" |
| Conjunction | P ∧ Q | AND | "P and Q" |
| Disjunction | P ∨ Q | OR | "P or Q" |
| Implication | P → Q | IF-THEN | "if P then Q" |
| Biconditional | P ↔ Q | IFF | "P if and only if Q" |

Each connective is defined by its **truth table**: a complete list of all possible combinations of truth values for the input propositions, together with the resulting truth value of the compound.

Let us define each one carefully. We will use P and Q to denote arbitrary propositions.

---

## Negation (¬)

The **negation** of a proposition P, written ¬P, is true when P is false and false when P is true.

| P | ¬P |
|---|-----|
| T | F |
| F | T |

This is the simplest connective — it simply flips the truth value.

**Example**: If P = "The curvature of spacetime is zero everywhere," then ¬P = "It is not the case that the curvature of spacetime is zero everywhere," which is equivalent to "The curvature of spacetime is nonzero somewhere." Since we observe gravitational effects that require nonzero curvature, ¬P is true and P is false.

**A subtlety**: Negation does not mean the *opposite*. The negation of "All physicists believe in string theory" is not "No physicists believe in string theory" — it is "Not all physicists believe in string theory," which is equivalent to "At least one physicist does not believe in string theory." We will return to this subtlety at length in Section 1.2 when we discuss the negation of quantified statements.

---

## Conjunction (∧)

The **conjunction** of P and Q, written P ∧ Q, is true only when *both* P and Q are true.

| P | Q | P ∧ Q |
|---|---|-------|
| T | T | T |
| T | F | F |
| F | T | F |
| F | F | F |

**Example**: Let P = "The Schwarzschild metric is an exact solution to the vacuum Einstein equations" (True) and Q = "The Schwarzschild metric was derived in 1916" (True). Then P ∧ Q is True.

In formal mathematics, "and" is symmetric: P ∧ Q has exactly the same truth value as Q ∧ P for all truth values of P and Q. This is **commutativity of conjunction**, and we can verify it from the truth table. In natural language, "and" sometimes implies a temporal sequence ("she opened the door and entered the room" suggests she opened the door first), but in formal logic, no such ordering is implied.

---

## Disjunction (∨)

The **disjunction** of P and Q, written P ∨ Q, is true when *at least one* of P, Q is true.

| P | Q | P ∨ Q |
|---|---|-------|
| T | T | T |
| T | F | T |
| F | T | T |
| F | F | F |

This is **inclusive disjunction** — the case where both P and Q are true also makes P ∨ Q true. In everyday language, "or" is sometimes used exclusively ("Would you like soup or salad?" usually means one or the other, not both). The logical connective ∨ is inclusive unless stated otherwise.

There is also an **exclusive or** (XOR), sometimes written P ⊕ Q, which is true when exactly one of P, Q is true:

| P | Q | P ⊕ Q |
|---|---|-------|
| T | T | F |
| T | F | T |
| F | T | T |
| F | F | F |

Exclusive or arises in everyday reasoning but is less common in formal mathematics. It can be expressed in terms of the other connectives: P ⊕ Q ≡ (P ∨ Q) ∧ ¬(P ∧ Q).

---

## Implication (→)

The **implication** P → Q (read "if P then Q," or "P implies Q") is the connective that causes the most confusion for beginners, because its truth table does not match the intuitive reading of "if...then."

| P | Q | P → Q |
|---|---|-------|
| T | T | T |
| T | F | F |
| F | T | T |
| F | F | T |

The only case where P → Q is *false* is when P is true and Q is false: the hypothesis is satisfied but the conclusion fails. This makes sense: if I claim "if it rains, the ground will be wet" and it rains but the ground is dry, my claim is wrong.

**The cases that confuse people**: rows 3 and 4. If P is false — if it does not rain — then P → Q is true regardless of whether Q is true or false. In other words, a false hypothesis makes the implication vacuously true.

*Why is this the right definition?* Consider the mathematical statement: "If x = 0, then x² = 0." This is obviously true. Now consider: for x = 3, the hypothesis (x = 0) is false, and the conclusion (x² = 0) is also false. The overall implication is still true — the statement does not claim anything about what happens when x ≠ 0. And for x = 2, the hypothesis is false and the conclusion is also false (4 ≠ 0), yet the implication is still true for the same reason.

The key insight is that P → Q makes a *conditional* claim. It claims: *whenever* P holds, Q holds. If P never holds (in a given context), the conditional claim is never put to the test, and so it cannot be falsified.

**Vocabulary**: In the implication P → Q:
- P is called the **antecedent**, **hypothesis**, or **premise**
- Q is called the **consequent** or **conclusion**

**Related implications**: Given P → Q, there are three related forms:
- **Converse**: Q → P (does *not* follow from P → Q)
- **Inverse**: ¬P → ¬Q (does *not* follow from P → Q)
- **Contrapositive**: ¬Q → ¬P (*equivalent* to P → Q)

The contrapositive is logically equivalent to the original implication — they have the same truth table. This is not obvious at first, but you can verify it:

| P | Q | P → Q | ¬Q | ¬P | ¬Q → ¬P |
|---|---|-------|----|----|---------|
| T | T | T | F | F | T |
| T | F | F | T | F | F |
| F | T | T | F | T | T |
| F | F | T | T | T | T |

The columns for P → Q and ¬Q → ¬P are identical, confirming equivalence. This equivalence is the basis for **proof by contrapositive**, which we study in Section 1.3.

**In physics**: Implications are everywhere. "If the spacetime is flat, the Riemann curvature tensor vanishes everywhere." "If a particle is freely falling, its worldline is a geodesic." "If energy conditions hold and a trapped surface forms, a singularity must develop." The last is the Penrose singularity theorem, which we will prove much later in this book.

## Biconditional (↔)

The **biconditional** P ↔ Q (read "P if and only if Q," abbreviated "P iff Q") is true when P and Q have the same truth value.

| P | Q | P ↔ Q |
|---|---|-------|
| T | T | T |
| T | F | F |
| F | T | F |
| F | F | T |

The biconditional is equivalent to (P → Q) ∧ (Q → P): P if and only if Q means P implies Q *and* Q implies P.

In mathematics, "if and only if" statements are called **equivalences**. When we prove a theorem of the form P ↔ Q, we typically prove the two directions separately: first P → Q (the "if" direction) and then Q → P (the "only if" direction). Equivalences are particularly powerful because they identify two seemingly different conditions as logically the same.

**Example**: "A metric space is compact if and only if every sequence has a convergent subsequence." This is the Bolzano-Weierstrass characterization of compactness in metric spaces (see Chapter 13), and it tells us that "compact metric space" and "metric space in which every sequence has a convergent subsequence" are two names for the same condition.

## Truth Tables for Compound Propositions

Once we have the basic connectives, we can evaluate the truth value of any compound proposition built from them. The method is systematic: build the truth table by evaluating the innermost connectives first, working outward.

**Example**: Evaluate the truth table for (P ∧ Q) → (P ∨ Q).

We need a column for each sub-formula:

| P | Q | P ∧ Q | P ∨ Q | (P ∧ Q) → (P ∨ Q) |
|---|---|-------|-------|-------------------|
| T | T | T | T | T |
| T | F | F | T | T |
| F | T | F | T | T |
| F | F | F | F | T |

The final column is all T — this compound proposition is true for every possible combination of truth values of P and Q. Such a proposition is called a **tautology**. We will study tautologies and their significance in the next subsection.

## The Functional Completeness of {¬, ∧, ∨}

An important (and non-obvious) fact is that the three connectives {¬, ∧, ∨} are **functionally complete**: any truth function — any mapping from {T, F}ⁿ to {T, F} — can be expressed using only these three connectives. This means any logical statement, however complex, can be written using only NOT, AND, and OR.

In fact, even smaller sets are functionally complete. The **NAND** operation (¬(P ∧ Q)) alone is functionally complete — a fact exploited in digital circuit design, where NAND gates are used as universal building blocks (Sheffer, 1913).

For our mathematical purposes, this completeness means we never need to introduce new primitive connectives — everything can be expressed using the ones we have.

## Precedence of Connectives

Just as multiplication takes precedence over addition in arithmetic (so 2 + 3 × 4 = 14, not 20), logical connectives have a conventional precedence order that reduces the need for parentheses:

1. ¬ (highest precedence — binds most tightly)
2. ∧
3. ∨
4. →
5. ↔ (lowest precedence — binds most loosely)

So ¬P ∨ Q ∧ R → S means (¬P) ∨ (Q ∧ R) → S, which means ((¬P) ∨ (Q ∧ R)) → S.

When in doubt, use parentheses. Clarity is more important than brevity.

---

## Summary

The five logical connectives are:
- **¬P**: True iff P is False.
- **P ∧ Q**: True iff both P and Q are True.
- **P ∨ Q**: True iff at least one of P, Q is True.
- **P → Q**: False iff P is True and Q is False; True otherwise.
- **P ↔ Q**: True iff P and Q have the same truth value.

The contrapositive P → Q is logically equivalent to ¬Q → ¬P. The converse Q → P and inverse ¬P → ¬Q are *not* in general equivalent to P → Q.
