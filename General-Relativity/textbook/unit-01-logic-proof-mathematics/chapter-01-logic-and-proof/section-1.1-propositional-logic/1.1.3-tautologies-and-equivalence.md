# 1.1.3 Tautologies, Contradictions, and Logical Equivalence

---

## Special Propositions: Always True or Always False

When we evaluate a compound proposition across all possible truth values of its components, one of three things can happen:

1. The proposition is **always true** — every row of its truth table is T. Such a proposition is called a **tautology**.
2. The proposition is **always false** — every row of its truth table is F. Such a proposition is called a **contradiction** or **unsatisfiable**.
3. The proposition is **sometimes true and sometimes false** — it is called **contingent**. Most propositions we encounter are contingent.

---

## Tautologies

A **tautology** is a compound proposition that is true for every possible assignment of truth values to its components. It is true not because of any particular fact about the world, but purely by the logical form of the statement.

**Example 1**: P ∨ ¬P.

| P | ¬P | P ∨ ¬P |
|---|----|--------|
| T | F | T |
| F | T | T |

This tautology, sometimes called the **law of the excluded middle**, says that any proposition is either true or its negation is true. No proposition sits outside this dichotomy.

**Example 2**: (P → Q) ↔ (¬Q → ¬P).

This asserts that an implication is equivalent to its contrapositive. We verified this truth table in subsection 1.1.2. Since the table is all T, this is a tautology — the equivalence holds regardless of the content of P and Q.

**Example 3**: ((P → Q) ∧ P) → Q.

This is the tautology called **modus ponens**:

| P | Q | P → Q | (P → Q) ∧ P | ((P → Q) ∧ P) → Q |
|---|---|-------|-------------|-------------------|
| T | T | T | T | T |
| T | F | F | F | T |
| F | T | T | F | T |
| F | F | T | F | T |

Modus ponens says: if "P implies Q" is true, and P is also true, then Q must be true. This is the most fundamental rule of inference in logic, dating to Aristotle, and it is the basic mechanism by which mathematical proof works — we apply known implications to known truths to derive new truths.

**Why do tautologies matter?** A tautology expresses something true purely by logical necessity — it is not an empirical claim about the world. In mathematics, a theorem amounts to a tautology of the form "axioms → conclusion": if you accept the axioms, the conclusion is logically forced. The proof itself is the demonstration that the corresponding proposition is a tautology.

---

## Contradictions

A **contradiction** is a compound proposition that is false for every possible assignment of truth values.

**Example**: P ∧ ¬P.

| P | ¬P | P ∧ ¬P |
|---|----|--------|
| T | F | F |
| F | T | F |

This says a proposition is simultaneously true and false — the very thing the law of non-contradiction forbids. Hence this is always false.

Contradictions are important in *proof by contradiction* (Section 1.3.3): if assuming a proposition P leads logically to a contradiction, then P must be false.

---

## Logical Equivalence

Two propositions P and Q are **logically equivalent**, written P ≡ Q (or sometimes P ⟺ Q), if they have exactly the same truth table — that is, they have the same truth value for every possible assignment of truth values to their component atomic propositions.

Logical equivalence is equivalent to saying P ↔ Q is a tautology.

**Important logical equivalences:**

**(1) Double negation:**
$$\neg(\neg P) \equiv P$$

Negating a proposition twice returns to the original. This seems obvious, but it fails in intuitionistic logic — a reason constructivists reject classical logic.

| P | ¬P | ¬(¬P) |
|---|----|-------|
| T | F | T |
| F | T | F |

**(2) De Morgan's Laws** (De Morgan, 1847):
$$\neg(P \land Q) \equiv \neg P \lor \neg Q$$
$$\neg(P \lor Q) \equiv \neg P \land \neg Q$$

The first says: the negation of a conjunction is the disjunction of the negations. The second says: the negation of a disjunction is the conjunction of the negations.

These are among the most used logical identities in mathematics. They are what allow us to correctly negate complex statements. For instance, the negation of "P is continuous AND P is bounded" is "P is not continuous OR P is not bounded."

Verification of the first De Morgan's law:

| P | Q | P ∧ Q | ¬(P ∧ Q) | ¬P | ¬Q | ¬P ∨ ¬Q |
|---|---|-------|----------|----|----|---------|
| T | T | T | F | F | F | F |
| T | F | F | T | F | T | T |
| F | T | F | T | T | F | T |
| F | F | F | T | T | T | T |

The columns for ¬(P ∧ Q) and ¬P ∨ ¬Q are identical — confirmed.

**(3) Commutativity:**
$$P \land Q \equiv Q \land P$$
$$P \lor Q \equiv Q \lor P$$

The order of operands does not affect the result for AND and OR.

**(4) Associativity:**
$$(P \land Q) \land R \equiv P \land (Q \land R)$$
$$(P \lor Q) \lor R \equiv P \lor (Q \lor R)$$

**(5) Distributivity:**
$$P \land (Q \lor R) \equiv (P \land Q) \lor (P \land R)$$
$$P \lor (Q \land R) \equiv (P \lor Q) \land (P \lor R)$$

**(6) Absorption:**
$$P \land (P \lor Q) \equiv P$$
$$P \lor (P \land Q) \equiv P$$

**(7) Implication as disjunction:**
$$P \to Q \equiv \neg P \lor Q$$

This is a crucial equivalence: every implication can be rewritten as a disjunction. It says "if P then Q" means the same as "either P is false or Q is true." This is particularly useful when negating implications:
$$\neg(P \to Q) \equiv P \land \neg Q$$

The negation of "if P then Q" is "P and not Q" — the hypothesis holds but the conclusion fails. This is exactly when an implication is false.

---

## Logical Equivalence vs. Material Biconditional

A subtle but important distinction: **logical equivalence** (P ≡ Q) and the **biconditional** (P ↔ Q) are related but different.

- P ↔ Q is a *proposition* that may be true or false depending on the truth values of P and Q.
- P ≡ Q is a *meta-statement* that says the proposition P ↔ Q is a tautology — true for *all* assignments of truth values.

When we write P ≡ Q, we are making a claim about the logical relationship between the formulas P and Q, not about any particular truth values. This is a statement in the meta-language, not the object language.

In practice, when we prove a logical equivalence, we verify that P ↔ Q is a tautology (by truth table or by a chain of equivalences), and then we can freely substitute one for the other in any larger expression.

---

## The Algebra of Logic

The logical equivalences above give propositional logic the structure of a **Boolean algebra** — an algebraic system with two elements (T and F), two binary operations (∧ and ∨), and a unary operation (¬), satisfying commutativity, associativity, distributivity, and certain identity and complement laws. George Boole identified this algebraic structure in 1854, and it underlies digital circuit design, database query optimization, and formal verification of computer programs.

The correspondence is:
- ∧ corresponds to multiplication (min in {0,1})
- ∨ corresponds to addition (max in {0,1})
- ¬ corresponds to complementation (1 − x in {0,1})
- T corresponds to 1
- F corresponds to 0

In this algebraic language, De Morgan's laws become: ¬(a · b) = ¬a + ¬b and ¬(a + b) = ¬a · ¬b, and the distributive laws become: a(b + c) = ab + ac and a + bc = (a + b)(a + c) — the second of which differs from ordinary algebra and is the distinctly Boolean character of the system.

---

## Summary

- A **tautology** is a proposition that is true for all truth-value assignments. Tautologies capture logical necessities.
- A **contradiction** is a proposition that is false for all truth-value assignments.
- Two propositions are **logically equivalent** (P ≡ Q) if P ↔ Q is a tautology.
- Key equivalences: double negation, De Morgan's laws, commutativity, associativity, distributivity, implication as disjunction.
- Negation of an implication: ¬(P → Q) ≡ P ∧ ¬Q.
- Propositional logic has the structure of a Boolean algebra.
