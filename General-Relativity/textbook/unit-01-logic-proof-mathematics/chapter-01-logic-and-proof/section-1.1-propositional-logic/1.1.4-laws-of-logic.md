# 1.1.4 Laws of Logic

---

## The Laws as a Toolkit

The logical equivalences of the previous subsection are not merely theoretical curiosities. They are a *toolkit* — a set of transformations that let you manipulate logical statements with confidence, knowing that each step preserves truth. Just as a physicist manipulates equations using algebraic identities, a logician (and a mathematician, and a careful physicist) manipulates propositions using these laws.

We collect them here as a reference and discuss their applications.

---

## Complete List of Propositional Laws

Let P, Q, R denote arbitrary propositions, T denote any tautology, and F denote any contradiction.

### Identity Laws
$$P \land T \equiv P$$
$$P \lor F \equiv P$$

The conjunction with a tautology and the disjunction with a contradiction leave P unchanged.

### Domination Laws
$$P \lor T \equiv T$$
$$P \land F \equiv F$$

Disjoining with T makes any statement true; conjoining with F makes any statement false.

### Idempotent Laws
$$P \land P \equiv P$$
$$P \lor P \equiv P$$

Conjoining or disjoining a proposition with itself gives back the same proposition.

### Double Negation Law
$$\neg(\neg P) \equiv P$$

### Commutativity
$$P \land Q \equiv Q \land P$$
$$P \lor Q \equiv Q \lor P$$

### Associativity
$$(P \land Q) \land R \equiv P \land (Q \land R)$$
$$(P \lor Q) \lor R \equiv P \lor (Q \lor R)$$

### Distributivity
$$P \land (Q \lor R) \equiv (P \land Q) \lor (P \land R)$$
$$P \lor (Q \land R) \equiv (P \lor Q) \land (P \lor R)$$

### De Morgan's Laws
$$\neg(P \land Q) \equiv \neg P \lor \neg Q$$
$$\neg(P \lor Q) \equiv \neg P \land \neg Q$$

### Absorption Laws
$$P \land (P \lor Q) \equiv P$$
$$P \lor (P \land Q) \equiv P$$

### Complement Laws
$$P \land \neg P \equiv F$$
$$P \lor \neg P \equiv T$$
$$\neg T \equiv F$$
$$\neg F \equiv T$$

### Implication Laws
$$P \to Q \equiv \neg P \lor Q$$
$$\neg(P \to Q) \equiv P \land \neg Q$$
$$P \to Q \equiv \neg Q \to \neg P \quad \text{(contrapositive)}$$

### Biconditional Laws
$$P \leftrightarrow Q \equiv (P \to Q) \land (Q \to P)$$
$$P \leftrightarrow Q \equiv (P \land Q) \lor (\neg P \land \neg Q)$$

## Using the Laws to Simplify

The laws can be chained together to simplify complex propositions or prove equivalences without building a full truth table. This is exactly analogous to simplifying an algebraic expression.

**Example**: Simplify ¬(P → Q).

$$\neg(P \to Q) \equiv \neg(\neg P \lor Q) \quad \text{[Implication law]}$$
$$\equiv \neg(\neg P) \land \neg Q \quad \text{[De Morgan]}$$
$$\equiv P \land \neg Q \quad \text{[Double negation]}$$

So the negation of "if P then Q" is "P and not Q." Intuitively: the only way an implication can fail is if the hypothesis is true and the conclusion is false.

**Example**: Show that (P ∧ Q) → R ≡ P → (Q → R).

Start with the left side:
$$(P \land Q) \to R$$
$$\equiv \neg(P \land Q) \lor R \quad \text{[Implication law]}$$
$$\equiv (\neg P \lor \neg Q) \lor R \quad \text{[De Morgan]}$$
$$\equiv \neg P \lor (\neg Q \lor R) \quad \text{[Associativity]}$$
$$\equiv \neg P \lor (Q \to R) \quad \text{[Implication law]}$$
$$\equiv P \to (Q \to R) \quad \text{[Implication law]}$$

This equivalence, called **currying** in computer science (after Haskell Curry, 1958), says that an implication with a conjunctive hypothesis is equivalent to a chain of implications. It is used constantly in formal proofs.

## The Laws as Axioms: An Alternative Foundation

There is an illuminating alternative way to develop propositional logic: instead of defining the connectives by truth tables and then deriving the laws as theorems, we can take a small set of the laws as *axioms* and derive the rest. This is the **axiomatic approach** to propositional logic, developed in the late 19th century.

One standard axiom system (due to Jan Łukasiewicz, 1924) uses only implication and negation as primitives, with three axiom schemas:

**L1.** P → (Q → P)
**L2.** (P → (Q → R)) → ((P → Q) → (P → R))
**L3.** (¬Q → ¬P) → (P → Q)

And one rule of inference:
**Modus Ponens.** From P and P → Q, infer Q.

From these three axioms and one rule, all of propositional logic can be derived. This demonstrates that propositional logic can be made entirely formal — the axioms are just strings of symbols, the rule of inference is purely syntactic, and truth tables are not needed.

This axiomatic approach is important for the foundations of mathematics (it was central to Hilbert's program) and for automated theorem proving (computers can check derivations syntactically without needing to understand "meaning").

## Logical Consequence and the Role of the Laws

We say that a proposition Q is a **logical consequence** of a set of propositions {P₁, P₂, ..., Pₙ}, written P₁, P₂, ..., Pₙ ⊨ Q, if Q is true in every possible assignment of truth values that makes all of P₁, P₂, ..., Pₙ true simultaneously.

This is different from Q being a logical consequence of the *specific truth values* of P₁, ..., Pₙ — it is a structural relationship that holds regardless of what the propositions are *about*.

The laws of logic encode the most basic logical consequences. For instance, {P, P → Q} ⊨ Q (modus ponens) — this holds regardless of the content of P and Q. The entire project of mathematical proof rests on the idea that logical consequence relationships can be discovered through reasoning, not just by checking all possible cases.

## Connection to Physics: Inference in Experimental Settings

In physics, we frequently reason from experimental data to theoretical conclusions. This inference is almost never deductive in the strict logical sense — we cannot prove from first principles that a new experiment will confirm a theory. But the *language* in which we structure the inference is logical. When we say "if the theory is correct, then the predicted effect should be observed; the effect is not observed; therefore the theory is incorrect," we are using the logical law of *modus tollens*:

**Modus tollens**: From P → Q and ¬Q, conclude ¬P.

This is a theorem of propositional logic, derivable from the contrapositive equivalence: if P → Q ≡ ¬Q → ¬P, then from ¬Q we can infer ¬P.

The 1887 Michelson-Morley experiment is a famous example. The prevailing theory (luminiferous ether exists and Earth moves through it) implied (ether drift should be detectable). The experiment showed (ether drift was not detected). Modus tollens: the ether theory was wrong.

The logical chain is valid. The question of whether the experimental observation was correct, and whether the connection between theory and observation was exactly as claimed, is a separate (and deep) matter — but the logical structure of the inference is sound. Mastery of propositional logic makes you a clearer thinker about the relationship between evidence and theory.

---

## Summary

The laws of propositional logic form a Boolean algebra and provide a complete toolkit for manipulating propositions. The key laws are:
- Identity and domination laws
- Idempotent laws
- Double negation
- Commutativity and associativity
- Distributivity
- De Morgan's laws
- Absorption laws
- Complement laws
- Implication laws (including the contrapositive equivalence)
- Biconditional laws

Physical and mathematical reasoning — from Michelson-Morley to the Penrose singularity theorem — is structured by logical inference, principally modus ponens and modus tollens.
