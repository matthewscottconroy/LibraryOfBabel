# 1.2.3 Negating Quantified Statements

---

## The Art of the Negation

One of the most practically important skills in mathematics — and in rigorous thinking generally — is correctly negating a given statement. The negation of "all swans are white" is not "no swans are white": it is "there is at least one swan that is not white." Getting this wrong leads to invalid proofs and faulty reasoning.

Predicate logic gives us precise rules for negating quantified statements.

---

## Negating Universal Statements

The negation of a universal statement swaps the quantifier to existential and negates the predicate:

$$\neg(\forall x\, P(x)) \equiv \exists x\, \neg P(x)$$

"Not every x satisfies P" means "there exists some x that does not satisfy P."

**Intuition**: A universal statement claims P holds for all x. To show it fails, we need to show it fails for at least one x — a counterexample.

**Examples:**

- ¬(∀n ∈ ℕ, n² > n) ≡ ∃n ∈ ℕ, n² ≤ n. (The negation is true: n = 1 gives 1² = 1 ≤ 1.)
- ¬(∀x ∈ ℝ, x² ≥ 0) ≡ ∃x ∈ ℝ, x² < 0. (The negation is false; the original universal statement is true.)
- ¬(∀f ∈ C(ℝ), f is bounded) ≡ ∃f ∈ C(ℝ), f is not bounded. (The negation is true: f(x) = x is continuous and unbounded.)

---

## Negating Existential Statements

The negation of an existential statement swaps the quantifier to universal and negates the predicate:

$$\neg(\exists x\, P(x)) \equiv \forall x\, \neg P(x)$$

"There does not exist an x satisfying P" means "every x fails to satisfy P."

**Intuition**: An existential statement claims P holds for some x. To show it fails, we must show it fails for every x — no witness exists.

**Examples:**

- ¬(∃x ∈ ℚ, x² = 2) ≡ ∀x ∈ ℚ, x² ≠ 2. (This is true — the irrationality of √2.)
- ¬(∃n ∈ ℕ, n is divisible by 7 and by 11 and n < 100) ≡ ∀n ∈ ℕ, if n < 100 then n is not divisible by both 7 and 11. (This is false: 77 = 7 × 11 < 100.)

---

## De Morgan's Laws for Quantifiers

The rules for negating quantified statements are the exact analogs of De Morgan's laws for propositional connectives:

| Propositional | Quantificational |
|---------------|-----------------|
| ¬(P ∧ Q) ≡ ¬P ∨ ¬Q | ¬(∀x P(x)) ≡ ∃x ¬P(x) |
| ¬(P ∨ Q) ≡ ¬P ∧ ¬Q | ¬(∃x P(x)) ≡ ∀x ¬P(x) |

The analogy is precise: ∀ behaves like ∧ (it is a generalized conjunction over the domain), and ∃ behaves like ∨ (it is a generalized disjunction over the domain).

## Negating Nested Quantifiers

For nested quantifiers, we apply the rules one at a time, working from the outermost quantifier inward, flipping each one and negating the predicate at the end.

**Example**: Negate the limit definition.

Original:
$$\forall \varepsilon > 0\; \exists \delta > 0\; \forall x\; (0 < |x - a| < \delta \to |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon)$$

Step 1: Negate the outer universal ∀ε → ∃ε:
$$\exists \varepsilon > 0\; \neg[\exists \delta > 0\; \forall x\; (0 < |x - a| < \delta \to |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon)]$$

Step 2: Negate ∃δ → ∀δ:
$$\exists \varepsilon > 0\; \forall \delta > 0\; \neg[\forall x\; (0 < |x - a| < \delta \to |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon)]$$

Step 3: Negate ∀x → ∃x:
$$\exists \varepsilon > 0\; \forall \delta > 0\; \exists x\; \neg[(0 < |x - a| < \delta \to |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon)]$$

Step 4: Negate the implication using ¬(P → Q) ≡ P ∧ ¬Q:
$$\exists \varepsilon > 0\; \forall \delta > 0\; \exists x\; [(0 < |x - a| < \delta) \land (|f(x) - L| \geq \varepsilon)]$$

So: **lim_{x→a} f(x) ≠ L** means: there is a specific level of precision ε such that no matter how small a neighborhood δ you choose around a, there is always a point x within that neighborhood where f(x) is at least ε away from L.

This is a beautiful result. The negation of a limit statement is not something vague like "f doesn't approach L" — it is a precise quantitative statement with a specific adversarial structure.

## Physical Application: Negating the Equivalence Principle

The equivalence principle (informally) states:

"At every event p in spacetime, the effects of gravity are locally indistinguishable from acceleration."

More precisely (in the ∀p ∀ε ∃δ form): for every event p and every required precision ε, there exists a neighborhood of size δ within which all measurements are consistent with special relativity to within ε.

The **negation** is: there exists an event p and a precision ε such that, for every δ, there is a measurement within the δ-neighborhood of p that deviates from special-relativistic predictions by more than ε.

This negation is the definition of a **physical singularity**: a point at which the spacetime geometry cannot be approximated by flat Minkowski spacetime to any required accuracy — a place where the equivalence principle breaks down. The singularity theorems (Chapter 59) prove that such points must exist under certain conditions. When we encounter the concept of a singularity for the first time, it is helpful to recognize that its definition is, at its core, a negated quantifier statement.

## A Common Mistake: Negating Too Shallowly

A classic error is to negate only the outermost part of a complex statement.

**Wrong**: "The negation of 'All particles in the system are in the ground state' is 'No particles are in the ground state.'"

**Right**: "The negation is 'At least one particle is not in the ground state.'"

The error comes from reading "not all" as "none" — a confusion between "¬∀x P(x)" (not all) and "∀x ¬P(x)" (none). These are very different:

- ¬∀x P(x) ≡ ∃x ¬P(x): at least one fails.
- ∀x ¬P(x): all fail.

In quantum statistical mechanics, the difference between "at least one particle is excited" and "all particles are excited" is physically enormous — the difference between a partially excited system and a fully excited one.

---

## Summary

- **¬(∀x P(x)) ≡ ∃x ¬P(x)**: negate universal by switching to existential and negating the predicate.
- **¬(∃x P(x)) ≡ ∀x ¬P(x)**: negate existential by switching to universal and negating the predicate.
- For nested quantifiers, apply these rules iteratively from outermost to innermost.
- The negation of an implication: ¬(P → Q) ≡ P ∧ ¬Q.
- Negating "for all" is not "for none" — it is "for at least one not."
