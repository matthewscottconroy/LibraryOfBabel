# 1.2.4 Free and Bound Variables

---

## Variables and Their Scope

In predicate logic, variables play two distinct roles, and confusing them leads to subtle errors.

A variable x in a formula is **bound** if it appears within the scope of a quantifier (∀x or ∃x) that "claims" it. It is **free** if it is not bound by any quantifier.

Consider the formula:

$$\forall x\, (x > y)$$

Here, x is **bound** (it is captured by the ∀x quantifier), and y is **free** (no quantifier claims it). This formula is not a proposition — its truth depends on the value of y. If y = 0, the formula says "every real number is greater than 0," which is false. If y = -100, it says "every real number is greater than -100," which is also false (since -200 > -100 is false). Actually, ∀x (x > y) is false for any specific y, since we can always find x ≤ y. But the point is: the formula's truth value depends on y.

A formula with no free variables is a **sentence** — it has a definite truth value (in any given interpretation). A formula with free variables is an **open formula** or **predicate**.

---

## Scope of a Quantifier

The **scope** of a quantifier is the subformula to which it applies. In:

$$\forall x\, (P(x) \land \exists y\, Q(x,y))$$

- The scope of ∀x is the entire formula P(x) ∧ ∃y Q(x,y).
- The scope of ∃y is just Q(x,y).
- x is bound (by ∀x) throughout.
- y is bound (by ∃y) within Q(x,y).

---

## Bound Variable Renaming (Alpha-Equivalence)

The specific name of a bound variable is irrelevant — what matters is the *pattern* of binding. The formulas:

$$\forall x\, P(x) \quad \text{and} \quad \forall z\, P(z)$$

say exactly the same thing. This is called **alpha-equivalence** in type theory and formal logic. Renaming a bound variable consistently throughout its scope yields a logically equivalent formula.

This freedom is not merely technical — it prevents a subtle error called **variable capture**, which occurs when substituting a term into a formula causes a free variable in the term to be accidentally bound by a quantifier in the formula. Careful renaming of bound variables prevents this.

---

## Why This Matters in Mathematics

The distinction between free and bound variables shows up in every corner of mathematics:

**Integrals**: In ∫₀¹ x² dx, the variable x is bound by the integral — it is a "dummy variable." The result is the number 1/3, independent of x. Contrast with the *open* formula "the integral from 0 to a of x² dx," which is a function of the free variable a.

**Sums**: In ∑ₙ₌₁^N 1/n², the variable n is bound by the sum. The result depends on the free variable N.

**Equations**: In the Einstein field equations G_{μν} = (8πG/c⁴) T_{μν}, the indices μ and ν are free variables ranging over {0, 1, 2, 3} — the equation is actually a family of 16 equations (10 independent ones, due to symmetry).

---

## Free Variables as Parameters

A free variable in a predicate acts as a **parameter**: it is a placeholder for a value to be specified by context. When we write "f is continuous at a," we might define this as:

$$\text{Cont}(f, a) \equiv \forall \varepsilon > 0\, \exists \delta > 0\, \forall x\, (|x - a| < \delta \to |f(x) - f(a)| < \varepsilon)$$

Here, a and f are free variables. The predicate Cont(f, a) is true for some choices of (f, a) and false for others. When we say "f is continuous everywhere," we quantify: ∀a Cont(f, a).

This parameter view of free variables is fundamental for understanding how definitions work in mathematics: a definition introduces a predicate (possibly with free variables), and theorems quantify over those free variables.

---

## Summary

- A **bound variable** is one captured by a quantifier within its scope.
- A **free variable** is one not captured by any quantifier.
- A **sentence** has no free variables and has a definite truth value.
- An **open formula** has free variables and is a predicate.
- Bound variables can be renamed freely (alpha-equivalence) without changing meaning.
- Free variables act as parameters that give the formula its variability.
