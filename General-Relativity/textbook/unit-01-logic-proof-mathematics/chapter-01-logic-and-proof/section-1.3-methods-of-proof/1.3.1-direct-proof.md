# 1.3.1 Direct Proof

---

## The Most Natural Path

A **direct proof** of a statement of the form P → Q begins by assuming P is true and then, through a sequence of logically valid steps, derives that Q must also be true. It follows the logical arrow directly from hypothesis to conclusion.

This is the most natural proof strategy when the connection between P and Q is constructive — when you can explicitly build the path from one to the other. It tends to work well when the conclusion is a consequence of carrying out a specific computation or when the hypothesis provides all the information needed.

---

## Structure of a Direct Proof

A direct proof has the following skeleton:

> **Theorem**: If P, then Q.
>
> **Proof**: Assume P. [Derive Q using definitions, previously proved theorems, and logical inference.] Therefore Q. □

The □ symbol (or "QED," from *quod erat demonstrandum* — "what was to be demonstrated") marks the end of a proof.

---

## Example 1: An Arithmetic Identity

**Theorem**: If n is an even integer, then n² is divisible by 4.

**Proof**: Assume n is an even integer. By the definition of even, there exists an integer k such that n = 2k. Then:

$$n^2 = (2k)^2 = 4k^2$$

Since k² is an integer, n² = 4k² is divisible by 4. □

Observe the structure: we unpack the definition of "even" (P), compute n² (calculation), and recognize the result matches the definition of "divisible by 4" (Q). Each step is explicit.

---

## Example 2: A Geometric Identity

**Theorem**: If a and b are real numbers, then (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b².

**Proof**: Assume a and b are real numbers. Using the distributive law of real multiplication:

$$(a + b)^2 = (a + b)(a + b) = a(a + b) + b(a + b) = a^2 + ab + ba + b^2$$

Since real multiplication is commutative (ab = ba):

$$= a^2 + ab + ab + b^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2. \quad \square$$

---

## Example 3: Divisibility

**Theorem**: If a | b and a | c (where a | b means "a divides b"), then a | (b + c).

**Proof**: Assume a | b and a | c. By definition, there exist integers m and n with b = am and c = an. Therefore:

$$b + c = am + an = a(m + n)$$

Since m + n is an integer, a | (b + c). □

---

## Example 4: A Key Inequality (Used Later in Analysis)

**Theorem** (Triangle inequality for real numbers): For all real numbers a and b, |a + b| ≤ |a| + |b|.

**Proof**: We use the fact that for any real number x, −|x| ≤ x ≤ |x|.

Applying this to a and b:
$$-|a| \leq a \leq |a|$$
$$-|b| \leq b \leq |b|$$

Adding:
$$-(|a| + |b|) \leq a + b \leq |a| + |b|$$

This is equivalent to |a + b| ≤ |a| + |b|, by the definition of absolute value (|x| ≤ M iff −M ≤ x ≤ M). □

The triangle inequality is one of the most-used inequalities in analysis and geometry. Its generalization to normed vector spaces and metric spaces is foundational to the study of topological spaces (Chapter 13) and hence to the notion of distance that underlies the metric tensor in GR.

---

## Example 5: A Useful Lemma from Linear Algebra

**Lemma**: If A is an n × n matrix with Ax = 0 for all vectors x ∈ ℝⁿ, then A = 0 (the zero matrix).

**Proof**: Assume Ax = 0 for all x. In particular, for each standard basis vector eᵢ (the vector with 1 in position i and 0 elsewhere):

$$A e_i = 0$$

But Aeᵢ is the i-th column of A. Since Aeᵢ = 0 for each i, every column of A is zero, so A = 0. □

This lemma will be used much later (Chapter 29) to show that a tensor that vanishes when contracted with all vectors must itself be zero — a fact used constantly in tensor calculations in GR.

## When Direct Proof Works Best

Direct proof is most effective when:
- The hypothesis gives you an explicit representation to work with (e.g., a formula for n in terms of other quantities)
- The conclusion follows by straightforward calculation or algebraic manipulation
- The definitions of the hypothesis and conclusion suggest a natural computational bridge

It is less effective when:
- The hypothesis gives you information by negation ("P is not prime")
- The connection between P and Q is indirect
- You are trying to prove something *does not* exist

In those cases, the other strategies in this section may be more natural.

## Proof Style and Mathematical Writing

A few words on style. A good proof is not merely correct; it is readable. Here are principles that help:

**1. State what you are going to prove before you prove it.** The reader should know the destination before the journey begins.

**2. Say what you are assuming.** "Assume P" at the start of a direct proof is not redundant — it signals to the reader that a direct proof follows.

**3. Justify each step.** Every equation or inequality should be accompanied by a reason (even if brief): "by the triangle inequality," "since n is even," "by the commutativity of addition."

**4. End clearly.** Mark the end of the proof with □ or QED. Don't trail off.

**5. Use words to guide the reader.** "Therefore," "hence," "since," "because," "we have," "it follows that" — these connective phrases turn a list of equations into an argument.

These principles apply to all proof strategies, not just direct proof.
