# 1.3.3 Proof by Contradiction

---

## The Strategy: Assume the Opposite

**Proof by contradiction** (*reductio ad absurdum*) is one of the most powerful and frequently used proof strategies in mathematics. The idea is this: to prove a statement P, assume ¬P (the negation of P) and show that this assumption leads to a logical impossibility — a contradiction.

Since ¬P leads to a contradiction, ¬P must be false, and therefore P must be true.

The logical structure: we prove (¬P → F) is a tautology, where F denotes some specific contradiction (a statement known to be false, such as "0 = 1" or "n is both even and odd"). From ¬P → F, and the fact that F is false, modus tollens gives ¬F → ¬(¬P), i.e., T → P, i.e., P.

---

## Structure of a Proof by Contradiction

> **Theorem**: P.
>
> **Proof** (by contradiction): Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that ¬P. [Derive a contradiction — a statement known to be false.] This contradicts [name what is contradicted]. Therefore our assumption ¬P was false, and P must be true. □

---

## Example 1: The Irrationality of √2

This is one of the oldest proofs in mathematics, attributed to the Pythagoreans (ca. 5th century BCE), possibly discovered by Hippasus of Metapontum. It is a landmark in intellectual history: the first time a seemingly concrete quantity (the diagonal of a unit square) was shown to be impossible to express as a fraction.

**Theorem**: √2 is irrational — it cannot be expressed as p/q where p, q are integers and q ≠ 0.

**Proof** (by contradiction): Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that √2 is rational. Then √2 = p/q for some integers p and q with q ≠ 0. We may assume this fraction is in **lowest terms** — meaning gcd(p, q) = 1, i.e., p and q share no common factor other than 1. (Any rational number can be reduced to lowest terms.)

Then:
$$\sqrt{2} = \frac{p}{q} \implies 2 = \frac{p^2}{q^2} \implies p^2 = 2q^2$$

This means p² is even (it equals 2q², a multiple of 2). By the result of Example 1 in Section 1.3.2 (if p² is even, then p is even), p is even. So p = 2m for some integer m.

Substituting:
$$(2m)^2 = 2q^2 \implies 4m^2 = 2q^2 \implies q^2 = 2m^2$$

This means q² is even, and again by the result from 1.3.2, q is even.

But now p is even and q is even — they share a common factor of 2. This **contradicts** our assumption that gcd(p, q) = 1 (the fraction is in lowest terms).

Since our assumption that √2 is rational leads to a contradiction, √2 must be irrational. □

**Reflection**: Notice how the proof does not construct √2 or give any explicit description of it. It simply rules out the rational possibility. This is proof by contradiction at its most elegant — ruling out one category (rational) without constructing the object in the remaining category (irrational). Constructivists sometimes object to such proofs on grounds that they fail to provide explicit witnesses, but for most of mathematics and physics, this form of proof is completely satisfactory.

The same technique can prove that √3, √5, and more generally √p for any prime p are irrational. The proof for √3 is nearly identical; see the exercises.

## Example 2: Infinitely Many Primes

**Theorem** (Euclid, *Elements*, Book IX, Proposition 20, ca. 300 BCE): There are infinitely many prime numbers.

**Proof** (by contradiction): Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that there are only finitely many primes: p₁, p₂, ..., pₙ. Consider the number:

$$N = p_1 \cdot p_2 \cdot p_3 \cdots p_n + 1$$

N is an integer greater than 1. Every integer greater than 1 has at least one prime divisor (this is a fact we establish in Chapter 3). Let p be a prime divisor of N.

Since p is prime, p must appear in our list: p = pᵢ for some i. Therefore pᵢ divides N. But pᵢ also divides the product p₁p₂...pₙ. Hence pᵢ divides N - p₁p₂...pₙ = 1. But no prime divides 1 (primes are greater than 1 by definition). **Contradiction**.

Therefore our assumption that there are finitely many primes was false, and there are infinitely many primes. □

**Historical note**: Euclid's original argument in the *Elements* is phrased slightly differently (not using the phrase "contradiction" explicitly, but using the same logical structure). This is one of the most beautiful proofs in mathematics. The number N is sometimes called the **Euclid number** for the prime set {p₁, ..., pₙ} — note that N itself need not be prime; it just has a prime factor not in the list. For example, 2·3·5·7·11·13 + 1 = 30031 = 59 × 509, neither of which is in the original list.

## Example 3: A Topological Result (Preview of Chapter 13)

**Theorem**: The interval [0, 1] is not countable (cannot be put in bijection with the natural numbers).

This is Cantor's diagonal argument (1891), which we will prove in Chapter 2 in full generality. Here we sketch it to illustrate proof by contradiction in a topological-combinatorial context.

**Proof sketch** (by contradiction): Suppose [0, 1] is countable. Then we can list all its elements as a sequence: x₁, x₂, x₃, ... Write each xₙ in decimal form. Construct a new number y ∈ [0, 1] by: the n-th decimal digit of y differs from the n-th decimal digit of xₙ. Then y differs from every xₙ in at least one decimal digit, so y is not in the list. But y ∈ [0, 1], so y should appear in the list. **Contradiction**. Therefore [0, 1] is uncountable. □

This result has profound consequences for physics: the configuration space of any field theory is uncountably infinite-dimensional, which requires the mathematical machinery of infinite-dimensional function spaces — a topic we touch on in Chapter 10.

## Proof by Contradiction vs. Proof by Contrapositive

These strategies are related but distinct:

- **Contrapositive**: Prove P → Q by proving ¬Q → ¬P. Still an implication proof; no contradiction is introduced.
- **Contradiction**: Prove P by assuming ¬P and deriving a contradiction. Works for proving any statement, not just implications.

When proving an implication P → Q by contradiction: assume P ∧ ¬Q and derive a contradiction. When the contradiction you reach is specifically "¬Q leads to ¬P" when combined with P, you have essentially proved the contrapositive — the strategies merge.

A practical guide:
- Use **contradiction** when you want to prove an absolute statement ("there are infinitely many primes," "√2 is irrational") where there is nothing to take as a hypothesis.
- Use **contrapositive** when you are proving an implication and the negation of the conclusion provides a useful starting point.

---

## A Note on Constructivism Again

Proof by contradiction is perhaps the strategy most challenged by constructivists. The concern: proving something exists by assuming it doesn't exist and reaching a contradiction tells you it must exist — but gives you no information about *what* it is. In some areas of mathematics, existence proofs by contradiction are unavoidable and routinely accepted (analysis, topology, measure theory). In others (computation, type theory), constructive proofs are required. For physics, where we ultimately want to compute observables, knowing that something exists non-constructively is often insufficient — we need to know *how to find it*.

We will be alert to this distinction throughout the book: when a proof is constructive (explicitly producing the object) and when it is not.

---

## Summary

- **Proof by contradiction** assumes ¬P and derives a logical impossibility, concluding P.
- The logical basis: if ¬P → F (contradiction), then ¬F → P, and since F is false, P is true.
- Key examples: irrationality of √2, infinitely many primes, uncountability of [0, 1].
- Differs from proof by contrapositive: contradiction proves absolute statements, contrapositive proves implications.
- Constructivists object to contradiction proofs that do not produce witnesses; in physics, constructive proofs are often preferable but not always available.
