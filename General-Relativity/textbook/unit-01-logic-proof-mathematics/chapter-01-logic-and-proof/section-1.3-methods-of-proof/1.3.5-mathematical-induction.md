# 1.3.5 Mathematical Induction

---

## The Principle

Mathematical induction is the proof strategy used to establish statements about all natural numbers (or all integers beyond some starting point). It is, in a deep sense, the proof strategy that defines the natural numbers — the structure of induction is exactly what distinguishes ℕ from other sets.

The principle is this. To prove ∀n ∈ ℕ, P(n):

1. **Base case**: Prove P(0) (or P(1), depending on where we start).
2. **Inductive step**: Prove that for any k, if P(k) is true, then P(k+1) is true. That is, prove ∀k (P(k) → P(k+1)).

If both steps succeed, then by induction, P(n) is true for all natural numbers n.

---

## Why Induction Works

The principle of induction follows from the **Peano axioms** for the natural numbers, which we develop rigorously in Chapter 3. For now, the intuition is clear: the base case establishes P(0). The inductive step then gives P(0) → P(1), so P(1) follows. Then P(1) → P(2) gives P(2). Then P(2) → P(3) gives P(3). And so on. No matter how large n is, P(n) is reached by a finite chain of implications from P(0).

This is sometimes illustrated by the image of dominoes: push the first one (base case), and if each falling domino pushes the next (inductive step), every domino eventually falls. But unlike physical dominoes, mathematical induction guarantees this for *every* natural number, no matter how large.

---

## Structure of an Induction Proof

> **Theorem**: For all n ∈ ℕ, P(n).
>
> **Proof** (by mathematical induction):
>
> **Base case**: [Prove P(0).]
>
> **Inductive step**: Let k be any natural number, and assume P(k) (the **induction hypothesis**). [Prove P(k+1), using the assumption that P(k) holds.] Therefore P(k+1) follows.
>
> By the principle of mathematical induction, P(n) holds for all n ∈ ℕ. □

---

## Example 1: The Gauss Sum

**Theorem**: For all n ≥ 1, ∑_{i=1}^{n} i = n(n+1)/2.

**Proof** (by induction):

**Base case** (n = 1): ∑_{i=1}^{1} i = 1. And 1·(1+1)/2 = 1. ✓

**Inductive step**: Assume ∑_{i=1}^{k} i = k(k+1)/2 for some k ≥ 1. We must show ∑_{i=1}^{k+1} i = (k+1)(k+2)/2.

$$\sum_{i=1}^{k+1} i = \left(\sum_{i=1}^{k} i\right) + (k+1) = \frac{k(k+1)}{2} + (k+1) = (k+1)\left(\frac{k}{2} + 1\right) = \frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{2}$$

This is exactly what we needed to prove. □

Note that the inductive step used the induction hypothesis (∑_{i=1}^{k} i = k(k+1)/2) in an essential way — without it, the step could not be completed. Identifying the right place to use the hypothesis is the key skill in induction proofs.

## Example 2: The Binomial Theorem

**Theorem** (Binomial Theorem): For all n ≥ 0 and all real numbers a, b:

$$(a + b)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} a^k b^{n-k}$$

where $\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}$ is the binomial coefficient.

**Proof** (by induction on n):

**Base case** (n = 0): (a + b)⁰ = 1. The sum has only the k = 0 term: $\binom{0}{0} a^0 b^0 = 1$. ✓

**Inductive step**: Assume the result for n = m. We prove it for n = m + 1.

$$(a + b)^{m+1} = (a + b)(a + b)^m = (a + b)\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}a^k b^{m-k}$$

$$= a\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}a^k b^{m-k} + b\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}a^k b^{m-k}$$

$$= \sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}a^{k+1} b^{m-k} + \sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}a^k b^{m-k+1}$$

Re-indexing the first sum (let j = k+1, so k = j-1) and collecting terms with matching powers of a^j b^{m+1-j} using Pascal's identity $\binom{m}{j-1} + \binom{m}{j} = \binom{m+1}{j}$:

$$= \sum_{j=0}^{m+1}\binom{m+1}{j}a^j b^{m+1-j}$$

This is the statement for n = m + 1. □

The binomial theorem will reappear when we develop the Taylor series for (1+x)ⁿ (Chapter 6), which is the **binomial series** — one of the most useful expansions in physics for the weak-field limit of general relativistic formulas.

## Example 3: An Inequality

**Theorem** (Bernoulli's Inequality): For all n ∈ ℕ and all real x ≥ -1, (1 + x)ⁿ ≥ 1 + nx.

**Proof** (by induction on n):

**Base case** (n = 0): (1 + x)⁰ = 1 ≥ 1 + 0·x = 1. ✓

**Inductive step**: Assume (1 + x)^k ≥ 1 + kx for some k ≥ 0. Since x ≥ -1, we have 1 + x ≥ 0. Multiply both sides of the inductive hypothesis by (1 + x) ≥ 0:

$$(1 + x)^{k+1} = (1+x)^k \cdot (1+x) \geq (1 + kx)(1 + x) = 1 + (k+1)x + kx^2$$

Since kx² ≥ 0 (being a non-negative number times a square):

$$(1+x)^{k+1} \geq 1 + (k+1)x$$

This proves the inequality for k + 1. □

Bernoulli's inequality is useful in analysis and will be used to establish properties of exponential functions.

## Strong Induction

There is a variant called **strong induction** (or **complete induction**) in which the inductive hypothesis assumes P(0), P(1), ..., P(k) all hold (not just P(k)), and from this we derive P(k+1).

Formally, the strong induction hypothesis is: "for all j ≤ k, P(j) is true."

Strong induction is logically equivalent to ordinary induction — they are two names for the same principle — but strong induction is more convenient in situations where proving P(k+1) requires information not just about P(k) but about earlier cases as well.

**Example**: Prove that every integer n ≥ 2 can be written as a product of prime numbers (the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, first step).

**Proof** (by strong induction): 

**Base case** (n = 2): 2 is itself a prime, so it is a product of primes (a product with one factor). ✓

**Inductive step**: Assume that every integer m with 2 ≤ m ≤ k can be written as a product of primes. Consider k + 1. Either k + 1 is prime (and we are done), or k + 1 is composite: k + 1 = a · b where 2 ≤ a, b ≤ k. By the inductive hypothesis (since a ≤ k and b ≤ k), a and b are both products of primes. Therefore k + 1 = a · b is also a product of primes. □

Here we needed both P(a) and P(b) — two cases less than k + 1 — and ordinary induction would only have given us P(k). Strong induction handles this naturally.

## Induction as a Bridge to Differential Equations

There is a deep connection between mathematical induction and solutions to recurrence relations — equations that define a sequence in terms of earlier terms. For example, the Fibonacci sequence is defined by F₀ = 0, F₁ = 1, Fₙ = Fₙ₋₁ + Fₙ₋₂. Proving properties of Fibonacci numbers typically requires strong induction.

Recurrence relations are the discrete analog of differential equations, and many of the same solution techniques apply. We will use this connection in Chapter 10 when we study series solutions to ordinary differential equations — in particular, Legendre's equation and Bessel's equation, whose solutions appear directly in the angular and radial parts of wave equations in spherical and cylindrical coordinates. These solutions, in turn, underpin the partial wave expansions used in scattering theory and in the study of perturbations of the Schwarzschild black hole.

The logical chain: induction → recurrences → ODE series solutions → special functions → GR perturbation theory. This book traces it all the way through.

---

## Summary

- **Mathematical induction** proves ∀n ∈ ℕ P(n) by establishing a base case and an inductive step.
- The inductive step assumes P(k) (the induction hypothesis) and proves P(k+1).
- **Strong induction** assumes all previous cases and is logically equivalent to ordinary induction.
- Key examples: Gauss sum, binomial theorem, Bernoulli's inequality, prime factorization.
- Induction is the proof-theoretic heart of the natural numbers (Peano axiom schema of induction).
