# Section 1.3: Methods of Proof

---

## Section Introduction

A proof is a finite sequence of statements, each either an axiom or a previously proved theorem, connected by rules of inference, that establishes a conclusion with logical necessity. That is the formal definition. But a proof is also something else: it is an *explanation* — an argument that convinces a careful reader that something is true and, ideally, illuminates *why* it is true.

The best proofs do both things simultaneously. Gauss's proof that the sum 1 + 2 + ... + n equals n(n+1)/2, given at age perhaps ten (the story is probably apocryphal, but the proof is genuinely elegant), pairs the terms from the two ends of the sum: 1 + n, 2 + (n-1), ..., each summing to n+1. There are n/2 such pairs, giving n(n+1)/2. The proof is short, it is correct, and you immediately see why the formula works. That is the ideal.

Not every proof achieves this ideal. Some proofs are correct but opaque — they verify the result without illuminating it. The existence of extremely long proofs (the classification of finite simple groups runs to tens of thousands of pages across hundreds of papers) shows that correctness and elegance do not always go together. But even in those cases, correctness is the minimum standard.

This section develops the major strategies of proof: direct proof, proof by contrapositive, proof by contradiction, proof by cases, and mathematical induction. Each strategy is a different way of building the logical pathway from hypotheses to conclusion. Knowing which strategy to use in a given situation — and recognizing when to switch strategies — is more an art than a science, developed through practice.
