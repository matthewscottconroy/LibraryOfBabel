# Section 3.1: The Natural Numbers and Integers

---

## Section Introduction

The natural numbers are where mathematics begins — and, it turns out, where mathematics becomes subtle. We all know that $1, 2, 3, \ldots$ are the counting numbers. But what are they, precisely? Two strategies present themselves: we can describe the natural numbers by their properties (the axiomatic approach), or we can construct them from more primitive materials (the set-theoretic approach).

The **Peano axioms** (1889) give an axiomatic characterization. They assert: (1) $0$ is a natural number; (2) every natural number $n$ has a unique successor $s(n)$; (3) $0$ is not the successor of any natural number; (4) the successor function is injective; (5) the principle of mathematical induction holds. From these five axioms, all of arithmetic can be derived. The axioms capture the intuitive structure of counting without committing to any particular model.

The **set-theoretic construction** builds the natural numbers from the empty set: $0 = \emptyset$, $1 = \{\emptyset\}$, $2 = \{\emptyset, \{\emptyset\}\}$, and so on, with $n+1 = n \cup \{n\}$. This is von Neumann's construction. It is a model of the Peano axioms and shows that if ZFC is consistent, then the natural numbers exist within it. The construction looks strange — a natural number "is" a set of sets — but it satisfies all the required properties.

The **integers** $\mathbb{Z}$ extend $\mathbb{N}$ to include negative numbers and zero. The construction: take pairs $(a, b)$ of natural numbers, with $(a, b)$ representing "the integer $a - b$." Define equivalence $(a, b) \sim (a', b') \iff a + b' = a' + b$, and define the integer as the equivalence class. Operations on $\mathbb{Z}$ are inherited from $\mathbb{N}$. This construction is elementary but important: it illustrates the general technique of extending a number system by taking equivalence classes of pairs — the same technique will be used to construct $\mathbb{Q}$ from $\mathbb{Z}$ (Section 3.2) and, later, various completions and localizations in algebra.

The integers have an important algebraic structure: they form a **commutative ring** with a multiplicative identity, and more specifically a **principal ideal domain** — every ideal is generated by a single element. The fundamental theorem of arithmetic (unique prime factorization) is a theorem about this ring structure.
