# Section 3.4: The Complex Numbers

---

## Section Introduction

The real numbers are algebraically deficient in one respect: not every polynomial has a root. The equation $x^2 + 1 = 0$ has no real solution. This is not a deep deficiency — one can simply declare that real polynomials sometimes have no roots and move on. But it turns out that filling this single gap produces something extraordinary: a number system in which *every* polynomial with complex coefficients has all its roots. This is the **Fundamental Theorem of Algebra**, and it makes the complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$ algebraically complete in a way the reals are not.

The **complex numbers** are defined as pairs $(a, b)$ of real numbers, with $(a, b)$ written as $a + bi$ where $i = (0, 1)$ satisfies $i^2 = -1$. Addition and multiplication are defined so that $i$ behaves like a square root of $-1$. The complex numbers form a field — in fact, an algebraically closed field, and up to isomorphism the unique algebraically closed field of characteristic $0$ and cardinality $|\mathbb{R}|$.

The **geometric interpretation** is essential. Identifying $\mathbb{C}$ with $\mathbb{R}^2$, complex numbers are points in the plane, and multiplication has a beautiful geometric meaning: to multiply by $re^{i\theta}$ is to scale by $r$ and rotate by $\theta$. This is **Euler's formula**: $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$, perhaps the most celebrated formula in mathematics, connecting the five fundamental constants $e, i, \pi, 1, 0$ in the single relation $e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0$.

For the physicist, complex numbers are not just algebraically convenient — they are physically real in quantum mechanics, where the state of a system is a complex vector and the interference of probability amplitudes requires complex phases. In GR, complex numbers appear in the Newman-Penrose spinor formalism, in the Petrov classification of the Weyl tensor, and in the analytic continuation of spacetimes (e.g., the Hartle-Hawking no-boundary proposal). The complex extension of real manifolds — complex manifolds and Kähler geometry — underpins string theory.
