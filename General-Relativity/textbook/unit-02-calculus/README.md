# Unit II: Calculus

---

## Unit Introduction

Newton and Leibniz created calculus in the 1660s–1680s to solve problems of motion: the instantaneous velocity of a planet, the area under a curve, the shape of a chain hanging under its own weight. They worked with extraordinary power and productivity, but with ideas they could not quite make precise. What is an "infinitesimal"? Can you really divide by a quantity that is "infinitely small but not zero"? Newton called them "fluxions" and Leibniz called them "differentials," but neither gave a satisfying account of what these objects were.

For 150 years, calculus proceeded on the basis of intuition. The results were spectacular — celestial mechanics, fluid dynamics, the theory of heat, electromagnetism — but the foundations remained shaky. Then, in the early 19th century, Augustin-Louis Cauchy undertook the project of making calculus rigorous. His *Cours d'analyse* (1821) introduced the concept of a limit as the foundational idea, replacing infinitesimals with precise statements about how functions behave as their inputs approach given values. Karl Weierstrass completed this program in the 1850s–1870s, introducing the now-standard ε-δ definitions that we will use throughout this book.

The result is a calculus that is both rigorously justified and extraordinarily powerful. We can now prove the theorems we use, not merely assume them.

---

## What Calculus Does and Why It Matters for GR

Calculus gives us two fundamental operations — differentiation and integration — and shows they are inverse to each other (the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus). These operations apply to functions of one real variable. In later units, we will generalize them:

- **Multivariable calculus** (Chapter 7): differentiation and integration for functions of several variables. The Jacobian and Hessian matrices appear; Stokes' theorem and the divergence theorem connect integrals over regions to integrals over their boundaries.

- **Differential geometry** (Chapters 27–33): the calculus of functions on curved manifolds. The derivative becomes the covariant derivative, the integral becomes integration of differential forms, and Stokes' theorem becomes a theorem about exterior derivatives on manifolds. This is the mathematical language of GR.

But the road to differential geometry begins here, with the calculus of functions of a single real variable. The limit, the derivative, and the integral are not just technical tools — they are conceptual foundations. Getting them right the first time, with full rigor, means you will never be confused by their generalizations.

This unit is demanding. The ε-δ definitions require careful thought. The techniques of integration take practice. The convergence theory for series is subtle. Do not rush. Every concept here will be used again, and again, and again.
