# Chapter 4 Further Reading and References

---

## Primary Sources

**Cauchy, A.L. (1821).** *Cours d'analyse de l'École Royale Polytechnique*. Paris: de Bure frères.  
The text that first introduced the ε-δ concept, though in Cauchy's informal language ("approaches as closely as one wishes"). The derivative is defined as the limit of the difference quotient. Historically the most important calculus text ever written. A French original and English translation (Bradley & Sandifer, 2009, Springer) are available.

**Weierstrass, K. (1872).** "Über continuirliche Functionen eines reellen Arguments, die für keinen Werth des letzeren einen bestimmten Differentialquotienten besitzen." *Königliche Akademie der Wissenschaften, Berlin*.  
The original paper introducing the everywhere-continuous, nowhere-differentiable function. Weierstrass presented this function in a lecture; the 1872 publication is the record. It destroyed the intuition that "continuous" and "differentiable" were nearly synonymous.

**Leibniz, G.W. (1684).** "Nova methodus pro maximis et minimis." *Acta Eruditorum*, 467–473.  
The first published account of the differential calculus, introducing the notation dy/dx that we still use. The paper is short and somewhat cryptic, but contains the essential rules: product rule, quotient rule, chain rule. Available in English translation in Struik (1969), *A Source Book in Mathematics 1200–1800*, Harvard University Press.

**Newton, I. (1687).** *Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica*. London: Royal Society.  
Newton's masterwork, in which calculus is used (but not exposed — Newton suppressed his fluxional methods, presenting proofs in geometric form to be accepted by his contemporaries). The first book, on motion, makes repeated use of limiting arguments. English translation by Cohen & Whitman (1999), University of California Press.

## Foundational Textbooks

**Rudin, W. (1976).** *Principles of Mathematical Analysis*, 3rd ed. McGraw-Hill.  
Chapters 4–5 on continuous functions and differentiation are the gold standard for rigorous real analysis at the advanced undergraduate level. The proofs of the MVT, Taylor's theorem, and L'Hôpital's rule are clean and complete. Rudin's style is austere — he proves the minimum necessary and leaves the rest to the reader. Essential.

**Spivak, M. (1994).** *Calculus*, 3rd ed. Publish or Perish.  
The most beautifully written rigorous calculus text. Chapters 9–12 cover differentiation with full proofs, excellent motivation, and a strong emphasis on understanding the ideas rather than just the techniques. The treatment of the derivative as a linear map is especially valuable for preparing readers for multivariable calculus and differential geometry.

**Apostol, T.M. (1967).** *Calculus*, Vol. 1, 2nd ed. Wiley.  
More comprehensive than Spivak; includes integral and differential calculus in a single volume with complete proofs throughout. An excellent reference for all the standard results. The exercises are excellent.

**Courant, R. and John, F. (1989).** *Introduction to Calculus and Analysis*, Vol. I. Springer.  
Courant's treatment (originally written in the 1920s) has exceptional physical intuition alongside complete mathematical rigor. The discussion of differentials, implicit functions, and the geometric meaning of derivatives is particularly good. Courant was one of the great mathematical physicists of the 20th century, and it shows.

## Historical and Conceptual

**Boyer, C.B. (1949).** *The History of the Calculus and Its Conceptual Development*. Dover (reprint).  
The definitive history of calculus from ancient Greek infinitesimals through Newton and Leibniz to Cauchy and Weierstrass. Traces the gradual precision of the limit concept over two centuries. Not technically demanding; suitable for any serious reader.

**Edwards, C.H. (1979).** *The Historical Development of the Calculus*. Springer.  
Complementary to Boyer; particularly good on the 17th-century precursors (Cavalieri, Fermat, Barrow) and on Newton's and Leibniz's methods in detail. Includes primary source excerpts.

**Grabiner, J.V. (1981).** *The Origins of Cauchy's Rigorous Calculus*. MIT Press (Dover reprint available).  
A scholarly study of how Cauchy transformed the intuitive calculus of Euler into the rigorous analysis of the 19th century. Shows that Cauchy's innovations were driven by the need to teach calculus to engineering students at the École Polytechnique — the same pedagogical pressure that produces rigorous textbooks today.

## Connections to Physics and GR

**Arnold, V.I. (1989).** *Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics*, 2nd ed. Springer.  
A rigorous treatment of classical mechanics from the variational perspective — stationary action, Hamilton's principle, the Euler-Lagrange equations — building on exactly the optimization concepts developed in this chapter. Written in the style of modern differential geometry. The prerequisite for understanding the variational formulation of GR.

**Feynman, R.P., Leighton, R.B., and Sands, M. (1964).** *The Feynman Lectures on Physics*, Vol. II. Addison-Wesley.  
Chapter 19 is "The Principle of Least Action" — Feynman's brilliant intuitive explanation of why nature follows extremal principles. Read alongside the rigorous treatment in this chapter and in Chapter 18.

**Will, C.M. (2014).** "The Confrontation between General Relativity and Experiment." *Living Reviews in Relativity*, 17, 4.  
A comprehensive review of experimental tests of GR, many of which reduce to comparison with post-Newtonian approximations — Taylor expansions in the small parameters v/c and GM/rc². The connection between Taylor's theorem (this chapter) and experimental tests of GR is direct and explicit. Freely available online at livingreviews.org.

**Misner, C.W., Thorne, K.S., and Wheeler, J.A. (1973).** *Gravitation*. W.H. Freeman.  
The comprehensive GR reference. Relevant here: Chapter 21 on variational principles, Chapter 25 on the Einstein-Hilbert action. The physical content of Section 4.3.5's optimization discussion becomes technically precise in these chapters.

## For Further Exploration

**Hardy, G.H. (1916).** "Weierstrass's Non-differentiable Function." *Transactions of the American Mathematical Society*, 17, 301–325.  
Extends and simplifies Weierstrass's proof. The paper is technically accessible to anyone who has mastered this chapter.

**Strichartz, R.S. (1994).** *The Way of Analysis*. Jones & Bartlett.  
A more conversational real analysis text, emphasizing intuition alongside rigor. Useful as a supplement to Rudin for readers who want more motivation.

**Stewart, J. (2015).** *Calculus*, 8th ed. Cengage.  
The standard computational calculus text used in most undergraduate courses. No proofs, but an exhaustive collection of examples and problems at all difficulty levels. Useful as a problem bank alongside this text's more theoretical treatment.
