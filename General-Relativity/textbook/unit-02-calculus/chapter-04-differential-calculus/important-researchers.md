# Chapter 4 Important Researchers

---

## Isaac Newton (1643–1727)

**Nationality**: English  
**Key work for this chapter**: Invention of the calculus of "fluxions" (c. 1666–1671, published 1687–1704)  

Newton developed the calculus independently of Leibniz, driven primarily by the need to handle orbital mechanics. His "method of fluxions" regarded quantities as continuously flowing, with the fluxion being the instantaneous rate of change — what we now call the derivative. He called the independent variable the "fluent" and its instantaneous rate the "fluxion," written ẋ (dot notation still used for time derivatives in physics).

Newton suppressed his calculus methods for years, presenting his *Principia* (1687) using geometric proofs he knew his contemporaries would accept. The calculus was the scaffolding, removed from the final building. The notation ẋ for time derivatives survives to this day in physics.

---

## Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716)

**Nationality**: German  
**Key work for this chapter**: Independent invention of calculus (c. 1675–1684), introduction of dy/dx notation

Leibniz developed calculus independently, driven by philosophical interest in the infinite and infinitesimal. Where Newton thought of rates of flow, Leibniz thought of infinitely small differences: dy and dx are "differentials," and dy/dx is their ratio. He introduced the notation d/dx, ∫, and the product rule — notation so well-designed that it has survived, essentially unchanged, for 350 years.

The priority dispute with Newton was bitter and politically damaging to both men. Modern historians agree: Newton came first chronologically, Leibniz independently and essentially simultaneously; the dispute was manufactured and prolonged by nationalistic partisans, not the principals themselves.

Leibniz's notation was superior for the continental European tradition that would go on to develop analysis, and it is the standard today. When we write dy = f'(x) dx, or the chain rule as dy/dx = (dy/du)(du/dx), we are using Leibniz's insight directly.

## Augustin-Louis Cauchy (1789–1857)

**Nationality**: French  
**Key work for this chapter**: Rigorous foundations of limits and derivatives (*Cours d'analyse*, 1821; *Résumé des leçons*, 1823)

Cauchy transformed intuitive calculus into a rigorous theory. In the *Cours d'analyse*, he gave the first systematic treatment of limits (though still somewhat informal by Weierstrass's later standards). In the *Résumé des leçons* (1823), he defined the derivative as the limit of the difference quotient — essentially the definition we use today.

Cauchy was prolific almost beyond belief: his collected works run to 27 volumes. He made foundational contributions to analysis, complex analysis, algebraic topology (the Cauchy-Riemann equations), and mechanics. He also gave the first rigorous definition of the definite integral (Chapter 5).

His rigor was not always welcomed. Legend has it that when Cauchy assigned proof of the intermediate value theorem as a student exercise at the École Polytechnique, his students complained that it was obvious. Cauchy replied that the theorem was indeed obvious — but it required proof.

## Karl Weierstrass (1815–1897)

**Nationality**: German  
**Key work for this chapter**: ε-δ definition of limit (c. 1861), nowhere-differentiable function (1872)

Weierstrass completed the rigorization of analysis that Cauchy had begun. He introduced the precise ε-δ formulation of limits — eliminating infinitesimals entirely and replacing them with quantified statements about real numbers. The definition in Section 4.1 is essentially Weierstrass's.

His nowhere-differentiable continuous function (1872) shocked the mathematical world. It showed that "continuity" and "differentiability" were genuinely distinct — that intuition about smooth curves was systematically wrong about the most "typical" continuous function. This result launched the field of mathematical pathology and eventually contributed to fractal geometry and stochastic analysis (Brownian motion is essentially a nowhere-differentiable path).

Weierstrass studied mathematics in his spare time while working as a high school teacher, publishing nothing until age 40. His 1854 paper on analytic functions attracted international attention and secured him a professorship in Berlin, where he became one of the most influential mathematical teachers of the 19th century.

## Brook Taylor (1685–1731)

**Nationality**: English  
**Key work for this chapter**: Taylor series (*Methodus incrementorum*, 1715)

Taylor proved the theorem bearing his name in 1715, showing that (under suitable conditions) any sufficiently differentiable function can be represented as a power series about any point. His proof was incomplete by modern standards, but the essential result was correct.

The Taylor series is one of the most powerful computational tools in mathematics and physics. Nearly every calculation in theoretical physics begins with a Taylor expansion — perturbation theory in quantum mechanics, post-Newtonian approximations in GR, small-angle approximations in optics. The ability to replace complicated functions with polynomials is central to making problems tractable.

Colin Maclaurin (1698–1746, Scottish) independently developed the special case of Taylor series about the origin (now called Maclaurin series), and gave a more careful treatment in his *Treatise of Fluxions* (1742).

## Joseph-Louis Lagrange (1736–1813)

**Nationality**: Italian-French  
**Key work for this chapter**: Mean Value Theorem, Lagrange remainder for Taylor series; variational calculus

Lagrange gave the MVT in essentially its modern form and provided the Lagrange remainder formula for Taylor's theorem — the error term that makes Taylor's theorem quantitatively useful, not just existentially true.

Lagrange also developed the **calculus of variations**, finding the conditions under which a functional (an integral) achieves a stationary value. His **Euler-Lagrange equations** are the mathematical expression of Hamilton's principle. The notation f' for the derivative is due to Lagrange.

In mechanics, Lagrange reformulated Newton's laws into **Lagrangian mechanics**, using generalized coordinates and the principle of stationary action. This reformulation is the direct ancestor of the action principle in GR. The Einstein-Hilbert action is, philosophically and mathematically, a direct descendant of Lagrange's work.

## Guillaume de l'Hôpital (1661–1704)

**Nationality**: French  
**Key work for this chapter**: L'Hôpital's rule (*Analyse des infiniment petits*, 1696)

L'Hôpital published the first textbook of differential calculus in 1696, presenting what is now called L'Hôpital's rule. In a somewhat controversial arrangement, he had paid Johann Bernoulli for private lessons and the right to use Bernoulli's results. The rule for indeterminate limits was discovered by Bernoulli and communicated to l'Hôpital, who included it in his book with l'Hôpital's name. It has been called "l'Hôpital's rule" ever since — a small injustice to Johann Bernoulli, one of the most brilliant (and cantankerous) mathematicians of the period.

The rule is genuinely useful and its proof via the Cauchy MVT is elegant. Whatever its provenance, the rule is a standard tool.
