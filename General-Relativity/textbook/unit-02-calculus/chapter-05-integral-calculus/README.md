# Chapter 5: Integral Calculus

---

## Chapter Introduction

The integral is the other half of calculus. Where the derivative asks "how fast is this changing right now?", the integral asks "how much has accumulated over this interval?" Area under a curve, distance from velocity, work done by a force, mass of an object with varying density — these are all integrals. The power of calculus is the Fundamental Theorem: differentiation and integration are, in a precise sense, inverse operations. Newton and Leibniz both discovered this, and it is the theorem that transformed geometry and mechanics.

But before we can state the Fundamental Theorem, we must define the integral with care. What does "area under a curve" mean precisely? The answer is not obvious. We do not know, in advance, that the area exists — that the limiting process of adding up thinner and thinner rectangles actually converges. Making this rigorous is Riemann's achievement, and the result (the Riemann integral) is still the standard for single-variable calculus.

The story does not end there. Riemann's integral has limitations: it cannot handle some important functions, and it does not interact well with limits (the integral of a limit may not equal the limit of the integrals). Lebesgue's integral (Chapter 11) resolves these issues, at the cost of considerable additional machinery. For the purposes of this chapter, the Riemann integral is sufficient.

The integral reappears, in generalized form, throughout this book:
- Line integrals and surface integrals in multivariable calculus (Chapter 7)
- Integration of differential forms on manifolds (Chapter 28)
- The covariant volume element √(−g) d⁴x in GR (Chapter 34)
- Path integrals in quantum field theory (Chapter 59)

The definition we give now — area as a limit of Riemann sums — carries all the way through.
