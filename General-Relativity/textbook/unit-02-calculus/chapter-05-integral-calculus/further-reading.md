# Chapter 5 Further Reading and References

---

## Primary Sources

**Riemann, B. (1854/1868).** "Über die Darstellbarkeit einer Function durch eine trigonometrische Reihe." *Abhandlungen der Königlichen Gesellschaft der Wissenschaften zu Göttingen*, 13, 87–132.  
Riemann's Habilitation lecture, published posthumously. The Riemann integral was defined here in the process of investigating which functions have Fourier series representations. The definition via upper and lower sums, and the integrability condition, are essentially as we use them today.

**Lebesgue, H. (1902).** "Intégrale, longueur, aire." *Annali di Matematica Pura ed Applicata*, 7, 231–359. Also published as a doctoral thesis (Université de Paris) in 1902.  
Lebesgue's doctoral thesis introducing the Lebesgue integral, which generalizes the Riemann integral to a far broader class of functions. The key idea: instead of dividing the domain into subintervals, divide the range into subintervals and measure the "size" of the set where f takes values in each range interval. This requires measure theory, developed in Chapter 11.

**Newton, I. (1671/1736).** *The Method of Fluxions and Infinite Series*. London: Henry Woodfall.  
Newton's calculus treatise (written 1671, published posthumously 1736). Contains the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus in the language of fluxions.

**Leibniz, G.W. (1686).** "De geometria recondita et analysi indivisibilium atque infinitorum." *Acta Eruditorum*, 292–300.  
Leibniz's paper introducing the ∫ notation and his version of the Fundamental Theorem.

## Foundational Textbooks

**Spivak, M. (1994).** *Calculus*, 3rd ed. Publish or Perish.  
Chapters 13–22 cover integration from the Riemann sum definition through techniques, improper integrals, and the relationship between integration and series. Spivak's chapter on the FTC is among the clearest expositions ever written. The chapter on "Integration in Elementary Terms" (motivated by Liouville's theorem on elementary antiderivatives) is exceptional.

**Rudin, W. (1976).** *Principles of Mathematical Analysis*, 3rd ed. McGraw-Hill.  
Chapter 6 on the Riemann-Stieltjes integral. The Riemann-Stieltjes integral ∫f dg generalizes the Riemann integral and is useful for integration against measures. The treatment is rigorous and concise.

**Apostol, T.M. (1967).** *Calculus*, Vol. 1, 2nd ed. Wiley.  
Apostol's unusual approach begins with the integral (defined axiomatically) before the derivative. This reversal emphasizes that the integral has its own existence independent of the FTC. Chapters 1–5 develop the integral, then Chapter 5 introduces the derivative and proves the FTC.

**Courant, R. and John, F. (1989).** *Introduction to Calculus and Analysis*, Vol. I. Springer.  
Chapter 4 on integration; Chapter 5 on applications. Courant's treatment of the Gamma function and its applications to physics is excellent.

## Integration Techniques

**Gradshteyn, I.S. and Ryzhik, I.M. (2007).** *Table of Integrals, Series, and Products*, 7th ed. Academic Press.  
The most comprehensive table of integrals ever assembled. Contains thousands of definite and indefinite integrals. Essential reference for anyone doing serious calculations in physics or mathematics.

**Abramowitz, M. and Stegun, I.A. (1964).** *Handbook of Mathematical Functions*. National Bureau of Standards (Dover reprint).  
Standard reference for special functions (Gamma, Beta, Bessel, Legendre, etc.) and their integral representations. Now superseded in some respects by DLMF (Digital Library of Mathematical Functions, dlmf.nist.gov) but still widely cited.

---

## Historical and Conceptual

**Boyer, C.B. (1949).** *The History of the Calculus and Its Conceptual Development*. Dover.  
Chapters 6–8 on the integral from Archimedes to Riemann. Excellent account of how the notion of area was made rigorous.

**Hawkins, T. (1970).** *Lebesgue's Theory of Integration: Its Origins and Development*. University of Wisconsin Press (Chelsea reprint).  
Scholarly history of the path from Riemann integration to Lebesgue integration. Shows how the limitations of the Riemann integral in handling limits of functions drove the development of measure theory.

## Connections to Physics and GR

**Misner, C.W., Thorne, K.S., and Wheeler, J.A. (1973).** *Gravitation*. W.H. Freeman.  
Chapter 21 on variational principles. The action principle for GR involves a 4D integral; the variation is computed using exactly the techniques in this chapter.

**Feynman, R.P. (1965).** *The Feynman Lectures on Physics*, Vol. II. Addison-Wesley.  
Chapter 19 "The Principle of Least Action" develops the action integral with Feynman's characteristic clarity. Essential reading before Chapter 18 of this text.

**Zee, A. (2013).** *Einstein Gravity in a Nutshell*. Princeton University Press.  
Chapter I.11 on the action principle in GR, with the Einstein-Hilbert action. Shows explicitly how varying the action integral ∫R√(−g)d⁴x with respect to the metric yields Einstein's field equations.
