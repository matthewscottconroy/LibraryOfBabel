# Section 5.5: Applications of Integration

---

## Section Introduction

The techniques of integration in Section 5.3 are a means to an end. The end is computing quantities that arise naturally in geometry, physics, and analysis: areas, volumes, arc lengths, surface areas, centers of mass, moments of inertia, work done by a force, and probability distributions. Integration is the mathematical formalization of "adding up infinitely many infinitely small things" — a description vague enough to apply to almost any physical quantity built from infinitesimal contributions.

The connections to physics are direct and unavoidable. Work done by a variable force over a path is an integral; mass of an object with variable density is an integral; the pressure on a submerged surface is an integral. In electrostatics, the potential energy of a charge distribution is an integral. In fluid mechanics, the flux of a vector field through a surface is an integral. In probability, the expectation of a random variable is an integral. The Schrödinger equation in quantum mechanics involves integrals; the path integral in quantum field theory is (formally) an infinite-dimensional integral. One could argue that the integral is the single most important mathematical operation in physics.

For general relativity specifically: the Einstein-Hilbert action is an integral of the Ricci scalar over spacetime; Hawking's derivation of black hole temperature involves integrals of Green's functions; the area theorem for black holes integrates the expansion of null congruences. Before encountering any of these, one needs a thorough understanding of what integrals compute and how to evaluate them.

This section concentrates on geometric and physical applications: areas between curves, volumes by slicing and by shells, arc length, surface area, and physical interpretations. The goal is to develop fluency in setting up integrals from physical descriptions — a skill that will recur throughout the textbook.
