# Unit III: Multivariable Calculus, Vector Calculus, and Linear Algebra

---

## Unit Introduction

Physics does not happen on a line. Space has three dimensions; spacetime has four. Temperature varies from point to point in a room. A fluid has a velocity field — a vector at every point in space. The electromagnetic field is a tensor at every point in spacetime. To describe these phenomena mathematically, we need calculus for functions of several variables, algebra for the multi-dimensional spaces these functions live in, and the language of vectors and tensors.

This unit develops the three foundational subjects that underlie all of classical and modern physics:

**Multivariable calculus** (Chapter 7) generalizes derivatives and integrals to functions of several variables. The derivative becomes the Jacobian matrix — a linear map between tangent spaces. The integral becomes a multiple integral over regions in ℝⁿ. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus becomes Stokes' theorem, relating integrals over a region to integrals over its boundary. This is the mathematical backbone of Maxwell's equations, fluid dynamics, and the stress-energy tensor in GR.

**Vector calculus** (Chapter 8) develops the specific language of vector fields in ℝ³: the gradient, divergence, and curl. These operators appear in Maxwell's equations, Navier-Stokes, the heat equation, and Newtonian gravity. They also appear in the geometric language of differential forms — the exterior derivative is the unified generalization of gradient, curl, and divergence that extends to manifolds of any dimension.

**Linear algebra** (Chapter 9) provides the algebraic structure for working with vectors and linear transformations. Matrices, determinants, eigenvectors, inner products, and tensor products — these are not merely tools for solving linear equations but the fundamental objects of theoretical physics. The metric tensor in GR is a bilinear form; the Riemann curvature tensor is a multilinear map; the stress-energy tensor is a symmetric 2-tensor. To read GR, you must be fluent in linear algebra.

The three subjects are deeply intertwined. The derivative of a smooth function at a point is a linear map. The geometric objects we integrate over (curves, surfaces, volumes) are linear-algebraic objects (tangent vectors and normal vectors). Stokes' theorem, in its final form, is a statement about exterior algebra and integration on manifolds — pure linear algebra and multivariable calculus fused.

By the end of this unit, the reader should be able to:
- Compute Jacobians, Hessians, and directional derivatives in multiple dimensions.
- Apply the chain rule for functions of several variables and the implicit function theorem in full generality.
- Evaluate multiple integrals by change of variables.
- State and apply the divergence theorem and Stokes' theorem.
- Work fluently with matrices, determinants, eigenvectors, and inner product spaces.
- Understand tensors as multilinear maps and compute with them in index notation.

These skills are indispensable for Chapters 10–26 and for all of GR.
