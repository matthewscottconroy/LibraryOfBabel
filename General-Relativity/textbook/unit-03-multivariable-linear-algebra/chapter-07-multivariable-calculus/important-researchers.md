## Founders of Multivariable Calculus

**Augustin-Louis Cauchy (1789–1857)**
French mathematician and engineer, perhaps the greatest analyst of the nineteenth century. Cauchy put differential and integral calculus on a rigorous footing, introduced the ε-δ definition of limits, and developed the theory of functions of several variables. His 1823 *Résumé* at the École Polytechnique was the first systematic treatment of partial derivatives in the modern sense. In complex analysis, his Cauchy integral theorem and Cauchy-Riemann equations are foundational. His influence on analysis — multivariable calculus, ODEs, complex analysis, linear algebra — is pervasive and direct.

**Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi (1804–1851)**
German mathematician of extraordinary productivity, who died at 46. Jacobi's 1841 paper introduced what we now call the Jacobian matrix and determinant — "the functional determinant" — and established its role in the change-of-variables formula. He also invented the theory of elliptic functions, Hamilton-Jacobi theory (the bridge between classical and quantum mechanics), and made fundamental contributions to number theory. His name appears in this textbook at multiple points: the Jacobi equation for geodesic deviation, the Jacobi identity for the Lie bracket, and the Jacobian matrix.

**Joseph-Louis Lagrange (1736–1813)**
Italian-French mathematician and astronomer, often considered the most elegant mathematical stylist of the eighteenth century. His *Mécanique analytique* (1788) reformulated all of classical mechanics in terms of a single variational principle — the action integral — without a single diagram ("I have no figures in this work," he wrote). This book introduces Lagrange multipliers for constrained optimization and the Lagrangian for a mechanical system. The Lagrangian formulation is the direct ancestor of the Einstein-Hilbert action principle for GR.

**Alexis Claude Clairaut (1713–1765)**
French mathematician who entered the Académie des sciences at 18. He proved (under appropriate hypotheses) the equality of mixed partial derivatives ∂²f/∂x∂y = ∂²f/∂y∂x — a result so useful it is taught in every multivariable calculus course. He also made major contributions to celestial mechanics, confirmed Newton's theory of the Earth's shape, and calculated the return of Halley's comet.

**Hermann Amandus Schwarz (1843–1921)**
German mathematician, student of Weierstrass and Kummer. He gave the modern proof of Clairaut's theorem (equality of mixed partials under continuity of partials), which is sometimes called the Schwarz theorem. He also proved the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (independently of Cauchy's earlier result), and his work on conformal mappings laid groundwork for later developments in complex analysis.

## The Inverse and Implicit Function Theorems

**Ulisse Dini (1845–1918)**
Italian mathematician who gave one of the first careful proofs of the implicit function theorem, which is sometimes called the Dini theorem in the Italian literature. His rigorous treatment of the conditions under which an equation F(x,y) = 0 defines y as a function of x was an important step in the arithmetization of analysis.

**Stefan Banach (1892–1945)**
Polish mathematician and one of the founders of functional analysis. His 1922 thesis introduced Banach spaces and the contraction mapping theorem (Banach fixed-point theorem). This theorem is used in the proof of the implicit function theorem given in Section 7.3, and recurs throughout analysis, ODEs, and differential geometry. Banach spent most of his career in Lwów; he survived the German occupation working as a louse feeder at Rudolf Weigl's typhus research institute, and died of lung cancer shortly after WWII.

## Integration and the Change of Variables

**Guido Fubini (1879–1943)**
Italian mathematician who proved the fundamental theorem on iterated integration: under appropriate conditions, the order of integration can be exchanged. Fubini's theorem (1907) is the tool that makes multiple integral calculations tractable. He later developed the theory of differential forms and integrals on manifolds, and contributed to projective geometry and number theory. He was removed from his professorship under Mussolini's racial laws in 1938 and emigrated to the US.

**Leonhard Euler (1707–1783)**
Swiss mathematician of phenomenal output (800+ papers). Euler's contributions to the mathematics of this chapter include the theory of partial derivatives, the formula for the determinant of a product (det(AB) = det(A)det(B)), and the development of multiple integrals. His notation is still used today: f(x,y) for a function of two variables, ∂ for partial derivatives (introduced by Legendre but popularized by Euler's notation conventions).

## Modern Analysis on Manifolds

**Michael Spivak (1940–2020)**
American mathematician whose *Calculus on Manifolds* (1965) distilled the theory of multivariable calculus — the Fréchet derivative, inverse and implicit function theorems, change of variables, Stokes' theorem — into a lucid 85-page treatment that became a standard reference. His five-volume *A Comprehensive Introduction to Differential Geometry* is the most thorough treatment of classical differential geometry in the English language.

**Hassler Whitney (1907–1989)**
American mathematician and one of the founders of differential topology. His embedding theorems (1944) showed that every smooth n-manifold embeds in ℝ^{2n}, placing the intrinsic theory of manifolds firmly in relation to the Euclidean setting of this chapter. Whitney also developed the theory of singular homology and cobordism, and later worked in mathematics education.
