# Chapter 10: Ordinary Differential Equations

---

## Chapter Introduction

An ordinary differential equation (ODE) is a relation between an unknown function y(x) and its derivatives. The theory of ODEs is the mathematical backbone of classical dynamics: Newton's second law is a second-order ODE for position; the geodesic equation in GR is a second-order ODE for the coordinates of a free-falling particle; the Jacobi equation governing geodesic deviation is a linear second-order ODE; the Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff equation of stellar structure is a first-order ODE system.

The two fundamental questions are existence and uniqueness: does a solution exist (even locally), and is it unique given initial conditions? The **Picard-Lindelöf theorem** answers both: if the right-hand side is Lipschitz continuous, a unique local solution exists. The proof uses the **Banach fixed-point theorem** — contraction mapping — a central tool of analysis.

For linear ODEs, the solution space is a vector space. The theory of linear independence, the Wronskian, and variation of parameters is pure linear algebra applied to function spaces. For nonlinear ODEs, global existence is more subtle; solutions can blow up in finite time (as physical singularities do in GR).
