## Foundations of ODE Theory

**Émile Picard (1856–1941)**
French mathematician whose 1890 paper introduced the method of successive approximations for ODEs — the Picard iteration — and proved that the iteration converges to the unique solution under a Lipschitz condition. Picard's existence and uniqueness theorem remains the foundational result of ODE theory. He also made major contributions to complex analysis, algebraic geometry, and the theory of linear differential equations. Picard spent his entire career in Paris and was the teacher of several generations of French mathematicians.

**Ernst Lindelöf (1870–1946)**
Finnish mathematician who in 1894 identified the Lipschitz condition as the key condition for uniqueness of solutions to the IVP, completing the result now called the Picard-Lindelöf theorem. Lindelöf also worked in complex analysis and made contributions to the study of analytic functions. The Lindelöf hypothesis in number theory (about the growth rate of the Riemann zeta function) bears his name.

**Rudolf Lipschitz (1832–1903)**
German mathematician who introduced the Lipschitz continuity condition: |f(x) − f(y)| ≤ K|x − y|, a condition stronger than ordinary continuity but weaker than differentiability. The Lipschitz condition is the natural hypothesis for ODE uniqueness and appears throughout analysis: in the Picard-Lindelöf theorem, in the Rademacher theorem (Lipschitz functions are differentiable almost everywhere), and in the theory of Sobolev spaces.

**Aleksander Mikhailovich Lyapunov (1857–1918)**
Russian mathematician whose 1892 doctoral thesis, "The general problem of the stability of motion," created the modern theory of stability of dynamical systems. Lyapunov defined stability, asymptotic stability, and instability; proved the linearization theorem for stability; and introduced the direct method (Lyapunov functions) that allows stability to be proven without solving the ODE. His work was not widely known in the West until translated decades later. Lyapunov suffered a mental breakdown when his wife died of tuberculosis, and died three days later, in 1918.

## Special Functions

**Adrien-Marie Legendre (1752–1833)**
French mathematician who introduced the polynomials bearing his name in 1782, studying the gravitational potential of an oblate spheroid. Legendre also developed the theory of elliptic integrals (which he worked on for 40 years, only to be superseded by Jacobi and Abel in a few months), contributed to number theory (the quadratic reciprocity law, independently of Gauss), and developed the method of least squares (simultaneously with Gauss). His Legendre polynomials are the angular eigenfunctions of Laplace's equation in spherical geometry.

**Friedrich Wilhelm Bessel (1784–1846)**
German astronomer and mathematician who introduced the functions bearing his name in 1824 while computing the perturbations of planetary orbits. Bessel functions J_n(x) are the radial eigenfunctions of the 2D Laplacian in cylindrical coordinates. Bessel was primarily an astronomer: he made the first measurement of stellar parallax (for 61 Cygni, 1838), establishing the scale of the stellar neighborhood, and he predicted the existence of companion stars to Sirius and Procyon (binary star companions, confirmed decades after his death).

**George Biddell Airy (1801–1892)**
British astronomer and mathematician who introduced the Airy functions (solutions of y'' = xy) in 1838, in the context of optics and the theory of the rainbow. Airy functions appear in quantum mechanics near classical turning points, in gravitational lensing (caustics), and in the oscillatory behavior of special functions at large arguments (the Airy approximation). As Astronomer Royal, Airy oversaw the standardization of Greenwich Mean Time.

## Dynamical Systems and Qualitative Theory

**Henri Poincaré (1854–1912)**
French mathematician and physicist who invented the qualitative theory of ODEs. His three-volume *Les méthodes nouvelles de la mécanique céleste* (1892–1899) introduced phase portraits, Poincaré sections (surfaces of section), stable and unstable manifolds, and topological methods for studying ODEs without finding explicit solutions — all in the context of the three-body problem in celestial mechanics. Poincaré discovered that three-body dynamics can be chaotic (sensitive to initial conditions), anticipating chaos theory by 70 years. He also made foundational contributions to topology, special relativity (independently of Einstein), and the philosophy of science.

**Alexander Andronov (1901–1952)**
Soviet mathematician and physicist who developed the rigorous theory of structural stability of dynamical systems (1937) and introduced the notion of a limit cycle. His textbook *Theory of Oscillations* (with Vitt and Khaikin) applied phase portrait methods to nonlinear electrical circuits and mechanical systems, and was enormously influential in applied mathematics.

**Stephen Smale (1930–)**
American mathematician who proved the Smale-Sard theorem, introduced the horseshoe map (a canonical example of chaotic dynamics), and transformed the qualitative theory of ODEs. His 1967 survey "Differentiable dynamical systems" established the modern framework for dynamical systems theory. Smale also proved the Poincaré conjecture in dimensions ≥ 5 (the higher-dimensional version). His horseshoe construction shows explicitly how chaotic behavior — sensitive dependence, fractal structure — arises from simple geometric operations.

## Black Hole Perturbation Theory

**Tullio Regge (1931–2014) and John Wheeler (1911–2008)**
Co-authors of the 1957 paper introducing the perturbation theory of the Schwarzschild metric, which leads to the Regge-Wheeler equation — a linear second-order ODE for the perturbation amplitude as a function of the tortoise coordinate. Regge was an Italian physicist who also invented Regge calculus (a lattice approach to GR) and made contributions to particle physics. Wheeler was the dominant American relativist of the mid-twentieth century, who coined the terms "black hole," "wormhole," and "quantum foam," supervised many important theses (including Everett, Misner, and Thorne), and wrote (with Misner and Thorne) the textbook *Gravitation*.

**Saul Teukolsky (1947–)**
South African-American physicist who derived the Teukolsky equation (1973), the separable equation governing perturbations of the Kerr black hole. The separation of variables was surprising — the Kerr metric does not separate in the obvious way, but a clever choice of spin-weighted spheroidal harmonics achieves it. Teukolsky's equation is the ODE whose quasi-normal mode solutions describe gravitational wave emission by rotating black holes, and whose study underlies modern gravitational wave templates.
