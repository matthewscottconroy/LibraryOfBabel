# Chapter 12: Complex Analysis

---

## Chapter Introduction

Complex analysis is the calculus of functions f: ℂ → ℂ. Its power is extraordinary and somewhat unexpected: a function that is differentiable at every point of an open region (analytic or holomorphic) is automatically infinitely differentiable, expressible as a convergent Taylor series, and determined by its boundary values. The rigidity of analytic functions is in stark contrast with the flexibility of real smooth functions, where C∞ does not imply analyticity.

The core tools are:
- The **Cauchy-Riemann equations**: necessary and sufficient conditions for complex differentiability.
- **Cauchy's integral theorem**: the integral of an analytic function around a closed curve is zero.
- **Cauchy's integral formula**: the value of an analytic function inside a region is determined by its values on the boundary.
- The **residue theorem**: contour integrals of meromorphic functions equal 2πi times the sum of residues at poles inside the contour. This is the principal tool for evaluating real integrals.

In GR and quantum field theory, complex analysis is used for:
- **Green's functions and propagators**: the Feynman propagator and the retarded/advanced propagators are evaluated using contour integration and residues.
- **Analytic continuation**: extending real-variable functions to the complex plane to reveal hidden structure. The Hawking temperature of a black hole is derived by analytically continuing the Schwarzschild metric to Euclidean signature (t → iτ) and demanding periodicity.
- **The Penrose transform and twistors**: the spinor and twistor approaches to GR use complex projective spaces and sheaf cohomology.
- **Characteristic functions and Fourier/Laplace transforms**: both are contour integrals in ℂ.
