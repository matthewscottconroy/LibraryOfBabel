## Founders of Complex Analysis

**Augustin-Louis Cauchy (1789–1857)**
See also Chapter 10. Cauchy's contributions to complex analysis are foundational: Cauchy's theorem (the integral of a holomorphic function around a closed contour is zero), the Cauchy integral formula (the value of f at any interior point is determined by its boundary values), the Cauchy-Riemann equations (the conditions for complex differentiability), and the residue theorem. Cauchy essentially created complex analysis as a rigorous subject between 1814 and 1832, publishing over 800 papers and 7 books.

**Bernhard Riemann (1826–1866)**
See also Chapter 4. Riemann's 1851 doctoral thesis introduced the Riemann surface (the natural domain for multi-valued functions like √z and log z), the Riemann mapping theorem (every simply connected proper domain is conformally equivalent to the unit disk), and the geometric view of holomorphic functions as conformal transformations. His 1859 paper on the Riemann zeta function — introducing ζ(s) as a complex function, using analytic continuation, and stating the Riemann hypothesis — is one of the most influential papers ever written.

**Karl Weierstrass (1815–1897)**
German mathematician who, alongside Cauchy and Riemann, founded modern complex analysis. While Cauchy used geometric and computational methods and Riemann used geometric intuition, Weierstrass emphasized rigor and algebraic precision. His power series approach — showing that any holomorphic function can be locally represented as a convergent power series — provided the algebraic complement to Riemann's geometric view. Weierstrass also proved the Weierstrass approximation theorem (every continuous function on [a,b] can be uniformly approximated by polynomials), the Weierstrass factorization theorem (entire functions as products of linear factors), and he constructed the nowhere-differentiable continuous function.

**Karl Friedrich Gauss (1777–1855)**
Gauss appears throughout this textbook because his influence was so pervasive. In complex analysis: Gauss proved the fundamental theorem of algebra (every polynomial has a complex root) in his 1799 doctoral thesis — using topological winding number arguments. He worked on conformal mapping (Gauss conformal coordinates for geographic maps), introduced complex numbers as genuine mathematical objects (not just formal symbols), and did much of the foundational work in complex function theory before Cauchy and Riemann, but left it largely unpublished.

## Hawking Radiation and Complex Analysis

**Stephen Hawking (1942–2018)**
British theoretical physicist whose 1975 derivation of black hole radiation is one of the most profound results in theoretical physics. Hawking showed that, when quantum field theory is applied in a black hole spacetime, the black hole radiates as a perfect blackbody at the Hawking temperature T_H = ℏc³/(8πGMk_B). The derivation in Section 12.3.4 (Euclidean continuation) is due to Gibbons and Hawking (1977); Hawking's original derivation uses Bogoliubov transformations of quantum field modes. Hawking's other major result, the Penrose-Hawking singularity theorems (Unit XV), established the inevitability of spacetime singularities in physically realistic cosmologies and gravitational collapses.

**Gary Gibbons (1946–)**
British mathematical physicist and long-time collaborator of Hawking. The Gibbons-Hawking paper (1977) derived the Hawking temperature from the periodicity of Euclidean imaginary time — the calculation described in this chapter. They also introduced the Gibbons-Hawking boundary term for the gravitational path integral, and developed Euclidean quantum gravity (path integrals over Riemannian metrics, rather than Lorentzian spacetimes). Their work placed Hawking radiation on a firm path integral foundation.

## Conformal Maps and GR

**Roger Penrose (1931–)**
British mathematical physicist who introduced the conformal compactification (Penrose diagram) in 1963, which uses conformal maps to bring infinite spacetime regions into finite diagrams while preserving causal structure. Penrose diagrams are now the standard tool for visualizing the global structure of spacetimes (black holes, cosmologies). Penrose also proved the first singularity theorem (1965, Unit XV), introduced twistor theory (reformulating GR using complex geometry of twistor space), and made foundational contributions to quantum mechanics and consciousness. He received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2020 for the singularity theorem.

**Hendrik Antoon Lorentz (1853–1928)**
Dutch physicist who made the Lorentz transformation (connecting inertial frames) and contributed to the theory of the electron. In the context of complex analysis: Lorentz studied the dispersion of electromagnetic waves through matter. The Kramers-Kronig dispersion relations (Section 12.4.2) are named partly after him through the Lorentz oscillator model that Kramers and Kronig applied.

**Hendrik Anthony Kramers (1894–1952) and Ralph Kronig (1904–1995)**
Dutch and German-American physicists respectively. Kramers (1926) and Kronig (1926) independently derived the dispersion relations connecting the real and imaginary parts of the susceptibility, based on the analyticity of the response function in the upper half-plane (causality). The Kramers-Kronig relations are used in optics, spectroscopy, condensed matter physics, and (through the analogous relations for scattering amplitudes) in particle physics and GR.

## Modern Complex Geometry

**Charles Ehresmann (1905–1979)**
French mathematician who developed the theory of fiber bundles and introduced the modern notion of a connection — the mathematical structure underlying gauge fields in physics and the covariant derivative in GR. The holomorphic structure of a complex manifold (a manifold modeled on ℂⁿ with holomorphic transition functions) is the setting for Kähler geometry, which generalizes Riemannian geometry and has applications to string theory and complex algebraic geometry.

**Shiing-Shen Chern (1911–2004)**
See Chapter 8. Chern's work on characteristic classes uses complex differential geometry to extract topological invariants from curvature. For a complex manifold, the Chern classes are characteristic classes of the holomorphic tangent bundle. The Chern numbers of the Calabi-Yau manifolds used in string theory are topological invariants that constrain the physics of string compactifications.
