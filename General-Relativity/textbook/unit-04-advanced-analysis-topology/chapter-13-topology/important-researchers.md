## Founders of Topology

**Henri Poincaré (1854–1912)**
See also Chapter 10. Poincaré founded algebraic topology with his 1895 paper "Analysis situs," introducing the fundamental group, homology groups, and the Euler characteristic as topological invariants. His Poincaré conjecture — every simply connected compact 3-manifold is homeomorphic to S³ — remained open for over a century and was finally proved by Perelman in 2002–2003. Poincaré also introduced the concept of a manifold (a space that is locally Euclidean), the notion of a topological invariant, and the qualitative theory of ODEs, all of which are essential tools for this textbook.

**Felix Hausdorff (1868–1942)**
German mathematician who introduced the modern definition of a topological space with separation axioms in his 1914 *Grundzüge der Mengenlehre*. The Hausdorff condition (T₂) — requiring distinct points to have disjoint neighborhoods — is named after him. He also introduced the Hausdorff metric and made fundamental contributions to set theory. Hausdorff was Jewish; he and his wife took their own lives in January 1942 rather than be deported to a concentration camp.

**Leonhard Euler (1707–1783)**
See also earlier chapters. In topology: Euler proved in 1736 that the Königsberg bridge problem has no solution, effectively founding graph theory and discrete topology. He also discovered the Euler characteristic V − E + F = 2 for convex polyhedra — the topological invariant that equals 2 for all surfaces homeomorphic to S².

**August Ferdinand Möbius (1790–1868)**
German mathematician and astronomer who introduced the Möbius strip (1858) — a non-orientable surface with only one side and one boundary component. The Möbius strip is the canonical example of a non-orientable manifold. Non-orientability has physical implications: physical laws (especially involving chirality, like the Standard Model) may distinguish orientations.

## Algebraic Topology

**Emmy Noether (1882–1935)**
See also Chapter 9. In algebraic topology: Noether introduced homology groups (replacing the earlier "Betti numbers") and identified the correct algebraic structure (groups, not just numbers). This made algebraic topology properly algebraic. Noether's ideas, transmitted through her lectures and students, transformed algebraic topology into the modern subject.

**Allen Hatcher (1944–)**
American mathematician at Cornell whose textbook *Algebraic Topology* (2002) is the standard modern reference. Freely available online. His book is notable for its geometric intuition, many examples, and comprehensive treatment of fundamental groups, covering spaces, homology, and cohomology.

**John Milnor (1931–)**
American mathematician who discovered exotic spheres in 1956: 7-dimensional manifolds homeomorphic to S⁷ but not diffeomorphic to it (there are 28 exotic 7-spheres). This showed that smooth structure is more subtle than topological structure — a discovery that changed both topology and differential geometry. Milnor received the Fields Medal (1962) and the Abel Prize (2011) for contributions including exotic spheres, K-theory, and dynamical systems.

## GR and Global Topology

**Roger Penrose (1931–)**
See also Chapter 12. Penrose's 1965 singularity theorem, using the compactness of trapped surfaces, proved that spacetime singularities are unavoidable inside black holes (and in the past of the expanding universe). He introduced conformal compactification (Penrose diagrams) for visualizing spacetime topology. His twistor theory reformulates GR using complex geometry of twistor space. He received the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics "for the discovery that black hole formation is a robust prediction of the general theory of relativity."

**Stephen Hawking (1942–2018)**
See also Chapter 12. In GR topology: Hawking's singularity theorems established that a Big Bang singularity in the past is unavoidable for an expanding universe with physically reasonable matter content. With Ellis (1973), Hawking wrote the comprehensive treatment of GR global structure. With Penrose, he extended Penrose's techniques to prove the most general singularity theorem (Hawking-Penrose, 1970). Hawking also proposed the "no-boundary" boundary condition for the wave function of the universe — a topological statement about the beginning of time.

**Robert Geroch (1942–)**
American theoretical physicist who proved (1970) that global hyperbolicity is equivalent to the existence of a Cauchy surface, and that globally hyperbolic spacetimes have topology ℝ × Σ. He also showed that topology change in classical GR requires singularities or closed timelike curves. Geroch's results established the topological structure of well-behaved spacetimes in GR.

**Yvonne Choquet-Bruhat and Robert Geroch (1969)**: proved the existence of a unique maximal Cauchy development for the Einstein equations, placing the global well-posedness of GR on firm mathematical ground. (See also Chapter 11.)

## Singularity Theorems

**Roger Penrose (1965)**: The first singularity theorem. Uses compactness of trapped surfaces and the focusing of null geodesics (Raychaudhuri equation) to prove geodesic incompleteness inside black holes.

**Stephen Hawking (1966–1970)**: A series of increasingly powerful singularity theorems, culminating in the Hawking-Penrose theorem (1970) that applies to both black holes and cosmological singularities. Hawking's theorems use compact Cauchy surfaces (or closely related conditions) rather than trapped surfaces.

**Robert Penrose and Stephen Hawking together** showed that classical GR inevitably predicts singularities — both in the future (black holes) and in the past (Big Bang). This established the "singularity problem" as a fundamental issue requiring quantum gravity for resolution.

## Ricci Flow and 3-Manifold Topology

**Richard Hamilton (1943–)**
See also Chapter 11. Hamilton introduced Ricci flow (1982) and proved the geometrization conjecture for 3-manifolds with positive Ricci curvature, showing they are spherical space forms. His program to use Ricci flow to prove the full geometrization conjecture was completed by Perelman. Hamilton's technique of "surgery" (cutting out neighborhoods of singularities in the Ricci flow) was a key innovation.

**Grigori Perelman (1966–)**
See also Chapter 11. Perelman proved the Poincaré conjecture and the full geometrization conjecture using Hamilton's Ricci flow, adding the entropy functional, reduced volume, and surgery analysis. He also proved the soul conjecture in Riemannian geometry, completing another long-open problem. Perelman declined both the Fields Medal and the Millennium Prize (\$1M). His three preprints (2002–2003) are among the most important mathematical papers of this century.
