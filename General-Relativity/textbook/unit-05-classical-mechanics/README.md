# Unit V: Classical Mechanics

---

## Unit Introduction

Classical mechanics is the theory of how bodies move under the influence of forces. Newton's formulation — F = ma, three laws, universal gravitation — is familiar from introductory physics. But for the path to GR, the Newtonian formulation is the wrong framework. The right framework is **Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics**, formulated in the language of variational principles and symplectic geometry.

The reason is philosophical as much as technical. Newtonian mechanics asks: what force acts on this particle, and how does it accelerate? Lagrangian mechanics asks: what trajectory makes the action stationary? The second question generalizes to field theories, to constrained systems, and — most importantly — to GR, where the "action" is the Einstein-Hilbert functional and the "trajectory" is the spacetime geometry itself.

Hamiltonian mechanics introduces phase space — the space of positions and momenta — and the Poisson bracket structure that is the classical precursor of the commutator in quantum mechanics. The flow on phase space generated by the Hamiltonian is a symplectomorphism: it preserves the symplectic structure ω = dp ∧ dq. This geometric structure on phase space is a finite-dimensional prototype of the geometric structure of quantum field theory.

This unit also introduces **Noether's theorem** — the profound connection between symmetries and conservation laws. Every differentiable symmetry of the action corresponds to a conserved quantity: time translation → energy conservation; space translation → momentum conservation; rotation → angular momentum conservation. In GR, Noether's theorem applied to diffeomorphism invariance gives the contracted Bianchi identity, which in turn implies the covariant conservation of the stress-energy tensor.
