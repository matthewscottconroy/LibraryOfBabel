# Chapter 14: Further Reading and References

## Primary Sources

**Newton, I. (1687). *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica.* London: Royal Society.**
The founding document of classical mechanics. Book I develops the mathematical mechanics (orbital theory, central forces, Kepler's problem). Book III applies it to the solar system. The famous Scholium on absolute space and time appears in Book I. Translated into English by Andrew Motte (1729) and revised by Florian Cajori (1934). Newton's prose is dense and geometric — he deliberately avoided algebra to appeal to classical geometric readers — but the logical structure is extraordinarily clear.

**Galileo Galilei (1638). *Discorsi e Dimostrazioni Matematiche Intorno a Due Nuove Scienze.* Leiden.**
The foundation of kinematics: uniform acceleration, free fall, projectile motion. The first statement of the equivalence of inertial mass and gravitational mass (all bodies fall with the same acceleration in vacuum). Translated as *Two New Sciences* by Henry Crew and Alfonso de Salvio (1914).

**Cavendish, H. (1798). "Experiments to determine the density of the Earth." *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society*, 88, 469–526.**
The Cavendish torsion balance experiment: the first laboratory measurement of $G$. Cavendish's language is to "weigh the Earth" — he reports the Earth's mean density, from which $G$ follows. The paper is a model of careful experimental physics: systematic error analysis, multiple trials, independent checks.

**Eötvös, R.V. (1890). "Über die Anziehung der Erde auf verschiedene Substanzen." *Mathematische und naturwissenschaftliche Berichte aus Ungarn*, 8, 65–68.**
The first precise test of the weak equivalence principle (WEP): gravitational and inertial masses are equal for different materials to 1 part in $10^9$. Updated by: **Eötvös, R.V., Pekár, D., and Fekete, E. (1922). *Annalen der Physik*, 68, 11–66** (sensitivity $5 \times 10^{-9}$).

**Braginsky, V.B. and Panov, V.I. (1971). "Verification of the equivalence of inertial and gravitational mass." *Soviet Physics JETP*, 34, 463–466.**
Improved the Eötvös experiment to $10^{-12}$ precision using a torsion pendulum with aluminum and platinum test masses in the gravitational field of the Sun. The best laboratory tests of WEP.

## Standard Textbook References

**Goldstein, H., Poole, C., and Safko, J. (2002). *Classical Mechanics*, 3rd ed. Addison-Wesley.**
The standard graduate textbook in classical mechanics. Comprehensive coverage of Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics, canonical transformations, action-angle variables, small oscillations. The GR student will use this book extensively — it bridges Newtonian and relativistic mechanics at exactly the right level.

**Landau, L.D. and Lifshitz, E.M. (1976). *Mechanics*, 3rd ed. Pergamon. (Volume 1 of *Course of Theoretical Physics*.)**
The most elegant classical mechanics text. Shorter and more incisive than Goldstein. Particular strengths: the Lagrangian method, conservation laws, the principle of least action, and the two-body problem. The pedagogy is to start from the Lagrangian, not Newton's laws — a genuinely different pedagogical philosophy that connects more directly to field theory and GR.

**Arnol'd, V.I. (1978). *Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics.* Springer (2nd ed. 1989).**
The mathematical foundations: symplectic geometry, Poisson manifolds, integrable systems, KAM theory. For the student who wants to understand classical mechanics at the deepest mathematical level. Chapter 2 on the principle of least action; Chapter 9 on the Poincaré-Cartan integral invariant and Liouville's theorem.

**Thirring, W. (1992). *Classical Mathematical Physics: Dynamical Systems and Field Theories*, 3rd ed. Springer.**
A rigorous modern treatment that unifies classical mechanics and classical field theory. The language of differential geometry (manifolds, forms, flows) is used throughout — excellent preparation for GR.

## Historical and Philosophical

**Barbour, J. (1989). *Absolute or Relative Motion? Vol. 1: The Discovery of Dynamics.* Cambridge University Press.**
A historical study of the debate between absolute and relational views of space and time, from Aristotle through Newton and Leibniz to Mach. Essential reading for understanding why Einstein felt compelled to go beyond Newton.

**Jammer, M. (1961). *Concepts of Mass in Classical and Modern Physics.* Harvard University Press.**
The conceptual history of mass: inertial mass, gravitational mass, relativistic mass, rest mass. How our understanding of "mass" has evolved from Newton through Einstein through quantum field theory.

**Schlamminger, S. et al. (2008). "Test of the equivalence principle using a rotating torsion balance." *Physical Review Letters*, 100, 041101.**
The most precise current test of the WEP: $|(\eta_{Be} - \eta_{Ti})| < 1.8 \times 10^{-13}$. The apparatus is a rotating torsion balance at the University of Washington (the "Eöt-Wash" group). A landmark measurement.

## Connections to GR

**Misner, C.W., Thorne, K.S., and Wheeler, J.A. (1973). *Gravitation.* W.H. Freeman.**
Chapter 12: "Newtonian gravity in the language of curved spacetime." Chapter 1: "Geometrodynamics in brief" — the geodesic deviation equation, tidal forces as curvature. These chapters make explicit the connection between Newtonian mechanics and GR that we have emphasized throughout this chapter.

**Will, C.M. (2014). "The confrontation between general relativity and experiment." *Living Reviews in Relativity*, 17, 4.**
The comprehensive review of experimental GR tests, including the WEP, post-Newtonian parameters, and solar system tests. Freely available online at *Living Reviews in Relativity*. Updated periodically. Essential reference for anyone interested in how well GR is tested.
