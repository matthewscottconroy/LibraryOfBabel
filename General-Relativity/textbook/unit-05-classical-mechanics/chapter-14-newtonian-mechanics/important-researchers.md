# Chapter 14: Important Researchers

---

**Isaac Newton (1643–1727)**
Invented calculus, proved Kepler's laws from the inverse-square law, formulated mechanics in the *Principia*, and discovered universal gravitation. The *Principia* (1687) is arguably the most important scientific work ever published. Newton was reclusive and difficult; his priority disputes with Leibniz (over calculus) and Hooke (over the inverse-square law) were bitter. He spent more years on alchemy and biblical chronology than on physics. His three laws and his law of universal gravitation remained the foundation of physics for 228 years, until Einstein's GR.

**Galileo Galilei (1564–1642)**
The father of modern physics: established kinematics experimentally (inclined planes, pendulums, projectile motion), confirmed the equivalence of inertial and gravitational mass (all bodies fall at the same rate), and used telescopic observations to support the Copernican system. His trial by the Inquisition for defending heliocentrism (1633) is one of history's most famous conflicts between science and authority. His *Two New Sciences* (1638) established the mathematical study of motion.

**Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716)**
Invented calculus independently of Newton (his notation $dy/dx$ and $\int$ is what we use today). Challenged Newton's absolute space with a relational view: only relative positions are physically meaningful. His critique, developed in the Leibniz-Clarke correspondence (1715–1716), anticipates Mach's principle and, ultimately, Einstein's general covariance. Also invented binary arithmetic, developed formal logic, and corresponded with virtually every intellectual in Europe.

**Ernst Mach (1838–1916)**
Physicist and philosopher of science who argued that Newton's absolute space was a metaphysical fiction — only observable relative motions matter. Proposed that inertia arises from the interaction of a body with the totality of distant matter ("Mach's principle"). Einstein explicitly credited Mach's critique of Newton as a major inspiration for GR. Mach stubbornly refused to accept the atomic theory of matter even after it was established — a reminder that even brilliant critics can be wrong.

**Joseph-Louis Lagrange (1736–1813)**
Developed analytical mechanics (*Mécanique analytique*, 1788), replacing Newton's geometric approach with algebra and calculus. Introduced generalized coordinates, the Lagrangian $L = T - V$, and the Euler-Lagrange equations. His work made mechanics applicable to any coordinate system and any set of constraints. Also made major contributions to number theory, algebra, and celestial mechanics. Lagrange's formulation is the direct precursor of the principle of least action, Noether's theorem, and the action principle of field theory.

**William Rowan Hamilton (1805–1865)**
Reformulated mechanics in terms of phase space and the Hamiltonian function (*On a General Method in Dynamics*, 1834–1835). Discovered quaternions (a generalization of complex numbers) — the first non-commutative algebra, discovered by walking across a bridge in Dublin and carving the key formula into the stone. Hamilton's equations are the foundation of statistical mechanics, quantum mechanics, and the Hamiltonian formulation of GR (ADM formalism).

**Leonhard Euler (1707–1783)**
Euler derived the equations of rigid body rotation, fluid dynamics, and the calculus of variations (including the Euler-Lagrange equations). The most prolific mathematician of all time: his collected works fill 70 volumes. Despite going nearly blind in his 40s, he continued to produce mathematics at an accelerated rate. His work on the calculus of variations (1744) established the foundation for Hamilton's principle.

**Henry Cavendish (1731–1810)**
Measured the gravitational constant $G$ for the first time (1798) using a torsion balance. Also discovered hydrogen ("inflammable air"), measured the composition of water and air, and anticipated Ohm's law and Coulomb's law by decades (but published very little). A famously reclusive figure who reportedly communicated with his household staff only by written notes.

**Roland Eötvös (1848–1919)**
Hungarian physicist who made the first precise tests of the equivalence principle (1890–1909), showing that inertial and gravitational mass are equal to 1 part in $10^9$ for different substances. The Eötvös torsion balance became the standard instrument for gravitational surveys. His results directly motivated Einstein's search for a theory in which equivalence of inertial and gravitational mass is exact.

**Emmy Noether (1882–1935)**
Although primarily known for abstract algebra (Noetherian rings), her 1918 theorem on symmetry and conservation laws is one of the deepest results in mathematical physics. Barred from academic positions in Germany because of her sex, she worked unpaid at Göttingen for years. Fled to the United States in 1933 when the Nazis dismissed Jewish faculty. Einstein called her "the most significant creative mathematical genius thus far produced." Her theorem — every continuous symmetry gives a conservation law — is invoked in every chapter of this textbook.
