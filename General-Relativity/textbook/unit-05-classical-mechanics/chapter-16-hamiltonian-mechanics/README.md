# Chapter 16: Hamiltonian Mechanics

---

## Chapter Introduction

The Lagrangian approach is elegant: a single scalar function $L(q^i, \dot{q}^i, t)$ contains all the dynamics. But the equations of motion are second-order differential equations in $q^i$ — you need to specify both $q^i(t_0)$ and $\dot{q}^i(t_0)$ as initial conditions.

William Hamilton's reformulation in 1833–1834 makes a simple but profound substitution: instead of velocities $\dot{q}^i$, use momenta $p_i = \partial L/\partial\dot{q}^i$ as the fundamental variables. The state of the system is then a point $(q^i, p_i)$ in **phase space** — a $2n$-dimensional space. The equations of motion become *first-order*. The fundamental object is the **Hamiltonian** $H(q^i, p_i, t)$ — the energy expressed in terms of position and momentum.

The Hamiltonian formulation seems like a minor technical change. But it reveals the deep geometric structure of classical mechanics: phase space has a natural symplectic structure, and the time evolution of any physical quantity is generated by the Poisson bracket. This structure is the foundation of quantum mechanics (canonical quantization replaces Poisson brackets with commutators), statistical mechanics (Liouville's theorem), and chaos theory. The Hamiltonian formalism also connects most directly to quantum field theory and to the Hamiltonian (ADM) formulation of GR.

---

## The Legendre Transform

The passage from Lagrangian to Hamiltonian mechanics is a **Legendre transform**: a change of variables from $(q^i, \dot{q}^i)$ to $(q^i, p_i)$.

Given $L(q^i, \dot{q}^i, t)$, define:
$$p_i = \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}^i}$$

Assuming this can be solved for $\dot{q}^i = \dot{q}^i(q^j, p_j, t)$ (which requires the Hessian $\partial^2 L/\partial\dot{q}^i\partial\dot{q}^j$ to be nonsingular), define:
$$H(q^i, p_i, t) = p_i\dot{q}^i - L(q^i, \dot{q}^i, t)$$

where $\dot{q}^i$ is expressed in terms of $p_i$ via the inverse of the momentum relation.

**For a standard kinetic term** $T = \frac{1}{2}g_{ij}(q)\dot{q}^i\dot{q}^j$:
$$p_i = g_{ij}\dot{q}^j \implies \dot{q}^i = g^{ij}p_j$$
$$H = p_i g^{ij}p_j - \frac{1}{2}g_{ij}g^{ik}p_k g^{jl}p_l + V = \frac{1}{2}g^{ij}p_ip_j + V$$

where $g^{ij}$ is the inverse of the metric $g_{ij}$ on configuration space. The Hamiltonian is $H = T + V$ (total energy, expressed in terms of momenta).

---

## Hamilton's Equations

From the definition of $H$, taking the total differential:
$$dH = \dot{q}^i dp_i + p_i d\dot{q}^i - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q^i}dq^i - \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}^i}d\dot{q}^i - \frac{\partial L}{\partial t}dt$$

Using $p_i = \partial L/\partial\dot{q}^i$, the $d\dot{q}^i$ terms cancel:
$$dH = \dot{q}^i dp_i - \dot{p}_i dq^i - \frac{\partial L}{\partial t}dt$$

Comparing with $dH = \frac{\partial H}{\partial q^i}dq^i + \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}dp_i + \frac{\partial H}{\partial t}dt$:

$$\boxed{\dot{q}^i = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}, \qquad \dot{p}_i = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q^i}}$$

These are **Hamilton's equations** — $2n$ first-order ODEs in place of $n$ second-order ones.

Also: $\partial H/\partial t = -\partial L/\partial t$. If $L$ has no explicit time dependence, neither does $H$, and $\dot{H} = 0$ — energy is conserved.

---

## Phase Space and Symplectic Geometry

The space of all $(q^i, p_i)$ is **phase space** $\mathcal{P} = T^*Q$ (the cotangent bundle of the configuration space $Q$). A state of the system is a point in phase space; a trajectory is a curve.

The fundamental structure of phase space is the **symplectic form**:
$$\omega = dq^i\wedge dp_i$$

(sum over $i$). This is a closed ($d\omega = 0$), non-degenerate 2-form — the definition of a symplectic manifold.

Hamilton's equations can be written as:
$$\dot{q}^i = \omega^{ij}\frac{\partial H}{\partial x_j}, \quad \text{i.e.,} \quad \dot{x}^a = \omega^{ab}\partial_b H$$

where $x^a = (q^i, p_i)$ and $\omega^{ab}$ is the inverse symplectic matrix:
$$\omega^{ab} = \begin{pmatrix}0 & \delta^i_{\ j} \\ -\delta_i^{\ j} & 0\end{pmatrix}$$

**Canonical transformations**: A transformation $(q^i, p_i)\to(Q^i, P_i)$ is **canonical** (or symplectic) if it preserves $\omega$: $dQ^i\wedge dP_i = dq^i\wedge dp_i$. Canonical transformations are the symmetries of Hamiltonian mechanics. They preserve Hamilton's equations: if $(q, p)$ obey Hamilton's equations with $H$, then $(Q, P)$ obey Hamilton's equations with $H(q(Q,P), p(Q,P))$.

---

## Poisson Brackets

For two functions $f(q^i, p_i, t)$ and $g(q^i, p_i, t)$ on phase space, the **Poisson bracket** is:
$$\{f, g\} = \sum_i\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial q^i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial q^i}\right)$$

**Properties**:
- Antisymmetry: $\{f,g\} = -\{g,f\}$
- Leibniz rule: $\{f,gh\} = \{f,g\}h + g\{f,h\}$
- Jacobi identity: $\{f,\{g,h\}\} + \{g,\{h,f\}\} + \{h,\{f,g\}\} = 0$

The Poisson bracket makes the algebra of smooth functions on phase space into a **Lie algebra**.

**Fundamental brackets**:
$$\{q^i, q^j\} = 0, \quad \{p_i, p_j\} = 0, \quad \{q^i, p_j\} = \delta^i_j$$

**Time evolution**: For any observable $f(q,p,t)$:
$$\frac{df}{dt} = \{f, H\} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial t}$$

$f$ is conserved iff $\{f, H\} = 0$ and $f$ has no explicit $t$-dependence.

Hamilton's equations themselves: $\dot{q}^i = \{q^i, H\}$, $\dot{p}_i = \{p_i, H\}$.

**Connection to quantum mechanics**: Dirac's canonical quantization replaces Poisson brackets with commutators:
$$\{q^i, p_j\} = \delta^i_j \quad\longrightarrow\quad [\hat{q}^i, \hat{p}_j] = i\hbar\delta^i_j$$

This is not a derivation — it is a postulate. But the structural parallel is exact, and it motivates the entire framework of quantum mechanics.

---

## Liouville's Theorem

**Liouville's theorem**: The phase space density $\rho(q^i, p_i, t)$ — the density of a cloud of initial conditions evolving under Hamilton's equations — is preserved along trajectories:
$$\frac{d\rho}{dt} = \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \{\rho, H\} = 0$$

Equivalently: the volume of any region in phase space is preserved under Hamiltonian evolution. The phase space flow is incompressible.

**Geometric meaning**: Liouville's theorem says that the Hamiltonian flow preserves the symplectic form $\omega^n$ (the $n$-th power of $\omega$, which is the phase space volume form). This is a consequence of $\omega$ being closed ($d\omega = 0$).

**Physical significance**: 
- In statistical mechanics: the equilibrium (microcanonical) ensemble is uniform on an energy surface — consistent with Liouville's theorem.
- In optics: Liouville's theorem corresponds to conservation of phase-space volume (étendue) — you cannot focus a beam without increasing its angular spread.
- In accelerator physics: Liouville's theorem constrains the emittance of a particle beam.

**Non-Hamiltonian dissipation**: Systems with friction violate Liouville's theorem (phase space contracts). Dissipative systems are not Hamiltonian.

## The Hamilton-Jacobi Equation

The **Hamilton-Jacobi equation** is a partial differential equation for a function $S(q^i, t)$ (Hamilton's principal function):
$$\frac{\partial S}{\partial t} + H\left(q^i, \frac{\partial S}{\partial q^i}, t\right) = 0$$

If $S$ can be found (as a complete integral), the equations of motion reduce to quadratures:
$$p_i = \frac{\partial S}{\partial q^i}, \quad \beta_i = \frac{\partial S}{\partial\alpha_i}$$

where $\alpha_i$, $\beta_i$ are constants of integration.

**Connection to wave mechanics**: The Hamilton-Jacobi equation is the classical limit of the Schrödinger equation. Writing $\psi = R\,e^{iS/\hbar}$ and taking $\hbar\to 0$ gives $\partial_t S + H(\partial_i S) = 0$. This is the origin of the WKB approximation and of Feynman's path integral.

**Separation of variables**: If $H$ is separable in some coordinate system, $S$ can be written as $S = \sum_i W_i(q^i, \alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n) - Et$, and the problem reduces to $n$ one-dimensional integrals. This is the method of **action-angle variables** — crucial for perturbation theory and for understanding integrable systems.

---

## Action-Angle Variables and Integrable Systems

For a one-dimensional periodic system (like a harmonic oscillator or librating pendulum), define:
$$J = \frac{1}{2\pi}\oint p\,dq$$

(the action, equal to the phase-space area enclosed per cycle). The conjugate **angle variable** $\theta$ evolves uniformly: $\dot\theta = \partial H/\partial J = \omega(J)$. In action-angle variables, $H = H(J)$ (independent of $\theta$), and the motion is simple — uniformly rotating angles.

For an $n$-dimensional integrable system with $n$ independent conserved quantities (in involution, i.e., mutually Poisson-commuting), the motion lies on $n$-dimensional tori in $2n$-dimensional phase space. This is the **Liouville-Arnold theorem**.

**Non-integrable systems and chaos**: Most systems are not integrable. Small perturbations of integrable systems can lead to **chaotic behavior** — sensitive dependence on initial conditions. The KAM (Kolmogorov-Arnold-Moser) theorem describes the structure of phase space near an integrable system: most tori survive small perturbations, but some (with rational frequency ratios) are destroyed. This is the foundation of Hamiltonian chaos theory.

## Important Concepts

- **Hamiltonian**: $H = p_i\dot{q}^i - L$; for standard systems $H = T + V$; energy in terms of $(q,p)$
- **Legendre transform**: Change of variables from $\dot{q}^i$ to $p_i$; the mathematical heart of the Lagrangian-to-Hamiltonian passage
- **Hamilton's equations**: $\dot{q}^i = \partial H/\partial p_i$, $\dot{p}_i = -\partial H/\partial q^i$; first-order ODEs on phase space
- **Phase space**: $T^*Q$; $2n$-dimensional; every point = complete state
- **Symplectic form**: $\omega = dq^i\wedge dp_i$; the geometric structure of phase space; canonical transformations preserve it
- **Poisson bracket**: $\{f,g\}$; encodes dynamics; $df/dt = \{f,H\}$; classical analogue of quantum commutator
- **Liouville's theorem**: Phase space volume is conserved under Hamiltonian flow; incompressible dynamics
- **Hamilton-Jacobi equation**: PDE for principal function $S$; classical limit of Schrödinger equation
- **Action-angle variables**: Natural variables for integrable systems; periodic motion as uniform angular rotation
- **KAM theorem**: Persistence of integrable behavior under small perturbations; foundation of Hamiltonian chaos

## Important Figures

**William Rowan Hamilton** (1805–1865): Reformulated mechanics in phase-space language (1833–1834); introduced characteristic function and Hamilton-Jacobi method; discovered quaternions.

**Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi** (1804–1851): Developed Hamilton-Jacobi theory systematically; proved integrability results.

**Joseph Liouville** (1809–1882): Proved the phase-space volume conservation theorem (1838); also proved Liouville's theorem in complex analysis and found Liouville numbers.

**Andrey Kolmogorov** (1903–1987), **Vladimir Arnold** (1937–2010), **Jürgen Moser** (1928–1999): Proved the KAM theorem (1954–1963) — the most important result in classical mechanics in the 20th century.

**Sofia Kovalevskaya** (1850–1891): Discovered a new integrable case of rigid body rotation; first woman to earn a doctorate in mathematics in modern Europe.

**Thought Experiment T16.1.** *The quantum correspondence.*

Dirac's quantization rule: replace $\{q^i, p_j\} = \delta^i_j$ with $[\hat{q}^i, \hat{p}_j] = i\hbar\delta^i_j$. And replace any function $f(q,p)$ with the corresponding operator $\hat{f}(\hat{q},\hat{p})$.

(a) For the harmonic oscillator $H = p^2/(2m) + m\omega^2 q^2/2$: the classical frequency $\omega$ appears in the phase-space rotation. In quantum mechanics, the energy levels are $E_n = \hbar\omega(n+1/2)$. The quantum frequency $\omega$ is the same. Is this a coincidence?

(b) Classically, $\{A, BC\} = \{A,B\}C + B\{A,C\}$ (Leibniz rule). In quantum mechanics, $[\hat{A}, \hat{B}\hat{C}] = [\hat{A},\hat{B}]\hat{C} + \hat{B}[\hat{A},\hat{C}]$. The structures are identical. What does this suggest about the relationship between classical and quantum mechanics?

(c) The Poisson bracket satisfies the Jacobi identity, making the observables a Lie algebra. The commutator also satisfies the Jacobi identity. Is this structural coincidence accidental, or does it reveal something deep about the relationship between classical and quantum mechanics?
