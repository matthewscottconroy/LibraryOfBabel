# Chapter 16: Important Researchers

---

**Johannes Kepler (1571–1630)**
German mathematician and astronomer. The most passionate advocate of heliocentrism before Galileo. Using Tycho Brahe's precise data on Mars, Kepler spent years computing the orbit, trying circle after circle, before concluding — against his own aesthetic preference — that the orbit was an ellipse. Laws I and II appeared in *Astronomia Nova* (1609); Law III in *Harmonices Mundi* (1619). He spent 11 years on the calculation between the two publications. The discovery of the ellipse required abandoning the Platonic ideal of circular perfection — a conceptual leap as important as the mathematical one.

**Tycho Brahe (1546–1601)**
Danish astronomer who built and operated the finest pre-telescopic observatories in history (on the island of Hven, 1576–1597, and later in Prague). His measurements of planetary positions, accurate to 2 arcminutes, were ten times more precise than any previous data. Without Brahe's data, Kepler could not have discovered the ellipse. Brahe himself rejected heliocentrism, proposing instead the "Tychonic" system (planets orbit the Sun, Sun orbits the Earth). He died before knowing how his data would be used.

**Isaac Newton (1643–1727)**
See Chapter 14. His derivation of Kepler's three laws from the inverse-square law (1687) unified terrestrial and celestial mechanics, proving they obey the same laws.

**Urbain Le Verrier (1811–1877)**
French mathematician and astronomer. In 1846, used perturbation theory to predict the position of Neptune from anomalies in Uranus's orbit — Neptune was found the same evening. In 1859, applied the same methods to Mercury and found the 43 arcsec/century residual. He proposed a hypothetical inner planet "Vulcan" to explain it. No such planet was ever found. He died in 1877, 38 years before Einstein solved the problem.

**Albert Einstein (1879–1955)**
In November 1915, Einstein presented the Einstein field equations to the Prussian Academy of Sciences. In the same month, he computed the perihelion precession of Mercury and got exactly 43 arcsec/century. He is reported to have been so moved by this result that he was unable to work for days. The calculation — a three-line computation once you have the field equations and the Schwarzschild metric — resolved a 56-year-old mystery without introducing any free parameters.

**Simon Newcomb (1835–1909)**
American astronomer who compiled the definitive tables of planetary motion (1895–1900). His analysis of Mercury's observations from 1697 to 1895 gave the precession residual as 43 arcsec/century — the modern value. Newcomb's tables were the authoritative reference that Einstein compared his GR prediction against.

**Karl Schwarzschild (1873–1916)**
Found the first exact solution of the Einstein equations (the Schwarzschild metric) in December 1915, mere weeks after Einstein presented his field equations. He sent the solution to Einstein from the Russian front, where he was serving in the German army. Einstein replied: "I had not expected that one could formulate the exact solution of the problem in such a simple way." Schwarzschild died of an illness contracted at the front in May 1916.

**Joseph Bertrand (1822–1900)**
French mathematician. His 1873 proof that only two force laws give all closed orbits is one of the most elegant results in classical mechanics. He also contributed to probability theory (Bertrand's ballot problem, Bertrand's paradox), combinatorics, and the theory of differential equations.

**Brandon Carter (1942–)**
See Chapter 15. His discovery of the Carter constant (1968) showed that Kerr geodesics are fully integrable, resolved the question of separability for Kerr perturbation theory, and enabled the construction of gravitational waveforms for compact binary inspiral — the signals detected by LIGO.

**Andrea Ghez (1965–) and Reinhard Genzel (1952–)**
Led the teams (at UCLA and at the Max Planck Institute for Extraterrestrial Physics, respectively) that measured the orbits of stars within 0.1 parsec of the galactic center, demonstrating the presence of a $4\times10^6 M_\odot$ black hole (Sagittarius A*). The stellar orbits follow Kepler's laws to high precision, with the Schwarzschild precession detectable for the star S2 (GR precession first reported 2020). Shared the Nobel Prize in Physics 2020 (with Roger Penrose).
