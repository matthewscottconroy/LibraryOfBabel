## Primary Sources

**Weyl, H. (1918). "Gravitation und Elektrizität." *Sitzungsberichte der Königlich Preußischen Akademie der Wissenschaften*, 465–480.**
Weyl's first attempt to unify gravity and electromagnetism via a "scale invariance" (hence "gauge" — from German *Eich*, calibration). He showed that extending GR to include local scale freedom requires introducing a new field — which he identified with the electromagnetic potential. Einstein pointed out a physical objection (atomic spectra would depend on their history), and the theory was abandoned as a unification program. However, in 1929 Weyl realized the correct interpretation: the freedom is not scale invariance but *phase invariance* of the quantum wavefunction.

**Weyl, H. (1929). "Elektron und Gravitation." *Zeitschrift für Physik*, 56, 330–352.**
The paper that established the modern form of gauge theory. Weyl showed that the U(1) phase invariance of the Dirac equation, when made local, requires introducing the electromagnetic field $A_\mu$ as the gauge field. The coupling $D_\mu = \partial_\mu - ieA_\mu/\hbar$ follows from demanding local invariance. Every modern gauge theory descends from this paper.

**Aharonov, Y. and Bohm, D. (1959). "Significance of Electromagnetic Potentials in the Quantum Theory." *Physical Review*, 115, 485–491.**
Predicts that a charged particle can detect the magnetic flux through a region even if the fields vanish everywhere along its path. The phase shift $\Delta\phi = e\Phi_B/\hbar$ depends on the potential, not the field. This demonstrated that $A_\mu$ has physical content beyond $F_{\mu\nu}$.

**Dirac, P.A.M. (1931). "Quantized Singularities in the Electromagnetic Field." *Proceedings of the Royal Society of London A*, 133, 60–72.**
Introduces the magnetic monopole as a consistent quantum-mechanical object, provided the Dirac quantization condition $qg = n\hbar c/2$ holds. Shows that the existence of even one monopole in the universe would explain the quantization of electric charge. One of the most elegant papers in 20th-century physics.

**Yang, C.N. and Mills, R.L. (1954). "Conservation of Isotopic Spin and Isotopic Gauge Invariance." *Physical Review*, 96, 191–195.**
Extends gauge theory from the Abelian group U(1) to the non-Abelian group SU(2). The resulting non-Abelian gauge fields (gluons, W and Z bosons in the modern formulation) are the building blocks of the standard model of particle physics.

**Tonomura, A. et al. (1986). "Evidence for Aharonov-Bohm effect with magnetic field completely shielded from electron wave." *Physical Review Letters*, 56, 792–795.**
Definitive experimental confirmation of the Aharonov-Bohm effect using a toroidal magnet that confines the field completely. The experiment rules out all alternatives based on field leakage. Uses electron holography in a field-emission electron microscope.

## Textbooks

**Ryder, L.H. (1996). *Quantum Field Theory.* 2nd ed. Cambridge University Press.**
Chapter 2 on Lagrangian field theory; Chapter 3 on canonical quantization; Chapter 7 on gauge theories. Develops the connection between gauge invariance, minimal coupling, and the structure of QED. The best introductory treatment of gauge theory in the QFT context.

**Peskin, M.E. and Schroeder, D.V. (1995). *An Introduction to Quantum Field Theory.* Addison-Wesley.**
The standard graduate QFT text. Chapter 15 on non-Abelian gauge theories; Chapter 16 on QCD. The derivation of Feynman rules from the Yang-Mills Lagrangian.

**Nakahara, M. (2003). *Geometry, Topology and Physics.* 2nd ed. IOP Publishing.**
Chapter 10 on connections on fiber bundles; Chapter 11 on characteristic classes; Chapters 12–13 on gauge theory and general relativity from the fiber bundle perspective. The most complete treatment of the mathematical underpinnings of gauge theory for physicists.

**Eguchi, T., Gilkey, P.B., and Hanson, A.J. (1980). "Gravitation, Gauge Theories and Differential Geometry." *Physics Reports*, 66, 213–393.**
The original review article explaining the fiber bundle formulation of gauge theories and GR to a physics audience. Still the clearest exposition of the mathematical analogy.

**Wald, R.M. (1984). *General Relativity.* University of Chicago Press.**
Appendix B on spinors; Appendix C on fiber bundles and connections. Concise treatment of GR in the principal bundle language.

## On the Conceptual Status of Gauge Invariance

**Healey, R. (2007). *Gauging What's Real: The Conceptual Foundations of Contemporary Gauge Theories.* Oxford University Press.**
Philosophical analysis of what gauge freedom means physically. Chapter 3 on holonomies and the Aharonov-Bohm effect; Chapter 4 on whether gauge potential or holonomy is the fundamental physical quantity.

**O'Raifertaigh, L. (1997). *The Dawning of Gauge Theory.* Princeton University Press.**
Historical development of gauge invariance from Weyl (1918) through the Yang-Mills revolution (1954) to modern gauge theories.
