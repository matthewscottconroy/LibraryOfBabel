# Chapter 18: Important Researchers

---

**Hermann Weyl (1885–1955)**
German mathematician and physicist. His 1918 attempt to unify gravity and electromagnetism via local scale invariance failed physically (Einstein pointed out that atomic spectra would depend on history), but introduced the idea that physical law should be invariant under *local* transformations — the germ of all modern gauge theories. In 1929, Weyl identified the correct interpretation: the gauge freedom in quantum mechanics is the local phase of the wavefunction, and electromagnetism is the gauge field enforcing this local invariance. His paper "Elektron und Gravitation" (1929) founded the modern theory of gauge fields. Also made fundamental contributions to group theory (Weyl group, Weyl character formula), differential geometry (Weyl tensor, Weyl spinors), and the foundations of mathematics. One of the most versatile mathematicians of the 20th century.

**Yakir Aharonov (1932–)**
Israeli-American physicist. With David Bohm, predicted (1959) that the electromagnetic vector potential has physical effects even where the fields vanish — the Aharonov-Bohm effect. This challenged the classical view that only fields (not potentials) are physical and demonstrated that gauge theory has observable topological content. Aharonov has continued to develop the foundations of quantum mechanics, including the theory of weak measurements and the two-state vector formalism.

**David Bohm (1917–1992)**
American physicist who worked in England and Brazil. Co-predicted the Aharonov-Bohm effect (1959). Better known for his 1952 interpretation of quantum mechanics (Bohmian mechanics or pilot wave theory), an explicitly non-local hidden-variable theory that reproduces all quantum mechanical predictions. Bell's theorem (1964) was directly motivated by Bohm's reformulation of the EPR paradox. Bohm was blacklisted in the McCarthy era, moved to Brazil then to England, and spent the rest of his career at Birkbeck College, London.

**Paul Adrien Maurice Dirac (1902–1984)**
British physicist, one of the founders of quantum mechanics and quantum field theory. His 1931 paper on magnetic monopoles is the origin of the Dirac quantization condition $qg = n\hbar c/2$ and the prediction that the existence of monopoles would explain charge quantization. Also introduced the Dirac equation (1928, first relativistic quantum equation for electrons), positrons (predicted 1928, discovered 1932), and the formalism of quantum mechanics (bra-ket notation, the Dirac delta function, quantization of the electromagnetic field). Nobel Prize in Physics 1933 (with Schrödinger).

**Chen-Ning Yang (1922–)**
Chinese-American physicist. With Robert Mills (1954), extended gauge theory from Abelian U(1) to non-Abelian groups, founding the theory of Yang-Mills fields. This was initially dismissed as unphysical (the gauge bosons would be massless, like photons, but no massless spin-1 particles had been seen). The resolution — spontaneous symmetry breaking giving mass to gauge bosons via the Higgs mechanism — came a decade later. The Yang-Mills action forms the theoretical core of the entire standard model of particle physics. Nobel Prize 1957 (with T.D. Lee) for the discovery of parity violation in weak interactions.

**Robert Mills (1927–1999)**
American physicist. Co-invented (with Yang, 1954) the non-Abelian gauge theory that became the foundation of the standard model. In the original Yang-Mills paper, both authors were postdoctoral researchers at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton. Mills went on to a career at Ohio State University. The Yang-Mills equations are now a central object in both theoretical physics and pure mathematics — the Yang-Mills millennium problem (existence and mass gap for Yang-Mills theories in 4D) is one of the seven Clay Mathematics Institute Millennium Prize Problems.

**Akira Tonomura (1942–2012)**
Japanese physicist and electron microscopist at Hitachi. Led the experimental program that provided definitive confirmation of the Aharonov-Bohm effect (1986) using electron holography with completely shielded magnetic toroids. Also performed landmark experiments demonstrating the single-particle buildup of electron interference patterns (directly confirming the statistical interpretation of quantum mechanics) and Abrikosov vortex dynamics in superconductors. His work showed that electron holography could be used as a precision probe of quantum phase.

**Élie Cartan (1869–1951)**
See Chapter 23. His reformulation of differential geometry using moving frames ("repères mobiles") is the geometric language in which the analogy between gauge theory and GR is most naturally expressed. Cartan's connection forms $\omega^a_{\ b}$ and curvature forms $\Omega^a_{\ b} = d\omega^a_{\ b} + \omega^a_{\ c}\wedge\omega^c_{\ b}$ are the precise GR analogs of $A_\mu$ and $F_{\mu\nu}$.

**Tullio Regge (1931–2014)**
Italian physicist and mathematician. Developed Regge calculus (1961) — a discrete formulation of GR using simplicial complexes — which can be viewed as a lattice gauge theory of gravity. Also contributed to the classification of scattering amplitudes (Regge poles) and to the mathematical foundations of gauge theory. His work anticipated many connections between gauge theory and topology that became central in the 1980s.
