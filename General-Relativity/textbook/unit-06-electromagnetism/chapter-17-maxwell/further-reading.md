# Chapter 17: Further Reading and References

---

## Primary Sources

**Maxwell, J.C. (1865). "A Dynamical Theory of the Electromagnetic Field." *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London*, 155, 459–512.**
The paper unifying electricity, magnetism, and optics. Maxwell presents his equations (in a form different from the modern compact presentation — he used 20 equations for 20 unknowns) and derives that the speed of electromagnetic waves equals the measured speed of light, concluding that light is electromagnetic. One of the greatest single papers in the history of physics.

**Maxwell, J.C. (1873). *A Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism.* 2 vols. Clarendon Press, Oxford.**
The full systematic development of Maxwell's theory, including the electromagnetic stress tensor, radiation pressure, and the energy of the electromagnetic field. The source from which Heaviside and Hertz later extracted the compact four-equation form.

**Hertz, H. (1888). "Über die Ausbreitung der elektrischen Kraft." *Annalen der Physik*, 34, 551–569.**
Experimental demonstration of electromagnetic waves — specifically radio waves — with finite propagation speed. Measured wavelength and frequency and confirmed $c = \lambda f$. The experimental vindication of Maxwell's prediction, made 23 years after the theory.

**Faraday, M. (1831). "Experimental Researches in Electricity." *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London*, 122, 125–162.**
The discovery of electromagnetic induction. Faraday showed that changing magnetic flux through a circuit induces an EMF. He conceptualized field lines and the idea that the electromagnetic field has physical reality — not merely a mathematical convenience.

**Ørsted, H.C. (1820). "Experiments on the Effect of a Current of Electricity on the Magnetic Needle." *Annals of Philosophy*, 16, 273–276.**
The discovery that electric current produces a magnetic field — the first evidence that electricity and magnetism are related phenomena. Ørsted's discovery prompted Ampère's mathematical analysis and eventually Maxwell's synthesis.

**Poynting, J.H. (1884). "On the transfer of energy in the electromagnetic field." *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London*, 175, 343–361.**
Derives the theorem now bearing his name: energy flows in the electromagnetic field in the direction of $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}$, with flux density $|\mathbf{S}| = |\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}|/\mu_0$.

## Textbooks

**Griffiths, D.J. (2017). *Introduction to Electrodynamics.* 4th ed. Cambridge University Press.**
The standard undergraduate text. Clear, rigorous, and beautifully written. Chapter 8 on energy and momentum in the electromagnetic field; Chapter 9 on electromagnetic waves; Chapter 10 on potentials and fields; Chapter 11 on radiation. The best first textbook on electromagnetism.

**Jackson, J.D. (1999). *Classical Electrodynamics.* 3rd ed. Wiley.**
The graduate-level reference. Comprehensive treatment of everything electromagnetic. Chapter 6 on Maxwell's equations, macroscopic media, and charge conservation; Chapter 7 on plane waves; Chapter 9 on radiation from accelerating charges; Chapter 14 on radiation reaction. The standard graduate electrodynamics text for decades.

**Landau, L.D. and Lifshitz, E.M. (1975). *The Classical Theory of Fields.* 4th ed. Butterworth-Heinemann (Course of Theoretical Physics, vol. 2).**
The relativistic treatment from the start. Derives electrodynamics as the unique theory of a massless spin-1 field consistent with special relativity. Chapters 1–6 on special relativity; Chapters 7–11 on the electromagnetic field; Chapters 13–16 on electromagnetic waves and radiation. The most elegant treatment of classical field theory.

**Misner, C.W., Thorne, K.S., and Wheeler, J.A. (1973). *Gravitation.* W.H. Freeman.**
Chapter 3 on the geometry of special relativity; Chapter 5 on stress-energy tensor; §3.4 on the electromagnetic stress-energy tensor. Essential for the connection between EM and GR.

## On the Differential Forms Formulation

**Burke, W.L. (1985). *Applied Differential Geometry.* Cambridge University Press.**
Develops electrodynamics entirely in the language of differential forms, emphasizing physical intuition. Chapter 6 on EM forms. Less standard than Misner-Thorne-Wheeler but very readable.

**Misner, C.W., Thorne, K.S., and Wheeler, J.A. (1973). *Gravitation.* Chapter 4.**
"Electromagnetism and Differential Forms." The first systematic presentation of $F = dA$, $dF = 0$, $d\star F = \mu_0\star J$ in a physics textbook.

## On the History of Electromagnetism

**Darrigol, O. (2000). *Electrodynamics from Ampère to Einstein.* Oxford University Press.**
The definitive historical study of how electrodynamics developed from Ampère through Maxwell to the confrontation with mechanics that produced special relativity. Chapters 1–3 on Ampère and Faraday; Chapters 4–6 on Maxwell; Chapter 7 on Hertz; Chapters 8–9 on Lorentz and Poincaré.

**Hunt, B.J. (1991). *The Maxwellians.* Cornell University Press.**
How Oliver Heaviside, Heinrich Hertz, and other "Maxwellians" took Maxwell's 20-equation Treatise and reformulated it into the four compact equations now taught in physics. The modern form of Maxwell's equations is largely due to Heaviside (1884).
