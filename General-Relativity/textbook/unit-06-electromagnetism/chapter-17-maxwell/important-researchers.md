# Chapter 17: Important Researchers

---

**James Clerk Maxwell (1831–1879)**
Scottish physicist, one of the three greatest physicists of all time alongside Newton and Einstein. Unified electricity, magnetism, and optics in his 1865 paper and 1873 Treatise. His equations predicted electromagnetic waves with speed $c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}$ — identical to the measured speed of light — and he concluded that light is electromagnetic radiation. Also made fundamental contributions to statistical mechanics (Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution, the Maxwell-Boltzmann equations), thermodynamics (Maxwell relations, Maxwell's demon thought experiment), and color photography (first color photograph, 1861). Died of abdominal cancer at 48, having compressed a lifetime of discoveries into a short career.

**Michael Faraday (1791–1867)**
English physicist and chemist, son of a blacksmith, largely self-educated. Discovered electromagnetic induction (1831), the magneto-optical effect (1845), and diamagnetism. He conceptualized the "field" as a physical reality — field lines as real objects carrying energy — rather than as a mathematical convenience. Without Faraday's physical intuition about fields, Maxwell's mathematical synthesis would not have been possible. Faraday was arguably the greatest experimentalist in the history of physics. His experimental notebooks, running to thousands of entries, are still read today for their directness and honesty.

**Heinrich Hertz (1857–1894)**
German physicist who experimentally demonstrated electromagnetic waves in 1887–1888 using oscillating electrical circuits. He measured the wavelength and frequency of radio waves and confirmed that their product gave $c$. The unit of frequency (Hz) is named for him. Hertz also made the first observation of the photoelectric effect (light ejecting electrons from metals), which Einstein later explained in terms of photons (1905). Died of granulomatosis at 36.

**Hans Christian Ørsted (1777–1851)**
Danish physicist who discovered in 1820 that an electric current deflects a magnetic compass needle — the first experimental evidence that electricity and magnetism are related. This discovery prompted Ampère's mathematical analysis and set the stage for Maxwell's synthesis 45 years later.

**André-Marie Ampère (1775–1836)**
French mathematician and physicist. Immediately upon hearing of Ørsted's discovery (1820), Ampère developed a complete mathematical theory of the force between current-carrying conductors. The Ampère-Maxwell law (with displacement current) is one of Maxwell's four equations. The unit of electric current (A) is named for him.

**John Henry Poynting (1852–1914)**
English physicist who derived the theorem (1884) showing that electromagnetic energy flows in the direction of $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}$. The Poynting vector $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}/\mu_0$ is the key to understanding electromagnetic energy transport and the radiation reaction force.

**Oliver Heaviside (1850–1925)**
English self-taught engineer and physicist. Reformulated Maxwell's 20-equation theory into the four compact vector equations now standard in physics textbooks (1884). Independently developed vector calculus. Introduced many concepts now standard in electromagnetic theory: the Poynting vector (simultaneously with Poynting), telegrapher's equations, and skin effect. Also predicted the ionosphere (Heaviside-Kennelly layer) from electrical considerations. Worked in near-total obscurity; his manuscripts were rescued from his gas-lit rooms.

**Hendrik Antoon Lorentz (1853–1928)**
Dutch physicist. Developed the electron theory of electromagnetism — identifying the carriers of electric charge as small particles (electrons, later discovered by Thomson in 1897). Derived the Lorentz transformation of electrodynamics (1904), which became the foundation of special relativity. Showed that Maxwell's equations are invariant under these transformations while Newtonian mechanics is not. Nobel Prize in Physics 1902 (with Zeeman). Mentored Einstein and was enormously respected — Einstein called him "the greatest and noblest man of our times."

**Karl Friedrich Gauss (1777–1855)**
German mathematician (the "prince of mathematics"). Gauss's law $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = \rho/\varepsilon_0$ is one of Maxwell's equations. His deeper contributions to electromagnetism include the systematic development of potential theory and the theory of Earth's magnetic field. His 1835 notes (unpublished) contain early versions of what became the theory of retarded potentials — he understood that electromagnetic effects propagate at finite speed, though he never published this insight.

**Albert Einstein (1879–1955)**
See Chapter 19. His 1905 special relativity paper was motivated directly by the incompatibility of Newtonian mechanics with Maxwell's electrodynamics. The starting point: Maxwell's equations are the same in all inertial frames (they predict the same speed $c$ for light in all frames), but Newtonian mechanics says velocities add. Einstein resolved the contradiction by modifying mechanics rather than electrodynamics. In the same year, his paper on the photoelectric effect introduced the photon — the quantum of the electromagnetic field. Nobel Prize 1921 for the photoelectric effect.
