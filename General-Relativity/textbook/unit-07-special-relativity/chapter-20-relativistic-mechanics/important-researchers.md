# Chapter 20: Important Researchers

---

**Albert Einstein (1879–1955)**
See Chapter 19. The mass-energy equivalence $E = mc^2$ appeared in his 1905 follow-up paper to SR, three pages long: if a body emits light of energy $L$, its mass decreases by $L/c^2$. The general statement $E_0 = mc^2$ for the rest energy followed naturally. Einstein spent several years working out the full relativistic mechanics: 4-momentum, the energy-momentum relation $E^2 = p^2c^2 + m^2c^4$, and relativistic dynamics.

**Hermann Minkowski (1864–1909)**
See Chapter 19. His reformulation introduced the 4-vector formalism that made relativistic mechanics transparent. The 4-momentum $p^\mu = (E/c, \mathbf{p})$ is the natural relativistic generalization of the 3-momentum, and the mass-shell condition $p_\mu p^\mu = -m^2c^2$ is the geometric statement of the energy-momentum relation.

**Arthur Holly Compton (1892–1962)**
American physicist. His 1923 paper on X-ray scattering treated photons as relativistic particles with momentum $p = h/\lambda$ and used relativistic 4-momentum conservation to derive the Compton shift formula $\Delta\lambda = (h/m_e c)(1-\cos\theta)$. The agreement with experiment was decisive proof that photons carry momentum and that relativistic mechanics applies to radiation-matter interactions. Nobel Prize in Physics 1927 (with Wilson).

**Louis de Broglie (1892–1987)**
French physicist whose 1924 Ph.D. thesis introduced the wave-particle duality for matter: if photons have momentum $p = h/\lambda$, then particles with momentum $p$ have an associated wavelength $\lambda = h/p$. This is the de Broglie relation, which follows naturally from treating a particle's 4-momentum $p^\mu = \hbar k^\mu$ where $k^\mu$ is the 4-wavevector. Nobel Prize in Physics 1929.

**Enrico Fermi (1901–1954)**
Italian-American physicist. Developed the theory of beta decay (1934) using relativistic quantum mechanics — the first application of the stress-energy framework to weak interactions. Led the construction of the first nuclear reactor (Chicago Pile-1, 1942), directly demonstrating the conversion of nuclear binding energy ($E = mc^2$) to heat. The Fermi-Dirac statistics for fermions (1926, simultaneously with Dirac) and Fermi acceleration (1949) are also named for him.

**J. Robert Oppenheimer (1904–1967)**
American physicist and director of the Manhattan Project at Los Alamos. With George Volkoff, derived the Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff (TOV) equation (1939) for hydrostatic equilibrium in neutron stars — the GR generalization of the stellar structure equation, in which the stress-energy tensor (including pressure as a source of gravity) limits the maximum mass. The TOV equation is a direct application of the Einstein equations with the relativistic perfect fluid stress-energy tensor.

**Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar (1910–1995)**
Indian-American astrophysicist. Derived the Chandrasekhar limit (1931): a white dwarf supported by electron degeneracy pressure has a maximum mass of $\approx 1.44 M_\odot$ (in GR). Above this, the star collapses to a neutron star or black hole. The derivation requires relativistic mechanics (electrons become relativistic at high density, changing the equation of state). Nobel Prize in Physics 1983 (shared with Fowler).

**Richard Feynman (1918–1988)**
American physicist. Developed the path integral formulation of quantum mechanics (1948), which is the natural relativistic generalization of classical mechanics. The Feynman diagrams that visualize particle interactions are explicitly covariant: each line represents a particle with 4-momentum $p^\mu$, each vertex represents a local interaction. Feynman's formulation made manifest the connection between the relativistic stress-energy tensor and the propagators of quantum field theory. Nobel Prize in Physics 1965 (with Tomonaga and Schwinger) for QED.

**John Archibald Wheeler (1911–2008)**
American physicist who coined the terms "black hole" (1967), "wormhole" (1957), and "it from bit" (1990). With Misner and Thorne, authored the monumental *Gravitation* (1973). Wheeler's "geometrodynamics" program — the attempt to build all of physics from the geometry of spacetime — connects the stress-energy tensor to the curvature of spacetime as a two-way street. His dictum "mass tells spacetime how to curve, spacetime tells mass how to move" captures the Einstein equations in one sentence.
