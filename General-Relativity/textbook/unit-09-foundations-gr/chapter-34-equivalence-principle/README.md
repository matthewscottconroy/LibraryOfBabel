# Chapter 34: The Equivalence Principle

---

## Chapter Introduction

Galileo is supposed to have dropped two balls from the top of the Leaning Tower of Pisa — a heavy one and a light one — and shown that they fell at the same rate. Whether or not the story is true, the observation it represents is real. In 1592, Galileo demonstrated experimentally that all objects fall with the same acceleration in a gravitational field, independent of their mass or composition.

For Newton, this was a coincidence — a puzzling one. In his mechanics, there are two entirely different concepts of mass. *Inertial mass* $m_i$ appears in Newton's second law $\mathbf{F} = m_i\mathbf{a}$: it measures a body's resistance to acceleration, its "quantity of matter" in the dynamical sense. *Gravitational mass* $m_g$ appears in Newton's law of gravity $\mathbf{F} = -Gm_g M/r^2\,\hat{r}$: it measures a body's response to the gravitational field, its "gravitational charge." There is no reason, a priori, why these two quantities should be equal. Yet they are, to one part in $10^{15}$ or better.

For Einstein, this was not a coincidence. It was a clue — the deepest clue in all of physics. If $m_i = m_g$ exactly (not approximately, but exactly), then the gravitational force is always proportional to the inertial mass, and the acceleration $a = F/m_i = (m_g/m_i)(GM/r^2)$ is independent of the body's mass. This means that *in a gravitational field, all objects are equivalent* — they all move identically. And if all objects move identically in a gravitational field, then a freely falling observer cannot distinguish the gravitational field from the absence of gravity. In a freely falling elevator, the floor and ceiling fall at the same rate; there is no tendency for things to fall relative to the elevator. For that observer, gravity has disappeared.

This is Einstein's equivalence principle, and it led him, over the course of eight years (1907–1915), to the general theory of relativity. If gravity can be "switched off" locally by choosing a freely falling frame, then gravity cannot be a force in the usual sense — it must be a property of the coordinate system, of the reference frame, of the *geometry*. The freely falling frame is the inertial frame; the fixed frame (standing on the surface of the Earth) is the accelerating frame. What we call "gravity" is the pseudoforce that appears in the accelerated frame.

More precisely: if a freely falling elevator is equivalent to an inertial frame in empty space, then the locally observable physics in that elevator must be the physics of special relativity. The metric in the elevator is $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ (the Minkowski metric). But the elevator is only small — over large regions, the elevator accelerates differently at different altitudes (tidal forces), and these are the unmistakable signature of genuine curvature. Gravity is the curvature of spacetime; the equivalence principle says that this curvature is locally zero in any freely falling frame.

This chapter develops the equivalence principle from its experimental basis (Galileo, Eötvös, modern tests) through its Einstein reformulation (locally equivalent to inertia) to its precise mathematical expression (the existence of normal coordinates, the vanishing of Christoffel symbols in a freely falling frame). We examine what the principle implies: that light bends in a gravitational field, that clocks run slower in a gravitational potential, and that there is no global reference frame in which gravity disappears. The principle constrains the theory and simultaneously motivates it.
