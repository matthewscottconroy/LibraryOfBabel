# Chapter 35: The Einstein Field Equations

---

## Chapter Introduction

The greatest equation in physics has ten components. It reads:
$$G_{\mu\nu} = \frac{8\pi G}{c^4}T_{\mu\nu}$$

On the left: the Einstein tensor $G_{\mu\nu} = R_{\mu\nu} - \frac{1}{2}g_{\mu\nu}R$ — a combination of the Riemann curvature tensor and its contractions, built from second derivatives of the metric. On the right: the stress-energy tensor $T_{\mu\nu}$ — the energy density, momentum density, and stress of all matter and radiation in spacetime.

The equation says: curvature = matter. The distribution of energy and momentum tells spacetime how to curve; the curvature of spacetime tells matter and light how to move. Wheeler's epigram captures it: *"Spacetime tells matter how to move; matter tells spacetime how to curve."*

But how did Einstein arrive at this equation? And why does it have exactly this form and no other?

The path from the equivalence principle (1907) to the field equations (November 1915) took Einstein eight years. It was not a simple deduction. He had the right physical idea — gravity is curvature — from the beginning. What he lacked was the right mathematics. In 1912, his friend Marcel Grossmann pointed him to the Ricci-Levi-Civita tensor calculus, the differential geometry of Riemannian manifolds. With this tool, Einstein could write generally covariant equations.

But writing down the right equation from the mathematical possibilities took three more years. He published incorrect versions (with errors in the Bianchi identity, in the Newtonian limit, and in the degrees of freedom). The final equation emerged on November 25, 1915 — five days after Hilbert independently derived the same equation from a variational principle.

The equations have a beauty that obscures their complexity. They are ten coupled nonlinear partial differential equations for the ten components of the symmetric metric tensor $g_{\mu\nu}$. In full generality, they are almost impossible to solve analytically. But their physical content is profound: they unify Newtonian gravity, the equivalence principle, and the conservation of energy-momentum into a single geometric statement.

This chapter derives the field equations, discusses their structure and degrees of freedom, examines the Newtonian limit, and explores the variational formulation (the Einstein-Hilbert action). We will see why the form $G_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G T_{\mu\nu}$ is not merely plausible but *forced* by symmetry, general covariance, the correct Newtonian limit, and Lovelock's uniqueness theorem.
