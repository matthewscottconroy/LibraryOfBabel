# Chapter 35: Important Researchers

---

**Albert Einstein (1879–1955)**
The creator of general relativity. Eight years (1907–1915) of struggle led from the equivalence principle to the field equations. The November 1915 papers presented to the Prussian Academy are the culmination: the correct generally covariant field equations $G_{\mu\nu} = \kappa T_{\mu\nu}$, the computation of Mercury's perihelion precession (43"/century, matching the observed anomaly exactly), and the correct light deflection prediction (1.75"). In a letter to Arnold Sommerfeld on November 28, 1915: "This past month has been one of the most stimulating and exciting periods of my entire life." Nobel Prize in Physics 1921 (for the photoelectric effect, not GR — the Nobel committee was unwilling to award GR without further observational confirmation).

**David Hilbert (1862–1943)**
German mathematician, arguably the most influential mathematician of the 20th century. Independently derived the Einstein field equations from the Einstein-Hilbert action (November 20, 1915 submission, five days before Einstein's November 25 presentation). The variational derivation — the simplest action for a geometric gravity theory is $\int R\sqrt{-g}\,d^4x$ — is now the standard derivation. Priority dispute: recent scholarship shows Hilbert's original submission had a gap (he hadn't proved the uniqueness needed to identify the gravitational tensor), and both arrived simultaneously. The equations are rightly named Einstein-Hilbert. Hilbert also posed the 23 famous problems (1900) that shaped 20th-century mathematics.

**Marcel Grossmann (1878–1936)**
Swiss mathematician and Einstein's closest friend from ETH Zürich. In 1912, when Einstein was struggling to find the mathematical framework for GR, Grossmann identified the Ricci-Levi-Civita absolute differential calculus (tensor calculus) as the right tool. Co-authored the "Entwurf" paper with Einstein (1913), which was the first attempt at generally covariant field equations (with errors). Without Grossmann's intervention, Einstein might have abandoned GR or found the right equations years later. Grossmann later had a severe illness (multiple sclerosis) that ended his scientific career; he died in 1936.

**Emmy Noether (1882–1935)**
German mathematician. Her theorems on the relationship between symmetries and conservation laws (1918) are crucial to understanding GR: Noether's second theorem shows that the local symmetry (diffeomorphism invariance) of the Einstein-Hilbert action implies the contracted Bianchi identity $\nabla_\mu G^{\mu\nu} = 0$ — the GR conservation law — as an identity. Also contributed to abstract algebra (Noetherian rings). Forced to leave Germany in 1933 by the Nazi persecution of Jewish academics; moved to Bryn Mawr College, Pennsylvania, where she died two years later.

**David Lovelock (1938–2021)**
South African mathematician at the University of Arizona. Proved (1971) the uniqueness theorem that bears his name: in 4D, the unique symmetric, divergence-free, rank-2 tensor built from $g_{\mu\nu}$ and its first two derivatives is $\alpha G_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu}$. This theorem retroactively explains why the Einstein equations are "correct" — they are forced by the mathematical constraints. Lovelock also found the higher-dimensional generalizations (Gauss-Bonnet and Lovelock gravity) that allow additional divergence-free curvature tensors in $n \geq 5$ dimensions.

**Yvonne Choquet-Bruhat (1923–)**
French mathematician. Proved (1952) that the Einstein equations with appropriate matter have a well-posed Cauchy problem — the first rigorous proof of the existence and uniqueness of solutions to the Einstein equations as an initial value problem. This established GR on a firm mathematical foundation. Continued to work on global solutions and singularity structure for decades. First woman elected to the French Academy of Sciences (1979). Her 2009 book *General Relativity and the Einstein Equations* is the definitive mathematical treatment.

**Richard Arnowitt (1928–2014), Stanley Deser (1931–2021), Charles Misner (1932–)**
American physicists who developed the ADM (Arnowitt-Deser-Misner) formalism (1962) — the 3+1 decomposition of GR into spatial metric, lapse, shift, and extrinsic curvature. The ADM formalism is the foundation of numerical relativity, Hamiltonian GR, and quantum gravity (loop quantum gravity and path integral approaches all begin from the ADM decomposition). Also defined the ADM mass and angular momentum of asymptotically flat spacetimes.

**Frans Pretorius (1974–)**
South African physicist. Achieved the first successful long-term numerical simulation of binary black hole merger (2005), solving the full nonlinear Einstein equations. The simulation ran for multiple orbits, merger, and ringdown, producing the gravitational waveform that matches the structure of GW150914. Previous numerical relativity codes had been unstable; Pretorius's breakthrough used a generalized harmonic gauge. Now at Princeton.
