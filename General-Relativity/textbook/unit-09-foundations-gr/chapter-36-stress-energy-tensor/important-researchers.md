# Chapter 36: Important Researchers

---

**J. Robert Oppenheimer (1904–1967)**
American physicist. With George Volkoff, derived the Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff equation (1939) for relativistic stellar structure, and computed the maximum mass for a neutron star. In the same year, with Hartland Snyder, showed that a sufficiently massive star undergoing gravitational collapse would form what we now call a black hole — the exterior metric approaches Schwarzschild, and the collapsing matter crosses $r = 2GM/c^2$ in finite proper time. Oppenheimer was the scientific director of the Manhattan Project at Los Alamos. After the war, he was stripped of his security clearance in a politically-motivated hearing (1954). Later director of the Institute for Advanced Study at Princeton.

**George Michael Volkoff (1914–2000)**
Russian-Canadian physicist. Co-authored the TOV equation with Oppenheimer (1939). Went on to a long career at the University of British Columbia, working on nuclear physics and particle physics. The TOV equation that bears his name is the foundation of neutron star structure theory.

**Richard Chace Tolman (1881–1948)**
American physical chemist and cosmologist. Derived the hydrostatic equilibrium equation in GR (1934), independently of and before Oppenheimer-Volkoff. The full equation is correctly called the Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff equation. Also contributed to relativistic thermodynamics and the temperature-pressure relations in GR (the Tolman-Ehrenfest theorem: temperature is not uniform in hydrostatic equilibrium in GR — it is lower where the gravitational potential is lower).

**Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar (1910–1995)**
Indian-American astrophysicist. Derived the Chandrasekhar limit (1931) — the maximum mass for a white dwarf ($\sim 1.44 M_\odot$) — while on a steamship from India to England. The calculation requires relativistic quantum mechanics: at high density, electrons become relativistic, and the pressure support softens (from $p\propto \rho^{5/3}$ non-relativistic to $p\propto\rho^{4/3}$ ultra-relativistic), reducing the maximum supportable mass. Eddington publicly dismissed the result as physically unreasonable ("I think there should be a law of Nature to prevent a star from behaving in this absurd way"). History vindicated Chandrasekhar completely. Nobel Prize in Physics 1983 (shared with Fowler).

**Walter Baade (1893–1960) and Fritz Zwicky (1898–1974)**
German-American and Swiss-American astronomers. Predicted (1934) that supernovae represent the collapse of a star to a neutron star — a compact object with density comparable to nuclear density — releasing $\sim 10^{53}$ erg of gravitational energy, most in neutrinos. The prediction was confirmed 53 years later by the neutrino burst from Supernova 1987A. Zwicky also discovered galaxy clusters and coined the term "dark matter" for the missing mass needed to gravitationally bind them (1933).

**Jocelyn Bell Burnell (1943–)**
British astrophysicist. As a graduate student at Cambridge, discovered the first pulsar (PSR B1919+21) in 1967. The discovery (and subsequent identification of pulsars as rapidly rotating neutron stars) provided the first confirmation of neutron stars and validated the TOV equation's predictions. The Nobel Prize in Physics 1974 was awarded to her supervisor Anthony Hewish and Martin Ryle for this and related work, controversially excluding Bell (widely considered a Nobel oversight).

**Subir Chandrasekhar (1910–1995)** — see above.

**Kip Thorne (1940–)**
American physicist at Caltech, co-founder of LIGO. Made fundamental contributions to the theory of compact objects — neutron stars, black holes, gravitational waves — including the theory of tidal deformability of neutron stars (which determines the gravitational waveform during binary merger). The tidal deformability $\Lambda = k_2(2R^5/3M^5)$ (where $k_2$ is the tidal Love number) was measured from GW170817, constraining the neutron star radius to $11$–$13$ km. Nobel Prize in Physics 2017 (with Weiss and Barish) for LIGO and gravitational wave detection.

**Feryal Özel (1975–) and Paulo Freire (1971–) (and many collaborators)**
Özel and collaborators have made precision measurements of neutron star masses and radii from X-ray observations, constraining the nuclear equation of state. Freire and collaborators have discovered the most massive neutron stars (PSR J0952-0607: $2.35\pm 0.17 M_\odot$), ruling out soft equations of state. These observational constraints, combined with GW170817, are converging on a detailed picture of nuclear matter at supranuclear density.
