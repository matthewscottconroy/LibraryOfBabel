# Section 40.1: The Event Horizon

---

## Section Introduction

The **event horizon** of the Schwarzschild black hole, located at $r = r_s = 2GM/c^2$, is one of the strangest objects in physics: a surface with no local physical significance whatsoever, yet of profound global importance. An infalling observer crosses it without noticing anything special — the curvature at $r_s$ is finite, the tidal forces are finite (and small for large black holes), and no physical signpost marks the boundary. And yet, once crossed, the horizon can never be recrossed: it is a one-way membrane. The future is irrevocably committed.

This one-way character is what makes the event horizon an **event** horizon in the global sense: it is defined not by any local measurement but by the entire causal future of the spacetime. The event horizon is the boundary of the region from which signals can never reach distant observers — equivalently, the boundary of the **causal past** of future null infinity $\mathscr{I}^+$. This global definition means the horizon cannot be located by an observer in finite time: to know whether you have crossed the event horizon, you would need to know the entire future evolution of the spacetime.

The apparent coordinate singularity at $r = r_s$ in Schwarzschild coordinates — where $g_{tt}\to 0$ and $g_{rr}\to\infty$ — is precisely that: a coordinate artifact. Transforming to **Eddington-Finkelstein** or **Kruskal-Szekeres** coordinates removes the singularity, revealing the event horizon as a null hypersurface (a surface whose normal vector is null: $g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu r\partial_\nu r = 0$ at $r = r_s$). Null hypersurfaces have a unique and unusual geometry: they are simultaneously tangent to and perpendicular to themselves.

An important practical consequence for astrophysics: an observer watching matter fall into a black hole never sees it cross the horizon. The infalling matter appears to asymptotically freeze at $r_s$ and its light is infinitely redshifted. An observer who falls in, by contrast, crosses the horizon in finite proper time and — for a large enough black hole — notices nothing unusual at the moment of crossing.
