# Section 40.2: Black Hole Thermodynamics

---

## Section Introduction

In 1973, Bardeen, Carter, and Hawking noticed a remarkable analogy between the laws of classical black hole mechanics and the laws of thermodynamics. They formulated four laws of black hole mechanics, each parallel to a thermodynamic law, with the surface gravity $\kappa$ playing the role of temperature and the horizon area $A$ playing the role of entropy. At the time, this was regarded as a mathematical curiosity — a formal analogy with no physical content, since a classical black hole absorbs everything and emits nothing, so its temperature should literally be zero.

The analogy became physical in 1974, when Hawking proved that quantum fields in the Schwarzschild geometry produce thermal radiation at a temperature $T_H = \hbar\kappa/(2\pi ck_B)$ — the **Hawking temperature**. For Schwarzschild, $\kappa = c^4/(4GM)$, so $T_H = \hbar c^3/(8\pi GMk_B)$. The laws of black hole mechanics are not merely an analogy: they are the laws of thermodynamics, applied to a new type of thermodynamic system.

The **entropy** of a black hole is $S_{\rm BH} = k_B A/(4\ell_P^2)$ where $\ell_P = \sqrt{\hbar G/c^3}$ is the Planck length. This is the **Bekenstein-Hawking entropy**. Its magnitude is extraordinary: a solar-mass black hole has entropy $\sim 10^{77}k_B$, comparable to the entropy of the observable universe. The entropy is proportional to area (a 2D quantity), not volume (a 3D quantity) — this is the origin of the **holographic principle**: the maximum entropy of a region of space is proportional to its boundary area, suggesting that the fundamental degrees of freedom of quantum gravity are encoded on surfaces.

The microscopic origin of black hole entropy — what quantum gravitational microstates does $e^{S_{\rm BH}/k_B}$ count? — is one of the deepest open questions in theoretical physics. String theory provides a partial answer for extremal charged black holes (Strominger-Vafa 1996); loop quantum gravity provides another. But a complete answer requires a theory of quantum gravity.
