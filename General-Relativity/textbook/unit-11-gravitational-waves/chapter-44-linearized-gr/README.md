# Chapter 44: Linearized General Relativity and Gravitational Waves

---

## Chapter Introduction

The full nonlinear Einstein equations are extraordinarily difficult to solve. But for weak gravitational fields — when the metric differs only slightly from flat Minkowski spacetime — the equations linearize into a wave equation, and the solutions are gravitational waves: ripples in the fabric of spacetime that propagate at the speed of light.

Gravitational waves were predicted by Einstein in 1916, one year after the field equations. He derived them from linearized GR and showed that accelerating masses radiate gravitational energy, just as accelerating charges radiate electromagnetic energy. The energy radiated per unit time is given by the quadrupole formula:
$$P = \frac{G}{5c^5}\dddot{Q}_{ij}\dddot{Q}^{ij}$$
where $Q_{ij} = \int\rho(x_ix_j - \frac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}r^2)d^3x$ is the mass quadrupole tensor.

For 50 years, the existence of gravitational waves remained controversial — not because of any observational evidence against them, but because of persistent confusion about whether they carried "real" energy or were a gauge artifact. This was resolved in the 1950s–1960s (by Bondi, Robinson, Trautman, Pirani, and others), and the indirect evidence for their existence came from the Hulse-Taylor binary pulsar (1974–1993).

The direct detection came on September 14, 2015, when LIGO detected the gravitational wave signal GW150914 — the merger of two black holes 1.3 billion light-years away. The signal was a chirp lasting $\sim 0.2$ seconds, sweeping from 35 Hz to 150 Hz, with a peak strain of $\sim 10^{-21}$. The event released $\sim 3 M_\odot c^2$ of energy in gravitational waves in $\sim 0.01$ seconds — briefly exceeding the combined electromagnetic luminosity of all stars in the observable universe.

This chapter develops linearized GR, derives the gravitational wave equation, identifies the two polarization states, and presents the quadrupole formula for gravitational wave emission.
