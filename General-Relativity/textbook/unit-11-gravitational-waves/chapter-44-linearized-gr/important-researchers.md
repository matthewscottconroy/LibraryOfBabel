# Chapter 44: Important Researchers

---

**Albert Einstein (1879–1955)**
Einstein derived the linearized gravitational wave equation from his field equations in November 1916, just one year after the final field equations themselves. His 1916 paper showed that accelerating masses produce ripples in spacetime propagating at the speed of light, and that the radiation rate depends on the third time derivative of the quadrupole moment. The 1916 paper contained a factor-of-2 error in the quadrupole formula that he corrected in 1918 — the corrected formula stands today. Einstein subsequently had doubts about whether gravitational waves were real or coordinate artifacts, and in the 1930s briefly co-authored (with Nathan Rosen) a paper claiming GWs did not exist. The paper was withdrawn after a referee (Howard Robertson) identified an error. This episode illustrates how even the deepest physical intuition can be mislead by the complexity of GR's gauge freedom. Einstein himself never expected to see gravitational waves detected experimentally.

**Hermann Bondi (1919–2005)**
Austrian-British mathematician and cosmologist. Resolved the decades-long controversy over gravitational wave energy with his analysis of "news functions" at null infinity (1962). The Bondi mass decreases monotonically as a system radiates, proving that gravitational waves carry genuine energy away from an isolated system. The Bondi-Sachs formalism for asymptotically flat spacetimes became the foundation for modern GW theory. Bondi is also famous for steady-state cosmology (with Hoyle and Gold) and for the sticky bead argument (attributed sometimes to Feynman but also discussed by Bondi).

**Richard Feynman (1918–1988)**
Feynman's "sticky bead" argument at the 1957 Chapel Hill Conference on the Role of Gravitation in Physics broke the impasse over GW energy. His argument: a bead threaded on a rough rod would slide back and forth as a GW passed, generating heat; since heat is frame-independent, the GW must have carried real energy. Though not a derivation, this physical argument convinced many who had remained confused by the mathematics. Feynman was at Chapel Hill as a skeptical outsider to GR and left more convinced than he arrived.

**Richard Isaacson (born 1942)**
Derived the effective stress-energy tensor for gravitational waves (the "Isaacson tensor," 1968), providing a rigorous, covariant formula for GW energy density and flux that is valid beyond the linearized theory. The averaging over several wavelengths (the "Isaacson averaging") makes the procedure well-defined in curved spacetime. This settled the mathematical foundation for computing GW energy fluxes.

**Joseph Weber (1919–2000)**
Inventor of the resonant bar ("Weber bar") gravitational wave detector. In the 1960s, Weber built the first operating gravitational wave detectors — cylinders of aluminum $\sim 1$ m long tuned to their resonant frequency, with piezoelectric crystals to detect vibrations. In 1969, he announced coincident detections at his Maryland and Argonne labs, claiming gravitational wave detection. The community spent years trying to reproduce the results, failed, and concluded his analysis was flawed. Nevertheless, Weber had proved that serious experimental GW physics was possible. His work inspired the next generation of detectors and established the field as experimental science. He was never able to accept that his results were not reproducible.

**Rainer Weiss (born 1932)**
Proposed the laser Michelson interferometer as the foundation for gravitational wave detection in his 1972 MIT Quarterly Progress Report. His analysis was quantitative: he estimated all major noise sources (seismic, thermal, shot noise) and showed that with 1-km arms, a laser interferometer could reach sensitivity $h \sim 10^{-21}$ — the level needed to detect astrophysical sources. His proposal was the conceptual and technical blueprint for LIGO. He co-founded LIGO with Drever and Thorne and led the MIT experimental program for decades. Nobel Prize in Physics 2017.

**Kip Thorne (born 1940)**
Led the theoretical program at LIGO and made foundational contributions to understanding gravitational wave sources, black hole perturbation theory, and the theoretical foundations of the detector. His review of GW sources in the 1987 "300 Years of Gravitation" volume laid out the scientific case for LIGO. He also supervised much of the foundational work on matched filtering, template banks, and parameter estimation. Nobel Prize in Physics 2017.

**Barry Barish (born 1936)**
Particle physicist who became LIGO Director in 1994 and transformed it from a small academic project into a large-scale scientific organization. He created the LIGO Scientific Collaboration (LSC), bringing together $\sim 1000$ scientists worldwide. He managed the construction of the two observatories in Hanford, Washington and Livingston, Louisiana and the transition to Advanced LIGO. Nobel Prize in Physics 2017.

**Ronald Drever (1931–2017)**
Scottish experimental physicist who developed key innovations for LIGO at Glasgow and Caltech. His contributions include power recycling cavities (to build up circulating laser power), the design of the Fabry-Perot arm cavities, and techniques for mirror stabilization. Drever was notoriously difficult to work with and was eventually removed from the LIGO project's management, but his technical ideas were central to its design. He died six months after the Nobel Prize was awarded to Weiss, Thorne, and Barish.

**Russell Hulse (born 1950) and Joseph Taylor (born 1941)**
Hulse, then a graduate student, discovered the first binary pulsar PSR B1913+16 in 1974 using the Arecibo radio telescope. Taylor recognized its significance for testing GR. Over the following 20 years, Taylor and collaborators measured the orbital period decrease due to gravitational wave emission and confirmed the quadrupole formula to 0.1% — the first (indirect) observational proof that gravitational waves exist and carry energy. This was the result that convinced the community to invest in LIGO. Nobel Prize in Physics 1993.
