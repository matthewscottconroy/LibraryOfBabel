# Chapter 48: The Standard Cosmological Model ($\Lambda$CDM)

---

## Chapter Introduction

The $\Lambda$CDM model is one of the greatest triumphs of twentieth-century science. With just six free parameters, it accounts for the entire large-scale structure of the universe — the CMB temperature anisotropies to $10^{-5}$ precision, the abundance of light elements from Big Bang nucleosynthesis, the accelerating expansion measured with supernovae, the baryon acoustic oscillations in galaxy surveys, and the growth of cosmic structure from inflation-seeded fluctuations to the cosmic web of filaments and voids we observe today.

The six parameters are: the Hubble constant $H_0$, the baryon density $\Omega_b h^2$, the cold dark matter density $\Omega_{\rm DM} h^2$, the amplitude of primordial perturbations $A_s$, the spectral index $n_s$, and the optical depth to reionization $\tau$. Everything else — the dark energy density $\Omega_\Lambda$, the spatial curvature, the matter power spectrum, the CMB anisotropies — is a derived quantity.

The model has two great mysteries built into it. The first is **dark matter**: 85% of the matter in the universe is invisible, clustering gravitationally but not interacting electromagnetically. We have overwhelming evidence for its existence (galaxy rotation curves, gravitational lensing, cluster dynamics, CMB anisotropies) but no confirmed direct detection of any dark matter particle. The second is **dark energy**: 68% of the universe's energy budget is in a form that causes the expansion to accelerate, with equation of state $w \approx -1$. This could be Einstein's cosmological constant (vacuum energy), but the observed value is $10^{120}$ times smaller than naive quantum field theory predictions.

This chapter describes the key observational pillars supporting $\Lambda$CDM, what dark matter and dark energy are (or might be), and the current tensions and open questions at the frontier of cosmology.

## Important Concepts

**The CMB**: The cosmic microwave background is relic radiation from when the universe cooled to $T \approx 3000$ K at $z \approx 1100$ (recombination), neutral hydrogen formed, and the universe became transparent. The CMB has a perfect blackbody spectrum at $T_0 = 2.7255 \pm 0.0006$ K. The temperature anisotropies $\Delta T/T \sim 10^{-5}$ encode the primordial density perturbations and their evolution. The angular power spectrum $C_\ell$ of these anisotropies contains multiple acoustic peaks whose positions and heights constrain all six $\Lambda$CDM parameters with extraordinary precision.

**Dark matter**: No Standard Model particle accounts for dark matter. Candidates include WIMPs (weakly interacting massive particles, $m \sim$ GeV–TeV), axions ($m \sim 10^{-5}$ eV), sterile neutrinos, primordial black holes, and fuzzy dark matter ($m \sim 10^{-22}$ eV). All direct detection experiments have null results so far. Dark matter is essential for structure formation — without it, the universe would be too smooth (baryons are coupled to photons until recombination and can't collapse).

**Dark energy**: The cosmological constant $\Lambda$ fits all current data with $w = -1$. But is it constant? Dynamic dark energy models ("quintessence") allow $w = w(z)$. The $w_0 w_a$ parameterization $w(a) = w_0 + w_a(1-a)$ is the standard way to test for evolution. Current constraints: $w_0 = -1.03 \pm 0.04$ (consistent with $-1$). The DESI 2024 results hinted at $w_0 > -1$, $w_a < 0$ at $2\sigma$ — if confirmed, this would require new physics beyond $\Lambda$.

**Baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO)**: In the early universe, photon-baryon fluid oscillations created sound waves, leaving a preferred length scale $r_s \approx 150$ Mpc imprinted in both the CMB (angular power spectrum) and galaxy distributions (correlation function). The BAO scale acts as a "standard ruler" for measuring $H(z)$ and $d_A(z)$ across cosmic history. Combined with CMB and SNe Ia, BAO provides the most precise constraints on the expansion history.

**Large-scale structure**: The cosmic web — filaments, sheets, voids, and clusters — formed by the gravitational amplification of tiny primordial perturbations over 13.8 billion years. N-body simulations (Millennium Simulation, IllustrisTNG, FLAMINGO) reproduce the observed distribution with $\Lambda$CDM parameters. The matter power spectrum $P(k)$ encodes the distribution of fluctuations; its shape constrains $\Omega_m$, $H_0$, and the neutrino mass.

**The Hubble tension**: Two independent measurement chains give different values of $H_0$. The CMB+BAO ("early universe") chain gives $H_0 = 67.4 \pm 0.5$ km/s/Mpc. The Cepheid/SNe Ia ("local distance ladder") chain gives $H_0 = 73.0 \pm 1.0$ km/s/Mpc. The discrepancy is now $5\sigma$ — unlikely to be statistical. If not systematic error, it implies new physics beyond $\Lambda$CDM: extra radiation, early dark energy, modified gravity, or something else.

**The $\sigma_8$ tension**: Weak gravitational lensing surveys (KiDS, DES, HSC) measure the amplitude of matter fluctuations on $8h^{-1}$ Mpc scales. Their $S_8 = \sigma_8\sqrt{\Omega_m/0.3}$ values are $2$–$3\sigma$ lower than predicted by Planck CMB. Could indicate modified gravity or unmodeled baryonic feedback.

## Important Figures

**Vera Rubin (1928–2016)**: Provided the first compelling observational evidence for dark matter in galaxies by mapping the rotation curves of spiral galaxies (1970s). Instead of falling off as $v \propto r^{-1/2}$ beyond the stellar disk (as expected from the visible mass), rotation curves are flat: $v \approx \text{const}$ to large radii. This requires unseen mass distributed in a halo extending far beyond the visible galaxy.

**Fritz Zwicky (1898–1974)**: First postulated dark matter (1933) from the velocity dispersion of galaxies in the Coma cluster. The galaxies moved far too fast to be gravitationally bound by the visible matter, implying $\sim 400$ times more mass than observed. His "dunkle Materie" was largely ignored for decades.

**Vera C. Rubin's work, Zwicky's cluster analysis, and X-ray observations of clusters, gravitational lensing, and the Bullet Cluster (Chandra X-ray Observatory, 2006)** together make a strong case: the Bullet Cluster shows dark matter (via lensing) separated from the X-ray-emitting gas after a cluster merger, ruling out most modified gravity alternatives to dark matter.

**James Peebles (born 1935)**: Theoretical architect of physical cosmology. Made foundational contributions to BBN, the CMB, large-scale structure, dark matter dynamics, and galaxy formation. Developed the theory of CMB anisotropies (1965, with Dicke), the correlation function approach to galaxy clustering, and the $\Lambda$CDM model. Nobel Prize in Physics 2019.

**George Smoot (born 1945) and John Mather (born 1946)**: Leaders of the COBE satellite (1989–1993) that measured the CMB blackbody spectrum to extraordinary precision and detected the CMB temperature anisotropies for the first time ($\Delta T/T \sim 10^{-5}$, announced 1992). Nobel Prize in Physics 2006.

## Exercises

**48.1.** *CMB temperature and the blackbody spectrum.*

The CMB has a perfect blackbody spectrum. The spectral intensity as a function of frequency $\nu$ is:
$$I_\nu = \frac{2h\nu^3}{c^2}\frac{1}{e^{h\nu/(k_B T)}-1}$$

(a) Show that the peak frequency of $I_\nu$ is at $h\nu_{\rm peak} = 2.82\,k_B T$ (from $dI_\nu/d\nu = 0$). For $T = 2.7255$ K, compute $\nu_{\rm peak}$ and $\lambda_{\rm peak} = c/\nu_{\rm peak}$.

(b) Compute the total energy density in the CMB photons: $\rho_\gamma c^2 = (4\sigma_{\rm SB}/c)T^4$ where $\sigma_{\rm SB}$ is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant. Express as $\Omega_\gamma h^2$.

(c) At what redshift $z_{\rm eq}^\gamma$ did the CMB photon energy density equal the matter density (ignoring neutrinos)? How does this compare to the full radiation-matter equality $z_{\rm eq} \approx 3400$ (which includes neutrinos)?

**48.2.** *Galaxy rotation curves and dark matter.*

A spiral galaxy has a stellar disk with surface mass density $\Sigma(r) = \Sigma_0 e^{-r/r_d}$ where $r_d = 3$ kpc.

(a) By integrating the Newtonian gravitational acceleration, show that the circular velocity from the disk alone is:
$$v_{\rm disk}^2(r) = 4\pi G\Sigma_0 r_d y^2[I_0(y)K_0(y) - I_1(y)K_1(y)]$$
where $y = r/(2r_d)$ and $I_n$, $K_n$ are modified Bessel functions. (Don't derive this; just use it.)

(b) Observations show $v(r) \approx 220$ km/s out to $r = 20$ kpc (flat rotation curve). Model the dark matter as a Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) profile:
$$\rho_{\rm DM}(r) = \frac{\rho_s}{(r/r_s)(1+r/r_s)^2}$$
Show that for $r \ll r_s$: $v^2 \propto r^2\ln(r)$ (diverges logarithmically), and for $r \gg r_s$: $v^2 \propto 1/r$ (falls off). Flat rotation curves require $r \sim r_s$.

(c) Fit $\rho_s$ and $r_s$ to give $v \approx 220$ km/s at $r = 10$ kpc. What is the total dark matter mass within 50 kpc?

**48.3.** *CMB acoustic peaks and cosmological parameters.*

The positions of the CMB acoustic peaks in multipole space are approximately $\ell_n \approx n\pi d_A(z_*)/r_s$ where $d_A(z_*) = \chi_*/(1+z_*)$ is the angular diameter distance to last scattering ($z_* \approx 1100$), and $r_s \approx 147$ Mpc is the sound horizon at recombination.

(a) Compute the comoving distance $\chi_*$ to last scattering for a flat $\Lambda$CDM universe with $\Omega_m = 0.315$, $\Omega_\Lambda = 0.685$, $H_0 = 67.4$ km/s/Mpc. (Integrate numerically.)

(b) Show that the first acoustic peak is at $\ell_1 \approx 220$ — consistent with Planck observations.

(c) Suppose $\Omega_k = -0.02$ (slightly closed). How would the peak positions shift? Would they move to higher or lower $\ell$?

**Thought Experiment T48.1.** *Dark matter: particle vs. modified gravity.*

The $\Lambda$CDM model requires that 85% of matter be an unknown dark matter particle. An alternative is Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND, Milgrom 1983): at accelerations below $a_0 \approx 1.2\times 10^{-10}$ m/s², the effective gravitational force scales as $F \propto 1/r$ instead of $1/r^2$, naturally producing flat rotation curves.

MOND explains galaxy rotation curves with remarkable accuracy. But it fails on cluster scales (can't explain the Bullet Cluster), lacks a covariant relativistic formulation (though Bekenstein's TeVeS and its successors try), and cannot reproduce the CMB acoustic peaks.

Does the Bullet Cluster definitively rule out MOND in favor of particle dark matter? What would it take to definitively distinguish between the two hypotheses? Is there an observation that would support MOND and refute particle dark matter?

**Thought Experiment T48.2.** *The cosmological constant as vacuum energy.*

The observed cosmological constant $\Lambda = 1.11\times 10^{-52}$ m$^{-2}$ corresponds to a vacuum energy density $\rho_\Lambda c^2 \approx 7\times 10^{-10}$ J/m³. Quantum field theory predicts vacuum energy density from zero-point fluctuations up to the Planck scale: $\rho_{\rm vac} \sim M_{\rm Pl}^4 c^5/\hbar^3 \approx 10^{113}$ J/m³. 

The discrepancy is $10^{123}$ — the worst disagreement between theory and experiment in all of physics.

Why might this not be a "real" disagreement? Is vacuum energy in QFT actually observable (in the Casimir effect, for instance)? Does the Casimir effect measure the absolute vacuum energy or only energy differences? Can you construct an argument that the cosmological constant problem is partly a conceptual confusion rather than a pure numerical discrepancy?
