Every proof technique is a recipe for constructing an object. That object — the proof — is not a persuasive essay or a confident gesture toward truth. It is a formal artifact whose correctness can, in principle, be verified mechanically, step by step, by something that neither understands nor cares about the mathematics involved. The machine-checkability of proofs is not a limitation; it is the whole point.

What this section makes explicit is that each technique — direct proof, contrapositive, contradiction, cases — corresponds to a rule for manipulating logical connectives. Direct proof constructs implications by assuming their hypotheses. Proof by contradiction uses the principle of explosion. The correspondence between techniques and connectives is the Curry-Howard isomorphism in its most elementary form: every proof strategy is a program, and every program strategy is a proof method. The type-theoretic reading is noted alongside each technique not as an aside but as a parallel grammar you are learning simultaneously.

You should leave this section with a specific habit of mind: before writing a proof, identify which connective or quantifier in your goal governs the structure of the argument. That identification tells you which technique to use. The techniques are not a bag of tricks but a complete case analysis on the shape of logical formulas.
