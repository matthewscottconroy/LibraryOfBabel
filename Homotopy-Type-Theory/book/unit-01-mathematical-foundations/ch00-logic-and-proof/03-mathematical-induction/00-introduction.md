Induction is not merely a proof technique — it is a revelation about how mathematical structures are built. When you prove something by induction, you are exploiting the fact that the natural numbers are generated from a single starting point by repeated application of a single operation. The proof mirrors the construction. This is not coincidence; it is the deep reason why induction works at all, and why it generalizes so far.

The surprise, which type theory makes precise, is that induction and recursion are the same thing viewed from different angles. In dependent type theory, the natural numbers are *defined* as the type whose eliminator is exactly the induction principle. A function defined by recursion on ℕ and a proof constructed by induction on ℕ are syntactically the same kind of term — differing only in whether the output is a data type or a proposition type. When you write a recursive function in Lean or Coq, you are doing induction. This identification, which may sound like a technical coincidence, turns out to be the reason automated theorem provers can handle inductive reasoning at all.

This section develops induction in increasing generality: simple induction, strong induction, well-founded induction on arbitrary orders, and structural induction on inductively defined data. Each generalization expands the class of structures amenable to the same core idea. By the end, you will have the vocabulary to recognize induction wherever it appears — which is everywhere.
