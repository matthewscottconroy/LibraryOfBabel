The gap between propositional logic and mathematical practice is enormous. Propositions like "every prime greater than two is odd" or "for every ε there exists a δ" require quantifiers ranging over domains of individuals — precisely what propositional logic cannot express. Predicate logic closes this gap, and in doing so becomes the language in which virtually all of classical mathematics can be stated.

What is non-obvious here is the depth of the type-theoretic reinterpretation. The universal quantifier ∀x:A. P(x) becomes, under Curry-Howard, the dependent product type — a function that takes each element a of A and produces a proof of P(a). The existential quantifier ∃x:A. P(x) becomes the dependent sum type — a pair of an element a and a proof of P(a). These are not mere analogies; they are the definitions that Homotopy Type Theory uses. The passage from first-order logic to dependent type theory is exactly the passage from viewing ∀ and ∃ as quantifiers to viewing Π and Σ as type constructors. Understanding predicate logic well — its syntax, its semantics, its proof rules — makes that passage feel inevitable rather than arbitrary.

This section covers the syntax of predicate logic languages (signatures, terms, formulas), the semantics of first-order structures, natural deduction rules for quantifiers, and the completeness theorem. The completeness theorem for first-order logic is harder than its propositional counterpart and carries important philosophical weight: it says that first-order provability and first-order semantic validity coincide. That coincidence breaks at higher orders, and the story of *why* it breaks points directly toward the incompleteness phenomena and the limits of formalization.
