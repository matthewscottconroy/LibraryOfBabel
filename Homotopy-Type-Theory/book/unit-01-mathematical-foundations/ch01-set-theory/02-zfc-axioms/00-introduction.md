When a foundational theory collapses under contradiction, you have two options: abandon the theory, or identify precisely which principles caused the collapse and replace them with something weaker. Zermelo chose the second path. His 1908 axioms do not define what a set *is* — they specify only which constructions are permitted. The result is a system strong enough to do all of ordinary mathematics and (as far as we can tell) free of contradiction.

The ten ZFC axioms are not a random list. Each one sanctions a specific, minimal construction: pairing two existing sets, taking the union of a family, forming the power set, separating a subset by a formula. The crucial restriction — and this is what distinguishes ZF from naive set theory — is that Separation (the safe version of comprehension) requires you to separate elements *from an existing set* rather than collecting them from nowhere. You cannot form {x | φ(x)} in the abstract; you can only form {x ∈ A | φ(x)} for some set A you already have. Russell's paradox requires collecting from the universe itself, which this restriction blocks.

What makes studying ZFC worthwhile for HoTT is not the axioms themselves but the philosophical pressure they reveal. The Axiom of Extensionality says two sets are equal iff they have the same elements. In HoTT, the Univalence Axiom says two types are equal iff they are equivalent — a strictly stronger and more informative principle. Understanding extensionality in ZFC, and the ways it falls short of mathematical practice (isomorphic structures are equal *enough* but not literally equal), makes the motivation for Univalence vivid.
