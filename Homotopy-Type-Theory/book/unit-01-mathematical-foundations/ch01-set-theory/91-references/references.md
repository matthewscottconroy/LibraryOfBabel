# References: Set Theory

## Primary Sources

**Cantor, G. "Ueber eine Eigenschaft des Inbegriffes aller reellen algebraischen Zahlen." *Journal für die reine und angewandte Mathematik* 77 (1874): 258–262.**
The original paper proving the countability of algebraic numbers and the uncountability of the reals, using nested intervals (not yet the diagonal argument). Translated in Ewald (1996). Start here to see what Cantor was actually trying to do before it became "set theory."

**Cantor, G. "Ueber eine elementare Frage der Mannigfaltigkeitslehre." *Jahresbericht der Deutschen Mathematiker-Vereinigung* 1 (1891): 75–78.**
The diagonal argument paper. Two pages. The proof that |𝒫(A)| > |A| for any set A, and that the reals are uncountable. Translated in Ewald (1996). Read this; it is short and completely self-contained.

**Zermelo, E. "Untersuchungen über die Grundlagen der Mengenlehre I." *Mathematische Annalen* 65 (1908): 261–281.**
The first axiomatic set theory. Translated in van Heijenoort (1967). Zermelo introduces Separation, motivates each axiom, and shows that the paradoxes are avoided. The response to critics of the 1904 well-ordering paper is also here.

**Russell, B. "Mathematical Logic as Based on the Theory of Types." *American Journal of Mathematics* 30 (1908): 222–262.**
Russell's type-theoretic response to the paradoxes, before *Principia Mathematica*. Accessible and clearly motivated. Reprinted in van Heijenoort (1967).

**Gödel, K. "The Consistency of the Axiom of Choice and of the Generalized Continuum Hypothesis with the Axioms of Set Theory." *Annals of Mathematics Studies* 3. Princeton: Princeton University Press, 1940.**
Gödel's proof that CH and AC are consistent with ZF, using the constructible universe L. The construction of L and the verification of the axioms in L are explained step by step. Still the clearest source for this material.

**Cohen, P. *Set Theory and the Continuum Hypothesis*. New York: W. A. Benjamin, 1966.**
Cohen's own account of forcing and the independence of CH. More readable than the original papers, though still demanding. The forcing construction is presented from scratch. Essential for understanding what ZFC cannot decide.

## Modern Textbooks

**Jech, T. *Set Theory: The Third Millennium Edition*. Berlin: Springer, 2003.**
The comprehensive reference for modern set theory. Covers everything from the ZFC axioms through large cardinals, forcing, and descriptive set theory. Not a textbook for learning from scratch, but the right place to look up anything about ZFC, ordinals, cardinals, and independence results.

**Kunen, K. *Set Theory: An Introduction to Independence Proofs*. Amsterdam: North-Holland, 1980.**
The standard graduate introduction to set theory and forcing. Kunen's exposition of forcing is particularly clear, and his treatment of consistency results is the model for subsequent textbooks. More accessible than Jech.

**Halmos, P. *Naive Set Theory*. New York: Springer, 1960.**
A short, elegant introduction to set theory at the level of this chapter. Halmos writes beautifully and covers the basic constructions — ordinals, cardinals, transfinite induction — without the full axiomatic apparatus. Perfect for a first pass.

**Devlin, K. *The Joy of Sets: Fundamentals of Contemporary Set Theory*. 2nd ed. New York: Springer, 1993.**
A readable introduction to axiomatic set theory, including the consistency of CH and an account of the basics of forcing. More approachable than Kunen for a first encounter with independence results.

**Ewald, W., ed. *From Kant to Hilbert: A Source Book in the Foundations of Mathematics*. 2 vols. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1996.**
Translations of primary sources in foundations of mathematics, 1780–1930. Includes Cantor's major papers, Dedekind's foundational work, and much else. Indispensable for historical and philosophical context.
