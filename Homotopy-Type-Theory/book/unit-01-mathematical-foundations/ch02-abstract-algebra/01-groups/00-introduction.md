Four axioms define a group, and one of the four — associativity — is at first glance the most boring. It just says that the order of parenthesization does not matter. Yet associativity is the load-bearing axiom: without it, the structure collapses; with it, entire theories of representation, classification, and symmetry become possible. The other three axioms (closure, identity, inverses) say what elements are present. Associativity says how the operation *behaves*, and behavior is what algebra studies.

Groups are the mathematical distillation of symmetry. Every symmetry you have encountered — the rotations of a triangle, the permutations of a set, the invertible transformations of a vector space — is a group, and the group axioms capture exactly what all of these have in common. This abstraction has enormous power: a theorem proved for abstract groups holds simultaneously for rotation groups, permutation groups, Galois groups, and the fundamental group of a topological space. The fundamental group is the connection that matters most here: the symmetries of a space's loop structure form a group, and that group is an algebraic invariant of the space. This is the bridge between algebra and topology that Homotopy Type Theory eventually internalizes as a definition rather than a theorem.

You will see throughout this section that even the simplest consequences of the group axioms require careful proof — the uniqueness of the identity, the uniqueness of inverses, cancellation. This care is not pedantry. It is practice for the kind of formal reasoning that proof assistants require, where nothing is too obvious to need justification.
