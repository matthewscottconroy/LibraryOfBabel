A group, in isolation, is a purely algebraic object. A group action puts that algebra to work: it is the precise formulation of what it means for a group to be the symmetry group of something. The rotations of a triangle are not interesting as an abstract group; they become interesting when they act on the triangle, permuting its vertices, moving its edges, and preserving its overall structure. The action is where the symmetry becomes visible.

The orbit-stabilizer theorem is the central result here, and it is subtler than it first appears. It says that the size of a group equals the product of the size of any orbit and the size of the stabilizer of a point in that orbit. This is a counting fact, but it encodes something structural: the stabilizer is the subgroup of symmetries that fix a point, and the orbit is the coset space G/Stab(x). The theorem says these two quotient structures — the elements of the orbit and the cosets of the stabilizer — are in natural bijection. Counting is just the shadow of this bijection on sizes.

The topological examples in this section — deck transformations of covering spaces, the action of the Galois group on roots — are not decoration. They are the cases that most directly lead to HoTT. A covering space p: E → B with deck transformation group G gives a quotient E/G ≅ B, and the fiber over a point is a G-set. In HoTT, this becomes the statement that a pointed connected type with fundamental group G is the quotient of its universal cover by the G-action. The algebra of group actions, developed here concretely, will reappear as a theorem about identity types.
