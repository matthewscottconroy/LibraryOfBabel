Rings arise when you want a structure that behaves like the integers: two operations, one additive and one multiplicative, interacting through the distributive law. The distributive law is the essential constraint — it is what prevents the two operations from being entirely independent. Nearly every algebraic structure in number theory, algebraic geometry, and representation theory is either a ring, a module over a ring, or a morphism between them, which makes rings one of the most pervasive structures in all of mathematics.

The conceptual move from groups to rings is not merely "add another operation." The passage introduces zero divisors, ideals, quotient rings, and the question of unique factorization — phenomena with no group-theoretic analogue. An integral domain (a commutative ring with no zero divisors) is the setting where arithmetic works "like in ℤ": you can factor elements, cancel, and ask whether factorization is unique. Fields are integral domains where every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse, and they are the setting where linear algebra lives.

Modules — abelian groups with a compatible ring action — unify vector spaces (modules over a field), abelian groups (modules over ℤ), and representations (modules over a group ring). This unification is not just notational convenience; the structure theory of modules, particularly over principal ideal domains, gives you the classification of finitely generated abelian groups and the rational canonical form of matrices as special cases of a single theorem. For HoTT, the relevance is indirect but real: algebraic structures defined by operations and axioms are precisely the kind of structure that type theory encodes as dependent records, and the interplay between carriers and operations is what the type-theoretic notion of algebraic structure captures.
