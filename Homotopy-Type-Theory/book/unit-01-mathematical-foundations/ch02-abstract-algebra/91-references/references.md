# References: Abstract Algebra

## Core Textbooks

**Dummit, D. S., and R. M. Foote. *Abstract Algebra*. 3rd ed. Hoboken: Wiley, 2004.**
The standard graduate algebra textbook. Comprehensive coverage of groups, rings, modules, fields, Galois theory, and representation theory. The exercises are well-curated and range from routine to research-adjacent. The chapter on free groups and group presentations is particularly clear. This is the book to work through if you want to master the material of this chapter in depth.

**Lang, S. *Algebra*. Rev. 3rd ed. New York: Springer, 2002.**
The comprehensive reference for graduate algebra. More abstract and categorical than Dummit-Foote, with excellent coverage of module theory, Galois theory, and algebraic structure theory. Lang's style is dense but precise; the exercises are famous for their difficulty. Essential as a reference once you have some background.

**Artin, M. *Algebra*. 2nd ed. Upper Saddle River: Pearson, 2011.**
An undergraduate-level text with an unusually geometric perspective. Artin emphasizes matrix groups, representations, and connections to geometry throughout. The treatment of the symmetric group and its representations is particularly good. More approachable than Dummit-Foote for a first encounter with abstract algebra.

**Rotman, J. *An Introduction to the Theory of Groups*. 4th ed. New York: Springer, 1995.**
The best dedicated group theory textbook. Covers everything from the basic axioms through the Sylow theorems, free groups and presentations, amalgamated free products, and HNN extensions. The chapter on group presentations and its connections to topology is directly relevant to this curriculum.

## For the Connection to Topology

**Hatcher, A. *Algebraic Topology*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2002. (Also freely available at pi.math.cornell.edu/~hatcher/)**
The standard introduction to algebraic topology. Chapter 1 covers the fundamental group and van Kampen's theorem in detail. The exposition of covering spaces and their connection to group theory is the clearest available. Free online and widely used.

**Massey, W. S. *A Basic Course in Algebraic Topology*. New York: Springer, 1991.**
A careful, classical treatment of the fundamental group, covering spaces, and the Seifert-van Kampen theorem. More leisurely than Hatcher. Good for a first encounter with the material.

**Stillwell, J. *Classical Topology and Combinatorial Group Theory*. 2nd ed. New York: Springer, 1993.**
Treats topology and group theory together, with emphasis on surfaces, fundamental groups, and group presentations. The connection between the presentation of the fundamental group and the topology of surfaces (the classification theorem) is particularly illuminating.

## Primary Sources

**Galois, É. "Mémoire sur les conditions de résolubilité des équations par radicaux." 1832. Published posthumously in *Journal de mathématiques pures et appliquées* 11 (1846): 381–444.**
Galois's foundational paper, written the night before his death. Translated by H. M. Edwards in *Galois Theory* (Springer, 1984). The introduction of normal subgroups and the correspondence between fields and groups are here in embryonic form.

**Noether, E. "Idealtheorie in Ringbereichen." *Mathematische Annalen* 83 (1921): 24–66.**
The paper that established the structural approach to ring theory and introduced Noetherian rings. Not available in English translation, but discussed and contextualized in most histories of algebra.

**Cayley, A. "On the Theory of Groups, as Depending on the Symbolic Equation θⁿ = 1." *Philosophical Magazine* 7 (1854): 40–47.**
The first abstract definition of a group, and the paper containing Cayley's theorem. Short and readable. Available in various historical collections.
