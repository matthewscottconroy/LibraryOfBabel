Distance is so fundamental to spatial intuition that it takes effort to see it as an assumption. The real line has a distance. The plane has a distance. Function spaces have distances — the supremum norm, the L² norm — that make precise what it means for two functions to be "close." Each of these is a different structure on a different set. The metric space axioms identify exactly which properties all these distances share: non-negativity, symmetry, and the triangle inequality. Any set equipped with a function satisfying these three axioms becomes a metric space, and any theorem proved from the axioms alone holds in all of them simultaneously.

The triangle inequality is the non-trivial axiom, and nearly every estimate in analysis reduces to applying it repeatedly. It encodes the geometric fact that the direct route is never longer than any two-leg detour. In a function space, it becomes the statement that the maximum deviation of f from h is at most the sum of the maximum deviations of f from g and g from h — a fact that sounds obvious geometrically and requires proof algebraically.

For the path toward topology and HoTT, the metric space perspective is transitional. Metrics generate open sets, open sets generate topologies, and topologies can be defined without any reference to a metric. The passage from metric spaces to topological spaces is a further act of abstraction: we keep the open sets and discard the function that generated them. Continuity, compactness, and connectedness — the key concepts of analysis — all have definitions that survive this abstraction, and their metric-space versions here are the ground floor.
