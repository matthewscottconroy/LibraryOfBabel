The ε-N definition of convergence is one of the great intellectual achievements in the history of mathematics — not because it is difficult, but because it is the right way to make precise a concept that mathematicians used informally for centuries without being sure what they meant. A sequence converges to L if you can force the terms arbitrarily close to L by going far enough out. The alternation of quantifiers — for every ε, there exists N, such that for all n beyond N — is exactly the logical structure of this challenge and response, and learning to read and write that quantifier alternation fluently is one of the core skills of mathematical analysis.

Completeness is where the conceptual surprise lives. A space can fail to contain the limits of its own Cauchy sequences. The rationals are the canonical example: sequences of rationals can converge in every internal sense — the terms approach each other — without converging to any rational number. The limit simply isn't there. Completeness says the space has no such holes. The real numbers are complete; the rationals are not. This is not merely a property that ℝ happens to have — it is the *definition* of ℝ, via Dedekind cuts or Cauchy completions of ℚ. The real numbers are what you get when you fill all the holes.

For the HoTT perspective, completeness is an early example of a notion that type theory handles very differently from set theory. In HoTT, a type can be *n-truncated* — having no non-trivial higher identity proofs — and the process of truncating a type to make it "complete" in this sense is called h-truncation. The parallel with metric completions is more than formal; both are processes of adding missing witnesses that the structure "morally" contains.
