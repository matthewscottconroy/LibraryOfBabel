Compact spaces behave like finite sets in ways that infinite spaces do not. On a compact space, a continuous function achieves its maximum value — it does not merely approach a supremum. Every sequence has a convergent subsequence — you cannot keep running indefinitely without returning near where you started. Every open cover has a finite subcover — you never need infinitely many open patches to cover the whole space. Each of these is the statement of an important theorem, and each theorem fails dramatically without compactness.

The finite subcover definition is the right one, and the argument that explains why is the key to using compactness in practice. When you want to prove a global fact about a compact space, you often start by establishing a local fact at each point — "near every x, the function is bounded," say — which gives you an open cover. Compactness converts that infinite collection of local facts into a single finite collection, and from a finite collection you can extract a global bound by taking the maximum. This "local-to-global" extraction is the universal pattern of compactness arguments.

For the path toward topology and HoTT, the topological definition of compactness — stated entirely in terms of open sets, without reference to any metric — is what survives the abstraction from metric spaces to topological spaces. The Tychonoff theorem (arbitrary products of compact spaces are compact) is equivalent to the Axiom of Choice over ZF, which connects compactness back to the foundational concerns of the set theory chapter. And in HoTT, compactness has a type-theoretic interpretation involving the relationship between types and their universe-level truncations — a connection that becomes visible only later but whose analytic content lives here.
