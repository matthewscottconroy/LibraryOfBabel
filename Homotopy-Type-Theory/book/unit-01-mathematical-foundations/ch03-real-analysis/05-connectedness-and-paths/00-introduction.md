A space is connected if it cannot be split into two disjoint non-empty open pieces. A space is path-connected if any two points can be joined by a continuous curve. These two definitions capture the same intuition — "the space is in one piece" — but they are not equivalent, and the topologist's sine curve stands as a permanent warning: it is connected but not path-connected, a space that cannot be separated by open sets yet cannot be traversed by a continuous path. For "nice" spaces — manifolds, CW complexes, the spaces that appear in algebraic topology — the distinction collapses. For the general theory, it matters.

Path-connectedness is the right notion for homotopy theory, and the reason becomes clear here. A path from x to y is a continuous function γ: [0,1] → X with γ(0) = x and γ(1) = y. Paths compose, reverse, and deform. The collection of homotopy classes of loops at a basepoint forms the fundamental group. The key insight that this section reaches — and that the following section develops fully — is that path-connectedness is not just a property of spaces but the beginning of a whole algebraic hierarchy: the fundamental group is the first level, and higher homotopy groups capture how paths between paths behave.

For HoTT, this section is the conceptual bridge. Path-connectedness here means: for any two points, there exists a path. In HoTT, the statement that a type A is path-connected becomes: for any a, b : A, the identity type a =_A b is inhabited. A path in the topological sense and a term of an identity type are the same object, viewed through different lenses. You are developing the topological intuition that the type-theoretic language will later make precise.
