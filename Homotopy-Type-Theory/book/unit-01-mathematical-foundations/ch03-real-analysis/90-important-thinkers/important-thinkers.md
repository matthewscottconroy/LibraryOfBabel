# Important Thinkers in Real Analysis

## Augustin-Louis Cauchy (1789–1857)

Cauchy gave analysis its foundations. Before Cauchy, calculus worked on the basis of Leibniz's infinitesimals and Newton's fluxions — powerful but imprecise. Cauchy's *Cours d'analyse* (1821) introduced the ε-δ definition of limit (though his notation and presentation differed from the modern form), defined continuity in terms of limits, and proved the first rigorous version of the mean value theorem and intermediate value theorem.

He also introduced Cauchy sequences — sequences where the terms become mutually close — as the criterion for convergence without needing to know the limit. The Cauchy completeness condition (every Cauchy sequence converges) is now the standard definition of completeness. This is his most important contribution to the material of this chapter.

Cauchy's work was not without error: he famously (and incorrectly) claimed that the limit of a pointwise convergent sequence of continuous functions is continuous. The correction — requiring *uniform* convergence — was provided by Weierstrass. But Cauchy's program of rigorous analysis fundamentally changed the subject and made the next two centuries of mathematical analysis possible.

## Karl Weierstrass (1815–1897)

Weierstrass was the "father of modern analysis." He filled in the gaps in Cauchy's program, introduced the modern ε-δ notation (replacing Cauchy's more ambiguous formulations), and proved many of the key theorems that are now standard.

His most striking contribution to this chapter: the construction of a continuous nowhere-differentiable function. Before Weierstrass (1872), most mathematicians believed that continuous functions were differentiable except at isolated points. Weierstrass showed this was wrong: f(x) = Σ_{n=0}^∞ aⁿ cos(bⁿπx) for 0 < a < 1 and ab > 1 + 3π/2 is continuous everywhere and differentiable nowhere. The function oscillates so rapidly at every scale that no tangent line can be drawn. This was shocking and was resisted by many contemporaries (including Hermite, who called it "a lamentable affliction" against mathematical common sense).

Weierstrass also proved the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem (every bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence) and the Weierstrass approximation theorem (every continuous function on [a,b] can be uniformly approximated by polynomials), both central results in analysis. His systematic rigorization of calculus set the standard for all subsequent mathematical analysis.

## Georg Cantor (1845–1918)

Cantor appears here as well as in Chapter 1 because his motivations were analytic: he was studying the structure of point sets in ℝ (sets of discontinuity of trigonometric series) and found that he needed a systematic theory of infinite sets. His analysis of the structure of closed sets in ℝ — leading to the Cantor-Bendixson theorem and the theory of perfect sets — is one of the founding results of point-set topology.

Most relevantly: Cantor defined the Cantor set (the "middle thirds" construction: remove the middle third of [0,1], then the middle thirds of the remaining intervals, and so on). The Cantor set is uncountable, compact, nowhere dense, and has measure zero. It is the prototypical counterexample in real analysis and topology, showing that "large" (in cardinality) and "large" (in measure) are completely different.

## Émile Borel (1871–1956)

Borel proved what is now called the Heine-Borel theorem: a subset of ℝ is compact (in the open-cover sense) if and only if it is closed and bounded. He recognized this as a foundational result connecting the covering definition of compactness to more elementary properties.

Borel also founded modern measure theory and the theory of Borel sets (the σ-algebra generated by open sets). The Borel hierarchy — open sets (Σ⁰₁), closed sets (Π⁰₁), countable unions of closed sets (Σ⁰₂), and so on — is the foundation of descriptive set theory and directly relevant to questions about the measurability of complicated sets. Borel was one of the "French semi-intuitionists" who had philosophical reservations about the Axiom of Choice, insisting that mathematical objects should be "effectively definable."

## Felix Hausdorff (1868–1942)

Hausdorff gave topology its modern form. His *Grundzüge der Mengenlehre* (1914) defined topological spaces axiomatically (as sets with a collection of "neighborhoods" satisfying axioms) and developed the theory of metric spaces, set theory, and topology in a unified framework. He introduced the Hausdorff separation axiom (T₂): distinct points have disjoint open neighborhoods. Hausdorff spaces are the right class of topological spaces for most of analysis.

Hausdorff also introduced the Hausdorff dimension — a real-valued dimension for fractals that lies between the topological dimension and the embedding dimension. The Cantor set has Hausdorff dimension log₂/log₃ ≈ 0.63; a space-filling curve has Hausdorff dimension 2 despite being a 1-dimensional curve. Hausdorff dimension is one of the key tools in the study of fractal geometry.

He died in a Nazi concentration camp in 1942. His mathematical legacy, including the topology text that shaped the subject for decades, survived him.

## Henri Poincaré (1854–1912)

Poincaré's contribution to analysis is analysis situs — what we now call topology — including the definition of the fundamental group and the recognition that topology is the right framework for understanding the global behavior of differential equations. His qualitative theory of differential equations asked not "what is the explicit solution?" but "what does the solution look like globally?" — a question that requires topology to answer.

For this chapter specifically, Poincaré's recognition that paths and homotopies between paths form a group — the fundamental group — is the direct source of the connection between analysis and algebra that drives HoTT. He also recognized that higher-dimensional loops (maps from Sⁿ into a space) form higher homotopy groups, setting the agenda for algebraic topology.

## Eduard Heine (1821–1881)

Heine proved the theorem that bears his name (with Borel), in the specific form: every continuous function on a closed bounded interval is uniformly continuous. This theorem — now the Heine-Cantor theorem in this generalization — was one of the first results showing that compactness (then understood as "closed and bounded") has strong consequences for the behavior of functions.

Heine's work on uniform continuity, uniform convergence, and the careful distinction between pointwise and uniform properties contributed to the rigorous foundations of analysis in the generation after Cauchy and Weierstrass. His name in the Heine-Borel theorem reflects his priority in recognizing the covering property of closed bounded sets.
