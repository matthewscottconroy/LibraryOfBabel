Before you can prove anything, you must decide what a proof *is*. This is the question that proof theory answers — and it turns out that answering it precisely requires distinguishing two things that intuition tends to collapse together: the claim that something is true, and the demonstration that compels you to accept it. A judgment is the formal version of the latter: a declaration with the shape $\Gamma \vdash A$, asserting that the proposition $A$ follows by explicit steps from the hypotheses in $\Gamma$. The turnstile $\vdash$ does not merely record a fact; it specifies that a derivation exists.

What is surprising here is the power of this small distinction. Once you separate syntactic provability ($\vdash$) from semantic truth ($\models$), you get two precisely different notions that turn out to be coextensive — that is Gödel's completeness theorem, and it is not obvious. The rules of a formal system might be too weak to prove everything true, or too strong and prove things that are not true. Soundness and completeness together say the formal system hits the target exactly.

Keep this in mind as you work through the machinery: the judgment $\Gamma \vdash A$ will evolve across the entire text. In type theory it becomes $\Gamma \vdash a : A$, where the proof term $a$ is made explicit, and this is the judgment of Martin-Löf Type Theory and ultimately of HoTT. Understanding the simple version — the contextual judgment without an explicit proof object — is the foundation for understanding everything that follows.
