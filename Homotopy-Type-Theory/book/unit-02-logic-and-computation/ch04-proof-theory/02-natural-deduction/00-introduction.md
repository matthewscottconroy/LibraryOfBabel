Gentzen observed in 1935 that mathematicians do not reason from axioms. They assume things, work with those assumptions, and discharge them when the argument is finished. The older Hilbert-style proof systems, built around axioms and modus ponens, were logically correct but proof-theoretically alien — no one actually thinks that way. Natural deduction is the alternative: a system organized not around axioms but around *connectives*, each given exactly an introduction rule and an elimination rule.

The non-obvious insight is that this organization is not a notational preference but a structural commitment. An introduction rule says how to *build* a proposition with a given connective as its main connective. An elimination rule says how to *use* one. The two kinds of rules are inverses of each other, and this inverse relationship is precisely what normalization captures. When you introduce a connective and immediately eliminate it, you have made a detour — and detours can always be removed. That removal process, as the next section shows, is computation.

This is where the Curry-Howard correspondence becomes visible at the level of inference rules. The introduction rule for $A \to B$ — assume $A$, derive $B$, discharge the assumption — is exactly the rule for forming a lambda abstraction. The elimination rule — apply $A \to B$ to a proof of $A$ — is function application. Every connective in this chapter corresponds to a type constructor, and every inference rule corresponds to a typing rule in STLC, MLTT, and ultimately the type theory underlying Lean and Agda. Natural deduction is where that correspondence first appears in full.
