A proof that introduces a connective and immediately eliminates it is doing unnecessary work. From $A \wedge B$, you can certainly derive $A$ directly — but a proof that extracts $B$, re-pairs $A$ and $B$, and then extracts $A$ again also works. The two proofs have the same conclusion and the same hypothesis, but the second is bloated. Normalization is the theorem that says every proof can be reduced to a lean form without such detours, and that this reduction always terminates.

The surprising depth here is the connection between logical normalization and computation. The process of removing a detour — taking an introduction immediately followed by its corresponding elimination and collapsing the pair — is not merely proof tidying. Under the Curry-Howard correspondence, it is exactly $\beta$-reduction in the lambda calculus. Removing the conjunction detour is projection onto a pair. Removing the implication detour is function application: substituting the argument for the bound variable. The proof is a program, and normalization is execution.

The consequences are twofold. Logically, normal proofs have the *subformula property*: every formula appearing in a normal proof is a subformula of either a hypothesis or the conclusion. This implies consistency — a proof of $\bot$ from no hypotheses would have to contain only subformulas of $\bot$, and there are none. Computationally, normalization is termination: every well-typed program halts. Both conclusions — logical consistency and computational termination — rest on the same theorem. This convergence is not a coincidence; it is one of the central insights of proof theory, and it reappears at every level of the type-theoretic hierarchy.
