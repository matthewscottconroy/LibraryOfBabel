Natural deduction is asymmetric: there is one conclusion, many hypotheses, and the rules are organized around building up the conclusion. Sequent calculus breaks the asymmetry. Gentzen's second innovation in his landmark 1935 paper is the sequent $\Gamma \Rightarrow \Delta$, where both sides may contain multiple formulas. The left side holds the hypotheses; the right side holds the possible conclusions. Allowing multiple conclusions is not just a generalization — it induces a left-right symmetry that makes certain meta-theoretic arguments dramatically cleaner.

The payoff is the *Hauptsatz*: the cut elimination theorem. The cut rule allows you to use an intermediate lemma — derive a formula and then use it downstream — which is how proofs are actually written. Cut elimination says this can always be removed: every proof with cuts has a cut-free proof of the same conclusion, and no logical power is lost in the removal. This is remarkable because cuts are ubiquitous in mathematical practice. The theorem says the detour through lemmas is always in principle eliminable, even if in practice the cut-free proof may be exponentially longer.

For the story this book is building, sequent calculus matters for two reasons. First, the proof of cut elimination is most natural in the sequent setting, and that proof technique — transforming proofs by structural induction on derivations — recurs throughout type theory. Second, sequent calculus gives rise to *proof search* algorithms: working backwards from a goal by decomposing the sequent. This backward-chaining structure underlies tactic-based proof assistants. When you write a tactic in Lean or Coq, you are performing sequent calculus proof search by hand, guided by the system's type checker.
