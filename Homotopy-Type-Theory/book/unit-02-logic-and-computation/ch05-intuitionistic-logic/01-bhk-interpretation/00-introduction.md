Classical logic is about truth. Intuitionistic logic is about *construction*. The Brouwer-Heyting-Kolmogorov interpretation makes this precise before any formalism appears: it gives a meaning to each kind of proposition by specifying what it means to have a proof of it. A proof of $P \wedge Q$ is a pair of proofs. A proof of $P \to Q$ is a function converting any proof of $P$ into a proof of $Q$. A proof of $\exists x, P(x)$ is a witness together with a proof of $P$ applied to it. These are not axioms or inference rules — they are informal explanations that constrain which formal systems count as constructively valid.

What is genuinely surprising is how much the BHK interpretation does with how little. It has no formal content, yet it immediately explains why the Law of Excluded Middle is constructively invalid: a proof of $P \vee \neg P$ must specify *which* holds, but for an arbitrary $P$ no general method for this exists. It explains why double negation elimination fails: converting "any proof of $P$ leads to contradiction" into an actual proof of $P$ requires information that the double negation simply does not supply. Classical logic's tautologies are tautologies about truth values; constructive logic's provable formulas are exactly those for which you can give an explicit construction.

Every subsequent section of this chapter — the formal proof system, the Kripke semantics, the double-negation translation — is making the BHK interpretation rigorous in a different way. And beyond this chapter, the BHK interpretation *is* the Curry-Howard correspondence: a proof of $P$ is a term of type $P$, and the BHK clauses are exactly the typing rules for the lambda calculus extended to dependent types. The informal meaning comes first; the formal machinery follows.
