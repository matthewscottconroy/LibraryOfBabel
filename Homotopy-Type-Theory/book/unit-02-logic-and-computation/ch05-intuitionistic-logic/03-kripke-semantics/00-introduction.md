Truth tables are the semantics of classical logic: two values, a formula is a tautology if it is true in every assignment. Intuitionistic logic cannot be captured this way. No finite set of truth values works — this is a theorem, not a conjecture. The reason is built into the BHK interpretation: whether $A \vee \neg A$ is provable depends not on what $A$ "really is" but on what information is currently available, and that information might grow. A semantics adequate to intuitionistic logic must model this temporal, knowledge-relative character of provability.

Kripke semantics, introduced in 1965, provides exactly that. A Kripke model is a partial order of *worlds* — stages of knowledge — equipped with a valuation that is monotone: what is true at a world remains true at every more-informed world. The crucial clause is for implication: $A \to B$ is forced at world $w$ if, at every future world where $A$ is forced, $B$ is also forced. This is the BHK clause for implication turned into a semantic condition. Negation $\neg A$ means $A$ is forced at no future world — a permanent refutation, not merely the current absence of a proof.

The result is a soundness and completeness theorem: IPC proves $\varphi$ if and only if $\varphi$ is forced at every world in every Kripke model. This gives you an alternative route to showing that LEM is not provable in IPC: construct a Kripke model with two worlds where $A$ is not forced at the base world (and cannot be permanently refuted either), and verify that $A \vee \neg A$ fails at that world. The model becomes a mathematical certificate of unprovability — and the technique of building countermodels is exactly how automated tools verify that intuitionistic non-theorems are genuine non-theorems.
