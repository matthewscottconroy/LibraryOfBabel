Classical and intuitionistic logic appear to be genuinely different theories — and they are. But one of the more startling results of 20th-century logic is that classical propositional logic is *interpretable inside* intuitionistic logic. Every classical theorem translates into an intuitionistic theorem via a systematic insertion of double negations, and the translation is faithful: classically provable if and only if the translation is intuitionistically provable. The two logics are not as far apart as they look from the outside.

The translation — the Gödel-Gentzen translation, discovered independently in 1933 — works by placing double negations precisely where classical logic would invoke LEM or DNE. Atomic propositions get double-negated, disjunctions and existentials are wrapped in a double negation, implications and conjunctions pass through unchanged. What makes this work is that $\neg\neg(A \vee \neg A)$ is intuitionistically provable even though $A \vee \neg A$ is not: the stabilized form of LEM is available constructively. The translation exploits this gap between a proposition and its double negation.

For automated theorem proving and for HoTT, the double-negation translation has two important consequences. First, it shows that classical mathematicians working in classical logic are not working in a separate universe — their work is accessible to constructive foundations, just with an altered reading of the logical constants. Second, in type theory, the translation corresponds to the continuation monad: $\neg\neg A = (A \to \bot) \to \bot$ is the type of a computation that, given any continuation from $A$, produces a final answer. Classical reasoning becomes a control effect. This connection between classical logic and control operators (call/cc) is one of the more surprising bridges that Curry-Howard builds.
