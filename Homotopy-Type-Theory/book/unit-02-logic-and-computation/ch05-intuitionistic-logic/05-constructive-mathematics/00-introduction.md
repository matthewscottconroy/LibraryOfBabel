Errett Bishop proved, in 1967, that all of real analysis can be developed constructively — not in a weakened or approximate form, but rigorously and in full: continuous functions, differential calculus, measure theory, functional analysis. The mathematical community largely ignored him. This was a mistake. Bishop's program demonstrated that the choice between classical and constructive mathematics is not a choice between rigor and intuition but between two different standards of *what a proof is for*. Constructive proofs carry computational content that classical proofs discard.

The demands of constructive mathematics follow directly from the BHK interpretation. Existence must be witnessed: you cannot prove $\exists x, P(x)$ by showing that the non-existence of a witness leads to contradiction. Disjunctions must be decided: a proof of $A \vee B$ must specify which holds. Functions must be computable: not merely asserted to exist, but given as explicit procedures. These requirements are severe. The classical intermediate value theorem — if $f(0) < 0 < f(1)$ there exists $c$ with $f(c) = 0$ — fails constructively in general, and this is a theorem, not a deficiency. The constructive substitute gives an *approximation* algorithm: binary search to arbitrary precision.

What is easy to miss is that this restriction is informative, not punishing. A constructive proof of an existence statement is an algorithm. A constructive proof that two real numbers are equal carries a modulus of convergence. When you formalize mathematics in Lean or Agda, you are doing constructive mathematics in Bishop's sense: every existence proof must exhibit a construction, and that construction can be executed. The proof assistant does not just verify the theorem — it extracts the program.
