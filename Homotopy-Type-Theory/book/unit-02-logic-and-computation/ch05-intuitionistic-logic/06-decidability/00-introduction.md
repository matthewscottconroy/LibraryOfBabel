The Law of Excluded Middle, taken as an axiom schema, is non-constructive. But for many specific propositions, $P \vee \neg P$ is provable — not as an axiom but as a theorem backed by an explicit algorithm. Equality of natural numbers is decidable: given $m$ and $n$, you can determine by finite computation whether they are equal. Halting of arbitrary programs is not decidable: no algorithm can make this determination in general. Decidability is the distinction between cases where LEM happens to be provable and cases where it is genuinely unavailable.

The surprising connection in this section is Hedberg's theorem: if a type has decidable equality, then it is an h-set — any two proofs that $a = b$ are themselves equal. Decidability controls not just whether equality can be decided, but how many ways there are to be equal. This bridges the computational notion of decidability and the homotopical notion of h-level, and it is one of the places where the concerns of constructive mathematics and HoTT make direct contact. The h-level hierarchy — which classifies types by the complexity of their identity types — turns out to be intimately related to the decidability of equality.

This section matters for automated proof for a direct reason: decidable equality means the proof assistant can automatically close goals of the form $a = b$ or $a \neq b$ for concrete values, without user intervention. Types with decidable equality — booleans, natural numbers, finite types, lists of such types — support computational equality checking, which is the backbone of the `decide` and `norm_num` tactics. Understanding which types are decidable, and why, is essential for knowing when the machine can be trusted to finish a goal and when you need to guide it by hand.
