The identification of propositions with types is not a metaphor. It is a precise mathematical correspondence: the proposition $A \wedge B$ is the product type $A \times B$; the proposition $A \to B$ is the function type; a proof of $A$ is a term of type $A$. Every logical connective corresponds to a type constructor, and the inference rules for deriving propositions correspond exactly — rule by rule, symbol by symbol — to the typing rules for constructing terms. When you see the natural deduction introduction rule for conjunction alongside the typing rule for pair formation, you are looking at the same rule written in two notations.

The non-obvious depth of this correspondence is that it is not merely syntactic. The logical notion of *consistency* — no proof of $\bot$ from no hypotheses — is the same as the type-theoretic notion of *non-triviality* — no closed term of the empty type. The proof-theoretic notion of *normalization* — every proof simplifies to a detour-free form — is the same as the computational notion of *termination* — every well-typed program halts. The correspondence is not one between similar-looking systems; it is an identity between the same structure described in two vocabularies.

This chapter makes the correspondence fully explicit, type constructor by type constructor. As you work through the dictionary, keep in mind where it leads: dependent types extend the correspondence to predicate logic, and from there to Martin-Löf Type Theory, where propositions can depend on values and the proofs themselves become mathematical objects. That is the foundation of every modern proof assistant, and the propositions-as-types identification made here is its conceptual heart.
