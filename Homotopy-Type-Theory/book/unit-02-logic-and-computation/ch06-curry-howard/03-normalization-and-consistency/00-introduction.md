Strong normalization for the simply typed lambda calculus is two theorems at once: it says every well-typed program terminates, and it says intuitionistic propositional logic is consistent. These are not two separate results wearing the same name. Under Curry-Howard, they are the same statement. If every well-typed term reduces to a value, and the empty type $\mathbf{0}$ has no values, then there is no well-typed term of type $\mathbf{0}$ — and that is exactly consistency. The normalization theorem *is* the consistency proof.

The proof technique — Tait's logical relations method — is where the real content lies. You cannot prove strong normalization by induction on terms, because the inductive structure of terms does not align with the inductive structure of types. Instead, you define a reducibility predicate $\text{Red}(A)$ by induction on the *type* $A$: at base type, reducibility is just strong normalization; at function type, a term is reducible if it sends reducible arguments to reducible results. Then you show that all typing derivations produce reducible terms. The key lemma is that lambda abstractions are reducible — and this requires the inductive hypothesis in the function-type clause to transfer the property from body to abstraction.

The method appears here in its simplest setting, but you will encounter it repeatedly. Logical relations are the standard technique for proving strong normalization of System F, of the calculus of constructions, and of each extension thereof. They are also the standard technique for proving parametricity — the uniformity property of polymorphic functions. When proof assistants argue that their type theories are consistent, they are ultimately invoking this method, or a semantic argument of the same structure. Understanding it now, in the clean STLC case, is investment in every subsequent consistency argument.
