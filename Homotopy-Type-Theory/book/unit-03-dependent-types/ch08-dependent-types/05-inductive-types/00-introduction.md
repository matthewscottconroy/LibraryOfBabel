When mathematicians define the natural numbers, they give two clauses: zero is a natural number, and the successor of any natural number is a natural number. When they prove something about all natural numbers, they use exactly the same two clauses in reverse: handle the base case, handle the successor case. This parallel is not a coincidence. It is a theorem about the structure of inductive definitions — and it is the theorem that inductive types in MLTT make precise.

The surprising content of this section is that the construction principle and the reasoning principle are the same thing seen from different angles. Specifying the constructors of an inductive type — the ways you can build elements — simultaneously and uniquely determines the eliminator — the way you reason about all elements. You do not write down the constructors and then separately figure out what induction looks like. The induction principle is forced by the constructors. Recursion (defining functions by structural descent) and induction (proving properties by structural descent) are a single principle applied to different motives: a constant type family gives recursion, a dependent type family gives induction.

This unification is not just intellectually satisfying — it is the mechanism that makes dependent type theory work as a foundation for mathematics. Every data structure you care about (booleans, natural numbers, lists, trees, integers, rationals) is an inductive type. Every proof over those structures is a term produced by the appropriate eliminator. In Coq, Lean, and Agda, the `induction` tactic is literally an application of an eliminator. When you understand that recursion and induction are one, you understand why those proof assistants can define and verify arbitrarily complex mathematical structures — and why they terminate, since the eliminator only accepts structurally smaller inputs.
