You have already seen the Curry-Howard correspondence — propositions as types, proofs as programs — but you saw it in the thin version, for propositional logic and the simply typed lambda calculus. What dependent types reveal is that the correspondence was never really about propositional logic at all. Propositions as types is a fact about logic in its full first-order extent, and dependent types are what let the type theory keep up.

The table of correspondences expands with dependent types: the universal quantifier ∀x.P(x) becomes the Π type Π(x:A).P(x); the existential ∃x.P(x) becomes the Σ type Σ(x:A).P(x); and equality a = b becomes the identity type, a type whose elements are proofs of that specific equality. These are not analogies. They are identities. The logic and the type theory are the same system described in two vocabularies, and this section makes that identification explicit. What is non-obvious is that the extended correspondence comes with a new property: *proof relevance*. In classical logic, two proofs of the same proposition are interchangeable — a proof is just a certificate. In dependent type theory, proofs are terms, and terms carry computational content. Two different proofs of the same proposition are genuinely different objects and can behave differently when extracted or computed with.

Proof relevance is not a defect. It is what makes dependent type theory suitable for verified computation. A proof of Σ(l:List).IsSorted(l) does not just assert the existence of a sorted list; it contains the sorted list, extractable by projection. A proof of Π(n:ℕ).IsEven(2 · n) does not just say even multiples of two exist; it computes the witness for any specific n. This computational content is precisely what automated theorem provers exploit when they extract certified programs from proofs, and it begins here, in the full correspondence that dependent types make possible.
