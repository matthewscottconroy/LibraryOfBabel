Formal systems are usually presented with one kind of assertion: "this formula is provable." Martin-Löf Type Theory requires four. That number is not arbitrary, and the gap between one and four is not bureaucratic overhead — it is the precise cost of taking types seriously as mathematical objects in their own right.

The four judgments of MLTT distinguish things that simpler systems collapse together. You need to say not only that a term has a type, but that a type is well-formed; not only that two terms are equal, but that they are equal *as terms of a specific type*, and moreover that there is a distinction between equality that the type checker verifies by computation — definitional equality — and equality that requires a proof — propositional equality. This last distinction is the most consequential. Definitional equality (written ≡) is a judgment: the type checker decides it automatically by normalizing both sides and comparing. Propositional equality (written =) is a type: it requires you to construct a term that witnesses the equality. In extensional type theory, these two notions are collapsed by a reflection rule, and the price is that type-checking becomes undecidable. In intensional MLTT — the version that leads to HoTT — they are kept apart, and this separation is what allows the identity type to have multiple distinct inhabitants and eventually to be interpreted as a space of paths.

Understanding the four judgments is understanding what MLTT is actually asserting at each step. Every rule in the system is a rule for deriving one of the four judgment forms, and every question about whether a proof is valid reduces to whether a certain judgment is derivable. The judgmental infrastructure may feel like scaffolding, but it is the foundation on which everything from path induction to the univalence axiom is built.
