Category theory begins with an act of radical abstraction: strip away all properties of mathematical objects except the maps between them, and ask what structure remains. What remains turns out to be everything that matters. The objects — sets, groups, topological spaces, types — recede into the background. What takes center stage is *composition*: how maps chain together, and what that chaining preserves.

The surprise is how little the definition requires. A category is three pieces of data (objects, morphisms, composition) and three axioms (associativity, left unit, right unit). Yet from this almost-nothing, the entire landscape of modern mathematics can be reconstructed. Every mathematical structure you know — preorders, monoids, groups, vector spaces — is a special case of a category or lives naturally inside one. The abstract framework is not impoverished by comparison with the concrete; it is *richer*, because it isolates what the concrete examples have in common.

For you, the payoff is twofold. First, category theory provides the *semantic models* for type theory: the category of types and functions is the most natural category you will encounter, and every type constructor (product, function type, identity type) corresponds to a categorical universal property. Second, the move from ordinary categories to higher categories — groupoids, 2-categories, ∞-categories — is the move from classical mathematics to homotopy type theory. You cannot understand HoTT without understanding why types are not sets but ∞-groupoids, and that story begins here, with the simplest version of the categorical framework.
