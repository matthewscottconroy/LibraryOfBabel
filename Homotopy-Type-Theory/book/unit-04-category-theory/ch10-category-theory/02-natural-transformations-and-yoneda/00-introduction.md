Natural transformations were not invented to satisfy abstract curiosity — they were invented because mathematicians kept noticing that certain isomorphisms were "canonical" while others required arbitrary choices, and no one could say precisely what that meant. Eilenberg and Mac Lane created category theory specifically to answer this question. A natural transformation is a map between functors that commutes with all the structure in sight; a natural isomorphism is one where every component is invertible. The double-dual embedding $V \hookrightarrow V^{**}$ is natural; the identification $V \cong V^{*}$ is not. This distinction, once made precise, turns out to be one of the most important in all of mathematics.

What is non-obvious is the self-referential richness that immediately appears. Once you have functors and natural transformations, you can build *functor categories* — categories whose objects are functors and whose morphisms are natural transformations. This means categories themselves form a 2-category: $\mathbf{Cat}$, with categories as objects, functors as 1-morphisms, and natural transformations as 2-morphisms. The theory lifts one level and becomes its own subject matter.

The Yoneda lemma — developed in this section as the central theorem of basic category theory — says something startling: an object $A$ in a category is completely determined by the functor $\mathsf{Hom}(A, -)$ it represents. Every natural transformation out of $\mathsf{Hom}(A, -)$ is determined by a single element — what happens at $A$ itself. You do not need to look inside the object; all its information is visible in how other objects map into it. This is the categorical version of a principle that runs through all of HoTT: identity is relational, not intrinsic.
