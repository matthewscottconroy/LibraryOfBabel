Natural transformations were not invented to satisfy abstract curiosity — they were invented because mathematicians kept noticing that certain isomorphisms were "canonical" while others required arbitrary choices, and no one could say precisely what that meant. Eilenberg and Mac Lane created category theory specifically to answer this question. A natural transformation is a map between functors that commutes with all the structure in sight; the naturality square is the condition that the transformation does not depend on which path you take through it.

The double-dual embedding $V \hookrightarrow V^{**}$ is natural; the identification $V \cong V^{*}$ (which requires a choice of basis) is not. This distinction, once made precise, turns out to be one of the most important in all of mathematics — it marks the boundary between what is canonical and what is conventional.

What is non-obvious is the self-referential richness that immediately follows from the definition. Once you have functors and natural transformations, you can build *functor categories* — categories whose objects are functors and whose morphisms are natural transformations. This means categories themselves form a 2-category, $\mathbf{Cat}$, with natural transformations as its 2-morphisms. The theory lifts one level and becomes its own subject matter. For you, this matters because the passage from categories to higher categories — from $\mathbf{Cat}$ to bicategories, to ∞-categories — is the categorical underpinning of HoTT. Natural transformations are the first step in that ascent.
