The Yoneda lemma is the most important theorem in category theory, and it says something that should sound impossible: an object is completely determined by the ways other objects map into it. You do not need to look inside $A$ to know everything about $A$; all its information is encoded in the representable functor $\mathsf{Hom}(A, -)$. And every natural transformation out of $\mathsf{Hom}(A, -)$ is determined by a single element — what happens at $A$ itself.

The proof is short. The philosophical consequences are large. The lemma implies that the functor $A \mapsto \mathsf{Hom}(A, -)$ is fully faithful: a fully faithful embedding of $\mathcal{C}$ into the functor category $[\mathcal{C}^{op}, \mathbf{Set}]$. Every category embeds into a category of presheaves. This means the abstract world of categories is no larger, in an essential sense, than the concrete world of set-valued functors. The presheaf category $[\mathcal{C}^{op}, \mathbf{Set}]$ is the "free cocompletion" of $\mathcal{C}$ — the smallest category containing $\mathcal{C}$ that has all colimits.

For HoTT, the Yoneda perspective is philosophically resonant: identity is relational, not intrinsic. An object is what it does — how it relates to everything else — not what it "is made of." This principle reappears in univalence, where two types are equal if and only if they are equivalent (have the same relational profile). The Yoneda lemma is the classical shadow of a much deeper idea.
