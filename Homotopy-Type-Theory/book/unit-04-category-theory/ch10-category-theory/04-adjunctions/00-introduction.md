Saunders Mac Lane called adjunctions "the most important concept in category theory," and the more mathematics you do, the harder this claim is to dispute. An adjunction between $F$ and $G$ captures a pattern you will recognize from dozens of contexts: giving a map *out of* $F(A)$ is the same as giving a map *into* $G(B)$. Free groups and forgetful functors. Products and exponentials — currying. Left and right Kan extensions. Quantifiers in logic. The pattern is everywhere, and the adjunction is its precise formulation.

What is non-obvious is that this is not merely a notational convenience. Adjunctions determine their adjoints up to unique isomorphism; they preserve limits and colimits in precise ways; they generate monads; they characterize the relationship between syntax and semantics in type theory. The unit and counit — the canonical maps $\eta : A \to G(F(A))$ and $\varepsilon : F(G(B)) \to B$ — measure how far the round-trips $G \circ F$ and $F \circ G$ deviate from the identity, and the triangular identities constrain that deviation completely.

For you, adjunctions matter at every level of the curriculum. The product-exponential adjunction $\mathsf{Hom}(C \times A, B) \cong \mathsf{Hom}(C, [A,B])$ is currying — the fundamental operation of the simply typed lambda calculus. The adjunctions $\Sigma_t \dashv t^* \dashv \Pi_t$ in a locally cartesian closed category are dependent sum and dependent product — the $\Sigma$ and $\Pi$ types of dependent type theory. Every time you form a type in MLTT, you are using an adjunction. The concept is not an abstraction layered on top of type theory; it is the categorical content of type theory itself.
