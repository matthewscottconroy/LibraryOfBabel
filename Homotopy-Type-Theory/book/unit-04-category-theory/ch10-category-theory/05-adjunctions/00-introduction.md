Adjunctions are the categorical formalization of one of the most productive patterns in mathematics: two apparently different kinds of data that turn out to be interchangeable. Giving a group homomorphism from a free group $F(S)$ to a group $G$ is the same as giving a function from the generating set $S$ to the underlying set of $G$. Giving a function $C \times A \to B$ is the same as giving a function $C \to [A, B]$. In each case, you have two functors — a "free" or "left" functor and a "forgetful" or "right" functor — and an adjunction is exactly the bijection that makes this interchangeability precise.

What is non-obvious at first contact is how strong this relationship is. Adjoint functors are not merely correlated — they determine each other, up to unique isomorphism. The left adjoint preserves colimits; the right adjoint preserves limits. The unit $\eta$ and counit $\varepsilon$ encode the "almost-inverse" relationship precisely, and the triangular identities say that the round-trips are as close to the identity as they can be without being isomorphisms. When the round-trips *are* isomorphisms, you have an equivalence of categories.

For dependent type theory, adjunctions are not background machinery — they are the type constructors. Substitution $t^*$, dependent sum $\Sigma_t$, and dependent product $\Pi_t$ form an adjoint triple $\Sigma_t \dashv t^* \dashv \Pi_t$ in any locally cartesian closed category. The rules for forming and eliminating $\Sigma$ and $\Pi$ types are exactly the unit and counit of these adjunctions, and the $\beta$- and $\eta$-rules are exactly the triangular identities. The syntax of type theory is the algebra of adjunctions.
