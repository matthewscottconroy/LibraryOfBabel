Every adjunction gives a monad — not as a construction you perform, but as a theorem about what the adjunction contains. And conversely, every monad arises from some adjunction: in fact, from two canonical ones, the Kleisli construction and the Eilenberg-Moore construction. Monads and adjunctions are two presentations of the same underlying structure, and knowing both presentations doubles your ability to recognize the structure when it appears.

A monad on a category $\mathcal{C}$ is an endofunctor $T$ equipped with a unit $\eta$ and a multiplication $\mu$ satisfying associativity and unit laws. The axioms are precisely those of a monoid, transposed to the setting of functors. This is not an analogy: a monad is literally a monoid in the monoidal category of endofunctors of $\mathcal{C}$ under composition. The algebraic content is real.

What is non-obvious is the breadth of what monads classify. The Eilenberg-Moore theorem says that the algebras for a monad are exactly the "structured sets" that the monad presents: groups for the free group monad, rings for the free ring monad, propositions for the propositional truncation monad in HoTT. Monads are thus algebraic theories, expressed without reference to operations and equations — purely in terms of functors and natural transformations. For you, this matters because every logical modality in HoTT (truncations, squash types, localizations) is a monad, and understanding their monad structure is understanding their logical behavior. The algebra governs the logic.
