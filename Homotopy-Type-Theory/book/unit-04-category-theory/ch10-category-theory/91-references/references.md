# References: Category Theory

## Primary Texts

**Mac Lane, S. (1971). *Categories for the Working Mathematician*.** Springer. The classical reference, written by the co-inventor of the subject. Part I covers the basics (categories, functors, natural transformations, universal properties, limits). Part II covers adjunctions, monads, and special limits. Part III covers ends, Kan extensions, and topics in enriched category theory. Dense but rewarding; every serious student of category theory reads this. The second edition (1998) adds a chapter on Higher Dimensional Categories.

**Riehl, E. (2016). *Category Theory in Context*.** Dover. (Free online.) The best modern introduction. Written for mathematics graduate students, with excellent exercises and contemporary perspective. Covers categories and functors, natural transformations and the Yoneda lemma, limits and colimits, adjunctions, and monads. The treatment of adjoint functors is particularly clear. Recommended as the primary text before Mac Lane.

**Awodey, S. (2010). *Category Theory* (2nd ed.).** Oxford University Press. Written with the connection to logic in mind — Awodey is both a category theorist and a philosopher of mathematics. The categorical semantics chapters are especially relevant for type theorists. Good companion to Riehl or Mac Lane for those coming from logic.

## For Categorical Logic and Semantics of Type Theory

**Jacobs, B. (1999). *Categorical Logic and Type Theory*.** Elsevier. (Free online.) The most complete reference for the correspondence between dependent type theory and categorical structures. Covers fibered categories, comprehension categories, CCCs for STLC, LCCCs for dependent types, toposes. Technical but thorough. The reference for anyone formalize categorical semantics.

**Lambek, J. and Scott, P.J. (1986). *Introduction to Higher Order Categorical Logic*.** Cambridge University Press. The foundational text for the connection between CCCs and typed lambda calculi, and toposes and higher-order logic. Proves the soundness and completeness of the categorical semantics for each type theory. Historical and authoritative.

**Pitts, A. (1995). *Categorical Logic*.** (Notes available online.) Concise treatment of the internal language of a topos and its connection to intuitionistic logic. Particularly good on the subobject classifier and higher-order logic.

## For Topos Theory

**Johnstone, P. (1977). *Topos Theory*.** Academic Press. The first systematic treatment, and still a valuable introduction alongside the *Elephant*. Shorter and more accessible than the *Elephant*.

**Johnstone, P. (2002). *Sketches of an Elephant: A Topos Theory Compendium*, Vols. I–II.** Oxford University Press. The comprehensive reference. Covers fibered categories, sites and Grothendieck toposes, geometric morphisms, internal category theory, and much more. Definitive but requires substantial categorical background.

**Goldblatt, R. (1984). *Topoi: The Categorial Analysis of Logic*.** North-Holland. (Free online.) More accessible than Johnstone, with an explicit focus on the connection to logic. A good bridge between logic and topos theory for those coming from the logic side.

## For Higher Category Theory

**Leinster, T. (2014). *Basic Category Theory*.** Cambridge University Press. (Free online.) A short, elegant introduction to the core concepts. Chapter on universal properties is excellent. Good preparation for higher category theory.

**Lurie, J. (2009). *Higher Topos Theory*.** Princeton University Press. (Free online.) The foundational reference for ∞-categories and ∞-toposes. Chapter 1 (on quasi-categories as ∞-categories) is accessible; the rest requires substantial background. Essential for understanding the mathematical foundations of HoTT.

**Riehl, E. and Verity, D. (2022). *Elements of ∞-Category Theory*.** Cambridge University Press. (Free online.) A more accessible approach to ∞-categories, emphasizing the model-independent approach via ∞-cosmoi. Better for a reader coming from algebraic topology.

## For the Connection to HoTT

**Awodey, S. and Warren, M. (2009). Homotopy-Theoretic Models of Identity Types.** *Mathematical Proceedings of the Cambridge Philosophical Society*, 146(1), 45–55. The paper that launched the categorical perspective on HoTT. Shows that any category with a weak factorization system models MLTT with the identity type. The abstract framework for "path objects model identity types."

**Hofmann, M. and Streicher, T. (1998). The Groupoid Interpretation of Type Theory.** *Twenty-Five Years of Constructive Type Theory*, Oxford University Press. Proves that the groupoid model satisfies MLTT but not UIP. This independence result is what motivated HoTT: if UIP fails in the groupoid model, then identity types must carry more information than classical mathematics assumed.
