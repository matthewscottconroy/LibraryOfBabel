Simply typed lambda calculus and cartesian closed categories are the same mathematical structure, written in different notations. This is not a loose analogy — it is a theorem. Types correspond to objects, terms to morphisms, substitution to composition, abstraction to the currying bijection, and the $\beta$- and $\eta$-rules to the counit equation and uniqueness clause of the exponential adjunction. Every derivation in STLC translates to an equation of morphisms in any CCC, and every CCC gives a model of STLC in which all derivable equations hold.

The Curry-Howard correspondence — proofs as programs — gains its categorical foundation here. In any CCC, the exponential object $[A, B]$ represents the "space of morphisms from $A$ to $B$," and the evaluation map $\mathsf{ev} : [A, B] \times A \to B$ is the categorical version of function application. The universal property of the exponential is currying: a "two-input" morphism $C \times A \to B$ is the same as a "one-input" morphism $C \to [A, B]$. This bijection is both the $\beta$-rule (going one way) and the $\eta$-rule (going back), and both follow from the adjunction alone.

What you should track through this section is the classifying CCC: the CCC $\mathcal{C}_{\mathsf{STLC}}$ whose objects are STLC types and whose morphisms are $\beta\eta$-equivalence classes of terms. This object is both the *syntax* of STLC (it is built from the type grammar) and the *universal model* (any other CCC-model factors through it). The syntactic category is simultaneously a syntactic and semantic object — a pattern that recurs at every level of the categorical logic hierarchy, up through dependent types and HoTT.
