When types are allowed to depend on terms, the cartesian closed structure of a CCC is no longer enough — you need every *slice* of the category to be cartesian closed. This is the locally cartesian closed category, and it is the categorical home of dependent type theory. The move from STLC to dependent type theory is, categorically, the move from a CCC to an LCCC, and every new syntactic feature of the dependent calculus corresponds to a new piece of structure in the slice categories.

The central insight is that a type family $B : A \to \mathcal{U}$ is not a family of objects — it is a single morphism $p : \tilde{B} \to A$, the projection from the total space to the base. The slice category $\mathcal{C}/A$ is the category of type families over $A$. Substitution — pulling a type family back along a context morphism — is the pullback functor $t^* : \mathcal{C}/A \to \mathcal{C}/\Gamma$. And the dependent type formers $\Sigma$ and $\Pi$ are not primitive constructions but *adjoints* to this pullback: $\Sigma_t \dashv t^* \dashv \Pi_t$. The entire syntax of dependent types is encoded in this adjoint triple.

What is non-obvious is the Beck-Chevalley condition: the requirement that substitution commutes with quantification. This is the condition that makes the interpretation of quantifiers well-behaved — that $\forall$ and $\exists$ commute with substitution in the expected way. In classical logic this is trivially true; in the categorical setting it is a non-trivial coherence condition on the fibered structure. Understanding why this condition is needed — and what fails without it — is one of the key lessons of categorical logic, and it points directly toward the fibered category framework that makes the coherence explicit.
