Dependent type theory has a dirty secret: in the LCCC formulation, "types in context" and "type families" are both modeled as morphisms with a specified codomain, and the distinction between them becomes blurred. A type $A$ in context $\Gamma$ and a family $B : A \to \mathcal{U}$ are both just objects of some slice category, and tracking which is which requires constant vigilance. Fibered categories — Grothendieck fibrations — resolve this by separating the roles explicitly: the base category $\mathcal{B}$ holds the contexts, the total category $\mathcal{E}$ holds the types, and the functor $p : \mathcal{E} \to \mathcal{B}$ says which type lives in which context.

The Grothendieck fibration is built around one key concept: the cartesian lift. Given a substitution $f : \Gamma \to \Delta$ and a type $B$ in context $\Delta$, the cartesian lift of $f$ at $B$ is the "pullback" type $f^*(B)$ in context $\Gamma$, together with a morphism that is universal among all ways to lift $f$ to the total category. The fiber $\mathcal{E}_\Gamma$ over each context $\Gamma$ is the category of types in that context; substitution is the reindexing functor between fibers. The fibration axiom is exactly the statement that substitution always exists and is unique up to unique isomorphism.

For HoTT, fibered categories are the natural setting for the interpretation of *all* dependent structure. Comprehension categories — fibrations with a context-extension operation — model the judgment $\Gamma, x : A \vdash B$ directly. And when you move to higher-dimensional type theory, the fibrations become homotopy fibrations — maps with the homotopy lifting property — and the fiber categories become ∞-categories. The Grothendieck fibration is the classical version of a structure that, in the ∞-categorical setting, models dependent types in HoTT.
