A topos is a category that behaves like the category of sets — not by having elements or membership, but by having the same categorical structure that makes set theory work. The key ingredient beyond the CCC structure is the *subobject classifier* $\Omega$: an object that plays the role of $\{0,1\}$ in $\mathbf{Set}$, classifying all "subobjects" (monomorphisms) via characteristic morphisms. Every predicate on an object $A$ is a morphism $A \to \Omega$, and the logical connectives $\wedge, \vee, \Rightarrow, \neg$ become morphisms on $\Omega$ itself.

What is non-obvious — and historically important — is that the internal logic of any topos is intuitionistic, not classical. The law of excluded middle ($p \vee \neg p$) does not hold in every topos; it holds only if $\Omega$ has exactly two elements. In the effective topos, for instance, the internal logic is the logic of computability: a proposition is "true" only if it has a computable proof. Different toposes embody different logics, and the topos-theoretic framework makes the relationship between geometry, logic, and computation visible in a single structure.

For HoTT, toposes matter at two levels. Elementary toposes model MLTT plus higher-order logic: they are the classical-logic endpoint of the categorical semantics hierarchy. But the deeper connection is to *∞-toposes* — the ∞-categorical generalization — which model full HoTT including univalence. Every ∞-topos has an internal language that is exactly HoTT, and Voevodsky's simplicial set model is the ∞-topos of Kan complexes. Understanding ordinary toposes, and especially the role of $\Omega$ as the classifier of propositions, gives you the template for understanding what changes and what stays the same in the ∞-categorical case.
