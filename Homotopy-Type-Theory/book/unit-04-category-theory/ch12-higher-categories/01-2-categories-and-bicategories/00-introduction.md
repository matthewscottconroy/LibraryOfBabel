The category $\mathbf{Cat}$ of small categories has three levels of structure: categories as objects, functors as morphisms, and natural transformations as morphisms between morphisms. When you notice that natural transformations can be composed in two distinct ways — vertically (sequentially between the same functor pair) and horizontally (side by side across composable functors) — you have discovered 2-categorical structure. This is not an artifact of $\mathbf{Cat}$ specifically. It is a pattern that recurs throughout mathematics wherever there are morphisms between morphisms.

The central tension introduced here is strict versus weak: in a strict 2-category, associativity of composition holds as a literal equality; in a bicategory, it holds only up to a specified isomorphism (the associator), which must itself satisfy a coherence condition (the pentagon identity). Most naturally occurring 2-categories are bicategories — the monoidal category of vector spaces, the 2-category of rings and bimodules, the fundamental 2-groupoid of a space. Mac Lane's coherence theorem says every bicategory is equivalent to a strict 2-category, so the weakness is harmless in low dimensions. In higher dimensions, this theorem fails, and the weakness becomes inescapable.

For HoTT, the strict-versus-weak distinction is the key conceptual divide. A 2-truncated type in HoTT behaves like a bicategory, not a strict 2-category: path concatenation is only associative up to a 2-path, and that 2-path is only coherent up to a 3-path. Insisting on strict associativity would mean imposing UIP-like constraints at every dimension. The move from strict 2-categories to bicategories is the first step in the direction HoTT actually travels: toward structures that are weak at every level, coherent without being strict.
