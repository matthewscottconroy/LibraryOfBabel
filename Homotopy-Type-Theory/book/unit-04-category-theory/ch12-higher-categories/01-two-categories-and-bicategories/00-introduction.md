The category $\mathbf{Cat}$ of small categories has three levels of structure: categories as objects, functors as morphisms, and natural transformations as morphisms between morphisms. When you notice that you can compose natural transformations in two ways — vertically (one after another between the same pair of functors) and horizontally (side by side across different functors) — you have discovered the 2-categorical structure of $\mathbf{Cat}$. This is not a curiosity. It is the first instance of a pattern that extends all the way to ∞.

The distinction between strict 2-categories and bicategories is the first place where a fundamental tension in higher category theory appears: associativity and unit laws can hold *strictly* (as equalities) or *up to isomorphism* (with specified isomorphisms satisfying coherence conditions). Most naturally occurring 2-categories are bicategories — only weakly associative — and the coherence theorem (Mac Lane's coherence theorem for monoidal categories, and its 2-categorical generalization) says that this weakness is harmless: every bicategory is equivalent to a strict 2-category. But the proof of the coherence theorem is itself non-trivial, and in dimensions higher than 2, the analogous result fails.

For HoTT, 2-categories are the first step in understanding why *strict* higher structure is the wrong thing to look for. A type in HoTT is not a strict 2-groupoid (where associativity holds on the nose); it is a weak 2-groupoid, where associativity holds up to a coherent 2-path, which itself satisfies coherence up to a 3-path, and so on. The bicategory — not the strict 2-category — is the right model for 2-truncated types. Understanding the strict-versus-weak distinction here is essential preparation for the ∞-categorical theory that follows.
