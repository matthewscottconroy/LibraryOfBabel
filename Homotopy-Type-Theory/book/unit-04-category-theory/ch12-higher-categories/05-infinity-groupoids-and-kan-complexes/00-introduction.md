What is a homotopy type? Not a topological space — two homeomorphic spaces might be considered distinct, and two non-homeomorphic spaces might be considered the same (if they are homotopy equivalent). The right notion is the homotopy type: an equivalence class under weak homotopy equivalence. The homotopy hypothesis says these are exactly the ∞-groupoids. The question then becomes: what is an ∞-groupoid?

The answer that has proved most useful in practice — and that underlies Voevodsky's model of HoTT — is the Kan complex: a simplicial set in which every horn can be filled. A simplicial set is a combinatorial object assembling 0-simplices (points), 1-simplices (edges), 2-simplices (triangles), and higher simplices with compatible face and degeneracy maps. A Kan complex is one where any "cup" — any horn with one face and the interior missing — can be completed to a full simplex. The inner horn fillers give composition; the outer horn fillers give inverses; higher fillers give all the higher coherence data of an ∞-groupoid.

The singular simplicial set $\mathsf{Sing}(X)$ of a topological space $X$ is always a Kan complex: the face maps record the boundaries of simplices in $X$, and the horn-filling condition follows from the contractibility of the simplex. Conversely, the geometric realization $|K|$ of a Kan complex $K$ is a topological space, and these two constructions are adjoint and Quillen-equivalent. For HoTT, the universe of Kan complexes *is* the semantic model: types are Kan complexes, dependent types are Kan fibrations, and the identity type is the path-space fibration. The combinatorial and the homotopical are the same thing, expressed in different languages.
