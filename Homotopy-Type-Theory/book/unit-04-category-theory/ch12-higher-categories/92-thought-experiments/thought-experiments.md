# Thought Experiments: Higher Category Theory

## 1. The Coherence Theorem Is Not Trivial

The coherence theorem for bicategories says: every bicategory is equivalent to a strict 2-category. This might seem to mean: "weakness doesn't matter — you can always strictify."

But this is wrong. The coherence theorem for bicategories does not extend to tricategories (3-categories). Gordon, Power, and Street (1995) proved that not every tricategory is equivalent to a strict 3-category. The "strictification" fails.

This means something deep: weakening — allowing composition to be defined only up to higher isomorphisms — is not a superficial generalization that can always be undone. At dimension 3 and above, weak and strict higher categories are genuinely different.

Why does strictification work for 2-categories but fail for 3-categories? The answer involves the nature of the coherence data. For 2-categories, the pentagon and triangle axioms for the associator and unitors are sufficient to capture all coherences. For 3-categories, there are additional "interchanger" coherences that cannot all be strictified simultaneously.

The question: Is there a fundamental reason why dimension 3 is where strictification fails? Is there a connection to the failure of the Eckmann-Hilton argument to work in lower dimensions, or to the existence of non-trivial Postnikov invariants starting at level 2 in homotopy theory? What is the "depth" of the coherence data, and at what dimension does it become irreducibly complex?

## 2. The Circle Has One Group, Not None

The fundamental *group* of a point is trivial: $\pi_1(\{*\}, *) = 0$. A point has no non-trivial loops. The fundamental group of $S^1$ is $\mathbb{Z}$.

In HoTT, the circle $S^1$ is defined by:
- One point constructor: $\mathsf{base} : S^1$
- One path constructor: $\mathsf{loop} : \mathsf{base} = \mathsf{base}$

The loop constructor creates a nontrivial element of the identity type. But why does this give $\mathbb{Z}$ and not just $\{1, \mathsf{loop}\}$ (a two-element group)?

The answer: the loop can be *iterated*. The path $\mathsf{loop}^2 = \mathsf{loop} \cdot \mathsf{loop}$ is a new element of $\mathsf{base} = \mathsf{base}$, and $\mathsf{loop}^n$ for each integer $n$ (with $\mathsf{loop}^{-n}$ using path inversion) gives a copy of $\mathbb{Z}$.

The proof that $\pi_1(S^1) = \mathbb{Z}$ — not just that $\pi_1(S^1)$ is generated by $\mathsf{loop}$, but that $\mathsf{loop}^m \neq \mathsf{loop}^n$ for $m \neq n$ — requires the full strength of the encode-decode method. It is not trivial.

The thought experiment: what would mathematics look like if $\pi_1(S^1)$ were not $\mathbb{Z}$ but some other group? What HoTT axioms would you need to change? And does the fact that $\pi_1(S^1) = \mathbb{Z}$ (a provable theorem in HoTT) show that HoTT is strong enough to be an honest foundation for algebraic topology?

## 3. The Homotopy Hypothesis Is a Completeness Theorem

The homotopy hypothesis says: homotopy types = ∞-groupoids. This is a *completeness* statement: every ∞-groupoid can be realized as the homotopy type of a space, and every homotopy type can be captured by an ∞-groupoid.

Compare this to Gödel's completeness theorem for first-order logic: every consistent first-order theory has a model. The completeness theorem says that the semantic notion (truth in a model) and the syntactic notion (provability) coincide.

The homotopy hypothesis is analogous: the "semantic" notion (homotopy type, defined geometrically) and the "syntactic" notion (∞-groupoid, defined combinatorially/algebraically) coincide. Geometry and algebra capture the same mathematical content.

This analogy suggests: is there a "Gödel incompleteness" theorem for homotopy theory? Is there a "homotopy type" that cannot be described by any finitely presented ∞-groupoid? (The answer is yes: there are uncountable homotopy types, and only countably many finitely presented ∞-groupoids. But this is a size issue, not a conceptual incompleteness.)

The deeper question: are there "homotopy facts" that are not provable within HoTT? By Gödel's theorems (applied to HoTT, which is strong enough to formalize arithmetic), yes — there are statements that are true in all ∞-toposes but not provable in HoTT. What would such a statement look like?

## 4. Composition in ∞-Categories Is Not Unique

In an ordinary category, composition is strictly unique: given $f : a \to b$ and $g : b \to c$, the composite $g \circ f : a \to c$ is a specific morphism. No ambiguity.

In a quasi-category, given 1-simplices $f$ (an edge from $a$ to $b$) and $g$ (an edge from $b$ to $c$), a "composite" is a 1-simplex $h$ from $a$ to $c$ together with a 2-simplex witnessing that $h = g \circ f$. But this 2-simplex is not unique: there may be many 2-simplices witnessing the same composition.

This means: in an (∞,1)-category, the composite of two morphisms is not a specific morphism, but a morphism *up to homotopy* — defined only up to the choice of the witnessing 2-simplex. Different choices of witness give different (but homotopic) composites.

This seems alarming: how can composition be a well-defined operation if it's not unique?

The resolution: in an ∞-category, composition is only well-defined *up to contractible ambiguity*. The space of composites $h$ (together with witnessing 2-simplices) is *contractible* — it has a single "homotopy type" even if it has multiple elements. Any two composites are homotopic, and the homotopy is itself unique up to higher homotopy, and so on.

This is the categorical content of the Kan horn filling condition: the filling is not unique, but the *space of fillings* is contractible (or more precisely, the nerve of the category of fillings is contractible). This contractibility is what makes ∞-categorical composition well-defined.

In type theory: this is why the groupoid laws hold only *propositionally* in MLTT — the witnesses of the laws are not unique, but the type of witnesses is contractible (in h-level $-2$, i.e., the witnesses are all propositionally equal).

## 5. ∞-Toposes as Foundations

Lurie's theorem says: HoTT is the internal language of ∞-toposes. This means every ∞-topos gives a model of HoTT, and every statement provable in HoTT is true in every ∞-topos.

The ∞-category of ∞-groupoids $\mathcal{S}$ is the "simplest" ∞-topos. The ∞-toposes are the "natural" environments for homotopy-theoretic mathematics.

Now ask: which HoTT axioms correspond to which ∞-topos-theoretic properties?

- **Univalence:** The existence of an object classifier $\mathcal{U}$ (an object whose path spaces are spaces of equivalences). This is a defining property of ∞-toposes.
- **Higher inductive types:** The existence of homotopy pushouts, suspensions, and other homotopy colimits. These exist in every ∞-topos (which is cocomplete by definition).
- **Propositional truncation:** The existence of the (-1)-truncation modality. This exists in every ∞-topos.
- **LEM (Law of Excluded Middle):** NOT a consequence of ∞-topos axioms. It fails in most ∞-toposes (e.g., sheaves on non-Boolean locales). LEM is an additional axiom for "Boolean" ∞-toposes.
- **Axiom of Choice:** Also NOT implied by ∞-topos axioms. The "choice" whether ∞-toposes satisfy AC varies.

The thought experiment: Can we use ∞-topos theory to classify HoTT-independent statements? Just as forcing classifies ZFC-independent statements (by constructing models where the statement holds and models where it fails), can we classify HoTT-independent statements by constructing ∞-toposes that validate or invalidate them?

## 6. Why ∞ Is the Right Cutoff

We have n-categories for each $n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$, and ∞-categories as the limit. But why is ∞ the "right" notion? Why not work with $n$-categories for large finite $n$?

The answer: because in homotopy theory, the higher dimensional structure genuinely never stabilizes. The homotopy groups of spheres $\pi_n(S^m)$ are nontrivial for arbitrarily large $n$ (and are not finitely generated in general). The stable homotopy groups of spheres (the limit as $m \to \infty$) are a graded ring that continues indefinitely.

For any finite $n$, an $n$-groupoid would miss the homotopy structure above level $n$. Two spaces that are equivalent as $n$-groupoids might differ as $(n+1)$-groupoids — they might have different $(n+1)$-st homotopy groups.

The ∞ is necessary: only at the limit do you get all the homotopy information.

In HoTT, this shows up as: there is no natural finite $n$ at which the h-level hierarchy stabilizes for all types. The circle $S^1$ is a 1-type; the 2-sphere $S^2$ is not — it has a nontrivial $\pi_2 = \mathbb{Z}$ and a nontrivial (though more complex) $\pi_3 = \mathbb{Z}$ (the Hopf fibration). Higher spheres have higher homotopy groups. No finite truncation captures all the information.

This means: HoTT must work at all h-levels simultaneously. The h-level hierarchy is not "up to some bound" — it goes on forever. And the universe must contain types at every h-level. This is why HoTT is genuinely "homotopy type theory" — it is the theory of homotopy types, without any finite-dimensional truncation.
