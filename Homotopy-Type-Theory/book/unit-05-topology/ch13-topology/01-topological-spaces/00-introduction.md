The move from metric spaces to topological spaces is not a generalization for its own sake — it is the discovery that continuity never needed distance in the first place. Once you see that the $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ definition is secretly a statement about open sets, you realize the metric was always a scaffold: useful for building the intuition, inessential to the concept. What remains when you remove it is a topology: a bare specification of which subsets count as "open," satisfying three axioms read off from the metric case.

What is surprising here is how much structure follows from so little. From three axioms about open sets you recover continuous maps, convergence, connectedness, compactness, and the entire apparatus of point-set topology. The axioms are not arbitrary — they are precisely what is needed to make the preimage definition of continuity behave correctly — but their economy is striking. You are not building a rich geometric world; you are stripping the notion of "nearness" down to its logical skeleton.

This section lays that skeleton bare. The topological spaces you meet here — metric topologies, discrete spaces, subspace topologies, product topologies — are the raw material for everything that follows. But keep the larger story in view: topology's role in Homotopy Type Theory is to supply geometric intuition for what types *are*. A type, in HoTT, behaves like a topological space, and the identity type $a = b$ behaves like a path space. To understand why that analogy is not merely decorative but mathematically precise, you first need to understand what a topological space actually is.
