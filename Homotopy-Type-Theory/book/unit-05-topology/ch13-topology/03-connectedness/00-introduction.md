Being "in one piece" is one of the most intuitive properties a space can have, and yet its formalization immediately reveals a surprise: there are two natural definitions, and they do not agree. Connectedness says the space cannot be split into disjoint open pieces. Path-connectedness says any two points can be joined by a continuous path. Every path-connected space is connected, but the converse fails — the topologist's sine curve is connected but not path-connected, sitting in the plane as a set that cannot be separated yet cannot be traversed.

This divergence between the two notions is not pathological. It points at something deep: connectedness is a negative condition (no separation exists), while path-connectedness is a positive one (a witness — an actual path — is required). You will see this distinction again in HoTT, where the difference between a type being *merely* inhabited and having an explicit element runs through the whole theory. In type-theoretic terms, path-connectedness corresponds to having a proof that any two elements are equal, not merely the absence of a proof that they are distinct.

The central role of paths here is worth noticing. A path in a topological space is a continuous function $[0,1] \to X$: a formal object that encodes "moving from one point to another." In HoTT, a proof that $a = b$ plays exactly this role. The Intermediate Value Theorem — a corollary of connectedness — illustrates why paths matter: continuity plus connectedness forces a function to take on all intermediate values, precisely because you cannot jump discontinuously across a gap. The geometry of paths and the logic of proofs are, at this level, already the same thing.
