Compactness is the topologist's answer to a problem that analysts had been solving by hand for decades: how do you control infinitely many local properties simultaneously? The classical theorems — a continuous function on a closed interval attains its maximum, uniform continuity on $[0,1]$, the Heine-Cantor theorem — all share a common structure: you cover the space by infinitely many open sets and need to extract a finite subcover. Compactness names this structure and makes it the hypothesis.

What is surprising is both the economy and the reach of the definition. You ask only that every open cover has a finite subcover — an assertion purely about set-level combinatorics. From this single condition follow theorems about continuous functions, metric spaces, product spaces (Tychonoff's theorem), and the behavior of maps between spaces. Compact spaces are, in the precise sense the definition captures, those that behave like finite sets for the purposes of covering arguments. The Heine-Borel theorem — closed and bounded in $\mathbb{R}^n$ if and only if compact — gives you a concrete grip on which spaces qualify.

Compactness interacts with the rest of the unit in ways you should track. It is what forces a continuous bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space to be a homeomorphism — a fact used repeatedly in quotient-space and CW-complex arguments. It is what makes fibrations over compact bases well-behaved. And in the model-category framework of Chapter 15, compactness appears in disguised form as the finiteness conditions that make the small object argument work. For HoTT, the philosophical lesson is broader: compactness is how infinite things are tamed by finite data, a theme that recurs whenever you replace an infinite tower of coherences with a finitely-specified structure.
