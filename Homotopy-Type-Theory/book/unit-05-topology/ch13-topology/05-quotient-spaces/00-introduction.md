Every interesting space in topology is built by gluing. The circle is an interval with its endpoints identified. The torus is a square with opposite edges glued. The projective plane is a disk with antipodal boundary points identified. These constructions are not informal — they are instances of a single categorical operation: the quotient space, which takes a space and an equivalence relation and produces a new space whose points are equivalence classes, with the finest topology that makes the quotient map continuous.

What you should notice is the universal property: a map out of the quotient $X/{\sim}$ is continuous precisely when the composite through $X$ is continuous and respects the relation. This is not a technicality — it is the entire content of the construction. Quotient spaces are to topology what quotient groups are to algebra: they kill relations by making them equalities, and they are characterized entirely by their maps out. The topology on the quotient is defined so that continuous maps recognize what has been identified.

The circle constructed as a quotient — $[0,1]$ with $0 \sim 1$, or equivalently $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$ — is the geometric seed of much of homotopy theory, and of HoTT. Its fundamental group is $\mathbb{Z}$, generated by the loop that winds once around. In HoTT, the circle is presented not as a quotient but as a higher inductive type: a type with a point constructor and a path constructor, where the path is a loop. The relationship between these two descriptions — classical quotient and synthetic higher inductive type — is one of the central themes this unit is building toward, and it illustrates precisely why topology is the geometric intuition that HoTT formalizes.
