# Quotient Spaces

## Building New Spaces by Gluing

Quotient spaces are how topology creates. You begin with a space you understand — say the unit interval $[0,1]$ or the unit square $[0,1]^2$ — and you declare which points are "the same," gluing them together. The result is a new space with a new topology, built by identification.

This construction underlies every interesting space in topology. The circle is an interval with its endpoints glued. The torus is a square with its opposite edges identified. The Möbius band is a rectangle with one pair of opposite edges identified with a twist. The Klein bottle is a square with two pairs of opposite edges identified, one pair with a twist. These are not just examples of quotient spaces; they are, in a precise sense, the fundamental objects of topology.

## The Quotient Topology

**Definition.** Let $(X, \tau_X)$ be a topological space and $\sim$ an equivalence relation on $X$. The *quotient space* $X/\sim$ is the set of equivalence classes $[x] = \{y \in X : y \sim x\}$, with the *quotient topology*: a subset $V \subseteq X/\sim$ is open if and only if its preimage $q^{-1}(V) = \{x \in X : [x] \in V\}$ is open in $X$, where $q : X \to X/\sim$ is the quotient map $q(x) = [x]$.

The quotient topology is the finest topology making $q$ continuous. Its universal property: a function $f : X/\sim \to Z$ is continuous if and only if the composition $f \circ q : X \to Z$ is continuous. This is the defining property of the quotient: all continuous maps out of $X/\sim$ come from continuous maps out of $X$ that respect the equivalence relation.

## The Circle

The most important quotient construction in homotopy theory is the circle.

**$S^1 = [0,1]/\{0 \sim 1\}$:** Take the unit interval and identify its two endpoints. The resulting space is homeomorphic to the standard circle $\{(x,y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : x^2 + y^2 = 1\}$.

Explicitly, the homeomorphism is $[t] \mapsto (\cos(2\pi t), \sin(2\pi t))$. This is well-defined because the identified points $[0]$ and $[1]$ map to the same point $(1,0)$. It is a continuous bijection from the compact space $[0,1]/\sim$ to the Hausdorff space $S^1$, hence a homeomorphism.

Alternatively: $S^1 = \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$. The real line modulo the equivalence $x \sim y$ iff $x - y \in \mathbb{Z}$ gives the circle, with the identification map $t \mapsto e^{2\pi i t}$.

The circle is the fundamental example for homotopy theory. Its fundamental group is $\pi_1(S^1) = \mathbb{Z}$, generated by the loop that goes around once. This single computation underlies the entire theory: the winding number, covering spaces, degree of a map, and ultimately the HoTT proof that the identity type of the circle is $\mathbb{Z}$.

## The Torus

**$T^2 = [0,1]^2/\sim$:** Take the unit square and identify opposite edges in the same direction. The equivalence relation is: $(x, 0) \sim (x, 1)$ for all $x \in [0,1]$ (identify top and bottom edges) and $(0, y) \sim (1, y)$ for all $y \in [0,1]$ (identify left and right edges).

Equivalently, $T^2 = \mathbb{R}^2/\mathbb{Z}^2$: the plane modulo the lattice $\mathbb{Z}^2$ of integer translations. Every point $(x,y) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ is identified with $(x+m, y+n)$ for all $m, n \in \mathbb{Z}$.

The torus is topologically a product: $T^2 \cong S^1 \times S^1$. Its fundamental group is $\pi_1(T^2) = \mathbb{Z}^2$, generated by the two fundamental loops (going around in the $x$-direction and the $y$-direction). These generators commute because the torus is a product of circles.

## The Klein Bottle and Non-Orientable Surfaces

**Klein bottle $K$:** Take the unit square and identify opposite edges, but with the left-right edges identified with a twist. Formally: $(x, 0) \sim (x, 1)$ (same direction) and $(0, y) \sim (1, 1-y)$ (reversed direction).

The Klein bottle cannot be embedded in $\mathbb{R}^3$ without self-intersection — it is a non-orientable surface. Its fundamental group is $\pi_1(K) = \langle a, b \mid abab^{-1} = e \rangle$, which is non-abelian (unlike the torus's $\mathbb{Z}^2$).

**Real projective plane $\mathbb{RP}^2 = S^2/\{x \sim -x\}$:** The 2-sphere with antipodal points identified. Equivalently, $\mathbb{RP}^2 = D^2/\sim$ where antipodal boundary points are identified. Its fundamental group is $\pi_1(\mathbb{RP}^2) = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$: the generator is a path that lifts to a path from a point to its antipode on $S^2$.

## CW Complexes as Iterated Pushouts

CW complexes are a systematic generalization of the above constructions. They build spaces inductively by attaching cells in increasing dimension.

A CW complex $X$ has a *filtration* $X^0 \subseteq X^1 \subseteq X^2 \subseteq \cdots$ where:
- $X^0$ (the *0-skeleton*) is a discrete set of points (0-cells).
- $X^{n+1}$ is obtained from $X^n$ by *attaching $(n+1)$-cells*: each $(n+1)$-cell is a copy of the closed disk $D^{n+1}$, attached to $X^n$ via a continuous *attaching map* $\phi : S^n \to X^n$ from the boundary sphere. The pushout is:
$$X^{n+1} = X^n \cup_{\phi} D^{n+1}$$
where $x \in S^n \subset D^{n+1}$ is identified with $\phi(x) \in X^n$.

The resulting space $X = \bigcup_n X^n$ (with the weak topology) is the CW complex.

Every familiar space in topology is (or is homotopy equivalent to) a CW complex:
- The circle $S^1$: one 0-cell $e^0$ and one 1-cell $e^1$ with attaching map $S^0 \to e^0$ (both boundary points of the interval go to the single 0-cell).
- The sphere $S^n$: one 0-cell and one $n$-cell, with attaching map $S^{n-1} \to e^0$ (the entire boundary collapses to the basepoint).
- The torus $T^2$: one 0-cell, two 1-cells $a$ and $b$, and one 2-cell attached via the word $aba^{-1}b^{-1}$ (the commutator).
- Real projective space $\mathbb{RP}^n$: one cell in each dimension $0, 1, \ldots, n$.

The CW structure encodes the algebraic structure of a space: the attaching maps determine the boundary maps in cellular homology, and the van Kampen theorem for CW complexes computes the fundamental group from the 1-skeleton and 2-cells.

## Why CW Complexes Are the Right Notion

For homotopy theory, CW complexes are the "right" notion of space for several reasons:

1. **Whitehead's theorem.** A weak homotopy equivalence (inducing isomorphisms on all homotopy groups) between CW complexes is a homotopy equivalence. This fails for general spaces.

2. **CW approximation.** Every topological space has a weakly homotopy equivalent CW complex (its CW approximation). So for homotopy-theoretic purposes, you can always work with CW complexes.

3. **Computability.** The cellular chain complex of a CW complex is finitely generated (if the complex has finitely many cells), making homology computation algorithmic.

4. **Van Kampen for CW complexes.** Adding a 2-cell via attaching map $\phi : S^1 \to X^1$ kills the element $[\phi] \in \pi_1(X^1)$. This gives an algebraic algorithm for computing $\pi_1$ of any CW complex from its presentation by cells and attaching maps.

## The HoTT Translation

This is where the topology becomes type theory.

In HoTT, a *higher inductive type* (HIT) is a type defined by:
- *Point constructors* (0-dimensional data): terms of the type.
- *Path constructors* (1-dimensional data): equalities between terms.
- *Higher path constructors* ($n$-dimensional data for $n \geq 2$): equalities between paths, equalities between equalities, and so on.

The correspondence with CW complexes is exact:
- 0-cells ↔ point constructors
- 1-cells (with attaching maps to 0-cells) ↔ path constructors (declaring two points equal)
- 2-cells (with attaching maps to 1-cells) ↔ 2-path constructors (declaring two paths equal)

**The circle $S^1$ as a HIT:**
```
data S¹ : Type where
  base : S¹
  loop : base = base
```
One point constructor (`base`) and one path constructor (`loop`). This is exactly the CW structure: one 0-cell and one 1-cell glued at both ends to the 0-cell.

**The sphere $S^2$ as a HIT:**
```
data S² : Type where
  north : S²
  merid : north = north   -- wait, this isn't quite right
```
Actually: the sphere needs one 0-cell, no 1-cells, and one 2-cell. In HoTT:
```
data S² : Type where
  base : S²
  surf : refl base = refl base  -- a 2-cell: a path between paths
```
The 2-path constructor `surf` is a path between the reflexivity path and itself — a 2-dimensional cell.

The translation from CW complexes to HITs is not just an analogy. It is a precise mathematical correspondence: HITs are the synthetic version of quotient constructions, where instead of "identifying points" by an equivalence relation, you "declare paths" between points. The two descriptions are equivalent for spaces that arise in practice, and HITs are strictly more general — they can express quotients that cannot be described by a classical equivalence relation.

This is the payoff of the chapter: classical topology's quotient constructions, systematized as CW complexes, are the direct precursor to HoTT's higher inductive types. Understanding how quotient spaces work is understanding how HITs work.
