CW complexes give spaces a syntax. You describe a space by declaring its cells — points, edges, triangles, tetrahedra, and their higher-dimensional analogues — along with attaching maps that specify how each cell is glued to the existing skeleton. From this finite or countable list of generators and relations, you can read off the space's homotopy groups, fundamental group, and homology groups. The space is presented, exactly as a group can be presented by generators and relations, as a combinatorial object with geometric content.

This syntactic character is what makes CW complexes the bridge to Homotopy Type Theory. In HoTT, a type is specified by its constructors: point constructors introduce elements, and path constructors introduce identities between elements. The circle has one point constructor and one loop constructor. The torus has one point, two loops, and a 2-cell filling the commutativity square. These are CW-complex presentations, translated directly into type-theoretic language — and the correspondence is not an analogy but a mathematical equivalence, made precise by the theory of higher inductive types.

What is non-obvious is how much homotopy information can be controlled by low-dimensional data. The fundamental group of a CW complex is entirely determined by its 2-skeleton: 0-cells are the basepoints, 1-cells are the generators, and 2-cells impose relations. Cells of dimension 3 and above do not affect $\pi_1$ at all — they operate at higher homotopy levels. This dimensional stratification of information, where $n$-cells affect $\pi_{n-1}$ and above, is the topological precursor to the $n$-truncation hierarchy in HoTT, where sets, groupoids, and $n$-types are organized by the complexity of their identity structure.
