Homeomorphism is too rigid. Two spaces are homeomorphic if there is a continuous bijection with a continuous inverse — they are literally the same space, just relabeled. But for most geometric and algebraic purposes, a coarser notion of sameness suffices and is far more natural: two spaces are *homotopy equivalent* if one can be continuously deformed into the other, not necessarily bijectively, not necessarily through a family of bijections. A disk and a point are homotopy equivalent. A coffee cup and a torus are not. The invariants that matter for algebraic topology — fundamental groups, higher homotopy groups, homology — depend only on homotopy type.

The definition is conceptually clean but requires attention. A homotopy equivalence $f : X \to Y$ has a homotopy inverse $g : Y \to X$ such that $g \circ f$ and $f \circ g$ are each homotopic to the respective identity maps. The compositions are not equal to the identity — they are continuously deformable to it. This "up to deformation" weakening is exactly what allows the disk to be equivalent to a point: you can continuously collapse all of $\mathbb{R}^n$ toward the origin. The deformation is the content of the equivalence.

In HoTT, this structure becomes the definition of equivalence for types. An equivalence $f : A \to B$ is a map with a homotopy inverse: maps $g$ and $h$ such that the composites are homotopic to identity. The univalence axiom then asserts that such equivalences of types are themselves terms of the identity type $A = B$. The geometric picture — that equivalent spaces *are* the same, up to deformation — is the intuition that Voevodsky elevated into a foundational axiom. Homotopy equivalence is not merely useful for organizing topology; it is constitutive of what equality means in the type-theoretic universe.
