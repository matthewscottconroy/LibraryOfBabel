A loop based at a point is a continuous path that starts and returns to the same location. Two loops are equivalent if one can be continuously deformed into the other while keeping the basepoint fixed throughout. Under concatenation — running one loop and then the other — the equivalence classes of loops form a group. This is the fundamental group $\pi_1(X, x_0)$, and it is the first and most important algebraic invariant of a topological space.

The computation $\pi_1(S^1) = \mathbb{Z}$ is the prototype of the entire theory. Intuitively, loops on the circle are classified by their winding number: how many times they go around, and in which direction. Two loops with the same winding number are deformable into each other; two loops with different winding numbers are not. The integers arise because winding numbers add under concatenation of loops. What makes this non-trivial is proving it rigorously — that the winding number is well-defined on homotopy classes, that it is surjective, and that it is injective. The proof requires covering space theory, which gives you the lifting of loops to the universal cover $\mathbb{R} \to S^1$.

In HoTT, paths are proofs of identity: $p : a = b$ is a term of the identity type, and loops are terms of the type $a = a$. Concatenation of paths is composition of identities, and homotopies between paths are identities of identities — elements of $p = q$ for paths $p, q : a = a$. The fundamental group of a type $A$ at a basepoint $a$ is $\pi_1(A, a)$ defined by exactly this structure, and the proof that $\pi_1(S^1) = \mathbb{Z}$ within HoTT is one of the landmark results of the field, establishing that synthetic homotopy theory can recover classical computations entirely within the type-theoretic formalism.
