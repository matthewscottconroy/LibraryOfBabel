The fundamental group detects 1-dimensional holes: loops that cannot be contracted to a point. The natural question is whether spaces have holes of higher dimension — whether there are maps from 2-spheres, 3-spheres, or $n$-spheres into a space that cannot be continuously deformed to a constant map. The answer is yes, and the resulting groups $\pi_n(X, x_0)$ are the higher homotopy groups. They are harder to compute than $\pi_1$, stranger in their behavior, and central to the deepest results in algebraic topology and HoTT alike.

The first surprise is that $\pi_n$ is abelian for $n \geq 2$. For the fundamental group, any group can appear. For higher homotopy groups, the Eckmann-Hilton argument forces commutativity: maps from $S^2$ can be composed in two independent spatial directions, and having two binary operations that each distribute over the other forces them to be equal and abelian. The argument is purely combinatorial and requires no geometry beyond the structure of the cube model — yet it captures a profound topological fact. In HoTT, the Eckmann-Hilton argument is a theorem about path types: elements of $\Omega^2(A, a)$ commute because horizontal and vertical composition of 2-paths coincide.

The second surprise is $\pi_3(S^2) = \mathbb{Z}$. Maps from a 3-sphere to a 2-sphere carry non-trivial homotopy information — the Hopf fibration being the generator — despite the maps going from higher dimension to lower. This is the first indication that the higher homotopy groups of spheres are not controlled by any simple dimension-counting argument, and their computation remains one of the central open problems in algebraic topology. In HoTT, these groups can be defined and partially computed synthetically, and their complexity is reflected in the depth of the identity types of higher inductive types like the sphere.
