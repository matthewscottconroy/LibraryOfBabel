# Important Thinkers: Homotopy Theory

## Henri Poincaré (1854–1912)

Poincaré invented homotopy theory. His 1895 *Analysis Situs* defined the fundamental group and homology, but it was his 1904 paper — the fifth and final complement to *Analysis Situs* — that posed the question that would drive topology for a century: is every simply-connected closed 3-manifold homeomorphic to $S^3$? The Poincaré conjecture was proved by Perelman in 2003, a hundred years after it was posed. Poincaré also introduced the notion of dual cell decompositions (Poincaré duality), the Euler characteristic as a topological invariant, and the idea that algebraic invariants could detect topological differences. Chapter 14 begins with Poincaré's question and ends with the technology needed to understand Perelman's answer.

## Heinz Hopf (1894–1971)

Hopf was the founder of what we now call homotopy theory proper — the study of maps between spaces up to homotopy, with all the algebraic invariants this generates. His 1931 paper "Über die Abbildungen der dreidimensionalen Sphäre auf die Kugelfläche" introduced the Hopf fibration $S^3 \to S^2$ and proved $\pi_3(S^2) = \mathbb{Z}$, upending the belief that $\pi_k(S^n) = 0$ for $k > n$ and launching the study of higher homotopy groups of spheres. His earlier papers (with Alexandrov) on the topology of manifolds and his work on characteristic classes, the Hopf invariant, and the Hopf algebras (developed for studying H-spaces) all remain foundational. He spent his career at ETH Zürich. Hopf's geometric intuition — his ability to see fibrations where others saw only maps — is the model for the approach taken in this chapter.

## Witold Hurewicz (1904–1956)

Hurewicz defined the higher homotopy groups $\pi_n(X)$ in a series of four papers in 1935–36 — an extraordinary achievement in mathematical productivity. Before Hurewicz, it was not clear how to generalize the fundamental group to higher dimensions. Hurewicz's definition (homotopy classes of maps from $S^n$, with the obvious group structure) was exactly right: it gave abelian groups for $n \geq 2$ and a rich theory of exact sequences and fibrations. His Hurewicz theorem (1935) relating $\pi_n$ to $H_n$ for simply-connected spaces is one of the central theorems of algebraic topology. He proved the equivalence of the different definitions of fibration (what are now called Hurewicz fibrations) and initiated the long exact sequence of a fibration. Hurewicz died tragically in 1956, falling from a stepped pyramid in Uxmal, Mexico, during a conference. He was 51.

## Jean-Pierre Serre (b. 1926)

Serre is one of the most decorated mathematicians of the twentieth century (Abel Prize 2003, Fields Medal 1954, Wolf Prize 2000) and one of the most influential. His 1951 doctoral thesis "Homologie singulière des espaces fibrés" introduced spectral sequences into homotopy theory and used them to compute homotopy groups of spheres in a range that had been completely inaccessible. Serre proved that $\pi_k(S^n)$ is finite for $k > n$ except for $\pi_{4n-1}(S^{2n})$ (which contains a $\mathbb{Z}$ summand corresponding to the Hopf invariant), established the long exact sequence of a fibration in the form used today, and developed the theory of Serre fibrations (the weaker notion, requiring only cube-lifting rather than the full homotopy lifting property, but sufficient for computing homotopy groups). His later work on algebraic number theory, algebraic geometry, and group theory is of comparable depth.

## Samuel Eilenberg (1913–1998) and Saunders Mac Lane (1909–2005)

Eilenberg and Mac Lane invented category theory — but they invented it for homotopy theory. Their 1945 paper "General Theory of Natural Equivalences" defined categories, functors, and natural transformations because they needed a precise language to express the relationship between different algebraic constructions in topology. They also invented homological algebra and, with their study of Eilenberg-MacLane spaces $K(G,n)$, established the cohomology of groups and the Postnikov tower decomposition of spaces. Mac Lane wrote the foundational text *Categories for the Working Mathematician* (1971); Eilenberg co-wrote with Steenrod the foundational text *Foundations of Algebraic Topology* (1952), which axiomatized homology theory in the modern sense. The Eilenberg-MacLane spaces $K(G,n)$ are named for both of them and are central to the understanding of Postnikov towers, cohomological operations, and the classification of fibrations.

## Daniel Quillen (1940–2011)

Quillen won the Fields Medal in 1978. His 1967 monograph *Homotopical Algebra* introduced *model categories* — a formal framework for doing homotopy theory in arbitrary categories, not just in **Top**. A model category has three classes of morphisms (cofibrations, fibrations, weak equivalences) satisfying axioms that enable the construction of the homotopy category and the derived functor theory. Quillen proved the equivalence (Quillen equivalence) between the homotopy categories of simplicial sets and topological spaces — establishing definitively that simplicial sets are the right combinatorial model for homotopy theory. He also proved the foundational theorems about algebraic K-theory and developed the plus construction and the Q-construction, which are now standard tools. The Quillen model structure on simplicial sets (Chapter 15) is named for him, and the axiomatics of model categories are the foundation of all of modern abstract homotopy theory.

## Vladimir Voevodsky (1966–2017)

Voevodsky won the Fields Medal in 2002 for his work on motivic cohomology and the proof of the Milnor conjecture. He later turned to foundations, motivated in part by a discovered error in a published proof of his. He concluded that machine-verified proof assistants were the future of rigorous mathematics, and spent the last decade of his life developing HoTT and the univalent foundations program at the Institute for Advanced Study. His simplicial set model of HoTT — showing that the univalence axiom is consistent — is the subject of Chapter 15. The Voevodsky model makes the connection between homotopy theory and type theory mathematically precise: the homotopy theory in this chapter is the semantics of HoTT, and Voevodsky's model is the proof that it is consistent.
