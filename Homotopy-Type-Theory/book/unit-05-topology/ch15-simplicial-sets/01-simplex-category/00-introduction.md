Before you can work with simplicial sets, you need the indexing category that organizes them — the simplex category $\Delta$, whose objects are finite ordered sets $[0], [1], [2], \ldots$ and whose morphisms are order-preserving maps between them. This category is small, explicit, and completely described by two families of generating maps: face maps that drop a vertex and degeneracy maps that repeat one. Everything that follows — simplicial sets, Kan complexes, the model structure, and ultimately the Voevodsky model of HoTT — is built from this single combinatorial skeleton.

What is non-obvious is that the simplex category encodes both geometry and logic simultaneously. The objects $[n]$ model the vertex sets of $n$-simplices, and the morphisms model the inclusions and collapses of faces. But $\Delta$ can equally be viewed as the category of finite non-empty linearly ordered sets and order-preserving maps, with no geometry mentioned at all. This duality — between the combinatorial order structure and the geometric simplex structure — is what gives the theory its power. You can work purely categorically, never drawing a triangle, and still recover all the geometry you need.

The simplicial identities satisfied by the face and degeneracy maps are not arbitrary equations; they encode the coherence of gluing. When you compose two face maps, the result must be independent of the order in which you drop vertices. When you follow a degeneracy by a face map, the outcome depends on their relationship in a precise case-by-case way. Memorizing these identities is less important than recognizing what they mean: they ensure that the combinatorial structure of simplices is genuinely consistent, that no choices made during a construction can produce contradictions. The simplex category is the grammar of simplicial methods, and fluency with it is the prerequisite for everything else in this chapter.
