# The Simplex Category

## Simplices Before Categories

A *simplex* is a generalization of a triangle. A 0-simplex is a point. A 1-simplex is a line segment (two vertices connected by an edge). A 2-simplex is a filled triangle (three vertices, three edges, one interior). A 3-simplex is a solid tetrahedron (four vertices, six edges, four triangular faces, one interior). An $n$-simplex has $n+1$ vertices and is the convex hull of those vertices in "general position" in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

The critical observation: the vertices of an $n$-simplex have a natural ordering — call them vertex 0, vertex 1, ..., vertex $n$. The faces of an $n$-simplex (its $(n-1)$-dimensional boundary pieces) are obtained by dropping one vertex. The $i$-th face of an $n$-simplex is the $(n-1)$-simplex spanned by all vertices except the $i$-th.

The simplex category $\Delta$ is the categorical encoding of this structure: the objects are the ordered sets $[n] = \{0, 1, \ldots, n\}$ (modeling the vertex sets of simplices), and the morphisms are the order-preserving maps between them (modeling the face and degeneracy operations).

## The Simplex Category $\Delta$

**Definition.** The *simplex category* $\Delta$ has:
- **Objects:** The finite non-empty ordered sets $[n] = \{0, 1, \ldots, n\}$ for $n \geq 0$.
  - $[0] = \{0\}$ — a single point (models a vertex)
  - $[1] = \{0, 1\}$ — two ordered points (models an edge)
  - $[2] = \{0, 1, 2\}$ — three ordered points (models a triangle)
  - $[n] = \{0, 1, \ldots, n\}$ — $(n+1)$ ordered points (models an $n$-simplex)
- **Morphisms:** Order-preserving (non-decreasing) functions $f : [m] \to [n]$, i.e., $i \leq j$ implies $f(i) \leq f(j)$.
- **Composition:** Function composition.
- **Identity:** The identity function $\mathsf{id}_{[n]}$.

The ordering is crucial: it encodes the vertex labeling of simplices. A morphism $f : [m] \to [n]$ sends the vertices of an $m$-simplex to vertices of an $n$-simplex, in an order-preserving way.

## Face Maps and Degeneracy Maps

All morphisms in $\Delta$ are generated by two families of special maps: the face maps (injections) and the degeneracy maps (surjections).

**Face maps.** For $0 \leq i \leq n$, the *$i$-th face map* is the unique injective order-preserving map $d^i : [n-1] \to [n]$ that *omits $i$*:
$$d^i(j) = \begin{cases} j & \text{if } j < i \\ j + 1 & \text{if } j \geq i \end{cases}$$

Geometrically: $d^i$ picks out the face of the $n$-simplex opposite to vertex $i$. When you apply $d^i$ to the vertices of an $(n-1)$-simplex, you get the $i$-th face of the $n$-simplex.

For $n = 2$ (triangles):
- $d^0 : [1] \to [2]$ sends $0 \mapsto 1, 1 \mapsto 2$ — the edge $\{1, 2\}$, opposite vertex $0$.
- $d^1 : [1] \to [2]$ sends $0 \mapsto 0, 1 \mapsto 2$ — the edge $\{0, 2\}$, opposite vertex $1$.
- $d^2 : [1] \to [2]$ sends $0 \mapsto 0, 1 \mapsto 1$ — the edge $\{0, 1\}$, opposite vertex $2$.

**Degeneracy maps.** For $0 \leq i \leq n$, the *$i$-th degeneracy map* is the unique surjective order-preserving map $s^i : [n+1] \to [n]$ that *doubles $i$* (maps both $i$ and $i+1$ to $i$):
$$s^i(j) = \begin{cases} j & \text{if } j \leq i \\ j - 1 & \text{if } j > i \end{cases}$$

Geometrically: $s^i$ collapses vertex $i$ and vertex $i+1$ together, "degenerating" an $(n+1)$-simplex into an $n$-simplex. In a simplicial set, degeneracy maps insert "degenerate simplices" — simplices that are really just lower-dimensional simplices, padded with extra vertices.

For $n = 1$ (edges):
- $s^0 : [1] \to [0]$ sends both $0$ and $1$ to $0$ — collapsing the edge to a point.

For $n = 2$ (triangles):
- $s^0 : [2] \to [1]$ sends $0 \mapsto 0, 1 \mapsto 0, 2 \mapsto 1$ — collapse vertices $0$ and $1$ to $0$.
- $s^1 : [2] \to [1]$ sends $0 \mapsto 0, 1 \mapsto 1, 2 \mapsto 1$ — collapse vertices $1$ and $2$ to $1$.

## The Simplicial Identities

The face and degeneracy maps are not arbitrary — they satisfy a precise set of compatibility relations called the *simplicial identities*. These identities are the axioms that govern the internal consistency of the simplex combinatorics.

**Face-face identity:** For $i < j$:
$$d^j \circ d^i = d^i \circ d^{j-1} : [n-2] \to [n]$$

Both sides skip vertices $i$ and $j$ of $[n]$ (just in different order). This ensures that the composition of two face inclusions is well-defined regardless of which face you take first.

**Degeneracy-degeneracy identity:** For $i \leq j$:
$$s^i \circ s^j = s^{j+1} \circ s^i : [n+2] \to [n]$$

Both sides collapse vertices $i$ and $j+1$ of $[n+2]$.

**Face-degeneracy identities:**
$$s^j \circ d^i = \begin{cases} d^i \circ s^{j-1} & \text{if } i < j \\ \mathsf{id} & \text{if } i = j \text{ or } i = j+1 \\ d^{i-1} \circ s^j & \text{if } i > j+1 \end{cases}$$

The middle case is the key: if you include the $j$-th face and then immediately collapse that same vertex back down ($s^j$ or $s^{j-1}$), you get the identity. This encodes the fact that a "degenerate simplex" formed by doubling a vertex can be "undegenerated" by taking the appropriate face.

## The Factorization Theorem

Every morphism in $\Delta$ factors uniquely as a surjection followed by an injection.

**Theorem.** Every morphism $f : [m] \to [n]$ in $\Delta$ factors uniquely as:
$$f = d^{i_1} \circ \cdots \circ d^{i_k} \circ s^{j_1} \circ \cdots \circ s^{j_l}$$
where $n = m - l + k$, $i_1 > \cdots > i_k$, and $j_1 < \cdots < j_l$.

In other words: every morphism in $\Delta$ is a composition of face maps (ordered by decreasing index) and degeneracy maps (ordered by increasing index), applied in that sequence.

This means the face and degeneracy maps *generate* all morphisms in $\Delta$, and the simplicial identities give a *complete set of relations*. The simplex category is the category with generators $d^i$ and $s^i$ and relations given by the simplicial identities.

This algebraic presentation of $\Delta$ is what makes the definition of simplicial sets so clean: a simplicial set is exactly a presheaf on $\Delta$, and the simplicial identities ensure that the face and degeneracy operations on a simplicial set are internally consistent.

## Why $\Delta$ Is the Right Category

The simplex category is "the right" indexing category for combinatorial homotopy theory for several deep reasons:

**1. Simplices are contractible.** The geometric $n$-simplex $|\Delta^n|$ is contractible (convex, hence deformation retracts to any point). This means simplices themselves carry no homotopy-theoretic information — they are the "trivial pieces" from which all other spaces are built. This is exactly what you want for building blocks.

**2. The nerve construction.** For any small category $\mathcal{C}$, the *nerve* $N(\mathcal{C}) : \Delta^{op} \to \mathbf{Set}$ is a simplicial set whose:
- 0-simplices are objects of $\mathcal{C}$
- 1-simplices are morphisms
- 2-simplices are composable pairs of morphisms
- $n$-simplices are composable strings of $n$ morphisms

The nerve takes categories to simplicial sets, and the geometric realization of the nerve is the *classifying space* $B\mathcal{C}$. This connects category theory, simplicial sets, and topology in a single elegant construction.

**3. Kan complexes model $\infty$-groupoids.** The horn-filling condition on simplicial sets (Section 3) captures exactly the property of being an $\infty$-groupoid — a structure where all morphisms at all levels are invertible. This is why simplicial sets are the right model for HoTT's types.

**4. Computability.** The simplex category is small and finitely presented (finitely many generator types, finitely many relations per degree). This makes it ideal for computer implementation: simplicial sets can be represented as finite data structures, and their operations (face maps, degeneracy maps) are explicitly computable.

## Summary

| Object in $\Delta$ | Geometric interpretation | Morphism type |
|---|---|---|
| $[0]$ | Point (vertex) | — |
| $[1]$ | Edge (two vertices, ordered) | — |
| $[2]$ | Triangle (three vertices, ordered) | — |
| $[n]$ | $n$-simplex ($n+1$ ordered vertices) | — |
| $d^i : [n-1] \to [n]$ | Inclusion of $i$-th face | Injection (missing $i$) |
| $s^i : [n+1] \to [n]$ | Collapse of $i$-th degeneracy | Surjection (doubling $i$) |

The simplex category is the algebraic skeleton of all simplicial geometry. It tells you exactly what the faces and degeneracies of simplices are, in purely categorical terms — no geometry, no topology, just ordered sets and order-preserving maps.
