A simplicial set is a functor $X : \Delta^{op} \to \mathbf{Set}$ — a contravariant set-valued functor on the simplex category. This single line of categorical notation conceals a rich combinatorial structure: a sequence of sets $X_0, X_1, X_2, \ldots$ (the vertices, edges, triangles, and higher simplices), equipped with face maps that extract boundaries and degeneracy maps that embed lower simplices as degenerate higher ones, all satisfying the simplicial identities. What you are describing is a purely combinatorial object that encodes, without any topology or metric, the information needed to reconstruct topological spaces up to homotopy.

The surprise is that this works at all. A simplicial set has no topology: it is a sequence of *sets* with operations between them. Yet the geometric realization functor $|\cdot|$ converts a simplicial set into a topological space by gluing together actual simplices according to the combinatorial instructions, and the result preserves all the homotopy-theoretic information. The Yoneda lemma gives you the standard simplices $\Delta[n]$ as representable functors, horns $\Lambda^n_k$ as combinatorial shadows of open simplices, and boundaries $\partial\Delta[n]$ as skeletal inclusions. These basic shapes are the vocabulary of the entire theory.

What this functor-of-sets presentation buys you is access to the full power of categorical machinery. Limits and colimits of simplicial sets are computed levelwise: a product is the product in each degree, a pushout is the pushout in each degree. The category $\mathbf{sSet}$ is a topos — cartesian closed, with an internal logic, and well-behaved in ways that the category of topological spaces is not. This sets up the key insight of Chapter 15: homotopy theory can be done entirely in $\mathbf{sSet}$, using categorical tools rather than topological ones, and the resulting framework is both more flexible and more amenable to formalization. That formalization is the Voevodsky model.
