# Simplicial Sets

## The Definition

A *simplicial set* is a functor $X : \Delta^{op} \to \mathbf{Set}$.

Unpacked: a simplicial set $X$ assigns to each object $[n] \in \Delta$ a set $X_n = X([n])$ (the *set of $n$-simplices*), and to each morphism $f : [m] \to [n]$ in $\Delta$ a function $X(f) : X_n \to X_m$ (the *pullback along $f$*). The functoriality conditions require $X(\mathsf{id}) = \mathsf{id}$ and $X(g \circ f) = X(f) \circ X(g)$.

Since all morphisms in $\Delta$ are generated by face and degeneracy maps, a simplicial set is equivalently specified by:
- Sets $X_0, X_1, X_2, \ldots$ (the simplices in each dimension)
- *Face maps* $d_i : X_n \to X_{n-1}$ for $0 \leq i \leq n$ (the image of $d^i : [n-1] \to [n]$)
- *Degeneracy maps* $s_i : X_n \to X_{n+1}$ for $0 \leq i \leq n$ (the image of $s^i : [n+1] \to [n]$)

satisfying the *simplicial identities* (the duals of those for $\Delta$):
- $d_i d_j = d_{j-1} d_i$ for $i < j$
- $s_i s_j = s_{j+1} s_i$ for $i \leq j$
- $d_i s_j = \begin{cases} s_{j-1} d_i & i < j \\ \mathsf{id} & i = j \text{ or } i = j+1 \\ s_j d_{i-1} & i > j+1 \end{cases}$

The elements of $X_0$ are *vertices* (or 0-simplices), elements of $X_1$ are *edges* (1-simplices), elements of $X_2$ are *triangles* (2-simplices), and so on.

## The Standard Simplex

The most basic simplicial sets are the representable functors.

**Definition.** The *standard $n$-simplex* is $\Delta[n] = \text{Hom}_\Delta(-, [n]) : \Delta^{op} \to \mathbf{Set}$.

Explicitly: $\Delta[n]_m = \text{Hom}_\Delta([m], [n])$ = the set of order-preserving maps from $[m]$ to $[n]$.

The Yoneda lemma tells us: maps from $\Delta[n]$ to any simplicial set $X$ are in bijection with $n$-simplices of $X$:
$$\text{Hom}_{\mathbf{sSet}}(\Delta[n], X) \cong X_n$$

The $n$-simplices of $\Delta[n]$ itself are order-preserving maps $[n] \to [n]$, of which there are $\binom{2n+1}{n}$ (the Catalan numbers... actually not, but the count is combinatorial). The non-degenerate $n$-simplex of $\Delta[n]$ is the identity map $\mathsf{id}_{[n]}$.

## Boundaries and Horns

**Boundary.** The *boundary* $\partial\Delta[n]$ of the standard $n$-simplex is the simplicial subset of $\Delta[n]$ generated by all faces: it consists of all simplices of $\Delta[n]$ except the top-dimensional non-degenerate $n$-simplex (the identity $\mathsf{id}_{[n]}$) and its degeneracies. Geometrically, $\partial\Delta[n]$ corresponds to the boundary sphere $S^{n-1}$ of the $n$-simplex.

**Horn.** For $0 \leq k \leq n$, the *$(n,k)$-horn* $\Lambda^n_k$ is the simplicial subset of $\partial\Delta[n]$ obtained by further removing the $k$-th face. It has all faces of $\partial\Delta[n]$ except the face $d^k : [n-1] \to [n]$.

Geometrically: $\Lambda^n_k$ is a "partly open" simplex — the boundary of the $n$-simplex with one face removed. Like a cup with no top, or a tetrahedron with one face missing.

The *inclusion* $\Lambda^n_k \hookrightarrow \Delta[n]$ is the horn inclusion, and "filling" a horn means finding an extension of a map $\Lambda^n_k \to X$ to a map $\Delta[n] \to X$. This is the Kan condition.

## Geometric Realization

Simplicial sets encode combinatorial information, but we want to connect them to topology. The *geometric realization* functor does this.

**Definition.** The *geometric realization* of a simplicial set $X$ is:
$$|X| = \bigsqcup_n X_n \times |\Delta^n| \; / \sim$$

where $|\Delta^n| = \{(t_0, \ldots, t_n) \in \mathbb{R}^{n+1} : t_i \geq 0, \sum t_i = 1\}$ is the standard geometric $n$-simplex, and the equivalence relation is generated by:
$$(X(f)(\sigma), x) \sim (\sigma, |f|(x))$$
for all morphisms $f : [m] \to [n]$, all $\sigma \in X_n$, and all $x \in |\Delta^m|$. Here $|f| : |\Delta^m| \to |\Delta^n|$ is the affine extension of $f$.

Intuitively: the geometric realization glues together geometric simplices along the face and degeneracy relations specified by the simplicial set. Each $n$-simplex $\sigma \in X_n$ contributes a copy of the geometric $n$-simplex $|\Delta^n|$; the face maps $d_i$ say how these copies are glued along faces.

**Key properties of geometric realization:**
- $|\Delta[n]| \cong |\Delta^n|$ — the geometric simplex.
- $|\partial\Delta[n]| \cong S^{n-1}$ — the boundary sphere.
- $|\Lambda^n_k| \cong$ a contractible space (a simplex with one face removed, homotopic to a disk).
- $|X \times Y| \cong |X| \times |Y|$ for "nice" simplicial sets (up to homotopy equivalence; exactly true in the compactly generated topology).

Geometric realization sends simplicial sets to CW complexes: the $n$-simplices of $X$ become $n$-cells of $|X|$.

## The Singular Complex

In the opposite direction: given a topological space $Y$, its *singular complex* $\text{Sing}(Y)$ is a simplicial set.

**Definition.** $\text{Sing}(Y)_n = \text{Hom}_{\mathbf{Top}}(|\Delta^n|, Y)$ — the set of continuous maps from the geometric $n$-simplex to $Y$.

The face and degeneracy maps of $\text{Sing}(Y)$ are defined by precomposition with the face and degeneracy maps of $|\Delta^n|$: $(d_i \sigma)(x) = \sigma(|d^i|(x))$ for $\sigma \in \text{Sing}(Y)_n$.

The simplicial identities hold because the geometric face and degeneracy maps satisfy the same relations. So $\text{Sing}(Y)$ is indeed a simplicial set.

An element $\sigma \in \text{Sing}(Y)_n$ is a *singular $n$-simplex* in $Y$ — a continuous map from the standard geometric simplex to $Y$. The singular complex $\text{Sing}(Y)$ is the same data used in the definition of singular homology: $H_n(Y) = H_n(\text{Sing}(Y))$.

## The Adjunction $|-| \dashv \text{Sing}$

The geometric realization and singular complex are adjoint functors:
$$\text{Hom}_{\mathbf{Top}}(|X|, Y) \cong \text{Hom}_{\mathbf{sSet}}(X, \text{Sing}(Y))$$

naturally in simplicial sets $X$ and topological spaces $Y$.

*Proof sketch.* A map $|X| \to Y$ specifies, for each $n$-simplex $\sigma \in X_n$, a continuous map $|\Delta^n| \to Y$ (its image in $|X|$, composed with the map to $Y$). This is exactly the data of a map $X \to \text{Sing}(Y)$: for each $\sigma \in X_n$, a singular $n$-simplex in $Y$.

The adjunction $|-| \dashv \text{Sing}$ is the bridge between the combinatorial world of simplicial sets and the topological world of spaces. It allows us to translate between the two, carrying homotopy-theoretic information across.

The unit of the adjunction $X \to \text{Sing}(|X|)$ sends each simplex of $X$ to its corresponding singular simplex in $|X|$. The counit $|\text{Sing}(Y)| \to Y$ is a homotopy equivalence (in fact, a weak homotopy equivalence) for nice spaces $Y$ — this is one of the key facts about the adjunction.

## Why Simplicial Sets Combinatorially Encode Continuous Shapes

The geometric realization sends simplicial sets to topological spaces in a way that preserves all homotopy-theoretic information. Conversely, the singular complex turns every topological space into a simplicial set that knows everything about the space's homotopy theory.

But the simplicial set $\text{Sing}(Y)$ is often much larger than necessary — it has uncountably many simplices even for a simple space like $S^1$. For computation and for model theory, we want simplicial sets that encode the right homotopy type with a manageable amount of data.

This is what Kan complexes (Section 3) provide: simplicial sets satisfying the horn-filling condition, which ensures they have enough structure to correctly model homotopy types. Every simplicial set is weakly equivalent (in the homotopy-theoretic sense) to its singular complex, which is always a Kan complex.

The passage from a general simplicial set to a Kan complex is the *Kan fibrant replacement* or *Kan completion* — a systematic way of "completing" a simplicial set by adding all the missing horn fillers. This is the simplicial analog of the CW approximation theorem in classical topology.
