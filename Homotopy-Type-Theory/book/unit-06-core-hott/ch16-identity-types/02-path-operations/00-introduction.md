Paths, as elements of identity types, are not inert data — they compose, they reverse, they obey coherence laws. The question is where these operations come from. The answer is that they all come from one place: the J eliminator. No additional axioms are needed, no special structure is bolted on. The operations that make paths behave like paths in a space are already encoded in the single rule for eliminating identity types.

This is worth sitting with. Concatenation of paths — going from a to b to c by way of the intermediate path — is derived from J by a clean induction argument. Inversion — reversing a path from a to b to get a path from b to a — is likewise derived. The groupoid laws, the associativity and unit laws and inverse laws, are all theorems proved by J induction, not axioms. What looks like rich geometric structure turns out to be a logical consequence of a single elimination principle.

The subtlety here is that these derivations have computational content that differs depending on how you set them up. Whether you induct on the left or right argument of concatenation determines which unit law holds definitionally and which holds only propositionally. This asymmetry is not a defect — it is the honest expression of what J can and cannot provide in one step. It is also your first lesson in the gap between definitional and propositional equality, a gap that will matter throughout everything that follows.
