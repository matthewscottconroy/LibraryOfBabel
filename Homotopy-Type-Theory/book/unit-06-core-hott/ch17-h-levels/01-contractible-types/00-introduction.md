Not every type is interesting. Some types are as simple as a type can be: they have exactly one element, in the sense that every element is equal to every other. These are the contractible types, and they sit at the bottom of a hierarchy that organizes all of type theory by the complexity of their identity types.

A contractible type is not simply a type with one constructor. It is a type where all the paths collapse inward to a single center. The type of paths between any two of its elements is itself contractible, and so on up the tower. To say a type is contractible is to say it contains no homotopy-theoretic information at all — it is, from every perspective that matters, the same as the unit type. This equivalence is a theorem, not a definition, and proving it requires the full J eliminator.

Contractibility is the starting point for the h-level hierarchy because it is the clearest case of what it means for a type to have trivial higher structure. Everything that follows — propositions, sets, groupoids, and all n-types — is defined by asking that path types be contractible, or propositional, or of some other bounded complexity. If you understand why contractibility is the right notion of "simplest," the entire hierarchy unfolds naturally. If you misread it as merely "one element," the hierarchy will seem arbitrary. Take the definition carefully: a center of contraction together with a specific path from that center to every element, all packed as a sigma type. The structure is the point.
