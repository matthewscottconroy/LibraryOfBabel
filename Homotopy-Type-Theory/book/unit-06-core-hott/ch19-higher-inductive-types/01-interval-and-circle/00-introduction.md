Ordinary inductive types declare points. The natural numbers have zero and successor, lists have nil and cons, and every element is built by finite application of these constructors. The paths between elements are not declared — they must be proved. Higher inductive types change this. A higher inductive type can declare paths as constructors alongside points, making specific equalities part of the definition of the type rather than propositions to be established afterward.

The interval and the circle are the two fundamental examples. The interval has two points and one declared path between them. The circle has one point and one declared loop — a path from that point back to itself. Both are simple to state and immediately non-trivial. The interval, despite having two points and a path between them, is contractible: it is equivalent to the unit type, and the segment is "collapsible" by a contracting homotopy. The circle is not contractible: its loop generates infinite non-trivial path structure, and the fundamental group turns out to be the integers — a fact proved in Chapter 20.

What the circle and interval demonstrate is that higher inductive types are not a technical device for encoding awkward constructions. They are the type-theoretic language for spaces. You define them by saying what their points and paths are, exactly as a topologist would describe a CW complex by its cells, and the theory handles the rest. The shapes of mathematics — circles, spheres, tori, projective spaces — can now be introduced directly, without embedding them in set-theoretic constructions, as native inhabitants of the type theory.
