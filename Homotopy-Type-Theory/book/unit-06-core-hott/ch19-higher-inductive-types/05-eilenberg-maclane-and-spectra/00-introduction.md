Cohomology is one of the central invariants of algebraic topology. Given a space X and an abelian group G, the cohomology groups H^n(X; G) capture information about n-dimensional holes in X — and they do so in a way that is functorial, computable, and connected to the rest of mathematics through a web of long exact sequences, cup products, and duality theorems. The fact that cohomology can be defined directly inside HoTT, using higher inductive types, is one of the strongest signals that HoTT is not a reformulation of existing mathematics but a new foundation for it.

The key objects are the Eilenberg-MacLane spaces K(G, n). For each abelian group G and each non-negative integer n, K(G, n) is the unique type with πₙ(K(G,n)) = G and all other homotopy groups trivial. The space K(G, 1) is the classifying space of G, constructed as a HIT by declaring a loop for each group element and path-of-path constructors encoding the group law and the requirement of being a 1-type. Higher K(G, n) are defined by the delooping process — taking K(G, n) to be the type whose loop space is K(G, n-1).

What makes this more than a technical exercise is the representability theorem: H^n(X; G) is equivalent to the type of maps from X to K(G, n), up to homotopy. Cohomology becomes internal. You do not need to define chain complexes, cochains, or coboundary operators. Maps into Eilenberg-MacLane spaces are cohomology classes, and HoTT provides the language to state and prove this cleanly, with all the naturality built in by the functoriality of the type theory itself.
