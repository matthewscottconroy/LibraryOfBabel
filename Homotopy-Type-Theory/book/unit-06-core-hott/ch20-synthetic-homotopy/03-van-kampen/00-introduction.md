The Seifert-van Kampen theorem tells you how to compute the fundamental group of a space assembled from pieces whose fundamental groups you already know. When you glue two spaces along a common subspace, the fundamental group of the result is the amalgamated free product of the fundamental groups of the pieces over the fundamental group of the overlap. This is one of the most practically useful theorems in algebraic topology: it lets you compute π₁ of complicated spaces from simpler components, and it underlies the classical computations of fundamental groups of surfaces, knot complements, and combinatorial group theory.

In HoTT, the pushout is the type-theoretic gluing construction, and the van Kampen theorem is a consequence of its universal property. If P is the pushout of A ← C → B, then a map from P to any type D is exactly a pair of maps from A and B that agree on C. Applying this universal property to maps into types with a specified groupoid structure gives you van Kampen. The proof does not require appeal to covering spaces, edge-path groups, or any classical topological machinery — it is an immediate consequence of what pushouts are.

This is the proper illustration of synthetic homotopy theory's power. In classical topology, van Kampen is a theorem that requires careful geometric argument. In HoTT, it follows from the elimination principle of a HIT. The theorem is not made easier by cheating — the full content is there — but the proof lives at the right level of abstraction, where the universal property does the work directly.
