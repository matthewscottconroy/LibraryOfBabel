Stable homotopy theory begins with a single observation: the homotopy groups of spheres eventually stabilize. Fix a dimension k and compute πₙ₊ₖ(Sⁿ) as n increases. For large enough n, this group stops changing. The stable value — the k-th stable stem — is independent of n, a pure invariant of the dimension offset. This stabilization is the Freudenthal Suspension Theorem, and it is one of the foundational results of modern algebraic topology.

In HoTT, the Freudenthal theorem takes a particularly clean form. If A is (n-1)-connected — meaning all its homotopy groups below dimension n vanish — then the natural map from A into the loop space of its own suspension is (2n-1)-connected. In the range below 2n-1, suspending a sphere does not change the homotopy groups; you can move between πₖ(Sⁿ) and πₖ₊₁(Sⁿ⁺¹) freely. The range of stability grows with the connectivity.

The HoTT proof uses the Blakers-Massey theorem — a statement about connectivity of pushouts that is itself proved synthetically using the internal logic — and is notable for its level of formalization: the Freudenthal theorem was verified in the Lean and Agda proof assistants as part of the HoTT library projects, with the proof essentially following the synthetic argument. What this means for the mathematics is not merely that the proof is correct, but that the proof is machine-checkable without any gap between informal argument and formal derivation. The geometry is the logic.
