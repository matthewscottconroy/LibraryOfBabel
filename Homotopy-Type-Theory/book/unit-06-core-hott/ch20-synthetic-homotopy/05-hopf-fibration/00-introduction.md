In 1931, Heinz Hopf constructed a map from the 3-sphere to the 2-sphere and proved it was not null-homotopic — not equivalent to a constant map, even though it induces the zero map on all homology groups. Before Hopf, this seemed impossible: maps between spheres of different dimensions were expected to be homotopically trivial. Hopf showed that homotopy sees structure that homology misses, and in doing so launched stable homotopy theory. The Hopf fibration generates π₃(S²) = Z, the first of infinitely many non-trivial homotopy groups of spheres that topologists are still computing.

In HoTT, the Hopf fibration is constructed using the join: S¹ * S¹ ≃ S³, and the Hopf map h : S³ → S² is defined by projecting out one circle coordinate. The key property — that every fiber h⁻¹(x) is equivalent to S¹ — is the content of the fibration, and proving it requires a careful analysis of the join construction and the way the circle acts on itself by multiplication.

The significance of the Hopf fibration in HoTT goes beyond the single computation. It is the capstone example demonstrating that synthetic homotopy theory can handle the same material as classical algebraic topology — not by encoding classical proofs but by finding genuinely new proofs, internal to the type theory, that establish classical results. The Hopf fibration was verified formally in HoTT before it was fully understood whether classical proofs could be automatically translated. The synthetic proof came first. This is what it looks like when a new foundation catches up to and begins to surpass the tradition it was built to formalize.
