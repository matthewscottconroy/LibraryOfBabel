The move that makes everything else possible is the decision to take paths seriously as functions. In ordinary HoTT, the identity type $a =_A b$ is generated inductively: you have `refl`, and J tells you how to eliminate. This is clean, but paths remain opaque — you cannot compute with them in the way you compute with functions. `ua(e)` is a formal path; you cannot look inside it and find a reduction. Univalence is an axiom precisely because there is nothing to evaluate.

Cubical type theory breaks open the black box. The interval $\mathbb{I}$ is introduced as a new primitive sort equipped with a De Morgan algebra structure — endpoints, complement, meet, and join — and a path is simply a function out of $\mathbb{I}$ with specified endpoints. Path reversal is no longer an axiom; it is the substitution $\lambda i.\, p(\sim i)$. The unit laws for concatenation follow from meet and join acting as connection operations on cubes. What was a judgment about formal objects is now a computation.

You are about to see what this shift in ontology costs and what it gains. The interval must be a sort, not a type, because you cannot quantify over dimension variables the way you quantify over ordinary terms. The De Morgan laws must hold definitionally, not propositionally, because path reversal must reduce without residual proof obligations. Every constraint on $\mathbb{I}$ serves the goal of making paths compute. Understanding the interval's algebra — not just its endpoints, but its full structure as a De Morgan algebra — is the prerequisite for everything that follows in cubical type theory.
