The most striking fact about functors in simplicial type theory is that they require no definition. In classical category theory, a functor is a pair of functions — one on objects, one on morphisms — satisfying functoriality: identities map to identities, composites map to composites. To use a functor, you must verify these conditions. In simplicial type theory, a function $f : A \to B$ between Segal types is automatically a functor. No conditions to check. No extra structure to supply. Functions are functors, definitionally.

This is not a convenience — it is a theorem, and the proof is informative. Because morphisms are functions out of $\mathbf{2}$, applying $f$ to a morphism $g : \mathsf{hom}_A(a, b)$ is just post-composition: $f_*(g) = f \circ g$. Functoriality of $f$ follows from associativity of function composition and the fact that identities are constant functions. The categorical axioms reduce to computation. Natural transformations collapse equally dramatically: a natural transformation between $f, g : A \to B$ is just a morphism $\alpha : \mathsf{hom}_{B^A}(f, g)$ in the function type — a directed path from $f$ to $g$. Naturality is automatic because $\alpha$ is a function.

The Yoneda lemma then becomes a theorem of simplicial type theory, not a metatheorem about models. For a Rezk type $A$ and a presheaf $F : A^{op} \to \mathsf{Type}$, the Yoneda equivalence $\mathsf{hom}_{A^{op} \to \mathsf{Type}}(\mathsf{y}(a), F) \simeq F(a)$ holds internally. The proof is the same as the classical one, but every step is a computation inside the type theory. This is the payoff of internalization: category theory becomes something you prove, not something you model, and the Yoneda lemma is a fact about types.
