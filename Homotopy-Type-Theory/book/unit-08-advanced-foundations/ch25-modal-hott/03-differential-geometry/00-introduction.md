Classical differential geometry pays an enormous notational tax for coordinate-dependence. A differential form on a manifold is not a single object — it is a collection of component functions, one per coordinate chart, together with verification that they transform correctly under every chart transition. A connection is Christoffel symbols in each chart, changing by a specific rule under coordinate change. The theorems — Stokes, Gauss-Bonnet, Chern-Weil — are coordinate-invariant in their conclusions, but their proofs wade through coordinates to get there. The language works but it obscures what is actually happening.

In cohesive HoTT, a differential form is a map. A connection is a lift. A locally constant function is a function that factors through $\flat$. The coordinate chart apparatus is gone entirely, not because it has been swept under a rug, but because it was always a description of something more fundamental: the cohesive relationship between a smooth type and its flat and shape modalities. A function $f : M \to V$ is locally constant if and only if it factors through $\varepsilon^\flat : \flat V \to V$. This is not a theorem about charts; it is the definition, and it is coordinate-free by construction. Any statement you can express using only the cohesive structure of $M$ is automatically diffeomorphism-invariant, because diffeomorphism is just identity in the type theory.

What is technically significant is that this coordinate-free presentation is not abstract nonsense avoiding computation — it is the right level of abstraction for the invariants that matter. De Rham cohomology, characteristic classes, and the Atiyah-Singer index theorem all have natural homes in cohesive HoTT. You are seeing differential geometry through the lens that reveals its true structure: not a theory of functions on $\mathbb{R}^n$ glued together, but a theory of cohesive types and the modalities that probe their geometry.
