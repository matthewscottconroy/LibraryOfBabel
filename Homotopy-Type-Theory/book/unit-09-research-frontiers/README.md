# Unit 09: Research Frontiers

## You Have Arrived at the Frontier. Here Is a Map of What Lies Beyond.

Something shifts when you stand at the edge of what is known. You have spent this curriculum building — identity types and h-levels, univalence and higher inductive types, synthetic homotopy theory, cubical type theory, simplicial type theory, modal HoTT. Each chapter added structure to a growing edifice. Now the scaffolding falls away, and what you see ahead is not the next floor of a building under construction but the open sky.

This is not a metaphor for difficulty. It is a description of what the frontier actually looks like: not a wall but a horizon, with specific landmarks visible and specific paths leading toward them. The mathematician's job at the frontier is not to stare into the void but to read the map carefully, identify the next achievable summit, and start walking.

The map for HoTT in 2026 has remarkable features. It is very new — the HoTT Book was 2013, CCHM cubical type theory was 2015, simplicial type theory was 2017. Many of the researchers whose theorems appear in this curriculum are in their thirties. The Brunerie number was computed from first principles in Cubical Agda only recently. The synthetic Yoneda lemma was machine-verified for the first time in 2024. These are not ancient achievements; they happened while the field was growing, while the tools were still being built, while the community was still learning what questions to ask.

This unit is an invitation, not a conclusion. It names what is known, what is unknown, what would count as progress, and who is doing the work. It points toward specific problems that are approachable today and specific problems that will define the next decade of the field. It tells you how to join the conversation that is already underway.

## What This Unit Contains

**Chapter 26: Research Frontiers** is the heart of the unit. It is organized as follows.

**01 — Open Problems.** The canonical open questions as of 2025–2026: Brunerie's number and the computational content of π₄(S³); canonicity for Book HoTT; coherence for higher inductive types; directed univalence in simplicial type theory; the computation of π₅(S⁴); the Blakers-Massey sharpness problem; stable homotopy theory synthetically. For each problem: what is known, what is not known, what a resolution would require, and how approachable it is.

**02 — Formalization Frontiers.** The active formalization projects: the Cubical Agda library and its gaps; the sHoTT library in Rzk and the Riehl-Shulman program; the UniMath library in Coq; Lean 4 and the question of HoTT in Mathlib; what it would take to fully formalize Brunerie's proof; concrete entry points for a reader who has finished this book.

**03 — Connections to Other Fields.** Where HoTT reaches beyond its own borders: algebraic K-theory, topological field theories, chromatic homotopy theory, condensed mathematics (Clausen-Scholze), programming language theory. These are not analogies — they are mathematical connections, some of which have already produced theorems and some of which are still speculative but well-posed.

**04 — How to Contribute.** The practical guide: the HoTT Zulip, the HoTTEST seminar, the arXiv, the conferences. How to find an advisor. How to start contributing to the Cubical Agda library. How to read the literature without drowning in it. Specific steps, in order, for a reader who has just finished this book.

**90 — Important Thinkers.** The contemporary community: Voevodsky (founder, 1966–2017), Shulman, Lumsdaine, Awodey, Coquand, Mörtberg, Riehl, Brunerie, Licata, Rijke, Buchholtz, Anel, Cavallo, Weaver, Barton, and others. What each person has built and where their current work is heading.

**91 — References.** The essential bibliography for research-level HoTT: primary texts, foundational papers, the current frontier papers from 2020–2025.

**92 — Thought Experiments.** Seven genuinely open questions for which no one has a complete answer: What would it mean if Brunerie's number were provably uncomputable? Can every classical homotopy-theoretic result be proved synthetically? What comes after ∞-groupoids?

**93 — Exercises.** Thirty exercises that range from exposition to genuine research work — reading Brunerie's thesis, finding and attempting to fill a `sorry` in the Cubical Agda library, stating the Blakers-Massey theorem in HIT language, engaging with the Zulip community. These are not problems with answers in the back. Several are problems the field is still working on.

**94 — Applications.** How research-level HoTT connects to active work outside pure mathematics: formal verification of cryptographic protocols, type theories for quantum computation, Schreiber's program to formalize M-theory, the long-term vision of machine-verified science.

## How to Use This Unit

Unlike every previous unit, this one does not have a fixed endpoint. You will not "finish" it in the sense of completing a set of exercises and moving on. The exercises in Chapter 26 include open research problems. The references lead to papers that themselves lead to more papers. The connections to other fields are doorways, not destinations.

The unit is designed to be used as a launch pad. Read the open problems section and find one problem that you understand well enough to think about — not solve, but genuinely think about. Read the formalization frontiers section and find one concrete gap you could attempt to fill. Read the important thinkers section and find one researcher whose work connects to what you care about, then read one of their papers.

The mathematical substance you have built in this curriculum — identity types, univalence, HITs, homotopy groups, cubical computation rules, the Yoneda lemma in simplicial type theory — is genuine mathematical knowledge. It is not preparation for real mathematics; it is real mathematics. What this unit asks you to do with it is begin.

The next result in HoTT could be yours. That is not rhetoric. Several significant results in the field have come from people who worked through a curriculum like this one, found a problem they could think about, and pushed until something moved. The community is small. The library has gaps. The open problems are real. The welcome is genuine.

Step through the door.
