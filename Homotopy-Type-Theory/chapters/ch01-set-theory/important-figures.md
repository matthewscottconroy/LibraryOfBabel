# Important Figures

## Georg Cantor (1845–1918)
*Creator of set theory; first mathematician to rigorously study infinite sets and prove that infinities can differ in size.*

Cantor was born in Saint Petersburg to a German merchant family and educated in Germany, completing his doctorate at Berlin in 1867. After a position at Halle, where he spent his entire career, he began the work that would make him the most controversial and most important mathematician of his generation. His original motivation was technical: studying the uniqueness of trigonometric series representations required understanding the structure of certain infinite point sets. This led, through a decade of deepening abstraction, to the general theory of infinite sets — a theory he had largely invented himself, with no predecessors in the technical sense.

Cantor's central contributions to this chapter are the notion of *cardinality* (the size of a set, defined via one-to-one correspondence), the proof that different infinite cardinalities exist, and the *diagonal argument*. The 1874 paper established the uncountability of the reals using nested intervals; the 1891 paper gave the cleaner diagonal argument and proved the general theorem $|\mathcal{P}(A)| > |A|$ for every set $A$. Cantor developed the theory of *ordinal numbers* (to measure "how far along" a transfinite process has gone) and *cardinal numbers* (to measure size) as distinct but related notions. The *Continuum Hypothesis* — that there is no cardinality strictly between $|\mathbb{N}|$ and $|\mathbb{R}|$ — was Cantor's conjecture, which he worked on intensely but could not resolve. Its independence from ZFC (proved by Gödel and Cohen 60–80 years after Cantor's death) is a defining result in modern foundations.

Cantor's legacy is paradoxical in its own way: the mathematician who created infinite set theory suffered from severe depression and was repeatedly institutionalized; the theory he invented was bitterly attacked by Kronecker (who controlled academic positions in Germany) and initially ignored or dismissed. Yet by the end of his life, Hilbert had declared that "no one shall expel us from the paradise that Cantor has created," and ZFC — the axiom system designed to formalize and protect Cantor's theory — had become the standard foundation of mathematics. Every result in this chapter about infinite cardinalities, ordinals, and the Axiom of Choice lives in the conceptual space Cantor opened.

## Ernst Zermelo (1871–1953)
*Architect of the first axiomatic set theory; prover of the well-ordering theorem; pioneer of the method of axiomatization in foundations.*

Zermelo was born in Berlin and studied mathematics, physics, and philosophy, completing his doctorate under H. A. Schwarz in 1894. His early research was in the calculus of variations, but a position at Göttingen put him in contact with Hilbert, who drew him toward foundational questions. His 1904 proof that every set can be well-ordered (the Well-Ordering Theorem) — using what he called the "axiom of choice" explicitly for the first time — was both celebrated and attacked. Critics objected to the non-constructive nature of the choice function. Zermelo's response was to axiomatize: to make explicit exactly what principles were being used, so the argument could be evaluated on its merits.

The 1908 paper "Untersuchungen über die Grundlagen der Mengenlehre" introduced the first axiom system for set theory. Zermelo's seven axioms — Extensionality, Empty Set, Pairing, Union, Power Set, Separation (Aussonderung), and Infinity — were designed to capture the set-theoretic reasoning of Cantor's work while blocking the known paradoxes (Russell's paradox is blocked because Separation only permits forming $\{x \in A \mid \varphi(x)\}$ for some already-existing set $A$, rather than $\{x \mid \varphi(x)\}$ over all sets). The Axiom of Choice appeared as a separate axiom precisely because Zermelo recognized it was not provable from the others.

Zermelo's axioms, extended by Fraenkel's Replacement and von Neumann's Foundation, became ZFC. He was an early critic of Skolem's move to treat ZFC as a first-order theory (Zermelo preferred a second-order formulation, which avoids the Löwenheim-Skolem paradox but is less tractable proof-theoretically), and he continued publishing on foundations into the 1930s. The tension between Zermelo's and Skolem's approaches — second-order vs. first-order — is precisely the tension between a "categorical" (unique up to isomorphism) and a "syntactic" (studied through its formal provability relation) view of set theory, which is a central theme of the limitations discussed in Section 5.

## Abraham Fraenkel (1891–1965)
*Identified the gap in Zermelo's axioms and contributed the Replacement Axiom; clarified the independence of the Axiom of Choice from ZF.*

Fraenkel was born in Munich and studied mathematics at several German universities, eventually settling in Germany before emigrating to Palestine in 1929 and spending the rest of his career at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem. His early work was in number theory and algebra, but after reading Zermelo's 1908 axioms he recognized a significant weakness: Zermelo's system cannot prove the existence of the set $\{\omega, \mathcal{P}(\omega), \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\omega)), \ldots\}$, because Separation can only carve out subsets of existing sets, not build new ones by replacement. More problematic, without Replacement, the ordinal $\omega + \omega$ cannot be proved to exist as a set.

Fraenkel's contribution, the *Axiom of Replacement* (Ersetzungsaxiom), states: if $F$ is a definable function (a class function) and $A$ is a set, then $F[A] = \{F(x) \mid x \in A\}$ is also a set. This single axiom dramatically increases the strength of ZF, allowing the construction of all ordinals and cardinals needed for ordinary mathematics. Skolem independently arrived at the same axiom in the same year (1922), and the attribution is typically shared: the axiom schema is sometimes called the "Fraenkel-Skolem" or just "Replacement" schema.

Fraenkel also gave the first clear informal argument for the independence of the Axiom of Choice from the other axioms, constructing a "permutation model" (now called a Fraenkel-Mostowski model) in which the other axioms hold but Choice fails. This technique, though limited to set theory with atoms rather than pure ZF, was the conceptual predecessor to Cohen's forcing and Gödel's constructible universe. Fraenkel's textbook *Abstract Set Theory* (1953) was for decades a standard graduate reference, and his historical and philosophical writings on the foundations of mathematics provide valuable context for understanding why the axioms are what they are.

## John von Neumann (1903–1957)
*Gave the canonical definition of ordinal numbers as sets; contributed the Axiom of Foundation; developed the von Neumann hierarchy and the NBG axiom system.*

Von Neumann was born in Budapest and was recognized as a mathematical prodigy from childhood. He published his first mathematical paper at 17 and completed a doctorate in mathematics (on axiomatic set theory) and a degree in chemical engineering simultaneously by 1926. His contributions span logic, set theory, quantum mechanics, game theory, economics, and computer architecture — an intellectual range with few parallels in the twentieth century.

In set theory, von Neumann's decisive contribution is the *definition of ordinals as sets*: an ordinal is defined to be a transitive set well-ordered by membership. Concretely, $0 = \emptyset$, $1 = \{0\} = \{\emptyset\}$, $2 = \{0,1\}$, $\omega = \{0, 1, 2, \ldots\}$, $\omega + 1 = \{0, 1, 2, \ldots, \omega\}$, and so on. This *von Neumann ordinals* definition (proposed in his 1923 paper) replaced earlier more complicated definitions and is now universal. It allows ordinal arithmetic to be carried out using set-theoretic operations directly. Von Neumann also introduced the *Axiom of Regularity* (Foundation): every non-empty set has an element disjoint from it, which rules out sets like $x = \{x\}$ (a set containing only itself). Foundation ensures that the universe of sets is well-founded and eliminates circular membership, which is the set-theoretic version of ruling out non-terminating programs.

Von Neumann introduced the *cumulative hierarchy* $V_\alpha$ of sets (sets built up in stages, indexed by ordinals), which is now the standard picture of the set-theoretic universe: $V_0 = \emptyset$, $V_{\alpha+1} = \mathcal{P}(V_\alpha)$, and at limit ordinals $V_\lambda = \bigcup_{\alpha < \lambda} V_\alpha$. Foundation is equivalent to the assertion that every set belongs to some $V_\alpha$. This picture — sets as built up in stages, with no circularity — is the correct intuition behind ZFC, and it is also the intuition behind the universe of types in Martin-Löf type theory, where types are introduced at "levels."

## Thoralf Skolem (1887–1963)
*Reformulated set theory as a first-order theory; proved the Löwenheim-Skolem theorem; independently discovered the Replacement Axiom.*

Skolem was born in Sandsvaer, Norway, and spent most of his career at the University of Oslo. He was a major figure in mathematical logic, contributing to number theory, recursive function theory, and — most relevantly here — the foundations of set theory and model theory. His 1920 paper on the Löwenheim-Skolem theorem and his 1922 address on Zermelo's axioms are foundational documents.

Skolem's critical contribution to this chapter is the *Löwenheim-Skolem theorem*: any first-order theory with an infinite model has a countable model. Applied to set theory, this yields the *Skolem paradox*: ZFC (a first-order theory) has a countable model, yet ZFC proves the existence of uncountable sets. The resolution is that uncountability is *relative to a model* — a set that is uncountable "inside" the model (no bijection with $\omega$ exists *within the model*) may be countable from outside. This paradox is not a contradiction but a profound illustration of the limits of first-order axiomatization: first-order logic cannot pin down a unique model "up to isomorphism," and properties like cardinality are not absolute across models.

Skolem was also among the first to argue (in the 1922 address) that set theory should be formulated in first-order logic rather than the higher-order logic Zermelo preferred — a choice with far-reaching consequences for what set theory can and cannot determine. His influence is visible in the standard formulation of ZFC as a first-order theory, in the use of the Löwenheim-Skolem theorem as a basic tool, and in the perspective that foundational theories are formal systems whose models must be studied carefully — not self-evident descriptions of a unique mathematical universe.

## Paul Cohen (1934–2007)
*Invented the method of forcing; proved the independence of the Continuum Hypothesis and the Axiom of Choice from ZF.*

Cohen was born in Long Branch, New Jersey, and completed his doctorate at the University of Chicago in 1958 in harmonic analysis — a field remote from set theory. He became interested in the Continuum Hypothesis as one of the famous unsolved problems (it was Hilbert's first problem) and developed the method of *forcing* to resolve it. His 1963 papers established that, if ZF is consistent, then both the negation of the Axiom of Choice and the negation of the Continuum Hypothesis are consistent with ZF — completing, together with Gödel's 1938 results, the proof that these statements are independent of ZFC.

Forcing is a technique for building new models of set theory by adding "generic" sets — sets that are in some sense as undetermined as possible, consistent with the axioms. The key concept is the *forcing relation* $p \Vdash \varphi$: a *condition* $p$ (an element of a partially ordered set) *forces* a sentence $\varphi$ if $\varphi$ is true in every "generic extension" built using $p$. The forcing relation is definable within the ground model, which allows one to establish consistency results without stepping outside ZFC. Cohen received the Fields Medal in 1966 — the only Fields Medal ever awarded for work in mathematical logic — and the method of forcing has since become the dominant technique in set theory, used to produce models with virtually any combination of properties about cardinals, definable sets, and combinatorial principles.

For this chapter, Cohen's work is relevant primarily as the definitive demonstration that the Continuum Hypothesis is genuinely independent — not just unproved. This changes how one should think about the relationship between axioms and mathematical truth: the natural questions that Cantor was asking about cardinality have no answer in ZFC, not because we lack the proof technique, but because ZFC genuinely does not determine the answer. This independence result is one of the strongest motivations for examining what set theory does and does not provide as a foundation, and for taking seriously the alternative perspectives (constructive mathematics, type theory) that the rest of this curriculum develops.
