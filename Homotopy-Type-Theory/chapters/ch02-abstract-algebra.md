# Chapter 2: Abstract Algebra — Groups, Symmetry, and the Seeds of Topology

## Introduction

Abstract algebra is the study of *structure* — the patterns that emerge when you strip a mathematical system down to its essential rules and study those rules directly, without reference to any particular model. A group might be rotations of a cube, integers under addition, or bijections of a set; the same theorems apply to all of them because they satisfy the same axioms.

For our purposes, group theory plays a double role. First, it provides the algebraic machinery that underlies topology: fundamental groups, covering space theory, and the classification of surfaces all use group theory heavily. Second, *free groups* — the most "structureless" groups, generated by a set of symbols with no relations — are the algebraic shadow of the loops in a topological space and, ultimately, of the identity types in homotopy type theory.

This chapter focuses on groups but treats them as a template for the style of reasoning we will use throughout the curriculum.

## 1. Groups

### 1.1 The Definition

**Definition 2.1 (Group).** A *group* is a pair $(G, \cdot)$ where $G$ is a set and $\cdot : G \times G \to G$ is a binary operation satisfying:
1. **Associativity:** $(a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)$ for all $a, b, c \in G$.
2. **Identity:** There exists $e \in G$ such that $e \cdot a = a \cdot e = a$ for all $a \in G$.
3. **Inverses:** For each $a \in G$ there exists $a^{-1} \in G$ such that $a \cdot a^{-1} = a^{-1} \cdot a = e$.

A group is *abelian* (or *commutative*) if additionally $a \cdot b = b \cdot a$ for all $a, b \in G$.

We often write the group operation as juxtaposition ($ab$ instead of $a \cdot b$) or as $+$ when the group is abelian.

**Immediate consequences:**

**Lemma 2.2.** In a group $G$:
1. The identity is unique.
2. Inverses are unique: if $ba = e$, then $b = a^{-1}$.
3. $(ab)^{-1} = b^{-1}a^{-1}$ (the "socks and shoes" rule).
4. $(a^{-1})^{-1} = a$.
5. Cancellation: $ab = ac \Rightarrow b = c$ (left cancellation); $ba = ca \Rightarrow b = c$ (right cancellation).

*Proof of (1):* If $e$ and $e'$ are both identities, then $e = e \cdot e' = e'$. $\square$

*Proof of (2):* If $ba = e$, then $b = b \cdot e = b \cdot (a \cdot a^{-1}) = (ba) \cdot a^{-1} = e \cdot a^{-1} = a^{-1}$. $\square$

### 1.2 Examples

**Example 2.3.**
- $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$: the integers under addition. Identity $0$, inverse of $n$ is $-n$. Abelian.
- $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}, +)$: integers modulo $n$. Identity $[0]$, inverse of $[k]$ is $[n-k]$. Abelian. Order $n$.
- $(S_n, \circ)$: the *symmetric group* of all permutations of $\{1, \ldots, n\}$ under composition. Non-abelian for $n \geq 3$. Order $n!$.
- $(\mathbb{R}^*, \cdot)$: nonzero reals under multiplication. Identity $1$, inverse of $r$ is $1/r$.
- $(\text{GL}_n(\mathbb{R}), \cdot)$: invertible $n \times n$ real matrices. The *general linear group*.
- Trivial group: $\{e\}$ with $e \cdot e = e$.
- $(\text{Sym}(X), \circ)$: all bijections $X \to X$, for any set $X$. This is the *most general* group: every group embeds in some $\text{Sym}(X)$ (Cayley's theorem).

**Example 2.4 (Dihedral group).** The group $D_n$ of symmetries of a regular $n$-gon consists of $n$ rotations and $n$ reflections — a total of $2n$ elements. It is generated by a rotation $r$ (of angle $2\pi/n$) and a reflection $s$, satisfying:
$$r^n = e, \quad s^2 = e, \quad srs^{-1} = r^{-1}.$$
This is a *group presentation* — more on this when we discuss free groups.

### 1.3 Subgroups

**Definition 2.5 (Subgroup).** A subset $H \subseteq G$ is a *subgroup* (written $H \leq G$) if $H$ is itself a group under the restriction of $G$'s operation.

**Theorem 2.6 (Subgroup Criterion).** $H \leq G$ if and only if:
1. $H \neq \emptyset$ (equivalently, $e \in H$).
2. $H$ is closed under multiplication: $a, b \in H \Rightarrow ab \in H$.
3. $H$ is closed under inverses: $a \in H \Rightarrow a^{-1} \in H$.

For a finite group, conditions (1) and (2) alone suffice (why?).

**Example 2.7.**
- Even integers $2\mathbb{Z} = \{\ldots, -4, -2, 0, 2, 4, \ldots\}$ form a subgroup of $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$.
- $A_n \leq S_n$: the *alternating group* of even permutations.
- For any $g \in G$, the *cyclic subgroup* $\langle g \rangle = \{g^n \mid n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is a subgroup.

### 1.4 Cosets and Lagrange's Theorem

**Definition 2.8.** For $H \leq G$ and $a \in G$, the *left coset* $aH = \{ah \mid h \in H\}$.

**Lemma 2.9.** The left cosets of $H$ in $G$ partition $G$ into equal-size pieces.

*Proof.* The cosets $aH$ and $bH$ are either equal or disjoint (if $aH \cap bH \neq \emptyset$, pick $c = ah_1 = bh_2$, then for any $ah \in aH$, $ah = bh_2h_1^{-1}h \in bH$, so $aH \subseteq bH$; by symmetry $aH = bH$). Every $g \in G$ is in $gH$ since $e \in H$. The map $H \to aH$ by $h \mapsto ah$ is a bijection. $\square$

**Theorem 2.10 (Lagrange).** If $G$ is a finite group and $H \leq G$, then $|H|$ divides $|G|$, and $|G| = |H| \cdot [G : H]$ where $[G : H]$ is the number of left cosets.

*Proof.* The left cosets partition $G$ into $[G:H]$ pieces each of size $|H|$. $\square$

**Corollary 2.11.** Every element $g$ of a finite group $G$ has order dividing $|G|$, i.e., $g^{|G|} = e$.

### 1.5 Normal Subgroups and Quotient Groups

**Definition 2.12 (Normal Subgroup).** $N \unlhd G$ (read "$N$ is normal in $G$") if $gNg^{-1} = N$ for all $g \in G$, i.e., if $gng^{-1} \in N$ for all $n \in N, g \in G$.

**Why normality matters:** When $N \unlhd G$, the set of cosets $G/N = \{gN \mid g \in G\}$ forms a group under the operation $(aN)(bN) = (ab)N$. This is the *quotient group* $G/N$.

Without normality, $(aN)(bN) = abN$ might not be well-defined (the coset could depend on the choice of representatives $a$ and $b$, not just the cosets $aN$ and $bN$).

**Example 2.13.** The center $Z(G) = \{g \in G \mid gx = xg \text{ for all } x\}$ is always normal (since $g \cdot z \cdot g^{-1} = z$ for $z \in Z(G)$). Every subgroup of an abelian group is normal.

**Example 2.14.** $A_n \unlhd S_n$ (the kernel of the sign homomorphism; see next section).

### 1.6 Homomorphisms and Isomorphisms

**Definition 2.15 (Homomorphism).** A *group homomorphism* is a function $\phi : G \to H$ satisfying $\phi(ab) = \phi(a)\phi(b)$ for all $a, b \in G$.

**Immediate properties of homomorphisms:**
- $\phi(e_G) = e_H$
- $\phi(a^{-1}) = \phi(a)^{-1}$
- The image $\text{Im}(\phi) = \{\phi(g) \mid g \in G\}$ is a subgroup of $H$
- The kernel $\ker(\phi) = \{g \in G \mid \phi(g) = e_H\}$ is a normal subgroup of $G$

**Definition 2.16.** A homomorphism is an *isomorphism* if it is bijective. Groups $G$ and $H$ are *isomorphic* (written $G \cong H$) if there exists an isomorphism between them.

Isomorphic groups are "the same group up to relabeling of elements." This is the correct notion of equivalence for groups — and it anticipates the key HoTT question: when are two types "the same"?

### 1.7 The Isomorphism Theorems

**Theorem 2.17 (First Isomorphism Theorem).** If $\phi : G \to H$ is a homomorphism, then $G/\ker(\phi) \cong \text{Im}(\phi)$.

*Proof.* Define $\bar{\phi} : G/\ker(\phi) \to \text{Im}(\phi)$ by $\bar{\phi}(g\ker\phi) = \phi(g)$. This is well-defined (if $g\ker\phi = g'\ker\phi$, then $g^{-1}g' \in \ker\phi$, so $\phi(g') = \phi(g)\phi(g^{-1}g') = \phi(g)$), a homomorphism, injective (if $\phi(g) = e$, then $g \in \ker\phi$, so $g\ker\phi = \ker\phi$), and surjective onto $\text{Im}(\phi)$. $\square$

**Theorem 2.18 (Second Isomorphism Theorem).** If $H \leq G$ and $N \unlhd G$, then $H \cap N \unlhd H$ and $H/(H \cap N) \cong HN/N$.

**Theorem 2.19 (Third Isomorphism Theorem).** If $N \unlhd G$ and $K \unlhd G$ with $N \leq K$, then $K/N \unlhd G/N$ and $(G/N)/(K/N) \cong G/K$.

## 2. Free Groups

Free groups are the most important groups for our purposes: they are the algebraic model of "paths in a space" and the direct algebraic precursor to the identity types in HoTT.

### 2.1 Words and Reduction

**Definition 2.20.** Let $S$ be a set (an *alphabet*). A *word* over $S$ is a finite sequence of *letters*, where each letter is either $s \in S$ (a *positive* letter) or $s^{-1}$ (a *negative* or *inverse* letter). The *empty word* $\varepsilon$ is the word of length 0.

A word is *reduced* if it contains no *cancellable pair* — a consecutive pair $ss^{-1}$ or $s^{-1}s$ for any $s \in S$.

**Definition 2.21.** Two words are *equivalent* if one can be obtained from the other by a finite sequence of insertions or deletions of cancellable pairs.

**Theorem 2.22.** Every word is equivalent to a unique reduced word, obtained by repeatedly cancelling pairs.

*Proof sketch.* Define the *reduction* of a word by cancelling any available pair, repeating until no pairs remain. We must show the result is independent of the order of cancellation (a *confluence* argument) and terminates. Both follow from a careful analysis of how cancellations can interact. $\square$

**Definition 2.23 (Free Group).** The *free group* $F(S)$ on a set $S$ is the group of reduced words over $S \cup S^{-1}$, with:
- *Multiplication:* concatenation followed by reduction.
- *Identity:* the empty word $\varepsilon$.
- *Inverse:* reverse the word and swap each $s$ with $s^{-1}$.

**Example 2.24.** In $F(\{a, b\})$:
- $a \cdot a^{-1} = \varepsilon$ (the cancellable pair cancels)
- $(ab)(b^{-1}a^{-1}) = a(bb^{-1})a^{-1} = a\varepsilon a^{-1} = aa^{-1} = \varepsilon$ ✓
- $aba^{-1}b^{-1} \neq \varepsilon$ (this word is already reduced and non-empty)

$F(\{a\}) \cong \mathbb{Z}$: the free group on one generator is the integers, where $a$ corresponds to $1$ and $a^{-1}$ to $-1$.

### 2.2 The Universal Property

The defining characteristic of free groups is not their construction but their *universal property*.

**Theorem 2.25 (Universal Property of Free Groups).** For any group $G$ and any function $f : S \to G$, there exists a *unique* group homomorphism $\bar{f} : F(S) \to G$ such that $\bar{f}(s) = f(s)$ for all $s \in S$.

$$\begin{array}{ccc} S & \xrightarrow{f} & G \\ \downarrow & \nearrow_{\bar{f}} \\ F(S) \end{array}$$

In other words, a function from the generating set $S$ to any group $G$ extends uniquely to a homomorphism from $F(S)$ to $G$.

*Proof.* Define $\bar{f}$ on words by: $\bar{f}(\varepsilon) = e$, $\bar{f}(sw) = f(s)\bar{f}(w)$, $\bar{f}(s^{-1}w) = f(s)^{-1}\bar{f}(w)$. Check that $\bar{f}$ is well-defined (respects the equivalence relation), is a homomorphism, and is unique. $\square$

**What universal properties mean:** The universal property of $F(S)$ *characterizes* it up to unique isomorphism. If $H$ is any group with a function $\iota : S \to H$ satisfying the same universal property, then $H \cong F(S)$. This is categorical thinking: objects are characterized by their relationships to other objects, not by their internal construction.

This pattern — *define something by its universal property, prove uniqueness up to isomorphism* — is pervasive in mathematics and type theory.

### 2.3 Group Presentations

**Definition 2.26.** A *presentation* $\langle S \mid R \rangle$ of a group $G$ consists of a generating set $S$ and a set $R$ of *relations* — words in $F(S)$ that are required to equal the identity in $G$. The group defined by this presentation is $F(S)/N(R)$, where $N(R)$ is the normal subgroup generated by $R$.

**Example 2.27.**
- $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} = \langle a \mid a^n \rangle$: one generator, one relation.
- $D_n = \langle r, s \mid r^n, s^2, srsr \rangle$ (the dihedral group).
- $\mathbb{Z}^2 = \langle a, b \mid aba^{-1}b^{-1} \rangle$: two generators that commute.
- Free group $F(S) = \langle S \mid \rangle$: generators, no relations.

**Connection to homotopy theory:** The fundamental group of a topological space $X$ (Chapter 14) can often be computed as a group presentation:
- $\pi_1(S^1) = \langle a \mid \rangle = \mathbb{Z}$ (the circle has one loop with no constraints)
- $\pi_1(\text{torus}) = \langle a, b \mid aba^{-1}b^{-1} \rangle = \mathbb{Z}^2$
- $\pi_1(\text{Klein bottle}) = \langle a, b \mid abab^{-1} \rangle$

## 3. Group Actions

### 3.1 Definition and Examples

**Definition 2.28 (Group Action).** A *left action* of a group $G$ on a set $X$ is a function $\cdot : G \times X \to X$ such that:
1. $e \cdot x = x$ for all $x \in X$.
2. $g \cdot (h \cdot x) = (gh) \cdot x$ for all $g, h \in G, x \in X$.

We say $G$ *acts on* $X$ (written $G \curvearrowright X$).

**Example 2.29.**
- $S_n$ acts on $\{1, \ldots, n\}$ by $\sigma \cdot i = \sigma(i)$.
- $G$ acts on itself by *left multiplication*: $g \cdot h = gh$.
- $G$ acts on itself by *conjugation*: $g \cdot h = ghg^{-1}$.
- $\text{GL}_n(\mathbb{R})$ acts on $\mathbb{R}^n$ by matrix multiplication.
- The fundamental group $\pi_1(X)$ acts on the fiber of a covering space — this is the key connection.

### 3.2 Orbits and Stabilizers

**Definition 2.30.** For $x \in X$:
- The *orbit* of $x$: $G \cdot x = \{g \cdot x \mid g \in G\}$.
- The *stabilizer* of $x$: $\text{Stab}(x) = \{g \in G \mid g \cdot x = x\} \leq G$.

**Theorem 2.31 (Orbit-Stabilizer).** $|G \cdot x| = [G : \text{Stab}(x)]$. For finite $G$: $|G| = |G \cdot x| \cdot |\text{Stab}(x)|$.

*Proof.* The map $G/\text{Stab}(x) \to G \cdot x$ by $g\text{Stab}(x) \mapsto g \cdot x$ is a well-defined bijection. $\square$

**Example 2.32.** The rotation group $SO(3)$ acts on the sphere $S^2$. The stabilizer of the north pole is $SO(2)$ (rotations around the vertical axis). The orbit of the north pole is all of $S^2$. So $|SO(3)|/|SO(2)| = |S^2|$ — which, made precise, gives the fibration $SO(2) \to SO(3) \to S^2$.

### 3.3 Cayley's Theorem

**Theorem 2.33 (Cayley).** Every group $G$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of $\text{Sym}(G)$ (the group of all bijections $G \to G$).

*Proof.* Define $\phi : G \to \text{Sym}(G)$ by $\phi(g)(h) = gh$ (left multiplication by $g$). This is a homomorphism (since $\phi(gg')(h) = (gg')h = g(g'h) = \phi(g)(\phi(g')(h))$) and injective (if $\phi(g) = \phi(g')$, then $g = \phi(g)(e) = \phi(g')(e) = g'$). $\square$

## 4. Rings and Fields (Brief)

For completeness, we mention the algebraic structures built from groups by adding more operations.

**Definition 2.34 (Ring).** A *ring* $(R, +, \cdot)$ is an abelian group $(R, +)$ (the *additive group*) together with an associative multiplication $\cdot$ distributing over $+$.

**Definition 2.35 (Field).** A ring $F$ is a *field* if every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse. Examples: $\mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}, \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ (for prime $p$).

These structures appear in the algebraic data attached to topological spaces (homology groups, cohomology rings) and in the algebra of type-theoretic constructions.

## 5. Preview: Algebra and Homotopy

The following correspondences animate the later chapters and give this chapter its significance in the curriculum:

| Algebra | Topology / HoTT |
|---|---|
| Group $G$ | Fundamental group $\pi_1(X)$ of a space $X$ |
| Group elements $g \in G$ | Loops (paths from basepoint to itself) |
| Group multiplication | Path concatenation |
| Identity $e$ | Constant loop |
| Group inverse $g^{-1}$ | Reversed path |
| Normal subgroup $N \unlhd G$ | Based covering space |
| Quotient $G/N$ | Deck transformation group |
| Free group $F(S)$ | Fundamental group of a bouquet of circles |
| Group presentation $\langle S \mid R \rangle$ | CW complex built from cells |
| Group homomorphism $\phi : G \to H$ | Continuous map inducing map on $\pi_1$ |
| Group isomorphism | Homotopy equivalence (of $K(G,1)$ spaces) |

In HoTT, this table becomes a *definition*: paths in a type *are* the elements of the (higher) group associated to that type. The identity type `a = b` is literally the type of paths from `a` to `b`.

## Exercises

**2.1.** Prove that every group of order $p$ (prime) is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$. (*Hint:* Use Lagrange's theorem to determine the order of every non-identity element.)

**2.2.** Show that $S_3 \cong D_3$. Construct an explicit isomorphism.

**2.3.** Show that every subgroup of index 2 is normal. (Index 2 means $[G:H] = 2$, i.e., $H$ has exactly two left cosets.)

**2.4.** Prove the first isomorphism theorem in full detail. What goes wrong if the kernel is not normal?

**2.5.** In $F(\{a, b\})$, determine whether the following words are equal (i.e., represent the same group element):
  - $aba^{-1}$ and $b$ — are they equal in $F(\{a,b\})$? In $\mathbb{Z}^2$? In $S_3$?
  - $[a, b] = aba^{-1}b^{-1}$ and $e$ — when does this hold?

**2.6.** Show that the free group $F(\{a, b\})$ is non-abelian by exhibiting two elements that do not commute.

**2.7.** Prove the universal property of free groups (Theorem 2.25) in detail.

**2.8.** The *commutator subgroup* $[G, G]$ is the subgroup generated by all commutators $[a,b] = aba^{-1}b^{-1}$. Show that:
  - $[G,G] \unlhd G$
  - $G/[G,G]$ is abelian
  - If $N \unlhd G$ and $G/N$ is abelian, then $[G,G] \leq N$
  (So $G/[G,G]$ is the "largest abelian quotient" of $G$, called the *abelianization*.)

**2.9.** Compute the fundamental group of a figure-eight (bouquet of two circles) using the van Kampen theorem (stated here without proof: $\pi_1$ of a pushout is the pushout of the $\pi_1$'s). The answer should be a free group — which one?

**2.10 (Challenge).** The *word problem* for groups: given a group by a presentation $\langle S \mid R \rangle$ and a word $w$ over $S \cup S^{-1}$, determine whether $w = e$ in the group. Show that for the trivial group (no generators, no relations), the word problem is trivial. Show that for free groups, the word problem is solvable (just reduce and check if empty). Novikov (1955) showed that there exist finitely presented groups with *undecidable* word problem — no algorithm can solve it. What does this say about the difficulty of automated reasoning about groups?
