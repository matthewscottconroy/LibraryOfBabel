# 1.3 Subgroups

## The Idea

A group can contain smaller groups within it — subsets that are themselves groups under the same operation. This idea is so natural and so important that it gets its own name: a *subgroup*.

Think about the integers $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$. The even integers $\{\ldots, -4, -2, 0, 2, 4, \ldots\}$ form a subset. Is this subset a group? Check: adding two even numbers gives an even number (closed); $0$ is even (identity); the negative of an even number is even (inverses); and addition is still associative. Yes — the even integers are a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}$.

What about the odd integers? Not a subgroup: $1 + 1 = 2$, which is even, not odd. The odd integers aren't closed under addition.

What about $\{0, 1, -1\}$? Not a subgroup either: $1 + 1 = 2 \notin \{0, 1, -1\}$. Not closed.

The pattern: a subset is a subgroup when it's "stable" under the group operations. You can multiply any two elements and stay in the subset; you can invert any element and stay in the subset; and the identity is there.

## The Formal Definition

**Definition (Subgroup).** Let $(G, \cdot)$ be a group. A subset $H \subseteq G$ is a *subgroup* of $G$, written $H \leq G$, if $(H, \cdot)$ is itself a group under the restriction of $G$'s operation.

Unpacking this: we need associativity (inherited automatically from $G$), identity, and inverses all to hold within $H$.

But we can be more efficient with our checking:

**Theorem (Subgroup Criterion).** A nonempty subset $H \subseteq G$ is a subgroup if and only if:
1. $H$ is closed under multiplication: for all $a, b \in H$, we have $ab \in H$.
2. $H$ is closed under inverses: for all $a \in H$, we have $a^{-1} \in H$.

*Proof.* ($\Rightarrow$) If $H \leq G$, both conditions hold by definition of a group.

($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $H$ is nonempty and satisfies (1) and (2). We need to verify all group axioms:
- *Closure:* given by (1).
- *Associativity:* for all $a, b, c \in H \subseteq G$, $(ab)c = a(bc)$ because this holds in $G$.
- *Identity:* since $H \neq \emptyset$, pick any $a \in H$. By (2), $a^{-1} \in H$. By (1), $a \cdot a^{-1} = e \in H$.
- *Inverses:* given by (2). $\square$

**Alternative One-Step Criterion.** $H \leq G$ if and only if $H \neq \emptyset$ and for all $a, b \in H$, $ab^{-1} \in H$.

*Proof sketch.* If $a = b$, then $ab^{-1} = aa^{-1} = e \in H$ (identity). If $a = e$, then $ab^{-1} = b^{-1} \in H$ (inverses). For general $a, b$: $b^{-1} \in H$ so $a(b^{-1})^{-1}... hmm$, apply the condition twice: first get $b^{-1}$ from $e, b$; then get $ab$ from $a, b^{-1}$ applied in reverse. ## Finite Subgroups Are Easier to Check

For *finite* groups, there's a simplification: you don't need to separately check for inverses.

**Theorem.** If $G$ is a finite group and $H \subseteq G$ is a nonempty finite subset closed under multiplication, then $H \leq G$.

*Proof.* We need to find inverses. Let $a \in H$. Consider the sequence $a, a^2, a^3, \ldots$ Since $H$ is finite, this sequence must eventually repeat: $a^i = a^j$ for some $i < j$. By cancellation (which holds in $G$, hence in $H$), $a^{j-i} = e$. So $e \in H$. Moreover, $a \cdot a^{j-i-1} = a^{j-i} = e$, so $a^{-1} = a^{j-i-1} \in H$ (since $H$ is closed under multiplication and $j - i - 1 \geq 0$). $\square$

This theorem relies essentially on finiteness: the argument breaks down for infinite groups. In $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$, the positive integers $\{1, 2, 3, \ldots\}$ are closed under addition but do not form a subgroup (no identity, no inverses).

## Examples of Subgroups

Let's see subgroups arising naturally in several contexts.

**In $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$:**
- The multiples of $n$: $n\mathbb{Z} = \{\ldots, -2n, -n, 0, n, 2n, \ldots\}$ for any integer $n \geq 0$. This is a subgroup: sum of multiples of $n$ is a multiple of $n$, and the negative of a multiple of $n$ is a multiple of $n$.
- Conversely, *every* subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}$ is of this form. (Proof: let $H \leq \mathbb{Z}$. If $H = \{0\}$, take $n = 0$. Otherwise, let $n$ be the smallest positive element of $H$; show $H = n\mathbb{Z}$ by the division algorithm.)

This characterization of subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}$ is the algebraic foundation of number theory: divisibility, GCDs, and prime factorization all live here.

**In $S_n$:**
- The alternating group $A_n \leq S_n$: even permutations. Closed under composition (even + even = even), and inverses of even permutations are even.
- The cyclic group generated by a single permutation: $\langle \sigma \rangle = \{\sigma^k \mid k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$.
- For $n \geq 3$: the subgroup fixing element $1$ is isomorphic to $S_{n-1}$.

**In $\text{GL}_n(\mathbb{R})$:**
- $\text{SL}_n(\mathbb{R}) = \{M \mid \det(M) = 1\}$: the kernel of the determinant homomorphism (more on this language soon).
- $O(n) = \{M \mid M^T M = I\}$: orthogonal matrices.
- $SO(n) = O(n) \cap \text{SL}_n(\mathbb{R})$: special orthogonal matrices (rotations).

**The cyclic subgroup generated by an element:**

For any $g \in G$, define $\langle g \rangle = \{g^n \mid n \in \mathbb{Z}\} = \{\ldots, g^{-2}, g^{-1}, e, g, g^2, \ldots\}$.

This is a subgroup: $g^m \cdot g^n = g^{m+n}$ (closed), $e = g^0$ (identity), $(g^n)^{-1} = g^{-n}$ (inverses). It's the *smallest subgroup containing $g$*.

The *order* of $g$, written $|g|$ or $\text{ord}(g)$, is the smallest positive integer $n$ such that $g^n = e$, or $\infty$ if no such $n$ exists. If $|g| = n$, then $\langle g \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$; if $|g| = \infty$, then $\langle g \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}$.

**Trivial subgroups:** Every group $G$ has two obvious subgroups: the trivial subgroup $\{e\}$ and $G$ itself. Subgroups strictly between $\{e\}$ and $G$ are called *proper nontrivial subgroups*.

## Intersections and Unions of Subgroups

**The intersection of subgroups is a subgroup.**

*Proof.* Let $H_i \leq G$ for $i \in I$, and let $H = \bigcap_{i \in I} H_i$. Then:
- $e \in H_i$ for all $i$, so $e \in H$ (nonempty).
- If $a, b \in H$, then $a, b \in H_i$ for all $i$, so $ab \in H_i$ for all $i$, so $ab \in H$ (closed).
- If $a \in H$, then $a \in H_i$ for all $i$, so $a^{-1} \in H_i$ for all $i$, so $a^{-1} \in H$ (inverses). $\square$

This means: given any collection of subsets of $G$, there's a smallest subgroup containing all of them — the intersection of all subgroups that contain them. This smallest subgroup is called the *subgroup generated by* those elements.

**The union of subgroups is generally NOT a subgroup.** Consider $\mathbb{Z}$ and the two subgroups $2\mathbb{Z}$ (even numbers) and $3\mathbb{Z}$ (multiples of 3). Their union contains $2$ and $3$, but $2 + 3 = 5$ is neither even nor a multiple of 3, so the union is not closed under addition.

The union $H \cup K$ is a subgroup only when $H \subseteq K$ or $K \subseteq H$ (i.e., one contains the other). Proof: if $a \in H \setminus K$ and $b \in K \setminus H$, then $ab$ can't be in $H$ (because $b \notin H$, and if $ab \in H$ then $b = a^{-1}(ab) \in H$) and can't be in $K$ by similar reasoning.

## The Lattice of Subgroups

All subgroups of a group form a *lattice*: a partially ordered set where any two elements have a greatest lower bound (their intersection) and a least upper bound (the subgroup generated by their union).

Visualizing the subgroup lattice of a group gives enormous intuition. For example, the subgroup lattice of $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ looks like:

```
       Z/12Z
      /      \
   Z/6Z    Z/4Z
    /  \   /  \
 Z/3Z  Z/2Z   Z/4Z
    \       /
      {0}
```

(More precisely: subgroups correspond to divisors of 12, and the lattice is ordered by divisibility.)

For $S_3$, the subgroup lattice has $S_3$ at the top, three subgroups of order 2 (generated by each transposition), one subgroup of order 3 ($A_3 = \{e, (123), (132)\}$), and the trivial group at the bottom.

Understanding this lattice is often the key to understanding the group itself.

## Generators and Presentations (Preview)

A *generating set* for a group $G$ is a subset $S \subseteq G$ such that every element of $G$ can be written as a product of elements of $S$ and their inverses. The subgroup generated by $S$ is $G$ itself.

For example:
- $\mathbb{Z}$ is generated by $\{1\}$ (or equivalently by $\{-1\}$, or by $\{2, 3\}$).
- $S_n$ is generated by any set of transpositions, or by just two permutations: the transposition $(1\ 2)$ and the cycle $(1\ 2\ \ldots\ n)$.
- $D_n$ is generated by $\{r, s\}$ where $r$ is a rotation and $s$ a reflection.

A *finitely generated group* is one with a finite generating set. Most groups you'll encounter in algebra and topology are finitely generated.

The question "what are the relations among the generators?" leads to the notion of a group presentation, which we'll discuss in Section 2.3.

## Connection to Future Topics

Subgroups are the building blocks of all further structure in group theory:

- **Lagrange's theorem** (next section) constrains which subgroups can exist by connecting subgroup size to group size.
- **Normal subgroups** (Section 1.5) are special subgroups that allow quotient constructions.
- **The isomorphism theorems** (Section 1.7) relate homomorphisms to subgroup structure.
- **Covering spaces** in topology are classified by subgroups of the fundamental group.
- **The Galois correspondence** between field extensions and subgroups is the heart of Galois theory.

Every time you understand the subgroup structure of a group, you understand the group itself more deeply.
