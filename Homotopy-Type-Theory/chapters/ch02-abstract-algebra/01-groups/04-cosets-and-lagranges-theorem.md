# 1.4 Cosets and Lagrange's Theorem

## Partitioning the Group

When you have a subgroup $H$ of a group $G$, a natural question arises: how do the elements of $H$ "sit inside" $G$? The answer involves a beautiful decomposition of $G$ into equal-sized pieces called *cosets*.

Here's the key idea. For any element $g \in G$, we can form the set $gH = \{gh \mid h \in H\}$ — the "left translate" of $H$ by $g$. This is all elements of $H$ with $g$ multiplied on the left. Different choices of $g$ give different translates, and these translates turn out to either be identical or completely disjoint. Together, they cover all of $G$.

## Definition and First Properties

**Definition (Left Coset).** For $H \leq G$ and $g \in G$, the *left coset* of $H$ by $g$ is:
$$gH = \{gh \mid h \in H\}$$

**Definition (Right Coset).** Similarly, the *right coset* is:
$$Hg = \{hg \mid h \in H\}$$

Unless $G$ is abelian or $H$ is a special kind of subgroup (normal — coming soon), left and right cosets are generally different things.

**Examples:**

In $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$, with $H = 3\mathbb{Z} = \{0, \pm 3, \pm 6, \ldots\}$:
- The coset $0 + 3\mathbb{Z} = 3\mathbb{Z}$ (the multiples of 3)
- The coset $1 + 3\mathbb{Z} = \{1, 4, 7, -2, -5, \ldots\}$ (integers congruent to 1 mod 3)
- The coset $2 + 3\mathbb{Z} = \{2, 5, 8, -1, -4, \ldots\}$ (integers congruent to 2 mod 3)
- And $3 + 3\mathbb{Z} = 3\mathbb{Z}$ again.

So there are exactly three distinct cosets, and they partition $\mathbb{Z}$.

In $S_3$, with $H = \{e, (12)\}$ (the subgroup generated by the transposition swapping 1 and 2):
- $eH = \{e, (12)\}$
- $(13)H = \{(13), (13)(12)\} = \{(13), (132)\}$
- $(23)H = \{(23), (23)(12)\} = \{(23), (123)\}$

And these three cosets of size 2 partition $S_3$ (which has 6 elements).

## The Cosets Partition the Group

**Lemma.** The left cosets of $H$ in $G$ form a partition of $G$: every element of $G$ belongs to exactly one left coset.

*Proof.* We need two things: (1) the cosets cover $G$, and (2) distinct cosets are disjoint.

(1) *Coverage:* For any $g \in G$, we have $g = g \cdot e \in gH$ (since $e \in H$). So every element belongs to some coset.

(2) *Disjointness:* Suppose $gH$ and $g'H$ share an element: say $c \in gH \cap g'H$. Write $c = gh_1 = g'h_2$ for some $h_1, h_2 \in H$. Then $g' = gh_1h_2^{-1}$. For any $g'h \in g'H$:
$$g'h = gh_1h_2^{-1}h \in gH$$
since $h_1h_2^{-1}h \in H$ (closed under multiplication and inverses). So $g'H \subseteq gH$. By symmetry, $gH \subseteq g'H$. Therefore $gH = g'H$.

In other words: two cosets are either equal or disjoint, with no middle ground. $\square$

**Corollary.** All left cosets of $H$ have the same size, namely $|H|$.

*Proof.* The map $\phi: H \to gH$ defined by $\phi(h) = gh$ is a bijection (it's clearly surjective; if $gh = gh'$ then $h = h'$ by cancellation). $\square$

## The Index

**Definition (Index).** The *index* of $H$ in $G$, written $[G : H]$, is the number of distinct left cosets of $H$ in $G$.

The index measures "how many copies of $H$ fit into $G$." For $G = S_3$ and $H = \{e, (12)\}$: index is 3 (we found three cosets).

## Lagrange's Theorem

We now have all the pieces for one of the most important results in finite group theory.

**Theorem (Lagrange).** If $G$ is a finite group and $H \leq G$, then $|H|$ divides $|G|$, and:
$$|G| = |H| \cdot [G : H]$$

*Proof.* The cosets partition $G$ into $[G:H]$ pieces, each of size $|H|$. Therefore $|G| = [G:H] \cdot |H|$. $\square$

The proof is almost trivially short once you have the partition lemma. But the result is remarkably constraining.

## Consequences of Lagrange's Theorem

**Corollary 1.** The order of any element divides $|G|$.

*Proof.* If $g \in G$, then $\langle g \rangle \leq G$ has order $|g|$ (the order of $g$). By Lagrange, $|g|$ divides $|G|$. $\square$

**Corollary 2.** For any $g \in G$: $g^{|G|} = e$.

*Proof.* Since $|g|$ divides $|G|$, write $|G| = k \cdot |g|$. Then $g^{|G|} = (g^{|g|})^k = e^k = e$. $\square$

This corollary is behind Fermat's Little Theorem in number theory: if $p$ is prime and $p \nmid a$, then $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$. This is just Corollary 2 applied to $G = (\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^*$, which has order $p-1$.

**Corollary 3.** Every group of prime order is cyclic, hence isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$.

*Proof.* Let $|G| = p$. Pick any non-identity element $g \in G$. The cyclic subgroup $\langle g \rangle$ has order $|g| > 1$, and by Lagrange, $|g|$ divides $p$. Since $p$ is prime, $|g| = p$, so $\langle g \rangle = G$. $\square$

This is a strong result: among all groups of prime order, there is only one, up to isomorphism.

**Corollary 4.** If $|G|$ is prime, then $G$ has no proper nontrivial subgroups.

*Proof.* Any subgroup has order dividing $|G|$. If $|G| = p$, the only divisors are $1$ and $p$, corresponding to $\{e\}$ and $G$. $\square$

## The Converse Is False

Lagrange's theorem tells us that if $H \leq G$, then $|H|$ divides $|G|$. But the converse fails in general: if $d$ divides $|G|$, there might not exist a subgroup of order $d$.

**Example.** $A_4$ has order 12. Does it have a subgroup of order 6? The divisors of 12 include 6, but $A_4$ has no subgroup of order 6. You can verify this by examining all elements of $A_4$ and checking that no subset of size 6 forms a group.

However, for certain $d$, subgroups of that order are guaranteed by Sylow's theorems — a deeper result that we won't prove here. Sylow's theorems give conditions under which subgroups of order $p^k$ (prime power) exist, which is the best general converse available.

## Cosets in the Context of Equivalence Relations

Here's a clean reformulation: define the relation $a \sim_H b$ if $a^{-1}b \in H$. This is an equivalence relation:
- *Reflexive:* $a^{-1}a = e \in H$.
- *Symmetric:* if $a^{-1}b \in H$, then $(a^{-1}b)^{-1} = b^{-1}a \in H$.
- *Transitive:* if $a^{-1}b \in H$ and $b^{-1}c \in H$, then $a^{-1}c = (a^{-1}b)(b^{-1}c) \in H$.

The equivalence class of $a$ under $\sim_H$ is exactly $aH$: $b \in aH \iff a^{-1}b \in H \iff a \sim_H b$. So the cosets are the equivalence classes.

This reformulation makes clear why cosets partition $G$: they're equivalence classes, and equivalence classes always partition a set. The magic of Lagrange's theorem is that all these classes have the same size.

## A Taste of Topology

The coset construction appears in topology as follows. Suppose $G$ is a *topological group* (a group that's also a topological space, with continuous operations) and $H$ is a closed subgroup. The coset space $G/H$ (the set of left cosets with the quotient topology) is a topological space that $G$ acts on transitively.

For example:
- $O(n)/O(n-1) \cong S^{n-1}$ (the sphere): the action of $O(n)$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$ restricts to an action on the unit sphere $S^{n-1}$, with stabilizer $O(n-1)$.
- $SO(3)/SO(2) \cong S^2$: the rotation group modulo stabilizer of north pole is the sphere.
- $U(n)/U(n-1) \cong S^{2n-1}$: complex analogs.

These quotient spaces are the building blocks of fiber bundle theory, which is the geometric framework behind gauge theory in physics and vector bundles in topology.

The algebraic simplicity of the coset construction (just divide out by a subgroup!) underlies a huge amount of modern geometry.
