# 1.7 The Isomorphism Theorems

## What These Theorems Do

The isomorphism theorems are the "calculus" of quotient groups and homomorphisms — they give us tools for understanding how maps and subgroups interact, and they let us identify quotients without explicitly computing them.

There are three main theorems (sometimes four, depending on how you count). Together, they describe:
- How every homomorphism factors through a quotient (First)
- How subgroups and quotients interact with each other (Second)
- How quotients of quotients simplify (Third)

These theorems appear again and again throughout algebra, topology, and even type theory. Getting comfortable with them pays dividends everywhere.

## The First Isomorphism Theorem

**Theorem (First Isomorphism Theorem).** If $\phi: G \to H$ is a group homomorphism, then:
$$G / \ker(\phi) \cong \text{Im}(\phi)$$

*Proof.*

Let $K = \ker(\phi)$. We know $K \unlhd G$ (proved earlier), so $G/K$ makes sense. Define $\bar{\phi}: G/K \to \text{Im}(\phi)$ by:
$$\bar{\phi}(gK) = \phi(g)$$

**Step 1: Well-definedness.** If $gK = g'K$, does $\phi(g) = \phi(g')$? 

$gK = g'K$ means $g^{-1}g' \in K = \ker(\phi)$, so $\phi(g^{-1}g') = e_H$, so $\phi(g)^{-1}\phi(g') = e_H$, so $\phi(g) = \phi(g')$. ✓

**Step 2: Homomorphism.** $\bar{\phi}((gK)(g'K)) = \bar{\phi}((gg')K) = \phi(gg') = \phi(g)\phi(g') = \bar{\phi}(gK)\bar{\phi}(g'K)$. ✓

**Step 3: Injective.** If $\bar{\phi}(gK) = e_H$, then $\phi(g) = e_H$, so $g \in K$, so $gK = K$ (the identity of $G/K$). So $\ker(\bar{\phi}) = \{K\}$, meaning $\bar{\phi}$ is injective. ✓

**Step 4: Surjective.** For any $\phi(g) \in \text{Im}(\phi)$, we have $\bar{\phi}(gK) = \phi(g)$. ✓

So $\bar{\phi}$ is an isomorphism $G/K \xrightarrow{\sim} \text{Im}(\phi)$. $\square$

## Factoring Homomorphisms

The First Isomorphism Theorem says every homomorphism $\phi: G \to H$ factors as:
$$G \xrightarrow{\pi} G/K \xrightarrow{\bar\phi} \text{Im}(\phi) \xrightarrow{\iota} H$$

where $\pi$ is the quotient map (surjective), $\bar\phi$ is an isomorphism, and $\iota$ is the inclusion (injective).

Diagrammatically: $\phi$ factors through the quotient, and the quotient is isomorphic to the image. This decomposition is universal — every homomorphism breaks into a surjection, an isomorphism, and an injection.

**Example.** The sign homomorphism $\text{sgn}: S_n \to \{1,-1\}$ has kernel $A_n$. By the FIT: $S_n/A_n \cong \{1,-1\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example.** The determinant $\det: \text{GL}_n(\mathbb{R}) \to \mathbb{R}^*$ has kernel $\text{SL}_n(\mathbb{R})$. By the FIT: $\text{GL}_n(\mathbb{R})/\text{SL}_n(\mathbb{R}) \cong \mathbb{R}^*$.

**Example.** The reduction-mod-$n$ map $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is surjective with kernel $n\mathbb{Z}$. By the FIT: $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. (Circular but correct — the FIT confirms the quotient we defined by the construction.)

## The Second Isomorphism Theorem

The second theorem handles the interaction between a subgroup and a normal subgroup.

**Setup.** Let $H \leq G$ and $N \unlhd G$. Define $HN = \{hn \mid h \in H, n \in N\}$ — the "product" of $H$ and $N$.

**Lemma.** $HN$ is a subgroup of $G$.

*Proof.* $HN$ is nonempty ($e \cdot e \in HN$). For $h_1n_1, h_2n_2 \in HN$:
$$(h_1n_1)(h_2n_2)^{-1} = h_1n_1n_2^{-1}h_2^{-1} = h_1 (n_1n_2^{-1}) h_2^{-1}$$

We want to write this as $h' n'$ for some $h' \in H, n' \in N$. Use the normality of $N$: $h_2^{-1}(n_1n_2^{-1}) h_2 \in N$ (since $N \unlhd G$), call it $n_3$. Then:
$$(h_1n_1)(h_2n_2)^{-1} = h_1 h_2^{-1} \cdot h_2 n_1 n_2^{-1} h_2^{-1} = (h_1h_2^{-1})(h_2 n_3 h_2^{-1}) \cdot ...$$

hmm, let me be more careful. We have $h_1 n_1 n_2^{-1} h_2^{-1}$. Write $n_1 n_2^{-1} = n_3$. Then $h_1 n_3 h_2^{-1} = (h_1 h_2^{-1})(h_2 n_3 h_2^{-1})$. Since $N \unlhd G$, $h_2 n_3 h_2^{-1} \in N$. So we get $(h_1 h_2^{-1}) \cdot (h_2 n_3 h_2^{-1}) \in HN$. ✓ $\square$

**Theorem (Second Isomorphism Theorem).** If $H \leq G$ and $N \unlhd G$, then:
1. $H \cap N \unlhd H$
2. $H/(H \cap N) \cong HN/N$

*Proof.* Consider the composition:
$$H \xrightarrow{\iota} G \xrightarrow{\pi} G/N$$

where $\iota$ is the inclusion and $\pi$ is the quotient map. This composition $\phi = \pi \circ \iota: H \to G/N$ sends $h \mapsto hN$.

The kernel is: $\ker(\phi) = \{h \in H \mid hN = N\} = \{h \in H \mid h \in N\} = H \cap N$.

The image is: $\text{Im}(\phi) = \{hN \mid h \in H\} = HN/N$ (since every element of $HN$ can be written as $hn$ for $h \in H$, $n \in N$, and $hnN = hN$).

By the First Isomorphism Theorem: $H/\ker(\phi) \cong \text{Im}(\phi)$, i.e., $H/(H \cap N) \cong HN/N$. $\square$

**Intuition.** The second theorem says: the piece of $H$ that doesn't overlap with $N$ is isomorphic to the cosets of $N$ generated by $H$ inside $G/N$. It measures "how much of $H$ survives" when you mod out by $N$.

## The Third Isomorphism Theorem

The third theorem handles quotients of quotients.

**Theorem (Third Isomorphism Theorem).** If $N \unlhd G$ and $K \unlhd G$ with $N \leq K$, then:
1. $K/N \unlhd G/N$
2. $(G/N)/(K/N) \cong G/K$

*Proof.* Consider the map $\phi: G/N \to G/K$ defined by $\phi(gN) = gK$.

*Well-defined:* If $gN = g'N$, then $g^{-1}g' \in N \leq K$, so $gK = g'K$. ✓

*Homomorphism:* $\phi((gN)(g'N)) = \phi((gg')N) = (gg')K = (gK)(g'K) = \phi(gN)\phi(g'N)$. ✓

*Kernel:* $\ker(\phi) = \{gN \mid gK = K\} = \{gN \mid g \in K\} = K/N$. 

*Surjective:* For any $gK \in G/K$, $\phi(gN) = gK$. ✓

By the First Isomorphism Theorem: $(G/N)/\ker(\phi) \cong G/K$, i.e., $(G/N)/(K/N) \cong G/K$. $\square$

**Intuition.** If you have a big quotient $G/N$ and want to further quotient by $K/N$, you get the same result as directly quotienting $G$ by $K$. "Quotienting in stages" gives the same result as "quotienting all at once."

The mnemonic: $(G/N)/(K/N) \cong G/K$ looks like cancelling $N$ from a fraction. That's not rigorous, but it's a useful memory device.

**Example.** Let $G = \mathbb{Z}$, $K = 6\mathbb{Z}$, $N = 2\mathbb{Z}$. Then $N \leq K$ (multiples of 6 are multiples of 2). We have $G/K = \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, $G/N = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$... wait, $N$ should be inside $K$. Let me redo: $N = 6\mathbb{Z}$, $K = 2\mathbb{Z}$. Then $N \leq K$.

$(G/N)/(K/N) = (\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}) / (2\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z})$. 

$2\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} = \{[0], [2], [4]\}$ (the multiples of 2 inside $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$). This is a subgroup of order 3. $(\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z})/\{[0],[2],[4]\}$ has 2 cosets, so it's $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

And directly: $G/K = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. ✓ The theorem checks out.

## The Lattice Isomorphism (Fourth Isomorphism Theorem)

A fourth theorem is sometimes stated separately:

**Theorem (Correspondence Theorem / Fourth Isomorphism Theorem).** If $N \unlhd G$, there is a bijection between:
- Subgroups of $G/N$
- Subgroups of $G$ that contain $N$

given by $\bar{H} \leftrightarrow \pi^{-1}(\bar{H})$ (where $\pi: G \to G/N$ is the quotient map). Moreover, this bijection:
- Preserves normality (a subgroup of $G/N$ is normal iff the corresponding subgroup of $G$ is normal in $G$)
- Preserves index

This theorem is the "lattice isomorphism theorem": the lattice of subgroups between $N$ and $G$ is isomorphic to the lattice of subgroups of $G/N$.

## Why These Theorems Matter

The isomorphism theorems are tools for *identifying* unknown quotient groups. Instead of computing a quotient group directly (listing cosets, verifying the operation), you can:
1. Find a homomorphism $\phi$ whose kernel is your normal subgroup
2. Compute the image of $\phi$
3. Conclude the quotient is isomorphic to the image

This strategy of "recognize the quotient via a homomorphism" is used constantly. Some examples:

**Classifying groups of order 6:** We know $S_3$ has order 6 and the sign map $\text{sgn}: S_3 \to \{1,-1\}$ is surjective with kernel $A_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$. So $S_3/A_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, and the full structure of $S_3$ is a "semidirect product" of $\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

**Fundamental groups of covering spaces:** If $p: \tilde{X} \to X$ is a covering map, the induced map $p_*: \pi_1(\tilde{X}) \to \pi_1(X)$ is an injective homomorphism. Its image is a subgroup of $\pi_1(X)$, and by covering space theory, there's a bijection between subgroups of $\pi_1(X)$ and covering spaces of $X$. The isomorphism theorems and the correspondence theorem describe all of this algebraically.

**In HoTT:** The isomorphism theorems hold for $\infty$-groups (∞-groupoids), and the First Isomorphism Theorem is the statement that the fiber of a map corresponds to the homotopy quotient. The algebraic theorems we've proven in this section are shadows of homotopy-theoretic facts.

## Summary

| Theorem | Statement |
|---------|-----------|
| First | $\phi: G \to H$ homomorphism $\Rightarrow$ $G/\ker\phi \cong \text{Im}\phi$ |
| Second | $H \leq G$, $N \unlhd G$ $\Rightarrow$ $H/(H\cap N) \cong HN/N$ |
| Third | $N \leq K$, both normal in $G$ $\Rightarrow$ $(G/N)/(K/N) \cong G/K$ |
| Fourth (Correspondence) | Subgroups of $G/N$ correspond to subgroups of $G$ containing $N$ |

These four theorems, together with Lagrange's theorem, form the foundational toolkit of group theory.
