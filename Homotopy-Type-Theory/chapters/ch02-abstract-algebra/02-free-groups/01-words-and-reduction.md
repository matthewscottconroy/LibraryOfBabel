# 2.1 Words and Reduction

## The Most Unconstrained Group

Recall how we defined groups: we gave axioms, and then we found many different groups satisfying those axioms. But there's a natural question lurking: among all groups generated by a given set of elements, is there one that is "the most general" — the one with the fewest constraints, where the generators are as independent as possible?

The answer is yes, and it's called the *free group* on that generating set. Free groups are the algebraic model of "pure strings of symbols," where the only simplifications you can make are the ones forced by the group axioms (like $a \cdot a^{-1} = e$), and nothing else.

This might sound abstract, but free groups are deeply concrete. An element of the free group on two generators $\{a, b\}$ looks like:
$$a \cdot b \cdot a^{-1} \cdot b \cdot b \cdot a$$
which we'd typically write as $aba^{-1}b^2a$. There's no rule saying $ab = ba$ (that would require an additional relation), no rule saying $a^3 = e$ (another additional relation), nothing. The only rules are the group axioms themselves.

## Alphabets and Letters

Let's build this up carefully from scratch.

**Definition (Alphabet).** An *alphabet* is a set $S$. Elements of $S$ are called *generators* or *letters*.

For each $s \in S$, we have a *formal inverse* $s^{-1}$. We require that $s \neq s^{-1}$ (even as symbols) and that the sets $S$ and $S^{-1} = \{s^{-1} \mid s \in S\}$ are disjoint. The *extended alphabet* is $S \cup S^{-1}$.

**Definition (Word).** A *word* over $S$ is a finite sequence of letters from $S \cup S^{-1}$. We write words as concatenated strings: $s_1 s_2 \cdots s_n$ where each $s_i \in S \cup S^{-1}$.

The *empty word* $\varepsilon$ is the word of length 0.

**Examples.** If $S = \{a, b\}$, then:
- $aba$ is a word (length 3)
- $a^{-1}ba^{-1}b^{-1}$ is a word (length 4)
- $aaa^{-1}b$ is a word (length 4), but notice $aa^{-1}$ can be cancelled
- $\varepsilon$ is the empty word (length 0)

The set of all words over $S$ is closed under concatenation: if $w_1$ and $w_2$ are words, so is $w_1 w_2$ (concatenate them). Concatenation is associative ($w_1(w_2 w_3) = (w_1 w_2)w_3$), and $\varepsilon$ is the identity for concatenation ($\varepsilon w = w\varepsilon = w$).

But this gives us a *monoid*, not a group: there are no inverses. To get a group, we need to define when two words are "equal" — and the right notion of equality is that you can cancel adjacent inverse pairs.

## Cancellable Pairs and Reduction

**Definition (Cancellable Pair).** A *cancellable pair* in a word is a consecutive pair of letters of the form $s s^{-1}$ or $s^{-1} s$ for any $s \in S$.

**Definition (Reduced Word).** A word is *reduced* if it contains no cancellable pair.

Examples:
- $ab$ is reduced (no adjacent inverse pair)
- $aba^{-1}$ is reduced
- $aa^{-1}b$ is *not* reduced ($aa^{-1}$ is a cancellable pair)
- $a^{-1}a$ is not reduced
- $\varepsilon$ is reduced (vacuously)

**The Reduction Process.** Given any word, we can *reduce* it by finding and eliminating cancellable pairs, one at a time, until no pairs remain. The result is a reduced word.

**Example.** Reduce $a b a^{-1} a b^{-1}$:
- Find $a^{-1} a$ in the middle: $ab \underbrace{a^{-1} a}_{e} b^{-1} \to ab \cdot b^{-1}$
- Now find $bb^{-1}$: $a \underbrace{bb^{-1}}_{e} \to a$
- $a$ is reduced. Done.

**But what if we cancel in a different order?**

Reduce $a b a^{-1} a b^{-1}$:
- Find $b^{-1}$ at the end... hmm, there's no immediate cancellable pair involving $b^{-1}$ there. Let me pick a different example.

Consider $a a^{-1} a$:
- Cancel the first $aa^{-1}$: get $a$.
- Or cancel $a^{-1}a$: get $a$ again.

Same result! Is this always the case?

## The Confluence Theorem

**Theorem (Uniqueness of Reduced Form).** Every word reduces to a unique reduced word, regardless of the order in which cancellable pairs are cancelled.

This is a *confluence* theorem — the reduction process is confluent, meaning all reduction paths lead to the same result. The proof uses an elegant combinatorial argument.

*Proof sketch.* The key observation is that two different cancellable pairs in a word are either:
1. *Non-overlapping:* They don't share letters. Cancelling either one first leads to the same result after reducing the other.
2. *Overlapping:* They share a letter. This can only happen in the form $s s^{-1} s$ (three consecutive letters where the middle letter is the inverse of its neighbors). But then both overlapping cancellations give $s$, so again the result is the same.

Since every reduction step decreases word length, the process terminates. And since any two reductions of the same word eventually reach the same reduced word, the result is unique. $\square$

We write $\text{red}(w)$ for the unique reduced form of a word $w$.

## The Free Group

**Definition (Free Group).** The *free group* $F(S)$ on a set $S$ is:
- *Elements:* reduced words over $S \cup S^{-1}$
- *Multiplication:* $w_1 \cdot w_2 = \text{red}(w_1 w_2)$ (concatenate and reduce)
- *Identity:* the empty word $\varepsilon$
- *Inverse:* $w^{-1}$ = the word $w$ written backwards with each letter replaced by its formal inverse

Let's verify the axioms:

**Associativity:** $(w_1 w_2) w_3 = \text{red}(\text{red}(w_1 w_2) w_3) = \text{red}(w_1 w_2 w_3)$ (since $\text{red}(w_1 w_2)$ and $w_1 w_2$ have the same reduced form) $= \text{red}(w_1 \text{red}(w_2 w_3)) = w_1(w_2 w_3)$. ✓

**Identity:** $\varepsilon \cdot w = \text{red}(\varepsilon w) = \text{red}(w) = w$ (since $w$ is already reduced). ✓

**Inverses:** If $w = s_1 s_2 \cdots s_n$ (reduced), then $w^{-1} = s_n^{-1} \cdots s_2^{-1} s_1^{-1}$. The product $w \cdot w^{-1} = s_1 \cdots s_n s_n^{-1} \cdots s_1^{-1}$, which reduces from the inside out: $s_n s_n^{-1} \to \varepsilon$, then $s_{n-1} \varepsilon s_{n-1}^{-1} = s_{n-1} s_{n-1}^{-1} \to \varepsilon$, etc., leaving $\varepsilon$. ✓

So $F(S)$ is indeed a group.

## Examples

**$F(\{a\}) = F_1 \cong \mathbb{Z}$.** The reduced words on one generator are: $\ldots, a^{-3}, a^{-2}, a^{-1}, \varepsilon, a, a^2, a^3, \ldots$ (since any word in $a$ and $a^{-1}$ reduces to $a^n$ for some $n \in \mathbb{Z}$). The group operation is $a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n}$. This is exactly $\mathbb{Z}$.

The free group on one generator is the integers. This makes sense: with one generator, the only constraint is group theory itself, which forces the cyclic structure $\mathbb{Z}$.

**$F(\{a, b\}) = F_2$.** The *free group on two generators*. This is more interesting: elements look like $a^2 b a^{-1} b^3 a \cdots$ with no simplification possible. $F_2$ is non-abelian (since $ab \neq ba$ — these are both reduced words representing different group elements) and infinite.

$F_2$ contains $F_1 \cong \mathbb{Z}$ as a subgroup (generated by $a$ alone), and in fact every free group $F_n$ for $n \geq 2$ contains free groups of every countable rank as subgroups. This makes $F_2$ surprisingly rich.

**$F(\emptyset) = \{e\}$.** The free group on the empty set has only the empty word. It's the trivial group.

**$F(S)$ for infinite $S$.** Works the same way. The free group on countably many generators $\{a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots\}$ contains any countably generated group as a quotient (by the universal property — coming next).

## Connection to Paths in Topology

Here's the connection that motivates everything.

Think of the generators $s \in S$ as *moves* in some system. A word $w = s_1 s_2 \cdots s_n$ is a *sequence of moves*. The inverse $s^{-1}$ is the *reverse move*. Two words are "equal" (in $F(S)$) when one can be obtained from the other by inserting or deleting "go forward then immediately go back" pairs.

In topology, loops in a space based at a point $x_0$ can be composed (follow one loop, then the other). Two loops are "the same" (homotopic) if one can be continuously deformed into the other. The free group is the algebraic model of this: generators are loops, words are sequences of loops, cancellation corresponds to "follow a loop then immediately reverse it" (which is contractible).

In HoTT, this is literal: the type of paths $a = a$ in a type is modeled by the free group structure on the loops. The identity type `a = a` is the "loop space" at `a`, and its structure is that of a group — specifically, the free group generated by the non-trivial paths.

We'll make this precise in the chapters on homotopy theory, but keep this picture in mind: **words in a free group are paths, and reduction corresponds to cancelling backtracking**.
