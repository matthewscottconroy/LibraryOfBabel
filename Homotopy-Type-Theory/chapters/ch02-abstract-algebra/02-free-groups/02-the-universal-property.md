# 2.2 The Universal Property of Free Groups

## Two Ways to Think About Free Groups

We just built $F(S)$ explicitly — as reduced words over $S \cup S^{-1}$ under concatenation-and-reduce. This construction works, but it's quite specific. It depends on the particular description as "reduced words."

Here's the key question: is there a *characterization* of free groups that doesn't depend on the specific construction? A description that tells you what $F(S)$ *does*, rather than what it *is made of*?

Yes — and it's called the *universal property*. The universal property is not just a prettier way to state the same thing; it's a fundamentally different kind of definition, one that captures $F(S)$ by its relationships with other groups rather than by its internal structure. This is the beginning of *categorical thinking*, and it's the style of reasoning that permeates HoTT.

## The Universal Property

**Theorem (Universal Property of Free Groups).** Let $S$ be a set and $F(S)$ the free group on $S$ with inclusion $\iota: S \to F(S)$ (sending each $s \in S$ to the corresponding one-letter word). For any group $G$ and any function $f: S \to G$, there exists a *unique* group homomorphism $\bar{f}: F(S) \to G$ such that the following diagram commutes:

$$\begin{array}{ccc}
S & \xrightarrow{f} & G \\
\downarrow^{\iota} & \nearrow^{\bar{f}} \\
F(S)
\end{array}$$

That is: $\bar{f} \circ \iota = f$, meaning $\bar{f}(s) = f(s)$ for all $s \in S$.

In plain language: **a function from the generating set $S$ to any group $G$ extends uniquely to a homomorphism from $F(S)$ to $G$.**

## Why This Is the Right Definition

Think about what this says. If I want to define a homomorphism $F(S) \to G$, I just need to decide where to send each generator $s \in S$. Once I've made those decisions, there's exactly one way to extend to a full homomorphism.

This is the meaning of "free": $F(S)$ imposes no constraints on where the generators go. Any assignment of generators to group elements extends to a homomorphism. If there were relations among the generators (like $a^2 = e$), then not every assignment would extend — you'd need $f(a)^2 = e_G$ to hold. But in the free group, there are no such constraints.

Contrast with a non-free group: in $\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z} = \langle a \mid a^3 \rangle$, a map from $\{a\}$ to a group $G$ extends to a homomorphism only if $f(a)^3 = e_G$. The free group $F(\{a\}) = \mathbb{Z}$ has no such constraint — $f(a)$ can be any element of any group, and there's always a unique homomorphism.

## Proof of the Universal Property

*Proof.* We need to show that, given $f: S \to G$, there is a unique homomorphism $\bar{f}: F(S) \to G$ with $\bar{f}(s) = f(s)$.

**Existence.** Define $\bar{f}$ on reduced words as follows:
- $\bar{f}(\varepsilon) = e_G$
- $\bar{f}(s w) = f(s) \cdot \bar{f}(w)$ for $s \in S$
- $\bar{f}(s^{-1} w) = f(s)^{-1} \cdot \bar{f}(w)$ for $s^{-1} \in S^{-1}$

(Here we're defining $\bar{f}$ by "reading off the letters one at a time.")

More explicitly, for a reduced word $w = s_1^{\epsilon_1} s_2^{\epsilon_2} \cdots s_n^{\epsilon_n}$ (each $s_i \in S$, each $\epsilon_i \in \{+1, -1\}$):
$$\bar{f}(w) = f(s_1)^{\epsilon_1} \cdot f(s_2)^{\epsilon_2} \cdots f(s_n)^{\epsilon_n}$$

**$\bar{f}$ is a homomorphism:** For reduced words $w_1$ and $w_2$, $\bar{f}(w_1 \cdot w_2) = \bar{f}(\text{red}(w_1 w_2))$.

The key point: when we reduce $w_1 w_2$, we cancel pairs like $s s^{-1}$ or $s^{-1} s$. When we apply $\bar{f}$ to such a pair: $\bar{f}(s) \cdot \bar{f}(s^{-1}) = f(s) \cdot f(s)^{-1} = e_G$. So cancellations in the word correspond to cancellations in $G$. Therefore $\bar{f}(\text{red}(w_1 w_2)) = \bar{f}(w_1) \cdot \bar{f}(w_2)$.

More formally: $\bar{f}$ maps cancellable pairs to the identity, so $\bar{f}(w)$ depends only on the equivalence class of $w$ under reduction. Since $w_1 \cdot w_2 = \text{red}(w_1 w_2)$ in $F(S)$, and $\bar{f}(w)$ is constant on equivalence classes, $\bar{f}(w_1 w_2) = \bar{f}(w_1)\bar{f}(w_2)$. ✓

**Uniqueness:** Any homomorphism $h: F(S) \to G$ with $h(s) = f(s)$ must satisfy:
$$h(s_1^{\epsilon_1} \cdots s_n^{\epsilon_n}) = h(s_1)^{\epsilon_1} \cdots h(s_n)^{\epsilon_n} = f(s_1)^{\epsilon_1} \cdots f(s_n)^{\epsilon_n} = \bar{f}(s_1^{\epsilon_1} \cdots s_n^{\epsilon_n})$$

So $h = \bar{f}$ on all of $F(S)$. There is exactly one extension. $\square$

## Universal Properties and Categorical Thinking

The universal property says $F(S)$ is the "most efficient" group built from $S$: it's the group you get from $S$ with only the group axioms, no extra structure. Any group $G$ generated by an image of $S$ is a quotient of $F(S)$.

This is what "free" means in mathematics: a free object in a category is one with no additional relations, characterized by the property that functions from its generators extend uniquely to structure-preserving maps.

Here's the categorical language: $F$ is a *functor* from the category of sets to the category of groups. It's *left adjoint* to the forgetful functor $U$ (which takes a group to its underlying set). The universal property is exactly the statement of this adjunction:
$$\text{Hom}_{\text{Grp}}(F(S), G) \cong \text{Hom}_{\text{Set}}(S, U(G))$$

Homomorphisms from $F(S)$ to $G$ are in natural bijection with functions from $S$ to (the underlying set of) $G$. This is the bijection given by $\bar{f} \leftrightarrow f$ in the universal property.

Adjunctions — pairs of functors related in this way — are everywhere in mathematics. Understanding the free-forgetful adjunction for groups is the template for understanding free objects in ring theory, module theory, topology, and type theory.

## Consequences of the Universal Property

**Every group is a quotient of a free group.**

*Proof.* Let $G$ be any group. Let $S = G$ (take the underlying set). Then the identity function $\text{id}: S \to G$ is a function from the set $S = G$ to the group $G$. By the universal property, it extends to a surjective homomorphism $\bar{\text{id}}: F(G) \to G$ (surjective since every $g \in G$ is in the image of the generator $g \in S \subseteq F(S)$). By the First Isomorphism Theorem: $G \cong F(G)/\ker(\bar{\text{id}})$.

So $G$ is a quotient of a free group — specifically, $F(G)$ modulo the "relations" in $G$. This is the basis for group presentations.

**The universal property uniquely determines $F(S)$ up to isomorphism.**

*Proof.* Suppose $H$ is a group with a function $\iota': S \to H$ satisfying the same universal property. Apply the universal property of $F(S)$ to $f = \iota'$: get $\bar{\iota'}: F(S) \to H$ with $\bar{\iota'} \circ \iota = \iota'$. Apply the universal property of $H$ to $f = \iota$: get $\bar{\iota}: H \to F(S)$ with $\bar{\iota} \circ \iota' = \iota$.

Now consider $\bar{\iota} \circ \bar{\iota'}: F(S) \to F(S)$. It satisfies $(\bar{\iota} \circ \bar{\iota'}) \circ \iota = \bar{\iota} \circ \iota' = \iota$. By uniqueness in the universal property of $F(S)$, the only homomorphism $F(S) \to F(S)$ that restricts to $\iota$ on $S$ is the identity. So $\bar{\iota} \circ \bar{\iota'} = \text{id}_{F(S)}$. By the same argument, $\bar{\iota'} \circ \bar{\iota} = \text{id}_H$.

So $\bar{\iota'}$ and $\bar{\iota}$ are inverse isomorphisms, and $F(S) \cong H$. $\square$

This is the categorical argument for uniqueness up to unique isomorphism. It's a template: any two objects satisfying the same universal property are uniquely isomorphic. This principle is used everywhere in mathematics to show that constructions are "canonical" or "well-defined up to isomorphism."

## Universal Properties in Type Theory

The universal property of free groups is one instance of a general theme that becomes central in HoTT.

In type theory, defining a type $T$ by its *elimination rule* (how you map out of $T$) is exactly specifying its universal property. The type of natural numbers $\mathbb{N}$ has an elimination rule (recursion/induction) that says: to define a function $\mathbb{N} \to X$, give an element of $X$ (the value at 0) and a function $X \to X$ (the step function). This is exactly the universal property of the free monoid on one generator.

The identity type `a = b` has an elimination rule (path induction) that says: to define a function from proofs of $a = b$ (paths), it's enough to handle the case $a = a$ (reflexivity). This is the universal property of the free path space.

Free groups, free monoids, and free algebras generally are characterized by their mapping-out properties, and these mapping-out properties are their elimination rules in type theory. The algebraic and type-theoretic perspectives are the same thing.

## The Word Problem for Free Groups

Given two words over $S$, are they equal in $F(S)$? This is the *word problem* for the free group: given $w_1$ and $w_2$, determine whether $w_1 = w_2$ in $F(S)$.

The answer: reduce both words and check if the reductions are identical letter by letter. This algorithm runs in linear time (in the length of the words).

So the word problem for free groups is decidable and efficient. This is in stark contrast to other groups: some finitely presented groups have *undecidable* word problems (Novikov, 1955) — no algorithm can determine whether two words represent the same element.

Why is the free group's word problem solvable? Because the free group has *no relations* — no non-trivial equations between words. Every distinct reduced word represents a distinct element. There's no hidden equality to worry about.

This computational property of free groups is precisely what makes them useful as models: they have the richest possible structure given their generators, without any unexpected coincidences.
