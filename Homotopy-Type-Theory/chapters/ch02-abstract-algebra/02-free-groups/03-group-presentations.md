# 2.3 Group Presentations

## From Free Groups to All Groups

We've established that every group $G$ is a quotient of a free group. This means we can *describe* any group by specifying:
1. A set of generators — elements that together produce everything in $G$ under multiplication
2. A set of relations — equations among those generators that must hold

This description is called a *group presentation*.

## The Formal Definition

**Definition (Group Presentation).** A *presentation* of a group consists of:
- An *alphabet* (generating set) $S$
- A set $R$ of *relators* — words in $F(S)$ that are required to equal the identity

The group presented by $\langle S \mid R \rangle$ is:
$$F(S) / N(R)$$
where $N(R)$ is the *normal closure* of $R$ in $F(S)$: the smallest normal subgroup of $F(S)$ containing every element of $R$.

Why the normal closure rather than just the subgroup generated by $R$? Because when we mod out by $R$, we don't just want the elements of $R$ to become trivial — we want *all conjugates* $g r g^{-1}$ (for $r \in R$, $g \in F(S)$) to become trivial too, so that the result is actually a quotient group (which requires a normal subgroup).

In practice, a *relation* is an equation $w_1 = w_2$ in $F(S)$, which we convert to the relator $w_1 w_2^{-1}$ (the left side times the inverse of the right side, which must equal the identity). So "$a^2 = e$" becomes the relator $a^2$, "$r^n = e$" becomes $r^n$, "$ab = ba$" becomes $aba^{-1}b^{-1}$ (also written as the commutator $[a,b]$).

## Standard Examples

**Cyclic group of order $n$:**
$$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} = \langle a \mid a^n \rangle$$
One generator $a$, one relator $a^n$ (meaning $a^n = e$). Every element is $a^k$ for $0 \leq k < n$, and $a^n = e$ is the only constraint.

**Dihedral group:**
$$D_n = \langle r, s \mid r^n, s^2, srsr \rangle$$
Two generators: rotation $r$ and reflection $s$. Three relations: $r^n = e$ (rotating $n$ times is identity), $s^2 = e$ (reflecting twice is identity), $srs = r^{-1}$ (reflected rotation is inverse rotation; this is the relator $srsr$, since $srsr = e \iff srs = r^{-1}$).

**Free abelian group of rank 2:**
$$\mathbb{Z}^2 = \langle a, b \mid aba^{-1}b^{-1} \rangle$$
Two generators, one relation: $[a,b] = aba^{-1}b^{-1} = e$, i.e., $ab = ba$. The generators commute but are otherwise unconstrained.

**Free group:**
$$F(S) = \langle S \mid \rangle$$
Generators: $S$. Relations: none. This is the free group with no extra constraints — the most unconstrained group on $S$.

**Symmetric group $S_3$:**
$$S_3 = \langle r, s \mid r^3, s^2, (sr)^2 \rangle$$
where $r = (123)$ and $s = (12)$.

**Fundamental group of the torus:**
$$\pi_1(\text{torus}) = \langle a, b \mid aba^{-1}b^{-1} \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}^2$$

**Fundamental group of the Klein bottle:**
$$\pi_1(\text{Klein bottle}) = \langle a, b \mid abab^{-1} \rangle$$
Note the subtle difference from the torus: the relation is $abab^{-1} = e$ (i.e., $aba = b$), not $[a,b] = e$. The Klein bottle's fundamental group is non-abelian.

## The Connection to Topology

Group presentations are not just algebraic tools — they come directly from topological constructions.

**CW complexes.** A CW complex is built by attaching cells:
- 0-cells (points) form the base
- 1-cells (arcs) are attached at their endpoints
- 2-cells (disks) are attached by maps from their boundary circles
- Higher cells...

Given a CW complex $X$ with one 0-cell (basepoint), the fundamental group $\pi_1(X)$ has a presentation:
- **Generators:** one generator $e_\alpha$ for each 1-cell
- **Relations:** one relation $w_\alpha$ for each 2-cell, where $w_\alpha$ is the word in the generators traced by the attaching map of the 2-cell

This is the Seifert-van Kampen theorem in disguise.

**Examples:**
- Circle $S^1$: one 0-cell, one 1-cell, no 2-cells. $\pi_1(S^1) = \langle a \mid \rangle = F(\{a\}) \cong \mathbb{Z}$.
- Torus $T^2$: one 0-cell, two 1-cells ($a$, $b$), one 2-cell (attached along $aba^{-1}b^{-1}$). $\pi_1(T^2) = \langle a, b \mid aba^{-1}b^{-1} \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}^2$.
- Sphere $S^2$: one 0-cell, one 2-cell. No 1-cells. $\pi_1(S^2) = \langle \mid \rangle = \{e\}$ (trivial group). The sphere is simply connected.
- Real projective plane $\mathbb{RP}^2$: $\pi_1(\mathbb{RP}^2) = \langle a \mid a^2 \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.
- Figure-eight (wedge of two circles): $\pi_1(\vee S^1) = \langle a, b \mid \rangle = F_2$. Two generators, no relations — free group on 2 generators.

This direct correspondence between topological constructions and group presentations is one of the great unifications in mathematics.

## The Word Problem and Decidability

Given a presentation $\langle S \mid R \rangle$ and a word $w \in F(S)$, the *word problem* asks: does $w = e$ in the presented group?

This is a fundamental computational question. The answer depends entirely on the presentation:

- **Free groups** $\langle S \mid \rangle$: reduce $w$ and check if it's empty. Decidable in linear time.
- **Finite groups**: Decidable (just check the Cayley table).
- **$\mathbb{Z}$** ($\langle a \mid \rangle$): Obvious.
- **$\mathbb{Z}^2$** ($\langle a, b \mid [a,b] \rangle$): Reduce to normal form $a^m b^n$. Decidable.
- **General finitely presented groups**: Novikov (1955) showed there exist finitely presented groups where the word problem is *undecidable* — no algorithm can solve it for all inputs. This is a theorem of mathematical logic: the word problem is Turing-complete.

The word problem is undecidable for the same reason the halting problem is undecidable: you can encode computation in group theory. The group relations encode transition rules, and membership in the trivial element corresponds to program termination.

This has profound implications: **automated reasoning about groups is fundamentally limited**. No theorem prover can decide membership in the trivial element for an arbitrary finitely presented group. This is one of the reasons that formalized mathematics in proof assistants (like Lean) requires human guidance — some questions are provably beyond algorithmic reach.

## Tietze Transformations

Different presentations can describe the same group. The *Tietze transformations* are moves that change a presentation without changing the group it presents:

1. **Add a relation:** If $w$ is already trivial in the group, add $w$ to $R$.
2. **Remove a relation:** If $w \in R$ and $w$ follows from the other relations, remove it.
3. **Add a generator:** Add a new generator $c$ and a new relation $c = w$ (where $w$ is a word in the existing generators).
4. **Remove a generator:** If a relation has the form $c = w$ with $c$ appearing nowhere else, remove $c$ and $c = w$.

**Theorem (Tietze, 1908).** Two finite presentations describe isomorphic groups if and only if one can be obtained from the other by a finite sequence of Tietze transformations.

**Example.** $\langle a, b \mid a^2, b^2, abab \rangle$ and $\langle r, s \mid r^4, s^2, srsr \rangle$ both present $D_4$. One can be transformed to the other by Tietze moves: substitute $r = ab$, $s = a$, verify the relations correspond, etc.

This provides, in principle, a way to check if two presentations give the same group. In practice, it's not an algorithm — you might need infinitely many transformations, and knowing *which* transformations to apply is not automatic.

## The Reidemeister-Schreier Method

How do you find a presentation for a subgroup, given a presentation for the full group?

The *Reidemeister-Schreier method* provides a systematic procedure: given a presentation for $G$ and a subgroup $H$ (of finite index), produce a presentation for $H$. The algorithm uses the coset action of $G$ on $G/H$.

The number of generators for $H$ is $(n-1)[G:H] + 1$ where $n$ is the number of generators of $G$ (Nielsen-Schreier theorem for free groups: every subgroup of a free group is free, with rank determined by the index).

**Theorem (Nielsen-Schreier).** Every subgroup of a free group is free.

This is a non-trivial result! Subgroups of "unconstrained" groups are themselves unconstrained. The proof uses covering space theory (a subgroup of $\pi_1(X)$ corresponds to a covering space, and covering spaces of a graph are graphs, whose fundamental groups are free).

## Presentations in HoTT

In Homotopy Type Theory, types can be presented in a way directly analogous to group presentations:

- *Generators* correspond to the base cases of a higher inductive type (HIT): the points and paths you start with.
- *Relations* correspond to the path constructors: equations between paths that must hold.

For example, the circle $S^1$ in HoTT is a HIT with:
- One 0-cell: the point `base`
- One 1-cell: the loop `loop : base = base`
- No 2-cells

Its fundamental group is $\langle \text{loop} \mid \rangle = F_1 \cong \mathbb{Z}$.

The torus is a HIT with two loops $a, b: \text{base} = \text{base}$ and one 2-cell $p: ab = ba$ (a path between paths). Its fundamental group is $\langle a, b \mid [a,b] \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}^2$.

Group presentations and higher inductive type specifications are the same idea, one level up. This is one of the central insights of HoTT: topology and algebra are unified, with type theory providing the common language.
