# Applied Exercises

Abstract algebra's power — identifying and exploiting structure shared across wildly different domains — makes it one of the most practically potent areas of pure mathematics. The same abstract notion of a group and a quotient group appears in the RSA cryptosystem, the error-correcting codes that protect data transmissions, the gauge symmetries underlying the Standard Model of particle physics, the Rubik's cube puzzle, and the merge operation in distributed version control. The exercises below pursue these connections concretely, requiring you to apply the definitions and theorems of this chapter — group axioms, Lagrange's theorem, normal subgroups, quotient groups, homomorphisms, and the isomorphism theorems — to non-trivial problems in each domain.

---

## Exercise C.1: RSA Cryptography and the Structure of $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$
*Domain: Cryptography / Number Theory*

**Setup:** The RSA cryptosystem is built on the multiplicative group $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$ of units modulo $n = pq$ (the product of two large primes). A *unit* modulo $n$ is an integer $a$ such that $\gcd(a, n) = 1$ — i.e., $a$ has a multiplicative inverse modulo $n$. The set of all units forms a group under multiplication modulo $n$.

**Questions:**
1. Verify directly from the group axioms that $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$ is indeed a group: check closure (if $\gcd(a,n) = 1$ and $\gcd(b,n) = 1$, is $\gcd(ab, n) = 1$?), associativity (inherited from integer multiplication), identity ($1$ is always a unit), and inverses (use Bézout's identity). What is the order $|(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times|$? Express this in terms of Euler's totient function $\varphi(n)$.

2. *Euler's theorem* states: if $\gcd(a, n) = 1$, then $a^{\varphi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod{n}$. Prove Euler's theorem using Lagrange's theorem: every element of a finite group $G$ satisfies $g^{|G|} = e$. (This is an immediate corollary of Lagrange — identify $|G|$ in this context.)

3. In RSA, the public key is $(n, e)$ and the private key is $d$ with $ed \equiv 1 \pmod{\varphi(n)}$. Encryption is $c \equiv m^e \pmod{n}$; decryption is $m \equiv c^d \pmod{n}$. Prove that decryption recovers the original message: $c^d \equiv (m^e)^d = m^{ed} \equiv m \pmod{n}$. Your proof should use Euler's theorem (i.e., Lagrange's theorem for this group). What subgroup of $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$ is generated by $m$, and how does its order relate to $\varphi(n)$?

4. (Extension) The Chinese Remainder Theorem (CRT) gives an isomorphism $\mathbb{Z}/pq\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}$ (for primes $p \neq q$). State this as a ring isomorphism and verify that it restricts to a group isomorphism $(\mathbb{Z}/pq\mathbb{Z})^\times \cong (\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times \times (\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^\times$. What does the structure theorem for finite abelian groups imply about the structure of $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times$ for prime $p$?

*Abstract concept illustrated: Lagrange's theorem (Section 1.4); order of elements; group isomorphisms (Section 1.6); the connection between subgroup structure and cryptographic security.*

## Exercise C.2: Elliptic Curve Groups and Diffie-Hellman
*Domain: Cryptography / Algebraic Geometry*

**Setup:** Elliptic curve cryptography (ECC) uses the group structure on an elliptic curve $E$ over a finite field $\mathbb{F}_p$. An elliptic curve is defined by $y^2 = x^3 + ax + b$ (with discriminant $\Delta \neq 0$). The points of $E(\mathbb{F}_p)$ — solutions $(x,y)$ with $x,y \in \mathbb{F}_p$ together with a "point at infinity" $\mathcal{O}$ — form an abelian group under a geometric addition law: the sum of two points $P$ and $Q$ is defined by the "chord-and-tangent" rule.

**Questions:**
1. Without proving the full addition formulas, verify that $\mathcal{O}$ satisfies the identity axiom: $P + \mathcal{O} = \mathcal{O} + P = P$ for any point $P$. What is the inverse of a point $P = (x, y)$ on the curve $y^2 = x^3 + ax + b$? (The inverse is the point $P' = (x, -y)$; verify that $P + P' = \mathcal{O}$ according to the geometric rule, by checking that the line through $P$ and $P'$ is vertical and a vertical line "intersects the curve at $\mathcal{O}$.")

2. The Diffie-Hellman key exchange on an elliptic curve works as follows: Alice and Bob agree publicly on a curve $E/\mathbb{F}_p$ and a base point $G \in E(\mathbb{F}_p)$. Alice chooses a secret integer $a$ and publishes $A = aG$ (meaning $G$ added to itself $a$ times); Bob chooses secret $b$ and publishes $B = bG$. The shared secret is $K = abG$. Alice computes $aB = a(bG) = abG$; Bob computes $bA = b(aG) = abG$. Explain why the commutativity $aB = bA$ follows from the fact that $E(\mathbb{F}_p)$ is an *abelian* group. What goes wrong if the group were non-abelian?

3. The security of ECC depends on the *elliptic curve discrete logarithm problem*: given $G$ and $A = aG$, finding $a$ should be computationally infeasible. This is a problem in the cyclic subgroup $\langle G \rangle \leq E(\mathbb{F}_p)$ generated by $G$. By Lagrange's theorem, $|\langle G \rangle|$ divides $|E(\mathbb{F}_p)|$. If $|E(\mathbb{F}_p)| = N$ and $G$ has order $q$ (a large prime divisor of $N$), what is the structure of $\langle G \rangle$? Why is it important for security that $q$ be large and prime (rather than, say, having many small prime factors)?

*Abstract concept illustrated: Abelian groups; cyclic subgroups; Lagrange's theorem; the order of an element; group generators.*

## Exercise C.3: Linear Codes as Quotient Groups
*Domain: Information Theory / Error-Correcting Codes*

**Setup:** A *binary linear code* of length $n$ is a subgroup $C \leq (\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^n$ — a subgroup of the group of binary strings of length $n$ under bitwise XOR (which is addition in $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$). The *cosets* of $C$ in $(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^n$ partition the set of all $n$-bit strings, and this partition is exactly the *syndrome decoding* structure used to correct errors.

**Questions:**
1. Let $C = \{000, 011, 101, 110\} \subseteq (\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^3$. Verify that $C$ is a subgroup by checking closure under XOR, existence of identity ($000$), and existence of inverses (every element of $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is its own inverse, so every codeword is its own inverse). What are the cosets of $C$ in $(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^3$? List them. How many cosets are there, and what does Lagrange's theorem predict?

2. The *quotient group* $(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^n / C$ has elements that are cosets of $C$. In coding theory, the coset of a received string $r$ is called its *syndrome*, and two strings with the same syndrome differ only by a codeword. If a string is transmitted with at most one bit error, and $C$ is chosen so that each error pattern (weight-1 string) is in a *different* coset, then syndrome decoding corrects the error by identifying the coset and choosing the coset leader (the error pattern). Formalize this: if the channel introduces an error $e$ so that the received string is $r = c + e$ for some codeword $c \in C$, show that $r + C = e + C$ (the syndrome depends only on the error, not the codeword). Why does this make decoding well-defined?

3. The *Hamming $[7,4]$ code* is a binary linear code of length 7 and dimension 4 (4 information bits, 3 parity-check bits) that corrects all single-bit errors. Its parity-check matrix is a $3 \times 7$ matrix $H$ whose columns are all seven non-zero binary strings of length 3. The code $C = \ker(H) = \{c \in (\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^7 \mid Hc = 0\}$ is a subgroup. By the First Isomorphism Theorem (applied to the linear map $H : (\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^7 \to (\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^3$), what is the relationship between $|C|$, $|(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^7|$, and $|\mathrm{Im}(H)|$? Use this to compute $|C|$. (The First Isomorphism Theorem for groups: $(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^7 / \ker(H) \cong \mathrm{Im}(H)$.)

*Abstract concept illustrated: Subgroups and cosets; Lagrange's theorem; quotient groups; the First Isomorphism Theorem (Section 1.7) applied to the kernel-image decomposition of a group homomorphism.*

## Exercise C.4: Gauge Symmetry and Normal Subgroups in Physics
*Domain: Theoretical Physics / Particle Physics*

**Setup:** The Standard Model of particle physics is organized around *gauge groups*: groups of symmetry transformations that leave the laws of physics invariant at each point in spacetime. The gauge group of the Standard Model is $U(1) \times SU(2) \times SU(3)$, where $U(1)$ is the circle group (electromagnetism), $SU(2)$ is the special unitary group in 2 dimensions (weak interaction), and $SU(3)$ is the special unitary group in 3 dimensions (strong interaction). The *electroweak unification* combines $U(1) \times SU(2)$ into a single structure, illustrating the role of normal subgroups and quotient groups in physics.

**Questions:**
1. The unitary group $U(n) = \{A \in M_n(\mathbb{C}) \mid A^\dagger A = I\}$ (complex matrices whose conjugate transpose equals their inverse) is a group under matrix multiplication. Verify all four group axioms for $U(n)$. The *special unitary group* $SU(n) = \{A \in U(n) \mid \det(A) = 1\}$ is a subgroup. Show that $SU(n) \unlhd U(n)$ (i.e., it is a normal subgroup) by using the fact that the determinant map $\det: U(n) \to U(1)$ is a group homomorphism and $SU(n) = \ker(\det)$. What is the quotient group $U(n) / SU(n)$?

2. In the electroweak theory, electromagnetism arises as a quotient: the physical photon corresponds to a certain combination of the $U(1)$ and $SU(2)$ fields, determined by the Higgs mechanism. Abstractly, this is modeled by a group homomorphism $\phi: U(1) \times SU(2) \to U(1)_{\mathrm{EM}}$ (where $U(1)_{\mathrm{EM}}$ is the electromagnetic gauge group). The kernel $\ker(\phi)$ is a normal subgroup of $U(1) \times SU(2)$. Without working out the physics, identify which group-theoretic data (a homomorphism and its kernel) are needed to describe "combining two symmetry groups into one with a quotient," and explain why the First Isomorphism Theorem guarantees that the image of $\phi$ is isomorphic to $(U(1) \times SU(2)) / \ker(\phi)$.

3. *Spontaneous symmetry breaking* occurs when a physical system has a symmetry group $G$ but its lowest-energy state is invariant only under a subgroup $H \leq G$. The *Goldstone theorem* then predicts the existence of massless particles corresponding to elements of the coset space $G/H$. Identify $G/H$ as a quotient: what group structure does it have if $H \unlhd G$? If $H$ is not normal in $G$, why is $G/H$ only a coset space and not a group? (This connects directly to Section 1.4: Lagrange shows $G/H$ is a partition, but quotient-group structure requires normality.)

*Abstract concept illustrated: Normal subgroups; quotient groups; group homomorphisms and kernels; the First Isomorphism Theorem; the distinction between coset spaces (for non-normal subgroups) and quotient groups (for normal subgroups).*

## Exercise C.5: The Rubik's Cube Group
*Domain: Combinatorics / Group Theory / Puzzle Design*

**Setup:** The Rubik's cube is a physical realization of a group. Each configuration of the cube is a permutation of its 48 colored facelets (ignoring the fixed centers), and each legal move (rotating a face 90°, 180°, or 270°) is a permutation. The set of all reachable configurations forms a group $G$ under composition of moves — the *Rubik's cube group*. It is a subgroup of the symmetric group $S_{48}$, and has order $|G| = 43{,}252{,}003{,}274{,}489{,}856{,}000 \approx 4.3 \times 10^{19}$.

**Questions:**
1. The six generators of $G$ are the face turns $F, B, L, R, U, D$ (Front, Back, Left, Right, Up, Down). Each can be applied 1, 2, or 3 times (quarter turn, half turn, three-quarter turn); applying the same face turn 4 times gives the identity. Write the relations $F^4 = e$, $B^4 = e$, etc. as equations in $G$. Identify which pairs of generators commute (opposite face turns: $F$ and $B$, $L$ and $R$, $U$ and $D$) and which do not (adjacent face turns). What does this say about whether $G$ is abelian?

2. There is a normal subgroup $H \unlhd G$ consisting of all configurations reachable using only half-turns ($F^2, B^2, L^2, R^2, U^2, D^2$). The group $H$ is called the "squares group" and has order $|H| = 663{,}552$. Compute $|G/H| = |G|/|H|$ using Lagrange's theorem. The quotient $G/H$ has a specific structure: it is a group of order $|G/H|$. Describe what the cosets of $H$ in $G$ represent physically: two configurations $c_1, c_2$ are in the same coset iff one can be reached from the other using only half-turns. What does it mean to "solve the cube modulo $H$"?

3. The God's Number theorem (proved computationally in 2010) states that every configuration of the Rubik's cube can be solved in at most 20 moves (in the face-turn metric). This means the *diameter* of the Cayley graph of $G$ with respect to the 18 generators (face turns and their inverses) is 20. The Cayley graph is a graph with $|G|$ vertices (one per configuration) and edges connecting configurations that differ by one generator. Cayley's theorem (Section 1.7) says $G$ acts on itself by left multiplication, making $G$ a subgroup of $S_{|G|}$. Describe the Cayley graph construction in terms of group actions (Section 3): what is the group, what is the set being acted on, and what is the action? What does a "path" in the Cayley graph correspond to in terms of solving the cube?

*Abstract concept illustrated: Group generators and relations; normal subgroups and Lagrange's theorem; Cayley's theorem and group actions; group presentations (Sections 1.2, 1.4, 1.5, 1.7).*

## Exercise C.6: Homomorphisms and Software Version Control
*Domain: Distributed Systems / Software Engineering*

**Setup:** In distributed version control systems like Git, multiple developers maintain their own copies of a repository and need to *merge* their changes. An idealized merge operation has algebraic structure: if we think of repository states as group elements and patches (diffs) as group operations, then merging is related to the group-theoretic structure of transformations. The study of *operational transformation* (OT) and *conflict-free replicated data types* (CRDTs) formalizes this using algebraic structures closely related to those in this chapter.

**Questions:**
1. Consider a simplified "counter" state: each developer maintains an integer value, starting at 0. The allowed operations are $+k$ for integer $k$ (add $k$ to the counter). The set of operations $\{+k \mid k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ under composition is the group $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$. An *update* is a group element; *applying* an update is the group action. Verify that this action satisfies the group action axioms: (i) the identity operation $+0$ does nothing, and (ii) composing operations corresponds to adding their values. Why does the commutativity of $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ guarantee that two independent updates can be merged in either order with the same result?

2. A *conflict* in version control occurs when two operations do not commute: the result of applying $A$ then $B$ differs from applying $B$ then $A$. This corresponds to $AB \neq BA$ in the group of transformations. Consider operations on a text document: $\mathrm{Insert}(i, c)$ (insert character $c$ at position $i$) and $\mathrm{Delete}(i)$ (delete character at position $i$). Explain informally why these operations generally do not commute, and why this non-commutativity is the algebraic root of merge conflicts. In what sense does a *conflict resolution policy* define a choice of coset representative in a quotient?

3. A *CRDT* (Conflict-free Replicated Data Type) is a data type with an update operation that is always commutative and associative, so that concurrent updates always produce the same result regardless of order. Formally, the state space $S$ with merge operation $\sqcup$ forms a *commutative monoid*: a set with an associative, commutative binary operation and an identity. Compare this to the definition of an abelian group: what axiom does a commutative monoid lack? Give an example of a CRDT operation (e.g., a set where the operation is union $\cup$, or a counter where the operation is componentwise maximum) and verify the commutative monoid axioms. Would the group axiom (existence of inverses) correspond to anything useful in the CRDT context?

*Abstract concept illustrated: Group actions; abelian vs. non-abelian groups; the role of commutativity in algebraic structures; monoids as groups without inverses; the practical consequences of non-commutativity.*
