# References and Primary Sources

## Foundational Texts

**Serge Lang.** *Algebra.* Revised 3rd ed. Springer, 2002.
The standard comprehensive graduate reference for abstract algebra, covering groups, rings, fields, modules, Galois theory, and homological algebra. Lang's style is terse and demanding but authoritative; every major theorem is stated in full generality. The chapters on groups (I–II) and fields and Galois theory (V–VI) are definitive treatments at the graduate level. An essential reference throughout this chapter and the rest of the curriculum.

**David S. Dummit and Richard M. Foote.** *Abstract Algebra.* 3rd ed. Wiley, 2004.
The most commonly used graduate algebra textbook in North American universities. More expansive than Lang, with more examples, computations, and carefully worked exercises. Covers groups (including group actions, Sylow theorems, and free groups), rings, fields, modules, and Galois theory. The isomorphism theorems, normal subgroups, and quotient group material in this chapter follow the structure of Dummit and Foote closely.

**Israel N. Herstein.** *Topics in Algebra.* 2nd ed. Wiley, 1975.
A classic undergraduate algebra text, elegant and concise. Herstein's proofs are models of clarity, and his exercise sets are legendary for their difficulty and insight. For the core group theory in this chapter (subgroups, cosets, Lagrange's theorem, normal subgroups, homomorphisms, and the isomorphism theorems), Herstein provides an ideal first treatment.

**Emil Artin.** *Algebra* (edited lecture notes by Michael Rosen from Artin's Harvard lectures). Prentice Hall, 1991.
Artin's lectures on algebra, reorganized by his son Michael, with an unusual perspective: geometry and symmetry are placed at the center from the start, and matrix groups appear as primary examples alongside abstract groups. This approach makes the connection between group theory and geometry vivid, which directly supports the topology-algebra bridge in Section 5 of this chapter.

**Évariste Galois.** "Mémoire sur les conditions de résolubilité des équations par radicaux." Manuscript, 1831; first published in *Journal de Mathématiques Pures et Appliquées* 11 (1846): 381–444.
The founding document of group theory. Written by Galois at age 20 (and partially at 19), it introduces the concept of a group as the symmetry group of the roots of a polynomial equation, and proves that an equation is solvable by radicals if and only if its Galois group (the group of symmetries of the splitting field) is solvable. The technical machinery is impressionistic by modern standards, but every idea in the theory of groups and fields traces its origin here.

## Seminal Papers

**Arthur Cayley.** "On the Theory of Groups, as Depending on the Symbolic Equation $\theta^n = 1$." *Philosophical Magazine* (4) 7 (1854): 40–47.
The paper that first defined groups abstractly (rather than as concrete groups of substitutions). Cayley's abstract definition — a set with a binary operation satisfying closure and the group laws — is the precursor to the modern definition. The paper also contains what is now called *Cayley's theorem*: every group is isomorphic to a subgroup of a symmetric group (i.e., a group of permutations), showing that abstract groups are "the same as" concrete symmetry groups.

**Emmy Noether.** "Idealtheorie in Ringbereichen." *Mathematische Annalen* 83 (1921): 24–66.
Noether's foundational paper on ring theory, which introduced the ascending chain condition (ACC) and the concept of Noetherian rings. This paper transformed algebra by showing that finiteness conditions (like ACC) are the right abstraction for studying ideals in rings, and established the structural approach — studying algebraic objects through their ideals and quotients — that became the hallmark of twentieth-century algebra. The ring-theoretic material in Section 4 of this chapter is shaped by the perspective of this paper.

**Emmy Noether.** "Abstrakter Aufbau der Idealtheorie in algebraischen Zahl- und Funktionenkörpern." *Mathematische Annalen* 96 (1927): 26–61.
Noether's second major algebraic paper, which completed the abstract treatment of ideals for Dedekind domains and connected the abstract ring theory of the 1921 paper to algebraic number theory. Together, these papers established the algebraic approach to number theory that is now standard.

**Otto Hölder.** "Zurückführung einer beliebigen algebraischen Gleichung auf eine Kette von Gleichungen." *Mathematische Annalen* 34 (1889): 26–56. Also: "Die einfachen Gruppen im ersten und zweiten Hundert der Ordnungen." *Mathematische Annalen* 40 (1892): 55–88.
Hölder coined the term *isomorphism* (in the group-theoretic sense) and made the first steps toward classifying finite groups, including the notion of the composition series and what we now call the Jordan-Hölder theorem (that the composition factors of a group are unique up to order and isomorphism). The isomorphism theorems in this chapter bear his name in some traditions (along with Noether's).

**Évariste Galois.** Works, as edited by Joseph Liouville. *Journal de Mathématiques Pures et Appliquées* 11 (1846).
The first published version of Galois's manuscripts, which had been rejected or lost during his short life. The editorial work by Liouville brought Galois's ideas to the mathematical community and initiated the development of Galois theory and group theory as independent subjects. The preview of Galois theory in Section 4 of this chapter traces its content directly to these manuscripts.

**Niels Henrik Abel.** "Mémoire sur les équations algébriques, où l'on démontre l'impossibilité de la résolution de l'équation générale du cinquième degré." Self-published pamphlet, Christiania (Oslo), 1824. Reprinted in *Journal für die reine und angewandte Mathematik* (Crelle's Journal) 1 (1826).
The proof that no general formula (using radicals) exists for the roots of a degree-5 polynomial — the impossibility that Galois's theory later explained via group theory. Abel's proof predates group theory and works by a direct but intricate argument about the structure of such a formula. The result is the "Abel-Ruffini theorem," and it is the motivating problem for the entire development of group theory and Galois theory.

**C. Jordan.** *Traité des substitutions et des équations algébriques.* Paris: Gauthier-Villars, 1870.
Jordan's comprehensive treatise on substitution groups (what we now call permutation groups) synthesized and extended the work of Galois and Cayley. It gave the first systematic development of the theory of groups from a structural standpoint, proved the Jordan-Hölder theorem, and classified finite simple groups of small order. This text established group theory as an independent mathematical discipline.

## Textbooks and Modern Treatments

**Michael Artin.** *Algebra.* 2nd ed. Pearson, 2011.
An undergraduate algebra text that places matrix groups and symmetry at the center, giving abstract group theory a concrete geometric foundation. Artin's approach is unusual and illuminating: students encounter $GL_n(\mathbb{R})$, $SO(3)$, and dihedral groups before the abstract axioms. This directly supports the connection between groups and geometry that motivates the topology content of this curriculum.

**Joseph J. Rotman.** *An Introduction to the Theory of Groups.* 4th ed. Springer, 1995.
A focused, rigorous treatment of group theory alone, covering everything from the basic axioms through free groups, presentations, amalgamated products, and the Schur-Zassenhaus theorem. The free groups and group presentations material (Section 2 of this chapter) is developed more thoroughly here than in general algebra texts.

**John F. Humphreys.** *A Course in Group Theory.* Oxford University Press, 1996.
A clean, concise undergraduate text that covers the core group theory of this chapter — subgroups, cosets, Lagrange, normal subgroups, quotients, homomorphisms, isomorphism theorems, and group actions — without unnecessary digressions. Good as a companion text to the main curriculum.

**Ian Stewart.** *Galois Theory.* 4th ed. CRC Press, 2015.
The most accessible introduction to Galois theory available, written by a skilled expositor. Covers the historical problem (solvability by radicals), the field theory background, the fundamental theorem of Galois theory, and applications. The Chapter 2 preview of Galois theory is supported by the first four chapters of Stewart's book.

**Nathan Jacobson.** *Basic Algebra I.* 2nd ed. Dover, 2009.
A comprehensive and rigorous treatment of undergraduate and early graduate algebra. The treatment of group theory in Chapters 1–2 and ring theory in Chapter 2 is unusually complete and careful about the axiomatic foundations, making it a useful reference for the connections between algebra and the formal proofs in this curriculum.

## Online Resources and Lecture Notes

**The Stacks Project.** https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/
An open-source online reference for algebraic geometry, but its foundational sections on algebra (category theory, modules, rings, fields) are among the most carefully written algebraic references available anywhere. The treatment of rings and modules is particularly relevant to the rings-and-fields material in Section 4.

**Keith Conrad.** Expository Papers on Algebra. https://kconrad.math.uconn.edu/blurbs/
Conrad's collection of expository notes on topics in group theory, ring theory, and number theory are among the best-written freely available algebraic resources. His notes on group actions, the isomorphism theorems, normal subgroups, and free groups are directly relevant to this chapter and provide alternative presentations of every major concept.

**Lean 4 / Mathlib: Algebra.Group.** https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/Algebra/Group/
Mathlib formalizes groups, subgroups, quotient groups, homomorphisms, and the isomorphism theorems in Lean 4. Browsing the Group hierarchy shows exactly how the definitions and theorems of this chapter are encoded as formal types and propositions in dependent type theory — a preview of the type-theoretic content of the curriculum.

**3Blue1Brown.** "Group Theory, Abstraction, and the 196,883-Dimensional Monster." YouTube, 2020. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mH0oCDa74tE
An accessible visual introduction to the idea of group theory and the classification of finite simple groups. Not a technical reference, but an excellent motivator for why the abstract definition of a group captures genuinely deep mathematical structure. Suitable as an introduction before the formal development.

**Benedict Gross and Joseph Harris.** "Algebra." Lecture videos, Harvard University. Available via Harvard Extension School.
Graduate-level algebra lectures covering the material of this chapter and beyond. Gross's lectures in particular are notable for their clarity and their attention to the relationship between algebraic structure and geometric meaning.

## Historical Context

The history of group theory begins not with an abstract definition but with a concrete problem: the solvability of polynomial equations by radicals. Mathematicians had known formulas for solving cubic and quartic equations since the sixteenth century (Cardano, Ferrari), and for two centuries the question of whether a general quintic formula existed remained open. Ruffini (1799) and Abel (1824) proved that no such formula exists, but the deeper question — which equations are solvable? — was answered by Évariste Galois in 1830–1831 in manuscripts he left behind before dying in a duel at age 20. Galois identified the symmetry group of an equation's roots (the Galois group), and proved that solvability by radicals is equivalent to the group being solvable (having a composition series with abelian factors). To do this, he needed the concept of a normal subgroup and a quotient group — concepts he essentially invented.

The abstraction of group theory from concrete permutation groups to abstract groups was largely the work of Cayley (1854) and, more completely, of Jordan (1870) and Dedekind. By the late nineteenth century, Hölder, Frobenius, and Burnside had established group theory as an independent discipline, proving fundamental theorems about finite groups and their representations. The decisive algebraic revolution of the twentieth century, however, was the work of Emmy Noether. Noether saw that the right way to understand algebraic structures — groups, rings, fields, modules — was not through computation but through the relationships between structures: homomorphisms, ideals, quotients, and exact sequences. Her structural approach, sometimes called "abstract algebra" to distinguish it from the computational algebra that preceded it, is the style that dominates this chapter and all modern treatments. The Noether isomorphism theorems (which appear here in their group-theoretic form) capture the fundamental relationships between subgroups, quotient groups, and homomorphisms; they are among the most-used results in all of algebra and, via the Curry-Howard correspondence, translate into statements about type-theoretic constructions in HoTT.
