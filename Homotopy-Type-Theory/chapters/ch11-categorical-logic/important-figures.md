# Important Figures

## F. William Lawvere (1937–present)
*Mathematician and philosopher; founder of categorical logic and topos-theoretic foundations*

F. William Lawvere studied at Indiana University and then at Columbia, where he completed his 1963 thesis under Samuel Eilenberg. The thesis introduced *Lawvere theories* — a purely categorical reformulation of universal algebra — and established the thesis that algebraic structures should be described by the morphisms between their theories rather than by their underlying sets. This was a radical departure from the standard set-theoretic approach to algebra, and it launched the program of categorical foundations.

Lawvere's most direct contribution to categorical logic is the concept of the *hyperdoctrine* (1969–1970): a functor $P : \mathcal{C}^{op} \to \mathbf{Heyt}$ (contravariant from a category with finite products to the category of Heyting algebras) that models predicate logic. The key insight is that substitution of variables is a functor (contravariant) and that quantifiers $\forall$ and $\exists$ are its right and left adjoints respectively. This single observation — that the quantifiers of first-order logic are adjoint to substitution — unified logic and category theory at a foundational level and is the direct ancestor of the categorical semantics of dependent type theory (where Π and Σ types are the adjoints of substitution in a locally cartesian closed category).

Working with Myles Tierney in 1969–1970, Lawvere co-invented the *elementary topos*: a category with finite limits, exponentials, and a subobject classifier, whose internal logic is intuitionistic higher-order logic. The *Lawvere-Tierney topology* (an idempotent closure operator $j : \Omega \to \Omega$ on the subobject classifier, satisfying $j \circ \mathsf{true} = \mathsf{true}$, $j \circ j = j$, and $j(p \wedge q) = j(p) \wedge j(q)$) is the categorical generalization of both the topological notion of closure and the logical notion of modality. Sheaves with respect to a Lawvere-Tierney topology form a subtopos — a "smaller universe" with a richer logical structure.

Lawvere's philosophical writings on "conceptual mathematics" and "cohesion" argue that mathematics is fundamentally about the adjunctions between opposing tendencies (discrete/continuous, local/global, syntax/semantics), and that category theory makes these oppositions precise. His influence on categorical foundations, on the philosophy of mathematics, and indirectly on the type-theoretic semantics underlying HoTT is immeasurable.

## Joachim Lambek (1922–2014)
*Mathematician and linguist; established the CCC / typed lambda calculus correspondence*

Joachim Lambek was born in Leipzig and emigrated to Canada in 1945, eventually becoming a professor at McGill University in Montreal, where he remained for his entire career. He made major contributions to abstract algebra, mathematical linguistics, and categorical logic. In linguistics, the Lambek calculus (a sequent calculus for syntactic types in grammar) preceded by decades the modern revival of substructural logics. In categorical logic, his central contribution is the precise correspondence between cartesian closed categories and typed lambda calculi.

In a series of papers from 1968 to 1972, Lambek proved that the free cartesian closed category generated by a set of base types is isomorphic (as a category) to the category of typed lambda terms modulo $\beta\eta$-equivalence. This is the *Curry-Howard-Lambek correspondence*: proofs in intuitionistic propositional logic, programs in the simply typed lambda calculus, and morphisms in a free CCC are three different descriptions of the same thing. The morphisms in the free CCC are the proofs; the objects are the types; the structural rules of the sequent calculus correspond to the categorical operations (identity, composition, products, exponentials).

Lambek extended this correspondence to higher-order logic and toposes in his joint book with Phil Scott, *Introduction to Higher Order Categorical Logic* (1986). Part III of that book develops the correspondence between the theory of an elementary topos and a form of higher-order intuitionistic type theory, essentially establishing the Mitchell-Bénabou language as a complete internal language for toposes. This book remains the definitive reference for the precise version of the internal language correspondence.

## Dana Scott (1932–present)
*Logician and computer scientist; domain theory, denotational semantics, and categorical models*

Dana Scott is a professor emeritus at Carnegie Mellon University (where Steve Awodey is a direct intellectual successor) and one of the founders of theoretical computer science. His joint work with Christopher Strachey on *denotational semantics* (1969–1972) created the first mathematically rigorous semantics for programming languages, using domain theory (a theory of partially ordered sets with specific completeness properties) to model recursive programs.

Scott's most important contribution to categorical logic is his independent discovery of categorical models for the lambda calculus. He showed that the category of domains (or, more precisely, the category of dcpos and Scott-continuous functions) is a CCC, and that every type in a typed lambda calculus can be interpreted as an object in this CCC. This provided the first non-trivial models for type theory and showed that the types of programming languages have categorical content.

Scott also made fundamental contributions to the model theory of intuitionistic logic: Scott models (Kripke models extended to partially ordered sets with upward-closed truth) are a precursor to the presheaf semantics of intuitionistic logic. His forcing semantics for set theory (independent of Cohen's combinatorial approach) gave a topos-theoretic perspective on set-theoretic independence proofs. Scott's work demonstrated that categorical semantics is not merely a reformulation of existing mathematics but a source of new models that reveal independence results and logical distinctions invisible to purely syntactic methods.

## Myles Tierney (1937–2017)
*Mathematician; co-inventor of elementary topos theory*

Myles Tierney was a professor at Rutgers University and one of the originators of the categorical approach to sheaf theory and topology. His collaboration with Lawvere in 1969–1970 produced the elementary topos axioms — the distillation of Grothendieck's geometric topos theory into a minimal set of categorical axioms that still support a full internal logic.

Tierney's specific contribution was the *Lawvere-Tierney topology*, which generalizes both the geometric notion of a Grothendieck topology (a notion of "covering" on a category, used to define sheaves) and the logical notion of a modality. A Lawvere-Tierney topology $j : \Omega \to \Omega$ on a topos $\mathcal{E}$ is an idempotent closure operator on the subobject classifier, satisfying $j(\mathsf{true}) = \mathsf{true}$ and $j(p \wedge q) = j(p) \wedge j(q)$. The sheaves with respect to $j$ — objects $A$ for which every "dense" subobject of $A$ is the whole of $A$ — form a subtopos $\mathbf{Sh}_j(\mathcal{E}) \hookrightarrow \mathcal{E}$ with the same subobject classifier as $\mathcal{E}$ but with the truth values restricted to $j$-closed ones.

In terms of logic: a Lawvere-Tierney topology is a modal operator on the propositions of the topos, and passing to the sheaves is like adding the axiom $\neg\neg P \Rightarrow P$ (double negation shift) or some other closure principle. The sheaf subtopos has a "denser" logic than the ambient topos. This is the categorical version of forcing: in forcing models, you enlarge the universe by sheaves with respect to a topology, and the new universe satisfies logical axioms (like the negation of CH) that were not provable in the original.

## Peter T. Johnstone (1948–present)
*Mathematician; leading authority on topos theory and categorical logic*

Peter Johnstone is a professor at Cambridge University and the author of the two most important books on topos theory: *Topos Theory* (1977) and *Sketches of an Elephant* (2002, 2 volumes published; a third volume is in preparation). Johnstone was a student of Martin Hyland and has spent his career systematically developing and documenting the field of topos theory.

Johnstone's *Sketches of an Elephant* is organized around a metaphor: topos theory is like the proverbial elephant, each of its aspects (fibered category theory, internal logic, geometric morphisms, classifying toposes, cohomology) a different part of the animal, with different researchers emphasizing different parts. The book attempts to give the full picture. Volume 1 covers fibered categories, the internal language, and geometric morphisms; Volume 2 covers sites, classifying toposes, and model theory. The planned Volume 3 will cover homotopy theory and cohomology from the topos-theoretic perspective, connecting to the $\infty$-topos program relevant to HoTT.

Johnstone's specific contributions include: the theory of geometric morphisms and their factorization systems; the classification of localic toposes (toposes whose objects are generalized spaces); and the connections between Heyting algebras, frames, and localic geometry. His work on *pointless topology* (topology done in terms of frames/locales rather than sets of points) is relevant to HoTT because pointless topology is naturally constructive and does not require the axiom of choice for basic results.

## Gonzalo Reyes (1941–2022)
*Mathematician; co-author of First Order Categorical Logic; categorical model theory*

Gonzalo Reyes was a Colombian-Canadian mathematician who worked at the Université de Montréal and made essential contributions to the categorical model theory of first-order logic. His most important contribution is the joint monograph with Michael Makkai, *First Order Categorical Logic* (Springer, 1977), which established the correspondence between coherent theories (first-order theories whose axioms are coherent sequents) and coherent categories (categories with finite limits and stable images, the categorical analog of coherent theories).

The Makkai-Reyes completeness theorem is the categorical analog of Gödel's completeness theorem: every coherent theory has a model in a coherent category, and the universal model is the syntactic category of the theory. This gives a canonical way to associate a category to any first-order theory and to interpret logical operations (quantifiers, connectives) as categorical operations (images, limits, colimits).

Reyes also contributed to synthetic differential geometry — the application of topos-theoretic methods to differential geometry, using nilpotent infinitesimals as elements of the real-number object in a suitable topos. This program (developed with Anders Kock, Bill Lawvere, and others) shows that the infinitesimal calculus can be developed internally in a topos without external limits, using only the axioms of the topos. Synthetic differential geometry is a precursor of Schreiber's "cohesive homotopy type theory," which extends HoTT with axioms for smooth and differential structure.
