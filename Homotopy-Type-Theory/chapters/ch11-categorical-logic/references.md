# References and Primary Sources

## Foundational Texts

**F. William Lawvere.** "Adjointness in Foundations." *Dialectica* 23, 1969. Reprinted in *Reprints in Theory and Applications of Categories* 16, 2006.
The paper in which Lawvere argues that adjoint functors are the fundamental organizing principle of logic and mathematics. Quantifiers are adjoints; substitution is a functor; the logic of a theory is determined by the category of its models. This ten-page paper contains more ideas per sentence than almost any other paper in the foundations of mathematics.

**Joachim Lambek and Phil Scott.** *Introduction to Higher Order Categorical Logic.* Cambridge University Press, 1986. (Cambridge Studies in Advanced Mathematics 7)
The definitive text on the correspondence between typed lambda calculi and cartesian closed categories (and between intuitionistic higher-order logic and toposes). Part I covers the CCC / STLC correspondence; Part II covers the hyperdoctrine approach to predicate logic; Part III develops the topos connection. Dense but complete — the best single reference for the internal language apparatus.

**Saunders Mac Lane and Ieke Moerdijk.** *Sheaves in Geometry and Logic: A First Introduction to Topos Theory.* Springer, 1992. (Universitext)
The most widely read introduction to topos theory. After developing the theory of presheaves and sheaves, the book covers Lawvere-Tierney topologies, the internal language (Mitchell-Bénabou), geometric morphisms, and classifying toposes. The treatment of the internal language is clearer than in many other sources. Essential.

**Peter T. Johnstone.** *Sketches of an Elephant: A Topos Theory Compendium.* Oxford University Press, 2002 (Volumes 1–2; Volume 3 in preparation).
Known informally as "the Elephant," this is the comprehensive modern reference for topos theory. Volume 1 covers fibered categories, internal logic, and geometric morphisms; Volume 2 covers classifying toposes, cohomology, and model theory. At nearly 1200 pages across the two published volumes, it is not a textbook but the authoritative reference. Every serious student of categorical logic should own a copy.

**Bart Jacobs.** *Categorical Logic and Type Theory.* Elsevier, 1999. Freely available from [http://www.cs.ru.nl/B.Jacobs/CLT/bookinfo.html](http://www.cs.ru.nl/B.Jacobs/CLT/bookinfo.html).
The most thorough treatment of the correspondence between dependent type theory and categorical logic via fibered categories. Jacobs develops the theory of comprehension categories, fibered categories, hyperdoctrines, and first-order categorical logic, culminating in the full correspondence between dependent type theory and locally cartesian closed categories. Indispensable for understanding Chapter 11 in depth.

## Seminal Papers

**F. William Lawvere.** "Quantifiers and Sheaves." *Actes du Congrès International des Mathématiciens*, Nice, 1970.
Introduces the hyperdoctrine — the categorical structure that models predicate logic — and shows how quantifiers arise as adjunctions to substitution. The hyperdoctrine is the key structure linking category theory to first-order logic and to the dependent type theories of MLTT.

**F. William Lawvere and Myles Tierney.** (Independent announcements, 1969–1970; see Lawvere's "Elementary Theory of the Category of Sets," *Reprints in Theory and Applications of Categories* 11, 2005, and Johnstone's account in the Elephant.)
The joint introduction of the elementary topos axioms. Lawvere and Tierney defined toposes as categories with finite limits, exponentials, and a subobject classifier, and proved that the internal logic of such a category is intuitionistic higher-order logic. The Lawvere-Tierney topology (a morphism $j : \Omega \to \Omega$ satisfying certain axioms) models the passage from a topos to its sheaf subtopos and generalizes the topological notion of closure.

**Joachim Lambek.** "Deductive Systems and Categories II." *Lecture Notes in Mathematics* 86, Springer, 1969.
The paper that established the CCC / typed lambda calculus correspondence. Lambek showed that the morphisms in the free CCC generated by a set of types are exactly the $\beta\eta$-equivalence classes of typed lambda terms. This theorem makes the internal language of a CCC precise and is the starting point for all subsequent work on categorical semantics of type theories.

**Michael Barr and Charles Wells.** "Toposes, Triples and Theories." Springer, 1985. Revised edition freely available at [https://www.tac.mta.ca/tac/reprints/articles/12/tr12.pdf](https://www.tac.mta.ca/tac/reprints/articles/12/tr12.pdf).
A comprehensive treatment of the three central topics: topos theory, monads (triples), and sketch-based model theory. The chapter on toposes includes a clean presentation of the internal language and the relationship between geometric morphisms and logical morphisms.

**Michael Makkai and Gonzalo Reyes.** *First Order Categorical Logic.* Springer Lecture Notes in Mathematics 611, 1977.
The definitive treatment of the relationship between first-order logic and geometric categories (coherent categories, geometric morphisms). Makkai and Reyes prove completeness and consistency theorems for first-order logic using categorical methods, anticipating the model-theoretic applications of topos theory.

**Martin Hyland and Andrew Pitts.** "The Theory of Constructions: Categorical Semantics and Topos-Theoretic Models." *Categories in Computer Science and Logic*, Contemporary Mathematics 92, AMS, 1989.
A key paper connecting Coquand-Huet's Calculus of Constructions (the type theory underlying Coq) to topos theory, using the realizability topos as the categorical model. This paper is representative of the line of work connecting categorical logic to proof assistants.

## Textbooks and Modern Treatments

**Peter T. Johnstone.** *Topos Theory.* Academic Press, 1977. Reprinted by Dover, 2014.
Johnstone's earlier, more accessible textbook on topos theory (as opposed to the Elephant). Covers the elementary topos axioms, the internal language, geometric morphisms, and several key examples. The Dover reprint makes it affordable; it remains a good first introduction to topos theory for mathematicians.

**Saunders Mac Lane and Ieke Moerdijk.** *Sheaves in Geometry and Logic.* (See Foundational Texts above.) Recommended again as the best single-volume introduction for readers coming from either algebra/geometry or logic.

**Steve Awodey.** *Category Theory.* Oxford, 2nd ed. 2010.
(See Chapter 10 references.) Chapter 14 of Awodey covers the internal language of a CCC and the categorical semantics of the simply-typed lambda calculus. This is the most accessible introduction to the CCC / STLC correspondence.

**Robert Goldblatt.** *Topoi: The Categorial Analysis of Logic.* North-Holland, 1979. Revised Dover edition freely available.
A gentler introduction to topos theory from a logician's perspective, emphasizing the logical content. Goldblatt's treatment is more accessible than Johnstone and is excellent for readers whose primary interest is the logic rather than the geometry.

**Andrew M. Pitts.** "Categorical Logic." In *Handbook of Logic in Computer Science*, Vol. 5, Oxford, 2000.
An authoritative survey article covering the CCC / STLC correspondence, hyperdoctrines, and the categorical semantics of type theories. Particularly clear on the difference between the simple and dependent type theories and the corresponding categorical structures.

## Online Resources and Lecture Notes

**Steve Awodey.** *Logic in Topoi: Functorial Semantics for Higher-Order Logic.* Course notes available from Awodey's Carnegie Mellon website.
Awodey's graduate course on categorical logic, covering the Mitchell-Bénabou internal language, the topos-theoretic interpretation of higher-order intuitionistic logic, and the connection to type theory. Freely available and among the best pedagogical presentations of the material.

**nLab: Internal Logic of a Topos.**
[https://ncatlab.org/nlab/show/internal+logic+of+a+topos](https://ncatlab.org/nlab/show/internal+logic+of+a+topos)
The nLab article gives a concise summary of the Mitchell-Bénabou language and the internal logic, with references to the primary literature. As always, use the nLab for cross-referencing and for pointers to related concepts.

**nLab: Subobject Classifier.**
[https://ncatlab.org/nlab/show/subobject+classifier](https://ncatlab.org/nlab/show/subobject+classifier)
The article on the subobject classifier explains the relationship between $\Omega$, the power object $P(A) = \Omega^A$, and the Heyting algebra structure on subobjects. Essential for understanding the logic of a topos.

**Mathlib4 `Mathlib.CategoryTheory.Topos`.**
[https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/CategoryTheory/Topos/](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/CategoryTheory/Topos/)
The Lean 4 formalization of topos theory in Mathlib. The subobject classifier, the definition of an elementary topos, and the basic properties are formalized here. Reading the Lean source alongside the mathematical treatment is the most effective way to achieve precision.

## Historical Context

Categorical logic emerged from two directions in the late 1960s. On one side was the topos theory of Grothendieck (developed for algebraic geometry in the 1960s) and its reformulation as an elementary theory by Lawvere and Tierney (1969–1970). Lawvere had already shown, in his 1963 thesis, that algebraic structures could be described by categories (Lawvere theories), and his 1969 "Adjointness in Foundations" paper argued that adjunctions were the fundamental connective tissue of logic. Tierney, a topologist, contributed the insight that the Grothendieck topos axioms could be reformulated elementarily (without assuming the category is a category of sheaves on a site) and that the resulting structure had a well-defined internal logic.

On the other side was the work of Lambek (1968–1969) on the relationship between proof theory and category theory: Lambek showed that the free cartesian closed category generated by a set of base types has as its morphisms the $\beta\eta$-equivalence classes of typed lambda terms. This established the CCC / STLC correspondence as a theorem rather than an analogy. Scott's work on denotational semantics (domain theory, 1969–1972) showed how to construct concrete CCC models for the untyped lambda calculus and for more expressive type theories.

The synthesis of these strands — topos theory, the internal language, and the connection to type theory — was developed through the 1970s and 1980s, primarily in the work of Makkai, Reyes, Johnstone, Hyland, Pitts, and Scott. The Mitchell-Bénabou language formalized the internal language of an elementary topos as a higher-order intuitionistic type theory. The hyperdoctrine (Lawvere 1970) gave the categorical semantics of predicate logic, identifying quantifiers as adjunctions in fibered categories. By the time Lambek and Scott published *Introduction to Higher Order Categorical Logic* in 1986, the correspondence was essentially complete for simple and higher-order type theories.

The extension to dependent type theory came later: Seely's 1984 paper and Cartmell's work on contextual categories (1978 thesis, 1986 paper) established the correspondence between locally cartesian closed categories and dependent type theory, though with some coherence issues (the equality of substitution functors is only up to isomorphism, not strictly). The Hofmann-Streicher groupoid model (1994) showed that MLTT has non-trivial models — models where UIP fails — and initiated the chain of categorical insights that led to the HoTT book in 2013. The connection to $\infty$-toposes (Joyal, Lurie, Rezk, Shulman, and collaborators, 2000s–2020s) is the current frontier.
