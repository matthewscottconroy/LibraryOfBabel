# References and Primary Sources

## Foundational Texts

**R. Boardman and R. Vogt.** *Homotopy Invariant Algebraic Structures on Topological Spaces.* Lecture Notes in Mathematics 347, Springer, 1973.
The paper that introduced the notion of weak Kan complexes (later called quasi-categories), establishing the first rigorous framework for homotopy-coherent algebraic structures. Every subsequent development of ∞-categories is indebted to this work.

**A. Grothendieck.** *Pursuing Stacks.* Unpublished manuscript, 1983. (Written as a letter to Daniel Quillen; later typed and circulated; now available via the Grothendieck Circle.)
The foundational document for the homotopy hypothesis: Grothendieck's vision that ∞-groupoids should model homotopy types, and that a satisfactory theory of ∞-categories would transform algebraic geometry, topology, and foundations simultaneously. Reading this text in its original form is essential for understanding the scope of the program.

**A. Joyal.** *Quasi-categories and Kan Complexes.* Unpublished notes, 2002 (circulated 2002–2008; partially published in *Journal of Pure and Applied Algebra* 175, 2002).
Joyal's foundational development of the theory of quasi-categories, establishing the model structure on simplicial sets (now called the Joyal model structure) and proving that quasi-categories provide a complete model for homotopy-coherent category theory.

**J. Lurie.** *Higher Topos Theory.* Princeton University Press, 2009. Also available on arXiv (math/0608040).
The definitive modern reference for the theory of ∞-categories and ∞-toposes, developing quasi-categories systematically and establishing the equivalence between various models of ∞-categories. At roughly 1000 pages, this is the standard reference text for the field.

**J. Lurie.** *Higher Algebra.* Preprint, available at https://www.math.ias.edu/~lurie/.
A companion to Higher Topos Theory focused on the theory of structured ring spectra, operads, and monoidal ∞-categories. Together with HTT, this constitutes the foundation of derived algebraic geometry. Freely available from Lurie's webpage.

## Seminal Papers

**A. Grothendieck.** "La théorie des classes de Chern." *Bulletin de la Société Mathématique de France* 86 (1958), 137–154.
An early paper establishing key technical tools that fed into Grothendieck's later categorical thinking; important for understanding how his geometric intuition about functors between categories eventually led to the homotopy hypothesis.

**D. Quillen.** "Homotopical Algebra." *Lecture Notes in Mathematics* 43, Springer, 1967.
Introduced model categories — the abstract framework for doing homotopy theory in a general categorical setting. Quillen proved the Quillen equivalence between simplicial sets and topological spaces, which is the precise form of the homotopy hypothesis for Kan complexes.

**C. Rezk.** "A Model for the Homotopy Theory of Homotopy Theory." *Transactions of the American Mathematical Society* 353(3) (2001), 973–1007.
Introduced complete Segal spaces as a model of (∞,1)-categories and proved that the category of complete Segal spaces is a model category. This provided a fully internal description of ∞-categories as simplicial spaces satisfying specific completeness and Segal conditions.

**A. Joyal.** "Quasi-categories vs. Segal Spaces." *Categories in Algebra, Geometry and Mathematical Physics*, Contemporary Mathematics 431, AMS, 2007, 277–326.
Establishes the Quillen equivalence between the Joyal model structure on simplicial sets and the Rezk model structure for complete Segal spaces, proving that the two main models for (∞,1)-categories are equivalent.

**J. Lurie.** "On the Classification of Topological Field Theories." *Current Developments in Mathematics* (2008), 129–280. Also arXiv:0905.0465.
Proves the cobordism hypothesis: a fully extended topological field theory is determined by a fully dualizable object in a symmetric monoidal (∞,n)-category. This is the flagship application of ∞-categorical machinery to mathematical physics, using the language of (∞,n)-categories developed from Boardman-Vogt.

**C. Simpson.** "A Giraud-type Characterization of the Simplicial Categories Associated to Closed Model Categories as ∞-Groupoids." Preprint, arXiv:math/9903167, 1999.
Proves that strict ∞-groupoids are insufficient to model all homotopy types — an important negative result showing that weakness in the ∞-groupoid structure is not optional.

**G. Maltsiniotis.** "Grothendieck ∞-Groupoids, and Still Another Definition of ∞-Categories." Preprint, arXiv:1009.2331, 2010.
A careful exposition of Grothendieck's globular approach to ∞-groupoids, clarifying the relationship between different algebraic definitions.

## Textbooks and Modern Treatments

**E. Riehl.** *Category Theory in Context.* Dover, 2016. (Free PDF available from the author's website.)
An excellent modern introduction to classical category theory that carefully develops the universal property language essential for understanding higher-categorical generalizations. The treatment of limits, adjunctions, and Kan extensions provides the essential background for understanding their ∞-categorical analogues.

**E. Riehl and D. Verity.** *Elements of ∞-Category Theory.* Cambridge University Press, 2022. (Free PDF available on arXiv as 1710.09565.)
A comprehensive textbook treatment of ∞-categories using the framework of ∞-cosmoi, providing a uniform approach to quasi-categories, complete Segal spaces, and Segal categories simultaneously. Written at the level of a graduate student; the most accessible rigorous text on ∞-categories currently available.

**T. Leinster.** *Higher Operads, Higher Categories.* London Mathematical Society Lecture Note Series 298, Cambridge University Press, 2004. (Free on arXiv: math/0305049.)
An introduction to the zoo of definitions of higher categories (operads, globular sets, computads, etc.) with careful comparisons. Essential background for understanding why defining "weak ∞-category" is so difficult.

**J. Bergner.** *The Homotopy Theory of (∞,1)-Categories.* London Mathematical Society Student Texts 90, Cambridge University Press, 2018.
A concise and pedagogical introduction to the main models of (∞,1)-categories — quasi-categories, complete Segal spaces, Segal categories, simplicially enriched categories — and the Quillen equivalences between them. The most direct path to understanding the equivalence between the different models.

**P. Hirschhorn.** *Model Categories and Their Localizations.* Mathematical Surveys and Monographs 99, AMS, 2003.
A thorough reference for the theory of model categories, localizations (Bousfield localizations), and their applications. The technical machinery underlying the comparison of models of (∞,1)-categories.

## Online Resources and Lecture Notes

**The nLab.** https://ncatlab.org/
A wiki-style reference for higher category theory and homotopy type theory written by researchers in the field. Coverage is uneven but often at the research frontier. Particularly useful for: quasi-category, Kan complex, (∞,1)-category, homotopy hypothesis, model category, complete Segal space. Use it as a pointer to papers, not as a primary source.

**E. Riehl.** "A Leisurely Introduction to Simplicial Sets." Expository note, available at https://math.jhu.edu/~eriehl/.
A friendly, example-driven introduction to the category of simplicial sets that develops the intuition for the combinatorial models underlying ∞-category theory.

**J. Lurie.** "What is an ∞-Category?" *Notices of the American Mathematical Society* 55(8) (2008), 949–952.
A short, readable overview of ∞-categories aimed at a general mathematical audience. The best first thing to read for context.

**D. Gepner.** "Introduction to Higher Category Theory." Lecture notes, available on the author's webpage.
Graduate lecture notes covering quasi-categories, complete Segal spaces, and their applications, written at a level accessible after graduate category theory.

**The Homotopy Type Theory Book.** https://homotopytypetheory.org/book/ (also Chapter 2 in this curriculum.)
Chapter 2 of the HoTT book covers the groupoid structure of identity types, and Chapter 6 covers higher inductive types. These provide the internal HoTT perspective on what higher-categorical structures mean inside the type theory.

## Historical Context

The subject now called higher category theory grew out of two separate traditions that converged in the 1990s. The first tradition is algebraic topology: from Eilenberg-MacLane's invention of category theory in 1945, through Serre's use of fibrations, through Quillen's model categories (1967), toward the recognition that homotopy theory itself has a deep categorical structure. The homotopy hypothesis — the conjecture that ∞-groupoids are the same as homotopy types — was stated by Grothendieck in his 1983 manuscript "Pursuing Stacks," though it had precursors in the work of Kan, Milnor, and others who constructed simplicial models for topological spaces. The precise version of the hypothesis using Kan complexes was proved by Quillen in 1967, a decade before Grothendieck's manuscript, but Grothendieck's formulation was more ambitious: he wanted a purely algebraic notion of ∞-groupoid (using globular sets, not simplicial sets) and a full equivalence with this algebraic notion. This globular version remains an active research program.

The second tradition is category theory applied to logic and computer science: Lawvere's categorical foundations, topos theory (Lawvere-Tierney, 1969–1970), and the recognition that toposes are models of intuitionistic higher-order logic. The higher-categorical generalization — that ∞-toposes are models of HoTT — was developed in the 2000s by Lurie, building on Rezk's complete Segal spaces and Joyal's quasi-categories. Lurie's "Higher Topos Theory" (2009) synthesized these threads into a single coherent framework: quasi-categories as a model for (∞,1)-categories, ∞-toposes as the higher-categorical analog of Grothendieck toposes, and the internal language of ∞-toposes as HoTT. This synthesis made precise a slogan that had been in the air since Voevodsky's 2006 proposal: HoTT is not just inspired by homotopy theory, it *is* homotopy theory.
