# Chapter 13: Point-Set Topology

## Introduction

Point-set topology abstracts the notion of "nearness" beyond metric spaces (Chapter 3). By specifying which sets count as "open" without any underlying metric, we gain a language powerful enough to describe function spaces, quotient constructions, and spaces arising in algebraic geometry and category theory — all of which are central to homotopy theory.

This chapter develops the core of point-set topology: topological spaces, continuous maps, connectedness, compactness, and quotient spaces. The emphasis is on building the toolkit needed for homotopy theory (Chapter 14), where these concepts take on their deeper meaning.

---

## 1. Topological Spaces

### 1.1 The Definition

**Definition 13.1 (Topological Space).** A *topological space* is a pair $(X, \tau)$ where $X$ is a set and $\tau \subseteq \mathcal{P}(X)$ is a *topology* — a collection of *open sets* — satisfying:
1. $\emptyset \in \tau$ and $X \in \tau$
2. Arbitrary unions: if $\{U_\alpha\} \subseteq \tau$, then $\bigcup_\alpha U_\alpha \in \tau$
3. Finite intersections: if $U, V \in \tau$, then $U \cap V \in \tau$

A set $C \subseteq X$ is *closed* if $X \setminus C$ is open.

**Example 13.2.**
- *Metric topology:* Given $(X, d)$, define $\tau = \{U \subseteq X \mid \forall x \in U, \exists \varepsilon > 0, B(x,\varepsilon) \subseteq U\}$. This is the topology from Chapter 3.
- *Discrete topology:* $\tau = \mathcal{P}(X)$ (every set is open). The metric topology of the discrete metric.
- *Indiscrete (trivial) topology:* $\tau = \{\emptyset, X\}$ (as few open sets as possible).
- *Sierpiński space:* $X = \{0, 1\}$, $\tau = \{\emptyset, \{1\}, \{0,1\}\}$. Despite its simplicity, this space classifies open sets in any topological space.
- *Zariski topology:* On $\mathbb{R}^n$ (or on the prime spectrum of a ring), with closed sets being varieties — zero-sets of polynomials. The "standard" topology of algebraic geometry.

### 1.2 Bases and Sub-Bases

**Definition 13.3 (Basis).** A *basis* for a topology is a collection $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathcal{P}(X)$ such that:
1. Every $x \in X$ is in some $B \in \mathcal{B}$
2. If $x \in B_1 \cap B_2$ for $B_1, B_2 \in \mathcal{B}$, there exists $B_3 \in \mathcal{B}$ with $x \in B_3 \subseteq B_1 \cap B_2$

The topology generated by $\mathcal{B}$ consists of all unions of elements of $\mathcal{B}$.

**Examples:** Open balls form a basis for the metric topology. Rectangular boxes form a basis for the product topology on $\mathbb{R}^n$.

### 1.3 Subspace, Product, and Quotient Topologies

**Subspace topology:** For $A \subseteq X$, the subspace topology on $A$ is $\{U \cap A \mid U \in \tau_X\}$.

**Product topology:** For spaces $X$ and $Y$, the product $X \times Y$ has the topology generated by "rectangular open sets" $U \times V$ for $U$ open in $X$ and $V$ open in $Y$. The projections $\pi_1 : X \times Y \to X$ and $\pi_2 : X \times Y \to Y$ are continuous.

**Quotient topology:** For a surjection $q : X \to Y$, the *quotient topology* on $Y$ is the largest topology making $q$ continuous: $V \subseteq Y$ is open iff $q^{-1}(V)$ is open in $X$.

**Example 13.4.** The circle $S^1$ is the quotient $[0,1]/\{0 \sim 1\}$: identify the endpoints of the unit interval.

**Example 13.5.** The torus $T^2 = [0,1]^2/{\sim}$ where $(x,0) \sim (x,1)$ and $(0,y) \sim (1,y)$.

## 2. Continuous Maps and Homeomorphisms

### 2.1 Continuity

**Definition 13.6.** A function $f : X \to Y$ between topological spaces is *continuous* if for every open $V \subseteq Y$, the preimage $f^{-1}(V)$ is open in $X$.

This generalizes the $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ definition: for metric spaces, the two notions agree (Theorem 3.16).

**Theorem 13.7.** The composition of continuous maps is continuous. Identity maps are continuous.

*Proof.* $(g \circ f)^{-1}(V) = f^{-1}(g^{-1}(V))$. If $V$ is open in $Z$, then $g^{-1}(V)$ is open in $Y$ (since $g$ is continuous), then $f^{-1}(g^{-1}(V))$ is open in $X$ (since $f$ is continuous). $\square$

**Theorem 13.8.** Continuous maps form the morphisms of the category $\mathbf{Top}$.

### 2.2 Homeomorphisms

**Definition 13.9.** A *homeomorphism* is a continuous bijection whose inverse is also continuous. Spaces are *homeomorphic* ($X \cong_{\text{Top}} Y$) if a homeomorphism exists.

Homeomorphic spaces are topologically identical — they have the same open sets after relabeling.

**Example 13.10.** $\mathbb{R} \cong_{\text{Top}} (0,1)$ via $f(x) = \arctan(x)/\pi + 1/2$. Despite looking geometrically different ($\mathbb{R}$ is unbounded, $(0,1)$ is bounded), they are topologically the same.

**Example 13.11.** A square and a circle are homeomorphic. A sphere $S^2$ and a cube surface are homeomorphic. But $S^1$ and $S^2$ are *not* homeomorphic (they differ in homotopy groups).

## 3. Separation Axioms

The separation axioms give a hierarchy of how well a topological space can "separate" distinct points.

**$T_0$ (Kolmogorov):** For any two distinct points, there is an open set containing one but not the other.

**$T_1$:** For any two distinct points, each has an open neighborhood not containing the other. Equivalently: all singletons $\{x\}$ are closed.

**$T_2$ (Hausdorff):** Any two distinct points have *disjoint* open neighborhoods. This is the most important separation axiom: it ensures limits of sequences are unique.

**$T_3$ (Regular):** A closed set and a point not in it can be separated by disjoint open sets.

**$T_4$ (Normal):** Any two disjoint closed sets can be separated by disjoint open sets.

**Theorem 13.12 (Urysohn's Lemma).** A topological space is normal ($T_4$) if and only if for any two disjoint closed sets $A$ and $B$, there exists a continuous function $f : X \to [0,1]$ with $f|_A = 0$ and $f|_B = 1$.

Urysohn's lemma is both topological and constructive in flavor — it gives an explicit function, not just existence of separation.

## 4. Connectedness

**Definition 13.13.** A space $X$ is *connected* if it cannot be written as a disjoint union of two non-empty open sets.

**Definition 13.14.** A space $X$ is *path-connected* if for any $x, y \in X$ there is a continuous path $\gamma : [0,1] \to X$ with $\gamma(0) = x$ and $\gamma(1) = y$.

Path-connected $\Rightarrow$ connected (but not conversely).

**The path components** of $X$: the equivalence classes under the relation $x \sim y$ iff there is a path from $x$ to $y$. These are the connected components of $X$ for path connectivity.

**Example 13.15.** The topologist's sine curve: $\{(x, \sin(1/x)) \mid 0 < x \leq 1\} \cup \{(0, y) \mid y \in [-1,1]\}$ is connected but not path-connected (no path can reach the "$y$-axis" piece from the "sine" piece in finite time while remaining in the set).

**Theorem 13.16 (Invariance of Domain).** If $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is open and $f : U \to \mathbb{R}^n$ is injective and continuous, then $f(U)$ is open.

This is a deep theorem (requires algebraic topology to prove) that shows $\mathbb{R}^n$ and $\mathbb{R}^m$ are not homeomorphic for $n \neq m$.

## 5. Compactness

**Definition 13.17.** A space $X$ is *compact* if every open cover of $X$ has a finite subcover.

**Theorem 13.18 (Heine-Borel for $\mathbb{R}^n$).** A subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is compact iff it is closed and bounded.

**Theorem 13.19.** The continuous image of a compact space is compact.

**Theorem 13.20.** A compact Hausdorff space is normal.

**Theorem 13.21.** A closed subspace of a compact space is compact.

**Theorem 13.22 (Tychonoff's Theorem).** The product of any collection of compact spaces is compact (with the product topology).

This theorem requires the Axiom of Choice (in full generality). For finite products, it is provable without AC.

**Application:** The Hilbert cube $[0,1]^\omega$ (countable product of copies of $[0,1]$) is compact by Tychonoff.

## 6. Quotient Spaces and Identification

Quotient spaces are the primary way to build new spaces from old ones in topology and homotopy theory. Every CW complex, every surface, every lens space is built by quotienting.

**Definition 13.23.** Given $X$ and an equivalence relation $\sim$ on $X$, the *quotient space* $X/{\sim}$ has:
- Underlying set: equivalence classes $[x]$
- Topology: $V \subseteq X/\sim$ is open iff $\pi^{-1}(V)$ is open in $X$, where $\pi : X \to X/\sim$ is the quotient map

**Example 13.24 (CW complexes).** CW complexes are built inductively:
- Start with a discrete set of 0-cells (points)
- Attach 1-cells (edges) by identifying endpoints with 0-cells
- Attach 2-cells (discs) by identifying boundaries with 1-cells
- Continue in higher dimensions

This is a quotient space at each step. Every CW complex has a natural quotient topology.

**Example 13.25 (The real projective plane $\mathbb{RP}^2$).** $\mathbb{RP}^2 = S^2/{\sim}$ where $x \sim -x$ (antipodal identification). This space is non-orientable; its fundamental group is $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

**Theorem 13.26 (Quotient Universal Property).** A function $f : X/{\sim} \to Y$ is continuous iff $f \circ \pi : X \to Y$ is continuous.

This universal property characterizes the quotient topology: it is the *final topology* (the largest topology making $\pi$ continuous).

## 7. Compactly Generated Spaces

Standard topology has a technical problem: the function space (set of continuous functions from $X$ to $Y$ with an appropriate topology) does not always work well categorically.

**Definition 13.27 (k-Space / Compactly Generated).** A space $X$ is *compactly generated* if a set $U \subseteq X$ is open iff for every compact subset $K \subseteq X$, $U \cap K$ is open in $K$ (with the subspace topology).

The full subcategory $\mathbf{CG}$ of compactly generated Hausdorff spaces is *cartesian closed*: the function space $[X, Y]$ (with the compact-open topology) is well-behaved.

This is why homotopy theory typically works in $\mathbf{CG}$ rather than all of $\mathbf{Top}$: the category $\mathbf{CG}$ is cartesian closed, allowing the homotopy-theoretic exponential $[A, B]$ (the space of maps from $A$ to $B$) to behave like an internal hom.

---

## 8. Looking Ahead: Topology and HoTT

The topological spaces of this chapter are the *classical* models of what HoTT axiomatizes synthetically. In HoTT:
- A *type* $A$ plays the role of a topological space
- A *term* $a : A$ plays the role of a point
- A *path* $p : a = b$ plays the role of a continuous path
- A *homotopy* $H : p = q$ plays the role of a homotopy between paths
- The *unit interval* $[0,1]$ is abstracted to the HIT interval type $\mathbb{I}$

The key conceptual shift: in classical topology, spaces are given with a topology; in HoTT, the "topology" is implicit in the identity type structure. A type is "like" a space, but we never need to specify which sets are open — the path structure is all we need.

This is why HoTT is called *synthetic* homotopy theory: it axiomatizes homotopy-theoretic properties directly, without building an underlying point-set foundation.

**13.6.** Show that compactness is a topological invariant: if $X \cong Y$ and $X$ is compact, then $Y$ is compact.

  - Nowhere dense (has empty interior)
  - In bijection with $[0,1]$ (and with $2^\omega$, the Cantor space)
