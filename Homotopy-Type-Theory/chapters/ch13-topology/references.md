# References and Primary Sources

## Foundational Texts

**H. Poincaré.** "Analysis Situs." *Journal de l'École Polytechnique* (2) 1 (1895), 1–123.
The paper that founded algebraic topology: Poincaré defined what is now called the fundamental group $\pi_1$, introduced the notion of homology for manifolds via chains and cycles, and stated (without proof) the Poincaré conjecture. Remarkably readable as a historical document; the key ideas of Chapter 13 — loops, holes, connectivity — are all here in embryonic form.

**F. Hausdorff.** *Grundzüge der Mengenlehre* (Foundations of Set Theory). Veit & Company, Leipzig, 1914.
The book that established point-set topology as an independent discipline. Hausdorff gave the first systematic treatment of topological spaces (in what is now called the Hausdorff axiom version), defined metric spaces, and proved foundational results on compactness, completeness, and the structure of separable spaces. The modern definition of a topological space in terms of open sets is largely due to Hausdorff's systematization.

**J.R. Munkres.** *Topology.* 2nd edition, Prentice Hall, 2000.
The standard undergraduate and beginning graduate textbook. Part I (point-set topology) covers topological spaces, continuity, connectedness, compactness, metrization theorems, and the separation axioms with great clarity and completeness. Part II (algebraic topology) introduces the fundamental group and covering spaces. If you are reading only one book alongside this chapter, Munkres is the right choice.

**J.L. Kelley.** *General Topology.* Graduate Texts in Mathematics 27, Springer, 1975 (originally Van Nostrand, 1955).
A classic graduate reference for the deeper parts of general topology: nets, filters, compactification, metrizability, function spaces. Kelley's treatment is more comprehensive and more abstract than Munkres's; use it as a reference when Munkres is insufficient.

**A. Hatcher.** *Algebraic Topology.* Cambridge University Press, 2002. (Free PDF at https://pi.math.cornell.edu/~hatcher/)
While primarily a book on algebraic topology, Chapter 0 (CW complexes and homotopy) and Appendix A (topology of CW complexes) provide an essential complement to Chapter 13. Freely available online; the writing is exceptionally clear, with an emphasis on geometric intuition.

## Seminal Papers

**H. Poincaré.** "Deuxième complément à l'Analysis Situs." *Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society* 32 (1900), 277–308.
A sequel to the 1895 paper, introducing Betti numbers and their duality theorem (Poincaré duality), and giving the first treatment of torsion in homology. Together with the 1895 paper, this established the program of using algebra to study topology.

**L.E.J. Brouwer.** "Über Abbildungen von Mannigfaltigkeiten." *Mathematische Annalen* 71 (1912), 97–115.
Proved the Brouwer fixed-point theorem (every continuous map from the closed $n$-disk to itself has a fixed point) and introduced the concept of topological degree of a map. This paper established degree theory as a topological tool and is one of the first applications of the then-new algebraic topology to a non-trivial geometric result.

**E. Noether.** (reported in P. Alexandrov and H. Hopf, *Topologie*, Springer, 1935.)
Noether's key contribution — presenting to Alexandrov and others the idea that the Betti numbers and torsion coefficients of a space should be organized as a single algebraic structure, an abelian group called the homology group — was made verbally at Göttingen around 1925 and is attributed to her in Alexandrov and Hopf's text. This shift from numbers to groups is what made algebraic topology algebraic in the modern sense.

**P. Alexandrov.** "Sur les ensembles de la première classe et les ensembles abstraits" and related papers, 1920s. Key reference: P. Alexandrov and P. Urysohn, "Mémoire sur les espaces topologiques compacts." *Verhandelingen der Koninklijke Akademie van Wetenschappen* 14 (1929).
Established the theory of compact spaces and continuous maps between them; the Alexandrov-Urysohn theorem on metrization and the characterization of compactness in terms of open covers (making precise what Heine-Borel had proved for intervals in $\mathbb{R}^n$). Also developed the theory of inverse limits of compact spaces.

**P. Urysohn.** "Über die Mächtigkeit der zusammenhängenden Mengen." *Mathematische Annalen* 94 (1925), 262–295.
Contains the Urysohn lemma: in a normal space, any two disjoint closed sets can be separated by a continuous function. This is the key lemma for metrization theorems and for constructing partitions of unity. Also contains the Urysohn metrization theorem (a second-countable regular Hausdorff space is metrizable).

**H. Seifert and W. Threlfall.** *Lehrbuch der Topologie.* Teubner, Leipzig, 1934. (English translation: *A Textbook of Topology*, Academic Press, 1980.)
A seminal textbook-paper in the sense that it codified the computational aspects of algebraic topology — including the Seifert-van Kampen theorem (computing $\pi_1$ of unions) and classification of surfaces — in a form that influenced all subsequent pedagogy.

## Textbooks and Modern Treatments

**J.M. Lee.** *Introduction to Topological Manifolds.* 2nd edition, Graduate Texts in Mathematics 202, Springer, 2011.
An excellent graduate text covering point-set topology through fundamental groups and covering spaces, with a focus on manifolds. The treatment of surfaces, classification of compact surfaces, and covering space theory is particularly good. Suitable as a second text after Munkres.

**G.K. Pedersen.** *Analysis Now.* Graduate Texts in Mathematics 118, Springer, 1989.
Covers topology as needed for functional analysis: metric spaces, Baire category, compactness in function spaces, Stone-Čech compactification. Useful for the connections between topology and analysis that are prerequisites for understanding the topological models of HoTT.

**N. Bourbaki.** *Topologie Générale* (General Topology). Elements of Mathematics, Springer. (Multiple volumes, originally published by Hermann, Paris, 1940–1949.)
The rigorous, comprehensive, and formidably systematic treatment of general topology in the Bourbaki style. Not for casual reading, but the definitive reference for precise statements of foundational results about filters, nets, uniform spaces, and compact spaces.

**R. Engelking.** *General Topology.* Revised and completed edition, Heldermann, Berlin, 1989.
The encyclopedic reference for general topology, covering far more than any other single source. Useful when Munkres is insufficient and the question is about a specific theorem (metrizability, paracompactness, etc.) rather than general understanding.

## Online Resources and Lecture Notes

**A. Hatcher.** *Algebraic Topology.* Free PDF at https://pi.math.cornell.edu/~hatcher/
The most widely used free reference for the transition from point-set to algebraic topology. Chapter 0 is essential for this chapter; Chapters 1–2 cover the fundamental group and homology.

**T. Tao.** *Analysis, Measure Theory, and Topology* blog posts at https://terrytao.wordpress.com/
Tao has written numerous posts explaining topological concepts with clarity and connections to analysis. Particularly useful for the intuition behind compactness, the uses of the axiom of choice in topology, and the relationship between metric and topological spaces.

**MIT OpenCourseWare: 18.901 Introduction to Topology.** https://ocw.mit.edu/
Lecture notes and problem sets for a first topology course, covering the material of Chapter 13 in a traditional sequence. Useful for additional exercises and a second perspective on the exposition.

**P. May.** *A Concise Course in Algebraic Topology.* Free PDF at https://math.uchicago.edu/~may/CONCISE/ConciseRevised.pdf
Covers algebraic topology with unusual efficiency. The first few chapters give a rapid, precise treatment of the fundamental group and covering spaces that complements the more leisurely Munkres account.

**The Stacks Project.** https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/
For the connections between topology and algebraic geometry (Chapter 13 connects to these through quotient spaces and sheaves), the Stacks Project is the definitive online reference. The chapters on sites, sheaves, and topological spaces are freely available.

## Historical Context

Topology as a discipline emerged in the late 19th century from two separate mathematical traditions: analysis and geometry. The analytic tradition came from Cauchy's rigorous formulation of continuity (1821) and the recognition that many properties of continuous functions depend not on distances but on a more abstract notion of "nearness." Cantor's development of point-set theory in the 1870s–1880s provided the language for making this abstract. The geometric tradition came from Riemann's work on surfaces (1851, 1857), Möbius's work on closed surfaces (1863), and the discovery that surfaces can be classified by their topological type — something Riemann grasped intuitively and which required the development of algebraic tools to prove.

Poincaré united these traditions in his 1895 "Analysis Situs," showing that the abstract structure of a space's "holes" could be captured by algebraic invariants — what he called Betti numbers (now the ranks of homology groups) and the fundamental group. The paper introduced the first genuinely algebraic topology: using algebra to distinguish topological spaces. Poincaré's approach was refined and formalized by the next generation: Hausdorff's axiomatic treatment of topological spaces (1914), Brouwer's fixed-point theorem and degree theory (1912), and Weyl's treatment of Riemann surfaces (1913) established point-set and algebraic topology as rigorous disciplines.

Emmy Noether's insight around 1925 — that homology invariants should be organized as abelian groups rather than just numbers — transformed algebraic topology into a branch of modern algebra and made it suitable for the generalizations that followed. Alexandrov and Urysohn's work on compactness and metrization (1920s) completed the point-set foundations. The Seifert-van Kampen theorem (1930s) gave the first systematic computational tool for the fundamental group. By 1950, the subject was mature enough to be the foundation for Serre's and Cartan's work on higher homotopy groups and spectral sequences — the tools developed in Chapter 14.
