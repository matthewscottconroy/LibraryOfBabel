# Applied Exercises

Homotopy theory is sometimes described as "topology modulo continuous deformation," but its machinery — fibrations, long exact sequences, the Hopf fibration, homotopy groups of spheres — appears concretely in robotics (orientation spaces), physics (gauge fields and monopoles), quantum computation (braiding anyons), and computer vision (equivariant neural networks). The exercises below show that the abstract tools of Chapter 14 answer concrete questions in these domains, and that the answers depend on the homotopy-theoretic structure in an essential way.

---

## Exercise C.1: Orientation Space of a Rigid Body (Robotics)
*Domain: Robotics / Kinematics*

**Setup:** A rigid body in 3-dimensional space — a robot end-effector, a spacecraft, a camera — can be oriented in any direction. The **orientation space** is the set of all possible orientations, which forms the special orthogonal group $SO(3)$: the group of $3 \times 3$ rotation matrices with determinant 1. A continuous motion of the body traces a path in $SO(3)$.

To understand the topology of $SO(3)$: identify $\mathbb{R}^3$ with the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ via $v \mapsto R_v$ (rotation by angle $|v|$ around axis $v/|v|$). Every rotation is given by a vector $v \in B^3$ (the closed 3-ball of radius $\pi$) with the identification that antipodal points on the boundary sphere $S^2 = \partial B^3$ give the same rotation (since rotating by $\pi$ around $v$ is the same as rotating by $\pi$ around $-v$). Thus $SO(3) \cong B^3 / \sim \cong \mathbb{RP}^3$ (real projective 3-space).

**Questions:**
1. Compute $\pi_1(SO(3))$. Since $SO(3) \cong \mathbb{RP}^3$, use the covering space $S^3 \to \mathbb{RP}^3$ (the universal cover of $\mathbb{RP}^3$ is $S^3$, because $S^3 \cong \mathrm{SU}(2)$ double covers $SO(3) \cong \mathbb{RP}^3$). The covering space classification says $\pi_1(\mathbb{RP}^3) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. Interpret this topologically: what does a non-contractible loop in $SO(3)$ look like as a motion of a rigid body? (Hint: consider the "Dirac belt trick" — attaching a belt from a rotating object to a fixed wall, rotating the object $4\pi$, and showing the belt can be untwisted.)
2. The homotopy group $\pi_1(SO(3)) = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ has a striking physical consequence: a rotation by $2\pi$ (going around a loop once in $SO(3)$) is *not* contractible — but a rotation by $4\pi$ (going around twice) *is* contractible. This is the source of spinors in quantum mechanics: spin-$\frac{1}{2}$ particles pick up a sign under $2\pi$ rotation (because their wavefunction is a section of a bundle over $SO(3)$ that corresponds to the nontrivial element of $\pi_1(SO(3)) = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$). Describe the connection: what is the principal bundle over $SO(3)$ whose structure group is $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ and whose total space is $S^3 \cong \mathrm{SU}(2)$?
3. For a robotic arm with a spherical wrist (three rotational degrees of freedom), the **workspace topology** is $SO(3)$. A **motion planner** must find a path from one orientation to another in $SO(3)$ avoiding joint limits (a subset $O \subset SO(3)$ of forbidden configurations). Explain why two paths in $SO(3)$ between the same orientations may be topologically inequivalent even if neither passes through $O$: this is exactly the content of $\pi_1(SO(3) \setminus O)$ being nontrivial. Give a concrete example (describe $O$ and two topologically distinct paths around it).

*Abstract concept illustrated: Covering spaces and the classification of covering spaces by subgroups of $\pi_1$; $\pi_1(SO(3)) = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$; the Hopf fibration $S^1 \hookrightarrow S^3 \to S^2$ as the associated bundle.*

## Exercise C.2: The Hopf Bundle as the Dirac Monopole (Physics)
*Domain: Mathematical Physics / Gauge Theory*

**Setup:** In classical electromagnetism, a magnetic monopole would be a point source of magnetic field (analogous to an electric charge). Dirac argued in 1931 that a magnetic monopole can be described by a vector potential $\mathbf{A}$ defined everywhere on $\mathbb{R}^3 \setminus \{0\}$ except along a "Dirac string" — a ray from the origin. The potential $\mathbf{A}$ is a connection on a $U(1)$-bundle over $S^2$ (the unit sphere surrounding the monopole).

The **Hopf fibration** $\eta : S^3 \to S^2$ is exactly this bundle: $S^3$ is the total space (the unit sphere in $\mathbb{C}^2$), $S^2 \cong \mathbb{CP}^1$ is the base, and the fibers are circles $S^1 \cong U(1)$. The **first Chern class** $c_1 \in H^2(S^2; \mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ of this bundle equals 1, which is the magnetic charge of the monopole (the "Dirac charge quantization condition").

**Questions:**
1. The first Chern class $c_1(L) \in H^2(S^2; \mathbb{Z})$ classifies complex line bundles over $S^2$. The set of isomorphism classes of complex line bundles over $S^2$ is in bijection with $\pi_1(U(1)) = \pi_1(S^1) = \mathbb{Z}$ via the clutching construction. Describe this bijection: given an integer $n \in \mathbb{Z}$, construct the corresponding line bundle $L_n$ over $S^2$ by specifying the transition function on the equatorial circle $S^1 \subset S^2$. For $n = 1$, verify that $L_1$ is the tautological line bundle $\gamma$ over $\mathbb{CP}^1 \cong S^2$ (whose total space is $\{(l, v) \mid l \in \mathbb{CP}^1, v \in l\} \subset \mathbb{CP}^1 \times \mathbb{C}^2$).
2. The long exact sequence of the Hopf fibration $S^1 \hookrightarrow S^3 \to S^2$ gives (from the calculation in Chapter 14):
$$\cdots \to \pi_2(S^1) \to \pi_2(S^3) \to \pi_2(S^2) \xrightarrow{\partial} \pi_1(S^1) \to \pi_1(S^3) \to \cdots$$
Use this to derive $\pi_2(S^2) = \mathbb{Z}$, identifying the connecting homomorphism $\partial : \pi_2(S^2) \to \pi_1(S^1) = \mathbb{Z}$ as an isomorphism. Interpret $\pi_2(S^2) = \mathbb{Z}$ physically: what does the generator of $\pi_2(S^2)$ represent as a "wrapping" of the 2-sphere around itself?
3. Dirac's quantization condition says: the magnetic charge of a monopole must be an integer multiple of a minimum charge $e_{\min} = \frac{2\pi\hbar c}{g}$. This is a topological constraint: the magnetic flux through a sphere surrounding the monopole must equal an integer (because it equals the first Chern class, which is an integer). The topological argument: a $U(1)$-bundle over $S^2$ has $c_1 \in H^2(S^2; \mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}$, and the magnetic flux is $\int_{S^2} F$ where $F$ is the curvature 2-form of the connection. Explain why $\int_{S^2} F$ must be an integer (up to a constant) by connecting it to the clutching integer $n \in \pi_1(U(1)) = \mathbb{Z}$ from Question 1. What is the homotopy-theoretic content of Dirac's quantization?

*Abstract concept illustrated: The Hopf fibration as a principal $U(1)$-bundle; $\pi_3(S^2) = \mathbb{Z}$ via the long exact sequence; classifying spaces and characteristic classes; the connection between $\pi_1(U(1))$ and the quantization of charge.*

## Exercise C.3: Topological Quantum Computation and Braid Groups
*Domain: Quantum Computing / Condensed Matter Physics*

**Setup:** Topological quantum computers encode information in **anyons** — exotic quasiparticles in 2-dimensional systems that obey neither bosonic nor fermionic statistics. When two anyons are exchanged (one moved around the other), the quantum state picks up a factor (for abelian anyons) or undergoes a unitary transformation (for non-abelian anyons). The key point: this transformation depends only on the *topology* of the worldlines — which anyon went around which — and not on the exact path. This topological robustness is what makes topological quantum computation resilient to local noise.

The algebraic structure governing anyon exchanges is the **braid group** $B_n$: the fundamental group of the configuration space of $n$ unordered, unlabeled points in the plane, $\pi_1(\mathsf{Conf}_n(\mathbb{R}^2))$. Here $\mathsf{Conf}_n(\mathbb{R}^2) = \{(z_1, \ldots, z_n) \in (\mathbb{R}^2)^n \mid z_i \neq z_j \text{ for } i \neq j\} / S_n$ is the unordered configuration space.

**Questions:**
1. The ordered configuration space of $n$ points in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is $\widetilde{\mathsf{Conf}}_n(\mathbb{R}^2) = \{(z_1, \ldots, z_n) \in (\mathbb{R}^2)^n \mid z_i \neq z_j\}$. This is the total space of the covering $\widetilde{\mathsf{Conf}}_n(\mathbb{R}^2) \to \mathsf{Conf}_n(\mathbb{R}^2)$ with deck transformation group $S_n$. The braid group $B_n = \pi_1(\mathsf{Conf}_n(\mathbb{R}^2))$ surjects onto the symmetric group $S_n = \pi_1(\mathsf{Conf}_n(\mathbb{R}^2)/S_n)$, via the map that records the permutation a braid induces. The kernel is the **pure braid group** $P_n$. For $n = 2$: describe $\mathsf{Conf}_2(\mathbb{R}^2)$ explicitly (it is homotopy equivalent to $S^1$) and conclude $B_2 = \pi_1(S^1) = \mathbb{Z}$.
2. The braid group $B_n$ has the **Artin presentation**: generators $\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_{n-1}$ (the $i$th generator swaps the $i$th and $(i+1)$th strand) with relations $\sigma_i \sigma_{i+1} \sigma_i = \sigma_{i+1} \sigma_i \sigma_{i+1}$ (braid relation) and $\sigma_i \sigma_j = \sigma_j \sigma_i$ for $|i - j| \geq 2$. For $n = 3$: compute $\sigma_1 \sigma_2 \sigma_1$ and $\sigma_2 \sigma_1 \sigma_2$ in terms of a picture of three strands. The braid relation is a topological fact: it says that "strand 1 passes over strand 2 which passes over strand 3" is isotopic to "strand 2 passes over strand 3 which passes over strand 1." Draw both braids and verify they are isotopic by an ambient isotopy of the plane.
3. In a topological quantum computer using **Fibonacci anyons**, the unitary transformation applied to the quantum state by a braid $\beta \in B_n$ is a matrix $\rho(\beta)$ in a unitary representation $\rho : B_n \to U(V)$. The key property: the image $\rho(B_n) \subset U(V)$ is **dense** (for the Fibonacci representation). This means that any unitary transformation can be approximated to any precision by a product of elementary braids — quantum universality follows from the density of the braid representation. Explain the connection to the homotopy theory of $\mathsf{Conf}_n(\mathbb{R}^2)$: why does the homotopy equivalence $\mathsf{Conf}_n(\mathbb{R}^2) \simeq K(B_n, 1)$ (the classifying space of $B_n$) imply that $B_n$ is torsion-free, and why does this mean every non-trivial braid gives a genuinely distinct quantum gate?

*Abstract concept illustrated: The fundamental group as an algebraic invariant of a configuration space; the connection between $\pi_1$ and symmetry groups of particles; covering spaces and braid groups.*

## Exercise C.4: Equivariant Neural Networks and Homotopy Invariants
*Domain: Machine Learning / Computer Vision*

**Setup:** A convolutional neural network (CNN) for image recognition has translational equivariance built in: translating the input image translates the feature maps. For 3D shapes — point clouds, molecular structures, robotic perception — we want networks equivariant not just to translation but to **rotation**, i.e., equivariant to the action of $SO(3)$. This requires understanding the representation theory of $SO(3)$ and its covering group $\mathrm{SU}(2) \cong S^3$.

The representations of $SO(3)$ are labeled by non-negative integers $l = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$ (the angular momentum quantum number), with dimension $2l+1$. The representations of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ are labeled by half-integers $j = 0, 1/2, 1, 3/2, \ldots$, with dimension $2j+1$. The representations with integer $j = l$ descend to representations of $SO(3)$; those with half-integer $j$ do not — they are **spinor representations**, and they require the double cover $\mathrm{SU}(2) \to SO(3)$.

**Questions:**
1. The covering map $\mathrm{SU}(2) \to SO(3)$ is a group homomorphism with kernel $\{\pm I\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ (where $I$ is the identity matrix). By the long exact sequence of homotopy groups of a fibration applied to $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathrm{SU}(2) \to SO(3)$, derive $\pi_1(SO(3)) = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ and $\pi_2(SO(3)) = 0$. (Use the fact that $\mathrm{SU}(2) \cong S^3$ is simply connected with $\pi_2(S^3) = 0$.)
2. An equivariant layer in a neural network for 3D data maps features transforming as the $l$-th representation of $SO(3)$ to features transforming as the $l'$-th representation, via a **Clebsch-Gordan decomposition**: $\rho_l \otimes \rho_{l'} \cong \bigoplus_{l'' = |l - l'|}^{l + l'} \rho_{l''}$. This decomposition is determined by the representation theory of $SO(3)$. Explain why the **homotopy groups** of $SO(3)$ constrain the architecture: specifically, why can a network that processes scalar features ($l = 0$) not suddenly output spinor features ($j = 1/2$) without first computing the double-covering structure — and why does this mean spinor-equivariant networks must use $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ rather than $SO(3)$ as the symmetry group?
3. The **Hopf fibration** $S^1 \hookrightarrow S^3 \to S^2$ appears in equivariant network theory as follows: the unit vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$ form the 2-sphere $S^2$, and the unit spinors form $S^3 \cong \mathrm{SU}(2)$. The map $S^3 \to S^2$ sends a spinor to the direction it points. An equivariant network that processes unit directions (elements of $S^2$) can be "lifted" to a spinor network (on $S^3$) using the Hopf map. Describe this lift: given a feature $f : S^2 \to V$ (a function on the sphere with values in a representation $V$), what additional data is needed to "lift" $f$ to a function $\tilde{f} : S^3 \to V$, and why does $\pi_1(S^2) = 0$ but $\pi_2(S^2) = \mathbb{Z}$ (from the Hopf fibration's long exact sequence) matter for the existence and uniqueness of this lift?

*Abstract concept illustrated: Covering spaces and representation theory; the long exact sequence for the covering $\mathrm{SU}(2) \to SO(3)$; the Hopf fibration and its role in the geometry of spinors.*

## Exercise C.5: Persistent Homotopy Groups in Topological Data Analysis
*Domain: Data Science / Topological Data Analysis*

**Setup:** **Persistent homology** (sketched in the Chapter 13 exercises) tracks topological features across a scale parameter $\varepsilon$. The analogous theory for homotopy groups — **persistent homotopy groups** — tracks $\pi_n$ of a filtration of simplicial complexes. While harder to compute than persistent homology, persistent $\pi_1$ (tracking the birth and death of loops as $\varepsilon$ grows) detects features that homology misses: it distinguishes spaces with the same $H_1$ but different $\pi_1$ (since $H_1 = \pi_1^{\text{ab}}$, the abelianization).

**Questions:**
1. Consider the figure-eight space $S^1 \vee S^1$ (two circles joined at a point) and the torus $T^2$ (a product of two circles). Both have $H_1 = \mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}$ (first homology is the same). But $\pi_1(S^1 \vee S^1) = \mathbb{Z} * \mathbb{Z}$ (the free group on two generators) while $\pi_1(T^2) = \mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}$ (the free abelian group on two generators). Give an explicit pair of loops in $S^1 \vee S^1$ whose concatenation is not homotopic to the reverse-order concatenation (exhibiting non-commutativity of $\pi_1$). Why can't homology $H_1$ detect this non-commutativity?
2. A data set of 50 points sampled from the figure-eight space (a curve in $\mathbb{R}^2$ shaped like a figure-8) forms a Vietoris-Rips complex $VR_\varepsilon$ for varying $\varepsilon$. At the scale $\varepsilon^*$ where the figure-eight topology appears, $\pi_1(VR_{\varepsilon^*}) = \mathbb{Z} * \mathbb{Z}$ (free group on 2 generators). As $\varepsilon$ increases, the figure-eight fills in and $\pi_1$ collapses to the trivial group. Define the **persistent fundamental group** for this filtration: describe the sequence of groups $\{\pi_1(VR_\varepsilon)\}_{\varepsilon > 0}$ and the maps between them (induced by inclusions $VR_\varepsilon \hookrightarrow VR_{\varepsilon'}$ for $\varepsilon < \varepsilon'$). What algebraic data captures the "birth" and "death" of the generators of $\pi_1$?
3. Computing persistent $\pi_1$ is computationally much harder than persistent $H_1$ (the latter reduces to persistent singular value decompositions of boundary matrices; the former involves solving the word problem in a free group, which is linear but requires careful tracking of relations). The **Hurewicz theorem** offers a shortcut: for simply connected spaces, $\pi_2 \cong H_2$. Given a data set sampled from a space $X$ with $\pi_1(X) = 0$, persistent $\pi_2$ would coincide with persistent $H_2$. For a data set known (by domain knowledge) to come from a simply connected manifold, explain how to use this theorem to detect 2-dimensional holes (voids in a protein structure, for example) using only persistent homology. What topological assumption is being made, and how would you verify it from the data?

*Abstract concept illustrated: The fundamental group $\pi_1$ and higher homotopy groups $\pi_n$; the Hurewicz theorem relating $\pi_n$ to $H_n$; the distinction between homology (abelian) and homotopy (non-abelian); filtrations and persistent invariants.*
