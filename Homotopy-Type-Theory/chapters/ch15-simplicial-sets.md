# Chapter 15: Simplicial Homotopy Theory

## Introduction

Simplicial sets are one of the central objects of modern homotopy theory and the direct mathematical model of types in HoTT. They provide a purely combinatorial way to capture homotopy-theoretic information without reference to topology: no continuity, no open sets, just combinatorial data.

The key theorem — that *Kan complexes* (a special class of simplicial sets) model homotopy types — is the mathematical foundation of Voevodsky's simplicial set model of HoTT. Understanding simplicial sets is understanding *why* HoTT works.

---

## 1. The Simplex Category

**Definition 15.1 (Simplex Category).** The *simplex category* $\Delta$ has:
- **Objects:** Finite ordinals $[n] = \{0, 1, \ldots, n\}$ for $n \geq 0$
- **Morphisms:** Order-preserving functions $f : [m] \to [n]$ (i.e., $f(i) \leq f(j)$ whenever $i \leq j$)

The key morphisms are:
- **Face maps:** $d^i : [n-1] \to [n]$ for $0 \leq i \leq n$: the injection that skips $i$
  $$d^i(j) = \begin{cases} j & j < i \\ j + 1 & j \geq i \end{cases}$$
- **Degeneracy maps:** $s^i : [n+1] \to [n]$ for $0 \leq i \leq n$: the surjection that repeats $i$
  $$s^i(j) = \begin{cases} j & j \leq i \\ j - 1 & j > i \end{cases}$$

**The simplicial identities:** Face and degeneracy maps satisfy specific relations:
$$d^j d^i = d^i d^{j-1} \quad (i < j)$$
$$s^j s^i = s^i s^{j+1} \quad (i \leq j)$$
$$s^j d^i = \begin{cases} d^i s^{j-1} & i < j \\ \mathsf{id} & i = j \text{ or } i = j+1 \\ d^{i-1} s^j & i > j+1 \end{cases}$$

Every morphism in $\Delta$ factors uniquely as a surjection (composed of degeneracies) followed by an injection (composed of faces).

## 2. Simplicial Sets

**Definition 15.2 (Simplicial Set).** A *simplicial set* is a functor $X : \Delta^{op} \to \mathbf{Set}$.

Unpacking: a simplicial set consists of:
- A set $X_n = X([n])$ of *$n$-simplices* for each $n \geq 0$
- *Face maps*: $\partial_i = X(d^i) : X_n \to X_{n-1}$ for $0 \leq i \leq n$
- *Degeneracy maps*: $\sigma_i = X(s^i) : X_n \to X_{n+1}$ for $0 \leq i \leq n$

satisfying the dual simplicial identities.

**Intuition:**
- $X_0$: the *vertices* (0-simplices, or "points")
- $X_1$: the *edges* (1-simplices, with two endpoints $\partial_0, \partial_1 : X_1 \to X_0$)
- $X_2$: the *triangles* (2-simplices, with three edge faces)
- $X_n$: the *$n$-dimensional simplices*
- Degeneracy maps: the *degenerate simplices* — "thin" simplices that come from lower-dimensional ones

**Morphisms:** A morphism $f : X \to Y$ of simplicial sets is a natural transformation — a collection of functions $f_n : X_n \to Y_n$ commuting with all face and degeneracy maps.

---

## 3. The Standard Simplex and Geometric Realization

**Definition 15.3 (Standard Simplex).** The *standard $n$-simplex* is the representable simplicial set:
$$\Delta[n] = \mathsf{Hom}_\Delta(-, [n]) : \Delta^{op} \to \mathbf{Set}$$

By the Yoneda lemma: $\mathsf{Hom}_{\mathbf{sSet}}(\Delta[n], X) \cong X_n$ (naturally in $X$). So $\Delta[n]$ is the "classifier" for $n$-simplices.

**Definition 15.4 (Boundary).** The *boundary* $\partial\Delta[n]$ is the simplicial subset of $\Delta[n]$ consisting of all *non-surjective* maps $[k] \to [n]$ — i.e., all faces of $\Delta[n]$.

**Definition 15.5 (Horn).** The *horn* $\Lambda^n_k$ (for $0 \leq k \leq n$) is the simplicial subset of $\Delta[n]$ consisting of all maps $[m] \to [n]$ whose image omits at most $k$. Concretely: $\Lambda^n_k$ is $\partial\Delta[n]$ with the $k$-th face removed.

**Geometric Realization:** The *geometric realization* $|X|$ of a simplicial set $X$ is the topological space:
$$|X| = \bigsqcup_{n \geq 0} X_n \times |\Delta^n| / \sim$$
where $|\Delta^n| = \{(t_0, \ldots, t_n) \in \mathbb{R}^{n+1} \mid \sum t_i = 1, t_i \geq 0\}$ is the standard geometric $n$-simplex, and $\sim$ is generated by the face and degeneracy relations.

**Theorem 15.6.** $|{\Delta[n]}| \cong |\Delta^n|$, $|\partial\Delta[n]| \cong S^{n-1}$, $|\Lambda^n_k| \cong$ a contractible subspace of $|\Delta^n|$.

**The singular complex:** For a topological space $X$, the *singular simplicial set* $\mathsf{Sing}(X)$ has:
$$\mathsf{Sing}(X)_n = \mathsf{Hom}_{\mathbf{Top}}(|\Delta^n|, X) = C(|\Delta^n|, X)$$
(continuous maps from the geometric $n$-simplex to $X$).

**Theorem 15.7 (Adjunction).** $|-| \dashv \mathsf{Sing}$ is an adjunction:
$$\mathsf{Hom}_{\mathbf{Top}}(|X|, Y) \cong \mathsf{Hom}_{\mathbf{sSet}}(X, \mathsf{Sing}(Y))$$

## 4. Kan Complexes: The Correct Notion of ∞-Groupoid

### 4.1 The Horn-Filling Condition

**Definition 15.8 (Kan Complex).** A simplicial set $X$ is a *Kan complex* if for every $n \geq 1$ and $0 \leq k \leq n$, every horn inclusion $\Lambda^n_k \hookrightarrow X$ (i.e., every map $\Lambda^n_k \to X$) extends to a map $\Delta[n] \to X$:

$$\Lambda^n_k \hookrightarrow \Delta[n]$$
$$\downarrow \qquad \exists \searrow$$
$$X$$

In other words: every horn can be filled.

**Intuition:** A horn $\Lambda^n_k$ is an $n$-simplex with one face missing. Filling the horn means: given all but one face of an $n$-simplex, there exists a filling (a full $n$-simplex).

**Why this gives an ∞-groupoid:** 
- Filling $\Lambda^2_1$ (the "inner horn" of a triangle): given two composable edges, there is a composite.
- Filling $\Lambda^2_0$ or $\Lambda^2_2$ (outer horns): inverses exist.
- Filling higher-dimensional horns: all coherence conditions hold up to higher homotopy.

**Theorem 15.9.** $\mathsf{Sing}(X)$ is a Kan complex for any topological space $X$.

*Proof.* Horns $|\Lambda^n_k| \hookrightarrow |\Delta^n|$ are homotopy equivalences (they are inclusions of a deformation retract). By the homotopy extension property, any map $|\Lambda^n_k| \to X$ extends to $|\Delta^n| \to X$. $\square$

### 4.2 Homotopy Groups of Kan Complexes

**Definition 15.10.** For a Kan complex $X$ with basepoint $x_0 \in X_0$:
- $\pi_0(X)$: connected components (equivalence classes of vertices under paths)
- $\pi_1(X, x_0)$: homotopy classes of 1-simplices from $x_0$ to $x_0$ (loops), with composition given by horn filling
- $\pi_n(X, x_0)$: the $n$-th homotopy group, defined similarly using $n$-dimensional horns

**Theorem 15.11.** For a topological space $X$ and the Kan complex $\mathsf{Sing}(X)$:
$$\pi_n(\mathsf{Sing}(X), x_0) \cong \pi_n(X, x_0)$$

The homotopy groups of the singular complex agree with the classical homotopy groups of the space.

## 5. Weak Homotopy Equivalences

**Definition 15.12.** A map $f : X \to Y$ of simplicial sets is a *weak homotopy equivalence* if the geometric realization $|f| : |X| \to |Y|$ is a weak homotopy equivalence of topological spaces (induces isomorphisms on all homotopy groups).

Equivalently, $f$ is a weak homotopy equivalence iff $f_* : \pi_n(X, x) \to \pi_n(Y, f(x))$ is an isomorphism for all $n$ and all $x \in X_0$.

**The Quillen equivalence:**

**Theorem 15.13 (Quillen, 1967).** The adjunction $|-| \dashv \mathsf{Sing}$ is a *Quillen equivalence* between:
- Simplicial sets with the Kan-Quillen model structure (weak equivalences = weak homotopy equivalences, fibrations = Kan fibrations)
- Topological spaces (in the appropriate model structure)

This means: for the purposes of homotopy theory, simplicial sets and topological spaces carry the same information. A homotopy type can equally well be represented as a Kan complex or as a topological space (up to weak homotopy equivalence).

## 6. The Quillen Model Structure on Simplicial Sets

A *model structure* on a category is a specification of three classes of maps — *weak equivalences*, *fibrations*, and *cofibrations* — satisfying the model category axioms. It provides a framework for doing homotopy theory internally to any category.

**Theorem 15.14 (Quillen 1967).** The category of simplicial sets has a model structure where:
- **Weak equivalences:** Maps inducing isomorphisms on all homotopy groups (via realization)
- **Fibrations (Kan fibrations):** Maps $p : X \to Y$ with the right lifting property against all horn inclusions $\Lambda^n_k \hookrightarrow \Delta[n]$
- **Cofibrations:** Monomorphisms (injective on all simplices)

Fibrant objects (objects where $X \to *$ is a fibration) are exactly the Kan complexes.

**Every object is cofibrant** (since maps from $\emptyset$ are always injective).

The model structure is a precise formalization of "doing homotopy theory combinatorially."

---

## 7. The Simplicial Set Model of HoTT

**Theorem 15.15 (Voevodsky, 2009-2012).** The category of Kan simplicial sets, with the fibration structure from the Quillen model structure, provides a model of:
1. Martin-Löf Dependent Type Theory (MLTT)
2. The univalence axiom
3. Higher inductive types

Moreover, univalence is a *theorem* in this model, not an additional assumption.

**What this means:**
- Types correspond to Kan complexes (∞-groupoids)
- Dependent types correspond to Kan fibrations $E \to B$
- Terms are sections of fibrations
- The identity type $a =_A b$ corresponds to the path space $\{p : [0,1] \to |A| \mid p(0) = a, p(1) = b\}$ (realized topologically)
- The univalence axiom corresponds to the fact that equivalences of Kan complexes are the same as paths in the "universal Kan complex" (the universe of types)

## 8. From Simplicial Sets to Cubical Sets

The cubical approach (used in cubical type theory, Chapter 23) replaces simplices with *cubes*.

**Definition 15.16 (Cubical Set).** A *cubical set* is a functor $X : \Box^{op} \to \mathbf{Set}$, where $\Box$ is the *cube category* (whose objects are $\{0,1\}^n$ for $n \geq 0$ and whose morphisms are generated by face and degeneracy maps between cubes).

Cubical sets have a more computational flavor: the cube $\{0,1\}^n$ models the $n$-dimensional type-theoretic interval $\mathbb{I}^n$.

**The advantage of cubes for type theory:** The interval type $\mathbb{I}$ in cubical type theory has two points $0$ and $1$, and a path from $0$ to $1$ is literally an element of $\mathbb{I}$. This matches the intuition directly, without the indirection of simplicial topology.

**Theorem 15.17 (Coquand et al., Cohen et al.).** Cubical sets (with the appropriate model structure, over the De Morgan cube category) provide a model of cubical type theory, in which:
- Univalence is a theorem (proved via the Glue type)
- Higher inductive types are constructively defined
- Computation rules hold definitionally (not just propositionally)
