# Domain Survey: Ontology

## Overview

Ontology is the philosophical investigation of being as such — the inquiry into what exists, what kinds of things exist, and what it means to exist at all. It stands as the most fundamental branch of metaphysics, providing the conceptual scaffolding within which every other philosophical and scientific inquiry is conducted. Where physics asks about the structure of the physical world, and biology about living systems, ontology asks the prior question: what is it for anything to be part of the world? Aristotle described it as the study of "being qua being" — being considered purely as such, stripped of every particular scientific framing. The word "ontology" entered philosophical usage in the seventeenth century (Goclenius 1613; Wolff systematized it), but the inquiry is ancient, tracing at minimum to Parmenides' claim that only being exists and non-being is unintelligible.

The central questions of ontology cluster into three interconnected groups. The **existence question** asks whether entities of a given kind — numbers, properties, possible worlds, moral facts, fictional characters — exist at all. The **category question** asks, for entities that do exist, what fundamental type of thing they are: substances, properties, relations, events, facts, tropes, sets. The **dependence question** asks which entities are fundamental and which are grounded in, constituted by, or otherwise dependent on others. These questions are not sequential — they advance together. Whether properties exist may depend on whether one can reduce property-talk to talk about sets; whether sets exist may depend on whether abstract objects in general are countenanced; and the dependence structure one posits may determine which entities are ultimately "real."

Two broad methodological orientations have dominated analytic ontology since mid-twentieth century. The **Quinean tradition** takes as its central task the identification of the ontological commitments of our best theories, deploying the criterion that what we are committed to is what must fall within the domain of our existential quantifiers when those theories are properly regimented in first-order predicate logic. The **neo-Aristotelian tradition**, associated especially with Kit Fine, Jonathan Schaffer, and E. J. Lowe, holds that the Quinean picture is too thin: being has structure, and the most important ontological question is not which entities there are but which entities are fundamental — which entities ground others in the hierarchy of being. These two traditions are not incompatible, but they assign different priorities to different questions.

The domain of ontology encompasses four major sub-topics treated here: Being and Existence, Substance, Properties and Relations, and Abstract Objects. Each sub-topic carries its own debates, terminological disputes, and logical puzzles, but they intersect at every turn. The theory of substance is partially constituted by one's view of properties; the status of abstract objects bears on the plausibility of realist versus nominalist ontologies; one's view of ontological commitment shapes which entities one is required to acknowledge. Understanding the domain requires tracking these intersections as well as the internal debates.

## Major Positions and Their Logical Relations

### The Existence-Structure Axis

**Ontological realism** holds that there are mind-independent features of reality — entities, kinds, properties, or facts — whose existence does not depend on human thought, language, or practice. Realism divides along multiple axes:

- **Platonism**: abstract objects (numbers, properties, propositions) exist in a mind-independent domain separate from the spatiotemporal world.
- **Aristotelian immanent realism**: universals exist, but only *in* their instances; there is no separated abstract realm.
- **Trope realism**: the fundamental property-like entities are particular property-instances (tropes) rather than universals.
- **Structural realism**: what is real is relational structure rather than individual objects.

**Ontological anti-realism** encompasses positions that deny robust mind-independent reality to some or all putative entities:

- **Nominalism**: only particular concrete objects exist; universals, numbers, and other abstract entities either do not exist or can be reduced to arrangements of particulars.
  - *Predicate nominalism*: the predicate "is red" applies to red things, but there is no entity redness.
  - *Resemblance nominalism*: red things form a class because they resemble one another; no universal is needed.
  - *Class nominalism*: the property redness is identified with the class of red things.
  - *Mereological nominalism* (Goodman): the extension of a predicate is a mereological sum of its instances.
- **Fictionalism**: certain kinds of talk (mathematical, moral, modal) are systematically useful fictions; the entities apparently referred to do not literally exist.
- **Conventionalism** / **Deflationism** (Carnap, Hirsch): ontological disputes are disputes about which conceptual scheme to adopt, not about how the world is independently.

### The Dependence Axis

Even granting an inventory of what exists, further questions arise about the structure of that inventory:

- **Foundationalism about being**: there are fundamental entities that are not grounded in anything else; derivative entities exist in virtue of the fundamental ones. (Fine, Schaffer)
- **Coherentism about being** (rare): no entities are absolutely fundamental; all exist in webs of mutual dependence.
- **Quinean flat ontology**: existence is univocal; all entities that exist do so in the same sense; hierarchy is a confusion.

The **grounding** relation (Fine 2001, 2012) is now the dominant tool for articulating ontological dependence. *A grounds B* means roughly that B obtains in virtue of A, where this is a real explanatory relation (not logical entailment, not causal dependence, not mere supervenience). Grounding is meant to be irreflexive, asymmetric, and transitive — a strict partial order on facts or entities.

### Substance Theory

**Substance realism** holds that there are objects — substances — that persist through change, bear properties, and are ontologically more basic than the properties they bear. Substance theory divides:

- **Substratum theory** (bare particular theory): a substance = a bare particular (thin object) + its properties; the bare particular individuates the object and supports its properties.
- **Bundle theory**: a substance = a bundle of properties (tropes or universals); there is no underlying substratum.
- **Four-category ontology** (Lowe): substances, kinds, attributes, and modes are all irreducible; the ontological square has substances instantiating kinds, attributes characterizing kinds, and modes instantiating attributes.

**Substance eliminativism**: some philosophers argue there are no substances — only events (Whitehead), only processes (Rescher), only field-theoretic structures (French), or only properties instantiated at spacetime points.

## Key Arguments and Counterarguments

### The Indispensability Argument (Quine-Putnam)

*For the existence of mathematical abstracta:*

P1. We ought to be ontologically committed to all and only entities that are indispensable to our best scientific theories.
P2. Mathematical entities are indispensable to our best scientific theories.
C. We ought to be ontologically committed to mathematical entities.

**Objections**: (i) Field (1980) argues mathematics is dispensable — physics can be nominalized by replacing quantification over numbers with quantification over spacetime regions. (ii) Maddy argues scientific practice does not treat all posited entities as equally real; scientists apply continuity assumptions they know are false. (iii) The indispensability argument proves too much: if it works for mathematics, does it work for frictionless planes and ideal gases?

### The One Over Many Argument (for Universals)

P1. Multiple distinct particulars can be genuinely (not merely apparently) the same way — genuinely red, genuinely triangular.
P2. If multiple distinct particulars are genuinely the same way, there is a universal that they all instantiate.
C. There are universals.

**Nominalist responses**: (i) *Predicate nominalism*: the "sameness" is just the application of the same predicate to different objects; no further entity needed. (ii) *Resemblance nominalism*: objects do not share a universal; they resemble one another, and resemblance relations among particulars is all we need. *Realist response to resemblance nominalism*: resemblance itself seems to be a universal shared by pairs of resembling things — the objection iterates.

### The Ontological Commitment Argument (Quine)

*Method*: Determine a theory's ontological commitments by examining what must fall within the domain of its bound variables when regimented in first-order logic. "To be is to be the value of a variable."

**Objections**: (i) *The logical pluralism objection* (Boolos): plural quantification — "the Cheerios are such that no one of them fills the bowl" — does not commit to sets; it refers plurally to many particulars. The canonical language is not uniquely first-order. (ii) *The deflationary objection* (Carnap, Hirsch): the choice of logical framework is not itself a factual matter but a pragmatic one; Quine conflates the selection of a framework with theoretical discovery within it. (iii) *The grounding objection* (Fine): existence is not univocal; to ask whether shadows exist is to ask a different, shallower question than to ask whether physical objects exist. Quine's criterion ignores the distinction between fundamental and derivative being.

### The Grounding Argument (for Ontological Hierarchy)

P1. Some facts hold *in virtue of* other facts in a non-causal, explanatory sense.
P2. This "in-virtue-of" relation (grounding) is real, not reducible to causation or supervenience.
P3. The grounding relation generates an ordering of facts from more fundamental to less fundamental.
C. Reality has an ontological hierarchy with fundamental facts at the bottom.

**Objections**: (i) *The conceptualism objection* (Wilson): grounding claims are true only relative to a description or conceptual scheme. (ii) *The parsimony objection* (Sider): positing grounding as a primitive relation alongside the entities themselves inflates ontology without clear benefit. (iii) *Infinite regress*: if every fact is grounded in a further fact, there is no bottom; but an infinite regress of grounds seems either vicious or unexplanatory.

### The Bundle Theory vs. Substratum Theory

**For the bundle theory**: The substratum is an unintelligible "bare particular" — it bears no properties of its own, yet it somehow supports the properties we attribute to an object. *Locke's "I know not what."*

**Against the bundle theory**: The Identity of Indiscernibles (Leibniz's Law's converse) — that qualitatively identical bundles are numerically identical — seems contingently false: there could be two exactly similar spheres (Max Black's spheres argument), and if there could, then bundles cannot individuate.

**Substratum response**: Bare particulars individuate numerically distinct but qualitatively identical objects. **Bundle theory response** (Hawley, French): quantum mechanics suggests that the Identity of Indiscernibles may be violated at the particle level, and bundle theorists can accept numerical distinctness grounded in relational properties rather than bare particulars.

## Key Papers and Books

1. **Quine, W. V. O., "On What There Is" (1948)** — The founding document of analytic ontology. Introduces the criterion of ontological commitment and inaugurates the modern debate about how to adjudicate existence claims.

2. **Armstrong, D. M., *Universals and Scientific Realism* (1978)** — The definitive defense of Aristotelian immanent realism about universals; develops the theory of states of affairs as the basic ontological units.

3. **Lewis, David, *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986)** — Defends modal realism; contains sustained argument for ontological commitment via semantic indispensability and discussion of the supervenience of mental and modal properties on the physical.

4. **Fine, Kit, "The Question of Ontology" (2009)** — Argues that the Quinean framework misidentifies the central questions; the real question is not what exists but what is real in the sense of being part of the fundamental furniture of the world.

5. **Schaffer, Jonathan, "On What Grounds What" (2009)** — Develops the neo-Aristotelian framework of grounding and priority; argues that the priority monist view (the cosmos is the one fundamental entity) is the most defensible position.

6. **Lowe, E. J., *The Four-Category Ontology* (2006)** — Develops a systematic neo-Aristotelian ontology with four irreducible categories (substances, kinds, attributes, modes) and argues for the ontological square as the correct framework.

7. **Carnap, Rudolf, "Empiricism, Semantics, and Ontology" (1950)** — Defends deflationism about ontological disputes via the internal/external question distinction; the most influential anti-realist challenge to Quinean ontology.

8. **Sider, Theodore, *Writing the Book of the World* (2011)** — Defends ontological realism via the notion of "structure" — reality has a joint-carving structure that determines what the correct fundamental description of the world is.

## Live Debates and Open Questions

1. **Ontological pluralism vs. univocity of being**: Is existence a single univocal concept, or do different kinds of things exist in different ways (Meinong's distinction between Sein and Sosein; Fine's distinction between real and derivative being)?

2. **The metaontological debate**: Is the deflationist (Carnap, Hirsch, Thomasson) or the heavyweight realist (Lewis, Sider, Schaffer) correct? Can ontological disputes be non-trivially resolved, or are they always at least partially verbal?

3. **Grounding: its nature, logic, and ideology**: Is grounding a genuine primitive relation? What is its logic — is it always irreflexive? Can it be partial? What distinguishes it from causation, supervenience, and reduction?

4. **The proliferation problem**: Mainstream ontology now posits abundant entities — arbitrary mereological sums, possible worlds, sets of all sets-like things, tropes. Does this represent theoretical progress or philosophical inflation?

5. **The relationship between ontology and fundamental physics**: Should the fundamental ontology of our best physical theories — field theories, quantum mechanics with its non-individual particles, general relativity's curved spacetime — determine our ontological commitments? This threatens to make ontology an extension of physics rather than a prior inquiry.

6. **Abstract objects and causation**: If abstract objects are non-spatiotemporal and causally inert, how can we have knowledge of them? (Benacerraf's challenge.) Does this provide a decisive objection to Platonism, or are there epistemologies that do not require causal contact?

## Connections to Other Domains

**Ontology and Universals/Particulars (Domain 02)**: The debate between realism and nominalism is both a topic within ontology and the organizing subject of Domain 02. The positions on universals — Platonism, Aristotelianism, tropism, nominalism — directly implement alternative ontological frameworks. One cannot adjudicate the universal/particular debate without first settling at least provisionally which ontological tools (quantification, grounding, class membership) one will use.

**Ontology and Modality (Domain 05)**: Modal realism (Lewis) is a position in ontology — it claims that possible worlds are real concrete entities. Actualist alternatives (Adams, Plantinga, Stalnaker) are equally ontological positions about the status of possible worlds as abstract objects, sets of propositions, or maximal consistent properties. The Quine-Lewis indispensability framework directly applies.

**Ontology and Metametaphysics (Domain 09)**: Metametaphysics is ontology's epistemology. It asks: are ontological questions genuine, answerable, and substantive, or are they merely verbal disputes about linguistic frameworks? The positions of Carnap, Sider, Hirsch, and Fine are explicitly metaontological, and the internal/external question distinction structures the whole debate.

**Ontology and Metaphysics of Science (Domain 10)**: Scientific realism is a thesis about the ontological status of theoretical entities posited by science; structural realism is an attempt to preserve ontological commitment to scientific theories while retreating from commitment to individual objects. The indispensability argument directly connects mathematical and scientific ontology.

**Ontology and Truth/Reality (Domain 12)**: Truthmaker theory — the view that every truth is made true by some entity — is an ontological thesis about the relation between language and reality. Correspondence, deflationary, and coherence theories of truth each carry different ontological presuppositions about the existence of facts, states of affairs, or truth-making entities.
