# Ontology

There is a question so basic that it tends to disappear behind every other inquiry, absorbed silently into the background of whatever we happen to be investigating. Physics asks how matter behaves. Economics asks how markets function. History asks what happened and why. But none of these disciplines pauses to ask — indeed, none of them can ask, from within their own resources — what it means for anything to be there at all. What is it for something to exist? What makes something a thing rather than nothing? What categories carve nature at its joints, and are those categories discovered or imposed? These are not scientific questions, not because they are beyond investigation, but because they are prior to it. They constitute the inquiry we call ontology.

Ontology is the branch of philosophy that asks what there is, and what it is to be. Its scope is maximal: it concerns not physics or biology specifically but the most general features of reality — the categories under which anything whatsoever falls, the conditions under which a thing can be said to exist, the relations of dependence and priority that structure the furniture of the world. Aristotle, who gave ontology its first systematic expression, called it the science of "being qua being" — being considered as such, not as physical or mathematical or biological, but simply as *being*. Two and a half millennia later, the discipline he founded remains philosophically indispensable, and in some respects more contested than ever.

This unit develops the four foundational areas of ontological inquiry. We begin with the most general questions: *what is ontology itself*, and what kind of inquiry is it? This is not a merely scholastic matter. How we understand the discipline shapes what counts as evidence, which methods are appropriate, and which disputes are substantive rather than merely verbal. We examine Aristotle's foundational questions, the Quinean framework of ontological commitment that dominates contemporary analytic philosophy, and the challenge posed by deflationists who question whether canonical ontological debates are genuinely substantive.

From these foundations we move to the theory of **substance** — the attempt to identify the primary bearers of reality, the things that exist in their own right rather than as features or aspects of something else. Substance has been the central category of ontology from Aristotle to the present. We trace its history from the ancient conception of *ousia* through the rationalist systems of Descartes, Spinoza, and Leibniz, through the empiricist critiques of Locke and Hume, and into the contemporary debates between bundle theorists, substratum theorists, and neo-Aristotelian revivalists. The theory of substance is not a dry historical matter: it determines how we think about persons, natural kinds, God, and the persistence of things through change.

The third domain is **properties and relations**. If substance theory asks what the basic individual things are, the theory of properties asks what makes those things the way they are. When two roses are both red, is there a single universal — redness — that both share? Or are their red-making features numerically distinct property-instances, each belonging only to its own rose? And are relations genuine additions to the ontological inventory, or are they reducible to non-relational features of their terms? These questions connect directly to debates about natural laws, causation, and the structure of science.

Finally, we examine **abstract objects** — entities like numbers, sets, propositions, and universals that, if they exist, do so outside space and time, without causal powers, untouchable and unchanging. The existence of abstract objects is one of the most contested questions in contemporary metaphysics. Platonists insist that mathematics could not be true unless numbers existed; nominalists insist that positing causally inert objects we can never perceive violates every sound epistemological principle. The debate is not a curiosity; its resolution shapes the philosophy of mathematics, of language, and of mind.

To read this unit is to begin a reckoning with the deepest questions about what is real. The answers are not easy, and the reader should expect genuine difficulty. But the questions themselves are among the most important we can ask — because every discipline, every inquiry, and every act of understanding presupposes some answer to them, whether acknowledged or not.
