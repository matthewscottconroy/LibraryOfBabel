# Being and Existence

Before ontology can investigate what kinds of things exist, it must first clarify what it means to exist at all. This prior question — the question of being itself — has driven philosophical inquiry since the Presocratics, and its difficulty has not diminished with time. Existence seems obvious until we try to say what it is. We know, for example, that dogs exist and that unicorns do not. But what does the word "exist" contribute to these claims? Does it describe a property that dogs have and unicorns lack? Is it the same property in both cases? And what are we asking, exactly, when we ask whether numbers, or moral properties, or fictional characters "really" exist?

This chapter opens with these foundational questions and develops the conceptual apparatus that the rest of the textbook presupposes. We examine the scope and method of ontological inquiry, asking what distinguishes genuinely ontological questions from questions that are merely empirical or merely verbal. We develop the diagnostic Aristotle first deployed — the study of being qua being — and trace the development of the discipline from its ancient origins to the contemporary debates dominated by Quine's criterion of ontological commitment.

The **fundamental question** — why is there something rather than nothing? — occupies the second section of this chapter. This question may be the most radical that philosophy poses, and it resists easy dismissal. Leibniz posed it with maximum force and proposed an answer grounded in the principle of sufficient reason: there must be a sufficient reason for everything, including the existence of the world rather than nothing; that reason must itself be necessary; therefore, there is a necessary being. Contemporary responses to this question range from accepting the contingency of existence without demanding an explanation, to exploring whether the question itself is well-formed, to proposing that the answer lies in the logic of being itself. However one responds, reckoning seriously with the question transforms how one thinks about existence.

**Ontological commitment** rounds out this chapter by examining the question: what does it mean for a theory or a discourse to be committed to the existence of certain entities? Quine's famous criterion — a theory is committed to all and only those entities that must be in the range of its bound variables for the theory's claims to be true — provides a precise framework for adjudicating ontological disputes. To ask whether there are numbers, on this view, is to ask whether mathematical discourse, taken at face value, quantifies over entities that are in some domain independent of us. This criterion has been enormously influential but has also been subjected to important challenges: What if there are legitimate ways of paraphrasing away the apparent quantification? What if quantifier variance is real and there is no single privileged sense of "exists"?

These three sections together prepare the reader for every subsequent chapter of this textbook. The issues they raise — about existence, explanation, and commitment — recur throughout ontology, philosophy of mind, philosophy of science, and metaethics. Understanding them is not merely preliminary; it is the beginning of seeing how deep the questions go.
