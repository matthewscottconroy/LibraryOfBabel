# What Is Ontology?

Ontology has a founding question and a founding act of discipline. The founding question is Aristotle's: what is being qua being — what is being considered purely as such, stripped of every particular scientific framing? The founding act is the decision to treat this question as susceptible to rational investigation, as having an answer that can be arrived at by argument and analysis rather than by faith or intuition alone.

This section examines that founding question and the methodology appropriate to answering it. Three subsections develop the inquiry progressively. The first — on scope and method — asks what kind of questions ontology asks, what methods are appropriate for answering them, and how ontological inquiry relates to both the natural sciences and the other branches of philosophy. The second — on the fundamental question — confronts the most radical ontological question of all: why is there something rather than nothing? This question is easy to state and enormously difficult to answer; working through the serious responses to it illuminates what ontology can and cannot deliver. The third — on ontological commitment — examines the Quinean framework that has structured most analytic ontology for the last half-century: what does it mean for a theory to be committed to the existence of certain entities, and how do we determine what our best theories commit us to?

These three topics constitute the foundation of ontological inquiry. They do not answer the ontological questions that subsequent chapters address — about substance, properties, abstract objects, and the rest — but they clarify what those questions are and what would count as progress in answering them. A reader who has worked through this section will have not just preliminary knowledge but a genuine philosophical orientation: an understanding of what ontology is attempting and why the attempt matters.
