# Defining Ontology

Ask yourself what a science looks like. Physics investigates moving bodies; biology investigates living organisms; economics investigates markets and exchange. Each takes a certain domain for granted and then studies the features of things within that domain. But there is a prior question, one that no special science can settle, because it underlies them all: what does it take for there to be things in the first place? What does it mean to exist? This is the question that generates ontology — the inquiry into being, into what there is and what being something consists in.

Aristotle called it the study of "being qua being" — being considered purely as such, stripped of any particular scientific framing. The word "ontology" itself came later, coined in the seventeenth century and systematized by Christian Wolff, but the inquiry is ancient. Its opening move is always the same: step back from every particular domain and ask what it means for anything at all to fall within a domain, to be real, to count as something rather than nothing.

The scope of this inquiry is, accordingly, very wide. It encompasses debates about God, numbers, universals, causal powers, possible worlds, persons, moral properties, and fictional characters. That breadth can feel daunting, but ordinary life raises ontological questions constantly. Is a corporation a genuine entity distinct from its members? Is a melody distinct from its performances? Is the nation something over and above the people who comprise it? In each case we are asking whether there is really something there — and ontology provides the conceptual framework for pursuing such questions rigorously.

## The Central Questions

Ontological inquiry clusters around three interconnected questions that we can state simply even if they resist simple answers. First is the *existence question*: do entities of a given kind exist at all? Do numbers exist? Do properties exist? Do possible worlds exist? Second is the *category question*: if entities of a kind exist, what basic type of thing are they — objects, properties, relations, events, facts? Third is the *dependence question*: which entities are fundamental, and which depend on or are constituted by others?

These questions are interconnected in ways that matter for how we pursue them. The existence question often requires settling the category question first: if we discover that numbers, if they exist, would have to be abstract objects, and if we are skeptical about abstract objects, we may conclude that numbers do not exist. The dependence question feeds back into the existence question in the other direction: a nominalist who holds that only concrete particulars are fundamental may argue that apparent reference to properties can always be reduced to reference to those particulars. The questions advance together or not at all.

Aristotle's founding insight, articulated in the *Metaphysics*, was that all the different ways things can be — a substance is, a quality is, a relation is, a place is — relate back to a single central case: substance. Qualities, relations, and actions exist only as features of substances; they are ontologically dependent on substances in a way that substances are not dependent on them. This doctrine of "focal meaning" or *pros hen* predication structures Aristotle's entire ontology and sets a precedent for the discipline: the task is always to locate what is primary.

## The Diagnostic for Ontological Questions

We can put this more sharply. Consider the difference between asking whether there are electrons and asking whether there is such a thing as the average family. The first is a scientific question we answer by doing physics. The second is more complicated: we know perfectly well there is no family consisting of 2.4 children, yet we do not simply dismiss statistical averages as fictional. What distinguishes these cases? An ontological question, we can say, is one that asks whether entities of type F exist, or what their fundamental nature or category is, where this cannot be settled by purely empirical investigation alone — since it concerns the general framework within which empirical investigation operates — and where it is not merely verbal, not simply a question about how we use the word "exists."

That is the diagnostic:

P1. A question Q asks whether there are entities of type F, or asks what the fundamental nature or category of F-entities is.
P2. Q cannot be settled by purely empirical investigation alone.
P3. Q is not merely verbal.
C. Q is a philosophical question about the basic furniture of reality.

Notice that this does not make ontological questions unanswerable. It means they require different methods: philosophical argumentation, conceptual analysis, inference to the best explanation, and attention to the logical structure of our theories. The discipline is difficult not because the questions are meaningless but because they are genuinely foundational.

## An Influential Objection

One challenge presses itself immediately. Eli Hirsch, developing a thought that echoes Rudolf Carnap's earlier distinction between *internal* and *external* questions, argues that many apparently deep ontological disputes are merely verbal — disputes about how to use words rather than about how the world is. If two philosophers disagree about whether mereological sums exist — whether this hand and this glove compose a further entity, the hand-glove aggregate — are they disagreeing about reality or just about which conventions to adopt for the word "exists"? Perhaps they agree on all the physical facts and differ only in their choice of vocabulary.

This objection is serious. But notice that the deflationist cannot easily account for cases where the choice of ontological framework has genuine theoretical consequences. If accepting the existence of properties gives us a better explanation of natural resemblances and the laws of nature — an explanation unavailable to the nominalist — then the disagreement is not merely verbal. The explanatory stakes distinguish genuine ontological disputes from conventions about usage. Where there are such stakes, Carnap's dissolution does not apply.

Ontology intersects with nearly every area of philosophy for precisely this reason. In philosophy of mind, the ontological status of mental states — whether identical to brain states, functionally defined, or irreducible — determines what kind of causal explanation psychology can offer. In ethics, whether moral properties exist as features of the world or are mere projections of human attitudes is an ontological question with immediate normative implications. In philosophy of science, whether theoretical entities genuinely exist or are merely predictive fictions shapes how we interpret scientific theories. What ontology is, then, is itself a substantive matter — and understanding it requires knowing what kind of inquiry is being undertaken, what methods are appropriate, and how the questions connect to adjacent debates.
