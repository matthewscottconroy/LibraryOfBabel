# Ontology and Metaphysics

The terms "ontology" and "metaphysics" are used almost interchangeably in contemporary philosophy, and the conflation is understandable: both ask the most basic questions we can ask about reality. But a distinction is worth marking. Metaphysics is the broader inquiry — it asks not only what exists but what causation, modality, time, identity, and persistence consist in, and what the overall structure of reality looks like. Ontology, on this division, is the core subdiscipline specifically devoted to existence: what things there are, in what basic categories those things fall, and in what modes they exist. The relationship is something like that between general biology and cell biology — the latter is genuinely part of the former, but with its own focus.

The word "metaphysics" has a complicated etymology. It derives from the title given to a collection of Aristotle's writings compiled by Andronicus of Rhodes in the first century BCE — the books that came *after* (*meta*) the *Physics* in the editorial arrangement. What began as an accident of filing became the name for the inquiry itself: the study of the first principles of being, causation, and knowledge that underlie natural science. Within the scholastic tradition, Francisco Suárez's *Disputationes Metaphysicae* (1597) distinguishes ontology — the general science of being as being — from special metaphysics, which investigates God, the soul, and the world as a whole. Christian Wolff codified this division in his *Philosophia Prima sive Ontologia* (1730), making "ontology" the canonical term. Alexander Gottlieb Baumgarten further systematized it in his *Metaphysica* (1739), which became the textbook Kant used for his own lectures.

## The Kantian Disruption and the Analytic Revival

Kant's intervention was decisive in a way that still shapes how we read earlier metaphysics. In the *Critique of Pure Reason* he attacked the entire project of special metaphysics — the attempt to derive substantive truths about God, the soul, and the world as a whole from pure reason — as a "dialectical illusion." Reason, Kant argued, inevitably overreaches the bounds of possible experience when it ventures beyond those limits. But his critique presupposed a version of ontology rather than eliminating it. The "Analytic of Concepts" — Kant's own table of categories — described the fundamental structures that must be imposed on experience for it to be experience of objects at all. Kant's ontology was transcendental rather than transcendent: a doctrine of the conditions of possible experience, not of things as they are in themselves. This Copernican revolution — objects must conform to our cognitive forms rather than our cognitive forms conforming to objects — was enormously influential, but it produced a reaction.

The early analytic tradition, driven by Russell and Moore's rejection of British Idealism, returned to something closer to the pre-Kantian ambition of investigating reality directly, while insisting on logical rigor rather than Wolffian rationalism. Logical positivism then attacked metaphysics altogether: any statement not verifiable in principle by experience, the Vienna Circle argued, is not false but literally meaningless. Questions like "Why is there something rather than nothing?" express no genuine cognitive content. This anti-metaphysical phase was short-lived. Quine's "On What There Is" (1948) reestablished ontological inquiry on naturalistic grounds: we ask what entities our best theories commit us to, and those are the entities we should believe in. From the 1970s onward — with Kripke's work on possible worlds and de re necessity, Lewis's modal realism, and Armstrong's universals — analytic ontology flourished into the rich, technically precise discipline it is today.

## Two Models for the Relationship to Science

Contemporary metaphysicians broadly divide on how metaphysics relates to science, and this division matters practically for how ontological questions are pursued. The *naturalist* camp, following Quine, holds that metaphysics is continuous with science — it is the most general and systematic part of our attempt to understand reality, and its claims must be responsive to and constrained by what science tells us. Positing entities that play no role in any possible scientific theory, on this view, is irresponsible speculation.

The *neo-Aristotelian* camp holds that metaphysics investigates structures that are in some sense prior to science: the categories of object, property, causation, and modality that science itself presupposes and employs without analyzing. Science can inform metaphysics, but it cannot replace it — what objects, properties, and causal relations fundamentally *are* cannot be answered by empirical investigation alone.

A worked example clarifies the contrast. Quantum field theory posits quantum fields as the fundamental physical entities. The naturalist asks: should we take quantum fields to be in the ontology? What does the theory commit us to? The neo-Aristotelian asks further: are quantum fields better understood as substances — enduring things with intrinsic features — as processes, or as patterns in a more fundamental structure? The second set of questions goes beyond reading off the theory's formal commitments; it requires philosophical analysis of what kind of being fields can coherently have. The two approaches are not incompatible, but they assign different weights to different considerations.

One objection to post-Kantian metaphysics is that it is inevitably self-defeating: Kant argued that our categories yield genuine knowledge only when applied to objects of possible experience. Applied beyond that limit — to God, the soul, the world as a whole — they generate only antinomies and illusions. But this restriction was motivated by Kant's transcendental idealism, the view that objects must conform to our cognitive forms. Philosophers who reject transcendental idealism — as most analytic metaphysicians do — can maintain that our best theories give us justified beliefs about the structure of reality, without requiring that reality be constructed by our minds. The neo-Aristotelian tradition argues specifically that Kant's own categories — substance, causation, modality — are genuine features of reality that philosophical inquiry can investigate, not merely projections of the understanding. Whether or not one finds this convincing, the post-Kantian revitalization of metaphysics is an established fact of the discipline's history, and engaging with it requires understanding the Kantian challenge it was responding to.
