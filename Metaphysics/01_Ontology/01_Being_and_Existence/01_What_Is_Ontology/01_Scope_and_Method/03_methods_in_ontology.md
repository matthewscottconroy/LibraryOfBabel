# Methods in Ontology

How do we figure out what exists? The question sounds almost too simple to be interesting, but it is genuinely contested — and disagreements about method often drive substantive disputes in ontology before any specific claim about what there is has even been put on the table. Three broad approaches have been especially influential: conceptual analysis, Quinean naturalism, and the neo-Aristotelian appeal to theoretical virtues. They are not mutually exclusive, and skilled ontologists draw on all three. But they start from different assumptions and sometimes pull in different directions.

Conceptual analysis promised to answer ontological questions by carefully examining the concepts we deploy in thought and language. To know whether numbers exist, analyze the concept of number; to know whether events are entities, analyze event-talk. The approach traces to Plato's dialogues, where Socrates characteristically asks for the definition of a concept — justice, piety, knowledge — and tests proposed definitions against counterexamples. G. E. Moore and Russell elevated this method into a systematic program: to analyze a concept is to break it into simpler components until we reach the conceptually primitive elements that can be directly grasped. Applied to ontology, this promised to tell us what kinds of things we are committed to by our concepts. The fatal blow came from Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951). Quine argued that there is no principled distinction between what belongs to a concept by definition and what belongs to it as a firmly held empirical belief. If so, conceptual analysis cannot deliver the a priori ontological results it promised. There are no purely conceptual truths that can settle substantive questions about what kinds of things exist.

## Quine's Naturalist Method

Quine's positive proposal is elegant. Take our best overall theory of the world — the theory that science, in its most mature form, endorses. Regimented in first-order predicate logic, this theory contains bound variables that range over a domain of objects. The ontological commitments of the theory are precisely the entities that must be in that domain for the theory's statements to be true. To be, in Quine's famous slogan, is to be the value of a variable.

The method is usefully illustrated. Suppose our best physical theory includes the sentence "There exist gravitational fields that permeate spacetime." Regimented: ∃x(Gx ∧ Px). This statement is true only if there is something that is a gravitational field and permeates spacetime. We are thus committed to gravitational fields. If, by contrast, our theory can be restated without quantifying over fields — purely in terms of particle interactions — then we are not committed to fields as independent entities. The method is elegantly simple: read off ontological commitments from the existential quantifications of our best accepted theories.

But Quine's method faces real limitations. First, it presupposes that first-order logic is the uniquely correct canonical language. Second-order logic and plural quantification may be needed for mathematics; modal logic may be needed for modality; and the ontological import of these extended logics is disputed. Second, the method assumes that our scientific theories are the uniquely authoritative guide to what exists — which privileges natural science and may too quickly dismiss questions about moral properties, aesthetic values, and other domains that science does not directly address. Third, scientists sometimes use convenient fictions — ideal gases, point masses — that they do not literally believe in. Is a scientist who quantifies over ideal gases thereby ontologically committed to them? Quine's criterion seems to say yes, which appears to be the wrong answer.

## Neo-Aristotelian Theoretical Virtues

The contemporary neo-Aristotelian approach, associated with philosophers like E. J. Lowe, Kit Fine, and Jonathan Schaffer, holds that ontology is a genuine inquiry with its own methods, distinct from but complementary to science. The central tool is inference to the best ontological explanation: the theory that best explains the phenomena — including both scientific phenomena and the phenomena of ordinary conceptual practice — is the one we should accept.

Several theoretical virtues guide this theorizing. *Parsimony* urges us not to multiply entities beyond what is needed to explain the phenomena; this comes in two forms, quantitative (minimize the number of entities) and qualitative (minimize the number of kinds or categories). *Explanatory power* requires that the ontological theory explain why the phenomena are as they are — why similar things are similar, why causal relations have the structure they do. *Internal coherence* demands that the theory be consistent and that its parts fit together without arbitrary epicycles. *Fit with intuition* treats strong intuitive judgments as data points an adequate theory must accommodate or explain away. None of these virtues is decisive on its own; the skill lies in weighing them appropriately given the specific question.

## A Worked Example: The Existence of Properties

Consider the debate over whether properties exist as entities distinct from the things that have them. Three candidate positions frame the problem. Realism holds that properties — universals — exist as repeatable entities capable of being fully present in multiple locations simultaneously: "Red" is an entity shared by all red things. Trope theory holds that properties exist but as individual, non-repeatable particular property-instances — each red thing has its own redness, numerically distinct from every other redness. Nominalism holds that there are no property-entities at all; apparent reference to properties can be paraphrased into talk that quantifies only over concrete particulars.

Rather than stating these as a list to be memorized, notice how each position emerges from pressure on the others. The natural first response to the one-over-many problem — why do distinct red things all deserve the same predicate? — is realism: there must be something they share. But the realist faces Bradley's regress: if a and b are both red in virtue of instantiating the universal REDNESS, what is the relation between a and REDNESS? If it is a further universal, and instantiation of that requires yet another universal, we are off on a regress. This drives some philosophers toward tropes: particular property-instances avoid the regress because they do not need to be instantiated — they simply are properties of their particular bearers. But tropes face their own pressure: if each red thing has its own numerically distinct redness, what makes those tropes genuinely similar? Appealing to a resemblance relation risks reintroducing a universal through the back door. Nominalism tries to dispense with all this by denying property-entities entirely, at the cost of making natural similarity and causal laws hard to explain.

The conceptual analyst asks: what does our concept of a property imply? The Quinean naturalist asks: does our best science quantify over properties? The neo-Aristotelian asks: which theory best explains natural similarity, causal laws, and the truthmakers for predications? Realism does best on explanatory power; nominalism does best on parsimony; trope theory attempts a middle ground. The methodological choice is never purely formal — it reflects substantive commitments about which phenomena need explaining and which theoretical costs are acceptable.

One objection to the neo-Aristotelian approach is that it leaves ontological inquiry unconstrained by empirical evidence, generating theories that are internally coherent but untethered from reality. This is the verificationist worry in softer form. But the neo-Aristotelian does not claim that ontology is entirely a priori. Ontological theorizing is constrained by its fit with scientific results and the wide range of considered judgments that constitute the phenomena to be explained. The method is holistic — we test ontological theories against the full range of what we take ourselves to know, adjusting the theory where it conflicts with well-established findings. The methodological question is thus not preliminary but ongoing: it often determines the substantive outcome, and understanding why philosophers reach such different ontological conclusions frequently requires understanding the methodological commitments driving them.
