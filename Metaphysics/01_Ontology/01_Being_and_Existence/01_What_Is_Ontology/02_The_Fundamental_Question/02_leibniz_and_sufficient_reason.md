# Leibniz and the Principle of Sufficient Reason

Leibniz did not merely pose the question of why there is something rather than nothing. He also believed he had a systematic framework for answering it — and for answering any question of the same general form. That framework is the Principle of Sufficient Reason (PSR): every fact that obtains has a sufficient reason that explains why it obtains rather than not. Nothing happens without explanation. No brute, ungrounded facts exist. Reality is fully intelligible, all the way down.

The principle is stated in the *Monadology* (§32): "there can be no fact real or existing, no statement true, unless there be a sufficient reason, why it should be so and not otherwise." In *Principles of Nature and Grace* (§7): "nothing takes place without a sufficient reason; that is, nothing happens without it being possible for someone who knows enough things to give a reason sufficient to determine why it is thus and not otherwise." The absoluteness of this formulation — *every* fact, with no exceptions — is what distinguishes Leibniz's PSR from weaker causal principles like "every event has a cause." A causal principle restricts the demand to events; the PSR applies to all facts, including abstract, mathematical, and modal ones. Why is 7 prime? Why is the square root of 2 irrational? Even these necessary truths require a sufficient reason — their reason, Leibniz thought, is God's intellect, which contains all possible truths.

The PSR is a powerful philosophical instrument. If it holds, then the existence of the contingent world — the totality of things that exist but might not have — must have an explanation. That explanation cannot consist entirely of contingent facts, since each would itself require further explanation. The chain must terminate in something not contingent, something whose existence carries its own explanation within itself. That necessary being Leibniz identified with God.

## The Cosmological Argument

Leibniz's deployment of the PSR in the cosmological argument can be stated precisely:

P1. Everything that exists has a sufficient reason for its existence, either in itself or in another. (PSR)
P2. The totality of contingent things — call it the World — exists.
P3. The World is contingent (it might not have existed, or might have existed differently).
P4. No contingent series of facts, however long, can provide a sufficient reason for a contingent fact that falls outside the series.
P5. Therefore, the World's sufficient reason must be a necessary being — something whose existence is explained by its own nature.
C. A necessary being (God) exists and is the sufficient reason for the World.

The argumentative force depends almost entirely on P1 (the PSR) and P4 (the rejection of infinite regress as sufficient explanation). Hume attacked P4 in the *Dialogues*: "Did I show you the particular causes of each individual in a collection of twenty particles of matter, I should think it very unreasonable, should you afterwards ask me what was the cause of the whole twenty. This is sufficiently explained in explaining the cause of the parts." Hume's point is that explaining each part of a series may suffice to explain the whole.

Leibniz would reply that explaining each contingent member by another contingent member never addresses *why* there is a contingent series at all rather than no series. The series as a whole cries out for explanation even if each member is explained by another. This disagreement — between those who think the whole can be explained by its parts and those who think the whole still requires its own explanation — runs through every subsequent version of the cosmological argument.

## The Necessitarianism Objection

The most powerful internal criticism of the PSR is that it threatens to eliminate contingency altogether. If every fact has a sufficient reason that makes it inevitable, then every fact is determined to be what it is — which seems to imply that every truth is a necessary truth. Contingency vanishes from the world.

Leibniz tried to block this by distinguishing *metaphysical necessity* (absolute, logical necessity) from *moral necessity* (the necessity imposed by God's free choice of the best). The world is not metaphysically necessary — God could have created a different world. But God's nature — infinite wisdom and goodness — made it morally certain that God would create the best possible world. The world is contingent in that it might not have existed, but morally necessary in that a rational God could not have done otherwise.

The difficulty is that "moral necessity" appears to be a form of necessity, not genuine contingency. If God could not have done otherwise than create the best world, then the world's existence seems necessitated by God's nature, which seems necessitated by God's essence. Where exactly does genuine contingency enter? Alexander Pruss (*The Principle of Sufficient Reason*, 2006) argues that Leibniz's God is free in the relevant sense: God's nature makes the best world probable, not metaphysically guaranteed. The PSR requires an explanation, not determination. God's free choice is itself a brute fact about God, but since God is a necessary being, this is a necessary brute fact — one that does not violate the spirit of the PSR, which targets contingent brute facts.

Whether this move is convincing depends on whether we think a necessary brute fact is genuinely less troubling than a contingent one. The question is not merely technical; it goes to the heart of what we are demanding when we demand sufficient reasons.

## Weakened Versions of the PSR

Contemporary philosophers have explored whether a restricted PSR can do the philosophical work Leibniz wanted without generating necessitarianism. The options span a spectrum. Pruss's *modal PSR* holds that every contingent proposition has an explanation — stated in terms of propositions rather than facts, which may be easier to evaluate. Peter van Inwagen's *weak PSR* holds that every contingent fact possibly has an explanation, allowing that some facts might not actually have explanations while insisting that explanations are at least possible; this avoids necessitarianism but is insufficient to derive Leibniz's conclusion. Richard Taylor's *explanatory principle* holds that every existing thing has a sufficient reason for its existence, either in itself or in something else, and applies this to derive a necessary being without requiring explanations for abstract facts. Weakest of all is the *global PSR*, which requires only that the universe as a whole has a sufficient reason, even if individual contingent events do not.

Each weakened version preserves something of Leibniz's insight while limiting its scope. None is obviously correct; each comes with its own constellation of commitments and objections. The philosophical interest lies precisely in this spectrum: we can see which explanatory demands we are willing to sustain, and at what price.

## Brute Facts and the Denial of the PSR

The most direct response to the PSR is to deny it. Some facts are brute — they simply are without any explanation. Hume, Russell, and van Inwagen have endorsed this position explicitly. Van Inwagen argues that the existence of the universe is a brute fact: there is simply no explanation for why there is something rather than nothing. The appeal of this position is its simplicity: it terminates the explanatory regress without requiring mysterious necessary beings.

The Leibnizian reply is that the PSR is not just a pragmatic preference for explanations but a deep metaphysical principle about intelligibility. If the universe could exist as a brute fact, then anything could be a brute fact; the demand for explanation collapses everywhere. We would have no principled way to distinguish genuine scientific questions from questions that simply have no answer. The PSR, or some version of it, is the foundation of rational inquiry itself. This reply has force, but it proves too much if we use it to infer the existence of God from the mere desire for intelligibility. The question remains whether the world's intelligibility requires a necessary being to ground it, or whether the PSR itself simply runs out at some level — and if so, where.

## Connection to Modal Ontology and Theology

The PSR connects directly to debates about modal ontology. If the PSR is true, the existence of any possible being may demand explanation, and David Lewis's modal realism — the view that all possible worlds are equally real — can be read as a form of the PSR: there is no sufficient reason why only one possible world should be actual, so all are. Parfit's "why anything?" question raises the same issue in secular dress.

In philosophy of religion, the PSR grounds the Leibnizian and Kalam cosmological arguments and connects to debates about aseity, divine simplicity, and the freedom of God. Understanding Leibniz's original formulation — with its strengths and its tensions — is essential to following any of these subsequent debates rigorously.
