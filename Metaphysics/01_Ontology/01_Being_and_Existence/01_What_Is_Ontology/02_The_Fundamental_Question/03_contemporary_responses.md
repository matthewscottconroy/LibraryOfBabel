# Contemporary Responses to the Fundamental Question

Every philosophical answer to "Why is there something rather than nothing?" arrives with baggage. The Leibnizian answer requires a necessary being. The brute-fact answer requires accepting that the universe's existence has no explanation. Quantum cosmology offers the appearance of an explanation that dissolves on inspection. Modern philosophers have not resolved the question, but they have sharpened the alternatives and identified what any satisfying answer must accomplish. Understanding those alternatives is itself philosophically valuable.

The most important contemporary contribution comes from Derek Parfit, whose essay "Why Anything? Why This?" (1998, *London Review of Books*) is the most sustained philosophical treatment of the question in recent decades. Parfit begins by observing something that deflates the initial grip of the question. If we consider all possible states of reality — from the null state (nothing exists) through states of increasing complexity — the null state is just one among countless possibilities. If no Selector gives any possibility a privileged probability of obtaining, the probability that *something* exists is overwhelmingly greater than the probability that *nothing* exists, simply because "something" possibilities vastly outnumber the "nothing" possibility.

This argument does not strictly explain why something exists — it cannot, since any explanation would already presuppose something. But it undermines the intuition that existence requires special explanation while non-existence would be the default. The null possibility has no special claim on prior probability.

## Parfit's Selectors

The deeper question is what determines which state of affairs obtains. Parfit calls this a Selector — a feature that makes one type of possibility obtain rather than another — and considers four:

The *Null Selector* holds that the simplest possibility, nothingness, obtains by default. But this is the position we are trying to explain away: it assumes nothingness is the default without argument.

The *Fullness Selector* holds that all possibilities obtain simultaneously. If all possibilities are real, we need not explain why any particular one was selected. This has a certain meta-level parsimony: no selection is needed if everything obtains. But it raises its own questions — why should fullness rather than emptiness be the meta-level default?

The *Goodness Selector* holds that the best possible reality obtains. This is Leibniz's position. It has the appeal of explaining not only why there is something but why there is the particular something there is — but it depends on the PSR and on the coherence of a necessary being whose goodness necessitates the creation of the best world.

The *No Selector* holds that there is no Selector; existence is simply brute. This is Hume's and Russell's position: the universe simply is, with no explanation.

Parfit found each Selector independently motivated but none conclusively established. His tentative suggestion — that the Fullness Selector may be the most internally stable — is more of a gesture than a developed theory. But his framework has the significant virtue of making the structural options visible: any answer to the fundamental question must, implicitly or explicitly, be choosing among something like these options.

## Quantum Cosmology and Its Limits

Physical cosmologists since Hawking and Hartle have proposed models of the universe's origin that do not require a prior cause. The Hartle-Hawking "no boundary" proposal represents the universe's quantum state as a path integral over compact Euclidean geometries — geometries with no initial boundary, hence no "first moment" before which one could ask what came before. On this model, the universe has no cause in the ordinary temporal sense.

The philosophical problem is that this is not an answer to Leibniz's question. The Hartle-Hawking model specifies the universe's quantum state; it tells us, given that quantum mechanics holds, what the probability of various cosmic histories is. But it does not explain why there is a quantum state rather than nothing, why quantum mechanics holds rather than nothing, or why there are laws of nature at all. As Adolf Grünbaum observed in his debates with physicists on this topic: quantum cosmology can relocate the "why" question but cannot eliminate it.

Lawrence Krauss's *A Universe from Nothing* (2012) attracted popular attention and significant philosophical criticism. David Albert, reviewing it in the *New York Times*, pointed out that Krauss's "nothing" is the quantum vacuum — a state with definite physical structure governed by quantum field theory. A universe arising from the quantum vacuum is a universe arising from a prior physical state. The truly primitive question — why is there any physical state rather than no state at all? — is untouched.

## Self-Explanation and Modal Realism

Two further responses deserve attention, because they point toward genuinely different types of answer. The first is the self-explanation option: the universe might be self-explanatory, its existence explained by its own nature rather than by anything external. This was Spinoza's position — reality, properly understood, is a single necessary substance (God or Nature) whose existence follows necessarily from its own nature. Contemporary versions appear in Robert Nozick (*Philosophical Explanations*, 1981), who explores whether there might be a "self-subsumption" principle by which the actual universe is the one that best satisfies its own conditions of existence. This is more gesture than theory, but it points toward the idea that self-grounding is at least conceptually possible, and not obviously less coherent than external-grounding.

The second is David Lewis's modal realism. If all possible worlds are equally real, concrete entities, then the question "why is there something?" receives a simple answer: there must be something because every possible state of affairs obtains somewhere in logical space. The null possibility would itself be a world, and there would be something — the totality of worlds — even in that world. Lewis himself did not emphasize this application, and it faces the obvious objection that modal realism is itself an extraordinary ontological commitment requiring justification. Moreover, if all possibilities obtain, we still want to know why all possibilities obtain.

## Deflationary Responses

Two important deflationary responses deny that the question calls for, or could receive, a genuine philosophical answer. Following the Wittgensteinian tradition, the first holds that the question is grammatically well-formed but semantically empty. We understand what it means to ask why one fact obtains rather than another within the world; we cannot coherently ask why the world as a whole exists rather than not, because there is no available contrast class — no nothingness against which existence can be compared. The question arises from a grammatical illusion, the feeling that we can generalize the "why?" question indefinitely.

The second deflationary response, pressed by Rudolf Carnap and echoed by Eli Hirsch, treats the question as a choice between frameworks rather than a factual inquiry. "There is something rather than nothing" is a trivially true internal statement within any framework that includes any objects at all; asked as an external question — a question about which framework is the right one — it either has a pragmatic answer or none at all.

Both responses seem too quick. The existence of any reality at all is a fact that needs explaining, and the deflationary responses stipulate that this fact requires no explanation. Stipulation is not an argument. The brute-fact realists — who accept the question's legitimacy and simply say that existence has no explanation — at least engage with the structure of the problem directly rather than dissolving it by fiat. That position may ultimately be right, but it should be arrived at rather than assumed.

The fundamental question thus functions as a stress test for the entire framework of contemporary metaphysics. Any complete ontological theory must have something to say about it. Contemporary work on grounding, ontological dependence, and fundamentality — by Jonathan Schaffer, Kit Fine, and Gideon Rosen — provides new tools for analyzing what it would mean for existence to be self-explaining or to require an explanation. We have not solved the problem, but we have a much clearer view of its shape.
