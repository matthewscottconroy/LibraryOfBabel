# Quine on Ontological Commitment

Consider a simple question: what do you believe about numbers? If pressed, most people will say that the number 5 is odd, that there is a prime between 4 and 6, that mathematics is in some sense true. But do these beliefs commit us to thinking that numbers *exist* — that they are genuine constituents of reality? W. V. O. Quine's landmark 1948 essay "On What There Is" proposed a clean method for answering such questions. We discover what a theory commits us to by determining which entities must populate the domain of its bound variables when the theory is regimented in first-order predicate logic. "To be," Quine wrote, "is to be the value of a variable."

This criterion has a satisfying clarity. Instead of asking nebulous questions about the ultimate nature of reality, we ask: which variables must be assigned values for the sentences we accept to come out true? If our best mathematics quantifies over numbers — if we write "there exists an x such that x is a prime number greater than two" — then we are committed to the existence of numbers. If our moral discourse quantifies over moral properties, we are committed to those properties. The ontological question is not separate from the question of what our best theories say; it *is* that question, made explicit.

The criterion also provides a diagnostic for apparent commitments we want to escape. Apparent commitment to embarrassing entities — like the non-existent golden mountain or the round square — can often be dissolved by paraphrase. Russell showed that "The golden mountain does not exist" need not commit us to a non-existent object; it can be paraphrased as "It is not the case that there exists exactly one x such that x is golden and x is a mountain." Once paraphrased, the apparent entity vanishes from the scope of our quantifiers. More formally:

Let T be a theory (a set of sentences), and let T* be the regimentation of T in first-order predicate logic. Then entity e is within the ontological commitment of T if and only if e must be among the values of the bound variables of T* for T* to be true.

## Quine Versus Carnap

Quine's criterion was developed in explicit opposition to Rudolf Carnap's deflationary ontology. Carnap distinguished between *internal questions* — questions asked within a linguistic framework about entities of a given type — and *external questions* — questions about whether to adopt the framework itself. "Are there numbers?" asked internally (within the framework of arithmetic) has a trivially affirmative answer. Asked externally — as a question about whether to adopt the numerical framework — it is merely a pragmatic question about which framework is useful, not a factual question about reality.

Quine rejected this internal/external distinction. He denied that there is a principled boundary between the choice of a logical framework and the empirical hypothesis that certain entities exist. Both are equally revisable in light of evidence; both are ontological posits. The pragmatic considerations that Carnap thought determined framework choice — simplicity, fruitfulness — are the same considerations that determine what we believe exists. There is no privileged standpoint from which framework choices are made that is exempt from ontological scrutiny.

This dispute matters because, if Carnap is right, many apparent ontological disputes dissolve. The debate about whether numbers exist becomes merely a question about which linguistic framework to use. If Quine is right, the debate is genuine: we really must settle whether numbers are in the domain of our existential quantifiers, and pragmatics cannot substitute for ontology.

## The Role of Paraphrase

Paraphrase is the decisive tool in Quine's ontological practice. The goal is to identify the most austere theory — the one with the fewest ontological commitments — that can capture all the data we wish to explain. Quine himself was committed to the ontology of set theory and mathematics (because mathematics is indispensable to physics) and to physical objects (because science requires them), but not to properties, propositions, or possible objects — whose apparent reference, he thought, could always be paraphrased away.

A critical case: "John is taller than Mary." This sentence appears to involve a relation, tallness, that applies between John and Mary. Does this commit us to the existence of the relation being-taller-than? Quine's strategy: relations can be reduced to ordered sets, and sets are already in his ontology as a mathematical posit. So the sentence does not add to our ontological commitments beyond what set theory already requires. The question in each case is whether the paraphrase succeeds without changing the subject — and this is often where the real philosophical argument is.

## Objections

Four objections to Quine's criterion deserve attention. The first: why first-order logic? Why should the canonical language of ontological commitment be specifically first-order predicate logic? Mathematics standardly requires second-order quantification, and some philosophers (Boolos) argue that plural quantification — "some things are such that..." — can do much of this work without adding abstract objects to the ontology. If the canonical language is second-order or plural, Quine's criterion delivers different results.

The second objection concerns theoretical versus ordinary ontology. Quine's criterion makes our ontological commitments hostage to our best scientific theories. But scientists sometimes use convenient fictions in their theories — quantifying over ideal gases or point particles that they know do not literally exist. Is such a scientist ontologically committed to these fictions? Quine's criterion seems to say yes, which appears wrong.

The third objection is metaontological. Quine's criterion tells us how to read off commitments from theories we already accept. But it does not tell us how to decide which theory to accept in the first place, nor does it resolve disputes between philosophers who agree on the data but disagree about the correct formalization.

The fourth objection comes from Kit Fine and Jonathan Schaffer, who argue that Quine's criterion misses crucial ontological distinctions. A theory that quantifies over both physical objects and their shadows is committed to the existence of shadows — but are shadows part of fundamental reality, or do they exist only derivatively, in virtue of physical objects and light? Quine's criterion does not distinguish fundamental from derivative existence; it treats all existential commitments as ontologically equivalent. This, neo-Aristotelians argue, is precisely the wrong lesson to draw: being is not univocal, and not every quantified entity has the same ontological status.

Quine's 1948 essay launched modern analytic ontology as a systematic discipline and influenced an entire generation of work — from Davidson's event ontology (derived from the structure of action sentences) to Lewis's possible-world realism (derived from taking possible-world talk at face value). To engage with contemporary ontology is, in large part, to engage with Quine's criterion and its consequences, whether one accepts it or resists it.
