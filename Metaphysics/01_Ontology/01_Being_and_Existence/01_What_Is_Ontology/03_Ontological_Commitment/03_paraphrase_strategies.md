# Paraphrase Strategies

Here is the challenge in its simplest form. We say things like "Redness is a color" and "The number of planets is eight" and "It is possible that there could be more people than there actually are." Each of these sentences appears to refer to an abstract entity — the property Redness, the number Eight, a possible world. If we take these sentences at face value, we seem committed to an ontology that includes properties, numbers, and possible worlds. But if we can *paraphrase* these sentences into logically equivalent ones that make no reference to such entities, we can deflate that commitment. The question is whether the paraphrase succeeds.

Russell's theory of definite descriptions is the paradigm case of successful paraphrase. "The present king of France is bald" appears to refer to someone — the present king of France — but since France has no king, the sentence seems to be about a nonexistent entity, which creates puzzles. Russell's paraphrase eliminates the apparent reference. The sentence says that there exists exactly one present king of France, and whoever it is is bald:

∃x [Fx ∧ ∀y(Fy → y = x) ∧ Gx]

Since no such person exists, the whole conjunction is false — no nonexistent entity is invoked. The apparent singular reference to "the present king of France" is distributed across an existential quantifier, a uniqueness clause, and a predication. This is the template: transform an apparent referring expression into a quantified description, so that the ontological work is done by the quantifier rather than the name.

## The Indispensability Objection

The most powerful objection to nominalist paraphrase strategies comes from the Quine-Putnam indispensability argument. Even if we can sometimes replace apparent reference to abstract objects with complex concrete paraphrases, the resulting paraphrases are often more complex, less elegant, and less workable than the originals. And in mathematics, the paraphrase strategy seems to break down entirely: no one has produced an adequate paraphrase of the full content of modern mathematics — analysis, topology, abstract algebra — in terms that quantify only over concrete things.

The argument runs:

P1. We ought to believe the entities that are indispensable to our best scientific theories.
P2. Mathematical entities (numbers, functions, sets) are indispensable to our best scientific theories — we cannot state the laws of physics without quantifying over them.
C. We ought to believe in mathematical entities.

Hartry Field's *Science Without Numbers* (1980) is the most ambitious attempt to execute the nominalist paraphrase program for physics. Field argues that Newtonian gravitational theory can be stated using only quantification over spacetime regions and physical fields, understood as physical particulars rather than mathematical objects, without invoking real numbers or other mathematical abstracta. This is technically impressive. But whether it generalizes to the full scope of modern physics remains deeply doubtful, and most philosophers have found it unconvincing as a global strategy.

## Modal Paraphrase

Sentences like "It is possible that there could have been more people than there actually are" and "It is necessarily true that triangles have three sides" appear to quantify over possible worlds or possible states of affairs. David Lewis argued that such quantification is genuine — possible worlds are concrete entities as real as the actual world. Actualists reject Lewis's ontology and attempt to paraphrase modal claims without quantifying over concrete possible worlds.

The options reveal how much work paraphrase is being asked to do. Robert Adams treats possible worlds as maximal consistent sets of propositions: "It is possible that p" means there is a maximal consistent set of propositions that includes p. This trades commitment to concrete worlds for commitment to abstract propositions — which may not be progress. Gideon Rosen's modal fictionalism treats possible-world talk as a useful fiction: "It is possible that p" is paraphrased as "According to the fiction of modal realism, there is a world where p." This avoids commitment to both concrete worlds and abstract propositions, but faces the question of whether fictional contexts can preserve truth value. D. M. Armstrong's combinatorialism holds that possible worlds are recombinations of actual particulars and universals — but this cannot accommodate alien properties and individuals that do not exist in the actual world.

Each paraphrase strategy has costs. The pattern is consistent: avoiding one ontological commitment typically requires either accepting another or changing the truth conditions of the original claims.

## Nominalist Paraphrase of Property Talk

Consider "Redness is a color." This appears to refer to an abstract entity — the property Redness — and to predicate colorhood of it. The nominalist wants to paraphrase this as saying something about red things: "Every red thing is a colored thing." But this paraphrase does not say the same thing. "Redness is a color" seems necessarily true — necessarily, any property that is redness is a color. "Every red thing is a colored thing" is a generalization about concrete objects with a different modal profile. The paraphrase has changed the subject.

More troublingly: "Redness resembles orangeness more than it resembles blueness." This seems to be an intrinsic fact about the properties themselves, not a generalization about objects that instantiate them. Any paraphrase that reduces this to object-level talk must somehow capture the similarity ordering of properties without invoking properties — which seems nearly impossible without begging the question.

## The Fundamental Dilemma

The fundamental dilemma for any paraphrase strategy can be stated cleanly. On the *equivalence horn*: if the paraphrase says exactly what the original says, then the original sentence did not really commit us to the apparently offending entities — they were never truly invoked, merely represented. Good news for the paraphraser. But it also reveals that the original language was never ontologically committed to those entities in the first place, which shifts the question to why the sentences appeared to have such commitments.

On the *revision horn*: if the paraphrase says something different — if it changes the truth conditions, the modal profile, or the inferential role — then we have not shown that the original claims can be made without the objectionable entities. We have merely changed the claims. The original discourse, with its apparent commitments intact, remains the only adequate expression of the content we wanted to express.

Most nominalist strategies fall on the revision horn: they provide paraphrases that are useful and clear but do not fully capture everything the original sentences expressed. The defender of abstract objects then argues that this gap reveals the genuine ontological commitment of the original discourse. Understanding this dilemma — and which horn any specific paraphrase strategy falls on — is essential to evaluating the many nominalist programs that continue to be developed.

The broader metaontological point, pressed by Eli Hirsch, is that ontological disputes are sometimes disputes about which paraphrase strategy to adopt — about which way of talking about the phenomena is correct. Quine's response was that the choice of paraphrase strategy is not merely verbal: it reflects substantive commitments about which theories are simpler, more powerful, and more credible. Choosing to paraphrase number-talk into concrete-object talk is not a neutral linguistic decision; it is a bet that the world contains no abstract objects, and this bet has consequences for how we understand mathematics, logic, and science.
