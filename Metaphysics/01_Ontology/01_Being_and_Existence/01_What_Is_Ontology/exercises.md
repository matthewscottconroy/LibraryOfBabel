# Exercises and Thought Experiments

## Exercise 1: Your Own Ontology
Before reading further in this textbook, write a list of everything you believe exists. Try to be systematic: start with concrete physical objects, then ask whether abstract entities like numbers and laws make your list, then consider whether mental states, possibilities, fictional characters, and moral facts belong there. Then compare your list to those of your classmates. Where do you disagree? Can you identify the philosophical commitments that drive the disagreements?

## Exercise 2: Applying Quine's Criterion
Take the following sentences and, for each, identify the ontological commitments it generates according to Quine's criterion:
- "The average family has 2.3 children."
- "Beethoven's Fifth Symphony is more complex than any telephone ring tone."
- "There is a possible world in which Napoleon won at Waterloo."
- "Causing unnecessary suffering is wrong."
For each, ask: what must exist for this sentence to be true, once we regiment it in first-order logic? Is the sentence's apparent ontological commitment real or paraphrasable away?

## Exercise 3: The Paraphrase Challenge
Nominalists claim that apparent reference to numbers can be paraphrased away. Try to paraphrase the following sentences to avoid quantifying over numbers:
- "The number of moons of Jupiter is greater than the number of moons of Mars."
- "7 is a prime number."
- "Mathematics is indispensable for science."
After attempting the paraphrases, assess: do your paraphrases say exactly the same thing as the originals, or do they subtly change the subject?

## Exercise 4: The Fundamental Question
Leibniz asked: "Why is there something rather than nothing?" Evaluate each of the following potential responses:
1. "God exists necessarily and created everything else."
2. "The question is unanswerable, and that is not troubling."
3. "The question is meaningless—'nothing' is not a coherent alternative."
4. "Some facts are brute, and the existence of something is one of them."
Which response do you find most satisfying, and what would it take to make you change your mind?

## Exercise 5: Existence as a Predicate
Kant argued that "existence is not a predicate." Consider the sentence "Santa Claus does not exist." This seems to be a genuine, true statement about Santa Claus. But if existence is not a predicate, what is the sentence saying? How does Frege's analysis handle this case? Does it succeed, or does it face problems of its own?

## Thought Experiment: The Empty World
Imagine a possible world that contains nothing whatsoever—no matter, no space, no time, no laws of nature, no minds, no abstracta. Can you coherently imagine such a world? If you can, does this show that the actual world's existence is contingent and in need of explanation? If you cannot coherently imagine it, does this show that something must necessarily exist?
