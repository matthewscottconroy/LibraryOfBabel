# Further Reading and Primary Sources

## Primary Sources

**Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, Books IV and VII** — Book IV introduces the study of being qua being and the principle of non-contradiction. Book VII (Zeta) contains Aristotle's deepest investigation into substance and essence. Any translation with commentary is valuable; the Ross translation (Oxford) and the Sachs translation (Green Lion Press) are both widely used.

**Leibniz, "Principles of Nature and Grace" and "Monadology" (1714)** — Short texts in which Leibniz poses the fundamental question and sketches his answer through the PSR and his monadology. Both are available in Ariew and Garber's anthology *Leibniz: Philosophical Essays* (Hackett).

**W. V. O. Quine, "On What There Is" (1948)** — Quine's most accessible statement of his criterion of ontological commitment, reprinted in *From a Logical Point of View* (Harvard). Essential reading for any student of analytic ontology.

**Gottlob Frege, *The Foundations of Arithmetic* (1884)** — Frege's logicist program and his analysis of existence as a second-order predicate. The J. L. Austin translation (Northwestern) is standard.

## Secondary Literature

**Michael Loux and Dean Zimmerman (eds.), *The Oxford Handbook of Metaphysics* (Oxford, 2003)** — Comprehensive survey of all major areas in analytic metaphysics, with accessible overview articles by leading figures.

**Peter van Inwagen, *Metaphysics* (4th ed., Westview, 2014)** — An excellent undergraduate textbook covering existence, identity, modality, and free will with exceptional clarity and rigor.

**David Chalmers, David Manley, and Ryan Wasserman (eds.), *Metametaphysics* (Oxford, 2009)** — A collection of essays on the status and methodology of ontology, featuring debates between Quinians, neo-Aristotelians, and deflationists.

**E. J. Lowe, *The Four-Category Ontology* (Oxford, 2006)** — A rigorous defense of a neo-Aristotelian ontology organized around four basic categories: kinds, attributes, individual substances, and particular properties.

**Derek Parfit, "Why Anything? Why This?" (1998)** — A fascinating exploration of the fundamental question, published in the *London Review of Books* and widely anthologized. Parfit's accessible style makes it ideal for students.
