# Important Terms and Ideas: What Is Ontology?

**Ontology**: The branch of philosophy concerned with what exists, what categories of things there are, and what their most general features are. Sometimes used interchangeably with "metaphysics," though ontology typically refers specifically to questions of existence and being.

**Being qua being**: Aristotle's description of the subject-matter of metaphysics — the study of things insofar as they are, without restriction to any particular domain or kind. The phrase captures ontology's universality: it studies everything, but only from the angle of its existence.

**Existence**: The property (if it is one) of being real, of there being something rather than nothing. Philosophers debate whether existence is a genuine first-order property of objects or merely a second-order logical predicate.

**Ontological commitment**: Quine's notion that a theory is committed to the existence of whatever entities are required by its variables when written in first-order logic. A theory commits us to Fs if it has a true sentence of the form "there is an x such that x is F."

**Existential quantifier**: The logical operator "there exists" (written ∃). Quine argued that asserting existential truths is the canonical way to make ontological commitments: to say something exists is to say that the existential quantifier ranges over it.

**Metaontology**: The second-order inquiry into the nature of ontological inquiry itself: what ontological questions are, what methods are appropriate for answering them, and whether ontological debates are substantive or merely verbal.

**Categories of being**: The most general kinds or types of entity — substance, property, relation, event, set, proposition, and so on. Categorizing being is one of ontology's fundamental tasks.

**Formal vs. material ontology**: A distinction drawn by Husserl. Formal ontology studies the most abstract structures common to all possible objects (part-whole, identity, dependence). Material ontology studies the structures of particular domains (physical objects, numbers, minds).
