# Aristotle's Ten Categories

Every scientific inquiry takes a domain for granted. Physics takes moving bodies; chemistry takes chemical elements; psychology takes mental states. What Aristotle wanted was a prior investigation — one that would tell us what the most general kinds of things there are, independently of any particular science's framing. His *Categories* is the result: a systematic attempt to enumerate all the highest genera under which anything can fall. Aristotle identifies ten: substance (*ousia*), quantity (*poson*), quality (*poion*), relation (*pros ti*), place (*pou*), time (*pote*), position (*keisthai*), state (*echein*), action (*poiein*), and passion (*paschein*). Everything that can be said about anything falls into exactly one of these categories.

Notice how Aristotle derives the list rather than simply announcing it. He begins from a familiar observation: we can say many different kinds of things about Socrates. We can say what he is — a man, a human being — or we can say how large he is, or what he is like, or how he stands relative to others, or where he is, or when, or how he is arranged, or what he has, or what he does, or what is done to him. These are not merely grammatical variations; they are different types of answers to the question "What is X?" Each type of answer tracks a different *way of being*. The categories are thus derived from the range of possible predicates, which Aristotle takes to exhaust the fundamental ways things can be said to be.

The asymmetry within the ten is crucial. Substance is the primary category because all other categories depend on substance for their existence. Qualities, quantities, and relations exist only as features of substances; there would be no quantities, qualities, or relations if there were no substances to be quantified, qualified, or related. We call this the doctrine of *pros hen* predication, or "focal meaning": things are said to be in different ways, but all those ways relate back to a central case.

## Primary and Secondary Substance

Within substance itself, Aristotle makes a distinction that runs through the entire tradition. *Primary substances* are the concrete individual things of ordinary experience: this particular horse, that particular human being, this tree. They are called primary because they are the ultimate subjects of predication — everything else is either said of them or is in them. Nothing is more fundamental. *Secondary substances* are the species and genera to which primary substances belong: horse, human, animal. Secondary substances are substances derivatively, because they express what primary substances are.

This gives Aristotle's ontology its characteristic shape: reality is fundamentally a world of individual things, not universal types or Platonic Forms. The species "horse" does not exist over and above individual horses; it exists only as predicated of individual horses, in virtue of the natures those individuals share. This is immanent realism: universals exist, but they exist *in* particulars, not separately from them.

The contrast with Plato is direct. For Plato, the Forms — like the Form of Beauty — exist independently of the many beautiful things that participate in them, and are indeed more real than those particulars, which are merely imperfect images. Aristotle reversed this order of priority. Individual substances are ontologically primary; universals exist only in relation to those individuals. The formal contrast:

**Platonic Realism (universalia ante rem)**:
P1. There is a Form corresponding to every genuine kind, existing independently of particular things.
P2. Particulars are real only derivatively, by "participating" in Forms.
C. Forms are more real than particulars.

**Aristotelian Immanent Realism (universalia in re)**:
P1. Universals exist only in the particulars that instantiate them — they are not separately real.
P2. Particulars are ontologically primary; universals depend on particulars.
C. Particulars are more real than universals.

Aristotle's primary objection to Platonism is the Third Man Argument (*Metaphysics* Z): if Socrates and Callias both instantiate the Form of Man, and if forms and their instances are similar in a way that requires a Form, then Socrates, Callias, and the Form of Man must all be united by another Form of Man — and so on in a vicious regress. The explanatory coherence of the theory is compromised from the start.

## Reception, Criticism, and Contemporary Relevance

Aristotle's list was enormously influential through the medieval period, shaping scholastic metaphysics for over a thousand years. From early on, though, critics questioned its completeness and coherence. Why exactly ten? Some categories — action and passion, position and state — seem very close to one another. The category of relation has a strikingly different character from the others: a quality is in a substance, but a relation seems to be *between* substances rather than in any one of them.

Porphyry's *Isagoge* (third century CE) raised the problem of universals as an explicit puzzle: do genera and species exist independently, exist only in things, or exist only in the mind? This question, which Porphyry declined to answer, drove medieval philosophical debate for centuries. Peter Abelard defended a form of moderate realism: universals exist as concepts (*voces*), genuinely predicable of many, but not as independently real entities. Aquinas held that universals exist in things and are abstracted by the intellect. Ockham's nominalism denied real universals entirely — only particular things exist, and universal terms are just names (*nomina*) that apply to many things because of their resemblances.

Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* offered a rival table of categories derived from the forms of judgment, explicitly challenging Aristotle's scheme. Kant's categories are not features of the world but of the understanding; they are the forms through which the mind organizes experience.

Contemporary ontology has largely replaced Aristotle's particular list while retaining his insight that ontology should identify the basic categories of things. Most analytic ontologists now work with something like a four-category scheme: particular objects (substances), universal properties and relations, tropes (particular property instances), and facts or states of affairs. E. J. Lowe's *The Four-Category Ontology* (2006) is the most developed contemporary version. Jonathan Schaffer's priority monism — the view that the cosmos is the fundamental entity and all parts are derivative — challenges the Aristotelian assumption that individual substances are the ontologically primary entities. Both can be understood as responses to problems first posed by Aristotle's *Categories*. The specific list may have been superseded; the project has not.
