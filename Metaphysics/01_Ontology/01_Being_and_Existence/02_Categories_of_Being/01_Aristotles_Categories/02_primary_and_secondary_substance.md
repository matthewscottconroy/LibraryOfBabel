# Primary and Secondary Substance

What is it that grounds everything else? Aristotle's answer is given in his most fundamental ontological distinction, the one that organizes everything in the *Categories* and much of the *Metaphysics*: the distinction between primary and secondary substance. *Primary* substances are the concrete individual things of ordinary experience — this particular horse, that particular human being, this tree. They are primary because they are the ultimate subjects of predication: everything else is either said of them or is in them. *Secondary* substances are the species and genera to which primary substances belong — horse, human, animal. Secondary substances are substances derivatively: they express what primary substances are.

Aristotle makes this explicit with a two-by-two matrix. Things that are *said of* a subject but not *in* a subject are secondary substances — "human being" is said of Socrates (it applies to him) but is not in him (it does not inhere in him as whiteness inheres in his skin). Things that are *in* a subject but not *said of* any subject are particular accidents — individual property instances, like this particular whiteness that is in Socrates. Things that are both *in* a subject and *said of* a subject are universal accidents — the universal property Whiteness, which is in particular substances and predicable of many. Things that are neither *in* a subject nor *said of* a subject are primary substances themselves. Socrates is not in anything else and is not said of anything more fundamental. This logical analysis gives primary substances their special status: they are the ultimate subjects, never predicates.

## The Individuation Problem

Accepting that primary substances are basic raises an immediate question: what makes a primary substance *this* individual rather than some other? If Socrates and Callias are both human beings — both instances of the secondary substance "human being" — what makes Socrates specifically Socrates and Callias specifically Callias?

Aristotle's answer in the *Metaphysics* is controversial. He sometimes suggests that what individuates primary substances is matter (*hyle*): Socrates is individuated by *this* particular matter, Callias by *that* particular matter. But this generates difficulties — the same matter cannot make a thing what it *is* (that is the job of form), and in any case individual matter seems to face the same individuation problem. What makes this matter *this* matter?

The medieval tradition debated this intensively. Aquinas held that individuation is by "designated matter" (*materia signata*) — matter as marked by quantity, which is the principle of individuation for material substances. Scotus rejected this and proposed that each individual has an individual form (*haecceitas*, "thisness") — a primitive, irreducible feature that distinguishes it from all others of the same species. Ockham simplified radically: there is no principle of individuation beyond the individual itself; each thing is simply itself and does not need a further ground for its individuality.

These medieval debates are not merely historical. The question of what grounds the numerical distinctness of individuals — individuals that may share all their qualitative properties — returns in contemporary discussions of the identity of indiscernibles (Leibniz's Law), haecceitism about possible worlds, and the metaphysics of quantum particles, where the standard notion of individuation may break down altogether.

## Aristotelian Immanent Realism vs. Platonic Realism

The distinction between primary and secondary substance marks Aristotle's position in the most fundamental dispute in the history of ontology: whether universals exist independently of particulars. For Plato, the Forms — like the Form of Beauty — exist independently of the many beautiful things that participate in them. They are, for Plato, more real than particulars, which are merely imperfect images of the Forms. Aristotle reversed this: individual substances are ontologically primary, and universals exist only in relation to those individuals.

The formal contrast:

**Platonic Realism (universalia ante rem)**:
P1. There is a Form (universal) corresponding to every genuine kind.
P2. Forms exist independently of any particular things that instantiate them.
P3. Particulars are real only derivatively, by "participating" in Forms.
C. Forms are more real than particulars.

**Aristotelian Immanent Realism (universalia in re)**:
P1. There are universals corresponding to natural kinds and genuine properties.
P2. Universals exist only *in* the particulars that instantiate them — they are not separately real.
P3. Particulars are ontologically primary; universals depend on particulars.
C. Particulars are more real than universals.

Aristotle's primary objection to Platonism is the Third Man Argument, sketched in the *Metaphysics*: if Socrates and Callias both instantiate the Form of Man, and if forms and their instances are similar in a way that requires a Form, then Socrates, Callias, and the Form of Man must all be united by another Form of Man — and so on in a vicious regress. The explanatory power of the theory collapses.

Secondary substances — the species and genera — raise a problem for immanent realism even so. What holds the members of a species together? What makes all individual horses members of the species horse? Aristotle's answer: the shared form (secondary substance). But the shared form, being immanent, is somehow present in each individual — and how can the numerically same universal be fully present in many distinct locations simultaneously? This pressure motivates trope theory: rather than positing a single shared universal present in all red things, trope theorists posit individual redness-tropes in each red thing, with tropes being similar to one another without being identical.

## Contemporary Developments

The distinction between primary and secondary substance feeds directly into contemporary debates about natural kinds and essential properties. Neo-Aristotelian philosophers like E. J. Lowe and Brian Ellis argue that natural kinds — biological species, chemical elements — are genuine ontological categories, not merely sets of similar things. A natural kind like water or gold has a real essence: a set of intrinsic properties that all members of the kind necessarily share and that ground the kind's causal powers and modal profile. Kripke's work in *Naming and Necessity* (1980) gave this Aristotelian position new force: natural kind terms like "water" and "gold" are rigid designators that pick out a natural kind by its actual constitution, and so water is necessarily H₂O — not because of any conceptual truth, but because that is what water actually is. The species or kind is not a merely nominal grouping but a genuine feature of reality with a discoverable essence. Secondary substance, in this light, is not a relic of ancient philosophy but a live option in contemporary metaphysics.
