# The Category of Relation

Among Aristotle's ten categories, relation (*pros ti*) has always been the most philosophically troublesome. Consider why. A quality, like whiteness, seems to be squarely *in* a substance: this piece of paper is white, and the whiteness is in the paper. But a relation, like being-taller-than, seems to be *between* substances rather than in any one of them. If relations are genuine features of reality, they seem to require a different kind of ontological home than ordinary properties — and this asymmetry raises the question of whether relations have the same claim to reality that properties do.

Aristotle noted the peculiarity and placed relations at the bottom of his ontological hierarchy, suggesting they exist in a weaker sense than substances or qualities. Many later Aristotelians went further, arguing that relations are not genuinely real — mere "beings of reason" or aspects of the mind's activity rather than features of things themselves. Leibniz notoriously held that all relations are reducible to intrinsic properties of their relata: to say that A is taller than B is ultimately to say something about A's intrinsic height and B's intrinsic height, with no additional relational fact. His strategy:

- "Paris is north of Rome" → "Paris has such-and-such a location" ∧ "Rome has such-and-such a location"
- "John is taller than Mary" → "John has height h₁" ∧ "Mary has height h₂" ∧ h₁ > h₂

The third conjunct introduces a mathematical ordering, which Leibniz takes to be a necessary truth of reason grounded in God's intellect rather than an additional contingent relational fact. This follows from the core of his monadology: each substance is a "windowless monad" containing all its properties internally, with no genuine causal or ontological dependence on external things.

The crucial objection: asymmetric relations seem to resist this reduction. "A is north of B" and "B is north of A" have the same intrinsic monadic facts about A and B (their positions), but they differ in direction. How can this directional asymmetry be captured by intrinsic properties alone? Bertrand Russell's argument, in *The Principles of Mathematics* (1903), is that it cannot. Asymmetric relations are irreducibly relational; they are not reducible to any conjunction of monadic facts. Russell's vindication of *external relations* — relations that are not grounded in intrinsic properties of their relata — was seen as a decisive argument against idealism and in favor of a realistic, pluralistic ontology.

## Bradley's Regress

F. H. Bradley, in *Appearance and Reality* (1893), offered the famous regress argument that has shaped all subsequent discussion of relations:

P1. Relations are either internal (reducible to properties of their relata) or external (irreducibly relational).
P2. External relations must relate their relata — there must be a connecting relation R between them.
P3. But R must also be related to its relata by a further relation R' — and so on ad infinitum.
P4. This vicious regress shows that external relations are impossible.
C. All relations are internal (= monadic properties).

Russell's reply: the regress is not vicious. We do not need R' to be a further relation relating R to A and B; R already relates A and B, and this is a primitive fact that does not require a mediating connecting relation. The regress arises only if we assume that every relational connection requires a further connector — but that assumption is unmotivated. Instantiation, binding, and relating are primitive ontological connections, not themselves entities that need further analysis.

Notice that Bradley's regress applies equally to properties: if a is red in virtue of instantiating the universal REDNESS, there must be a relation between a and REDNESS, and then a further relation connecting that relation to its relata, and so on. If the argument showed that relations are impossible, it would show that properties are impossible too. Russell takes this to show that the argument proves too much — and that the correct conclusion is simply that some ontological connections are primitive.

## Internal and External Relations

Contemporary metaphysics distinguishes internal from external relations with more precision. A relation R is *internal* if it supervenes on the intrinsic properties of its relata: necessarily, if a and b have the intrinsic properties they have, then R holds between them. A relation is *external* if it is possible for a and b to have exactly the same intrinsic properties yet differ in whether R holds.

Consider the difference: *being taller than* is plausibly internal — if John has height h₁ and Mary has height h₂ with h₁ > h₂, then necessarily John is taller than Mary. *Being two feet apart* is external — two objects could have exactly the same intrinsic properties yet be two feet apart or five feet apart. *Being a parent of* is external — it depends on historical causal connections, not intrinsic properties alone.

Structural realists in philosophy of physics argue that spatiotemporal and quantum-mechanical relations are genuinely external and fundamental — they cannot be reduced to intrinsic properties of the relata. This supports a relational ontology on which relations are as basic as, or more basic than, objects. Ontic structural realism (Ladyman, French) takes this further: objects may be metaphysically dispensable, and structure — understood as networks of relations — may be the most fundamental ontological category. This is a radical reversal of Aristotle's hierarchy: rather than relations being the least real of the categories, they may turn out to be the most fundamental.

Whether relations require relata in the traditional sense, or whether structure can be ontologically primary without presupposing independent bearers, remains a question at the cutting edge of contemporary ontology. The ancient puzzle about how to locate a relation — in one substance, or in both, or somehow between them — has not gone away. It has simply been given much sharper tools.
