# Objects, Properties, and Relations

Contemporary analytic ontology generally operates with a tripartite scheme of basic categories: objects (or particulars), properties, and relations. Objects are the concrete individual things that populate the world — rocks, organisms, electrons, persons. Properties are features that objects have — being red, being massive, being conscious. Relations are features that hold between two or more objects — being-taller-than, being-north-of, being-the-parent-of. While this scheme is less elaborate than Aristotle's ten categories, it is simpler and more closely tied to modern formal logic, where subject-predicate structure and relational structure have clear representations.

The tripartite scheme immediately raises a cascade of questions. Are properties genuinely real entities in their own right — universals — or are they merely features of how we represent things (nominalism), or are they particular property-instances (tropes)? Do relations exist over and above the objects they relate, or are they reducible to intrinsic features of those objects? What grounds the distinction between an object and a property — is there a deep metaphysical difference, or is the distinction partly notational? And which of these three categories, if any, is most fundamental?

## Objects and the Problem of Individuation

Objects are characterized by their particularity: each object is numerically distinct from every other, even if they are qualitatively identical. Two iron spheres of exactly the same size, weight, and composition are two objects, not one. What makes an object *this* particular object rather than some other qualitatively identical one? This is the problem of individuation.

The natural first answer is haecceitism: there are primitive individual essences — "thisnesses," haecceities — that make each object numerically distinct from every other. Each object has its own haecceity, which is not reducible to its qualitative properties. This preserves the intuition that two truly indistinguishable objects are still two, but it requires positing a category of primitive non-qualitative individuation.

Aristotle's answer for material substances was matter: what individuates them is the particular matter they are made of. But this generates a regress — what individuates the matter? A more deflationary answer locates individuation in spatiotemporal region: two objects cannot occupy the same region, and occupation of a region is what makes an object this one. This works well for material objects but faces difficulties for abstract objects and possible objects. Bundle theory dissolves the problem by denying that there is a bare particular needing individuation — objects are constituted by their properties, and numerical distinctness is grounded in qualitative distinctness plus spatiotemporal differentiation.

## Properties: Universals, Tropes, or Nothing?

Three major positions frame the question of the ontological status of properties. Realism about universals holds that properties are entities that can be literally shared by numerically distinct objects. The redness of this tomato and the redness of that fire truck are the same universal, REDNESS, wholly present in each. Universals are the "one over many" — they account for the objective similarity of qualitatively identical things. This is the position of Plato, Aristotle in his immanentist version, and contemporary philosophers like D. M. Armstrong and E. J. Lowe.

Trope theory holds that properties are particular: tropes are individual property-instances, each numerically distinct. The redness of this tomato is numerically distinct from (though exactly resembling) the redness of that fire truck. Tropes are "abstract particulars" — abstract in that they are property-instances rather than concrete objects, but particular in that each is numerically unique. Defended by D. C. Williams, Keith Campbell, and Simons, trope theory tries to secure the explanatory benefits of realism (grounding similarity) while avoiding the metaphysical extravagance of shared universals.

Nominalism holds that there are no property-entities at all — no universals, no tropes. What there is: concrete particular objects, some of which resemble others in various respects. The predicate "is red" applies to many things not because they share a common universal but because they belong to a resemblance class, or because the predicate applies to them by convention, or because they instantiate some primitive resemblance relation. This is the most parsimonious view, but it faces serious difficulty explaining natural similarity and the laws of nature without property-entities.

## The Sparse and Abundant Conceptions

David Lewis's distinction between sparse and abundant properties has shaped much of contemporary property theory. On the *abundant* conception, there is a property for every predicate, no matter how gerrymandered — "being either a quark or the Eiffel Tower" picks out a perfectly genuine property. On the *sparse* conception, there are only the "elite" properties that carve nature at its joints — the properties that ground objective similarity, feature in causal laws, and play a role in scientific explanation. Natural properties include mass, charge, spin, and the other fundamental physical magnitudes; "being grue" (green before time T, blue after) is not natural.

Lewis uses naturalness to analyze objective similarity, laws of nature, and the content of thought and meaning. The sparse/abundant distinction is itself contested: nominalists may reject sparse properties as metaphysically obscure; realists may identify sparse properties with genuine universals; and the question of what makes a property "natural" may require a further account — perhaps naturalness is primitive, or derives from causal powers, or is a matter of simplicity in total theory.

## Relations: Internal and External

Relations present a special challenge because they hold *between* objects rather than inhering in single objects. The key question is whether relations are additional entities in the ontology or whether they supervene on — are grounded in — the intrinsic properties of their relata.

*External relations* do not supervene on the intrinsic properties of their relata: *being-two-meters-apart* is external, since two objects could have exactly the same intrinsic properties yet be two meters apart or two kilometers apart. External relations are additional facts about the world, not deducible from the intrinsic profiles of the relata.

*Internal relations* supervene on the intrinsic properties of their relata: *being-darker-than* is internal, since if an object A is darker than B in virtue of their intrinsic color properties, then necessarily A is darker than B given those properties. Internal relations are "free" — they are not additions to the ontology beyond the intrinsic properties.

This distinction has significant consequences: if all fundamental relations are internal, the only additional entities required beyond objects and their intrinsic properties are the internal relations — and these may be ontologically reducible to properties. If some fundamental relations are external, they are irreducibly additional entities. Structural realists in philosophy of physics argue that spatiotemporal and quantum-mechanical relations are genuinely external and fundamental — a result that supports a relational ontology on which relations are as basic as, or more basic than, objects. Jonathan Schaffer's priority monism — the view that the cosmos is the fundamental entity and all parts are derivative — inverts the standard picture entirely: rather than objects being fundamental and their properties dependent, the whole may be more fundamental than its parts. The question of which category is foundational is genuinely open, and which answer we give ripples through the entire systematic picture of reality.
