# Facts and States of Affairs

Suppose we have the cat, the mat, and the relation of being-on. Do we thereby have the fact that the cat is on the mat? Not necessarily — all three could exist without the cat being on the mat. There is something additional that makes the difference between a world where the cat sits on the mat and one where it sits beside it: not a further object or property, but a fact, a state of affairs, the obtaining of a certain configuration. Wittgenstein opened the *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* with precisely this thought: "The world is the totality of facts, not of things." The choice to make facts rather than things the fundamental ontological category is deliberate. If the world were a totality of *things*, we would still need to say how those things are arranged — which facts obtain. Facts are the things-arranged; they are the world as it actually is rather than the inventory of things that might be arranged in multiple ways.

The central argument for facts as a distinct ontological category comes from truthmaker theory. What makes the sentence "the cat is on the mat" true? It cannot simply be the cat, the mat, and the relation taken separately — all three could exist even if the cat were not on the mat. What makes the sentence true must be something that *necessitates* it, something whose very existence guarantees the sentence's truth. This guarantor is the fact that the cat is on the mat — a complex entity distinct from its constituents. The argument:

P1. Every contingent truth requires a truthmaker — an entity whose very existence necessitates the truth.
P2. The sentence "a is F" cannot be made true by a alone or by F alone (either could exist without the other).
P3. The state of affairs *a's being F* (if it exists) necessitates the truth of "a is F."
C. States of affairs are the truthmakers for contingent predications.

## Armstrong's States of Affairs

D. M. Armstrong was the most rigorous defender of states of affairs in the analytic tradition. In *A World of States of Affairs* (1997), his ontology has three key components. First, he distinguishes *thin* particulars — objects stripped of all their properties, pure subjects of predication — from *thick* particulars — the thin particular together with all its intrinsic properties. States of affairs are formed from thin particulars and universals. Second, universals are immanent: they exist only in the particulars that instantiate them; there are no uninstantiated universals. Third, states of affairs are fundamental: *a's being F* is not reducible to a together with F, because the same constituents could exist without constituting that state of affairs.

Armstrong extends this to natural laws, which he analyzes as second-order states of affairs — a necessitation relation N holding between universals: N(F, G) is the state of affairs of F-ness necessitating G-ness, and it explains why all F's are G. Laws are not mere regularities but real necessitation relations between universals.

## Bradley's Regress

F. H. Bradley's regress argument specifically targets states of affairs ontology. If a state of affairs is composed of a particular and a universal, something must bind them together — call it the instantiation relation I. But I is itself a universal; so the state of affairs *a instantiating F* seems to require a further binding relation I' relating a, F, and I. And so on ad infinitum.

Armstrong's response: the instantiation relation is not an additional constituent of the state of affairs — it is the state of affairs itself, considered as a unity. The unity of a state of affairs is not achieved by a further entity but is a primitive feature of states of affairs. States of affairs are "thick" unities — not mere collections of their constituents but genuinely unified complexes. This is a response, but it is worth acknowledging that it stops the regress by stipulation: by declaring that the unity of a state of affairs is primitive, we foreclose the regress without explaining what that unity consists in.

An alternative response is to abandon constituent ontology. Instead of analyzing "a is F" as a complex of constituents a, F, and instantiation, treat the state of affairs as a primitive whole, with the apparent constituents being aspects of a single complex entity rather than independently existing parts. This "non-constituent" view is defended by Gonzalo Rodriguez-Pereyra.

## The Truthmaker Principle and Its Problems

The truthmaker principle — for every truth, there is a truthmaker — is the fundamental motivation for including facts in the ontology. The principle comes in several strengths. The *weak* version requires only that every contingent truth have a truthmaker. The *moderate* version requires truthmakers for every positive truth (negative truths like "there are no unicorns" may lack truthmakers). The *strong* version requires truthmakers for every true proposition, including necessary truths and negative truths.

Negative truths create the most pressure. What is the truthmaker for "there are no unicorns"? Armstrong's answer is a "totality fact" — the fact that the sum of all positive facts is all there is. Critics find this obscure: totality facts seem to be facts about what does not exist, which is precisely what we were hoping facts would explain away. General truths create similar problems: what is the truthmaker for "all ravens are black"? Not any particular raven or set of ravens — it must be something about all ravens collectively, which again seems to require totality facts.

Some philosophers want the benefits of truthmaker theory without positing facts as extra entities. Trope theory offers one path: the truthmaker for "a is F" is the F-trope that is in a — the particular property-instance — and tropes are particular entities whose very existence grounds the predication. Kit Fine and others argue that we can have a robust notion of grounding — metaphysical explanation — without positing facts as entities at all: grounding holds between truths or propositions, without those truths corresponding to extra entities in the world.

Whether facts or states of affairs are genuinely needed, or whether their work can be distributed between objects, properties, and a primitive notion of grounding, is one of the central open questions in contemporary systematic ontology.
