# Events and Processes

Objects persist through time, but things also *happen* — battles occur, migrations unfold, reactions take place, decisions are made. Events and processes are the happenings of the world, as distinct from the enduring things involved in them. Whether events deserve their own ontological category, or whether they can be reduced to objects and their properties, is a substantial question with consequences that extend well into philosophy of mind, action, and causation.

The case for events as a basic category was made powerfully by Donald Davidson, who observed that natural-language action sentences quantify over events. Consider: "Jones buttered the toast slowly, in the bathroom, with a knife, at midnight." This sentence modifies what appears to be a single event — the butter-spreading — with multiple adverbial qualifications. The logical form, Davidson argues, involves existential quantification over an event:

∃e (Buttered(Jones, e, the-toast) ∧ Slow(e) ∧ In(e, the-bathroom) ∧ With(e, a-knife) ∧ At(e, midnight))

There exists an event e such that Jones buttered the toast in e, and e was slow, and e was in the bathroom, and e was done with a knife, and e was at midnight. The adverbs become predications about the event; the event is what all the adverbs modify. This analysis explains the inference from "Jones buttered the toast slowly in the bathroom" to "Jones buttered the toast slowly" — the existential quantifier allows us to drop conjuncts. It also provides a natural analysis of causation: "The short circuit caused the fire" expresses a relation between two individual events, not between types or facts.

## Davidson's Individuation Criterion

Davidson proposed that events are individuated by their causal roles: events e₁ and e₂ are the same event if and only if they have the same causes and the same effects. This "causal criterion" of event identity has the consequence that many apparently distinct events are in fact identical.

The canonical case: the stabbing of Caesar and the killing of Caesar — are these one event or two? Davidson says one: a single event (the stabbing) was both a stabbing and a killing, described in two different ways. The stabbing *is* the killing, because the same event had the effect of Caesar's death.

Jaegwon Kim argued that stabbing and killing cannot be the same event because they are realizations of different properties. On Kim's fine-grained view, an event is a "property exemplification" — a structured entity consisting of a substance, a property, and a time. The event of Socrates dying at t₁ is the exemplification of the property *dying* by Socrates at t₁. Two events are the same only if they involve the same substance, the same property, and the same time. So Socrates's dying and Socrates's ceasing-to-breathe are distinct events (different properties), even if they coincide in time.

Kim's account faces the "explosion" problem: because any property can generate an event, and because there are abundant properties (one for each predicate), the ontology of events becomes fantastically large. Every object at every time instantiates infinitely many properties — and so there are infinitely many events involving any object at any moment. This proliferation seems extravagant.

The individuation debate has significant consequences for philosophy of mind. If mental events are individuated coarsely by causal role, they may be identical to physical brain events even if described in irreducibly mentalistic vocabulary — this is the basis of Davidson's anomalous monism: every particular mental event is identical to some physical event, but there are no strict psychophysical laws connecting mental and physical *types*. If events are individuated finely by property, mental and physical events must be type-identical for identity to hold — a much stronger and more demanding requirement.

## Whitehead and Process Ontology

Alfred North Whitehead's *Process and Reality* (1929) offers the most fully developed process ontology in the Western tradition, and it challenges the entire assumption that objects are the primary category. For Whitehead, the fundamental ontological unit is not a substance but an "actual occasion" or "actual entity" — a brief event of "concrescence" (coming together) in which prior occasions are synthesized into a novel unity through the occasion's own "subjective aim." The universe is composed of actual occasions; "things" — rocks, tables, organisms — are stable, recurrent patterns in an underlying flux rather than independent persisting substances.

Key features of Whitehead's picture: *Creativity* is the most fundamental category — the power of the new to emerge from the many; reality is irreducibly dynamic, and stasis is derivative. *Prehension* replaces causal interaction between substances with a model of inheritance and transformation: each actual occasion "prehends" (grasps, includes) prior occasions as data for its own concrescence.

Process ontology reverses the usual priority. Rather than treating objects as the primary category and events as secondary, it treats events as primary and objects as patterns. This has appeal in philosophy of biology — where organisms are best understood as ongoing processes — and in quantum mechanics — where what exists seems more like a pattern of activity than a collection of things. Contemporary process ontologists include Johanna Seibt, whose general process theory argues that classical substance ontology faces systematic problems with identity through time and with homeomerous entities that process ontology can resolve, and Nicholas Rescher, who argues on broadly pragmatist grounds that reality is fundamentally dynamic.

## Events, Processes, and Causation

The debate between event ontology and process ontology has direct consequences for the analysis of causation. On Davidson's event ontology, causation is a relation between individual events: event c causes event e. The relation is extensional — it holds between the events however they are described — but causal explanations invoke descriptions of events, and it is under particular descriptions that events can be cited in causal laws.

On a process ontology, causation is better understood as the transmission of structure through a continuous process rather than a discrete two-term relation between events. Wesley Salmon's "mark transmission" theory analyzes causation in terms of continuous causal processes and their interactions. Phil Dowe's "conserved quantity" theory treats causation as a feature of processes: a causal process is one that transmits conserved quantities — energy, momentum, charge — in a spatiotemporally continuous way. This makes causation a property of processes rather than a relation between discrete events.

The choice between these views is not merely technical: it reflects deep commitments about whether the fundamental structure of reality is best captured by a world of things that interact, or a world of happenings that coalesce into the apparent stability we call objects.
