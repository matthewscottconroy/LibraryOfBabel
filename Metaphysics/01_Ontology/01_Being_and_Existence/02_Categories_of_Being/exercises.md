# Exercises and Thought Experiments

## Exercise 1: Mapping the Categories
For each of the following items, determine which ontological category (or categories) it belongs to, and explain why:
- The color of the Eiffel Tower
- The Battle of Waterloo
- The number 7
- The relationship between a mother and her daughter
- The eruption of Vesuvius in 79 CE
- Justice (considered as a property that societies can have)
- The universe itself

Compare your answers with Aristotle's ten categories and with the contemporary tripartite scheme (objects, properties, relations). Which scheme handles all the cases more satisfactorily?

## Exercise 2: The Priority of Substance
Aristotle claimed that substance is ontologically prior to all other categories—all other entities depend on substances for their existence but not vice versa. Consider the following objections:
1. Relations seem to require *at least two* substances; so do relations depend on substances, or on the pair of substances, or on neither?
2. Mathematical entities (numbers, sets) seem to exist without depending on any substance. Does this show that substance is not really the most fundamental category?
3. Processes and events seem to be as basic as substances. Is Whitehead right that we should put processes first?

## Exercise 3: Facts vs. Objects
Armstrong says "the world is a world of states of affairs." Wittgenstein said "the world is the totality of facts, not of things." But a more commonsense view says the world is a collection of objects. Construct the best argument you can for each view, then assess which best explains the phenomenon of truth—what makes true sentences true.

## Thought Experiment: The Reversed World
Imagine a world physically identical to ours in every way but in which *events* rather than *objects* are ontologically basic—the world is fundamentally constituted by happenings, and objects are nothing more than recurrent patterns in the flux of events. Would this world look or feel different from the inside? What philosophical commitments would you need to revise if you accepted this view?

## Exercise 4: Category Mistakes
Gilbert Ryle introduced the idea of a "category mistake"—treating something as if it belonged to a category it does not. For each of the following, determine whether a category mistake is being committed and what the correct categorization should be:
- "Where does the university exist?" (asked by someone shown the buildings, staff, and students)
- "What color is the number 4?"
- "When did the average family stop having 3.5 children?"
