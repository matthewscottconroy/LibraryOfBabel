# Further Reading and Primary Sources

## Primary Sources

**Aristotle, *Categories*** — The founding text of category theory. Short and accessible; any edition with commentary works well. The Ackrill translation (Oxford) comes with excellent philosophical notes.

**Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, Book VII (Zeta)** — Aristotle's most complex investigation into substance. Requires effort but repays it: Zeta is where Aristotle works out the relationship between individual, form, matter, and essence.

**Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, "Transcendental Analytic"** — The section where Kant derives and applies his table of categories. The Guyer-Wood translation (Cambridge) is now standard.

**D. M. Armstrong, *Universals: An Opinionated Introduction* (Westview, 1989)** — An excellent, brief overview of the problem of universals by one of its leading defenders, accessible to advanced undergraduates.

**Donald Davidson, "The Individuation of Events" (1969)** — In *Essays on Actions and Events* (Oxford). The classic argument for event ontology.

## Secondary Literature

**Michael Loux, *Metaphysics: A Contemporary Introduction* (4th ed., Routledge, 2017)** — Excellent textbook coverage of categories, universals, substance, and related topics.

**Johanna Seibt, "Process Philosophy," *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*** — The best entry point into process ontology and its historical roots in Whitehead.

**Barry Smith and Kevin Mulligan, "Framework for Formal Ontology" (1983)** — A rigorous exploration of formal ontology and its connections to Husserl's material ontology; *Topoi* 2(1): 73–85.

**Jonathan Lowe, *The Four-Category Ontology* (Oxford, 2006)** — Defends a neo-Aristotelian scheme with four basic categories and argues for its superiority over reductive alternatives.

**Peter Simons, *Parts: A Study in Ontology* (Oxford, 1987)** — The classic study of mereology and its connections to ontological categories, particularly substance and event.
