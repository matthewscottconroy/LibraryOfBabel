# Kant's Rejection of Existence as a Real Predicate

Suppose I ask you to imagine a hundred gold coins, and then I tell you that those very coins actually exist. Has your concept of the hundred coins changed? Kant's answer, in the *Critique of Pure Reason*, is no — and this negative answer lies at the heart of one of philosophy's most consequential arguments. "Existence is not a predicate" is Kant's most repeated dictum in this context, and while it sounds paradoxical (for surely we predicate existence when we say things exist), Kant's point is precise: existence is not a *real* predicate, not a first-order property that enriches or modifies a concept, not something that can be built into a definition and then read off.

The context is the ontological argument for God's existence. In Descartes's clean version (*Meditation V*): God is defined as having all perfections; existence is a perfection; therefore God has the perfection of existence; therefore God exists. Kant's objection targets the second premise. If existence were a genuine perfection — a property that makes something better than it would be without it — then we could define beings into existence. But this is manifestly wrong. The hundred thalers (coins) I merely conceive are not *less* than the hundred thalers that actually exist with respect to what they are; they simply are not. Existence does not add content to a concept; it affirms that the concept has an instance.

The formal reconstruction: what Kant calls a "real predicate" is what we now call a first-order property — a property that enriches or modifies the concept of a thing, telling us what a thing is like. "Red," "heavy," "spherical" are real predicates. They add content. But "exists" adds no content — it shifts from talking about what things of a certain kind are like to asking whether there are any such things at all. This is predication at a different logical level.

## Anselm and Descartes: What Kant Was Responding To

To understand the argument, we need to understand what Kant was responding to. Anselm of Canterbury, in the *Proslogion* (1078 CE), offered the original version:

God is (by definition) "that than which nothing greater can be conceived."

P1. Even the fool who denies God's existence has the concept of this being — it exists at least in the understanding.
P2. It is greater to exist in reality than only in the understanding.
P3. If the greatest conceivable being existed only in the understanding, we could conceive of something greater — the same being, but also existing in reality.
P4. That would contradict the definition: something greater than the greatest conceivable being can be conceived.
C. The greatest conceivable being must exist in reality.

Descartes's version is simpler: God is defined as having all perfections; existence is a perfection; therefore God exists. Leibniz added the qualification that the argument works only if the concept of a most perfect being is consistent — and he thought he could show that it is.

Kant's analysis in the *Critique* (A592/B620–A602/B630): "Being is obviously not a real predicate; that is, it is not a concept of something which could be added to the concept of a thing" (A598/B626). If I have the concept of a hundred thalers and add "existing," I have not made the concept richer — I have the same concept, now with an assertion that it is instantiated. "When I think a thing... and when I say it exists, I add no new predicate to it."

## The Formal Picture

The distinction Kant is drawing maps onto the distinction between first-order and second-order predication in modern logic. First-order predication is predicating a property of an individual: "Socrates is wise" → Wise(Socrates). Second-order predication is predicating a property of a concept: "Wisdom is rare" → Rare(Wise). "Rare" says something about the concept Wisdom — namely, that it has few instances.

Kant's claim: "Exists" is second-order. "Tigers exist" → ∃x Tiger(x). This says that the concept Tiger is instantiated — it predicates something of the *concept*, not of any individual tiger. Existence cannot be included in the concept of a thing as a first-order property, because existence is a second-order fact about whether a concept has instances. Frege later stated this explicitly: existence is a second-level predicate, a predicate of concepts, not a predicate of objects.

## Plantinga's Modal Reformulation

Kant's critique has been enormously influential, but it has been contested, particularly by defenders of modal ontological arguments. Plantinga (*The Nature of Necessity*, 1974) argues that Kant's critique applies to Anselm's original but not to a modally reformulated version. Plantinga defines a "maximally great being" as one that has maximal excellence — omniscience, omnipotence, moral perfection — in every possible world. The key premise: it is *possible* that a maximally great being exists. If it is possible, then there is a possible world W where a maximally great being exists. But a maximally great being is maximally great in *every* possible world; so the actual world contains one. Therefore, God exists.

The difficulty is that the key premise — "it is possible that a maximally great being exists" — is precisely what is in question. Graham Oppy's response: an atheist who denies that God exists has equal reason to deny that God possibly exists. The modal reformulation does not escape Kant's objection; it reformulates it. The question of whether the concept of a maximally great being is possibly instantiated cannot be settled by inspecting the concept alone — which was Kant's point about existence from the beginning.

Kant's argument remains philosophically fertile precisely because the temptation to treat existence as a first-order property — and to run existence-based arguments — remains powerful in many domains. Any time we say "there are entities of type F," we need to ask: are we asserting a genuine property of Fs, or are we asserting that the concept F has instances? The distinction matters, and Kant was the first to see clearly why.
