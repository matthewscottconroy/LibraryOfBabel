# Meinong's Theory of Objects

We can think about the golden mountain. We can ask questions about it — whether it is tall, whether it is located in Europe, whether anyone has climbed it. These thoughts are genuine and meaningful, not mere nonsense. Yet the golden mountain does not exist. Alexius Meinong, the Austrian philosopher working in the early twentieth century, took this observation seriously and drew from it a remarkable conclusion: there are objects that do not exist. The domain of objects of thought is vastly larger than the domain of existing things, and an adequate ontology must acknowledge this.

Meinong's central principle is the *independence of Sosein from Sein*: an object's having properties (Sosein, being-so) is independent of whether the object exists (Sein, being-there). The golden mountain has the properties of being golden and being a mountain regardless of whether any golden mountain exists. This allows Meinong to maintain that we genuinely refer to and truly predicate properties of nonexistent objects, without this implying their existence. The theory also posits "impossible objects" — the round square, for example — which not only do not exist but cannot exist. Yet we can think about the round square, distinguish it from the square circle, and even truly predicate properties of it: the round square is both round and square.

Meinong distinguished three ontological statuses. *Existence* (*Existenz*) is the mode of being characteristic of concrete, spatiotemporal, causally active things. *Subsistence* (*Bestand*) is the mode of being characteristic of abstract objects — numbers, universals, relations, possibilities. *Aussersein* — being-outside-being — is the status of impossible and merely indeterminate objects: neither existence nor subsistence, yet the objects have Sosein. The famous formulation: "There are objects of which it is true that there are no such objects." The first "there are" ranges over objects in the broad technical sense; the second "no such objects" ranges over existents. Many things (in the broad sense) are not things (in the narrow, existential sense).

## The Philosophical Motivation

Meinong's object theory arose from reflection on intentionality — the "aboutness" of mental states. Every thought is a thought *of* something; every perception is a perception *of* something; every desire is a desire *for* something. This directedness toward an object is what Franz Brentano — Meinong's teacher — called intentionality, and it seems to be the defining mark of the mental.

The problem: many intentional states are directed toward objects that do not exist. We can think about unicorns, fear monsters, desire immortality, and believe false propositions about things that never were. If every mental state is directed toward an object, and many mental states are directed toward nonexistent things, then there must be nonexistent things for them to be directed toward. Otherwise, intentional states about nonexistent things would have no object — which contradicts the claim that every intentional state is directed toward something.

Meinong's solution: accept a domain of "objects" in his technical sense — a domain that includes existing, subsisting, and neither-existing-nor-subsisting things. The domain of what there is to think about is vastly larger than the domain of what exists.

## Russell's Objection

Russell found this ontology scandalous. His primary objection was logical inconsistency. Consider the round square: it is both round and square (by the independence principle, it has those nuclear properties). But squares cannot be round — every square is necessarily not-round. So the round square is both round and not-round. This violates the law of non-contradiction.

P1. The round square has the property of being round (Sosein independence).
P2. The round square has the property of being square (Sosein independence).
P3. Everything square is not round (logical necessity).
C. The round square is both round and not-round. (Contradiction)

Russell took this to be a *reductio* of Meinong's theory. Meinong responded that the independence of Sosein from Sein means the law of non-contradiction need not hold for impossible objects — impossible objects are precisely those that violate consistency. But this seems ad hoc: if we allow impossible objects to violate non-contradiction, we have abandoned a fundamental logical principle.

A related objection concerns indeterminacy. The golden mountain — is it tall or short? Is it located in Europe or Asia? Meinong's principle says that objects have properties independently of their existence, but what properties does the golden mountain have beyond being golden and being a mountain? The theory gives no answer: the golden mountain simply lacks a determinate height or location. But then consider: "The tall golden mountain is tall." By Sosein independence, this is true. And: "The short golden mountain is short." This is also true. But then: is the golden mountain tall or short? Neither? Both? The theory seems to make reasoning about nonexistent objects unreliable.

Neo-Meinongians handle this by distinguishing between the properties that *constitute* an object (its nuclear properties — specified in its description) and the properties it would have in virtue of being in the actual world. The golden mountain is constituted only by being golden and being a mountain; questions about its height have no determinate answer because those properties are not part of its constitutive description.

## Meinong's Significance for Contemporary Philosophy

Despite Russell's dismissal, Meinong's object theory has had significant influence on contemporary philosophy. In philosophy of fiction, Meinongian approaches provide natural semantics for sentences about fictional characters — "Hamlet is a prince of Denmark" can be straightforwardly true without committing us to a literally existing person. In intensional logic, Meinongian domains can model belief, desire, and perception contexts more flexibly than purely extensional domains. In the metaphysics of modality, impossible worlds — useful for the semantics of counterpossibles ("If water were not H₂O, then chemistry would be different") — find a natural home in a Meinongian framework. Whether the theory ultimately provides a more illuminating analysis than the Russellian alternative, or whether it trades one set of problems for another, is a live and unresolved debate.
