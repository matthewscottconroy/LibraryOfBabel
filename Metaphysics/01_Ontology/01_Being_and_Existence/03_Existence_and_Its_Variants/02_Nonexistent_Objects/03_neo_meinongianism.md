# Neo-Meinongianism

The Meinongian tradition did not die with Russell's critique. The core insight — that we can think about and truly predicate properties of nonexistent objects — has been rehabilitated by a series of contemporary philosophers who have developed sophisticated theories designed to secure Meinong's conclusions while avoiding the logical inconsistencies that troubled Russell. These neo-Meinongian theories share a common structure: they distinguish between different kinds of predication or different levels of property-attribution, so that nonexistent objects can have properties without generating contradiction.

The most influential framework is Terence Parsons's nuclear/extranuclear distinction (*Nonexistent Objects*, 1980). Parsons divides all properties into nuclear and extranuclear types. *Nuclear properties* are ordinary, constitutive properties that figure in an object's description — being golden, being round, being a mountain, being a detective. Nuclear properties are what give an object its character; an object is defined (at least partially) by its nuclear properties. *Extranuclear properties* are higher-order or ontological properties — existing, being possible, being impossible, being fictional, being thought about. These characterize an object's ontological status or its relation to minds and possible worlds.

The key principle: nuclear predications are independent of existential status; extranuclear predications are not. The golden mountain is golden and is a mountain — these nuclear predications are true of it regardless of whether it exists. But "the golden mountain exists" is an extranuclear predication and is false. The law of non-contradiction holds for nuclear properties: no object can both have and lack a nuclear property. The round square does have both round and square as nuclear properties, but it does not also have non-round or non-square as nuclear properties — the non-properties are blocked by the law of non-contradiction applied at the nuclear level. Russell's apparent contradiction arises only if we assume that an object that is square must also have non-roundness as a nuclear property, but this is not nuclear; it is derived via logical inference, and Parsons can block the inference at the extranuclear level.

## Zalta's Abstract Object Theory

Edward Zalta's framework (*Abstract Objects*, 1983) offers a neo-Meinongian structure with a distinctive metaphysical architecture. Zalta distinguishes two ways of "having" a property: *exemplifying* a property, which is the ordinary sense in which a concrete, existing object has a property (this red apple exemplifies redness), and *encoding* a property, which is the special sense in which an abstract object "has" a property as part of its constitutive description (the abstract object corresponding to Sherlock Holmes encodes the property of being a detective).

Abstract objects are those that can encode properties without exemplifying them. The number 2 encodes being even, being greater than 1, being the successor of 1, and so on. Sherlock Holmes encodes the properties attributed to Holmes in Conan Doyle's stories. Possible but non-actual objects can be modeled as abstract objects that encode properties compatible with existence in some possible world.

The key advantage over raw Meinongianism: Zalta's abstract objects are consistent — they only encode properties, and encoding does not generate the paradoxes that full predication would. Holmes encodes being a detective but does not thereby instantiate a real detective in the world. The theory allows for principled talk about fictional characters, mathematical objects, and possible individuals without ontological extravagance.

## Routley and Priest

Richard Routley (Sylvan) developed what he called "noneism" (*Exploring Meinong's Jungle and Beyond*, 1980): the most metaphysically permissive neo-Meinongian position. The domain of "items" includes absolutely everything — existing things, nonexisting things, merely possible things, impossible things. The quantifier "there is" ranges over all items; existence is one predicate among many. Routley's logical framework uses relevance logic or paraconsistent logic to handle the impossible objects that violate classical laws: contradictions are locally contained, rather than leading to logical explosion.

Graham Priest's *Towards Non-Being* (2005) is the most radical position: there are objects that do not exist; such objects can have genuinely contradictory properties (the round square really is both round and non-round); and a paraconsistent logic — his "Logic of Paradox" — handles such contradictions without explosion. Priest's key move is that impossible objects inhabit impossible worlds — worlds where contradictions are true — and we can reason about impossible worlds using a suitably extended modal semantics.

Most philosophers regard dialetheism — the view that some propositions are both true and false — as a last resort. Accepting genuine contradictions seems to undermine the very idea of rational argument. Priest has developed elaborate defenses across many works, but even among neo-Meinongians, the dialetheist position is controversial.

## Applications and the Core Objection

Neo-Meinongianism has been applied productively in the philosophy of fiction (where sentences like "Hamlet is a prince of Denmark" can be assigned truth values directly, without the elaborate paraphrases required by Russellian views), in the semantics of intentionality (where the objects of belief and perception may be nonexistent), and in the logic of counterpossibles (where impossible worlds are useful for evaluating conditionals with impossible antecedents).

The standard objection to all neo-Meinongian theories is ontological proliferation. The round square, the golden mountain, centaurs, all fictional characters, all possible but non-actual objects — all of these must be counted among the "objects" in the Meinongian domain. This is an enormous expansion.

The neo-Meinongian response: the expansion is merely of the *logical domain* — the range of the quantifiers — not of the *ontology* in the robust sense. Nonexistent objects do not add to the furniture of the world; they are not additional things that "are" in any metaphysically weighty sense. The logic recognizes them; the metaphysics does not commit to them in the way it commits to existing things.

Whether this distinction between "logical domain" and "metaphysical ontology" is coherent — and whether it suffices to block the proliferation objection — is precisely what the debate between Meinongianism and the Russellian tradition is about. The neo-Meinongians have at least shown that Meinong's core insights can be formalized in rigorous and consistent ways. Whether those formalizations illuminate genuine ontological truths or merely re-describe our logical practices is a question that remains genuinely open.
