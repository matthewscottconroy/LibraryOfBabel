# Being and Subsistence

Consider the number 2. It is not located in any particular place. It does not persist through time, since it is not in time at all. Nothing causes it or is caused by it in the ordinary physical sense. Yet we seem to make true claims about it — that it is even, that it is prime, that it is the only even prime. If those claims are true, and truth requires that what true claims are about have some form of being, then the number 2 has some form of being. But it seems to exist very differently from the way a chair or a person exists. Does this mean there are different kinds or modes of existence?

Bertrand Russell, in *The Problems of Philosophy* (1912), proposed just this. He distinguished *existence* — the mode characteristic of concrete, spatiotemporally located entities like persons and tables — from *subsistence* — the mode characteristic of abstract entities like numbers, universals, and the relations that hold in logical and mathematical truths. The number 2 does not *exist* in the sense that a chair exists, but it does *subsist*: it has being of a different kind. This was not a novel thought. Bernard Bolzano (1781–1848) had distinguished between Sein (being in the ordinary sense) and the domain of "propositions in themselves" (*Sätze an sich*) — abstract, language-independent propositions that are true or false independently of whether anyone thinks them. Meinong extended the distinction into his full theory: some entities exist (concrete, causally active things), some subsist (abstract entities like numbers, relations, and propositions), and some have neither mode.

Russell explicitly contrasts the judgment "Edinburgh is north of London" — a fact about existing cities — with the universal *north of*, which the cities stand in. The universal must have being if it is to be what the cities stand in relation to. But the universal is not located in Edinburgh or London or between them; it has a timeless, non-spatial being. Subsistence is meant to give universals a kind of being without making them concrete.

## The Difficulty: What Are the Criteria?

The main difficulty with the existence/subsistence distinction is specifying the criteria that mark it. Russell's criterion seems to be spatiotemporality: existing things are in space and time; subsisting things are not. But this raises immediate questions.

Are mathematical objects really spacetime-free? If numbers are sets (as set-theoretic reductionism holds), and sets have elements that may be spatiotemporal, does this give numbers a spatiotemporal location? Almost certainly not, but the connection is obscure. Are relations definitely subsisting? The relation *north of* holds between London and Edinburgh. Does the relation have a location? It seems to "be" somewhere between London and Edinburgh — but this is metaphor, not metaphysics. And what about abstract particulars (tropes)? The particular redness-instance of this tomato is both particular (not shareable by other things) and abstract (it is a property-instance, not a concrete object). Does it exist or subsist?

Quine's response was to eliminate subsistence entirely. The existential quantifier captures everything that is: numbers exist (if they are in the domain of our best theories) or they do not. There is no third option. "Exists" is univocal — there is precisely one sense of existence, captured by ∃x. The appearance of different modes of being traces to the different properties of existing things (abstract vs. concrete, necessary vs. contingent), not to any genuine difference in how they exist.

P1. Everything that is falls in the domain of ∃x.
P2. There is one domain, not separate domains for existing and subsisting things.
C. There is one mode of being, not two.

This simplicity is attractive. But it raises its own puzzle: what distinguishes abstract and concrete existents, if they both "exist" in the same sense? The Quinean answer is that the distinction is a matter of different properties, not different being. Abstract things lack spatiotemporal location and causal powers; concrete things have them. But this property-level explanation does not obviously exhaust what the existence/subsistence distinction was tracking.

## The Contemporary Revival

Recently, philosophers like Kris McDaniel (*The Varieties of Being and the Order of Things*, 2017) and Jonathan Turner have revived the idea of ontological pluralism — the view that there are irreducibly different modes or kinds of being. McDaniel argues from the semantics of natural language: different types of entities are quantified over using systematically different expressions, and the best explanation of why these restricted quantifiers cannot be freely substituted for one another is that they range over genuinely different modes of being. The ordinary, unrestricted ∃ is defined as a disjunction of more fundamental being predicates: ∃x Fx = ∃_c x Fx ∨ ∃_a x Fx ∨... Being is genuinely plural at the fundamental level.

Peter van Inwagen responds that the pluralist conflates different modes of being with different properties of being. Numbers lack spatiotemporal location — this is a different *property* from rocks, not a different *mode of being*. Existence is maximally general and univocal; what differs is the predicates that apply to different existents. Theodore Sider (*Writing the Book of the World*, 2011) adds that a univocal existence predicate carves nature at its joints more cleanly than multiple mode-of-being predicates, which would fragment what should be a unified notion.

Whether the existence/subsistence distinction tracks a genuine ontological difference or merely a useful classification of properties remains genuinely open. The question connects directly to the debate about abstract objects: if abstract objects genuinely *subsist* in a different mode from concrete things, their epistemological accessibility might be different in kind. If they merely *exist* (in the same sense as rocks), we need to explain why we access them so differently — through a priori reasoning rather than sensory perception. Neither answer is without difficulty.
