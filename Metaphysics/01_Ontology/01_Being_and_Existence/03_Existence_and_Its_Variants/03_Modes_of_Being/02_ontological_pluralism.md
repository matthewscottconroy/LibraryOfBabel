# Ontological Pluralism

When we say that the number 5 exists and that this rock exists, are we using "exists" in the same sense? The Quinean tradition says yes: existence is univocal, captured by the existential quantifier, and questions about how things exist reduce to questions about which properties they have. Ontological pluralism says no: there are irreducibly distinct ways of being, and the differences between numbers, rocks, events, and mental states run deeper than mere property differences — they reflect different modes of being.

The most compelling contemporary case for ontological pluralism comes from Kris McDaniel. McDaniel argues that if different kinds of things exist in fundamentally different ways, we should expect these modes of being to generate distinct logical and semantic phenomena. His formal proposal: there is a family of being predicates — Exists_concrete, Exists_abstract, Exists_mental — which are genuine being predicates picking out different modes. The ordinary, unrestricted ∃ is defined as a disjunction: ∃x Fx = ∃_c x Fx ∨ ∃_a x Fx ∨ ∃_m x Fx ∨... The restricted quantifiers are more fundamental; the unrestricted ∃ is derived from them. Being is genuinely plural at the fundamental level and unified only derivatively.

The view has ancient roots. Aristotle's doctrine of *pros hen* predication — being said in many ways with a focal meaning — is a form of ontological pluralism. Substances exist primarily; qualities, quantities, and relations exist derivatively. Different categories of being are genuine kinds of being, not merely different properties of things that all "are" in the same basic sense. Heidegger's *Being and Time* offers the most radical modern version: Dasein (human being) has a fundamentally different mode of being from equipment (ready-to-hand) and theoretical objects (present-at-hand). The traditional ontological categories — substance, property, relation — apply only to theoretical objects and are inadequate to capture the being of Dasein.

## The Debate Crystallized

To see what is at stake, consider numbers and rocks:

Numbers are not located in space or time. They do not enter into causal relations. We know facts about them a priori, not through perception. Their properties are necessary: it is not contingent that 2 is prime.

Rocks are located in space and time. They enter into causal relations. We know facts about them a posteriori. Their properties are contingent: this rock could have been elsewhere.

The pluralist says: numbers and rocks have such profoundly different characteristics that it is most perspicuous to say they *exist* in different ways — that "exist" means something different when applied to each.

The monist says: numbers and rocks both *exist* (they are in the domain of the existential quantifier); what differs are their properties, not their mode of being. "Exist" is no more ambiguous than "is causally active" — one applies to rocks, the other doesn't, but this is a difference in properties, not in being itself.

## The Monist Response

Peter van Inwagen has argued extensively for univocalism. In "Being, Existence, and Ontological Commitment" (2009), he distinguishes between the claim that there are different modes of being and the claim that different kinds of things have different properties. The pluralist conflates these. Numbers lack spatiotemporal location (different property from rocks) — this is not a different mode of being; it is a different property. Existence is maximally general and univocal.

Jonathan Turner argues from intentional contexts: the behavior of quantifiers in belief reports and desires is best explained by positing a domain of nonbeings — things with a weaker mode of being or no being at all — toward which intentional states are directed. The logic of fearing a monster or searching for the Fountain of Youth seems to require quantification over these objects, and Turner takes this to support distinct quantifier domains corresponding to different modes of being.

Theodore Sider (*Writing the Book of the World*, 2011) argues that a good metaphysics should use predicates that "carve nature at its joints." A univocal existence predicate carves more cleanly than multiple mode-of-being predicates, because the divisions between modes — abstract/concrete, modal/actual, mental/physical — are not perfectly sharp and may be context-dependent. Parsimony favors monism: why multiply modes of being when a single, univocal notion does the job?

## The Epistemological Dimension

Ontological pluralism has epistemological implications that are often cited in its favor. If abstract objects have a different mode of being from concrete ones, it is less puzzling that we access them differently — through a priori reasoning rather than sensory perception. Benacerraf's epistemological challenge to Platonism — how can we have knowledge of abstract objects if we cannot causally interact with them? — is more naturally addressed if abstract objects have a different mode of being that carries with it a different epistemic route.

The monist can respond that positing different modes of being does not resolve the epistemological puzzle: we still need to explain how the mind has access to any mode of being that transcends the causal-physical order, and the label "abstract existence" does not illuminate this. Modes of being are not explanations; they are labels for what needs to be explained.

The debate ultimately connects to what we expect ontology to accomplish. If ontology should carve the domain of quantification into natural kinds and show how those kinds differ from one another, pluralism has appeal. If ontology should identify the minimal commitments of our best theories, monism is more economical. The disagreement runs through Aristotle, Heidegger, and contemporary analytic metaphysics without resolution. That is, perhaps, evidence that it is genuinely deep.
