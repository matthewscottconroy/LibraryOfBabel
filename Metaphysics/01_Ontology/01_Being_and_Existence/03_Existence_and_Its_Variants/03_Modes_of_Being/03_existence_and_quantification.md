# Existence and Quantification

The identification of existence with the existential quantifier is one of Quine's most influential and most contested ideas. The claim is elegant: to say that something exists is to say that a variable in an existential formula can be assigned to it — ∃x Fx is true ↔ there is at least one thing in the domain D such that it is F. Our ontological commitments are whatever must be in D for our true sentences to be true. "Being is the value of a variable."

But this identification faces persistent challenge from multiple directions. In pure mathematics, we quantify over infinitely many abstract objects — numbers, sets, functions — and many philosophers are reluctant to say that all these objects literally exist in the fullest ontological sense. When we say "there could have been more stars than actually exist," we seem to quantify over possible but nonexistent stars. And higher-order quantification — "for every property F, if x has F then x has F" — seems to quantify over properties, raising questions about whether such quantification commits us to properties as entities. In all three cases, the existential quantifier and the concept of existence appear to come apart.

## Mathematical Quantification

The most persistent challenge concerns mathematics. "There exists a prime number between 4 and 6" (true: 5 is prime). If the existential quantifier expresses existence, we are committed to the existence of the number 5. But what is the number 5, where is it, how do we know about it?

Three responses have been pressed. Quinean Platonism accepts the commitment: numbers exist; they are abstract objects, justified because they are indispensable to our best scientific theories (the Quine-Putnam indispensability argument). Mathematical fictionalism (Hartry Field, *Science Without Numbers*, 1980) denies the commitment: mathematical quantification does not carry genuine existential weight; "∃x Prime(x)" in mathematics is true *according to the mathematical fiction* in the way that "Holmes is a detective" is true *in the Doyle stories*. Field attempts to show that physics can be reformulated without quantification over mathematical abstracta — technically impressive but widely regarded as insufficient to cover the full scope of modern mathematics.

Plural quantification (George Boolos) offers a third path. Some of the work done by quantification over sets can be done by plural quantification over objects: "The Fs" can refer to many things collectively without requiring a set. "There are some things such that any set is a member of one of them" quantifies over things, not over sets. Boolos argues that second-order logic, when read "plurally," does not commit us to properties or sets. The limitation: relational second-order logic and higher-order mathematics resist pluralization, so plural quantification cannot fully replace abstract ontology if the full scope of mathematics is needed.

## Modal Quantification and Possible Worlds

"There could have been more stars than actually exist" — do we quantify over possible but non-actual stars? David Lewis (*On the Plurality of Worlds*, 1986) took this seriously and posited a plurality of concrete possible worlds to provide the values for such quantifiers. Possible but non-actual stars really exist — they exist at other possible worlds, which are as real as ours. This resolves the modal challenge to Quine's equation: Lewis accepts the equation and simply expands the domain to include all possible worlds and their contents.

Most philosophers reject this expansion. Actualist alternatives seek ways to interpret modal quantification without committing to non-actual concrete objects. One option: possible objects are represented by actually existing abstract entities — propositions, sets of properties, haecceities. "There could have been a talking donkey" is true because there is an actually existing abstract object that represents a world containing a talking donkey. The modal quantification is redeemed by quantification over abstracta in the actual world. A second option is modal fictionalism: modal discourse is best interpreted as fiction — we treat possible-world semantics as a useful theoretical device without committing to its literal truth. A third option is primitivism about modality: accept ◻ and ◇ as primitive operators not analyzable in terms of quantification over possible worlds at all.

Each option has costs. Actualism with abstracta requires a commitment to propositions or haecceities that is not obviously cheaper than Lewis's concrete worlds. Modal fictionalism faces the challenge of specifying the fictionalist base without itself presupposing possible worlds. Primitivism trades one theoretical demand for another: if we take modality as primitive, we face the question of what makes modal claims true.

## The Open-Textured Debate: What Does "Existential Commitment" Mean?

A deep difficulty underlying these debates is that "existential commitment" is ambiguous. Does quantifying over x commit us to (1) x being in the domain of the existential quantifier (semantic commitment), (2) x genuinely existing in a robust metaphysical sense (ontological commitment), or (3) x playing a role in the best explanation of observable phenomena (explanatory commitment)?

Quine identified (1) with (2) — that is the substance of his criterion. But this identification is precisely what mathematical fictionalists deny: they accept (1) (we quantify over numbers in arithmetic) while denying (2) (numbers don't genuinely exist) and perhaps accepting (3) in a deflationary way (talking as if numbers exist is useful). The debate between mathematical realism and fictionalism is a debate about whether (1) and (2) come apart in mathematics, and if they do, which notion is philosophically central.

A recent trend in metaontology — "easy ontology" (Amie Thomasson, *Ontology Made Easy*, 2015) — argues that many ontological questions are easy to answer in the affirmative: we can derive the existence of numbers, properties, and tables from conceptual truths and uncontroversial empirical facts. Given that there are two dogs, it follows by conceptual analysis that the number of dogs is 2, and hence that the number 2 exists. On this view, the identification of existence with the existential quantifier is correct, but it does not generate deep ontological puzzles — it just tells us to look at the commitments of our ordinary conceptual practices.

Van Inwagen and Sider respond that easy ontology deflates the ontological questions without answering them. It tells us that *given our concepts*, numbers exist; it does not tell us whether those concepts are *tracking anything real*. The substantive ontological question is not answered by appeal to how our concepts work. Whether this objection is decisive depends on what we think ontology is trying to accomplish — whether it aims to track mind-independent reality or to systematize our conceptual commitments. This methodological question is not separable from the substantive questions about existence and quantification it is supposed to illuminate.
