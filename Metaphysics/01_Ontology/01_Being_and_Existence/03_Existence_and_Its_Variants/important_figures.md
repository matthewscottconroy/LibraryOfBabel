# Important Figures

## Alexius Meinong (1853–1920)
The Austrian philosopher and psychologist who systematically developed the theory that there are nonexistent objects—objects of thought that have properties without existing. His *On the Theory of Objects* (1904) is the founding document of Meinongianism, and despite Russell's famous critique, his tradition has proven remarkably resilient. Meinong was also Brentano's student and a key figure in the phenomenological tradition's analysis of intentional acts and their objects.

## Bertrand Russell (1872–1970)
Russell's "On Denoting" (1905) offered the canonical analytic response to Meinong. His theory of definite descriptions analyzed apparent reference to nonexistent objects as disguised existential quantification, eliminating Meinong's jungle without trace. Russell's logical analysis became the template for how to dissolve apparent ontological commitments rather than accept them.

## Gottlob Frege (1848–1925)
Frege's logical distinction between objects and concepts, and his analysis of existence as a second-order property of concepts, gave the Kantian insight about existence-as-not-a-predicate its modern rigorous form. His *Foundations of Arithmetic* and *Basic Laws of Arithmetic* are the key texts.

## W. V. O. Quine (1908–2000)
Quine championed ontological monism: "exists" is univocal and captured by the classical existential quantifier. His polemics against Meinongianism and for the identification of ontological commitment with bound variables set the tone for three decades of analytic ontology.

## Terence Parsons (1939– )
Parsons's *Nonexistent Objects* (1980) is the most systematic development of neo-Meinongianism in the analytic tradition, introducing the nuclear/extranuclear distinction to handle Russell's contradiction objection.

## Kris McDaniel (1975– )
McDaniel's defense of ontological pluralism—the view that there are irreducibly different modes of being—represents the most rigorous contemporary revival of the idea that "exists" is not univocal. His *The Fragmentation of Being* (Oxford, 2017) develops this view in detail.
