# Review Questions — Being and Existence

1. The chapter distinguishes three interconnected questions at the heart of ontological inquiry: the existence question, the category question, and the dependence question. Why does Aristotle's "focal meaning" or *pros hen* doctrine suggest that these questions cannot be answered independently? What does it mean for one category to be "primary" in a way that makes other categories parasitic on it?

2. Leibniz's Principle of Sufficient Reason (PSR) is presented in its strong form — every fact has a sufficient reason — and then in a series of weakened versions (modal PSR, global PSR, Taylor's explanatory principle). What is at stake in each weakening? Does any restricted version of the PSR still support Leibniz's conclusion that a necessary being exists, or does weakening the PSR inevitably undercut the cosmological argument?

3. Quine's criterion of ontological commitment — to be is to be the value of a bound variable — is offered as a method for determining what our theories commit us to. But the chapter raises four objections: the question of canonical language, the fictional-entity problem, the metaontological gap, and Kit Fine's distinction between fundamental and derivative existence. Which of these objections seems most damaging to Quine's criterion, and why?

4. The Frege-Russell analysis treats existence as a second-order predicate: to say that tigers exist is to say that the concept *tiger* is instantiated, not to predicate a property of individual tigers. How does this analysis explain why the ontological argument fails? And why does Kripke's theory of rigid designation create problems for extending this analysis to proper names like "Socrates"?

5. Aristotle's ten categories and Kant's twelve categories of the understanding both claim to be systematic and complete, but they differ fundamentally in what the categories are *about*. For Aristotle, they describe the highest genera of being; for Kant, they are conditions of possible experience imposed by the understanding. Evaluate this contrast: does accepting Kant's transcendental idealism require abandoning the neo-Aristotelian project of investigating categories as mind-independent features of reality?

6. Meinong holds that the domain of objects vastly exceeds the domain of existing things, and that we can truly predicate properties of nonexistent objects because Sosein (being-so) is independent of Sein (being-there). Russell objects that this generates contradictions, using the round square as his example. How do neo-Meinongians (Parsons's nuclear/extranuclear distinction, Zalta's encoding/exemplifying distinction) attempt to block Russell's objection, and does either response fully succeed?

7. Contemporary responses to "why is there something rather than nothing?" fall roughly into four camps: Leibnizian necessary-being answers, brute-fact answers, Parfit-style probabilistic deflations, and deflationary dissolutions (Wittgenstein, Carnap). The chapter argues that the deflationary responses are "too quick." What would a deflationary theorist need to show in order to genuinely dissolve the question rather than merely declare it unanswerable? Is there a principled difference between dissolving a question and refusing to engage with it?

8. Davidson's argument that action sentences quantify over events, and Whitehead's process ontology, both challenge the assumption that objects are the primary ontological category — but they do so in different ways and with different consequences. How do these challenges bear on the Aristotelian claim that primary substances are the ultimate subjects of predication? What would it mean for causation if process ontology rather than event ontology turned out to be correct?
