# Substance

Aristotle's most important insight in ontology was also his simplest: some things are more basic than others. A property like redness cannot exist on its own — it must be the redness of something, present in some subject that bears it. A relation like being taller than cannot exist without relata — things that stand in the relation to each other. But the things that bear properties and stand in relations — the substances, in Aristotle's terminology — exist on their own account, not by being features or aspects of something further. The table, the cat, the person — these are substances: the primary bearers of reality, the entities in which everything else inheres.

This asymmetry — the asymmetry between what exists independently and what exists only as a feature or mode of something else — is the heart of the theory of substance. It is an asymmetry that seems both obvious and profound. Obviously, there must be some things that exist in their own right; we cannot have properties all the way down with no properties of anything. Profoundly, identifying which things those are, and saying what it is about them that makes them primary, has proven extraordinarily difficult.

This chapter traces the theory of substance from Aristotle to the present. The historical journey is long and philosophically rich. Aristotle distinguished primary substances (this individual human, this individual horse) from secondary substances (the species they fall under), and analyzed substance in terms of the hylomorphic composition of matter and form. Medieval philosophers — Aquinas, Scotus, Ockham — refined and contested this account, debating whether haecceity (primitive individuality) is needed over and above form. The rationalists of the seventeenth century reconceived substance dramatically: Descartes posited two finite substances (thinking things and extended things) plus one infinite substance (God); Spinoza dissolved the plurality into a single substance with infinite attributes; Leibniz posited an infinity of immaterial substances (monads) with no genuine relations between them. These debates are not merely historical curiosities; they shaped the conceptual vocabulary we still use.

The empiricist critique — Locke's "something I know not what," Hume's dissolution of substance into a bundle of impressions — forced a reckoning. If substance is supposed to be the substratum that bears properties, but is itself entirely propertyless and hence unknowable, what work is it doing? Contemporary philosophy has responded with two major positions: substratum theory (there is a bare particular that individuates objects and grounds property instantiation) and bundle theory (objects just are collections of properties, with no additional substratum needed). Each has costs and benefits that continue to motivate refinement.

Beyond the central substratum/bundle debate, this chapter examines special cases of substance that raise distinctive problems: material substances and their boundaries; persons as substances, with the puzzle of personal identity over time; artifacts and functional kinds, where function rather than intrinsic composition seems to determine identity; minds and persons as a special case of substance; and the process-philosophical tradition that challenges the primacy of substance altogether in favor of events and activities as the basic ontological category. The question of substance ultimately shapes every other area of ontology: what kinds of things are there, and which of them are genuine, basic, non-derivative?
