# History of Substance

The history of substance theory is the history of philosophy's attempt to identify what is most fundamentally real — the basic bearers of existence on which everything else depends. This history is not a tale of steady progress; it is a series of powerful positions that each face devastating objections, generating successors that face different but equally serious challenges. Understanding this history is essential for understanding why contemporary debates have the shape they do.

The story begins in ancient Greece with the Presocratics' search for a single fundamental stuff — Thales's water, Anaximenes's air, Heraclitus's fire — and reaches its first sophisticated articulation in Aristotle. For Aristotle, substance (ousia) is what exists primarily: individual things like Socrates and this horse. Form and matter are the constituents of substance; essence — the what-it-was-to-be for a thing — defines what each substance is.

Medieval philosophy transformed Aristotelian substance theory in response to theological concerns. Aquinas worked out the relationship between Aristotelian substance and Christian creation; Scotus introduced haecceity to ground the individuality of substances; Ockham's nominalism challenged the ontological status of universals and essences. Each of these moves was philosophically motivated by genuine difficulties in the inherited picture.

The early modern period brought radical transformations. Descartes' sharp division between thinking substance and extended substance made interaction between them mysterious. Spinoza's monism dissolved all finite things into modes of a single infinite substance. Leibniz's monadology replaced extended substance with an infinity of immaterial, windowless simples. These rationalist positions were followed by the empiricist critiques of Locke (substance as an unknown substratum), Berkeley (the incoherence of matter), and Hume (dissolution of substance into bundles of impressions).

This section traces the key developments in this history, preparing the reader for the contemporary debates that follow.
