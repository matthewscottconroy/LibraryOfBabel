# Ousia and the Categories

Aristotle opens his *Categories* with a distinction that would shape two millennia of metaphysical thought: some things are said of a subject, and some things are in a subject, but primary substances are neither said of nor in anything else. A particular horse is not predicated of anything — it is the very thing that other predicates attach to. Whiteness is *in* the horse; "animal" is *said of* the horse; but the horse itself is neither in nor said of anything more fundamental. This is *ousia* — the term usually translated "substance," though it more literally means "being" or "essence."

The *Categories* taxonomizes beings into ten kinds: substance, quantity, quality, relation, place, time, position, state, action, and passion. Of these, substance is primary. Without primary substances — individual horses, particular people, this oak — nothing else would exist. Quantities and qualities can only exist as features of particular things. The categories are not ten equally fundamental kinds of being; they are organized around the one fundamental kind that makes all the others possible.

Within substance, Aristotle adds a further distinction. *Secondary substances* — the species horse, the genus animal — count as substances in a derivative sense, because they reveal what primary substances are. When asked what that particular animal is, the most illuminating answer is "a horse," not merely "a mammal." Secondary substances carry explanatory weight because they capture the essential nature of the things that fall under them. But they exist only because primary substances exist; the species exists in and through its individual instances.

## The Predication Matrix

Aristotle's two-by-two matrix classifies all beings:

|                           | Said of a subject      | Not said of a subject |
|---------------------------|------------------------|----------------------|
| **In a subject**          | Universal accidents    | Particular accidents  |
| **Not in a subject**      | Secondary substances   | Primary substances    |

Primary substances — this particular man, this particular horse — are neither predicated of any subject nor in any subject. They are the ultimate subjects of predication, what everything else is predicated of or is in. Secondary substances — the species *man*, the genus *animal* — are predicated of primary substances but not in any subject. Universal accidents — the color white in general — are both predicated of subjects and in them. Particular accidents — this specific whiteness inhering in Socrates's skin — are in a subject but not predicated of anything. The key mark of primary substance is ontological independence: primary substances are not in anything else.

This independence criterion became the standard definition of substance in the tradition. Descartes took it over: a substance is "a thing which exists in such a way as to depend on no other thing for its existence." Spinoza radicalized it: strictly speaking, only God is a substance, since only God has genuinely independent existence. Aristotle himself, by contrast, meant independence as a relative rather than absolute matter — primary substances depend on nothing else to be *subjects*, but they are embedded in causal and teleological relations with other things.

## The Capacity to Receive Contraries

Aristotle identifies another mark of primary substance at *Categories* 4a10–b9: substances are capable of receiving contraries while remaining numerically one. Socrates can be first sitting, then standing — he receives the contrary properties of sitting and standing at different times, yet remains one and the same individual. This temporal flexibility is a mark of substantiality. Qualities, quantities, and relations cannot do this: 4 cannot be both prime and composite; a particular color patch cannot be both red and green simultaneously.

This criterion is the root of the philosophical analysis of persistence through change — the question of how substances retain identity while undergoing qualitative alteration. It feeds into the medieval distinction between accidental change (which preserves substance) and substantial change (which replaces one substance with another), and into contemporary debates about endurantism and perdurantism.

## From the Categories to the Metaphysics

The *Categories* account seems clear: particular concrete things are the primary substances. The *Metaphysics* complicates this. At Zeta.1, Aristotle announces that substance is the primary mode of being and proceeds to ask what substance is, giving candidates: the substratum (what everything is predicated of), the essence, the universal, and the genus.

The *Metaphysics* suggests that form — the organizational structure that makes something the kind of thing it is — has a stronger claim to being substance than the concrete individual composite. The form is what the composite "is," what gives it its being, what makes it a substance at all. This apparent reversal — from the concrete individual (*Categories*) to form (*Metaphysics*) — has generated long-standing scholarly controversy. The most plausible reading holds that the *Categories* addresses the logical question (what is the ultimate subject of predication?) while the *Metaphysics* addresses the explanatory question (what makes something the kind of thing it is?). Both accounts are correct for their respective purposes.

Neo-Aristotelians in contemporary metaphysics have revived this framework. E. J. Lowe's *The Four-Category Ontology* (2006) updates the categorical scheme with individual substances, individual modes (tropes), universal kinds, and universal attributes — recognizably Aristotelian in its structure. Kit Fine has argued that essence — Aristotle's *to ti ên einai* — must be reclaimed as a primitive ontological notion not reducible to modal facts about necessity and possibility. And Jonathan Schaffer's priority monism — the view that the cosmos as a whole is the fundamental substance — is a direct contemporary echo of the debate between the one and the many that Aristotle was responding to when he first distinguished primary from secondary substance.
