# Form, Matter, and Hylomorphism

What makes this thing a knife? Not the handle alone or the blade alone — we can have those separately without having a knife. Not the material composition alone — a knife made of wood is still a knife, and a knife made of steel is still a knife, yet the material is different. What makes it a knife is the arrangement, the functional organization, the form that the matter instantiates. Remove the form and you still have matter; remove the matter and you still have a form (at least in thought); but what you encounter in the world is always the two together.

Hylomorphism — from the Greek *hyle* (matter) and *morphe* (form) — is Aristotle's answer to a cluster of interconnected questions: What makes a thing the kind of thing it is? How can something change while remaining the same thing? What distinguishes a living organism from a heap of its component parts? The central claim is that every physical substance is a composite of matter and form. Matter is the underlying stuff — bronze in the case of a statue, flesh and bone in the case of an animal. Form is the organizational principle that makes the matter into *this kind of thing* rather than another.

Matter and form are not two separate things that happen to be combined. They are better understood as two aspects of a single reality, analytically distinguishable but never found apart in the ordinary world. Pure matter without form — what Aristotle calls "prime matter" — is a limit concept, the bare potentiality to receive any form whatsoever. It has no determinate nature of its own and cannot exist independently. Form without matter is also problematic in the physical domain, though Aristotle thinks some immaterial forms may exist — the divine intellect, for instance.

## Change, Matter, and Privation

Aristotle's *Physics* (Books I–II) introduces hylomorphism primarily as a solution to the Presocratic and Eleatic puzzles about change. Parmenides had argued that genuine change is impossible: either the thing that comes to be already is (and there is no coming-to-be) or it is not (and nothing comes from nothing). Aristotle's solution invokes three factors in every change: *matter* — what persists through the change, the substrate that has contraries in succession; *form acquired* — what is gained, the new determination; and *privation* — the absence of the form before it is acquired.

When bronze is shaped into a sphere, the matter (bronze) persists; the spherical form is acquired; the absence of spherical form (the "privation") was what was present before. The bronze *was* — it existed before, during, and after the change; the spherical form comes to be, but only in matter that already existed. "Nothing from nothing" is preserved, and Parmenides' challenge is answered. This analysis distinguishes accidental change (a substance gains or loses a non-essential property: Socrates becomes tanned) from substantial change (one substance ceases and another comes to be: the wood is burned and becomes ash) — a distinction that would occupy medieval philosophy for centuries.

## The Soul as Form

Aristotle's *De Anima* applies hylomorphism to living beings with immediate and striking results. The soul (*psyche*) is the form of the natural body — specifically, "the first actuality of a natural body that has life potentially" (*De Anima* II.1, 412a27–28). This is a functional definition. The soul is not a separate substance imprisoned in the body, as Plato held; it is the very organization that makes the body a living body. A dead body is not a body without a soul; it is not really a body in the relevant sense at all — it is only matter that was once organized by a soul.

This has immediate implications for substance theory. For Aristotle, a living organism is the paradigm substance: it has a material substrate (flesh, blood, bone), a formal organization (the soul), an efficient origin (the parent who generated it), and a final purpose (the actualization of its capacities). All four causes converge in the substance, and the complete explanation of any living thing requires all four. Hylomorphism is sometimes classified as a form of functionalism — the soul is defined by its functional capacities, not by its material constitution — but it is not reductionist: the form is the actuality of which different material configurations may all be realizations.

## Prime Matter and the Unity Problem

Aristotle's "prime matter" is one of the most debated concepts in his metaphysics. It is the matter that persists through all changes of form — the ultimate substrate with no determinate properties of its own. When fire is transformed into water, what persists? Not fire-stuff or water-stuff, but something more basic: prime matter, pure potentiality. The puzzle is that prime matter has no properties of its own, yet it must be *something* that persists. Aquinas defended prime matter as a genuine (though not independently existing) constituent of material substances. Ockham doubted its existence, arguing that ordinary matter with its own properties suffices. The debate about whether there is a "formless matter" at the fundamental physical level continues in contemporary philosophy of physics.

The unity problem is equally pressing: what makes form and matter constitute *one* thing rather than two? Aristotle addresses this in *Metaphysics* H, chapter 6: matter is potentiality and form is actuality, and a potential sphere and the actuality of being-a-sphere are not two things but one thing at two stages of determination. There is no "third thing" needed to unite them — the unity of the composite is just the actualization of the potential. This is elegant, but it requires the act/potency distinction to do a great deal of work.

Contemporary neo-Aristotelian philosophers — including Mark Johnston, Kit Fine, and William Jaworski — have defended updated versions of hylomorphism as more adequate to the phenomena of organisms, persons, and material objects than either reductive materialism or Cartesian dualism. The key advantage claimed: hylomorphism captures the genuine unity of complex things (organisms especially) in a way that pure reductive materialism cannot. A heap of parts is not a substance; a living organism is. The difference is form — the organizational principle that makes the parts constitute a unified individual capable of self-maintenance and functional activity. Whether "form" is an explanation or merely a label for what needs explaining remains the central objection.
