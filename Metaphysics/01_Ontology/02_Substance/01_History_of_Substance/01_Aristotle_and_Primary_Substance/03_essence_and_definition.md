# Essence and Definition

Consider two questions about Socrates. The first: why does Socrates use language? The second: why does Socrates have exactly the number of hairs he has today? These questions call for different kinds of answers. The first traces back to what Socrates essentially is — a rational animal — and language use follows from rationality in a way that connects systematically to everything else about him. The second is merely accidental: a different physical constitution would have given him a different number of hairs without making him a different kind of thing. Aristotle's distinction between essence and accident draws precisely this line.

For Aristotle, knowing what something is requires grasping its essence — the *to ti ên einai*, literally "the what it was to be" for that thing. The essence of a human being is not tallness or skill at chess but rationality, since it is in virtue of being rational animals that humans are the kind of thing they are. Essence is intimately connected to definition: a proper Aristotelian definition proceeds by genus and differentia — humans are animals (genus) distinguished by rational capacity (differentia). This is not merely a linguistic convention but an ontological claim; the definition reveals the real structure of the thing being defined. To know the definition is to know the essence, and to know the essence is to understand why the thing has the other properties it does. Rationality explains why humans use language, form societies, pursue knowledge, and feel shame — features that fall out of the essence rather than being merely coincidentally associated with it.

Aristotle imposes strict conditions on real definitions: no circularity, necessary and sufficient conditions for the kind, priority of the definiens over the definiendum in the order of nature, and genuine unity — the definition must be a structured whole, not a list of unrelated features.

## The Modern Revival: Kripke-Putnam Essentialism

The Aristotelian program seemed to many early analytic philosophers like metaphysical excess. Quine argued in "Reference and Modality" (1953) that essential properties make sense only relative to a mode of description, not as objective features of things: "Mathematicians necessarily know that 9 > 5; cyclists do not necessarily know that 9 > 5. Is 9 necessarily greater than 5? Only relative to the description..." On Quine's view, there is no essence of the thing independently of how it is described.

Kripke's *Naming and Necessity* (1980) and Putnam's "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" (1975) changed the situation decisively. Natural kind terms like "water" and "gold" are rigid designators — they pick out the same thing in every possible world. The reference of "gold" is fixed by the actual thing called "gold" in our world; in every possible world, "gold" refers to that same substance. This means "gold is element 79" is necessary (gold could not have been a different element) even though it is discovered empirically rather than known a priori. Kripke's response to Quine: descriptions and rigid designators behave differently. "The number of planets" is not rigid (it could have denoted a different number); "9" is rigid. Modal claims made using rigid designators are not relative to a mode of description.

Putnam added the "twin earth" argument: a speaker on a planet where water is XYZ rather than H₂O would not mean the same thing by "water" as we do, even if the speaker's phenomenal experience and descriptions are identical. Meaning is not in the head. Natural kinds have essences — their microstructural constitution — discovered by science rather than stipulated by linguistic convention.

This Kripke-Putnam essentialism differs from Aristotle's in important respects. Aristotle's essence is the functional organization — the soul for living things, the form for artifacts. Kripke-Putnam essence is the microstructural constitution — molecular or atomic structure. Aristotle's definitions can be arrived at by rational analysis; Kripke and Putnam insist that essence is discovered by empirical science. And Kripke extended essentialism to individuals — Aristotle is essentially born of his parents; Nixon is essentially of human origin — not just to species.

## Kit Fine on Essence and Modality

Kit Fine has sharpened the discussion with a challenge to the identification of essence with necessary property. In "Essence and Modality" (1994), Fine argues that every entity necessarily has the property *being such that 2+2=4*, but this is not part of any entity's essence. The number 2 is essentially even; Socrates is not essentially such that 2+2=4, even though this is necessarily true of him. Essence must be more than mere necessary property — it must be a property that belongs to a thing *in virtue of what it is*, not just in virtue of necessary truths that hold universally.

Fine's preferred criterion: a property is essential to x if the real definition of x mentions it. The essence of x is what the real definition of x reveals. This is more faithful to Aristotle: essence is tied to real definition, not merely to modal necessity. Fine's framework has been highly influential in contemporary metaphysics, reviving Aristotelian essentialism in a logically precise form.

The question of whether essences are mind-independent features of reality or merely conceptual projections remains live. Social constructionists argue that what counts as a natural kind, and what counts as its essence, is determined by social practices and interests rather than by mind-independent natural structure. The essentialist response: some carvings are better than others for *explanation* — they predict, organize, and explain phenomena in ways that arbitrary carvings cannot. The division of substances into chemical elements tracks real structural differences that explain a vast range of chemical behavior. This explanatory success is evidence that the carving tracks real structure, not merely human interests. Whether that evidence is conclusive is a question that connects essence to the metaphysics of natural kinds, properties, and the laws of nature — debates that continue in full force.
