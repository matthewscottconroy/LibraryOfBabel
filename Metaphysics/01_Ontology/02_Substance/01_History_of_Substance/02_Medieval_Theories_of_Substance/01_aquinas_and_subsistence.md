# Aquinas and Subsistence

Aquinas inherited Aristotle's hylomorphism through Arabic commentators — Avicenna and Averroes — and through the Latin translations that transformed European intellectual life in the thirteenth century. His task was not merely to reproduce Aristotle but to integrate Aristotelian categories with the demands of Christian theology — a project that required genuine creative adaptation. His most original contribution: the real distinction between *essence* (what a thing is) and *existence* (that it is).

In creatures, essence and existence are genuinely distinct — a creature's essence does not by itself guarantee that the creature exists. We can understand what a phoenix is without knowing whether phoenixes exist. If essence and existence were identical in creatures, we could not understand the essence without understanding the existence. But we can. Therefore, in creatures, essence and existence are really distinct. Only in God are they identical: God's essence *is* to exist, which is why God's non-existence is impossible and why the divine nature is unlike any creaturely nature. God is *ipsum esse subsistens* — subsistent being itself — absolutely simple, not composed of essence and existence as of two distinct metaphysical factors.

This has a formal argument structure:

P1. We can conceive of what any creature is without knowing whether it exists.
P2. If essence and existence were identical in creatures, we could not understand the essence without understanding the existence.
P3. But we can (P1).
C. In creatures, essence and existence are really distinct.

The distinction is controversial — Ockham attacked it as metaphysical excess, arguing that if existence is really distinct from essence it must itself be a thing with its own essence, generating a regress. Aquinas's implicit response: existence (*esse*) is not a thing with its own essence; it is the act of being, the actuality in virtue of which a substance *is*, not itself one of the substances. The regress applies only if existence is a substance or accident; but existence is neither.

## Subsistence and Individuation

The concept of *subsistence* plays a key role in Aquinas's account of persons and substances. A subsistent being is one that exists *in itself* rather than *in another* — it does not depend on some further subject for its being. Properties and accidents are "in-beings" (*in-esse*); genuine substances are "through-themselves" (*per-se-esse*). Aquinas deploys this concept in his accounts of the Trinity (the divine persons as subsistent relations within a single divine essence) and the Incarnation (Christ's human nature subsisting in a divine person rather than in a human person of its own). These applications stretch the Aristotelian framework in ways that generated centuries of theological and philosophical dispute.

Aquinas's account of individuation for material substances follows from hylomorphism. Two humans share the same specific form (the human soul). What makes them two? Not their form but their matter — specifically *materia signata quantitate*, matter as designated by quantity, matter as actually extended in a determinate spatial location. The particular spatial extension is what makes Socrates's matter *this* matter and not Callias's.

The account has a structural limitation: it cannot individuate immaterial things. For angels, Aquinas concludes consistently that each constitutes its own species — its form is so comprehensive a realization that there can be no second member of the same angelic species. This conclusion follows from the premises but strikes many as counterintuitive.

## Scotus and Ockham: Medieval Alternatives

Scotus challenged Aquinas's individuation by matter. Matter is itself a general thing — it shares the same form (prime matter) across all material substances. If matter requires individuating, it cannot be the principle of individuation. Scotus proposed *haecceity* (*haecceitas*, "thisness") — a positive, formal individuation principle irreducible to matter or to common nature. Each individual has an individual differentia that makes it *this* individual, a feature that is not a universal property but a primitive particular. Socrates has the haecceity of Socrates; Callias has the haecceity of Callias. These are not qualitative differences but primitive individuating facts.

Ockham simplified radically: there is no principle of individuation beyond the individual itself. Individuals are simply themselves and do not need a further ground for their individuality. Universals do not exist in things or before things; they exist only as concepts in the mind. This nominalism eliminates the need for either matter or haecceity as individuators — there simply are individuals, and their individuality is primitive.

These medieval responses — Aquinas, Scotus, Ockham — each address the same problem that Aristotle raised but left only partially resolved. What makes this individual numerically distinct from that one, when they share the same essential nature? The answer matters not just for medieval metaphysics but for the contemporary debates about identity, individuation, and the persistence of particulars that occupy analytic ontology today. Sartre's famous inversion — "existence precedes essence" in humans — is a direct response to the Thomistic priority of essence: for Sartre, human beings have no pre-given nature; they first exist, and then create their essence through their choices. That the medieval framework is still generating responses in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries is evidence of how deeply it shaped the terms of debate.
