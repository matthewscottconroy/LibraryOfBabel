# Scotus and Haecceity

Here is a puzzle that Aquinas left unresolved. Suppose two drops of water form from the same cloud — perfectly alike in their specific nature, sharing every formal property that water drops can have. They differ only in location. What makes them two rather than one? Aquinas's answer invokes *materia signata quantitate* — matter as designated by determinate quantity and spatial extension. This particular drop is constituted by this matter, individuated by its specific location. The answer seems intuitive. But Duns Scotus found it genuinely unsatisfying, and the argument he developed in response remains one of the most discussed in the history of metaphysics.

Matter, Scotus points out, is itself a common nature. Prime matter — the ultimate substrate of material things — is the same in kind for all material substances; it is a general thing that many particulars share. If we need to explain what makes two drops of water numerically distinct, and the answer is that they are composed of numerically distinct parcels of matter, we have only pushed the problem back a step. What makes those parcels of matter numerically distinct? If we say that matter individuates form, something must individuate matter. The regress is real, and invoking matter to stop it does not so much solve the problem as relocate it.

Scotus's alternative is radical: what individuates any entity — material or immaterial — is a positive, primitive feature irreducible to any general properties or to matter. He calls it *haecceitas*, from the Latin *haec* ("this"): literally, "thisness." The haecceity of Socrates is not his specific nature as a human, not his matter, not any qualitative or formal property he possesses. It is the individual differentiator that makes him *this* human being and no other — something over and above the common nature of humanity that all humans share.

The first reaction is that this merely names the problem. To say that Socrates is individuated by "Socrateity" sounds like saying that Socrates is made to be this individual by his being-this-individual. Scotus's defenders have a reply, and it is worth taking seriously: every explanatory chain must terminate somewhere in something accepted as primitive. The question is not whether we posit a primitive but which primitive we posit and at what level. Haecceity terminates the individuation regress at the level of the individual itself; matter-based accounts terminate it at the level of a common substrate that generates its own version of the same problem. If a primitive must be accepted, the haecceity theorist can argue that hers is the more honest one.

## The Formal Distinction and the Structure of Individuation

To understand how haecceity fits into Scotus's metaphysics, we need his doctrine of the *formal distinction* (*distinctio formalis a parte rei*). A real distinction exists between two entities when they can exist separately — my left and right hands are really distinct because each can exist without the other. A conceptual distinction exists between two ways of thinking about the same thing — "the Morning Star" and "the Evening Star" are conceptually distinct though they pick out one object. The formal distinction is Scotus's intermediate category: two aspects of a single entity are formally distinct when they have different formal characters — different definitions or essences — but are nonetheless inseparable features of the one entity.

Consider what is in Socrates. There is his common nature, humanity — the formally characterizable aspect in virtue of which he resembles all other humans. There is also his individual differentiator, his haecceity — the formally characterizable aspect in virtue of which he is this human and no other. These are not two things, not two separately existing entities. But they are not merely two ways of describing the same thing either: "the nature that Socrates shares with Callias" and "the feature that makes Socrates numerically distinct from Callias" have genuinely different formal characters. The formal distinction is meant to capture precisely this — less than a real distinction, more than a verbal one.

The common nature, Scotus insists, is genuinely common in the sense of being really predicable of many — but as such it is neither universal nor particular. It is, in a technical sense, *indifferent* to both. What makes it universal is the intellect's abstraction; what makes it individual is the haecceity's contraction of it to a single instance. The haecceity does not add a new property to Socrates but contracts his common nature downward into particularity. It is not a quality he has but the individuating principle in virtue of which he has all his qualities as *his* rather than as generic features of humanity.

Ockham attacked the formal distinction as simply incoherent: either there are two things (real distinction) or there is one thing thought of in two ways (conceptual distinction). There is no intelligible middle ground. The debate — whether a formal distinction genuinely captures something between real and conceptual — has continued in philosophy of mind and metaphysics to the present day. Those who think that mental and physical properties are the same property described differently tend toward the conceptual side; those who think they are genuinely different but necessarily co-instantiated are in Scotist territory.

## Haecceities and Modal Metaphysics

The consequences for modal metaphysics are significant, and they connect medieval debates to contemporary ones with surprising directness. If individuals have haecceities — primitive, non-qualitative individuating features — then the same individual can in principle be identified across possible worlds. Socrates could have been a shoemaker rather than a philosopher, but it would still be *Socrates* doing the cobbling. His haecceity, which is not constituted by any descriptive properties, travels with him across worlds. This is *haecceitism*: the view that facts about which individual exists at a world are not settled purely by qualitative facts about that world.

David Kaplan made haecceitism explicit in the framework of possible worlds semantics. On haecceitist approaches, two possible worlds can be qualitatively identical yet differ in which individual plays which role — because individual identity is not grounded in qualitative features. Robert Adams, in "Primitive Thisness and Primitive Identity" (1979), argued for this on the grounds that the complete qualitative description of a possible world underdetermines its individual-facts. Two possible universes could be qualitatively indistinguishable yet differ in which individuals they contain. This motivates positing primitive individual essences that are not constituted by qualitative features but are irreducibly individual — Scotus's haecceities by another name.

David Lewis took the opposite view. His counterpart theory ("Counterpart Theory and Quantified Modal Logic," 1968) rejects transworld identity entirely. No individual literally exists at more than one possible world. When we say "Socrates could have been a shoemaker," we are not describing a world where Socrates himself exists but cobbles; we are describing a world where a counterpart of Socrates — someone sufficiently similar to Socrates in the relevant respects — cobbles instead. There are no haecceities, no primitive thisness; modal claims are analyzed entirely in terms of qualitative similarity relations between individuals at different worlds.

The debate between haecceitism and counterpart theory is one of the central debates in contemporary modal metaphysics. Notice that the structural issue is exactly the one Scotus raised: does individual identity require something over and above qualitative and formal properties? Scotus said yes, and called it haecceity. Lewis said no, and analyzed individual identity in qualitative terms. The Scotist intuition that individuals are irreducibly *this* rather than merely *thus* remains, seven centuries later, a serious and contested philosophical position.
