# Ockham and Parsimony

Scotus's metaphysics is built by multiplication — formal distinctions, common natures, haecceities, all postulated to do explanatory work that a sparser theory might accomplish without them. William of Ockham thought this multiplication was precisely the wrong direction. His program was to strip the ontology down, eliminating everything for which genuine explanatory necessity could not be demonstrated. The principle associated with his name — Ockham's Razor — is sometimes stated as "entities must not be multiplied beyond necessity," though the formulation he actually used is more pointed: *frustra fit per plura quod potest fieri per pauciora* — it is futile to do with more what can be done with fewer. The focus is on explanatory futility, not mere numerical excess. The question is always: what work is this entity doing, and can that work be done without it?

Applied to universals, this question has a definite answer for Ockham: nothing. Scotus had argued that Socrates and Plato share a real common nature — humanity — that is genuinely in each of them, though individuated in each by his haecceity. Ockham's objection is blunt. If humanity is really in both Socrates and Plato, it is either numerically one entity present in two places at once — which Ockham finds impossible — or there are two numerically distinct humanities in them, in which case they are not genuinely common to both. Neither option yields the real universal the Scotist wants. The common nature generates more problems than it solves, and the formal distinction Scotus invoked to handle the problem is, Ockham argues, simply incoherent: a distinction is either real (two things that can be separated) or conceptual (one thing described two ways). There is no intelligible intermediate. Scotus's elaborate architecture collapses if the formal distinction cannot be sustained.

What remains? Individual things. Socrates exists; Plato exists; each is a numerically distinct substance. They resemble each other — they are both human, both rational animals — but this resemblance does not require a third entity, "humanity," in addition to the two of them. It is a brute fact about two individuals. Our general concept "human" is a mental term that applies to both, but it picks out no real entity beyond them. Universals exist only in the mind, as concepts formed by abstracting features from particular experiences. They have, in Ockham's terminology, *esse objectivum* — a kind of being as objects of thought — but not *esse reale*, real being in the world. The world contains only individuals.

## The Razor and What It Requires

The distinction between quantitative and qualitative parsimony is worth making explicit here, because they track different considerations. Quantitative parsimony is a preference for fewer individual entities; qualitative parsimony is a preference for fewer *kinds* of entities. We do not ordinarily object to a theory that posits ten trillion electrons rather than ten billion — quantitative excess of that sort is fine, given that the entities all belong to the same well-understood kind. What generates genuine theoretical suspicion is positing a new *type* of entity — a new category that does not reduce to or supervene on already-accepted categories.

Ockham's Razor is primarily about qualitative parsimony. His target is not the sheer number of things in the ontology but the proliferation of metaphysical categories: common natures, haecceities, formal distinctions, real distinctions between essence and existence, multiple substantial forms in a single substance. Each of these represents an additional *kind* of entity or of ontological complexity. The question the Razor asks is whether this additional kind is genuinely required for explanation.

Ockham's answer, in case after case, is no. Consider the Thomistic real distinction between essence and existence in creatures. Aquinas argued that we can understand what a phoenix is without knowing whether phoenixes exist, so essence and existence must be genuinely distinct. Ockham's response: the argument establishes only that we can form different *concepts* — the concept of phoenix-essence and the concept of phoenix-existence — not that there are two genuinely distinct metaphysical factors in every creature. Distinguishability in thought does not entail real distinction in things. The conceptual distinction suffices; the real distinction is excess.

## Nominalism and the Positive Ontology

Having cleared away what he takes to be illegitimate posits, Ockham's positive ontology is strikingly sparse: individual substances and individual qualities. Individual substances are particular concrete things — Socrates, this stone, this tree — that exist in themselves, are not predicated of anything, and are not ontologically dependent on some further subject. Individual qualities are particular property-instances — the whiteness of this patch of skin is not the universal Whiteness but a numerically particular instance of white, a *trope* in contemporary terminology, that is ontologically dependent on the substance it qualifies.

Relations are treated with particular suspicion. When we say "John is taller than Mary," it seems to commit us to a relation — a taller-than that holds between them. Ockham argues that this apparent commitment is eliminable: the sentence is made true by John's intrinsic height and Mary's intrinsic height, with no additional relational entity required. The world's relational structure is grounded entirely in the intrinsic properties of individuals. Whether this strategy succeeds for all relations — particularly for causal, spatial, and temporal relations — is a question that occupied later empiricists and continues to occupy metaphysicians.

The resulting picture is strikingly reminiscent of what would come later. Hume's reduction of experience to particular impressions and ideas, with no necessary connections in the things themselves, follows the same structural pattern: a sparse ontology of particulars, with our general concepts understood as habits of the mind rather than tracings of objective universal structures. Locke's skepticism about abstract ideas, Berkeley's elimination of material substance — these moves repeat, in different registers, Ockham's core intuition that abstract entities postulated to explain ordinary experience are not required and should be eliminated.

## Legacy and Contemporary Resonances

The line from Ockham to contemporary debates is remarkably direct. Quine's naturalist program — attempting to show that the ontological commitments of science can be discharged without abstract entities — is Ockhamism in analytic dress. The project of *paraphrase*, showing that apparent commitment to universals or propositions can be restated as commitment only to concrete individuals, is exactly the eliminative strategy Ockham applied to common natures. Nelson Goodman's systematic nominalism in *The Structure of Appearance* (1951) made the Ockhamist program explicit as a foundational project for analytic philosophy.

Contemporary debates about scientific realism repeat the medieval argument in a new setting. Are quarks, spacetime points, and electromagnetic fields genuine posits required by the best theory? Or can they be "razored away" — either by treating them as convenient fictions or by finding paraphrases that carry the explanatory load without the ontological commitment? The Razor does not answer these questions by itself; it is a *ceteris paribus* principle, a tiebreaker when theories are otherwise equal. When they are not equal — when a sparser theory is also less explanatorily powerful — the Razor must be weighed against the losses in explanatory comprehensiveness.

Notice that the opposition between Ockham and the neo-Aristotelian tradition is not merely a disagreement about universals. It is a disagreement about methodology. The neo-Aristotelian holds that metaphysical complexity is often warranted because the explananda — the unity of organisms, the grounding of modal truths, the structure of causation — are genuinely complex and require complex explanations. Ockham holds that positing extra entities to explain what can be explained without them is futile by definition. The debate between explanatory richness and parsimony that began in fourteenth-century Paris continues to structure contemporary metaphysics, and no one on either side has yet delivered a knock-down argument.
