# Descartes' Two Substances

The Meditations begins with an experiment that every philosophy student is invited to try. Descartes asks us to doubt everything we can doubt — the evidence of the senses, the existence of the external world, even the reliability of mathematics — and then to ask what, if anything, survives. One thing survives: the doubting itself, and the thinker doing the doubting. *Cogito ergo sum* — I think, therefore I am. Notice what this gives us. It gives us a thinking thing. It does not give us an extended thing, a located thing, a material thing. We seem to know our minds more directly than we know anything in the material world.

Descartes presses this further and draws a metaphysical conclusion from it. He can conceive of his mind — a thinking thing — without conceiving of any body. He can conceive of his body — an extended thing occupying three dimensions — without conceiving of any mind. Each, therefore, can be conceived without the other. And if each can be conceived without the other, Descartes concludes, each can exist without the other. They are, in the technical vocabulary he inherits and sharpens, *really distinct* substances.

The result is the most influential dualism in Western philosophy. On one side: *res cogitans*, thinking substance, defined by its essential attribute of thought and encompassing every mental act — doubting, understanding, imagining, willing, feeling. On the other side: *res extensa*, extended substance, defined by its essential attribute of extension in three dimensions and governed entirely by mechanical laws. Secondary qualities — color, heat, taste, smell — drop out of the material world; they are merely the effects of matter in motion on minds that register them as sensory qualities. The material world is mathematized: what is really there is extension and motion, both fully describable in geometrical terms.

## The Problem That Would Not Stay Solved

The difficulty with this picture is visible almost immediately. Descartes himself acknowledged it. If mind and matter are two entirely different kinds of substance — one unextended, immaterial, not subject to mechanical laws; the other extended, material, governed entirely by mechanics — how can they interact? When a pin pricks my finger, a mechanical event in matter somehow produces a mental event (pain) in an unextended mind. When I decide to raise my arm, a mental event (the will) somehow produces a mechanical event (muscle contraction) in matter. Each direction of the transaction seems to require exactly what the dualism denies: a causal bridge between things that share no properties.

Descartes appealed to the pineal gland as the point of interaction — a small structure at the center of the brain where, he believed, the immaterial soul and the mechanical body meet. Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia, in her correspondence with Descartes from 1643 to 1649, pressed the problem as sharply as anyone: to exert force, surely, requires contact; contact requires surface area; surface area requires extension. An unextended thing cannot push or pull anything. Descartes replied that he was positing a sui generis kind of mind-body union that neither pure thought nor pure extension fully captures, but he never gave a satisfactory account of how the interaction works.

The responses to this difficulty defined the philosophical landscape for a century. Nicolas Malebranche concluded that finite substances do not interact at all — when I will my hand to move, God directly causes the hand to move on the *occasion* of my will. Occasionalism eliminates the mind-body interaction problem by making all causation divine, but it raises the question of whether Malebranche has really solved anything or merely relocated the mystery to divine agency. Leibniz's solution was different in structure: God created each substance with a complete internal program that harmonizes with all other substances. Mind and body develop in parallel because God pre-established their agreement at creation; they do not interact, they correspond. This is the clock analogy — two perfectly synchronized clocks appear to interact because they always show the same time, but they are causally independent. Spinoza dissolved the problem entirely by denying that mind and body are two substances at all. Both are attributes of a single infinite substance, and "interaction" between them is as unnecessary as explaining how the length and width of a rectangle interact.

## The Durable Problem

Contemporary philosophy has not escaped the problem that Descartes' formulation crystallized. Current debates about consciousness are direct descendants of it, and they reveal that the problem is not simply a consequence of Cartesian dualism but of the features that drove Descartes to dualism in the first place.

We can put the contemporary versions this way. Physicalists want to say that mental states are identical to physical states or functional states realized in physical systems. This dissolves the interaction problem — mental events are physical events, so mind-to-body causation is just physical causation. But the identity theorist faces David Chalmers's hard problem: even if we specify every functional and physical feature of a system, we seem to have left something out — namely, why there is subjective experience at all, why there is something it is like to be in pain rather than merely a neural state with certain causal properties. Donald Davidson's anomalous monism identifies each mental event with a physical event, but denies that there are strict psychophysical laws; mental causation is real (because mental events are physical events) but mental descriptions are not law-governed (because mental vocabulary involves constitutive rationality that physical descriptions do not). This preserves mental causation while denying the reduction of the mental to the physical — a position with obvious Cartesian echoes.

The formulation Descartes achieved in the *Meditations* was this: there are two things about the world that resist integration. Thought, subjectivity, the first-person perspective — these seem to require a different mode of explanation from anything we apply to extension, mechanism, and matter. Descartes' answer — two substances — is now almost universally rejected. But the problem that led him there, the problem of how to integrate the mental and the physical into a unified account, is still on the table. Whatever solution eventually succeeds will need to explain why Descartes' picture was so compelling and where exactly it went wrong.
