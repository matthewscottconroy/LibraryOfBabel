# Spinoza's One Substance

Descartes defined substance as that which exists in such a way as to depend on no other thing for its existence. Then he applied the definition to two kinds of created things — minds and bodies — and also, in a different sense, to God. Spinoza saw immediately that the application was incoherent. If a substance is something that depends on nothing else for its existence, then created things are not substances: they depend on God for their being. Strictly, on Descartes' own definition, there is only one substance — the one that is absolutely self-caused and self-sufficient, the one whose essence entails its own existence. Descartes, Spinoza concluded, had drawn the right definition and then failed to follow it where it led.

Where does it lead? The opening propositions of the *Ethics* (1677) work out the answer with the form of a geometrical demonstration. If two substances shared an attribute — both being extended, or both being thinking things — they would need some further principle to distinguish them, but the only candidate would be their modes, and modes presuppose substances. There cannot, therefore, be two substances sharing an attribute. God is defined as the substance with infinite attributes; any other supposed substance would share an attribute with God; therefore, no other substance can exist. *Except God, no substance can be nor be conceived*. There is one substance, absolutely infinite, absolutely self-determined — *Deus sive Natura*, God or Nature.

This is the cleanest argument for monism in the history of philosophy. It starts from a premise Descartes himself accepted, follows steps that are individually compelling, and arrives at a conclusion that Descartes would have rejected as heresy. Spinoza thought the conclusion followed from the premises. If Descartes wanted to avoid it, he needed to reject the definition, not the logic.

## Attributes, Modes, and the Structure of the One Substance

The one substance has infinitely many attributes, of which we know two: thought and extension. An attribute is "that which the intellect perceives as constituting the essence of substance." This formulation is deliberately careful. Spinoza does not say that attributes *are* the essence of substance — he says they are what the intellect *perceives as* the essence. Whether attributes are features of the substance itself or features of how the intellect conceives the substance is a question Spinoza's interpreters have not resolved, and the answer matters enormously for understanding whether his view is a form of idealism, a form of materialism, or something genuinely different from either.

What is clear is that each attribute is in some sense complete. The attribute of extension includes all of extension; the attribute of thought includes all of thought. Mind and body are not partial aspects that need to be combined; each is a full expression of the one substance in its own domain. This is the key to understanding Spinoza's response to the Cartesian mind-body problem. If my mind and my body are both expressions of the one substance under different attributes — the same individual reality described in two conceptual frameworks — then there is no interaction to explain. The apparent causal connection between mental events and physical events is not causation across a metaphysical gap; it is the single necessity of the one substance expressing itself simultaneously in two modes.

The proposition that crystallizes this is *Ethics* II, Proposition 7: "The order and connection of ideas is the same as the order and connection of things." Every physical event has a mental correlate; every mental event has a physical correlate. Not because they cause each other, but because they are one thing expressed through two attributes. My body's being in a certain physical state just is my mind's being in the corresponding mental state, described from the perspective of extension rather than thought.

Below the level of substance and attributes, everything else in the universe falls under the category of modes — particular, finite expressions of the one substance. This stone, this thought, this human being: all finite modes. There is no genuine independence for any finite thing. Every finite mode is causally determined by other finite modes (the universal causal necessity Spinoza calls *natura naturata*, nature as it has been constituted) and is ultimately grounded in the one substance itself (*natura naturans*, nature as constituting itself through its own necessity). There is no contingency, no genuine alternative possibilities, no free will in the sense of exemption from causal necessity. What we call freedom is at best the freedom of the whole — the self-determination of the one substance through its own internal laws.

## Priority Monism in Contemporary Metaphysics

Jonathan Schaffer revived a recognizably Spinozan position in contemporary analytic metaphysics. His priority monism — developed most fully in "Monism: The Priority of the Whole" (*Philosophical Review*, 2010) — holds that the cosmos as a whole is ontologically prior to its parts. Individual objects exist, but they are derivative aspects of the one whole; their natures are determined by their place in the whole, not the other way around. This is different from existence monism (the bizarre claim that there is only one thing) and compatible with ordinary commitments about particular objects. The thesis is about the direction of ontological dependence: the parts depend on the whole, not the whole on the parts.

The evidence Schaffer cites is in part empirical. In quantum mechanics, entangled particles cannot be described by specifying their individual states independently. The state of the system as a whole determines the states of the parts, but the states of the parts do not independently fix the state of the whole. This is a form of holism that priority monism captures and that standard pluralist pictures of fundamental physics — where the world is built up from independent fundamental particles — do not. Whether the argument from quantum entanglement to ontological priority monism is successful is contested; the counterpart literature is extensive. But the argument represents a serious attempt to ground in contemporary physics the Spinozan intuition that the whole is more fundamental than the parts.

The broader Spinozan challenge to substance pluralism remains: if there is one causally unified universe governed by single laws, if nothing within it is genuinely independent of everything else, in what sense do individual objects deserve the title of substance? Aristotle's mark of substance was ontological independence. Spinoza argued that nothing finite is genuinely independent; only the whole is. Whether that makes the whole the only substance, or whether it simply shows that no finite thing is a substance in the strict Cartesian sense, is precisely the question that priority monism and its critics are still arguing over.
