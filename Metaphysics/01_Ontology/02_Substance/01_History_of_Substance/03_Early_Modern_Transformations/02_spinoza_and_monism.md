# Spinoza and Monism

Take Descartes' own definition of substance — that which requires nothing else to exist — and apply it consistently. What does it yield? Spinoza's answer, argued with geometrical rigor in the *Ethics* (1677), is that only one thing satisfies the definition strictly: the infinite, self-sustaining whole that he calls *Deus sive Natura*, God or Nature. Finite minds and finite bodies do not satisfy the definition. They depend on other things — on God's continued creative activity, on other finite causes — for their existence at every moment. They are modes of the one substance, not substances in their own right.

The formal core of the argument runs through the opening propositions. No two substances can share the same attribute: if they did, we would need some further principle to distinguish them, and the only candidates would be their modes — but modes presuppose substances and cannot individuate them. God is the substance with infinitely many attributes. Any other putative substance would share at least one attribute with God, violating the preceding result. Therefore, no other substance can exist. The conclusion is stark: everything that exists is either the one substance (God or Nature) or a mode — a finite, transient modification — of that substance. *Except God, no substance can be nor be conceived.*

This is not mysticism. It is a formally rigorous argument from a premise Descartes himself accepted. Where Descartes drew back from the consequences, Spinoza followed the logic.

## The Dissolution of Interaction and the Two Attributes

The payoff for the mind-body problem is immediate. We know the one substance under two attributes: thought and extension. An attribute is "that which the intellect perceives as constituting the essence of substance." Thought and extension are not two substances that need to interact; they are two modes of expressing the one substance. My mind is the same individual reality as my body, described under the attribute of thought rather than extension. The order and connection of ideas is the same as the order and connection of things (*Ethics* II, Proposition 7): every physical event has an exactly corresponding mental correlate, not because they cause each other, but because they are the same event in two descriptions.

Causation, on this picture, runs in parallel series. Ideas cause ideas; bodies cause bodies. There is no mental-to-physical causation and no physical-to-mental causation, because there is no gap between the mental and the physical for causation to bridge. This is parallelism, and it elegantly dissolves the Cartesian problem — at the cost of denying that your decision to raise your arm causes your arm to rise in any sense that crosses the attribute-divide. The two series are synchronized, but neither produces the other.

What is it that underlies both series? The one substance, considered neither as thought nor as extension but as the infinite self-determining whole. Whether Spinoza's attributes are genuine distinct features of the substance or merely different ways that finite intellects conceptualize it — whether the attribute-distinction is ontological or epistemological — is one of the enduring interpretive controversies of Spinoza scholarship. The heterodox reading (attributes are genuinely distinct features) makes it difficult to maintain strict monism; the epistemic reading (attributes are conceptual frameworks) makes it seem like the mental and physical are projections of our cognitive limitations rather than real distinctions in the world.

## Determinism, Freedom, and the Explosive Implications

From the single-substance metaphysics, Spinoza draws conclusions that made his contemporaries regard him as the most dangerous thinker in Europe. Everything that happens in the universe follows necessarily from the nature of the one substance through the laws governing it — there is no contingency, no genuine alternative possibility, no free will in any libertarian sense. What we call freedom is not the absence of determination but the adequate understanding of the causes that determine us. A free person is one who acts from adequate ideas, understanding the necessity of their own situation, rather than from confused passive affects driven by inadequate, partial knowledge.

The theological implications are equally radical. God does not transcend nature, choosing to create or not to create; God is nature, necessarily self-expressing through infinite modes. There is no divine providence, no personal God intervening in human affairs, no personal immortality. Spinoza was excommunicated from the Amsterdam Jewish community in 1656 in the harshest terms recorded in its documents; his equation of God with Nature was read, by his contemporaries and for decades afterward, as barely concealed atheism.

## The Contemporary Resonances

Spinoza's metaphysics has proven extraordinarily productive for later philosophy, partly because his solutions to the Cartesian problem anticipate several positions in contemporary philosophy of mind. The double-aspect view — that mental and physical descriptions are two ways of characterizing a single underlying reality — recurs in Donald Davidson's anomalous monism (each mental event is identical to a physical event, but mentalistic descriptions are not law-governed) and in various forms of neutral monism (the underlying reality is neither mental nor physical but neutral, with mental and physical properties as its modes of manifestation). Panpsychist positions that take consciousness to be a fundamental feature of nature, expressed in all things to varying degrees, are recognizably Spinozan in structure.

Jonathan Schaffer's priority monism — the thesis that the cosmos as a whole is ontologically prior to its parts — is a contemporary heir to this tradition, though it does not require Spinoza's identification of the whole with God. And the broader naturalist impulse in contemporary philosophy — the demand for a unified picture of nature that does not bifurcate reality between inner experience and outer mechanism — is exactly the impulse that drove Spinoza's geometrical demonstration that one substance is all there is.
