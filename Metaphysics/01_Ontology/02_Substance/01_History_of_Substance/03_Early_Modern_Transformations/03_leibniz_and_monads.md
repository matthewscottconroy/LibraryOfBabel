# Leibniz and Monads

Descartes left matter indefinitely divisible: every piece of extension has smaller pieces, without limit. Spinoza absorbed individual substances into modes of the one infinite whole, seemingly eliminating genuine individuality. Leibniz found both positions deeply unsatisfying — the first for lacking any account of genuine substantial unity, the second for dissolving the real distinctness of persons and things. His alternative is one of the most extraordinary metaphysical systems in Western philosophy: a world composed entirely of immaterial, simple, mind-like substances, each developing by its own internal principle, coordinated with all the others by divine pre-established harmony.

The argument begins with a principle about composites. Every composite thing depends for its existence on the simples that compose it. Something genuinely real cannot depend on something else in this way — it must be self-sufficient in its constitution. Extended objects are always divisible, always composed of parts, never genuinely simple. Therefore, no extended object is a genuine substance. Whatever the fundamental substances of reality are, they must be partless — and if partless, they cannot be extended. Simple, unextended, immaterial: these are the monads, from the Greek for "unity." The *Monadology* (1714) opens with these steps, drawing from them a picture of reality that inverts common sense: what we see as matter is appearance; what is real is an infinity of mind-like points of view.

Each monad perceives the entire universe from its own distinctive perspective, with varying degrees of clarity and distinctness. Bare monads — underlying what we perceive as inorganic matter — have only the most obscure, confused perceptions. Animal souls add sensation and memory. Rational souls, human minds, perceive distinctly enough to form rational thoughts and achieve genuine self-consciousness: *apperception*, awareness of one's own perceptions as one's own. God is the supreme monad, perceiving all things with perfect clarity and completeness, and the ground of the coordination among all other monads.

## Windowless Monads, Pre-Established Harmony, and Space

Monads have no "windows" — nothing passes into or out of them from outside. Each develops entirely from its own internal principle. This raises the obvious question: if monads do not interact, why do things appear to us as if they cause each other? When a billiard ball strikes another, when a decision produces an action, what is happening?

The answer is pre-established harmony. God created each monad with a complete internal program of developing perceptions that harmonizes perfectly with the programs of all other monads throughout time. The apparent causation is not genuine causal influence between substances; it is synchronized parallel development, guaranteed by divine design at creation. The clock analogy makes it vivid: two perfect clocks set to the same time appear to influence each other — when one strikes, the other strikes — but there is no signal passing between them, only coordinated independent running.

The motivation is not mere convenience. Leibniz believed that genuine causal transmission between distinct substances is metaphysically unintelligible. If substance A causes a state in substance B, something must pass from A to B; but anything that passes would itself need causal explanation, and the regress has no principled stopping point. Pre-established harmony is Leibniz's diagnosis that the Cartesian interaction problem is insoluble, not accidentally but in principle, and that the correct response is to eliminate cross-substance causation entirely.

Space and time, on this picture, are ideal rather than real. Space is the order of possible coexistences of monadic perceptions; time is the order of possible successions. This places Leibniz in direct opposition to Newton's absolute space and time — containers that would exist even if no substance were in them. The Newton-Leibniz debate, conducted through the correspondence between Newton's defender Samuel Clarke and Leibniz in 1715–16, is still regarded as a foundational text in philosophy of physics. The relational theory of spacetime that Leibniz defended — space as a system of relations among things, not a substance in its own right — has influential modern defenders and connects to questions about the ontological status of spacetime in general relativity.

## The Identity of Indiscernibles and Its Aftermath

From the monadology, Leibniz derives the Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles: no two distinct substances can be exactly alike in all their intrinsic properties. The argument appeals to the Principle of Sufficient Reason. If two substances were qualitatively identical in every intrinsic respect, God would have had no sufficient reason to create them as numerically distinct rather than as one; since God always has sufficient reason, no such pair can exist.

Formally: ∀x∀y [∀P(Px ↔ Py) → x = y]. If every property is shared, identity follows.

Max Black's famous counterexample ("The Identity of Indiscernibles," 1952) imagines a universe containing only two qualitatively identical iron spheres — same size, composition, temperature, all intrinsic properties matching. Black argues they are clearly numerically two; PII says they are one. The defender of PII typically responds that the spheres differ in relational properties: one is five feet to the left of the other, which is five feet to the right of the first. But this requires relational properties to individuate — which seems to violate the spirit of the principle, intended to show that intrinsic properties suffice. The debate about primitive thisness versus qualitative individuation, which Scotus and Ockham fought in the fourteenth century, continues in exactly this form in contemporary analytic metaphysics, now using the formal resources of modal logic and possible worlds.

Extended matter is, for Leibniz, *phaenomenon bene fundatum* — a well-founded phenomenon, not an illusion but a structured appearance grounded in underlying monadic reality. The material world we perceive is real but not ultimately real in the way monads are. This idealism, combined with Leibniz's pioneering work on possible worlds as alternative complete descriptions of what God might have created, gave analytic philosophy many of its central conceptual tools. Kripke's possible-worlds semantics, Lewis's modal realism, contemporary work on essentialism and de re necessity — all operate in a conceptual space that Leibniz shaped, even when reaching conclusions he would have rejected. The monadology is, among other things, the origin of possible-worlds talk; the price of that origin is a metaphysics of immaterial perceivers that most analytic philosophers have declined to endorse.
