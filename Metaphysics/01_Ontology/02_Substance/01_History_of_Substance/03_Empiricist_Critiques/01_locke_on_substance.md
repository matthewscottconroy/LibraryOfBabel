# Locke on Substance

Locke is a philosopher who commits a revealing act of honesty. In the *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1689), he argues that the concept of substance is indispensable — we cannot make sense of qualities floating free without something that has them — and then confesses that he has no clear positive idea of what substance is. The qualities we observe in gold: a shining yellowness, great weight, certain hardness, malleableness, fusibility. These consistently appear together. We assume there is something that supports or underlies them, some substratum that "has" them. But when we examine this notion of a supporting substratum, Locke writes, we arrive eventually at "something I know not what." The thing we posit to explain the unity of qualities turns out to be opaque to us. We know it must exist; we cannot say what it is.

The strategy that generates this result is worth following carefully. Locke's empiricism holds that all ideas derive from experience — sensation or reflection. The idea of substance, if it is a genuine idea, must have this origin. But when Locke traces the origin of the idea, he finds not a direct impression of substrata but a process of inference. We notice that certain simple ideas regularly co-occur, group them together, and assume they require a support. We form an idea of the support — the complex idea of "substance in general" — but this idea is, as Locke acknowledges, "nothing but a supposed I know not what" that we need to posit because we cannot conceive of qualities existing "subsisting of themselves." Step by step the derivation is clean; the result is a concept so thin that it carries almost no descriptive content.

Berkeley drew the obvious conclusion: if "substance" is just a label for something we cannot form any positive idea of, the concept is empty and should be dropped. Hume went further, arguing that the concept was not even a well-formed fiction but a habitual projection. These are fair responses to Locke's own account. But Locke himself was not prepared to abandon the concept; he was prepared only to be honest about its limits.

## Real Essence and Nominal Essence

Locke's most enduring contribution to substance theory is his distinction between real and nominal essence, developed in Book III of the Essay. The nominal essence of gold is the set of observable properties we use to classify things as gold: a certain bright yellow color, heaviness, malleableness, fusibility, solubility in aqua regia. This is what we mean by "gold" for ordinary purposes. The real essence is the underlying microstructure of gold — the arrangement of its ultimate particles that causes those observable properties. We use the nominal essence for classification; the real essence is what the thing genuinely is.

Locke was pessimistic about our access to real essences. The microstructure of matter was unknown in his time and, he thought, might always remain beyond our cognitive reach. We were confined to nominal essences for practical purposes, with the real essence as a theoretical posit we knew must exist but could not characterize. The lesson he drew was that our classifications of natural kinds are somewhat arbitrary — we carve nature by observable similarities that may not map onto the underlying natural structure.

Kripke and Putnam recognized in this distinction a precursor to their own essentialism. Their claim, developed in *Naming and Necessity* (1980) and "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" (1975), is that natural kind terms like "water" and "gold" are rigid designators that pick out kinds by their real essence — H₂O and atomic number 79, respectively. What Locke thought we would never know, science has discovered; and the discovery is necessary rather than contingent. Water could not have failed to be H₂O. This is the Lockean distinction taken seriously: real essences are what they are necessarily, while nominal essences are our contingent epistemic access to them. Locke's pessimism about access was not pessimism about the metaphysical fact.

## The Substratum Problem

The substratum Locke posits generates a dilemma that has structured debate ever since. Either the substratum has properties — it has some determinate nature — or it has no properties, it is "bare." If it has properties, we seem to need something to support those properties in turn, which threatens a regress. If it has no properties, we face a different problem: a completely propertyless substratum is undifferentiated from every other propertyless substratum, leaving no account of how one bare particular differs from another. And if we cannot say how one differs from another, it is unclear that the concept is coherent at all.

Contemporary substratum theorists — J. P. Moreland, Richard Swinburne — argue that bare particulars are exactly what we need for individuation. Two qualitatively identical iron spheres cannot be distinguished by their properties (which are, by hypothesis, the same); they must be distinguished by their bare particulars, their substrata, which are numerically distinct even without qualitative difference. The bundle theorist's response is to deny that qualitatively indiscernible spheres are genuinely conceivable as two distinct objects — or to argue that spatial distinctness already provides the required difference without need for bare particulars.

What is clear is that Locke's "I know not what" identified a genuine tension in the concept of substance, one that was not created by his empiricism but revealed by it. The tradition that runs from Aristotle through the medievals assumed that we understood what a substance was — a particular concrete thing, the ultimate subject of predication, what has properties. Locke took that assumption seriously enough to ask what we actually know about the thing that plays that role, and his answer was both honest and unsettling. What has properties — the substratum — turns out to be the thing we understand least. The explanans becomes obscurer than the explanandum.
