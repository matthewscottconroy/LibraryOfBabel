# Hume and the Bundle Theory

When Hume looks for the impression that corresponds to "substance" — the sensory experience that gives the concept its content — he finds nothing. Running through his experience of a billiard ball, he finds its shape, its color, its hardness, its motion. What he does not find is a bare underlying substratum that *has* these properties. He finds properties, and only properties. Locke confessed ignorance about what the substratum is while insisting it must exist; Hume concludes that if we can form no impression of it, we can form no genuine idea of it, and the concept is empty. The *Treatise of Human Nature* (1739) develops the consequences: physical objects are bundles of co-occurring impressions; the self is a bundle of perceptions; substance is a fiction generated by the imagination's habit of projecting unity onto what is really a succession of distinct experiences.

The Copy Principle drives everything. Every simple idea, for Hume, is a copy of a corresponding simple impression. A concept that copies no impression is either complex (analyzable into components that do trace to impressions) or meaningless — a verbal form without genuine cognitive content. "Substance" understood as Locke's substratum, as a bearer of properties distinct from the properties themselves, copies no impression. Therefore it is not a genuine concept but a construct of the associating imagination. We observe qualities co-occurring, form the habit of grouping them together, and project onto this group a supposed unified bearer — a substance — that is nowhere to be found in experience. The result is one of the cleanest applications of empiricist methodology to a traditional metaphysical concept: put it to the genetic test, ask what impression it derives from, and find that it fails the test entirely.

The bundle theory of the self carries the critique to its most striking conclusion. In Book I, Part IV, Section VI of the *Treatise*, Hume reports that whenever he "enters most intimately into what I call myself," he "always stumbles on some particular perception or other, of heat or cold, light or shade, love or hatred, pain or pleasure." He never catches himself at any time without a perception; there is no self beyond the bundle. Personal identity is not the persistence of a Cartesian *res cogitans* but the result of resemblance and causal connection among perceptions over time — we find it easier to "feign" a continuous identical self than to track the complex succession of distinct and constantly changing perceptions.

## The Bundle Problem and Its Contemporary Forms

Hume's appendix to the *Treatise* contains one of the most honest philosophical admissions in the literature. He acknowledges that he cannot explain what *unites* the perceptions into a single bundle: "There is a principle of union among the perceptions, which I cannot explain." What makes all my current and past perceptions mine — part of the same bundle — rather than belonging to distinct bundles or to nobody's bundle? Resemblance and causation are necessary but not sufficient: many perceptions resemble mine and are caused by my past perceptions without being mine. The bundle theorist needs a relation of *co-ownership* or *compresence*, and Hume cannot provide one.

Contemporary bundle theorists have developed various responses to this challenge. D. C. Williams proposed that tropes — individual property instances, a particular redness rather than the universal red — are bundled by the relation of *compresence*: being present at the same spatiotemporal region. A physical substance is the maximal compresence class, all the tropes that are compresent with each other. Keith Campbell's *Abstract Particulars* (1990) develops this trope-bundle theory systematically: every property is a trope, the substance is the bundle of compresent tropes, and the familiar framework of bare particulars instantiating universals is replaced by a more economical ontology of particular instances.

The problem is that compresence alone may not do enough work. Two tropes located at exactly the same region — like the yellowness and hardness of a piece of gold — are compresent with each other; but the yellowness of the gold and the brownness of the table it rests on are also compresent at their boundary. The compresence class needs further articulation to avoid including tropes from distinct objects that merely happen to be adjacent. Some accounts appeal to causal and nomic relations among tropes: a substance is a bundle of tropes that are internally related by causal connections and natural law, not merely by co-location. Others appeal to a primitive relation of *belonging-to-the-same-thing* that cannot be further analyzed. Both moves look like steps toward the very notion the bundle theory was meant to replace.

## Substratum vs. Bundle: The Dialectic

The debate between substratum theories and bundle theories is one of the persistent fault lines in contemporary metaphysics. Both positions face genuine difficulties; neither has a clear decisive advantage.

The bundle theorist's main motivation is parsimony. There is no need to posit a bare particular in addition to properties; the substance just is the bundle of properties (or tropes). This fits better with empiricist epistemology and avoids the incoherence of a propertyless something that somehow grounds property instantiation. The substratum theorist's main motivation is individuation. Two qualitatively identical iron spheres — Max Black's example — would, on the bundle theory, be the same bundle and therefore the same sphere. But they are clearly two. What distinguishes them must be something over and above their properties: bare particulars, substrata, what contemporary metaphysicians sometimes call "thin particulars."

Bundle theorists typically respond that Black's spheres are spatially distinct, and spatial distinctness is either itself a property (difference in location) that individuates them, or evidence that absolute identity of all properties is impossible for concretely distinct things. Substratum theorists typically respond that spatial location, even if it individuates, is a relational property that presupposes the identities of the relata — and those identities must be grounded in something prior to the relational facts.

Hume's legacy is broader than the bundle theory itself. His regularity theory of causation — causation is constant conjunction, not necessary connection — launched the "Humean" tradition in metaphysics that Lewis developed into the view that the world's modal facts supervene on the distribution of intrinsic properties across spacetime points, with no necessary connections between distinct existences. Derek Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* (1984) revived the Humean approach to personal identity: what matters in survival is psychological continuity and connectedness, not the persistence of an underlying substance; and personal identity admits of degrees rather than being an all-or-nothing affair. The empiricist pressure on substance concepts that Hume applied in 1739 has not spent itself. It continues to generate, and to complicate, the most fundamental questions in metaphysics.
