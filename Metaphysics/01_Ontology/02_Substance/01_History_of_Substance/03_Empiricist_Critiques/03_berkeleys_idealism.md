# Berkeley's Idealism

Locke had confessed that he could form no clear positive idea of material substratum — the supposed bearer of qualities that he called "something I know not what." Berkeley noticed that this confession had an obvious consequence that Locke was unwilling to draw: if we cannot form a clear idea of material substance, we have no reason to posit it. The entire Lockean apparatus of mind-independent matter underlying our ideas of color, texture, and extension can be dropped. What we are left with — what experience actually delivers — is ideas and the minds that have them. Berkeley's famous thesis, *esse est percipi*, is not a piece of mysticism but a methodological conclusion: for material things, to be is to be perceived.

The argument that Berkeley calls his "master argument" appears in *Principles of Human Knowledge* §22–23. He challenges anyone to conceive of a mind-independent material object — an object that exists without being perceived by any mind. Whenever we try to form such a conception, we find ourselves conceiving of ourselves perceiving it; the supposed mind-independent object becomes the object of a mental act. We cannot form an idea of something non-ideational, because conceiving is itself a mental act and its objects are always ideas. Therefore, the concept of matter existing independently of all minds is not just unknown to us but strictly inconceivable. What cannot be coherently conceived cannot exist. Material substance, in the Lockean sense, is incoherent.

Critics have pressed the obvious reply: conceiving *of* something is not the same as conceiving it *as perceived*. I can form the thought "there exists a rock that no one is currently perceiving" — that is a coherent thought — without conceiving of myself perceiving the rock. Berkeley's response is that this objection assumes conceiving can reach beyond ideas to mind-independent objects, which is precisely what his argument denies. Whether this is a genuine reply or a circular one remains contested. But the pressure the argument exerts is real: it forces us to ask what exactly we are doing when we claim to conceive of mind-independent reality, and what grounds the claim that such conception succeeds.

## The Structure of Idealist Ontology

If material substance is eliminated, what remains? Berkeley's ontology has two kinds of entity: spirits (minds, selves) and ideas. Ideas are immediately present to consciousness — the redness I see, the hardness I feel, the sound I hear. They are passive, lacking any causal power of their own. Spirits are active; they perceive ideas and produce ideas in themselves by imagination. The world of physical objects turns out to be a collection of ideas — or more precisely, a structured system of ideas in the mind of God, produced in the minds of finite perceivers according to regular, law-like patterns.

This is where God becomes indispensable to Berkeley's system. The commonsense objection is immediate: if the table exists only when perceived, does it cease to exist when everyone leaves the room? Berkeley's answer is that God perceives everything always. The table persists because God's continuous perception sustains it. Physical objects are not collections of any finite mind's ideas but of God's ideas — or rather, God produces structured systems of ideas in finite minds in ways that constitute what we call the physical world. The laws of nature, on this picture, are God's general habits of producing ideas: it is not that fire causes burning by mechanical action on matter, but that God reliably produces the idea of burning whenever the conditions for fire are present.

Berkeley thought this picture had two advantages over Lockean dualism. First, it defeats skepticism about the external world. Descartes had raised the question of how we know our ideas correspond to mind-independent matter; Berkeley's answer is that there is no mind-independent matter for our ideas to correspond to or fail to correspond to. Our ideas just are the objects of knowledge. Second, it defeats mechanist atheism. If reality is fundamentally mental — if minds and their ideas constitute what exists — then the mechanist worldview of mindless matter operating by blind causal laws cannot even be stated coherently. Berkeley was explicit that the *Principles* was in part a defense of theism against what he saw as the atheistic implications of the Cartesian-Newtonian worldview.

## The Attack on Primary Qualities

Berkeley's argument against Locke's primary/secondary quality distinction is one of the most effective pieces of critical philosophy in the early modern period. Locke had held that secondary qualities — color, taste, sound, warmth — are merely powers to produce ideas in minds, with no resemblance between the ideas and anything in the objects. Primary qualities — extension, figure, motion, solidity — do resemble their ideas; they are genuinely in the objects as the ideas represent them.

Berkeley's challenge: try to form an idea of extension without any color or texture — a purely spatial expanse with no sensory qualities whatever. He argues this is impossible. Spatial properties are always given to us as properties of something with secondary qualities as well; we never perceive pure extension unaccompanied by color, texture, and the rest. If secondary qualities are merely in the mind, then primary qualities — which are never perceived apart from secondary ones — must be too. The distinction Locke needed to preserve a mind-independent material world collapses: all qualities are ideas, and all are equally mind-dependent.

## Kant's Response and the Contemporary Legacy

Kant recognized Berkeley as a serious challenge while refusing to accept his conclusion. The Critique of Pure Reason distinguishes "empirical idealism" — Berkeley's view, on which physical objects are just mental — from Kant's own "transcendental idealism," on which space, time, and the categories are contributions of the subject to experience, but this does not make objects merely mental. The phenomena that appear to us in space and time are genuine objects of knowledge; things-in-themselves, which we cannot know, are independent of our cognitive constitution. Kant thought Berkeley had confused the transcendental question (what are the conditions of possible experience?) with the empirical question (what exists in the world?). Berkeley answered the transcendental question and drew the wrong empirical conclusion.

The Berkeleyan tradition continued in phenomenalism — the view, developed by Mill, Ayer, and Carnap, that physical objects are logical constructions from actual and possible sensory experiences. The table just is a structured set of conditional facts: if one were in such-and-such conditions, one would have such-and-such experiences. This is Berkeley without God, which turns out to be logically difficult: the counterfactual analyses required to capture the full content of physical-object statements proved unwieldy, and phenomenalism was largely abandoned by mid-twentieth century. Contemporary idealism has seen a revival in different form: Philip Goff's panpsychism and Bernardo Kastrup's cosmopsychism are both positions that take the mental as fundamental and matter as derivative — a structure Berkeley would recognize, even if the details differ considerably from his eighteenth-century formulation.
