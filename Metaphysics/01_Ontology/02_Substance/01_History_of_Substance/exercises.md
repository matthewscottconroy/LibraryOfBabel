# Exercises and Thought Experiments — History of Substance

*Problems and reflections for working through the history of substance theory.*

---

**Exercise 1 — Aristotelian Categories**
Aristotle claims that primary substances are neither said of nor in any subject, while qualities and relations are always in a subject. Test this distinction: Is the color red in the apple, or is it the apple that is red? Does this distinction tell us something about the structure of reality or about the structure of predication? Can the distinction be made without circularity?

**Exercise 2 — The Ship of Theseus and Substance**
The famous Ship of Theseus has each of its planks replaced one by one until no original material remains. Is it still the same ship? Now suppose someone collects all the original planks and reassembles them — which is the original ship? Aristotle would ask: what is the *form* of a ship? Apply the hylomorphic framework and assess whether it gives a satisfying answer.

**Exercise 3 — Haecceity and Its Critics**
Explain the problem of individuation in your own words. Compare Aquinas's matter-based answer with Scotus's haecceity answer. Which do you find more compelling, and why? Does positing haecceities genuinely explain individuation, or does it merely give the phenomenon a name?

**Exercise 4 — Ockham's Razor**
Ockham argues that universals are mental constructs, not real entities shared by multiple individuals. What reasons might he give? What do we lose, if anything, by abandoning realism about universals? Construct the strongest objection to nominalism you can, then consider how Ockham might respond.

**Exercise 5 — The Interaction Problem**
Formulate the mind-body interaction problem as precisely as you can. Evaluate the three main seventeenth-century responses: Malebranche's occasionalism (God causes all apparent mind-body interactions), Leibniz's pre-established harmony (mind and body run on synchronized parallel tracks), and Spinoza's double-aspect view (mind and body are the same thing described differently). Which solution is most attractive? Are any of them genuinely satisfying?

**Thought Experiment — One Substance or Many?**
Spinoza argues that there can be only one substance, God or Nature. Suppose he is right. What follows for the reality of individual persons? Are you a substance, a mode of one substance, or something else? Does the answer matter for how we think about moral responsibility, personal identity, or survival after death?
