# Important Terms and Ideas: History of Substance

**Ousia**: Aristotle's Greek term for substance, meaning roughly "being" or "essence." Primary ousia are individual substances (Socrates, this horse); secondary ousia are species and genera (man, animal). The term combines notions of independent existence and determinate nature.

**Prime matter**: In Aristotle's hylomorphism, the purely potential, propertyless substrate that receives form. Prime matter is not a particular thing but the principle of potentiality that underlies all physical change.

**Form**: The organizing principle or essence that makes matter into a specific kind of thing. In Aristotle's hylomorphism, the form of a human being is what makes a particular parcel of matter a person rather than a heap.

**Hylomorphism**: The doctrine that physical substances are composites of matter (hyle) and form (morphe). The form determines what kind of thing something is; the matter is what is thus determined.

**Descartes' two substances**: Descartes distinguished two kinds of finite substance: res cogitans (thinking thing, mind) characterized by thought, and res extensa (extended thing, matter) characterized by spatial extension. God is the only infinite substance.

**Spinoza's one substance**: Spinoza argued that there can be only one substance (God or Nature) with infinitely many attributes. What we call minds and bodies are two attributes (thought and extension) of the single infinite substance.

**Locke's substratum**: Locke's notion of the "something I know not what" that underlies and supports a thing's observable qualities. The substratum is not itself perceived but is posited as the bearer of properties — a notion Locke found obscure but indispensable.

**Leibniz's simple substances**: For Leibniz, true substances must be simple, indivisible, and self-contained — the monads. They have no windows (they do not causally interact with each other) but reflect the whole universe from their perspective.
