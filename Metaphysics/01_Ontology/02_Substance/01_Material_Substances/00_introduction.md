# Material Substances

What is it for something to be a material object? The question seems almost too basic to require investigation: a rock, a tree, a human body — these are obvious cases of material objects, things that exist in space and time, have mass and extension, and interact causally with other material things. But what exactly makes something a material object rather than a property, a relation, or an abstract entity? And what makes the boundaries of material objects determinate — what determines where one material object ends and another begins?

Material substances raise distinctive problems about their identity and persistence. Physical matter is in constant flux: atoms are exchanged with the environment; cells die and are replaced; rivers flow continuously with new water. Yet the tree persists through this material flux; the river remains the same river. What makes a mass of matter count as a single persisting material object, and what determines its persistence conditions?

The philosophical tradition has approached material substances through two competing frameworks. On one framework, a material substance is a bare particular — a substratum — that bears properties and is individuated independently of them. On the other, a material substance is a bundle of co-located properties, with no additional substratum required. As applied to material objects specifically, these frameworks generate different accounts of what makes a material object the particular object it is and how it persists.

Natural kinds — the categories of material substance that scientific inquiry recognizes — add another dimension. Is a lump of gold a material substance in virtue of being gold, or is "gold" merely a convenient label for stuff with certain observable properties? The essentialist answers that natural kinds have real essences; the anti-essentialist answers that the categories are human impositions on a more continuous reality. This section develops the metaphysics of material substances with attention to both these foundational questions and their connection to scientific practice.
