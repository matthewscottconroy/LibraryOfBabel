# Artifacts and Natural Kinds

Is there a metaphysical difference between gold and a knife? Both are physical objects, both have properties, both can be named and referred to. But intuitively, gold is a natural kind — whatever is gold is gold because of what it is, independent of what we call it or what we use it for. A knife is an artifact — what makes something a knife is its functional role in human practice, not any intrinsic microstructural property. You can make a knife out of steel, obsidian, bone, or ceramic; what matters is that it cuts. The distinction between natural kinds and artifacts is real and important, but it is also philosophically contested: what exactly makes a kind "natural," and why should the artifact/natural distinction matter to the metaphysician?

The Kripke-Putnam framework provides the sharpest formulation of the natural-kind side. Natural-kind terms — "water," "gold," "tiger" — are rigid designators that pick out their kind by its real essence, not by any cluster of observable properties. Water is necessarily H₂O: in every possible world where something is water, it has that molecular constitution. Artifact-kind terms — "chair," "knife," "computer" — seem to work differently. They are functional terms: their extensions are determined by the functions objects are designed to perform, not by intrinsic essence. Replace all the steel in a knife with ceramic and you still have a knife; replace the hydrogen in water with deuterium and you have heavy water, not ordinary water. The asymmetry suggests that natural kinds and artifact kinds have different modal profiles — different necessity structures — because they have different kinds of essences.

## Positions on Artifact Existence

Peter van Inwagen's eliminativism (*Material Beings*, 1990) denies that artifacts exist as composite objects at all. The only genuinely unified material objects are living organisms; what we call tables and chairs are pluralities of particles arranged table-wise or chair-wise, without any genuine substantial unity. This resolves puzzles about artifact identity — when does a repaired knife cease to be the original knife? — by denying that the knife is an object whose identity can be asked about. The cost is radical revisionism: van Inwagen tells us that when we say "there is a table," we are not, strictly speaking, saying anything true.

The constitution view (Amie Thomasson, Lynne Rudder Baker) is more permissive. Artifacts exist as genuinely real objects with genuine identity conditions, but their identity conditions are tied to their functional and historical origins. The knife exists because it was made for the function of cutting, by these craftspeople, from these materials; it persists as long as it can fulfill that function (with reasonable tolerance for repair). Thomasson's *Ordinary Objects* (2007) argues that eliminativist attempts to "explain away" artifacts in terms of particles arranged artifact-wise are methodologically confused: the existence conditions for artifacts are tied to human practices, but that does not make artifacts any less real — it makes them a different kind of real thing from water or gold.

Essentialist functionalism takes the middle path: artifacts have real essences, but their essences are functional rather than microstructural. A knife is essentially a cutting instrument; this is a necessary truth about the kind *knife*, even if the material basis of any particular knife is accidental. The essence is real (it supports necessary truths), but it is constituted by function rather than by underlying physical structure.

## The Boundary Problem

The artifact/natural-kind distinction faces a challenge from cases that seem to fall on both sides. Cultivated varieties of wheat are biological organisms with a natural chemical constitution, but they were created by intentional selective breeding — an artifact process producing a naturally characterized result. Elements synthesized in particle accelerators (einsteinium, tennessine) are natural kinds by their atomic structure but artificial by origin. Social objects — money, nations, marriages — are human artifacts in a broad sense, but their existence conditions are more complex than ordinary physical artifacts: a dollar's being money is not a physical fact but a social one.

These cases suggest the distinction is not binary. It tracks several dimensions — microstructural essence, functional determination, intentional origin, social constitution — that typically co-vary in paradigm cases but can come apart. Natural kinds paradigmatically have microstructural essences, non-intentional origins, and observer-independent properties. Artifacts paradigmatically have functional essences, intentional origins, and observer-dependent identity conditions. The interesting cases are those where these dimensions come apart, and working out what to say about them forces us to be more precise about which dimension of the distinction is doing which explanatory work.

John Searle's analysis of social objects in *The Construction of Social Reality* (1995) shows the complexity at the socially constituted end. Social artifacts are created by constitutive rules of the form "X counts as Y in context C": certain pieces of paper count as money, certain organizational structures count as corporations, certain human relationships count as marriages. What distinguishes social artifacts from physical artifacts is that their existence conditions are entirely institutional — there is no physical substrate that makes something money in the way there is a substrate that makes something gold. Whether social artifacts have genuine essences determined by the rules that constitute them, or only nominal essences, or no essences at all, is an open question connecting the metaphysics of artifacts to social ontology and political philosophy.
