# Persons as Substances

Are you a substance? The question sounds almost absurd — of course you are; you are the paradigm case of a thing that exists. But the philosophical question is more specific: are you a *primary* substance in the Aristotelian sense, something that exists in its own right and is not merely a feature or aspect of something else? And if you are, what kind of substance are you — a material substance (a human animal), a mental substance (a mind or soul), or something else entirely?

The question of persons as substances connects to virtually every major debate in philosophy of mind, personal identity, and ethics. If persons are substances with determinate persistence conditions, then there are facts about when a person begins (with conception? with birth? with the development of self-consciousness?) and when a person ends (with biological death? with the permanent cessation of psychological continuity?) that do not depend merely on our conventions. If persons are not substances — if the appearance of a unified, persisting self is generated by more primitive facts rather than being a genuinely basic feature of the world — then personal identity may be less deep, and perhaps less important, than we typically suppose.

Animalism holds that persons are human animals — that I am fundamentally a biological organism, and that the persistence conditions of persons are the persistence conditions of the organism. This view is ontologically economical (one substance, not two) but faces challenges from cases of brain transplant and from the intuition that what I most fundamentally am is not my body but my psychological life.

Psychological continuity theories hold that persons persist in virtue of overlapping chains of psychological connections — memories, intentions, beliefs, desires. This handles the intuitions that animalism struggles with but faces the challenge of explaining why psychological continuity is what matters for survival rather than biological continuity. This section develops the metaphysics of persons as substances and the tensions among the leading positions.
