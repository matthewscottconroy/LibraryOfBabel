# What Makes a Person

The concept of a person does not simply coincide with the concept of a human being, and recognizing this is the starting point for one of philosophy's most practically consequential debates. A human being is a biological organism of a particular species. A person, in the philosophically relevant sense, is a being with a particular kind of moral and metaphysical standing — a being who is an end in itself rather than merely a means, a being who can be held responsible, a being for whom things can go well or badly in the deepest sense. The two concepts typically co-extend — most humans are persons, most persons we know are humans — but they need not: there might be non-human persons (sufficiently sophisticated artificial minds, rational aliens), and there are disputed cases of human non-persons (early-term fetuses, individuals in persistent vegetative states). Getting clear on what makes something a person matters not only philosophically but ethically.

Locke introduced the distinctly psychological account in *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* Book II Chapter 27. A person is "a thinking intelligent being that has reason and reflection, and can consider itself as itself, the same thinking thing, in different times and places." Three features: rationality, self-consciousness, and the capacity for temporal self-identification. The account is explicitly *forensic* — "person" is, for Locke, a term of moral and legal accountability. Persons are beings who can be held responsible for their actions, praised and blamed, punished and rewarded. The persistence conditions for persons follow from the psychological account: two temporal stages belong to the same person if consciousness — especially memory — extends from one to the other.

Kant's account builds on this but takes it in a different direction. Personhood for Kant is grounded in rational autonomy — the capacity to give oneself the moral law through pure practical reason. A person is an end in itself, not merely a means. The Formula of Humanity in the *Groundwork* (1785): treat humanity — rational nature — always as an end and never merely as a means. The criterion is normative through and through: what makes a being a person, on Kant's account, is not any particular psychological configuration but participation in the moral law, the capacity for rational self-governance. This grounds the unconditional dignity of persons in a way that does not depend on the degree or development of psychological capacities.

## Contemporary Complications and the Neo-Aristotelian Response

The difficulty with both psychological accounts is well-known: they risk excluding from personhood beings we intuitively regard as persons. Infants lack robust self-consciousness and memory-based temporal self-identification; severely cognitively disabled individuals may lack rationality in the relevant sense; people in dreamless sleep lack consciousness entirely. If personhood requires current exercise of these capacities, none of these individuals is currently a person. The conclusion is unacceptable; something must give.

Mary Anne Warren's influential 1973 account proposed five criteria — consciousness, reasoning capacity, self-motivated activity, communicative capacity, and self-awareness — and suggested that full personhood requires most of them, with graded moral status for those who possess some. This allows for a spectrum but raises the uncomfortable implication that animals with well-developed psychologies (great apes, dolphins) might have stronger personhood claims than some humans.

The neo-Aristotelian alternative grounds personhood in natural-kind membership rather than individual psychological capacity. An entity is a person if it instantiates a kind whose characteristic members are rational, self-conscious beings. The kind — not the individual exercise — carries the normative weight. This preserves personhood for infants, severely cognitively disabled individuals, and temporarily unconscious people, because they belong to a kind (*human person*, or *rational animal*) whose characteristic members are rational and self-conscious, even when those members are not currently exercising those capacities. The formal structure:

P1. An entity is a person if it instantiates a kind whose characteristic members are rational, self-conscious beings.
P2. Human infants and severely cognitively disabled individuals instantiate the kind *human person*.
P3. Normal members of the kind *human person* are rational and self-conscious.
C. Human infants and severely cognitively disabled individuals are persons.

This view ties personhood to kind membership rather than to token-level psychological facts. Whether it adequately captures the reasons we care about persons — or whether it simply relocates the difficulty by requiring an account of which kinds are person-kinds — is the central challenge it faces.

## The Practical Stakes

The metaphysical and moral questions about personhood are intertwined but distinguishable, and the practical stakes are significant. Abortion, euthanasia, the moral status of animals, the treatment of individuals with severe dementia, and the potential moral status of artificial intelligence all turn on how the underlying metaphysical questions are answered. If personhood is psychological, early fetuses are not persons but sufficiently developed primates may be. If personhood is tied to human biological kind membership, early fetuses are persons but the grounds for extending moral status to animals are harder to establish. If personhood is grounded in rational autonomy, the criterion applies in principle to artificial minds but leaves unclear whether it applies to non-rational humans.

These are not questions with clean philosophical resolutions that simply need to be communicated to the public. They are genuinely hard, and the difficulty is not just technical — it reflects deep uncertainty about what features of beings ground the obligations we have toward them. What the philosophical analysis can do is clarify the structure of the debate, identify hidden assumptions, and show which arguments are valid and which are not. Whether the conceptual clarification translates into better practical judgments is itself a philosophically interesting question.
