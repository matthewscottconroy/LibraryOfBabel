# Animalism and Its Rivals

There is a human animal in your chair reading this text. That animal is thinking these thoughts. Are you that animal, or are you something else — a person, a mind, a psychological continuant — who is distinct from but coincides with the animal? Eric Olson's animalism says you are the animal. The argument for this is blunt and effective: if you are not the animal, there are two distinct thinking things in your chair — you and the animal. But there cannot be two distinct thinking things in the same place; the multiplication of thinkers is absurd. Therefore, you are the animal. The view has a Ockhamist simplicity: it posits only one kind of entity — biological organisms — and identifies persons with a subclass of them.

The implications of animalism are distinctive and, in some cases, counterintuitive. Personal identity over time just is organismic identity. Psychological continuity — sameness of memories, personality, beliefs, desires — is not what grounds your persistence; your biological continuity as a living organism is. You were once an infant who had none of your current beliefs or memories, and you might in principle survive complete psychological disruption — say, the gradual replacement of one personality by another through severe amnesia — while remaining numerically the same organism and hence the same person.

Conversely, the animalist must say something uncomfortable about brain-transplant thought experiments. If a surgeon transplanted your entire brain into another body, most people's intuitions say you would go with the brain — the person is where the psychology is. Animalism says the opposite: the organism remains with the original body (or ceases to exist if the brain is entirely removed), and the "person" who wakes up with your memories and personality in the new body is not you but a new organism with your psychological history. This bullet biting strikes many as the decisive objection to animalism.

## Psychological Continuity Theories

The rival tradition grounds personal identity in psychological rather than biological facts. Locke's memory account — personal identity just is the continuity of consciousness, especially memory — was refined by Parfit in *Reasons and Persons* (1984) into the concept of Relation R: psychological continuity-and-connectedness. Direct psychological connections (memories, intentions, beliefs, desires) chain together over time to form the psychological continuity that constitutes personal identity. Where no such chain holds, identity does not hold; but Relation R can hold without strict identity (in fission cases, where a single person splits into two psychological continuants), which is why Parfit concluded that identity is not what matters in survival — Relation R is.

A different psychological account emphasizes narrative. Marya Schechtman (*The Constitution of Selves*, 1996) argues that personal identity consists in having a continuous self-narrative: a coherent story told from one's own perspective that integrates one's experiences and actions over time. What makes you the same person as the infant in an old photograph is not merely a causal chain of psychological connections but the narrative unity of a life — the way that infant's experiences are owned and interpreted as part of the same ongoing story that is yours. This approach is less reductive than Parfit's and more sensitive to the phenomenology of self-understanding, but it raises questions about what happens when self-narrative fails (severe dementia, confabulation) and about how objectively determinable the relevant narrative unity is.

Stage theorists (Sider, Hawley) dissolve the debate by rejecting the assumption that persons are enduring three-dimensional things at all. What we call a person is either a four-dimensional temporal worm (perdurantism) or a current temporal stage related to past and future stages by psychological counterpart relations (stage theory). "You" refers to a current stage; your relation to past and future stages is not numerical identity but a counterpart relation grounded in psychological continuity. This handles fission cases cleanly — the pre-fission stage is the first stage of two distinct but overlapping worms — but at the cost of saying that you do not literally persist through time; you are instantaneous, and what we ordinarily call your persistence is really a series of related stages.

## The Practical Dimensions

The practical implications are significant enough to make this more than an academic dispute. If animalism is true, biological life matters more than psychological continuity for the persistence of persons, with consequences for abortion (does personal existence begin when the organism begins?), end-of-life care (a patient in a persistent vegetative state — biologically alive, psychologically absent — is on animalism the same person as the one who was conscious before), and the ethics of radical cognitive enhancement.

If psychological continuity theories are true, what matters tracks the psychological rather than the biological, raising the possibility that a sufficiently advanced artificial system with appropriate psychological connections to a human person might in some sense *be* that person — or at least stand in the relation that matters for personal survival. And if narrative identity is what grounds personhood, then beings with severely disrupted self-narratives — through dementia, psychosis, or radical conversion — raise questions about whether what persists is really the same person.

What the debate makes clear is that the concept of personal identity is not one concept but several, and separating them — persistence through time, what matters in survival, the grounds of moral status, narrative unity — is philosophically necessary before practical conclusions can be drawn.
