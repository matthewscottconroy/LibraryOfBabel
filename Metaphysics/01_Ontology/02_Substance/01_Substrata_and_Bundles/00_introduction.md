# Substrata and Bundles

What is an ordinary material object, at its most fundamental ontological level? Two great theories have competed in answering this question. Substratum theory holds that every object has two ontological components: its properties (the features that characterize it) and a substratum (the entity that bears those properties and individuates the object). The substratum is what holds the properties together and makes them the properties of this particular object; without it, the properties would be floating free, not belonging to anything. Bundle theory holds that there is no substratum — the object just is the bundle of its co-located properties, a complex of features with no additional "bare" bearer.

The substratum theory has intuitive appeal: it explains how two qualitatively identical objects can be numerically distinct (they have different substrata), and it explains what we mean when we say that an object "has" or "exemplifies" a property (the substratum instantiates the property). Its fundamental difficulty is the infamous "bare particular": a substratum that has no intrinsic properties of its own is, precisely, a nothing — how can it do the work of individuating objects and grounding property instantiation?

Bundle theory avoids the mystery of the bare particular but faces the challenge of explaining numerical diversity without any non-qualitative individuator. If objects just are bundles of properties, then two objects with exactly the same properties should be identical — since they would be the same bundle. This is essentially the challenge posed by Black's two-sphere thought experiment. Bundle theorists have responded by denying the possibility of such cases (by appeal to Leibniz's Principle), by building spatial location into the bundle, or by admitting that their view has this consequence.

This section develops both theories in detail, examines the classical objections and responses, and asks what the persistent difficulty of choosing between them reveals about our deepest assumptions concerning what objects are.
