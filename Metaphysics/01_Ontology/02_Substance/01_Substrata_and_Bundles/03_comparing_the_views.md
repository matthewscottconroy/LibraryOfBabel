# Comparing Substratum and Bundle Theories

Here is the dialectical situation. The substratum theorist says: there must be something in addition to an object's properties that individuates it — a bare particular, a non-qualitative thisness. The bundle theorist says: that "something in addition" is either a fiction or is simply the properties organized in a certain way; there is no epistemically accessible, metaphysically respectable entity you are pointing to. Each side can make this accusation: the substratum theorist accuses the bundle theorist of being unable to ground numerical distinctness; the bundle theorist accuses the substratum theorist of positing an incoherent entity precisely at the point where explanation runs out. The debate has been live for centuries and no knock-down resolution is in sight. What we can do is map the structure of the dispute and identify what is at stake.

The substratum view's core strength is a principled answer to the individuation problem. Two qualitatively identical spheres — Max Black's thought experiment — are numerically two because they have numerically distinct substrata. The "thisness" of a particular does not derive from its qualitative nature; it is primitive, located in the bare particular itself. The view also preserves the intuitive structure of predication: when we say "the apple is red," we seem to be attributing a property to a subject, and the substratum is the ontological correlate of that subject. Property change through time is handled naturally: the same substratum persists and instantiates different universals at different times.

The bundle view's core strength is parsimony and epistemological tractability. Everything knowable about an object is exhausted by its properties and their relations. A bare particular with no qualitative nature is, as Locke said, "something I know not what" — an entity posited to ground individuation that cannot itself be distinguished, identified, or characterized. Removing the bare particular leaves a more austere ontology: properties (or tropes), the compresence relation, and nothing further. The trope-bundle version solves the individuation problem on different terms: each trope is numerically particular, so two qualitatively indistinguishable objects can still have numerically distinct tropes.

## Shared Vulnerabilities and Bradley's Regress

Both views face a version of Bradley's regress. If properties are related to the substratum by "instantiation" — a further relation — we need a further relation to relate instantiation to its relata, and so on. Bradley's regress threatens any view that treats the connection between an object and its properties as a genuine relation requiring its own ontological ground.

Bundle theorists face an analogous problem with compresence. If properties are unified in a bundle by a compresence relation, and compresence is itself a property or relation, something must bind compresence to the properties it unifies — generating the same regress. Both sides typically respond by accepting some primitive: the substratum theorist accepts instantiation as primitive; the bundle theorist accepts compresence as primitive. Whether these primitives are equally acceptable is disputed, but both sides are in the business of accepting something basic.

Armstrong's states of affairs ontology is the most serious attempt to navigate between the two positions. A state of affairs — *a's being F* — is a fundamental unit that includes both the particular *a* (the thin particular) and the universal *F* as genuine constituents, without reducing either to the other. The thin particular is neither a separately existing bare particular nor a mere abstraction from the bundle; it is a real constituent of the state of affairs, individuated by its instantiation of universals. Whether this genuinely escapes the problems of both positions or merely relocates them is contested: the thin particular still plays the individuating role of the bare particular, and the question of what makes two thin particulars numerically distinct — when they instantiate qualitatively identical universals — has the same structure as the original problem.

## Alternative Frameworks

E. J. Lowe's four-category ontology (developed in *The Four-Category Ontology*, 2006) attempts to transcend the substratum/bundle dichotomy by distributing ontological work across four categories: individual substances, individual modes (tropes), universal kinds, and universal attributes. Objects are instances of kinds; modes are instances of attributes; kinds characterize objects through their instances. There is no bare substratum (objects are individuated by being instances of kinds) and no pure bundle (the object is not just the collection of its modes). Whether the four-category ontology genuinely avoids the problems or multiplies entities beyond necessity is debated.

Ontic structural realism, developed by Ladyman and French in the philosophy of physics, takes the most radical departure: what exists fundamentally are structures and relations, not individual substances at all. Objects are derivative features of relational structures, not the other way around. This challenges the framing question that generates both substratum and bundle theory — the question of what grounds the identity and individuation of objects — by denying that objects are fundamental. If the deepest level of reality is described by quantum field theory or category-theoretic physics, and those descriptions do not carve reality into individual objects, then both the search for substrata and the project of bundling properties may be looking for structure at the wrong level.

The debate is ultimately connected to one's broader commitments: to the status of universals, the nature of predication, the basis of identity, and the metaphysics of modality. Neither substratum nor bundle theory has achieved consensus. Both continue to develop in response to each other and to developments in the philosophy of physics and the metaphysics of persistence. The exchange between them has sharpened our understanding of what individuation requires and what parsimony allows — which is itself a philosophical achievement, even in the absence of resolution.
