# What Is an Artifact?

A rock becomes a hammer when a human picks it up and uses it to drive a nail. But has anything metaphysically significant happened? The rock's physical properties have not changed. What has changed is its relation to human intentions and practices: it is now used for a purpose, regarded as a tool, perhaps modified with that purpose in mind. This raises the central puzzle about artifacts: they seem to depend for their existence — as the kinds of things they are — on human intentions and practices in a way that natural objects do not. But they are also genuinely real: we count them, identify and reidentify them over time, and regard them as capable of surviving certain changes while being destroyed by others. How can something be both intention-dependent and genuinely real?

The intentionality-dependence of artifacts is the key fact to explain. Randall Dipert's account in *Artifacts, Art Works, and Agency* (1993) analyzes it carefully. He distinguishes objects that have been physically modified for a purpose without the agent intending others to recognize them as tools (tools), from objects modified with the intention that others recognize their functional role (instruments), from objects created with the intention that their designed function be recognized as such (artifacts proper). The same physical object can be a tool in one context and an artifact in another, depending on the intentions with which it was created and the social practices within which it operates. This makes the category of artifact irreducibly intentional and social: there is no purely physical property that makes something an artifact.

Risto Hilpinen presses the *authorship relation*: an artifact is an object that stands in the right authorship relation to some agent — it was made, and made to be the kind of thing it is, by that agent. The artifact's identity is partly constituted by its origin. This is why two physically identical objects can differ in identity: a handmade violin and a factory-produced replica, if physically identical, are still different kinds of objects because they stand in different authorship relations to different kinds of agents. The origin is constitutive, not merely causal.

## Realism vs. Eliminativism

Amie Thomasson's *Ordinary Objects* (2007) defends simple realism about artifacts. Artifacts are genuinely real objects with genuine identity conditions, but those conditions are tied to functional and historical origins rather than to microstructural essences. The knife is the knife because of what it was made to do and who made it with what intentions from what materials. Once the relevant facts about design, construction, and use are in place, the existence of the artifact follows — not as a substantive metaphysical mystery but as a conceptual consequence of what it takes for an artifact of that kind to exist.

Thomasson's argument against eliminativist skepticism is methodological: we are not entitled to raise a philosophical worry about whether chairs exist while granting that the relevant micro-level facts obtain. The existence of chairs follows from the facts about particles arranged chair-wise in the right historical and intentional context. There is no further "deep" question about whether chairs really exist; the question has been answered once we specify the existence conditions for the kind *chair*.

Peter van Inwagen's position in *Material Beings* (1990) is almost eliminativist. Composite material objects exist only if their parts are "caught up in a life" — the distinctive self-organizing causal activity of living organisms. Tables and chairs are not composite objects; they are mere pluralities of particles that we talk about as if they were unified. There are no tables in the ontology; there are only particles arranged table-wise. Van Inwagen accepts the radical revisionist consequence: our ordinary artifact-talk is useful but not strictly true.

The disagreement tracks deeper commitments about the relationship between ontology and common sense. Thomasson thinks philosophy should vindicate ordinary object discourse by identifying what it takes for the relevant objects to exist; van Inwagen thinks philosophy should revise ordinary discourse when it conflicts with a principled ontological account. The dispute is not merely technical — it reflects substantive views about what ontology is for.

## The Metaphysics of Artifact Types

A further dimension concerns artifact types as opposed to artifact tokens. This knife (token) is an individual object; the kind *knife* (type) is a general category. What is the ontological status of the type? Natural kinds like gold and water have objective essences independent of human practices. Artifact types seem different: the kind *knife* exists because there is a practice of designing and using objects for cutting; it did not exist before such a practice arose, and it would not exist if the relevant intentions and practices did not exist.

This suggests that artifact types are socially constituted kinds: real but dependent on human practices for their constitution. John Searle's framework from *The Construction of Social Reality* (1995) captures this with constitutive rules of the form "X counts as Y in context C." Certain physical configurations count as knives in contexts where the relevant design intentions and practical uses are in place. The kind is real — it has genuine application conditions and genuine extension — but it is constituted by social practice rather than by microstructural essence. Whether artifact types can have genuine essences in the way natural kinds do, or only nominal essences defined by function, or essences that are real but social rather than physical, is a question that connects artifact metaphysics to social ontology.
