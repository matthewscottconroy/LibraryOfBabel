# Contemporary Substance Theory

Contemporary substance theory is not a single position but a live debate among positions that each inherit different aspects of the tradition examined in the historical chapters. The debate is structured by three central questions: what individuates substances (what makes each substance the particular thing it is), what makes something a substance rather than a property or relation, and what persistence through time and change consists in for substances.

Bundle theory and substratum theory — developed in earlier sections — represent the two main positions on individuation. Contemporary neo-Aristotelian substance theory challenges both, offering a richer picture in which substances are not complexes of independently existing elements (either bare particulars or properties) but unified individuals whose existence as substances is ontologically basic. On this view, substances are not constructed from properties and a substratum but are the fundamental units that bear properties without being reducible to them.

Process philosophy offers perhaps the most radical challenge to the substance paradigm. Whitehead argued that the basic constituents of reality are not enduring substances but momentary events or "occasions of experience" — brief, finite processes of experiencing and becoming. What we call "substances" are patterns or routes of inheritance among occasions, not persisting things in the traditional sense. This view connects the metaphysics of substance to the philosophy of time and to our understanding of what causation and change amount to.

The debates in this section — between substrata and bundles, between neo-Aristotelian substance and process ontology, between endurantism and perdurantism — are not merely technical. They determine how we understand what a thing is, how things can change while remaining the same, and what the most basic level of ontological description looks like. The reader who works through these debates will be equipped to evaluate every subsequent ontological claim in this textbook.
