# Hume and the Bundle Theory of Substance

Start with what Locke honestly admitted. He thought there must be some substratum underlying the properties we perceive — the redness and roundness and sweetness of the apple are properties, so there must be something for them to be properties *of*, some featureless bearer distinct from the qualities themselves. But when pressed on what this substratum is, all he could say was that it is "something I know not what." Hume found this admission devastating. If we do not know what it is — if there is no impression corresponding to it — then we do not have an idea of it at all, and any word purporting to name it is empty. The doctrine of substance as substratum, Hume argued, was nothing more than a grammatical shadow: we say "the apple is red," which suggests a subject distinct from a predicate, but this grammatical structure does not require a corresponding metaphysical structure of bearer and property. Strip away the philosophical fiction, and what we are actually left with is just the properties themselves, bundled together.

This is the positive doctrine Hume drew from the critique: a substance is a *bundle* of perceptions or qualities, a collection of features constantly conjoined that we associate together and project a fictional unity and identity onto. The apple is the bundle of redness, roundness, sweetness; the self — most notoriously — is a "bundle or collection of different perceptions, which succeed each other with inconceivable rapidity, and are in perpetual flux and movement" (*Treatise* I.IV.vi). There is no apple beyond the bundle, no self behind the perceptions.

The epistemological motivation is powerful: bundle theory refuses to posit anything beyond what experience actually delivers. The *Copy Principle* states that every simple idea is derived from a corresponding impression; the *Meaning Criterion* says that any term that cannot be traced to an impression is either analyzable or meaningless. Applied to "substance as substratum": when we look for the impression of a featureless bearer, we find nothing. So either "substance" names the bundle — the complex collection of qualities that experience does deliver — or it names nothing at all. Hume opts for the first: substance just is the bundle.

Contemporary bundle theorists have developed the view with greater precision than Hume allowed. On the universal version, every particular is identical to a bundle of co-instantiated universals, unified by a compresence relation among them at a location. On the trope version — which has become more popular — every particular is identical to a bundle of tropes: numerically distinct, individual property-instances, each existing only where it is located. The key claim, common to both versions, is that there is no constituent of a substance over and above its properties. The substance *is* the bundle; the substratum is eliminated.

## The Identity of Indiscernibles and the Binding Problem

Two challenges press on any bundle theory. The first is the identity of indiscernibles. If a substance just is its bundle of properties or tropes, and two objects have exactly the same qualitative profile, are they one object or two? On the universal version of bundle theory, two objects sharing all their universals are the same bundle, hence the same object — which is the Leibnizian principle ∀x∀y[∀P(Px↔Py)→x=y]. But Black's famous counterexample (*"The Identity of Indiscernibles,"* 1952) imagines a universe containing exactly two qualitatively identical iron spheres: same size, composition, temperature, and symmetrical spatial relations. They are qualitatively indistinguishable. By the principle, they must be identical — yet surely there are *two* spheres. Bundle theorists have several moves. They can include spatiotemporal properties in the bundle (being-at-L₁ differs from being-at-L₂), yielding numerically distinct bundles. They can switch to the trope version: tropes are already numerically distinct individuals, so two spheres' redness-tropes are distinct even if exactly similar, and this yields distinct bundles. Or they can accept the counterintuitive verdict — in a perfectly symmetric universe, there really is only one thing — and argue that our intuition of twoness is an artifact of perspective.

The second challenge is the binding problem. What makes these properties a unified substance rather than a random co-location? The redness and the surrounding air are also in the same vicinity, but they do not form a single object. Several proposals have been made: compresence as a primitive unanalyzed relation (honest but unsatisfying), mutual ontological dependence (each trope in the bundle depends for its existence on the others), causal integration (the bundled properties share a common causal trajectory and enter together into causal relations), or spatiotemporal continuity (the properties are connected in a single continuous region without qualitative discontinuity). None of these proposals has been found fully satisfying, and the binding problem remains the most serious objection the bundle theory faces. Simons's nuclear theory, discussed in the next section, is the most sophisticated response: it distinguishes essential nuclear tropes — which are mutually dependent and whose persistence is the persistence of the substance — from peripheral accidental tropes, which depend on the nucleus but not on each other. This two-level structure answers both the individuation question and the persistence question in a single framework, at the cost of building modality into the fundamental ontology.

What the bundle theory has going for it, above all, is parsimony. It eliminates one category — the bare substratum — and pays the ontological bill by doing more work with properties and their relations. Whether that is enough to cover the costs of the binding problem and the identity of indiscernibles is the question that has occupied bundle theorists from Hume to the present.
