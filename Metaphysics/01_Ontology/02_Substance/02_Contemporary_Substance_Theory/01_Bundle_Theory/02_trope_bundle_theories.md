# Trope Bundle Theories

Classical bundle theory faces a dilemma about what its bundles are made of. If the bundles are bundles of universals — repeatable, multiply-instantiable properties — then numerical distinctness of objects that share all their universals becomes difficult to secure. Two objects could, in principle, be qualitatively identical and hence, on the universal version, constitute the same bundle. More immediately, the bundling relation must work hard: it must explain how abstract, repeatable entities combine to yield concrete, unrepeatable particulars. If instead the bundles are bundles of *tropes* — individual, non-repeatable property instances — these difficulties dissolve in important ways, though new ones arise.

A trope is an individuated instance of a property: not redness in general but *this redness here*, located in this particular apple, numerically distinct from the redness of any other apple however exactly similar. Two red apples are both red not because they share a universal redness but because each has its own redness-trope that exactly resembles the other's. Tropes are simultaneously particular (each exists only where it is) and property-like (each is a qualitative determination of some dimension of reality). Donald Williams, who gave the position its name in 1953, called them "abstract particulars" — not abstract in the sense of non-spatial, but abstract in the sense of being partial aspects of a concrete whole, extracted in thought from the full concrete particular. Keith Campbell's *Abstract Particulars* (1990) is the most systematic modern defense; Anna-Sofia Maurin and others have continued the program.

The trope bundle theory then says: ordinary objects are bundles of co-located tropes standing in compresence relations. The apple is the set of all tropes that are compresent with one another in the apple's region — its redness-trope, roundness-trope, sweetness-trope, mass-trope, and so on. Campbell calls this the *maximal compresence set*: the bundle consists of all and only the tropes that are compresent with one another in the relevant region. Identity conditions for objects are given by identity conditions for tropes and their compresence relations, without any additional substrate.

## Tropes versus Universals

Consider what the competing view — universal realism — must say about instantiation. Armstrong defends the view that properties are universals wholly present wherever they are instantiated, and that the instantiation relation connecting a universal to its instances is either itself a further universal (generating the familiar regress) or a primitive unanalyzed fact. Trope theory sidesteps this: the trope of redness in this apple simply *is* the redness here; no further instantiation relation is needed to connect a general property to a particular case. The trope is both the particular case and the property-instance in one.

Several advantages follow. Individuation is easy: two objects with exactly similar trope-profiles are nonetheless distinct, because their tropes are numerically distinct individuals. The identity of indiscernibles — ∀x∀y[∀P(Px↔Py)→x=y] — does not follow from trope bundle theory, because two bundles can consist of exactly resembling but numerically distinct tropes. Black's two qualitatively identical spheres present no problem: each sphere has its own numerically distinct mass-trope, redness-trope, and so on; two distinct bundles yield two distinct objects. Causation is also tidier on the trope view: this particular mass-trope exerts this particular gravitational pull; causation is grounded in the interactions of particular property-instances rather than in the co-instantiation of abstract universals.

The main challenges concern the resemblance relation and natural kinds. Two gold atoms each have their own atomic-number-79-trope, numerically distinct. What makes them both gold? The trope theorist must say: they resemble one another in the relevant way. The natural kind *gold* is the resemblance class of all atomic-number-79-tropes. But what grounds this resemblance? If resemblance is a universal — a relation that any two resembling tropes stand in by virtue of sharing a common universal — then universals have been reintroduced through the back door. If resemblance is itself a trope, then two tropes T1 and T2 resemble in virtue of a resemblance-trope R, but then R must resemble the resemblance-trope between T2 and some third trope T3, and we are off on a regress. The standard response is to treat exact resemblance as a primitive, unanalyzed relation among tropes — a brute relational fact that does not require further grounding. This is defensible, but it requires accepting that the fundamental structure of reality includes primitive resemblance facts not further analyzable in terms of shared properties or common universals.

## Simons's Nuclear Theory and the Persistence Question

Peter Simons (*Parts: A Study in Ontology*, 1987; "Particulars in Particular Clothing," 1994) developed the most sophisticated account of how trope bundles are individuated and how they persist. He distinguishes two layers. The *nuclear tropes* are the essential tropes — those without which the substance would not exist at all. These tropes are mutually existentially dependent: the nuclear trope of being-a-certain-mass depends for its existence on the nuclear tropes of being-a-certain-charge and being-a-certain-kind-of-particle, and vice versa. Together they form a tightly integrated inner bundle. The *peripheral tropes* are the accidental tropes — those the substance happens to have but could lack. These depend on the nuclear tropes (because they are tropes *of this substance*, hence presuppose its existence) but are not mutually dependent on each other.

This two-level structure earns its keep in several ways. The nuclear tropes individuate the substance: two numerically distinct substances must have numerically distinct nuclear tropes, even if their tropes exactly resemble each other qualitatively. Persistence through change is explained naturally: the substance persists as long as its nuclear tropes persist; accidental change — the apple ripening, changing color, losing mass — is the replacement of peripheral tropes while the nuclear bundle remains intact. And the binding problem, at least for the nuclear layer, is handled without regress: the nuclear tropes are bound by mutual essential dependence, not by an additional compresence trope whose own binding would require explanation.

Applied to personal identity, trope bundle theory yields a picture close to Parfit's (*Reasons and Persons*, 1984), though without Parfit's specific vocabulary. A person is a bundle of psychological tropes — particular belief-instances, memory-instances, desire-instances — and personal identity over time is the continuity of this bundle through connected and overlapping trope-sequences. What matters in survival is not strict numerical identity of a persisting substratum but the degree of psychological connectedness and continuity that the trope-bundle maintains. This is a thoroughly reductionist view of persons: the bundle is all there is, and how much bundle-continuity survives determines how much of the person survives. Whether that conclusion is a liberation — freeing us from what Parfit called the "further fact" we irrationally cling to — or a cost that reveals a deficiency in the trope bundle framework, is a question that cannot be settled by the ontology alone.
