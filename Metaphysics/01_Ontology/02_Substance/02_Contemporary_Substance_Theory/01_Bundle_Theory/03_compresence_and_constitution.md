# Compresence and Constitution

Bundle theories need the bundles to be real unities, not mere aggregates. Consider: the redness of this apple and the blueness of the nearby poster occupy approximately the same region of space. They are not thereby bundled into a single substance. The apple's redness and its roundness are bundled together. What makes the difference? Something must bind the right properties into the right objects, and that something — the compresence relation — does the unifying work that the substratum did in older ontologies. The question is whether compresence can do this work without either generating new regresses or quietly reintroducing the very thing bundle theory was meant to eliminate.

The simplest answer is that compresence is spatial co-location: two tropes are compresent if and only if they occupy the same spatial region. This is intuitive but too permissive. The apple's surface tropes and the air immediately surrounding the apple overlap spatially, but they belong to different objects. A finer-grained criterion — exact co-location, occupying precisely the same spatial subregion — is too restrictive: an extended object has different subregions, and we cannot require every trope of the object to be exactly co-located with every other. Various intermediate proposals have been tried: tropes are compresent if they are connected by spatiotemporal continuity without qualitative gap, or if they are all located *within* some single well-bounded region. Each captures part of what we want but leaves cases that resist clean treatment.

The deeper answer treats compresence not as a spatial relation at all but as a relation of mutual ontological dependence. On this account, the tropes in a bundle are not independently existing items that happen to be co-located; each depends for its existence on the others. The apple's redness-trope cannot exist without the apple — and without the roundness, mass, and chemical-composition tropes that together constitute the apple's nature. Compresence, on this reading, is the mutual existential dependence that holds among the tropes of a single substance. This deflates the regress worry: compresence is not itself a further trope requiring its own compresence with the other tropes; it is a mode of dependence that constitutes the bundle as a unified whole. The cost is that this analysis may be too broad: ecosystems involve mutually dependent tropes without thereby constituting single substances. The mutual-dependence theorist must explain why biological or chemical dependence constitutes a substance-bundle while ecological interdependence does not, and the answer will require some independent grip on what counts as the relevant unit.

## The Constitution Problem

A different but related puzzle concerns what happens when a sculptor takes a lump of clay and shapes it into a statue. Before the sculpting, one object: the clay. After the sculpting: how many objects? The clay (call it Lumpy) still exists — it could be reshaped into something else without ceasing to be clay. The statue (David) also exists — and would cease to exist if reshaped, even though the clay would survive. If Lumpy and David have different modal properties (one can survive reshaping, the other cannot), then by Leibniz's Law they are distinct objects: ∀F(Fa↔Fb)→a=b, applied contrapositively. Yet they share all their material parts, occupy exactly the same region, and have the same mass, shape, and microstructure. Two distinct objects coinciding in every material respect: coincident entities.

The coincidence view accepts this conclusion but finds it puzzling. What grounds the difference between two objects that share all their matter? The grounding problem presses here: if Lumpy and David are distinct, there must be something that makes them distinct, but their material composition and spatiotemporal location are the same. Defenders of coincidence typically appeal to different sortal concepts with different persistence conditions: Lumpy falls under the sortal *piece of clay*, David under the sortal *statue*, and these sortals carry different modal profiles. The difference in what they are — their kind-membership — grounds the difference in how they can persist.

The identity view denies that there are two objects and insists Lumpy and David are one, described in two ways. The apparent modal difference is description-relative: under the description "lump of clay," the object can be reshaped; under "statue," it cannot. Many find this implausible: modal properties should be intrinsic to the object, not vary with how we choose to describe it. Wiggins's relative identity view offers a more sophisticated alternative: Lumpy is the *same piece of clay* as David without being the *same statue* as David. Identity is always identity under a sortal concept, and the two sortals yield different identity verdicts on the same pair. Four-dimensionalism dissolves the puzzle in a different direction: Lumpy and David are distinct four-dimensional spacetime worms that overlap for the duration of the sculpting period but diverge in their temporal extents — Lumpy extends back before the sculpting and forward through any later reshaping; David extends only through the period of the statue's existence.

## Constitution, Bundles, and Persistence

Bundle theory faces a specific difficulty with constitution. If the statue is a bundle of tropes and the clay is a bundle of tropes, and they share all the same tropes at the moment of constitution — same material composition, same shape, same location — then they are the same bundle, hence the same object. Bundle theory cannot easily accommodate coincident entities. One response introduces modal tropes: Lumpy has the trope of being-accidentally-statue-shaped, while David has the trope of being-essentially-statue-shaped. These are distinct tropes — different property-instances with different modal characters — so the bundles differ. Whether modal tropes are a coherent addition to the trope-theoretic framework, or whether they smuggle in a dependence on possible worlds that undermines the trope theory's claim to ontological autonomy, is debated.

The alternative is to resist the coincidence intuition from the outset: there is not a clay object and a statue object; there is one bundle, and we can truly say of it both that it is a statue and that it is a lump of clay, without these descriptions picking out numerically distinct entities. The modal difference then has to be explained contextually or relative to purposes, not as a difference in the intrinsic properties of distinct objects. This move connects bundle theory to expressivist or deflationist approaches to modality that treat modal claims as projections of our conceptual schemes rather than as descriptions of mind-independent necessity.

What the compresence and constitution debates reveal, taken together, is how deeply the apparently narrow question of what substances are spreads into every corner of ontology. To say what binds a bundle together is to take a stand on ontological dependence, on the metaphysics of persistence, on the status of modal properties, and on whether coincident entities are possible. The philosophy of substance is not a sub-discipline of metaphysics; it is the organizing framework within which most of the central questions of metaphysics find their natural place.
