# Substratum Theory

Max Black's thought experiment sets the challenge neatly. Imagine a universe containing only two qualitatively identical iron spheres — same size, same composition, same temperature, same history. Every property one has, the other has. Now ask: what makes them two rather than one? If objects are nothing over and above their properties, and both have exactly the same properties, they are the same bundle — and hence the same object. But they are clearly two. Something must ground their numerical distinctness beyond their qualitative similarity. The substratum theorist's answer is: each sphere has a bare particular, a substratum, that is numerically distinct from the other sphere's substratum even though the two substrata have no qualitative difference. The "thisness" of a particular — what makes it *this one* rather than *that one* — is not carried by its qualitative nature but by something prior to and independent of all its properties.

This is the central motivation for substratum theory, also called bare particular theory. On the standard view, an ordinary concrete object is analyzed as a complex of two ontologically distinct components: a substratum *s* that serves as the owner of properties, and the properties themselves — universals F₁, F₂, F₃,... — that the substratum instantiates. The substratum is what individuates; the properties are what characterize. Formally: the object *x* = ⟨s, F₁, F₂, F₃,...⟩, where *s* is the bare particular and the F's are universals it instantiates. The substratum is "bare" in the sense that it has no intrinsic qualitative properties of its own — it is the pure subject of predication, what properties are predicated of without itself being predicable of anything further.

The argument for substrata has a clean structure. P1: Two numerically distinct objects can be qualitatively identical. P2: If objects were nothing but bundles of properties, qualitatively identical objects would be identical. P3: But by P1, they are not identical. C: Objects are not mere property bundles; something grounds their numerical distinctness. Substratum theory supplies the ground by positing a non-qualitative individuating particular for each object.

## The Bare Particular Problem and Responses

The most persistent objection to substratum theory is that the posited entity is incoherent. A bare particular — having no intrinsic qualitative properties — seems like nothing at all. If the substratum has no properties, we cannot know anything about it (knowing requires distinguishing via properties), we cannot refer to it (reference requires some identifying description), and we cannot explain how one bare particular differs from another (lacking qualitative differences, they are utterly undifferentiated). It looks as if the theory posits an unknowable, indescribable X to do the work of individuation — which is no better than saying individuation is a brute unexplained fact.

Defenders have developed several responses. The first is Martin's strategy: bare particulars do stand in relations, even if they have no intrinsic properties. Being-distinct-from is a relational property, and this is enough to differentiate substrata numerically. The second is Armstrong's "thin particular": the substratum is not entirely bare but is the object considered abstractly as the subject of instantiation, without its properties. The thin particular is not a separately existing entity but an abstraction from states of affairs — it is what all states of affairs involving the same object have in common. The object as a whole (the "thick particular") includes its properties; the thin particular is the object stripped of them in thought, not in reality.

Armstrong's mature position is instructive here. He moved away from bare particulars entirely, arguing they are not just costly but incoherent — a "nothing" pressed into service as an individuator. His alternative: states of affairs as fundamental units. The apple is not a substratum having redness; it is the state of affairs *the apple's being red* together with *the apple's being round*, *the apple's being sweet*, and so on. The particular component of each state of affairs provides individuation without being a separately existing bare particular.

Whether this is genuinely different from substratum theory or merely relabels its components is contested. The thin particular that appears in Armstrong's states of affairs still plays the individuating role that the bare particular was meant to play; it is just tied more tightly to the universals it instantiates, with no independent existence outside of states of affairs. The question of what makes two thin particulars numerically distinct — when they instantiate the same universals — has the same structure as the original bare particular problem.

## Substratum Theory and Modal Metaphysics

Substratum theory connects naturally to haecceitism — the view that individuals have primitive individual essences that determine their identity across possible worlds, independently of their qualitative properties. The bare particular can be understood as the haecceity-bearing component of an object: what makes this particular *this one*, across all possible worlds where it exists, is its substratum, not its qualitative nature.

Lewis's counterpart theory is the haecceitist's main rival. On Lewis's account, no individual literally exists at more than one possible world; "Socrates could have been a shoemaker" is made true by facts about Socrates's counterparts in other worlds, not about Socrates himself. There are no primitive individual essences, no haecceities, no substrata needed to carry identity across worlds — individual identity is analyzed in terms of qualitative similarity and spatiotemporal relations. The choice between substratum theory and counterpart theory is thus not merely about the structure of individual objects; it determines the whole shape of modal metaphysics.

Contemporary defenders — J. P. Moreland in *Universals* (2001), Richard Swinburne in work on personal identity, Howard Robinson on mental substances — argue that the objections to bare particulars are not decisive. Moreland distinguishes bare particulars from their properties and argues that bare particulars can have a primitive non-qualitative thisness. Swinburne deploys bare particulars specifically for the individuation of mental substances: the soul is a bare particular that persists through radical qualitative change, providing the ground for personal identity across time in a way that bundle theories cannot.
