# Bundle Theory

The bundle theorist begins with a diagnostic claim about the substratum view. When Locke confesses that he has no idea of material substratum beyond "something I know not what," he is not being accidentally imprecise. He is hitting a genuine wall: a bare particular — a thing with no intrinsic qualitative properties — is either indistinguishable from nothing or is posited precisely to be epistemologically inaccessible. Either way, the concept is doing no work. What we actually encounter in experience, and what science identifies and characterizes, are properties: colors, shapes, masses, charges. What is called an "object" or a "substance" is, on careful inspection, a bundle — a collection of co-instantiated properties. There is no additional substratum; the object just *is* the properties, appropriately organized.

The bundle theory was associated historically with Hume's analysis of the self and carried forward in Russell's logical atomism, but its most sophisticated contemporary development is the trope-bundle theory. The key innovation: instead of bundling universals — shareable, repeatable properties like Redness-in-general — bundle theorists who use tropes work with individual property instances. The redness of this apple is a numerically particular trope, distinct from the redness of the fire truck even if they are qualitatively identical. Socrates is not a bundle of universal properties that he shares with others; he is a bundle of *his* particular tropes — his particular wisdom, his particular snub-nosedness, each of which is unrepeatable and belongs uniquely to him.

This move is crucial because it dissolves the most powerful objection to universals-based bundle theory. If objects are bundles of universals, then two qualitatively identical iron spheres share all the same universals and therefore constitute the same bundle — the same object. But they are clearly two. Trope bundle theory avoids this: each sphere has its own numerically distinct tropes — its own sphericity-trope, its own iron-composition-trope, its own mass-trope. The two bundles are numerically distinct even though qualitatively indistinguishable, because they are composed of distinct particular instances.

## The Compresence Problem

The bundle theory's central technical challenge is the compresence problem: what holds the tropes together in a bundle? If the properties are just floating free — wisdom somewhere, snub-nosedness somewhere else — what makes them both belong to *Socrates* rather than existing as unattached? The usual answer is compresence: a relation of co-location or co-instantiation. Two tropes are compresent if they occupy the same spatiotemporal region. A substance is the maximal compresence class — all the tropes that are compresent with each other.

The difficulty is that compresence must itself be explained. If compresence is itself a trope, it is a relational trope that holds among the other tropes in the bundle. But then what unites the compresence trope with the tropes it relates? If another compresence trope — we are on the road to a regress. The most common response is to treat compresence as a primitive, unanalyzable relation among tropes. This is not regressive (analysis must stop somewhere) but it leaves compresence unexplained. It also raises the question of what distinguishes intuitively similar relations — why does the compresence of wisdom and snub-nosedness in Socrates differ from the near-adjacency of Socrates's wisdom and Callias's snub-nosedness?

Keith Campbell in *Abstract Particulars* (1990) develops the trope-bundle view in detail and acknowledges compresence as a primitive. D. C. Williams, who introduced the term "trope" and the contemporary form of the theory, similarly treats compresence (spatial co-location) as basic. The appeal to spatiotemporal co-location is the most natural move: two tropes are compresent if and only if they occupy overlapping regions of spacetime. This works well for paradigm cases but introduces complications for non-spatiotemporal entities — if there are abstract objects like numbers or propositions, they presumably lack spatial location, so compresence cannot apply to them.

## Bundle Theory and Persistence

One important application of bundle theory is to the problem of persistence through change. If an object is a bundle of properties, and properties change over time, in what sense is the object at *t₂* the same object as at *t₁*? Two answers are available, tracking the broader debate between endurantism and perdurantism.

The endurantist bundle theorist says the same bundle persists — it is wholly present at each moment — though its composition changes as tropes are gained and lost. Numerical identity through qualitative change is grounded in spatiotemporal continuity: the bundle at *t₂* is continuous with the bundle at *t₁* if the changes are gradual and the location is connected. The difficulty is explaining what makes it the *same* bundle rather than a successor bundle.

The perdurantist bundle theorist — Theodore Sider in *Four-Dimensionalism* (2001) defends a version of this — avoids the problem by denying strict numerical identity over time. Objects are four-dimensional: they have temporal parts just as they have spatial parts. Each temporal stage is a bundle of tropes; the enduring object is the aggregate of its temporal stages. Qualitative change is analyzed as different stages having different tropes, not as the same bundle changing — which means there is no puzzle about how the same thing can have contrary properties at different times.

For the self, Derek Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* (1984) revived the Humean bundle approach with sophisticated arguments. The self is nothing over and above its psychological states; personal identity just is psychological continuity and connectedness. There is no "deep further fact" about whether I survive in split-brain fission cases or gradual-replacement scenarios beyond the facts about the continuity and connectedness of psychological states. Parfit thought this was not a cause for alarm but for liberation: once we see that personal identity is not what matters in survival, we can care about psychological continuity directly and stop asking the metaphysically unanswerable question of whether we "really" persist.

The debate between bundle theory and substratum theory ultimately connects to one's deepest commitments in ontology. If one demands a ground for numerical distinctness beyond qualitative facts, substratum theory is compelling. If one finds bare particulars metaphysically suspect and prefers an ontology in which everything real is in principle epistemically accessible, bundle theory is the more parsimonious option. Neither has achieved consensus; both continue to develop in response to each other.
