# Bare Particulars

There is a persistent intuition behind substance-and-attribute ontology that seems hard to shake. When we say "this apple is red," we seem to be referring to something — the apple — that is not itself the redness but the thing that *has* the redness. If the apple just were its bundle of properties, then "the apple is red" would amount to something like "the bundle containing redness also contains redness," which is a tautology rather than a genuine predication. There appears to be a subject distinct from any of its predicates — a *this*, a particular, that bears the properties but is not reducible to them. The doctrine of bare particulars attempts to articulate what this subject is.

A bare particular is the particularizing component of a concrete object — stripped of all its qualitative properties, the pure principle of numerical individuation. Two exactly similar spheres differ because they have numerically distinct bare particulars, even though they instantiate identical properties. The "thisness" of the particular is not derived from its qualitative nature; it is primitive, located in the substratum itself. Formally: a concrete object *x* is analyzed as a complex of its bare particular *s* and the universals it instantiates F₁, F₂, ..., Fₙ, where *s* is characterized entirely by its role as instantiator rather than by any qualitative content.

Gustav Bergmann is the most systematic twentieth-century defender. In *Realism: A Critique of Brentano and Meinong* (1967) and related works, he argues that bare particulars are the principle of individuation — what makes this particular this one rather than that one. They contribute nothing to the qualitative nature of the object; they are purely the principle of numerical distinctness. Herbert Hochberg refined this by arguing that bare particulars have at least a second-order formal property: the property of being an instantiator. This is not a qualitative characterization but a structural role — the bare particular is the entity that stands in the instantiation relation to first-order properties.

## Armstrong's Objection and the Thin Particular Response

D. M. Armstrong's critique of bare particulars in *Universals and Scientific Realism* (1978) and *A World of States of Affairs* (1997) has two components that together are formidable. The epistemological objection: bare particulars are qualitatively characterless, and everything knowable about an object is a matter of its qualitative properties. A bare particular, having no qualitative nature, is unknowable in principle — Locke's "something I know not what" in its most explicit form. The incoherence objection: can an entity exist with literally no properties at all? Even existence and particularity look like properties of some kind. If the bare particular must be a particular (rather than a universal), it has at least the property of being a particular. If it exists, it has the property of existing. These seem to be genuine properties — which means the bare particular is not really bare, and the concept is self-undermining.

Armstrong's alternative, the "thin particular," is designed to preserve what is useful in the bare particular doctrine without the incoherence. The thin particular is the particular-pole of a state of affairs — a genuine ontological constituent of *a's being F* that is not identical to *a's* full profile of properties, but that also has no existence independent of the states of affairs in which it figures. The thin particular provides the same individuating function as the bare particular but is not stripped of all connection to universals; it exists only as a constituent of facts, not as a free-floating propertyless entity.

Whether this is a genuine advance or merely a relabeling is contested. The thin particular still plays the individuating role that the bare particular was meant to play, and the question of what makes two thin particulars numerically distinct — when they instantiate qualitatively identical universals — has the same structure as the original problem. Armstrong's response is that the state of affairs as a whole is the fundamental unit, and numerical distinctness of states of affairs is not further grounded: it is a primitive feature of the world's constitution.

## Haecceitism as Support

Contemporary haecceitism provides a different line of support for bare particulars. If individuals have primitive individual essences (*haecceities*) that determine their identity across possible worlds independently of their qualitative properties, then the bare particular can be understood as the ontological ground of haecceity — what makes this particular irreducibly this one, and not any other, even a qualitative duplicate. David Kaplan's haecceitism and Robert Adams's defense of primitive thisness ("Primitive Thisness and Primitive Identity," 1979) both rely on something structurally like a bare particular: a component of the individual that grounds its identity without being constituted by qualitative features.

Lewis's counterpart theory takes the opposite line: there are no primitively individual essences, no haecceities, no bare particulars needed for transworld identity — individual identity is analyzed in terms of qualitative similarity and spatiotemporal relations. The choice between bare-particular theories and counterpart theories is thus not merely about the microstructure of objects. It determines the shape of modal metaphysics, the analysis of de re necessity, and the account of what makes individuals the same across possible scenarios. The bare particular is not a minor technical device; it sits at the intersection of some of the most fundamental questions in analytic ontology.
