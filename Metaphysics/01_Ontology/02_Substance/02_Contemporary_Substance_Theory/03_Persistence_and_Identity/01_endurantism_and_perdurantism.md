# Endurantism and Perdurantism

Here is a puzzle that seems trivial until you think hard about it. Socrates at twenty is pale; Socrates at seventy is tanned. These predicates attribute contrary properties to what is, intuitively, one and the same individual. The principle of non-contradiction says that nothing can be both F and not-F. But Socrates appears to be both pale and not-pale. How is this possible? The endurantist and the perdurantist give radically different answers — answers that have ramifications for every major question in the metaphysics of persistence, personal identity, and the coincidence of material objects.

The endurantist holds that Socrates is *wholly present* at each moment of his existence. The very same whole individual — not a temporal stage, not a temporal part, but Socrates — exists at twenty and at seventy. This is what we ordinarily assume when we say Socrates *changed*. He used to be one way; now he is another way; he is numerically the same individual throughout. The apparent contradiction dissolves because the properties are indexed to different times: Socrates is pale-at-t₁ and tanned-at-t₂. These are relational properties involving temporal indices, not incompatible monadic properties. Non-contradiction is preserved.

David Lewis found this response unsatisfying. If being pale is really a relation to a time rather than a simple intrinsic property, then there are no genuine intrinsic properties at all — no way an object simply *is*, independent of temporal relations. The problem of temporary intrinsics is the problem of explaining how an object has genuine intrinsic character if all its properties are really relations to times. The endurantist has dissolved the apparent contradiction by treating properties as disguised relations, but in so doing has made intrinsic character difficult to defend.

The perdurantist avoids this by postulating temporal parts. On this view, Socrates is a four-dimensional object — a temporal worm — extended through spacetime just as he is extended through space. "Socrates" refers to the entire worm. At twenty, only the Socrates-at-twenty temporal part exists; at seventy, only the Socrates-at-seventy temporal part. Paleness belongs simpliciter to the young temporal part; tannedness belongs simpliciter to the old temporal part. Their having contrary properties is no more paradoxical than the left side of a table being brown while the right side is white. Lewis developed this view in *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986); Ted Sider defended it systematically in *Four-Dimensionalism* (2001).

## Formal Structure and Stage Theory

Let t₁ < t₂ be distinct times and *F* an intrinsic property. The problem for endurantism:

- Endurantism: ∀x(x persists → x is wholly present at each t at which x exists).
- But: Socrates has *F* at t₁ and not-*F* at t₂, and *F* and not-*F* cannot both be true of x simpliciter.

Endurantist solution: replace "x has *F*" with "x has *F-at-t*" — time-indexed relational properties rather than genuine intrinsics.

Perdurantist solution: replace "Socrates has *F* at t₁" with "Socrates's temporal part at t₁ has *F* simpliciter" — the monadic property is instantiated by the temporal part, not the whole worm.

A variant, the *stage theory* (Sider, Hawley), proposes that when we use "Socrates" we refer to an instantaneous temporal stage, not the whole worm. "Socrates is wise" is true because the current stage is wise. "Socrates *was* snub-nosed" is true because the current Socrates-stage has a snub-nosed counterpart-stage at the earlier time, where counterpart is a relation among stages analogous to Lewis's counterpart relation among possible worlds individuals. Stage theory captures the endurantist's intuition that "Socrates" refers to something wholly present, while analyzing temporal predications in terms of counterpart relations among distinct stages.

## Fission, Coincidence, and the Arguments

Each view handles characteristic cases with characteristic ease. Consider the fission problem: if a person's cerebral hemispheres are transplanted into two separate bodies, which resulting person is the original? The perdurantist gives a clean structural answer: the pre-fission worm splits into two diverging worms that share an earlier temporal part. Neither resulting worm is strictly identical to the original (identity requires uniqueness), but both stand in the appropriate temporal-part relation to the shared earlier segment. The endurantist must say something more difficult: either one of the resulting persons is the original (but why?) or neither is (but then where did the original go?).

The coincidence problem has the same structure. A lump of clay and a statue occupy the same spatiotemporal region. They seem to be one thing (they are spatially coincident) but two things (they have different modal properties: the lump persists when squashed, the statue does not). Perdurantists can distinguish the clay-worm and the statue-worm as sharing some temporal parts while differing in their temporal extent. Endurantists typically respond with a constitution view: the clay *constitutes* the statue without being identical to it — a distinction in kind of entity that shares a spatiotemporal location.

The costs of perdurantism are equally real. It multiplies entities aggressively — every object at every time generates a temporal part — and its account of personal identity seems to distort ordinary self-understanding. To say "I am related to my past and future stages by temporal counterparthood rather than strict identity" is to say something counterintuitive about ordinary self-concern. And the claim that there are temporal parts at all — that objects are genuinely four-dimensional — raises the question of why we should believe this rather than accepting that objects simply persist by being wholly present, which is what common sense says.

The debate has not reached resolution. Both views have articulate defenders; both face genuine objections; both have important consequences for how we understand personal identity, material coincidence, and the metaphysics of time. Choosing between them requires taking a position on what counts as a satisfying account of change — and that is itself a substantive metaphysical question.
