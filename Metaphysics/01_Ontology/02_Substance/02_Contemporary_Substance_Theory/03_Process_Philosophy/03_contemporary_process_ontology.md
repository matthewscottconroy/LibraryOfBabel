# Contemporary Process Ontology

The process tradition in philosophy has often been associated with Whitehead's metaphysically ambitious system and its theological applications. But the revival of process philosophy in the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries is different in character: technical, engaged with analytic metaphysics, and motivated in significant part by developments in the natural sciences rather than by theology. What drives contemporary process ontologists is a convergent dissatisfaction with substance ontology from multiple directions simultaneously — philosophy of biology, quantum physics, cognitive science, and formal ontology all providing arguments that reality is more processual and less thing-like than the standard substance framework assumes.

The core argument can be stated simply. What we call things — rocks, organisms, persons — are not static, self-subsistent entities that happen to undergo changes. They are patterns of ongoing process, temporarily stable configurations of activity that we conceptually organize into things for practical purposes. The rock maintains its form through constant microphysical interaction; the organism maintains its organization through continuous metabolic exchange with its environment; the person is constituted by ongoing psychological and biological processes. What persists is not a substance underlying the processes but the processual organization itself. Treating the abstracted pattern — the "rock" or the "organism" — as more fundamental than the process that instantiates it is, in Whitehead's phrase, the fallacy of misplaced concreteness.

Johanna Seibt's General Process Theory develops this into a rigorous formal ontology. She distinguishes *occurrents* — events with definite temporal boundaries, the Davidsonian events of analytic philosophy — from *free processes* — ongoing, open-ended activities without fixed start and stop points. A metabolic cycle, the evolution of a language, the development of a river system: these are not occurrents but free processes, activities that go on without being bounded by a determinate beginning and end. On Seibt's account, free processes are ontologically basic; objects (substances) are relatively stable, recurrent patterns of free processes that we isolate and individuate for practical and cognitive purposes. Properties become sub-types of processes; kinds become patterns of processual similarity; relations become processual dependencies.

## Process Biology and the Metabolic Organism

The philosophy of biology has provided some of the strongest arguments for a processual ontology. Denis Walsh, Matteo Mossio, and collaborators have argued that living organisms are best understood as self-organizing, self-maintaining processes rather than collections of material parts that happen to be organized. The organism is not a thing that *has* a metabolism; the organism *is* its metabolism — a process that temporarily recruits material from the environment, maintains its own organization through that material, and returns the material to the environment while the processual pattern persists.

What persists through biological change is not material substance — the material composition of an organism changes entirely over years — but the organizational pattern that maintains itself through the exchange. This connects to classical functional notions of biological form: the organism's form is not a static spatial arrangement but a dynamic temporal pattern of self-organization. The distinction between what is essential to an organism and what is accidental maps onto the distinction between processual patterns that constitute the organism's persistence and material or qualitative variations that occur within that persistence.

Nicholas Rescher's process philosophy (*Process Metaphysics*, 1996) makes the epistemological case: we never perceive substances directly. What we perceive are patterns of activity — colors, sounds, textures, temperatures — that we conceptually organize into things. The concept of a persisting substance is a cognitive construction useful for organizing experience, but it does not correspond to a fundamental feature of reality. Quantum field theory describes fields and excitations, not enduring particles. The "particles" of particle physics are stable patterns of field excitation — processes, not substances.

## Objections and Responses

Three objections press hardest on process ontology. The individuation problem: substances are relatively easy to count (seven apples on the table), but how many processes are occurring on the table right now? The difficulty of providing identity criteria for free processes suggests a principled problem with process ontology's basic category. Process ontologists typically respond that the difficulty is real but not greater than the difficulty of providing identity criteria for events in Davidsonian event ontology, and that the individuation of processes can be handled through causal and organizational criteria analogous to those used for substances.

The reference problem: ordinary language is substantival; we refer to things, not processes. If processes are basic, every ordinary reference to a thing is a superficially adequate but metaphysically misleading way of talking. Seibt's response is that substance-talk is legitimate shorthand for process-talk — the same way that talking about the Earth "rising" is legitimate shorthand even given heliocentrism. The conceptual revision required is real but manageable.

The stability problem: what explains why some processes are stable enough to appear to us as things? Process ontologists say stable processes are processes with self-maintaining organizational patterns, but specifying what counts as self-maintenance seems to require reference to structural and material features that look very much like the properties of substances. The suspicion is that process ontology has not eliminated substances but merely replaced the noun "substance" with the gerund phrase "stable process" while leaving the underlying structure intact.

Contemporary process ontology takes these objections seriously and responds to them with increasing technical sophistication — Peter Simons's mereology of processes, Seibt's formal ontology of free processes, Rescher's systematic process metaphysics. Whether the responses succeed is contested. What is clear is that process ontology has moved from programmatic protest to technical engagement, and the debate between it and substance ontology is now conducted at the same level of rigor.
