# Minds and Persons

The mind presents a special challenge for substance theory. If mind is a substance — a genuinely existing thing with its own intrinsic nature — then it is either a material substance (the brain), an immaterial substance (the Cartesian soul), or something in between. Each option is philosophically demanding. Material substance theory of mind faces the hard problem of consciousness: how does a physical substance give rise to the subjective, first-person character of experience? Immaterial substance theory faces the interaction problem: how does an immaterial mind cause changes in a material body, and vice versa? An intermediate position must specify what is intermediate and how it avoids the problems of the extremes.

Persons add another layer of complexity. Even if we settle on a view about the nature of mind, there remains a question about persons specifically: what is it to be a person, as opposed to a mere mind or a mere body? Persons seem to have properties that neither minds nor bodies have in isolation — they are agents, they are moral patients, they are subjects of rights and obligations, they persist through time in a way that grounds the practices of promising, planning, and holding responsible.

Whether persons are a distinct ontological kind — a kind of substance not reducible to biological organisms or to psychological complexes — has implications for ethics, for the philosophy of action, and for our understanding of what matters in survival. The debates examined in this section range from Descartes's substance dualism through contemporary animalism and psychological continuity theory to the position that persons are "nothing over and above" certain biological or functional systems, and that the apparent distinctness of persons is a kind of useful fiction that tracks important features without marking a genuine ontological category.
