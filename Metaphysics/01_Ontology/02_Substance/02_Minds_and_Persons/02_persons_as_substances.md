# Persons as Substances

Is a person a substance? The question sounds trivially affirmative — of course persons are substances, they are paradigmatically the kinds of things that substances are. But the question is not trivial. It asks whether persons form a genuine ontological category, distinct from and irreducible to physical organisms on the one hand and bundles of mental states on the other. Three positions have been developed with care: Strawson's claim that persons are a primitive, irreducible category; Baker's constitution view; and Lowe's psychological substance dualism. Each is a serious philosophical position; none is obviously correct.

Strawson's argument in *Individuals* (1959) begins with a diagnostic point about mental-predicate ascription. We apply mental predicates — "is in pain," "believes that P" — both to ourselves and to others. But consider how we could learn to apply "is in pain" to others. If we begin from our own case — having pain — and then argue by analogy to other minds, we face the well-known problem that the analogy gives us no grip on others' experience. We cannot "see" others' pain the way we see their behavior. Strawson's claim: we do not in fact proceed this way. We learn to apply mental predicates as part of a single practice of applying them to *persons* — a category to which both mental and physical predicates apply primitively. The concept of a person is the logically primary concept; the concept of a mind is an abstraction from it, not its foundation.

This is a transcendental argument: the concept of a person is a condition of possibility for ordinary mental-predicate ascription. We could not reconstruct the practice of ascribing mental states from any more primitive elements. Persons are not reducible to minds plus bodies; they are a basic category within which both mental and physical predicates find their home.

## Baker's Constitution View and Lowe's Substance Dualism

Lynne Rudder Baker's *Persons and Bodies* (2000) defends a constitution account. You are constituted by your body without being identical to it. The clay constitutes the statue without being the statue; the clay and statue have different modal properties (the clay could survive being reshaped; the statue could not). Similarly, the human animal and the person are distinct entities that coincide spatiotemporally but differ in their persistence conditions. What makes you a person, on Baker's account, is the possession of a first-person perspective — the capacity to think of oneself as oneself, from the inside. Biological development generates the capacity; the capacity grounds personal existence.

The view handles cases at the margins of personhood with some precision. A fetus in early development is a body without a person — the capacity for a first-person perspective has not yet developed. A body that has permanently and irreversibly lost all capacity for first-person perspective is a body that no longer constitutes a person — the person has ceased to exist. A person in dreamless sleep retains the capacity even without exercising it, and so continues to exist.

Lowe's psychological substance dualism in *Subjects of Experience* (1996) takes a different route. Persons are genuine substances, but their individuation is psychological rather than material. Your body is a material substance, individuated by material and spatiotemporal criteria. You are a psychological substance, individuated by the continuity of mental states, memories, and experiences. The two are distinct substances that stand in a relation of embodiment — you are implemented in your body and depend on it, but your identity conditions are different from your body's identity conditions. This avoids the Cartesian picture of an immaterial ghost while preserving the genuinely distinct ontological status of persons.

Both Baker and Lowe face the thinking animal objection (developed against neo-Lockean views by Eric Olson): there is a human animal in your chair, thinking your thoughts. If you are not that animal, there are two distinct thinkers in your chair — you and the animal. This seems absurd. Baker and Lowe must respond that the multiplication of thinkers is not absurd when one thinker constitutes or embodies the other — that the animal thinks derivatively, in virtue of constituting or being the body of the person. Whether "derivative thinking" is a coherent notion, or whether it simply reinstates the problem in different terms, is the heart of the debate.

## Ethics, Law, and the Substance Question

Whether persons are substances bears on legal and ethical frameworks in ways that exceed academic philosophy. Legal personhood — the status of being a bearer of legal rights and obligations — is applied to human organisms, corporations, and (in some jurisdictions) natural features like rivers. Legal personhood is explicitly constructed by legal systems for practical purposes; it does not follow the metaphysical contours of personhood. The question of whether a sufficiently sophisticated artificial system could be a moral person — an end in itself, not merely a means — depends on which criterion of personhood is fundamental: rational agency, the capacity for first-person perspective, biological kind-membership, or something else. As artificial systems become more sophisticated, this question migrates from science fiction to live philosophical debate, and the metaphysical framework for answering it needs to be worked out in advance of the cases that will demand an answer.
