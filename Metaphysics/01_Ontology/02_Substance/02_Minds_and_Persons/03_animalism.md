# Animalism

There is an animal in your chair. You are in your chair. Are you that animal, or are you something else — a mind, a person, a psychological continuant — that merely happens to occupy the same location as the animal? Most people find this question strange. Of course you are the animal. Animalism, developed most carefully by Eric Olson in *The Human Animal* (1997), takes this intuitive answer and turns it into a systematic metaphysical position: human persons are biological organisms of the species *Homo sapiens*, and personal identity over time just is the biological continuity of a living organism.

The contrast is with neo-Lockean psychological continuity theories, which hold that personal identity consists in psychological relations — memory, personality, beliefs, desires. On the neo-Lockean picture, if your psychology were gradually transferred to a new biological substrate through some future neuroscience, you would go with your psychology into the new body. You are where your mind is. Olson rejects this, and the thinking animal argument makes the rejection vivid.

If you are not the animal in your chair — if the person (psychologically defined) and the animal (biologically defined) are distinct — then there are two thinkers in your chair: the person and the animal. Both have brains; both are conscious; both are thinking your thoughts. But surely there is only one conscious thinker here, not two. The neo-Lockean has multiplied entities without necessity. The most parsimonious solution is that there is one entity — the animal — and it is also the person.

Animalism also handles persistence puzzles that trouble psychological theories. The fetus problem: psychological continuity theories imply that you were never a fetus, because fetuses lack the psychological capacities required for personhood. But you *were* a fetus; you began there. Animalism handles this naturally: you were always the organism, and the organism began as a fetus before developing psychological capacities. The vegetative state problem: if a person enters persistent vegetative state, psychological theories imply the person has ceased to exist (no psychological continuity) while the organism persists. Animalism says the person persists in a severely diminished state.

## The Brain-Transplant Objection and Olson's Response

The most compelling objection is the brain-transplant case. If a surgeon transplanted your entire brain into a different body, most people's intuitions say you would go with the brain — the resulting person would have your memories, personality, and beliefs, and would be you. Animalism implies the opposite: you would stay with your original body (the organism), while the brain-recipient would be a different organism that happens to have your memories.

Olson bites this bullet. He argues that the intuition that "you" go with your brain reflects the importance we attach to psychological continuity, not a fact about personal identity. The resulting person in the new body has your memories and your personality, and this matters greatly. But it does not follow that that person is *you*. We care deeply about psychological continuity; we should not confuse this caring with a fact about numerical identity.

The formal structure of animalism:

- You are a human animal.
- You persist through time if and only if the human animal that you are persists.
- A human animal persists through time if and only if its biological life continues.

This gives a clean account of persistence: biological life-continuity is the criterion, avoiding puzzles about degrees of psychological continuity (how much is enough?) and about branching (can psychological continuity divide into two successors?).

## Persons and Animals as Different Kinds

The deeper objection is that persons and animals simply are different kinds of entity, with different persistence conditions, and that identifying them is a mistake. Baker's constitution view and Lowe's psychological substance dualism both rest on this intuition. A person can in principle survive a body change; an organism cannot. A person has a first-person perspective as an essential property; an organism does not. Therefore persons and organisms are distinct.

Olson's response is to reframe the question. He distinguishes being a person (having the psychological capacities associated with personhood — rationality, self-consciousness, moral agency) from being the same entity as a person. You are a human animal; human animals at the right stage of development are persons; but the persistence conditions for the entity (the animal) are biological, not psychological. When the animal's biology continues, you continue — even if your personhood is temporarily or permanently interrupted.

This generates the embryo implication: since you were once an embryo, and you are a human animal, embryos are the same entities as adult human persons — which has implications for the ethics of abortion and embryo research that Olson acknowledges as the honest cost of the view. Whether those implications are acceptable, or whether they constitute a *reductio* of animalism, depends on prior ethical commitments that the metaphysics itself cannot settle. What animalism does is make those ethical commitments explicit and show exactly which metaphysical claims they depend on.
