# The Revival of Substance

For a substantial stretch of the twentieth century, "substance" was a term of philosophical embarrassment. The logical empiricists of the Vienna Circle — Carnap, Schlick, Ayer — had made metaphysics suspect by declaring its claims unverifiable and therefore meaningless. Talk of substance, essence, and being seemed to them like pseudo-problems generated by the surface grammar of language. The Wittgensteinian strand in ordinary language philosophy added a different critique: philosophical puzzlement about substance was a symptom of confusion about how language works, not genuine insight into the structure of reality. On this picture, the long history of substance metaphysics from Aristotle through the scholastics to Descartes, Spinoza, and Leibniz was a history of grammatical seduction — the subject-predicate structure of sentences tricking philosophers into thinking there must be a subject-entity behind every predicate.

Despite these pressures, the substance concept never fully collapsed. It persisted in philosophy of mind — any account of persons has to address what the subject of experience is — and in philosophy of science — any account of natural kinds has to address whether kinds carve reality at genuine joints. And by the 1970s and 1980s, a combination of formal ontology, new work in semantics, and systematic neo-Aristotelian philosophy had restored substance as a central metaphysical category.

Peter Strawson's *Individuals* (1959) was an early catalyst. Strawson distinguished descriptive metaphysics — articulating the categorial framework implicit in our actual thought and language — from revisionary metaphysics. His descriptive argument: material bodies and persons are the two fundamental kinds of particular in our conceptual scheme. We identify and reidentify events, places, and other things by reference to material bodies and persons, not the other way around. This was not a revolutionary metaphysical thesis but a demonstration that substantial categories are structurally indispensable — we cannot coherently think about the world without them.

## Kripke, Putnam, and the Return of Essence

The decisive turning point was the Kripke-Putnam revolution in semantics and its metaphysical implications. Kripke's *Naming and Necessity* (1980) and Putnam's "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" (1975) rehabilitated Aristotelian essentialism in a form that analytic philosophers could not easily dismiss. The claim that water is *essentially* H₂O — not merely a linguistic convention but a metaphysical necessity discoverable empirically — revived the concept of real essence. Natural kinds have determinate natures prior to and independent of our concepts of them. Gold is the element with atomic number 79; it could not have been a different element. This is necessary *a posteriori* — known empirically but holding across possible worlds.

This is the Aristotelian picture of secondary substances updated for modern chemistry and modal semantics. Aristotle's *eidos* — the form that makes something the kind of thing it is — reappears as the microstructural constitution of natural kinds. The difference is that Aristotle thought essence was grasped by intellectual analysis; Kripke and Putnam insisted it is discovered by science. But the metaphysical structure is similar: things have real natures that are prior to and independent of how we describe them, and knowing what something is requires knowing its essence.

Kit Fine's "Essence and Modality" (1994) sharpened the picture. Fine argued that essential properties cannot be identified with necessary properties: it is necessary that Socrates is a member of the singleton set {Socrates}, but this is not part of Socrates's essence — it is a fact about the set, not a revelation of what Socrates fundamentally is. Essence must be understood through real definition — what a thing fundamentally is — not through modal operators alone. This opened a research program in which essence, grounding, and ontological dependence are primitive explanatory categories, richer than what possible-worlds semantics alone provides.

## Armstrong's Immanent Realism and Contemporary Neo-Aristotelianism

David Armstrong's systematic work on universals (*Universals and Scientific Realism*, 1978) provided the formal framework. Against nominalism (which denies universals) and Platonic realism (which places universals in a transcendent realm), Armstrong defended Aristotelian immanent realism: universals exist, but only in their instances, with no existence independent of some instantiation. This *in rebus* position grounds natural kinds in genuine universals that are multiply realized in particulars, connecting metaphysics directly to the philosophy of science: laws of nature are contingent relations between universals; causal powers are grounded in the universals things instantiate.

E. J. Lowe's four-category ontology (*The Four-Category Ontology*, 2006) explicitly revives and modernizes Aristotle's *Categories*. Four irreducible categories: individual substances, substantial kinds, individual modes (tropes), and attributes (property universals). Objects instantiate kinds; kinds are characterized by attributes; objects possess modes. The kind *tiger* determines what properties tigers essentially have; individual tigers instantiate the kind and possess particular modes that characterize them. This framework captures the Aristotelian insight that both the kind and the individual are ontologically significant — neither reducible to the other — while connecting to contemporary debates about properties, causation, and persistence.

Kathrin Koslicki's work on material constitution, David Wiggins's sortal-based account of persistence, and Jonathan Lowe's formal ontology collectively constitute a renaissance of serious substance metaphysics. Whether it is more than a sophisticated restatement of Aristotle in contemporary vocabulary — whether neo-Aristotelianism genuinely advances our understanding rather than redescribing the same problems — is a challenge its defenders take seriously and continue to address.
