# Essential vs. Accidental Properties

Socrates has many properties. He is snub-nosed. He is Athenian. He is the husband of Xanthippe. He is human. He is an animal. The question is: which of these does he have necessarily — in a sense connected to what he is — and which does he merely happen to have? The distinction between essential and accidental properties is ancient; Aristotle marked it with his distinction between *per se* and *per accidens* predication. But the contemporary discussion took a sharp new turn when Kit Fine argued in 1994 that the standard modal analysis of essence was inadequate, and that the correct analysis requires a notion of real definition irreducible to necessity.

The modal account, associated with Kripke's *Naming and Necessity*, identifies essential properties with necessary properties: *F* is essential to *x* if and only if *x* could not have existed without being *F*. Socrates is essentially human because there is no possible world where Socrates exists but is not human. He is accidentally snub-nosed because he could have existed with a different nose. The analysis is clean and formally tractable, using possible worlds to cash out the modality.

Fine's counterexample punctures this. It is necessary that Socrates is a member of the singleton set {Socrates}: in every world where Socrates exists, {Socrates} also exists (sets exist necessarily if their members do) and Socrates belongs to it. So by the modal criterion, being a member of {Socrates} is essential to Socrates. But this is absurd — being a member of the singleton set formed from Socrates is a fact about the set {Socrates}, not a revelation of what Socrates fundamentally is. The set depends on Socrates, not the other way around. Essential properties are supposed to be what something is *in virtue of its own nature*; the singleton set membership fails this test completely.

Fine's diagnosis: necessity is too broad a criterion. What we need is the notion of a real definition — the account of what a thing fundamentally is. A property is essential to *x* if and only if it belongs to *x*'s real definition. Formally, let *E(x, F)* stand for "F is essential to x" and *□(∃x → Fx)* for the modal claim that necessarily, if *x* exists then *x* is *F*. Fine's point:

- □(∃Socrates → Socrates ∈ {Socrates}) holds.
- E(Socrates, λx. x ∈ {Socrates}) does not hold.

Hence E(x, F) cannot be defined as □(∃x → Fx). Essentialist truths are, on Fine's view, prior to necessary truths: it is necessary that Socrates is human *because* being human is part of Socrates's real definition, not the reverse. Modality is grounded in essence; essence is not analyzed in terms of modality.

## The Neo-Aristotelian Framework

Aristotle's own account connects essence to form. The essential properties of a thing flow from its form — the formal principle that organizes its matter and determines its characteristic activities. For a tiger, the form determines that it is a carnivore, a mammal, a member of a particular biological lineage; these are essential. Being three years old or being in a particular location — these are accidental, properties the tiger happens to have that do not follow from what it is.

Contemporary neo-Aristotelians deploy this in three main areas. First, natural-kind classification: what makes something gold is its essential property (atomic number 79), not its accidental properties (color in ordinary light, malleableness). The natural-kind structure of reality is the structure of essential properties. Second, personal identity over time: what makes me *me* across time involves essential properties — being this particular biological organism, having this particular origin — while changes in accidental properties (beliefs, personality, body composition) do not threaten identity. Third, artifact individuation: knives are essentially cutting instruments, which raises the puzzle that this functional essence seems assigned by design intention rather than intrinsic nature. If functional essence is partly conventional, artifact essences have a weaker metaphysical status than the essences of natural kinds.

David Wiggins's work in *Sameness and Substance Renewed* (2001) ties essential properties to *sortal concepts*. A sortal is a concept that supplies both a criterion of application — what it takes to fall under it — and a criterion of identity — what it takes to be the same F as before. "Tiger" and "water molecule" are sortals; "red thing" and "object on the table" are not. On Wiggins's account, every concrete entity falls under a substance sortal that determines its fundamental nature and persistence conditions. Essential properties are those that the substance sortal requires; accidental properties are those it merely permits. This connects the essential/accidental distinction directly to questions of persistence and reidentification.

The epistemological problem is genuine. If essence is not simply necessity, how do we know what is essential? Fine invokes real definitions, but this requires an account of how we access them. Aristotle appealed to *nous* — a form of intellectual intuition — but this is obscure. Contemporary essentialists appeal to reflective equilibrium among intuitions, comparison of cases, and inference from what scientific investigation reveals about underlying natures. Whether this yields a reliable method for distinguishing essential from accidental properties, or merely systematizes our prior intuitions, is an ongoing dispute. The suspicion that essentialism is projecting our conceptual interests onto a world that does not really divide into essential and accidental features remains a standing challenge that neo-Aristotelians must answer.
