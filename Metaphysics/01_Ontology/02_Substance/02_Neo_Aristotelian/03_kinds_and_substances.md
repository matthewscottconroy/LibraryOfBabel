# Kinds and Substances

Consider what we say when we identify something. "That's a tiger." "This is gold." "She's a person." In each case we are not just attaching a label to an individual; we are locating it within a kind that carries systematic implications — about what it can and cannot do, about what changes it can survive, about what is possible for it. The tiger is necessarily a mammal, necessarily a carnivore, necessarily subject to the biological history of *Panthera tigris*. The gold sample is necessarily composed of atoms with atomic number 79. These are not accidental features we happen to find correlated with our samples; they are what those things *are*. Neo-Aristotelian substance theory holds that the answer to "what is it?" connects individual substances to natural kinds that determine their essential properties and causal powers.

Aristotle's *Categories* marks this with the distinction between primary and secondary substances. Primary substances are individual things — this particular horse, this particular Socrates. Secondary substances are the species and genera they fall under — horse, mammal, animal, human. Secondary substances are not merely nominal groupings; they are real features of the world that determine the nature of the primary substances instantiating them. To know that something is a horse is to know something about its essential nature — its capacities, its characteristic activities, its persistence conditions. The kind is ontologically significant, not just epistemologically convenient.

Contemporary neo-Aristotelianism follows Kripke and Putnam in treating natural kinds as genuinely mind-independent categories with real essences. Water is not just "whatever we call water" but a determinate natural kind whose essential property is molecular constitution (H₂O). The kind determines both what changes an instance can undergo while persisting — a water molecule can survive changes in temperature, pressure, and state, but not changes in molecular constitution — and what causal powers the instance has, grounded in the universal structure shared by all members of the kind.

## Scientific Essentialism and Kinds

Brian Ellis and Caroline Lierse developed *scientific essentialism* as a systematic extension of this framework. Each natural kind has an essence that determines its characteristic dispositions and causal powers. The essence of gold includes not just atomic number 79 but all the dispositional properties that flow from that atomic structure: electrical conductivity, chemical reactivity, melting point. These powers are essential — remove them and you no longer have gold — and they are discovered empirically. Scientific investigation tells us not just what gold happens to be like but what it must be like.

This connects to a *powers ontology*: the fundamental properties of natural kinds are dispositions rather than categorical features. A salt crystal is disposed to dissolve in water; an electron is disposed to attract protons. These dispositions are essential to the kinds whose members have them, and they ground the laws of nature as generalizations about what members of kinds will do. On this picture, natural laws are not contingent regularities imposed on an indifferent natural world; they flow from the essential natures of the kinds they govern.

## Sortal Concepts and Persistence

David Wiggins, in *Sameness and Substance Renewed* (2001), connects kinds to persistence through the notion of a *sortal concept*. A sortal is a concept that supplies both a criterion of application — what it takes for something to fall under it — and a criterion of identity — what it takes for something to be the same F at different times. "Tiger," "person," "water molecule," and "nation" are sortals; "red thing" and "object on the table" are not, because they do not supply identity criteria.

On Wiggins's view, every concrete entity falls under a *substance sortal* — the sortal that determines its fundamental nature. Questions about persistence and identity are only well-formed relative to a sortal: "Is this the same?" is defective; "Is this the same tiger?" is well-formed. Essential properties are those the substance sortal requires; accidental properties are those it merely permits. The result is a systematic connection between natural kinds, essential properties, and the persistence conditions of individual substances.

E. J. Lowe's four-category ontology (*The Four-Category Ontology*, 2006) provides the formal framework. Individual substances instantiate substantial kinds; individual modes (tropes) characterize individual substances; attributes characterize kinds. The kind *tiger* is a universal that determines what properties tigers essentially have; individual tigers instantiate the kind and possess particular modes. Neither the kind nor the individual is reducible to the other: the kind is not an abstraction from individual tigers, and the individual tiger is not merely an instance of a type with no further ontological significance.

## The Problem of Biological Species

Biological species present a serious challenge. They might seem to be paradigmatic natural kinds, but the species concept is contested and may not satisfy the conditions neo-Aristotelians require. Different species concepts — the biological species concept (Mayr: reproductive isolation), the phylogenetic concept (monophyletic lineage), the ecological concept (niche occupation) — often disagree on their verdicts. More fundamentally, species may lack the microstructural essences that Kripke-Putnam essentialism requires. Different tigers have different genomes; there is no DNA sequence that all and only tigers possess. Tigers are defined partly by evolutionary history and genealogical relations, not purely by intrinsic qualitative properties.

David Hull and Michael Ghiselin pressed this into a provocative thesis: species are not kinds but *individuals* — spatiotemporally extended historical lineages. If they are right, individual organisms are not instances of the kind "tiger" but parts of the individual entity *Panthera tigris*, the way cells are parts of organisms. The relation between Shere Khan and *Panthera tigris* would be part-whole, not instance-kind. This would mean that the neo-Aristotelian framework, which works well for chemical and physical kinds, does not extend to the biological domain without significant revision.

Kathrin Koslicki has argued that the metaphysical and biological questions are partly independent. Even if species are historical lineages, individual organisms may still fall under natural kinds — determined by their developmental plan, their functional organization, their characteristic causal powers — that fix their persistence conditions and essential properties. Whether the kinds organisms fall under are species or something more fine-grained is a question for biology; whether there are any such kinds at all is the metaphysical question. The neo-Aristotelian answer is yes, and the detail remains to be worked out.
