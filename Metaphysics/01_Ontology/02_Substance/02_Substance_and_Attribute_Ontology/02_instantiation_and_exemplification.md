# Instantiation and Exemplification

Suppose we accept that substances and properties are genuinely distinct kinds of entity. This apple and redness are two different kinds of thing: the apple is a particular, redness is a universal. We then face the question: what connects them? What makes the apple *red* rather than merely adjacent to redness? The natural answer is that the apple instantiates redness — there is a relation, instantiation, that holds between particular and universal when the particular has the property. But this answer immediately generates a puzzle that F. H. Bradley identified in *Appearance and Reality* (1893) and that has haunted substance-and-attribute ontology ever since.

Bradley's argument: if instantiation is a genuine relation, it is a further entity connecting the particular and the universal. But then we need to ask how the particular stands in the instantiation relation to the universal. The answer must be: by instantiation — but now we have a further instance of instantiation, requiring a further relation, and so on without end. The regress is infinite.

Formally: let Inst(s, P) mean "substance *s* instantiates property *P*."

- Socrates instantiates wisdom: Inst(Socrates, wisdom).
- This is itself a relational fact, seemingly requiring: Inst(Inst, ⟨Socrates, wisdom⟩).
- And so on for every further step.

The question is whether this regress is vicious or benign. A vicious regress would prevent the original predication from being grounded — to ground "Socrates is wise," we would need to complete an infinite series, which we cannot do. A benign regress is merely an infinite series of truths that holds, without each step needing to be "assembled" before the next is available. Michael Loux and others argue the regress is benign: the truth of "Socrates is wise" requires only the first-order fact, and the regress is a series of further truths that follow from it without needing to precede it. The regress does not block the grounding; it is, in a sense, superfluous.

## The States-of-Affairs Response

The most influential contemporary response is Armstrong's states-of-affairs ontology. The fundamental units of reality are states of affairs — *a's being F*, *b's being G* — in which a particular instantiates a universal as a single, indivisible fact. Neither the particular nor the universal exists independently of any state of affairs; they are abstractions from the facts in which they figure. The unity of *a's being F* is primitive; there is no separate instantiation relation that could generate the regress.

The structure: *a* instantiates *F* if and only if the state of affairs [*a*'s being *F*] obtains; neither *a* nor *F* exists independently of some state of affairs; the unity of [*a*'s being *F*] is not grounded in any further entity. The regress does not arise because there is no separate instantiation relation to iterate — the state of affairs is the basic unit, and the connection of particular to universal is internal to it.

Armstrong developed this in *A World of States of Affairs* (1997). One consequence: the particular (the "thin particular") is an abstraction from states of affairs, not a separately existing entity. The thin particular is what all states of affairs involving the same object have in common — a genuine ontological constituent, but not one with independent existence. This avoids the bare particular problem (the thin particular is not stripped of all connection to properties) while preserving the individuating role.

The states-of-affairs approach connects naturally to *truthmaker theory*: for every contingent truth, there is an entity in virtue of whose existence the proposition is true. "Socrates is wise" requires a truthmaker — something whose existence necessitates the truth of the proposition. The natural candidate is the state of affairs *Socrates's being wise*: if this state of affairs obtains, the proposition is true; if it does not obtain, the proposition is false. Truthmaker theory thus links the metaphysics of substance and property to the theory of truth, grounding semantic notions in ontological structure.

## Trope Theory as an Alternative

A different approach abandons universals and the instantiation relation entirely in favor of tropes — particular, unrepeatable property instances. The redness of this apple is a numerically unique particular, distinct from the redness of any other apple, even if qualitatively indistinguishable from it. To say "this apple is red" is to say that this apple has a red trope — a particular instance of redness that is a concrete part of the apple. The regress does not arise because tropes are not universals requiring instantiation; they are already particulars, and their relation to the objects that "have" them is the intimate relation of part to whole (or compresence to compresence class).

Keith Campbell's *Abstract Particulars* (1990) is the most systematic contemporary defense. On his view, the world is a world of tropes; ordinary objects are bundles of compresent tropes; there are no universals, no bare particulars, no instantiation relation. The cost is explaining what makes two tropes of the same type — why the redness of this apple is the *same kind* as the redness of that apple — without appealing to a shared universal. Campbell's answer is resemblance: tropes of the same type resemble each other exactly. Whether resemblance itself requires a universal (a resemblance-universal instantiated by pairs of similar tropes) or can be treated as a primitive relation among tropes is the central objection to trope theory and the focus of ongoing debate.
