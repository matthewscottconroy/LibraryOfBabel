# Neo-Aristotelian Substance

Mid-twentieth-century analytic philosophy had little patience for traditional substance metaphysics. Logical empiricism dismissed ontological categories as meaningless; ordinary-language philosophy treated puzzlement about substance as a symptom of grammatical confusion. Then something shifted. By the 1990s and into the twenty-first century, notions of substance, form, essence, and natural kinds had returned to the center of analytic metaphysics, reconstructed with the formal resources of predicate logic, possible-worlds semantics, and contemporary biology. This neo-Aristotelian turn is not a nostalgic retrieval but a genuine theoretical development, and its core claim is that the Aristotelian categories capture structural features of reality that leaner, more empiricist ontologies cannot adequately represent.

The substance-accident distinction is one place to start. Aristotle distinguished between substances — Socrates, this horse — and the accidents that qualify them at a time. A substance can undergo accidental change while remaining numerically the same substance; Socrates can become paler or bald while remaining Socrates. But a substance cannot change in its essential properties — it cannot survive losing the kind-membership that constitutes its nature. E. J. Lowe's *The Four-Category Ontology* (2006) gives this distinction contemporary precision. Lowe distinguishes individual substances (particular countable things), substantial kinds (species and genera under which substances fall), attributes (repeatable property universals), and modes (particular, unrepeatable instances of attributes). Substances instantiate kinds; attributes characterize kinds; modes characterize substances. This four-fold structure captures the Aristotelian insight that substances stand in dual relations to both their kind and their particular properties, without collapsing into either the bundle-theory problem (properties without an owner) or the bare-particular problem (an owner with no qualitative connection to the properties it owns).

Hylomorphism is the second major strand. Kathrin Koslicki's *The Structure of Objects* (2008) takes seriously both matter and form as irreducible constituents of composite objects. Against the view that composition is simply mereological overlap — that a composite object is just whatever its parts compose into — Koslicki argues that the form, the structural principle, plays an irreducible ontological role in explaining the unity, identity conditions, and characteristic properties of composite substances. Two heaps of wood do not become one entity by being spatially adjacent; they become one entity when organized by a form — a structural principle that grounds the unity of the composite. A heap is not a substance; a house is.

## Essence, Grounding, and the Primacy of Real Definitions

Kit Fine's "Essence and Modality" (1994) reorganized the field. Fine's argument — that essence cannot be analyzed in terms of necessity, because necessary properties (like being such that 2+2=4) are not thereby essential to anything — opened the program of *essence-first* metaphysics. What a thing essentially is cannot be read off from what is necessarily true of it in all possible worlds; it must be read off from its real definition, the account of what the thing fundamentally is. And modality flows from essence: it is necessary that Socrates is human *because* being human is part of Socrates's real definition, not the other way around.

This connects to the contemporary program of *grounding*. Some facts hold *in virtue of* other facts; reality has a layered structure of ontological dependence. Fundamental substances and their essences are at the ground floor; their dispositions and causal powers are grounded in their essences; laws of nature are grounded in the essential natures of the kinds they govern; higher-level facts — chemical, biological, psychological, social — are grounded in lower-level facts, ultimately in the essences of whatever is fundamental. This essence-first, grounding-structured picture gives the neo-Aristotelian program a distinctive shape: it starts from real definitions and asks what flows from the natures of things, rather than starting from modal logic and asking what exists in possible worlds.

David Wiggins's sortal-relative account of identity (*Sameness and Substance Renewed*, 2001) connects substance ontology to philosophy of language. A substance sortal — "tiger," "person," "gold atom" — supplies both a criterion of application and a criterion of identity: what it takes to fall under the concept, and what it takes to be the same F over time. Every individual substance falls under a substance sortal that determines its fundamental nature and persistence conditions. Questions about identity and persistence are only fully formed when a sortal is specified. Wiggins's account thus grounds the metaphysics of substance in the logical structure of sortal concepts — a move that connects classical Aristotelianism to philosophy of language in a way that Aristotle himself did not anticipate.

Whether neo-Aristotelianism is more than sophisticated relabeling — whether it genuinely advances our understanding rather than describing the same puzzles in updated vocabulary — is the persistent methodological challenge its defenders must face. What they can say is that the Aristotelian categories generate fruitful research programs, connect to independently motivated results in biology and physics, and avoid the explanatory gaps left by leaner ontologies. That is a serious argument, even if it is not a knock-down one.
