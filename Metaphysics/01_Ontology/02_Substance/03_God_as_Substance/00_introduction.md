# God as Substance

The question of God's existence is the subject of Unit 13 of this textbook. But there is a prior ontological question: if God exists, what kind of thing is God? How does the concept of God relate to the concept of substance? This question has been central to theology and metaphysics from the Presocratics to the present, and it reveals tensions in the concept of substance that are not visible when we restrict attention to finite material things.

Classical theism holds that God is a substance — indeed, the primary substance, the one on which all other substances depend for their existence. Aquinas held that God is ipsum esse subsistens — subsistent being itself — the only substance whose essence is identical to its existence. God does not merely have being; God is being. This view makes God ontologically unique: all other substances have contingent existence (they exist but might not have existed), while God exists necessarily, by the nature of what God is.

But if God is a substance, what are God's properties? Classical theism adds divine simplicity to the picture: God has no real distinctions of properties — no distinction between essence and existence, between attributes and the divine nature. God is utterly simple; the multiplicity of divine attributes (omnipotence, omniscience, goodness) reflects our limited perspective on a unity that admits no internal distinction. This creates the puzzle of predication: if "God is powerful" and "God is knowing" ascribe the same thing to God (since divine simplicity eliminates distinctions among attributes), what do these predications mean?

Process theology offers a radically different picture: God is not a substance at all but a process — the most inclusive and comprehensive of Whitehead's "actual occasions," engaged in ongoing creative interaction with the world rather than timelessly complete. This section examines these contrasting ontological pictures of the divine and what they require from the general theory of substance.
