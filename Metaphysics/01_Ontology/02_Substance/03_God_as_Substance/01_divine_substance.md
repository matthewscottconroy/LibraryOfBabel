# Divine Substance

The philosophical tradition has consistently tried to apply the concept of substance to God, and consistently encountered the problem that God, as traditionally conceived, does not fit the category comfortably. Ordinary substances are composite, dependent, contingent, and characterized by properties they might have lacked. God is supposed to be none of these things. If substance means what Aristotle and the tradition meant — a particular, individuated, contingently existing bearer of properties — then God is not a substance in that sense, or is a substance only in some stretched, analogical sense. Working out what sense is the central problem of philosophical theology.

Aristotle's unmoved mover is his closest approach. The mover is pure actuality — form without matter — with no potentiality, no composition, no capacity for being otherwise. It is the most fully actual thing there is, a self-thinking thought (*noêsis noêseôs*) that sustains the motion of the heavens by being the object of their attraction. This is substance in the richest Aristotelian sense: fully self-sufficient, fully actual, the most real of all things. But it is not the personal God of the Abrahamic traditions; it is closer to a principle of pure intellectual activity than to a creator and sustainer of the cosmos.

Aquinas builds on Aristotle through Neoplatonism and Christian theology to arrive at a more radical claim. God is not a being that has existence; God is *esse subsistens*, subsistent being itself. In every creature, essence and existence are really distinct: being human does not entail existing; existence must be received from outside, from God as efficient, exemplary, and final cause. In God alone, essence and existence are identical: God's essence just is to exist, which is why God necessarily exists and why the divine nature is unlike any creaturely nature. God is not a being among beings — an entity of a certain type — but the ground of being itself, by participation in which all other things have whatever being they have.

This makes the theological concept of substance significantly different from its ontological use. God as *esse subsistens* is substance in a sense analogically related to, but not identical with, creaturely substance. Aquinas's analogical predication — we apply the same terms to God and creatures, but the terms apply differently — is the attempt to hold onto the concept while acknowledging its inadequacy to the divine reality.

## Spinoza and Plantinga: Two Extremes

Spinoza's identification of God with the single infinite substance of which all finite things are modes (*Ethics*, I) is the most radical use of substance vocabulary in the tradition. God *is* the totality of nature — infinite, self-caused (*causa sui*), the sole genuine substance. This deity has no purposes, does not intervene in history, cannot be prayed to in any personal sense. Spinoza's equation *Deus sive Natura* is deliberately meant to dissolve the distinction between the sacred and the natural. Whether this is theism, pantheism, or atheism in substance has been debated ever since.

Plantinga's analytic theism in *The Nature of Necessity* (1974) takes the opposite approach. There is a possible world in which a maximally great being exists — omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good in every possible world. Since maximal greatness entails existence in every possible world, such a being exists necessarily, in all possible worlds including the actual world. God is a necessarily existing individual substance with maximal essential properties — a person, in a non-human sense — distinct from and prior to the natural world. This is classical theism with the tools of modal semantics.

Contemporary debates in philosophical theology sort into four positions. Classical theism (Aquinas, Dolezal) holds that God is a fully determinate substance — simple, immutable, atemporal — with essential properties. Process theology (Whitehead, Hartshorne) holds that God is not omnipotent but supremely responsive, enriched by the world's experiences and genuinely affected by what happens. Open theism (Sanders, Pinnock) preserves divine personhood and responsiveness while revising classical omniscience — God knows free future choices are possible but not determined. Relational theism holds that the divine being is inherently constituted by relations to the world.

Each of these positions involves a different metaphysical picture of God's relation to the category of substance, and the differences are not merely terminological. Whether God can be composite or must be simple, whether God can change or must be immutable, whether God is in time or outside it — these are questions about what kind of substance (if any) God is, and they connect the metaphysics of substance directly to the central questions of philosophical theology.
