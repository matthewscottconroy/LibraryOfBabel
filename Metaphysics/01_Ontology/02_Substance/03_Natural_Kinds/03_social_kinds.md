# Social Kinds

A piece of paper with certain markings on it is worthless — intrinsically, it is just paper and ink. Yet it is also worth twenty dollars. A spoken sequence of words in front of witnesses is just sounds in the air. Yet it is also a legally binding contract. A hand gesture across a table is a physical event like any other. Yet it is also an acceptance of an offer, which creates obligations and transfers rights. What makes the difference? Nothing in the physics or chemistry distinguishes these cases. The difference lies in what people collectively take these things to mean and in the institutional structures within which they are embedded. Money, contracts, property rights, marriages, corporations — these are social kinds, and their existence is a genuine philosophical puzzle. They are real — they have causal powers, they structure lives, they can be violated and exploited — but they exist in a mode entirely different from natural kinds like gold or water.

Searle's account in *The Construction of Social Reality* (1995) is the most systematic analysis. Social kinds, on his view, are created by constitutive rules with the form "X counts as Y in context C." A piece of paper counts as a dollar in the context of the United States financial system. An exchange of vows counts as a marriage in the context of a recognized ceremony. A checkmate position counts as a win in the context of chess. These rules are not merely descriptive generalizations about what tends to happen; they are constitutive — they bring the relevant institutional entities into existence by establishing the framework within which certain physical objects or actions count as having certain institutional statuses. Before the institution of money existed, there were no dollars. The constitutive rules of the financial system create the kind *dollar*, not merely a new instance of a prior kind.

The dependence goes all the way down to collective intentionality. Money is money because enough people collectively treat certain objects as money — because they have shared beliefs, attitudes, and commitments directed toward that institutional status. If all those attitudes were simultaneously to disappear, dollars would cease to be money. The paper would remain, but its dollar-status would vanish. This makes social kinds metaphysically dependent on minds in a way that natural kinds are not: gold would be gold whether or not anyone believed it.

## Structure, Power, and Constructed Reality

Searle's account treats collective intentionality as the fundamental ground of social reality. Sally Haslanger's structuralist approach in *Resisting Reality* (2012) challenges this by insisting that the most important social kinds — race, gender, social class — are constituted not primarily by shared attitudes but by structural arrangements of power, labor, recognition, and resources. To be a woman, on Haslanger's account, is to be systematically positioned in a structure of subordination and to be marked as a target of that treatment by perceived or imagined bodily features. To be racialized as Black in the United States is to occupy a structural position constituted by historical and ongoing arrangements of advantage and disadvantage.

Notice what this does to the Searlean picture. If race and gender were constituted by collective attitudes in Searle's sense, then changing those attitudes — persuading everyone to stop treating bodies in race-marking ways — would be sufficient to eliminate race. Haslanger's account implies otherwise: a society that had eliminated the beliefs and attitudes associated with racial categories but retained the structural arrangements — differential access to housing, education, employment, legal protection — would still have race, because the structural positions that constitute racial categories would still exist. What determines the existence of a social kind, on the structuralist account, is not what people think but how social arrangements actually work.

This is an explicitly political claim as well as a metaphysical one. If social kinds are real but structurally constituted and historically contingent, then they are genuine objects of social scientific study and political intervention, but they require structural change rather than merely attitudinal transformation. The ontology connects directly to practical conclusions about what kind of work justice demands.

Several objections press on this whole enterprise. The ontological inflation objection notes that if social kinds are real, reality is populated by vast numbers of institutional entities — dollars, marriages, laws, corporations, social positions — that exist only in virtue of human practices. The natural response is to accept the proliferation: it is not an embarrassment but an accurate description of the complexity of human social life. Social reality is as real as physical reality; it is just differently grounded. The mind-dependence objection asks whether anything genuinely real can depend on what people think. The response is that real and mind-independent are not the same thing: social kinds have genuine causal powers (money causes people to work; gender shapes life trajectories) even though they would not exist without the relevant practices. The stability objection notes that social kinds change as social practices change, making them less fixed than natural kinds. But social kinds can be stable enough for scientific study and political intervention even if they are not as fixed as gold: the homeostatic mechanisms maintaining social kinds — institutions, norms, laws, practices — are different from those maintaining natural kinds, but the structure is analogous.

What emerges from the debate about social kinds is a picture of reality as genuinely stratified: physical reality at one level, natural kinds at another, and social-institutional reality at a third, each with its own mode of existence and its own dependence relations. The third level is no less real for being dependent on human practice, and its analysis requires tools from social ontology, philosophy of language, and political philosophy alongside the standard toolkit of analytic metaphysics. The social kinds debate thus illustrates one of the recurring themes in the metaphysics of substance and kinds: the categories through which we organize reality are not mere descriptions of a pre-given natural order but are partly constitutive of the world we inhabit — and how we get those categories right matters, metaphysically and practically, in ways that affect the concrete conditions of human life.
