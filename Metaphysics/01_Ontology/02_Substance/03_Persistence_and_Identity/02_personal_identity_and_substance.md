# Personal Identity and Substance

You share almost no atoms with yourself of ten years ago. Your beliefs, desires, and personality have changed substantially. Your body has undergone continuous replacement and transformation. Yet you are, we assume, the same person. What grounds this? The question of personal identity is not merely academic: it matters for moral responsibility (should we punish someone for what they did twenty years ago?), for prudential rationality (why care specially about your future self rather than future persons generally?), and for the metaphysics of persons as a kind of substance. The history of this debate runs from Locke through Kant through Parfit to the contemporary neo-Aristotelian revival, and it has not yet reached consensus.

Descartes's answer is the simplest: you are a soul, an immaterial thinking substance, and personal identity consists in the persistence of that soul. The soul may survive radical bodily change and bodily death entirely. The difficulty is that this answer does not so much explain personal identity as relocate the problem. What makes two soul-stages stages of the *same* soul? If the soul is simple and indivisible, its identity might be declared primitive — but that removes it from rational inquiry. Kant pressed the point in the Paralogisms of Pure Reason: the logical form of first-person thought does not guarantee any substantive metaphysical conclusion about a simple, persisting thinking substance. "I think" does not entail "there is a substance that thinks."

Locke's alternative was decisively influential. In *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* Book II Chapter 27, personal identity consists in *psychological continuity*, specifically memory. To be the same person as a past individual is to be able to remember that individual's actions and experiences as one's own. This severs personal identity from both material continuity and soul identity — the same soul might constitute different persons if it lost all memory of its earlier life.

Thomas Reid raised the circularity objection immediately: memory presupposes personal identity rather than constituting it. To remember doing something is to remember *oneself* doing it — the first-person perspective of memory already embeds an identity claim. Using memory to define identity is circular. Reid's objection identifies a genuine problem with Locke's specific formulation. Neo-Lockeans responded by reformulating the account in terms of broader psychological continuity rather than memory specifically.

## Parfit's Reductionism and What Matters

Derek Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* (1984) transformed the debate. He argues for reductionism: personal identity consists in nothing over and above physical and psychological facts about continuity and connectedness. There is no further fact about whether some future person is *really* you, over and above the psychological and physical connections. Personal identity is not a deep, further fact.

The key argument is through fission cases. Suppose your cerebral hemispheres are transplanted into two separate bodies. Both resulting persons are psychologically continuous with you; each has strong memory connections, shares your beliefs and personality. But numerical identity requires uniqueness. So at most one of the resulting persons can be numerically identical to you. Suppose it is the left-hemisphere recipient. But why? The case is perfectly symmetric — the right-hemisphere recipient has equal psychological connections to you. By parity of reasoning, the right-hemisphere recipient is equally well the same person as you. But they cannot both be identical to you without being identical to each other, which they are not. The conclusion Parfit draws: *neither* is strictly identical to you, yet both stand in the only relations that matter — psychological continuity and connectedness. If you would survive if only one hemisphere were transplanted, and fission just doubles this, why is death more appropriate than survival? Parfit concluded that what matters in survival is not strict numerical identity but psychological continuity-and-connectedness, which can come in degrees and can branch.

This has radical moral implications. If personal identity is not what matters, special self-interested concern for one's own future is less rationally mandatory than we thought. The continuity of what we care about does not require that the future person be strictly identical to us.

## Neo-Aristotelian Responses

Contemporary neo-Aristotelians resist this reductionist conclusion by grounding personal identity in substance-kind membership. David Wiggins's *Sameness and Substance Renewed* (2001) argues that personal identity is grounded in the persistence of a substance falling under a substance sortal. For persons, the relevant sortal is something like *human being* or *person*; its persistence conditions are not purely psychological but biological and organizational. What makes you the same person over time is that you continue to instantiate the natural kind that grounds personal existence in the first place — a kind whose essential properties include both biological and psychological features.

Lynne Rudder Baker's constitution view (*Persons and Bodies*, 2000) offers a different neo-Aristotelian response. You are not identical to your body, but your body constitutes you. You and your body are distinct entities that coincide spatially but differ in persistence conditions: the body could persist through psychological erasure that would destroy the person; in principle the person might persist through a substrate change that would destroy the body. What is distinctive about persons — and what grounds their being a distinct kind of substance — is possession of a first-person perspective: the capacity to think of oneself as oneself, from the inside. This constitutes persons as a distinctive kind of entity irreducible to any purely biological or physical description.

The debate between animalist, psychological, neo-Aristotelian, and constitution views has not been resolved. What has become clear is that the question of personal identity cannot be answered without taking a position on the metaphysics of substance more broadly — on what kinds of things exist, what grounds their persistence, and whether there are irreducibly mind-dependent features of reality. Personal identity is not a detached puzzle about an edge case; it is where the central questions of substance theory become most practically significant.
