# Events vs. Substances

Suppose you ask what the most basic things in the world are. The substance theorist says: things — rocks, atoms, persons — persisting particulars that undergo change. The event ontologist says: happenings — explosions, collisions, migrations — particular occurrences located in spacetime. The question is not merely verbal. It determines what kinds of things can be causally efficacious, what the logical form of causal statements is, and whether mental events can be identified with physical events in a philosophically tractable way.

Donald Davidson's influential event ontology, developed in *Essays on Actions and Events* (1980), treats events as particular, unrepeatable concrete occurrences — as fundamental as the objects of substance ontology, but of a different ontological kind. My stubbing my toe yesterday is an individual event, not a type. Events enter causal relations, have spatiotemporal location, and can be described in multiple ways. Davidson's individuation criterion for events: two events are identical if and only if they have the same causes and the same effects. This criterion does real work. It allows Davidson to argue that the event described as "my raising my arm" and the event described as "my signaling a turn" are identical — the same concrete event under different descriptions — in the same way that the Morning Star and the Evening Star are identical.

The philosophical payoff was Davidson's anomalous monism: every particular mental event is identical to some particular physical event, but mental and physical descriptions are not systematically reducible — there are no strict psychophysical laws. Mental causation is real (through the physical identities of mental events) but the mental vocabulary is not law-governed. This preserves the causal efficacy of mental states while resisting the claim that psychology reduces to physics.

## Strawson and the Priority of Substances

P. F. Strawson argued in *Individuals* (1959) that substance ontology has a conceptual priority over event ontology that Davidson's account cannot dissolve. We identify events by reference to the substances in which they occur: "the collision of the cars," "Socrates' death." We do not identify substances by reference to events as a standard procedure. The asymmetry reveals that event-description presupposes substance-identification at every step. If we try to analyze substances away as patterns of events, we find that we can only identify those events by referring to the substances — the regress is vicious.

Strawson is making a descriptive metaphysical point: this is the actual structure of our conceptual scheme, and revisionary proposals that invert the priority face the burden of explaining how event-descriptions can be understood without implicit substance-reference. The burden has not been discharged.

Jaegwon Kim's *property exemplification* account tries to honor both positions. On Kim's view, an event is a structure: ⟨object, property, time⟩ — a substance's exemplifying a property at a time. This preserves the close connection between events and substances: there are no events without substances in which they occur. The identity criterion is more determinate than Davidson's: two events are identical if and only if they involve the same object, the same property, and the same time. Kim's account is conservative precisely where Davidson's is liberal — by individuating events finely (by object, property, and time), Kim gets more events but with cleaner identity conditions.

The Davidson-Kim debate is not merely academic. If events are individuated coarsely (Davidson), the same event can be described as mental or physical, and mental causation is preserved by identity. If events are individuated finely (Kim), mental and physical events may come apart as distinct events, raising the overdetermination problem: if the physical event and the mental event are distinct but both cause the same physical effect, we face systematic causal overdetermination — every action is caused twice, once physically and once mentally. Kim concluded that this pushed toward eliminativism about mental causation; Davidson's coarse-grained individuation was motivated precisely by avoiding this conclusion.

## Process Philosophy's Deeper Challenge

Process philosophers — Whitehead, Johanna Seibt, Nicholas Rescher — argue that both substance ontology and the Davidson-Kim event ontology miss a deeper point. The issue is not merely which kind of discrete particular is more basic. The issue is whether a framework of discrete, identifiable particulars (whether things or events) adequately captures the genuinely processual character of nature.

Seibt distinguishes occurrents — things that happen at a time, with clear temporal bounds — from *processes* — things that go on, open-endedly, without fixed boundaries. Davidsonian events are occurrents: my stubbing my toe has a determinate beginning and end. But genuine processes — the biological life of an organism, the evolution of a language, the development of a river delta — are not occurrents. They are ongoing activities that cannot be fully specified by a ⟨object, property, time⟩ triple. Seibt's General Process Theory treats free processes as the basic ontological category: objects are stable recurrent patterns of processes; events are bounded slices of processes. Neither classical substances nor Davidsonian events are fundamental.

The argument from physics is relevant here. Quantum field theory describes the world in terms of fields and excitations, not particles enduring through time. Fundamental physics does not obviously contain entities that satisfy the classical criteria for substance: spatiotemporal continuity, numerical identity through change, genuine independence. Whether this is evidence against substance ontology or merely evidence that the fundamental level of physics is unusual compared with the macroscopic level where substances clearly apply is a question that connects philosophy of physics to the metaphysics of substance in ways that remain incompletely resolved.
