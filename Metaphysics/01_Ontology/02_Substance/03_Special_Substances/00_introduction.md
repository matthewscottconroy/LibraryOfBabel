# Special Substances

The category of substance is broad enough to be applied to things that share little beyond the category label: atoms and nations, persons and rivers, artifacts and organisms. But some entities push the concept of substance to its limits, demanding either a more capacious theory or the concession that not everything that seems like a substance genuinely is one. These are the special substances — entities that exhibit unusual features with respect to the standard criteria of individuation, persistence, and ontological independence.

Events present the first challenge. When we say that the collision between two cars caused the traffic jam, we seem to be talking about events as causes — as ontological items in their own right. But events are not substances in the traditional sense: they are not what predicates are predicated of, they do not persist through change, and they lack determinate boundaries in space and time. Davidson argued that events are particulars on a par with physical objects and that physical event-identity is key to understanding mental causation. Others hold that events are not genuine additions to the ontology but are reducible to facts about substances and times. This debate bears directly on the adequacy of the substance framework.

Holes and absences present a different kind of challenge. What is the ontological status of a hole in a doughnut, or of the absence that causes the plant to die? These seem like causes, and they seem like items we refer to in ordinary discourse. But they are not substances — they are defined by what is not there. Some philosophers hold that apparent reference to holes is reference to the material objects that surround them; others grant holes genuine ontological status as immaterial particulars. The debate illustrates how far the concept of substance extends and where its natural limits lie.

Numbers, sets, and abstract structures present perhaps the most acute challenge: if they exist, what kind of substances are they? They are not material, not spatiotemporally located, and not causally active — yet mathematical discourse seems committed to their existence. This section surveys these cases in depth, examining what they reveal about the scope of substance ontology and whether the category needs to be supplemented, restricted, or replaced.
