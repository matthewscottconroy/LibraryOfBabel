# Functional Essentialism

What makes a knife a knife? The obvious answer — it has a sharp blade — immediately generates a puzzle. If the blade is replaced by a better one, we still have the same knife. If the handle is replaced by a more comfortable one, the same knife again. If both are replaced, and then replaced again, over years of repair: still the same knife, we would normally say. What has remained constant through all these material changes is the function — the knife is still a cutting instrument. But if a smith melts the knife down and reforges it as a spoon, the knife has ceased to exist, even though the same atoms continue. The function has been abandoned, and the knife has gone with it. Functional essentialism draws the explicit conclusion: the essence of an artifact is its function, not its material constitution.

Aristotle anticipated this in his account of form. The form of a saw is the arrangement that makes it capable of cutting; a saw-shaped lump of wax is not a saw because the relevant function cannot be performed. In *De Anima*, the analogous point about the soul: if the eye were an organism, sight would be its soul — the functional organization that makes it what it is. An eye that cannot see is an eye in name only. Functional essentialism about artifacts generalizes this: artifacts are individuated by their design function, and their persistence conditions are tied to the maintenance of that function. The same individual artifact survives material replacement but not functional transformation.

The view has considerable intuitive support. A car remains the same car after an engine replacement because the relevant function (transportation) is preserved. A piano converted into a planter is no longer a piano. A worn pair of shoes remains the same pair after resoling. These judgments are not arbitrary; they track something real about what the artifact is for, and functional essentialism makes explicit what drives them.

## The Social Relativity Problem and the Response

The obvious objection: function seems like an extrinsic, relational property rather than something intrinsic to the object. The same physical configuration could be a knife in a culture that uses it to cut, a ritual weapon in a culture that uses it ceremonially, a paperweight in a context where it is purely decorative. If function determines essence, and function is socially relative, then artifact essence is socially relative — and there is no fact of the matter about what a physical object is, independently of the social context.

The standard response from functional essentialists is to distinguish design function from current use. What constitutes the essence of an artifact is its *proper function* — what it was designed or selected for — not what it happens to be used for at any particular time. An instrument converted into a flowerpot retains its design function as a musical instrument; it is now a badly used instrument, not a flowerpot. The design function is fixed at creation, as a historical fact about the artifact's origin, and constitutes the essence of the artifact type independently of subsequent social use.

Beth Preston's *narrow functional essentialism* develops this most carefully. The proper function of an artifact is what it was selected for through design or cultural evolution, understood in terms of the history of its type. This grounds artifact essence in design history rather than current practice, avoiding social relativity while preserving the insight that artifacts are essentially characterized by function rather than material.

## Multiple Realizability and Individuation

Functional essentialism connects directly to the philosophical discussion of multiple realizability. If the essence of a knife is its function, any object that realizes the cutting function is a knife — regardless of material constitution. This parallels functionalism in philosophy of mind: if the essence of pain is its functional role, any physical state that plays that role is pain, regardless of substrate. Multiple realizability explains the appropriateness of material replacement: since the knife's identity is functional, any material that realizes the function is a legitimate material base.

The individuation problem is harder. Imagine a violin gradually restored, one part at a time, until no original part remains — but throughout the process the violin remains functional and continues to be used and maintained as the same violin. Is it the same violin? Functional essentialism says yes, if the functional integrity is maintained. But now suppose that as each original part is removed, someone builds a second violin from the collected original parts. Two violins, both standing in reasonable continuity relations to the original. Functional essentialism alone cannot distinguish between them.

Thomasson's solution adds historical-causal origin to functional continuity: the artifact that stands in the right causal history to the original — being continuously maintained as the same artifact — is the original. The replica assembled from original parts is not the original because the relevant causal-historical connection has been broken. Function is necessary but not sufficient for artifact identity; the historical chain matters too.

Whether this combination of functional and historical criteria fully accounts for artifact individuation, or whether it faces residual vagueness about what counts as "continuous maintenance," is a live question. The threshold problem — how much replacement is compatible with identity preservation? — may not have a sharp answer, and the artifact case may genuinely involve metaphysical vagueness rather than merely epistemological uncertainty about where a sharp line falls.
