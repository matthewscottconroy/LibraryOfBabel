# The Ship of Theseus

The mythological ship of Theseus was preserved in Athens, but as its planks rotted they were replaced, one at a time. Eventually, after many years of continuous repair and maintenance, no original plank remained. The question: is the ship in the harbor still the Ship of Theseus? Now add the philosophically useful twist. Suppose that each original plank, as it was removed, was carefully stored. Eventually someone assembled all the original planks into a second ship. We now have two ships: the continuously maintained harbor ship (no original parts), and the reassembled ship (all original parts, but not continuously maintained). Which, if either, is the original?

The case generates three competing intuitions, each corresponding to a defensible criterion of artifact identity.

Material continuity: the original is the one made of original parts. What makes something the same ship is material constitution; the reassembled ship is the original, while the harbor ship is a long-running replacement. Spatiotemporal continuity: the original is the continuously maintained harbor ship. Identity through time requires an unbroken spatiotemporal chain; the ship that was never taken apart and never left the harbor is the original, regardless of part replacement. Functional continuity: the original is the harbor ship, because it has continuously played the functional role — being docked, used, identified as the ship — while the planks in storage were merely material. Each criterion gives a principled answer; all three cannot be jointly satisfied.

## The Threshold Problem and Vagueness

One temptation is to look for a threshold: perhaps replacing one plank preserves identity while replacing all planks destroys it, with some unclear middle region. This temptation should be resisted as a response to the puzzle, because it generates a sorites problem that may be unsolvable. If replacing one plank preserves identity, and if each successive plank replacement is no different in principle from the previous one, then replacing all the planks preserves identity — by a long series of individually acceptable steps. Mereological essentialism (Chisholm) bites this bullet in one direction: every part replacement creates a numerically distinct object, so the ship after one plank replacement is already a new ship. This preserves sharp identity conditions at the cost of radical multiplication of ships and wholesale revision of ordinary discourse.

Whether artifact identity is genuinely vague — whether there is no fact of the matter about when identity is lost, as opposed to our merely being ignorant of where the sharp line falls — is an instance of the broader debate between epistemic and semantic accounts of vagueness. Timothy Williamson's epistemic theory holds that there is always a sharp fact about identity, but we cannot know where the threshold is. The competing view is that identity conditions for artifacts are constitutively indeterminate in some range of cases, not just epistemically inaccessible. The Ship of Theseus does not resolve this dispute; it makes it vivid.

## Four-Dimensionalist Dissolution

Four-dimensionalism handles the case by treating the harbor ship and the reassembled ship as two distinct four-dimensional entities that initially overlap completely and then diverge as parts are removed. Both exist throughout the history of the case — the continuously-maintained-ship worm and the planks-of-the-original worm. Initially the two worms are spatiotemporally coincident (the ship in the harbor). As planks are removed and stored, the worms diverge: the harbor worm continues with replacement planks; the planks worm continues as a scattered object in storage, later reconstituted.

Neither worm is determinately the "original" Ship of Theseus — the title can be applied to either, depending on which continuity criterion is privileged. There is no single determinate fact about which ship is the original because "the original" is ambiguous between two distinct four-dimensional objects. The puzzle is dissolved rather than solved: we see that we were asking an ambiguous question.

Whether dissolution is satisfying as an answer depends on whether one thinks the puzzle is genuinely asking about competing four-dimensional objects, or genuinely asking something else — namely, which three-dimensional thing persisting through time deserves the name. If the latter, the four-dimensionalist has changed the subject.

## Generality and Significance

The puzzle generalizes far beyond ships. Nations undergo gradual population change; legal systems undergo gradual statutory replacement; languages undergo continuous phonological and lexical drift. People undergo continuous cellular replacement. In each case, the same trio of criteria applies — material continuity, spatiotemporal continuity, functional or organizational continuity — and the same questions arise about thresholds, vagueness, and which criterion is decisive for which kind of continuant.

The general conclusion the Ship of Theseus supports is that persistence conditions are kind-relative: what makes a ship the same ship over time is different from what makes a person the same person over time. The ship case foregrounds material and functional criteria; the personal identity case foregrounds psychological criteria. The puzzle does not show that there is no fact of the matter about identity through change — it shows that the fact is kind-sensitive, and that working out the persistence conditions for each kind is a genuine philosophical task that requires more than abstract reflection on identity in general.
