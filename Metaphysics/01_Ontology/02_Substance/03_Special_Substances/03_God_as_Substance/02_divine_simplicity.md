# Divine Simplicity and Substance Ontology

Socrates is wise. Ordinary substance ontology interprets this as: Socrates is a subject that has wisdom as a property, where wisdom is distinct from Socrates himself. The subject and the property are different things; having a property is a relation between two distinct entities. The doctrine of divine simplicity denies that this structure applies to God. When we say "God is wise," we are not attributing a distinct property to a subject. God's wisdom *is* God. God's power is God. God's goodness is God. God has no parts, no real internal distinctions, no properties that are distinct from God.

This doctrine, associated with Aquinas and rooted in Neoplatonism (Plotinus's One is absolutely simple), has several interlocking motivations. The argument from aseity: God is absolutely self-sufficient and dependent on nothing. If God were composed of parts — even metaphysical parts like essence and existence, substance and attribute — God would depend on those parts for God's being. But anything God depends on is prior to God, which contradicts divine aseity. For God to be absolutely independent, God must be absolutely simple. The argument from necessity: composites are contingent, since their parts could in principle be otherwise arranged. A necessarily existing being cannot be composite, because the composition might not have held. The argument from infinity: God is unlimited in being. A composed being is limited by its composition; its being is distributed among its parts. An infinite being cannot be composed but must be identical to its own being.

Aquinas's metaphysical formulation: in every creature, essence and existence are really distinct, but in God they are identical. God's essence is "to exist" (*esse*); God is *esse subsistens*, subsistent being itself. This is the metaphysical core of simplicity: God is not a being that happens to exist but the ground of being as such.

## The Paradox of Identical Attributes

The doctrine generates an immediate paradox. If God's wisdom = God's power = God, then by the transitivity of identity, God's wisdom = God's power. But wisdom and power are clearly different properties — a creature can have one without the other. The doctrine of simplicity appears to require identifying things that are manifestly distinct.

Plantinga pressed this objection in *Does God Have a Nature?* (1980). On his reading, simplicity requires asserting the identity of creaturely wisdom with creaturely power, which is flatly contradictory. His alternative: God has a nature — a set of essential properties — but this does not compromise divine sovereignty. The essential properties are necessary features of any possible being, not constraints imposed on God from outside.

Defenders of simplicity respond that Plantinga misunderstands the doctrine. Simplicity does not identify creaturely wisdom with creaturely power and then claim God has both; it holds that the divine reality transcends all creaturely distinctions, and that our multiple attribute-terms are multiple imperfect approximations to a unity that finite intellects cannot fully grasp. The distinctions are on our side, in our way of knowing, not in the divine being itself. The Thomistic formulation: the divine attributes are *virtually distinct* (in the divine power to produce distinct effects in creatures) but not really distinct (not different things in God). James Dolezal (*God Without Parts*, 2011) defends this classical position against contemporary theists who allow real distinctions in God, arguing that any real complexity would compromise aseity.

Jeffrey Brower's neo-Thomistic account attempts a more technically precise synthesis: divine properties are particularized in a distinctive way. God's wisdom is identical to God but is not identical to the universal *wisdom* as it exists in creatures. God has properties, but God's properties are not shared with creatures; they are uniquely, wholly, and simply God. Whether this technically escapes Plantinga's objection or merely reformulates it in more baroque language is debated.

## Simplicity and Responsiveness

The doctrine of divine simplicity connects to divine timelessness and immutability. If God is simple — no real internal distinctions — then God is also immutable (no internal changes) and eternal in the sense of being outside time altogether. This picture of God as absolutely timeless and changeless is philosophically coherent, but it conflicts with religious traditions that emphasize God's love, responsiveness, and genuine engagement with the world. A timeless, immutable deity cannot genuinely respond to prayer, cannot be moved by human suffering, cannot change plans in response to events.

Process theology and open theism represent explicit attempts to recover divine responsiveness at the cost of simplicity, immutability, and classical omniscience. Whitehead's God has a consequent nature — genuinely enriched by the world's experiences — but is not omnipotent and does not control outcomes. Open theism's God knows all possible futures but not which free choices will be made, and so responds to events as they unfold. Whether these revisions preserve what is theologically essential about God while jettisoning what is metaphysically over-demanding, or whether they compromise the divine nature in ways that undermine the religious significance of the God they describe, is a live debate in philosophical theology that connects directly to the metaphysics of substance and its applicability to the divine.
