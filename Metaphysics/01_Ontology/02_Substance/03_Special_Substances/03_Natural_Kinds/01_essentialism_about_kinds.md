# Essentialism About Natural Kinds

Imagine you are holding two clear liquids, both tasteless, both at room temperature, both behaving in every perceptible way exactly like water. One is H₂O; the other is a hypothetical substance — call it XYZ — that Putnam introduced as a thought experiment. They are qualitatively identical. Are they the same substance? Putnam's answer, and Kripke's independently, is no: they are radically different substances despite their qualitative indistinguishability. What makes water water is not how it looks or behaves but what it is — its molecular structure, H₂O, which could in principle have been discovered only through chemistry. This claim — that natural kinds have real essences that fix their identity across all possible worlds, essences discoverable only empirically — is natural kind essentialism, and it represents one of the most significant shifts in the philosophy of language and metaphysics of the twentieth century.

The argument begins with Kripke's account of rigid designation (*Naming and Necessity*, 1972/1980). Names and natural-kind terms designate rigidly: they pick out the same thing in every possible world, not merely the thing that satisfies some description in that world. "Water" designates whatever substance actually has the structure responsible for the familiar behavior of water — the substance that actually fills the lakes and rivers and falls as rain. Once we discover that substance is H₂O, the term "water" rigidly designates H₂O across all possible worlds. A possible world in which some XYZ plays the water-role but H₂O doesn't exist is a world without water — not a world with a different kind of water. Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment makes this vivid. On Twin Earth, qualitatively identical to Earth, XYZ fills the lakes and rivers. Twin Earth speakers who use the word "water" are referring to XYZ, not H₂O. When an astronaut discovers this, she learns that what they call "water" isn't water at all, despite being qualitatively indistinguishable. Meaning, Putnam famously concluded, "ain't in the head." What our natural-kind terms mean depends on the actual constitution of our environment, not just on our mental states.

This generates the thesis of *a posteriori necessity*: necessary truths that can only be known empirically. We had to do chemistry to learn that water is H₂O — no armchair reflection on the concept "water" could have told us. But once we know it, we know a necessary truth: there is no possible world in which water lacks the property of being H₂O. The formal statement is:

- For any natural-kind term *K* with essential property *E*: □(*x* is K ↔ *x* has E)
- *E* is discovered empirically; knowledge of *E* is not a priori.
- Once discovered: necessarily, *x* is water iff *x* is H₂O.

## Scientific Essentialism and Its Critics

Brian Ellis and Caroline Lierse extended this picture into scientific essentialism. On their account, the essential properties of natural kinds are not merely structural but dispositional: gold's atomic number 79 is inseparable from gold's disposition to conduct electricity in certain ways, to resist most acids but dissolve in aqua regia, to have a melting point of 1064°C. These powers flow necessarily from the essential property. This connects natural-kind essentialism to a powers ontology: what a substance is determines how it must behave, so the laws of nature governing gold are grounded in what gold essentially is rather than being brute regularities imposed from outside.

The natural first response to essentialism is to challenge whether biology cooperates. The gradation objection presses hard here: the genome of tigers varies across individuals; there is no single DNA sequence that all and only tigers possess. If essence requires a single necessary microstructural property, chemical kinds provide examples but biological kinds do not. Essentialists reply by distinguishing two cases. Chemical kinds — gold, water, table salt — have sharp microstructural essences. Biological kinds may require a different treatment: perhaps their essences are historical-relational (shared ancestry, reproductive isolation) rather than intrinsic-microstructural. We return to this in the discussion of biological species.

John Dupré's promiscuous realism pushes further (*The Disorder of Things*, 1993). There is no single natural classification of living things; there are many equally legitimate cross-cutting classifications depending on whether one is doing molecular biology, ecology, evolutionary biology, or medicine. The kinds that matter for predator-prey dynamics differ from those that matter for gene-expression networks. No one of these carves nature "at its joints" in any privileged sense. If Dupré is right, then natural-kind essentialism captures at most one level or one purpose-relative projection onto a nature that admits of multiple valid classificatory schemes.

Boyd's homeostatic property cluster (HPC) theory offers a middle path worth pausing on. Rather than requiring a single essential property, Boyd proposes that natural kinds are characterized by *clusters* of properties that co-occur because of causal mechanisms maintaining their co-occurrence. The properties of tigers — their typical genome, morphology, physiology, behavior — form a stable cluster because the homeostatic mechanisms of genetics, development, and ecology maintain it. Species are real natural kinds on this view, not because every tiger shares a single essence, but because the clustering is causally sustained and explanatorily significant. This avoids the sharp-essence requirement while preserving the reality and explanatory importance of natural kinds, and it fits the biological evidence more naturally than strict essentialism does.

What the Kripke-Putnam framework established, and what subsequent debate has refined without overturning, is that natural-kind terms are not mere abbreviated descriptions. When we say "tigers are carnivores," we are not reporting a definitional truth about the concept *tiger* but a discovered fact about what tigers actually are. And what they actually are — however that "what" is ultimately analyzed, whether as microstructural essence or homeostatic cluster or historical lineage — is not fixed by our conceptual schemes but by the world.
