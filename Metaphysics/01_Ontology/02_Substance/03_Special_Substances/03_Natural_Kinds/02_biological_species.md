# Biological Species

What is a species? The biologist's answer seems obvious until you try to make it precise. Tigers are a species. Wolves are a species. The wolf *Canis lupus* and the dog *Canis lupus familiaris* are by some accounts the same species, by others different ones. Ring species — chains of adjacent populations where each can interbreed with its neighbors but the terminal populations cannot — produce genuine cases where nothing in the biology settles whether we have one species or two. Darwin's great achievement was to show that species evolve continuously from one another, which means that there is never a sharp moment when one species becomes another. A population of ancestral tigers gradually became tigers. At what point did it cross a boundary that Linnaean taxonomy draws as a bright line? The question has no natural biological answer, and this generates the species problem: there may be no single concept of species that does all the work we want species concepts to do.

Contemporary biologists work with several distinct species concepts. Ernst Mayr's biological species concept defines species by reproductive isolation: members of a species are those that can interbreed and produce fertile offspring, and species are separated by barriers to such interbreeding. This concept is powerful and widely used, but it fails for asexual organisms — bacteria and many plants reproduce without interbreeding, yet we speak easily of bacterial and plant species. It also handles ring species awkwardly, since the continuous chain of interbreeding populations breaks transitivity in a way the concept cannot absorb. The phylogenetic species concept focuses instead on genealogy: a species is the smallest monophyletic group — the smallest set of organisms sharing a unique common ancestor. This applies to asexual organisms and tracks evolutionary history cleanly, but it tends to multiply species, fragmenting what were previously unified as single species into many distinct lineages. The ecological species concept identifies species with organisms occupying distinct niches, connecting species identity to functional ecology. These concepts frequently agree in their verdicts but sometimes diverge, which suggests they are tracking different real features of biological reality rather than converging on a single underlying thing.

## Species as Individuals

The deepest challenge to thinking about species as natural kinds came from Ghiselin and Hull in the 1970s. Ghiselin (*Ontological Status of Species*, 1974) and Hull (*Are Species Really Individuals?*, 1978) argued that species are not natural kinds but individuals — spatiotemporally extended historical lineages with a beginning, a continuous existence, and (in many cases) an end through extinction or speciation. The argument turns on what natural kinds require. Natural kinds — gold, water, table salt — are defined by essential properties that every member necessarily possesses. No individual gold atom could fail to have atomic number 79 and still be gold. But no individual tiger necessarily has any particular genetic sequence: tiger genomes vary, and the variation is continuous with populations that are no longer tigers. If there is no essential property that all and only tigers must have, then tigers cannot be instances of a natural kind in the essentialism-presupposing sense.

What does unify tigers into a group is history: they are all descended from a common ancestral population and connected through ongoing evolutionary dynamics. Historical-genealogical relations are exactly the kind of relations that constitute individuals — organisms are unified by their internal historical development, nations are unified by historical continuity — not the relations that constitute kinds. The conclusion: *Panthera tigris* is an individual, extended in time and geographic space, and Shere Khan is a part of that individual rather than an instance of it. This has consequences that feel strange: two tigers arising through independent parallel evolution on different planets would not both be tigers, because they would not both be parts of the same individual lineage. Being a tiger, on this view, is a historical-relational property — it depends on where in the tree of life you come from.

## Essentialist Responses

The Hull-Ghiselin position has not gone uncontested. Michael Devitt (*Resurrecting Biological Essentialism*, 2008) argues that biological individuals have intrinsic essential properties — call them their *underlying natures* — that ground kind membership, even if these natures are complex, multidimensional, and not capturable in any single genetic sequence. The essential property of being a tiger is having the typical underlying biological nature of tigers: a complex profile of genomic, developmental, and physiological properties that cluster together precisely because they are causally integrated. This does not require a single necessary property but allows for the structured variation that biological species exhibit, while grounding kind membership in what the organism intrinsically is.

Boyd's homeostatic property cluster (HPC) theory occupies similar territory. Species are natural kinds not because they have sharp essences but because their characteristic property clusters — morphological, behavioral, physiological, genetic — are causally maintained by homeostatic mechanisms: the stable developmental programs, ecological pressures, and reproductive dynamics that keep the cluster coherent across individuals and generations. The HPC theory preserves the reality and explanatory significance of species while dispensing with the demand for a single essential property. It is probably the most widely accepted framework for thinking about biological kinds in contemporary philosophy of biology.

The debate matters beyond taxonomy. If species are individuals rather than kinds, natural selection operates not on kinds but on populations — historical entities whose evolution is a matter of contingent history, not instantiation of general laws. Conservation biologists face the question of what exactly is being preserved when we protect an endangered species: a historical lineage (the individual view) or a distinctive form of life that could in principle be recreated (the kind view). These are not merely academic distinctions. And if species are individuals that can have interests — as organisms can — the moral status of species extinction looks different from what it looks like if species are abstract kinds, which standardly cannot be harmed. The species problem, far from being a narrow taxonomic dispute, is one of the richest intersections in contemporary philosophy between metaphysics and empirical science, between the abstract question of what kinds of things exist and the urgent practical questions of biology, conservation, and ecology.
