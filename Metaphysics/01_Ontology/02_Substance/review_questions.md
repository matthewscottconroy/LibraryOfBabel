# Review Questions — Substance

1. Aristotle defines primary substance through its logical role — neither said of nor in any subject — and also through its capacity to receive contraries while remaining numerically one. Are these two criteria extensionally equivalent, or could something satisfy one without the other? What work does each criterion do in distinguishing substances from accidents, and how do they jointly motivate the conclusion that individual concrete things are ontologically primary?

2. Hylomorphism analyzes every physical substance as a composite of matter and form, yet Aristotle also says that form has a stronger claim to being substance than the concrete composite does. Explain this apparent tension between the *Categories* (where the individual composite is primary) and the *Metaphysics* (where form seems primary). Does the distinction between the logical question of what is the ultimate subject of predication and the explanatory question of what makes a thing the kind of thing it is resolve the tension, or does it reveal a deeper ambiguity in Aristotle's project?

3. Descartes defines substance as that which depends on no other thing for its existence, then applies this definition to minds and bodies as *created* substances. Spinoza argues that this is incoherent: created things depend on God for their existence and therefore cannot be substances in Descartes' own sense. Evaluate Spinoza's argument. Does the conclusion — that only the one infinite substance exists, with minds and bodies as modes — solve the Cartesian mind-body problem, or merely relocate it?

4. Substratum theory posits a bare particular that individuates objects and serves as the owner of their properties. Bundle theory denies any such entity and identifies objects with compresence classes of tropes. Both face signature objections: the bare particular is unknowable and seemingly indistinguishable from nothing; compresence is either viciously regressive or a primitive left unexplained. Which objection is more damaging, and is there a position that avoids both?

5. The chapter covers three accounts of individuation: Aquinas's individuation by designated matter, Scotus's haecceity, and Ockham's nominalism (individuals simply are themselves, with no further principle needed). How does each account bear on the contemporary debate between haecceitism and counterpart theory in modal metaphysics? Does rejecting a principle of individuation commit one to counterpart theory, or are there positions between primitive haecceities and counterpart-theoretic representation?

6. Endurantism holds that substances are wholly present at each moment of their existence; perdurantism holds that they have temporal parts and are extended through time as through space. The debate turns partly on how to handle "temporary intrinsics" — properties like being bent or being pale that an object has at one time but not another. Does Lewis's problem of temporary intrinsics actually favor perdurantism, or can the endurantist give an independently motivated account of time-indexed properties that does not simply concede the point?

7. The neo-Aristotelian revival (Kripke-Putnam essentialism, Kit Fine on essence and modality) claims that essences are mind-independent features of kinds and individuals, not merely modal profiles derived from how we describe things. Fine argues that a property can be necessary to an object without being part of its essence (e.g., every object necessarily satisfies "2+2=4"). What work is the notion of "real definition" doing in Fine's account, and how does it respond to Quine's argument that essential properties are always relative to a mode of description?

8. Schaffer's priority monism — the view that the cosmos as a whole is the fundamental substance and all parts are derivative — is presented as a contemporary revival of Spinozan insights, in part motivated by quantum entanglement. If priority monism is correct, what happens to the Aristotelian criterion of ontological independence as the mark of substance? Can a position that grants that individual objects exist but denies they are fundamental still be called a substance theory, or does it dissolve the category of substance altogether?
