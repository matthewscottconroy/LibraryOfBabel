# Properties and Relations

When we say that an apple is red, that a planet is massive, that two people are friends — what exactly are we saying? We appear to be attributing features to things: redness to the apple, mass to the planet, friendship to the pair of people. But what is a feature — a property, an attribute, a relation? This question is as old as Greek philosophy and as current as this morning's debates in the philosophy of language and science.

The challenge comes into focus when we notice that different objects can share properties. Two apples can both be red; two planets can both be massive. That shared redness seems to be one thing, not two — the very same feature, present in both apples. Yet the two apples are clearly distinct things. If redness is what they share, and redness is one thing, then there is something — a universal — that is fully present in two different locations at the same time. This is the metaphysics of universals, and it is either a profound truth about reality or a philosophical confusion that needs to be dissolved.

Those who accept universals — realists — take the shared character of similar things seriously and explain it by positing properties that are multiply instantiable, present wherever they are instantiated, and genuinely identical across instances. Those who reject universals — nominalists — maintain that the world contains only particular things, each entirely distinct from every other, and that what we call "shared properties" is nothing more than a pattern of resemblance among distinct particular things. Those who posit tropes take a middle path: the redness of this apple is a particular, non-repeatable feature — different from the redness of that apple — but properties are not universals and need not be.

Relations introduce additional complexity. A dyadic relation like *is taller than* holds between two things and seems irreducible to properties of the things considered individually. Bradley's regress poses a challenge: if the relation between two things is itself a further entity, we need a further relation to connect the first relation to its relata, and so on without end. Whether this regress is genuinely vicious, and how the theory of relations should respond, remains contested.

This chapter develops the full range of positions and problems in the theory of properties and relations. We examine the nature of properties — sparse versus abundant conceptions, natural versus gerrymandered properties, the role of properties in grounding resemblance and supporting laws — alongside the theory of relations and the debate between universalists and trope theorists. Throughout, the connection to scientific practice is kept in view: which properties does physics recognize, and what does that commitment tell us about the metaphysics of properties? The answers matter not just for ontology but for understanding what science tells us about the world.
