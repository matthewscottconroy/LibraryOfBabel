# The Nature of Properties

"Socrates is wise." This simple predication — subject, copula, predicate — seems utterly transparent. Yet ask what wisdom is, and the philosophical difficulty begins immediately. Wisdom is not Socrates, and it is not identical to the predicate "wise." It is something shared by Socrates, Aristotle, and anyone else who deserves the attribution — but shared how, exactly? Is wisdom a single entity that is wholly present in each wise person, or is it a resemblance that holds among distinct, individual instances? Is it real at all, or is it a projection of our classificatory activity onto a world that contains only particular things?

These questions go to the heart of what properties are. The problem of universals — the problem of how distinct things can share a common nature — is one of the oldest in philosophy, but it takes a distinctive form when we focus not on the question of existence (realism versus nominalism) but on the internal nature of properties themselves. Even granting that there are genuine properties, we face further questions: are properties categorical or dispositional? Are they intrinsic to the things that have them, or relational and context-dependent? Do properties have identity conditions of their own, and if so, what are they?

The distinction between intrinsic and extrinsic properties is fundamental. Intrinsic properties are those that a thing has in virtue of itself alone — independently of what else exists. Extrinsic properties are had in virtue of relations to other things. Being spherical seems intrinsic; being an uncle is obviously extrinsic. But the distinction resists easy analysis: what precisely does it mean for a property to hold in virtue of a thing itself? This is not a merely classificatory question — it bears on the analysis of causation, on the semantics of counterfactuals, and on the nature of scientific properties.

This section lays the conceptual groundwork for the chapters on universals, tropes, and relations that follow. It examines what a theory of properties must explain, how properties relate to the predicates we use to attribute them, and what the main theoretical options are for giving a metaphysical account of what properties are.
