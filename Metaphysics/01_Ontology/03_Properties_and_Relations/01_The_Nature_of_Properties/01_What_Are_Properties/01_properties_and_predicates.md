# Properties and Predicates

Language and reality interlock in ways that are easy to overlook. When we say "the ball is red," we use the predicate "is red" to attribute something to the ball. But what, exactly, is being attributed? One answer is: a property. A property is whatever it is in the world that corresponds to the correct application of a predicate. Another answer is more deflationary: there is nothing in the world beyond the ball itself and the predicate; talk of properties is just a way of talking about how predicates apply.

The gap between these answers marks a fundamental divide in the metaphysics of properties. Realists hold that predicates correspond to genuine features of reality — properties that exist independently of language. Nominalists (in various forms) hold that predicate application does not require any further ontological commitment: the ball is red, full stop, without a property redness floating somewhere in the metaphysical background.

## The Semantic Question

The relationship between predicates and properties raises a prior semantic question: do predicates refer? Names clearly refer — "Aristotle" picks out a particular person. But predicates occupy a different grammatical position. Frege distinguished objects (the referents of singular terms) from concepts (the referents of predicates), arguing that concepts are essentially unsaturated or incomplete: the predicate "is red" is not a name for anything but a device for attributing. On Frege's picture, the concept expressed by "is red" is not itself an object, though we can form the singular term "the concept red" to talk about it — with notoriously paradoxical results.

Russell offered a different analysis. Where Frege had a two-sorted ontology of objects and concepts, Russell recognized a hierarchy of types. Predicates express propositional functions — entities that take arguments and yield propositions. The predicate "is red" expresses the propositional function x is red, which yields the proposition that the ball is red when saturated by the ball. On this account, properties are propositional functions, and the question of their ontological status becomes the question of whether propositional functions are genuinely real.

## Quine on Predication

Willard Van Orman Quine famously argued that standard first-order logic requires no commitment to properties. In a sentence like "Socrates is wise," the predicate "is wise" is applied to a name, but this application does not require a property of wisdom in our ontology. Quine's criterion of ontological commitment — we are committed to the existence of whatever our best theories quantify over — does not commit us to properties when we formalize "Socrates is wise" as **Ws**, since **W** is a predicate variable, not a name. Quantification over properties, however, does commit us: "There is some property that Socrates has and Plato lacks" introduces commitment if we take this sentence at face value.

The argument runs:

- **P1**: We are ontologically committed to whatever our best first-order paraphrases quantify over.
- **P2**: "Socrates is wise" can be paraphrased as **Ws** in first-order logic, which does not quantify over properties.
- **Conclusion**: "Socrates is wise" does not commit us to the existence of a property of wisdom.

Opponents respond that this merely shows how to avoid commitment through paraphrase, not that the commitment was absent in the original. The success of Quinean paraphrase depends on whether the paraphrase is genuinely meaning-preserving — a contentious matter.

## Predicates and Ontological Inflation

The realist faces questions about which predicates correspond to real properties. Not every grammatically well-formed predicate seems to pick out a genuine feature: "being grue," "being either a quark or the Eiffel Tower," "being thought of by someone on Tuesday" all seem gerrymandered. Goodman's grue paradox, introduced in "Fact, Fiction, and Forecast" (1954), showed that purely extensional criteria cannot distinguish projectible predicates — those suitable for inductive generalization — from artificial ones.

David Lewis responded by distinguishing sparse from abundant properties precisely to handle this challenge: abundant properties include absolutely everything predicates can express, while sparse properties are those that correspond to genuine regularities in nature. The predicate "is grue" expresses an abundant property but not a sparse one; it does not correspond to a genuine joint in nature.

Chris Swoyer's work on properties developed the idea that properties have intrinsic structure and determine inferential patterns — the property of being equilateral is related to the property of having equal sides in a way that grounds logical entailments, and this relational structure is not reducible to predicate grammar.

## The Nominalist Response

The nominalist faces a different challenge: explaining why some predicates apply and others do not, without invoking properties to do the explanatory work. Predicate nominalism says predicates apply when they apply — but this seems to leave the application facts unexplained. Class nominalism says the predicate "red" applies to the members of the class of red things — but what makes that class the right one? This class is just the class {x : x is red}, and the question recurs.

Resemblance nominalism, defended by Gonzalo Rodriguez-Pereyra, offers a more substantive answer: a predicate "F" correctly applies to x iff x resembles sufficiently many paradigm Fs. But the notion of sufficient resemblance must be cashed out without reference to properties, on pain of circularity.

## Predicates, Properties, and the Direction of Explanation

Both sides must grapple with the fact that predicates and properties seem to come apart in multiple directions. The same property can be expressed by different predicates: "water" and "H₂O" pick out the same stuff, but the predicates function differently in our thought and language. The same predicate can express different properties in different contexts: "bank" covers financial institutions and riverbanks.

These divergences suggest that predicates are not simply read off from properties, or vice versa. The realist typically holds that the world's genuine properties are discovered, not invented; the predicate expresses the property, but the property exists independently. The nominalist holds that predicate application is more basic: what makes a predicate apply is a fact about the object, the predicate, and perhaps objective resemblance relations — but no additional entity called a property.

Getting the semantics of predication right requires getting the metaphysics of properties right, and vice versa. The direction of explanation is itself disputed: does the property explain why the predicate correctly applies, or does correct predicate application (perhaps in ideal conditions) help constitute what it is to have the property?
