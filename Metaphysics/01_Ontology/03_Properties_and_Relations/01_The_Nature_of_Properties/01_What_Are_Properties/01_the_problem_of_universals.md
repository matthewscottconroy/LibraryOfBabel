# The Problem of Universals

Multiple things can share the same property. Two roses can both be red. Two circles can both be perfectly round. This seems obvious and unremarkable until you ask what it really means. If redness is genuinely the same thing present in both roses, then the same entity must exist in two different places at once, which seems paradoxical. If redness is not the same thing, then in what sense do the roses share anything at all? This is the problem of universals, and it has occupied philosophers since antiquity.

## The Historical Background

The problem originates with Plato's theory of Forms. In dialogues like the *Phaedo* and *Republic*, Plato argued that particular beautiful things participate in Beauty itself — an eternal, unchanging Form that exists independently of all its instances. The Form of Beauty is not the same as any particular beautiful object; it is what makes beautiful things beautiful. This posits a stark ontological gap between particulars (mutable, spatiotemporally located) and universals (immutable, located nowhere in particular).

Aristotle modified this picture significantly. For Aristotle, forms are immanent in particulars rather than transcendent: the form of horse exists only in actual horses, not in some separate Platonic heaven. When two horses share a form, they literally have the same formal constituent — but this form exists in them, not apart from them. This immanent realism influenced the medieval debate between realists (who held that universals are real entities, whether in or apart from things) and nominalists (who denied universals altogether, insisting that only particulars exist and that general terms are merely words — *nomina* — rather than names of real entities).

## The Contemporary Statement

The label "universals" names whatever it is — if anything — that makes distinct particular things share a feature. A universal, on the traditional conception, is a single entity that can be wholly present in numerically distinct individuals simultaneously. When two objects are both negatively charged, the universal *negative charge* is genuinely present in each; it is not that each object has its own charge-thing that merely resembles the other's.

This generates the One-over-Many argument, which can be formalized as follows:

- **P1**: When two or more things are both F, there is something — their F-ness — that they genuinely share.
- **P2**: Whatever is genuinely shared by numerically distinct particulars is not itself a particular.
- **P3**: Therefore, there is a non-particular — a universal — that grounds their sharing.
- **Conclusion**: Universals exist.

The nominalist rejects P1 or P2. Rejecting P1 involves denying that genuine sharing occurs — "sharing" is just a way of speaking about predicate application or resemblance. Rejecting P2 involves proposing a category of entities that are not particulars but are not universals in the traditional sense either — tropes occupy this position.

## Nominalist Strategies

Opponents of universals — nominalists in the broad sense — deny that there is any such shared entity. When we say two roses are both red, we are doing one of several things:

**Predicate nominalism**: We are grouping them under a common predicate. The fact that both fall under "red" is primitive and requires no further explanation in terms of shared entities. The worry: this makes predicate application explanatorily brute in a way that seems unsatisfying. Why do both roses fall under "red"? Predicate nominalism says: they just do.

**Resemblance nominalism**: We are noting that they are sufficiently similar to each other and to paradigm cases. Resemblance nominalism, associated with H.H. Price and developed formally by Gonzalo Rodriguez-Pereyra in *Resemblance Nominalism* (2002), grounds all property-talk in objective resemblance relations among particulars. The challenge is that resemblance itself appears to be a universal — all resemblance relations resemble each other — generating the very regress nominalism aimed to avoid.

**Trope theory**: Each rose has its own particular redness — a *trope* — that resembles but is numerically distinct from the redness of any other rose. Trope theory, developed by D.C. Williams and Keith Campbell, avoids universals while still positing property-like entities. The theory appeals to those who want a more economical ontology than realism while still acknowledging that objects have genuine features.

## Realist Strategies

The contemporary realist debate is dominated by David Armstrong's immanent realism. Armstrong, in his *Universals and Scientific Realism* (1978) and later works, argues that genuine universals exist but only where instantiated. There are no uninstantiated universals — the Platonic picture of redness existing even if nothing were red is rejected. Universals are needed because they provide the best explanation of:

1. The objective similarity between distinct things (two electrons have identical charge because they instantiate the numerically identical universal *negative charge*).
2. The existence of laws of nature — laws, on Armstrong's account, are not brute regularities but relations of necessitation between universals: **N(F, G)** — the universal F necessitates the universal G.
3. The success of induction — if universals ground genuine natural kinds, induction is not mysterious coincidence.

A different form of realism, associated with Bertrand Russell's later work and with Lewis's treatment of natural properties, treats universals (or at least their sparse counterparts) as the truthmakers for objective similarity claims without necessarily requiring the full Armstrong machinery.

## Why It Matters

The debate matters for several reasons. It bears directly on how we understand predication: what happens when we say of some object that it is F? It matters for the theory of natural laws, since if laws relate universals (as Armstrong argues), laws have a different character than if they are merely regularities among particulars. It connects to mathematics and logic: are properties the kind of thing that can themselves have properties, forming the hierarchy of higher-order features that formal systems require? And it has implications for the theory of mind — mental properties, if they are genuine universals, would be suited for multiple realizability in a way that purely extensional properties are not.

Philosophers distinguish first-order properties (redness, mass, charge) from second-order properties (being a property, being multiply instantiated), and the question of universals arises at every level. Whether we take the realist or nominalist path, the move we make here will constrain positions throughout the rest of ontology.
