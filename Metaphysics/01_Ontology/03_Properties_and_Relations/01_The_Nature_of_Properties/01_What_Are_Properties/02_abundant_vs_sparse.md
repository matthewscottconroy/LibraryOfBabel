# Abundant vs. Sparse Properties

One of the most useful distinctions in the contemporary metaphysics of properties is David Lewis's distinction between abundant and sparse properties. Abundant properties are cheap: for every predicate, there is an abundant property corresponding to it — including wildly gerrymandered ones like "being either a quark or the Eiffel Tower." Sparse properties are expensive: they are the elite subset of properties that genuinely carve nature at its joints — that correspond to real, objective similarities in the world.

## Lewis's Original Distinction

Lewis introduced the distinction in *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986) and elaborated it in "New Work for a Theory of Universals" (1983). His motivation was to serve two different theoretical purposes simultaneously. Abundant properties are needed for semantic theory: to give truth-conditions for arbitrary predicates, we need something for every predicate to express. Sparse properties are needed for metaphysics: to state laws of nature, analyze objective similarity, and explain causation, we need a privileged set of properties that reflects the objective structure of reality.

Lewis treated abundant properties as sets of possible objects — a relatively deflationary construal. On this account, for every set of possible objects whatsoever, there is a corresponding abundant property. The property of being a quark-or-the-Eiffel-Tower is just the set containing all quarks (from any possible world) together with all counterparts of the Eiffel Tower. There is nothing metaphysically weighty about abundant properties — they are just set-theoretic constructs.

Sparse properties, by contrast, are the genuine structural features of reality. Lewis wrote: "There are the sparse properties. Their sharing makes for qualitative similarity, they carve at the joints, they are intrinsic, they are highly specific, the sets of their instances are not altogether miscellaneous, there are not many of them." These are the properties that matter for natural science, for causation, and for objective similarity.

## What Makes a Property Sparse?

Lewis's answer appeals to *perfect natural similarity*: perfectly natural properties are those that ground perfectly natural objective similarities. An intrinsic duplicate is a thing with all the same sparse properties as another; two things are perfectly similar just when they are intrinsic duplicates. But this seems circular unless we can specify what makes a similarity objective.

Lewis acknowledged the circularity but argued that it was not viciously circular — we can understand natural properties via the role they play in an interconnected network of concepts (similarity, lawhood, causation, duplicate) without any single analysis being prior. This is the "natural properties as primitive" move: naturalness itself is a primitive feature, not analyzable in further terms.

The formal structure:

- **P1**: Some properties are more natural than others.
- **P2**: The most natural properties are those that appear in the fundamental laws of physics.
- **P3**: Naturalness comes in degrees — from fundamental physical properties (perfectly natural) through biological kinds (moderately natural) to gerrymandered predicates (highly unnatural).
- **Conclusion**: The sparse/abundant distinction is a matter of degree along the naturalness gradient.

## Objections and Responses

**Objection 1 (Circularity)**: The account of sparse properties as "carving nature at its joints" uses the metaphor of nature having joints, which presupposes the very thing to be explained. If we explain natural properties in terms of objective similarity, and objective similarity in terms of natural properties, we have a circle.

*Response*: Lewis embraces this as a theoretical role definition. Natural properties are whatever play the naturalness role in our total theory — whatever properties make the cluster of naturalness-associated claims come out true. This is a familiar strategy in philosophical analysis (cf. Ramsey sentences for theoretical terms).

**Objection 2 (Epistemic)**: We cannot directly perceive sparse properties. We observe regularities and then infer that certain properties are sparse. This gives sparse properties an elusive, theory-laden character.

*Response*: All fundamental theoretical posits are theory-laden. The sparse/abundant distinction is no worse off epistemically than positing electrons or quarks.

**Objection 3 (Goodman's Grue)**: Nelson Goodman showed in *Fact, Fiction, and Forecast* (1954) that purely extensional or syntactic criteria cannot distinguish projectible predicates from grue-like ones. Any account of sparse properties must explain why "green" is sparse and "grue" is not, without appealing to mere linguistic conventions.

*Response*: Lewis accepts that "grue" expresses only an abundant property. The sparse property corresponding to "green" is whatever physical-optical property makes objects interact with light in the relevant way. Goodman's challenge shows that projectibility is a matter of naturalness — projectible predicates are those expressing sparse properties.

## The Abundant/Sparse Distinction and Other Debates

**Natural laws**: On Lewis's Humean account, laws of nature are regularities in the mosaic of particular fact. But not all regularities are laws — the "best system" analysis says that laws are the generalizations in the best systematization of all the particular facts, where "best" balances simplicity and strength. Crucially, simplicity is assessed relative to the sparse properties: a systematization is simpler if its laws are stated in terms of natural properties.

**Causation**: Causal relevance requires sparse properties. When a rock's mass causes a pendulum to swing in a certain way, it is the sparse property of mass — not the abundant property of being-a-rock-or-a-chair — that is doing the causal work. Lewis's counterfactual analysis of causation works best when restricted to events involving sparse properties.

**Mental properties**: One significant debate is whether mental properties (pain, belief, desire) are sparse. If they are, they are genuine natural kinds; if they are only abundant, they are gerrymandered constructs from a physical point of view. This matters for the prospects of a distinctively psychological level of scientific explanation.

**Intrinsicality**: Lewis also used sparse properties to define intrinsicality: a property is intrinsic iff it is shared by all intrinsic duplicates. Intrinsic duplicates are things with all the same sparse properties. This makes the definition of intrinsicality dependent on which properties are sparse — a consequence that is both a virtue and a potential source of instability.

On Lewis's view, which properties are sparse is not settled by philosophy alone but by empirical science. Physics discovers what the fundamental properties are — charge, mass, spin — and higher-level sciences may discover further natural properties that are not fundamental but still carve nature appropriately. A property like "being a predator" is not a fundamental physical property, but it may be sparse enough at the biological level to figure in genuine generalizations.

The abundant/sparse distinction has been influential far beyond Lewis's work, shaping debates in philosophy of language, philosophy of science, and the metaphysics of causation.
