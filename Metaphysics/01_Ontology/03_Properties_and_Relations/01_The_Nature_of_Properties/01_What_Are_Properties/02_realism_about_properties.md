# Realism About Properties

Realism about properties holds that there are genuine, mind-independent features of reality — that when we say the electron has charge, we are not merely imposing a classification but tracking something real. The strongest version of realism posits universals: repeatable entities that are literally shared by their instances.

## Two Varieties of Realism

The primary division is between *transcendent* and *immanent* realism. Transcendent realism, following the Platonic tradition, holds that universals exist independently of whether anything instantiates them. There would be a property of redness even if nothing were red. Immanent realism — the position favored by David Armstrong — holds that universals exist only where instantiated; uninstantiated universals are rejected as metaphysically profligate.

The argument for immanent realism over Platonism runs:

- **P1**: We should posit only entities for which we have positive evidence.
- **P2**: Uninstantiated universals play no explanatory role — they explain no similarity, figure in no law, ground no causation.
- **P3**: Therefore, uninstantiated universals are not posited by the best theory.
- **Conclusion**: Immanent realism is preferable to transcendent Platonism.

The Platonist responds that mathematical and logical properties seem to be genuine universals that are necessarily uninstantiated by physical things (the number 7, as an abstract entity, cannot be in space), yet they are real. Armstrong's immanent realism seems to leave mathematical properties unexplained.

## Armstrong's Account

David Armstrong is the most influential contemporary realist. On his account, universals are immanent — they exist only where they are instantiated, not in some Platonic realm beyond space and time. When two objects are both negatively charged, the universal *negative charge* is genuinely present in each. This grounds the objective similarity between them, and it explains why laws of nature are not mere coincidences: laws relate universals, and this relation necessitates the corresponding regularities among particulars.

Armstrong's states of affairs provide the basic ontological units. A state of affairs is a particular instantiating a universal: **[a instantiates F]**. The particular *a* and the universal *F* are both constituents of this state of affairs, but the state of affairs itself is not reducible to either — it is a further entity with both as parts. This structure allows Armstrong to avoid the Bradley regress (discussed below) by treating the instantiation relation as an internal feature of states of affairs rather than a further relational universal.

The formal picture: for Armstrong, the world is the totality of states of affairs. Atomic states of affairs have the form **[a, F]** (particular *a* has monadic universal F) or **[a, R, b]** (particulars *a* and *b* stand in relational universal R). Complex states of affairs are conjunctions, disjunctions, or other combinations of atomic ones. Laws of nature are higher-order states of affairs: **N(F, G)** — the universal F necessitates the universal G — which is itself a relation between universals.

## Shoemaker's Causal Structuralism

Sydney Shoemaker offers a distinct realist account. In "Causality and Properties" (1980), Shoemaker argues that properties are to be individuated by their causal powers. The property of being electrically charged is the property it is because of what it does — how it contributes to the causal histories of its instances. If two properties conferred exactly the same causal powers in all circumstances, they would be the same property.

This yields the causal structuralist thesis: the identity of a property is constituted by its causal-nomological role. Properties are, in a sense, clusters of dispositions or powers. The argument:

- **P1**: Properties are identical iff they confer the same causal powers on their instances.
- **P2**: The causal powers conferred by a property constitute its essence.
- **Conclusion**: Properties are individuated by — and in some sense constituted by — causal powers.

Shoemaker's view connects to the broader *powers ontology* defended by George Molnar, Brian Ellis, and Alexander Bird, which holds that fundamental properties are essentially dispositional rather than categorical. On this view, charge is not a categorically described intrinsic feature that happens to dispose its bearer to behave in certain ways; its very nature is to be a disposition to attract or repel.

## Bradley's Regress and the Instantiation Problem

A central challenge for realism is Bradley's regress, stated by F.H. Bradley in *Appearance and Reality* (1893). If a universal F relates to a particular *a* by a relation of instantiation **I**, then **I** itself relates **F** and **a** — which requires a further relation **I*** to hold between **I**, **F**, and **a**, and so on *ad infinitum*. The regress seems vicious: if instantiation is a genuine relation, it requires further relations to hold, and we never complete the explanation of how a universal is related to its particular.

Armstrong responds that instantiation is not a further universal but a fundamental tie — a non-relational nexus — that does not require additional grounding. States of affairs have their constituents tied together as a brute metaphysical fact. Critics disagree about whether this response is principled or merely terminological: if we can simply declare some relations to be non-relational ties, what prevents the nominalist from declaring predication to be a non-relational tie?

An alternative response, associated with Wilfrid Sellars, is that the regress is harmless — it generates an infinite series of facts, but each fact is explained by the previous, and no vicious circularity or explanatory gap arises.

## The Scientific Realism Connection

Realism also faces the problem of figuring out which predicates correspond to genuine universals. Not every predicate picks out a real feature: "grue" seems artificial. Armstrong argues that science is the arbiter — genuine universals are those that figure in the fundamental laws that science discovers. This makes the ontology of properties partly an empirical matter, which some find attractive and others find problematic.

The connection to scientific realism is significant: the realist about properties is committed to the view that science is in the business of discovering genuine features of reality, not merely constructing useful fictions or empirically adequate systematizations. If physics posits charge, mass, and spin as fundamental, these are genuine universals. The nominalist who accepts the authority of physics while rejecting universals faces a tension: what does it mean to "accept" that electrons have charge if there is no genuine property of charge?

Chris Swoyer's work on properties developed the view that properties have their own structure — they stand in relations of entailment, exclusion, and determination — and that these structural relations are themselves necessary features of reality. This *structural realism about properties* holds that the web of relations among properties is as real as the properties themselves.
