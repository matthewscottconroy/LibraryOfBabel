# Nominalism and Alternatives

Nominalism — in the strict sense — holds that only particulars exist. There are no universals, no abstract objects, no multiply-instantiable entities. When two things appear to share a property, this apparent sharing must be explained without positing any genuinely shared entity.

## Historical Background

Medieval nominalism, associated with William of Ockham, denied that general terms name any real entity beyond the individual things that fall under them. "Redness" does not name some additional entity distinct from the red things; it is merely a useful label — a *nomen* — for grouping similar particulars. The medievals debated whether universals existed in the things (as in Aristotelian moderate realism), before the things (as in Platonic transcendent realism), or only in the mind after the things (as in nominalism proper).

Modern nominalism, as developed in the analytic tradition by W.V.O. Quine and Nelson Goodman, takes as its motto the parsimony of commitment to abstract entities. Quine and Goodman's joint paper "Steps Toward a Constructive Nominalism" (1947) expressed the aspiration to reconstruct scientific discourse without quantifying over abstract objects, including properties. The project was ultimately abandoned as too difficult, but the aspiration shaped subsequent debates.

## Predicate Nominalism

The most straightforward nominalist move is predicate nominalism: to say that two things are both red is just to say that the predicate "red" applies to both. Properties are nothing over and above the sets of things to which predicates apply. Critics charge that this gets things backwards — predicates apply because things are similar, not the reverse — and that it makes the applicability of predicates inexplicably arbitrary.

The explanatory order objection is formalized as follows:

- **P1**: The predicate "red" applies to *a* and *b* because *a* and *b* are both red.
- **P2**: "Because" here expresses an explanatory or grounding relation.
- **P3**: Predicate nominalism reverses this order, making redness a consequence of predicate application rather than its explanation.
- **Conclusion**: Predicate nominalism is explanatorily backwards.

The predicate nominalist might respond by denying that there is any substantive explanatory relation here — predicate application is a brute fact, and demands for further grounding are misplaced. This response has force if we are independently skeptical of metaphysical grounding claims, but most metaphysicians find the reversal of explanatory order troubling.

## Resemblance Nominalism

Resemblance nominalism, associated with H.H. Price and developed formally by Gonzalo Rodriguez-Pereyra in *Resemblance Nominalism* (2002), grounds property-talk in objective resemblance relations among particulars. Two things are both red because they sufficiently resemble each other and the paradigm cases of red things. No universal redness is needed; just the resemblance facts.

Rodriguez-Pereyra's version is sophisticated. Properties are identified with resemblance classes — the classes of things that resemble each other sufficiently closely. The property of being red is the maximal resemblance class of red things. This avoids the crude extensionalism of class nominalism (see below) by grounding the class in resemblance relations rather than stipulation.

The resemblance regress objection: resemblance itself seems like a relation that holds between any two resembling things. All resemblance relations resemble each other — they are all resemblances. This generates the problem of universals all over again for resemblance. Rodriguez-Pereyra's response is that resemblance is primitive — it is the one universal (if that is what it is) that the resemblance nominalist must accept, and the account still represents a significant ontological economy compared to full-blown realism.

## Trope Theory

Trope theory occupies middle ground. Tropes are particular properties — *this particular redness* of *this particular rose*, numerically distinct from the redness of any other rose. Trope theory avoids universals while still positing property-like entities. Resemblance among tropes can then explain why we group things under common predicates.

The key figures are D.C. Williams, who introduced the term "trope" and developed the foundational theory in "The Elements of Being" (1953), and Keith Campbell, who systematized trope theory in *Abstract Particulars* (1990). For Campbell, the entire edifice of ontology can be built from tropes: particular substances are bundles of compresent tropes; general terms group tropes into resemblance classes; laws of nature are regularities in the mutual accompaniment of tropes.

Tropes handle the one-over-many problem differently from both realism and resemblance nominalism: when two roses are both red, each has its own trope of redness, and these tropes exactly resemble each other. The resemblance is a primitive fact about the tropes. This avoids positing a universal redness while still acknowledging that something in reality grounds the grouping.

## Class Nominalism

Class nominalism identifies properties with sets of their instances: the property of being red just is the set of all red things. This is tidy but notoriously extensional — sets with the same members are identical, so the property of being a creature with a heart would be identical to the property of being a creature with kidneys, even if these properties seem intuitively distinct.

Lewis's response is to use sets of *possible* objects: the property of being a creature with a heart is the set of all possible creatures with hearts, while the property of being a creature with kidneys is the set of all possible creatures with kidneys — these sets are distinct even though all actual members of one are also members of the other. This requires commitment to possible objects, which Lewis accepts in his modal realism, but many philosophers find this cure worse than the disease.

## Ostrich Nominalism

Quine himself proposed what is sometimes called "ostrich nominalism" or "paraphrase nominalism": the view that there simply is no philosophical problem of universals to be solved. When we say "Socrates is wise," we are not committed to a property of wisdom — the sentence is just a subject-predicate statement in which a name is paired with a predicate, full stop. The predicate does not refer; it merely applies. No further account of how or why it applies is needed.

Michael Devitt defended this view under the label "ostrich nominalism" (though Devitt himself rejects the pejorative label). He argues that the demand for a metaphysical explanation of predication is a relic of bad philosophy, generated by the mistaken assumption that every meaningful expression must refer to something. The realist's supposed explanation — that predicates apply because the object instantiates a universal — does not actually explain anything but merely redescribes the original fact.

## Common Threads and Open Questions

Each nominalist strategy trades one set of difficulties for another. Predicate nominalism leaves predicate application unexplained. Resemblance nominalism must accept resemblance as a primitive relation that threatens to reintroduce universals. Trope theory posits a new category of entities (particular properties) that resemblance nominalists and realists alike may find suspicious. Class nominalism is extensional and must appeal to possible objects to distinguish co-extensive properties.

The debate between realism and nominalism remains genuinely open, with sophisticated defenses of every position on offer. The challenge for any view is to explain why certain groupings — certain predicates, classes, or resemblance clusters — are natural or objective without simply positing a new set of entities to explain what was supposed to be explained without them.
