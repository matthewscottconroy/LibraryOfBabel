# Property Individuation

Even if we accept that properties exist, we face questions about how to individuate them — how to determine when we have one property versus two. Several principles have been proposed, each with different consequences for the ontology of properties and for downstream debates about causation, laws of nature, and the philosophy of mind.

## The Extensional Criterion

The coarsest-grained individuation principle is extensionalism: two properties are identical if and only if they have the same instances. The property of being a creature with a heart and the property of being a creature with kidneys happen to be co-extensive in the actual world (every actual creature has both or neither), so they are the same property. This gives a very lean ontology but produces the wrong results: these are clearly different properties — they could in principle come apart in other possible worlds.

The extensional criterion also has implications for modal properties. The property of being water and the property of being H₂O are co-extensive, but many philosophers hold that they are the same property — the identity of water and H₂O is a necessary truth, discovered empirically (Kripke, Putnam). Extensionalism handles this case correctly (both are the same property) but for the wrong reason — it would identify them even if the identity were merely contingent.

## The Intensional Criterion

Intensionalism refines the criterion: two properties are identical if and only if they have the same instances across all possible worlds — if they are necessarily co-extensive. Being a trilateral (having three sides) and being triangular (having three angles) are necessarily co-extensive: every possible triangle is both trilateral and triangular. So by the intensional criterion, these are the same property. But many philosophers think they are still distinct — after all, being triangular and being trilateral seem to be about different things, even if they always coincide.

The intensional criterion also generates counterintuitive results about mathematical properties. The property of being a prime number greater than 2 and the property of being an odd prime are necessarily co-extensive (every prime greater than 2 is odd, and every odd prime is greater than 2), but these seem to be genuinely distinct properties — one concerns magnitude, the other parity.

## Hyperintensional Individuation

Fine-grained individuation avoids these problems by treating properties as hyperintensional: two properties are distinct if there is any conceptual or explanatory difference, even if they are necessarily co-extensive. The property of being H₂O and the property of being water are necessarily co-extensive (given that water is H₂O), but the conceptual contents differ — the first is a chemical description, the second an ordinary-language classification.

The hyperintensional approach is associated with Bealer's property theory and with neo-Fregean approaches that distinguish sense from reference. On such views, properties are individuated by something like their cognitive significance or conceptual role, not merely by their extensions.

The formal distinction: let **Ext(F)** be the extension of F (the set of its instances in the actual world), **Int(F)** be the intension of F (the function from possible worlds to extensions), and **Cont(F)** be the content or cognitive significance of F (something like its conceptual role or Fregean sense). Extensionalism identifies properties where Ext coincides; intensionalism where Int coincides; hyperintensionalism only where Cont coincides.

## Shoemaker's Causal Criterion

Sydney Shoemaker proposed a causal individuation criterion in "Causality and Properties" (1980): properties are identical iff they confer exactly the same causal powers. This ties property identity to causal role and is popular among those who think properties are constitutively defined by what they do. The account connects property individuation directly to causal structure:

- **P1**: For any property F and G, F = G iff, necessarily, for any possible object x: x's having F contributes to causation in exactly the ways that x's having G contributes.
- **P2**: Causal powers are the essential, identity-determining features of properties.
- **Conclusion**: Properties are identical iff they are constituted by the same causal powers.

The causal criterion faces two challenges. First, two properties might play the same causal role in the actual world — and in all possible worlds — while remaining genuinely distinct (the challenge of "swapped" properties that are nomically connected but conceptually distinct). Second, mathematical and logical properties seem not to have causal powers, yet we want to individuate them too.

George Molnar's *Powers: A Study in Metaphysics* (2003) develops a version of causal structuralism that answers the first challenge by making causal powers essential to properties, not merely nomically attached. On this view, there could not be two properties with exactly the same causal powers — causal powers individuate properties completely.

## Structuralist Individuation

A different approach, associated with Chris Swoyer and with structural realism in the philosophy of science, holds that properties are individuated by their structural relations to other properties. The property of being 1 kilogram is the property it is partly in virtue of being heavier than 0.5 kg, lighter than 2 kg, related to force by F = ma, and so on. Its identity is constituted by its position in a network of quantitative relations.

This yields a *holistic* individuation criterion: no property has its identity independently; each is individuated by its place in the structural web. This is attractive for physics, where mass, charge, and spin are defined by their roles in equations. It faces the regress worry: what individuates the relational structure itself if properties get their identity from the structure?

## The Implications for Other Debates

The question of property individuation bears on every other area of property theory.

**Laws of nature**: Armstrong's account of laws requires that N(F, G) — the necessitation relation between universals — is not identical to N(G, F) or to other necessitation relations. This requires properties to be finely individuated; coarsely individuated properties might collapse what Armstrong wants to be distinct laws.

**Mental causation**: If mental properties are individuated by their causal powers (Shoemaker-style), then mental properties that play the same causal role as certain physical properties may just be those physical properties — yielding a form of type identity theory. If properties are individuated more finely (hyperintensionally), multiple realizability becomes easier to accommodate.

**The philosophy of chemistry**: The property of being water and the property of being H₂O are an ongoing test case. Kripke's arguments in *Naming and Necessity* suggest they are the same property (water is essentially H₂O). Others think these are distinct properties that happen to be necessarily co-extensive — the water property is an ordinary-kind property, while H₂O is a chemical-composition property.

**Resemblance nominalism**: Rodriguez-Pereyra's resemblance nominalism effectively individuates properties by resemblance classes of possibilia. Two properties are distinct when the resemblance classes differ. This provides a nominalist-friendly individuation criterion that does not appeal to universals but requires a robust modal ontology.

The resolution of laws, causation, mental-physical identity, and the nature of properties themselves all depend on how finely we individuate. There is no consensus, and the choice of individuation criterion effectively encodes a substantive metaphysical picture about what properties are for.
