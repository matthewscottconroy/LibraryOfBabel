# Defining Intrinsicality

Some properties seem to belong to a thing in virtue of what it is in itself, independently of its surroundings or relations to other things. A sphere has its shape intrinsically — it would be spherical even if nothing else existed. Other properties depend essentially on how a thing relates to its environment. Being the tallest person in the room is extrinsic: it can be gained or lost without any change in the person, simply by other people entering or leaving.

## The Core Intuition

This intuitive distinction — intrinsic versus extrinsic — turns out to be surprisingly difficult to make precise. The most natural approach defines intrinsic properties as those that do not vary across duplicates. Two objects are duplicates if they share all their intrinsic properties; and a property is intrinsic if it cannot differ between duplicates. But this circularity is obviously vicious if we want to use intrinsicality to explain duplication.

The intuitive test is easy enough to apply: would the object have this property if it were the only thing in existence? If yes, the property is plausibly intrinsic. Being spherical: yes. Being married: no. Being five miles from a lake: no. But the test breaks down for many interesting cases. Being a unit charge: arguably yes, since charge seems like an internal feature of an electron — but charge is also defined relative to a field that presupposes a broader physical framework. Being the unique red thing: in a world with only one red thing, yes — but this seems to be an extrinsic property.

## The Lewis-Langton Analysis

David Lewis and Rae Langton developed an influential approach in "Intrinsic Properties" (1998) that tries to break the circle using the notion of a lonely object — one that exists in a possible world where nothing else exists. Their account classifies properties as:

- **Lonely**: F is lonely if it can be had by a lonely object.
- **Non-lonely**: F is non-lonely if it cannot be had by a lonely object.
- **Intrinsic**: F is intrinsic if it is both lonely and never differs between duplicates.
- **Extrinsic**: F is extrinsic if it is not intrinsic.

This gives the right verdicts in paradigm cases. Being spherical is intrinsic — a lonely sphere is spherical, and all sphere-duplicates are spherical. Being accompanied (having at least one co-existing concrete object) is extrinsic — a lonely thing lacks it. Being the only sphere is extrinsic — a lonely sphere has it vacuously, but any non-lonely duplicate in a world with other spheres lacks it.

The argument for this analysis:

- **P1**: If a property genuinely concerns only the object's internal constitution, it should be had by a lonely object in the same way as any non-lonely duplicate.
- **P2**: Properties that can differ when we change only what exists alongside the object are extrinsic — they depend on accompaniment.
- **Conclusion**: Intrinsic properties are those that neither require nor are prevented by loneliness, and never differ between duplicates.

## Problems and Refinements

**The loneliness problem**: The property of being lonely is extrinsic by intuitive lights — it depends on the absence of companions, not on the object's internal nature. But on the Lewis-Langton analysis, being lonely is classified as extrinsic (a lonely thing has it, a non-lonely duplicate lacks it) — which is arguably the right result, but for reasons that highlight the analysis's sensitivity to modal facts about accompaniment.

**Spatiotemporal properties**: Being located at a particular region of spacetime seems intrinsic in substantivalist spacetime theories (location is a genuine feature of the object, not merely a relation to other objects) but extrinsic in relationalist theories (location is fundamentally relational). The analysis does not settle this but reflects it.

**Quantum entanglement**: Entangled particles lack definite states for certain properties independent of their correlational relations to their partners. If spin is an intrinsic property, quantum mechanics seems to show that it is not fully determinate at the individual level. This motivates some philosophers of physics to move toward holistic or relational conceptions of quantum states.

## Alternative Approaches

Brian Weatherson has refined the analysis, and others have proposed accounts based on qualitative distinctness or on categorical versus hypothetical powers. Sider's analysis uses the notion of "not making essential reference to contingently co-existing things." Francescotti's approach ties intrinsicality to whether the property's specification involves constitutive reference to entities outside the object.

All these approaches share the conviction that the intrinsic/extrinsic distinction is metaphysically fundamental, even if hard to articulate. The distinction matters enormously for personal identity (what determines whether you persist through change must be intrinsic properties), for causation (causal powers are typically intrinsic), and for debates about supervenience (supervenience on intrinsic properties is stronger than supervenience on all properties).

Getting the analysis right is not merely scholastic but has downstream consequences throughout metaphysics.
