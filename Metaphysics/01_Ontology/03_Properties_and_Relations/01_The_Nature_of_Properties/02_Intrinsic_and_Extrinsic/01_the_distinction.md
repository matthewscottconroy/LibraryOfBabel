# The Intrinsic/Extrinsic Distinction

Among all the distinctions philosophers draw within the domain of properties, the intrinsic/extrinsic distinction may be the most fundamental for metaphysics. An intrinsic property is one that a thing has purely in virtue of how it is, independently of its surroundings, history, or relations to other things. An extrinsic property is one that depends on something beyond the thing itself.

## Clear Cases

The clearest cases are not hard to find. A sphere's shape is intrinsic: it would be spherical even in an otherwise empty universe. Being the largest sphere is extrinsic: it depends on how the sphere relates to other spheres in its environment. Being five kilometers from the Eiffel Tower is extrinsic: it is a relational fact about the object's position, not about its internal constitution.

Physical quantities provide additional test cases. Mass, charge, and spin all appear to be intrinsic: an electron's charge is a feature of the electron itself, not a matter of how it relates to surrounding objects. Color (in the physically-described sense of spectral reflectance) seems intrinsic: the object's reflectance profile is determined by its internal physical structure. Color in the perceiver-relative sense — being the color that looks red to normal perceivers in normal conditions — is plausibly extrinsic.

## Hard Cases

More difficult cases reveal how subtle the distinction is. Is having a mass of 5 kilograms intrinsic? Intuitively yes — mass seems like an internal feature. But mass is defined relative to a unit system (kilograms), and unit systems are conventional. Does that make mass partly extrinsic? Most philosophers say no: the measured value depends on the unit, but the underlying mass property — the physical quantity itself — is intrinsic. The numerical representation is conventional; the quantity it represents is not.

Is being human intrinsic? Being human seems to be something one is independently of one's surroundings. But "being human" may involve relations to a biological lineage, and lineage is a relational matter. Whether kind membership is intrinsic is disputed. Essential kind theorists like Kit Fine hold that being human is part of an object's essence and hence intrinsic; externalists about natural kinds hold that kind membership is at least partly fixed by the environment.

Is being in pain intrinsic? Functionalists say no — pain is defined by its functional-causal role, which involves relations to stimuli and behavior. Phenomenal realists say yes — the phenomenal character of pain (what it is like to be in pain) is a purely intrinsic, qualitative feature of the mental state. This disagreement mirrors broader disputes about mental content and the nature of consciousness.

## The Formal Characterization

David Lewis analyzed intrinsic properties as those that can never differ between perfect duplicates. Two objects are perfect duplicates if they have exactly the same basic physical properties in exactly the same arrangement. A property P is intrinsic if duplicates always share P: there is no possible world where two duplicates differ in P.

Formally: P is intrinsic iff for all possible worlds w1, w2 and objects x in w1, y in w2, if x and y are duplicates, then x has P in w1 iff y has P in w2.

This analysis connects intrinsicality to the notion of an intrinsic duplicate, but the account is threatened by circularity: duplicates are defined as sharing all intrinsic properties, and intrinsic properties are defined as those shared by all duplicates. Lewis and Langton addressed this by developing the lonely-object analysis (see the companion entry on analyzing intrinsicality).

## Intrinsicality and Change

Lewis emphasized that the distinction between intrinsic and extrinsic properties is connected to the question of what it takes for something to genuinely change. If Mary increases in weight, she undergoes an intrinsic change — a change in her internal constitution. If she becomes the tallest person in the room because a taller person leaves, she undergoes only an extrinsic change — she is merely differently related to her environment.

This distinction matters for persistence theory. The view known as endurantism holds that persisting objects are wholly present at each time, characterized by their intrinsic properties at that time. But then how can the same object have different intrinsic properties at different times (being hot now, cold later)? Lewis's solution is to relativize property instantiation to times: the object has the property *at a time*, making temporal predication three-place (x has F at t). The competing view, perdurantism, holds that objects have temporal parts with different intrinsic properties at different times.

## Philosophical Importance

The intrinsic/extrinsic distinction is not merely technical. It underlies:

**Personal identity**: What must be preserved for personal identity are arguably intrinsic psychological or physical features, not merely relational ones. Locke's memory theory makes psychological continuity (an intrinsic feature?) the criterion; animalism makes biological continuity (arguably intrinsic) the criterion.

**Mental externalism**: Hilary Putnam's Twin Earth argument shows that "water" thoughts can differ between mental duplicates (internally identical beings) living in different environments. If so, mental content is at least partly extrinsic — determined by facts about the environment, not just the internal states of the thinker.

**Supervenience**: A set of properties A supervenes on a set B iff no A-difference is possible without a B-difference. When B is the set of intrinsic physical properties, strong supervenience on B is a particularly powerful relation — it says that all differences are ultimately differences in physical constitution, which is what physicalist positions typically require.
