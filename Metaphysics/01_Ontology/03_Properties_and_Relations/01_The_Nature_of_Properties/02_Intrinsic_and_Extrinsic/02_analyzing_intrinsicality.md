# Analyzing Intrinsicality

Philosophers have attempted many analyses of intrinsicality, none of which is fully satisfying, but each of which illuminates different aspects of the concept. The difficulty is not incidental — it reflects genuine complexity in the target notion.

## The Lewis-Langton Analysis

Lewis and Langton's analysis, building on earlier work by Jaegwon Kim and others, uses the notion of a "lonely" object — one that exists in a possible world containing nothing else. They propose: a property P is intrinsic if and only if:

1. No lonely thing differs from any of its non-lonely duplicates in P.
2. Any two duplicates (whether lonely or not) never differ in P.

Condition (1) rules out properties that depend on accompaniment. Condition (2) rules out properties that can differ even between duplicates in the same world.

This analysis handles many cases well:

- **Being spherical**: A lonely sphere has it, any duplicate has it — intrinsic. ✓
- **Being the only sphere**: A lonely sphere has it (vacuously), but a non-lonely duplicate sphere may lack it — extrinsic. ✓
- **Being accompanied**: A lonely thing lacks it, a duplicate in a populated world has it — extrinsic. ✓

The formal argument for the approach:

- **P1**: Intrinsic properties are those that hold solely in virtue of the object's internal nature.
- **P2**: If a property depends on what co-exists with the object, it is not purely a matter of internal nature.
- **P3**: The lonely-object test identifies exactly those properties whose instantiation is independent of accompaniment.
- **Conclusion**: Intrinsic properties are those that pass the lonely-object test and are shared by all duplicates.

## Problems for the Lewis-Langton Analysis

**The loneliness problem**: The property of being lonely is an extrinsic property in the intuitive sense — it depends on the absence of companions. Yet on the Lewis-Langton analysis, being lonely turns out to be extrinsic, which is arguably correct. But the analysis seems to get this right for the wrong reasons — or at least in a way that is awkward, since being lonely seems like a relational property rather than an intrinsic one, yet the analysis must classify it as extrinsic through the duplication condition.

**The problem of "impure" intrinsics**: Consider the property of *being taller than 6 feet*. This seems intrinsic — it depends only on the object's height, not on relations to other objects. But the Lewis-Langton analysis might classify it as extrinsic if the value "6 feet" makes implicit reference to a conventional standard. The analysis seems sensitive to how properties are described rather than what they are.

**Entanglement**: In quantum mechanics, entangled particles lack definite values for properties like spin without reference to their correlational relations. If spin is an intrinsic property but is indeterminate in isolation, the analysis faces pressure. Some philosophers of physics conclude that quantum mechanics shows there are no genuinely intrinsic properties at the fundamental level.

## Weatherson's Refinement

Brian Weatherson refined the Lewis-Langton account, addressing some of its edge cases. Weatherson introduced the notion of *qualitatively identical* objects: objects that share all purely qualitative properties, where "qualitative" is itself analyzed in terms of not involving essential reference to particular individuals or times. On his account:

- A property is *qualitative* if it does not involve essential reference to any particular.
- An *intrinsic* property is a qualitative property shared by all intrinsic duplicates.
- Duplicates are characterized by sharing all natural properties at the sub-individual level.

This refinement helps with cases involving particular individuals (the property of being taller than Napoleon is extrinsic, since it makes essential reference to a particular) while preserving the core insights of the Lewis-Langton approach.

## Alternative Approaches

**Vallentyne's account**: Peter Vallentyne analyzes intrinsicality via the supervenience of the intrinsic on the qualitative internal state. A property is intrinsic if and only if it supervenes on the intrinsic qualitative states of the object and its parts. This avoids appeal to lonely objects but requires independent specification of what counts as "internal."

**Yablo's account**: Stephen Yablo distinguishes intrinsic properties from what he calls *stable* properties — those that are not threatened by the removal of contingent accompaniments. A property F is intrinsically had by x if x would still have F in any world where the same intrinsic facts obtain about x, regardless of what accompanies x.

**Francescotti's constitutive account**: Robert Francescotti ties intrinsicality to whether the property's instantiation involves constitutive reference to entities outside the object's boundaries. Being spherical does not constitutively reference anything outside the sphere. Being the largest sphere constitutively references other spheres (their absence or smaller size is required). This is intuitive but faces edge cases about what counts as "outside the object."

**Powers-based accounts**: Some philosophers tie intrinsicality to the possession of categorical powers. On this view, a property is intrinsic just in case it confers dispositions on the object that are in principle exercisable regardless of contingent environmental conditions. This connects the intrinsic/extrinsic distinction to the debate between categorical and dispositional properties.

## The Persistence of Difficulty

The persistence of these difficulties suggests that intrinsicality may not be a single, unified concept but a family of related notions that come apart in edge cases. There may be multiple phenomena in the vicinity: locality (not involving distant regions), independence (not depending on accompaniment), categoriality (not being essentially relational), and purity (not making essential reference to particulars). Each captures part of what we mean by "intrinsic," and they come apart in precisely the problematic cases.

For most philosophical work, the intuitive core — that intrinsic properties are those that concern only the object itself — is sufficient, even if a precise analysis remains elusive. The practical implication is that metaphysicians must be careful to specify which aspect of intrinsicality they intend when using the term in arguments.
