# Extrinsic and Relational Properties

Extrinsic properties are those that an object has in virtue of its relations to other things. Being five miles from a lake, being the mother of Caesar, being the most populous city on Earth — these are extrinsic. The object that has them could lose them without undergoing any intrinsic change, and it could acquire them without any internal transformation.

## The Extrinsic and the Relational

The category of extrinsic properties is not simply identical to the category of relational properties, though there is significant overlap. Some philosophers hold that all extrinsic properties are ultimately relational — they are had in virtue of some relation the object bears to something else. Others disagree: perhaps being such that 2 + 2 = 4 is extrinsic (it seems to concern a mathematical fact about numbers, not the object itself) yet it is not a genuine relation between the object and anything else.

A first-pass analysis: F is relational if the canonical specification of F explicitly mentions a relation to other things. Being five miles from a lake: explicitly relational. Being spherical: not explicitly relational. Being the largest sphere: explicitly relational (involves comparison to other spheres).

But this analysis is sensitive to redescription. "Being taller than 6 feet" does not explicitly mention a relation to other individuals — but it can be redescribed as "bearing the relation taller-than to 6 feet of height." Whether the relational redescription reveals a genuine underlying relational structure or is merely a syntactic artifact is disputed.

## Intrinsic Relations

More importantly, there are relational properties that are still arguably intrinsic. Consider the property of having a part that is red: this is a relational property in the sense that it involves a part-whole relation, but the part is internal to the object. Most philosophers classify internal relations — those that relate parts of an object to each other or to the whole — as intrinsic properties of the object.

Similarly, being composed of hydrogen atoms is relational in that it involves the atoms' relations to the larger molecule, but it seems intrinsic to the molecule. The distinction between *relations to co-present external objects* (extrinsic) and *relations among proper parts or to constitutive components* (intrinsic) is doing a lot of work here.

## The Relational Properties of Marriage and Social Ontology

Consider being married: it is obviously relational. But some argue that the relational structure can itself be intrinsic to the pair. In social ontology, whether an institution like marriage grounds genuinely intrinsic properties of its participants is a serious question. John Searle's analysis of social facts as institutional facts built on collective intentionality suggests that being married is a status property — a property conferred by collective recognition — rather than a natural kind property. If so, it is extrinsic in a strong sense: it depends not just on other individuals but on entire social practices and institutions.

## Persistence, Change, and Cambridge Changes

Lewis emphasized that the distinction between intrinsic and extrinsic properties is connected to the question of what it takes for something to genuinely change. If Mary increases in weight, she undergoes an intrinsic change. If she becomes the tallest person in the room (because a taller person leaves), she undergoes only an extrinsic change. What matters for personal identity and persistence is whether continuity tracks intrinsic properties across time.

This connects to the notion of a *Cambridge change*, introduced by Geach: a Cambridge change is any change in the properties of an object, including those that result from changes elsewhere. If Napoleon dies, the property of "being shorter than Napoleon" is lost by some short people — but this is a Cambridge change in them, not a genuine change in their intrinsic nature. The genuine changes are intrinsic; Cambridge changes are typically extrinsic.

The formal criterion: x undergoes a genuine change between t1 and t2 iff x differs intrinsically between t1 and t2. x undergoes merely a Cambridge change iff x differs only extrinsically between t1 and t2.

## Mental Content and Externalism

Extrinsic properties also figure in debates about mental content. Externalists argue that mental content is partly extrinsic — what my "water" thoughts are about depends not just on my internal states but on the environment I was raised in and what substance actually fills the local lakes. Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment makes this vivid: an internal duplicate of me on Twin Earth, where the watery stuff is XYZ rather than H₂O, has thoughts with a different content even though we are internal duplicates. Content is extrinsic.

The argument:

- **P1**: My internal duplicate on Twin Earth and I are perfectly intrinsically alike.
- **P2**: My thought "water is wet" is about H₂O; my duplicate's thought is about XYZ.
- **P3**: Therefore, mental content differs between intrinsic duplicates.
- **Conclusion**: Mental content is (at least partly) extrinsic.

Internalists resist P2, arguing that we share a narrow content (determined by our internal states) that gets applied to different substances in different environments. The dispute about content is directly a dispute about whether content is intrinsic or extrinsic.

## Dispositional Properties and Environmental Dependence

Some philosophers worry that dispositional properties are extrinsic in a problematic way. Being soluble means being disposed to dissolve in water — but water is something external to the soluble thing. Does this make solubility an extrinsic property?

Most respond that dispositions are intrinsic properties that happen to have *manifestation conditions* that involve external objects. The glass is soluble because of its internal molecular structure; what conditions trigger the manifestation of this solubility involve external stuff (water), but the disposition itself is grounded in the glass's internal nature. The property of being soluble is intrinsic; the property of *being currently dissolving* is a relational fact involving both the glass and the water.

These debates show how central the intrinsic/extrinsic distinction is to philosophy of mind, action theory, and the metaphysics of causation, not merely to abstract discussions of property theory.
