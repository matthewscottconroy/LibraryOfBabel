# Grounding and Dependence

Contemporary metaphysics has developed a rich vocabulary for talking about the ways in which facts, properties, and entities depend on and are explained by others. The notion of grounding — introduced or popularized by Kit Fine and others in the early 2000s — has become central to these discussions. Grounding is a relation of non-causal, metaphysical explanation: the fact that the surface is red is grounded in facts about the distribution of microphysical properties; the fact that it is wrong to lie is grounded in facts about suffering and autonomy.

## The Structure of Grounding

Grounding is conceived as irreflexive, asymmetric, and transitive — it is a strict partial ordering that moves from the more fundamental (the grounds) to the less fundamental (the grounded). It is distinguished from causal dependence and from logical entailment. A mental state might logically entail some physical state without the mental fact being grounded in the physical fact; conversely, the mental fact might be grounded in the physical even if the entailment does not hold (in cases of multiple realizability).

Kit Fine's papers "The Question of Realism" (2001) and "Guide to Ground" (2012) articulate the central features of the grounding relation:

- **Irreflexivity**: No fact grounds itself.
- **Asymmetry**: If A grounds B, then B does not ground A.
- **Transitivity**: If A grounds B and B grounds C, then A grounds C.
- **Non-monotonicity**: If A grounds B, it does not follow that A together with C grounds B — additional grounds can disrupt rather than reinforce the grounding relation.

The argument for grounding as a primitive:

- **P1**: We routinely make explanatory claims of the form "the whole exists *because of* its parts," "the disjunction is true *because of* the truth of one disjunct," and so on.
- **P2**: These explanatory claims are not causal — the part does not cause the whole to exist; the true disjunct does not cause the disjunction to be true.
- **P3**: The relevant explanatory relation is not reducible to supervenience, entailment, or conceptual analysis.
- **Conclusion**: There is a primitive explanatory relation — grounding — that connects facts at different metaphysical levels.

## Grounding and Properties

For the theory of properties, grounding connects to questions about which properties are fundamental and which are derivative. Fundamental properties — mass, charge, spin — are not grounded in anything more basic; they appear at the bottom of the grounding hierarchy. Higher-level properties — temperature, hardness, solubility — are grounded in complex arrangements of fundamental properties.

A key question: what is the ontological status of grounded properties? If the property of being a table is grounded in certain physical properties of particles arranged in a certain way, is the property of being a table a *further entity* in the world, or is it merely a way of speaking about the physical facts? Eliminativists say the latter; non-eliminative reductionists say the property exists but is nothing over and above its physical basis; strong emergentists say the property is genuinely distinct from its physical ground.

The grounding relation itself must be explained. Fine argues it is primitive — we understand it through examples and formal characterization but cannot reduce it to more basic terms. Others, like Jonathan Schaffer, analyze grounding in terms of necessitation together with a priority ordering on entities. Opponents of grounding, like Hofweber, argue that grounding talk is merely a way of expressing explanatory preferences without any corresponding metaphysical relation.

## Ontological and Constitutive Dependence

Dependence comes in multiple varieties:

**Ontological dependence**: x cannot exist without y. The set {a} cannot exist without a. The smile cannot exist without the face. This is a modal relation: necessarily, if x exists, y exists.

**Constitutive dependence**: x's nature or essence essentially involves y. What it is to be this smile is to be a facial feature of this face. Kit Fine argues that essence is more fundamental than mere necessitation: it is part of the essence of Socrates that he is human, but not part of the essence of the singleton set {Socrates} that Socrates exists — even though the singleton cannot exist without Socrates (by Extensionality).

**Causal dependence**: x would not exist or have its current properties without the causal influence of y. The statue causally depends on the sculptor's action; the property of being red in this rose causally depends on the genetic and biochemical processes that produced the pigmentation.

Distinguishing these precisely matters for debates about emergence, reduction, and the relationship between special sciences and fundamental physics. A property can be causally dependent on physical states without being ontologically dependent on any particular physical state — which is what emergentists exploit. Water is constitutively identical to H₂O (constitutive dependence), but a particular instance of water also has causal dependence on the chemical processes that produced it.

## Grounding and the Metaphysics of Properties

Two debates about properties directly involve grounding:

**The determinable/determinate relation**: The property of being colored is a determinable; being red is one of its determinates. When an object is red, the fact that it is colored is grounded in the fact that it is red (not vice versa). The grounding direction goes from determinate to determinable. This is important: the object's being colored is not a further fact over and above its being red; it is grounded in the specific fact.

**Natural properties and naturalness**: Lewis's natural properties may be understood as the properties at the bottom of the grounding hierarchy — or at least the properties that appear in the fundamental laws. Non-natural (abundant) properties are grounded in natural ones: the property of being grue is grounded in the properties of being green and being blue (and temporal facts), which are themselves grounded in spectral reflectance properties, which may be grounded in microphysical properties.

**Functional properties**: Functionalism in the philosophy of mind holds that mental properties are functionally defined — they are grounded in whatever physical properties play the relevant causal-functional roles. On this view, the property of being in pain is grounded in the physical properties that occupy the pain-role (being caused by tissue damage, causing avoidance behavior, etc.).

## Critique and Alternatives

Critics argue that grounding is explanatorily vacuous — saying that the mental is grounded in the physical is just saying the physical explains the mental, which we already knew. Jonathan Schaffer's *monism* program uses grounding but reverses the typical direction: the whole grounds the parts, so fundamental entities are wholes rather than micro-components. Grounding thus does not have a fixed direction toward the micro-physical.

Jessica Wilson has argued that grounding can be reduced to specific determination relations — supervenience, realization, identity — rather than being a primitive. On her view, "grounding" is a useful umbrella term but does not name a single unified metaphysical relation.

These debates about grounding are inseparable from questions about what properties there are, which are fundamental, and how the world is hierarchically organized.
