# Problems for the Intrinsic/Extrinsic Distinction

Despite its intuitive appeal, the intrinsic/extrinsic distinction faces several serious challenges that have led some philosophers to question whether it is as clear as it initially seems. The challenges come from physics, from the metaphysics of spacetime, from borderline cases, and from semantic considerations about how the distinction is framed.

## Quantum Entanglement

The first challenge is from quantum mechanics. Quantum states of composite systems are not always determined by the states of their parts — this is the phenomenon of entanglement. If two particles are entangled, neither has a definite spin independently; only the pair has a definite joint state. The state |↑↓⟩ − |↓↑⟩ (the singlet state) is a property of the pair, not of either individual particle.

This means that what we might naively call the intrinsic properties of a particle — its definite spin — may always be embedded in a holistic, relational context at the quantum level. Some philosophers conclude that there are no *fundamental* intrinsic properties in the sense of properties that are fully determinate at the level of individual particles, independent of their relational situation.

Howard and Teller have argued that quantum mechanics requires us to adopt a *non-separability* thesis: the complete physical state of a composite system is not determined by the states of its subsystems together with their spatial configuration. If so, the most fundamental level of reality has irreducibly relational states, and the standard notion of intrinsic properties as "what the object is like in itself" may be inapplicable.

Ontic structural realism draws the most radical conclusion from this: at the fundamental level, there are only relations, and the notion of an intrinsic property of an individual object has no application. Objects themselves may be derivative from the relational structure.

## Spacetime and Location

A second challenge involves the question of spacetime. Being located at a particular region of spacetime seems intrinsic to many philosophers — the object is where it is independently of other objects. But this is only defensible on a substantivalist view of spacetime, where spacetime is a genuine entity in its own right and objects stand in location-relations to spacetime regions.

On a relationalist view of spacetime, location is fundamentally relational: objects stand in spatial and temporal relations to each other, and there are no absolute locations. On this view, being at a particular location is an extrinsic property — it depends on the object's relations to other objects. Whether location is intrinsic or extrinsic thus depends on one's philosophy of spacetime.

Further, even if spacetime is substantival, it is not obvious that location relations to abstract spacetime regions should count as extrinsic in the standard sense. Lewis's analysis of intrinsicality in terms of lonely objects seems to give the wrong result here: a lonely object in an otherwise empty universe would still have a location in substantival spacetime, so location would count as intrinsic.

## Impure Intrinsic Properties

There is a third challenge: the problem of *impure intrinsic properties* — properties that seem intrinsic but make essential reference to something outside the object. The property of being taller than the Eiffel Tower seems extrinsic (it involves a comparison to an external object). But the property of being taller than 324 meters is more difficult: height is intrinsic, and 324 meters is just a unit measurement of height. Is being-taller-than-324-meters intrinsic?

If we say yes, then the property seems to make implicit reference to a conventional unit system (the meter), which is something external and social. If we say no, we face the awkward conclusion that being 2 meters tall is extrinsic, which contradicts the intuition that height is intrinsic.

The analysis needs to explain why "being taller than the Eiffel Tower" is extrinsic (it makes essential reference to a particular external object, the Eiffel Tower) while "being taller than 324 meters" is intrinsic (it merely reports a fact about height, with "324 meters" serving as a way of specifying a height value rather than referring to an external object). The distinction is real but requires careful specification.

## The Theory-Relativity Worry

Finally, there is a metaphysical-semantic concern: our intuitions about what is intrinsic track both our common-sense categorizations and our scientific picture of the world, but these do not always align. From the folk-psychological perspective, being in pain seems intrinsic — it is purely a matter of what is happening in one's own mind. From the functionalist perspective in philosophy of mind, being in pain is defined by functional role and is hence extrinsic to some degree.

The worry is that "intrinsic" is not a unified, theory-neutral notion but one that is implicitly relative to a background theory. What counts as "inside" the object depends on how we individuate objects and what we take their constitutive properties to be. This suggests that the intrinsic/extrinsic distinction may be best understood as *theory-relative*: intrinsic relative to a particular theory or level of description, rather than intrinsic absolutely.

## Living with the Problems

Most metaphysicians accept that the intrinsic/extrinsic distinction is theoretically indispensable despite these complications. The responses to the challenges tend to follow two strategies:

**Refinement**: Distinguish multiple notions of intrinsicality (local versus global, fundamental versus derived, constitutive versus causal) and be explicit about which notion is relevant in each context.

**Idealization**: Treat the ideal of "purely intrinsic" properties as a limiting case that may not be realized in our actual world, but that serves as a useful theoretical benchmark. Even if all actual properties have some relational component, the intrinsic/extrinsic spectrum is real, and the core cases are sufficiently clear for most philosophical purposes.

The challenges from quantum mechanics and spacetime are especially serious for those who want a fully general metaphysical account. For most purposes in ethics, philosophy of mind, and ordinary metaphysics, however, the intuitive core of the distinction — that some properties concern only the object itself while others depend on its surroundings — remains serviceable and important.
