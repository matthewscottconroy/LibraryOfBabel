# Categorical Properties

Categorical properties are those that characterize how a thing actually is, independent of how it might behave or what it would do in counterfactual circumstances. A categorical property is fully actualized in the present: it describes the actual state of the object, not a potentiality or tendency. The shape, size, and constitution of an object are paradigm categorical properties — a cube is cubic whether or not it is ever placed in a position where its shape matters.

## The Nature of Categorical Properties

The philosophical significance of categorical properties becomes clear when contrasted with dispositional ones. Dispositions — like fragility, solubility, or conductivity — characterize objects in terms of what they would do in certain conditions. Categorical properties, by contrast, are present in full regardless of whether those conditions are ever realized.

The classic picture of categorical properties comes from the Humean tradition. David Hume argued that there are no necessary connections in nature — what actually happens is all there is. Properties are distinct existences; what one property contributes to what happens is fixed by the laws of nature, which are themselves contingent. The paradigm categorical properties are the "perfectly natural" properties in Lewis's sense: charge, mass, spin are fully characterized by saying what they are, without implicit reference to what they are *for*.

On this view, the world's fundamental level consists entirely of categorical facts: particular objects having particular properties at particular spatiotemporal locations. Lewis called this the Humean mosaic. Laws, counterfactuals, and dispositions are all analyzed in terms of this categorical base — they are not additional ingredients but features of the pattern of categorical facts.

## Categorical Properties and Laws

The distinction matters crucially for the theory of laws of nature. If all natural properties are categorical, then the laws that connect properties to their effects are external additions to the world — they are not built into the properties themselves. This is the Humean picture: the world is a vast mosaic of categorical facts, and laws are just regularities over those facts. The regularities could, in principle, have been otherwise while the categorical facts remained the same.

The argument from categorical properties to contingent laws:

- **P1**: Fundamental properties are categorical — they have their intrinsic nature independently of what they are lawfully associated with.
- **P2**: The laws connecting properties to their effects are distinct from the properties themselves (since the properties are fully characterized without reference to those laws).
- **P3**: Whatever is distinct from its categorical basis is not metaphysically necessary.
- **Conclusion**: Laws of nature are metaphysically contingent — the world could have had the same properties distributed the same way but different laws.

This yields the possibility of nomologically distinct but categorically identical possible worlds — what Lewis called "quiddistic" worlds. The charge of an electron in one possible world is a categorical property that could, in principle, play the role that mass plays in another world, and vice versa.

## Problems for Categorical Properties

**The identification problem**: If categorical properties have their natures independently of their causal roles, then two properties that play all the same causal roles in all possible worlds are nonetheless distinct (if they are categorically distinct). This generates "quiddism" — the view that properties have an identity over and above their causal profiles. But if properties are entirely characterized by what they are *like* rather than what they *do*, it is unclear how we could ever identify them or know which property is which.

Ramsey famously argued that theoretical properties are implicitly defined by their roles in our best theories. If so, categorical properties with the same causal role are the same property, undermining quidditism. But Armstrong and others insist that properties have real intrinsic characters — their "quiddities" — that are logically independent of causal roles.

**The Fregean problem**: Gilbert Ryle argued that categorical properties cannot fully characterize the natural world, because the world includes irreducible dispositions. The dormitive virtue of opium is not identical to opium's molecular structure — it is the molecular structure's causal power to sedate. Ryle's critique was often misread as dissolving dispositions into behaviors, but the sharper point is that categorical bases do not entail dispositions without laws, and laws themselves seem to have a dispositional character.

## The Powerful Qualities Response

C.B. Martin and John Heil challenged the distinction between categorical and dispositional properties, arguing that every genuine property is both categorical and dispositional: it is what it is (categorical) and it has the power to do what it does (dispositional). On this "powerful qualities" view, the categorical/dispositional distinction is a distinction within the concept of a property, not a distinction between two kinds of property.

The argument for powerful qualities:

- **P1**: Properties are real features of the world with genuine intrinsic natures (categorical aspect).
- **P2**: Properties also necessarily contribute to causal and modal facts — they confer powers on their bearers (dispositional aspect).
- **P3**: These two aspects are inseparable — the same property that has an intrinsic nature is also the property that confers a power.
- **Conclusion**: There are no purely categorical properties (properties that contribute no powers) and no purely dispositional properties (properties with no intrinsic nature).

The upshot is that even the seemingly innocent characterization of categorical properties as "non-dispositional" turns out to be philosophically contentious. Whether any properties are purely categorical — with no dispositional nature at all — is one of the central disputes in contemporary metaphysics of properties.
