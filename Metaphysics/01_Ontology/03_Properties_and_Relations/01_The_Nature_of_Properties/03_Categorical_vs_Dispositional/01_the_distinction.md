# The Categorical vs. Dispositional Distinction

Properties seem to come in two flavors. Some properties are categorical: they describe how a thing actually is, here and now, without reference to how it might behave or what it would do under circumstances that may never occur. The shape of a crystal, the mass of a particle, the color of a surface — these seem to be fully present as actual, occurrent features. Other properties are dispositional: they describe potentialities, powers, tendencies. Fragility, solubility, magnetism — these are characterized by what their bearers would do in suitable conditions, even if those conditions never arise.

## Framing the Distinction

The distinction has deep roots in the history of philosophy. Aristotle distinguished between actual and potential properties: the matter of a statue has the potential to be a statue; the finished statue has the actual property of being a statue. Medieval philosophers discussed primary and secondary qualities — primary qualities (shape, size, motion) being straightforwardly objective, secondary qualities (color, taste, smell) being powers to produce sensory experiences. Locke drew this distinction sharply: primary qualities are "really in the bodies whether we perceive them or no," while secondary qualities are powers relative to perceivers.

In contemporary metaphysics, the debate centers on whether the categorical/dispositional distinction is fundamental, or whether one category can be reduced to the other, or whether the distinction itself collapses.

## The Reductionist Challenge

One position holds that dispositional predicates pick out categorical properties that happen to play certain causal roles. Glass is fragile because it has a certain molecular structure; dissolving in water is what soluble things do because of their molecular bonding. On this view, dispositions are nothing over and above categorical properties plus laws of nature that connect them to their manifestations.

The argument for categorical reduction:

- **P1**: For every dispositional predicate "is F" (fragile, soluble, etc.), there is a categorical property C such that anything is F iff it has C.
- **P2**: The link between C and the manifestation of F is provided by a law of nature, not by the intrinsic nature of C.
- **P3**: C fully characterizes the property; the disposition is just the categorical property described functionally.
- **Conclusion**: Dispositions reduce to categorical properties plus contingent laws.

This is the orthodox Humean position. The laws of nature are contingent regularities over the categorical mosaic; dispositions are just these regularities described from the object's perspective.

## The Irreducibility of Dispositions

The opposing view holds that some properties are irreducibly dispositional. George Molnar and Sydney Shoemaker argued that what makes a property the property it is — its identity — is constituted by its causal role, its tendency to produce certain effects. On this view, there could not be a duplicate of a dispositional property that lacked its characteristic manifestation pattern: the power just is the tendency.

The identity thesis: Property P's identity is fully constituted by the manifestation M that P necessarily confers on its bearers (in conditions C). This means that laws of nature are not contingent additions but flow from property natures: the law "charge repels like charges" is not a contingent generalization but a necessary truth given the nature of charge.

This position — dispositionalism — has been developed most thoroughly by Alexander Bird (*Nature's Metaphysics*, 2007), Brian Ellis (*Scientific Essentialism*, 2001), and George Molnar (*Powers*, 2003). They argue that fundamental properties of physics are powers: what it is to be a charge just is to have the power to attract or repel. There is no further categorical basis; the power is the property.

## The Powerful Qualities View

A third view holds that the distinction itself is untenable. Every property is both categorical (it is what it is) and dispositional (it contributes certain powers). Properties are "powerful qualities" that cannot be cleanly separated into the how-it-is and the what-it-does dimensions.

C.B. Martin and John Heil developed this position in a series of papers and books. Their argument: the dispositional theorist needs categorical properties to specify the manifestation of a power (the power to produce fragmentation is a power to produce something categorically described). The categorical theorist needs dispositional properties to explain why categorical properties have the causal effects they do. Neither purely categorical nor purely dispositional properties are coherent; all genuine properties have both aspects.

## Why It Matters

This debate matters for understanding laws of nature, causation, and natural necessity:

**Laws**: If properties are purely categorical, laws must be contingent additions to the categorical structure — there is no necessity in property natures themselves. If properties are essentially dispositional, laws flow from the natures of the properties and are metaphysically necessary.

**Causation**: If causation involves the exercise of powers (dispositions), then causal relations are grounded in property natures. If causation is a matter of categorical regularities, then it is a pattern in the mosaic rather than a deep metaphysical relation.

**Natural necessity**: Dispositionalism promises to explain why the laws of nature are necessary without positing a brute necessitation relation (as Armstrong does) or accepting mere regularities (as Lewis does). The laws are necessary because properties could not be otherwise than they are — their essences are their dispositions.

The debate between categoricalism and dispositionalism is one of the most active areas of contemporary analytic metaphysics, with significant implications for philosophy of science and the foundations of modality.
