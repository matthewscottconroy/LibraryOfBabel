# Dispositional Properties

Dispositional properties are properties that characterize their bearers in terms of tendencies, powers, or liabilities — what they would do or what would happen to them in suitable conditions. Fragility is a disposition: a fragile glass is one that would break if struck in a certain way. Solubility is a disposition: a soluble substance is one that would dissolve if immersed in a solvent. Conductivity is a disposition: a conductor is one that would permit current to flow through it under a potential difference.

## The Conditional Analysis

The classic philosophical analysis of dispositions uses conditional statements. "x is fragile" is analyzed as "if x were struck in the appropriate way, x would break." This conditional analysis has the virtue of connecting dispositional predications to testable implications, but it faces serious problems.

**The finkish disposition**: A finkish disposition is one that would be lost if the trigger condition were fulfilled. Imagine a fragile glass that is protected by a powerful force field that would harden the glass if anyone were about to strike it. The conditional analysis says this glass is not fragile (since the counterfactual is false — if it were struck, it would not break), but intuitively it is fragile (the intrinsic structural properties that make glass fragile are all present). Lewis and Martin introduced this case to show that the conditional analysis is insufficient.

**The mimicking disposition**: A mimic is an object that lacks a genuine disposition but has properties that cause it to behave as if it had the disposition. A non-soluble crystal that is surrounded by a mechanism that dissolves it whenever it is placed in water mimics solubility without having it. The conditional analysis incorrectly classifies it as soluble.

**The always-triggering problem**: Some dispositions may be constantly triggered, so that the conditional becomes trivially true. The analysis must handle cases where the conditions for a disposition are always or never satisfied without losing its grip on what dispositions are.

## Beyond Conditionalism

A deeper worry is that conditional analyses treat dispositions as mere linguistic conveniences — shorthand for conditionals — rather than as genuine features of the world. If dispositions are real, they should be identified with categorical bases (the molecular structure that makes glass fragile) or with irreducible powers.

The reductionist identifies dispositions with their categorical bases. The property of being fragile is just the property of having such-and-such molecular bonding structure, described from the perspective of its characteristic effects. The law of nature connecting that structure to breaking explains the conditional; the disposition is the categorical property plus the law.

Critics of reduction argue that this gets the explanatory direction backwards. We explain why glass breaks when struck by citing its fragility (its disposition to break). If fragility just is the categorical basis, then the explanation becomes: we explain why glass breaks by citing its molecular structure. But this makes the disposition idle — it is doing no additional explanatory work over the molecular structure. The intuition is that fragility is a genuine property that mediates between the structure and the behavior.

## The Dispositionalist Tradition

The dispositionalist tradition, associated with George Molnar (*Powers*, 2003), C.B. Martin, and Alexander Bird (*Nature's Metaphysics*, 2007), holds that some dispositions are fundamental: they are not reducible to categorical properties plus laws. On this view, the fundamental properties of physics — charge, mass, spin — are themselves dispositions or powers.

What it is to have a charge just is to have a power to attract or repel other charges. There is no underlying categorical basis that the charge reduces to; the power is the property. The argument:

- **P1**: If fundamental properties had a categorical basis distinct from their powers, those categorical bases could in principle exist without the powers (since categorical properties are by definition independent of causal roles).
- **P2**: But then the laws connecting categorical bases to powers would be contingent, making it an open question which categorical bases give rise to which powers.
- **P3**: This generates the possibility of "swapped" laws and rampant underdetermination — we could never know whether charge actually plays the charge-role or has been secretly swapped with mass while the laws permuted.
- **P4**: This possibility is implausible and scientifically unmotivated.
- **Conclusion**: Fundamental properties are not categorical bases with contingently attached powers but are themselves powers.

This position promises to explain natural necessity (laws flow from the natures of the properties) but faces the challenge of explaining how powers can be real without there being any categorical structure underlying them.

## Intentionality and Powers

George Molnar drew attention to a striking feature of powers: they have *intentionality* or *directedness*. A power is essentially directed toward its manifestation — the fragility of the glass points toward breaking, the charge of the electron points toward attraction/repulsion. This directedness persists even when the manifestation never occurs: the power is there, pointing toward its manifestation, even in a world where no glass ever breaks.

This quasi-intentional character of powers is philosophically striking because intentionality is standardly associated with mental states. Molnar argued that physical powers have intentionality in the same sense as mental states, suggesting a deep connection between the mental and physical that is not reducible to the simple causal-functional picture.

Bird responds that the directedness of powers does not require minds or representation — it is a primitive metaphysical feature of irreducible dispositions. The power to break is directed toward breaking as an intrinsic, non-representational feature of its nature.

## Dispositions and Possible Worlds

On a modal realist account (Lewis), dispositions can be given a clean analysis: x is disposed to M in conditions C iff, in the closest possible worlds where x is in conditions C, x manifests M. This avoids the finkish disposition problem by modifying the relevant possible worlds rather than using a simple subjunctive conditional. But Lewis's own commitment to categorical properties means he does not take dispositions as fundamental — they are patterns over categorical facts at possible worlds.

For dispositionalists, possible worlds themselves are partly characterized by the powers at them. The question of which world is the actual world is settled by which powers are actually instantiated, and the modal facts (what is possible) flow from the natures of those powers.
