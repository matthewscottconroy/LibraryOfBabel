# Powers and Dispositions

The metaphysics of powers — also called dispositionalism — has experienced a significant revival in the past three decades. Where mid-twentieth-century philosophers were often suspicious of dispositional language, seeing it as imprecise shorthand for subjunctive conditionals, contemporary philosophers have developed sophisticated ontologies that take powers seriously as irreducible features of reality.

## The Core Commitment

The core commitment of powers ontology is that reality is fundamentally characterized by directed potentialities. Electrons do not merely happen to repel each other; repulsion is what they have a power for, and that power is part of the electron's very nature. The power is intentional or directed — it points toward its manifestation even when the manifestation never occurs. This gives powers a quasi-intentional character that makes them strange objects by the lights of standard naturalism.

Key figures in the revival include George Molnar, whose posthumous *Powers: A Study in Metaphysics* (2003) provides the most systematic ontological treatment; Sydney Shoemaker, whose "Causality and Properties" (1980) argued that properties are individuated by their causal powers; Brian Ellis, whose *Scientific Essentialism* (2001) developed a comprehensive alternative to Humean metaphysics; Alexander Bird, whose *Nature's Metaphysics* (2007) defends pandispositionalism; and C.B. Martin, who worked closely with Heil to develop the powerful qualities view.

## Pandispositionalism

Alexander Bird has developed a thoroughgoing dispositionalism he calls pandispositionalism: all natural properties are dispositional through and through. This is the strongest form of the powers ontology — there are no categorical properties at all; every fundamental property is essentially a power.

The appeal is that this explains why laws of nature are metaphysically necessary rather than contingent. If a property's identity is constituted by what it does — by the manifestations it necessarily produces — then the laws expressing what it does are necessary. Charge repels like charges in every possible world where charge exists, because repulsion is part of what it is to be charge. This is an attractive result: it explains natural necessity without positing primitive necessitation relations between distinct universals (as Armstrong does) and without reducing laws to mere regularities (as Lewis does).

The argument for pandispositionalism:

- **P1**: The identity of a natural property is constituted by its causal-dispositional role — by what it necessarily does.
- **P2**: If property identity is constituted by causal role, then any world with that property must have the corresponding causal regularities.
- **P3**: Therefore, the laws governing the property are metaphysically necessary.
- **Conclusion**: Fundamental laws of nature are metaphysically necessary, not contingent.

This conclusion — that natural laws are necessary — is a striking reversal of the standard Humean position. It gains support from Kripke's and Putnam's arguments that natural kind terms are rigid designators: water is necessarily H₂O, not merely in all actual cases, and by analogy charge may necessarily attract/repel, not merely in the actual world.

## The "Always Packing, Never Traveling" Objection

Critics of pandispositionalism worry about what Bird calls the "always packing, never traveling" objection. If every property is characterized purely by its manifestations, and manifestations are themselves properties, we get a potential regress: power A is directed toward manifestation M; M is itself a power directed toward manifestation M'; and so on — but nothing is ever categorical to ground the chain.

The regress problem: if P1 is a power directed toward manifestation P2, and P2 is a power directed toward manifestation P3, then the world consists entirely of powers pointing to powers, with no categorical "landing place." Some argue this makes the world a kind of pure intentionality with no categorical content — a strange metaphysical predicament.

Bird responds that the regress is not vicious: we do not need a final categorical landing place any more than we need an infinite series of causes to have a first cause. The powers form a coherent web of mutual directedness. Martin and Heil respond differently: they accept that manifestations include genuine qualities — categorical aspects of powerful properties. This avoids the regress but reintroduces categorical properties.

## Partial Dispositionalism

Partial dispositionalism — the view that some but not all properties are dispositional — avoids the regress at the cost of treating categorical properties as primitive. The best-developed versions try to preserve the insight that properties are power-conferring without committing to full pandispositionalism. On this view, there are two fundamental categories: powers (irreducibly dispositional properties) and qualities (categorical properties with no dispositional nature).

The challenge for partial dispositionalism is explaining how powers and qualities interact. If powers are directed toward manifestations that are purely categorical qualities, the laws connecting powers to qualities seem to be contingent — which undermines the core motivation for powers ontology. Mumford addresses this by arguing that the manifestations of powers are themselves powers, so the categorical/dispositional distinction is always between levels rather than at the fundamental level.

## Powers and Causation

Powers ontology promises a distinctive account of causation. If properties are powers, then causal relations are grounded in the exercise of powers: the cause and the effect are connected by the manifestation of a power. The fragility of the glass causes it to break when struck because the fragility is the power to break and striking is the triggering condition.

This picture makes causation more than mere counterfactual dependence or constant conjunction. There is a real productive relation between cause and effect, grounded in the power of the cause. Ellis and Bird argue that this gives us a more robust account of causation than Humean alternatives — one that explains why causes produce their effects rather than merely preceding them.

The connection to laws is direct: laws are not further facts over and above the distribution of powers. A law like "charge repels like charges" is not an extra item in the world's inventory; it flows from the nature of charge. On this view, there are no "governing" laws distinct from the properties that instantiate them — laws are immanent in the properties themselves.
