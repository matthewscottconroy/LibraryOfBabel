# Essentialism and Natural Necessity

One of the most striking payoffs of taking properties seriously as powerful essences is that it promises to explain natural necessity. Why is it that electrons must repel each other — not merely that they happen to do so in the actual world, but that they could not fail to? The traditional Humean answer is that they don't *must*: there are no necessary connections in nature, only regularities. But this leaves the regularities themselves as brute facts, and many philosophers find this unsatisfying.

## Scientific Essentialism

Brian Ellis developed scientific essentialism as a systematic response to the Humean picture, principally in *Scientific Essentialism* (2001) and *The Philosophy of Nature* (2002). According to Ellis, natural properties have their causal roles essentially — it is part of what it is to be charge that charge repels like charges. This means the laws expressing those roles are metaphysically necessary, not merely nomologically necessary. Electrons must repel each other across all possible worlds where they exist, because their intrinsic nature demands it.

The essentialist thesis:

- **P1**: Natural properties have their causal-dispositional roles essentially: it is part of the nature of property F that F disposes its bearers to behave in way M in conditions C.
- **P2**: If it is part of the nature of F that F produces M in C, then necessarily, whenever something has F and is in C, it manifests M.
- **Conclusion**: Laws of nature — statements connecting properties to their characteristic effects — are metaphysically necessary truths.

This connects essentialism directly to the Kripkean account of necessity. Kripke argued in *Naming and Necessity* (1980) that identity statements between natural kind terms are necessary: "Water is H₂O" is necessarily true. Ellis extends this: not just the composition of natural kinds but the causal profiles of natural properties are necessary. Heat is necessarily kinetic energy; charge necessarily attracts and repels; mass necessarily contributes to gravitational force. These are not merely true in all possible worlds but are metaphysically necessary in the strongest sense.

## The Contrast with the Humean Mosaic

This essentialist picture contrasts sharply with the Humean mosaic. For Hume and for Lewis, the world is a vast array of categorical facts, and laws are just generalizations about this array, with no modal force in the properties themselves. The best-system analysis (Lewis) says laws are those generalizations that achieve the best balance of simplicity and strength over the Humean mosaic. But this means laws are features of the pattern, not of the properties — they could have been different if the pattern had been different.

For the essentialist, this is explanatorily backward. We do not explain why electrons repel by noting that there is a pattern of repulsion among electrons — we explain the pattern by noting that charge repulsion flows from the essence of charge. The essentialist prioritizes the nature of properties over the patterns they generate.

## Armstrong's Alternative

Armstrong's account of laws — the necessitation account — tries to secure natural necessity without full essentialism. On Armstrong's view, the law that all Fs are G is a relation of necessitation **N(F, G)** between universals F and G. This relation is itself a universal, and its holding entails the corresponding regularity among particulars. Laws are not mere regularities but higher-order facts about relations between universals.

Armstrong distinguishes his view from essentialism: the necessitation relation N(F, G) is a contingent relation between universals. There are possible worlds where N(F, G) does not hold — where Fs are not necessarily G. So laws are necessary given that the necessitation relation holds, but the holding of the relation is itself contingent.

Ellis objects that this does not explain natural necessity in the deepest sense — it merely relocates it to the necessitation relation, which is itself contingently held. The essentialist route — making the causal role part of the property's essence — provides deeper necessity because the property could not exist without the causal role.

## Modal Recombination and the Essentialist

The essentialist position faces the challenge of modal recombination. If properties necessarily have their causal roles, then we cannot coherently imagine a world where electrons are positively charged or where gravity is repulsive. Many philosophers think such worlds are conceivable and hence possible. The essentialist seems to be imposing too strong modal constraints on natural properties.

Ellis responds that conceivability does not establish genuine possibility. We can conceive of a world with a different periodic table, but this does not mean such a world is genuinely metaphysically possible — it might be merely epistemic possibility (possibility relative to our ignorance of the constraints). The necessities of natural properties are a posteriori necessities: we discover them through science, not through conceptual analysis.

Bird adds that the appearance of conceivability is often an artifact of using terms in different ways across possible worlds. When we imagine a world where "electrons are positively charged," we may just be imagining positrons (positively charged particles) called "electrons." The genuine property of being an electron is necessarily negatively charged.

## Property Essentialism and Laws

Connecting essentialism to the categorical/dispositional debate: essentialists typically hold that natural properties are irreducibly dispositional, with their essences constituted by their causal roles. This makes essentialism a species of dispositionalism, and the arguments for essentialism largely overlap with those for powers ontology. Ellis and Bird both endorse pandispositionalism (or near variants): fundamental physical properties just are their dispositional essences.

The upshot for laws: if properties have their dispositions essentially, then laws are internal to properties rather than external impositions. The law "like charges repel" is not a contingent fact about the actual world; it is a necessary consequence of what charge is. This gives us a unified explanation of:

1. Why laws are universal (they flow from property natures, which are the same everywhere).
2. Why laws support counterfactuals (if charge necessarily repels, then even in counterfactual circumstances where like charges are placed near each other, they would repel).
3. Why induction is reliable (we are not merely projecting observed patterns onto the future; we are tracking genuine modal constraints built into the natures of things).

Scientific essentialism thus promises to solve several recalcitrant problems in the philosophy of science while providing a rich account of the metaphysics of properties.
