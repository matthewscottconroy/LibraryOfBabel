# The Categorical/Dispositional Debate

The debate between those who favor categorical properties and those who favor dispositional properties cuts across most of the major questions in the metaphysics of properties and beyond. Getting clear on what is really at stake helps to see why the debate has persisted and why it matters.

## The Humean Position

The Humean tradition — associated with Lewis's metaphysics — holds that the fundamental properties of the world are categorical. The world is a mosaic of categorical facts about the actual distribution of properties across spacetime points. Laws of nature are not fundamental; they are patterns over this mosaic. Modality is not fundamental; possible worlds are other mosaics of categorical facts. On this view, dispositions are analyzed away: to say that x is fragile is to say that x has some categorical base property that lawfully correlates with breaking when struck.

Lewis's Humean supervenience thesis: all facts about the world supervene on the distribution of local, intrinsic, categorical properties across the spacetime manifold. There are no necessary connections among distinct existences; what happens at one region of spacetime is logically independent of what happens at any other region.

The argument:

- **P1**: The world is fully characterized by the distribution of perfectly natural (categorical) properties across spacetime.
- **P2**: Laws, dispositions, counterfactuals, and causal relations are all determined by this distribution.
- **P3**: Nothing metaphysically more fundamental than categorical property instances is needed.
- **Conclusion**: Categorical properties are the metaphysical bedrock; dispositions are derivative.

The Humean pays a price: laws are regularities without necessity, causation is constant conjunction or counterfactual dependence, and there is no deep explanation of why the patterns are as they are rather than otherwise. Lewis accepted these costs as the price of ontological parsimony.

## The Non-Humean Position

The non-Humean tradition — dispositionalism — reverses this. Fundamental properties are dispositional: what makes a property the property it is is its contribution to causal and modal facts. Laws of nature are not external to properties but internal: they flow from what properties essentially are. This gives a unified explanation of natural necessity (why electrons must repel each other) and avoids treating laws as brute regularities.

On this picture:

- **P1**: The fundamental properties of physics are powers — irreducibly dispositional entities directed toward their characteristic manifestations.
- **P2**: The laws of nature express the essential causal profiles of these powers.
- **P3**: Laws so grounded are metaphysically necessary: they could not be otherwise given the nature of the properties.
- **Conclusion**: Natural necessity is real and grounded in property essences.

The non-Humean pays a different price: properties are essentially modal entities with necessary causal roles, which limits the space of metaphysical possibility and raises questions about how we could know the essences of properties from the outside.

## Quidditism and Anti-Quidditism

Between these positions lies a subtle further debate. "Quidditism" — associated with Lewis's acceptance of categorical properties — holds that each natural property has a primitive identity (its "quiddity") that is logically independent of its causal role. In one possible world, the property we call "charge" plays the mass-role; in another, it plays the charge-role. Properties are identified across worlds by their quiddities, not by their causal profiles.

Anti-quidditism denies this: properties are individuated by their causal roles, so there is no coherent possibility of "swapped" properties. Two properties with the same causal role in all possible worlds are the same property.

The quidditism debate matters for epistemology: if properties have quiddities that are logically distinct from causal roles, we can only know properties through their causal-functional manifestations — we are systematically unable to know the intrinsic nature of the categorical properties that underlie our observations. This is a form of Kantian ignorance about the noumenal realm. Anti-quidditism avoids this by making property nature transparent to causal inquiry.

## Intermediate Positions

Most philosophers hold that some properties are categorical and others dispositional, or that all properties are both (the powerful qualities view):

**Partial dispositionalism**: Some fundamental properties are pure powers (charge, spin), while others are pure qualities (spatio-temporal location?). The challenge is explaining why this division exists and how powers and qualities interact.

**Powerful qualities**: Every genuine property is both categorical (it has an intrinsic character — it is what it is) and dispositional (it necessarily contributes powers). C.B. Martin, John Heil, and David Armstrong (in his later work) moved toward this position. On this view, the categorical/dispositional distinction is a distinction between two ways of describing or conceiving a property, not a distinction between two kinds of property.

**Structuralism**: Some philosophers hold that properties have no intrinsic natures at all — they are individuated entirely by their structural relations to other properties. On this view, there are neither categorical quiddities nor irreducible powers; only structural positions in a web of mutual dependence. This is a form of thoroughgoing relationism about properties.

## The Debate's Connections

The debate connects to:

**The analysis of counterfactuals**: Counterfactuals depend on laws, which depend on the nature of properties. A Humean analysis grounds counterfactuals in patterns over categorical facts; a dispositionalist analysis grounds them in the necessary manifestations of powers.

**The problem of induction**: What grounds the reliability of inductive inference? Humeans say there is no metaphysical grounding — induction works because the world has been uniform, and we project past patterns onto the future. Dispositionalists say powers ground induction: because properties have their roles necessarily, past regularities track modal facts about what must happen.

**Philosophy of science**: Explanations in science typically cite properties and their effects. If properties are categorical, explanations are regularities expressed over categorical facts. If properties are dispositional, explanations cite genuine causal powers that necessitate their effects. The dispositionalist picture arguably provides a more substantive notion of scientific explanation.

The categorical/dispositional debate is thus not a narrow technical dispute about the analysis of dispositional predicates, but a fundamental disagreement about the modal structure of reality and the nature of scientific explanation.
