# Universals and Particulars

Two electrons have the same charge. This fact seems entirely unremarkable — until you ask what it means. One answer: the electrons share a property; there is a single thing, the property of having such-and-such charge, that is present in both. This is realism about universals. A radically different answer: there is no single shared entity; there are simply two distinct charge-instances, one in each electron, that resemble each other without being numerically identical. This is the view associated with trope theory and with various forms of nominalism.

The debate between realism and nominalism about universals is not a technical dispute that can be settled by looking more carefully at the data. It is a dispute about the fundamental structure of the world and the right method for answering questions about it. The realist holds that if two things genuinely share a property — not just resemble each other, but are of the same type — then there must be some one thing they share. The nominalist holds that this argument trades on an equivocation: "sharing a property" need not mean "being in a relation to a common entity." Two things can be exactly alike in the relevant respects without there being any entity over and above the two things.

The stakes of this dispute extend beyond the metaphysics of properties. Realism about universals offers a natural account of natural laws: a law of nature holds because the universal of being-negatively-charged necessitates the universal of mutually-repelling-when-proximate. Nominalist alternatives must explain nomic necessity in different terms — through regularities, or resemblance classes, or primitive facts about particular powers. The debate also connects to the epistemology of mathematics: if mathematical objects are universals, then realism about universals provides a natural route to Platonism in the philosophy of mathematics.

This section examines the main versions of realism (Platonic transcendent universals, Aristotelian immanent universals, Armstrong's universals as constituents of states of affairs) and the main alternatives (various forms of nominalism, trope theory), with attention to the arguments that favor each position and the theoretical costs each must bear.
