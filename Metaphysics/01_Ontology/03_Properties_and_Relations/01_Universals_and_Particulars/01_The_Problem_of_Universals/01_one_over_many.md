# The One Over Many Problem

When we say that two roses are both red, we seem to be attributing the very same property — redness — to two distinct objects. But what exactly is this shared redness? The *one over many* problem asks: what accounts for the fact that numerically distinct things share a single predicate? Is there some genuine entity — a universal — that both things have in common, or is the similarity merely a matter of how we choose to describe them?

## The Classical Formulation

The problem originates in ancient Greek philosophy. Plato's dialogues repeatedly press the question: if multiple things are beautiful, large, or just, what is the single thing that they all are? His answer — the Theory of Forms — posits that there is a single entity, the Form of Beauty, that each beautiful particular "participates in." Aristotle modified this by locating the universal in the particulars themselves rather than in a separate realm, but retained the core idea that numerically distinct things can genuinely share one thing.

The medieval debate among realists, conceptualists, and nominalists represents the most sustained historical engagement with the problem. Realists (following Plato or Aristotle) held that universals are real entities. Conceptualists (associated with Peter Abelard and Aquinas) held that universals are real as mental concepts but do not correspond to entities outside the mind. Nominalists (William of Ockham's position is the locus classicus) held that universals are merely words — *nomina* — and only particulars genuinely exist.

## The Metaphysical Stakes

The Platonic answer posits that there is indeed a genuine shared entity: the universal RED, which exists independently of both roses and indeed independently of whether any rose or colored thing exists. The two roses both *participate in* or *instantiate* this universal; it is precisely their sharing this one thing that explains their being genuinely alike. Without such shared universals, the realist argues, the genuine similarity between two red things would be inexplicable — merely coincidental rather than grounded in anything real.

The formal argument for universals from similarity:

- **P1**: Two red roses are genuinely, objectively similar.
- **P2**: Genuine, objective similarity requires a ground — something in virtue of which the similar things are similar.
- **P3**: The only adequate ground is a common entity shared by both — a universal.
- **Conclusion**: Universals exist.

The nominalist denies P3. When we say two roses are red, we do not commit ourselves to the existence of any third entity called Redness; we simply note that both roses satisfy the predicate "is red." What makes the predicate applicable to both is not a shared property but some combination of resemblance among the roses themselves, the causal history of our predicate-forming practices, and the conventions of our language.

## The Many-Over-One Problem

Armstrong noted a complementary puzzle: the *many over one* problem. A single particular can instantiate multiple universals simultaneously — Socrates is both wise and snub-nosed. If universals are present in particulars, how can a single particular be the locus of many distinct universals without those universals being confused or crowded out? The realist's answer involves the ontological category of states of affairs: Socrates-being-wise and Socrates-being-snub-nosed are distinct states of affairs that share a common particular constituent.

## The Problem of Resemblance

A central difficulty for the nominalist is that resemblance itself seems to be a universal. Two red things resemble each other, and two blue things resemble each other — and the resemblances resemble each other. If resemblance is itself a property shared by the resemblance pairs, the nominalist has not eliminated universals but merely relocated the problem to the level of resemblance relations.

Bertrand Russell pressed this point against resemblance nominalism in *The Problems of Philosophy* (1912): we cannot avoid universals entirely, because the resemblance relation is itself a universal. Rodriguez-Pereyra's response is that resemblance nominalism takes resemblance as a primitive relation — the one universal admitted by the nominalist — and that this represents a substantial economy over realism's many universals.

## Laws of Nature and the One Over Many

This dispute has practical philosophical stakes. Laws of nature — that all copper conducts electricity, that all water is H₂O — seem to involve genuine regularities across many particular instances. If there are no universals, what grounds these regularities?

The realist says: the universal COPPER and the universal CONDUCTS-ELECTRICITY stand in a necessitation relation, and it is this relation between universals that explains why particular copper things conduct. Armstrong's account treats laws as higher-order states of affairs connecting universals: N(F, G) — the necessitation of G by F — is a real relation that holds between real universals.

The nominalist must find an alternative grounding — whether in resemblance, in natural classes, or in primitive facts about particular dispositions. Lewis's best-system analysis grounds laws in the best systematization of particular facts, using the notion of perfectly natural properties (which are either primitively natural or are sparse universals) to define simplicity. The tension between realist and nominalist accounts of laws is one of the most active areas of metaphysics of science.

## The One Over Many and Induction

Hume's problem of induction takes on new significance in this context. If two pieces of copper conduct electricity, what justifies projecting this to all copper? The Humean answer is that there is no rational justification — only habit. Armstrong's universalist answer is that the universal COPPER is necessarily connected to the universal CONDUCTS-ELECTRICITY by a necessitation relation: given this relation, induction is not mere habit but tracks a genuine necessitation. The realist about universals thus has resources for a solution to Hume's problem that the nominalist lacks.
