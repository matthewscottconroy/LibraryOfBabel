# Platonic Realism

Platonic realism about universals holds that properties, relations, and kinds exist as abstract entities entirely independent of whether any concrete thing instantiates them. The universal REDNESS exists even if every red thing were destroyed; the universal BEING LARGER THAN exists even if no spatial objects existed. These universals inhabit a realm of abstract being — eternal, unchanging, non-spatial — and concrete things relate to them through the participation or instantiation relation.

## Plato's Original Motivations

Plato's original motivation was epistemological as much as ontological. We can know mathematical truths with certainty and generality — the Pythagorean theorem holds for all right triangles, not just the ones we have examined. But if mathematical objects were material things, we could at best have probable beliefs about them. Plato concluded that mathematical objects (numbers, geometric forms) must be non-material and that our knowledge of them comes not from sensory experience but from intellectual contemplation of the Forms.

The Form of the triangle is not any particular drawn triangle; it is the ideal entity that all triangles imperfectly approximate. Sensory triangles are triangular in virtue of participating in the Form of Triangle — they share in its nature without being identical to it. This participation relation grounds the objective similarity among triangles and explains why geometry yields necessary truths: its subject matter is eternal, changeless Forms, not mutable particulars.

Plato's epistemology of Forms — the theory of *anamnesis* (recollection) — held that the soul has pre-natal knowledge of the Forms and recollects this knowledge through philosophical dialectic. Contemporary Platonists typically abandon this epistemological apparatus while retaining the ontological core: abstract objects are real, non-spatiotemporal entities that exist independently of minds and physical things.

## Contemporary Platonic Realism

Contemporary Platonic realists about properties do not necessarily adopt all of Plato's theory of recollection or his cosmology, but they share the core commitment: some abstract entities genuinely exist, and the existence of such entities is what explains the objective similarity structures we observe in the world. Bertrand Russell, in *The Problems of Philosophy* (1912), argued that universals are indispensable: without them, we cannot account for the truth of relational propositions (A is to the north of B) or the reliability of inductive inference.

Platonic realism is distinguished from Armstrong's immanent realism by its acceptance of uninstantiated universals. If nothing were red, the universal REDNESS would still exist — it would simply be uninstantiated. This liberalism about universals is both a strength and a weakness: it can accommodate mathematical universals (the number 7, the property of being prime) that have no physical instances, but it generates a vast ontology of potentially uninstantiated abstracta.

## The Third Man Argument

The main classical objection to Platonic realism is the *Third Man* argument, attributed to Parmenides in Plato's self-critical dialogue of that name. If Socrates is large because he participates in the Form LARGENESS, and the Form LARGENESS is itself large (being a paradigm case of largeness), then there must be a third large thing — a further Form — that explains the largeness of both Socrates and the first Form. This regress threatens to generate an infinity of Forms for every predicate.

The argument formally:

- **P1**: Socrates is large in virtue of participating in the Form LARGENESS.
- **P2**: The Form LARGENESS is itself large (it is a paradigm case).
- **P3**: Socrates and the Form are both large, so (by P1's reasoning) there must be a further Form LARGENESS* that they both participate in.
- **P4**: By the same reasoning, LARGENESS* generates LARGENESS**, and so on without end.
- **Conclusion**: The participation relation generates an infinite regress of Forms, which is absurd.

Contemporary defenders of abstract universals typically deny P2: universals do not themselves instantiate the properties they are — the universal REDNESS is not itself red. This "non-self-predication" assumption blocks the Third Man by denying that Forms must be paradigm instances of themselves.

## Benacerraf's Epistemological Challenge

Paul Benacerraf's "Mathematical Truth" (1973) poses a devastating challenge to Platonic realism in the philosophy of mathematics that applies equally to abstract universals generally. Benacerraf argued that Platonism faces a dilemma:

1. Our semantic theory tells us that mathematical truths are about abstract objects (numbers, sets).
2. Our epistemological theory tells us that we can only know about things with which we stand in causal relations.
3. Abstract objects are causally inert — they cannot be causes or effects.
4. Therefore, we cannot know mathematical truths (which are about causally inert objects).

This challenge has become the central epistemological problem for all Platonic positions. The Platonist must either (a) reject the causal theory of knowledge, (b) accept a mysterious form of non-causal knowledge of abstracta, or (c) show how we can have knowledge of abstracta through some indirect causal route.

Gödel famously accepted (b): mathematical intuition is a kind of perception of mathematical structures, analogous to sensory perception of physical objects. Frege similarly held that we grasp the truth of logical truths by reason alone, without causal contact. Contemporary Platonists like Balaguer ("full-blooded platonism") and Shapiro develop sophisticated responses, but the challenge remains one of the most powerful objections to Platonism about any type of abstract entity.

## Abundant Platonic Realism

Lewis adopted a form of Platonic realism for his abundant properties: every property (in the abundant sense) corresponds to a set of possible objects, and properties are just sets. This is deflationary Platonism — the abstracta posited are sets, which Lewis accepts as the price of modal realism. For Lewis, sets are abstract but relatively unproblematic as they are constructed from concrete objects at possible worlds.

For sparse properties, Lewis took a more agnostic stance: he thought the question of whether sparse properties are universals or simply the most natural classes was not one his system needed to settle. The distinction between Platonic universals and natural classes is, in Lewis's estimation, a further question beyond what his overall theory requires.
