# Nominalist Responses

Nominalism holds that only particular things exist — there are no universals, no abstract entities shared by multiple individuals. The challenge for nominalism is to explain how general predicates can be true of many things, how natural laws can be genuine rather than accidental, and how resemblance among particulars can be objective rather than arbitrary.

## Predicate Nominalism

Predicate nominalism is the simplest strategy: a thing is red just in case it falls under the predicate "is red." There is no further entity, the universal REDNESS, over and above the predicate and its extension. The predicate applies to the things it applies to, full stop. Critics charge that this is vacuous: it says nothing about what it is *in virtue of which* the predicate applies.

Armstrong's label for predicate nominalism is the "ostrich" view — it simply refuses to answer the question of what grounds predicate application by denying that any such question arises. The ostrich sticks its head in the sand rather than facing the metaphysical problem. Michael Devitt defends the ostrich strategy, arguing that the demand for a "ground" of predicate application is itself philosophically misguided — predication is a primitive fact that needs no further grounding.

The central objection: predicates are conventional and language-dependent, but similarities among things are not. Two roses are similar whether or not we have a predicate "red" to group them under. Predicate nominalism gets the order of explanation reversed: the predicate applies because the things are similar, not the reverse.

## Resemblance Nominalism

Resemblance nominalism, associated with H.H. Price (*Thinking and Experience*, 1953) and developed with great systematic thoroughness by Gonzalo Rodriguez-Pereyra (*Resemblance Nominalism*, 2002), holds that a thing is red if and only if it resembles paradigmatic red things in the relevant respects. There is no universal REDNESS, but there is a genuine objective resemblance relation holding among red things.

The theory identifies properties with resemblance classes of possible objects (to handle the extensionality problem: co-extensive properties must be distinct, which requires going beyond actual instances). The property of being red is the maximal resemblance class — the class of all possible red things, where membership is determined by resemblance to paradigm cases.

**The companionship problem**: If every red thing happens also to be round, then redness and roundness would have the same resemblance class. Rodriguez-Pereyra's response uses possible objects: there are possible red non-round things, so the resemblance class of red things and the resemblance class of round things differ in their possible membership.

**The imperfect community problem**: A group of things might each resemble some other member of the group without there being any one property they all share. Suppose A is red and square, B is red and round, C is round and blue. A resembles B (both red), B resembles C (both round), but A and C do not share a property. Rodriguez-Pereyra responds by requiring resemblance among all members of the class, not merely pairwise.

**The resemblance regress**: Russell argued that resemblance itself appears to be a universal — all resemblance relations resemble each other. Rodriguez-Pereyra accepts this: resemblance is primitive, and the resemblance nominalist must accept that resemblance is a genuine universal if anything is. This is an admission of a single universal, but it is a significant economy over full-blown realism's many universals.

## Class Nominalism

Class nominalism identifies properties with classes (sets) of their instances. To be red is just to be a member of the class {red things}. The difficulty is that any two things belong to some class together — there are infinitely many gerrymandered classes — so not every class corresponds to a genuine property. Distinguishing *natural* classes from arbitrary ones requires either appeal to objective resemblance (collapsing into resemblance nominalism) or appeal to primitive naturalness.

Lewis's treatment of natural properties represents a sophisticated development of class nominalism. On Lewis's account, properties are sets of possible objects (yielding the abundant properties). Among these sets, some are more natural than others: the set of all charged things is more natural than the set of all grue things. Lewis treats naturalness as a primitive — a matter of "carving nature at its joints" — which is not further analyzed in terms of resemblance or universals.

This is class nominalism supplemented by a primitive notion of naturalness. It handles the extensionality problem (co-extensive properties in the actual world are different sets of possible objects), but it does not fully explain *why* some classes are natural and others are not. The naturalness is a primitive feature of certain classes of possible objects.

## Trope Nominalism

Trope nominalism (discussed more fully elsewhere) replaces universals with individual property-instances. Each particular redness is a trope — a particular entity that resembles but is numerically distinct from every other redness-trope. The resemblance among tropes is primitive and objective; general terms group tropes into resemblance classes.

Trope nominalism avoids universals while positing something more than bare particulars. It accounts for objective similarity (tropes resembling each other) without positing a common universal. The trope theorist can say that two roses are both red because each has a redness-trope and these tropes exactly resemble each other.

## The Fundamental Contrast

The debate between realism and nominalism is ultimately about the structure of similarity in reality: is it grounded in genuine shared entities (universals) or explained away by facts about particular things and their relations?

The realist's advantage: universals provide a single, unified explanation of predication, similarity, and laws of nature. The nominalist's advantage: avoiding the ontological commitment to additional abstract or multiply-instantiable entities, which raises questions about how we can know them and how they relate to the physical world.

Neither side has definitively won the debate. Contemporary metaphysics contains sophisticated defenders of each position, and the choice between them often reflects broader commitments about the nature of ontology, the relationship between philosophy and science, and the epistemology of a priori and a posteriori knowledge.
