# Immanent Realism

David Armstrong's universals are *immanent* rather than transcendent: they do not exist in a Platonic realm apart from the world but are fully present in the concrete particulars that instantiate them. The universal REDNESS does not exist if nothing is red; universals are ontologically dependent on their instances. This distinguishes Armstrong's naturalistic realism from Platonic realism while retaining the core commitment that universals are genuine entities rather than constructions from particulars.

## The Naturalistic Motivation

Armstrong's immanent realism is driven by a commitment to naturalism — the view that the natural world, as investigated by science, is all there is. A Platonic realm of timeless, non-spatial universals existing independently of all concrete things seems to violate naturalism: it posits entities beyond the causal order, inaccessible to empirical inquiry. Immanent realism avoids this by insisting that universals exist only where instantiated, making them part of the concrete, spatiotemporal world.

The principle of instantiation: every genuine universal must be instantiated somewhere in the actual world. There are no uninstantiated universals. This principle is not merely a matter of parsimony; Armstrong takes it to be a metaphysical truth about the nature of universals. A universal that is not instantiated by any particular is not merely absent from the world; it is not a genuine universal at all.

This has important consequences. Mathematical universals — if there are such things — cannot be Armstrongian universals, since many mathematical properties (being the largest prime, being a perfect set) have no physical instances. Armstrong's naturalistic program cannot easily accommodate the full range of mathematical objects, which is one reason he does not endorse a Platonic realism that would easily handle mathematics.

## The Argument for Immanent Universals

Armstrong's central argument for universals comes from the theory of laws of nature. He argues that laws of nature cannot be merely accidental regularities: that all copper conducts electricity is not just a brute coincidence involving every copper thing, but a genuine necessitation. On his view, laws are relations of necessitation between universals: the universal COPPER stands in a necessitation relation N to the universal CONDUCTS-ELECTRICITY, and it is this higher-order fact that makes it a law.

The argument:

- **P1**: Laws of nature are not mere regularities; they are genuine necessitations.
- **P2**: The only adequate grounding for necessitation among particulars is a relation between universals.
- **P3**: Such universals must be immanent — present in the particulars — to have their necessitating effect.
- **Conclusion**: Immanent universals exist and ground the necessity of laws.

The Humean alternative — that laws are just regularities without any further grounding — cannot explain why we are entitled to project past regularities into the future. Armstrong's view provides a ground for that projection: because the universals COPPER and CONDUCTS-ELECTRICITY stand in a necessitation relation, any new copper thing will conduct.

## Sparse Universals and Scientific Discovery

Armstrong also argues for *sparse* universals — only some of the concepts we use correspond to genuine universals. The natural science project of identifying the fundamental physical properties is, on his view, the project of discovering which universals there actually are. Disjunctive properties (being either gold or round), negative properties (not being red), and highly determinable properties (being colored) are not genuine universals. Only *perfectly natural* properties — those that figure in the fundamental laws of physics — are genuine universals.

This sparse view allies Armstrong with Lewis's notion of *naturalness*: some properties carve nature at its joints and others do not. The distinction between natural and unnatural properties is not a distinction in the concepts we employ but in the objective structure of reality. Science, Armstrong holds, is in the business of discovering this objective structure.

One consequence is that the ontology of properties is partly an empirical matter. What the fundamental universals are depends on what physics tells us about the fundamental properties of matter and energy. Philosophy cannot determine a priori whether there is a universal of being-red or being-electron; these are questions for empirical inquiry.

## The Instantiation Problem

A challenge for Armstrong is to explain the instantiation relation: in what does the relation between a particular and its universal consist? If instantiation is a genuine relation, it seems to require a further explanation of how the particular is related to the instantiation relation itself — generating a Bradley regress.

Armstrong invokes the notion of a *state of affairs* — Socrates-being-wise — as an irreducible entity. The instantiation relation is internal to the state of affairs; it is not a further universal that connects particulars to universals. States of affairs are primitive ontological units that have both the particular and the universal as constituents, but the having of the constituents is not a further relation requiring grounding.

Critics argue that this response is merely terminological: calling instantiation a "tie" rather than a "relation" does not explain how the particular and universal are related; it just labels the mystery. Armstrong's response is that no explanation is required here — some primitive ontological connections are simply fundamental and do not need further grounding. The alternative — requiring that every connection be explained by a further connection — leads to an infinite regress that no ontological theory can avoid.

## Immanent Realism vs. Trope Theory

Trope theory offers a nominalist-friendly alternative to Armstrong's immanent realism that preserves some of its advantages. Where Armstrong holds that two red roses share a numerically identical universal REDNESS, the trope theorist holds that each rose has its own particular redness-trope — numerically distinct but exactly resembling. Both views locate properties in the concrete world rather than in a Platonic realm; both accept that properties are sparse rather than abundant.

Armstrong's objection to trope theory is that it cannot explain natural laws as straightforwardly as the universals account. If laws are relations between universals (N(F, G)), the universalist has a uniform explanation of why all Fs are G. The trope theorist must either accept laws as brute regularities (which Armstrong finds unsatisfying) or posit a different account of laws in terms of relations among particular tropes — which faces its own difficulties.
