# States of Affairs

Armstrong's mature ontology takes states of affairs — also called *facts* — as the fundamental constituents of reality. A state of affairs is a particular's instantiating a universal: Socrates-being-wise, this-electron-having-negative-charge, these-two-objects-being-5-cm-apart. States of affairs are not merely the conjunction of a particular and a universal; they are sui generis entities with both as constituents.

## The Motivation for States of Affairs

The need for states of affairs beyond particulars and universals is driven by what Armstrong calls the *truthmaker argument*. The truth of "Socrates is wise" requires something in the world that *makes* it true — not just Socrates (he might not be wise) and not just wisdom (it might be uninstantiated), but the obtaining of the state of affairs of Socrates-being-wise. Particulars and universals, taken separately, cannot serve as truthmakers for predications.

The argument from truthmaking:

- **P1**: Every contingently true proposition has a truthmaker — something in the world whose existence necessitates the proposition's truth.
- **P2**: The proposition "Socrates is wise" is made true neither by Socrates alone nor by wisdom alone, since Socrates exists without being wise, and wisdom exists without Socrates instantiating it.
- **P3**: Therefore, there is a further entity — the state of affairs of Socrates's being wise — that is the truthmaker.
- **Conclusion**: States of affairs are genuine constituents of reality.

This argument makes states of affairs ontologically indispensable: they are what we must posit to explain the truth of atomic predications. Particulars and universals are constituents of states of affairs but are not themselves sufficient to ground truth.

## The Combinatorial Picture

States of affairs also provide the basis for Armstrong's combinatorial theory of possibility. Possible worlds, on Armstrong's account, are recombinations of actual particulars and universals into novel states of affairs. Any combination of actual particulars with actual universals generates a genuine possible world (subject to structural constraints). This avoids Lewis's modal realism — the commitment to infinitely many concrete possible worlds — while still providing a basis for modal claims.

The possible but non-actual state of affairs of Socrates-being-snub-nosed-but-not-wise does not obtain in the actual world, but the constituents (Socrates, wisdom, snub-nosedness) are all actual. The possible world where Socrates is unwise is just a recombination of actual ingredients.

## The Non-Mereological Unity of States of Affairs

A crucial feature of Armstrong's states of affairs is that they are not mere conjunctions or mereological fusions of their constituents. Socrates + wisdom is not the same as the state of affairs of Socrates-being-wise — the same constituents can be arranged in different ways (or fail to be arranged so as to form a state of affairs at all).

This non-mereological unity is both a strength and a challenge. It is a strength because it explains why "Socrates is wise" and "wisdom is instantiated by Socrates" are not mere recombinations of the same elements — the state of affairs has a structure that mere sums lack. It is a challenge because the notion of a "structured whole that is not merely the sum of its parts" is hard to make precise. Critics argue that Armstrong is positing a mysterious entity — a fact-blob — to do ontological work without adequate explanation.

## Wittgenstein and Facts

Wittgenstein's *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* (1921) similarly posited facts (*Tatsachen*) as the fundamental constituents of the world, with the world being "the totality of facts, not of things." The atomic facts of the Tractatus are structurally similar to Armstrong's states of affairs: they consist of objects combined in a certain configuration. Armstrong develops this insight within a naturalistic framework, connecting it to the theory of laws and to the program of scientific realism.

The key difference is that Wittgenstein's objects are simple and necessarily configure into all possible atomic facts (any object can be configured with any other), while Armstrong's particulars and universals have their own natures that constrain which states of affairs are possible. Armstrong's ontology is more richly structured and empirically sensitive than the Tractatus picture.

## Higher-Order States of Affairs and Laws

States of affairs also play the role of the fundamental units for laws of nature. The law that copper conducts electricity is the state of affairs N(COPPER, CONDUCTS-ELECTRICITY) — the universal of necessitation relating the universal copper to the universal of electrical conduction. The particular states of affairs involving individual copper wires (this-copper-wire-conducting-electricity) are grounded in this higher-order state of affairs.

This generates a hierarchy:
- First-order states of affairs: particular instantiating first-order universal.
- Second-order states of affairs: universals standing in relations to universals (laws).
- Higher-order states of affairs: relations among second-order relations (meta-laws?).

Critics worry that this hierarchy multiplies entities uncomfortably: we now have particulars, universals, states of affairs, *and* higher-order states of affairs. The ontological cost seems high. Defenders argue that the theoretical work done by states of affairs — grounding truth, explaining laws, accommodating the non-mereological unity of facts — justifies the expense. Without states of affairs, many apparently simple features of the world become inexplicable.

## The Connection to Truthmaker Theory

Armstrong was one of the most prominent defenders of truthmaker theory — the view that every true proposition has a truthmaker. His states of affairs are the truthmakers for atomic predications. This has become a significant part of the contemporary metaphysical landscape: truthmaker theory motivates the distinction between real and apparent ontological commitments, since only those things that serve as truthmakers need to be posited.

Negative truths ("Socrates is not flying") are a particular challenge: what is the truthmaker for a negative predication? Armstrong posited a "totality fact" — the fact that the list of all positive states of affairs is complete — to serve as the truthmaker for negative truths. This is controversial but reflects the systematic ambition of the states of affairs ontology.
