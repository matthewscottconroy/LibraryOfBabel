# Laws of Nature and Universals

One of the most powerful arguments for universals is its role in explaining the necessity of natural laws. Consider: all copper conducts electricity. Is this a mere coincidence — it just so happens that every piece of copper we encounter conducts — or is there something that *makes* it true, that grounds the necessity of the generalization?

## The Problem with Regularities

The Humean view says there is no further grounding: laws are just regularities, exceptionless patterns in the occurrent facts. This *regularity theory* has appealing parsimony, but it faces challenges. It cannot explain why we are rationally entitled to extrapolate observed regularities to unobserved cases, since on the regularity view, the fact that all observed copper has conducted gives us no guarantee about the next piece of copper. It also seems to make it a matter of luck that the patterns are uniform — and luck seems like the wrong explanation for fundamental physical structure.

Further, the regularity view cannot distinguish between laws and accidentally true generalizations. The generalization "all gold spheres have diameter less than one mile" may be accidentally true (no one has assembled enough gold). The generalization "all uranium spheres have diameter less than one mile" may be a law (critical mass prevents large uranium spheres). Both are exceptionless regularities; the regularity view cannot explain why one is a law and the other is not.

## The Necessitation Account

Armstrong, Fred Dretske ("Laws of Nature," 1977), and Michael Tooley ("The Nature of Laws," 1977) independently developed the *necessitation* view: laws of nature are relations of necessitation between universals. If it is a law that copper conducts, there is a relation N(COPPER, CONDUCTS) — a higher-order state of affairs — that metaphysically necessitates that every particular copper thing conducts.

The formal statement: N(F, G) — read as "F necessitates G" — is a relation between the universal F and the universal G. When N(F, G) obtains, it is a law that all Fs are G. The necessity flows from the relation between universals; particular instances of the law are grounded in this higher-order fact.

The argument for necessitation:

- **P1**: Laws of nature are not merely accidentally true generalizations — they support counterfactuals and license inductive inference.
- **P2**: Only genuine necessitations between universals can ground these features of laws.
- **P3**: Such necessitations are higher-order states of affairs relating universals.
- **Conclusion**: Laws of nature are relations of necessitation between universals.

This view captures the intuition that laws are necessary (not merely accidentally true) without claiming they are logically necessary (one can coherently describe a world without them). The necessity is *nomological*: it holds in virtue of the laws, not independently of them.

## Van Fraassen's Objection

Bas van Fraassen, in *Laws and Symmetry* (1989), posed the most influential objection to the necessitation view. He asked: what exactly is this "necessitation" relation N, and how does its holding between two universals guarantee that particular things will behave in the law-governed way?

The regress argument: if N(COPPER, CONDUCTS) holds, what makes it the case that every copper thing conducts? Armstrong says: the holding of N(COPPER, CONDUCTS) guarantees this by necessitation. But then what is the law connecting N(COPPER, CONDUCTS) to the behavior of particular copper things? If there is a further law, we face a regress. If not, we have a brute necessitation that is no better explained than the original regularity.

Armstrong's response: N is a primitive necessitation relation whose nature just is to guarantee the relevant particular-level regularities. There is no further law connecting N to particular instances — N's necessitating role is constitutive of what N is. This response is not universally found convincing: it seems to attribute to universals and their relations a kind of governance over particulars that is difficult to understand.

## Lewis's Best-System Analysis

Lewis's alternative — *best-system* regularism — holds that laws are those regularities that feature in the best systematic summary of the world's history (the system that best balances strength and simplicity). This requires neither universals nor necessitation but depends on a primitive notion of naturalness to identify which regularities count.

The best-system analysis defines laws functionally: laws are whatever appears as axioms or theorems in the system that best systematizes the Humean mosaic of particular categorical facts. Different world histories will have different best systems. The analysis is anti-necessitarian: laws are not necessary, and there are no necessary connections between distinct categorical properties.

Lewis argued that the best-system analysis captures everything we need from laws — they support counterfactuals (in the sense of being stable across a wide range of counterfactual suppositions), they are projectible (their truth is not sensitive to which cases we have observed), and they have predictive value. The necessitarian picture adds modal force that Lewis found mysterious and unmotivated.

## The Dispositionalist Alternative

A third approach, associated with scientific essentialism (Brian Ellis, Alexander Bird), holds that laws flow from property natures. If properties are essentially dispositional — if it is part of what it is to be charge that charge attracts opposite charges — then the laws are simply expressions of what the properties are. N(F, G) is not a contingent relation between distinct universals but a necessary consequence of F's essential nature.

This dispositionalist account makes laws necessarily true (given the properties involved) and explains natural necessity without Armstrong's primitive necessitation relation. It is Armstrong's account without the contingency of the N relation — the law is internal to the property rather than an external necessity holding between properties.

The debate between Armstrong's contingent necessitation, Lewis's best-system regularism, and Bird's dispositionalist essentialism represents one of the most active and substantive disputes in contemporary philosophy of science, with each position offering distinct explanations of the modal profile, the projectibility, and the epistemology of natural laws.
