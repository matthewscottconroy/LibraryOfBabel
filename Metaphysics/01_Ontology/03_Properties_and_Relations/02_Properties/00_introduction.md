# Properties

Properties are what predicates express and what grounds the truth of predications — but what are they, more precisely? The first-order question (do properties exist?) gives way to second-order questions about their inner nature, their identity conditions, and their relationships to the things that have them. Even among those who accept properties as genuine ontological items, there is deep disagreement about what kind of items they are.

One central dispute concerns the relationship between properties and their causal roles. On a categoricalist view, properties are intrinsically inert qualitative features; their causal powers are bestowed upon them by the laws of nature, which are external to the properties themselves. On a dispositionalist view, properties are essentially dispositional: to be negatively charged just is to be disposed to behave in certain ways toward other charged objects. A third view, "dual aspect" or "two-sided" theory, holds that properties are both qualitative and dispositional in a way that cannot be analyzed into separate components.

The categoricalism versus dispositionalism debate is not merely semantic. If properties have their causal powers essentially — if there is no possible world where negative charge exists but does not repel positive charge — then the laws of nature are metaphysically necessary rather than contingent. This would mean that when we imagine possible worlds with different laws, we are imagining worlds with different properties, not worlds where the same properties behave differently. The implications for modal metaphysics, the epistemology of laws, and the analysis of counterfactuals are substantial.

There is also the question of property identity. When are two predicates — "is equilateral" and "is equiangular" — predicates for the same property, and when are they predicates for different properties that happen to be coextensive? Some identify properties by their extensions (the set of things that have them); others identify them by their intensions (their modal profiles); still others identify them by their causal-nomological roles. Each approach has distinctive consequences for questions about reduction, emergence, and the metaphysics of natural kinds.

This section examines these questions systematically, providing the conceptual framework needed to evaluate the specific theories of universals, tropes, and relations that surround it.
