# Defining Intrinsicality

*What it means for a property to be intrinsic — held independently of what else exists.*

---

An intrinsic property is one that an object has in virtue of what it is in itself, independently of what other things exist or how it is related to them. Mass, charge, and spin are candidates for intrinsic properties of physical particles. An extrinsic property is one that depends on the object's relations to other things: being five miles from the Eiffel Tower, being the youngest sibling, being admired by philosophers.

Intrinsicality matters philosophically because it marks a distinction between what an object genuinely *is* in itself and what it is merely *in relation to* its surroundings. Many philosophical disputes turn on this distinction. Personal identity seems to depend on intrinsic properties of the person (psychological or physical constitution) rather than merely relational ones. Genuine causation seems to work through intrinsic properties rather than merely extrinsic ones. Objective value, some argue, must be intrinsic rather than relational.

Attempts to define intrinsicality precisely have proven difficult. The most common approach, developed by David Lewis and elaborated by Brian Weatherson, defines intrinsic properties as those shared by any two objects that are perfect duplicates (where duplicates are objects that are alike in all their most fundamental qualitative properties). On this view, P is intrinsic if and only if any duplicate of something that has P also has P. This is elegant but circular if we cannot independently identify the fundamental properties.

Rae Langton and David Lewis proposed a different approach: intrinsic properties are those that can be held by a lonely object — an object that exists in a world with no other contingent beings. The property of *being a sibling* requires the existence of another person, so it is extrinsic; the property of *being spherical* does not, so it is intrinsic. This captures many intuitive cases but generates puzzles: is *being self-identical* intrinsic (every lonely object has it) or trivial?

Intrinsicality also connects to the notion of *naturalness*: intrinsic properties tend to be the perfectly natural ones — those that figure in fundamental physics. But perfectly natural properties might still be relational (the two-electron distance relation), and some candidate intrinsic properties (phenomenal qualities, perhaps) may not be fundamental physical properties.

## The Duplication Account

The most influential modern analysis of intrinsicality proceeds via the concept of *perfect duplication*. Two objects are perfect duplicates if they share all their intrinsic properties. But this risks circularity unless we can independently characterize what it is for properties to be intrinsic. Lewis's strategy was to ground the account in the notion of *perfectly natural* properties: the intrinsic properties are those that supervene on the perfectly natural properties. Since perfectly natural properties are the ones figuring in the most fundamental physical laws (mass, charge, spin), we get an account of intrinsicality in terms that do not themselves presuppose the intrinsic/extrinsic distinction.

The duplication approach entails that:

- P1: Property F is intrinsic iff for any possible objects a and b that are duplicates, a has F iff b has F.
- P2: Objects a and b are duplicates iff they have all the same perfectly natural properties and stand in all the same perfectly natural relations to their own parts.
- Conclusion: Intrinsic properties are those that cannot differ between duplicates — their holding is entirely settled by the natural properties of the object itself.

## Objections and Responses

**The impure intrinsic objection**: Some properties are intuitively intrinsic but involve reference to external objects. The property of *being the same height as the Eiffel Tower* is extrinsic; but the property of *being 324 meters tall* seems intrinsic. Yet the number 324 is an abstract object external to the building. The natural response is that what matters is whether the property requires *causal* or *spatiotemporal* connection to other concrete particulars — purely abstract dependencies do not render a property extrinsic.

**The loneliness criterion problem**: Langton and Lewis's "lone object" criterion holds that P is intrinsic iff every lonely object has P iff it has P when accompanied. But *being alone in the universe* is then intrinsic by this criterion — yet it is paradigmatically relational. Defenders add that we must restrict to *qualitative* properties (those not defined in terms of specific other objects) and to *non-lonely* variants.

**Border-crossing cases**: Shoemaker's causal structuralism complicates the picture. If intrinsic properties are constituted by their causal powers — as the dispositionalist argues — then they are inherently relational in a certain sense, since powers are essentially directed toward possible relations to other objects. Dispositionalists typically respond that this directedness does not require the *actual* existence of the manifesting conditions, so powers can still be intrinsic in the sense of not requiring actual external relata.

## Applications

The intrinsic/extrinsic distinction bears on:

- **Personal identity**: Psychological continuity theorists hold that what makes a person persist is intrinsic psychological continuity; four-dimensionalists appeal to intrinsic temporal parts.
- **Truthmakers**: Intrinsic properties are natural candidates for truthmakers — what makes "a is F" true is a's having the intrinsic property F.
- **Causation**: Many theories hold that objects enter into causal relations in virtue of their intrinsic properties; extrinsic properties are causally irrelevant.
- **Naturalness**: Lewis held that the perfectly natural properties are all intrinsic — they are the properties figuring in the fundamental laws, and laws govern things in virtue of what they are in themselves, not in virtue of their relational context.
