# Essential vs. Accidental Properties

*The modal distinction between properties a thing must have and properties it merely happens to have.*

---

A property is *essential* to an object if the object could not exist without having it; a property is *accidental* if the object has it but could exist without it. Socrates is essentially human (he could not exist and fail to be human) but accidentally snub-nosed (he could have existed with a straight nose). This modal distinction — between what is necessary and what is merely contingent — marks a deep division in an object's properties.

The notion of essential property has an ancient lineage in Aristotle's account of essence and definition, but it was given rigorous contemporary treatment by Saul Kripke in *Naming and Necessity*. Kripke argued against the then-dominant descriptivist view that proper names abbreviate descriptions: "Aristotle" does not mean "the greatest philosopher of antiquity," because Aristotle might not have been a philosopher at all. Instead, names rigidly designate their bearers — they pick out the same individual in every possible world where that individual exists.

This rigid designation thesis has implications for essential properties. If a name rigidly designates an individual, then the individual's necessary properties are those that it has in every possible world in which it exists. Kripke argued that some seemingly contingent-seeming properties are in fact necessary: Aristotle is necessarily the son of his parents (he could not have had different parents and still been *Aristotle*), and water is necessarily H₂O. These are necessary *a posteriori* truths — not knowable by pure reason but necessary nonetheless.

Kit Fine challenged the purely modal analysis of essence. He argues that not all necessary properties are essential: the proposition "Socrates is such that {Socrates} exists" is necessarily true (necessarily, if Socrates exists, so does his singleton set), but this is not part of *Socrates's essence* — it does not reveal what Socrates is. Essential properties are those that belong to an object *in virtue of its nature*, not merely those that it has necessarily. This distinction between essence and necessity requires primitive notions of essence beyond modal analysis.

Contemporary neo-Aristotelians have built extensively on Fine's insight, developing a theory of real definitions (definitions that reveal natures) and grounding — the relation by which fundamental facts explain derivative ones.

## The Traditional Aristotelian Framework

For Aristotle, essence is articulated through *real definition* — the kind of definition that reveals what a thing essentially is, as opposed to merely giving nominal conditions for applying a term. The real definition of human is *rational animal*: it gives the genus (animal) and the differentia (rational) that jointly characterize what it is to be a human. Properties that follow from the real definition are *propria* — necessary but not essential in the strict sense; they flow from the essence but are not constitutive of it. Properties that belong to a thing but could be otherwise are accidents.

This framework was adapted in medieval philosophy into the distinction between *essential* and *accidental* forms: substantial form gives a thing its essential character; accidental forms add further determinations that could be otherwise. The scholastic debate about prime matter and substantial form is partly a debate about what the essential nature of a substance consists in.

## Kripke on Origin Essentialism

One of Kripke's most influential and controversial theses is *origin essentialism*: an organism essentially has the origin it actually has. Aristotle could not have had parents other than his actual parents. This wooden table could not have been made from a different piece of wood. The argument appeals to the following intuition: if we imagine a "different" Aristotle, born of different parents, raised in different circumstances, we are not imagining Aristotle himself but a different individual who happens to resemble him.

More formally:
- P1: If an individual a has property P essentially, then in every possible world in which a exists, a has P.
- P2: No individual could have originated from a fundamentally different origin (material, parental, etc.) while being the same individual.
- Conclusion: An individual's origin is among its essential properties.

Critics (Salmon, Robertson) have questioned whether origin essentialism is genuinely supported by modal intuitions or whether it is an artifact of our descriptions of possible scenarios.

## Fine's Real-Definitional Essence

Kit Fine's critique of modal essentialism in "Essence and Modality" (1994) distinguishes between:

1. **Modal essence**: a property P is modally essential to a just in case, necessarily, if a exists then a has P.
2. **Real essence**: a property P is really essential to a just in case P belongs to a *in virtue of a's nature* — P is part of what a is.

Fine argues that modal essence is too permissive. The proposition that Socrates is a member of his singleton set {Socrates} is necessarily true — in every world where Socrates exists, so does {Socrates}. Yet this is not part of Socrates's nature; it is rather a consequence of the nature of {Socrates} (the set essentially has Socrates as a member). The direction of explanation runs from the set's essence to the modal truth, not from Socrates's essence.

This means essential properties are *constitutive of natures*, not merely *modally necessary*. Real definitions — statements of essence — are not just statements of necessary truth but statements that reveal what it is to be the entity in question.

## Applications and Connections

The essential/accidental distinction ramifies throughout metaphysics:

- **Natural kinds**: The essential properties of water are its microstructural features (H₂O). Accidental properties (being in a glass, being cold) are features water happens to have without being constitutive of being water.
- **Persons**: Personal identity debates concern which properties are essential to persons. Must a person have the same body? The same memories? The same psychological character? Different answers reflect different views about what kind of thing a person essentially is.
- **Artifacts**: Whether artifacts have essential properties is contested. Is the function of a knife essential to it? Could the same knife have been used as a paperweight from the beginning?
- **Mathematical objects**: Mathematical objects seem to have all their mathematical properties essentially — the number 7 could not fail to be prime, or odd. This may support the view that mathematical objects are not fully analogous to contingent concrete particulars.
