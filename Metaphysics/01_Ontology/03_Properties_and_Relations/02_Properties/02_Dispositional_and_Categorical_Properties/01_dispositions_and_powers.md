# Dispositions and Powers

*Properties that consist in a tendency or capacity to bring about effects under appropriate circumstances.*

---

A disposition is a property that consists in a *tendency* or *liability* to behave in certain ways under certain conditions. Fragility is the disposition to break when struck; solubility is the disposition to dissolve in liquid; charge is the disposition to attract and repel other charged objects. Dispositions are distinguished from their *manifestations* — the actual breaking, dissolving, or attracting — which may or may not occur depending on whether the triggering conditions are present.

The philosophy of dispositions has become one of the most active areas of contemporary metaphysics, partly because dispositions seem to be the natural language of causal powers, and causal powers seem to be central to both scientific explanation and ordinary causal talk. If the fundamental properties of physics are dispositional — mass is the disposition to resist acceleration, charge is the disposition to interact electromagnetically — then dispositions are not merely a derivative or secondary category but are ontologically basic.

The traditional empiricist treatment of dispositions (associated with Ryle, Carnap, and early Quine) was *reductionist*: to say a glass is fragile is not to posit a further categorical property called fragility but merely to commit to a conditional — *if* the glass were struck, *then* it would break. This conditional analysis was shown to be inadequate by C. B. Martin's finkish disposition counterexample: a wire that has the disposition to conduct (categorical basis present) but where a sorcerer would remove the disposition the moment the wire were connected to a current. The wire is disposed to conduct even though the conditional "if connected, then would conduct" is false.

Martin's response, developed also by George Molnar and Brian Ellis, is to treat powers as *irreducibly real*: they are genuine properties that point toward their manifestations without those manifestations actually occurring. This teleological feature — dispositions pointing toward a future manifestation — is distinctive and cannot be captured in purely categorical terms.

The debate between *categorical* and *dispositional* accounts of properties is connected to fundamental questions about causation, necessity, and the nature of laws of nature. If properties are essentially dispositional, laws of nature flow from the intrinsic natures of the properties themselves — they are not externally imposed regularities but expressions of what the properties essentially are.

## The Conditional Analysis and Its Failure

The simple conditional analysis of dispositions holds:

> *x is fragile* iff *if x were struck with sufficient force, x would break*

This is the *subjunctive conditional* analysis, and it looks plausible for ordinary cases. However, Martin's counterexamples identify a class of dispositions that defeat it:

**Finkish dispositions**: A wire is disposed to conduct electricity (has the categorical basis for conduction). But a malevolent sorcerer has set things up so that, were the wire connected to a circuit, the sorcerer would immediately remove the disposition. In this case, the simple conditional is false (the wire would not conduct), yet the wire has the disposition. So dispositions cannot be identified with conditionals.

**Antidotes**: A fragile vase is fragile — it has the structural basis for breaking when struck. But a protecting coating has been applied that would neutralize any blow before it could break the vase. Again, the conditional fails, but the disposition remains.

**Mimickers**: A vase lacks the structural basis for fragility, but a protective coating would shatter before the vase did in any impact scenario. The conditional is true, but the disposition is absent.

These counterexamples show that dispositions and the conditionals that govern their manifestations come apart, supporting the view that dispositions are genuine ontological posits rather than mere shorthand for conditionals.

## The Directedness of Powers

George Molnar, in *Powers: A Study in Metaphysics* (2003), identified what he called *physical intentionality* as the distinctive feature of powers. Just as mental states are *about* their intentional objects without those objects needing to exist (one can believe in unicorns), powers are *directed toward* their manifestations without those manifestations needing to occur. A charged particle is directed toward electromagnetic interaction even in a world with no other charges; its power is pointed toward an absent manifestation.

This marks a radical departure from Humean ontology, according to which there are only *occurrent categorical* facts, and any seeming "reaching forward" to future states is merely a projection. For Molnar, pointedness or directedness toward manifestations is an irreducible feature of physical reality.

Mumford and Anjum (*Getting Causes from Powers*, 2011) develop a full causal ontology from powers. On their view:
- Causation is the *mutual manifestation* of complementary powers.
- The causal relation is not a contingent correlation between distinct events but an intrinsic, necessary connection between powers and their manifestations.
- Laws of nature are not external constraints on powers but descriptions of how powers manifest when they interact.

## Shoemaker's Causal Structuralism

Sydney Shoemaker argued that properties *just are* clusters of causal powers: to be a property of any kind just is to have a certain set of causal potentialities. The property of being charged is constituted by its powers to attract opposite charges, repel like charges, and interact with magnetic fields. If we stripped away all these causal powers, there would be nothing left — no remaining intrinsic categorical nature.

This view entails that the identity conditions of properties are given by their causal roles: two properties are identical iff they confer exactly the same causal powers in all possible circumstances. This resolves the Swapping problem (could two properties swap their causal roles while remaining the same properties?) in the negative: the causal role is constitutive of the property, not an accompaniment to it.

Shoemaker's view is an ancestor of the contemporary debates between *pure dispositionalism* (all properties are essentially dispositional), *pure categoricalism* (all properties are essentially categorical, with dispositions being contingent additions), and *dual aspect* (property-dualism) views that take properties to be simultaneously categorical and dispositional.
