# Categorical Properties

*Properties that are what they are independently of their causal or dispositional role.*

---

Categorical properties are properties that are fully characterized by what they are in themselves, without reference to how they would behave or what effects they would produce. On the categorical view, a fundamental property like *being charged* is some specific intrinsic quality — something that is simply *there*, present in the particle — and the particle's attraction and repulsion behavior is a further, contingent fact that happens to go along with being charged in the actual world. In a different world with different laws, particles with the same categorical property might behave differently.

David Lewis and David Armstrong both defended categorical conceptions of fundamental properties, though for different reasons. Lewis wanted to ground all facts about modality, laws, and causation in categorical facts about the actual world — his *Humean supervenience* thesis. If properties were essentially dispositional (essentially pointed toward manifestations in other possible worlds), then the truth about the actual world would depend on other possible worlds, threatening Humean supervenience. Categorical properties, by contrast, are fully present and characterized independently of modal facts.

Armstrong holds that properties are categorical but that laws connect them contingently: being copper does not by itself necessitate conducting electricity; it is the contingent law N(COPPER, CONDUCTS) that brings about the connection. Properties are categorical; their nomological relations are contingent facts.

This picture has a peculiar consequence: two possible worlds might have the same arrangement of categorical properties but different laws, making the actual world's laws look like brute, unexplained regularities. Critics press this as a cost: if the same categorical properties can play different causal roles in different worlds, then the actual world's laws seem arbitrary and unexplained — there is no answer to why copper conducts rather than insulates.

Dispositional monists (Ellis, Bird, Mumford) use this cost to motivate their alternative: fundamental properties are essentially dispositional, making laws of nature necessary rather than contingent. The debate between categorical and dispositional accounts is thus connected to deep questions about explanation, contingency, and the nature of necessity in science.

## The Case for Categorical Properties

The strongest philosophical argument for categorical properties comes from Lewis's account of modality. Lewis held that possible worlds are concrete realities, not abstract representations. What makes it possible for gold to conduct electricity differently is that in some other possible world, the arrangement of categorical properties is different — things with the categorical property we call "gold" behave differently there. The content of our modal claims is given by cross-world comparisons of categorical arrangements.

If properties were essentially dispositional — essentially pointing toward their manifestations in all possible worlds — then there would be no freedom in how things could be across worlds. The dispositional essence of charge would fix how charged things behave in every possible world, leaving no room for genuine modal variation in the laws. Lewis found this too restrictive: we should be able to imagine worlds where the same physical stuff behaves very differently.

On Lewis's view, fundamental properties are *quiddities* — categorical natures that are prior to any causal or nomic role. Two worlds could contain the same quiddities arranged differently, or different quiddities playing the same nomic roles. This generates the *quidditism* that many philosophers have found uncomfortable: there would be worlds that differ only by a "swapping" of quiddities with no observable difference, which seems like a distinction without a difference.

## Armstrong's Contingent Nomic Relations

Armstrong's variant of categorical realism holds that:

1. Properties (universals) are categorical — they are what they are independently of any law.
2. Laws of nature are second-order states of affairs: *N(F, G)* — the relation of *nomic necessitation* holding between the universals F and G.
3. This law-state necessitates (in some weaker sense) that all F-instances are G-instances, grounding the regularity.

The challenge is to characterize the necessity involved in *N(F, G)*: it must be weaker than logical necessity (since the law could have been otherwise) but stronger than mere correlation (since it is a genuine law, not a coincidence). Armstrong calls this *contingent necessitation* — a primitive relation among universals that is neither logical nor purely statistical.

Critics (van Fraassen, Lewis) pressed the "inference problem": how does the higher-order fact *N(F, G)* necessitate the lower-order regularity that all Fs are Gs? If the connection is contingent, we seem to need another law to connect the law-state to the regularity, generating a regress.

## The Quidditism Problem

If properties are categorical, then their intrinsic nature is fixed independently of any causal or nomic role. This means we can coherently describe *inverted qualia* for properties: a world where what we call "negative charge" plays the role we associate with "positive charge" in the actual world, and vice versa. All the laws are the same, all observations are the same, but the underlying categorical properties are swapped.

This *quidditism* about properties mirrors the haecceitism debate about objects. Many philosophers find it metaphysically uncomfortable: if two worlds are observationally and nomic equivalent but differ only in which property-quiddity plays which role, it is not clear that these are genuinely different worlds rather than different descriptions of the same world.

Black's and Holman's criticisms of quidditism have pushed some categorical realists toward *structuralist* or *functionalist* accounts of properties, in which what individuates a property is its nomic or structural role rather than a bare qualitative nature.
