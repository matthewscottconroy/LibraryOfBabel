# The Nature of Powers

*Contemporary debates about causal powers as fundamental features of reality.*

---

The revival of causal powers as a central metaphysical category is one of the most significant developments in late twentieth and early twenty-first century metaphysics. Where Humean and Quinean-influenced analytic philosophy had been suspicious of powers — treating them as remnants of scholastic obscurantism — philosophers like George Molnar, Brian Ellis, Stephen Mumford, and Ruth Porter Corlett have argued that powers are ineliminable from a correct ontology of properties.

The key feature of powers is their *directedness* or *intentionality*. A power is essentially pointed toward its manifestation: fragility points toward breaking, charge points toward electromagnetic interactions. This intentional or "pointing" structure is what distinguishes powers from categorical properties. George Molnar explicitly drew the parallel between physical intentionality (the directedness of powers toward their manifestations) and the intentionality of mental states (the directedness of beliefs and desires toward their objects). He argued that intentionality is not distinctively mental but is a fundamental feature of physical reality wherever powers are found.

Mumford and Anjum have developed a *powers ontology* that goes beyond individual properties to characterize causation itself as the mutual manifestation of powers. When two billiard balls collide, the causal process is not a matter of categorical properties standing in contingent regularities but of the ball's power (hardness, momentum) mutually manifesting with the surface's power (resistance, elasticity) to produce the outcome. Causation on this view is a *real productive process*, not a regularity between distinct categorical events.

Bird's *Nature's Metaphysics* argues for a thoroughgoing dispositionalism: all fundamental properties are pure powers (no categorical properties at the fundamental level), and laws of nature are necessary consequences of the identities of the fundamental powers. This makes the necessity of laws flow from the essences of the properties themselves — a result that satisfies explanatory demands without invoking external connections between distinct properties.

The main challenge for pure dispositionalism is what Swinburne called the *reciprocal disposition* problem and what Bird calls the *mutual manifestation* problem: if all properties are pure powers, and powers are defined by their relations to other powers (their manifestation conditions), then we may end up with a circular web of properties that are defined purely in terms of each other without any categorical anchor to fix the web to reality. Resolving this circularity challenge is an ongoing research program.

## The Taxonomy of Powers Views

Contemporary debates distinguish several positions:

**Pure dispositionalism (pandispositionalism)**: All fundamental properties are essentially dispositional. There are no categorical properties at the ground level. Championed by Alexander Bird (*Nature's Metaphysics*, 2007), Stephen Mumford (*Dispositions*, 1998), and Brian Ellis (*Scientific Essentialism*, 2001).

**Pure categoricalism**: All fundamental properties are essentially categorical — qualitative natures that are what they are independently of any disposition. Dispositions are then either identified with categorical properties plus laws, or with categorical properties plus contingent regularities. Associated with Lewis and (in a different way) Armstrong.

**Dual-aspect or "powerful qualities" view**: Properties are simultaneously categorical (intrinsic qualities) and dispositional (directed toward manifestations). Martin and Heil developed this view, arguing that the categorical and dispositional aspects are not two separate things but two ways of characterizing the same unified property. The property has a determinate qualitative nature *and* a disposition to produce certain effects, and these aspects are inseparable.

**Mixed view**: Some properties are purely categorical, others are purely dispositional. This is a position of last resort, combining the advantages and disadvantages of both pure views without the elegance of either.

## Bird's Dispositionalism and Its Consequences

Bird's *Nature's Metaphysics* (2007) offers the most systematic contemporary defense of pure dispositionalism. The key theses:

1. All fundamental properties are pure powers — having no categorical nature beyond their dispositional essence.
2. Laws of nature are metaphysically necessary: they follow from the essences of the powers.
3. There are no alien properties (properties not found in the actual world) — all possibilities are constrained by the dispositional web of actual powers.

From (1) and (2): the causal-nomic behavior of any fundamental property is essential to it. Charge must attract opposite charges in all possible worlds; mass must resist acceleration in all possible worlds. This is because having the power to attract opposite charges just *is* what it is to be negatively charged.

From (3): counterfactuals and modal truths are grounded in the actual dispositional structure of reality, not in a space of free-floating possible worlds.

The circularity problem: if charge is defined by its power to attract positive charges, and positive charge is defined by its power to be attracted by negative charges, then the definitions seem mutually dependent with no independent anchor. Bird's response: the web of mutual definitions is not viciously circular because it is globally satisfiable — there is a coherent assignment of dispositions to properties that makes the entire web consistent. The circularity is the circularity of a coherent system, not of individual isolated definitions.

## Molnar on Physical Intentionality

George Molnar's posthumous *Powers: A Study in Metaphysics* (2003) introduced the concept of *physical intentionality* as the central feature of powers:

- **Directedness**: Powers point toward their manifestations. A power is *for* something — it is directed at a specific type of manifestation.
- **Independence from the manifestation**: The power can exist even when the manifestation does not occur. A vase is fragile even if it never breaks.
- **Independence from the object**: The power is for a manifestation *of a certain type*, not for any particular object or event. Fragility is directed toward breaking in general, not toward any specific breaking event.

These features mirror the philosophical analysis of mental intentionality (Brentano, Husserl): mental states are about their intentional objects, which may not exist; they are directed toward object-types rather than particular objects. Molnar concluded that intentionality is not the exclusive property of the mental but is a fundamental feature of the physical world at the level of powers.

This has significant implications for philosophy of mind: if physical powers have intentionality, then the distinctiveness of mental intentionality is not a gulf separating mind from nature but a specification of a natural phenomenon. The philosophy of mind and the metaphysics of causation converge on the concept of directedness.
