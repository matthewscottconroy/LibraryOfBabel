# Relations

The history of philosophy is littered with attempts to reduce relations to properties of individual things. Leibniz held that all truths are ultimately reducible to truths about individual monads — that "A is larger than B" is really a matter of A's intrinsic size and B's intrinsic size, with the relation being an artifact of comparison rather than a genuine constituent of reality. The motivation is clear: if relations are real additions to the world over and above the things they relate, then the world is not merely a collection of self-contained individuals but something more structured, more relational, in a way that threatens the ontological independence of each thing.

But reduction fails for many relations. That two events are simultaneous cannot be reduced to intrinsic properties of each event separately; that two people are related as parent and child cannot be reduced to intrinsic properties of each person; that one quantity is greater than another cannot be reduced to intrinsic magnitudes of each quantity alone. Relations, it seems, are irreducibly real — the world has a relational structure that cannot be captured by cataloguing the intrinsic properties of its individual members.

Once we accept that relations are real, a host of questions arise. Are relations universals, repeatable across distinct pairs (or triples, or quadruples) of relata? Or are they tropes — particular, unrepeatable relational instances? What determines the direction of a non-symmetric relation: if A loves B but B does not love A, what grounds the asymmetry? And what is the ontological status of relations that hold across spacetime — relations between things that do not exist at the same time or place?

Contemporary metaphysics has witnessed a shift toward "structuralism" — the view that the relational structure of the world is ontologically primary, and that the individual things that stand in relations may be constituted by or even derivative of that structure. This structural realism has been driven partly by physics, where the fundamental ontology seems to consist not of things with intrinsic natures but of relational structures. The implications for traditional substance-property ontology are far-reaching.

This section examines the case for irreducible relations, the competing theories of what relations are, and the structuralist challenge to substance-based metaphysics.
