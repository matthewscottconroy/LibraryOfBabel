# Relations as Universals

If properties can be universals — entities that are wholly present in each of their instances — then relations should be universals too. A relational universal is a single entity that is instantiated by ordered n-tuples of objects. The relation being taller than is instantiated by every pair (x, y) such that x is taller than y. On the realist view, this universal is a genuine constituent of each such relational fact, and it is numerically the same entity across all its instances.

David Armstrong accepted relational universals alongside monadic ones. For him, a relational universal like being five meters from is just as real as the monadic universal mass. The world's fundamental facts include both "a has F" and "a stands R to b," and in both cases the universal F or R is a real, repeatable constituent of those facts.

One challenge for relational universals is symmetry. The relation being five meters from is symmetric: if a is five meters from b, then b is five meters from a. Is this one fact or two? And if it is one fact, then the relational universal must somehow relate its relata without ordering them — but many relations are not symmetric. Being taller than generates an ordered fact: if a is taller than b, this is a different fact from b being taller than a, even though both involve the same pair.

Kit Fine has analyzed this puzzle carefully. He distinguishes positionalist approaches — on which relations have argument positions that are filled by objects in a specific order — from anti-positionalist approaches, on which the directionality of asymmetric relations is not explained by positions at all. The dispute is subtle but consequential for how we think about the formal structure of relational facts.

An alternative to relational universals treats relations as multi-grade predicates reducible to sequences of monadic predications. This approach is favored by some nominalists, but it faces the challenge that many important relations — especially those in mathematics and physics — resist such reduction.

## The Formal Structure of Relational Universals

Relational universals raise formal questions not present for monadic universals. Monadic universals are straightforwardly instantiated by individual objects: *a instantiates F*. Relational universals require ordered sequences of relata: *⟨a, b⟩ instantiates R*. But what fixes the order? Two approaches:

**Positionalism**: Relations have abstract *argument positions* — the first position, the second position — which are filled by the relata in the appropriate order. The relation *loves* has a lover-position and a beloved-position; *Antony loves Cleopatra* is the state of affairs in which Antony fills the lover-position and Cleopatra fills the beloved-position. This differs from *Cleopatra loves Antony*, in which the positions are filled differently.

**Anti-positionalism** (Fine, MacBride): Positions are not genuine entities; the directionality of asymmetric relations is not explained by position-filling but by something more primitive. Fine argues that relational facts can be "non-symmetric" without this being explained by positions, since positions themselves would require positions to hold them to their relata, generating a regress.

MacBride has explored a radical *relationist* alternative: perhaps there are no relational universals at all, only relational *facts*, where the relational structure is primitive and cannot be analyzed into universal plus ordered relata.

## Relations and the Ontological Inventory

Armstrong's immanent realism requires relational universals because he holds that all genuine predicates of the form *xRy* must be made true by a state of affairs consisting of a universal and its relata. Relational universals are therefore as fundamental as monadic universals. The world's basic inventory includes:

- Particulars (individual objects)
- Monadic universals (properties)
- Relational universals (relations)
- States of affairs (particulars instantiating universals, individually or jointly)

This gives a rich but structured ontology. The alternative — allowing only monadic universals and deriving apparent relations from patterns of monadic instantiation — fails for asymmetric relations, as Russell showed. So Armstrong accepts relational universals as irreducible.

The question of *cross-temporal* and *cross-world* relations is a further complication. Can relations hold between objects at different times (temporal relations)? Between objects at different possible worlds (transworld relations)? Armstrong's immanent realism restricts universals to actual instantiated entities, which makes transworld relations especially problematic and pushes the modal realist toward positing a more abstract space of possible worlds.
