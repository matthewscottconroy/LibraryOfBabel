# Internal vs. External Relations

The British Idealists, especially Bradley, held that all genuine relations are internal: a relation is internal to its relata if it flows from their intrinsic natures, so that they could not have failed to stand in that relation given what they are. If that is right, then apparent external relations — ones that hold between things without flowing from their natures — are either not real or reducible to internal ones.

Russell rejected this vigorously. He insisted on the reality of external relations — relations that hold between their relata independently of the relata's intrinsic natures. The relation being five meters from is external: there is nothing about the intrinsic properties of a table and a chair that necessitates how far apart they are. This fact is simply given, not derived from what the objects are.

The internal/external distinction maps roughly onto the intrinsic/extrinsic distinction for properties, but they are not identical. An internal relation is one that supervenes on the intrinsic properties of the relata: given what a and b are intrinsically, their internal relations are fixed. Being taller than is often cited as an example: it is grounded in the heights of the relata, and heights are intrinsic. Being five meters from, by contrast, depends on the relative spatial positions of the objects, which is not fixed by their intrinsic natures.

The debate about internal and external relations has not disappeared. In discussions of causation and laws, Humeans argue that causal relations are external — they do not flow from the natures of cause and effect but are imposed by the pattern of regularities. Dispositionalists and essentialists push back: if properties are essentially powerful, then causal relations are internal to their terms in a certain sense.

Structural realists go further: they argue that in physics, what we discover are relations — wave functions, metric tensors, quantum fields — and the relata are either derivative or unknowable. If structural realism is right, the opposition between internal and external relations may need rethinking.

## The Standard Characterization

The most influential contemporary characterization of the internal/external distinction is:

- A relation R is **internal** to a and b iff: necessarily, if a and b exist and have the intrinsic properties they actually have, then aRb.
- A relation R is **external** to a and b iff: it is not the case that R is internal to a and b — the relation holds, but its holding is not determined by the intrinsic natures of a and b.

Examples of internal relations: *being taller than* (grounded in heights), *being the same color as* (grounded in color properties), *being similar to* (grounded in shared properties generally), *being numerically identical to* (trivially internal).

Examples of external relations: *being five meters from* (not determined by intrinsic natures), *being to the north of* (not intrinsic — an object could move without changing its intrinsic character), *being causally connected to* (on Humean views, causal connections are external facts about the pattern of events).

## Russell's Argument Against the Idealist Doctrine

Russell's argument focuses on the incompatibility of the doctrine that all relations are internal with the possibility of genuine plurality. If every relational fact about a thing is internal to it — flowing from its intrinsic nature — then knowing any one thing completely would, in principle, give one all facts, since everything follows from the nature of the Absolute. This monistic conclusion was what the idealists intended, but Russell found it both philosophically objectionable and at odds with scientific practice.

The argument reconstructed:
- P1: If all relations are internal, then every fact about a thing is determined by its intrinsic nature.
- P2: If every fact about a thing is determined by its intrinsic nature, then things are not genuinely distinct — each thing's nature includes all facts about all other things.
- P3: But things are genuinely distinct (the plurality and mutual independence of objects is a basic datum of ordinary experience and science).
- Conclusion: Not all relations are internal; there are genuine external relations.

This argument was not meant to show that *no* relations are internal — clearly some are. It was meant to show that the idealist thesis that *all* relations are internal is untenable.

## Contemporary Implications

The internal/external distinction has consequences for several contemporary debates:

**Humean supervenience**: Lewis held that all contingent facts supervene on the distribution of *local, intrinsic, perfectly natural* properties at spacetime points. External spatiotemporal relations are included as fundamental, but all other relations — causal, nomic, dispositional — supervene on the intrinsic plus spatiotemporal base. This means that causal and nomic relations are not external fundamental relations but supervene on the Humean mosaic.

**Dispositionalism**: If properties are essentially dispositional, their causal roles are *internal* to them in a sense — the nomic connections between properties flow from their natures, not from contingent external relations among properties. This promises to reduce the number of external relations in the ontology.

**Grounding**: Contemporary grounding theory provides a new vocabulary: an internal relation is one whose holding is *grounded in* the intrinsic properties of the relata. An external relation is one whose holding is a further *ungrounded* fundamental fact. Whether any relations are truly ungrounded depends on one's account of fundamentality.
