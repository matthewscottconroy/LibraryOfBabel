# The Bradley Regress

F.H. Bradley's regress argument is one of the most famous and contested arguments in the metaphysics of relations. Bradley argued that genuine relations are impossible — not because there are no relational facts but because the very attempt to posit a relation as a real entity leads to an infinite regress that cannot be stopped.

The argument runs as follows. Suppose we have two objects a and b, and they stand in some relation R. To explain how a and b are related by R, we need to say that a and b are connected to R somehow — call this connection C. But now C is itself a relation among a, b, and R — which requires a further connection C* to hold among a, b, R, and C. And C* requires a still further connection, and so on without end.

Bradley concluded that relations cannot be genuine entities in addition to their relata: if they were, we would need infinite relational chains just to explain a single relational fact. He took this to show that the relational appearance of reality is ultimately unreal — that true reality (the Absolute) is a non-relational unity.

Russell responded by drawing a distinction between the kind of entity a relation is and the kind of entity a substance is. A relation does not relate to its terms in the way one substance might relate to another. Rather, a relation simply holds between its terms as a matter of fundamental fact. Instantiation — the holding of a relation between terms — is not a further relation but a different logical category entirely. The regress is stopped not by analysis but by recognition that the demand for a further connecting relation is a category mistake.

Contemporary discussions have refined both sides. Bradley's argument has been reconstructed as demanding a foundational fact at every level — that facts themselves require grounding — and regresses of grounds have become a central topic. Bradley's worry re-emerges in the guise of: what makes it the case that R holds between a and b, and is whatever makes this the case itself a grounded fact?

## The Argument Formalized

Bradley's regress can be stated precisely as follows:

1. Suppose a and b are related by R: *aRb*.
2. For *aRb* to be a fact, R must somehow *unite* a and b — it must be a further relation connecting a, R, and b.
3. Call this connecting relation C₁. Now we need C₁ to unite a, R, and b: *C₁(a, R, b)*.
4. By the same reasoning, C₁ must be connected to a, R, and b by another relation C₂.
5. This regress continues without end.

Bradley took the regress to show that the hypothesis of genuine relations — entities that stand over and above the things they relate — generates a contradiction: each posited relation requires a further relation, so the assumption that any relation can be genuinely real is impossible.

## Russell's Response: Relations as Non-Relational Connectors

Russell's key move is to deny that relations relate to their relata in the same way that objects relate to each other. When R holds between a and b, this is a *primitive fact* of a different logical type from the fact that two substances are related. There is no further connection needed between R and its relata; R *just holds* — it is constitutively the relating of a and b.

More precisely, Russell distinguished the *substantive* use of "relation" (treating R as a term, a thing that can itself have properties and stand in relations) from the *predicative* use (where R figures as a relation, connecting terms without itself being a term). The Bradley regress arises from illegitimately treating a relation as a term and then demanding a further relation to connect it to its relata.

Modern logic encodes this insight: in a formula like *Rab*, the relation letter R is a predicate, not a singular term. It cannot itself appear as a subject without a change in logical category. The regress requires treating the predicate as a term, which is a category error in the formal system.

## Contemporary Developments: The Grounding Regress

While Russell's response is formally adequate, metaphysical discussions have identified a related regress that cannot be dismissed as a category mistake. The question is not "what further relation connects R to a and b?" but "what grounds the obtaining of the relational fact *aRb*?"

Jonathan Schaffer and others in the grounding tradition ask: for every fact, is there something in virtue of which it obtains? If so, then the fact that *aRb* must be grounded in something. But if that grounding fact is itself a relational fact, it too needs grounding, and so on.

Bradley's worry re-emerges here in a more tractable form:

- The fact *aRb* might be grounded in the intrinsic natures of a and b (if R is internal).
- But if R is external, the fact *aRb* is not grounded in anything else — it is a *fundamental* fact.
- This is not regressive but simply the terminus of explanation.

The lesson: Bradley's regress is a live threat only if we require every relational fact to be grounded in non-relational facts. If we allow fundamental relational facts (as structural realists and others do), the regress is stopped by accepting that some relational facts are ungrounded. Whether this is intellectually satisfying depends on one's views about fundamentality and the demand for explanation.
