# Relational Ontology

Relational ontology is the view that relations are, in some sense, more fundamental than the objects that stand in them — or at least that a complete metaphysical account of reality must give relations a starring role alongside (or instead of) individual substances.

In its most radical form, relational ontology claims that there are no objects prior to or independent of their relational context. Objects are constituted by the relations they stand in; they have no intrinsic nature beyond their relational roles. This view is associated with structural realism in philosophy of physics, certain interpretations of quantum mechanics, and process philosophy.

A more moderate relational ontology holds that while objects exist independently, many of their properties are constitutively relational. What it is to be an electron is to be the kind of thing that stands in electromagnetic relations to other charged things. The property is not merely accompanied by relational facts; it is partly constituted by them. This is compatible with objects being real, but it denies that objects have a purely intrinsic nature that is conceptually prior to their relational context.

Relational ontology is opposed to substance ontology — the view that reality is fundamentally composed of objects with intrinsic properties, with relations being derivative or secondary. For the substance ontologist, the basic furniture of the world is individual substances with their intrinsic natures; relations arise from the co-instantiation of properties.

The debate between substance and relational ontologies maps onto several empirical and theoretical disputes. In quantum gravity, background-independent approaches (like loop quantum gravity) treat spatial relations as fundamental rather than assuming a fixed spacetime backdrop. In social ontology, relational views hold that social entities like persons, institutions, and practices are constituted by their relations rather than by any intrinsic properties they possess in isolation.

Whether a relational ontology is ultimately coherent depends on whether one can make sense of relations without relata — or whether some minimal notion of object is ineliminable even from the most thoroughgoing relational framework.

## Varieties of Relational Ontology

**Process philosophy** (Whitehead, Rescher): Reality consists not of static substances with fixed properties but of events and processes that are constitutively relational. Each process or "actual occasion" is partly constituted by its prehension of (relatedness to) other occasions. There are no property-bearing substances prior to their relational context; substance is a derived notion from the web of processes.

**Ontic structural realism** (Ladyman, French): Motivated by the failure of particle individuality in quantum mechanics and the background-independence of general relativity, OSR holds that the fundamental ontology of physics is not objects-with-properties but *structures* — abstract relational patterns. On the most radical version, objects are eliminated in favor of structural nodes.

**Social-relational ontology**: Persons and institutions are constituted by their social relations. A person's identity is partly constituted by their relationships, roles, and social practices. This challenges the liberal individualist picture of persons as self-sufficient atomic entities whose social relations are additions to a prior individual nature.

## The Priority Debate

The central metaphysical question for relational ontology is one of *priority*: are relations prior to their relata, or relata prior to their relations?

**Substance-first view**: Objects come first with their intrinsic natures; relations arise from the co-instantiation of properties. The world is fundamentally a collection of substances-with-properties; relational structure is built up from the intrinsic character of the substances.

**Relations-first view**: Some or all relations are ontologically prior to their relata. The relata have no intrinsic nature independent of their position in the relational web; their nature is constituted by their relational roles.

**Mutual dependence view**: Relations and relata are mutually dependent, neither being prior. Objects cannot exist without standing in relations; relations cannot exist without relata. This symmetric dependence is a middle way between the extremes.

Jonathan Schaffer's *monism* offers an interesting hybrid: the whole is prior to its parts, and the parts are constituted by their position in the whole. This is a relational ontology in the sense that the relational structure of the whole is prior to the intrinsic natures of the parts, but it is not eliminativist about objects.

## Quantum Mechanics and Relational Ontology

Quantum mechanics provides perhaps the strongest physical motivation for relational ontology. Two considerations are decisive:

**Entanglement**: When two particles are in an entangled state, their quantum state is not decomposable into states of the individual particles. The pair has a joint quantum state that is irreducibly relational — it assigns probabilities to *joint* measurement outcomes, not to individual outcomes. This violates *separability* — the assumption that the state of a composite system is determined by the states of its parts.

**Particle indiscernibility**: Identical quantum particles (bosons, fermions) are indiscernible in a fundamental sense: there is no fact of the matter about which particle is which. This suggests that individual identity is not prior to the particle system; rather, the system — with its relational structure — is prior to the "particles" it contains.

Carlo Rovelli's *relational quantum mechanics* takes this further: quantum states are always relative to an observer or a reference system. There are no absolute, observer-independent quantum states — only relational ones. This is a thoroughgoing relational ontology in which the relata (systems) are defined by their relations to other systems.
