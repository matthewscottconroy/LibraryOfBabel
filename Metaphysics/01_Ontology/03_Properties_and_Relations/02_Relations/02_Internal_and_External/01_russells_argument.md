# Russell's Argument for External Relations

Bertrand Russell's argument for external relations was not presented as a single crisp argument but as a sustained campaign against the idealist view that all relations are internal. His most important point was mathematical: the relations used in arithmetic and logic are, on the face of it, external — they do not follow from the natures of the numbers or propositions involved.

Consider the relation being greater than as it holds between the numbers 3 and 2. The idealist must hold that this relation is internal to 3 and 2 — that it flows from their natures, so that 3 could not have failed to be greater than 2 given what 3 and 2 are. In one sense, this seems right: 3 is necessarily greater than 2. But Russell argued that the necessity here is logical or mathematical, not a matter of internal essence.

More pressing is the case of spatial and temporal relations. The distance from London to Edinburgh is a contingent fact: the cities could have been further or closer. There is nothing in the intrinsic nature of London as a place that determines how far it is from Edinburgh. This looks paradigmatically external. But the idealist cannot accept external relations without abandoning the core idealist thesis that reality is internally coherent.

Russell thought the idealist view led to absurdity because it would require every fact about a thing to be internal to it — including relational facts about entirely distinct things — which would mean that things are not genuinely distinct after all. If the idealist thesis were true, knowing any one fact completely would, in principle, give you all facts, since everything follows from the nature of the Absolute. Russell found this monistic result both philosophically objectionable and at odds with ordinary scientific reasoning.

## The Logical Argument

Russell's most powerful argument focuses on *asymmetric* relations. Consider:

- *a is to the left of b* (asymmetric: if true, then *b is to the left of a* is false).
- *a weighs more than b* (asymmetric: if true, then *b weighs more than a* is false).
- *a is earlier than b* (asymmetric: if true, then *b is earlier than a* is false).

The idealist reductionist must identify intrinsic properties of a and intrinsic properties of b whose combination grounds the directed relational fact. But:

- P1: If *aRb* reduces to intrinsic properties of a and b, then the same intrinsic combination must account for the directionality of R.
- P2: But a's intrinsic properties and b's intrinsic properties, in any combination, are *symmetric* with respect to a and b — they do not on their own fix which of *aRb* or *bRa* is true.
- Conclusion: Asymmetric external relations cannot be reduced to intrinsic properties of the relata.

This is the formal core of Russell's case. The upshot is that *at minimum*, asymmetric relations must be admitted as primitive, external facts about the relational ordering of objects.

## The Implications for Idealism

Russell's realism about external relations was a direct attack on British Idealism's central commitment. The idealists (Bradley, Bosanquet, McTaggart) held that all genuine relations are internal — that the relational structure of reality flows from the natures of the things related and ultimately from the nature of the Absolute as a whole.

This holism was connected to the idealist epistemology: genuine knowledge (not mere appearance) requires grasping the whole of reality, because each partial fact depends on all other facts. Partial knowledge is always distorted knowledge.

Russell's pluralism — the view that there are genuinely distinct objects standing in genuinely external contingent relations — broke this holism. On Russell's view, we can have knowledge of specific relational facts (London is north of Edinburgh) without thereby being committed to all facts. The independence of external relations from the natures of their relata underwrites the independence of particular relational facts from the totality of all facts.

## Mathematical Relations and the Foundations Debate

Russell's concern with external relations was partly driven by his work in the foundations of mathematics. In *The Principles of Mathematics* (1903), he needed to account for the *order* of numbers — the fact that 1 < 2 < 3 < ... — without reducing this order to non-relational facts about numbers individually. Order is irreducibly relational: you cannot capture the serial structure of the natural numbers by properties of individual numbers alone; you need the *less-than* relation.

This point connected to Frege's recognition that logical relations — entailment, instantiation — are genuine relations not reducible to properties of their relata. The entire logicist program depended on the reality of such relations: propositions stand in relations of entailment; concepts stand in relations of subsumption; numbers stand in arithmetical order relations. Without external relations, logic and mathematics would be unrealizable.
