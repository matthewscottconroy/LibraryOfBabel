# The Internal/External Distinction

The distinction between internal and external relations is one of the oldest in the metaphysics of relations, and one of the most consequential. An internal relation is one that holds in virtue of the intrinsic natures of its relata: given what the relata are intrinsically, the relation is fixed. An external relation is one that holds independently of the intrinsic natures of its relata: knowing everything about what the relata are intrinsically does not tell you whether the relation holds.

The clearest examples of internal relations involve necessary connections: being taller than holds between object a and object b in virtue of their respective heights, and heights are (plausibly) intrinsic properties. If we know the height of each, we can determine whether the taller-than relation holds. By contrast, being five meters from is external: two objects could have exactly the same intrinsic properties while being five meters apart or five miles apart; the spatial relation is not determined by their intrinsic natures.

Mathematical relations provide some of the clearest internal relations: being greater than is internal to the natural numbers. Given what 3 is and what 2 is — their mathematical identities — it is settled that 3 is greater than 2. You cannot conceive of 3 and 2 as they actually are while imagining that 2 might be greater than 3.

The distinction has a historical dimension as well as a metaphysical one. The British Idealists held that all genuine relations are internal — a position that motivated their monism (the world is one interconnected whole, not a collection of independent substances). Russell's realism about external relations was part of his pluralist response: there are genuinely distinct objects standing in external, contingent relations, and this is compatible with the world being fully intelligible.

Contemporary uses of the distinction include: explaining why some laws of nature are necessary (if the properties related by the law are internally connected) versus contingent (if the connection is external), and analyzing whether mental content is constituted by internal states or by external relations to the environment.

## Formal Characterizations

Several precise formulations of the internal/external distinction are available:

**Supervenience formulation**: A relation R is internal to a and b iff the holding of R supervenes on the intrinsic properties of a and b — i.e., any two objects with exactly the same intrinsic properties as a and b (respectively) must also stand in R to each other.

**Modal formulation**: A relation R is internal to a and b iff necessarily, if a has its actual intrinsic properties and b has its actual intrinsic properties, then aRb.

**Essence formulation** (Fine): A relation R is internal to a and b iff it follows from the essences (natures) of a and b that aRb — the relation is part of what a and b are, not a further contingent addition.

These formulations are subtly different. The supervenience formulation is the most widely used but is sometimes criticized for being too permissive (some external-seeming relations might supervene on intrinsic properties for structural reasons). The modal formulation is stronger but depends on the right analysis of de re modality. The essence formulation is perhaps the most demanding but also the most precise (following Fine's technical development of essence).

## The Intrinsic/Extrinsic Property Connection

The internal/external distinction for relations is intimately connected to the intrinsic/extrinsic distinction for properties. If we define intrinsic properties as those a thing has independently of what else exists, and extrinsic (relational) properties as those it has only in virtue of its relations to other things, then:

- An internal relation R between a and b generates intrinsic properties in a sense: the property *being taller than all things of height < h* is intrinsic to a if it supervenes on a's height.
- An external relation R between a and b generates extrinsic properties in a: *being five meters from b* is an extrinsic property of a.

David Lewis exploited this connection in his account of *Humean supervenience*: all contingent facts about the world supervene on the pattern of *local*, *intrinsic*, *perfectly natural* properties instantiated at spacetime points, together with the *external* spatiotemporal relations holding among those points. The only external relations countenanced at the fundamental level are spatiotemporal ones; all other apparent external relations (causal, nomic, dispositional) are held to supervene on the internal plus spatiotemporal base.

## Applications in Philosophy of Mind

The internal/external distinction has been extensively applied in philosophy of mind, particularly in debates about mental content. *Internalists* hold that mental content is determined by the internal states of the thinker — their intrinsic neural or computational organization. *Externalists* (Putnam, Burge) hold that mental content depends partly on external relations — relations to the environment or to social context.

Putnam's Twin Earth argument illustrates the point: a person on Earth and their molecule-for-molecule twin on Twin Earth (where XYZ plays the role of water) have the same intrinsic properties. But the Earth person's thought "water is wet" is about H₂O, while the twin's thought is about XYZ. Mental content is externally individuated — it depends on the external relation to the actual substance in the environment, not just on intrinsic brain states.

This is a case where the internal/external distinction for relations has direct consequences for a major debate in philosophy of mind.
