# Idealist Debates About Relations

The debate over relations was at the heart of late nineteenth and early twentieth century Anglo-American idealism. For the British Idealists — Bradley, McTaggart, Bosanquet, and their followers — the apparent plurality and externality of relations was a sign that ordinary experience is not an accurate guide to ultimate reality.

Bradley's position was the most radical. He argued that relations are contradictory: to say that a and b are related by R is to say that R connects a and b, which requires further relations between R and its terms, generating an infinite regress. The relational structure of experience is therefore not a feature of reality-as-it-is-in-itself but a distortion introduced by finite minds that carve up the seamless unity of the Absolute into apparently distinct terms.

The Absolute, for Bradley, is a non-relational whole — experience that transcends the subject/object and part/whole dichotomies that pervade ordinary thought. Appearances are real, but they are appearances of the Absolute's self-manifestation, not of a genuinely plural world of externally related particulars.

McTaggart took a different but related path. He argued that the apparent temporal relations among events — earlier than, simultaneous with, later than — cannot be real relations between things in themselves. His famous distinction between the A-series (past, present, future) and the B-series (earlier, simultaneous, later) was intended to show that time as we experience it (the A-series) is contradictory, and that the underlying B-series relations are real but static — not the dynamic flow of experienced time.

Russell and Moore's analytic revolt against idealism was partly a revolt against the doctrine that all relations are internal. Russell argued that accepting external relations does not lead to absurdity; it is simply the commonsense view that things can stand in contingent relations to one another without those relations flowing from what those things essentially are.

This debate shaped the whole trajectory of analytic philosophy, and its echoes can still be heard in contemporary discussions of necessity, grounding, and the structure of reality.

## Bradley's Argument from the Unreality of Relations

Bradley's case against relations, presented in *Appearance and Reality* (1893), is a complex philosophical argument, not merely a puzzle:

**Phase 1 — The regress**: As discussed in the Bradley Regress entry, positing a relation as a real entity requires a further relation to connect it to its relata, generating an infinite regress. No finite level of relations can account for how a relation relates its terms.

**Phase 2 — Internal contradiction**: Even if we accept a primitive relating, Bradley argues that relations are internally contradictory. A relation must be both distinct from its terms (otherwise there is no relation) and not distinct from them (otherwise it cannot relate them — a relation that stands apart from what it relates is inert). The relation must touch its relata without being them, which is, Bradley argues, incoherent.

**Phase 3 — The Absolute**: Since relations cannot be ultimately real, and yet the relational structure of experience seems necessary for any intelligible world-description, the conclusion is that the relational appearance reflects a deeper non-relational reality — the Absolute — which is systematically distorted into the relational form by finite cognition. The Absolute is *supra-relational*: it transcends the relational structure of appearances by being their unified ground.

## McTaggart's A-Series and B-Series

McTaggart's contribution to the relations debate focused on *temporal* relations. In "The Unreality of Time" (1908), he distinguished:

- **B-series**: Events ordered by the relations *earlier than*, *simultaneous with*, and *later than*. These are permanent, unchanging relations — if Caesar's crossing of the Rubicon is earlier than Waterloo, it is always earlier than Waterloo.
- **A-series**: Events characterized as *past*, *present*, or *future*. These are changing — what is present now will be past later.

McTaggart argued that time essentially involves change (otherwise it is not time but merely a fourth spatial dimension). Change requires the A-series. But the A-series is contradictory: every event is at some moment past, present, and future — three incompatible properties. Attempts to resolve this by making the properties relational ("past relative to now," "present relative to now") lead to regress.

The conclusion is that time — the genuine A-series — is unreal. What remains (the B-series) is not time but a static ordering without genuine passage. This deeply influenced analytic philosophy of time and remains a reference point for contemporary B-theory and A-theory debates.

## Russell and Moore's Revolt

Russell and G.E. Moore turned against idealism in the late 1890s, and their key move was precisely the acceptance of external relations as genuine. Moore's *Refutation of Idealism* (1903) argued that the idealist identification of the perceived and the perceiver depends on the illegitimate doctrine that all relations are internal. If relations between mind and world are external — if the world can stand in perceptual relations to the mind without being constituted by those relations — then idealism collapses.

Russell's contribution was to show that the mathematics required for Cantorian set theory and logicism requires external relations (order, membership, identity among distinct objects) and that the idealist's program of reducing all relational facts to intrinsic properties leads to an unintelligible mathematics. The technical point had philosophical consequences: idealism about relations was not just metaphysically confused but mathematically inadequate.
