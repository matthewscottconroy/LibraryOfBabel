# Internal Relations Defended

The idealist position that all relations are internal was not simply dogma — it had philosophical motivations, and its contemporary inheritors have developed more careful defenses than the original.

The core motivation is holism: the properties of things are not independent of the context in which they occur. A particular shade of red looks different depending on its surrounding colors. A belief is what it is — with the content it has — partly in virtue of its inferential relations to other beliefs. In each case, the properties of the part seem to be constituted partly by the relations it bears to the whole.

Contemporary metaphysicians who are sympathetic to internal relations often focus on the category of modal or essential relations. If two things are necessarily connected — if there is no possible world where one exists without the other — then their relation is, in a sense, internal. Mathematical objects are the clearest case: the number 3 is necessarily greater than 2, necessarily a prime, necessarily odd. These are not external, contingent features of the number; they are constitutive of what it is to be 3.

Kit Fine's work on essence and dependence has given tools for articulating this without committing to idealism. A relation R(a,b) is essential to a if it is part of a's essence — what a is — that a stands R to b. Fine uses this to analyze mathematical and logical objects: sets essentially contain their members; propositions essentially are about their constituents.

Even for physical objects, there are candidates for essential relations. Some argue that being the child of is essential to a person: Socrates could not have had different parents and been the same person. The debate about internal relations in this form connects directly to discussions of essentialism and modal metaphysics.

## The Holism Motivation

The clearest contemporary defense of internal-relation-like views comes from semantic holism and epistemic holism. If the meaning or content of a concept is partly constituted by its relations to other concepts — by inferential relations, contrastive relations, similarity relations — then those relations are "internal" to the concept in a philosophically significant sense.

Quine's holism holds that statements face the tribunal of experience collectively rather than individually. The meaning of any sentence is constituted partly by its logical relations to other sentences in the theory. This is not idealism, but it does show that relational facts (inferential connections among statements) are partly constitutive of semantic content rather than being merely contingent additions to fixed meanings.

Wittgenstein's later philosophy articulates a related holism about rule-following and language games: the meaning of a word is constituted by its use in a form of life, and that use involves complex inferential, practical, and social relations. No intrinsic feature of the word itself determines its meaning independently of this web of relations.

## Fine's Essentialist Account

Kit Fine's technical work provides a rigorous framework for vindicating a restricted form of internal relations — those that are *essential* to their relata. Fine distinguishes:

1. **Modal necessity**: Necessarily, if a exists, aRb. (Too broad — generates too many "essential" relations.)
2. **Real essence**: aRb belongs to a's essence — it is part of *what a is*. (Correct characterization of genuine internal relations.)

On Fine's account:
- The number 2 essentially stands in *less-than* to 3: it is part of 2's real essence to be less than 3.
- Socrates accidentally stands in *being-5-meters-from* to the Parthenon: this is not part of Socrates's essence.
- A set essentially contains its members: being a member of {Socrates, Plato} is essential to Socrates (with respect to the set) — it is part of the set's essence, if not Socrates's.

This gives a precise account of which relations are "internal" in the philosophically significant sense: those that figure in the real essences of their relata. Mathematical relations among mathematical objects are paradigmatically internal in this sense; contingent physical relations among concrete objects are typically external.

## Structural Dependence in Complex Systems

A different motivation for internal-relation-like views comes from complex systems where the properties of parts seem genuinely to depend on their relational context. Biological systems provide clear examples:

- The function of a protein is not intrinsic to the protein molecule itself but depends on its role in the biological system — its relations to other molecules, to the organism's development, and to evolutionary history.
- The meaning of an utterance is not intrinsic to the sound wave but depends on the relational context of the speech act — the speaker's intentions, the linguistic community, the conversational context.

In each case, the properties of the part are partly *constituted by* the relations it bears to the surrounding system. This is not idealism — the parts and their relations are all real — but it is a form of holism that challenges the substance-first picture of reality as composed of intrinsically characterized parts whose relational properties are additions to a prior individual nature.

Neo-Aristotelian metaphysics has attempted to capture this insight within a categories framework: substances have their essential properties intrinsically, but their accidental properties may be constitutively relational. The debate is then about which properties are essential (intrinsic) and which accidental (potentially relational).
