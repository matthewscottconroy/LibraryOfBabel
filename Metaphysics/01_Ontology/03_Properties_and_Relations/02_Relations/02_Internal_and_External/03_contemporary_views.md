# Contemporary Views on Internal and External Relations

Contemporary metaphysicians have largely inherited and refined the terms of the classical debate, rather than decisively resolving it. The consensus view, if there is one, is that both internal and external relations are real and that their distinction is philosophically significant.

The most influential contemporary treatment is Kit Fine's work on essence and ground. Fine argues that the notion of essence — what a thing is — can do the work that the notion of internal relation was meant to do. A relation is internal to a in the relevant sense if a's essence involves that relation: it is part of what it is to be a that a stands in that relation. This is more precise than the traditional formulation and avoids the ambiguities that plagued it.

On Fine's framework, mathematical relations are paradigmatically internal: it is part of the essence of the number 2 to be less than 3. Physical relations are typically external: it is not part of the essence of this table that it is five meters from that chair. Social relations occupy a more complex middle ground: it may be part of my essence that I was born of certain parents (if we accept origin essentialism), but not part of my essence that I am currently employed.

Grounding theory has given new resources for thinking about internal relations. An internal relation is one whose holding is grounded in the intrinsic properties of the relata. An external relation's holding is not so grounded — it is a further fundamental fact. This distinction between grounded and ungrounded relations maps onto the internal/external distinction in a useful way, though the two do not perfectly align.

Dispositionalism about properties has implications for the internal/external debate: if properties are constituted by their causal powers, then the causal relations among things bearing those properties are in some sense internal — fixed by the natures of the properties involved. This is one of the attractions of dispositionalism: it promises to reduce the number of external relations by showing that many apparently contingent causal connections flow from the essential natures of the properties concerned.

## Grounding and the Internal/External Distinction

Jonathan Schaffer's development of grounding theory provides a new framework for thinking about internal relations. The key distinction is between:

- **Grounded relations**: Those whose holding is explained by — grounded in — facts about the intrinsic properties of the relata. Internal relations are grounded.
- **Ungrounded (fundamental) relations**: Those whose holding is not explained by any more fundamental facts. These are the genuinely external relations.

On a typical Lewisian picture, the only fundamental external relations are spatiotemporal ones. All other relational facts — causal, nomic, dispositional, resemblance — are grounded in the intrinsic properties plus spatiotemporal structure. On a dispositionalist picture, even some causal relations are internal in a sense: the causal connection between objects bearing dispositional properties flows from the essences of those properties.

The question of what is genuinely ungrounded — what is truly external — is thus the question of what is fundamental. Different metaphysical frameworks give different answers, but all contemporary views recognize that there are *some* facts (at the ground level) that are not grounded in anything else, and many of these fundamental facts will be relational facts.

## Epistemic Externalism and Mental Content

One of the most productive applications of the internal/external distinction in contemporary philosophy is in the debate about mental content. Tyler Burge's *individualism* debate concerns whether the contents of psychological states are "narrow" (determined by the intrinsic organization of the thinker) or "wide" (determined by relations between the thinker and the environment).

Burge's arthritis thought experiment: suppose Bert has a condition that Bert mistakenly calls "arthritis," but which is actually a condition affecting not joints but muscles. In Bert's linguistic community, "arthritis" means a condition of the joints, so Bert's belief that he has arthritis in his thigh is a false belief about a joint condition. If Bert had grown up in a community that used "arthritis" to cover both joint and muscle conditions, his belief would have different content — even if Bert's intrinsic neural states were exactly the same. Mental content depends on external social relations, not just internal states.

This is a case where the analogue of external relations — the dependence of content on social and environmental context — plays a crucial role in individuating mental properties. Internalists resist this conclusion; externalists embrace it. The debate maps directly onto the classical dispute about internal and external relations.

## Structural Realism and the Primacy of Structure

The most radical contemporary position on external relations is ontic structural realism (OSR), which holds that the fundamental ontology of physics consists entirely of structural relations rather than objects with intrinsic properties. On this view:

- There are no intrinsic properties of fundamental physical objects (if there are "objects" at all).
- All properties are structural — they are relational in character.
- The internal/external distinction becomes less important because there are no intrinsic properties for internal relations to supervene on.

OSR thus collapses the internal/external distinction in a particular direction: if all properties are relational (external in the traditional sense), then there are no internal relations in the traditional sense. But OSR replaces the internal/external distinction with a distinction between *fundamental structural relations* and *derived or emergent* relational patterns. The fundamental structural relations are the basic posits; all other patterns supervene on these.

Critics of OSR argue that relations must relate *something*, and that something must have some character — even if only a "thin," structurally defined character. This reintroduces a minimal version of intrinsic properties and, with it, the internal/external distinction.
