# Relations in Physics

Physics offers some of the most important and puzzling cases of relations in metaphysics. The structure of physical theory is fundamentally relational: spacetime intervals relate events, forces relate particles, the quantum state encodes correlations among measurements. What this means for the metaphysics of relations is contested and important.

One striking case is quantum entanglement. When two particles are in an entangled state, measurements on one are correlated with measurements on the other in ways that cannot be explained by pre-existing local properties. The entangled pair seems to have a relational property — their quantum state — that is not reducible to properties of the individual particles. This has suggested to some philosophers that quantum mechanics provides empirical evidence for irreducibly relational facts.

The relation is especially interesting because it appears to be holistic: the entangled state is a property of the pair that is not composed of states of the individuals. This challenges separability — the assumption that the state of a composite system is determined by the states of its parts. If separability fails, then the world has genuinely irreducible relational facts at the quantum level.

Spacetime structure also raises questions about relations. In general relativity, the metric tensor expresses the distances and angles between events in curved spacetime. But what are the relata? Are they spacetime points (substantivalism) or just relations among material events (relationism)? The debate between spacetime substantivalists and relationists is precisely a debate about whether the fundamental relational facts of physics require an underlying substance or whether the relations themselves constitute reality.

Structural realists take all of this as pointing toward a deep metaphysical conclusion: that physics does not tell us about the intrinsic natures of things but only about their structural-relational properties. Whether this is a limitation of physics or a deep metaphysical truth is one of the central questions in philosophy of physics.

## Quantum Entanglement and Non-Separability

The EPR correlations, first discussed by Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen (1935) and later given operational content by Bell's theorem (1964), demonstrate that quantum mechanics predicts correlations between spatially separated systems that cannot be explained by any *local hidden variable* theory — any theory on which each system has pre-existing definite properties that determine the measurement outcomes.

Bell's theorem:
- P1: If physical systems have pre-existing definite values for measured quantities (locality + realism), then measurements on separated systems must satisfy certain statistical inequalities (Bell inequalities).
- P2: Quantum mechanics predicts violations of Bell inequalities.
- P3: Experiments (Aspect 1982, Hensen 2015, et al.) confirm violations of Bell inequalities.
- Conclusion: Either locality or realism (or both) must be abandoned.

The metaphysical upshot: the quantum correlations are *holistic* — they belong to the pair as a whole, not to the individual particles. The quantum state ψ of the entangled pair is not decomposable as ψ(a) ⊗ ψ(b) (a tensor product of individual states). This is a mathematically precise sense in which the pair has a relational property not reducible to properties of the individuals.

Philosophers of physics have drawn several metaphysical morals:
1. **Eliminativism about particle individuals**: There are no individual particles, only field modes. What we call "particles" are structural features of quantum fields, not individual objects with their own intrinsic states.
2. **Non-separable holism**: The world has fundamental holistic facts — facts about composite systems not reducible to facts about their parts. Relations are genuinely irreducible.
3. **Relational quantum mechanics** (Rovelli): Quantum states are always relative to a reference system; there are no absolute facts about individual quantum systems, only relational facts about their correlations.

## Spacetime: Substantivalism vs. Relationism

The debate between *spacetime substantivalism* and *spacetime relationism* is the philosophical heir of the Newton-Leibniz debate:

**Substantivalism**: Spacetime exists as a genuine substance — a container for material events. Spacetime points are real entities with properties (metric relations to neighboring points). Material objects are located in spacetime.

**Relationism**: Spacetime is not a substance but a system of relations among material events. There is no spacetime apart from the events and the relations (simultaneity, spatial distance, causal precedence) that hold among them.

General relativity's *hole argument* (Earman and Norton 1987) threatens substantivalism: if spacetime points are genuine individuals, then a diffeomorphism (a smooth permutation of points) would generate a physically distinct but observationally equivalent spacetime — violating *determinism*. The remedy seems to be either *relationism* (there are no spacetime points to permute) or *sophisticated substantivalism* (spacetime points exist, but their qualitative properties, not their identities, carry the physical content).

Ontic structural realists take the hole argument as motivation for eliminating spacetime points in favor of pure structure — the metric field relations — with no underlying point-individuals.

## Symmetry and Physical Structure

The symmetry groups of physical theories capture what is structurally invariant — the relational content that remains fixed across different representations. In general relativity, the diffeomorphism group; in gauge theories, the gauge group (U(1) for electromagnetism, SU(2)×U(1) for electroweak, SU(3) for QCD).

Physical laws are expressions of these symmetry structures. The conservation of energy follows (by Noether's theorem) from time-translation symmetry; conservation of momentum follows from space-translation symmetry; conservation of charge follows from gauge symmetry. The laws are not contingent regularities imposed on underlying categorical properties but expressions of the structural-relational symmetries of physical reality.

This connection between symmetry, structure, and physical law provides some of the strongest motivation for structural realism: what physics discovers is not the intrinsic natures of things but the relational-structural patterns that persist through theoretical change and that underlie the empirically confirmed regularities.
