# Epistemic Structural Realism

Epistemic structural realism (ESR) was articulated by John Worrall in an influential 1989 paper as a way of dissolving the pessimistic meta-induction. The pessimistic meta-induction argues: since successful past scientific theories were later rejected as false (caloric fluid, luminiferous ether, Newtonian mechanics), we have no reason to believe current successful theories are true. ESR responds by distinguishing what changes in theory change from what is preserved.

What changes in scientific revolutions, Worrall argued, is the ontology — the kinds of objects postulated. What is preserved is the mathematical structure: Fresnel's equations describing the reflection and transmission of light survived intact through the transition to Maxwell's electromagnetic theory, even though the underlying ontology was transformed. Structure is what science tracks, and structure is what is approximately true in successful theories.

This gives ESR its characteristic thesis: we can have knowledge of the structural features of the world, but not of the intrinsic nature of whatever instantiates those structures. The world has a structure — a system of relations among things — that we grasp through mathematical physics. But what those things are in themselves, their intrinsic character, remains inaccessible.

The view draws on Poincare and Helmholtz, who emphasized the structural character of physical knowledge, and on Ramsey sentences, which translate physical theories into statements about the existence of entities satisfying certain structural roles without specifying what those entities are intrinsically.

Critics argue that ESR faces a dilemma: either the intrinsic/extrinsic distinction is coherent — in which case we need something to instantiate the structure — or it is not, in which case ESR collapses into ontic structural realism. Worrall and his defenders have tried to navigate between these horns, but the challenge remains pressing.

## Historical Antecedents

ESR draws on a tradition of *structural* or *mathematical* realism that predates Worrall's 1989 formulation:

**Henri Poincaré** (1902) argued in *Science and Hypothesis* that what physical theories capture are not facts about the nature of things but *relations* among things. The differential equations of physics are true descriptions of structural relations; the "hypotheses" about the underlying nature of matter and energy are mere conveniences.

**Hermann Helmholtz** similarly argued that perception and knowledge grasp only the *order* of natural phenomena — the relational structure — not their intrinsic qualitative character. The intrinsic quality of a sensory experience (the particular "feel" of red) is not a reliable indicator of any intrinsic property of the world; what our experience tracks reliably is the relational structure of physical stimuli.

**Bertrand Russell**, in *The Analysis of Matter* (1927), developed a form of structural realism: physics tells us only the *structure* of the physical world — the network of causal-relational connections — not the *intrinsic qualities* of the physical entities. The ultimate "stuff" of the world, what instantiates the structural relations physics describes, remains unknown.

## The Structural Continuity Thesis

Worrall's central claim is that mathematical structure is preserved through theory change even when theoretical ontologies are replaced:

| Theory Change | Ontology Changed | Structure Preserved |
|---|---|---|
| Fresnel → Maxwell | Mechanical ether → electromagnetic field | Fresnel's equations (limiting case) |
| Newton → Einstein | Absolute space and time → spacetime | Newtonian mechanics (low velocity limit) |
| Classical → Quantum | Deterministic trajectories → probability amplitudes | Poisson brackets → commutators |

In each case, the earlier theory's mathematical structure survives as a limiting or special case of the successor theory. This suggests that what science tracks is not contingent ontological postulates but something more stable — the mathematical-structural content.

## The Newman Objection

M.H.A. Newman (1928) showed that if a domain of entities of cardinality n is given, any consistent abstract structure expressible in a standard set-theoretic language is trivially satisfiable by that domain (by appropriate stipulation of which subsets and relations count). This threatens to make the structural realist's claim nearly vacuous: "the world has structure S" is true of any sufficiently large domain, so it carries no substantive information.

ESR defenders respond that the relevant structures are not arbitrary abstract structures but *naturally or physically meaningful* ones — the specific mathematical equations and symmetry groups of physical theories, which are not freely stipulable but are strongly constrained by the requirement of empirical adequacy and theoretical unification. The Newman move is unavailable when the structure is specified by its role in a specific, empirically confirmed theory rather than by its abstract logical form alone.

## ESR vs. Ontic Structural Realism

ESR is epistemological: we can know only the structure, not the intrinsic nature. Ontic structural realism (OSR) is ontological: there *is* no intrinsic nature — the structure is all there is. ESR accepts that there is something — an unknown substrate — that instantiates the structure; OSR denies or deflates this.

The tension between ESR and OSR reflects a more general tension between epistemic humility (there are things we cannot know) and ontological parsimony (why posit unknowable entities?). If the intrinsic nature of the substrate is in principle unknowable, one might argue (with OSR) that we should simply not posit it. Worrall's ESR resists this move by noting that the structure-instantiation framework is coherent even if the intrinsic nature of the instantiator is inaccessible.
