# Relations as Fundamental

The thesis that relations are metaphysically fundamental — not derivative from or reducible to properties of individuals — has become increasingly important in philosophy of physics and in metaphysics more broadly. The traditional picture assigns priority to substances and their intrinsic properties; relations come second, holding between things that first exist with their own natures. The structural turn reverses this: relations come first, and what we call "objects" are nodes in a relational network, defined by the structural positions they occupy.

The motivation from physics is strong. In quantum field theory, what we call "particles" are not individual objects in the classical sense but quanta of excitation in quantum fields. A field is a relational entity: it assigns values to points or regions of spacetime. The structure of the field — the pattern of values and their relational constraints — is more fundamental than any particular "particle" within it.

In general relativity, spacetime itself is a relational entity: it consists of events related by the metric, with no absolute spatial or temporal positions. The spacetime manifold has structure — the topology, the metric, the causal structure — but the individual points of the manifold have no intrinsic identity; they are defined solely by their relational positions within the structure.

These physical cases motivate the philosophical thesis that structure — systems of relations — should be given ontological priority. Some philosophers (like Ladyman) argue that we should eliminate objects entirely and replace them with structures. Others (like French and Krause) argue for a more moderate view: objects exist, but their identity conditions are exhausted by their structural roles.

The thesis that relations are fundamental does not commit one to denying that there are any objects. It commits one to the view that the objects' nature is constituted by their relational situation, rather than the other way around. What it would mean for an object to exist independently of all its structural relations is, on this view, not a coherent question.

## The Argument from Quantum Indistinguishability

One of the most philosophically significant arguments for the fundamentality of relations comes from quantum statistics. In classical mechanics, particles are *individuals* — even qualitatively identical particles are numerically distinct, and permuting them produces a different physical state. In quantum mechanics, the situation is radically different:

- **Bosons** (photons, gluons, W/Z bosons): Quantum states are symmetric under particle exchange. The state does not change when identical bosons are permuted.
- **Fermions** (electrons, quarks, neutrinos): Quantum states are antisymmetric under particle exchange. The Pauli exclusion principle follows: two fermions cannot occupy the same quantum state.

The mathematical structure (symmetrization/antisymmetrization of state vectors) implies that there is no *fact of the matter* about which boson is which in a multi-boson system. The particles are *weakly discernible* at best — they can be distinguished by their relational properties (this particle is where that particle is not) but not by their intrinsic properties (they have exactly the same intrinsic properties).

French and Ladyman argue that this shows that quantum "particles" are not genuine individuals in the traditional sense. Their identity is not given by any intrinsic haecceity; it is given, at best, by their structural roles. This supports the view that relations — the structural patterns of the quantum field — are more fundamental than the "particles" that appear to populate it.

## Structural Priority vs. Substance Priority

The dispute about whether relations or substances are fundamental can be framed in terms of *metaphysical grounding*:

- **Substance priority**: Relations hold in virtue of the intrinsic natures of their relata. The relata and their intrinsic natures are prior; relations are grounded in them.
- **Structural priority**: Relata are constituted by their relational positions. The structural pattern is prior; individual objects are derived from or constituted by their positions in the structure.

The structural priority view is motivated by cases where individual objects seem to have no identity independent of their structural roles:

- Spacetime points in GR: their only properties are metrical (relational).
- Natural numbers: the number 3 has no properties beyond its structural position (being the successor of the successor of 0, being less than 4, etc.).
- Electrons: their only "intrinsic" properties (mass, charge, spin) may themselves be understood structurally as the representations of the relevant symmetry groups.

The substance priority view is motivated by the intuition that there must be *something there* to instantiate the structural relations — a subject of which the relations are predicated. Pure structure without any substrate seems like a ghost — the smile without the cat.

## Categorical and Relational Properties Reconsidered

The fundamentality of relations challenges the traditional categorical/relational property distinction. If the most fundamental properties of fundamental particles are charges, masses, and spins — and if these can be understood structurally as representations of gauge groups — then even what appeared to be intrinsic properties of particles turn out to be relational in character. The property of having charge +1 is the property of transforming in a certain way under the U(1) gauge symmetry — which is a relational, structural property.

This blurs the categorical/relational boundary at the fundamental level. What seemed categorically intrinsic (an electron's charge) may be structurally relational when properly understood. If this is right, then the fundamentality of relations extends even to apparent intrinsic properties, leaving no clear residue of genuinely categorical, non-relational properties at the ground level of physics.
