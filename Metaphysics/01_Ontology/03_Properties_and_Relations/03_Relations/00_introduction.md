# Relations: Advanced Topics

The existence of relations is one thing; their metaphysical analysis is another. Even granting that relations are genuine constituents of reality — that "A is larger than B" is not reducible to facts about A and B individually — we have not yet determined what kind of item a relation is, how it connects to the things it relates, or what its formal properties reveal about the structure of the world.

One cluster of issues concerns the internal structure of relations. Bradley's regress — the argument that any relation between A and B must itself be related to A and B by a further relation, generating an infinite regress — threatens to make all relational facts mysterious. The relation R connects A and B only if R is appropriately connected to A and to B; but those connections are themselves relational facts requiring further relations, and so on. Responses to Bradley's regress divide sharply: some hold that the regress is harmless (infinite but not vicious); others hold that relations must be "externally related" to their relata in a way that does not require further connecting relations; still others take the regress as a reductio of the relational view.

A second cluster concerns formal properties: symmetry, transitivity, reflexivity, and their negations. These formal properties structure the relations we use in logic, mathematics, and science — identity is symmetric and transitive, parenthood is asymmetric and intransitive, similarity admits of degrees. What is the metaphysical basis for these formal properties? Do they flow from the intrinsic natures of the relations themselves, or are they imposed by the structure of the situations in which the relations hold?

A third cluster concerns internal versus external relations. An internal relation holds of its relata in virtue of their intrinsic natures — if A and B have the intrinsic natures they have, it follows necessarily that R holds between them. An external relation can fail to hold even if the relata have the very natures they have. Spatial relations are paradigmatically external; resemblance relations are paradigmatically internal. The distinction matters for the theory of causation, the analysis of grounding, and the question of whether there is any ontological difference between a world and its modal counterparts.

This section develops these advanced topics in the theory of relations, connecting the formal analysis to substantive metaphysical debates.
