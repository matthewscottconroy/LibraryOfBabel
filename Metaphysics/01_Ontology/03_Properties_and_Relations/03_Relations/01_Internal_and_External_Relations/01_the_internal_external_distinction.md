# The Internal/External Distinction

*What it means for a relation to hold in virtue of the natures of the relata versus holding independently.*

---

A relation is *internal* if it holds in virtue of the intrinsic natures of its relata — if the relata's intrinsic properties necessitate the relation's holding. A relation is *external* if it holds contingently, not necessitated by the intrinsic natures of the things related.

Resemblance is the canonical internal relation: if A and B are both red, the resemblance relation between them holds necessarily in virtue of their (intrinsic) color properties. One cannot have exactly the same color properties without resembling; the natures of the relata guarantee the relation. The relation *being to the north of* is external: A's intrinsic properties and B's intrinsic properties do not necessitate their relative spatial positions. Two objects with exactly the same intrinsic properties could stand in different spatial relations.

This distinction matters for at least three reasons. First, it bears on the question of whether relations require ontological addition over and above the intrinsic properties of the relata. Internal relations may be thought to reduce to or supervene on intrinsic properties and thus not add to the ontological inventory. External relations, by contrast, seem to be genuine additions — relational facts not grounded in monadic facts.

Second, the internal/external distinction connects to the question of what is fundamental. Lewis held that all intrinsic facts about the world at any moment, combined with the pattern of external spatiotemporal relations, determine all other facts — this is Humean supervenience. On this view, external relations (spatio-temporal) are fundamental, but all other relations (causal, nomic, dispositional) supervene on this categorical-relational base.

Third, the distinction bears on identity across possible worlds. If resemblance is internal, then objects that are perfect duplicates must resemble each other — no possible world separates the intrinsic properties from the resemblance. If spatial relations are external, then two objects that are one meter apart in the actual world might be one kilometer apart in another world, even with the same intrinsic properties.

Moore, Russell, and later Lewis made the internal/external distinction central to their rejection of Bradleyan idealism and their defense of a pluralistic realism about relations.

## Historical Background

The distinction between internal and external relations was a battlefield in early twentieth century Anglo-American philosophy. Bradley, McTaggart, and Bosanquet held that all genuine relations are internal — a thesis connected to their monism. Russell and Moore's revolt against idealism was in part a revolt against this doctrine.

Russell argued that the reality of external relations is not only defensible but *required* for logic, mathematics, and science. The irreducibly asymmetric relations of mathematics (less than, is a member of) and science (is to the left of, causes) cannot be reduced to internal relations without making them either trivial or unintelligible.

The modern metaphysical literature has refined the distinction considerably. Lewis's framework identifies three types of facts:
1. **Intrinsic facts**: facts about what an object is like in itself, independently of what else exists.
2. **Internal relational facts**: facts about relations that supervene on intrinsic facts.
3. **External relational facts**: fundamental facts about relations that do not supervene on any intrinsic facts — paradigmatically, spatiotemporal relations.

## Fine's Essence-Based Refinement

Kit Fine's work has given a more precise characterization of internal relations:

A relation R is *essential internal* to a and b (with respect to a) iff: it is part of a's real essence that a stands R to b.

This is more demanding than modal necessity: it requires not just that aRb is necessary but that the necessity flows from a's nature. Thus:

- It is part of the nature of the set {Socrates} that it has Socrates as a member — so the membership relation is internal to the set.
- It is part of the nature of the number 3 that it is greater than 2 — so the greater-than relation is internal to 3 (in that direction).
- It is *not* part of Socrates's nature that he is five meters from the Agora — so that spatial relation is external.

Fine's approach allows for *directed* internal relations: the relation of membership is internal to the set (its nature determines what its members are) but need not be internal to Socrates (it may not be part of Socrates's essence which sets he belongs to).

## Applications to Causation and Dispositions

The internal/external distinction has direct application to debates about the nature of causation and laws:

**Humean causation** (Lewis, Ramsey): Causal relations are external — they hold as a matter of contingent co-occurrence in the world's history, not by necessity from the natures of cause and effect. On this view, the same cause could, in a world with different laws, be followed by a different effect.

**Dispositionalist causation** (Bird, Ellis, Mumford): Causal relations are internal in a significant sense — they flow from the natures of the causal properties involved. If fragility essentially includes being-disposed-to-break, then the causal connection between being struck and breaking is an internal consequence of the fragile object's nature. Laws are not external impositions but internal expressions of property natures.

This is one of the central contested issues in contemporary metaphysics: whether causal and nomic relations are internal (dispositionalism) or external (Humeanism). The answer affects the modal status of laws (necessary vs. contingent), the nature of causation (productive vs. regularist), and the content of possibility claims.
