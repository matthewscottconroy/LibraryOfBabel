# Relations in Science

*The role of relational properties and structures in fundamental physics.*

---

Contemporary physics is deeply relational in character. The properties that fundamental physics cares about — mass ratios, charge ratios, spatio-temporal intervals, quantum entanglement — are often relational rather than purely intrinsic. This empirical finding creates pressure on metaphysical views that privilege intrinsic properties as the fundamental level of reality.

The most radical case for the primacy of relations comes from structural realism in philosophy of science. John Worrall, James Ladyman, and Don Ross argue that what scientific theories reveal is *structure* — patterns of relations — rather than the intrinsic natures of the relata. On the *ontic* version of structural realism (Ladyman and Ross), there are no individual objects with intrinsic natures underlying the relational structure; structure is fundamental, and objects (if we can speak of them at all) are nodes in the relational web.

Quantum mechanics provides the most striking case. Quantum entanglement involves correlations between measurements on spatially separated particles that cannot be explained by any local intrinsic properties of the individual particles. The quantum state is irreducibly relational — it assigns probabilities to joint measurement outcomes, not to individual particle outcomes. This suggests that the fundamental description of reality involves relations between measurement events rather than intrinsic states of individual particles.

Spacetime itself is relational on the Leibnizian and Machian traditions: space is not an absolute container (Newton's view) but a system of relations between material objects. General relativity seems to support this in some respects — spacetime geometry is dynamically coupled to the distribution of matter and energy — though the ontological interpretation of spacetime remains contested.

These considerations from physics have fueled a growing interest in *structural* metaphysics as an alternative to the traditional substance-property paradigm, and they have reinvigorated debates about the ontological status of relations that seemed merely scholastic in earlier periods.

## The Relational Character of Physical Quantities

Several fundamental physical quantities are intrinsically relational — they are defined as relations between systems rather than as absolute properties of individual systems:

**Mass**: In Newtonian mechanics, absolute mass is postulated. But in practice, mass is measured by comparison — mass ratios are what is empirically accessible. Mach argued that inertia is a relational quantity, determined by the total mass distribution of the universe. While Mach's full program has not been vindicated, the relational character of mass in practice is noteworthy.

**Spacetime intervals**: In special relativity, the spacetime interval ds² = c²dt² - dx² - dy² - dz² between two events is a relational quantity — it depends on the coordinates of both events. It is invariant under Lorentz transformations (which change the individual coordinates), so it represents genuine structural content — but it is a relation between events, not a property of individual events.

**Electric charge**: Individual charge magnitudes are absolute in classical electrodynamics. But in quantum electrodynamics, the *renormalized* charge depends on the energy scale of interactions — which is a relational, contextual feature. This does not make charge purely relational, but it indicates that even apparently intrinsic quantities have relational aspects in quantum field theory.

**Wave functions**: Quantum mechanical wave functions encode probabilities of measurement outcomes — relational facts between the quantum system and possible measurement setups. The wave function is not an intrinsic property of the system but a relational description relative to a choice of basis, measuring apparatus, or reference frame (depending on the interpretation).

## Laws of Nature as Relational

On a Humean view, laws of nature are global relational facts — regularities in the pattern of events across all space and time. The law that all electrons have negative charge is a fact about the *relation* between having certain properties and having others across all instances. Laws are not intrinsic to individual objects but are patterns of co-instantiation across the world.

On a non-Humean view (Armstrong, Ellis, Bird), laws are also relational — they are relations among universals (Armstrong's necessitation relation N(F, G)) or among dispositional essences (Ellis/Bird's view that properties essentially confer dispositions). In either case, the law-like connection between properties is a relational fact, not an intrinsic feature of any individual.

The debate is thus not about whether laws are relational but about what kind of relational facts they are: contingent regularities (Humean) or necessary connections among property-natures (non-Humean).

## Structural Realism and the Scientific Image

The scientific image of the world — as revealed by our best physical theories — is predominantly a structural image. This is the lesson structural realists draw from the history and philosophy of physics:

1. The fundamental ontology of classical mechanics was particles with intrinsic masses and positions. Quantum mechanics replaced these with probabilistic wave functions in a Hilbert space — an inherently structural entity.
2. The fundamental ontology of Newtonian gravity was forces between point masses. General relativity replaced this with curved spacetime geometry — a relational structure relating events.
3. The fundamental ontology of pre-quantum chemistry was atoms with intrinsic properties. Quantum chemistry replaced this with molecular orbitals — collective electronic states extending over entire molecules.

At each step, individual objects with intrinsic properties give way to structural patterns and collective states. The structuralist takes this as a signal that the scientific image of reality is irreducibly relational: physics does not tell us about things in themselves but about the structural-relational organization of reality.

Whether this signals a deep metaphysical truth or merely a feature of our theoretical representations is one of the central unresolved questions in philosophy of science — and in the philosophy of relations more broadly.
