# Structural Realism

*The view that scientific knowledge describes structural relations rather than intrinsic natures.*

---

Structural realism is the thesis that what science tells us about the world is its *structure* — the pattern of relations and laws — not the intrinsic nature of the relata that stand in those relations. Proposed by John Worrall as a way to reconcile the success of science with the radical theory changes in the history of science, it occupies a middle position between full scientific realism (we know what the world is intrinsically like) and anti-realism (scientific theories are merely useful instruments).

The epistemological motivation is the *pessimistic meta-induction*: since past successful theories have been superseded and their posited entities (caloric, phlogiston, the luminiferous ether) have been eliminated, we should not expect our current theoretical entities to survive future revisions either. But Worrall notices that what *does* survive theory change is the mathematical structure: Fresnel's equations for light propagation survived the transition from the wave theory to Maxwell's electromagnetism. The structure was preserved even as the interpretation of what underlies the structure changed. Structural realism recommends believing in the structure while remaining agnostic about the underlying nature.

Ontic structural realism (Ladyman, Ross) takes a stronger position: not only is structure what we know, but structure is all there is. There are no individual objects with intrinsic natures underlying the structural relations; the relata are at best "thin" nodes in a relational network, with no nature beyond their position in the structure. This is a radical departure from traditional substance ontology: reality is fundamentally relational, and talk of individual objects is derivative from structural talk.

Critics press several objections. First, the very notion of *structure* seems to require relata — relations must relate something, and if the relata have no intrinsic natures, the relations themselves seem ungrounded. Second, the *permutation problem*: if all that matters is structure, then any permutation of the world's objects that preserves the structural relations should describe the same world — but this seems to overcomplicate the picture and challenge our ordinary concept of individual objects. Third, it is unclear how structural realism handles quantum mechanics, where the "structure" itself is deeply contested between different interpretations.

## The Epistemic Version

Worrall's *epistemic structural realism* (ESR) holds:

1. Scientific theories, when successful, are approximately true representations of the structural features of the world.
2. We have epistemic access only to structural features, not to the intrinsic natures of the underlying entities.
3. What is preserved through theory change is the mathematical-structural content; what is replaced is the theoretical interpretation.

ESR is conservative: it accepts that there is something (a substrate, an unknown intrinsic nature) that instantiates the structure but holds that we cannot know what it is. The Kantian resonance is deliberate — the "things in themselves" (the intrinsic natures) are unknowable, but the "phenomenal structure" (the mathematical relationships physics describes) is knowable.

## The Ontic Version

Ontic structural realism (OSR) holds:

1. The fundamental ontology of the world is structural — it consists of relations and structures, not objects with intrinsic properties.
2. Objects (if they exist at all) are derivative: they are nodes in structural networks, constituted by their structural positions.
3. The intrinsic-nature question is not merely unanswerable but ill-posed: there are no intrinsic natures, only structural features.

OSR is more radical. It takes the epistemological insight of ESR and converts it into an ontological claim: the reason we can know only the structure is that the structure is all there is to know. The traditional object-with-intrinsic-properties picture is simply wrong as an ontological picture.

## Structural Realism and the Practice of Science

An important question for structural realism is whether it accurately characterizes the practice and content of science, or whether it is a philosopher's reconstruction that distorts the scientific self-understanding:

Scientists typically do *not* describe their work as discovering "pure structures" while remaining agnostic about the nature of the underlying entities. Physicists speak of electrons, quarks, and photons as if they were genuine objects. Chemists speak of molecules and reactions as if they involved real substances.

Structural realists respond that the *self-understanding* of scientists is not the relevant criterion for the metaphysical truth about scientific knowledge. What matters is the formal content of the theories — the mathematical equations and the symmetry groups — and those are unambiguously structural. The "object talk" of scientists is heuristic and represents level of description, not a metaphysical commitment to substance ontology.

This appeal to the formal content of theories connects structural realism to the philosophy of mathematics and to debates about the ontological significance of mathematical representations in science.
