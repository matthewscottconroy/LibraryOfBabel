# Dependence and Grounding

*The relation in virtue of which some facts hold because of other facts.*

---

The concept of *grounding* has emerged as one of the central notions in contemporary analytic metaphysics. Where causation is a relation between events in time, and supervenience is a modal relation (no difference without a corresponding difference), grounding is a relation of *non-causal explanatory priority* — it tells us what holds *in virtue of* what, what is *because of* what, at the level of metaphysical explanation rather than efficient causation.

Kit Fine, Jonathan Schaffer, and others have argued that grounding is an irreducible and fundamental metaphysical relation that cannot be captured by mere supervenience. The fact that a mental state supervenes on physical states does not by itself tell us that the mental state holds *in virtue of* the physical — supervenience is compatible with the mental being primitive and the physical supervening on the mental. Grounding adds a directionality missing from supervenience.

Grounding relations are thought to structure reality into levels: the fundamental physical level grounds the chemical level, which grounds the biological level, which grounds the mental and social levels. At each level, facts are true in virtue of facts at lower levels, though each level may introduce genuinely new kinds of facts that are not merely reduplicative of lower-level descriptions.

The concept has been applied to many philosophical debates: moral facts are held to be grounded in natural facts; mathematical facts are (on some views) grounded in logical facts or in the physical facts that the structures model; social facts are grounded in psychological and physical facts; dispositional facts are grounded in categorical facts (on some views) or vice versa.

Grounding requires a relatum on each side — the ground and the grounded — and a relation of priority between them. Questions arise about whether grounding is transitive (if A grounds B and B grounds C, does A ground C?), whether it is irreflexive (nothing grounds itself), and whether it is well-founded (every chain of grounding reaches a terminus in the ungrounded). Answers to these structural questions constrain the picture of reality that grounding is meant to articulate.

Critics of grounding (notably Josh Dever and Timothy Williamson) have questioned whether the notion is sufficiently clear to do the philosophical work demanded of it, and whether it adds explanatory power beyond familiar notions like causation, realization, and supervenience.

## Grounding vs. Supervenience

The distinction between grounding and supervenience is crucial. Supervenience is a *modal* relation: there cannot be a difference in the A-facts without a difference in the B-facts. Grounding is an *explanatory* relation: the A-facts hold *because* the B-facts hold.

The difference matters because supervenience is symmetric in a way that grounding is not:

- If the physical facts necessitate the mental facts (mental supervenes on physical), it does not follow that mental facts hold *because of* physical facts — it is compatible with the mental being fundamental and the physical supervening on the mental.
- Grounding, by contrast, is asymmetric: if A grounds B, then B does not ground A (nothing grounds itself; grounding is irreflexive and anti-symmetric).

Additionally, supervenience is compatible with a world where the mental and physical are both fundamental, each supervening on the other (mutual supervenience). Grounding requires a strict priority — one side is *prior to* the other.

## The Structure of Grounding

Grounding is typically held to satisfy:

**Irreflexivity**: Nothing grounds itself. It would be circular and uninformative to say that P holds in virtue of P.

**Asymmetry**: If A grounds B, then B does not ground A. Grounding is a strict one-way relation of priority.

**Transitivity**: If A grounds B and B grounds C, then A grounds C. The grounds of the grounds are among the more ultimate grounds.

**Well-foundedness (contested)**: Every chain of grounding terminates in something that is not itself grounded — the *fundamental* or *ungrounded* facts. This ensures no infinite regress of grounds. Some philosophers (Schaffer) accept well-foundedness; others (Rosen) question whether it is required.

These structural properties make grounding analogous to the *strict partial ordering* relation in mathematics — irreflexive, asymmetric, transitive — but applied to facts or states of affairs rather than numbers.

## Applications to Relational Ontology

Grounding is the appropriate framework for analyzing the ontological status of relations in a relational ontology:

**External relations as fundamental grounds**: On Lewis's Humean supervenience, the spatiotemporal relational facts are among the fundamental grounds. Everything else — causation, laws, dispositions, mental states — is grounded in the Humean mosaic. The external spatiotemporal relations are themselves ungrounded; they are fundamental.

**Internal relations as grounded**: Resemblance, mathematical relations, and other internal relations are grounded in the intrinsic properties of the relata. They hold *because* the relata have the intrinsic properties they have. Since they are grounded, they do not add to the fundamental ontological inventory.

**Relational ontology**: If relations are fundamental — not grounded in any more basic intrinsic facts — then grounding theory accommodates this by allowing relational facts to be terminally ungrounded. The relational ontologist holds that some relational facts are themselves the grounds for higher-level facts, with no further monadic grounds.

## Schaffer's Priority Monism

Jonathan Schaffer has used grounding theory to defend *priority monism* — the view that the fundamental entity is the whole universe, and all parts of the universe are grounded in (derived from) the whole. This inverts the usual order of grounding: rather than the whole being grounded in its parts, the parts are grounded in the whole.

Priority monism connects to relational ontology in an interesting way: if the whole is prior to its parts, then the relational structure of the whole is ontologically prior to the intrinsic properties of the parts. The parts' natures are constituted by their relational position within the whole — which is a form of relational ontology.

Schaffer motivates priority monism partly from quantum mechanics: the global quantum state of the universe does not decompose into independent states of the parts. The universe is entangled as a whole, and local facts about parts are grounded in the global quantum state — supporting the priority of the whole over the parts.
