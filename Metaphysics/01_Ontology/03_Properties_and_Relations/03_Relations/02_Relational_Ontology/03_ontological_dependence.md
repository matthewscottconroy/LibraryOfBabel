# Ontological Dependence

*The relation in which the existence of one thing depends on the existence of another.*

---

Ontological dependence is the relation in which one entity cannot exist without another entity existing. A smile depends on the face it is on; a property-instance (trope) depends on the object that has it; an event depends on the individuals that participate in it; a set depends on its members. These dependence relations structure the ontological landscape, determining what is fundamental and what is derivative.

Philosophers distinguish several kinds of ontological dependence. *Rigid specific dependence*: x rigidly specifically depends on y if necessarily, if x exists, then y exists, and this necessity obtains because y is the particular entity x depends on. A smile depends rigidly on the face it is on — not on faces in general, but on *this* face. *Generic dependence*: x generically depends on the kind F if necessarily, if x exists, then some F exists. A set depends generically on having some members, not on any specific member.

The notion of dependence is asymmetric: faces do not depend on their smiles (a face can exist without smiling), but smiles depend on their faces. This asymmetry gives dependence its use in articulating what is fundamental (what depends on nothing else) and what is derivative (what depends on something else).

E. J. Lowe's four-category ontology is structured entirely by dependence relations: kinds depend on their instances and on the attributes that characterize them; individual substances are the ontologically independent nodes; modes depend on the substances they characterize. This hierarchical dependence structure is meant to replace the flatter, extensionalist picture of reality as a mere mosaic of particular facts.

Ontological dependence connects to the debate about *substantivalism* and *relationism* about spacetime: on the relationist view, spatial and temporal locations depend on the material objects that occupy them (there is no space apart from the spatial relations among things), while the substantivalist holds that spacetime exists independently of its material occupants. The dependence question is thus not merely one about properties and relations in the abstract but bears on some of the most fundamental questions in natural philosophy.

## Varieties of Ontological Dependence

Philosophers have distinguished several types of ontological dependence. Each captures a different way in which the existence of one thing can require the existence of another:

**Rigid specific dependence**: x necessarily exists only if y (the particular y) exists. This is the most demanding form. A particular trope — this particular redness of this rose — depends rigidly and specifically on the rose: not just on some rose or other, but on this very rose.

**Generic dependence**: x necessarily exists only if something of kind K exists. The existence of a wife depends generically on the existence of a husband (the institution of marriage requires that there be spouses), but not on any particular husband.

**Essential dependence** (Fine): x essentially depends on y iff it is part of x's essence that y exists. The set {Socrates} essentially depends on Socrates — it is constitutive of what this set is that Socrates is its member.

**Historical dependence**: x depends on y for its origin or coming-into-being, even if x could subsequently exist without y. A child historically depends on its parents for its origin even if it could survive orphanhood.

These types are independent: something can satisfy one without satisfying another. A set essentially depends on its members (essential dependence) and rigidly specifically depends on them (rigid specific dependence), but not generically (there is no kind K such that the set merely requires some K to exist).

## Dependence and Priority

Ontological dependence is the relation that underwrites claims about *ontological priority*: A is ontologically prior to B iff B depends on A but A does not depend on B. The independent entities are the *ontologically fundamental* ones; the dependent entities are derivative.

Different metaphysical frameworks assign priority differently:

**Substance-first ontology**: Substances (individual objects with their intrinsic properties) are ontologically fundamental. Properties, relations, events, states of affairs all depend on substances. This is the Aristotelian view.

**Fact-first ontology** (Wittgenstein, Armstrong): Facts or states of affairs are ontologically fundamental. Individual objects and properties are *abstractions* from facts — they depend on the facts, not the other way around.

**Structure-first ontology** (Ontic structural realism): Structural relations are ontologically fundamental. Individual objects depend on the structural network they participate in; they have no existence or nature independent of the network.

The choice among these priority schemes is not merely a matter of labeling but has substantive consequences: it determines what is "really there" in the most fundamental sense and what the world would contain if everything derivative were stripped away.

## Ontological Dependence in Contemporary Metaphysics

Contemporary metaphysicians have used ontological dependence to articulate fine-grained ontological positions:

**Trope theory**: Tropes depend on the objects that bear them (modes depend on substances). Individual tropes cannot exist without a substance to instantiate them — they are "incomplete entities" in Frege's sense. This asymmetric dependence is what makes tropes property-instances rather than free-floating particulars.

**Set theory**: Sets depend on their members. This is both an essential dependence (the set {Socrates, Plato} essentially has Socrates and Plato as members) and an existential dependence (if Socrates does not exist, {Socrates, Plato} does not exist). The iterative conception of set theory encodes this dependence: each set is formed from entities that already exist at a prior stage of the hierarchy.

**Social ontology**: Institutions (money, property rights, legal systems) depend on collective acceptance and practices. They depend on the mental states and social interactions of the participants — a form of generic essential dependence on agents with certain capacities and attitudes.

**Fundamentality**: An entity is *fundamental* iff it does not ontologically depend on anything else. Whether any concrete entities are fundamental is a contested question. Atoms, elementary particles, and spacetime points are candidates; each depends on deeper-level facts if there are any.

The dependence-theoretic framework is now standard in analytic metaphysics for organizing ontological commitments and clarifying what is meant by claims about what is fundamental, real, or basic.
