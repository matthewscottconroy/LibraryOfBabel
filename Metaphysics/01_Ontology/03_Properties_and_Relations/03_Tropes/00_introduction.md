# Tropes

Think of the redness of this apple — not redness in general, not the universal that all red things share, but this particular instance of redness, located here, belonging to this apple and no other. If the apple were destroyed, this particular redness would go out of existence; but redness as a universal, if there is such a thing, would persist in the redness of other red things. The question is whether the particular redness — what philosophers call a trope — is the fundamental unit of property-attribution, or whether it is merely a conceptual shadow cast by the genuine universal.

Trope theory holds that properties are particulars: not universals that are wholly present in multiple locations simultaneously, but individual, non-repeatable property-instances that are each wholly specific to the object that has them. When two red apples are both red, there are two numerically distinct redness-tropes, not one universal shared between them. The two tropes resemble each other — and this primitive resemblance relation among tropes is used by trope theorists to ground what universals were previously invoked to explain: the unity of natural kinds, the truth of general claims, the basis for natural laws.

Trope theory offers a distinctive solution to several classical metaphysical puzzles. The compresence of tropes — the way in which the various properties of a single object (its color, shape, texture) are bound together in one individual thing — replaces the substance-as-substratum of traditional ontology. On the trope bundle theory, an individual thing just is a bundle of compresent tropes; there is no further substratum required. This avoids the Lockean problem of the bare particular (the "something I know not what" that is supposed to be the ultimate bearer of properties) while preserving the distinctiveness of each individual.

The main challenge for trope theory is to give an account of resemblance that does the work previously done by universals without reintroducing universals through the back door. If the resemblance between the two redness-tropes is primitive and unanalyzable, that may seem acceptable for a two-place resemblance; but resemblance among many tropes, and especially the claim that all red things are related by primitive pairwise resemblances, threatens to multiply ontological commitments in unwelcome ways.

This section examines trope theory in depth, its theoretical advantages over both classical nominalism and realism about universals, and the challenges it must overcome to be viable.
