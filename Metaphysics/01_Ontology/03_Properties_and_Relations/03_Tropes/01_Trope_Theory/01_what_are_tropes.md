# What Are Tropes?

D.C. Williams introduced tropes — or "abstract particulars" — as the fundamental building blocks of reality. A trope is a particular instance of a property: not redness in general, but this particular redness here, numerically distinct from any other redness anywhere else. Two red roses do not share the universal redness; each has its own particular redness-trope, which resembles (but is not identical to) the other's.

The word "trope" was borrowed from rhetoric, where it means a turn or figure of speech. Williams thought the metaphysical term was apt because tropes are turns or aspects of reality — the particular ways things are. Others have called them "unit properties," "abstract particulars," "cases," or "modes." The terminology varies, but the core idea is consistent: properties are as particular as the objects that have them.

Tropes differ from universals in being numerically distinct across different instances. If universals are repeatable — the same entity wholly present in multiple instances — tropes are non-repeatable. My particular redness is mine alone; it is not shared with any object, however similar.

Tropes also differ from ordinary particulars like tables and electrons. A table is a concrete particular — it occupies a definite spatial region. Tropes are in a sense located — they are where their bearers are — but they are not concrete in the ordinary sense. The redness of a rose is not the rose itself; it is a feature or aspect of the rose, considered as a genuine entity in its own right.

Williams thought tropes were more fundamental than both universals and ordinary particulars. Ordinary particulars are bundles of tropes; universals are sets or classes of resembling tropes. On this view, tropes are the true atoms of ontology, and everything else is constructed from them. Whether this program can be carried through consistently remains one of the central debates in the metaphysics of properties.

## Historical Origins and Terminology

The modern trope concept has precursors throughout the history of philosophy. Aristotle's individual accidents — the particular paleness of Socrates as distinct from the paleness of Callias — are close ancestors. Medieval philosophers distinguished between concrete accidents (individual instances of properties in individual substances) and abstract accidents (the properties themselves), with some (like Scotus) arguing for the individuation of accidents as genuine particular entities.

In the modern period, Leibniz's individual accidents and Locke's particular qualities hint at trope-like entities, though neither develops the idea systematically. The most direct precursor is G.F. Stout's "abstract particulars," discussed in *The Nature of Universals and Propositions* (1921). Stout argued against universals and for the view that all entities, including properties, are particular.

D.C. Williams coined the term "trope" in *The Elements of Being* (1953). Keith Campbell's *Abstract Particulars* (1990) is the most systematic contemporary defense. Anna-Sofia Maurin's *If Tropes* (2002) is a rigorous examination of the trope-theoretic program.

## The Ontological Role of Tropes

Tropes are asked to play several ontological roles simultaneously:

**Grounding property attributions**: The truth of "this rose is red" is made true by the existence of the rose's redness-trope. The trope is the *truthmaker* for property attributions.

**Grounding similarity**: Two roses resemble in color because they have similar redness-tropes. Similarity among objects is grounded in resemblance among tropes.

**Replacing universals**: If universals were posited to explain what many things have in common, tropes + resemblance is meant to do the same job without positing a shared entity. The class of resembling redness-tropes plays the theoretical role of the universal redness.

**Grounding causation**: Particular causal interactions are grounded in particular tropes. This heat-trope caused this expansion-trope, not heat in general causing expansion in general.

Whether a single ontological category — tropes — can successfully play all these roles without generating paradoxes or infinite regresses is the central question for trope theory.

## Tropes and the Categories Debate

Trope theory is a one-category ontology in the sense that it takes one fundamental category — tropes — and derives all others from it. Objects are bundles of tropes; universals are resemblance classes of tropes; events are changes in trope distributions. The simplicity of a one-category ontology is attractive, but it generates pressure on every derived concept to be explained without appealing to the resources of the category being derived.

Compare this with:
- *Substance-attribute ontology*: Two fundamental categories (substances and attributes). Substances have attributes; attributes are had by substances.
- *Universals realism*: Two or more fundamental categories (particulars and universals). Universals are shared by particulars.
- *Bare particularism*: Bare particulars plus universals plus the instantiation relation — a three-category ontology.

The economy of trope theory is genuinely impressive if the derivations work. The program's success depends on whether resemblance among tropes can do the work of universals without presupposing them, and whether compresence among tropes can do the work of substance without presupposing it.
