# Tropes vs. Universals

The choice between tropes and universals is one of the central divides in the metaphysics of properties. Both views accept that there are property-like entities in addition to ordinary particular objects. But they differ on whether those entities are repeatable (universals) or non-repeatable (tropes).

The case for universals rests on the intuition that genuine identity underlies resemblance. When two electrons have exactly the same charge, they do not merely resemble each other; they have the exact same charge. This numerical identity in a property seems to require a shared entity — a universal — that is literally the same thing in each electron. Resemblance, the universalist argues, is always grounded in something deeper: genuine identity of nature.

The trope theorist denies this. Even exact resemblance does not require identity: two charge-tropes can be exactly alike without being the same trope. The world contains this charge and that charge — two numerically distinct things that happen to be exactly alike. There is no further entity — charge in general — that both of them instantiate.

One advantage tropes have over universals is parsimony at the fundamental level: tropes are just one category of entity (particular properties), while universals introduce a second fundamental category (abstract, repeatable entities) alongside particulars. Trope theory promises to do the same explanatory work with a smaller ontological toolkit.

But tropes face an explanatory challenge universals handle easily: what explains why two tropes resemble? For universals, the answer is simple: they are the same universal. For tropes, the resemblance is a further fact — there must be resemblance relations among tropes. And resemblance relations themselves look like universals (or further tropes with the same problem), threatening a regress.

Keith Campbell argued that the regress is not vicious: resemblance relations among tropes are themselves tropes, but at some point the resemblances are primitive and require no further grounding. Whether this response is satisfying depends on whether one accepts that some facts can be fundamental.

## The One-Over-Many Argument Applied to Tropes

The classical one-over-many argument argues for universals: two things share a property (both are red), so there must be one thing they both have — the universal redness. How does the trope theorist respond?

**Trope response**: Two things both being red consists in each having a redness-trope, and the redness-tropes resembling each other exactly. There is no single entity (the universal redness) that both have; there are two distinct redness-tropes that are qualitatively identical. The "one over many" that does explanatory work is the *resemblance class* of redness-tropes — a set of tropes, not a universal.

**Universalist reply**: This just pushes the question back. Why do the two redness-tropes resemble each other? If the answer is "because they are both instances of redness," we have reintroduced universals. If the answer is "it's a primitive fact about these tropes," we have a brute resemblance that universals would explain.

**Trope response**: Primitive facts must terminate somewhere. The trope theorist takes primitive resemblance among tropes as the terminus; the universalist takes primitive instantiation of a universal as the terminus. Both have primitive commitments; the question is which is more ontologically economical.

## Armstrong's Case for Universals over Tropes

Armstrong's preference for universals over tropes rests on several considerations:

**Instantiation vs. resemblance**: Universals explain property attribution through *instantiation* — a's having F consists in a's instantiating the universal F. Tropes explain property attribution through resemblance — a's having F consists in a's having an F-trope that resembles the paradigm F-tropes. Armstrong argues that instantiation is a more fundamental and perspicuous relation than resemblance.

**Exact resemblance**: When two electrons have exactly the same charge, Armstrong argues it is ad hoc to say they have two numerically distinct but qualitatively identical charge-tropes. The simpler explanation is that they share one and the same charge-universal. Tropes multiply entities without explanatory necessity.

**Laws of nature**: Armstrong holds that laws connect universals through the necessitation relation N(F, G). This account requires that F and G be universals — repeatable entities that can be related by a universal law. If properties are tropes (non-repeatable), it is unclear how a law can connect them, since each trope is numerically distinct from every other trope.

**Trope response to laws**: Trope theorists argue that laws can be analyzed in terms of regularities over tropes, or in terms of the dispositional natures of tropes themselves, without positing higher-order universals connecting them. But whether this is fully satisfactory is disputed.

## Comparison of Theoretical Roles

| Role | Universals | Tropes |
|---|---|---|
| Ground of predication | Instantiation of universal | Possession of resembling trope |
| Explanation of similarity | Shared universal | Resemblance among tropes |
| Foundation of laws | Necessitation between universals | Dispositional tropes or regularities |
| Ground of causal relations | Causal roles of universals | Particular causal tropes |
| Ontological category | Second category (alongside particulars) | One category (particulars of a special type) |

The comparison shows that tropes and universals compete across the board, not just on the question of resemblance vs. identity. The choice between them depends on which set of explanatory trade-offs is considered more tractable, which primitive commitments are considered more acceptable, and which connects better to the rest of one's philosophical framework.
