# Trope Bundle Theory

Trope bundle theory is the view that ordinary objects — the tables, electrons, and persons that populate our world — are not bare substrata with properties "attached" to them, but rather are bundles or collections of tropes. An object just is a bundle of particular property-instances; there is no additional constituent — no bare particular or thin substance — underlying the tropes and possessing them.

The view has considerable appeal. It avoids the mysterious entity of the bare particular — a featureless something that somehow holds all the properties together but has no nature of its own. The bundle view says: there is just the redness, the roundness, the mass, and whatever other tropes constitute the apple; the apple itself is nothing over and above these tropes considered together.

What holds the tropes in a bundle? The standard answer is compresence: the tropes that make up a single object are all spatiotemporally coincident. They are located at the same place at the same time, and this co-location is what makes them aspects of one object rather than free-floating property-instances distributed across the universe.

Problems arise immediately. First, compresence seems to be a relation — but the bundle theory wants to avoid positing relations as primitives alongside the tropes. If compresence is itself a trope, it must be bundled with the other tropes, but what bundles it? A further compresence trope? This regress threatens to undermine the bundling account.

Second, the bundle theory faces the problem of coincident objects. Two distinct objects — say, a statue and the lump of clay that constitutes it — might be co-located at the same time. If objects are just bundles of co-located tropes, and both the statue and the lump occupy the same location and share all their physical tropes, they seem to be the same bundle — and hence the same object. But many philosophers think the statue and the lump are distinct. Getting the right individuation conditions for bundles is crucial.

Despite these challenges, trope bundle theory remains an active and sophisticated position, developed by Campbell and others as a complete alternative to both substance-based and universal-based ontologies.

## The Bundling Relation

The central technical challenge for trope bundle theory is specifying the bundling relation that unifies a collection of tropes into a single object. Candidate relations:

**Compresence (co-location)**: Tropes t₁, t₂, ... form a bundle iff they are all located at the same spacetime region. The rose's redness-trope and roundness-trope are bundled because they are both located where the rose is.

*Problem*: Two distinct objects can be co-located (the statue and its constituent clay). If bundling is just co-location, the statue and the clay are the same bundle.

**Mutual necessary co-existence**: Tropes t₁ and t₂ are bundled iff each necessarily co-exists with the other. The redness-trope of the rose necessarily co-exists with its roundness-trope (in the actual world, the rose is both round and red).

*Problem*: Necessity of co-existence might hold between tropes that are not genuinely parts of the same object — any two tropes that necessarily co-occur (if there are any) would be bundled, which seems too broad.

**Ontological dependence**: Tropes t₁ and t₂ are bundled iff each depends on the same substance-like node or on each other in a constitutive network.

*Problem*: This introduces a substance-like node as the dependence base, potentially re-introducing the bare particular under a different name.

**Simons's Nuclear Bundle Theory**: Peter Simons distinguishes *nuclear* tropes (which constitute the essential nature of an object and are mutually dependent) from *peripheral* tropes (which depend on the nuclear core but are not mutually required). Nuclear tropes form a tight essential core; peripheral tropes are loosely attached. This gives a richer structure than simple compresence, approximating the traditional substance-accident distinction within a trope framework.

## Bundle Theory and the Identity of Indiscernibles

Bundle theory (whether of tropes or universals) is sometimes said to entail the *principle of the identity of indiscernibles* (PII): entities that share all their properties are identical. On the bundle view, an object just is its bundle of properties; two bundles with exactly the same constituents are identical.

**Trope bundle theory and PII**: Since tropes are numerically distinct even when exactly resembling, two trope bundles could have exactly resembling members while being distinct bundles. Trope bundle theory does *not* automatically entail PII: two objects can be exactly alike (have exactly resembling tropes) while being numerically distinct bundles. This is one advantage of trope over universal bundle theory — it avoids the controversial metaphysical claim that objects in exactly the same intrinsic state must be identical.

**Max Black's Two-Sphere World**: Black imagined a world consisting of two exactly identical spheres with no other contents. In such a world, the spheres have exactly the same qualitative properties. Universal bundle theory (objects are bundles of universals) has difficulty accounting for their distinctness. Trope bundle theory has no difficulty: the two spheres consist of two distinct bundles of exactly resembling tropes.

## Historical Context

Bundle theory has a long history. Hume's analysis of the self as a "bundle of perceptions" is perhaps the most famous instance. Berkeley's idealist bundle theory identifies material objects with bundles of ideas. In contemporary analytic metaphysics, bundle theories have been defended by Bertrand Russell (early period), and the trope-bundle version was developed by D.C. Williams and elaborated by Keith Campbell.

The main alternatives are:
- **Substratum theory**: Objects are bare particulars (substrata) that have properties attached to them by the instantiation relation. The substratum is what individuates the object; its properties are numerically distinct from it.
- **Haecceitism**: Objects have primitive individual essences (haecceities) that individuate them independently of their qualitative properties. Two objects could share all their qualitative properties while being distinct in virtue of having different haecceities.
