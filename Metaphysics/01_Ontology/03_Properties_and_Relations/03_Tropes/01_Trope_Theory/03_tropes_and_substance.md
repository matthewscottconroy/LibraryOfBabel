# Tropes and Substance

Trope theory faces a distinctive version of the traditional problem of substance. Ordinary objects — roses, electrons, persons — are more than collections of isolated properties. They are unities: the redness, the scent, the thorns all belong to one and the same rose. What unites a bundle of tropes into a single substance?

Keith Campbell proposed that what holds tropes together is compresence: tropes are co-located or spatiotemporally coincident. The tropes that constitute a rose are all at the same location. This spatial bundling is what makes them properties of one thing rather than scattered features of the universe.

Critics find compresence unsatisfying. Spatial coincidence is too weak: a ghost might spatially coincide with a person without their properties bundling into a single substance. And compresence itself seems like a relation among tropes — which means it is either a further trope (raising the question of what bundles it with the others) or a universal (which reintroduces the opponent's ontology).

Peter Simons developed a more nuanced account using dependence relations among tropes. He distinguished nuclear tropes — those that constitute the essential nature of an object — from peripheral tropes that belong to it accidentally. Nuclear tropes are mutually dependent: each requires the others for its existence, forming a tight essential core. Peripheral tropes depend on the nuclear core but not vice versa. This gives a richer account of substance structure than mere spatial bundling.

The trope-bundle theory contrasts with substratum theory, which posits a bare particular or substratum as the unifier of properties. On the substratum view, there is a thin particular — the object stripped of all its properties — that has or exemplifies its tropes. Trope theorists find substrata mysterious and prefer to eliminate them, but the bundling problem shows that the elimination has costs.

## The Problem of Substance

The problem of substance is one of the oldest in metaphysics. What is it that has properties? What unifies a collection of properties into a single object? Several answers have been proposed:

**Aristotelian substance**: Primary substance is the individual — this man, this horse — which exists independently and serves as the subject of attributes. The substance is not reducible to its attributes; it is the thing that has them, which is ontologically prior.

**Lockean substratum**: "Something I know not what" — a featureless support for qualities. The substratum has no qualities of its own; it is a bare subject of predication.

**Humean bundle**: An object is just the bundle of co-existing perceptions (for Hume) or properties (for bundle theorists). There is no further substratum; the bundle is all there is.

Trope theory's bundle version tries to capture Hume's insight (no mysterious substratum) without Hume's skeptical consequences (the self reduces to a bundle of perceptions with no unified subject). The key move is to make the bundling relation sufficiently rich that it constitutes genuine unity without positing a bare particular.

## Simons's Nuclear-Peripheral Distinction

Peter Simons (*Parts: A Study in Ontology*, 1987; and subsequent work) developed the most philosophically sophisticated trope-theoretic account of substance, distinguishing:

**Nuclear tropes**: The tropes that constitute the essential nature of the substance. For a human being, the nuclear tropes might include the distinctive biological tropes (DNA, neurological organization) that make this person the kind of thing they are. Nuclear tropes are mutually dependent: each requires the existence of the others. They form a tight compresence kernel.

**Peripheral tropes**: The tropes that belong to the substance accidentally — properties the substance has but could lack. The rose's being-in-Paris is a peripheral trope; the rose could exist without being in Paris. Peripheral tropes depend on the nuclear core (they cannot exist without attaching to the nuclear bundle), but the nuclear core does not depend on any particular peripheral tropes.

This two-level structure mirrors the traditional substance-accident distinction. The nuclear core corresponds to the *essence* of the substance (what it essentially is); the peripheral tropes correspond to its *accidents* (what it contingently is). The unity of the substance is secured by the mutual dependence of the nuclear tropes, with peripheral tropes hanging off this essential core.

## Substance and Persistence

A further challenge for trope bundle theory is explaining how substances persist through change. If an object is a bundle of tropes, and tropes are particular property-instances, then what happens when the rose loses a petal or changes color?

**Endurantist view**: The rose persists through time as numerically the same thing, gaining and losing tropes. At time t₁ the rose has a red-trope; at t₂ it has a yellow-trope (having changed color). The rose is the same rose because the nuclear tropes persist, even though the peripheral tropes change.

*Challenge*: If the rose is constituted by its tropes, and the tropes change, how is numerical identity preserved? The trope bundle at t₁ is not the same bundle as at t₂.

**Perdurantist view**: The rose has temporal parts. The rose-at-t₁ is a distinct entity from the rose-at-t₂, and "the rose" picks out the temporal whole composed of these temporal parts. Each temporal part is a distinct trope bundle; the temporal whole is a four-dimensional entity composed of temporal parts.

Simons's nuclear-peripheral distinction provides resources for the endurantist view: the rose persists because its nuclear tropes persist, even as its peripheral tropes change. But this requires that nuclear tropes themselves can persist — which may require them to be *immanent universals* rather than *particular tropes*, on pain of obscuring their particularity.
