# Resemblance Classes

A central application of trope theory is explaining how general terms work. When we use the predicate "red," we apply it to many distinct objects. What makes this application correct? The trope theorist's answer: all and only the red objects have redness-tropes, and all redness-tropes resemble one another and do not resemble non-redness tropes in the relevant way.

The resemblance class associated with a predicate is then a class of resembling tropes. Redness is not a universal but a resemblance class — the set of all particular redness-tropes. Property attribution becomes: "a is red" means "a has a trope that belongs to the redness-resemblance-class."

This approach faces the classic problem of resemblance nominalism, now applied to tropes: what makes two tropes resemble in the relevant respect? If resemblance is itself a universal, the view has smuggled universals back in. If resemblance is a trope, then we need resemblance-tropes to resemble, and so on.

Rodriguez-Pereyra's resemblance nominalism takes the regress head-on, arguing that primitive resemblance relations are not universals but particular relational facts. Two charge-tropes resemble because they stand in a particular resemblance relation — which is itself a trope. The regress of resemblance tropes is not vicious because at each level the resemblance in question is of a higher order and eventually the hierarchy terminates in a logically distinct domain.

An alternative is to take resemblance as a primitive and unexplained feature of tropes — they just do resemble or not, and no further analysis is available or needed. This primitive resemblance view is ontologically lean but epistemically unsatisfying: it leaves the resemblance facts brute rather than explained.

The challenge of resemblance classes shows that trope theory is not a free lunch: it trades the universals problem for the resemblance problem, and the latter must be addressed seriously.

## The Resemblance-Class Construction

The trope theorist's construction of property classes proceeds as follows:

1. Start with a domain of tropes: individual property-instances in the world.
2. Define resemblance among tropes: t₁ resembles t₂ (exactly or approximately) iff they are qualitatively alike in the relevant respect.
3. Form resemblance classes: the redness resemblance class is the set of all redness-tropes — those tropes that exactly resemble the paradigm redness-tropes.
4. Attribute the property: to say "a is red" is to say "a has a trope t that belongs to the redness resemblance class."

This construction faces the central difficulties of resemblance nominalism, applied now to tropes rather than to ordinary concrete objects:

**The membership problem**: What makes a trope belong to the redness class rather than the orangeness class? If resemblance is the criterion, then the boundary between red and orange resemblance classes must be fixed by resemblance facts. But resemblance is a matter of degree, and the trope theorist must explain why the classes have the sharp or fuzzy boundaries they do.

**The circular definition problem**: To define the redness class in terms of resemblance to paradigm redness-tropes, one must already know what a redness-trope is — which is what the class was meant to define.

## Goodman's Grue and Resemblance Classes

Nelson Goodman's *grue* predicate creates difficulties for any resemblance-class account of properties. Grue is defined as: x is grue iff (x is observed before time T and x is green) or (x is not observed before T and x is blue). All emeralds observed before T are grue-colored — they form a resemblance class of grue-tropes. Why should we use the redness class rather than the grueness class as the basis for inductive projections?

The trope theorist can appeal to the *naturalness* of the redness class versus the artificiality of the grueness class. Redness-tropes genuinely resemble each other in a way that grounds inductive projection; grue-tropes are a gerrymandered collection that only appears cohesive because of its disjunctive definition.

But what makes the redness resemblance class natural and the grueness class artificial? If resemblance is the criterion, both classes are equally defined by resemblance among their members. The trope theorist needs a primitive notion of *qualitative similarity* that distinguishes genuine from gerrymandered resemblance — which is precisely the notion that Lewis captured with his notion of *perfectly natural* properties.

This suggests that the resemblance-class construction cannot be carried out purely in terms of resemblance; it requires a primitive notion of naturalness or eligibility to select the right classes from among all the resemblance-definable ones.

## Rodriguez-Pereyra's Sophisticated Resemblance Nominalism

Gonzalo Rodriguez-Pereyra (*Resemblance Nominalism*, 2002) has developed the most sustained contemporary defense of resemblance nominalism — a view that combines resemblance with tropes as the primary relata of resemblance. His key moves:

1. Resemblance relations hold primarily *among tropes*, not among ordinary objects.
2. Resemblance is a *primitive*, unanalyzable relation — there is no further account of why tropes resemble.
3. The various nominalist puzzles (companionship, imperfect community) are solved by careful attention to *which* objects resemble *which*, at the level of tropes.

On this view, the regress is not vicious because resemblance among tropes is a logically distinct domain from the first-order predications it is meant to explain. The regress of "what makes resemblance-tropes resemble?" concerns second-order tropes, not first-order tropes, and the explanatory project is complete at each level.
