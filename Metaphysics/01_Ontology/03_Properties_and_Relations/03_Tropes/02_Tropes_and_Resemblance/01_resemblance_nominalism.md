# Resemblance Nominalism

Resemblance nominalism is the view that property-talk is grounded not in universals but in objective resemblance relations among particulars. Two things are both red not because they share the universal redness but because they sufficiently resemble each other — and resemble the paradigm cases of red things — in the relevant respects. Resemblance is taken as a primitive, objective, non-reducible relation among particulars.

The view has a long history, with versions in Locke and Hume, but its most developed contemporary form is due to H.H. Price and, more recently, Gonzalo Rodriguez-Pereyra. In Price's version, the resemblance is among concrete individuals; in Rodriguez-Pereyra's version, the resemblance is primarily among tropes — the particular property-instances of individuals.

The main theoretical advantages of resemblance nominalism are: (i) it avoids abstract universals and the accompanying worries about how concrete things can instantiate abstract entities; (ii) it gives a naturalistically respectable account of property attribution in terms of comparative facts about individuals; (iii) it takes seriously the idea that similarity is an objective feature of the world without reifying it into shared universals.

The central difficulty is the regress discussed elsewhere in this unit. Resemblance itself looks like a relation, and if it is a universal, the nominalist project has failed. Rodriguez-Pereyra's response is to treat resemblance as a primitive relational fact that does not require further grounding in a universal: it is simply a brute fact that these two things resemble each other.

A further challenge is the coextension problem: if two properties always accompany each other — being renate (having kidneys) and being cordate (having a heart) were thought to be necessarily coextensive in all actual animals — resemblance nominalism threatens to collapse them into one property, since all the instances of one resemble all the instances of the other. Avoiding this collapse requires distinguishing the respects in which objects resemble, which may reintroduce something like universals.

## The Main Problems for Resemblance Nominalism

Resemblance nominalism faces three classical challenges, identified by Price and refined by subsequent discussion:

**The Companionship Problem**: Suppose every red thing is also round (as a matter of law or metaphysical necessity). Then the class of red things and the class of round things are identical. The resemblance nominalist cannot distinguish redness from roundness as distinct properties, since their instances are the same.

*Trope-based response*: Even if every red thing is also round, the *redness-tropes* and the *roundness-tropes* of those things are numerically distinct. The class of redness-tropes is distinct from the class of roundness-tropes even if they always co-occur. Using tropes as the primary resemblance relata handles the companionship problem.

**The Imperfect Community Problem**: A set of objects might be such that each pair of members resembles each other in some respect without there being any single property that all members share. For example: {a red-round thing, a red-loud thing, a round-loud thing}. Each pair resembles (in one of the three properties), but there is no property that all three share. Simple pairwise resemblance does not suffice to characterize a genuine property class.

*Trope-based response*: The problem arises for object-level resemblance nominalism. With tropes, the relevant resemblance is among specific tropes of a specific type, not among objects as wholes. The imperfect community problem does not arise for trope-level resemblance because tropes are already individuated by their specific qualitative character.

**The Coextension Problem**: Two distinct properties whose instances always happen to coincide cannot be distinguished by their extensions. Resemblance nominalism, which identifies properties with their resemblance classes, cannot distinguish such co-extensive properties.

*Modal response*: Appeal to possible worlds — two properties are distinct if they have different extensions in some possible world. The class of red things and the class of round things diverge in some possible worlds, so they are distinct properties. But this requires either *modal realism* (possible worlds are real) or *modal primitivism* (the modal facts are primitive), each with its own metaphysical costs.

## Rodriguez-Pereyra's System

Rodriguez-Pereyra's *Resemblance Nominalism* (2002) is the most technically sophisticated contemporary defense. His system:

1. **Tropes as primary relata**: Resemblance holds primarily among tropes, not among concrete objects.
2. **Primitive resemblance**: Resemblance is an unanalyzable primitive. There is no further explanation of why tropes resemble; they just do or do not.
3. **Resemblance classes from tropes**: Properties are resemblance classes of tropes. The property red is the class of all redness-tropes.
4. **Object-level resemblance derivative**: Objects resemble each other in a respect iff their tropes of that type resemble.
5. **Handling the regress**: The resemblance regress is not vicious because at each level (object resemblance, trope resemblance, resemblance-trope resemblance) we are dealing with a logically and ontologically distinct domain.

Rodriguez-Pereyra argues that this system handles the three classical problems, avoids universals, and offers a parsimonious and naturalistically respectable account of properties. Critics question whether the primitive resemblance facts are truly explanatory and whether the trope-level machinery is genuinely simpler than immanent universals.
