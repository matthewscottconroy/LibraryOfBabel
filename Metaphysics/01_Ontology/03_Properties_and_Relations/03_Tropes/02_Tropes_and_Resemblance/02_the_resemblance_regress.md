# The Resemblance Regress

The resemblance regress is a problem for any view that tries to ground property-talk in resemblance rather than in universals. It runs as follows. Suppose we say that a and b are both red because they resemble each other and the class of paradigm red things. What grounds this resemblance? If there is a resemblance universal — a single entity instantiated by every pair of resembling things — then we have reintroduced universals and abandoned the nominalist program. If the resemblance relation is itself a particular, then we have a resemblance trope relating a and b. But what makes this resemblance trope resemble the resemblance trope relating c and d? A further resemblance... and so on.

Bertrand Russell made this regress argument against resemblance nominalism in *The Problems of Philosophy* (1912). He concluded that unless we accept at least one universal — the universal resemblance — nominalism cannot get started.

Several responses are on the table. One is to accept a single universal (resemblance) while maintaining nominalism about all other properties. This is H.H. Price's "moderate nominalism." It stops the regress at the cost of admitting one universal, which critics find ad hoc.

A second response is to hold that resemblance among tropes is an internal relation — it holds in virtue of the intrinsic characters of the tropes themselves and requires no further relational entity. Two charge-tropes resemble because of what they are, not because of a further relation between them. This is the most popular response among trope theorists, but it requires being careful about what "intrinsic character" means for a trope.

A third response accepts that resemblance is a relational universal but argues that this is the only universal needed, and that building an ontology around resemblance is less costly than building one around an unlimited range of universals.

## Russell's Original Argument

Russell's argument in *The Problems of Philosophy* is worth reconstructing carefully. Russell considers the resemblance nominalist's program:

- Step 1: All white things resemble each other.
- Step 2: Two white things share not the universal whiteness, but merely stand in the relation of resemblance.
- Problem: Resemblance itself is a *universal* — the same relation holds between each pair of resembling things. If resemblance is a universal, the nominalist has at least one universal. If resemblance is not a universal, then what grounds the claim that "resemblance relates a and b" is the same kind of fact as "resemblance relates c and d"?

Russell concludes: "It will be useless to say that there is a different resemblance for each pair, for then we shall have to say that these resemblances resemble each other, and thus at last we shall be forced to admit resemblance as a universal."

The argument is a *regress* argument: if we try to avoid universals by appealing to resemblance among particulars, we generate a new resemblance (between the resemblance-particulars) that requires explanation, and the explanation involves a new resemblance, and so on without end.

## The Internal Relations Response

The most influential contemporary response to the resemblance regress is the *internal relations* response, associated with trope theorists including Campbell and Maurin:

**Key claim**: Resemblance among tropes is an *internal* relation — it holds in virtue of the intrinsic characters of the tropes themselves, and requires no further relational entity to ground it.

If resemblance is internal, then the fact that t₁ resembles t₂ is not an additional fact over and above the fact that t₁ is a redness-trope and t₂ is a redness-trope. The resemblance supervenes on the natures of the tropes. Consequently, we do not need to posit a further entity (the resemblance universal or the resemblance-trope) to ground the resemblance fact.

**The regress is blocked**: Since resemblance among tropes is grounded in the intrinsic natures of the tropes, there is no need to explain "what grounds this resemblance" by appeal to a further entity. The resemblance is grounded in what the tropes are, not in a further relation between them.

**The challenge**: What is the "intrinsic character" of a trope? If a trope is a particular property-instance, its character seems to be what makes it the kind of trope it is (a redness-trope rather than a roundness-trope). But what makes it a redness-trope? Its resemblance to other redness-tropes. This threatens circularity: the trope's character determines its resemblances, but the trope's kind-membership is fixed by its resemblances.

The response: the circularity is not vicious. Tropes have their intrinsic character primitively — they are just the particular qualitative instances they are, prior to any resemblance relations. The resemblance relations are then internal consequences of this primitive character.

## Comparative Assessment

The resemblance regress is a genuine challenge, but all three responses have defensible forms:

1. **Accept one universal (resemblance)**: This is conceptually clean but loses the parsimony motivation for nominalism. If we accept the universal resemblance, why not accept other universals directly?

2. **Internal resemblance**: This is the most popular among contemporary trope theorists. Its success depends on whether "intrinsic character" of a trope can be explained without circularity.

3. **Primitive resemblance**: Accept that resemblance facts are primitive and brute — at some level, explanation must terminate. This is honest but may seem unsatisfying.

All three responses face versions of the demand for further explanation: what stops the regress? The honest answer from all sides is: explanation must stop somewhere, and the question is where to stop and with what primitive commitments. The regress is not an argument that nominalism is impossible — it is an argument that nominalism has a cost, just as universals realism has the cost of mysterious instantiation.
