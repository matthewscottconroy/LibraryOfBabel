# Tropes as a Solution to Resemblance Problems

One reason trope theory has attracted philosophers working on resemblance nominalism is that tropes offer a way to handle certain puzzles that pure resemblance nominalism struggles with. In particular, tropes promise a more fine-grained account of the respects of resemblance.

Pure resemblance nominalism faces difficulty explaining resemblance in a respect. Two red things resemble each other in color but not in shape; two round things resemble in shape but not in color. If resemblance is unanalyzed, we need some way to capture this "in a respect" qualifier — but respects of resemblance look suspiciously like properties, which the nominalist is trying to avoid.

Tropes provide a natural solution: resemblance is always between specific tropes, not between things in general. Two things resemble in color because their color-tropes resemble each other. Two things resemble in shape because their shape-tropes resemble each other. The "respect" of resemblance is just the type of trope involved: color-tropes, shape-tropes, mass-tropes, and so on. We do not need to invoke universals to capture the different respects of resemblance — we just invoke different types of trope.

This move shows why tropes and resemblance nominalism naturally go together. Rodriguez-Pereyra's version of resemblance nominalism is explicitly a trope-based account: what resemble are the tropes of individuals, not the individuals themselves (in the first instance). The resemblance relations among tropes are then the foundation for property attributions.

Of course, tropes do not dissolve all problems. The resemblance among tropes still needs to be explained, and the regress argument applies to tropes just as it does to individuals. But the trope version gives more precise resources for handling the regress, because it can exploit the fact that trope-resemblance is a relation between entities of a single unified type, unlike the heterogeneous resemblance relations among ordinary concrete objects.

## Respects of Resemblance: The Technical Problem

The technical problem for object-level resemblance nominalism is the failure to distinguish respects of resemblance without covertly appealing to properties:

Suppose we say: a and b resemble each other in a property P iff a and b resemble each other *in respect of P*. But this is circular — we have used "property P" to specify the respect. The nominalist needs to cash out "respecting" without appealing to properties.

One attempt: a and b resemble each other in a *part* of their character — the redness-aspect of a resembles the redness-aspect of b. But "redness-aspect" is just another name for the redness-trope. The attempt to avoid tropes by appealing to "aspects" or "ways of being" naturally generates trope-talk.

Trope theory makes this implicit trope-talk explicit: respects of resemblance just *are* the relevant tropes. Two things resemble in color because their color-tropes (which are distinct particular entities) resemble each other. The respect is given by the type of trope, and types of trope are individuated by the resemblance classes of tropes — completing the circle of trope-theoretic explanation.

## Comparison with Object-Level Resemblance Nominalism

H.H. Price's original resemblance nominalism took objects themselves as the primary resemblance relata. Red things resemble each other in color; the class of objects that resemble each other in the relevant way constitutes the property red.

Problems with the object-level view:

1. **Respects**: Objects resemble each other in many respects simultaneously. A red ball and a red block resemble in color but not in shape; a red ball and a blue ball resemble in shape but not in color. Without respects of resemblance, the similarity relation is undiscriminated.

2. **Companionship**: Objects that are always co-extensive collapse into the same property.

3. **Imperfect community**: As discussed, there can be classes of objects that pairwise resemble without sharing any property.

Trope-level resemblance nominalism handles all three:

1. **Respects**: Each trope is already specific to a respect — a redness-trope resembles other redness-tropes, not roundness-tropes. Respects are individuated by trope types.

2. **Companionship**: Even if all red things are round, their redness-tropes and roundness-tropes are numerically distinct. The resemblance classes are distinct.

3. **Imperfect community**: The problem arises at the object level because objects have many tropes. At the trope level, the resemblance is between specific tropes of a specific type, so the imperfect community configuration does not arise within a single trope-type.

## Tropes and the Sparse/Abundant Distinction

Trope theory naturally accommodates Lewis's distinction between *sparse* (natural) properties and *abundant* properties. On the trope view:

**Sparse properties** correspond to resemblance classes of *exactly resembling* tropes: those classes within which every pair of members exactly resembles every other. The class of all charge-tropes, all mass-tropes — these are sparse, natural properties. They carve nature at its joints because they are defined by *exact resemblance* among fundamentally similar entities.

**Abundant properties** correspond to broader resemblance classes or to arbitrary subsets of tropes. The "property" of being either red or round is the class of tropes that are either redness-tropes or roundness-tropes — a gerrymandered collection whose members do not exactly resemble each other.

The sparse/abundant distinction is thus built into the structure of resemblance classes: natural properties are defined by exact resemblance, arbitrary "properties" by partial or inexact resemblance. This gives the trope theorist resources for Lewis's naturalness doctrine without positing any additional primitive beyond resemblance among tropes.
