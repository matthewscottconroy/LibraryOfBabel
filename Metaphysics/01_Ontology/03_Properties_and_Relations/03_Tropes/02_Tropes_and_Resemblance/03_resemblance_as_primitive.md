# Resemblance as Primitive

One response to the resemblance regress is to accept that resemblance is a primitive, unexplained feature of reality. Tropes just do resemble or not; there is no further analysis of this fact. This view has the virtue of simplicity and the vice of appearing to leave an important metaphysical fact unexplained.

The primitivist about resemblance argues that all explanations must terminate somewhere, and there is no reason why resemblance among tropes cannot be one of the fundamental, unanalyzed features of the world. The alternative — universals — does not really explain resemblance either; it merely locates the explanation in a different place: two things resemble because they share a universal. But what grounds the universal's being the way it is? The realist about universals must also terminate somewhere.

David Lewis famously argued that natural properties — those that figure in genuine similarity and in the laws of physics — are primitive. He did not think similarity was itself analyzable into something deeper. Instead, he postulated a primitive distinction between natural and gerrymandered properties, with natural properties being those that "cut nature at its joints." This amounts to accepting resemblance (or similarity) among natural properties as a primitive, much as the trope theorist accepts resemblance among tropes.

The pragmatic question is then: whose primitivism is more costly? The trope theorist who takes primitive resemblance accepts: (1) particular properties as entities, and (2) primitive resemblance facts. The realist about universals accepts: (1) universal properties as entities, and (2) instantiation as a primitive tie. Neither view is free of primitive commitments.

What distinguishes the views may ultimately be the kinds of phenomena they explain most naturally. Trope theory is often said to be better suited to a particularist, empiricist outlook; universals realism fits naturally with a more rationalist picture in which the patterns we discover in science reflect real features of the universal structure of reality.

## The Role of Primitive Commitments

Every metaphysical theory must eventually accept some primitive, unexplained facts. The question is not whether there are primitives but which primitives are most theoretically economical. Candidate primitives and their associated theories:

**Universals realism**: Accepts *universals* and *instantiation* as primitives. Instantiation is the fundamental relation between a universal and its instances; it is not further analyzed.

**Resemblance nominalism**: Accepts *tropes* and *resemblance* as primitives. Resemblance is the fundamental relation between tropes that grounds property attributions; it is not further analyzed.

**Class nominalism**: Accepts *perfectly natural classes* (or *naturalness*) as primitive. The distinction between natural and gerrymandered classes is not further analyzed.

**Predicate nominalism**: Accepts *predicates* and their *extension* as primitive. There is no further explanation of what makes predicates true of their instances.

From the standpoint of primitive commitments, resemblance nominalism and universals realism are in the same boat: both accept a primitive "tying" relation (resemblance vs. instantiation) and a primitive kind of entity (tropes vs. universals). The choice between them is partly a matter of which primitive seems more intelligible or more theoretically fruitful.

## Lewis's Primitive Naturalness

Lewis's approach is instructive for understanding the role of primitives in the resemblance debate. In "New Work for a Theory of Universals" (1983), Lewis argued that a distinction between *perfectly natural* and *non-natural* properties is needed to do philosophical work across many domains: causation, laws, mental content, reference, and similarity.

Lewis's perfectly natural properties are not universals (he is nominalist-friendly), but they function like what universals were supposed to function like: they ground genuine similarity, figure in the laws of nature, serve as the reference of predicate terms, and individuate possible worlds.

Lewis left naturalness as a *primitive* feature of properties (conceived as sets of possible objects): some sets of possible objects are perfectly natural (the set of all actual and possible electrons, for instance); most are not (the set of all actual and possible things that are either red or square or prime). The distinction is not analyzable; it is a fundamental feature of modal reality.

This shows that even within a broadly nominalist framework, something like the resemblance-class structure requires a primitive to identify the right classes. The trope theorist's primitive resemblance, Lewis's primitive naturalness, and the universalist's primitive instantiation are all ways of marking the same fundamental fact: not all predicates are created equal; some carve nature at its joints, and some do not.

## Primitivism and Explanation

The objection to primitivism is that it provides no *explanation*: to say that resemblance is primitive is to say that there is no answer to "why do these tropes resemble?" This may seem unsatisfying.

But the demand for explanation must itself be examined. If we require that *every* resemblance fact be explained by something more fundamental, we generate a regress: each explanation invokes further resemblances (or further entities) that themselves require explanation. At some point, explanation must stop. The question is whether stopping at resemblance among tropes is more or less satisfying than stopping at instantiation of universals.

There is a pragmatic consideration in favor of primitive resemblance: resemblance is a familiar, phenomenologically accessible feature of experience. We encounter resemblance directly in perception and comparison. Accepting resemblance as a primitive at least grounds the metaphysics in something accessible, unlike the abstract relation of instantiation between a particular and a transcendent universal.

This phenomenological advantage may be worth something, even if it does not constitute a decisive argument. And given that all explanations must terminate, choosing to terminate at resemblance is at least as well-motivated as choosing to terminate at instantiation.
