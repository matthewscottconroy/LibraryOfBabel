# Contemporary Trope Theory

Trope theory has grown considerably in sophistication since D.C. Williams's founding papers. Contemporary trope theorists have addressed challenges in the foundations of physics, the theory of causation, and the ontology of mind, developing a rich and technically demanding framework.

One active area is the application of tropes to the theory of causation. If causes produce effects in virtue of specific property instances — not just properties in general but this particular heat here causing this particular expansion there — then trope theory provides a natural account of singular causation. E.J. Lowe, Chris Swoyer, and others have developed trope-based causal theories that appeal to individual tropes as the causal relata, rather than abstract universals or general event-types.

Another area is the trope ontology of mind. If mental events are distinct from physical events, one way to express this distinctness is in terms of their trope constituents: a mental event has mental tropes — particular instances of phenomenal properties — that are not identical to any physical tropes, even if the events are correlated. Trope theory thus provides a framework for articulating non-reductive views of the mental without appealing to abstract universals.

The relationship between tropes and natural kinds has also received attention. If natural kinds — gold, water, electron — are real, what makes them so? A trope-theoretic answer: members of a natural kind share exactly resembling tropes of the kind-constituting properties. The kind is not a universal but a maximal resemblance class.

Contemporary defenders of tropes include Jonathan Schaffer, who has used trope-theoretic resources in his account of monism; Markus Schrenk and Rani Lill Anjum, who have applied tropes to causation and dispositions; and Anna-Sofia Maurin, who has done careful foundational work on the logic and ontology of trope theory.

## The Grounding-Theoretic Turn

One of the most significant developments in contemporary trope theory is its articulation within the framework of grounding theory. The question "what grounds what?" now structures debates about tropes:

**Tropes ground property facts**: The truth of "a is red" is grounded in a's having a redness-trope. The trope is the *truthmaker* for the property attribution.

**Tropes ground resemblance facts**: The resemblance between a and b in color is grounded in the resemblance between a's color-trope and b's color-trope, which is in turn grounded in the intrinsic characters of the two tropes.

**Tropes ground event facts**: Events are changes in the trope-constitution of objects. The event of the rose's turning yellow is the replacement of a redness-trope by a yellowness-trope.

**Tropes ground causal facts**: Causal relations hold among tropes, not merely among events. The heat-trope of the stove caused the expansion-trope of the metal.

This grounding-theoretic framework gives trope theory precise resources for articulating the priority relations among facts at different levels of description and connecting the trope level to higher-level chemical, biological, and social facts.

## Tropes in the Philosophy of Mind

The application of tropes to philosophy of mind is one of the most philosophically interesting contemporary developments. Several positions use trope-theoretic resources:

**Non-reductive physicalism with tropes**: Mental events have mental tropes — particular instances of phenomenal or intentional properties — that are not identical to any physical tropes, even when mental and physical events are correlated. This articulates a form of property dualism (distinct mental and physical trope types) without substance dualism (mental and physical tropes belong to the same events or persons).

**Trope theory and qualia**: Phenomenal properties — the qualitative character of experiences — are naturally thought of as tropes: this particular phenomenal redness, numerically distinct from any other occurrence of phenomenal redness, located in the experiencer's mind. The hard problem of consciousness concerns why these phenomenal tropes exist and how they relate to physical tropes.

**Tropes and the exclusion problem**: If mental events are caused by mental tropes and physical events are caused by physical tropes, and if mental and physical tropes are distinct, then the question arises: are mental causes redundant given physical causes? Trope theory clarifies the exclusion problem by distinguishing the tropes (types of property-instances) from the events (complexes of tropes) and asking precisely how mental and physical trope-causation are related.

## Formal Trope Theory

Contemporary work has also developed formal logical frameworks for trope theory. The main challenges:

**Identity conditions**: When are two tropes identical? The standard answer: t₁ = t₂ iff they have the same bearer and the same qualitative character. But this presupposes that we can identify bearers and qualitative characters independently of the tropes — which is circular if bearers are bundles of tropes and qualitative characters are resemblance classes.

**Individuation**: How are tropes individuated from each other? If redness is a single trope in the rose, is it one trope or many (the redness of the petal, the redness of the stamen, etc.)? The question of trope-granularity is non-trivial.

**Existence conditions**: Can tropes exist uninstantiated — without a bearer? The standard view (tropes are "incomplete entities") says no: a trope essentially depends on its bearer. But this requires articulating the dependence relation precisely.

Anna-Sofia Maurin's foundational work (*If Tropes*, 2002) and Markus Schrenk's work on trope theory and dispositions have addressed these formal challenges with increasing rigor.
