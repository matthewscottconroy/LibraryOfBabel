# Tropes and Causation

The relationship between tropes and causation is one of the most actively developed areas of trope theory. The core idea is that causal facts involve not just objects and their general properties but specific tropes — the particular property-instances of particular objects at particular times.

When the heat from a stove causes water to boil, the relevant cause is not just the property of heat in general, nor the stove as a whole, but the specific heat-trope of this stove at this moment. The causal relatum is the trope: a particular instance of the property that plays the causal role in this specific interaction.

This move to tropes as causal relata solves a problem that arises for type-based causal theories. If we say that causes and effects are events described by general property-types, we face the problem of explaining how two events of the same type can differ in their causal efficacy in different contexts. In contrast, tropes individuate causally relevant factors at a finer grain: this heat-trope caused this boiling, even though another heat-trope of the same magnitude in a different context might have produced a different effect (because of different surrounding tropes).

C.B. Martin developed the idea that causation is the mutual manifestation of reciprocal partners: the heat-trope (a power) meets the water's solubility-trope (its complementary power to be heated and eventually boil), and together they produce the manifestation. The causal interaction is not mediated by laws imposing themselves on categorical properties; it is the direct expression of the dispositional natures of the tropes themselves.

This approach connects trope theory to powers ontology: tropes that are dispositional powers have their causal roles constitutively, not contingently. The causal history of the world is, at the trope level, the progressive mutual manifestation of particular powers — a picture that is both metaphysically rich and empirically grounded.

## Singular Causation and Tropes

Classical Humean accounts of causation characterize it in terms of *regularity*: C caused E iff events of type C are regularly followed by events of type E. This is a *type-level* account: it appeals to regularities among general event types, not to singular causal facts about particular events.

Critics of the Humean account press the *pre-emption* and *overdetermination* cases: two fires converge on a house; either would have caused the destruction, and one gets there first. Which is the *cause*? Type-level regularity cannot distinguish them; they are events of exactly the same type.

Trope causation offers a natural solution: the cause is the *particular fire-trope* that directly produced the *particular destruction-trope*. Even if both fires would have produced similar destruction-tropes, only one actually did — because only one actually interacted with the house's combustibility-tropes at the critical moment. Singular causation is thus a matter of particular trope interactions, not of type regularities.

This is sometimes called *process causation* or *productive causation*: the causal process is the direct interaction of particular power-tropes producing particular manifestation-tropes.

## The Powers Ontology and Tropes

The view that tropes are *powers* — dispositional entities directed toward specific manifestations — integrates trope theory with the broader powers ontology of Ellis, Mumford, and Molnar. On this combined view:

- Fundamental tropes are pure powers — dispositional entities constitutively directed toward specific manifestation-types.
- Causation is the mutual manifestation of complementary power-tropes: the heat-trope's power to raise temperature and the water-trope's power to vaporize at 100°C mutually manifest in the event of boiling.
- Laws of nature are necessary consequences of the essential natures of the power-tropes involved — not contingent regularities imposed from without.

This picture gives a deeply *particularist* account of causation: each causal interaction is a unique event of particular power-tropes manifesting together. General causal laws hold because all tropes of a given type have the same essential causal power — which is just to say that they are tropes of the same type.

## Causal Relevance and the Exclusion Problem

The *exclusion problem* in philosophy of mind concerns whether mental causation is possible if physical events causally close: if every physical effect has a sufficient physical cause, then mental causes seem epiphenomenal. Trope theory provides resources for addressing this:

**Trope solution 1 (Multiple realization)**: A mental event has both mental tropes (phenomenal, intentional properties) and physical tropes (neural firing patterns). The mental tropes are distinct from the physical tropes but are realized by them. The mental tropes cause the mental effects; the physical tropes cause the physical effects. If mental and physical causation are distinct causal processes (distinct trope interactions), there is no exclusion.

**Trope solution 2 (Token identity)**: At the token level, mental tropes and physical tropes are identical — the pain-trope just is the C-fiber-firing-trope in a given instance. The mental description and the physical description pick out the same trope under different concepts. Then mental causation is just physical causation under a mental description.

**Trope solution 3 (Emergent tropes)**: Mental tropes are genuine emergent entities — distinct from any physical tropes — that exercise their own causal powers. This is property dualism at the trope level. The exclusion problem is real: mental and physical tropes would causally overdetermine their joint effects. The problem is to explain why this overdetermination is systematic and not coincidental — which the trope dualist must do by positing emergence laws or other bridges.

Trope theory clarifies the exclusion problem by making explicit the fine-grained ontological commitments involved: which tropes are identical, which are distinct, and which causally interact with which.
