# Tropes and Causation

The theory of causation has been one of the most productive areas for the application of trope theory. Classical accounts of causation treat causes and effects as events, and events as changes in properties of objects. Trope theory offers a finer-grained analysis: the cause is not just an event of type F but a particular F-trope — this fragility here, not fragility in general.

The appeal of this move is to singular causation: the causal relation between a specific cause and a specific effect, not mediated by any generalization. When a particular billiard ball strikes another and the second moves, there is a direct causal connection between this impact-trope and this motion-trope. The causal relata are not types or universals but particular instances.

C.B. Martin and John Heil developed the "powerful qualities" view in which properties are simultaneously categorical (they are what they are) and dispositional (they are constitutive powers directed at their manifestations). On this view, causation is the mutual manifestation of complementary powers: when a match's power to ignite meets oxygen's power to support combustion in the right conditions, both powers are mutually manifested in the event of burning. These powers are tropes — particular instances of the property-types.

Trope causation also connects to debates about the exclusion problem in philosophy of mind. If mental events cause physical events, but the physical world is causally closed, then mental causes seem redundant. A trope solution distinguishes the mental tropes from the physical tropes even when they are co-located in the same event: the mental trope and the physical trope are numerically distinct, so the mental trope's causing something is not pre-empted by the physical trope's causing it — unless one accepts a further principle that rules out overdetermination.

The resulting debate is technically complex, but tropes provide the fine-grained ontological resources that make the problem tractable.

## Causation as Trope Interaction

The trope-theoretic account of causation develops in two main directions:

**Productive causation**: Causation is a real, productive process in which one trope brings about another. The heat-trope of the flame produces the light-trope of the photons emitted. This production is not mediated by any law or regularity — it is the direct "oomph" of the cause trope generating the effect trope. This view is associated with Anscombe, Cartwright, and (with tropes specifically) Martin and Heil.

**Neo-Humean causation with tropes**: Even if tropes are the fundamental entities, causal relations among them are just regularities — patterns of trope co-instantiation across the history of the world. This preserves the parsimony of the Humean account while using tropes as the fundamental level. Lewis's counterfactual analysis of causation can be applied to tropes: event e₁ (consisting of trope t₁) causes event e₂ (consisting of trope t₂) iff, if t₁ had not occurred, t₂ would not have occurred.

The productive view is more congenial to powers ontology; the neo-Humean view is more congenial to the categorical property view.

## The Martin-Heil "Powerful Qualities" View

C.B. Martin and John Heil developed a view that attempts to reconcile the categorical and dispositional aspects of properties within a trope framework. Their *powerful qualities* thesis holds:

- Every property (every trope) is both a quality (a categorical intrinsic character) and a power (a dispositional directedness toward a specific manifestation).
- These are not two separate aspects of a property but one and the same property considered from two sides: the *what-it-is* side (its qualitative character) and the *what-it-does* side (its causal power).
- Causation is the mutual manifestation of these powers: two tropes with complementary dispositional aspects jointly produce a manifestation when they interact.

The powerful qualities view avoids both pure categoricalism (properties have no intrinsic causal natures) and pure dispositionalism (properties have no intrinsic qualitative character). Both aspects — quality and power — are equally real and inseparable features of every trope.

This view has been developed further by Sophie Gibb, Jonathan Jacobs, and others, who have applied it to the metaphysics of mind, causation in quantum mechanics, and the problem of causal relevance.

## Tropes and Causal Powers Ontology

The integration of trope theory with causal powers ontology (Ellis, Bird, Mumford, Anjum) is one of the most productive theoretical moves in recent metaphysics. The combined view holds:

1. Fundamental properties are tropes — particular, non-repeatable property-instances.
2. These tropes are essentially dispositional — each is constitutively directed toward a specific type of manifestation.
3. Causation consists in the mutual manifestation of complementary power-tropes.
4. Laws of nature are not external constraints on tropes but expressions of their essential dispositional natures.

This view promises:
- **Necessity of laws**: If tropes are essentially dispositional, the regularities we observe are necessary consequences of the tropes' natures — not contingent Humean regularities.
- **Singular causation**: Each causal interaction is a particular event of specific power-tropes mutually manifesting — not a case of a general regularity.
- **Unification**: Causation, laws, and properties are unified in the single category of dispositional tropes. There is no need for separate theories of causation, laws, and properties — they all flow from the nature of the fundamental tropes.

The main challenge is the circularity problem identified for pure dispositionalism: if every trope is a pure power defined by its relations to other powers, the entire web of powers seems to float free of any qualitative anchor. The powerful qualities response — that tropes are simultaneously qualitative and dispositional — provides the anchor without abandoning the dispositional analysis of causation.
