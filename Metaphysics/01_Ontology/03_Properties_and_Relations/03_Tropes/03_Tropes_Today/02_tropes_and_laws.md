# Tropes and Laws

How trope theory relates to laws of nature is a question that has received increasing attention as both trope theory and the metaphysics of laws have matured. There are two broad approaches.

On the first approach, tropes are compatible with a Humean view of laws. Laws are regularities over the pattern of tropes across the history of the world: whenever a trope of type F is instantiated, a trope of type G is also instantiated. The law does not involve any additional necessary connection or governing relation; it is just a generalization over the mosaic of trope instantiations. This preserves trope theory's commitment to particulars while accepting Hume's denial of necessary connections in nature.

On the second approach, tropes support a non-Humean, necessitarian view of laws. If tropes are dispositional — if their natures include causal powers directed at specific manifestations — then the regularities we observe are not brute coincidences but expressions of what the tropes essentially are. A charge-trope necessarily attracts opposite charges because having that power is part of what it is to be a charge-trope. The law of electromagnetic attraction is not a pattern imposed on top of the tropes; it flows from their essential natures.

This second approach connects trope theory to the essentialist tradition in philosophy of science. Brian Ellis's scientific essentialism, Alexander Bird's pandispositionalism, and C.B. Martin and John Heil's powerful qualities view all make use of trope-like entities (individual property-instances) whose essential natures ground natural necessity.

The main challenge for the necessitarian trope view is explaining modal variety across possible worlds. If tropes necessarily have their causal powers, then any world with the same fundamental trope-types must have the same laws. But it seems conceivable that different worlds could have different laws — which requires either that trope-types can vary across possible worlds (and thus different worlds have different trope-types, not just different laws) or that some laws are only contingently connected to trope-types.

## The Humean Trope Account of Laws

The Humean view of laws holds that there are no necessary connections in nature — only regularities. Trope theory can accommodate this view:

**Lewis-Humean account with tropes**: Laws are the *best system* generalizations over the mosaic of trope instantiations. The mosaic is a vast collection of particular tropes located at spacetime points. The laws are the generalizations that achieve the best balance of simplicity and informativeness when describing this mosaic.

On this view:
- Laws are not fundamental — they supervene on the pattern of trope instantiations.
- No necessity is built into the tropes themselves; the regularities are just patterns.
- The modal claims we make about laws ("electrons must have negative charge") are not about intrinsic necessities in the tropes but about what holds in all nearby possible worlds with the same laws.

This preserves the economy of Humeanism while using tropes as the fundamental units of the mosaic. The tropes have no inherent causal powers; their dispositions are Rylean — they consist in patterns of instantiation, not in genuine powers.

## The Essentialist Trope Account of Laws

The essentialist view holds that the fundamental tropes are essentially dispositional: their causal roles are part of their essential natures, not contingent additions. On this view:

**Ellis-type view**: Natural kinds are defined by clusters of essentially connected tropes. All electrons have charge, mass, and spin tropes that essentially go together — they are the essential nature of being an electron. The laws connecting charge to electromagnetic behavior are necessary because they follow from the essential nature of the charge-trope.

**Bird's pandispositionalism**: All fundamental tropes are pure powers — they have no categorical quality beyond their dispositional role. Laws are necessary because the dispositional roles are constitutive of the trope-types. Every possible world with charge-tropes must have the same electromagnetic laws; there are no "charge-tropes" that don't behave electromagnetically.

This view makes laws *metaphysically necessary* — they hold in all possible worlds with the relevant tropes. Whether this is a virtue or a vice depends on whether we think laws are necessary or contingent. Scientific essentialism (Ellis) says necessary; Humeanism says contingent.

## The Laws-Tropes Interface in Practice

Contemporary philosophy of science has articulated several positions that use tropes (or trope-like entities) in accounts of laws:

**Cartwright's capacities**: Nancy Cartwright argues that what are fundamental in nature are not laws but *capacities* — particular causal abilities of things. Her capacities are close relatives of dispositional tropes: individual causal powers of specific kinds of things. Laws are derivative — they describe the manifestations of capacities under idealized conditions.

**Mumford's eliminativism**: Stephen Mumford argues that laws of nature do not exist as separate entities from the properties of things. What we call "laws" are facts about the dispositional natures of properties (or tropes) — not further entities over and above the properties themselves. Laws are *in the things*, not *imposed on them* from outside.

**Esfeld's minimal ontology**: Michael Esfeld has developed a parsimonious version of the Bohmian interpretation of quantum mechanics in which the fundamental ontology consists of "matter points" with no intrinsic properties and a "universal wave function" that describes their relational configuration. The laws of quantum mechanics are encoded in the wave function, which is a structural fact about the configuration of matter points. This approach combines elements of trope theory (particular property-instances of configuration) and structural realism (the structural facts are prior to the individual properties of the points).
