# Trope Theory: Assessments

After more than half a century of sustained development, trope theory is now a mature philosophical position with a large and sophisticated literature. An honest assessment must acknowledge both its genuine achievements and its remaining difficulties.

Among its achievements: trope theory provides a unified ontology that handles both the particular and the general aspects of the world. It avoids the mysterious entity of the bare particular while still explaining how objects have determinate natures. It provides natural resources for the theory of causation (via causal tropes), the theory of laws (via dispositional tropes), and the theory of similarity (via resemblance among tropes). It fits naturally with a broadly naturalistic worldview that takes scientific description seriously.

Among its difficulties: the bundling problem — explaining what unites a collection of tropes into a single object — remains contested. The compresence relation that bundles tropes is either itself a trope (with its own bundling problem) or a universal (which reintroduces what trope theory was supposed to replace). The resemblance regress is answered rather than dissolved. The individuation of tropes — what makes two exactly resembling tropes two rather than one — requires careful treatment.

Perhaps most fundamentally, trope theory must explain why tropes are the right level of granularity for an ontology of properties. Why are individual property-instances the fundamental entities, rather than something more or less fine-grained? What makes a redness-trope one entity rather than a collection of smaller entities or a determinable with no fully determinate individual instances?

These questions do not constitute decisive objections — every serious metaphysical position faces analogous foundational challenges. But they indicate that trope theory is best understood not as a solution that closes down discussion but as a framework that organizes discussion in a productive way. The best work in trope theory continues to engage these challenges with increasing precision and rigor.

## Genuine Achievements

**1. Overcoming the particularity/generality dichotomy**: The classic puzzle is how to account for both the particularity of individual things (this rose, not roses in general) and the generality of properties (redness is shared by many things). Trope theory dissolves this dichotomy by positing entities that are both: tropes are particular (this redness, not redness in general) yet property-like (they are qualitatively characterized, not bare).

**2. Naturalistic credentials**: Tropes are concrete particulars — they are located in space and time, they causally interact with each other and with other tropes, and they are the kinds of things that can be measured and quantified. This gives trope theory better naturalistic credentials than views that posit abstract universals existing outside space and time.

**3. Causal individuation**: Tropes allow for more precise causal individuation than event-type accounts. The specific heat-trope of this particular stove at this moment is the cause of this particular expansion, not heat-in-general. This matches our ordinary causal talk, which is typically about *particular* causes and *particular* effects.

**4. Truthmakers**: Tropes provide a natural account of truthmaking — the relation between truth and reality. "This rose is red" is true because the rose's redness-trope exists. The trope is the concrete particular in virtue of which the proposition is true.

## Remaining Difficulties

**1. The bundling problem**: As analyzed in the discussion of trope bundle theory, the question of what unites a collection of tropes into a single object remains a challenge. Compresence (co-location) is too weak and generates its own version of the problem; mutual dependence (Simons's nuclear bundle) is more sophisticated but requires careful characterization.

**2. The granularity problem**: What determines the grain of tropes? Is the rose's redness one trope or many (the redness of each petal, the redness of each point on each petal)? The natural answer — the redness of the rose *as a whole* — requires an account of how tropes can belong to composite objects, which is itself complex.

**3. The resemblance regress**: As discussed extensively in the resemblance entries, the regress is not dissolved by tropes but rather relocated. Trope theorists must accept either primitive resemblance among tropes (which Russell's argument targets) or a sophisticated internal-relations account (which requires careful development).

**4. Modal issues**: If tropes are particular, they exist only in the actual world (or in a specific possible world). Cross-world comparisons of tropes — required for modal claims about properties — are difficult without either (a) accepting *transworld* tropes or (b) developing a counterpart theory for tropes.

## Comparative Assessment

Trope theory occupies a distinctive niche in the metaphysics of properties:

| Criterion | Universals | Tropes | Predicate Nominalism | Class Nominalism |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Explains similarity | Shared universal | Resembling tropes | No principled account | Extension-based |
| Avoids abstract entities | No (universals are abstract) | Partially (tropes are concrete) | Yes | Partially (sets are abstract) |
| Grounds causation | Via property-instances | Via tropes directly | Difficult | Difficult |
| Grounds laws | Nomic universals | Dispositional tropes | Unclear | Regularity over classes |
| Handles individuation | Via bare particulars | Via trope compresence | Via objects | Via class membership |

Trope theory performs well on the causal and truthmaker dimensions, less well on the bundling and individuation dimensions. Its main competitors — universals realism and class nominalism — have complementary strengths and weaknesses. The choice among them is ultimately a matter of which theoretical costs are considered most tolerable and which explanatory virtues are considered most valuable.

The continued growth of trope theory in analytic metaphysics suggests that, whatever its remaining difficulties, it captures something important about the ontology of properties that alternative views do not capture as well.
