# Tropes in Current Debate

Trope theory remains a live option in contemporary analytic metaphysics. Far from being a minority position, it is one of the main contenders for the fundamental ontology of properties, alongside universals realism and various forms of nominalism.

Recent discussions have focused on the question of whether tropes are prior to objects or vice versa. Traditional trope theory treated tropes as the fundamental constituents out of which objects are constructed by bundling. But some recent trope theorists have argued for a more symmetric picture: tropes and their bearers are mutually dependent, neither being prior to the other. On this view, there could not be a trope without a substance to bear it, nor a substance without its trope-constituents.

The connection between tropes and grounding theory has become an active topic. In grounding-theoretic terms, trope bundles ground facts about their bearers, and trope-resemblance grounds facts about general properties. But grounding itself requires a theory of the relata of grounding: are they facts, propositions, entities? And are tropes identical to facts (as some property-token theories suggest) or distinct from them? Sorting out the ontological categories involved has kept metaphysicians busy.

Tropes have also been applied to truthmaker theory. The truth of "this rose is red" is made true by the rose's redness-trope — a concrete particular that exists if and only if the proposition is true. This trope-truthmaker approach avoids some difficulties with universals-based truthmaking, while raising its own questions about the nature of trope existence and individuation.

The field continues to develop, with trope theory now standard in textbook treatments of property metaphysics alongside universals and nominalism.

## Current Debates: Tropes and Grounding

The emergence of grounding theory as a central framework in analytic metaphysics has given trope theory new resources and new challenges. Key grounding-theoretic questions for trope theory:

**Do tropes ground truths or are they truthmakers?**
- *Truthmaker view*: A trope is the truthmaker for the proposition it makes true. The truth of "a is red" is grounded in (made true by) a's redness-trope, which is a concrete particular existing in the world.
- *Grounding-fact view*: A trope grounds the truth of "a is red" not by being the truth's direct maker but by being the fundamental fact from which the truth follows. On this view, grounding connects the trope (as a fact about a's constitution) to the truth.

The distinction matters: on the truthmaker view, tropes directly make truths true; on the grounding-fact view, tropes ground higher-level facts that in turn make truths true.

**Are tropes facts or entities?**
Some metaphysicians (e.g., Lowe) distinguish between *facts* (states of affairs, obtaining conditions) and *entities* (things that exist). Tropes are typically treated as *entities* — they are things, not facts about things. But the line can blur: a trope (this particular redness) seems closely related to the fact *that this thing is red*.

**Do tropes ground or constitute property-talk?**
On the constitutive view, tropes *are* the properties of concrete objects — there is no grounding relation, only identity. The rose's redness just *is* the rose's redness-trope. On the grounding view, tropes ground the rose's being red, which is a fact distinct from the trope itself.

## Tropes and Neo-Aristotelian Metaphysics

The revival of neo-Aristotelian metaphysics (Lowe, Fine, Koslicki, Johnston) has given fresh impetus to trope-like entities. E.J. Lowe's *four-category ontology* posits:

1. **Substantial kinds**: General categories (human beings, electrons).
2. **Individual substances**: Particular instances of substantial kinds (this person, this electron).
3. **Attributes**: General properties (wisdom, charge).
4. **Individual modes**: Particular instances of attributes in individual substances (Socrates's wisdom, this electron's charge).

Lowe's "individual modes" are closely related to tropes — they are particular property-instances that depend on both the substance (the individual they belong to) and the attribute (the general property they instantiate). The four-category ontology provides a sophisticated framework for trope-like entities that connects to Aristotelian substance theory and Fine's essentialist metaphysics.

## Tropes and Empirical Science

One of the attractions of trope theory is its alignment with scientific practice. Scientists routinely quantify over particular property-instances:

- "This electron's charge" (a particular quantity trope)
- "The temperature of this sample at t₁" (a particular temperature trope)
- "The mass of this body" (a particular mass trope)

These are not merely descriptions of objects but references to specific property-instances that play causal roles in specific physical interactions. The temperature of this sample at t₁ causes the temperature of that sample at t₂ through heat transfer. The causal relata in physics are typically particular property-instances — which is exactly what tropes are.

This alignment with scientific talk gives trope theory a naturalistic advantage: it takes the fine-grained ontological commitments of science seriously rather than reducing them to a less differentiated scheme of objects and types.

## Current Research Directions

The most active current research in trope theory includes:

1. **Formal ontology of tropes**: Developing rigorous formal systems (using mereology, part-whole relations, and dependence logic) that characterize trope interactions and bundle structure.

2. **Tropes and quantum mechanics**: Applying trope theory to the interpretation of quantum mechanics — particularly to the question of whether quantum properties are better understood as tropes (particular property-instances) or as structural relations.

3. **Tropes and dispositions**: Integrating trope theory with the powers ontology to give a unified account of property-instances, causation, and laws.

4. **Tropes and biological properties**: Applying trope theory to the ontology of biological properties — functional properties, fitness, adaptations — which seem to be context-dependent and relational in ways that challenge both universals and simple class-nominalist accounts.
