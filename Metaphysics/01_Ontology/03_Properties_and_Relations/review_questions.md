# Review Questions — Properties and Relations

1. The one-over-many problem asks what grounds the fact that numerically distinct things share a single predicate. Realism answers with a shared universal; trope theory answers with exactly resembling but numerically distinct tropes; nominalism denies that any further ground is needed. Assess the dialectic: does the trope theorist succeed in avoiding universals, or does the appeal to a primitive resemblance relation among tropes simply relocate a universal through the back door?

2. Armstrong's immanent realism requires that every genuine universal be instantiated somewhere in the actual world, while Platonic realism allows for uninstantiated universals. What exactly depends on this disagreement? Consider: (a) whether mathematics can be accommodated within each view, (b) how each view handles the possibility of newly instantiated properties (properties no actual thing ever had), and (c) whether the principle of instantiation is itself an empirical or a priori constraint.

3. Lewis distinguishes abundant properties (one for every predicate, however gerrymandered) from sparse properties (those that carve nature at its joints and feature in laws and objective similarity). The sparse/abundant distinction does significant theoretical work — grounding laws, defining similarity, fixing the content of thought. But what makes a property "natural"? Is naturalness a primitive fact, derivable from universals, or explicable in terms of dispositions? What hangs on which answer one gives?

4. Bradley's regress is presented as an argument against external relations (requiring a connecting relation between relata, then a further relation to connect the connector, and so on). Russell replies that the regress is not vicious: instantiation and relating are primitive ontological connections that do not require mediators. Evaluate this exchange. Does the same regress apply to properties? If so, does the regress prove too much — undermining properties and relations alike — or does it show that some ontological connections must be primitive?

5. The chapter examines the categorical/dispositional distinction: categorical properties are intrinsic, occurrent features; dispositional properties are defined by their causal profile — what they would do in various circumstances. The dispositionalist (Bird, Ellis) argues that all fundamental properties are essentially dispositional, which grounds natural necessity without Armstrong's contingent necessitation relation. What does this imply for laws of nature? If properties are essentially dispositional, are laws necessary rather than contingent, and what does that entail for scientific counterfactuals?

6. Structural realism in philosophy of physics holds that spatiotemporal and quantum-mechanical relations are fundamental and that objects may be metaphysically dispensable. How does ontic structural realism relate to the debate between internal and external relations? If all fundamental relations are external — not grounded in intrinsic properties of relata — does this vindicate Russell's argument against Leibniz's reduction of relations to intrinsic monadic facts? And what becomes of the concept of substance if relations are primary?

7. Trope theory is presented as a one-category ontology: tropes play the role of both particulars (they are numerically distinct) and properties (they ground predication and similarity). Compare the explanatory costs of trope theory and Armstrong's two-category ontology (thin particulars plus universals in states of affairs). Which theory handles the following better: (a) Bradley's regress, (b) the laws-of-nature problem, (c) the individuation of objects?

8. The debate between realism about universals and nominalism is partly methodological: realists argue that universals are needed to explain natural similarity and laws; nominalists argue that the ontological cost is too high and that resemblance classes or natural classes can do the work. How does this debate connect to the question of what ontological methodology requires? Should the choice between realism and nominalism be settled by inference to the best explanation, by parsimony considerations, by fit with scientific practice, or by some other criterion — and does the methodological answer itself favor one side?
