# Abstract Objects

Here is a remarkable fact about mathematics: it works. The equations of physics are written in the language of mathematics, and they predict the behavior of physical systems with extraordinary precision. The mathematical structures that physicists use were, in many cases, developed by pure mathematicians pursuing no practical application whatsoever — and then discovered, sometimes decades later, to describe physical reality with uncanny accuracy. Wigner called this "the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics." But there is a prior philosophical question: what are the mathematical objects that mathematics is about? Where are the numbers, the sets, the functions, the spaces?

They are not physical. The number seven does not have a location, a mass, or a temperature. It is not made of matter, does not move, cannot be perceived through the senses. Yet it exists — or at least, that is what mathematical practice seems to presuppose. When a mathematician proves a theorem about prime numbers, she is not describing anything in the physical world; she is, apparently, describing something abstract. Abstract objects, if they exist, are entities that are non-spatial, non-temporal, and causally inert. They exist necessarily if they exist at all: the number seven could not fail to exist, could not have been different from what it is.

This conception — mathematical Platonism — has both immense appeal and a serious problem. Its appeal is that it takes mathematics at face value: mathematical statements are true or false, mathematical truths are objective, and mathematics is genuinely about something. Its problem is epistemological: how do we come to know about abstract objects if they have no causal powers and we are embedded in a physical world of causes and effects? Benacerraf's dilemma makes this precise: any adequate account of mathematical knowledge must be both semantically continuous with our account of scientific knowledge (numbers are what number-terms denote) and epistemically continuous with our account of knowledge generally (knowledge requires some causal connection to what is known). Platonism satisfies the first condition but apparently fails the second.

The alternatives — nominalism, fictionalism, structuralism — each attempt to do justice to mathematical practice without the Platonist ontology. Nominalism denies the existence of abstract objects and attempts to reconstruct mathematics using only physical or concrete resources. Fictionalism holds that mathematical statements are literally false — there are no numbers — but that mathematical discourse is a useful fiction that we engage in for practical purposes. Structuralism holds that mathematics is not about objects at all but about structures — patterns of relations that can be instantiated in many different ways — and that we need not commit to any particular objects as "the" numbers.

This chapter examines the full range of positions about abstract objects, focusing on mathematical entities, sets, and propositions. The debate is deeply connected to questions in epistemology, philosophy of language, and the metaphysics of modality. It also raises, in the sharpest possible form, the fundamental question that runs through all of ontology: what is it for something to exist, and what would it take for us to know that it does?
