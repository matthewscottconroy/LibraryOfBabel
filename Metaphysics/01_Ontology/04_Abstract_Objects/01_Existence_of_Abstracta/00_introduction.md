# The Existence of Abstract Objects

The number seven is not located anywhere in space. It has no mass, emits no radiation, does not change over time, and cannot be perceived by any of our senses. Yet we seem to refer to it, to know things about it, and to stand in cognitive relations to it that cannot be dismissed as merely linguistic or fictional. Seven is prime; 49 is its square; any collection of seven things shares a genuine feature with any other collection of seven things. These seem like facts — and facts need truthmakers. If the facts about the number seven are true, what makes them true?

The existence of abstract objects — objects that are non-spatial, non-temporal, and causally inert — is one of the most contested questions in metaphysics. The Platonist holds that abstract objects exist as genuinely as concrete objects: the number seven is real, though it exists outside of space and time; mathematical sets exist necessarily, with the same degree of reality as any physical particle; propositions are the primary bearers of truth-values, and they are abstract objects with objective existence. The Platonist's picture has the advantage of making sense of mathematical practice: when mathematicians prove theorems, they discover facts, not merely derive consequences of arbitrary definitions.

The cost of Platonism is epistemological. How do we come to know things about abstract objects? We learn about concrete objects through causal contact — perception, testimony, measurement — but abstract objects, being causally inert, cannot be perceived, measured, or otherwise encountered. Benacerraf's challenge is stark: if mathematical knowledge is genuine knowledge, there must be some account of how we come to know mathematical truths; but the causal theory of knowledge, or any epistemology that requires some connection between knower and known, appears unable to account for knowledge of causally isolated abstracta.

This section examines the arguments for the existence of abstract objects — the indispensability argument, the argument from mathematical practice, the argument from semantics — alongside the most compelling objections. The aim is to understand what hangs on the question and why it has proved so resistant to resolution.
