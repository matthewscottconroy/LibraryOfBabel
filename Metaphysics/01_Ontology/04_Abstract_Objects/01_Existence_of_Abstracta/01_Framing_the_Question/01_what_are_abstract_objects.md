# What Are Abstract Objects?

Abstract objects are, by a first approximation, objects that are neither located in space and time nor causally efficacious. The number 7 does not sit in any place; it was not created at any time; it does not push or pull anything. Yet it seems to be a genuine object — we can refer to it, quantify over it, and make true statements about it. If "7 is prime" is true, then there must be something that is prime, and that something is 7. So runs the simple case for the existence of abstract objects.

Philosophers have tried to characterize abstractness in several ways. The causal criterion says: abstract objects are those that cannot enter into causal relations. The spatiotemporal criterion says: abstract objects are those that lack spatiotemporal location. The dependence criterion says: abstract objects are those that do not depend for their existence on minds or matter. Each criterion captures something important but faces counterexamples and edge cases.

The causal criterion may be too strong: some philosophers argue that abstract objects can have causal relevance even if they are not themselves causes. The laws of nature, which are arguably abstract, seem to govern what happens even if they do not themselves do any pushing. Others argue that the causal criterion rules out too many things: events, properties, and relations are often thought to be causes, but they have an abstract character.

The spatiotemporal criterion has the advantage of being relatively clear but may exclude entities we are inclined to call abstract: fictional characters, institutional facts, and cultural objects all seem abstract in some sense, yet they may be located in a broadly spatiotemporal context — at least in the sense that they depend on events that occur in space and time.

Despite these difficulties, the category of abstract objects is philosophically indispensable. Mathematical objects (numbers, sets, functions), logical objects (propositions, truth values), linguistic objects (types, meanings), and normative objects (laws, norms) are all plausibly abstract, and understanding what they are requires getting clear on what abstraction amounts to.

## Candidate Criteria for Abstractness

Lewis proposed that the abstract/concrete distinction might be irreducibly primitive — we simply recognize abstract objects as a cluster without being able to provide a reductive definition. But several criteria have been proposed:

**The Way of Negation** (Dummett): Abstract objects are characterized by what they lack — they are not in space or time, not causally active, not perceivable. This is a purely negative characterization but captures the intuitive picture.

**The Way of Abstraction** (Dummett, Frege): Abstract objects are obtained by abstracting on equivalence relations among concrete objects. Frege's account of numbers proceeds this way: the number of F-things is the extension of the concept "equinumerous with F." The number is obtained by abstracting on the equinumerosity relation among concepts.

**The Way of Example** (Lewis): We just point to paradigm cases — numbers, sets, properties, propositions — and note that these entities (if they exist) have the characteristic features (non-spatial, non-causal, etc.) that we call "abstract."

Frege's three-realm ontology is influential: the physical world (concrete spatiotemporal objects), the mental world (individual mental states, subjective and private), and the *third realm* of abstract objects (objective, non-spatiotemporal, non-mental). Frege insisted on this three-way distinction against both physicalism (mathematics concerns the physical world) and psychologism (mathematics concerns the mental world).

## Abstract Objects Across Philosophical Traditions

The notion of abstract object has deep roots in philosophical history:

**Plato**: The Forms are abstract in the key respects — eternal, unchanging, non-sensory, accessible through reason alone. The Form of Beauty is not any particular beautiful thing but the universal, perfect instantiation of beauty that all particular beauties participate in.

**Frege**: Numbers are abstract objects introduced by contextual definition — their existence is established by the truth of the relevant identity statements, not by any direct ostension or perception.

**Russell**: Properties and propositions are abstract, Platonic entities that can be the constituents of facts and the objects of propositional attitudes.

**Quine**: Challenged the Platonic picture by arguing that we should accept abstract objects only when our best scientific theories are committed to them (the indispensability argument) and otherwise should adopt a policy of ontological parsimony.

**Benacerraf**: Showed that accepting abstract objects creates an epistemological crisis — how can finite, causally-embedded creatures like us have knowledge of causally inert abstract objects?

These historical threads are woven together in contemporary debates, which continue to seek a satisfying account of what abstract objects are, whether they exist, and how we can know about them.
