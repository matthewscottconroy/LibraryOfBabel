# Plato's Heaven

"Plato's heaven" is a popular label for the posited realm of abstract objects: a domain that exists independently of the physical world and of human minds, populated by mathematical objects, properties, propositions, and perhaps other abstract entities. The label captures the idea that abstract objects, on the Platonist view, inhabit a kind of otherworldly realm — present everywhere and nowhere, eternal and unchanging.

Plato himself was concerned primarily with the Forms — the abstract essences that sensible things participate in. Beauty itself, Justice itself, Equality itself — these were the proper objects of philosophical knowledge, transcending the imperfect and transient instances found in the sensible world. For Plato, the sensible world is a shadow of the real; what is genuinely real is the world of Forms.

Contemporary Platonism about abstract objects does not require commitment to the full Platonic apparatus of participation, Forms as causes, or the Theory of Recollection. It requires only that there are abstract objects that exist mind-independently and that mathematical (and perhaps other) truths are truths about them.

The attraction of Platonism is straightforward: it gives a simple and uniform explanation of mathematical truth. "7 is prime" is true because the number 7 exists and has the property of being prime. Mathematical knowledge is possible because we somehow have access to the mathematical realm — though explaining this access is notoriously difficult.

The picture of a Platonic heaven has also attracted mockery and suspicion. Quine and Goodman called abstract objects "the slums of ontology." Benacerraf argued that Platonism makes mathematical knowledge mysterious. Field argued that we can do without the denizens of Plato's heaven entirely. Despite this criticism, Platonism remains the default view among mathematicians and a serious position among philosophers, precisely because the alternatives face equally serious difficulties.

## Plato's Original Account

In the *Phaedo*, the *Republic*, and the *Parmenides*, Plato develops the theory of Forms in stages. The key features:

**Separation**: The Forms exist separately from the sensible things that participate in them. The Form of Equality is not any pair of equal sticks; it is a distinct entity that the sticks imperfectly instantiate.

**Perfection**: The Forms are perfect instances of what the sensible particulars imperfectly are. The Form of Beauty is perfectly beautiful; no sensible thing is perfectly beautiful (it is beautiful in some respects, under some conditions, from some perspectives).

**Eternality**: The Forms do not come into or go out of existence. The Form of Justice exists before any just actions occur and will exist after all just actions have ceased.

**Knowability**: The Forms are the proper objects of *episteme* (knowledge) rather than *doxa* (opinion or belief). Sensory experience gives us only beliefs about changing particulars; philosophical reason gives us knowledge of the eternal Forms.

The Platonic metaphor of *participation* (methexis) describes how sensible things are related to Forms: a particular beautiful thing participates in (or imitates, or instantiates) the Form of Beauty. What makes something beautiful is its having the right relationship to the Form.

## Frege's Third Realm

Frege articulated the most philosophically rigorous modern version of Platonism in his distinction between three domains:

1. **The external world**: Physical objects in space and time, which can be perceived.
2. **The mental world**: Ideas, images, representations in individual minds — subjective and private.
3. **The third realm** (*drittes Reich*): Objective thoughts (propositions), numbers, and other logical/mathematical objects — objective but non-physical.

Frege insisted on the third realm because of the objectivity of mathematics: mathematical truths hold independently of what anyone thinks, and they are the same for all thinkers. A thought (Gedanke) — a proposition — is not owned by any thinker; it is merely *grasped* by thinkers. The same proposition can be grasped by many different thinkers without their grasping a numerically different proposition.

Frege's third realm resolves what he saw as the fundamental error of psychologism: treating mathematical objects and truths as features of individual minds rather than as objective features of an abstract realm.

## Balaguer's Full-Blooded Platonism

Mark Balaguer's *full-blooded Platonism* (FBP) offers a contemporary refinement that responds to the epistemological problem. FBP holds:

**All possible mathematical structures exist**: Every consistent mathematical theory is true of (describes) some part of the Platonic realm. There is no gap between mathematical possibility and mathematical existence — every coherent structure is instantiated.

**Epistemological consequence**: We do not need a causal or mysterious faculty to access the Platonic realm. Any consistent mathematical theory already describes some actual abstract structures. Mathematical intuition and mathematical proof are reliable because consistent mathematics is always true of *something* in the Platonic realm.

**Indeterminacy consequence**: Since multiple set-theoretic constructions work as models of arithmetic (Benacerraf's problem), all of them are part of the Platonic realm. There is no fact about which sets the numbers "really" are — many set-sequences exist, and arithmetic is equally true of all of them.

Balaguer argues that FBP and fictionalism are the only two coherent positions — and that the evidence does not decisively favor one over the other, making the question of abstract objects genuinely undecidable by philosophical argument.
