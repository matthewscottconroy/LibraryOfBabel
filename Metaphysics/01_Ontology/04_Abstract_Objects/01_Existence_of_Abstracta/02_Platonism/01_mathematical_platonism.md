# Mathematical Platonism

Mathematical Platonism is the view that mathematical objects — numbers, sets, functions, geometric shapes — exist independently of the physical world and of human minds. They are not invented or constructed by mathematicians; they are discovered. When a mathematician proves that there are infinitely many prime numbers, she is not creating a new mathematical fact; she is uncovering a fact about an independently existing mathematical structure.

The case for mathematical Platonism begins with the objectivity of mathematical truth. Mathematicians across different cultures, times, and theoretical frameworks converge on the same results. The Pythagorean theorem was true before humans existed and would be true if humans ceased to exist. Mathematical truths seem to be necessary and universal in a way that suggests they are not merely human constructions.

Frege's argument for Platonism is particularly influential. He argued that numbers are neither physical objects (there is no physical object that is the number 7) nor mental objects (the number 7 is not a mental image or a concept in anyone's mind). Since numbers are not physical and not mental, they must be abstract — a third category of objective, non-spatiotemporal entities.

The indispensability argument (Quine-Putnam) provides a different route to Platonism. Our best scientific theories quantify over mathematical objects: physics talks about functions, sets, and numbers. If we should believe in the entities that our best theories are committed to, then we should believe in mathematical objects. The argument is controversial but has been highly influential.

Contemporary Platonists have developed sophisticated versions of the view. Full-blooded Platonism (Balaguer) holds that all possible mathematical structures exist; this avoids the epistemological problem by noting that any mathematically consistent theory describes some actually existing structure. Structuralism (Shapiro) holds that what mathematics describes are abstract structures, not particular objects — a view that blurs the line between Platonism and some forms of anti-Platonism.

## The Indispensability Argument Formalized

The Quine-Putnam indispensability argument is one of the most discussed arguments in philosophy of mathematics:

- P1: We ought to believe in the existence of all entities that are indispensable to our best confirmed scientific theories.
- P2: Mathematical entities (numbers, functions, sets) are indispensable to our best confirmed scientific theories.
- C: We ought to believe in the existence of mathematical entities.

P1 reflects Quine's criterion of ontological commitment: "to be is to be the value of a bound variable." The ontological commitments of a theory are determined by what the theory's existential quantifiers range over in its canonical formulation. If physics says "there exists a real-valued function f such that ..." then physics is committed to the existence of real-valued functions.

P2 is defended by noting the pervasive and ineliminable role of mathematics in physics. The Schrödinger equation is formulated in terms of complex-valued wave functions and Hilbert space operators. Maxwell's equations use vector calculus. Statistical mechanics uses probability theory. It seems impossible to state these theories without quantifying over mathematical objects.

**Objections to P1**: Maddy argues that mathematical components of scientific theories do not receive the same confirmational support as empirical components. Scientists accept idealizations (frictionless surfaces, perfectly elastic collisions) that are literally false but mathematically convenient. If mathematics plays a similar idealizing role, it may be indispensable without being confirmed.

**Objections to P2**: Field's nominalization program attempts to show that physics can be reformulated without quantifying over mathematical objects. Even if the program is technically challenging, it shows that the indispensability of mathematics is not in principle necessary.

## Arguments from Mathematical Practice

Beyond the indispensability argument, there are several arguments from the practice of mathematics that support Platonism:

**The discovery phenomenon**: Mathematicians consistently describe their work as *discovering* truths rather than *inventing* them. Ramanujan described receiving theorems from the Hindu goddess Namagiri in dreams — clearly a metaphor, but a metaphor for the experience of encountering truths that seem to exist independently of the mathematician's choices.

**The objectivity of mathematical truth**: Mathematical truths are necessary — they could not be otherwise. The primeness of 7 is not a contingent feature of the actual world; it holds in all possible worlds. This modal robustness suggests that mathematical truths are not merely about what we have chosen to stipulate but about an independently existing mathematical structure.

**The unity of mathematics**: Different branches of mathematics — algebra, analysis, topology, number theory — connect in unexpected ways. The deep connections between apparently unrelated areas (the link between prime numbers and the Riemann zeta function, the connection between topology and algebra in homological algebra) suggest that mathematicians are exploring a unified realm rather than developing isolated formal games.

**The applicability of mathematics**: Mathematics developed for purely abstract reasons (e.g., non-Euclidean geometry in the nineteenth century) turns out to be applicable to physical reality (general relativity uses Riemannian geometry). This "unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics" (Wigner) is most naturally explained if mathematics describes real structures that are also instantiated in physical reality.

## The Epistemological Challenge Revisited

The central difficulty for mathematical Platonism remains epistemological. If mathematical objects are abstract — causally inert, non-spatiotemporal — then how do we know about them? The Platonist's options:

1. **Gödelian intuition**: A faculty of mathematical perception analogous to sensory perception but non-causal. This explains the phenomenology of mathematical discovery but does not explain the faculty's reliability.

2. **Logical access**: We know mathematical truths through pure logic. Frege's logicism tried to reduce arithmetic to logic; neo-Fregeans (Hale, Wright) continue this program. But the logicist reduction requires principles (like Hume's Principle) whose status is contested.

3. **Implicit knowledge**: Mathematical truths are implicitly encoded in our conceptual practices. We grasp mathematical facts by mastering the relevant mathematical concepts, which are defined by their role in mathematical reasoning. Knowledge of abstract objects is mediated by knowledge of concepts.

4. **Indispensability access**: We know about mathematical objects indirectly, through their role in confirmed scientific theories, just as we know about unobservable physical entities indirectly through theoretical prediction and experimental confirmation.

Each response faces challenges, and the epistemological problem remains one of the most active areas of debate in the philosophy of mathematics.
