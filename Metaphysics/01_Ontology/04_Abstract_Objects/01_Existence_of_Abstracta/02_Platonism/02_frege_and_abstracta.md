# Frege and Abstract Objects

Gottlob Frege is the most important figure in establishing the modern philosophical framework for thinking about abstract objects. His work on the foundations of arithmetic, especially *The Foundations of Arithmetic* (1884) and the *Basic Laws of Arithmetic* (1893, 1903), developed a rigorous philosophical and logical account of numbers as abstract objects.

Frege's key move was to argue against two popular views of numbers: psychologism and empiricism. Psychologism held that numbers are mental entities — ideas in minds. Frege argued that this makes arithmetic subjective and contingent on the existence of minds, whereas arithmetic is objective and necessary. If numbers were mental, then arithmetical truths would be truths about particular mental states, and different people might have different arithmetics — which is absurd.

Empiricism (in the tradition of Mill) held that numbers are properties of physical collections or are derived from experience of physical groupings. Frege argued that this makes arithmetic contingent on empirical facts, whereas 2+2=4 is necessarily true, not just as a matter of how things happen to be physically arranged.

His positive account holds that numbers are abstract objects — the extensions of concepts. The number 2 is the extension of the concept "concept with exactly two instances." This gave a logicist foundation for arithmetic: numbers are defined in purely logical terms, and arithmetic is reducible to logic.

The logicist program was undermined by Russell's paradox, which showed that Frege's Basic Law V is inconsistent. But the philosophical account of numbers as abstract objects survived the technical collapse. Frege's arguments that numbers are objective and non-mental remain foundational to the philosophy of mathematics, and subsequent Platonists have built on his framework even while modifying the details.

## Frege's Three-Pronged Critique

Frege's *Grundlagen* opens with a sustained critique of existing views of numbers:

**Against psychologism**: If the number 5 were a mental representation, it would be subjective — my 5 and your 5 might be different. But mathematics is objective: the same mathematical truths are established for all who reason correctly. Moreover, mental representations are private and particular; mathematical objects are public and universal. A mental representation of 5 is always *a* representation, not the number itself.

**Against formalism** (in the simple sense): Formalists hold that arithmetic is the manipulation of formal symbols according to rules. But symbols are physical marks; there are not enough physical marks to represent all numbers. Moreover, arithmetic seems to say something *true about* numbers, not merely record that certain symbol-manipulations are permitted.

**Against Mill's empiricism**: Mill held that "2 + 1 = 3" records an empirical fact about aggregates: two things together with one thing always yield three things. Frege objected that: (1) we do not verify 2+1=3 by experiment; (2) numbers apply to very different kinds of things (2 horses, 2 concepts, 2 acts), so they cannot be properties of physical aggregates; (3) 0 and 1 have no plausible empirical correlates (you cannot point to "nothing" or to "a single thing" in a way that grounds the concept).

## The Context Principle and Abstract Objects

One of Frege's most important methodological principles is the *context principle*: "never to ask for the meaning of a word in isolation, but only in the context of a proposition." This principle grounds Frege's strategy for introducing abstract objects without causal or spatiotemporal access to them.

For numbers, the context principle works as follows:
- We know the meaning of "the number of Fs" not by pointing to a number but by knowing the conditions under which identity statements involving this expression are true: "the number of Fs = the number of Gs" iff the Fs and the Gs are in one-to-one correspondence.
- This contextual definition — Hume's Principle — fixes the meaning of number-terms without requiring any direct acquaintance with numbers.
- Numbers exist as the objects referred to by number-terms, given that the relevant identity statements have determinate truth-values.

This *abstractionist* or *neo-Fregean* strategy has been developed by Crispin Wright and Bob Hale into a program for establishing the existence of abstract objects in general. Any *abstraction principle* of the form "Σ(a) = Σ(b) iff a ∼ b" (where ∼ is an equivalence relation) can introduce abstract objects corresponding to the equivalence classes under ∼, if the abstraction principle is acceptable (consistent, conservative, etc.).

## The Legacy: Neo-Fregeanism

Frege's program collapsed with Russell's paradox (Basic Law V generates the paradox of the class of all classes that do not contain themselves). But his philosophical insights survived:

**Hale and Wright's neo-Fregeanism**: The logicist program can be restored if Basic Law V is replaced by Hume's Principle (the number of Fs = the number of Gs iff F and G are equinumerous). Hume's Principle is consistent (it has models) and arguably analytic — it is part of our concept of number. From Hume's Principle plus second-order logic, all of standard arithmetic can be derived (*Frege's Theorem*). This is neo-logicism: arithmetic is reducible to second-order logic plus one analytic principle about numbers.

**Abstract objects via abstraction**: The neo-Fregean program generalizes: for any equivalence relation ∼ on objects of a certain type, there are abstract objects corresponding to the equivalence classes. Directions are introduced by "the direction of line l = the direction of line m iff l and m are parallel." Real numbers by Dedekind cuts. Property-types by a property-abstraction principle.

The philosophical achievement: abstract objects can be known through their implicit definition by abstraction principles, without requiring any mysterious non-causal access to Platonic entities. Their existence is guaranteed by the truth of the relevant identity statements, whose truth-conditions are fixed by the abstraction principle.
