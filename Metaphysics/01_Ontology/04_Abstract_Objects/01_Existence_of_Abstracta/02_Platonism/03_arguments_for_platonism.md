# Arguments for Platonism

Several converging arguments support Platonism about mathematical and other abstract objects. No single argument is decisive, but together they present a formidable case.

The semantic argument: mathematical sentences appear to have the same semantic structure as ordinary sentences about concrete objects. "The moon is spherical" has the same form as "7 is prime" — both attribute a property to an object. If the first is made true by the moon's existing and being spherical, the second is made true by the number 7's existing and being prime. To deny that 7 exists while accepting that "7 is prime" is true requires an implausible semantic revisionism.

The objectivity argument: mathematical truths are objective — not relative to cultures, not dependent on what anyone thinks or believes, not revisable by decision. This objectivity is most naturally explained if there are mathematical objects that mathematical truths are about. If there were no mathematical objects, it would be mysterious why mathematical statements have the definite truth-values they appear to have.

The indispensability argument: our best scientific theories — especially physics — make essential use of mathematics. They quantify over mathematical objects, make claims that are true only if mathematical objects exist. Since we are committed to the ontology of our best scientific theories, we are committed to mathematical objects.

The uniqueness argument: mathematicians treat mathematical structures as unique — there is one structure satisfying the Peano axioms (up to isomorphism), one real number line (up to isomorphism). If mathematical structures were merely possible or fictional, this uniqueness would be unexplained. The Platonist explains it by saying that mathematical structures are actualized abstract objects with determinate properties.

Each argument has been contested, but defenders of Platonism argue that the alternatives face greater difficulties than the view they are meant to replace.

## The Semantic Argument in Detail

The semantic argument is one of the most powerful, because it demands that anti-Platonists engage seriously with the semantics of mathematical discourse rather than dismissing mathematical talk as "merely conventional" or "just a game."

The argument rests on the *face-value interpretation* of mathematical sentences: sentences like "7 is prime," "π is irrational," and "there are infinitely many prime numbers" are to be interpreted at face value — as statements about specific objects (numbers) with specific properties. The face-value interpretation is the most natural and has the advantage of assigning uniform semantics to mathematical and empirical sentences.

If the face-value interpretation is correct, then:
- "7 is prime" is true iff there is an object called 7 and it has the property of being prime.
- "There are infinitely many prime numbers" is true iff there exist infinitely many numbers with the primeness property.

These existential claims require mathematical objects to exist. Quine's criterion: the ontological commitments of a theory are the objects that must exist for the theory's quantified sentences to be true.

Anti-Platonists (fictionalists, formalists, nominalists) must either (a) show that the face-value interpretation is wrong and the sentences do not have their apparent meaning, or (b) show that the apparent meaning is right but the sentences are false (fictionalism), or (c) show that the apparent existential commitments can be paraphrased away. Each option faces difficulties.

## The Objectivity Argument

The objectivity of mathematics is perhaps the most phenomenologically compelling consideration for Platonism. Mathematical investigation feels like discovery, not invention:

- When mathematicians worked out the digits of π, they were not making choices — they were computing determinate facts.
- When Wiles proved Fermat's Last Theorem, he proved that no integers a, b, c satisfy a^n + b^n = c^n for n > 2 — a statement that was either true or false long before Wiles was born.
- The independence of the Continuum Hypothesis (from ZFC) is a determinate mathematical fact about the relationship between the Continuum Hypothesis and the axioms, even if the Continuum Hypothesis itself may be indeterminate with respect to some imagined absolute mathematical truth.

This objectivity is best explained if there is a mathematical realm that constrains mathematical investigation, making some mathematical claims true and others false independently of what mathematicians believe or decide.

Anti-Platonist responses:
- **Formalism**: Mathematical objectivity is the objectivity of logical entailment — a claim is mathematically "true" iff it is derivable from the accepted axioms. But Gödel's incompleteness theorems show that not all mathematical truths (in any given sense) are derivable from any fixed consistent set of axioms.
- **Fictionalism**: Mathematical objectivity is the objectivity of the *mathematical story* — certain claims are true within the fiction, others false. But this makes mathematical truth relative to a convention about which fiction is being told.
- **Structuralism**: Mathematical objectivity is structural — there are objective structural facts about what relations hold within structures. This is compatible with Platonism (Shapiro's ante rem structuralism) or with a modal nominalism (Hellman).

## The Indispensability Argument and Its Limits

The indispensability argument (see the dedicated entry) is powerful but has important limitations:

**Confirmational holism**: The argument requires that the mathematical components of scientific theories receive the same empirical confirmation as the empirical components. Maddy argues that scientists routinely use mathematical frameworks (idealized as continuous, infinite, etc.) that they know are not literally true of the finite, discrete physical world. If scientists do not believe the mathematical components to be literally true, they cannot be cited as evidence for mathematical Platonism.

**Dispensability**: Field's program attempts to show that mathematics is dispensable — physics can be reformulated without mathematical ontology. If successful, mathematics is merely a *representational tool* that earns no ontological commitment.

**Alternative explanations**: Even if mathematics is indispensable, the best explanation of its indispensability might not be that mathematical objects exist. It might be that mathematical structures are necessarily applicable to any possible physical world, regardless of whether they exist as abstract objects.

Despite these limitations, the indispensability argument remains an important consideration in the overall case for Platonism, providing a science-respecting route to mathematical ontology that Quine himself endorsed.
