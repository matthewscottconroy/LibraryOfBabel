# Nominalism About Abstract Objects

Nominalism — in the sense relevant to this section — is the view that there are no abstract objects. Reality consists entirely of concrete particulars: physical objects, events, mental states, and perhaps spatiotemporal regions. Mathematical objects, properties construed as universals, propositions, and other putative abstracta do not exist.

The motivation for nominalism is partly ontological parsimony (why posit abstract objects if we can explain everything without them?) and partly epistemological caution (how could we know about entities with no causal powers?). Nominalism is also connected to naturalism: if reality is just the physical world, there is no room in it for causally inert abstract objects.

The challenge for nominalism is to account for the apparent truth of mathematical statements and for the indispensability of mathematics to science. If the number 7 does not exist, what does "7 is prime" mean, and is it true?

Several strategies are available. Fictionalism (discussed in the next subsection) denies that mathematical statements are literally true, treating them as useful fictions. Modal nominalism (Hellman) translates mathematical claims into modal claims about what is logically possible — claims that do not quantify over mathematical objects but instead say what would follow if certain axioms were satisfied by objects of the right structural type.

Nominalist theories of properties, as discussed elsewhere in this unit, replace universals with either concrete particulars (resembling individual things) or tropes (particular property-instances). These avoid positing abstract universals while still accounting for the objective patterns that property-talk tracks.

The cost of nominalism is typically complexity: nominalist analyses of mathematical and property-talk tend to be more complicated than their Platonist counterparts, and they often invoke modal or counterfactual notions that raise their own metaphysical questions.

## The Varieties of Nominalism About Mathematics

Several distinct nominalist strategies are available for dealing with mathematical claims:

**Paraphrase nominalism**: Mathematical claims are systematically paraphrased into claims that do not quantify over abstract objects. "There exists a prime number between 10 and 20" is paraphrased as "It is possible that there be a sequence of objects satisfying the Peano axioms such that the thirteenth would be prime" — no actual abstract objects are required.

*Challenge*: The paraphrases invoke modal operators (*possibly*, *necessarily*), and modal claims may themselves require abstract ontology (possible worlds, possibilia) for their truth conditions. The nominalist may be trading one form of abstracta for another.

**Modal structuralism** (Hellman): Mathematical truths are conditionals under a modal scope. "2 + 2 = 4" is true iff "In any possible system satisfying the Peano axioms, the fourth position in the successor sequence is what you get by adding the second position to itself." This requires primitive logical possibility, not abstract objects.

*Challenge*: The primitive notion of logical possibility (without possible worlds as abstract objects) may be as obscure as abstract objects themselves.

**Constructivism** (Brouwer, Dummett): Mathematical objects are *mental constructions*. A number exists only if we can construct it; a mathematical truth obtains only if we can prove it. This makes mathematical existence dependent on human cognitive activity.

*Challenge*: Constructivism conflicts with classical mathematics. Many classical theorems (e.g., the law of excluded middle, the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem) are not constructively provable. It also makes mathematical truths contingent on human cognitive capacities, which seems to undercut the objectivity of mathematics.

**Field's Fictionalism** (treated in detail in the next entry): Mathematics is useful fiction. Field showed how Newtonian mechanics can be reformulated without quantifying over mathematical objects using only spacetime regions and betweenness relations among spacetime points.

## Quine's Criterion and Nominalist Responses

Quine's criterion of ontological commitment holds that the ontological commitments of a theory are the objects that must exist for the theory's canonical first-order formulation to be true. Applied to mathematics, this means: if our best physical theories quantify over numbers and sets in their canonical formulation, and if we believe our best physical theories, then we are committed to the existence of numbers and sets.

Nominalists respond to Quine's criterion in several ways:

1. **Reject the indispensability claim**: Mathematics is not truly indispensable. Field's project shows (or aims to show) that physics can be reformulated without mathematical ontology.

2. **Reject the confirmational holism premise**: Mathematical components of physical theories may not receive the same confirmational support as empirical components. If we use mathematics merely as a notational convenience without believing it to be literally true, we incur no ontological commitment to mathematical objects.

3. **Accept the criterion but deny mathematical indispensability**: Accept Quine's criterion but argue that physics, when properly formulated, can dispense with mathematical ontology. The quantification over mathematical objects in textbook physics is a feature of our imperfect formulations, not of the physical theory itself.

4. **Reject the criterion**: Replace Quine's criterion with a more fine-grained account of ontological commitment that distinguishes between substantive and instrumental uses of quantification. Mathematical quantification may be *instrumental* (not truth-apt in the realist sense) even if it occurs in canonical formulations.

## The Epistemological Advantage of Nominalism

Nominalism's primary philosophical advantage is epistemological: if there are no abstract objects, then the *epistemological problem* for Platonism does not arise. We need not explain how finite, causally-embedded creatures can have knowledge of causally inert abstract objects. Mathematical knowledge — on the nominalist's account — is knowledge of something else: the structure of physical systems, the patterns of valid reasoning, the logical consequences of axioms, or the content of useful fictions.

This epistemological advantage comes at the cost of a less natural account of mathematical truth and a more complex account of mathematical practice. Whether the advantages outweigh the costs is the central question in the debate between Platonism and nominalism about abstract objects.
