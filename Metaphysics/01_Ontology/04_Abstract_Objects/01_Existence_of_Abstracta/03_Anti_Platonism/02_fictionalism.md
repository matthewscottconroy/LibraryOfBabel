# Fictionalism About Abstract Objects

Mathematical fictionalism, developed most influentially by Hartry Field in *Science Without Numbers* (1980), holds that mathematical statements are not literally true — because there are no mathematical objects — but that mathematics is nevertheless an indispensable and useful tool for scientific reasoning. Mathematical claims are like claims about fictional characters: the claim "Sherlock Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street" is true within the fiction, not literally true; similarly, "7 is prime" is true within the mathematical story, not literally true of any existing object.

Field's project was to show that physics can be reformulated without any commitment to mathematical objects. He developed a nominalized version of Newtonian gravitation theory, replacing numerical claims about physical quantities with comparative claims about physical regions. If this nominalization works, then mathematics is not indispensable after all — it is a conservative extension of nominalized physical theories, useful but not truth-conducive.

The conservativeness claim is crucial: Field argues that mathematics does not prove anything about the physical world that could not in principle be proved without it. It is a shortcut, not a source of new physical information. If true, this undermines the indispensability argument: even if physics uses mathematics, it does not commit us to mathematical objects.

Critics have questioned both the nominalization program (can all of physics be nominalized?) and the conservativeness claim (is it really true that mathematics adds no physical content?). Mary Leng and others have developed more sophisticated forms of fictionalism that do not depend on a completed nominalization. A common theme is "hermeneutic fictionalism" about ordinary mathematical practice: mathematicians do not really believe in abstract objects any more than novelists believe their characters are real.

Fictionalism faces the challenge of explaining the objectivity and necessity of mathematics. If mathematical claims are merely fictional, why do mathematical proofs feel like discoveries rather than inventions?

## The Fiction Analogy

Fictionalism exploits the analogy between mathematical discourse and fiction:

- "Sherlock Holmes lived at 221B Baker Street" — true *in the fiction*, false in reality (Holmes did not exist).
- "7 is prime" — true *in the mathematical story*, false in reality (7 does not exist as an abstract object).

Both types of statement can be used felicitously without commitment to the literal existence of their subjects. We can reason within a fiction, make true claims within a fiction, and use fictional discourse to organize thought — all without believing that fictional characters exist.

The key question is whether the analogy is close enough to be philosophically illuminating. Differences between mathematical and fictional discourse:

- **Objectivity**: What is true within the mathematical story is not conventional or authorial in the way that what is true within Holmes's story is. Conan Doyle could have written Holmes differently; mathematicians cannot choose to make 7 composite.
- **Indispensability**: Holmes-fiction is entertainment; mathematics is (apparently) indispensable to science. If mathematics is fiction, why does it work so well?
- **Methodology**: Mathematical proof is not creative writing. Mathematicians prove theorems from axioms using valid reasoning; this is not analogous to an author's decisions about a character.

Fictionalists respond: the objectivity of mathematics is the objectivity of the mathematical *story* — given the axioms (which are themselves stipulations), the theorems follow of necessity. The "discovery" experience is real, but what is discovered is what follows logically from the adopted axioms, not what is true of independent abstract objects.

## Field's Conservativeness Argument

Field's fictionalism rests on a technical claim: mathematics is *conservative* with respect to nominalist (mathematics-free) physical theories. More precisely:

**Conservativeness**: For any nominalist physical theory N and any nominalist sentence S, if N + M (where M is the relevant mathematical theory) implies S, then N alone implies S.

In other words, adding mathematical axioms to a nominalist physical theory never allows the derivation of new nominalist (mathematics-free) consequences. Mathematics contributes no new physical information beyond what is already derivable from the physical theory alone.

If conservativeness holds, then mathematics is a *representational tool* — a useful language for organizing physical reasoning — rather than a *source of new facts* about the physical world. The indispensability argument fails: mathematics is indispensable as a tool, not as a source of ontological commitments.

Field proved conservativeness for his nominalized version of Newtonian mechanics. The extension to all of physics (especially quantum mechanics) is technically challenging and remains contested. Hartry Field himself acknowledged that the quantum mechanical case raises serious difficulties.

## Yablo's Figuralism

Stephen Yablo's *figuralism* (*Things: Papers on Objects, Events and Properties*, 2010) develops a sophisticated form of fictionalism that avoids some of Field's difficulties. Yablo argues that mathematical discourse is best understood as *figurative* rather than *literal*:

- Mathematical claims are *figures of speech* — they express truths about the concrete world in a roundabout, indirect way.
- "The number of planets is 8" is a figure of speech for "there are exactly 8 planets" — a concrete claim made vivid by mathematical packaging.
- We use mathematical figures to describe cardinality, magnitude, proportion, and structural features of concrete reality.

On Yablo's view, mathematical statements are not *false* (as Field sometimes suggests) but *neither true nor false in the literal sense* — they are figurative truths, which are "true" in the sense that they effectively express what they are figuratively expressing.

**Yablo's advantage**: Figuralism explains why mathematical discourse functions smoothly in scientific contexts without requiring mathematical objects to exist. It also avoids the puzzle of why fictions should be "literally false" — mathematical claims are not failed attempts at literal truth but successful exercises in figurative expression.

**Challenge**: Distinguishing figurative from literal discourse is difficult. Mathematical sentences look exactly like other literal sentences; a principled account of their figurative status is needed.

## Balaguer's Assessment: Underdetermination

Mark Balaguer (*Platonism and Anti-Platonism in Mathematics*, 1998) argues provocatively that the debate between Platonism and fictionalism is genuinely undecidable by philosophical argument. His Full-Blooded Platonism and fictionalism are both coherent and face no decisive objection. The choice between them may be a matter of metaphysical temperament rather than philosophical argument — a conclusion that itself has implications for how we understand the methodology of metaphysics.
