# Ontology of Abstract Objects

Grant, for the sake of argument, that abstract objects exist. The question immediately arises: what kind of thing are they? Not all abstract objects are obviously alike. Numbers, sets, propositions, properties, possible worlds, fictional characters, musical works — all have been proposed as abstract objects, but they seem quite different from one another. A set is constituted by its members; a proposition is the content of a belief or assertion; a musical work can be performed but not touched. What do all these things have in common that makes them "abstract," and is that commonality metaphysically deep or merely a family resemblance?

The abstract-concrete distinction is notoriously difficult to define. The most natural proposal — that abstract objects lack spatiotemporal location — works well for numbers and propositions but faces problems for events and some properties, which might seem abstract but appear to be located in time or space. The causal criterion — that abstract objects have no causal powers — faces problems for propositions and mathematical facts, which seem causally relevant (the form of the argument caused me to believe its conclusion). The dependence criterion — that abstract objects depend on minds or languages — leads to conceptualism, which has its own difficulties.

Different kinds of abstracta raise different specific problems. Sets are puzzling because of their sensitivity to membership: the set {a, b} would not be the set it is if b were replaced by c, yet sets seem to exist necessarily if they exist at all. Propositions are puzzling because of their relationship to truth and to the mental states that express them: is the proposition that snow is white mind-dependent? Numbers are puzzling because of the structure of the natural numbers: are they positions in an abstract structure (as structuralism holds), or are they independent objects that happen to have the structural relations they do?

This section examines each major category of abstract object in depth, asking what the category includes, how its members are individuated, and what their relationship to concrete reality and to human thought is. The goal is to move from the bare claim that abstract objects exist to a systematic understanding of the domain they inhabit.
