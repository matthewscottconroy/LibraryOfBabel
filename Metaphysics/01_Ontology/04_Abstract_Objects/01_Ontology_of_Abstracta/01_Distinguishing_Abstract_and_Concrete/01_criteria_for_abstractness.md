# Criteria for Abstractness

*How philosophers distinguish abstract from concrete objects.*

---

The abstract/concrete distinction is among the most fundamental in metaphysics, yet it is surprisingly difficult to characterize precisely. Paradigm abstract objects include numbers, sets, properties, propositions, and mathematical structures; paradigm concrete objects include tables, stars, people, and electrons. But what is the underlying criterion that separates the two categories?

Several criteria have been proposed:

**The causal criterion**: concrete objects can enter into causal relations; abstract objects cannot. Numbers do not cause anything and cannot be caused by anything. This criterion aligns with much of our intuition — we cannot stub our toe on the number seven — but it faces counterexamples: we might say that the abstract structure of DNA *caused* the organism's development, and theoretical entities in physics (wave functions, fields) seem both causally active and abstract in some sense.

**The spatiotemporal criterion**: concrete objects exist at some location in space and time; abstract objects exist outside space and time (or perhaps exist necessarily at all times and places). The number 7 is not anywhere in particular; it does not persist through time and was not created at some moment. This criterion is intuitive but has trouble with entities like *events* (which are spatiotemporally located but often treated as abstract or abstract-like) and *tropes* (which are spatiotemporal but have an abstractness quality).

**The exemplification criterion**: concrete objects exemplify properties; abstract objects are properties (or propositions, or structures). This is really a taxonomic distinction rather than a criterion for the distinction itself.

**The way-of-being criterion**: concrete objects have existence in the robust sense; abstract objects have a thinner or different mode of existence. This amounts to saying abstractness just is having a different mode of being, which requires elaboration.

Lewis suggested that the most honest answer is that "abstract" and "concrete" are primitive terms: we recognize the difference without being able to give a reductive analysis of it. The distinction may be a matter of theoretical convenience — we use "abstract" to categorize a cluster of entities (mathematical objects, universals, propositions) that resist standard causal-spatiotemporal analysis.

## The Negative Criterion and Its Challenges

The most influential single-factor account is Frege's negative approach: abstract objects are those that lack spatiotemporal location and causal efficacy. Frege distinguished three realms — the physical (spatiotemporal), the mental (inner, private), and the logical-mathematical (objective, non-spatiotemporal). Abstract objects inhabit the third realm: they are objective without being physical.

The negative criterion has explanatory appeal but faces principled objections. Consider the following cases:

**Borderline cases**: Sets of concrete objects (the set {Mars, Jupiter}) are typically classified as abstract, yet they seem to inherit spatiotemporal properties from their members — is the set located where its members are? Tropes (particular instances of properties) are spatiotemporally located (the redness of this apple is where the apple is) yet seem to have an abstract quality. Abstract artifacts like symphonies and novels are created at times and places, yet are not confined to any one spatiotemporal region.

**The problem with negative criteria**: A purely negative criterion — abstract objects are those lacking causal power and spatiotemporal location — tells us what abstract objects are *not* but says nothing about what they *are*. Philosophers who take this route are effectively acknowledging that we have no positive characterization of the abstract/concrete distinction beyond paradigm cases and the absence of certain features.

## Four Criteria Compared

Philosophers have offered four main proposals for positively or negatively characterizing abstract objects:

**1. The Way of Abstraction** (traditional): Abstract objects are obtained by a cognitive operation of abstraction — attending to some features of concrete objects while ignoring others. The concept *red* is obtained by attending to the redness of red objects and abstracting away the rest. This criterion is epistemological rather than ontological: it tells us how we come to have concepts of abstract objects, not what those objects are.

**2. The Way of Negation**: Abstract objects lack spatiotemporal location and causal power. This is the most widely used criterion, associated with Frege and much contemporary practice, but it is purely negative.

**3. The Way of Example**: "Abstract objects are things like numbers, sets, propositions, and universals — the entities typically discussed under that heading." This is the least informative criterion but captures our actual usage: we extend "abstract" to new cases by judging their similarity to acknowledged paradigms.

**4. The Way of Possible Worlds**: Abstract objects exist necessarily (in all possible worlds) or not at all; concrete objects exist contingently (in some possible worlds but not others). This criterion handles some cases well — numbers, if they exist, exist necessarily — but fits badly with abstract artifacts (it is possible that Beethoven might never have composed his Fifth Symphony) and with some controversial entities (mathematical fictionalists deny that mathematical objects exist necessarily, since they deny that mathematical objects exist at all).

## Hale's Criterion: Sorts and Sortals

Bob Hale (*Abstract Objects*, 1987) offers a more sophisticated account based on sortal predicates. Sortal predicates are count nouns that provide criteria of identity for the objects they apply to — "symphony," "number," "chair" are sortals; "music" and "furniture" are not (they are mass terms that do not individuate). Hale proposes:

**Hale's criterion**: An object is abstract if and only if the sortal predicate most fundamental to its identity conditions does not require spatiotemporal location.

On this view:
- Numbers are abstract: "number" provides identity conditions (same successor sequence) without requiring spatiotemporal location.
- Persons are concrete: "person" provides identity conditions that include spatiotemporal continuity.
- Symphonies are borderline: "symphony" provides identity conditions (same sequence of notes, proper performance history) that do not require a single spatiotemporal location, yet symphonies are created in time and can cease to exist.

Hale's criterion avoids the purely negative approach and grounds the distinction in facts about sortal predicates and identity conditions. Critics have argued that it ultimately presupposes rather than explains the abstract/concrete distinction, since we must already know which sortals are "abstract" to apply the criterion.

## Lewis's Deflationary Account

David Lewis argued that "abstract" is best treated as having no single unified meaning. In different philosophical contexts, "abstract" can mean:
1. Lacking spatiotemporal location
2. Lacking causal power
3. Being a universal rather than a particular
4. Being an intensional entity (a property or proposition)
5. Being accessible only through reason rather than perception

These properties coincide for the central cases (numbers, sets, propositions) but come apart in borderline cases. Lewis recommended using the more specific terms — "causally inert," "non-spatiotemporal," "universal" — rather than the umbrella term "abstract," which obscures more than it reveals.

This deflationary stance is philosophically modest but practically significant: it suggests that the abstract/concrete distinction does not carve nature at its joints. There may be no single metaphysically unified category of abstract objects — just a loosely related family of entities that philosophers have grouped together for convenience.

## The Epistemological Dimension

Whatever criterion we adopt, it carries epistemological implications. If abstract objects lack causal power, then we cannot perceive them, and our knowledge of them cannot arise from ordinary sensory processes. This is the heart of Benacerraf's epistemological challenge to Platonism: mathematical knowledge seems to require causal contact with its objects (on a causal theory of knowledge), but mathematical objects are causally inert.

Three broad responses are available:

**Rationalist epistemology**: we know abstract objects through rational intuition — a faculty of a priori insight that operates independently of sensory causation. Gödel held that we have something like mathematical perception, a direct cognitive grasp of abstract structures.

**Abstraction-based epistemology** (neo-Fregean): we know abstract objects through understanding abstraction principles — rules that fix the identity conditions of abstract objects without requiring causal contact. Knowing Hume's Principle ("the number of Fs = the number of Gs iff F and G are equinumerous") is sufficient to know about numbers, without requiring non-causal access to a Platonic realm.

**Structural epistemology**: we know abstract objects by knowing about the structures they participate in — and we know structures through concrete instantiations of them. Knowing the abstract natural number structure is a matter of grasping the pattern common to all concrete progressions (collections of fingers, stones, symbols).

Each epistemological response has implications for how we characterize abstractness. If we know abstract objects structurally, then their abstractness lies in their being structure-types rather than concrete instantiations. If we know them via abstraction principles, their abstractness lies in being defined by equivalence classes of concrete or less-abstract entities.
