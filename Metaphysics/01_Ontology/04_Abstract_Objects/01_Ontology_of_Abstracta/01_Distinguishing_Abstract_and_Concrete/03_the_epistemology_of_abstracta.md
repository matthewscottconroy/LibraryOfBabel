# The Epistemology of Abstracta

*The challenge of explaining how we can know about abstract objects if they are causally inert.*

---

The most powerful objection to Platonism about abstract objects is epistemological, stated forcefully by Paul Benacerraf in "Mathematical Truth" (1973). The objection combines two premises:

1. **Truth premise**: The truth conditions of mathematical statements are Platonist — the statement "there is a prime number greater than 1000" is true because of facts about abstract mathematical objects.

2. **Epistemological premise**: We can only know things that stand in causal relations to us — knowledge requires appropriate causal connection between the knower and the known.

If abstract objects are causally inert — if they cannot be perceived, manipulated, or detected — then there is no causal connection between mathematicians and the abstract objects their theorems are about. How, then, can mathematicians have knowledge of abstract mathematical truths?

This epistemological challenge has generated several responses. Gödel himself believed that we have a faculty of mathematical *intuition* — a rational faculty analogous to perception but non-sensory — that puts us in cognitive contact with mathematical abstracta. This faculty is much debated and its credentials are unclear, but it at least names the problem rather than dissolving it.

Quine and Putnam's indispensability argument provides a partial response: we have *indirect* knowledge of mathematical objects through their role in empirically confirmed scientific theories. We know about electrons indirectly through their effects; perhaps we know about numbers indirectly through the mathematical theories embedded in physics. But this response is vulnerable to the objection that the indispensability of mathematics is the indispensability of a useful notation, not evidence for the existence of abstract objects.

Structuralists like Shapiro argue that what we know in mathematics is structures, not objects, and structures can be understood as possibilities — possible patterns of relations. Knowledge of structures is then knowledge of modal facts, which may be more tractable epistemologically than knowledge of Platonist objects.

The epistemological challenge remains one of the most serious difficulties for Platonist metaphysics, and many anti-Platonist positions are motivated primarily by the desire to give a more tractable epistemology for mathematics while preserving its objectivity.
