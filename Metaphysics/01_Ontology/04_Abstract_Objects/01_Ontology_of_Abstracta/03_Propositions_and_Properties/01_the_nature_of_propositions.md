# The Nature of Propositions

*The abstract objects that are the bearers of truth and the contents of beliefs.*

---

Propositions are the standard candidate for the primary bearers of truth and falsehood. When we say "the proposition that snow is white is true," we are attributing truth to an entity — a proposition — that is expressed by the sentence "snow is white" but is distinct from it. The same proposition can be expressed by sentences in different languages; the sentence is a linguistic vehicle, the proposition is the content conveyed.

Propositions serve several theoretical roles simultaneously. They are the objects of propositional attitudes: to believe that it is raining is to stand in a belief relation to the proposition *that it is raining*. They are the relata of entailment: proposition P entails proposition Q just in case the truth of P necessitates the truth of Q. They are the contents of assertions and questions, the primary bearers of modal properties (the proposition that 2+2=4 is necessarily true; the proposition that it is raining is contingently true if true at all).

But what *are* propositions? Several accounts compete:

**Russellian propositions**: propositions are structured entities composed of the very objects and properties they are about. The proposition *that Socrates is wise* contains Socrates (the man) and the property of wisdom as constituents. This view captures the intuitively direct connection between propositions and the world but faces puzzles about propositions involving non-existent objects (the proposition *that Pegasus flies* cannot contain Pegasus if Pegasus does not exist).

**Fregean propositions** (Thoughts): propositions are made of senses rather than objects. The proposition *that Socrates is wise* contains not Socrates himself but a *mode of presentation* of Socrates — the sense of the name "Socrates." This allows propositions to be informationally distinct even when they concern the same objects (the Morning Star and Evening Star propositions differ even though both are about Venus).

**Possible-worlds accounts**: the proposition *that P* is identified with the set of possible worlds at which P is true. This is mathematically precise but cannot distinguish necessarily equivalent propositions (all necessarily true propositions become identical).

**Representationalist accounts**: propositions are abstract structures whose constituents are concepts or mental representations. This connects propositions to the philosophy of mind but raises questions about which mental representations count.

## The Theoretical Roles of Propositions

Propositions were introduced into philosophy to do theoretical work across several domains:

**Semantic content**: The semantic content of a sentence is a proposition. "Snow is white" and "Schnee ist weiß" express the same proposition — the same content — despite being syntactically different expressions in different languages. A theory of meaning for a language specifies, for each sentence, the proposition it expresses.

**Propositional attitudes**: Beliefs, desires, fears, hopes, and intentions all have contents — what they are about. To believe that it will rain tomorrow is to stand in the *belief* relation to the proposition *that it will rain tomorrow*. This is the *representational* view of mind: mental states are related to propositions, and the propositions give the mind its aboutness (intentionality).

**Logical relations**: Propositions stand in logical relations to one another. P entails Q iff necessarily, if P is true then Q is true. P is consistent with Q iff it is possible for both to be true simultaneously. These logical relations — entailment, consistency, contradiction — are relations among propositions rather than among sentences.

**Truth conditions**: Propositions have truth conditions essentially and non-contingently. The proposition *that water is H₂O* is true at any world at which water is H₂O — this is not a contingent fact about the proposition, it is constitutive of it.

## Russellian versus Fregean Propositions

The deepest division in the theory of propositions is between Russellian (object-based) and Fregean (sense-based) views.

**Russellian propositions** contain the actual objects (individuals, properties, relations) the proposition is about. The proposition *that Hesperus is bright* contains Hesperus (Venus) itself, not a name or mode of presentation of Venus. This is a *direct reference* theory of propositions: names refer directly to their objects, and the objects themselves are constituents of propositions.

*Advantages*: Direct reference is semantically simple. There is no mysterious intermediate layer of senses between words and things.

*Problems*:
- *Frege's puzzle*: If "Hesperus is bright" and "Phosphorus is bright" both contain Venus, they express the same proposition. But it seems one can believe the first without believing the second (before learning that Hesperus = Phosphorus). Russellian propositions cannot explain this difference.
- *Non-existent objects*: The proposition *that Vulcan is a planet* (Vulcan being the hypothetical planet once posited to explain Mercury's orbit) cannot contain Vulcan if Vulcan does not exist. What is the proposition about?

**Fregean propositions** (Thoughts, in Frege's terminology) contain senses — modes of presentation — rather than objects. "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" express different senses (ways of thinking of Venus) despite referring to the same planet. The proposition *that Hesperus is bright* contains the sense of "Hesperus" and the sense of "is bright"; it differs from the proposition *that Phosphorus is bright* because the senses differ.

*Advantages*: Frege's puzzle is solved — the two propositions genuinely differ in content because they contain different senses.

*Problems*:
- *Proliferation of entities*: Senses are abstract objects requiring their own epistemology and ontology.
- *The sense of a proper name*: What is the sense of "Aristotle"? There are many descriptions associated with Aristotle; which one (if any) is the sense of his name? Kripke's arguments suggest that names do not have Fregean senses at all.

## Structured Propositions and Possible-Worlds Propositions

**Structured propositions** preserve the constituents of propositions while explaining propositional structure. A structured proposition is an ordered n-tuple: the proposition *that Socrates is wise* might be ⟨Socrates, Wisdom, Predication⟩ — with Socrates, the property of wisdom, and a mode of combination as constituents. Structured propositions can distinguish logically equivalent propositions by their internal structure.

**Possible-worlds propositions** identify the proposition *that P* with the set of possible worlds at which P is true. This account:
- Gives a clean, mathematically tractable account of propositional content
- Explains logical relations: P entails Q iff the worlds at which P is true are a subset of the worlds at which Q is true
- Permits fine-grained accounts of probability (probability measures over propositions)

The central problem: *impossible worlds*. All necessarily false propositions are false at all worlds, so all necessarily false propositions are identified with the empty set. All necessarily true propositions are true at all worlds, so they are identified with the set of all possible worlds. Necessarily equivalent propositions cannot be distinguished.

This matters for intentional contexts: "Necessarily, 2+2=4" and "Necessarily, the Pythagorean theorem holds" are necessarily equivalent — but they are distinct beliefs. A student might believe one without believing the other. The possible-worlds account cannot account for this difference.

## The Ontological Status of Propositions

If propositions are abstract objects, the standard concerns apply: they are causally inert, non-spatiotemporal, and known (if at all) without perceptual contact. Three positions on the ontological status of propositions:

**Platonism about propositions**: Propositions are genuine abstract objects in Frege's third realm — neither physical nor mental, but objective and mind-independent. The proposition *that 2+2=4* existed before any mind grasped it and will exist after all minds are gone. Mathematical propositions are necessarily true abstract objects; contingent propositions (like *that it is raining now*) are true at some worlds and false at others.

**Nominalism about propositions**: There are no propositions. Propositional-attitude talk is true but does not refer to abstract propositions; instead, it describes the functional organization of mental states, or stands in direct relations to utterances, or is given a deflationary analysis that avoids quantification over propositions.

**Deflationism**: The truth predicate applied to propositions is deflationary — "the proposition that P is true" is equivalent to "P." There is no need to posit proposition-objects as the bearers of truth; truth is a feature of sentences or utterances, and propositional talk is a useful paraphrase.

The practical importance of the debate about propositions: any satisfactory account of belief, assertion, linguistic meaning, and logical inference must take a position on the nature of propositional content. The choice between Russellian, Fregean, and possible-worlds accounts has downstream implications for the semantics of natural language, the theory of mental representation, and the logic of modality.
