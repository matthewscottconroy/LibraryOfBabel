# Properties as Abstracta

*Whether properties should be understood as abstract objects in their own right.*

---

Properties occupy an interesting position in the debate over abstract objects. On the one hand, they seem clearly abstract: redness is not anywhere, has no mass, and cannot be touched. On the other hand, properties seem more intimately connected to the concrete world than mathematical objects: redness is wherever red things are, and its nature is revealed by examining red things rather than through a priori mathematical investigation.

The sparse/abundant distinction is crucial here. *Abundant* properties correspond to every predicate or open sentence: being red or round, not being a kangaroo, being self-identical. There are as many abundant properties as there are predicates, and they include gerrymandered, negative, and disjunctive properties with no objective unity. *Sparse* properties are the genuinely natural ones — those that carve nature at its joints, figure in genuine causal relations, and are discovered by science rather than stipulated by logic. Lewis called these the *perfectly natural* properties.

For Platonic realists, properties are paradigm abstract objects. The property of redness exists in the eternal, non-spatial Platonic realm, and red things in the concrete world participate in or instantiate this property. Properties can exist uninstantiated (the property of being a unicorn exists even though no unicorns do), and they exist necessarily (there is no possible world in which redness does not exist).

For immanent realists (Armstrong), properties are universals that exist only insofar as they are instantiated. They are not abstract in the sense of being causally isolated from the concrete world; rather, they are wholly present in the concrete things that have them. On this view, "abstract" applies to how we consider universals (abstracting them from particular instances) rather than to their mode of existence.

For trope theorists, what play the role of properties are not abstract universals but concrete particular instances. The redness of this rose is a concrete particular trope, though it is "abstract" in the etymological sense that it has been isolated by a mental act of abstraction from the full concrete particular (the rose).

These distinctions matter for the epistemology of properties: if properties are Platonic abstract objects, our knowledge of them requires an account of how we access an a-causal realm; if they are immanent universals or tropes, we know them by knowing concrete things.

## Properties in the Metaphysical Economy

Properties do theoretical work in multiple areas of metaphysics and semantics:

**Semantic role**: Predicates express properties; properties are what predicates denote in an indirect sense. The predicate "is red" expresses the property of redness; an object satisfies the predicate iff it has the property. This semantic role requires that properties be the sort of thing that can be expressed by predicates and possessed by objects — which sits well with abundant properties but raises questions about what exactly corresponds to "not being a kangaroo."

**Causal role**: Properties, especially sparse ones, are the constituents of causally relevant conditions. The charge of an electron, the mass of a particle, the temperature of a gas — these are natural properties that appear in causal explanations. It is because the electron has negative charge that it is repelled by other negatively charged particles. If properties are Platonic abstracta with no causal power, this causal role requires explanation.

**Modal role**: Properties ground modal facts. That gold is necessarily a metal (if it is) is grounded in the nature of the property of being gold. Essential properties — those that an object has necessarily — are properties whose possession is constitutive of the object's identity. If properties are abstract objects with essential natures of their own, they can play this modal grounding role.

**Universals in laws**: Laws of nature (on universals-based accounts) are relations among properties. "All copper conducts electricity" expresses a contingent necessitation relation N(C, E) between the property of being copper and the property of conducting electricity. This analysis requires properties to be the relata of law-making relations — robust enough to stand in structural relations to one another.

## The Sparse/Abundant Distinction

Lewis's distinction between sparse and abundant properties is fundamental to contemporary property theory:

**Abundant properties**: For every open sentence in a language — "being red," "being red or round," "not being a kangaroo," "being identical with Aristotle or being the Eiffel Tower" — there is a corresponding abundant property. The abundant properties form a collection as large as the powerset of the universe (or of possible objects). They are posited liberally: any predicate picks out a property.

**Sparse properties**: The sparse properties are those that correspond to genuine similarities in nature — properties that are causally efficacious, that appear in laws of nature, that ground objective resemblances between objects that share them. Lewis suggests these are the properties that physics ultimately discovers: charge, mass, spin, and their combinations. The sparse properties are far fewer than the abundant properties.

The distinction serves different theoretical purposes:
- **Semantics** requires abundant properties (or something similarly liberal): for any predicate to have a semantic value, and for the logic of predicate modification, negation, and disjunction to work smoothly, something like abundant properties are needed.
- **Metaphysics** of causation, laws, resemblance, and natural kind membership requires sparse properties: only natural properties generate genuine objective resemblances, figure in laws, and ground causal relations.

**The ontological question**: Are both sparse and abundant properties abstract objects? Armstrong is a sparse-property realist: only natural properties exist, and they exist immanently in their instances. Lewis accepts abundant properties as abstract objects (construed as sets of possible individuals) but sparse properties as whatever genuine features of nature the predicates "charge," "mass," etc. pick out.

## Platonic versus Immanent Realism About Properties

The central division in the metaphysics of properties:

**Transcendent (Platonic) universals**: Properties exist as abstract objects in a Platonic realm, independently of any particular instances. They are multiply instantiable in principle even if never actually instantiated. The property of being a perfect philosopher existed before any philosopher was born and would exist if no philosopher ever existed. The *relation* between a property and its instances is participation or instantiation — a sui generis metaphysical relation.

*Epistemological challenge*: If properties are causally isolated Platonic objects, how do we know them? We know that fire is hot by experiencing fires, but this gives us causal contact with fires, not with the abstract property of hotness. The Platonist must appeal to some non-causal form of knowledge for properties.

**Immanent (Aristotelian) universals**: Properties exist only in their instances. They are wholly present — not merely represented — in each instance. The property of redness is in this red apple and in that red bus and in the red coat. It is not "spread across" these instances; it is wholly present in each. When all red objects are destroyed, redness ceases to exist.

*Challenge*: How can one thing (the property of redness) be wholly present in many different places simultaneously? The immanent realist's answer — that universals are a different ontological category from particulars, one for which the "single location" requirement does not apply — seems to invoke just the kind of sui generis category that the Platonist invokes.

**Tropes**: Instead of universals (which are by definition multiply instantiable), tropes are particular instances of properties — the particular redness of this apple, distinct from the particular redness of that bus, even if the two tropes exactly resemble each other. Tropes are concrete (spatiotemporally located) rather than abstract (Platonic).

*Challenge*: If two tropes are numerically distinct despite being qualitatively indistinguishable, what individuates them? Trope theorists typically appeal to spatiotemporal location — the two tropes are distinct because they are located in different places. But this seems to make location the ultimate individuator rather than any intrinsic property-feature.

## Property Identity and the Individuation of Properties

What makes two properties the same property? Several criteria have been proposed:

**Coextension**: Properties P and Q are identical iff they have exactly the same extensions — every object that has P also has Q, and vice versa. This criterion is too coarse: "being a human" and "being a rational animal" might be necessarily coextensive without being the same property.

**Necessary coextension**: P = Q iff it is metaphysically necessary that P and Q are coextensive. This handles "human" and "rational animal" if these are necessarily coextensive. But it makes "water = H₂O" the same property as "H₂O = H₂O" — a questionable result.

**Hyperintensional identity**: P = Q iff they are hyperintensionally indistinguishable — substitutable in all contexts, including hyperintensional ones (belief contexts, explanation contexts, essence contexts). This is very fine-grained and close to how Fregean senses would individuate properties.

**Causal role identity** (Shoemaker): Properties are identical iff they necessarily make the same causal contribution in all circumstances. Two properties with the same causal profile are the same property. This yields a naturalistic individuation criterion for sparse properties.

The choice among these criteria determines which entities count as properties and shapes the broader picture of how properties figure in the metaphysical economy.
