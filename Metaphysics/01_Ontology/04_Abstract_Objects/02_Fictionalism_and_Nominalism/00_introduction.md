# Fictionalism and Nominalism

The most straightforward response to the puzzles generated by abstract objects is denial: perhaps there simply are no numbers, sets, or propositions — or if there are, they are not the causally isolated, mind-independent entities that Platonism requires. This is the nominalist family of views, and it is a serious philosophical position backed by powerful motivations. The ontological parsimony of nominalism is one motivation; the epistemological tractability is another. If there are no abstract objects, we do not need to explain how we came to know things about them; and if our mathematical practice can be vindicated without positing them, so much the better.

Classical nominalism — the view associated with Ockham and later with Quine's more austere moments — holds that the world contains only concrete particulars, and that apparent reference to abstract entities can be explained away. The challenge is to make good on this promise: our mathematical language is deeply committed to the existence of numbers and sets, and our best scientific theories quantify over mathematical entities. Hartry Field's program of scientific nominalism attempts to show that physics can be reformulated without reference to numbers, replacing them with spacetime regions and physical relations. Whether this program succeeds is a contested question.

Fictionalism offers a different strategy. Rather than denying that mathematical sentences are literally true, the fictionalist holds that they are true within the "fiction" of mathematics — much as it is true within the fiction of Sherlock Holmes that Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street, without Holmes actually existing. Mathematical discourse is valuable for the same reason fiction is valuable: not because it accurately describes a domain of Platonic entities, but because it provides a useful representational framework that helps us organize our thought about the concrete world. The fictionalist can accept all the mathematical truths that the Platonist accepts; she merely denies that those truths commit us to Platonic ontology.

Structuralism provides a third alternative: mathematical objects just are positions in abstract structures, and those structures might themselves be understood in various ways — as logical constructions, as possible concrete systems, or as genuine abstracta. Each variant of structuralism has different commitments and vulnerabilities.

This section examines these alternatives to Platonism with care, assessing whether they can fully vindicate mathematical and scientific practice without incurring the costs of abstract ontology.
