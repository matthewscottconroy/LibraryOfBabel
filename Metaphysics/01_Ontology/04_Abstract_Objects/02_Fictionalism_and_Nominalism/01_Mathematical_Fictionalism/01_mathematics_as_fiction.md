# Mathematics as Fiction

*The view that mathematical statements are useful falsehoods rather than truths about abstract objects.*

---

Mathematical fictionalism holds that mathematical statements, taken at face value, are false — because there are no mathematical objects for them to be about — but that this is not a problem because mathematics is a supremely useful fiction. We no more need mathematical objects to exist in order to benefit from mathematical reasoning than we need Sherlock Holmes to exist in order to enjoy detective fiction.

The fictionalist contrast class is typically the Platonist: the Platonist says mathematical statements are literally true and explains their truth by reference to abstract objects. The fictionalist says mathematical statements are *fictionally* true within the mathematical story, much as "Holmes lived at 221B Baker Street" is true in the Conan Doyle stories even though it is false that Holmes (a non-existent person) lived anywhere. Mathematical reasoning is reasoning within a fiction.

Hartry Field's version of fictionalism is sophisticated: he argues that mathematics is useful not because it is true but because it is *conservative*. Adding mathematical axioms to a physical theory does not yield any new physical consequences. Mathematics is like a language for organizing physical reasoning rather than a description of a further domain of entities. The usefulness of mathematics is thus explained by its structural applicability (it models the relevant physical relations correctly) without requiring abstract objects to exist.

Mark Balaguer's *full-blooded Platonism* provides an interesting foil: Balaguer argues that if there are mathematical objects, there are as many as there can possibly be — every consistent mathematical structure is instantiated. On this view, all consistent mathematical theories are true (of different parts of the Platonic realm), which dissolves the question of which set theory is the "correct" one. But Balaguer also argues that fictionalism is equally coherent, so the choice between full-blooded Platonism and fictionalism may be underdetermined by any available evidence.

The most challenging objection to fictionalism is the *wording problem*: if mathematical statements are false, then when a physicist says "the electron has a mass of 9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg," the statement seems to be false (since mass values involve numbers, and numbers don't exist). But surely physics is telling us something true about the world. The fictionalist needs an account of how false mathematical statements can be components of physically true theories.

## The Fiction Analogy: Strengths and Limits

Fictionalism exploits the analogy between mathematical discourse and literary fiction. We recognize that:

- "Sherlock Holmes lived in London" is true *in the fiction*, even though Holmes did not exist and did not live anywhere.
- "Sherlock Holmes was a surgeon" is false *in the fiction* — not because surgeons don't exist, but because the stories portray Holmes as a detective.
- We can reason within the fiction, draw conclusions, make predictions about what Holmes would do next, all without believing that Holmes is real.

Fictionalists claim the same structure applies to mathematics: "7 is prime" is true in the mathematical fiction; "7 is composite" is false in the fiction. We reason within the fiction using proof and logic. The fiction is useful because its structural features mirror structural features of the physical world.

**Where the analogy holds**: Both mathematical and fictional truths are in some sense objective — once the story is fixed, what is true in it is not arbitrary. We cannot decide that Sherlock Holmes was really a farmer just by declaring it; the story constrains us. Similarly, given the mathematical axioms, the theorems follow of necessity.

**Where the analogy strains**: Literary fiction is a cultural product, deliberately created, with authorial intent. Mathematics does not feel like creation — mathematicians describe their activity as discovery. The fictionalist must explain why mathematics *feels* like discovery even if it is really the exploration of a narrative structure. Mathematical necessity seems stronger than fictional consistency: it is not just that 7 being composite is inconsistent with the story, but that it is logically impossible.

## The Wording Problem and Physical Applications

The most serious objection to fictionalism concerns mixed mathematical-physical statements. Consider:

- "The mass of an electron is 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ kg" — literally contains a reference to a number.
- "Jupiter has four large moons" — apparently commits to the number 4 being associated with a property of Jupiter.

If mathematical statements are literally false, do these statements inherit their falsity? If so, physics seems to be a body of false claims about the world, which is unacceptable. If not, what makes physics different?

Field's solution: the nominalistic content of physics can be stated without numbers. The claim about electron mass is shorthand for a claim about ratios and comparisons of physical magnitudes. "The electron's mass divided by the proton's mass = 1/1836" can be understood as a claim about the structural relationships among concrete physical properties — no numbers required, just proportional relationships.

This strategy requires that every physical application of mathematics can be paraphrased into a statement that makes no reference to abstract mathematical objects. Field's nominalization of Newtonian mechanics shows how this works for one physical theory; the extension to quantum mechanics, with its essential use of Hilbert spaces and complex numbers, remains deeply contested.

## Field's Conservativeness

A key component of Field's fictionalism is the claim that mathematics is *conservative* — that it generates no new empirical predictions beyond what the underlying physical theory already contains:

**Definition**: Mathematics M is conservative with respect to nominalist physical theory N if and only if: for any nominalist sentence S, if N + M entails S, then N alone entails S.

Conservativeness entails that mathematics contributes no new physical information. It is a compressional or representational device, not a source of new empirical content. Even if we cannot do without mathematics in practice (it makes derivations tractable), we can in principle do without it ontologically.

Field proved conservativeness via a model-theoretic argument: any model of the nominalist theory N can be extended to a model of N + M (by adding the standard mathematical universe as an additional domain, disjoint from the physical domain). If S is a nominalist sentence derivable from N + M, it must be true in all models of N + M, including all extensions of models of N — so it is derivable from N alone.

## Yablo's Figuralism as a Refined Fictionalism

Stephen Yablo's figuralism refines fictionalism by distinguishing *literal falsehood* from *figurative appropriateness*. Mathematical sentences are not literally false; they are figuratively apt. They function like metaphors that express real truths about the concrete world in an abstract idiom.

- "The ratio of my salary to yours is 2:1" figuratively expresses the concrete comparison "I earn twice as much as you."
- "There is no number n such that n² = 2 among the rationals" figuratively expresses a structural fact about the density and continuity properties of physical magnitudes.

On Yablo's view, the mathematical vocabulary carries *figurative content* — it conveys truths about the concrete world by exploiting abstract concepts as vehicles for expression. This avoids Field's claim that mathematical sentences are literally false: they are figuratively true when the concrete facts they represent actually obtain.

Yablo's figuralism handles the wording problem without requiring full-scale nominalization. Instead of paraphrasing "the mass of the electron is 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ kg" into a number-free sentence, we simply recognize that the sentence figuratively expresses a proportionality fact about the electron's mass relative to some conventional standard unit.
