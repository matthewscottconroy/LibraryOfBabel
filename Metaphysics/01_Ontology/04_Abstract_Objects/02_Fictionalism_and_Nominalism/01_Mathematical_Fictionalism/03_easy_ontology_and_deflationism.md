# Easy Ontology and Deflationism

*Amie Thomasson's deflationary approach to ontological debates about abstract objects.*

---

Amie Thomasson's "easy ontology" challenges the traditional framing of debates about abstract objects. The traditional debate asks: do numbers *really* exist? Do they exist independently of minds and practices? Thomasson argues that this framing presupposes a kind of metaphysical question that is either trivially answerable or not a genuine empirical or conceptual question at all.

The easy route to ontological conclusions proceeds by noting that terms like "number" are introduced by definition or by mastering the relevant rules of use. Once we have mastered the rules for using number terms — that numbers are abstract, that arithmetic claims are true if and only if their results hold, that we count by using natural numbers — then the existence of numbers follows *trivially*. Does the number 7 exist? Well, is there a prime number greater than 6? Obviously yes. Therefore, the number 7 exists. This is not a deep metaphysical discovery but a consequence of how we use the term "number."

This approach echoes Carnap's distinction between *internal* and *external* questions. Internal questions (does the number 7 exist within the framework of number theory?) have easy answers. External questions (does the number 7 *really* exist in some framework-independent sense?) either have trivial answers or are not genuine questions at all — they presuppose a kind of perspective-independent standpoint that we cannot occupy.

The deflationary stance has been extended to debates about properties, propositions, possible worlds, and material objects. For each domain, Thomasson argues that existence claims within the domain are easy to answer once we know how the relevant terms work, and that demands for further "heavyweight" justification rest on confused presuppositions.

Critics (Sider, van Inwagen, Dorr) argue that easy ontology deflects the genuine metaphysical questions rather than answering them. The question "do numbers exist?" is not the question "is '7 is prime' true within number theory?" but the question of whether there is anything in the mind-independent world that answers to number-talk. Thomasson responds that this further question makes sense only on a presupposed but unjustified picture of what metaphysical questions are.

## Carnap's Internal/External Distinction

Thomasson's easy ontology is a development of Carnap's distinction between internal and external ontological questions, drawn in "Empiricism, Semantics, and Ontology" (1950):

**Internal questions** are asked within an established linguistic framework. Within the framework of arithmetic, we can ask: "Is there a prime number between 10 and 20?" The answer is "yes" by mathematical proof. The question has a determinate method of resolution — apply the rules of the framework.

**External questions** allegedly ask whether the framework as a whole answers to something real, independently of any framework. "Do numbers *really* exist — not just within the number framework, but in the mind-independent world?" Carnap's diagnosis: this question has no determinate method of resolution; it is either a disguised practical question about which framework to adopt, or it is pseudo-question without cognitive content.

Thomasson revives this Carnapian analysis and extends it: existential questions (do numbers exist? do properties exist? do mathematical structures exist?) are always asked against a background of conceptual rules that fix what counts as a number, a property, or a structure. Once those rules are in place, the existence question has an easy answer. There is no "heavyweight" question left over.

**The application to mathematics**: 
- We learn what the term "number" means by learning how to count, add, and apply arithmetic.
- Part of what "number" means is that numbers are abstract, that there are infinitely many natural numbers, that 7 is prime, etc.
- Once we have this understanding, "does the number 7 exist?" is answered by determining whether there is a prime between 6 and 8 — obviously yes, so 7 exists.

## The Critique of Heavyweight Metaphysics

Thomasson's central target is what she calls "heavyweight" metaphysics — the view that genuine ontological questions require discovering deep, framework-independent facts about what kinds of things exist. Heavyweight ontologists (Sider, van Inwagen) hold that:

1. There is a single, correct ontology of the mind-independent world.
2. Questions like "do numbers exist?" have determinate answers that may conflict with ordinary language and scientific practice.
3. Philosophical argument can reveal the correct ontology.

Thomasson denies each of these. Ontological questions are not discovered but partially constituted by our linguistic and conceptual practices. There is no perspective-independent standpoint from which to assess whether numbers "really" exist.

**Sider's response**: There is a fundamental notion of *being* — existence in the deepest sense — and questions about whether numbers exist in this fundamental sense are genuine. The "joint-carving" vocabulary of fundamental physics picks out what really exists; the rest is useful but non-fundamental. Easy ontology is correct that numbers exist in the ordinary sense; it is wrong that there is no further question about whether they are ontologically *fundamental*.

**Thomasson's reply**: The notion of "fundamental existence" or "existence in the deepest sense" is itself in need of analysis. If it is defined by physics, then it is a scientific claim, not a philosophical one. If it is defined philosophically, the question becomes what the definer means by "fundamental" — and that question seems to recapitulate the problem.

## Neo-Fregean Abstractionism and Easy Ontology

Thomasson's easy ontology has affinities with the neo-Fregean abstractionism of Hale and Wright, though the two programs differ in detail. Neo-Fregeans argue that abstract objects can be introduced through *abstraction principles* — biconditionals of the form:

**Σ(a) = Σ(b) iff a ∼ b**

The number of Fs = the number of Gs iff F and G are equinumerous (Hume's Principle). The direction of line L = the direction of line M iff L and M are parallel.

On the neo-Fregean view, these abstraction principles are analytic — they are true in virtue of the meanings of the relevant concepts. From them, together with second-order logic, the existence of abstract objects (numbers, directions) can be inferred. The inference from Hume's Principle to the existence of numbers is not an a priori empirical discovery but a consequence of our conceptual practices.

Thomasson's easy ontology extends this: not only can we infer the existence of abstract objects from abstraction principles, but we can infer the existence of any object for which we have mastered the relevant application conditions — including ordinary material objects, events, and social entities.

**The common thread**: Both neo-Fregeanism and easy ontology hold that ontological questions are answered by articulating the conceptual or linguistic rules that govern the relevant terms, not by investigating a mind-independent metaphysical reality. Both face the challenge of explaining why conceptual rules should generate ontological commitments.

## Deflationism About Truth and About Ontology

Deflationary positions in ontology parallel deflationary positions in the theory of truth. The deflationist about truth holds that "the proposition P is true" says no more than P — the truth predicate is a logical device for disquotation, not a substantive predicate describing a correspondence with mind-independent reality.

Similarly, Thomasson's deflationist about ontology holds that "numbers exist" says no more than the relevant arithmetic truths — "there are prime numbers greater than 2," etc. The existential quantifier is not a substantive predicate identifying a fundamental feature of mind-independent reality; it is a logical device for generalizing within a framework.

Both forms of deflationism face the objection that they are revisionary: they replace the apparently robust notions of truth and existence with thinner notions that may not capture everything we wanted from those concepts. The heavyweight ontologist insists that existence is a substantive concept — that there is a real distinction between things that genuinely exist and things that merely exist within a framework. Thomasson's response is to challenge the coherence of this distinction: what would it be for something to exist *outside* all frameworks?
