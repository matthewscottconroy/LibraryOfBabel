# Predicate Nominalism

*The view that property-talk is just predicate-talk, requiring no abstract property-entities.*

---

Predicate nominalism is the simplest nominalist position about properties. It holds that for a thing to have a property F is simply for it to fall under the predicate "F" — to be truly describable by that predicate. There is no need to posit a further entity, the property Fness, over and above the predicate and the things it applies to. "Socrates is wise" is true not because Socrates instantiates an abstract entity called wisdom but simply because Socrates falls under the predicate "is wise."

The view has an obvious Ockhamite motivation: it refuses to multiply entities beyond what is needed for a minimum ontology of particular objects. If predicates can be true of things without those things sharing abstract properties, then abstract properties are dispensable.

But predicate nominalism faces immediate objections. First, it seems to get the explanation backwards: predicates are true of objects because those objects have certain natures, not the other way around. Socrates falls under "is wise" because he has the property of wisdom; the property does not consist in his falling under the predicate. To say otherwise reverses the explanatory order.

Second, the view seems anthropocentric in a problematic way. The predicate "is red" applies to red things because of a contingent fact about human language — in a different language, red things might be described differently. But the redness of red things is surely not contingent on our language. Two red things would resemble each other in color even in a world without language-using creatures.

Third, predicates can be applied wrongly: someone could apply "is wise" to a fool. The predicate's being applicable is a normative fact — it *should* be applied to wise things — which requires an account of what makes things wise, bringing us back to the property.

These objections motivate more sophisticated nominalist alternatives (resemblance nominalism, class nominalism, trope theory) that attempt to account for the objective similarity structure behind predication without positing universals.

## The Fundamental Problem: Explanation and Priority

The core difficulty with predicate nominalism is explanatory priority. Consider:

**P1**: The predicate "is red" applies to this apple.
**P2**: This apple is red.

The natural order of explanation runs from P2 to P1: the predicate applies because the apple is red. The predicate nominalist reverses this: the apple's being red consists in the predicate's applying. But this seems to make a linguistic-institutional fact (our practice of applying a predicate) metaphysically prior to a physical fact (the apple's color), which inverts the order of reality.

Price's formulation of the objection: predicate nominalism conflates the *semantic* question "what does it mean to call something red?" with the *metaphysical* question "what makes something red?" Even if we can answer the semantic question by describing predicate-use, we have not thereby answered the metaphysical question. The metaphysical question asks about the objective basis in reality for correct predicate application, and that basis seems to require something over and above predicates and their use.

## The Conventionality Problem

Predicate nominalism makes the following awkward prediction: if we adopted different predicates — if our language carved up the color spectrum differently — then the objects grouped together under the same predicate would be the ones "sharing a property," and different sets of objects would share that property.

On predicate nominalism, whether two objects share the property expressed by a predicate depends on whether we use the same predicate for both. In a language where the word "gred" applies to all red and green things, things that are gred "share the property of being gred" — even though they do not resemble each other in any particular way that is independent of our labeling.

This seems to conflate *classification* with *objective resemblance*. Predicate nominalism cannot account for the fact that red things objectively resemble each other (in color) in a way that red-and-green things do not, unless it appeals to something over and above the predicate — which is precisely what it refuses to do.

## Quine's Deflationary Nominalism

Quine's position is sometimes classified as a sophisticated form of predicate nominalism, though it is better described as eliminativism. Quine held that we should not quantify over properties in our canonical scientific theory: "There is a property common to Socrates and Plato" should be replaced by "Socrates and Plato are both wise." The ontological commitment of our theory is carried only by what we quantify over, and properties should not appear in the bound variables of our canonical first-order theory.

On Quine's view, predicate letters are schematic expressions, not terms referring to entities. The predicate "is wise" applies to Socrates; this does not require that there exists an entity wisdom to which the predicate refers. Predicates are not names.

**The limitation**: Quine's approach works for avoiding quantification over properties in ordinary contexts, but it cannot easily handle:
- Abstract reference: "Wisdom is a virtue" seems to refer to wisdom as an entity.
- Scientific predicates: "Charge" and "mass" seem to name properties that appear in laws of nature.
- Property-theoretic contexts: "Wisdom and courage are both virtues" requires quantification over properties if the "both" is to be explained.

## What Predicate Nominalism Lacks: The Demand for Objective Resemblances

The most systematic defense of universals against predicate nominalism is Armstrong's argument from objective resemblance. Armstrong's *A Theory of Universals* (1978) argues that predicate nominalism cannot account for the following range of facts:

**Objective resemblance**: Before humans evolved, early red nebulae and red sunsets resembled each other. This resemblance is not constituted by their both falling under some predicate — there were no predicates then. The resemblance is mind-independent and language-independent.

**Laws of nature**: Laws of nature are not facts about predicates; they are facts about how properties are related. "All copper conducts electricity" is not a fact about the predicates "copper" and "conducts electricity" but a fact about a relation between the properties of being copper and conducting electricity.

**Causal explanation**: When we explain why a glass broke by citing its brittleness, we are citing a property of the glass, not a predicate. If properties were just predicates, causal explanations would be deflated to claims about language.

**Natural science**: Physics discovers natural properties — charge, mass, spin — by investigating the world, not by investigating language. The natural properties are the ones that appear in physical laws; they are not determined by our predicate-use.

These considerations motivate moving beyond predicate nominalism to accounts that can explain the objective structure of resemblances and causal relations — either through universals, natural classes, or tropes.
