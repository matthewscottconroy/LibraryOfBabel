# Resemblance Nominalism

*The view that properties are grounded in objective resemblance relations among particulars.*

---

Resemblance nominalism holds that what makes two things share a property is their standing in an objective resemblance relation — not their both instantiating an abstract universal. The property of being red is not an entity; rather, the predicate "is red" applies to things that resemble paradigmatic red objects in the relevant respects. Resemblance is taken as a primitive, objective, non-reducible relation among particulars.

The central attraction of resemblance nominalism is that it grounds property attributions in something observable and concrete (resemblance among particular things) without positing universals. It is Ockhamistically parsimonious about abstract entities while acknowledging that the world has genuine objective structure.

However, resemblance nominalism faces several well-known challenges:

**The companionship problem**: suppose every red thing happens to be round. Then the class of red things and the class of round things coincide. Resemblance nominalism seems unable to distinguish redness from roundness, since they apply to exactly the same objects. Response: appeal to possible worlds or to possible red non-round things; but this may require modal realism.

**The imperfect community problem**: a set of objects might be such that every two of them resemble in some respect without there being any single property they all share. The mere pairwise resemblance relations among the set members do not suffice to make them all share a property. Response: require that the resemblances be *in the same respect* — but specifying respects without appealing to properties seems circular.

**The regress problem**: resemblance is itself a relation — two things resembling each other is a relational fact. If resemblance is a universal, then we have admitted one universal after all. If resemblance is particular (a trope), we are moving toward trope theory rather than resemblance nominalism proper.

Gonzalo Rodriguez-Pereyra's contemporary resemblance nominalism attempts to answer these problems systematically, arguing that they can all be handled with sufficient precision and without reintroducing universals.

## The Structure of Resemblance Nominalism

Resemblance nominalism has its clearest historical statement in Price (1953) and Russell's early work on universals, though Russell ultimately rejected it. The view has the following core commitments:

1. **Primitive resemblance**: The resemblance relation among particulars is primitive — it is not analyzed in terms of shared properties or universals. It is a directly apprehensible, objective relation.

2. **Paradigm-relative classification**: For each predicate, there are paradigm cases (paradigmatic red objects, for "red"). An object satisfies the predicate iff it sufficiently resembles the paradigms in the relevant respect.

3. **No abstract universals**: There is no entity redness that red things instantiate. The objective fact behind "a is red" is just that a resembles the red paradigms closely enough.

4. **Nominalism**: The only genuine particulars in the ontology are concrete objects. The resemblance relations are real, but they are relations among these concrete objects, not further entities.

## The Companionship Problem in Detail

The companionship problem arises when two distinct properties happen to be co-instantiated in all actual objects. Suppose (fantastically) that every red thing also happens to be round, and no non-red thing is round. Then the class of red things = the class of round things. But redness and roundness are different properties: a red square is possible even if there happen to be none.

Resemblance nominalists respond by appealing to *possible* objects: a possible red non-round object would resemble the actual red things more closely in color than it resembles the actual round things in shape, even if there are no actual red non-round things. This handles the case but at the cost of introducing quantification over possibilia.

If possible objects are allowed, the resemblance nominalist must say: a is red iff a resembles all the red paradigms (in actual worlds) in the color-respect, including possible objects that are red but not round. This extension to possibilia undercuts the original motivation of avoiding abstract entities by grounding things in the concrete actual world.

Rodriguez-Pereyra's response: the possible objects appealed to are not abstract; they are merely non-actual concrete objects. Lewis's modal realism provides a framework in which such objects are genuine concrete entities, not abstract posits. The cost is the Lewisian ontology of many concrete possible worlds.

## The Imperfect Community Problem

The imperfect community problem was raised by Goodman (1951). Consider a set S = {a, b, c} where:
- a is red and round
- b is round and heavy
- c is red and heavy

Every pair of members resembles: a resembles b (both round), b resembles c (both heavy), a resembles c (both red). Yet there is no single property shared by all three. The pairwise resemblances do not establish a common property.

The resemblance nominalist who defines properties as resemblance classes must distinguish S from a genuine resemblance class (a class of objects all sharing one property). The standard response requires analyzing resemblance *in a specific respect* — a resembles b in roundness, c in redness. But "in roundness" and "in redness" already appeal to the properties in question, making the account circular.

Rodriguez-Pereyra's solution: instead of analyzing properties as resemblance classes, define the property of being F as the set of all things that are F — where membership in the set is fixed by *exact* resemblance to paradigm F-things, not just pairwise resemblance. A genuine property-class is one where every member exactly resembles all the paradigms in the relevant respect. This avoids the imperfect community by requiring comprehensive, not just pairwise, resemblance to paradigms.

## The Resemblance Regress

Russell's classic objection in "The World of Universals" (1912): resemblance nominalism covertly reinstates universals. The argument:

**P1**: If a resembles b, this resemblance is a fact.
**P2**: If we analyze this fact nominally (in terms of resemblance between a and b), we have introduced a resemblance relation.
**P3**: If resemblance is a universal, we have admitted at least one universal — and the nominalism is incomplete.
**P4**: If resemblance is particular — a concrete resemblance relation instance — then two different resemblance relations (between a & b and between c & d) themselves resemble each other. This generates a second-order resemblance.
**P5**: The same problem arises for each new resemblance: we need resemblance relations between resemblance relations, ad infinitum.

**C**: Resemblance nominalism regresses infinitely unless it admits resemblance as a universal.

Resemblance nominalists respond by biting the bullet on one horn: either admit one universal (resemblance) as an exception, or accept the infinite regress as non-vicious (each level is grounded in the previous, so the regress is explanatory rather than viciously infinite). Rodriguez-Pereyra argues the regress is not vicious: each level of resemblance is explained by the previous, and there is no circularity or failure of explanation.

## Contemporary Resemblance Nominalism

Rodriguez-Pereyra's *Resemblance Nominalism: A Solution to the Problem of Universals* (2002) is the most technically developed contemporary defense. Key moves:

**Particularism**: Resemblance relations are particular — the resemblance between this apple and that fire truck is a concrete relation instance, not a universal.

**Quantification over possibilia**: The account uses possible as well as actual objects to handle the companionship problem, appealing to a Lewis-style modal realism.

**Resemblance as primitive**: The resemblance relation is primitive — not analyzed in terms of shared properties. This means the account cannot explain *why* resembling things resemble (in terms of their shared intrinsic natures), but Rodriguez-Pereyra accepts this as a consequence of the nominalist commitment.

**Avoiding circularity**: The imperfect community and companionship problems are solved by precise formulations that avoid the relevant circularities, without reintroducing universals.

The overall assessment: resemblance nominalism is a sophisticated and defensible position, but it requires modal realism about possible objects (a substantial ontological commitment) and accepts that certain facts about similarity are primitive and unanalyzed. Whether this is more or less ontologically parsimonious than admitting universals is a matter of philosophical judgment.
