# Class Nominalism and Naturalness

*Identifying properties with classes of their instances, and the problem of distinguishing natural from arbitrary classes.*

---

Class nominalism identifies properties with extensions — with the sets or classes of objects that have them. To have the property of being red is simply to be a member of the class {all red things}. This is ontologically parsimonious: classes are standard mathematical entities, and we do not need a separate category of universals. The property is identical to its extension.

The most obvious difficulty is that any two objects belong to some class together. There is a class containing Socrates and the Eiffel Tower and my left shoe; does this class correspond to a property? If so, then every predicate corresponds to a property, including gerrymandered ones like "is either Socrates or the Eiffel Tower or my left shoe." But this generates an absurdly abundant set of properties that do not carve nature at its joints.

The class nominalist needs to distinguish *natural* classes from *arbitrary* ones — the class of all electrons is natural; the class of all things that are either electrons or the number 3 is not. David Lewis developed a sophisticated response: some classes are *perfectly natural* — they correspond to the genuine, sparse properties that figure in fundamental physics and ground genuine similarities. The distinction between natural and non-natural classes is taken as primitive by Lewis, a primitive feature of the world rather than something we can reduce further.

This appeal to naturalness is controversial: it introduces a primitive notion that resembles the very thing the nominalist was trying to avoid (a "natural class" is suspiciously like a class defined by a shared universal). Some critics argue that the appeal to naturalness just relocates the explanatory work of universals without actually eliminating them. If two electrons are in the same natural class because they share the property of being an electron, then the natural class just *is* the extension of the universal ELECTRON.

Lewis responded that naturalness is a fundamental, unanalyzable feature: some classes just are natural in a way that others are not, and this is a brute fact about the structure of reality. The naturalness of natural classes is not explained by shared universals; it is just a primitive feature alongside or instead of universals.

## Class Nominalism and Possible Worlds

Standard class nominalism about properties identifies properties with their actual extensions — the class of all actual red things, the class of all actual electrons. This creates a difficulty for modality: two necessarily co-extensive predicates ("equiangular triangle" and "equilateral triangle") pick out the same class of actual objects, so they would express the same property. But intuitively they express different properties (being equiangular is not the same as being equilateral, even though every equiangular triangle is equilateral and vice versa).

Lewis's solution extends class nominalism to *possibilia*: properties are classes of possible objects, not just actual objects. The property of being red is the class {x : x is red at some possible world} — the class of all possible red objects across all possible worlds. Two predicates express different properties iff the classes of possibilia they pick out differ.

This handles the equiangular/equilateral case: even if every actual equiangular triangle is equilateral, there are possible objects that are equiangular but not equilateral (in worlds with non-Euclidean geometry, or in abstract spaces). So the two classes of possibilia differ, and the two predicates express different properties.

**The ontological cost**: Lewis's modal realism requires accepting the existence of possible worlds as concrete entities — not abstract possible worlds, but genuine spatiotemporally isolated universes. The class of all possible red things ranges over red things in all these concrete universes. This is a substantial ontological commitment, and the nominalist parsimony of class nominalism is purchased at the cost of modal realism's ontological extravagance.

## Lewis on Natural Properties

Lewis's account of naturalness is one of the most influential contributions to recent metaphysics. In *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986) and various papers, Lewis distinguishes:

**Perfectly natural properties**: The fundamental properties of physics — charge, mass, spin, and perhaps others not yet discovered. These properties ground objective similarities (things with the same charge genuinely resemble each other in charge), figure in laws of nature, and carve reality at its joints. They are not numerous: only the fundamental physical properties are perfectly natural.

**Relatively natural properties**: Properties that can be defined in terms of perfectly natural ones, or that correspond to genuine kinds recognized by the special sciences (biological species, chemical elements, etc.). These are natural *relative to* some level of description or scientific interest.

**Abundant properties**: All the rest — every class of possible objects corresponds to an abundant property. These are needed for semantic purposes (every predicate must have a semantic value) but they do no metaphysical work.

Lewis takes naturalness as primitive: there is no further analysis of what makes a property natural. It is a fundamental feature of the structure of reality that some classes of possibilia are natural and others are not. This primitivism about naturalness is his response to the charge that class nominalism just smuggles universals back in under a different name.

**The argument for primitive naturalness**: Armstrong's account of universals also takes something as primitive — the universal/particular distinction, and the instantiation relation. The class nominalist's primitive (naturalness) is no worse than the realist's primitive (universals). The question is which primitive is more theoretically parsimonious and more explanatorily fruitful. Lewis argues that a single primitive (naturalness of classes) is preferable to Armstrong's two primitives (universals and instantiation).

## Applications of Natural Properties

Lewis's notion of natural properties does considerable work across metaphysics:

**Laws of nature**: Laws connect natural properties — "all electrons have charge e" is a law because it connects the perfectly natural property of being an electron with the perfectly natural property of having charge e. Accidental generalizations connect non-natural properties (or natural and non-natural ones): "all gold things are valuable" is accidental because being valuable is not a natural property.

**Intrinsicality**: A property is intrinsic iff whether an object has it depends only on the object itself and how it is, not on its relations to other things. Lewis analyzes intrinsicality in terms of natural properties: the perfectly natural properties are all intrinsic, and intrinsic properties are those that can be built from perfectly natural properties without reference to the rest of the world.

**Similarity**: Two objects resemble each other objectively iff they share natural properties. Objective similarity (as opposed to similarity *in some respect*, which is trivial) requires sharing perfectly natural properties. Two electrons are objectively similar in a way that an electron and a quark are not, because the electron and quark differ in their perfectly natural properties.

**Reference and meaning**: Natural properties are what our predicates "latch onto" when they are successful in expressing objective classifications. When we use "water" and it successfully picks out a natural kind, this is because the extension of "water" — H₂O in all possible worlds — corresponds to a relatively natural class. The link between language and world is mediated by naturalness.

## Class Nominalism versus Trope Theory

Class nominalism and trope theory represent two distinct nominalist responses to the problem of universals:

| Feature | Class Nominalism | Trope Theory |
|---|---|---|
| Ontology | Sets of possibilia | Particular property-instances |
| Universality | No; each class is a set, not a universal | No; each trope is particular |
| Resemblance | Two things resemble iff they are in the same natural class | Two things resemble iff their tropes resemble |
| Primitives | Naturalness (of classes) | Resemblance (of tropes) |
| Extensionality | Yes; co-extensive predicates share a property (in actuality) | No; different tropes for co-extensive properties |

Both views are nominalist in rejecting transcendent universals, but they take different things as primitive and face different challenges. Class nominalism handles co-extension by extending to possibilia; trope theory avoids this by making each property instance particular. Neither fully escapes circularity concerns, but both represent sophisticated, defensible alternatives to universalism.
