# Exercises and Thought Experiments — Fictionalism and Nominalism

*Problems for engaging with nominalist and fictionalist positions.*

---

**Exercise 1 — Predicate Nominalism's Explanatory Order**
The predicate nominalist says a thing is red because it falls under the predicate "is red." The realist says a thing falls under "is red" because it is red (has redness). Which direction of explanation is correct, and how would you argue for your preferred direction? Does the answer depend on whether we are talking about predicates in natural languages or in ideal scientific languages?

**Exercise 2 — Resemblance Nominalism's Problems**
Construct your own version of the companionship problem (two properties that happen to apply to all and only the same things). Then propose a resemblance-nominalist response. Does your response require modal resources (talk of possible things)? If so, does this make nominalism more expensive than it seemed?

**Exercise 3 — The Naturalness Primitive**
Lewis appeals to a primitive notion of *naturalness* to distinguish natural classes from arbitrary ones. An objector says this naturalness does exactly the work that universals do, so Lewis has not eliminated universals but merely renamed them. Construct Lewis's best response to this objection. Is the objection decisive?

**Exercise 4 — Fictionalism's Wording Problem**
If mathematical statements are false (because there are no numbers), then "there are infinitely many prime numbers" is false. But this seems obviously true. The fictionalist says it is *fictionally* true. What exactly is the difference between fictional truth and truth? Can a statement be both fictionally true and genuinely useful in physics? Is there a principled line between mathematical fiction and physical reality on this view?

**Thought Experiment — Mathematics Without Mathematicians**
Suppose there were never any mathematicians, never any intelligent beings to invent or discover mathematics. Would there still be prime numbers? Would 7 still be prime? The Platonist says yes; the fictionalist says no (or: the question does not arise, since fiction requires tellers). Which view matches your intuition, and what are the consequences for mathematical methodology?
