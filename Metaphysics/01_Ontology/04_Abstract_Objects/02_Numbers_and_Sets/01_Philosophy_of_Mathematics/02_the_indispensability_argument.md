# The Indispensability Argument

The indispensability argument for mathematical Platonism, associated primarily with Quine and Hilary Putnam, is one of the most influential arguments in the philosophy of mathematics. Its conclusion is that we should believe in the existence of mathematical objects, on the grounds that they are indispensable to our best scientific theories.

The argument runs: (1) We ought to have ontological commitment to all and only the entities that are indispensable to our best scientific theories. (2) Mathematical entities are indispensable to our best scientific theories. (3) Therefore, we ought to have ontological commitment to mathematical entities.

The first premise reflects Quine's criterion of ontological commitment: "to be is to be the value of a bound variable." If a theory quantifies over entities of type T in its canonical first-order formulation, and that theory is our best theory of reality, then we should believe in T-entities. This connects ontology to science in a way that is empirically respectable.

The second premise is defended by pointing to the pervasive use of mathematics in physics, chemistry, biology, and economics. Differential equations describe the motion of particles; probability theory governs genetics; matrix algebra structures quantum mechanics. It is difficult to see how to formulate these theories without any quantification over mathematical objects.

Objections have focused on both premises. Against (1): why should scientific indispensability generate ontological commitment? Perhaps mathematics is a useful representational tool without being genuinely true of abstract objects. Against (2): Field's nominalization program is an attempt to show that physics can be reformulated without quantifying over mathematical objects. If successful, mathematics is dispensable.

A further objection (Sober, Maddy) questions whether mathematical components of scientific theories receive the same empirical confirmation as the physical components. If they do not, then even if mathematics is indispensable, this does not generate the kind of confirmation that would justify ontological commitment.

## Quine's Confirmational Holism

The indispensability argument depends critically on Quine's confirmational holism — the view that our beliefs face experience not individually but as a corporate body. No single statement is confirmed or disconfirmed by evidence; rather, the whole web of belief is tested against experience.

Applied to scientific theories: when a physical theory makes a successful prediction, this confirms the theory as a whole — including both its physical and its mathematical components. If the mathematical formalism is indispensable (there is no way to state the theory without it), then the mathematical components receive the same empirical confirmation as the physical components. By Quine's criterion, we are then committed to the existence of mathematical objects.

**The holism premise requires both**:
1. That mathematical components of theories are not mere notational conventions (if they were, their indispensability would be notational, not ontological).
2. That mathematical components receive genuine confirmation, not just pragmatic acceptance.

Quine was explicit: he accepted the existence of sets and numbers on exactly the same pragmatic grounds he accepted electrons — they are posited by our best theories.

## Putnam's Strengthening

Hilary Putnam strengthened the indispensability argument in "What is Mathematical Truth?" (1975) by arguing that mathematics is not just useful for physics but *essential*. Without mathematics, physics cannot even be stated:

- Quantum mechanics requires Hilbert spaces over the complex numbers.
- General relativity requires Riemannian manifolds and tensor calculus.
- Statistical mechanics requires probability theory and measure theory.

These are not mere computational conveniences — they are the very framework within which physical theories are formulated. There is no known nominalist reformulation of quantum mechanics or general relativity. Unlike Newtonian mechanics (which Field managed to nominalize), these theories seem to require mathematical ontology intrinsically.

Putnam's version: if quantum mechanics is true, then there are Hilbert spaces; if there are Hilbert spaces, there are complex numbers and linear operators; if there are complex numbers, there are abstract mathematical objects. Since we should believe quantum mechanics is at least approximately true (its predictions are extraordinarily accurate), we should believe in mathematical objects.

## Maddy's Objection: Scientific Practice

Penelope Maddy's *Realism in Mathematics* (1990) and subsequent work challenge the indispensability argument by examining how scientists actually treat the mathematical components of their theories.

**Idealization**: Physicists routinely use continuous mathematics (differentiable functions, smooth manifolds) to describe phenomena they believe are fundamentally discrete (quantum mechanics suggests spacetime may be discrete at the Planck scale). They use infinite idealizations (infinite populations in statistical mechanics, point masses in gravitational physics) while believing the physical world is finite and extended. If scientists genuinely believed the mathematical components of their theories, they would believe in actual physical infinities and actual physical continuities — which they do not.

**Selective confirmation**: The empirical success of a physical theory confirms its empirically testable components — its predictions about measurement outcomes, about the behavior of observable systems. It does not equally confirm its mathematical components, because the mathematical components are not directly empirically tested.

**Conclusion**: The holism required for the indispensability argument is not how science actually works. Scientists treat mathematical structures as idealized models, not as literal descriptions of reality. The indispensability of mathematics in physics is the indispensability of a modeling tool, not an indication that the tool's referents exist.

## Field's Nominalization Program

Hartry Field's *Science Without Numbers* (1980) provides the most systematic response to the second premise (indispensability). Field's strategy:

**Step 1**: Show that mathematics is *conservative* — that adding mathematical axioms to a nominalist physical theory generates no new nominalist consequences. (Formal proof using model theory.)

**Step 2**: Provide a nominalist reconstruction of a physical theory (Newtonian mechanics) that does not quantify over mathematical objects but uses only spacetime regions and betweenness/congruence relations among spacetime points.

**Step 3**: Argue that the mathematical formulation of the theory is useful (for computational purposes) but not ontologically committing, since the nominalist formulation is expressively equivalent.

Field's proof of conservativeness uses the fact that standard mathematical objects (numbers, sets) form a domain disjoint from the physical domain. Any model of the nominalist physical theory can be extended to a model of the mathematical theory plus the nominalist physical theory by simply adding the mathematical domain — and any nominalist consequence follows from the physical theory alone. This establishes that mathematics is conservative over the nominalist physical theory.

The extension to quantum mechanics remains the central challenge. Field himself acknowledged that quantum mechanics resists nominalization, and subsequent work has not found a satisfactory nominalist reformulation.
