# Mathematical Truth

What makes mathematical statements true? This question is deceptively simple. It seems obvious that "2+2=4" is true and "2+2=5" is false. But explaining what makes these the case — and doing so in a way that is consistent with a coherent epistemology and a coherent metaphysics — turns out to be genuinely difficult.

The Platonist answer is that mathematical statements are made true by facts about abstract mathematical objects. "2+2=4" is true because the number 4 is what you get when you add the number 2 to itself. This is a correspondence theory of mathematical truth: mathematical truth corresponds to the objective features of the mathematical realm.

The formalist answer (associated with Hilbert's program) is that mathematical truth is truth within a formal system: "2+2=4" is true because it is provable from the axioms of arithmetic. This sidesteps questions about abstract objects at the cost of making truth system-relative. Two different axiomatic systems might prove contradictory things, and formalism seems to have no resources for asking which system is correct.

The logicist answer (Frege, Russell) is that mathematical truths are truths of logic: they are analytic, necessarily true in virtue of the meanings of the concepts involved. This preserves the objectivity of mathematics while avoiding robust commitment to a Platonic realm.

Modal structuralism offers yet another account: "2+2=4" is true because in any possible system satisfying the Peano axioms, the sum of the second and second elements equals the fourth. Truth is truth-within-a-possible-structure, not truth about a particular existing object.

Each account faces challenges. Gödel's incompleteness theorems show that no consistent axiomatic system can prove all mathematical truths, undermining simple formalism. The epistemological problem challenges Platonism. The logicist program requires a robust conception of logical truth that is itself contentious. The metaphysics of mathematical truth remains one of the most open questions in philosophy.

## Benacerraf's Challenge to Mathematical Truth

Benacerraf's "Mathematical Truth" (1973) argued that any satisfactory account of mathematical truth must satisfy two conditions:

**Semantic condition**: The truth theory for mathematical statements should be continuous with the truth theory for empirical statements. Mathematical statements should have the same semantic structure — subject referring to an object, predicate expressing a property — as ordinary empirical statements.

**Epistemological condition**: The truth theory should be compatible with an account of how we come to know mathematical truths. Knowledge requires some appropriate causal or epistemic connection between the knower and the known.

These conditions are in tension. If mathematical truth is truth about abstract objects (satisfying the semantic condition), then mathematical knowledge requires contact with abstract objects — but abstract objects are causally inert, making such contact impossible (violating the epistemological condition). If mathematical truth is made epistemically accessible — perhaps by being truth about physical objects or about logical consequences of axioms — then mathematical semantics diverges from empirical semantics (violating the semantic condition).

Benacerraf's dilemma does not refute Platonism but sets a demanding standard for any account of mathematical truth. A satisfactory account must tell us both what makes mathematical statements true and how we know them.

## Gödel on Mathematical Truth

Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931) are relevant to the philosophy of mathematical truth in two ways:

**Against formalism**: The first incompleteness theorem shows that in any consistent formal system S strong enough to formalize arithmetic, there are statements that are neither provable nor disprovable in S. These "Gödel sentences" are mathematically true (assuming the system is consistent) but unprovable in the system. Mathematical truth outstrips formal provability. If mathematical truth = provability-in-a-system, then mathematical truth would be indeterminate for these statements — but the Gödel sentences are clearly true (they say, in effect, "this sentence is not provable in S," and they are not provable in S).

**For Platonism (Gödel's intended conclusion)**: The incompleteness theorems suggest that mathematical truth is not fully captured by any formal system. The true axioms of mathematics exceed what any finite system can capture. Gödel concluded that mathematical truth is objective and that we have a faculty of mathematical intuition — analogous to sensory perception — that gives us access to mathematical truths that outstrip formal proof.

Gödel's epistemological claim about mathematical intuition has been disputed: it seems to invoke a mysterious faculty without explaining how it works. But his logical conclusion stands: mathematical truth cannot be identified with formal provability.

## Mathematical Truth and Possible Worlds

A common approach to mathematical truth (compatible with both Platonism and some forms of anti-realism) identifies mathematical truths with *necessary* truths — truths that hold in all possible worlds.

**Necessity as a criterion**: "2 + 2 = 4" is necessarily true; it holds in every possible world. "The Earth has one moon" is contingently true; it could have been false. Mathematical truths are distinguished from contingent truths by their necessity.

This criterion coheres with Platonism (abstract mathematical objects, if they exist, exist necessarily) and with modalism (modal structuralist: "2+2=4" is necessarily true because in every possible omega-sequence, the arithmetic facts hold).

**The challenge from set theory**: Some set-theoretic statements — the Continuum Hypothesis (CH), for instance — are independent of the ZFC axioms. Neither CH nor its negation follows from ZFC. In possible-worlds terms: there are possible worlds (models of set theory) where CH is true, and possible worlds where CH is false. Neither is necessary. If mathematical truth = necessary truth, then CH is not mathematically true or false — but Platonists typically hold that CH has a determinate truth value, since the mathematical facts are objective.

The independence results create a genuine puzzle: if mathematical reality is determinate, why does our axiom system leave it underdetermined? Gödel believed that new axioms (large cardinal axioms, for instance) would settle the Continuum Hypothesis. The multiverse view (Hamkins) holds instead that there are multiple set-theoretic universes with different facts about CH, and that mathematical truth is universe-relative.

## Deflationism About Mathematical Truth

Deflationary theories of truth hold that "P is true" says no more than P. Applied to mathematics: "It is true that 2+2=4" says no more than "2+2=4." Truth is a logical device for disquotation, not a robust property that requires correspondence with reality.

If mathematical truth is deflationary, then the question "what makes '2+2=4' true?" receives a trivial answer: what makes it true is that 2+2=4. The demand for a deeper explanation — what abstract objects are involved? what does correspondence with the mathematical realm consist in? — is based on a confused picture of what truth is.

Deflationism about mathematical truth has attractions: it avoids the epistemological problem (there is no special mathematical faculty needed to access the truth-makers, since the truth-maker just is the mathematical fact itself) and it harmonizes mathematics with empirical discourse (both use the same deflationary truth predicate).

**The challenge**: Deflationism about truth does not settle questions about ontology. Even if "2+2=4 is true" says no more than "2+2=4," we still need to ask what makes "2+2=4" true in the first place — what the grounds or truth-conditions are. The ontological question about mathematical objects is not dissolved by deflationary truth; it is just re-expressed.
