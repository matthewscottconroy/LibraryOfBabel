# Propositions and Properties

When two people believe the same thing — when you and I both believe that the sun is a star — what exactly do we share? The obvious answer is that we believe the same proposition. But propositions are puzzling entities. They are the content of belief and assertion; they are the primary bearers of truth-values; they stand in logical relations of entailment, contradiction, and equivalence. If propositions are abstract objects, they are the most ubiquitous and practically indispensable kind: every thought, every sentence, every act of communication seems to involve them.

The metaphysics of propositions is contentious. Structured propositions — associated with the tradition of Russell and Kaplan — hold that a proposition is literally composed of objects, properties, and relations: the proposition that Socrates is mortal contains Socrates himself as a constituent, along with the property of mortality in a certain predicational mode. Unstructured propositions — associated with possible-worlds semantics — identify propositions with functions from possible worlds to truth-values, or equivalently with sets of possible worlds. Each approach makes different predictions about the individuation of propositions (do "Water is H₂O" and "Water is water" express the same proposition?), the semantics of belief reports, and the analysis of modality.

Properties and propositions are intimately connected. The proposition that Socrates is mortal involves the property of mortality; the property of being red is the propositional function that maps each thing to the proposition that it is red. If properties are universals, their appearance in propositions gives us universally shareable content — the same property can recur in indefinitely many distinct propositions. If properties are tropes, propositions can no longer be understood in the same way: there is no single entity corresponding to "redness" that appears in all propositions attributing redness to different things.

This section examines the leading theories of propositions, their relationship to the theory of properties, and the implications of each for the philosophy of language, logic, and epistemology.
