# What Are Propositions?

Propositions are the things that are believed, doubted, asserted, and denied — the contents of mental attitudes and the bearers of truth and falsity. When John believes that snow is white, the content of his belief — what it is that he believes — is a proposition. When Mary asserts that the economy is growing, the proposition that the economy is growing is what she asserts, and it is the same proposition that Peter might deny or question.

Propositions are distinct from sentences. "Snow is white" and "La neige est blanche" are different sentences (in English and French respectively), but they express the same proposition — the same content. Conversely, "I am tired" expresses different propositions depending on who utters it, because the proposition's subject changes with the context.

Propositions are also distinct from mental representations. Two people in different countries who have never met might both believe that water is H2O. Their mental states differ (they involve different neurons, different languages, different experiences), but they share the same propositional content. Propositions are objective, shareable entities, not internal psychological items.

This motivates the view that propositions are abstract objects: they are mind-independent entities (the proposition that snow is white would be true even if no minds existed), they are not located in space or time, and they are not causally efficacious. Frege called propositions "thoughts" (Gedanken) and emphasized their objectivity: a thought is not owned by any thinker, it is just grasped by thinkers.

The main alternatives to treating propositions as abstract objects are: (1) treating propositions as structured complexes of objects and properties (Russellian propositions); (2) treating propositions as sets of possible worlds (Stalnaker), which is technically tractable but may be too coarse-grained; and (3) eliminating propositions in favor of sentences or mental states, which faces the challenges mentioned above.

## Why We Need Propositions

The postulation of propositions is motivated by several theoretical demands:

**Attitude ascription**: We regularly say things like "Mary believes that it will rain," "John fears that the market will crash," "I hope that the train is on time." These sentences report mental states by specifying their contents. If contents are propositions, then the same content can be shared by different mental states (Mary and John might both believe that it will rain). Without propositions, it is unclear how to explain this content-sharing.

**Validity of inference**: The following argument is valid:
- Everyone believes what Napoleon believed.
- Napoleon believed that France is a republic.
- Therefore, everyone believes that France is a republic.

For this to be valid, we need "that France is a republic" to refer to the same entity in both premises — a proposition. Without propositions, the validity of such arguments is hard to explain.

**Translation equivalence**: "Snow is white" (English) and "Schnee ist weiß" (German) are equivalent — they say the same thing. The natural explanation is that they express the same proposition. Without propositions, translation equivalence becomes a primitive, unexplained relation.

**Truth bearers**: Some entity must be the primary bearer of truth — the thing of which we say it is true or false. Sentences are too language-relative; the same truth might be expressed in many sentences. Mental states are too person-relative; the same truth is accessible to multiple thinkers. Propositions, as abstract, shareable, mind-independent entities, are the natural candidates.

## Frege's Thoughts

Frege's account in "The Thought: A Logical Inquiry" (1918) provides the classical statement of propositions as abstract objects:

**Thoughts are not mental**: A thought is not an idea — not a mental representation in anyone's mind. Mental representations are private (my toothache is accessible only to me), but thoughts are public (any number of thinkers can grasp the same thought). Mental representations change as the mind changes; thoughts are timeless and unchanging.

**Thoughts are not physical**: The thought that 5 + 7 = 12 is not a physical object, not a brain state, not an inscription on paper. Physical objects are spatiotemporally located; the thought exists (if it exists) without being anywhere.

**Thoughts are objective**: Thoughts are objective in the sense that their truth or falsity is determined by how things are, not by what anyone thinks. The thought that Jupiter has 67 moons is true if Jupiter has 67 moons, regardless of what any thinker believes.

**Thoughts are grasped**: We come to know thoughts not by creating or constructing them but by grasping something pre-existing. Mathematical discovery is the discovery of thoughts (propositions) that were true before anyone grasped them.

Frege's three-realm ontology: the physical world (matter, causation), the mental world (ideas, representations), and the logical world (thoughts, truth-values, concepts). Propositions inhabit the third realm — objective, mind-independent, but not physical.

## The Propositional Attitude Context

Propositional attitudes — belief, desire, intention, fear, hope — all take propositional contents. The logical form is:

**[Agent] [Attitude] [Proposition]**: John believes that P, Mary fears that Q, Peter hopes that R.

For this logical form to be coherent, propositions must be genuine entities that can appear as the objects of attitudes. They must be:
- Shareable: the same proposition can be the object of multiple agents' attitudes.
- Accessible: agents can stand in the attitude relation to a proposition.
- Distinguishable: different propositions must be distinct, so that believing P and believing Q can differ even if P and Q have the same truth value.

The last requirement is important: two agents can have different beliefs even if all their beliefs are true. "John believes that water is wet" and "John believes that H₂O is wet" seem to describe the same belief from one angle and different beliefs from another (John might not know that water = H₂O). This *intensionality* of attitude contexts requires that propositions be individuated more finely than by their truth values, and arguably more finely than by their truth conditions across possible worlds.

## Approaches to Propositions: A Taxonomy

| Theory | Constituents | Grain | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fregean (Thoughts) | Senses | Fine-grained | Abstract objects in third realm |
| Russellian (Singular) | Objects + properties | Fine-grained | Abstract complex entities |
| Possible worlds | Sets of worlds | Coarse-grained | Sets (abstract objects) |
| Structured (King/Soames) | Linguistic structure | Fine-grained | Facts about language use |
| Eliminativist | N/A | N/A | Propositions do not exist |

Each theory makes trade-offs between semantic precision, ontological parsimony, and epistemological accessibility. The possible-worlds account is technically elegant but cannot distinguish necessarily equivalent propositions. Fregean and Russellian accounts are fine-grained but raise questions about the nature of senses and the mechanism of reference. The eliminativist view is parsimonious but must explain away the apparent theoretical work propositions do.
