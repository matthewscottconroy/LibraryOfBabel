# Structured Propositions

The view that propositions have internal structure — that they are not just coarse-grained objects like sets of possible worlds but fine-grained complexes that reflect the structure of the sentences that express them — is one of the most actively debated topics in the philosophy of language and metaphysics.

Russell held that propositions are structured entities composed of the very objects and properties that they are about. The proposition expressed by "Plato is wise" contains Plato (the man himself) and wisdom (the property). It is a complex consisting of an individual and a property standing in a certain relation. The structure mirrors the predicate-argument structure of the sentence.

The motivation for structured propositions is the phenomenon of attitude reports. "John believes that the morning star is the morning star" seems clearly true, while "John believes that the morning star is the evening star" (about the same planet Venus) may be false — John does not know they are the same. Possible-worlds propositions cannot handle this: since the two sentences express propositions true in exactly the same possible worlds, they are the same proposition on the possible-worlds view. Structured propositions can distinguish them by their different constituent expressions.

The main challenge for structured propositions is explaining what structure is. If a proposition is composed of objects and properties, how do these constituents combine to form a unified proposition? Russell's "multiple relations theory" and later attempts to use lambda abstraction or other formal tools face versions of the unity of the proposition problem.

Contemporary work (King, Soames, Schiffer) has explored various ways of explaining propositional structure: as facts about linguistic expressions, as cognitive acts, or as abstract entities with their own ontological category distinct from sets of possible worlds.

## The Unity Problem

The unity of the proposition is one of the oldest and hardest problems in the theory of propositions. A proposition like *that Plato is wise* must be a unified entity — not just a list or collection of constituents (Plato, wisdom) but a single thing that is true or false. The components must be combined in a way that produces something with truth conditions.

If the proposition were just the pair ⟨Plato, Wisdom⟩, then Plato and Wisdom would be listed side by side without any relation being predicated of Plato. The list would be true or false? A list is neither true nor false — only a proposition is. So the proposition must include not just the constituents but their manner of combination: Wisdom being *predicated of* Plato, rather than just associated with Plato.

**Russell's "multiple relations" theory**: Russell proposed that belief is not a binary relation between a thinker and a proposition but a multiple relation between a thinker and the constituents of the proposition in a specific order. "S believes that Plato is wise" is analyzed as: Belief(S, Plato, Wisdom, Predication). This eliminates the need for propositions as unified entities — belief relates the thinker directly to the propositional constituents.

*Problem*: Without propositions, it is unclear what makes a belief true or false. The belief that Plato is wise is true because Plato has wisdom; but if there is no proposition that Plato is wise, we need another way to explain what the belief corresponds to in the world.

**Frege's solution**: Each component of a proposition is a *saturated* or *unsaturated* expression. A concept-word like "wise" is unsaturated — it has an "argument place" that must be filled by a singular term. When the term "Plato" fills the argument place of "...is wise," the result is a complete proposition. The unity comes from the complementary saturated/unsaturated nature of the components.

## Contemporary Structured Proposition Theories

**King's linguistic account**: Jeffrey King argues that propositions are facts about linguistic expressions and their semantic values. The proposition that Plato is wise is a fact of the form: [the word 'Plato' stands in the 'Wise' predication relation to the property Wisdom, as used in the sentence 'Plato is wise']. Propositions are facts about actual language use, not abstract entities existing independently of language.

*Advantage*: Unity is explained by the syntax of sentences — the subject-predicate relation among words corresponds to the predication relation among propositional constituents.

*Challenge*: If propositions are facts about language use, then propositions did not exist before language users existed — but surely the proposition that dinosaurs existed was true millions of years before anyone formulated it.

**Soames's acts-based account**: Scott Soames argues that propositions are *cognitive act types* — types of cognitive acts of predicating properties of objects. The proposition that Plato is wise is the type of cognitive act of predicating wisdom of Plato. Tokens of this act type are actual cognitive episodes.

*Advantage*: Unity comes from the unity of the act — predicating is a unified activity, not a mere aggregation.

*Challenge*: If propositions are act types, they exist only if cognitive agents exist to perform the relevant acts — again conflicting with the intuition that propositions have truth conditions independently of minds.

## Hyperintensionality

Structured propositions are motivated by hyperintensional contexts — contexts in which co-referring terms or necessarily equivalent expressions cannot be interchanged salva veritate (without changing truth value):

- "John believes that Hesperus is Phosphorus" may be false even if "John believes that Hesperus is Hesperus" is true, despite "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" co-referring.
- "Alice proved that the angle sum of a Euclidean triangle is 180°" may be true while "Alice proved that 2 + 2 = 4" is false, even though both sentences are necessarily true.
- "The Ancient Greeks worshipped Zeus" is true even though Zeus does not exist and the proposition would be impossible to evaluate in a possible-worlds framework.

Hyperintensional contexts show that even necessarily equivalent propositions must be distinguished. Possible-worlds propositions (sets of possible worlds) are identical for all necessarily true propositions — so they are all the same proposition on the possible-worlds view. This is intuitively wrong: the proposition that 2 + 2 = 4 is different from the proposition that Fermat's Last Theorem holds.

Structured propositions handle hyperintensionality by distinguishing propositions with different constituent structure: ⟨2, +, 2, =, 4⟩ has different structure from ⟨For all n > 2 and all integers a, b, c: aⁿ + bⁿ ≠ cⁿ⟩, even if both are necessarily true.

## The Fine-Grainedness Debate

How fine-grained should propositions be? Two extremes:

**Coarse-grained** (possible-worlds): Two sentences express the same proposition iff they are true in exactly the same possible worlds. This identifies all logical truths as one proposition and all logical falsities as another — clearly too coarse for attitude contexts.

**Fine-grained** (structural): Two sentences express the same proposition iff they have the same logical structure with the same constituents. "P and Q" and "Q and P" would then express different propositions — but it seems odd to distinguish "John and Mary arrived" from "Mary and John arrived" as expressing different propositions.

The appropriate grain for propositions likely lies somewhere in between: fine enough to distinguish "Hesperus is Phosphorus" from "Hesperus is Hesperus," but coarse enough to identify propositions that are obviously the same (like "it is raining" expressed at the same time and place by two different speakers). Finding a principled account of propositional identity that captures the right grain remains an open problem.
