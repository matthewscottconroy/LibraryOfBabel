# Russellian vs. Fregean Propositions

The two most influential theories of propositions in analytic philosophy are Frege's and Russell's, and their disagreement shapes the entire subsequent discussion.

Frege distinguished between sense (Sinn) and reference (Bedeutung). The sense of an expression is what it contributes to meaning beyond merely fixing the reference — it is the "mode of presentation" of the referent. The sense of "the morning star" and the sense of "the evening star" are different, even though both refer to Venus. Propositions (Gedanken, thoughts) are composed of senses: the thought expressed by "The morning star is visible at dawn" is composed of the sense of "the morning star," the sense of "visible at dawn," and their mode of combination.

Russell initially agreed, but then radically rejected the notion of sense. In his direct reference theory, names refer directly to their objects without mediation by senses. The proposition expressed by "Plato is wise" contains Plato himself — not a sense of Plato, not a description of Plato, but the actual man. This is the Russellian or "singular" proposition.

The disagreement has major consequences. On Frege's view, the proposition expressed by "Hesperus is Phosphorus" (Venus is Venus under two different names) is not the trivial identity but a substantive claim connecting two different senses. On Russell's view, since both names refer directly to Venus, the proposition is the trivial identity Venus = Venus.

The "Fregean intuition" that co-referring names can differ in cognitive value is powerful. The "Russellian intuition" that propositions directly concern the objects they are about is equally powerful. Kripke's work in *Naming and Necessity* reinvigorated Russellian views and challenged Fregean accounts of proper names, while Fregean approaches remain popular for indexicals and for explaining substitution failures in attitude contexts.

## Frege's Puzzle

Frege's original puzzle from "Über Sinn und Bedeutung" (1892) motivates the sense/reference distinction:

If "a = b" means "the object a is the same as the object b," then "Hesperus = Phosphorus" says that Venus = Venus — a trivial logical truth. But the discovery that Hesperus (the Evening Star) is the same planet as Phosphorus (the Morning Star) was a significant astronomical discovery, not a trivial logical truth. How can "Hesperus = Phosphorus" be genuinely informative if it says the same thing as "Hesperus = Hesperus"?

**Frege's solution**: Names like "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" differ in sense even though they share a reference. "Hesperus = Phosphorus" says that the sense of "Hesperus" and the sense of "Phosphorus" have the same reference — that the thing presented in the evening mode is the same as the thing presented in the morning mode. This is genuinely informative because the two senses are different modes of presentation of the same object.

More precisely:
- Reference of "Hesperus" = reference of "Phosphorus" = Venus
- Sense of "Hesperus" ≠ Sense of "Phosphorus"
- The proposition "Hesperus = Phosphorus" contains the senses, not the references
- Therefore, the proposition is not trivially true but substantively informative

## Russell's Response: Against Sense

Russell rejected Frege's senses in "On Denoting" (1905) and later work. His central argument: senses are mysterious entities whose nature is unclear. What is the sense of "Aristotle"? Frege never gave a satisfactory account. Senses seem to be needed only to explain certain puzzles, but they multiply entities unnecessarily.

Russell's alternative: proper names (genuine logical proper names, as opposed to disguised descriptions) refer directly to their objects. The name "Aristotle" does not have a Fregean sense — a mode of presentation — but simply picks out the man Aristotle directly.

**Singular propositions**: The proposition expressed by "Aristotle is a philosopher" contains Aristotle himself as a constituent, along with the property being a philosopher. This is a singular (or Russellian) proposition: ⟨Aristotle, Being-a-philosopher⟩.

**The cost**: If Aristotle had not existed, the proposition could not have been expressed (there would be no object to be a constituent). For sentences with empty names ("Pegasus is white"), there is no singular proposition — the sentence fails to express a proposition. Russell handled this by treating most ordinary names as disguised definite descriptions ("the man known as 'Pegasus,' who was winged, etc.") rather than genuine logical proper names.

## Kripke's Neo-Russellianism

Saul Kripke's *Naming and Necessity* (1972, 1980) revived direct reference theories of names and provided arguments against Fregean descriptions theories:

**Against descriptions**: If the name "Aristotle" means something like "the philosopher who taught Alexander the Great," then the statement "Aristotle was the philosopher who taught Alexander the Great" would be analytic — true by definition. But this seems contingent: Aristotle might have failed to teach Alexander. The description associated with a name is not its meaning.

**Rigid designation**: A name rigidly designates its bearer — it refers to the same individual in every possible world in which that individual exists. "Aristotle" refers to Aristotle in every world, even worlds where Aristotle is not a philosopher. Descriptions are not rigid — "the teacher of Alexander" refers to different people in different worlds.

**Necessary a posteriori**: If names are rigid designators, then "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is necessarily true (both names rigidly designate Venus, so they refer to the same thing in every possible world). But it is known only a posteriori (through empirical investigation). This separates necessity from analyticity and the a priori — a major result.

Kripke's framework is broadly Russellian: names refer directly without the mediation of Fregean senses. The proposition expressed by "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is (in worlds where Venus exists) the same as the proposition expressed by "Hesperus is Hesperus" — a necessary identity. The informativeness of "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is explained pragmatically or by appealing to epistemic situation, not by different senses.

## The Fregean Response to Kripke

Fregean philosophers have not conceded defeat. The standard neo-Fregean response to Kripke:

**Two-dimensional semantics** (Kaplan, Chalmers, Jackson): Names and natural kind terms have two dimensions of meaning — a "character" (a function from contexts to contents) and a "content" (a function from possible worlds to extensions). The informativeness of "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is explained by the different characters of the two names, even if they express the same propositional content (given the actual context).

**Centered worlds**: Fine-grained propositional content can be recovered by evaluating propositions at *centered possible worlds* (worlds plus a designated individual and time). In centered-world semantics, "I am here now" is a priori but not necessarily true of the same individual in all centered worlds. This provides resources for distinguishing a priori from necessary truth.

**Phenomenal concepts** (Levine, Chalmers): Even in a direct-reference framework, there may be *narrow content* — content that is individual-relative and context-relative — that explains why "Hesperus = Phosphorus" is informative even though both names refer to the same object. Narrow content is the Fregean sense smuggled back in under a different name.

The Russellian-Fregean debate remains unsettled, with each side able to explain the key data (Frege's puzzle, the informativeness of identities, the necessity of "Hesperus = Phosphorus") though with different theoretical costs.
