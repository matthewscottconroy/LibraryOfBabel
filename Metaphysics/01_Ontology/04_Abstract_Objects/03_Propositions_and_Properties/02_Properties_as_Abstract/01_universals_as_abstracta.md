# Universals as Abstract Objects

If there are universals — properties and relations that are genuinely shared by their instances — are they abstract objects? The question seems to have a natural affirmative answer: universals are not located in space and time, they are not causally efficacious in the way that particular events are, and they are discovered rather than invented. Redness seems to be the kind of thing that would exist even if no red things existed (if we allow uninstantiated universals) or that exists wherever red things are but is not itself locatable (if we require instantiation).

David Armstrong's immanent realism complicates this. Armstrong holds that universals are real but immanent: they exist only where they are instantiated. On his view, universals are not abstract in the sense of being non-spatial. Rather, the universal redness is located exactly where red things are. This makes universals a peculiar kind of entity: repeatable, capable of being wholly present in multiple places at once, but still spatiotemporally located.

Platonic realism about universals (associated with Frege and with philosophers like Bealer) holds that universals are genuinely abstract — existing outside space and time, accessible through a kind of rational intuition. This fits naturally with the identification of properties with propositional constituents: the same entity (the universal wisdom) appears in the proposition "Socrates is wise" and is instantiated by Socrates.

The distinction between immanent and transcendent universals is philosophically important. Immanent universals avoid the epistemological problem (we encounter them in sensory experience) at the cost of giving them a strange metaphysical status (wholly present in multiple locations). Transcendent universals have a cleaner ontological profile (abstract, eternal, necessary) but face the full brunt of the epistemological challenge.

## The Case for Transcendent Universals

Transcendent universals — universals that exist in a Platonic realm, independently of their instances — are motivated by several considerations:

**Uninstantiated properties**: Some properties seem to exist without being instantiated. The property of being a unicorn exists (we can think about it, define it, reason about it) even though no unicorns exist. The property of being a perfect philosopher exists even if no perfect philosophers have ever lived. Immanent realism cannot accommodate uninstantiated properties; transcendent realism can.

**Necessary existence**: If universals exist necessarily — if redness would exist even in a possible world with no red things — then they are not confined to the spatiotemporal realm. Their existence does not depend on contingent physical facts. Transcendent universals exist in all possible worlds.

**Propositional roles**: Universals appear as constituents of propositions. The proposition *that Socrates is wise* contains the universal wisdom. If propositions are abstract objects (as Frege argued), their constituents — including universals — must also be abstract.

**Epistemological accessibility**: We know about properties through a priori reasoning, not purely through sensory contact. We know that the property of being prime is different from the property of being even through mathematical reasoning, not through physical experimentation. This a priori accessibility fits better with transcendent universals (accessible to reason) than with immanent universals (accessible only through their physical instances).

## Armstrong's Immanent Realism

Armstrong's immanent realism is the most developed anti-abstractionist account of universals. Key features:

**Strict immanence**: Universals exist only if instantiated. There are no uninstantiated universals. "Uninstantiated property" is a contradiction — if it is not instantiated, it does not exist.

**Spatial location**: The universal Redness is located wherever red things are. It is not a point or a region; it is a universally distributed entity. Its presence is exactly where red objects are. Armstrong speaks of universals being "wholly present" in each instance — the whole universal is in each instance, not a part of it.

**Multiple instantiation**: How can one thing (the universal Redness) be wholly present in many different places simultaneously? Armstrong's answer: universals are a different *category* of entity from particulars, for which ordinary spatial concepts do not fully apply. Being wholly present in multiple locations is not incoherent for universals — it is constitutive of their being universals rather than particulars.

**Sparse universals**: Armstrong accepts only *sparse* universals — those that correspond to genuine, natural properties. "Being red" might be a universal (if color is a natural property of surfaces); "being either red or round" is not a universal (it is disjunctive and does not correspond to any objective similarity). The sparse universals are those that figure in laws of nature and ground genuine causal relations.

## The Epistemological Contrast

The immanent/transcendent divide generates an epistemological contrast:

**Immanent universals (Armstrong)**: We know universals through sensory experience of their instances. To know the universal Redness, we examine red things. Our epistemic access is through causal contact with the concrete instances that instantiate the universal.

*Advantage*: Fits a naturalistic, empiricist epistemology. We do not need to posit mysterious non-causal faculties.

*Challenge*: How do we know about uninstantiated properties, if there are none? And how do we identify the universal itself — the repeatable feature — rather than just the particular instance?

**Transcendent universals (Plato, Frege, Bealer)**: We know universals through rational intuition or through understanding of concepts. The universal Triangle is known a priori, not through examining particular triangles. Mathematical properties (being prime, being continuous, being a group) are known through mathematical reasoning, not through physical observation.

*Advantage*: Explains the a priori character of mathematical and logical knowledge.

*Challenge*: What is the mechanism of rational intuition? How do finite, physically-embedded creatures gain cognitive access to abstract, causally inert entities?

Both positions face a genuine epistemological challenge; the immanent realist faces a lighter version of it, while the transcendent realist faces it in its fullest form (the Benacerraf dilemma: causally inert abstracta are epistemically inaccessible if knowledge requires causal connection).
