# Wittgenstein and Facts

In the *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* (1921), the young Wittgenstein developed a picture theory of language and reality in which the world is constituted not by things but by facts — specifically, by states of affairs (Sachverhalte) and the facts (Tatsachen) that consist in their obtaining.

For Wittgenstein in the Tractatus, the world is the totality of facts, not of things. Things (objects) exist, but by themselves they constitute no fact; a fact is the combination of objects in a state of affairs. The state of affairs is the simplest kind of fact: a direct combination of objects without further complexity.

The picture theory holds that propositions picture facts by having the same logical form. A proposition is a fact about signs that pictures a possible fact about the world; it is true if the fact it pictures obtains and false otherwise. The connection between language and reality is thus structural: signs stand for objects, and the arrangement of signs in a proposition mirrors the arrangement of objects in a state of affairs.

Wittgenstein later repudiated much of the Tractatus, especially its picture theory and its commitment to a hidden layer of atomic facts. But the fundamental idea — that facts or states of affairs are the basic units of reality, not things and properties considered separately — has been highly influential. It shapes the work of Russell, the logical positivists, and later philosophers including Armstrong.

The Tractarian framework raises questions that remain active: Are there negative facts (states of affairs that do not obtain)? Are there general facts (the fact that all ravens are black, not just particular raven-facts)? These questions about the extent and character of the fact-ontology are among the most discussed in contemporary analytic metaphysics.

## The Structure of the Tractatus

The *Tractatus* opens with a precise statement of Wittgenstein's ontology:

**1.**: The world is all that is the case.
**1.1**: The world is the totality of facts, not of things.
**2**: What is the case — a fact — is the existence of states of affairs.
**2.01**: A state of affairs (a state of things) is a combination of objects (things).

This ontology is hierarchical:
- At the base: *objects* (Gegenstände) — the simple, unalterable constituents of reality. Objects have *form* — the possible ways they can combine with other objects.
- States of affairs (Sachverhalte): configurations of objects. These are atomic; they have no internal structure beyond the objects they combine.
- Facts (Tatsachen): existing states of affairs. The world is the totality of obtaining states of affairs.
- Negative facts: states of affairs that do not obtain. Whether these exist is a matter of controversy.

The picture theory of meaning:
- Elementary propositions directly picture atomic facts: they have the same logical form as the states of affairs they picture.
- A proposition is true iff the state of affairs it pictures obtains.
- More complex propositions are truth-functions of elementary propositions.

## Atomic Facts and Logical Atoms

The Tractatus is committed to the existence of *logical atoms* — simple objects that cannot be further analyzed, whose combinations constitute all states of affairs. Wittgenstein was deliberately vague about what these atoms are; the *Tractatus* does not identify them with any particular physical or mental entities.

**Russell's version**: Bertrand Russell, in "The Philosophy of Logical Atomism" (1918-1919) — written under Wittgenstein's influence — is more concrete. Russell's logical atoms are sense-data (immediate objects of sensory experience) and perhaps universals. Atomic facts are facts about sense-data having properties or standing in relations.

**The logical structure**: Both Wittgenstein and Russell hold that the logical structure of language mirrors the structure of facts. An atomic proposition "Fa" (a has property F) pictures an atomic fact of a having F. Molecular propositions are truth-functional combinations of atomic propositions.

**The regress problem**: Russell worried about the relationship between particulars and universals in facts. If the atomic fact "Socrates is wise" consists of Socrates (a particular), Wisdom (a universal), and the relation of instantiation between them, then we need to explain what ties Socrates to Wisdom in the fact. Bradley's regress looms: any additional relation requires a further relation to connect it, generating an infinite regress.

Wittgenstein's response in the *Tractatus*: the combination of objects in a state of affairs is immediate — there is no further entity (relation of instantiation) that holds them together. The objects directly combine; this combination is itself the fact. No relation of instantiation is needed because the objects' forms determine how they can combine.

## Negative Facts

One of the most discussed questions about Tractarian ontology: are there negative facts? The fact that Socrates was not married to Plato? The fact that there are no unicorns?

**Arguments for negative facts**:
1. Negative propositions like "Socrates was not married to Plato" are true. On a correspondence theory of truth, true propositions correspond to facts. So there must be negative facts.
2. Negative facts serve as truthmakers for negative truths. If there were no negative facts, what would make "there are no unicorns" true?

**Arguments against negative facts**:
1. Negative facts seem ontologically peculiar — how can a non-obtaining state of affairs be a genuine constituent of reality?
2. We can explain the truth of "Socrates was not married to Plato" by citing the positive facts that exclude it (his actual marriage to Xanthippe, etc.) without positing a separate negative fact.
3. Russell, initially attracted to negative facts, eventually tried to eliminate them by appeal to *totality facts* — the fact that the totality of Socrates's marriages is exactly {marriage to Xanthippe}. The totality fact grounds the negation without requiring a specifically negative entity.

**General facts**: Similar issues arise for general facts — the fact that *all* electrons have negative charge. This seems to be more than the conjunction of individual facts about each electron; it includes the fact that there are no electrons without negative charge — which seems general and negative. Armstrong posited "totality states of affairs" to account for general truths.

## The Legacy of Tractarian Ontology

The *Tractatus* ontology of facts has been enormously influential even among philosophers who reject the picture theory of language:

**Russell's analytic philosophy**: The program of *logical analysis* — breaking complex propositions down into elementary propositions that directly describe atomic facts — shaped British philosophy for decades. The goal was to show how complex truths (about material objects, minds, the past) are grounded in simple facts about sense data or sense qualities.

**Logical positivism**: The Vienna Circle used Wittgenstein's framework to develop the verificationist theory of meaning: the meaning of a statement is its method of verification, and verification is ultimately a matter of which elementary propositions (about observations) the statement is equivalent to.

**Armstrong's states of affairs**: Armstrong's *A World of States of Affairs* (1997) develops the most systematic contemporary ontology of states of affairs, inheriting the Tractarian idea that facts — not things and properties separately — are the fundamental units of reality.

**Truthmaker theory**: The contemporary debate about truthmakers is directly shaped by the Tractarian question: for every true proposition, what in the world makes it true? This requires an account of facts/states of affairs as truth-makers — objective features of reality that ground the truth of propositions.
