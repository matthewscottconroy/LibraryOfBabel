# Armstrong on States of Affairs

David Armstrong developed the most systematic contemporary theory of states of affairs, presenting it as the core of his ontological system across a series of books from the 1970s through the 1990s. For Armstrong, states of affairs — what he also calls "facts" — are the fundamental elements of reality. A state of affairs is a particular instantiating a universal: the fact that this ball is red, the fact that Socrates is wise, the fact that the distance from London to Edinburgh is approximately 640 kilometers.

Armstrong's states of affairs combine a particular (the ball, Socrates, the distance) with a universal (redness, wisdom, being approximately 640 km). The particular and the universal are each genuine constituents of the state of affairs, but the state of affairs is not merely their conjunction or collection — it is an additional entity, a structural complex in which they are unified. This is why Armstrong calls states of affairs "truthmakers": the truth of "this ball is red" is made true by the obtaining of the state of affairs composed of this ball and redness, which is a genuine concrete entity in the world.

Armstrong distinguished states of affairs that obtain (facts) from states of affairs that do not obtain. The obtaining state of affairs composed of Socrates and wisdom grounds the truth of "Socrates is wise"; the non-obtaining state of affairs that would involve Socrates and musical talent does not ground any such truth.

Armstrong's system assigns states of affairs a central role in the theory of laws: a law of nature is a necessitation relation between universals, which is itself a higher-order state of affairs. Laws are not mere regularities but states of affairs involving relations among universals, and these higher-order states of affairs necessitate the corresponding first-order regularities. This gives Armstrong's metaphysics a distinctive structure: the actual world is a totality of states of affairs, including both first-order states and the higher-order states that constitute laws.

## The Ontological Structure

Armstrong's states of affairs have a distinctive formal structure:

**Components**: A state of affairs S = [a, F] consists of:
- A particular a (an individual, concrete object)
- A universal F (a property or relation)
- The *instantiation* of F by a

The state of affairs is not reducible to the pair ⟨a, F⟩ — it is an additional entity in which a and F are unified by instantiation. Armstrong sometimes calls this the "thin particular" a combined with F to form a "thick particular" — the concrete object a considered with its properties.

**First-order and higher-order states**: First-order states of affairs combine particulars with first-order universals. Higher-order states of affairs combine universals with higher-order universals. Laws of nature are higher-order states of affairs: the law that all Fs are G is the state of affairs N(F, G) — the necessitation relation N holding between the universals F and G.

**The combinatorial problem**: If states of affairs are combinations of particulars and universals, which combinations are possible? Not every particular can be combined with every universal — not every electron can have any mass value. Armstrong's *combinatorialism* holds that any particular can, in principle, be combined with any universal, and any combination is possible. Constraints come from the actual laws of nature (which restrict actual combinations), not from essentialist claims about what is intrinsically possible for any individual.

## Armstrong's Argument for States of Affairs

Armstrong argues that states of affairs are needed because the mere existence of a particular and a universal is not sufficient for the particular to instantiate the universal. We cannot say:

"a exists AND F exists → a is F"

The existence of Socrates and the existence of Wisdom does not entail that Socrates is wise. Something additional is required: the obtaining of the state of affairs [Socrates, Wisdom]. This additional entity — the state of affairs — is what makes the predication true.

**The regress problem revisited**: Bradley's regress threatens: if instantiation is a relation, then to say that a instantiates F, we need a further relation to connect a and F with the instantiation relation — and so on. Armstrong's response: instantiation is not a relation in the ordinary sense. A state of affairs is not a collection of a, F, and a connecting relation. Rather, a and F are *fused* or *unified* in the state of affairs, without any further relating entity. The unity is primitive.

This response requires treating states of affairs as a *sui generis* ontological category — not reducible to collections of objects and properties. This is controversial: some philosophers argue that it merely labels the problem (calling the unity of the state of affairs "primitive") without explaining it.

## Laws of Nature as Higher-Order States

Armstrong's most distinctive application of states of affairs is to laws of nature:

**The regularity account fails**: Laws of nature are not mere regularities. "All electrons have negative charge" is a law; "all golden spheres are less than 1km in diameter" might be an accidentally true generalization. Both describe regularities, but only the first is a genuine law. A theory of laws must explain what distinguishes laws from accidental generalizations.

**Armstrong's solution**: A law is a second-order state of affairs: N(F, G) — the necessitation relation N holding between the universals F and G. This necessitation relation is a contingent higher-order universal that relates first-order universals. It explains the regular co-instantiation of F and G (every F is G) by grounding it in a real relation between the universals themselves.

**The inference problem**: The law N(F, G) is supposed to explain why all Fs are Gs. But how? Armstrong's answer: the necessitation relation between universals is a genuine (contingent) necessitation: N(F, G) entails that all Fs are G. This entailment holds in the actual world because N holds between F and G in the actual world.

Critics (van Fraassen, Lewis) argue that this is mysterious: how does a higher-order relation between universals necessitate lower-order regularities among particulars? Armstrong's necessitation seems to be a brute metaphysical connection with no further explanation.

## The Totality State of Affairs

To account for negative and general truths, Armstrong posited a special totality state of affairs: the state of affairs that the world contains exactly the first-order states of affairs that it does — no more and no less.

Without totality states, we cannot account for truths like "there are no unicorns" from positive states alone. The positive facts about actual animals do not themselves entail that there are no unicorns; they would be compatible with additional, unregistered facts about unicorns. The totality state adds: "and that's all" — grounding negative existentials.

Armstrong acknowledged that totality states are metaphysically awkward — they seem to be meta-states about the extent of the world rather than ordinary first-order states of affairs. But they appear necessary for the completeness of the fact-ontology.
