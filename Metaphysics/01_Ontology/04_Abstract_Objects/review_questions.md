# Review Questions — Abstract Objects

1. Three criteria for abstractness are discussed: the causal criterion (abstract objects cannot enter causal relations), the spatiotemporal criterion (they lack location), and the dependence criterion (they are mind- and matter-independent). Each faces counterexamples and borderline cases — laws of nature, fictional characters, institutional facts. Is any single criterion necessary and sufficient for abstractness, or is "abstract object" a cluster concept with no single defining feature? What consequences does your answer have for the debate between Platonism and nominalism?

2. The Quine-Putnam indispensability argument concludes that we should believe in mathematical entities because they are indispensable to our best scientific theories. Maddy objects that mathematical components of physical theories do not receive the same confirmational support as empirical components — scientists use idealizations they do not literally believe in. Field objects by attempting to nominalize physics entirely. Which objection is more fundamental, and does Field's conservativeness claim — that mathematics adds no new nominalist-physical consequences — successfully undercut the argument even if the nominalization program is incomplete?

3. Benacerraf's epistemological challenge argues that Platonism faces a dilemma: if mathematical objects are causally inert, we cannot stand in the causal relations required for knowledge, yet the semantic theory says mathematical truths are about such objects. The chapter surveys responses: Gödelian mathematical intuition, neo-Fregean logical access via Hume's Principle, and indispensability-based indirect access. Assess one of these responses in detail. Does it genuinely explain how we know about abstract objects, or does it merely redescribe the mystery?

4. Mathematical fictionalism holds that mathematical claims are true within the mathematical story but not literally true, and that mathematics is a useful representational tool rather than a source of ontological commitments. The fiction analogy faces a challenge: mathematical proofs feel like discoveries, not authorial choices, and what is provable is not arbitrary in the way that fictional details are. How does the fictionalist explain the objectivity and necessity of mathematics — the fact that 7 is necessarily prime and this is not up to anyone's decisions — without conceding that 7 exists?

5. Thomasson's "easy ontology" holds that once we have mastered the conceptual rules governing a term like "number," existential questions about numbers are trivially answerable within that framework, and there is no "heavyweight" metaphysical question left over. Sider responds that easy ontology conflates ordinary existence with fundamental existence — numbers may exist in the ordinary sense while failing to be part of what fundamentally structures reality. Is there a principled distinction between ordinary and fundamental existence, or is Sider's response itself committed to a notion ("fundamental existence") that needs the same deflationary treatment Thomasson applies to "number"?

6. Propositions are invoked as the bearers of truth, the contents of propositional attitudes, and the shared meanings of synonymous sentences across languages. But propositions create their own epistemological problem: they are abstract, causally inert entities, yet we seem to grasp, believe, and communicate them constantly. Compare this problem with Benacerraf's challenge about mathematical objects. Is the epistemological situation with propositions better or worse than with numbers, and what does this suggest about whether propositions should be admitted into the ontology?

7. Structuralism about mathematics holds that mathematical objects are positions in structures — the number 3 just is the third position in the natural number structure — and that multiple instantiations of the structure are all equally the natural numbers. This seems to dissolve the question of which set-theoretic construction is "really" the number 3 (von Neumann ordinals? Zermelo ordinals?). But Benacerraf's original argument used this very fact to argue against identifying numbers with any particular objects. Does structuralism vindicate Benacerraf's challenge, reformulate it, or dissolve it?

8. The chapter on abstract objects connects to earlier discussions of universals (which are themselves candidates for abstract entities), to Frege's analysis of existence as a second-order predicate, and to the ontological pluralism debate (whether abstract objects have a different mode of being from concrete ones). If abstract objects exist but in a different mode from concrete things — if they "subsist" rather than "exist" — does this help or hinder answers to the epistemological challenge? And what does the existence of propositions and mathematical structures imply about the scope of the ontological questions opened in Chapter 1 — are those questions themselves about abstract objects?
