# Domain Survey: Universals and Particulars

## Overview

The problem of universals is among the oldest and most persistent problems in philosophy. At its core, it asks what the world must be like for our most basic practices of classification, predication, and explanation to succeed. When we say that two tomatoes are both red, or that copper and gold are both metals, or that Aristotle and Plato were both philosophers, we seem to be saying that numerically distinct things share something — that there is a single feature present in each. But what exactly is this "sharing"? Is there a genuine entity — a universal — that exists wholly and completely in each instance, such that the very same thing is in two places at once? Or is there no shared entity, only similar-but-distinct particulars, with no further entity binding them?

The stakes of this debate are not merely terminological. Scientific explanation depends on genuine regularities: the same gravitational law applies to every massive body because every massive body has a property — mass — that a single law quantifies over. If there are no genuine shared properties, it becomes puzzling how the law can apply uniformly. Taxonomy in biology, chemistry, and medicine presupposes that natural kinds — species, elements, diseases — carve nature at genuine joints rather than imposing arbitrary human categories. If the boundaries are not objectively grounded in shared natures, the success of systematic classification needs a different explanation. At the same time, if universals are real abstract entities multiply instantiated in concrete objects, they raise difficult questions about the nature of instantiation, how we could come to know them, and how they can be in many places simultaneously without being divided.

The debate is structured along two main axes. The first axis distinguishes **realism** from **nominalism**: realists hold that there are genuine shared entities (universals or universal-like structures), nominalists hold that only particular things exist and that apparent generality is not grounded in shared entities. The second axis, internal to realism, distinguishes **Platonism** (universals exist separately from their instances, in an abstract realm) from **Aristotelian immanent realism** (universals exist only *in* their instances, immanent in the concrete world). A third major position, **trope theory**, occupies an intermediate position: it accepts that there are genuine property-like entities, but holds that they are particular rather than universal — the redness of this tomato is numerically distinct from the redness of that tomato, though the two tropes may belong to an objective resemblance class.

Contemporary debate has been transformed by the work of David Armstrong, whose defense of universals as constituents of states of affairs and as the truthmakers of laws of nature has set the terms for discussion since the 1970s. But rival positions — trope theory (D. C. Williams, Keith Campbell), resemblance nominalism (Gonzalo Rodriguez-Pereyra), and various deflationary approaches — remain serious competitors. The debate has also expanded to include questions about natural kinds (are they grounded in universals or in more fundamental physical structures?), the abundance versus sparseness of properties (are there properties for every predicate, or only for predicates that correspond to real joints in nature?), and the relationship between properties and laws of nature.

## Major Positions and Their Logical Relations

### The Realist Family

**Platonic realism** (ante rem realism): Universals exist independently of whether they are instantiated. There is the universal triangularity even if no triangular thing exists. Frege held this view for mathematical objects; some contemporary philosophers extend it to all properties and relations. Platonic universals are abstract — non-spatial, non-temporal, causally inert.

- *Advantage*: explains mathematical truth (numbers and properties exist even when not instantiated).
- *Problem*: the "third man argument" (Plato himself recognized it): if Socrates and Plato both share largeness, they must resemble the Form of Largeness; but then we need a Third Man relating them to the Form...

**Aristotelian immanent realism** (in re realism): Universals exist only when instantiated; they are literally present in their instances. Armstrong's position: states of affairs (*a's being F*) are the basic ontological units; universals exist as constituents of states of affairs; uninstantiated universals are not countenanced. The universal *redness* does not exist unless something is red.

- *Advantage*: avoids the Third Man; keeps universals in contact with the concrete world.
- *Problem*: spatial co-location — how can the numerically same thing be wholly present at multiple distinct spatial locations simultaneously?

**Structural universals** (Lewis's critique, Bigelow and Pargetter's defense): Some universals appear to be structural — methane is a universal that includes as parts the universals hydrogen and carbon in particular bonding relations. Lewis argued that structural universals are incoherent. Others argue that they are indispensable for chemistry and molecular biology.

### Trope Theory

**Trope theory** (D. C. Williams 1953, Keith Campbell 1990): Properties are particular rather than universal. The redness of this apple is a concrete, particular entity — a trope — numerically distinct from the redness of any other apple. Tropes are abstract (thin, property-like slices of concrete objects) but particular (not universal — each is unique). Material objects are bundles of co-located tropes (the bundle theory with tropes rather than universals).

Two sub-varieties:
- *Simple trope theory*: tropes are the only basic entities; material objects are bundles of compresent tropes; classes of resembling tropes replace universals.
- *Trope + substrate theory* (some versions): tropes inhere in substrata (thin objects) that individuate them.

**Resemblance among tropes**: tropes share their "qualitative character" not by being numerically identical (they are particulars) but by standing in a primitive resemblance relation. The class of all exactly resembling tropes plays the role that the universal *redness* played for the realist.

*Problem*: Is resemblance itself a trope, or a universal? If a universal, trope theory needs universals after all. If a trope, we face Bradley's regress (how does the resemblance trope relate to the tropes it relates?).

### The Nominalist Family

**Predicate nominalism**: There is no entity corresponding to the predicate "is red" other than the predicate itself. Red things are red because they fall under the predicate; the predicate is applied to them because of some feature of language or cognition, not because of a shared entity in the world.
- *Problem*: this seems to get the order of explanation backward; red things are red, and the predicate applies to them because they are red, not vice versa.

**Resemblance nominalism** (Gonzalo Rodriguez-Pereyra 2002, Nelson Goodman): Red things form a resemblance class. There is no universal redness; instead, there is a primitive resemblance relation among red things, and red things are red in virtue of belonging to the resemblance class defined by paradigms.
- *The coextension problem*: two predicates that happen to apply to exactly the same things will correspond to the same resemblance class; but they may pick out distinct properties (triangularity and trilaterality).
- *The companionship problem* (Goodman): if all and only red things happen also to be round, the predicates "red" and "round" yield the same resemblance class.

**Class nominalism**: Properties are identified with classes. The property *redness* = the class of all red things.
- *Problem*: classes are abstract objects, so class nominalism does not avoid abstract objects; it replaces one abstract entity (the universal) with another (the class).

**Ostrich nominalism** (Quine, Devitt): Simply deny that there are any universals or tropes; there is no entity corresponding to "is red" beyond the red particular things themselves; "red" is a predicate we apply, and the application does not require grounding in a shared entity. This view denies that there is a genuine problem of universals — or that any answer other than "none" is needed.
- *Problem*: how does it explain the regularities in nature, the applicability of laws, and the apparent objectivity of predication?

### Particulars and Individuation

**Substratum theory**: A particular individual = a thin object (substratum or bare particular) + its properties. The bare particular is what makes the individual *this one* rather than that one — it is a "this" without being a "such." Defended by Herbert Bergmann and by some interpretations of Locke's substance.

**Bundle theory**: An individual is a bundle of universals (or tropes) standing in the compresence relation. There is no substratum; the individual just is the totality of co-occurring properties.
- *Black's spheres counterexample*: Two perfect spheres, exactly alike in all qualitative respects, are conceivable. On the bundle theory, two qualitatively identical bundles would be numerically identical — but the two spheres are two. So the bundle theory fails to individuate.
- *Bundle theory response*: (i) deny that Black's scenario is metaphysically possible; (ii) include relational properties (being two inches from the other sphere) in the bundle, which differ for each sphere.

**Haecceities**: Some philosophers posit primitive thisness (haecceitas, Duns Scotus) — a non-qualitative property of being this very individual that distinguishes individuals even when qualitative properties coincide. Plantinga, Adams, and Fine have defended haecceities in the context of modal individuation.

## Key Arguments and Counterarguments

### The One Over Many

**P1.** Multiple distinct particulars can genuinely share a property — be genuinely the same way (same color, shape, kind).
**P2.** For multiple distinct particulars to genuinely share a property, there must be a single entity that they all stand in relation to.
**C.** There are universals (entities that can be wholly present in multiple distinct particulars).

*Nominalist responses*: reject P2 via predicate nominalism, resemblance nominalism, or ostrich nominalism. *Realist response to resemblance nominalism*: resemblance itself appears to be a universal (all resemblance pairs share the property of resembling), generating a regress.

### Bradley's Regress

F. H. Bradley argued that the relation of instantiation — the "link" between a particular and its universal — must itself be related to each relatum by a further relation, generating an infinite regress. If the particular *a* instantiates the universal *F*, there must be a relation *R1* relating *a* to *F*; but then *R1* must be related to *a* and *F* by a further relation *R2*... No matter how many relations we posit, the regress never terminates.

**Responses**: (i) Armstrong: instantiation is not a relation; it is an internal tie that holds in virtue of its relata and does not require further grounding. (ii) Fine: the predicational nexus is primitive and sui generis. (iii) Lewis: Bradley's regress is harmless because it never requires more than what is already committed to — it is an infinite series of truths, not a series of distinct entities.

### The Argument from Natural Kinds

**P1.** The success of natural science requires that natural kinds — elements, species, fundamental physical properties — are real.
**P2.** Natural kinds are real only if there are genuine shared properties.
**C.** There are genuine shared properties (universals or tropes in resemblance classes).

**Objection**: the success of science does not require that kinds are grounded in universals; it requires only that there are reliable patterns of co-occurrence. Patterns are explicable nominalistically.

### The Sparse/Abundant Distinction (Lewis)

Lewis distinguished **sparse** (or natural) properties from **abundant** properties. Sparse properties are those that correspond to genuine joints in nature — the fundamental properties posited by physics. Abundant properties exist for every predicate, every open sentence, every class. Lewis was a realist about classes (he identified properties with classes of possibilia), but held that sparse properties are what ground similarity, laws, and causation, while abundant properties serve only to characterize possibilities.

This distinction is now standard: **Armstrong** identifies sparse properties with universals picked out by mature science; **Lewis** identifies them with classes of their instances among all possibilia; **trope theorists** identify them with classes of exactly resembling tropes.

## Key Papers and Books

1. **Armstrong, D. M., *Universals and Scientific Realism*, Vols. I–II (1978)** — Definitive defense of Aristotelian immanent realism; the standard reference against which nominalist alternatives are measured. Develops the theory that states of affairs, not substances or properties alone, are the basic ontological units.

2. **Williams, D. C., "On the Elements of Being" (1953)** — Founding paper of contemporary trope theory; introduces the notion of a trope as a "abstract particular" and develops the bundle theory with tropes as constituents.

3. **Rodriguez-Pereyra, Gonzalo, *Resemblance Nominalism* (2002)** — The most systematic contemporary defense of resemblance nominalism; works through the companionship and coextension problems with precision.

4. **Lewis, David, "New Work for a Theory of Universals" (1983)** — Introduces the sparse/abundant distinction, argues that natural properties are required to make sense of laws, causation, and supervenience, while declining to take a strong stand on whether they are universals or something else.

5. **Campbell, Keith, *Abstract Particulars* (1990)** — Develops trope theory with great care; shows that a trope ontology can handle the full range of phenomena that motivate universalism without the co-location problem.

6. **Lowe, E. J., "The Metaphysics of Abstract Objects" (1995)** — Argues for a four-category ontology that integrates universals and particulars within a structured framework that neither reduces one to the other.

7. **Gonzalo Rodriguez-Pereyra and Oliver, Alex, eds., *Properties* (1997)** — Standard anthology collecting the most important papers in the contemporary debate.

8. **Black, Max, "The Identity of Indiscernibles" (1952)** — Introduces the two-spheres thought experiment that challenges the bundle theory of particulars; still the defining text in the individuation debate.

## Live Debates and Open Questions

1. **The status of relations**: Properties seem to have an argument: red is a one-place property. Relations are multi-place: *larger than* takes two arguments. Do relations pose special problems for realism? How do universals for relations relate to the relata? (Russell's regress for relations parallels Bradley's regress for properties.)

2. **Higher-order properties**: Can universals themselves have properties? Is being-a-mass-property itself a property? If so, is it a universal? An infinite hierarchy of higher-order universals seems to be required. Armstrong resisted this; others have embraced it.

3. **Properties in quantum mechanics**: Are the "properties" posited by quantum mechanics — spin, charge, entanglement — genuine universals in Armstrong's sense? The non-individuality of quantum particles (the Pauli exclusion principle, bosonic bunching) seems to challenge both bundle theory and substratum theory. French and Ladyman have argued this motivates structural realism.

4. **The reference of natural-kind terms**: Kripke and Putnam argued that natural-kind terms like "water" and "gold" rigidly designate natural kinds, and that the essential natures of those kinds are discovered empirically. Does this commit one to realism about natural kinds as genuine universals, or can it be accommodated by trope theory or resemblance nominalism?

5. **Truthmakers for negative existentials**: If "there are no unicorns" is true, what makes it true? Armstrong's totality facts (it's a fact that the list of existing things is exhausted by...) are a response, but they seem to reintroduce holistic entities inconsistent with a sparse ontology.

6. **Determinates and determinables**: Is scarlet a universal distinct from red, or is it the same universal more specifically characterized? The determinate/determinable relation is distinct from instantiation, and it is unclear how to accommodate it in any of the main theories.

## Connections to Other Domains

**Universals and Ontology (Domain 01)**: The universals debate is the most concrete expression of the Quinean criterion of ontological commitment and of the grounding framework. Nominalists typically embrace Quinean parsimony; realists typically appeal to explanatory indispensability.

**Universals and Identity/Persistence (Domain 03)**: The identity conditions of universals (is triangularity one property or many?) connect directly to questions of identity across time and across possible worlds. Haecceities are posited in both the individuation debate (what makes this individual *this*) and the modal debate (what makes this individual the same individual across worlds).

**Universals and Modality (Domain 05)**: Essential properties — properties that an entity could not lack while remaining what it is — presuppose a distinction between universal-like features that constitute essence and those that are merely accidental. Armstrong's universals, because discovered by science, connect naturally to de re necessity.

**Universals and Laws of Nature (Domain 04)**: Armstrong, Dretske, and Tooley developed the DTA (Dretske-Tooley-Armstrong) account of laws, on which laws are relations between universals rather than universal generalizations. The viability of this account depends on the reality of universals. Regularity theorists (Humeans) about laws do not require universals.

**Universals and Metaphysics of Science (Domain 10)**: Natural kinds in science — elements, species, physical magnitudes — are the primary candidates for universals in a naturalistically acceptable ontology. The debate about whether science discovers real properties feeds directly into the universals debate.
