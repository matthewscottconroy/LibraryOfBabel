# Universals and Particulars

Consider a simple observation: two tomatoes, side by side, both red. You see two things, but you also see something they share. Whatever it is that makes each of them red seems to be the same feature — not merely similar, but identical. Both are crimson in the same sense; the redness of one is not a pale echo of the redness of the other but exactly the same quality. Now ask: what is that shared thing? Is there a property, redness, that literally exists in two places at once, present in each tomato and instantiated by both? Or are the tomatoes' colors really two distinct items — two particular bits of redness — that merely resemble each other very closely? And if we go the second route, what exactly is resemblance, and can we explain it without eventually appealing to something that the two tomatoes genuinely share?

This is the problem of universals, and it has structured ontology for more than two thousand years. It is not a problem that arose from abstruse philosophical speculation. It arises the moment you notice that different things can be the same way — that multiple objects can share a color, a shape, a kind, a role. The metaphysical question is whether that sameness is genuine or a construction, whether there are real universals present in many particulars or only particular things that happen to resemble one another.

The stakes are higher than they initially appear. Scientific explanation proceeds by identifying laws that apply universally — the same law of gravitation governs every massive body. Classification systems in biology, chemistry, and medicine presuppose that natural kinds are real, that the boundaries between species or elements or diseases cut nature at genuine joints rather than imposing arbitrary human categories. If there are no universals, no real shared properties, then it becomes deeply puzzling how our most successful theoretical practices could be as effective as they are. On the other hand, if universals exist as abstract entities instantiated in multiple concrete things, we face hard questions about how we come to know them and how they connect to the physical world we perceive.

This unit examines the debate from its historical roots to its contemporary ramifications. We begin with the **problem of universals** itself — its classical formulation, the realist tradition that embraces universals as genuine entities, and the nominalist tradition that denies them in favor of explanations that appeal only to individual things and their resemblances. The contrast between Platonic realism (universals exist separately from their instances) and Aristotelian immanent realism (universals exist only in their instances) is already philosophically rich and consequential.

We then examine **trope theory**, an influential contemporary alternative that tries to steer between extreme realism and austere nominalism. Tropes are property-instances — the particular redness of this tomato, numerically distinct from the redness of any other. They are concrete rather than abstract, particular rather than universal, yet they serve many of the theoretical functions that universals were invoked to perform. Whether tropes succeed in this mediating role, and whether they can handle the full range of phenomena that motivated universalism, is one of the liveliest questions in current ontology.

The third chapter addresses **particulars and individuation**: what makes a particular thing *this one* rather than that one, even when two things share all the same qualitative properties? The problem of individuation forces a choice between positing bare particulars — a mysterious substratum that individuates without characterizing — and the bundle theory, which identifies a thing with its collection of properties and must somehow explain how two qualitatively identical things could be numerically distinct.

Finally, we turn to **contemporary debates** that bring the classical questions into contact with current philosophy of science and metaontology. Are properties natural and sparse, corresponding to genuine similarities in nature, or are they abundant and gerrymandered? What do scientific predicates commit us to? How do we adjudicate ontological questions without begging them? These are not merely technical puzzles. They bear on what it means to take science seriously as a guide to what reality contains.
