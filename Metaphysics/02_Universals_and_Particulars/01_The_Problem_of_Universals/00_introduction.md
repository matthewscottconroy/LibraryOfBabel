# The Problem of Universals

Plato posed it first, though he posed it in a form that may seem strange to modern eyes. Consider the beautiful: individual beautiful things — faces, sunsets, mathematical proofs — are beautiful, but each in a different way and only imperfectly so. The Form of Beauty, by contrast, is perfectly and immutably beautiful, the thing that all beautiful things participate in and derive their beauty from. This Form is more real than the particulars that share in it; the particulars are its fleeting, imperfect copies. The problem of universals inherits this Platonic insight — that there is something shared among similar things — while stripping away (or at least questioning) the extravagant metaphysics of separately existing Forms.

In its most careful contemporary formulation, the problem is this: different particulars share properties. Two doves are both white; two humans are both rational; two triangles are both three-sided. This apparent sharing cries out for explanation. The realist explains it by positing a universal — whiteness, rationality, triangularity — that is genuinely identical across its instances, fully present wherever it is instantiated. The nominalist denies that there is any shared entity and offers instead an account in terms of resemblance among particulars, or in terms of linguistic conventions, or in terms of the facts about predication without any further ontological explanation.

The choice between realism and nominalism is not merely terminological. It determines whether the world has a kind of internal structure — a structure of shared features — that our concepts track, or whether the categories we use to organize the world are imposed by us rather than discovered in things. In the first case, science discovers genuine natural kinds with real boundaries; in the second, our classificatory practices impose those boundaries on a world that in itself has no such divisions.

This chapter traces the debate from its ancient origins through medieval discussions of realia versus nomina, to the systematic alternatives developed in contemporary analytic philosophy. The historical journey through Plato's Forms, Aristotle's immanent realism, and the scholastic controversy between realism and nominalism is not mere historical archaeology; it reveals the full range of options and illuminates why the problem has been so persistent. Contemporary realism, as developed by Armstrong and others, holds that universals are not Platonic Forms but real properties immanent in their instances; contemporary nominalism, in its various forms (predicate nominalism, class nominalism, resemblance nominalism), attempts to do without universals while preserving the phenomena they were invoked to explain.

The reader who works through this chapter will emerge with a precise grasp of what the problem is, why it matters, and why neither obvious answer is satisfactory.
