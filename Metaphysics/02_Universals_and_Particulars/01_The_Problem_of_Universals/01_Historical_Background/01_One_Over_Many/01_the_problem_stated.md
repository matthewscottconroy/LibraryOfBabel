# The Problem Stated

---

Here is a puzzling fact: two roses, an apple, and a London bus are all red. They are in different places, they are distinct things, and we can count them. Yet there is something they have in common — their redness. The question is what to say about that something.

If redness is a genuine shared entity, it is a very peculiar one. It appears to be wholly present in the rose in your garden and wholly present in the bus at Oxford Circus at the same time. One thing, entirely in two places at once — that is not how ordinary objects behave. Yet the claim that these things share a color seems too obvious to deny. What exactly is going on?

Realists about universals say: redness is a real entity — a universal — that is genuinely instantiated by each red thing. The universal is one; its instances are many. Hence the problem's traditional name: the "one over many." The predicate "is red" applies correctly to all these objects because they all stand in a common relation to a single shared entity.

Nominalists reject this. Each red thing has its own redness — its own individual quality — and there is no further entity called redness that they all possess. The predicate applies to all of them, but this is a fact about the predicate's range of application, or about resemblances among the things themselves, not about an abstract object they jointly instantiate.

This is not a merely verbal dispute. It bears on the structure of reality: whether genuine repeatable features exist in the world or whether similarity is a projection of our conceptual schemes onto a landscape of purely individual things. It matters for the theory of natural laws, for the epistemology of science, for the analysis of mathematical truth, and for ethics. Wherever we are tempted to say that multiple things "have the same property," the problem of universals is quietly present.

## The Argument at Its Core

We can put the pressure point more sharply with an argument Armstrong takes in spirit from Plato:

- P1. Multiple distinct particular things can share a single property (e.g., being red).
- P2. For any shared property F, there is some entity in virtue of which each F-thing is F, and which accounts for the objective similarity among F-things.
- P3. This entity cannot itself be one of the particular F-things — it would then not be shared.
- C. Therefore, there exists an entity — distinct from all particulars — that is the property F, and that is common to all F-things.

Notice that each premise is contestable. Nominalists reject P2, arguing that a predicate can apply to many things without there being any grounding entity beyond the particulars themselves. Trope theorists accept P2 but deny that the relevant grounding entity need be non-particular: it could be an individual property-instance, numerically distinct in each object but resembling its counterparts in others. Realists about universals accept all three premises and read the conclusion at face value.

The challenge is sharpest when we turn to *relations*. When we say "Edinburgh is to the north of London" and "Cambridge is to the north of Oxford," the shared relation *being to the north of* seems to resist elimination far more stubbornly than any monadic property. Russell's argument from relations has persuaded many philosophers that relational universals, at least, are very hard to avoid.

## What Counts as a Property?

Before positions can be compared, we need to register an important ambiguity. The word "property" carries at least three distinct weights in the literature.

First, there are *abundant* properties: for any grammatically well-formed predicate, there is a corresponding property. Being red, being non-red, being either an electron or a prime minister, being grue — all qualify. The abundant conception serves logic and semantics.

Second, there are *sparse* properties: only those that carve nature at its joints, that ground genuine similarities, and that figure in causal laws. This is Lewis's notion of *naturalness* and Armstrong's universals. Which properties are sparse is a question for empirical science, not conceptual analysis.

Third, there are *predicational* properties: whatever predicates express or apply in virtue of, left open as to whether these are entities, classes, Fregean concepts, or linguistic constructs.

The universals debate is primarily about sparse properties. The question is whether there are mind-independent, ontologically substantive entities corresponding to the objective similarities we find in nature — or whether such similarities can be accounted for without entities of this kind at all.

## The Long View

The problem received its first systematic treatment in Plato's theory of Forms, its first major internal critique in the *Parmenides*, and its most influential ancient revision in Aristotle's immanent realism. The medieval debates between Abelard, Aquinas, and Ockham represent the most intricate pre-modern development. Frege's logical analysis of predication, Russell's arguments from relations, and Armstrong's two-volume *Universals and Scientific Realism* (1978) brought the problem into the analytic tradition with new precision. Today it is conducted with the tools of formal ontology, possible-worlds semantics, and philosophy of physics — but the central question is recognizably the same one that troubled Plato in the *Republic*.
