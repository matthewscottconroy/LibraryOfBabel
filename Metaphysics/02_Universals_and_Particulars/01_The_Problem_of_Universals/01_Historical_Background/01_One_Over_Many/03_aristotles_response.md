# Aristotle's Response

---

Aristotle's response to Plato begins with an agreement and then a sharp revision. He agrees: universals are real, and they explain the genuine commonalities among things. What he denies is that they exist in some separate, transcendent realm. For Aristotle, universals are *in* particular things — wholly present in each instantiation — not above them or prior to them. The universal HORSE exists because and only because there are horses. When all the horses are gone, HORSE is gone too.

This is *immanent realism*. The motivation is partly explanatory. If the Form of Horse exists in a separate realm, how does its existence explain anything about particular horses? Participation — the relation between a horse and the Form — is, as the *Parmenides* showed, deeply obscure. And if Forms are causally inert, existing separately from the natural world, how do they cause horses to be horses? Aristotle's immanent universal sidesteps this problem entirely: HORSE is the form of a horse, present in each horse and explaining why it is the kind of thing it is. The form is causally efficacious precisely because it is present in the thing, not because it reaches down from a separate realm.

This also dissolves the Third Man regress. The regress was generated by the assumption that Forms are themselves instances of themselves — LARGENESS is large, being the paradigm. Aristotle denies this at the root: universals do not instantiate themselves. The universal REDNESS is not red; it is a repeatable entity that grounds the redness of particular things without itself being a further red thing.

## Aristotle's Critique Formalized

Aristotle's objections to Platonic Forms in *Metaphysics* I.9 and XIII–XIV can be organized as two connected arguments.

**The Separation Argument**:
- P1. Platonic Forms exist separately from the particulars that instantiate them.
- P2. What exists separately from sensible things cannot causally interact with sensible things (causal inertness of the transcendent).
- P3. If Forms are causally inert with respect to sensible things, they cannot explain why sensible things are as they are.
- C. Platonic Forms do not explain the properties of sensible things.

**The Regress Argument (Third Man)**:
- P1. If F-particulars share a Form F, there must be a single entity (F-ness) in virtue of which they are F.
- P2. On Plato's view, F-ness is itself F — being the paradigm of F-ness.
- P3. By P1 applied to F-ness and the F-particulars, there must be another Form, F-ness₂.
- C. The regress is infinite and vicious: Forms cannot explain predication.

Aristotle's alternative avoids both problems by locating the universal *in* the particular: the form of a horse is present in the horse itself, is causally efficacious through the horse, and is not itself a horse requiring a further form.

## Hylomorphism and the Role of Form

For Aristotle, each substance — a particular horse, a particular human — is a composite of *matter* (*hyle*) and *form* (*morphe*). The matter is what individuates; the form is what makes the thing the kind of thing it is. The universal HORSE is the form common to all horses: not a separately existing entity but the very principle that organizes equine matter into horses.

This hylomorphic analysis gives Aristotle's universals a dual function. They are at once ontological (constituents of substances) and epistemological (what the intellect grasps when it understands what a horse is). Knowledge is of the universal — the form — but the form exists in the particular. There is no gulf between the objects of knowledge and the objects of perception; they are the same entities apprehended by different faculties. This unification of epistemology and metaphysics is one of the most powerful features of the Aristotelian position.

## The Cost: Uninstantiated Universals

The central cost of Aristotle's revision becomes visible when we consider *uninstantiated* universals. If all the horses in the world were to die simultaneously, HORSE would cease to exist — universals exist only in their instances. But this creates genuine difficulties. Mathematical truths about unicorns, perfect geometrical figures, and merely possible structures seem to be genuine truths, not truths about nothing. If the relevant universals do not exist, what are these truths about?

Consider too that scientific laws often describe what *would* happen in conditions that never actually obtain. The law that gold melts at 1064°C remains true even if no gold is ever actually melting. On Aristotelian immanent realism, is the universal MELTING-AT-1064°C available to ground this law when no gold is melting? Armstrong, who defends a version of Aristotelian immanent realism, adds the *Principle of Instantiation* — every genuine universal is instantiated — and handles laws through necessitation relations between actually instantiated universals, accepting some revisionary consequences for counterfactual-supporting laws. Whether this is an acceptable cost remains contested.

## Historical Legacy

Aristotle's immanent realism is the model for all subsequent moderate realisms: Aquinas's moderate realism (*universale in re*), Armstrong's universals in states of affairs, and various neo-Aristotelian positions in contemporary metaphysics. The core commitment — that properties are real, objective, and shared, but exist only *in* the things that have them — remains one of the two dominant poles of the universals debate alongside Platonic transcendent realism.
