# Realism in the Middle Ages

---

The medieval dispute over universals was not, as it is sometimes portrayed, a scholastic curiosity. For several centuries it was the central controversy in Western philosophy — and the stakes were theological as much as metaphysical. Consider the Trinity: if the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are three persons who share a single divine nature, is that nature a universal? An extreme realist who identifies the persons with that shared nature risks either tritheism or the collapse of genuine personal distinction. Moderate realism, holding that the three persons share one numerically single divine nature, became the orthodox solution. Nominalism, meanwhile, threatened to make natural law merely a matter of divine command — if GOODNESS is not a real entity, there is nothing preventing God from having made cruelty virtuous by decree.

This is the context in which Boethius's commentary on Porphyry's *Isagoge* arrived in the medieval curriculum. Porphyry had raised, without answering, three questions about genera and species that defined the problem for a millennium: Do they exist, or are they bare intellectual conceptions? If they exist, are they corporeal or incorporeal? If incorporeal, do they exist separately from or within sensible things? The answers mapped directly onto the main positions:

- Extreme realist: they exist; they are incorporeal; they exist separately (Platonism).
- Moderate realist: they exist; they are incorporeal; they exist *in* sensible things (Aristotelian immanentism).
- Conceptualist: they exist, but only in the mind as concepts (*post res*).
- Nominalist: they are only words or mental signs; only individuals exist.

The most extreme form of realism — associated, controversially, with Eriugena and with William of Champeaux in his earliest phase — held that universals are real entities existing independently of and prior to individuals. On the most aggressive version, individual humans are not really distinct from each other: they are merely manifestations of a single universal humanity. Abelard's famous critique destroyed this view efficiently: if all humans are one, then when Socrates is in Athens and Plato is in Corinth, the same universal humanity is simultaneously present in both places doing contradictory things. This consequence was absurd, and everyone acknowledged it.

## Aquinas and the Threefold Distinction

Aquinas's synthesis — drawing heavily on Avicenna's Arabic Aristotelianism — provided the most sophisticated moderate realism. The key move was to distinguish three modes of existence for a nature: as an idea in the divine mind (*ante res* — before things), as a form in particular things (*in rebus* — in things), and as a concept in human minds (*post res* — after things).

On this view, HUMANITY as such is neither universal nor particular — universality and particularity are modes of existence, not intrinsic features of the nature itself. The nature HUMANITY becomes universal when abstracted by the intellect from many particular humans; it becomes particular when it exists in a specific human (Socrates's humanity). Considered in abstraction from both modes — considered just as HUMANITY — it is neither one nor many.

We should notice how subtle this is. It avoids the absurdity of extreme realism: there is no single spatially fragmented entity being pulled in contradictory directions when Socrates and Plato act differently. Yet it preserves genuine objectivity: the nature is not merely a mental fiction but corresponds to the real form present in particulars. And it aligns with the theological view that God's divine ideas serve as archetypes for created natures — the *ante res* existence of universals in the divine intellect grounds both the Platonic and the Aristotelian dimensions of the account.

## The Theological Stakes

Several theological disputes turned directly on the universals debate. The Eucharist required an Aristotelian hylomorphic framework: the substance (form) of the bread changes in transubstantiation while the accidents (individual properties) remain — a doctrine that presupposes a metaphysics of substance and form continuous with the universals debate. And for ethics: if universals are real, then GOODNESS and JUSTICE are real entities, and natural law can be grounded in objective facts about human nature. Nominalism threatened to make ethics entirely dependent on divine command, with no independent standard by which to measure God's commands. This is not a peripheral implication but cuts to the foundations of medieval moral theory.

## The Recovery of Aristotle

Before the recovery of Aristotle's logical and metaphysical works in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, medieval philosophy worked primarily with neo-Platonic and Augustinian resources. After the recovery — mediated through Arabic translations and commentaries by Avicenna and Averroes — Aristotle's *Categories*, *Prior Analytics*, *Metaphysics*, and *Physics* provided new conceptual tools and new arguments. Albertus Magnus and Thomas Aquinas labored to synthesize Aristotle with Christian theology, producing the most sophisticated moderate realisms of the period. The recovery of Aristotle is a turning point not only in medieval philosophy but in the entire history of the universals debate: it gave moderate realism the metaphysical machinery it needed to become a serious alternative both to Platonic transcendence and to the nominalism that would soon challenge it.
