# Ockham and Nominalism

---

Consider the multi-location problem for universals. If HUMANITY is a single entity wholly present in Socrates and wholly present in Plato, then we seem committed to saying that one thing is entirely in two places at once. This seemed strange already in ancient debate, and it was the weapon with which Abelard destroyed his teacher's extreme realism. William of Ockham turned the same argument into a systematic demolition of all universals — including the moderate, immanent variety Aristotle had proposed.

Ockham's core argument is simple. A universal entity would be a single thing wholly present in each of numerically many distinct particulars. But nothing can be numerically one and at the same time be wholly present as numerically one thing in many numerically distinct things — this would involve the same thing being both one and many, which is contradictory. Therefore, there are no universal entities. Full stop.

The realist has two lines of escape. First, deny the second premise by accepting that universals are a special kind of entity to which ordinary numericity rules do not apply. Second, distinguish type-identity from token-identity: universals are identical across instances in the sense of type-identity, not token-identity (this is roughly Armstrong's later response). Ockham found neither move convincing. The first simply stipulates that universals are mysterious in exactly the way needed to avoid the problem; the second trades one obscurity for another.

## The Mental Language Doctrine

Having cleared the ground, Ockham needed to explain general predication without universals. His constructive contribution is the doctrine of *mental language*: a system of mental terms prior to any conventional spoken or written language.

The mental concept "horse" is a natural sign of horses. It is produced in the mind by perception of horses, it signifies every horse, and it does so not by convention but by its cognitive nature — the kind of cognitive representation it is. Spoken and written terms get their significance by being conventionally imposed to express what the corresponding mental terms naturally signify.

This explains how general predication works without universals:

1. I perceive many horses and form the mental concept "horse."
2. This concept is a mental particular — a specific mental act or habit — that signifies every horse.
3. When I predicate "horse" of Bucephalus, I apply a mental sign that signifies Bucephalus and every other horse.
4. No universal entity is invoked — only the mental sign and the particular horses.

The account explains the *objectivity* of predication: the concept is constrained by the nature of the things it is formed to signify. We cannot just decide to form a concept of horses that also signifies rocks; the natural sign is calibrated by actual horse-perception. But this objectivity is grounded in particulars and their cognitive effects on us, not in shared universal entities.

## Ockham's Razor

*Entia non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem* — entities are not to be multiplied beyond necessity. Or more directly: *frustra fit per plura quod potest fieri per pauciora* — it is futile to do with more what can be done with fewer. These are the methodological principles motivating Ockham's economical ontology.

We should be careful about what the razor does and does not claim. It does not rule out universals a priori — it is a guide for choosing between theories of equal explanatory power, not a metaphysical axiom. Applied to universals: if we can explain predication, induction, and natural classification using only particular things and mental signs, there is no necessity to posit universal entities. The razor cuts them out — but only because Ockham believed nominalism was genuinely explanatorily adequate. Whether that belief was well-founded is the question subsequent debate has tried to answer.

## The Long Downstream

Ockham's nominalism initiated the *via moderna* — the modern way — in late medieval philosophy, contrasting with the *via antiqua* of Thomism and Scotism. His immediate successors, John Buridan and Gabriel Biel among them, developed various versions of nominalism that influenced late medieval logic and theology.

The influence on early modern philosophy is profound. Descartes inherits the mental-term framework but recasts it in the language of ideas. Locke's empiricism — that all ideas derive from experience and that there are no abstract general ideas in the Platonic sense — is a recognizable descendant of Ockham's account of mental terms and natural signs. Berkeley's rejection of abstract ideas and Hume's bundle theory of the self continue the tradition. Modern conceptualist and nominalist views in the philosophy of mathematics, logic, and language all bear the marks of Ockham's methodological and ontological revolution. When Armstrong wrote his two-volume case for universals in 1978, he was responding to a tradition whose source runs through Paris in the fourteenth century.
