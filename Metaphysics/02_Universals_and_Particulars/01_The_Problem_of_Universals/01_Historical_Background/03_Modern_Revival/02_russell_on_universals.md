# Russell on Universals

---

Consider this observation: Edinburgh is to the north of London. That is a relational fact. It concerns a relation — *being to the north of* — that holds between two things. Now consider: Cambridge is to the north of Oxford. The same relation appears again. It is not a new relation — it is literally the same one. The relation *being to the north of* is shared between two distinct pairs of cities, and it is shared in a way that seems non-negotiable.

This is the core of Bertrand Russell's argument for universals in *Problems of Philosophy* (1912). We might imagine eliminating universals for monadic predicates by some clever reduction — perhaps the redness of a particular red thing can be reduced to something about that thing alone, without positing a shared entity. But relations, Russell argued, resist this treatment. To say "Edinburgh is to the north of London" is to say something about a relation holding *between* them, not something analyzable as a separate fact about Edinburgh and a separate fact about London. Relations are irreducibly relational. And if the same relation holds between many pairs of things — which it obviously does — then relations are universals.

The nominalist might respond that relations are mental associations, patterns we impose on pairs of things. But Edinburgh's being to the north of London is an objective geographical fact; it holds regardless of whether anyone thinks about it. The relation is not imposed by us but discovered. This clinches the case, for Russell: if relations are objective, and if they recur across many instances, then there are relational universals.

## The Argument from Relations

The argument can be formalized:

- P1. There are true relational propositions: "a is north of b," "a resembles b," "a loves b."
- P2. The truth of relational propositions is grounded in facts, and facts involving relations are genuinely relational — they cannot be reduced to facts about the relata taken separately.
- P3. What grounds a relational fact is the relation itself: a relation is the shared entity that can hold between different pairs of relata.
- P4. Relations holding between multiple pairs of relata are universals (one entity, many instances).
- C. There are relational universals.

The corollary is immediate: if there are any universals at all — relational ones being ineliminable — the nominalist's general program fails. The nominalist must either show that relational facts reduce to non-relational ones, or accept at least some universals and then face pressure to accept more.

## Acquaintance with Universals

Russell's epistemology reinforces the ontological point. He distinguishes *knowledge by acquaintance* (direct cognitive contact with something, without mediation by description or inference) from *knowledge by description* (knowing something only under some characterization). Russell held that we can be acquainted not only with particular sense data and minds but also with universals. When I perceive two qualitatively identical white patches of paper, I am directly acquainted with the patches *and* with the universal WHITENESS that they share. The acquaintance is not mediated by description; it is direct recognition.

The epistemological point supports the ontological claim. If universals were nothing — if there were no such entities as whiteness — then acquaintance with whiteness would be acquaintance with nothing, which is a contradiction. The directness of our cognitive access to universals is evidence that there is something there to be accessed.

## Bradley's Regress

Russell engages with a version of the regress that F. H. Bradley had developed. Bradley argued that relations cannot be real: if a relation R holds between a and b, then the holding of R between a and b requires a further relation R' to hold between a and R, and between R and b, generating an infinite regress.

Russell's response is that Bradley's regress is not vicious. To say that R holds between a and b is to assert the truth of a proposition — a complex entity with a, b, and R as constituents. There is no further relation needed to "connect" these constituents, because propositions are complex unities, not mere aggregates of their parts. The unity of the fact [a-R-b] is a primitive feature of the fact, not the result of a further connecting relation. This response relies on facts being irreducible unities, which is a substantive commitment — but it is the core of Russell's strategy, and it influenced Armstrong's later doctrine that states of affairs are irreducible to their constituents.

## Legacy in Analytic Ontology

Russell's defense of universals established several themes that dominated analytic metaphysics for the rest of the twentieth century. The argument from relations remains one of the strongest arguments for universals; nominalists must show how relational predications can be handled without relational universals. The doctrine that *facts* (or states of affairs) are the fundamental truth-makers for atomic propositions became Armstrong's central thesis. The commitment to acquaintance as a model for direct epistemic access to abstract entities reappears in Gödel's Platonism about mathematics. And the distinction between particulars and universals as corresponding to the subject/predicate distinction in logic became the framework within which the debate is conducted throughout the twentieth century.

Russell's 1912 account in *Problems of Philosophy* remains one of the most readable introductions to the universals debate, and its core arguments have not been definitively answered by any subsequent nominalist.
