# Twentieth-Century Debate

---

The problem of universals did not disappear with the rise of analytic philosophy; it was transformed and given new life by the technical resources of modern logic — and first nearly killed off by the positivist critique of metaphysics.

The logical positivists of the Vienna Circle treated the problem as a pseudo-problem. Questions about whether universals "exist" are not empirically verifiable and therefore meaningless, on the verificationist criterion. Carnap's *Aufbau* attempted to construct all empirical knowledge from a basis of individual experiences and logical constructions — no universals needed. This approach influenced Goodman's later constructive nominalism, which tried to reconstruct science without quantification over abstract objects.

Quine transformed the debate by connecting it to his criterion of ontological commitment. In "On What There Is" (1948), he distinguished *names* (singular terms that designate individuals) from *predicates* (general terms that apply to individuals) and argued that predicates do not carry ontological commitment: "is red" can apply to many things without requiring the existence of a universal REDNESS. Universals would only be required if we needed to quantify over them — as in mathematical theories. This sets up the indispensability argument for abstract objects while dissolving the classic one-over-many problem for ordinary predicates.

Then came Armstrong. His two-volume *Universals and Scientific Realism* (1978) revived the traditional debate in its most systematic analytic form, arguing that nominalist accounts uniformly fail and that immanent universals are required for an adequate ontology of properties and natural laws. This work sparked a wave of activity that continues to the present, with nominalists, trope theorists, and various brands of realists developing increasingly sophisticated positions.

## The Positivist Interlude

Carnap's 1950 paper "Empiricism, Semantics, and Ontology" sharpened the deflationary challenge. For Carnap, the question "Do universals exist?" is either a *pragmatic* question about which linguistic framework to adopt — and then it has a trivial answer once we settle on a framework — or an *external* question asked outside any framework, where it has no cognitive content.

This deflationary move prompted Quine's most direct engagement. Quine denied Carnap's distinction between internal and external questions: all questions about existence are on the same level, and we answer them by constructing the best overall theory of the world. Ontological commitment follows from what the best theory quantifies over. The universals debate becomes, on this picture, a genuine question to be decided by philosophical theorizing — not trivially answered by framework-choice, and not meaningless.

## Quine and Goodman's Nominalism

Quine and Goodman's joint paper "Steps Toward a Constructive Nominalism" (1947) represents the most ambitious twentieth-century attempt to reconstruct mathematics without abstracta. Their project was to show that all of mathematics needed for science could be expressed using only concrete entities — physical objects, inscriptions, sequences of marks — without quantifying over abstract objects such as sets, numbers, or universals.

The project was ultimately abandoned. Quine publicly reversed his nominalism after concluding that quantification over numbers and sets was genuinely indispensable for physics. Mathematics cannot be replaced by mereological sums of concrete things when those theories require quantification over infinite sets. Goodman continued to pursue nominalism in *The Structure of Appearance* (1951) and in his later work on worldmaking, but even he accepted that the program was incomplete.

The Quine-Goodman nominalism had three key features: concrete entities only; mereological sums acceptable (since they are concrete); and predication without properties (the truth of "x is red" is not grounded in the existence of REDNESS as an entity). The difficulty — mathematics — revealed that nominalism about abstract objects is far harder to sustain than nominalism about properties.

## Armstrong's Revival and the Connection to Laws

Armstrong's strategy in *Universals and Scientific Realism* was both negative and positive. He systematically surveyed all major nominalist alternatives — predicate nominalism, class nominalism, resemblance nominalism, and trope theory — and argued that each fails on at least one crucial test case: predication, similarity, laws of nature, or the possibility of uninstantiated properties. Having cleared the field, he developed a positive theory: universals understood as immanent, instantiation-dependent, sparse entities that form *states of affairs* with the particulars that instantiate them.

The positive theory is connected throughout to the metaphysics of natural laws. Armstrong (and independently Tooley and Dretske) argued that natural laws cannot be mere regularities among particulars — they must be grounded in relations of necessitation between universals. The law that copper conducts electricity is analyzed as **N(COPPER, CONDUCTS-ELECTRICITY)**, where N is a second-order necessitation relation. The law is not merely a coincidence in the actual world; its necessity is explained by the universals COPPER and CONDUCTS-ELECTRICITY standing in that relation.

Lewis's alternative — the Best Systems Account of laws, combined with the primitive naturalness of certain classes — shows how a committed nominalist can preserve much of what the realist wants to say about laws without positing universals. The debate between Armstrong's view and Lewis's view remains one of the central disputes in contemporary analytic metaphysics. Its resolution — if there is one — will settle questions not only about ontology but about the foundations of physics, the nature of causation, and the possibility of scientific explanation.
