# Realism About Universals

The realist about universals holds that in addition to particular individuals — this rose, that sunset, your computer — there exist universals: entities that can be wholly present in multiple particulars simultaneously, and that ground the genuine qualitative similarity between those particulars. When two roses are both red, the realist says they genuinely share something: a single entity, redness, that is instantiated by each. This entity is not a third rose; it is not material; it does not have a unique spatiotemporal location. It is a universal — and its existence is what makes the predicate "red" truly applicable to multiple things.

The principal argument for realism is that only universals can ground natural laws and support counterfactuals. Laws of nature seem to say something about all things of a certain kind — not just all actual electrons, but any electron whatever. If laws are relations among universals (as Armstrong argues), then the modal force of laws becomes intelligible: N(F, G) — the universal F necessitates the universal G — is what backs the claim that all Fs are Gs, not merely accidentally but nomically. If there are no universals, laws collapse into regularities, and regularities cannot explain why we should expect future Fs to be Gs.

Within realism, there are two main positions. Platonic realism holds that universals exist independently of whether they are ever instantiated — redness exists even if there are no red things, just as mathematical structures exist even if there are no physical systems that exemplify them. This "ante rem" realism maximizes the existence of universals but faces the epistemological problem acutely: how do we ever come to know about uninstantiated universals? Aristotelian realism holds that universals exist only in re — in the particulars that instantiate them. If there were no red things, there would be no redness. This avoids the epistemological problem at the cost of making universals existence-dependent on their instances, which has consequences for the analysis of laws about absent and possible things.

This section examines the main versions of realism about universals, the arguments that support each, and the difficulties each must face.
