# Transcendent Universals

---

Consider a perfectly natural number, or a perfect circle, or the property of being a prime. No human being has ever drawn a perfect circle; no physical object perfectly instantiates that geometrical property. Yet there seems to be a fact of the matter — a determinate truth — about what it would be for something to be a perfect circle, and about which things approximate it more or less closely. Platonic transcendent realism holds that this is because the property of being a perfect circle exists, necessarily and independently, whether or not anything instantiates it.

Transcendent universals are universals that exist in their own right, independently of whether any concrete thing instantiates them. The property of being a prime number greater than 10^1000 exists even though no one has ever specified it. These properties are not causally active in the physical world — they do not produce effects — but they are genuinely real, constituting a domain of abstract being alongside the concrete physical world.

The most compelling arguments for transcendence come from mathematics. Mathematical truths seem to hold necessarily and universally, applying in every possible situation to every possible structure satisfying the relevant axioms. The Pythagorean theorem holds for any right triangle, not just those we have encountered. If mathematical truths are grounded in mathematical universals — properties of triangles, relations among numbers — and if these truths hold necessarily, then the universals that ground them must exist necessarily too. And necessary existence is incompatible with dependence on contingently existing particulars. Hence the universals must transcend their instances.

## The Ontological Argument for Transcendence

The strongest argument for transcendent (as opposed to immanent) universals turns on the modal status of mathematical truths:

- P1. Mathematical truths are necessary truths — they hold in every possible world.
- P2. What grounds a truth must exist as long as, or whenever, the truth holds.
- P3. If the universals grounding mathematical truths were immanent (existing only in their instances), then they would fail to exist in possible worlds where the relevant instances do not exist.
- P4. In such worlds, the mathematical truths would lack grounds and would therefore fail to hold (or hold vacuously, not substantively).
- P5. But mathematical truths hold substantively in every possible world — they are not vacuous.
- C. Mathematical universals are transcendent: they exist in every possible world, independently of whether there are instances.

One can dispute P2 — perhaps truths do not require grounding entities in every world where they hold, perhaps they can hold in virtue of logical necessity rather than in virtue of existing entities. But P2 captures a broadly truthmaker-theoretic intuition that has been widely influential, and rejecting it requires explaining how a necessary truth can hold with no entity grounding it.

## The Three Worlds Picture

Frege's mature philosophy, and later Popper's "third world" doctrine, articulate a three-realms picture: (1) the physical world of concrete, causally efficacious, spatiotemporal particulars; (2) the mental world of subjective experiences and beliefs; (3) the abstract world of objective contents — thoughts, propositions, numbers, properties — that are neither physical nor mental.

On this picture, a mathematical fact like "7 is prime" exists in the third world. It is objective (not dependent on any individual's mental state), universal (grasped by anyone who understands the relevant concepts), and non-physical (not located in space or time). Frege argues that the contents of thought — what he calls *thoughts* (*Gedanken*) — are entities of the third world, grasped by multiple thinkers without being located in any one mind.

This three-worlds picture has attracted mathematicians who find it natural to think of mathematical objects as discovered rather than invented. It also fits the phenomenology of mathematical inquiry: mathematicians report exploring a domain of objective truths, not constructing arbitrary formal systems.

## Transcendence vs. Immanence

The disagreement between Platonic transcendence and Aristotelian immanence can be stated sharply.

Transcendent realism holds that universals exist independently of their instances. The universal REDNESS would exist even if there were no red things. Its existence is necessary and does not depend on the existence of any concrete particular. Immanent realism holds that universals exist only in their instances. REDNESS exists because and only because there are red things. If all red things ceased to exist, REDNESS would cease to exist.

Transcendence is required if we want universals that have no actual instances — uninstantiated properties — and for the necessary existence of mathematical and logical universals. Immanence avoids the epistemological problem: if universals are in the particulars we perceive, we have causal access to them through those particulars, and need no special faculty of abstract intuition. But immanence cannot accommodate the modal fixity of mathematical truth.

Armstrong explicitly rejects transcendence in favor of immanence, accepting as a consequence that there are no uninstantiated universals. Plato explicitly endorses transcendence, accepting as a consequence that universals are causally inert and known by rational intuition rather than perception. The epistemological challenge this generates — how do we access causally inert entities? — is the subject of Benacerraf's 1973 challenge and remains the deepest problem for any Platonic view.
