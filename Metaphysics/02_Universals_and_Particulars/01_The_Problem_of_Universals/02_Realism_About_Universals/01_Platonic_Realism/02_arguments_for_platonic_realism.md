# Arguments for Platonic Realism

---

Several independent lines of argument converge on Platonic realism — the view that there are abstract, necessarily existing universals. We should work through them in turn, since they draw on different philosophical resources and have different vulnerabilities.

**The Indispensability Argument** begins with the observation that our best scientific theories quantify over mathematical objects — real numbers, matrices, Hilbert spaces. If these theories are true — not merely empirically adequate instruments but genuine descriptions of reality — then mathematical objects exist. And mathematical objects, if they exist, are abstract and necessary rather than concrete and contingent: they could not have failed to exist, and they are not located in space and time. This gives us indispensability-based grounds for transcendent abstract objects.

The argument (due in its modern form to Quine and Putnam) runs:

- P1. We ought to believe in the existence of all entities that are indispensable to our best scientific theories.
- P2. Mathematical entities — numbers, sets, functions, spaces — are indispensable to our best scientific theories.
- P3. Mathematical entities, if they exist, are abstract and non-spatiotemporal.
- C. We ought to believe in the existence of abstract entities.

Field's nominalist program (*Science Without Numbers*, 1980) attempts to defeat P2 by restating physical theories in purely nominalistic terms, using spacetime points and geometric relations without quantifying over numbers. The program is technically impressive but widely viewed as incomplete, and many philosophers regard P2 as well-confirmed by the actual practice of physics.

**The Semantic Argument** proceeds from the surface grammar of natural language. General predicates like "is red" and abstract nouns like "redness" function as singular terms in ordinary sentences: "Redness is a color," "Triangularity is a property of geometrical figures." If these sentences are literally true — as they seem to be — then "redness" and "triangularity" must denote entities. These entities are not concrete particulars (redness is not located anywhere), so they must be abstract. And since they apply to potentially many things, they are universals.

The nominalist's standard response is *paraphrase*: "Red is a color" can be paraphrased as "Every red thing is colored." This avoids a singular term referring to a property. But the quality of the paraphrase is contested. Does it preserve the original sentence's meaning, or does it change the subject? "Red is a color" and "Every red thing is colored" are logically related but do not seem to mean the same thing.

**The Argument from Objective Similarity** takes a different starting point — not linguistic or scientific practice but the pre-theoretical datum that some things genuinely resemble each other and others do not. Two electrons both have charge -e. Two rubies are both red. Two protons both have the same mass. These similarities are objective facts about the world, discoverable by science, and consequential for causal interactions. By contrast, a collection consisting of an electron, a ruby, and the Eiffel Tower shares no such objective similarity.

The best explanation for why some objects are genuinely similar and others are not is that genuinely similar objects share a universal — a single entity wholly present in each. Objects that share the universal ELECTRON-CHARGE are genuinely similar in charge; objects that do not share any common universal have no objective ground for similarity. Nominalist alternatives — resemblance nominalism and class nominalism — try to explain objective similarity without universals, but both face the challenge that resemblance and natural class membership seem to require further explanation in terms of objective features of the things involved, which appears to bring universals back in.

**The Argument from Laws of Nature** presses on a different flank. Natural laws are general truths that hold necessarily within nature — "copper conducts electricity" is not a lucky accident that happens to hold throughout the universe. The best explanation of this necessity invokes universals standing in necessitation relations. If properties were merely collections of individuals, the regularities would be accidental. Positing universals grounds their necessity.

## Uninstantiated Properties and the Modal Argument

Consider the property of being a unicorn. It has no actual instances. But "being a unicorn" is a meaningful predicate — there is a fact about what it would be for something to be a unicorn. The best account is that there is a universal UNICORN that exists but has no instances. A universal with no instances cannot be an immanent universal (which exists only in its instances). Therefore UNICORN is a transcendent universal.

Armstrong blocks this at the third step: he denies that there are uninstantiated universals. For Armstrong, "being a unicorn" is meaningful because it can be analyzed as a complex structural universal built from instantiated component universals — MAMMAL, HORN, and so on — even though the full complex is never instantiated. Platonists find this response strained: it seems to multiply constraints on what predicates can express, and it does not obviously work for all uninstantiated predicates (consider "being a particle with charge exactly 10^10 times the electron charge"). The debate here is not resolved, but the pressure toward transcendence remains.
