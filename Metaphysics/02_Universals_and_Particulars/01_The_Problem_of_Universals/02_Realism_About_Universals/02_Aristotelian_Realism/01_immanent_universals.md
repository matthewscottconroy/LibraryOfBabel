# Immanent Universals

---

Immanent realism is Aristotle's revision of Plato: universals are real, genuinely shared features of things, but they do not exist in a separate realm. They exist wherever and whenever the things that have them exist. The universal REDNESS is present — wholly present — in each red thing. When all red things cease to exist, the universal REDNESS ceases to exist too.

This move preserves the realist's core claim while avoiding the epistemological challenge facing Platonic realism. If universals are present in concrete things, and we perceive and causally interact with concrete things, then we have indirect epistemic access to universals through ordinary perception and induction. No separate faculty of abstract intuition is required. We perceive the universal REDNESS by perceiving red things, and we abstract the universal from those perceptions.

Immanent universals are characterized by what Armstrong calls "the strange feature of being wholly present in each of their instances simultaneously." The universal REDNESS is wholly in this rose and wholly in that tomato and wholly in the London bus — not distributed across them or partially present in each. This seems puzzling. But Armstrong's response is that universals are types, and types are not in locations the way tokens are. The rose is in London; the universal is not in London except in the derivative sense of being instantiated by something in London. The apparent mystery dissolves once we distinguish the type/token relationship from the ordinary spatial containment relationship.

## The Principle of Instantiation

Armstrong's version of immanent realism is committed to the **Principle of Instantiation**: every genuine universal is instantiated by at least one particular. This follows from the rejection of transcendence — if universals do not transcend their instances, they cannot exist without instances.

The Principle of Instantiation has revisionary consequences worth registering clearly. Uninstantiated universals do not exist: there is no universal UNICORN-NATURE since there are no unicorns. If all electrons were annihilated, the universal ELECTRON-CHARGE would cease to exist. Mathematical universals that have no physical instances — the property of being a perfect sphere — do not exist unless there are abstract mathematical instances, but if the ontology is naturalistic, there are no such abstract instances.

Armstrong handles uninstantiated universals by distinguishing actual universals (instantiated in the actual world) from possible universals (potentially instantiated in other possible worlds), treating the latter as logical constructions out of actually instantiated universals rather than as genuinely existing abstract entities. This is a more economical ontology, but it faces the challenge of explaining mathematical truth: if the mathematical universals are not genuinely instantiated, what grounds mathematical truths?

## Multi-Location and Structural Universals

The multi-location of immanent universals generates a challenge:

- P1. REDNESS is wholly in this rose (in London).
- P2. REDNESS is wholly in this tomato (in Tokyo).
- P3. London and Tokyo are distinct spatial locations.
- P4. No concrete entity can be wholly in two spatially distinct locations simultaneously.
- C. REDNESS is not a concrete entity.

Armstrong's response: universals are not the kind of thing for which spatial individuation is applicable. The rose is in London; the universal is not in London in the same sense — it is instantiated by something in London, which is a different relationship. This response is defensible but incurs a cost: if universals are not spatially located in the straightforward sense, the claim that they are *immanent* (in things) becomes more figurative than metaphysically substantive.

Lewis criticized Armstrong's account of *structural universals* — complex universals corresponding to molecular structures like BEING-A-METHANE-MOLECULE — on the ground that the standard notion of composition for universals leads to absurdities. If the universal METHANE is the mereological sum of the component universals CARBON, four HYDROGENS, and four BONDING-RELATIONs, then any molecule containing those components — including larger molecules like ethane — would also instantiate METHANE. The structural universal fails to distinguish METHANE from ETHANE.

Armstrong's response is that structural universals are not mereological sums but genuinely novel entities whose constituents are unified in a specific pattern. The structure is irreducible to the sum of its parts. Lewis was not convinced. The debate turns on whether there is a coherent notion of "composition" for universals that differs from mereological composition, and this remains unresolved.

## Connection to Armstrong's Naturalism

Armstrong's immanent realism is shaped throughout by philosophical naturalism: the view that reality consists of nothing over and above what natural science reveals, and that metaphysics should be continuous with science rather than a rival to it. Three consequences follow.

Universals are postulated because they are needed to explain natural regularities and laws — not because they are required by logical or semantic theory alone. The question of which universals exist is an empirical, not an a priori, question: physics tells us what the genuine natural properties are. And there is no Platonic realm: universals exist only in the natural world, as constituents of states of affairs.

This naturalistic orientation distinguishes Armstrong's immanent realism sharply from Platonic realism and from non-naturalist versions of property realism — Moore's non-natural properties in ethics, or Frege's third world of logical objects. If Armstrong is right, the universals are whatever our completed physics finds to be the genuine causal kinds. Metaphysics cannot specify them in advance; it can only articulate the framework within which scientific discovery fills in the details.
