# Armstrong on Universals

---

David Armstrong's defense of universals is the most comprehensive and influential in contemporary analytic philosophy. It is built on a negative argument and a positive theory, and both are essential to its force.

The negative argument comes first. In the first volume of *Universals and Scientific Realism* (1978), Armstrong surveys every nominalist alternative — predicate nominalism, class nominalism, resemblance nominalism — and argues that each fails to account for at least one of the following: the objective similarity structure of the world, the necessity of natural laws, or the distinction between natural and gerrymandered properties. We can put his critique of each briefly.

Against predicate nominalism: to say an electron has negative charge is not merely to say it falls under the predicate "has negative charge." Electrons had negative charge before there were humans with predicates. Properties are mind-independent features of the world; predicate nominalism makes them anthropocentric.

Against class nominalism: to be F is to be a member of the class of F-things. But classes have their members essentially. If the class of red things is {a, b, c...}, it is necessarily the class containing exactly those things. This collapses possible properties into actual ones and cannot handle modal variation in property instantiation.

Against resemblance nominalism: to be F is to sufficiently resemble the paradigmatic F-things. But resemblance itself seems to be a universal — A resembles B *in the same way* that C resembles D. The same type of resemblance recurs. The resemblance nominalist who uses the predicate "resembles" to ground all other properties must either posit a universal RESEMBLANCE or be in an explanatory regress.

## States of Affairs as the Fundamental Category

Armstrong's positive theory makes *states of affairs* — facts — the fundamental category, with both particulars and universals as abstractions from them. The state of affairs [a's being F] is the fundamental entity; the particular *a* and the universal *F* are each abstractable within it but do not exist separately. This is captured by Armstrong's dictum: "No universal without particulars that instantiate it; no particular without universals that it instantiates." Universals and particulars are always found together in states of affairs, never independently.

Truth-making follows naturally: the state of affairs [a's being F] is the truth-maker for the proposition "a is F." Neither a alone nor F alone can serve as truth-maker — a might exist without being F, and F might be uninstantiated by a. Only the state of affairs makes the proposition true. If the state of affairs exists, the proposition is true; if not, false.

Formally, using standard ontological notation:

- **Particular**: *a*, *b*, *c*... (an individual, the kind of thing that can fall under predicates)
- **Universal**: *F*, *G*, *R*... (a repeatable property or relation)
- **State of affairs**: [*a*'s being *F*], [*a*'s standing in *R* to *b*]... (the obtaining of a universal at a particular)

Armstrong holds that the ontological inventory of the world consists of states of affairs. Everything else is either analyzed in terms of states of affairs or is a derived entity.

## Higher-Order Universals and Laws of Nature

Armstrong's account of natural laws is built on *higher-order universals*: universals that hold between first-order universals. The law that copper conducts electricity is analyzed as:

**N(COPPER, CONDUCTS-ELECTRICITY)**

where N is the *necessitation* relation — a second-order universal holding between the first-order universals COPPER and CONDUCTS-ELECTRICITY. The law is not merely a regularity; it is a fact about universals that necessitates the regularity among particulars.

This analysis explains the necessity of laws: since the necessitation relation holds between universals, it holds in virtue of what those universals are, and any particular instantiating COPPER thereby necessitates a particular instantiating CONDUCTS-ELECTRICITY. It distinguishes laws from accidental generalizations: both might be expressed as "All F are G," but only the law involves N(F, G). And it grounds counterfactuals: "If this piece of non-copper metal were copper, it would conduct" is true because N(COPPER, CONDUCTS-ELECTRICITY) holds.

Van Fraassen raises what he calls the "inference problem": even if N(F, G) holds, why should it follow that each particular F-thing is G? The necessitation relation is a fact about universals, but the law is supposed to govern particulars. There seems to be a gap between the higher-order fact and the first-order regularities.

Armstrong's response: it is part of the *meaning* or *nature* of N that its holding between F and G entails that all F-things are G. The necessitation relation is constitutively such as to generate the first-order regularities. This is not a further postulate but analytic of what necessitation means. Whether this response adequately closes the gap — or merely redescribes it — remains a live question.
