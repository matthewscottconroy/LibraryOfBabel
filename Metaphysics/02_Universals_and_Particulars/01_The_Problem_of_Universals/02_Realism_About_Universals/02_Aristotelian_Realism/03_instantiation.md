# Instantiation

---

If universals are real entities and particulars are real entities, the relation that connects them — instantiation or exemplification — is one of the most philosophically scrutinized relations in metaphysics. When Socrates is wise, there is Socrates, there is WISDOM, and there is the tie between them that makes the fact a fact. What exactly is this tie?

The difficulty is made vivid by what the *Parmenides* already suggested and Bradley later formalized as a regress. If Socrates instantiates WISDOM via an instantiation relation I, then the fact of Socrates-instantiating-WISDOM involves three things: Socrates, WISDOM, and I. But how are Socrates and WISDOM connected to I? Does each of them instantiate a further relation I' that connects them to I? And so on? If each connection requires a further connecting entity, the chain never terminates and the original fact is never fully grounded — which seems to mean it is never explained.

We can put the regress precisely:

- Step 1: a is F (Socrates is wise).
- Step 2: "a is F" requires that a instantiates F, i.e., that instantiation I holds between a and F.
- Step 3: "I holds between a and F" requires a second-order instantiation I' holding between a, F, and I.
- Step 4: And so on infinitely.

Armstrong's response is to treat instantiation not as a *relation* (a universal that requires grounding by further relations) but as a *nexus* or *formal relation* — something that connects entities without being a further entity requiring connection. Instantiation is internal to the state of affairs; it is not a third relatum alongside the particular and the universal but the very mode in which the state of affairs holds. The doctrine of the **ontological primacy of states of affairs** states that a state of affairs is not reducible to its constituents: [a's being F] is a brute fact not constructed from a plus F plus I. The regress is stopped not by adding more entities at each level but by recognizing that states of affairs are fundamentally not constructable from independently existing constituents.

## Different Accounts of the Tie

There are at least three serious approaches to the instantiation relation, each with its strengths and problems.

**Internal Relations**: Instantiation is an *internal relation* — one that holds in virtue of the intrinsic natures of the relata, without requiring any additional entity. If F is the kind of universal that a particular a can have, then a's instantiating F is constituted by the intrinsic natures of a and F. No further entity I is required. The problem is circularity: what does it mean for something to be "the kind of universal a can have"? The account seems to presuppose the very relationship it is trying to explain.

**Nexus/Formal Relations**: Bergmann and some neo-Bergmannians treat instantiation as a *nexus* — a formal structure that is neither a particular nor a universal but belongs to a third ontological category. Nexuses connect entities into facts without themselves requiring connection by a further nexus. This blocks the regress, but introducing a third ontological category alongside particulars and universals seems to proliferate entities without clear justification, and the ontological status of a nexus remains puzzling.

**Primitive Tie**: Some philosophers accept that instantiation is primitive and unanalyzable: that a instantiates F is just a brute feature of reality, not explicable in terms of anything more fundamental. This stops the regress by refusing to ask for further explanation. The objection is that this looks like theoretical abdication. But notice: if we are allowed to take instantiation as primitive, the question arises why we should not take the original predication ("Socrates is wise") as primitive, without positing universals at all.

## Instantiation and Truthmaking

One important function of the instantiation relation in Armstrong's ontology is to ground truthmaking. Armstrong's Truthmaker Principle holds that for any true proposition p, there must exist an entity — the truth-maker — whose existence necessitates p's being true.

For the proposition "Socrates is wise," the truth-maker is the state of affairs [Socrates's being WISDOM]. Neither Socrates alone nor the universal WISDOM alone can serve as truth-maker: Socrates might exist without being wise, and WISDOM might exist without being instantiated by Socrates. Only the state of affairs — the obtaining of the universal in the particular — makes the proposition true.

Formally:

- Let p = "Socrates is wise"
- Let T(p) = the truth-maker for p
- T(p) = [Socrates's being WISDOM]
- The existence of T(p) logically necessitates that p is true.

This truthmaker analysis motivates the state-of-affairs ontology: if truth-makers must exist, and if propositions about particular-property combinations require truth-makers, then there must be facts that combine particulars and universals. Instantiation is the relation that constitutes these facts — which is why the question of its nature cannot be bypassed by any version of realism that takes truth-making seriously.
