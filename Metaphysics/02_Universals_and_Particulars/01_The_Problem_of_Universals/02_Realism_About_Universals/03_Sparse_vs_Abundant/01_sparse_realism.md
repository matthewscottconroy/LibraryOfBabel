# Sparse Realism

---

Among the infinitely many predicates we can form — "is red," "is grue," "is an electron or a prime minister," "is self-identical," "is not a kangaroo" — only a small elite correspond to properties that genuinely carve nature at its joints. This is the core commitment of sparse realism, associated most closely with David Armstrong and developed by David Lewis under the concept of *naturalness*.

The motivation is scientific. The genuinely real properties are those that figure in scientific laws and explanations. Mass, charge, spin — these are genuine universals because they play irreplaceable explanatory roles in physics. The property being-either-a-prime-number-or-an-electron has no such role; it is a logical construction from genuine properties that carves out no natural kind, supports no law, explains nothing.

Lewis articulates the sparse/abundant distinction with the concept of *naturalness*. A perfectly natural property is one that is genuinely shared by all and only the things that have it, figures in causal laws, makes for genuine objective similarities, and is discovered rather than constructed. Armstrong's universals are the perfectly natural properties: they are what physics is discovering when it identifies the fundamental kinds.

The sparse ontology has significant consequences. Most of our ordinary predicates — "is red," "is water," "is a chair" — do not correspond directly to fundamental universals. Their extensions are determined by the fundamental properties plus our interests, but the predicates themselves do not denote universals. This may seem too restrictive: surely there is a genuine property of being red, even if it is not fundamental? Sparse realists distinguish between the elite natural properties (universals in the strict sense) and the abundant properties we can construct or talk about, which are legitimate objects of discourse but not universals.

## The Argument for Sparseness

- P1. The purpose of positing universals is to explain objective similarity, natural kinds, and necessary laws.
- P2. Abundant universals — including those corresponding to negative, disjunctive, and gerrymandered predicates — do not ground any objective similarity, natural kind, or necessary law.
- P3. Entities that do no explanatory work should not be posited (Ockham's razor).
- C. Only sparse universals — those that do the relevant explanatory work — should be posited.

Consider the application to *negative universals*. Is there a universal NOT-BEING-AN-ELECTRON? If so, it is shared by every non-electron in the universe — grass, rocks, people, chairs — a collection with no objective unity. There is no causal law connecting all non-electrons; there is no natural kind of non-electrons. Positing such a universal serves no explanatory purpose. The sparse realist simply declines to posit it.

## Grading Naturalness and the Problem of Induction

Lewis's version of sparse realism introduces *grades* of naturalness rather than a sharp natural/unnatural distinction. Perfectly natural properties are the fundamental physical properties — mass, charge, spin, quark flavor. Relatively natural properties are defined in terms of perfectly natural properties but at higher levels of organization — temperature, chemical bonding, biological fitness. Highly unnatural (gerrymandered) properties — being either an electron or the Sydney Opera House, being grue — are maximally far from any natural joint.

This gradient structure matters because it provides a principled solution to Goodman's grue paradox. Goodman defines "grue" as: green before time t, or blue after time t. All emeralds observed before t are both green and grue. By simple enumerative induction, we can project either "all emeralds are green" or "all emeralds are grue" — but these predictions conflict after t.

Sparse realism offers a principled answer: GREEN is a natural property; GRUE is not. Natural properties are projectible; we can inductively project them from observed to unobserved cases. Unnatural properties are not projectible; grue-based inductions do not track genuine joints in nature. The asymmetry between green and grue is not conventional or pragmatic but reflects a genuine ontological asymmetry. This application of naturalness is one of Lewis's most compelling arguments for the centrality of natural properties in metaphysics: without them, we cannot distinguish valid from invalid inductive inferences, and science becomes impossible.

## Sparse Realism and Scientific Essentialism

Sparse realism connects naturally to scientific essentialism — the view (associated with Brian Ellis, Caroline Lierse, and Alexander Bird) that natural kinds have real essences discovered by science, not imposed by convention. On scientific essentialism, each fundamental natural kind (electron, quark, water molecule) has an essence — a set of properties individually necessary and jointly sufficient for membership in the kind. These essential properties are genuine universals in the sparse sense: they figure in natural laws and ground causal powers. The essences are discovered empirically, not stipulated by definition.

Scientific essentialism and sparse realism thus agree that the genuine natural properties are those discovered by science, that they have a kind of metaphysical necessity (being essential to their kinds), and that many of our ordinary predicates do not correspond to genuine universals. The view connects the abstract metaphysics of universals directly to the practice of science in a way that most philosophical positions do not achieve.
