# Abundant Realism

---

Sparse realism's selectivity comes at a cost. It cannot easily explain why complex logical compounds of predicates — negations, disjunctions, conditionals — have determinate truth conditions, since these do not typically correspond to any natural property. And it makes the quantification in ordinary sentences about properties — "some properties are not instantiated," "redness and roundness are both properties" — difficult to interpret. These pressures motivate the alternative: *abundant realism*, the view that there is a universal corresponding to every possible predicate.

Abundant realism holds that universals are not restricted to natural kinds or scientifically fundamental properties. There is a universal corresponding to every predicate, including negative, disjunctive, and gerrymandered ones: NOT-BEING-RED, BEING-EITHER-ROUND-OR-TRIANGULAR, BEING-GRUE, BEING-THE-SAME-HEIGHT-AS-THE-PRIME-MINISTER.

Two motivations drive abundance. The first is logical. If every predicate has a universal as its extension, logical inference becomes tractable. The universal corresponding to ¬F (not-F) is the complement of the universal corresponding to F; the universal corresponding to F∧G is the intersection. This algebraic structure makes logic clean, and Frege's framework of concepts (functions from objects to truth-values) is precisely an abundant view: concepts are closed under all the logical operations.

The second motivation is modal. If we want to say what *could* be possible — what properties something *could* have — we need to quantify over possible properties, and these include many properties that nothing actually has. The property of being a unicorn, the property of being a mile long and made of solid gold, the property of thinking about Vienna while standing on one's head: all seem like genuine possible properties even if never instantiated. Abundant realism accommodates this naturally; sparse realism has to work harder.

The cost of abundance is an inflation of the ontological inventory and a disconnection from natural kinds and causal explanations. If there is a universal for every predicate, the distinction between natural and unnatural properties must be articulated in a different vocabulary — Lewis does this with the notion of naturalness as a further property of properties. But this additional layer of theory may generate more questions than it resolves.

## The Semantic Case for Abundance

The semantic argument for abundant universals begins with logical inference. Consider:

1. "Socrates is wise and brave."
2. "Socrates is wise."

This inference is valid. But on the sparse view, suppose WISE and BRAVE are genuine universals but WISE-AND-BRAVE is not. Then what licenses the inference? Frege's solution was to treat every predicate, including complex ones, as expressing a *concept* — a function from objects to truth-values. The concept expressed by "is wise and brave" is the intersection of the concepts expressed by "is wise" and "is brave." The intersection of two Fregean concepts is itself a Fregean concept, and the logical algebra of concepts is well-defined. Abundance is needed for logical closure.

The semantic argument also notes that sentences like "Redness and roundness are both properties" and "Some properties are not instantiated" are apparently true. These sentences quantify over properties freely. If only sparse properties exist, many such quantifications range over nothing — but the sentences appear to be genuinely meaningful and true.

## The Possible Properties Argument

A powerful argument for abundant, specifically transcendent, universals comes from modal semantics. We want to say: "Water could have failed to dissolve salt," "There could have been purple dragons," "If the world had contained no carbon, organic chemistry would have been impossible." Each of these claims seems to require quantifying over possible properties — PURPLE, DRAGON-NATURE — that may not be instantiated in the actual world. If only instantiated (actual) universals exist, these modal claims lack truth-makers.

Abundant realism handles this naturally: PURPLE and DRAGON-NATURE are genuine universals, existing necessarily and transcendently, even if they happen to have no actual instances. The modal claims are grounded in facts about which possible universals could be instantiated. The sparse realist's response — Armstrong's combinatorial approach, in which modal possibilities are constructed from actually instantiated universals — is more strained and involves additional commitments about combinatorics that many find ad hoc.

## The Two-Level View

The most widely adopted contemporary position is a two-level picture. At the first level: sparse universals (or natural classes), which are the fundamental properties and relations discovered by science, grounding objective similarity, laws of nature, and causal powers. At the second level: abundant properties understood as sets or propositional functions, corresponding to every predicate, needed for logic and semantics but carrying no metaphysical weight beyond set theory.

The naturalness distinction operates between these two levels: some sets at the second level correspond to genuine natural universals at the first level; most do not. The fundamental metaphysics is sparse; the logical superstructure is abundant. This is Lewis's position in *On the Plurality of Worlds* (chapter 1). It allows Lewis to maintain both that natural properties are primitive and that "there are properties" in the abundant sense needed for logic and modal semantics — combining the ontological economy of sparseness with the logical tractability of abundance.
