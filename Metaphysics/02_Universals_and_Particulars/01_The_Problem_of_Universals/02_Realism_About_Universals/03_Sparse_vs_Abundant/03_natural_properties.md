# Natural Properties

---

What distinguishes genuine objective similarity from a mere collection? Two electrons are objectively similar; an electron and the Sydney Opera House are not — not in any way that matters for physics, chemistry, or any other science. Lewis's concept of *naturalness* is his attempt to capture this distinction without positing universals as a new ontological category.

A perfectly natural property, for Lewis, is one that is intrinsic and perfectly shared — one that makes for genuine similarity and difference, not relative to any conceptual scheme. Two electrons with the same charge share perfectly natural properties; a collection consisting of all electrons and all triangles shares none. Naturalness is not a matter of our interests or our language; it is an objective feature of certain classes of things.

Natural properties do enormous work throughout Lewis's philosophical system. They define *similarity*: two things are very similar to the extent they share many natural properties. They define *intrinsicality*: intrinsic properties supervene on natural properties — a property is intrinsic iff any two duplicates (things sharing all their natural properties) both have it or both lack it. They constrain *reference*: our terms refer in part because of the eligibility of their referents — natural properties are eligible to be referred to by our predicates because they are the joints in nature's structure. Without naturalness, there would be infinitely many possible meanings for "green" and no principled reason to assign it to green rather than grue. And they underlie *laws*: laws of nature are regularities stated in terms of natural properties, not arbitrary ones.

Lewis holds that naturalness is a primitive — a brute feature of certain properties (or, in his nominalist ontology, certain classes) that cannot be reduced further. This primitiveness is philosophically costly but seems unavoidable: any attempt to define naturalness in terms of further properties faces a regress or a circularity.

## Naturalness as Ideology vs. Ontology

An important debate concerns the status of naturalness in Lewis's system. Either naturalness is an ontological primitive — some classes of individuals are natural, and this is a brute ontological fact — or it is an ideological primitive — "natural" is a primitive predicate in our fundamental theory, a primitive concept we use without definition.

Lewis's own position seems closest to the first option: he speaks of natural properties as objectively elite, suggesting that naturalness is an objective feature of the world rather than merely a feature of our conceptual framework. Critics of Lewis argue that natural properties as primitives are not better off than Armstrong's universals as primitives — both are brute postulates at the foundation. The advantage of Lewis's framework is that it can be combined with nominalism (properties are classes), whereas Armstrong's requires a genuinely new ontological category (universals distinct from particulars and from classes). Whether the combination with nominalism is a genuine advantage or merely relocates the mystery is contested.

## The Many Applications of Naturalness

Lewis deploys naturalness across his philosophical system with striking results.

**Duplication**: x and y are *duplicates* iff they have all the same perfectly natural properties. Intrinsic properties are those that do not differ between duplicates. This gives a precise analysis of intrinsicality without circularity.

**Semantics**: The model-theoretic argument against realism (Putnam's version) threatens to show that any interpretation of our terms is as good as any other, making meaning indeterminate. Lewis's response: our terms refer to the most natural eligible interpretation. "Green" refers to greenness (not grueishness) because greenness is far more natural than grueishness, and naturalness is a constraint on reference.

**Humean Supervenience**: Lewis's thesis that everything supervenes on the distribution of perfectly natural intrinsic properties across spacetime points, together with the spatiotemporal relations among those points. This thesis presupposes that there is a definite set of perfectly natural intrinsic properties — the "Humean mosaic." The mosaic is constituted by natural classes; everything else is constructed from it.

## Alternatives to Primitive Naturalness

Critics of Lewis's primitive naturalness have proposed alternatives. Armstrong's universals replace primitive naturalness of classes with a genuinely new ontological category: a class is natural because it is the extension of a universal, and universals are the entities that do the explanatory work.

Dispositional essentialism (Ellis, Bird) replaces primitive naturalness with intrinsic dispositional essences: what makes mass a natural property is not a brute fact but the fact that mass has a specific dispositional essence (gravitational attraction, inertial resistance) that is part of its nature. Unnatural properties (grue, electron-or-rhombus) have no such intrinsic dispositional essence.

Structural realism replaces naturalness with structure: natural properties are those that figure in the fundamental relational structure of the world as described by our best physical theories. Naturalness is not a primitive feature of properties but a feature of their role in the structure.

Each alternative trades Lewis's primitive naturalness for a different primitive: universals, dispositional essences, or structure. The debate about which primitive is most theoretically economical and most explanatorily powerful remains open.
