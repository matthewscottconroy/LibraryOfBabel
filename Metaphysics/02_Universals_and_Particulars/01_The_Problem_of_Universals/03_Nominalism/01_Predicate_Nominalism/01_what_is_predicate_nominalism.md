# What Is Predicate Nominalism

---

Here is the simplest possible answer to the one-over-many problem: when we correctly apply the same predicate to multiple objects, there is nothing further to explain. The predicate applies to what it applies to, and that is all there is to say. To say that Socrates is wise is just to say that the predicate "is wise" applies to Socrates. No universal, no trope, no further entity grounds this — there is just the predicate and the object. This is *predicate nominalism*.

The view is motivated by a radical form of Ockhamism: if we can account for all the data of predication — the truth of "Socrates is wise," the inferences that follow, the explanatory role of property attributions — without positing universals, then universals are idle and should be eliminated. The ontology needed is just particular objects and the predicates we use to describe them.

Predicate nominalism can be stated precisely as a thesis about truth conditions. For any predicate P and any object x:

*"x is F" is true ↔ x falls under the predicate "F"*

There is no further condition — no universal that x must instantiate, no resemblance that x must bear to paradigms, no class that x must belong to. The predicate-application is the last word. In logical terms, the form is not [Instantiation(x, F-ness)] — "x stands in the instantiation relation to the universal F-ness" — but simply [F(x)]: "the predicate F applies to x," where F is an expression in a language, not an entity in the world.

## The Explanatory Reversal Problem

In its simplest form, predicate nominalism seems to get the explanatory direction backwards. We ordinarily think predicates apply to things *because* those things are a certain way — "is red" applies to this tomato because the tomato is red. But predicate nominalism seems to say the tomato is red *because* "is red" applies to it. This reversal seems wrong: redness is a feature of the world; our predicate is a tool we have developed to describe that feature. The feature is prior; the predicate is posterior.

Predicate nominalists can respond that they are not reversing the explanatory order but denying that there is an explanatory order at all. There is no "because" in "the predicate applies because the object is red" — the predicate applies, and that is a primitive fact. But this response leaves the nominalist without an account of the objectivity of predication: what prevents the predicate from applying arbitrarily?

Armstrong's diagnosis captures the structural problem precisely: predicate nominalism uses a single, repeatedly failing move. When asked why a predicate applies to certain things, it says "because it does." When asked what binds together the class of F-things — what they have in common — it says "just that the predicate applies to all of them." When asked why those particular things are the ones the predicate applies to — what makes it true — it says "it's just a fact." At every point, the predicate nominalist refuses to give an informative answer. The realist explains why "is red" applies to rubies and tomatoes and London buses: they all instantiate the universal REDNESS. The predicate nominalist can only record the application, not explain it.

## Historical Context: Ockham to Quine

Predicate nominalism in the medieval period was represented by Roscelinus's crude nominalism — universals are nothing but *voces*, sounds. Ockham improved on this by making universals mental signs (concepts), which carry genuine semantic content, though they are still particulars rather than abstract entities. Modern predicate nominalism is closest to Quine's view: predicates are linguistic items that apply to objects without requiring corresponding entities.

Quine's nominalism about ordinary predicates is not quite predicate nominalism in Armstrong's sense, because Quine accepts classes (sets) as abstract objects and does not require that all predication be grounded in particular-language relations. But Quine's claim that predicates do not carry ontological commitment — that "is red" does not commit us to the existence of REDNESS as an abstract entity — is the Quinean version of the predicate nominalist thesis. The predicate nominalist tradition in contemporary philosophy includes Quine, Sellars, and Price, though most contemporary nominalists have moved to more sophisticated positions to avoid the explanatory gaps in pure predicate nominalism.
