# Problems for Predicate Nominalism

---

Predicate nominalism faces several objections that collectively show it cannot serve as a complete account of properties. Each pressing from a different direction, they explain why most philosophers sympathetic to nominalism have sought more sophisticated alternatives.

**The Objectivity Objection** cuts deepest. Consider a universe containing no language-using beings — a universe before the evolution of minds and languages. In such a universe, gold and iron are distinct kinds of matter with different chemical and physical properties; all electrons have the same charge; gravitational attraction holds between massive bodies. These seem to be genuine features of the pre-linguistic universe. But on predicate nominalism, these facts hold (when they do) only in virtue of predicates applying. If there are no predicates, there are no properties, and therefore no facts about the chemical composition of matter. This is counterintuitive: the physical facts seem to be prior to any predication.

The predicate nominalist has two responses. The first appeals to *counterfactual predicates*: facts about pre-linguistic nature are grounded in what *would* be the case if there were language users — in what predicates would apply if applied. But counterfactuals are modal claims, and grounding objectivity in modal claims brings in the apparatus of possible worlds, which is at least as ontologically heavy as universals. The nominalist gains no parsimony. The second response is *deflationism about objectivity*: the very notion of mind-independent properties is a philosopher's invention; there is nothing more to being a property than being what a predicate applies to. But this is a refusal to engage with the question rather than an answer to it.

**The Coextension Problem** shows that predicate nominalism conflates properties that should be distinct. Suppose "is a creature with a kidney" and "is a creature with a heart" happen to apply to exactly the same creatures. On predicate nominalism, HAVING-A-KIDNEY and HAVING-A-HEART are the same property (or at least, have the same extension, and predicate nominalism has no resources for distinguishing properties beyond their extension). But clearly these are distinct properties: having a kidney causes urine production while having a heart does not; in a possible world where creatures with kidneys have no hearts, HAVING-A-KIDNEY still exists but HAVING-A-HEART does not apply to those creatures; evolutionary explanation of kidney development and heart development requires distinguishing these as different features.

The standard resolution is to move to *intensions*: properties are individuated by the function from possible worlds to extensions rather than by their actual-world extension alone. But this requires possible worlds, which is not a nominalist-friendly commitment in the strict sense. The move from extensions to intensions requires exactly the kind of modal apparatus that nominalists typically want to avoid.

**The Laws Problem** strikes at the theoretical motivation for property-talk. Natural laws are typically stated in terms of properties. "All copper conducts electricity" is a law, and its necessity derives from the nature of the properties involved — it is not an accident. On predicate nominalism, the "law" becomes a generalization about the application of predicates: "anything we call 'copper' also gets called 'conducting.'" This makes laws look linguistic rather than natural. But the law is a fact about the world, not a fact about our vocabulary. Laws hold in worlds where no language users exist, and their necessity is not a feature of our predicates.

## What Follows

These objections collectively establish that predicate nominalism, in its pure form, is untenable as a complete account of properties. The major nominalist alternatives — resemblance nominalism and class nominalism — are developed precisely to preserve nominalist ontology (no universals) while providing better answers to the objectivity, coextension, and laws problems. Trope theory accepts property-entities (tropes) but denies that they are universals, providing a different path. Each of these more sophisticated positions accepts the force of the objections against predicate nominalism and attempts to do better while remaining outside the universals camp.
