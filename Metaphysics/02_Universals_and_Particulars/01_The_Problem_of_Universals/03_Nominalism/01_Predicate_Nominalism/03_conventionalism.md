# Conventionalism

---

Conventionalism about properties holds that the distinctions we draw — the properties we recognize — reflect our conventions, interests, and practices rather than objective features of reality. There are no mind-independent natural kinds; any division of the world into kinds is one we impose for our own purposes. The world is, as Goodman puts it, "ready for description," but the descriptions are ours to make.

Goodman's *Ways of Worldmaking* (1978) is the most radical statement of this view. Goodman holds that there are many world-versions — many equally legitimate ways of describing reality — and that no one version is the one true description of the one real world. The entities we posit, the properties we attribute, the laws we formulate — all depend on the world-version we are working with. This is not idealism: Goodman is not saying the world is mind-dependent. But he is saying that which properties we recognize, which features of the world we make salient, reflects our conventions and interests rather than a fixed ontological structure.

This conventionalism is importantly different from simple predicate nominalism. Goodman is not saying that properties are whatever our predicates apply to — that would make every predicate correspond to a property. He is saying that which properties we *recognize* — which features we make central to our world-version — reflects our conceptual choices. Other choices are possible; there is no framework-independent fact about which joints are real.

## Goodman's Grue Paradox

Goodman's grue paradox connects directly to his conventionalism. Define:

- Grue: x is grue iff x is examined before time t and green, or not yet examined before t and blue.

All observed emeralds are both green and grue (since all have been examined before t and are green). Why do we project "all emeralds are green" rather than "all emeralds are grue"? Goodman argues that the answer is not that green is a more natural property than grue in some objective sense. The answer is *entrenchment*: "green" has been used in many more successful inductive projections in the past than "grue." Projectibility is a historical fact about linguistic practice, not an objective feature of properties.

Conventionalism follows: which properties are "real" for the purposes of induction depends on which predicates are entrenched in our practice, not on any objective natural structure.

Lewis's response reverses the order of explanation: "green" is entrenched because greenness is a natural property and grue is not. Our practice of using "green" has been successful because it tracks objective joints; grue-inductions would fail because grue is gerrymandered. The naturalist/realist about properties has a better explanation of why green is entrenched than the conventionalist does. The debate here is a direct confrontation between Lewis's primitive naturalness and Goodman's primitive entrenchment — and neither side has decisively won it.

## A Modest Conventionalism

A sophisticated conventionalist can acknowledge that not all distinctions are conventional. A reasonable view distinguishes framework-constitutive distinctions (what counts as an object, a property, the same thing at different times — partly conventional) from framework-internal facts (given a framework, facts about which objects have which properties — objective and non-conventional). The choice of framework is pragmatically motivated, but the facts within the framework are genuine.

This modest conventionalism acknowledges the objectivity of many scientific facts while denying that there is a single correct framework for describing reality. It is closer to pragmatism or pluralism than to strict conventionalism, and it avoids the most damaging objections to radical Goodmanian worldmaking.

The remaining question is whether framework-internal facts can be genuinely objective while framework-selection is conventional. Many philosophers think this distinction is unstable: if the framework is conventional, then the facts it generates are tainted by convention, and objectivity is compromised throughout. This is the deepest challenge for any form of conventionalism about properties — and it is why many philosophers who share Goodman's anti-essentialist instincts have nonetheless refused to follow him all the way to radical worldmaking.
