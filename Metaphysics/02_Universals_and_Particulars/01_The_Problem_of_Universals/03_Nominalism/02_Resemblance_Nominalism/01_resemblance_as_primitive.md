# Resemblance as Primitive

---

Predicate nominalism failed because it made properties dependent on our predicates rather than grounding them in objective features of the world. The natural remedy is to find an objective, mind-independent relation among things that can do the work universals were supposed to do — without positing any abstract shared entity. Resemblance is the obvious candidate. Two red things look alike; two electrons behave alike; two protons have the same mass. These resemblances are real relations among the things themselves, holding independently of whether we have a word for them or have ever noticed them.

Resemblance nominalism identifies the property of being F with the maximal resemblance class of F-things: all and only F-things resemble each other (and the paradigmatic F's) more closely than they resemble any non-F. The predicate "is F" applies to something iff it resembles the F-paradigms. Resemblance is taken as a primitive, objective, non-analyzable relation among particulars — not a further universal, but the bedrock on which property-attributions are built.

The key virtue of treating resemblance as primitive is that it avoids the anthropocentrism of predicate nominalism. A resemblance is a real relation among objects, holding independently of whether we have a word for it or whether we have noticed it. Two medieval paintings in different countries that no one has ever compared genuinely resemble each other in color, even without any act of comparison or any predicate in use.

## The Problem of Respects

For this to work, resemblance must be *specific* rather than *generic*: two red things resemble each other *as red* — in a specific respect — not merely in some respect or other. Everything resembles everything in some respect; a hammer and a raindrop are both spatially extended objects. The relevant resemblance is resemblance *in color*, or *in charge*, or *in mass*. But specifying the relevant *respect* without appealing to properties or universals threatens circularity: the relevant respect is *being red*, which is exactly the property we are trying to ground in resemblance.

The primitivist responds that we should simply accept resemblance as a primitive relation with an internal complexity that cannot be further analyzed. Respects are features of resemblance facts, not additional entities requiring their own analysis. This is uncomfortable, but perhaps no more so than the realist's primitive instantiation or the bare-particulars theorist's primitive thisness. The key question is which primitive is more intelligible — and here opinions differ sharply.

## Degrees of Resemblance

One underappreciated advantage of resemblance nominalism is that it naturally accommodates *degrees of resemblance*. Two perfectly matching red patches resemble each other maximally; a red and a pink resemble each other substantially; a red and a green resemble each other less; a red and a prime number resemble each other not at all in color. This gradation in resemblance corresponds to the gradation in color similarity.

Degrees of resemblance accommodate vagueness — the vagueness of predicates like "red" corresponds to the absence of a sharp cut-off in the resemblance near the margins of resemblance classes. They also fit Wittgenstein's *family resemblance* concepts: concepts like "game" do not have necessary and sufficient conditions but are applied to things that resemble paradigmatic games in various respects, with no single respect shared by all. Universals-based realism handles these cases less naturally, since if RED is a universal it has sharp extensions (things either instantiate it or they do not), and vagueness must be explained as a semantic phenomenon rather than as a reflection of ontological vagueness.

## Armstrong's Challenge

Armstrong's most focused objection is that resemblance is itself a universal. If two red tropes resemble each other, and two round tropes resemble each other, these are two instances of the *same* resemblance relation — RESEMBLANCE is repeated across different pairs, making it a universal. If resemblance is a universal, the nominalist has admitted at least one universal, and the nominalist program has not achieved its goal.

Rodriguez-Pereyra's response: RESEMBLANCE need not be a universal. It is a primitive relation, but "primitive relation" does not mean "universal." Universals are characterized by their type-identity — the very same entity is present in each instance. Resemblance, as the nominalist conceives it, is a primitive predicate in the nominalist's fundamental theory, not an entity that recurs across instances. Whether this distinction between "primitive relation" and "universal" is ultimately coherent is one of the central disputes between resemblance nominalists and their critics.
