# Problems and Objections

---

Despite Rodriguez-Pereyra's careful defense, resemblance nominalism faces several challenges that remain subjects of active debate.

**The Regress About Resemblance** is Armstrong's most focused objection. Resemblance nominalism holds that:

**(RN)** What makes a red and b red is that a and b both resemble all paradigmatic red things.

But this uses the predicate "resembles." On the nominalist's own terms, what grounds the truth of "a resembles paradigm p"? Does it require that the pair (a, p) resembles all paradigmatic resemblance-pairs? This threatens:

**(RN₂)** What makes a resemble p is that the pair (a, p) resembles all paradigmatic resemblance-pairs.

And (RN₂) uses "resembles" again, generating (RN₃), and so on.

Rodriguez-Pereyra's response: the regress is not vicious because resemblance is taken as a *primitive* — it is not itself explained in terms of further resemblance. "a resembles b" is a primitive fact about a and b; it is not analyzed as "(a, b) resembles all paradigmatic resemblance-pairs." The regress stops at the first level.

Critics find this unsatisfying. If resemblance is primitive at the first level, why was it not primitive at the zeroth level — why not simply say that "a is red" is primitive and does not need analysis in terms of resemblance? The nominalist uses resemblance as the explanatory primitive but cannot explain why resemblance gets to be primitive while property-attributions do not. The realist faces an analogous challenge with primitive instantiation, but that is the very comparison in dispute.

**The Modal Commitment** is the other major challenge. Rodriguez-Pereyra's solution to the companionship problem requires quantifying over possible objects — non-actual red things that resemble each other but are not round. This requires either Lewisian modal realism (with its ontological extravagance — infinitely many concrete possible worlds) or some other account of possibilia that may be no simpler than universals. Whether modal realism is "independently motivated," as Rodriguez-Pereyra claims, or whether it constitutes a cost as large as the cost it avoids, is contested.

**The Semantic Direction Problem** remains in view throughout. Resemblance nominalism faces the same explanatory order issue as predicate nominalism: things resemble each other *because* they have the same properties, not the other way around. The resemblance between two red things is grounded in their both being red — the property is explanatorily prior to the resemblance. The nominalist denies this "because" is legitimate, taking resemblance as primitive, but many find this reversal deeply counterintuitive.

## Prospects and Variants

Several variants have been proposed to handle these objections. *Resemblance Nominalism with Tropes* combines resemblance nominalism with trope theory: the resembling items are not ordinary concrete particulars but tropes. Two red tropes (the redness of this tomato and the redness of that rose) resemble each other as tropes; the property RED is the maximal resemblance class of red tropes. This handles the regress problem differently: each resemblance fact is itself a trope (a resemblance-trope), and the chain involves only concrete entities rather than abstract universals.

*Resemblance Nominalism with Natural Similarity* — rather than treating resemblance as entirely primitive, some nominalists appeal to a primitive notion of *natural similarity*: objective similarity that holds in virtue of the structure of the natural world. This is close to Lewis's naturalness, and the debate becomes one about whether "natural similarity" is more acceptable than "universal."

The debate remains live. Resemblance nominalism is a serious contender, especially in its Lewisian/Rodriguez-Pereyra form, though it requires either modal realism or some equivalent theoretical investment. Whether this investment is greater or lesser than the investment required by universals realism is the central comparative question — and the answer depends on which theoretical costs one finds more acceptable.
