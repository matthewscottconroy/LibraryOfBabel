# Class Nominalism

---

Class nominalism takes a different approach to avoiding universals. Rather than grounding property-attribution in resemblance, it identifies each property directly with the class (set) of all objects that have it. To be red is just to be a member of the class {all red things}. To be wise is to be in the class {all wise things}. Properties are not entities over and above their extensions; they just *are* their extensions — the sets of things that have them.

The attraction is clear. Classes are standard mathematical objects, accepted in any ontology that includes set theory, and their identity conditions are precise: the class of all red things is identical to the class {a, b, c...} just in case exactly those things are red. No new ontological category is needed beyond sets (which most philosophers accept for logical and mathematical purposes) and individuals. The ontology is parsimonious and mathematically tractable.

The formal setting makes the view explicit. If we denote properties by capital letters and their extensions by lower-case:

- P = p (the property is the class of its instances)
- x has P ↔ x ∈ p (an object has a property iff it is a member of the class)
- P = Q ↔ p = q ↔ (∀x)(x ∈ p ↔ x ∈ q) (property identity = class identity = same members)

Set-theoretic operations on classes correspond to property-logical operations: P ∧ Q = p ∩ q; P ∨ Q = p ∪ q; ¬P = ℘(U) \ p.

## Three Problems

The view faces immediate objections on three fronts.

**The Coextension Problem**: If two properties happen to apply to exactly the same things — "has a kidney" and "has a heart," if all and only kidney-havers are heart-havers — they get identified with the same class and hence are the same property. But we want to say they are distinct: one is the property of having a kidney, the other of having a heart. They differ in their causal roles, their modal profiles, and their explanatory functions even if they happen to coincide in extension. Class nominalism cannot accommodate this difference.

**The Arbitrariness Problem**: Every collection of objects forms a class. So for any arbitrary collection — {Socrates, the Eiffel Tower, the number 7} — there is a class and hence, on class nominalism, a property. But the notion of a *genuine property* cannot be captured within class nominalism without supplementary resources. The theory generates as many properties as there are sets, including utterly gerrymandered ones, with no principled way to distinguish natural from artificial groupings.

**The Modal Problem**: Properties hold modally — something could have been red even if it never actually was. The class of red things is actual; but the property of being red extends to merely possible red things. Class nominalism seems to need possible objects or possible classes to accommodate modal facts about what could have been red.

## Quine's View

Quine's mature view is a form of class nominalism about predicates combined with set-theoretic realism. Quine accepts that we are ontologically committed to whatever our best theories quantify over, and since our best theories quantify over sets (needed for mathematics), sets are real. But predicates do not quantify over properties; they apply to objects directly. Properties, when needed, are identified with sets (their extensions).

Quine is sensitive to the coextension problem and responds by distinguishing properties (which he finds metaphysically dubious, since they seem to require intensional individuation) from propositions (which he finds even more dubious). For purely extensional purposes, sets are sufficient. The intensional idioms that seem to require properties over and above their extensions — modal contexts, psychological contexts — are to be paraphrased away or treated as linguistically convenient but metaphysically suspect.

## Class Nominalism and Resemblance

A natural objection: if properties are classes, then the class of red things and the class of non-red things are both genuine classes, and hence both genuine properties. But intuitively, REDNESS is a genuine property while NON-REDNESS is at best derivative. Class nominalism treats both symmetrically, with no resources for explaining the asymmetry.

This symmetry objection connects to Armstrong's observation that class nominalism cannot explain why some class-membership facts are *similar* and others are not. All members of the class of red things are similar in color; the class {Socrates, the Eiffel Tower, 7} has no such internal similarity. Lewis's response is to add naturalness as a primitive: some classes are naturally natural. This supplementation resolves the symmetry problem but introduces exactly the kind of primitive that the sparse realist's universals were supposed to explain differently. We are back to a comparison of primitives.
