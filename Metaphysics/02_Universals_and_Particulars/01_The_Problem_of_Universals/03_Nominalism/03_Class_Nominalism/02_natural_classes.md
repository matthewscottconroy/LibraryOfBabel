# Natural Classes

---

Class nominalism's central challenge is distinguishing natural properties from arbitrary collections. Not every set of objects corresponds to a genuine property; the class {copper things, tennis balls, the prime ministers of Canada} defines no natural property. To handle this, Lewis introduced the notion of *naturalness* as an additional primitive feature distinguishing certain classes from others.

A *perfectly natural* class is one that corresponds to a genuine natural property — a property that grounds genuine objective similarities, figures in causal laws, and carves nature at its joints. The class of all electrons is perfectly natural; the class of all things that are either electrons or rhombuses is not. This distinction is real and objective, not a matter of our interests or our language.

But what makes a class natural? Lewis's answer is brute: naturalness is a primitive feature of some classes, not reducible to anything further. Two electrons are objectively more similar to each other (share more perfectly natural properties) than an electron and a bowling ball. This objective similarity is not grounded in shared universals but is simply a feature of how the world is.

This is the point where critics charge Lewis with doing with one hand what he refused to do with the other. The realist about universals says there are genuine properties that ground similarities; Lewis says there are natural classes that ground similarities. Both posit something primitive — the realist calls it a universal, Lewis calls it a natural class. Are these really different ontologies, or different terminologies for the same thing? Lewis's response: the class-based ontology is still preferable because it uses sets (already admitted) rather than universals (a new entity-type). But whether this is genuine parsimony or merely relabeling is contested.

## The Ideology vs. Ontology Trade

One important way to understand Lewis's strategy is through the distinction between *ontology* (what entities exist) and *ideology* (what primitive predicates or concepts the theory uses). Lewis aims to minimize ontology by admitting only sets and particulars — no universals as a separate category. But he introduces naturalness as a primitive *ideological* commitment: a primitive predicate in his fundamental theory.

Armstrong's universals theory minimizes ideology but expands ontology: the theory uses only ordinary logical vocabulary plus the predicate "instantiates," but it requires a genuinely new entity-type (universals) in addition to particulars. The debate between Lewis and Armstrong can be framed as: which is theoretically more economical — a greater ontological commitment (universals) with less ideology, or a greater ideological commitment (primitive naturalness) with less novel ontology? Neither answer is obviously correct. Parsimony with respect to ontology and parsimony with respect to ideology are both theoretical virtues, and there is no agreed framework for trading them off.

## Naturalness and Scientific Realism

The notion of a natural class connects class nominalism to scientific realism. Scientific realism holds that our best scientific theories describe the structure of the world accurately, at least approximately and for the entities and properties centrally invoked in theory. The classes that are naturally natural — electron, charge, mass — are precisely the ones that feature centrally in well-confirmed scientific theories.

This connection motivates *scientifically-constrained class nominalism*: the natural classes are those corresponding to the properties and kinds that our best science identifies as genuine. Naturalness is not a metaphysical primitive but an empirical discovery — we learn which classes are natural by doing science.

This naturalistic approach is attractive but raises questions. Does science discover which classes are *genuinely* natural (metaphysical realism about natural classes), or does it determine which classes we should *treat as* natural for predictive purposes (epistemic naturalism without full metaphysical commitment)? If naturalness is determined by our best current science and science changes its fundamental categories, does the naturalness of classes change? Phlogiston once seemed a natural kind; it turned out not to be. Was the class of phlogiston-containing substances once naturally natural and now not? These questions do not defeat the naturalistic approach, but they show that the notion requires careful development.

## Natural Classes and Humean Supervenience

Lewis's doctrine of Humean Supervenience relies essentially on natural classes. The thesis: **everything supervenes on the distribution of perfectly natural intrinsic properties across spacetime points and the spatiotemporal relations among those points.** This requires that there be perfectly natural intrinsic properties — the fundamental physical properties that occur point-by-point in spacetime. These are the naturally natural classes. All higher-level facts (biological, psychological, social) supervene on the pattern of natural property instantiation across spacetime. The debate about Humean Supervenience — whether consciousness, mathematical truth, and modal facts genuinely supervene on the natural mosaic — connects directly to the debate about natural classes and their adequacy as a foundation for the entire metaphysical enterprise.
