# Modal Class Nominalism

---

The class nominalist's most direct problems — coextension and modality — look as though they cannot be solved while staying within the actual world. If properties just are classes of actual things, then any two properties with the same actual extension are identical, and nothing can have a property it never actually has. Both results seem wrong: being renate and being cordate are distinct properties even if in fact the same animals have both; and something surely could have been red even if it never actually was. The natural remedy is to extend the classes beyond the actual world. Lewis's modal realism supplies the extension.

The move is elegant. On Lewis's account of *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986), possible worlds are concrete entities — as real as the actual world, differing from it only in being spatiotemporally disconnected from us. Each world contains its own domain of individuals. A property, then, is identified not with the class of actual things that have it but with the class of all things across all possible worlds — all *possibilia* — that have it. Modal class nominalism is the result. The coextension problem dissolves: being renate and being cordate are distinct because there are possible animals with kidneys but no hearts, and vice versa, so their respective sets of possibilia differ. The modal problem dissolves: something can have a property in a world where it exists and is a member of the relevant class, regardless of whether it has that property in the actual world.

We might pause to notice how much theoretical machinery this requires. Lewis's possible worlds are not linguistic constructs, not sets of propositions, not abstract representations — they are concrete universes as fully real as ours. The total ontology is vast: not just the actual world but infinitely many others, each teeming with concrete individuals. Critics, including van Inwagen and Plantinga, found this commitment staggering, and they were not wrong that it is enormous. Lewis's frank response was that the theoretical benefits justify the cost — clean analyses of modality, properties, propositions, counterfactuals, causation, and much else — and that the usual appeals to "Occam's razor" concern entities of new *kinds*, not merely more entities of familiar kinds. Concrete worlds are more of the same kind of thing as the actual world. Whether this response is compelling or a sophisticated rationalization is the central dispute about Lewisian modal realism.

## Three Consequences of the Possibilist Account

The possibilist extension of class nominalism has three noteworthy consequences that go beyond resolving the original problems. First, intrinsic properties can now be given a principled analysis. A property F is intrinsic just in case no two *duplicates* — individuals sharing all their perfectly natural intrinsic properties — are such that one is F and the other is not. Duplication is defined using perfectly natural properties (a separate primitive in Lewis's system), and the analysis avoids circularity given the overall apparatus. This is a significant gain: capturing intrinsicality without appealing to modal operators as primitives.

Second, modal truths are now grounded in the distribution of possibilia across worlds rather than taken as primitive. "Necessarily all water is H₂O" is true just in case no member of the water-set across all worlds fails to be a member of the H₂O-set. "Possibly there are flying pigs" is true just in case the class of flying pigs has some member somewhere. These analyses give modal claims determinate truth-conditions without invoking primitive modal notions — which is one of the main reasons Lewis found his ontological commitment worth making.

Third, and more troublingly, the analysis runs into what we might call the *carving problem* at the modal level. Not every class of possibilia corresponds to a genuine property; the same naturalness-primitiveness that was needed to distinguish natural from gerrymandered actual classes is needed at least as urgently for the full cross-world classes. Modal class nominalism must therefore retain the commitment to primitive naturalness. The possibilist extension resolves the coextension and modal problems but does not dissolve the ideology-vs.-ontology trade-off at the heart of the view.

## Alternatives for Those Who Reject Modal Realism

For philosophers who find concrete possible worlds unacceptable, two alternatives have been developed. *Ersatz modal realism*, associated with Plantinga and Adams, replaces concrete possible worlds with abstract ones — maximal consistent sets of propositions, or possible states of affairs, or books of properties. Properties are still sets across these ersatz worlds, but the worlds are themselves abstract rather than concrete. This is ontologically more modest than Lewis, but it introduces abstract entities at the world-level rather than the property-level. Whether this is genuinely less costly than positing universals is not obvious.

*Fictionalism about possible worlds* treats possible-world talk as useful fiction — we reason as if there are other worlds without asserting they exist. On this view, modal class nominalism provides the correct analyses of modal claims understood within the fiction, but those claims have no literal truth-conditions outside it. This allows modal class nominalism without ontological commitment to concrete worlds, but at the cost of making modal truths hold only relative to a fiction, which some find an unacceptable retreat from realism about modality.

*Actualism* — the view that only actual objects exist — forecloses the possibilist move entirely. On a strict actualism, modal claims must be analyzed using primitive modal operators (necessarily, possibly) rather than quantifiers over possible objects. Modal class nominalism then collapses back to class nominalism about actual objects, and the coextension and modal problems recur in their original forms. Some actualists accept this and propose other solutions; others see it as evidence that actualism comes at a price.

The relationship between modal class nominalism and the indispensability argument for abstract entities is also worth noting. Lewis's possible worlds are concrete rather than abstract, so his nominalism remains technically intact: he quantifies over concrete things, including concrete possibilia, but not over abstracta in the sense of non-spatiotemporal entities. Mathematical objects, on Lewis's account, are to be identified with set-theoretic constructions over concreta, avoiding independently abstract platonic entities. Whether this reduction succeeds is a further debate, but it shows that the possibilist strategy has implications beyond the problem of universals — it is part of a comprehensive nominalist program whose full assessment requires adjudicating the philosophy of mathematics as well.
