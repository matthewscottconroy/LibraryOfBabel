# Trope Theory

Neither wholly realist nor wholly nominalist, trope theory occupies a distinctive and increasingly popular position in contemporary ontology. A trope is a particularized property: not redness in general (the universal), but the redness of *this rose* — a non-repeatable, numerically distinct feature that belongs to that rose and to no other. The redness of this rose and the redness of that rose are not identical; they are two distinct tropes that resemble each other exactly, or very closely. Properties are thus particularized down to the individual objects that have them.

This might seem like an awkward middle ground — taking on the ontological commitment to properties while simultaneously particularizing them — but trope theory has powerful theoretical motivations. It offers a natural account of resemblance (two things resemble each other in virtue of their resembling tropes) without the regress problems that threaten resemblance nominalism. It can construct ordinary objects as bundles of co-present tropes without the problematic postulation of a bare substratum. And it can ground causal claims in the particular features of things — this trope, not the universal, is what interacts with this trope to produce this effect.

The chapter examines trope theory's credentials both as an account of properties and as a contribution to the sciences. Scientific practice often seems to involve not universals but precisely the particular features of individual systems: this electron's charge, this atom's mass, this organism's fitness in this environment. Trope theory is arguably the ontology that best matches the implicit commitments of physics, where field values at points are more fundamental than shared types.

But trope theory faces its own challenges. The claim that resemblance among tropes is primitive — not analyzable in terms of shared universals — may seem to abandon rather than solve the problem of universals. And the bundle theory of objects built from tropes must confront the same objections that afflict property-bundle theories generally: can co-presence of tropes alone explain the unity of an object, or do we need an additional unifying relation? This chapter takes these objections seriously and examines the responses trope theorists have developed.
