# Tropes and Universals

Trope theory positions itself as a middle path between the extravagance of realism about universals and the austerity of nominalism. The trope theorist agrees with the nominalist that there are no abstract universals — no single entity that is wholly present in each of the many red things. But the trope theorist agrees with the realist that properties are genuine constituents of the world, not mere reflections of our classificatory practices. The reconciliation: properties are real, but they are particular. The redness of this rose is a genuine constituent of reality — a concrete, particular instance of a property — but it is numerically distinct from the redness of any other rose.

The core commitment of trope theory is that particularized properties — property-instances — are the fundamental ontological units from which all else is built. Objects are bundles of compresent tropes; universals, if they are countenanced at all, are abstractions from resemblance classes of tropes rather than independently existing entities. This approach has the advantage of unifying the theory of properties with the theory of particulars: there is just one fundamental category, tropes, and everything else is constructed from it.

The comparison with universals reveals the key theoretical choice. The realist about universals holds that when two roses are both red, there is a single entity — redness — that is a constituent of both. The trope theorist holds that there are two distinct redness-tropes, one in each rose, that resemble each other. The realist's account makes qualitative similarity easy to explain but requires an account of what it means for a universal to be wholly present in multiple locations. The trope theorist's account avoids that puzzle but requires a primitive resemblance relation and a story about how resemblance classes are constituted.

One crucial issue is whether trope theory can handle the predicability of abstract terms. We say "redness is a color," and this seems to be a truth about redness — not about this or that redness-trope, but about redness as such. The trope theorist must explain such truths as claims about resemblance classes or kinds, without positing a genuine universal.

This section examines the relationship between trope theory and realism about universals, the structural parallels and contrasts between the two frameworks, and the considerations that favor one over the other.
