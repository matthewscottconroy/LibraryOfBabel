# Tropes as Middle Ground

---

The debate between universals realists and nominalists can seem like a forced choice. Realists say there must be genuine property-entities shared by the red tomato, the red bus, and the red apple — otherwise their similarity is unexplained. Nominalists say there are no such shared entities — only the concrete things themselves, perhaps resembling one another in relevant ways. Trope theory challenges the forced choice by questioning one of its assumptions: that property-entities, if they exist at all, must be the kind of thing that is shared. What if each object has its own individual property-instance — not redness as a universal held in common, but this redness, numerically distinct from every other redness, uniquely belonging to this tomato?

This is the trope theorist's proposal. A *trope* is a particular instantiation of a property: not the color red as an abstract repeatable, but the specific redness located in this tomato at this moment. Two red tomatoes do not share a universal; each has its own, numerically distinct redness-trope. The tropes resemble each other — they are qualitatively similar — but they are not identical; distinct entities that happen to share their qualitative character. The proposal captures what the realist wanted (genuine property-entities grounding similarity) without positing entities that are mysteriously present in many places at once.

This middle position promises to dissolve two of the deepest problems simultaneously. Against universals, tropes avoid the puzzling feature of being wholly present in multiple spatially distinct locations. Each trope exists where its object exists; there is no need to ask how the very same entity can be simultaneously in London and in Rome. Against predicate nominalism, tropes provide genuine property-entities that ground the truth of predications. When we say this tomato is red, the truth is grounded in the tomato's having a red-trope — a real entity in virtue of which the predicate applies. The emptiness of predicate nominalism's mere assertion that the predicate applies is avoided. Whether the middle position is stable, or whether it relocates rather than resolves the original puzzles, is the central question about trope theory.

## The One-Category Ambition

The universals realism of Armstrong posits two fundamental ontological categories: particulars and universals. Trope theory, as developed by D. C. Williams and Keith Campbell, has a more ambitious aim: a *one-category ontology* in which everything is a trope. Concrete particulars such as tables and electrons are bundles of tropes bound together by compresence. Abstract properties and kinds are resemblance classes of tropes. All of ontology is derived from a single primitive kind of entity.

The appeal is significant. Rather than explaining how two fundamentally different kinds of entity — particulars and universals — relate to each other through a puzzling instantiation relation, trope theory explains everything in terms of one kind of entity and the relations (compresence and resemblance) among its members. The one-over-many problem is handled without positing a distinct ontological category: the "over" in "one-over-many" is replaced by a resemblance class that supervenes on many.

But critics push back on whether the ambition is achieved. The compresence relation used to bundle tropes into objects and the resemblance relation used to group tropes into property-kinds are themselves entities — relations — that must be accounted for within the framework. If they are universals, the one-category ontology fails immediately. If they are further tropes, their individuation requires explanation, which may require further primitives. If they are treated as primitive formal relations with no ontological status as entities, a third category has quietly been introduced. The one-category aspiration faces pressure from every direction.

## A Note on Historical Antecedents

Contemporary trope theory did not emerge from nowhere. Aristotle distinguished individual accidents — this particular whiteness of Socrates — from universals, and individual accidents are recognizably trope-like: particular to their subject, not shareable. Duns Scotus's doctrine of individual difference (*haecceitas*) postulated a principle making each thing this particular thing, and his individual formal distinctions bear comparison to trope-theoretic property-instances. Husserl's moments — dependent parts such as the color of a surface, which cannot exist independently of the surface — are the phenomenological precursors to contemporary tropes. And G. F. Stout's early twentieth-century arguments for "particular qualities" against Russell's universals anticipate Williams's systematic development almost exactly.

What distinguishes the contemporary debate is its precision and its connections to philosophy of science, philosophy of mind, and formal ontology. In philosophy of science, tropes provide a natural account of individual causal factors: the specific mass of this particle, the specific charge of that electron, causally explaining particular events in a way that type-level universals cannot easily do. In philosophy of mind, trope theory supports token-identity theories of the mind-body relation without requiring type-identity, thereby accommodating multiple realizability. In metaontology, trope theory challenges the presupposition that the fundamental division in ontology is between particulars and universals, opening up a wider space of ontological categories than the tradition had recognized.
