# Williams on Tropes

---

D. C. Williams's 1953 paper "The Elements of Being" is the founding document of contemporary trope theory in analytic philosophy. Williams begins with what he takes to be the two irreducible data of philosophical ontology: there are concrete things, spatiotemporally located objects with many properties; and there are repeatable features, characteristics shared by many distinct objects. These two data seem to call for two ontological categories — particulars for the concrete things, universals for the repeatable features. Williams's ambition is to show that both data can be accommodated with a single basic kind: tropes, which he calls "abstract particulars."

The terminology requires care. Williams calls tropes "abstract" not in the Platonist sense — he does not mean non-spatial or non-causal — but in an etymological sense: they are aspects *abstracted* from a full concrete particular. The redness of this tomato is a genuine entity, but we isolate it by mentally focusing on one dimension of the full concrete thing. The tomato itself — the full concrete individual — is a *concretum*, a bundle of such abstract particulars. The abstraction is cognitive, not ontological: the trope is genuinely there, spatially located in the tomato, not merely a way we choose to describe it.

On Williams's account, then, a concrete particular just is a bundle of tropes: the tomato is its redness, roundness, weight, taste, smell, and other tropes held together by *compresence* — by being co-located in the same region of space-time. There is no further entity, no bare particular substratum, over and above the bundle. And the "universal" REDNESS is not a genuine entity either: it is the resemblance class of all individual redness-tropes, which are related by qualitative similarity. Both concrete things and repeatable features are derived from the single basic entity-kind. The two-category inventory reduces to one.

We can write the core ontological equation compactly:

- TOMATO (this tomato) = {redness-trope₁, roundness-trope₁, weight-trope₁, ...} [compresent]
- RED (the property of redness) = {redness-trope₁, redness-trope₂, redness-trope₃, ...} [resembling each other perfectly]

The same type of entity — the individual property-instance — appears in both analyses. The great ambition of the program is matched by its technical challenges, and Williams's paper raised issues it did not fully resolve, leaving a rich research program for Campbell, Maurin, and others.

## Tropes, Events, and Singular Causation

One of the most illuminating applications of Williams's framework is to the metaphysics of events and causation. An event like the apple's becoming red involves the acquisition of a new redness-trope: some process — sunlight, enzymatic change — produces a new individual property-instance in the apple. Events are trope-changes: the addition, removal, or qualitative alteration of tropes within a bundle.

This account is more fine-grained than universals-based accounts of causation, which tend to appeal to general laws and property-types. Trope causation is essentially singular: *this* trope caused *this* other trope, at this specific time, in this specific instance. The general law — sunlight causes redness in apples under such conditions — is a generalization over many such singular trope-causings, not its ground. This makes trope theory especially congenial to philosophers who think causation is fundamentally singular rather than nomic (Anscombe, Cartwright), and correspondingly in tension with regularity theories.

## Against Bare Particulars

One of Williams's key motivations is to eliminate bare particulars — the characterless substrata posited by substratum theories to individuate and unify property bundles. He argues that bare particulars are both unnecessary and incoherent. They are unnecessary because tropes can serve as the individuators of concrete objects: this tomato is distinct from that one because their respective bundles of tropes are numerically distinct. The redness-trope of this tomato is not identical with the redness-trope of that tomato, even if the two are qualitatively indistinguishable. They are incoherent because a bare particular is supposed to be a subject that has properties without being characterized by any of them — an existent that is of no kind whatsoever, which seems to be a nothing masquerading as a something.

The alternative Williams offers is that tropes are self-individuating: each trope is numerically distinct from every other as a matter of its primitive individuality. The individuation of a concrete object is then derived from the individuation of its constituent tropes, with no bare particular needed. Whether this primitive individuality of tropes is more intelligible than the primitive individuality of bare particulars is a fair question, but Williams would argue that tropes at least have intrinsic qualitative character — they are something, namely this specific quality — while bare particulars, by definition, have none.
