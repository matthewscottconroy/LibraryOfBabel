# Trope Bundles

---

If a concrete particular is a bundle of tropes, the bundle must be unified somehow. The color-trope, shape-trope, hardness-trope, and mass-trope of this table must be held together as the tropes of *one* thing — not scattered tropes that happen to be spatially adjacent, but co-constituents of a single concrete object. The bundling relation is the trope theorist's version of the traditional problem of the unity of the concrete thing, and it proves surprisingly resistant to clean resolution.

The standard candidate is *compresence*: two tropes are compresent when they are co-located in the same region of space-time. The table's brownness and its hardness are compresent because they occupy the same region; the table's brownness and the charge of a distant electron are not. The bundle of tropes constituting the table is the maximal set of tropes mutually compresent with each other. This is initially attractive — compresence is a natural, intuitively understood spatial relation — but the attraction fades when we ask about the ontological status of the compresence relation itself.

Consider: the compresence holding between the color-trope and the shape-trope of the table is itself a fact about these tropes. Is it grounded in a compresence-trope — a particular instance of the co-location relation? If so, what keeps this compresence-trope in the bundle? A further compresence-trope is needed, and then a further one still. The regress threatens to be vicious: the bundle never achieves unity because at every stage one more compresence-trope is required. The alternatives — treating compresence as a universal, or as a primitive formal relation with no entity to ground it — each compromise the one-category aspiration of trope theory in different ways.

## Three Options for Bundling

Three main proposals have been developed, each with its characteristic strengths and difficulties.

The *mutual ontological dependence* approach, developed by Simons in *Parts: A Study in Ontology* (1987), holds that the tropes constituting a bundle are mutually dependent: each requires the others for its existence. The brownness, hardness, and mass of the table form a unity not because a further binding entity holds them together but because they cannot exist apart. The unity is internal to the tropes themselves rather than imposed by an external relation. This avoids the compresence regress elegantly, but it requires a robust account of ontological dependence that can withstand scrutiny. The most natural formulation — specific dependence, where each trope requires these specific other tropes — faces the objection that it seems too strong: the table could presumably exist with slightly different brownness (if it had been painted a shade darker), so the brownness-trope is not strictly necessary for the other tropes. A weaker *generic* dependence is needed, requiring that each trope requires some tropes of certain kinds without specifying which ones — but generic dependence may not be sufficient to secure the unity of a particular bundle.

The *set-theoretic bundling* approach identifies the bundle with the set {t₁, t₂, t₃, ...} of compresent tropes. This is mathematically clean and avoids the regress by making bundles set-theoretically rather than relationally unified. The cost is that sets are abstract objects, and if concrete tables are sets of tropes, then concrete tables are abstract — which seems to misclassify them. There is also the problem that arbitrary sets of tropes will correspond to no concrete particular, so we need some principled account of which sets of tropes constitute genuine bundles.

The *compresence-as-primitive* approach takes the compresence relation as a brute, unanalyzable fact holding among certain collections of tropes, without positing any entity to ground it. Two tropes are compresent or they are not; the question "what makes them compresent?" has no further answer. This avoids the regress and the abstraction problem, but introduces a primitive that some find ontologically unsatisfying — we have not explained unity so much as relabeled it.

## Persistence and Change

A serious further challenge for trope bundle theory is accounting for persistence through change. An apple is green at time t₁ and red at time t₂. If the apple is identical with its bundle of tropes, and the greenness-trope at t₁ has been replaced by a redness-trope at t₂, then the t₁-bundle and the t₂-bundle are strictly different bundles — and hence, on the bundle theory, different objects. But we want to say it is the same apple persisting through a color change, not two distinct apples in succession.

The substratum theory handles persistence straightforwardly: the bare particular persists through changes in properties, remaining numerically identical while the properties it "has" vary. Trope bundle theory must find an alternative.

Three responses are available. *Four-dimensionalism* treats the apple as a four-dimensional entity extended through time just as it is extended through space: the greenness-trope is a temporal part of the apple, the redness-trope is a later temporal part, and the apple as a whole is the four-dimensional bundle of all its temporal trope-parts. Change is spatial-analogue variation across temporal extent, not genuine replacement of a property in a persisting subject. This handles change cleanly but requires commitment to the ontological reality of temporal parts. *Genidentity* posits a primitive relation among temporally successive trope-bundles that constitutes them as stages of the same object — not a further trope but a primitive relation among bundles. *Causal continuation* grounds the sameness of successive bundles in the right kind of causal connection between them: the t₁-bundle causes the t₂-bundle via the natural processes of the apple's internal constitution. Each response has its costs, but the trope theorist is not without resources.
