# Primitive Resemblance

---

Trope theory replaces universals with resemblance classes of tropes. The universal REDNESS — the one entity shared by all red things — is replaced by the class of all individual red-tropes, distinct entities that are qualitatively similar to one another. But two tropes can be similar only if similarity is a real relation among them, one that holds objectively and not in virtue of their both instantiating some further universal. The resemblance among tropes must therefore be taken as a primitive, objective, unanalyzable fact. This is both the move that makes trope theory work and the move that attracts the most skepticism.

The skeptic's challenge is straightforward. When we ask why two electron-mass tropes resemble each other exactly, the universals theorist has an answer: they both instantiate the same universal, ELECTRON-MASS, and that is what makes them qualitatively identical. The trope theorist says: they just do resemble each other. This looks not like an explanation but like a refusal to explain. The defender of tropes responds that the universals theorist is not in a better position: to say that two tropes instantiate the same universal is to invoke an instantiation relation that is itself primitive and unexplained. Each theory bottoms out in a primitive; the question is which primitive is more illuminating.

Defenders argue that trope-theoretic primitive resemblance has an advantage in intelligibility. Resemblance is a familiar, pervasively encountered relation — we understand what it is for two patches to resemble each other in color because we constantly encounter resembling things. Instantiation of a universal, by contrast, is a genuinely exotic relation: it involves a spatiotemporally located concrete entity somehow "having" an abstract entity that is itself wholly present in many locations simultaneously. The familiarity of resemblance makes it a more natural stopping point for explanation, on this view, even if it remains unexplained.

## Exact and Inexact Resemblance

Resemblance among tropes comes in degrees, and this gradability is one of the theory's significant advantages. Two electron-mass tropes resemble each other *exactly*: they are qualitatively indistinguishable, differing only numerically. A bright-red trope and a pale-pink trope resemble each other *inexactly*: they are qualitatively similar but not identical. A red trope and a blue trope resemble each other less; a red trope and a particular mass-trope may not resemble each other in color at all.

Trope theorists distinguish two kinds of resemblance accordingly. *Exact resemblance* or qualitative identity holds when two tropes are indistinguishable in their qualitative character — they differ only in being numerically distinct entities. *Inexact resemblance* or qualitative similarity holds when two tropes are similar but qualitatively distinguishable. The property-type for a determinate property such as ELECTRON-MASS is then the maximal exact-resemblance class: all and only those tropes that exactly resemble the electron-mass tropes. The property-type for a determinable property such as COLOR is a broader inexact-resemblance class: all tropes that resemble each other to at least some threshold degree in the relevant qualitative dimension.

This structure naturally accommodates the determinable/determinate hierarchy without requiring a special ontological account. Exact resemblance classes are the determinates; broader inexact-resemblance classes built by lowering the threshold are the determinables. The hierarchy is built into the graded structure of resemblance itself. It also accommodates vagueness: the vagueness of "red" corresponds to the absence of a sharp cut-off in the resemblance ordering near the margins.

## Armstrong's Challenge

Armstrong's most focused objection is that resemblance is itself a universal. If two red tropes resemble each other and two round tropes resemble each other, these are two instances of the *same* resemblance relation. RESEMBLANCE is repeated across different trope-pairs, making it a universal. If so, the trope theorist has admitted at least one universal, and the one-category ontology fails.

Campbell's response in *Abstract Particulars* (1990) is that resemblance is not a universal but a *formal* or *internal* relation — one that holds between tropes in virtue of their qualitative natures rather than in virtue of some further entity connecting them. An internal relation is one that is determined by the intrinsic natures of the relata: given what each trope is, their resemblance is fixed. No further entity — no universal RESEMBLANCE — is needed to make it hold. An external relation, by contrast, holds independently of the intrinsic natures of the relata and requires a further entity to ground it.

The defense is plausible for exact resemblance. Two electron-mass tropes that are qualitatively indistinguishable simply must resemble each other exactly; it is part of what they are. But the defense is less convincing for inexact resemblance. Why does this red trope resemble that red trope more than it resembles a blue trope? The ordering of degrees of resemblance seems to require an explanation that appeals to the structure of the relevant quality space — which begins to look like an implicit reference to universals or natural kinds. The trope theorist owes a more detailed account of inexact resemblance ordering if the appeal to internal relations is to do the work required of it.

A specific version of the resemblance regress also applies at the trope level: (1) two red tropes t₁ and t₂ resemble each other; (2) two other red tropes t₃ and t₄ resemble each other; (3) these two resemblance facts are "the same kind of resemblance," which seems to require that they themselves resemble each other; (4) and so on. The trope theorist's best response is that step (3) involves a grammatical illusion — treating resemblance-facts as objects that can themselves be resembled. Resemblance is a relation among tropes, not itself a trope; it cannot figure as a relatum in a further resemblance claim without a category error. Whether this response fully blocks the regress is contested, but it has more force at the trope level than at the level of ordinary resemblance nominalism, where the regress is more naturally felt.
