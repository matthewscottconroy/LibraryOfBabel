# Abstract Particulars

---

The term "abstract particular" might seem self-defeating. "Particular" suggests something concrete and individual; "abstract" suggests something general and non-spatiotemporal. Williams chose the conjunction deliberately, and the apparent paradox is instructive about what kind of entity a trope is supposed to be.

Tropes are *particular* in the sense of being numerically individual: this redness in this tomato is numerically distinct from that redness in that tomato, even if both tropes are exactly alike in their qualitative character. No other trope is identical with this one; it occupies a specific location and belongs to a specific particular in the way no other trope does. In this respect tropes are like ordinary concrete things: each is one entity, located here rather than there.

Tropes are *abstract* in an etymological rather than Platonist sense. They are aspects of a concrete thing that can be isolated by a process of mental abstraction — focusing on the color of the tomato rather than the tomato in its full concreteness. The "abstraction" is cognitive: we attend to one dimension of the object while setting aside the others. The trope itself is not abstract in the Platonist sense of being non-spatial and causally inert. The redness-trope of this tomato is located right here, in this tomato, causally affecting the visual systems of perceivers, participating in causal relations just as concrete things do. Platonist abstract entities lack causal powers; tropes have them.

This matters for epistemology. If tropes are where their objects are and are causally connected to their environments, we perceive them (at least indirectly) by perceiving the objects they characterize. There is no need for a faculty of abstract intuition or non-causal cognitive contact to gain epistemic access to tropes — one of the persistent difficulties that bedevils Platonic realism. The redness-trope of this tomato is accessible to ordinary perception precisely because it is located in an ordinary place.

## Modes, Moments, and the Vocabulary of Dependence

The variety of terms used by different philosophers for trope-like entities reflects genuine uncertainty about how to categorize them. "Trope" (Williams, Campbell) emphasizes numerical individuality and that the entity is itself a kind of property, not a relation between a substratum and a property. "Property instance" suggests an instance *of* a property and can implicitly reintroduce universals at another level, which the trope theorist wants to avoid. "Mode" (Lowe, Mertz), derived from Aristotle's individual accidents, treats modes as "ways things are" — the specific manner in which a thing has a property. E. J. Lowe's four-category ontology distinguishes universals (attribute-types), modes (attribute-tokens), individual substances, and substance-kinds, giving a more structured account than Williams's simpler one-category approach. "Moment" (Husserl, Simons) draws on Husserl's phenomenological analysis: moments are dependent parts that cannot exist except as parts of the whole, in contrast to pieces (independent parts). Color is a moment of a surface; it cannot exist apart from some colored thing.

Each of these terms carries different theoretical commitments, but all point toward the same core idea: an entity that is individual rather than universal, located where its bearer is located, and in some sense dependent on its bearer for its existence.

The question of *ontological dependence* is central. Most trope theorists hold that a trope cannot exist independently of the object it characterizes: the redness-trope of this tomato cannot float free of any bearer. Maurin distinguishes two forms of dependence. *Specific dependence* holds when the trope requires the existence of this particular bearer — the redness-trope requires this tomato specifically. *Generic dependence* holds when the trope requires some bearer of the appropriate kind but not any specific one. Most trope theorists prefer generic dependence, because specific dependence would make the individuation of tropes trivially parasitic on the individuation of objects. If a trope requires this specific object, the trope is individuated only when the object is individuated, but if we want tropes to help individuate objects, we cannot also have objects individuate tropes. Circularity threatens unless generic dependence is used.

## Spatiotemporal Location and Its Complications

The spatiotemporal location of tropes is one of their most important features — it is what distinguishes them from universals and makes them accessible to perception and causation. But giving a precise account of how tropes are located is surprisingly difficult. Concrete particulars are extended in space: they occupy spatial regions of determinate size. Tropes, as the properties of concrete particulars, are presumably located where their objects are located. But do they occupy the same spatial region as their object, or some proper part of it?

The most natural answer is that the redness-trope of the tomato is located wherever the tomato's surface is — in the outer region occupied by the tomato's exterior. The mass-trope might be located throughout the tomato's whole extent, or at its center of mass, or at every point. Getting the fine-grained account right requires careful attention to the different kinds of properties objects can have.

More troublingly, quantum mechanics introduces entities whose spatial location is indeterminate or non-classical. The charge of an electron, understood as a trope, is presumably wherever the electron is — but electrons in superposition do not have determinate locations. Does the charge-trope have a determinate location? If not, what grounds its numerical distinctness from the charge-trope of another electron in superposition? These questions push trope theory into territory where its standard resources may be insufficient, and they represent a serious challenge for those who want to apply trope theory to the foundations of physics.
