# Tropes and Causation

---

One of the persistent difficulties for universals-based accounts of causation is that universals, as abstract entities, cannot themselves do causal work. It is the tomato that causes our visual experience of red, not the universal REDNESS. The universal is abstract, non-spatiotemporal, outside the causal nexus. But then saying that REDNESS is causally relevant seems to mean only that objects which instantiate REDNESS tend to cause redness-experiences — a claim about the causal powers of concrete objects, not about the universal itself. On this picture, properties as universals do causal work only derivatively, through the concrete objects that bear them. Trope theory removes this awkwardness. If the tomato's redness is a trope — a concrete, spatially located entity — it can be causally efficacious in its own right, directly connected to its effects in the way concrete things are.

Consider the fragility of a vase. The universals theorist identifies fragility with a universal that many vases instantiate. But what causes *this* vase to break when dropped is not the abstract universal but something specific to this vase — its particular crystalline structure, its particular thickness, its particular composition. Trope theory names exactly this: the vase's fragility-trope, a concrete individual that is the fragility of this vase and no other. When the vase is dropped and breaks, it is this trope — not a universal, not a type — that causally explains the breakage. Causal explanation in terms of tropes is grounded in concrete particular entities, and its singular character is preserved.

This alignment between trope theory and dispositionalism is not accidental. A causal power such as electrical charge is a disposition: the tendency to attract opposite charges, repel like charges, and deflect in electromagnetic fields given appropriate stimulation. If causal powers are tropes, they are individual, spatiotemporally located entities that directly account for the behavior of the objects that have them. The electron's specific charge-trope is what deflects in the magnetic field; it is not a universal in a Platonic realm but a concrete feature of this electron, here and now, with its specific strength and direction of action. This has made trope theory increasingly popular among philosophers who want to combine naturalistic ontology with a powers-based account of causation.

## Singular Causation

The fit between trope theory and *singular causation* is distinctive. Universals-based regularity theories analyze singular causal claims in terms of general regularities among types: "A caused B" is true because there is a law relating A's property-type to B's property-type in the relevant conditions. But this analysis seems to lose the particularity of the singular claim. It is *this* impact that caused *this* window to break, not merely that impacts of this type cause breakings of this type. The singular "this" cannot be captured by a generalization.

Trope theory naturally supports singularism (Anscombe, Cartwright, Mumford): the impact's momentum-trope and the window's brittleness-trope are the concrete particular entities that interact to produce the breakage. The causal relation holds directly between these specific tropes, not mediated by a general law. The general law is a derivative generalization over many such singular trope-causings. The direction of explanatory priority runs from singular trope-causings to general regularities, not the reverse — which is exactly what singularists want.

## The Causal Exclusion Problem

The causal exclusion problem, developed by Kim, raises a sharp challenge for any theory that posits multiple causally relevant property-types for a single event. If every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, and mental events are distinct from physical events, mental causation appears excluded. The physical cause is already sufficient; where does the mental cause find a foothold?

Trope theory opens a path toward resolution. A decision event has both a mental-property trope (the phenomenal character of deciding) and a physical-property trope (the neural state) as compresent constituents. These are distinct tropes but belong to the same concrete event. If the mental trope causes certain effects (further mental states, behavioral outputs) and the physical trope causes other effects (further neural states), there need be no exclusion between them, since they are distinct entities in the causal field. The exclusion argument gains its force from treating properties as universals competing for the same causal slot; once properties are individuated as tropes, the competition is less obvious.

This solution is not without difficulties. It requires that a single event can have multiple distinct property-tropes and that different tropes can cause different effects without problematic overdetermination. Whether that is coherent, or whether it merely relocates the problem, is actively debated. C. B. Martin and John Heil's view of "powerful qualities" — *powerful tropes*, in the trope-theoretic version — offers a further synthesis: the fundamental properties of the world are both qualitative (they have a specific qualitative character) and dispositional (they are directed toward specific manifestations). These two aspects are not two separate tropes but two ways of characterizing a single trope. If the charge-trope is both the qualitative character of having charge -e and the disposition to attract protons, there is no gap between what the trope is and what it does, and the question of causal exclusion does not arise in the same form.
