# The Compresence Problem

---

If a concrete particular just is a bundle of tropes, those tropes must be unified somehow. The color-trope, shape-trope, hardness-trope, and mass-trope of this table must be held together as tropes of *one* thing, not merely a loose collection of property-instances that happen to be in the same vicinity. The compresence relation is the standard candidate for this binding role: two tropes are compresent if they are co-located in the same spatiotemporal region. The table's tropes are compresent; each of an electron's charge and mass are compresent; the table's color-trope and the charge of a distant electron are not.

The problem surfaces immediately when we ask what grounds the compresence fact. Is the compresence holding between the color-trope and the shape-trope of the table itself a further entity? If it is a compresence-trope — a particular instance of the co-location relation — then the bundle now includes three items: the color-trope, the shape-trope, and the compresence-trope. But what makes the compresence-trope part of the bundle? It must itself be compresent with the other two tropes, generating a further compresence-trope at the next level. The regress has the following structure:

- Stage 1: color-trope t₁ and shape-trope t₂ are compresent → c₁₂ grounds this
- Stage 2: c₁₂ and t₁ are compresent → c₁₂,₁ grounds this
- Stage 3: c₁₂,₁ and c₁₂ and t₁ are compresent → ...

Whether this regress is vicious or merely infinite is disputed. If vicious, the bundle never achieves unity. If benign, the bundle contains infinitely many compresence-tropes, which seems ontologically extravagant — though defenders note that the infinity is ordered and systematic, analogous to the infinite series of spatial relations among points in a region.

## Alternative Grounding Strategies

Three main alternatives to compresence-as-trope have been developed.

*Compresence as primitive formal relation*: the relation holds as a brute, unanalyzable fact about certain tropes, without any entity grounding it. Two tropes are simply compresent or they are not; no further explanation is available or required. This stops the regress cleanly and avoids commitment to an infinite hierarchy of compresence-tropes. The cost is that it introduces a primitive relation with no ontological status as an entity — neither a trope nor a universal — which some find metaphysically unsatisfying. Whether "primitive formal relation" is a coherent category or simply a way of refusing to answer the question is a genuine issue.

*Mutual ontological dependence*: Simons's approach in *Parts: A Study in Ontology* (1987). The tropes in a bundle are mutually dependent: each requires the others for its existence. The color-trope and the shape-trope of the table cannot exist apart from each other; they form a unity through mutual intra-dependence rather than through an external binding entity. This avoids the compresence regress and makes bundle-unity an internal feature of the tropes' natures. The objection is that specific dependence — requiring these specific tropes — seems too strong: the table could plausibly exist with slightly different brownness. A weaker *generic* dependence requiring tropes of certain kinds but not these specific ones may be needed, but generic dependence may not secure the unity of this particular bundle rather than some other.

*Set-theoretic bundling*: the bundle just is the set {t₁, t₂, t₃, ...} of co-located tropes. This is mathematically clean, but sets are abstract objects, and concrete tables — the very paradigm of concrete things — cannot be abstract. There is also the arbitrariness problem: arbitrary sets of tropes have no corresponding concrete particular, so we need some principled account of which sets constitute genuine bundles.

## The Circularity Worry

A further problem threatens any account that grounds compresence in spatial co-location. Location for tropes is typically understood as derivative: a trope is located where its bearer is located. But the bearer is constituted by the bundle, and the bundle is formed by compresence. This gives us: tropes are compresent because they are in the same bundle; they are in the same bundle because they are compresent. The circularity is tight.

Avoiding it seems to require taking either compresence or spatial location of objects as an unanalyzable primitive. Trope theory can tolerate one or more primitives — every theory has them — but the compresence problem shows that the one-category aspiration of trope theory comes under pressure at the very point where the theory must explain its central constructive move: how individual property-instances are assembled into concrete objects.
