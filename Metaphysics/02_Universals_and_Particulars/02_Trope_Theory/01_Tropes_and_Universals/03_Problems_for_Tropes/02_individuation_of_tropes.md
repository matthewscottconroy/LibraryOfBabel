# Individuation of Tropes

---

Trope theory earns much of its appeal by individualizing property-instances — replacing one universal REDNESS with many distinct redness-tropes, each particular to its bearer. But the individualization creates an obligation: if there are two exactly alike redness-tropes, one in this tomato and one in that, what makes them numerically distinct? They are qualitatively identical by hypothesis. The universals theorist has a ready answer: REDNESS is one entity, not two; what distinguishes the two tomatoes is that they are distinct concrete particulars instantiating the same universal. The trope theorist has multiplied where the universals theorist unified, and must now explain the multiplication.

Several accounts have been proposed. *Spatiotemporal individuation* holds that tropes are individuated by their location: this redness-trope is the one at this place and time; that redness-trope is located elsewhere. Since two distinct tropes cannot occupy exactly the same spatiotemporal region, location suffices for distinctness. This is initially attractive — it explains individuation without additional primitives and connects naturally to the claim that tropes are concrete, located entities. The complications arise at the boundaries. Quantum mechanics allows particles to exist in superposition, without determinate spatial locations. If the charge-trope of an electron lacks a determinate location, spatiotemporal individuation gives no grip on what makes it distinct from the charge-trope of another electron in superposition. And even in classical cases, successive tropes occupying the same region at different times (a surface's color-trope before and after painting) must be individuated by their temporal position, which inherits whatever difficulties attach to the individuation of moments of time.

*Primitive individuation* holds that tropes are primitively individual — it is a brute, unanalyzable fact that this trope is numerically distinct from that one, even if they are qualitatively identical. Maurin's view in *If Tropes* (2002) is the most systematic defense: being a trope just is being an individual in an irreducible sense. To ask what grounds trope-individuation is to demand an explanation of something that is its own ground. This has the virtue of honesty — it does not pretend to reduce individuation to something else — but critics note that it is structurally identical to the bare-particular theorist's claim that particulars are primitively individual. If primitive individuality in a bare particular is an explanatory failure, why is primitive individuality in a trope a success?

## The Structuralist Dilemma

The choice between spatiotemporal and primitive individuation can be framed as a dilemma. Either tropes are individuated by their spatiotemporal location, making trope-individuation parasitic on the individuation of spacetime points or regions; or tropes are primitively individual, making their individuality unanalyzable ground-floor. Option A inherits all the difficulties of spacetime individuation — what individuates spacetime points? Does the answer differ for substantivalist vs. relationist spacetime? Option B accepts an unexplained primitive but remains silent about what makes any particular trope *this* one rather than some other qualitatively identical trope.

Neither horn is comfortable. But notice that the universals theorist faces an analogous dilemma about the individuation of the concrete particulars that instantiate universals. What makes *this* electron distinct from *that* one, if both instantiate the same universals? Spatiotemporal location, primitive thisness, or haecceity — the same options are on the table. Trope theory has more to individuate (both tropes and the objects composed of them), but the forms of the problem are parallel.

## Consequences for Bundle Theory

The stakes of trope individuation extend beyond the theory of properties to the theory of objects. If concrete objects are bundles of tropes, the identity of an object is a function of the identity of its constituent tropes. Three scenarios illustrate the pressure.

First: two electrons, qualitatively identical in all their properties, are intuitively numerically distinct. On the bundle theory they are distinct because their constituent tropes are distinct. Spatiotemporal individuation gives the required answer in classical cases; quantum mechanics complicates it. Second: an apple was green and is now red. The successive bundles are numerically distinct. What makes them stages of the same apple? Genidentity, causal continuity, or four-dimensionalist temporal parts — each answer requires commitment beyond the bare bundle framework. Third: could the redness-trope of this rose, in principle, be "transferred" to that vase? If tropes are spatiotemporally individuated, this is impossible (the trope would need to change its location while remaining numerically the same trope). If tropes are primitively individual, transferability is conceptually coherent, even if the physics rules it out.

The deeper point is that trope bundle theory places the full weight of object-individuation on trope-individuation. The universals framework distributes this weight differently, outsourcing part of it to the concrete particulars (substrata, positions) that instantiate universals. One can see this as a virtue or a liability: trope theory is admirably unified in its account of what does the individuating work, but that account must bear the full load.
