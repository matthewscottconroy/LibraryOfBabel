# Trope Theory vs. Rivals

---

Trope theory is often presented as combining the advantages of universals realism and nominalism while avoiding their characteristic problems. The claim deserves scrutiny. What trope theory genuinely accomplishes, and at what cost, becomes clearest when it is compared directly with the positions it aspires to improve upon.

Against universals realism, tropes offer two genuine advantages. First, each trope is where it is and nowhere else: no mysterious multi-location, no need for the puzzling metaphysics of being wholly present in many places simultaneously. Second, because tropes are spatiotemporally located and causally active, we can access them through ordinary perception and causal interaction. The epistemological problem that bedevils Platonic realism — how do we cognize entities in a non-causal, non-spatiotemporal realm? — simply does not arise. Against these advantages, universals realism scores on laws of nature: Armstrong's necessitation relation N(F,G) between universals provides an elegant account of why regularities hold as a matter of nomic necessity, not mere coincidence. The trope theorist must appeal to Humean regularities among trope-types, or to the "natures" of tropes as building in certain regularities, neither of which is as elegant. Universals realism also handles uninstantiated properties, which trope theory cannot (there are no tropes for properties that nothing has), and it explains exact resemblance more economically (two electron-mass tropes are exactly alike because they share a universal; trope theory can only say their tropes exactly resemble, requiring primitive resemblance as brute).

Against predicate nominalism, trope theory provides genuine property-entities that ground the truth of predications. When we say this tomato is red, that statement is made true by the tomato's having a red-trope — a real entity in virtue of which the predicate applies. Predicate nominalism has no such ground: the predicate applies because it applies, or because we apply it, with no further ontological basis. The trope theorist's advantage here is real. The cost is a new ontological category: tropes are neither universals nor ordinary concrete objects, and adding them requires argument that the new entities earn their keep.

## The Methodological Question

Underlying the comparative assessment is a methodological disagreement about what metaphysics should defer to. Science uses types and kinds: ELECTRON is a type, CHARGE is a type, laws are stated in terms of types. If we take scientific language at face value, universals are the natural ontology — what the science is directly about. Trope theorists respond that scientific *measurement* is always of individual property-instances: the charge of *this* electron is measured by *this* apparatus in *this* context. The data of science are always particular-level; types are theoretical constructs over particular instances. If we follow the data rather than the theoretical vocabulary, tropes are more fundamental.

This disagreement reflects a deeper question: should metaphysics model itself on the theoretical vocabulary of our best science, which is type-level, or on the epistemic basis of that science, which is individual-level? Neither answer is obviously correct, and the debate about universals versus tropes partly turns on this unresolved methodological issue.

Campbell's *Abstract Particulars* (1990) and Maurin's subsequent work present trope theory at its most systematic and careful. The contemporary assessment seems to be that trope theory is a live and respectable option — neither a clear winner over universals realism nor clearly inferior to it. Both face irreducible primitives: universals require primitive instantiation, tropes require primitive resemblance. Both require some account of how their fundamental entities are bundled into objects: universals need a substratum or a different kind of bundle account, tropes need compresence or dependence. The choice between them may ultimately depend on which primitive is more intelligible and which problems are more tractable — a matter where philosophical preferences legitimately differ.

One genuine advantage of trope-bundle theory over universals-bundle theory (the view that objects are bundles of universals rather than bare particulars instantiating universals) deserves notice. Universals-bundle theory faces a stark form of the identity of indiscernibles: two objects with exactly the same universals would be numerically identical, making it impossible for two indiscernible objects to be distinct. Trope-bundle theory avoids this: even if two electrons share the same property-types (same mass, charge, spin), their constituent tropes are numerically distinct. The two electrons are distinct because their mass-tropes, charge-tropes, and spin-tropes are all numerically distinct, even though qualitatively identical. This is a real gain over universals-bundle theory, and it is one of the central motivations for moving from universals to tropes while retaining the bundle approach to concrete particulars.
