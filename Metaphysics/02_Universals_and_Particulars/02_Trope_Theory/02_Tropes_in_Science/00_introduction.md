# Tropes in Science

Metaphysics that is entirely divorced from science runs the risk of elegantly describing a world that does not exist. Trope theory, whatever its intrinsic philosophical merits, must also earn its place by being compatible with — and ideally illuminating of — the scientific picture of reality. Does the ontology of particularized properties cohere with what physics, chemistry, and biology tell us about the world?

There are reasons for optimism. Physics at its most fundamental level does not obviously describe universals floating in abstract space; it describes fields, charge distributions, spin states — features that are highly specific to particular regions and particles. The spin of this electron is a particular property of this electron; another electron may have the same spin value, but the property-instance is distinct. This is exactly the picture trope theory predicts: the world consists of particular property-instances, and what physics describes are types of tropes identified by their values and causal roles.

Scientific laws present a more complex case. Laws of nature seem to be universal in scope: F = ma applies to every massive body, not just to the ones in some particular region. If laws are regularities about tropes — all charge-tropes of a certain magnitude interact with all other charge-tropes in this way — then the generality is a matter of the pattern holding across all members of a resemblance class, not a relation between universals. Whether this account can ground the modal force of laws — the fact that they could not have been violated — is a more difficult question.

Biology raises the question of biological kinds. Are species real, and if so, are they universals? The historical view that species are natural kinds with essential properties has given way, under pressure from evolutionary biology, to the view that species are historical lineages — populations of organisms connected by descent, not by shared essential features. This "species are individuals" thesis aligns naturally with trope theory: membership in a species is not a matter of instantiating a universal but of standing in the right historical-causal relations.

This section examines the relationship between trope theory and scientific practice, asking how well the trope ontology accommodates the entities and laws that science posits.
