# Mass and Charge as Tropes

---

The case for tropes becomes most pressing when it is applied not to the color of tomatoes but to the fundamental properties of physics. Every electron has charge -1 (in units of the elementary charge). Every electron has exactly the same mass. These properties are not approximately shared, not nearly the same — they are identical to many decimal places, uniform across the entire observable universe, as far as experiment can determine. Something grounds this uniformity. The universals realist offers an elegant answer: all electrons instantiate one universal, ELECTRON-CHARGE, and sharing a single entity just is being exactly alike in the relevant respect. The trope theorist must explain the same uniformity while insisting that each electron's charge is numerically distinct from every other — its own individual property-instance, belonging uniquely to this electron.

The trope account runs as follows. Each electron has its own charge-trope, numerically distinct from the charge-trope of every other electron, but exactly resembling all of them. The exact resemblance is a brute fact about these tropes: they just are qualitatively identical, as a matter of how the world happens to be constituted at the deepest level. The universals theorist will object that this treats as a coincidence what ought to have an explanation — why do all these numerically distinct tropes happen to exactly resemble each other? The trope theorist's response is that the universals account's explanation is less satisfying than it appears. Saying that all electrons share the universal ELECTRON-CHARGE explains sameness by positing a shared entity, but it does not explain why sharing a single entity makes things exactly alike rather than merely similar, nor why instantiation has the character it has. At some point both accounts invoke something primitive; the dispute is about which primitive is more acceptable.

The trope interpretation has genuine advantages that are not just defensive moves. Physical properties are typically encountered through their effects on particular, concrete, individual events: this charge deflects this beam, with this specific force, measured by this apparatus at this time. The causal efficacy of a charge is always the efficacy of a specific charge at a specific location. Tropes capture this particularity directly. Universals, being abstract and general, do causal work only derivatively through the concrete objects that instantiate them, which can seem like an unnecessary detour.

## Quantum Mechanics as a Test Case

Quantum mechanics makes the application of trope theory both more interesting and more difficult. Several features deserve attention.

In *superposition*, a particle can lack a determinate value for a given property prior to measurement. An electron's spin in a particular direction is genuinely undefined before measurement in that direction. If spin is a trope, the trope-theorist must say either that the relevant trope simply does not exist before measurement — it is created by the measurement — or that there is an indeterminate-spin trope with no definite qualitative character until the state collapses. The first option is consistent with trope theory but unusual: property-instances come into existence through measurement interactions, which is a departure from the picture of tropes as stable features of their bearers.

*Quantum statistics* is more directly informative. Fermions obey the Pauli exclusion principle: no two fermions in the same quantum state can occupy the same location. This is consistent with trope individuation — each fermion's tropes are numerically distinct, so they are never literally identical — and may even receive a kind of explanation from it: two fermions cannot share quantum states because their property-tropes are always numerically distinct individuals, never literally the same entity.

*Entanglement* poses the deepest challenge. Two entangled electrons may have a determinate total spin (total = 0) while neither electron individually has a determinate spin. If properties are tropes of individual particles, entangled systems have properties that do not belong to either particle alone but to the pair. The trope theorist must posit composite-system tropes — tropes of the two-electron system taken as a unit — in addition to individual-particle tropes. This complicates the ontology but does not obviously destroy it. The universals theorist faces an analogous challenge: the universal SPIN-0 is instantiated by the composite system, not by either electron individually.

The physics of general relativity adds a further complication for the treatment of mass as an intrinsic trope. Invariant mass is frame-independent in special relativity, which makes it a natural candidate for a purely intrinsic property. But in general relativity, mass-energy is a source of spacetime curvature, and the curvature in turn affects how massive objects behave. The mass-trope of a particle is, in this sense, not self-contained but essentially connected to the global spacetime structure. Whether this means mass-tropes are not purely intrinsic, or whether it means the causal role of the mass-trope is partly constituted by its global relations, is an open question at the intersection of trope theory and the philosophy of physics.
