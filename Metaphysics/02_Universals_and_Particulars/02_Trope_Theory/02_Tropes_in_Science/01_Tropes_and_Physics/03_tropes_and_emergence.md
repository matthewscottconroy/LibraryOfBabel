# Tropes and Emergence

---

Individual water molecules are not wet. Individual neurons are not conscious. Individual atoms are not solid. Yet bodies of water are wet, functioning brains are conscious, and tables are solid. These higher-level properties seem to arise from the organization of lower-level constituents without being simply identical to any property of those constituents taken individually. This is the phenomenon of emergence, and it presents a characteristic challenge for any property ontology: if properties are tropes — individual, concrete property-instances — what exactly happens when a new property emerges? Is there a new trope? Where does it come from? And if there is one, what is its relationship to the tropes at the lower level?

The answer depends on the kind of emergence in play. *Weak emergence* — the kind that physicists and many philosophers accept as uncontroversial — holds that higher-level properties are fully determined by lower-level properties given sufficient information and computational resources, even if they are not practically derivable. The wetness of water is fully determined by the molecular structure of H₂O and the arrangement of water molecules; it is just that no human being could derive wetness from first principles of quantum mechanics without substantial experimental input. On the trope account, the wetness-trope of a body of water is *grounded* in the molecular tropes of its constituent molecules and their arrangement: the wetness could not fail to be present given that molecular configuration. The wetness-trope is a new trope — a property of the macroscopic body, not of any individual molecule — but it is ontologically dependent on the molecular tropes in a principled way. Introducing grounding alongside tropes and resemblance as a third element of the theoretical toolkit allows a coherent account of how macro-tropes arise from micro-tropes without being identical to them.

*Strong emergence* is more contentious. If consciousness is strongly emergent, it is a property that is not deducible even in principle from any physical description, regardless of how complete. The "explanatory gap" between neural descriptions and phenomenal descriptions is unbridgeable by derivation from below. In trope terms, consciousness-tropes would be genuinely novel: present in virtue of certain neural trope-configurations, but with qualitative characters that could in principle vary independently of those configurations (the inverted qualia and philosophical zombie thought experiments express exactly this worry). If strong emergence is real, trope theory can accommodate it only by accepting that consciousness-tropes are primitively related to physical tropes rather than grounded by them — which is to say, trope theory with strong emergence is a form of property dualism in trope-theoretic clothing.

## Grounding and the Layered Ontology

The grounding framework, increasingly central in contemporary analytic metaphysics, provides the most natural setting for trope-theoretic emergence. Grounding is a relation of ontological dependence: the fact that [a is F] is grounded in, or holds in virtue of, the fact that [b is G] when the first fact obtains because of the second, in a metaphysically explanatory sense. In trope terms, the wetness-trope T_W of a body of water is grounded in the molecular tropes T_M₁, T_M₂, ... and their arrangement; the solidity-trope T_S of a table is grounded in atomic tropes T_A₁, T_A₂, ... and their bonding relations.

This gives a layered ontology in which each level of scientific description has its characteristic tropes: physical tropes (mass, charge, spin), chemical tropes (bond energy, valence), biological tropes (metabolic rate, fitness), psychological tropes (pain, belief, desire). Each higher-level trope is grounded in lower-level tropes, with the grounding relation providing the relevant ontological connection without identity. The higher-level science deals with genuine tropes with genuine causal powers — the solidity-trope of the table really does cause the cup to stay on the table — and these tropes are not mere epiphenomena on the physical level, even though they are grounded by it.

The question of *downward causation* arises within this layered picture: does the solidity-trope of the table ever causally affect the atomic tropes that ground it? If higher-level tropes have their own causal powers, and if those powers can influence lower-level configurations, then higher-level grounded tropes would be acting on their own grounds. Whether this is coherent, and how it relates to the causal completeness of physics, is a live question. The trope framework does not resolve it, but it articulates it clearly: downward causation would mean that higher-level tropes cause changes in the lower-level tropes that ground them, while upward grounding means that those lower-level tropes constitute the higher-level ones. The two directions of influence are conceptually distinct, and trope theory gives the vocabulary to express both.

The hierarchy of sciences — physics, chemistry, biology, psychology — is, on this picture, a hierarchy of grounded tropes. Each science is autonomous in the sense that it deals with genuine entities at its level; it is not autonomous in the sense of being explanatorily independent of lower levels. The resulting picture is neither eliminative reductionism (higher-level tropes are identical to physical tropes) nor radical emergentism (higher-level tropes are primitive, ungrounded additions to the ontology) but a structured pluralism about properties grounded in the framework of ontological dependence.
