# Laws as Relations of Tropes

---

Any serious ontology of properties owes an account of natural laws. The challenge is not merely to identify what laws are but to explain why they are not just accidental regularities. "All copper conducts electricity" and "all the coins in my pocket are copper" may both be universally true, but only the first is a law. What makes it so? Armstrong's universals theory offers an elegant answer: the law is the universal of necessitation N(COPPER, CONDUCTS-ELECTRICITY), a higher-order fact about universals that directly entails the regularity and explains why it holds necessarily rather than accidentally. For the trope theorist, there are no universals to play this role. Each copper wire has its own conductivity-trope; the law cannot be a relation between universals because there are none. The trope theorist must find an alternative account.

Several strategies have been developed. The simplest treats laws as regularities among trope-types: "all copper conducts" means that every copper-trope is accompanied by a conductivity-trope, as a brute but universal pattern. This is Humean in spirit — the law just is the regularity — and the challenge is to explain why this regularity is lawful while the pocket-coins regularity is merely accidental. Neither involves universals; both are stated in terms of trope-types. The Best Systems Account (discussed separately) provides the most sophisticated Humean answer: laws are those regularities that earn a place in the simplest and strongest systematic description of the trope-mosaic.

Campbell's account in *Abstract Particulars* (1990) attempts to ground necessity more directly. On Campbell's view, laws hold in virtue of the *natures* of individual tropes: each copper-trope has a specific qualitative nature such that it must be compresent with a conductivity-trope. The necessity of the law is distributed across all individual copper-tropes — each one carries the necessity within itself as a feature of its intrinsic qualitative character. This is a form of immanent necessitarianism: the law is not a further entity over and above the tropes but is grounded in them, individually.

## The Problem with Trope Natures

Campbell's account is attractive but faces a sharp dilemma. If the nature of a copper-trope includes conductivity — if being a copper-trope just is being a copper-and-conducts-electricity trope — then the law "all copper conducts" follows trivially from the definition of copper-tropes, and is not a substantive metaphysical claim about the world but a semantic stipulation. The necessity is analytic rather than nomological.

If, on the other hand, the nature of a copper-trope is its specific qualitative character C without conductivity built in, then we need a further account of why C-tropes are always accompanied by conductivity-tropes. We seem to be back to a regularity: all tropes with qualitative character C are accompanied by conductivity-tropes. Campbell's answer — because it is their nature to be so — either reinstates the analyticity problem or leaves the regularity unexplained. The same regress that threatens the universals theorist's use of "instantiation" as a primitive connection threatens the trope theorist's use of "nature" as a primitive source of necessity.

The dispositionalist response to this dilemma is to embrace the essence route straightforwardly: the nature of a charge-trope just is its directedness toward its characteristic electromagnetic manifestations. This is not an arbitrary stipulation but a substantive metaphysical claim: what makes something a charge-trope is its having this specific intrinsic power. Laws of nature are then consequences of the essences of the relevant trope-kinds, with a necessity analogous to the way having three sides follows from being a triangle. The Humean rejects this picture of essences; the necessitarian accepts it. The trope theorist must choose a side in this further debate.

## Humean Laws and Anti-Humean Necessitation

The choice between Humean and anti-Humean accounts of laws applies at the trope level just as at the universals level, and neither tropes nor universals by themselves determine which account is correct. One combination that has attracted significant attention is Humeanism about laws combined with trope ontology: the world consists of nothing but individual tropes and their spatiotemporal relations, with no further necessity, no causal powers, no dispositional natures. Laws emerge from the pattern of regularities in this mosaic, identified by Lewis's Best Systems Account.

The competing combination pairs tropes with dispositionalism: each fundamental trope is an intrinsic power directed toward its manifestations, and laws are the necessary consequences of these powers. This makes laws metaphysically necessary rather than contingent: it is impossible for a charge-trope to fail to have its electromagnetic disposition, just as it is impossible for an unmarried person to be a bachelor. The necessity is not logical but flows from the essences of trope-kinds.

Armstrong's necessitation relation N(F, G) faces van Fraassen's *inference problem*: why does the higher-order fact N(COPPER, CONDUCTS-ELECTRICITY) entail that all copper things actually conduct? There seems to be a gap between the fact about universals and the first-order regularity. The Humean-trope account dissolves this problem: the law just is the regularity, so there is no gap to bridge. The dispositionalist-trope account solves it differently: the necessity flows from the natures of charge-tropes, so the connection is not between a higher-order entity and first-order instances but between the intrinsic character of each trope and its characteristic manifestations. Whether either solution is fully satisfying, or whether the inference problem afflicts all accounts of laws, is part of the ongoing debate.
