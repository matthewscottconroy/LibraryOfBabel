# Humeanism and Tropes

---

Hume's central metaphysical claim was that there are no necessary connections between distinct existences: what happens at one place and time does not necessitate what happens at any other. The world is a mosaic of particular matters of fact, each independent of all others, with regularities emerging from their patterns without anything in the world making those patterns necessary. Lewis updated this picture with modern resources: the Humean mosaic is a distribution of fundamental intrinsic properties at spacetime points, with no further structure beyond the spatiotemporal relations among those points. Laws are not entities over and above the mosaic but features of its systematic description.

Trope theory fits naturally into this Humean picture. Each spacetime point or region has its tropes — specific individual property-instances with no intrinsic directedness, no built-in connections to other tropes, no essential powers. The entire world is constituted by this mosaic of bare tropes and their spatiotemporal arrangement. Laws emerge from the regularities that pattern the mosaic, identified by Lewis's Best Systems Account: laws are the generalizations that appear in the simplest and strongest systematic description of the trope-distribution. The combination is attractive in its parsimony. Tropes replace universals as the bearers of qualitative character; Humean regularities replace Armstrong's necessitation as the account of laws; together they give a metaphysics with no abstract universals, no modally loaded causal powers, and no mysterious necessary connections.

The Best Systems Account applied to tropes runs as follows. The mosaic M is a distribution of tropes across spacetime: M = {(t₁, x₁), (t₂, x₂), ...} where each tᵢ is a trope and xᵢ is its spacetime location. Candidate axiomatic systems describe regularities in M — generalizations of the form "all tropes of type F are accompanied by tropes of type G." The best system is the one that optimally balances simplicity (fewer and less complex axioms) and strength (more consequences, covering more regularities in M). Laws are the generalizations that appear in the best system. "All copper conducts" is a law if and only if it appears in every optimal axiomatization of the trope-mosaic — not because any entity in the world necessitates it, but because the pattern of copper-tropes and conductivity-tropes is too systematic and too wide-ranging to be an accident on any reasonable description.

## Strengths and Difficulties

The Humean-trope combination has three distinctive advantages. First, it is parsimonious: only tropes and spatiotemporal relations are fundamental, with no universals, no necessitation relations, and no modal primitives. Second, it is epistemologically transparent: we observe individual property-instances and their regularities, and the laws we identify are the best systematic description of what we observe. No inaccessible abstract entities are invoked, and the route from observation to law-attribution is reasonably clear. Third, it dissolves Armstrong's inference problem: on Humeanism, the law just is the regularity, so there is no gap between a higher-order lawmaking fact and the first-order regularity it is supposed to entail. The problem only arises when laws are conceived as entities distinct from regularities.

Against these advantages stand three standard objections to Humean regularity theories that apply in the trope-theoretic version just as in any other. First, the account cannot straightforwardly explain why the regularities we find are the ones we do. On the Humean picture, it is a brute contingent fact that all electron-charge tropes in the mosaic exactly resemble each other and all behave electromagnetically in the same way. There is no explanation of why this regularity holds rather than some other — it just does, as a feature of how the tropes happen to be arranged. Anti-Humeans find this deeply unsatisfying. Second, counterfactuals are difficult to ground. Humean laws are patterns in the actual mosaic; it is not immediately clear why they should support claims about what would happen in non-actual situations. Lewis grounds counterfactuals via possible-worlds semantics, but this requires modal realism, adding significant ontological commitments and making the theory less spare than it initially appeared. Third, inductive inference faces its familiar challenge: the Humean can describe observed trope-patterns, but inferring from those patterns to unobserved tropes requires justification that the Humean account alone does not supply.

These difficulties are not unique to the trope version of Humeanism — they face all Humean accounts of laws. The trope theorist who embraces Humeanism inherits a sophisticated set of responses to these objections developed in the broader Humean literature, while contributing the distinctive advantage of a more concrete, particular-level ontology.
