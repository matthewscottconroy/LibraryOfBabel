# Maurin on Tropes

---

What distinguishes a philosophical theory from a philosophical program? A program identifies a problem, proposes a distinctive approach, and gestures at solutions. A theory specifies commitments precisely, examines consequences systematically, and engages the objections head-on with technical rigor. D. C. Williams's 1953 paper launched trope theory as a program; Keith Campbell's *Abstract Particulars* (1990) pushed it toward a theory. Anna-Sofia Maurin's *If Tropes* (2002) advances it further still, clarifying the metaphysical commitments that distinguish trope theory from its rivals and resolving several problems that earlier accounts had left vague.

Maurin's central contribution concerns the ontological dependence of tropes on their bearers and on each other within bundles. She argues that tropes are *generically* dependent: each trope requires the existence of some object to be a property of, but not any particular object. Socrates's wisdom-trope is generically dependent on the category of substance — it must be the wisdom of *something* — but not specifically dependent on Socrates. This is different from the view that tropes are wholly independent (which would make them look like objects rather than properties) and from the view that each trope is rigidly tied to a specific bundle (which would make intra-bundle trope identity trivially parasitic on object identity, introducing circularity). Generic dependence allows tropes to be genuinely property-like — they must be instantiated — without being locked to particular instantiating objects. It also makes trope transferability at least conceptually coherent, even if the physics never permits it.

On individuation, Maurin argues against both spatiotemporal individuation (which faces trouble with quantum mechanics, where particles lack determinate locations) and haecceistic individuation (which introduces primitive individual essences or "thisnesses" as additional ontological commitments). Her preferred account invokes what she calls *primitive distinctness*: being an individual — being numerically distinct from everything else — is simply part of what it is to be a trope. There is no further explanation available, and to demand one is to misunderstand what primitive individuality amounts to. Each trope is the particular entity it is as a matter of its fundamental nature; the individuation does not derive from location, essence, or any other further feature.

## Against Armstrong

Armstrong's most focused objection to trope theory targets the resemblance account: exactly similar tropes — two electron-mass tropes — cannot be explained in their similarity by sharing a universal (since there are no universals), so either similarity is left unexplained, or it is grounded in a further relation, which must itself be a universal or another trope, leading to regress. Maurin's response is precise. Both trope theory and universals realism invoke primitives; the choice between them should be made on theoretical economy rather than intuitive preference, and on this score tropes have the advantage.

Armstrong's theory requires particulars, universals as a distinct ontological category, the instantiation relation between them, and the higher-order necessitation relation N(F,G) for laws. Maurin's trope theory requires only tropes and resemblance. The primitive resemblance among two electron-mass tropes is, moreover, at least partially explicable in terms of their qualitative natures: both tropes have the specific qualitative character of being an electron-mass trope, and this shared character is what the resemblance consists in. The explanation is not circular if we understand "having the qualitative character of electron-mass" as a description of each trope's intrinsic nature, not as a reference to a universal that both instantiate. Whether this is fully satisfying or whether it quietly reintroduces something universal-like is the central dispute, and Maurin does not claim to dissolve it — she claims only that the cost of primitive resemblance is no greater than the cost of primitive instantiation, and that tropes have a smaller accompanying ontological inventory.

## Methodology and the Grounding Frontier

Maurin's approach exemplifies the standard methodology of systematic analytic metaphysics: identify the exact commitments of a theory, articulate them precisely, trace their consequences, and compare the resulting cost-benefit profile with rival theories. The precise articulation of what it means to accept tropes as fundamental comes to four claims: (1) there are entities — numerically individual property-instances — distinct from both ordinary concrete particulars and universals; (2) these entities have specific qualitative characters but are not repeatable across numerically distinct instances; (3) concrete objects are identical with or constituted by bundles of compresent tropes; (4) resemblance among tropes is the primitive relation that replaces universals in the account of objective similarity. Each claim has a specific cost and a specific payoff, and the overall theory can be evaluated against universals realism and nominalism on each dimension.

Maurin has continued to develop trope theory in connection with the contemporary grounding framework. The question is whether grounding — the relation in virtue of which higher-level facts hold because of lower-level facts — is itself a trope-theoretic relation or requires additional resources. If grounding is a particular relation holding between specific facts, is it a grounding-trope? If so, what grounds the grounding-trope? Maurin's tentative response is that grounding functions as a formal or structural relation in the theoretical framework — ideology rather than ontology — analogously to how instantiation functions in Armstrong's theory. This connects trope theory to one of the most active research programs in contemporary metaphysics and represents a genuine frontier where systematic work remains to be done.
