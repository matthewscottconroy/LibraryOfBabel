# Particulars and Individuation

Here is a puzzle about identity that is easy to state and surprisingly hard to resolve. Imagine two perfect iron spheres, qualitatively identical in every respect: same size, same composition, same temperature, same history, same surroundings. Every property one has, the other has. Now: are they really two things or one? Our intuition says two — they are right there, side by side. But if objects are nothing over and above their properties, and both spheres have exactly the same properties, there is nothing to distinguish them. They should be one and the same object.

This is Max Black's famous thought experiment, and it forces a choice between two fundamental positions. The first is that objects are individuated by something beyond their qualitative properties — a bare particular, a substratum, a primitive haecceity or "thisness" that makes each object this one rather than that one independently of any properties it may have. The second is that Leibniz's Principle of Identity of Indiscernibles is correct: if two things share all their properties, they are one thing, not two. If so, then Black's universe of identical spheres is simply impossible, and what our intuitions are tracking is not two objects but our tendency to think of objects from an external spatial perspective that presupposes further individuating facts.

The problem of individuation is the problem of what grounds the numerical distinctness of particulars. It connects to the bundle theory of objects (which must explain how two objects can be qualitatively identical if objects just are bundles of properties), to the substratum theory (which posits something extra to do the individuating), and to contemporary debates about haecceitism and transworld identity (what makes a given individual the same individual across possible worlds).

This chapter examines bare particulars, the Leibnizian principle, and qualitativist alternatives with the care they deserve. The question at stake is fundamental: are individual things irreducibly individual — is there something about each thing that makes it *this* thing beyond anything qualitative — or is the world entirely qualitative "all the way down," with individuality being a consequence of qualitative difference?
