# What Are Bare Particulars?

---

There is a tension in our ordinary talk about things that becomes visible once we press on it. When we say "this ball is red and round," we seem to be saying something about a subject — the ball — that *has* these properties. The subject is not identical with its redness, nor with its roundness, nor with any combination of them: if the ball is repainted it remains the same ball but loses its redness, so the ball cannot just be its redness. Something underlies and persists through the properties. But if we strip away the properties one by one — not red, not round, not massive, not located here — what remains? Locke called the residue "something, I know not what." Bergmann called it the bare particular.

The bare particular is the substratum of an object: the thing that has properties without itself being any of them. It supplies what properties alone cannot — numerical individuality. Two electrons can share all their properties: same mass, same charge, same spin. The principle of the identity of indiscernibles says that things with identical properties are identical, but we believe the two electrons are numerically distinct. The bare particular is the device that grounds their distinctness: each electron has its own numerically distinct bare particular, making them two even when all their qualitative features coincide.

"Bare" requires careful interpretation. The bare particular does not exist stripped of properties in the way a naked person exists stripped of clothes — it is not observable in a property-free state. "Bare" means that, considered in abstraction from the properties it instantiates, the substratum has no qualitative character of its own. It has what Bergmann called the formal property of exemplifying properties — a capacity, not a quality. Two bare particulars are intrinsically indistinguishable; they differ only numerically, not qualitatively. This numerical distinctness without qualitative basis is precisely what the bare particular is designed to supply.

Bergmann's ontological framework made the structure explicit: an ordinary object like a white sphere is an ontological complex consisting of universals (WHITE, SPHERE), a bare particular a, and the exemplification nexus connecting them. The bare particular a is the "thin particular" — as thin as possible, character-free — in contrast to the "thick particular," the full ordinary object that results from combining the thin particular with its universals. Armstrong later introduced his own version of the thin particular within his states-of-affairs ontology, though he insisted that his thin particular is not the same as Bergmann's bare substratum: for Armstrong, the thin particular is simply the particular aspect of a state of affairs, not an independently existing entity that could in principle float free of universals.

## Two Alternatives

Both haecceities and tropes have been proposed as alternatives to bare particulars that solve the individuation problem without positing characterless substrata. Haecceities are primitive non-qualitative properties — being Socrates, being this electron — that belong exclusively to one individual and ground its identity across possible worlds. They are not characterless entities but properties, though qualitatively empty ones. The difference from bare particulars is significant: bare particulars are substrata that "support" properties; haecceities are themselves properties (of an extreme and non-qualitative kind). Whether this distinction is sustainable — whether a qualitatively empty property is coherent — is contested.

Tropes take a different approach. Instead of positing a bare particular to individuate two otherwise identical electrons, tropes multiply the entities at the property level: each electron has its own numerically distinct mass-trope, its own numerically distinct charge-trope, even if both tropes are qualitatively identical to their counterparts in the other electron. The electrons are distinct because their tropes are distinct. This avoids bare particulars by locating primitive individuality in the property-instances rather than in a separate substratum. The cost is that trope individuation must itself be explained — and the trope theorist ultimately accepts primitive numerical distinctness of tropes in a way structurally parallel to the bare particulars theorist's acceptance of numerically distinct substrata. Both move the problem rather than dissolving it.
