# Arguments for Bare Particulars

---

The positive case for bare particulars rests on four considerations that converge on the same conclusion: properties alone cannot do everything we need a complete metaphysical account of objects to do.

The *individuation argument* is the most direct. Consider two electrons in a helium atom, occupying the 1s orbital. They have the same mass, the same charge, the same spin magnitude. In quantum mechanics, both electrons have the same spatial probability distribution — the same orbital. If their properties are all identical, the Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles (∀F(Fa ↔ Fb) → a = b) would require that they are the same electron. But the helium atom has two electrons, not one. Something beyond their qualitative profile must ground their numerical distinctness. Bare particulars provide exactly this: each electron has its own numerically distinct substratum, making them two even when their qualitative features coincide. No qualitative difference is required; the distinctness is primitive, grounded in the bare particulars themselves.

The *subject of predication argument* presses on the logic of predication. When we say "Socrates is wise," we have a subject and a predicate. If Socrates just *were* his bundle of properties, then attributing wisdom to Socrates would be a form of saying that the bundle containing wisdom contains wisdom — a tautology that trivializes predication. Genuine predication requires a subject that is distinct from its predicates, one to which predicates are genuinely attributed. The bare particular is this subject: the thing that has the predicates without being any of them.

The *persistence argument* concerns change over time. An apple is green at t₁ and red at t₂. If the apple were identical with its property bundle, the t₁-bundle (containing greenness) and the t₂-bundle (containing redness) would be numerically distinct — hence numerically distinct objects. But we want to say it is the *same* apple that changed color. A bare particular persists through the change: it is the same substratum at t₁ and t₂, even though the properties it instantiates have changed. The property-bundle changes; the subject endures.

The *unity argument* addresses what holds an object's properties together as one thing. The table's brownness and its hardness and its mass are all properties of one table, not free-floating universals. This unity requires a subject that owns them — a "locus" to which they all belong. The bare particular is this locus: it is what makes many properties the properties of one thing.

## Objections and Responses

The most serious objections to bare particulars target their characterlessness. The characterlessness objection runs as a dilemma: either bare particulars have some intrinsic character, or they do not. If they have character, they are not truly bare. If they have no character, a characterless entity seems indistinguishable from nothing — or from any other characterless entity, which re-raises the individuation problem at the level of bare particulars themselves. What makes bare particular₁ distinct from bare particular₂, if both are characterless? Defenders respond: their distinctness is primitive, exactly as claimed. But this is precisely what needed explaining.

Armstrong's response distinguishes a *thin* from a *bare* particular. His thin particular is not an entity with independent existence but the particular aspect of a state of affairs, analytically distinguishable from the universal aspect without being genuinely separable from it. The thin particular is always found combined with universals in states of affairs; it has no free-floating existence. This avoids the characterlessness objection while still providing numerical individuality. But Bergmann's defenders would say that Armstrong has not solved the individuation problem so much as redescribed it: calling the numerically individuating aspect of a state of affairs a "thin particular" names the phenomenon without explaining it.

The bundle theorist's response to the persistence argument is to adopt four-dimensionalism: the apple is a four-dimensional entity extended through time just as it is extended through space. The t₁-temporal-part and the t₂-temporal-part are numerically distinct (like the left and right halves of the apple), but both are parts of the same four-dimensional apple. Persistence through change is spatial-analogue variation across temporal extent, not endurance of a substratum. This avoids the bare particular while accommodating persistence, but at the cost of accepting temporal parts and four-dimensionalism as further theoretical commitments.

The overall situation is that each argument for bare particulars identifies a genuine explanatory need — individuation, predication, persistence, unity — but each alternative account of these phenomena raises further questions. The debate is not about whether there is a genuine phenomenon to be explained but about the best explanation. Whether bare particulars genuinely explain individuation or merely relocate the problem is the central question.
