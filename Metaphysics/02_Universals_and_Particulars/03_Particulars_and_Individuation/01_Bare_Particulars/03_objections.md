# Objections to Bare Particulars

---

The objections to bare particulars are powerful, and they have led most contemporary philosophers either to reject substrata entirely or to offer deflationary reinterpretations that drain the view of its original metaphysical content.

The *characterlessness objection* is the deepest. A bare particular, stripped of all properties, seems to be literally nothing — or at most a logician's placeholder. Existence without any qualitative character seems incoherent: for something to exist is for it to be *some way*, some kind of thing. A thing that is no way at all seems indistinguishable from the absence of a thing. Defenders reply that bare particulars have the formal property of instantiating properties, which is all the character they need. But this response generates a dilemma. Either the formal property of instantiation-capacity is a genuine property, or it is not. If it is genuine, bare particulars do have an intrinsic character — they are not truly bare — and this property must itself be a universal that the bare particular instantiates, requiring a further exemplification relation, and so on. If instantiation-capacity is not a genuine property, then the defender has offered bare particulars no character at all, and the original objection stands.

The dilemma can be sharpened. Consider two bare particulars. By hypothesis, both are characterized by nothing. What makes them two rather than one? Not any qualitative difference (there is none). Their distinctness must be primitive and numerical — which is exactly what bare particulars were invoked to explain. The posit has not solved the individuation problem; it has relocated it from concrete objects to bare particulars. We now face the same question one level down: what individuates two bare particulars? If the answer is "primitive distinctness," we could have said the same thing about concrete objects in the first place and not invoked bare particulars at all.

The *unknowability objection* adds an epistemological dimension. If bare particulars have no qualitative character, they leave no qualitative traces. Our epistemic contact with the world proceeds through qualitative features — colors, shapes, charges, masses. A characterless substratum is in principle undetectable and unknowable by any empirical means. Locke acknowledged this honestly: the substratum is "something, I know not what." But unknowability is a significant cost for a posit that is supposed to be fundamental. We can never have evidence for or against the existence of bare particulars, which raises the question of what theoretical work they are genuinely doing.

## The Regress Problem and Sider's Deflation

The *multiple exemplification problem* threatens a regress. If a bare particular is to unify an object's properties, it must exemplify each of them. But exemplification is itself a relation. What connects the bare particular to the exemplification relation? If a further relation is needed to connect the bare particular to exemplification, that relation itself needs to be connected to the bare particular, and so on. This is a form of Bradley's regress applied to the exemplification nexus.

Bergmann's response was to treat exemplification as a formal nexus — an ontological connection that is not itself a relation requiring further grounding. This parallels Armstrong's treatment of instantiation as a formal entity. But introducing formal entities with a special ontological status alongside the substantive entities (universals and particulars) complicates the framework without obviously resolving the regress.

Ted Sider's deflationary interpretation offers a different approach. On Sider's reading, "bare particular" just names the individuality that is a primitive feature of any concrete fact — the *particular* aspect of a state of affairs. This avoids the mysterious free-floating substratum of Bergmannian ontology: bare particulars are not entities over and above facts but abstractions from facts, the particular dimension of any concrete state of affairs. The cost is that this deflationary interpretation drains the bare particular theory of its metaphysical ambition. If "bare particular" just means "the primitive numerically individuating aspect of a fact," it is unclear that this is a substantive metaphysical position rather than a label for the phenomenon to be explained.

Armstrong's *thin particular* is similarly deflationary. For Armstrong, the thin particular is the concrete individual taken in abstraction from the universals it instantiates within a state of affairs. It exists only within states of affairs, always combined with universals, never free-floating. This is a significant departure from Bergmann's picture — in which bare particulars are genuine independent entities that could in principle exist without properties — and amounts to a concession that the characterlessness objection is partially correct. The defender of bare particulars must decide whether to follow Bergmann's original conception and accept the full metaphysical weight of a characterless substratum, or to follow Sider and Armstrong's deflationary readings and accept a thinner conception that may not be distinguishable from simply accepting primitive numerical individuality as a brute fact.
