# Primitive Thisness

---

Could there be a world exactly like the actual world — same qualitative distribution of properties, same laws, same history — but with Humphrey and Nixon switched? In this mirror world, Humphrey has exactly the properties that Nixon actually has, and Nixon has exactly the properties that Humphrey actually has. Every physical and qualitative fact about the world is the same; the only difference is *which individuals* play which roles. The haecceitist says yes, there could be such a world. The anti-haecceitist says no: a world qualitatively identical to ours just is our world, and there is nothing further — no primitive individual identity — for the "switch" to consist in.

The haecceitist's affirmative answer requires a distinctive kind of property: a *haecceity* or *primitive thisness*, a property that belongs exclusively to a single individual and is not constituted by any qualitative features of that individual. The haecceity of Humphrey is *being Humphrey* or *being identical with Humphrey* — a property that only Humphrey can have, not in virtue of his height, his personality, his political career, but in virtue of his sheer numerical identity. Robert Merrihew Adams introduced "thisness" into contemporary philosophical discussion to contrast with "suchness" — qualitative properties that could in principle be shared by multiple objects. A thisness picks out an individual as *this* one, without describing it as the one with such-and-such qualitative character.

The motivation for haecceities comes from cases where qualitative individuation seems insufficient. Duns Scotus's original argument was that Socrates and Plato share the universal nature HUMANITY, and their qualitative differences (height, intelligence, physical appearance) are themselves qualitative properties that could in principle be distributed differently. What makes Socrates numerically distinct from Plato — even setting aside all qualitative differences — must be something non-qualitative: a *haecceitas*, an individual principle of numerical distinctness that is essentially tied to that individual. The haecceity is not a universal; it cannot belong to any individual but the one it is the haecceity of.

## Haecceities and Possible Worlds

In contemporary modal metaphysics, haecceities play a central role in the semantics of singular terms across possible worlds. What determines whether the individual picked out by "Nixon" in a possible world W is the same person as actual Nixon? The haecceitist's answer: Nixon exists in W just in case Nixon's haecceity — *being Nixon*, *being identical with Nixon* — is instantiated in W. The haecceity is a specific non-qualitative property; its instantiation picks out the same individual regardless of qualitative variation. Nixon in W need not share all of Nixon's actual properties — he could have been born in a different year, become a different kind of politician, had different beliefs — as long as it is Nixon's haecceity that is instantiated there.

The formal structure is clean. Writing h_a for the haecceity of individual a:

- h_a is a property: it can be instantiated (only by a) and can be quantified over in second-order logic.
- h_a is non-qualitative: there is no purely qualitative description D such that h_a = BEING-AN-ENTITY-SATISFYING-D.
- h_a is necessarily uniquely instantiated: necessarily, if anything instantiates h_a, it is a.
- h_a individuates across possible worlds: a exists in W iff h_a is instantiated in W.

Plantinga (*The Nature of Necessity*, 1974) defends haecceities as genuine individual essences, the properties that are essential to an individual and uniquely characterize it across all possible worlds. Individual essences are as real as qualitative properties; they are just non-qualitative rather than qualitative. This is what supports Kripke's view that proper names rigidly designate individuals across possible worlds: "Nixon" designates Nixon in every possible world, not the most qualitatively similar individual in each world.

The anti-haecceitist position, defended by Lewis, holds that there is no need for haecceities because cross-world identity should be understood through the *counterpart* relation: the individual in W "is Nixon" just in case that individual is sufficiently similar to actual Nixon in the relevant qualitative respects to count as his counterpart. Identity across worlds reduces to qualitative similarity; there are no primitive individual essences. Whether the counterpart relation is itself qualitatively grounded or whether it too involves something haecceitistic is a further question in Lewis's framework.

## The Explanatory Problem

The standard objection to haecceities mirrors the objection to bare particulars: haecceities are qualitatively empty to the point of explanatory uselessness. *Being Humphrey* tells us nothing about what Humphrey is like — it merely reports the fact that he is numerically identical with himself. Adding h_a to our ontology seems to add a label rather than an explanation. Ockham pressed this objection against Scotus: if the common nature HUMANITY and the haecceity *Socrateitas* are formally distinct, they are in principle really distinct; but a floating HUMANITY with no individual and a floating *Socrateitas* with no nature are both absurd. Better to say individuals are primitively individual, without any separable individuating principle.

Whether haecceities are genuine explanatory primitives or merely notational devices that report primitive individuality without explaining it is the central dispute. The haecceitist can reply that the same objection applies to qualitative properties: "being red" tells us nothing about *why* red things are red — it merely reports that they are. Qualitative properties are primitives too; the question is not whether there are primitives but which primitives are most theoretically useful. Haecceities earn their place by grounding the Kripkean semantics of rigid designation and by explaining the sense in which individuals could have been differently qualitatively distributed than they actually are — facts that seem real and that qualitative properties alone cannot account for.
