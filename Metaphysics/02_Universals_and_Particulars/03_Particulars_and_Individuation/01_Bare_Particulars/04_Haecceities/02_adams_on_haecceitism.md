# Adams on Haecceitism

---

The canonical contemporary statement of haecceitism is Robert Merrihew Adams's 1979 paper "Primitive Thisness and Primitive Identity." Adams argues that there are primitive facts of individual identity — facts that cannot be reduced to or explained by any qualitative facts — and that these facts have fundamental consequences for how we understand possible worlds, modal claims, and the metaphysics of persons.

Adams's central argument proceeds by example. Consider a possible world qualitatively identical to the actual world in every respect — same distribution of properties, same laws, same history from start to finish — except that Humphrey and Nixon have been switched: in this world, Humphrey has exactly the qualitative profile that Nixon actually has, and Nixon has exactly the qualitative profile that Humphrey actually has. This world is qualitatively indistinguishable from the actual world. But Adams claims it is a *different* possible world, because different individuals fill the qualitative roles. The difference between the worlds is not a qualitative difference but a haecceitistic one: a primitive fact about which individual is which.

If Adams is right, then individual identity is not determined by qualitative facts — there is no qualitative description that picks out Humphrey, no matter how detailed, because there is always a possible world qualitatively identical to ours in which someone else has exactly Humphrey's qualitative profile. This is haecceitism: individual identity is primitive and irreducible. It is also required, Adams argues, for Kripke's claim that proper names are rigid designators. When we say "Nixon could have been a Democrat," we are making a claim about Nixon himself, not merely about whoever plays Nixon's qualitative role. For "Nixon" to designate the same individual across all possible worlds, there must be a non-qualitative property — BEING-NIXON — that Nixon has in every world he exists in. Haecceities provide this.

## Adams's Arguments and Their Reception

Adams distinguishes a moderate from an extreme haecceitism. The extreme haecceitist holds that any individual's haecceity could in principle be instantiated in any qualitative world — Humphrey's haecceity might be instantiated in a world that is utterly qualitatively different from the actual world, in a being that shares nothing of Humphrey's actual qualitative character. Adams accepts the existence of haecceities as actually instantiated but does not commit to unactualized haecceities. His more moderate claim is that there are primitive facts of individual identity in the actual world, and that modal claims about actual individuals (Humphrey could have been a shoemaker) are grounded in the haecceities of actually existing individuals.

This moderation is strategically important. The most damaging objection to extreme haecceitism is that it generates an uncountable infinity of merely possible individuals, each with its own haecceity, none of which ever exists. Adams's moderate haecceitism avoids this inflation while preserving the core claim that qualitative facts do not exhaust individual facts.

Lewis's counterpart theory is the most systematic anti-haecceitist response. Lewis holds that individuals exist only in one possible world; there is no trans-world identity. "Nixon could have been a Democrat" is analyzed as: there is a possible world W containing a *counterpart* of Nixon — a person in W who resembles Nixon in the relevant respects — who is a Democrat in W. The counterpart relation is qualitative; it is grounded in qualitative similarity, not in primitive individual essence. This is anti-haecceitism because there is no fact about which individual in another world "is" Nixon: there are only more or less similar individuals, related to Nixon by degrees of qualitative resemblance.

Adams's most pointed objection to counterpart theory is that it changes the subject. When I ask whether Nixon himself could have been a Democrat, I am asking about Nixon — whether he could have been different. Counterpart theory answers a different question: whether there is a possible world with a Nixon-like person who is a Democrat. These are not the same question. The haecceitist insists that we have genuine de re modal intuitions about specific individuals, and those intuitions resist translation into claims about counterparts without loss of meaning.

Whether this objection is decisive depends on whether one accepts that modal intuitions are more reliable guides to metaphysical necessity than the theoretical economy of counterpart theory. This is partly a question about the methodology of metaphysics — whether to revise intuitions in light of theory, or to revise theories in light of intuitions — that goes beyond the specific haecceitism debate.
