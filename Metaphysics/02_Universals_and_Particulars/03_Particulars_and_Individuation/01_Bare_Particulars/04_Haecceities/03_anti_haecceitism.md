# Anti-Haecceitism

---

Anti-haecceitism holds that there are no primitive facts about individual identity beyond qualitative facts. If two possible worlds are qualitatively identical — same distribution of properties, same relational structure, same laws — they are the same world. There is no further fact about which individuals play which qualitative roles, because individual identity just is a matter of qualitative profile. The question "but which individual is which?" when asked about qualitatively identical worlds, is not a deep metaphysical question awaiting a difficult answer: it is a question that admits of no answer because it is malformed.

The most systematic defense of anti-haecceitism is Lewis's counterpart theory, developed in his 1968 paper "Counterpart Theory and Quantified Modal Logic" and elaborated throughout his later work. Lewis holds that each individual exists only in one possible world — there is no trans-world identity — and that modal claims about individuals are analyzed through the counterpart relation. To say "Humphrey could have been a shoemaker" is to say there is a possible world W containing a counterpart of Humphrey — a person in W who resembles Humphrey sufficiently in the relevant respects — who is a shoemaker in W. The counterpart relation is qualitative: what makes someone Humphrey's counterpart is their qualitative similarity to Humphrey, not any primitive individual essence.

The systematic argument for anti-haecceitism rests on qualitative supervenience: all facts about the world — all truths — supervene on qualitative facts, facts about which objects have which intrinsic and relational properties.

- P1. All facts supervene on qualitative facts.
- P2. If haecceities were real, there would be non-qualitative individual facts not determined by any qualitative facts.
- P3. Therefore, haecceities are not real.

Haecceitists deny P1 by citing the switched-worlds thought experiment as a direct counterexample. The debate therefore turns on whether the switched-worlds intuition — the sense that a world with Humphrey and Nixon qualitatively switched is genuinely distinct from the actual world — is more credible than the supervenience thesis. Intuition versus parsimony.

## Counterpart Theory in Detail

Lewis's counterpart theory provides the most fully developed anti-haecceitist framework. The counterpart relation c_w(x) is defined as the set of individuals y in world W who resemble x sufficiently closely in the relevant respects. De re modal claims are then analyzed as:

- "Humphrey could have been a shoemaker" = There is a world W and a counterpart y of Humphrey in W such that y is a shoemaker in W.
- "Nixon is essentially human" = Every world W and every counterpart y of Nixon in W: y is human in W.

The context-sensitivity of the counterpart relation explains the flexibility of modal talk. Different modal contexts invoke different counterpart relations, which is why we can coherently say both "Humphrey could have been very different" (using a generous counterpart relation that requires only general similarity) and "Humphrey could not have been a robot" (using a specific counterpart relation that requires biological similarity). This context-sensitivity is a feature rather than a bug: it explains why modal claims about the same individual can vary in strength depending on what respects of similarity are salient.

Anti-haecceitism has consequences that haecceitists find counterintuitive. If personal identity reduces to qualitative facts — psychological and physical continuity — then in fission cases, where my brain is divided and each half transplanted into a different body, there may be no further fact about which resulting person "is really me." Both are qualitative continuants of me. The haecceitist preserves the common-sense intuition that there is always a fact of the matter (even if we cannot know it in difficult cases). The anti-haecceitist says the question has no answer in fission cases, because qualitative continuity branches and there is no primitive individual essence to determine a unique answer. This revisionary implication is a cost — whether it is acceptable depends on how much weight one gives to the pre-theoretic intuition that there is always a fact about personal identity.

Whether anti-haecceitism ultimately succeeds depends on whether Lewis's counterpart-theoretic translations of modal claims adequately capture our pre-theoretic commitments, or whether they systematically change the subject. Adams's objection — that counterpart theory makes modal claims about counterparts rather than about individuals themselves — is powerful and not definitively answered by any known response. The debate remains alive precisely because both sides have genuine philosophical resources and genuine costs.
