# Identity of Indiscernibles

Leibniz's principle — that no two distinct things can share all their properties — is one of the most elegant and controversial principles in metaphysics. If two things are truly identical in every qualitative respect, Leibniz argued, they would be not two things but one. The principle has an immediate appeal: if there is truly no difference between x and y — no property that one has and the other lacks — then what could the numerical difference consist in? What makes x and y two rather than one, if not some qualitative difference between them?

The principle is deceptively simple to state but extraordinarily difficult to evaluate. The first difficulty is modal: is it necessarily true, or only contingently true (if true at all), that distinct things are discernible? If it is necessarily true, then there cannot even be two perfectly similar peas in a possible world — the very idea is incoherent. If it is only contingently true, then there are possible worlds where distinct things share all their properties, and the principle, while true in the actual world, has no deep metaphysical significance.

The second difficulty is empirical. Max Black's famous thought experiment describes a symmetric universe containing nothing but two qualitatively identical iron spheres. Leibniz's principle predicts that this is impossible — the two spheres, being qualitatively identical, would have to be numerically identical, and hence be one sphere. But the scenario seems clearly coherent. Most philosophers take Black's example as a refutation of the unrestricted version of Leibniz's principle, requiring at most that distinct things be discernible by relational properties (their spatial relations to each other) rather than purely intrinsic ones.

Quantum mechanics adds another dimension to the debate. The statistics of identical quantum particles — bosons and fermions — suggest that quantum particles of the same type are not merely qualitatively indiscernible but lack individual identities in a sense more radical than classical physics countenances. Whether this is a violation of the identity of indiscernibles or a reinterpretation of what counts as "discernible" in a quantum context is a live debate in the philosophy of physics.

This section examines Leibniz's principle, its philosophical motivations, the leading counterexamples, and its implications for the metaphysics of particularity and individuation.
