# The Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles

---

If no two things can differ without differing in some qualitative respect, then the world is qualitatively "transparent" — every numerical distinction is grounded in a qualitative distinction, and there are no bare facts of numerical identity unmoored from qualitative character. Leibniz believed this transparency was guaranteed by the principle of sufficient reason: if two things were qualitatively identical, there would be no reason why *this* one is here and *that* one is there rather than the reverse. The principle of sufficient reason demands that every fact have a ground, and numerical distinctness without qualitative difference would be a groundless fact. Hence: for any two numerically distinct things, there must be some qualitative difference between them.

This is the Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles (PII): if two things share all their properties, they are numerically identical. Or, contraposing: no two numerically distinct things are qualitatively indistinguishable. The principle is typically paired with the Indiscernibility of Identicals (II): if a = b, then everything true of a is true of b. II is trivially true and universally accepted — the very same thing has all its properties necessarily. PII is the controversial converse: if ∀F(Fa ↔ Fb), then a = b.

The philosophical content of PII depends heavily on which properties are included in its scope. This matters because several distinct versions of the principle yield different consequences.

*PII-1* includes all properties whatsoever, including haecceitistic ones such as being identical with a or being identical with b. On this version, any two distinct things trivially differ in haecceitistic properties, so PII-1 is trivially true. This tells us nothing metaphysically interesting.

*PII-2* restricts to qualitative properties, including relational ones. Any two distinct things differ in some qualitative relational property: one might be closer to a tree than the other, or located on the left rather than the right. This version is substantive and plausible in many ordinary contexts.

*PII-3* restricts to purely intrinsic qualitative properties, setting aside all relational properties. This is the strongest and most contested version: it says there are no two distinct things that are intrinsically qualitatively identical — every numerical distinction is grounded in an intrinsic qualitative difference. Black's two-sphere thought experiment targets precisely this version.

## The Argument Structure and Its Dependencies

Leibniz's argument for PII via sufficient reason:

- P1. Every fact has a sufficient reason — there is always an explanation of why things are as they are.
- P2. If two numerically distinct things were qualitatively identical, there would be no sufficient reason why this one is here and that one is there.
- P3. Therefore, there can be no qualitatively identical but numerically distinct things.

This argument is only as strong as P1, the principle of sufficient reason, which is far from universally accepted. Philosophers who find brute facts acceptable — who think some facts simply have no further explanation — are not moved by the argument. Contemporary defenders of PII tend instead to appeal to theoretical parsimony: positing numerical distinctness without qualitative difference introduces numerical facts that do no explanatory work and are ontologically costly. This is a weaker argument, since it invites the response that parsimony is a methodological preference, not a metaphysical guarantee.

PII connects importantly to the bundle theory of objects. Bundle theory — the view that concrete objects are nothing over and above bundles of co-instantiated properties — requires PII for its coherence. If two distinct objects had exactly the same properties, they would be identical bundles and hence identical objects, contradicting the supposition that they are distinct. The bundle theorist must therefore accept that qualitatively identical but numerically distinct objects are impossible. This makes PII a consequence of bundle theory rather than an independent principle, and challenges to PII are correspondingly challenges to bundle theory.

The version of PII that is most relevant to contemporary debates is PII-2 — qualitative properties including relational ones — and the most pressing counterexamples are symmetric universes (like Black's two spheres) and quantum mechanical scenarios (like two electrons in the same orbital). These cases push the question of whether qualitative relational properties suffice to discern numerically distinct things, or whether something more — bare particulars, haecceities, or primitive numerical distinctness of tropes — is required.
