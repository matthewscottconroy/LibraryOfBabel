# Black on Two Spheres

---

Max Black's 1952 paper "The Identity of Indiscernibles" poses PII's most celebrated challenge through a single, sharply imagined scenario. Consider a universe consisting of nothing but two iron spheres, exactly alike in every qualitative respect: same size, same mass, same chemical composition, same temperature, same surface texture, everything. There is no asymmetric background — no fields, no observers, no spatial framework beyond the two spheres themselves and the distance between them. In this universe, the two spheres exist at some fixed distance from each other. They are clearly two things, not one: the sphere on the left is not the sphere on the right. But are they qualitatively distinguishable?

This is where the argument gets interesting. In a universe with nothing but two qualitatively identical spheres, each sphere has exactly the same relational profile as the other. Sphere A is one meter from an iron sphere exactly like itself; Sphere B is one meter from an iron sphere exactly like itself. Whatever relational properties we enumerate for A, the same properties hold of B, because B stands in the same relations to A that A stands in to B, and A and B are qualitatively identical. The two spheres are, intuitively, qualitatively indistinguishable — and yet there are plainly two of them.

Black's dialogue (a dialogue between a defender of PII and a skeptic) dramatizes the debate. The Leibnizian defender of PII insists that if the two spheres are genuinely indistinguishable, there can be no ground for calling them two rather than one. The skeptic replies that we can describe the scenario coherently: there is a sphere here and a sphere there. The Leibnizian presses: "here" and "there" must mark a qualitative difference if they are to do individuating work; in a symmetric universe, the spheres are equally "here" and equally "there" from their own perspectives. The skeptic maintains that the scenario is coherent regardless — we can intelligibly conceive of two distinct things that are qualitatively identical, and the conceivability establishes at least the epistemic possibility of such a world.

## Responses and Their Consequences

The response that Black's scenario is genuinely incoherent typically invokes substantivalism about spacetime: if spacetime is a genuine entity whose points are individually distinct, then the two spheres occupy different spatial points and are thereby qualitatively different — each has a location in absolute space, and those locations are distinct. This saves PII by grounding individuation in absolute spatial position. But it requires accepting substantivalism, which is contested, and it saves only PII-2 (qualitative properties including relational spatial ones), not PII-3 (intrinsic qualitative properties alone).

The response based on *weak discernibility*, developed by Saunders and others, is subtler. Two objects are weakly discernible if there is a relation R such that R(a, b) but not R(a, a). Applied to Black's spheres: the relation "is one meter from" holds between the two spheres but not reflexively (no sphere is one meter from itself). This relation discerns them, and PII can be preserved if formulated as requiring only weak discernibility rather than strong discernibility (differing in a monadic property). Critics object that this relation involves implicit reference to the other sphere as an individual — it is the relation "is one meter from *this other sphere*," which is not a purely qualitative relation but an identity-involving one. Whether this objection defeats weak discernibility or whether it mistakes structural relations for identity-involving ones is genuinely unresolved.

The implications of Black's thought experiment differ systematically across the major theories of individuation. The substratum theory handles it most easily: two bare particulars ground the numerical distinctness of the spheres independently of any qualitative difference. The haecceitist handles it equally well: each sphere has its own primitive thisness, and that is what makes them two. Trope bundle theory handles it naturally: even if the two spheres' property-tropes are qualitatively identical in every respect, the tropes themselves are numerically distinct. The sphere constituted by trope-bundle₁ is not the sphere constituted by trope-bundle₂, even though every trope in the first bundle exactly resembles its counterpart in the second.

Universals bundle theory faces the most difficulty. If each sphere is nothing but a bundle of universals, and both bundles contain exactly the same universals, then the two bundles are identical sets and the two spheres are one sphere. The universals bundle theorist must either deny that Black's scenario is coherent (accepting PII as a necessary truth), or supplement bundle theory with some individuating element beyond universals — which is to say, move toward haecceities, tropes, or bare particulars. The thought experiment thus functions as a pressure test on bundle theories: which versions can accommodate the possibility of qualitatively identical but numerically distinct objects, and at what ontological cost?

## Quantum Mechanics

The quantum case makes the pressure acute. Elementary particles of the same kind — all electrons, all protons — are genuinely indistinguishable in quantum mechanics. Two electrons in the 1s orbital of helium have the same mass, charge, spin magnitude, and spatial probability distribution. Their quantum states are antisymmetric under particle exchange (the Pauli exclusion principle), but this is a fact about the composite state of the two-electron system, not a fact that distinguishes the individual electrons. Whether quantum particles genuinely violate PII or can be weakly discerned by their relations to each other (Saunders) is an active debate at the intersection of philosophy and physics, with consequences that extend far beyond the classical sphere thought experiment.
