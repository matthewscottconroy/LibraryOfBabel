# Responses and Implications

---

The responses to Black's two-sphere case fall into three families, each with its own commitments and consequences. Together, the debate about PII makes visible something that discussions of universals and tropes can sometimes obscure: the theory of individuation and the theory of properties are not independent inquiries. What one says about whether numerical distinctness requires qualitative grounding shapes, and is shaped by, what one says about the ontological structure of objects and properties.

The first family of responses denies that Black's scenario is genuinely conceivable or metaphysically possible. The denial typically appeals to substantivalism about spacetime: spacetime is a genuine entity whose points are individually distinct, and two objects occupying different spatial points thereby differ in their location-properties — each has a unique position in the absolute spatial manifold. On this view, the two spheres are qualitatively distinguishable after all: one occupies region R₁ and the other occupies region R₂, and R₁ ≠ R₂ is a qualitative difference. PII is preserved. The cost is commitment to substantivalism, and the version of PII that is saved is only PII-2 (including relational properties) rather than PII-3 (intrinsic properties only). A sphere's location is a relational feature — its position relative to spacetime — not an intrinsic qualitative feature.

The second family accepts that Black's scenario is coherent but argues that the spheres can be discerned by their relational properties. Saunders's *weak discernibility* proposal is the most developed version: two objects are weakly discernible if some relation holds between them but not reflexively. The relation "is one meter from" holds between the two spheres but not reflexively (no sphere is one meter from itself). The spheres are therefore weakly discernible, and a version of PII requiring only weak discernibility is preserved. Critics press that this relation is not purely qualitative but involves implicit reference to the other sphere as an individual: the discernibility is identity-involving rather than genuinely qualitative. Whether this objection is decisive or whether structural relations of this form are genuinely qualitative — a matter of how broadly we cast "qualitative" — is not settled.

The third family accepts that Black's scenario is coherent and that the spheres are genuinely indistinguishable, concluding that PII is false. This is the haecceitist and bare-particularist response: the spheres are numerically distinct because each has its own primitive individual identity (haecceity or bare particular), and no qualitative difference is required. The trope theorist can say essentially the same thing at the level of property-instances: the spheres' constituent tropes are numerically distinct even if qualitatively identical, and this primitive numerical distinctness of tropes grounds the numerical distinctness of the spheres without any qualitative difference.

## Implications for the Universals Debate

The response one gives to Black's case commits one to a broader theory of individuation. If PII is true, numerical identity supervenes on qualitative identity, and we need no individuating elements beyond qualitative properties. This favors bundle theory and qualitativism, and it removes any need for bare particulars or haecceities. If PII is false, some individuating element beyond qualitative properties is needed: bare particulars, haecceities, or the primitive numerical distinctness of tropes. Each theory of properties handles the Black case differently, and the pressure test is real.

The quantum mechanics extension makes the issue urgent beyond thought experiments. French and Krause argue that quantum particles may not be genuine individuals at all in the classical sense — they are "non-individuals," entities for which identity and distinctness are not well-defined in the ordinary way. The entanglement of quantum particles means that individual particles sometimes lack determinate properties, making it unclear whether there are even well-defined tropes or universals at the individual-particle level. Structural realism emerges from this pressure: what physics discovers is relational structure, not the intrinsic natures of individual objects, and the individuation debate presupposes a framework of discrete individuals that quantum mechanics may not support.

The debate about PII thus connects upward to questions about modal metaphysics (can there be qualitatively indistinguishable possible worlds?), downward to questions about physics (are quantum particles genuine individuals?), and sideways to questions about the nature of properties (do bundles of universals or bundles of tropes adequately account for concrete particular objects?). It is one of the points where the problem of universals and the problem of individuation most tightly intersect, and the responses one develops here have ramifying consequences throughout the module.
