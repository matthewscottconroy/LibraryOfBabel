# Important Terms and Ideas: Identity of Indiscernibles

**Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles (PII)**: Leibniz's principle that no two distinct entities can share all their properties. If a and b have all the same properties, then a = b. The principle is logically equivalent (contrapositively) to the Indiscernibility of Identicals.

**Indiscernibility of Identicals (Leibniz's Law)**: The uncontroversial principle that if a = b, then a and b share all their properties. This direction (from identity to shared properties) is not disputed; the converse (PII) is.

**Black's two-sphere counterexample**: Max Black's thought experiment: imagine a symmetric universe containing only two qualitatively identical iron spheres a fixed distance apart. If PII holds, they would be identical — but they are clearly two distinct things.

**Haecceities (primitive thisness)**: Properties that are primitively individual — properties of the form "being this very thing (a)" that are not reducible to any qualitative properties. Haecceities would explain individuation without violating PII's qualitative reading.

**Pure and impure properties**: Pure properties are qualitative (being red, being 5kg); impure properties involve essential reference to specific individuals (being Socrates' child). A version of PII restricted to pure properties is more defensible than one including impure properties.

**Bundle theory and PII**: Bundle theories of substance are often taken to be committed to PII: if objects are bundles of properties, then qualitatively identical objects would be bundles of the same properties and hence identical. Black's example poses a problem for bundle theories.

**Numerical vs. qualitative identity**: Numerical identity is being one and the same thing; qualitative identity is sharing all properties. Numerically distinct things may be qualitatively identical. PII claims that qualitative identity entails numerical identity.
