# Qualitativism

Is there any difference between two possible worlds that share exactly the same distribution of qualitative properties across spacetime? If I describe a world down to its last qualitative detail — every particle, every field, every event, in their full spatiotemporal arrangement — is there any further question of which possible world it is? Qualitativism answers: no. All the facts about a possible world are fixed by its qualitative facts. There are no "primitive identities" over and above qualitative character — no haecceities, no irreducible thisnesses that could distinguish otherwise qualitatively identical worlds.

The denial of qualitativism — the view that there are primitive identities, or "haecceities," that go beyond qualitative character — seems at first to be an extravagant metaphysical posit. What would it mean for there to be a further fact about which individual is which, beyond all the qualitative information? If two possible worlds are exactly alike in their qualitative structure, differing only in which individual occupies which qualitative role, what could distinguish them? It is hard to see how such "merely haecceitistic" differences could matter to anything — to physics, to logic, to experience, or to decision.

Yet qualitativism faces its own puzzles. In a world with rotational symmetry — a world consisting of a perfectly symmetric universe — there seems to be a genuine distinction between two descriptions that differ only in which of two symmetric halves contains "you." If all the qualitative facts are the same, the qualitativist must say there is no further fact about which half you are in. But this seems phenomenologically implausible: surely there is something it is like to be in one half rather than the other, even if no qualitative description captures the difference.

The debate between qualitativism and haecceitism connects to the foundations of possible worlds semantics, to the philosophy of time (is there a qualitative fact about which moment is "now"?), and to personal identity (is there a qualitative fact about which future person is "me"?). This section examines the central arguments on each side and the implications of the choice for broader metaphysical theory.
