# Bundle Theory Revisited

---

The bundle theory holds that concrete particulars are nothing over and above bundles of properties — universals or tropes — co-instantiated in a region of space and time. There is no bare particular substratum; the table just is the collection of its brownness, its hardness, its mass, its shape, all unified by their compresence. The motivation is both ontological and epistemological. Ontologically, it is more parsimonious to posit properties without a separate substratum. Epistemologically, our contact with concrete objects is always through their qualities — we perceive colors, shapes, textures — and the postulation of a characterless something-I-know-not-what beneath the qualities is a theoretical excess unsupported by experience. Hume made this case for the self: introspection finds particular perceptions, emotions, and thoughts, but never a bare self that has them. The self is the bundle of successive perceptions; there is no additional substratum.

The bundle theory is a form of qualitativism — the view that what a thing is is fully determined by its qualitative profile. This has an elegant consequence: it provides a natural account of objecthood without any new ontological categories. But it faces two characteristic challenges.

The *individuation challenge* is the most fundamental. If two objects have exactly the same qualitative properties, the bundle theory identifies them: they are bundles of the same properties, hence identical bundles, hence identical objects. Two electrons with the same mass, charge, spin, and spatial probability distribution would be, on universals bundle theory, one electron. Most philosophers find this unacceptable. The bundle theorist must either accept the Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles as a necessary truth (denying the possibility of qualitatively identical but numerically distinct objects) or supplement bundle theory with some individuating element beyond the bundled properties. Both moves carry costs.

The *unity challenge* is the other major problem: what holds an object's properties together as one thing? If the apple's redness and its roundness are two distinct properties, what makes them properties of the *same* apple rather than free-floating qualities? The compresence relation — co-location in space and time — is the standard answer, but its ontological status is unclear. If compresence is itself a universal, it must be part of the bundle, which generates a regress. If compresence is a primitive formal relation, it has been quietly added alongside the bundled properties as an additional theoretical posit.

## Universals-Bundle Theory and Its Commitments

On universals bundle theory, each concrete object is a maximal bundle of universals co-instantiated in a region. This version is committed to PII in its strong form: two distinct objects must differ in at least one universal they instantiate, because if they instantiate all the same universals, they are identical bundles. Russell at some stages defended a version of this view, and its defenders today (O'Leary-Hawthorne among others) typically accept PII as a necessary truth.

The cost is significant. PII as a necessary truth means that Black's two-sphere universe — two qualitatively identical iron spheres in an otherwise empty universe — is metaphysically impossible, not just nomologically unlikely. The intuition that two qualitatively identical things could exist is direct and persistent, and dismissing it as confused requires confidence in the bundle theory that is hard to justify independently. The universals bundle theorist has in effect argued from the theory to the rejection of a seemingly coherent thought experiment, rather than the other way around.

## Trope Bundle Theory and Individuation

Trope bundle theory avoids the PII commitment because tropes are numerically individual. The apple is a bundle of tropes — specific individual property-instances — not universals. Two apples, even if qualitatively identical in all their properties, are distinct because their constituent tropes are numerically distinct. The redness-trope of apple₁ is not the redness-trope of apple₂; the roundness-trope of apple₁ is not the roundness-trope of apple₂. They exactly resemble each other, but they are numerically distinct entities.

The distinctness of numerically distinct tropes is taken as primitive: tropes are fundamentally individual, and two qualitatively identical tropes are simply two distinct entities. This is not a violation of any explanatory principle — it is a feature of what tropes are. Trope bundle theory can coherently maintain that Black's two-sphere universe is possible: each sphere is a bundle of tropes, and the trope-bundles are numerically distinct even though qualitatively identical. This is a genuine advantage over universals bundle theory, and it is one of the central motivations for moving from universals to tropes while retaining the bundle approach to concrete objects.

The unity challenge remains for both versions of bundle theory. Various responses have been developed — set-theoretic bundling, mereological summation, mutual ontological dependence — and none is fully satisfying. The set-theoretic approach imports abstract entities; the mereological approach is too permissive (any mereological sum of tropes would constitute a concrete particular); the dependence approach requires a robust account of ontological dependence that may generate its own circularity. The unity challenge shows that the parsimony of bundle theory — avoiding substrata — comes at the cost of a serious problem about what does the work that substrata were introduced to do.
