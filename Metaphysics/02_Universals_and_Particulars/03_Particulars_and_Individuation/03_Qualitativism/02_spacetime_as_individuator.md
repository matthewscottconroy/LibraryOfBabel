# Spacetime as Individuator

---

The most natural response to the individuation problem, and one that avoids positing bare particulars or haecceities, is to individuate objects by their spatiotemporal location. Two objects can share all their intrinsic qualitative properties — same mass, same charge, same internal structure — and still be numerically distinct because they occupy different regions of space and time. Location is a relational property, a matter of where an object stands relative to the spatiotemporal framework, and it is available even for objects with identical intrinsic profiles. On this view, spatial position does the individuating work that bare particulars and haecceities were introduced to do, without the metaphysical extravagance.

The view comes in two versions depending on one's account of spacetime. On *substantivalist spatiotemporal individuation*, spacetime is a genuine entity — a four-dimensional manifold of points that exists independently of the matter distributed over it. Newton's absolute space and absolute time are the historical paradigm; Einstein's spacetime is the contemporary version. Spacetime points are genuine individuals, individually distinct by their positions in the manifold, and objects occupying different spacetime points are thereby distinct regardless of any qualitative similarity. Black's two identical spheres are numerically distinct simply because they occupy different points of the absolute spatial manifold, even in a universe with no other landmarks. On this view, PII is saved: the spheres differ in the qualitative relational property of occupying region R₁ rather than region R₂.

On *relationist spatiotemporal individuation*, spacetime is not a genuine entity in its own right — there are only material objects and the spatiotemporal relations among them. This version faces an immediate difficulty with Black's two-sphere case: in a symmetric universe with only the two spheres, each sphere stands in the same spatial relations to the other as the other stands to it. The relational spatial profile of sphere₁ is identical to that of sphere₂. Relational individuation fails for symmetric universes, which is precisely where Black's challenge is directed.

## Substantivalism and Its Difficulties

Substantivalism has attractive features for the theory of individuation, but it is not without difficulties. The hole argument, developed by Earman and Norton from Einstein's own concerns, threatens substantivalism about general relativistic spacetime. General relativity is diffeomorphism-invariant: if M is a model of general relativity, any smooth, invertible mapping φ of the spacetime manifold produces another model φ(M) that satisfies the field equations equally well. If spacetime points have primitive individual identities (primitive haecceities of their own), then M and φ(M) represent physically distinct situations — different matter distributions over different spatiotemporal points — even though they are empirically indistinguishable by any physical measurement. This generates unacceptable indeterminism: the equations of general relativity would underdetermine which physical situation obtains.

The escape route favored by many philosophers of physics is *sophisticated substantivalism*: spacetime is a genuine entity, but its points do not have primitive individual identities. They are individuated by their relational properties within the spacetime manifold — their metric relations to other points — not by any primitive haecceity. On this view, spacetime points serve as individuators for material objects while being themselves relationally individuated. Individual identity for material objects is derived from spatiotemporal location; individual identity for spacetime points is derived from their roles in the metrical structure. This avoids the hole argument's indeterminism while preserving the individuating role of spacetime.

Whether sophisticated substantivalism is genuinely distinct from relationism is contested. If spacetime points are individuated by their relational roles in the metrical structure, they begin to look like nodes in a structure defined by its relations — which is a structuralist rather than a substantivalist conception.

## Quantum Mechanics and the Limits of Spatiotemporal Individuation

Quantum mechanics challenges spatiotemporal individuation at two points. First, particles can exist in superpositions of position: before a position measurement, a particle has no determinate location. If a particle's individuality is grounded in its spatial position, and its position is genuinely indeterminate (not merely unknown), then its individuality is similarly indeterminate. This is not merely an epistemological limitation but, on standard interpretations of quantum mechanics, an objective metaphysical fact about the particle's state.

Second, the quantum statistics of identical particles — the requirement that fermionic states be antisymmetric and bosonic states be symmetric under particle exchange — suggests that identical particles in the same system do not have individual identities over and above their role in the composite state. Two electrons in the 1s orbital of helium do not, in any physically meaningful sense, have individual trajectories or positions that could serve to distinguish them. The quantum state of the system is antisymmetric under exchange, meaning that there is no physical fact about which electron is "in which state" — the electrons are entangled in a way that dissolves their individual identities.

French and Krause's conclusion — that quantum particles are "non-individuals," entities for which classical individuation categories do not apply — is one response to these challenges. Another response, pursued by Saunders, maintains that the particles can be weakly discerned by the relations they bear to each other, preserving a thin form of individuality without requiring determinate positions. A third response, pursued by structural realists, draws the radical conclusion that at the quantum level there are no genuine individuals — only structural relations — and that the individuation framework of classical ontology simply does not apply to the fundamental level of physical reality.
