# Contemporary Debates

The debates about universals, tropes, and individuation might seem remote from practical concerns — abstract disagreements that have little bearing on anything beyond technical metaphysics. That impression is mistaken. The questions raised in the preceding chapters have consequences that ramify across philosophy of science, metaontology, and even ethics. Contemporary discussions have brought the classical problems into contact with the live concerns of philosophers working at the intersection of metaphysics, logic, and science.

Three clusters of questions dominate the contemporary landscape. The first is metaontological: What exactly are we disagreeing about when we debate the existence of universals? Is there a single fact of the matter about what exists, or do different conceptual schemes carve the world differently without one being privileged? The challenge of quantifier variance — the view that there is no single, objective meaning of "there exists" — pressures the realism/nominalism dispute by suggesting that both sides might be correct relative to their own frameworks. Taking this challenge seriously requires developing a view about what makes ontological questions substantive rather than verbal.

The second cluster concerns the relationship between properties and science. Physics operates with predicates like "has charge e" and "has mass m" — but does it quantify over properties as entities in their own right? The distinction between abundant properties (every meaningful predicate corresponds to a property) and sparse properties (only the properties that play genuine explanatory roles in science are real) bears on this question. Sparse properties are the ones that underwrite resemblance, causation, and natural kinds; abundant properties are available for any semantic purpose. The choice between abundant and sparse ontologies shapes the connection between metaphysics and natural science.

The third cluster examines applications of property theory in domains beyond the physical: moral properties, aesthetic properties, mathematical properties, social kinds. If there are moral properties — properties of actions or outcomes that make them morally right or wrong — what is their ontological status? Are they natural properties, accessible to science? Or irreducible, non-natural properties, accessible only to moral intuition? The metaethical implications of the realism/nominalism debate show that the stakes are not merely theoretical.

This chapter surveys these contemporary debates with an eye toward what the classical debates about universals and particulars look like when brought into contact with current concerns. The question of what properties are has not been settled; it has been deepened and extended.
