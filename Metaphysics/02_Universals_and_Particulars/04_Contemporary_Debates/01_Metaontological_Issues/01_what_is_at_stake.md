# What Is at Stake

---

It is tempting to regard the debate about universals as an antiquarian dispute — an inherited puzzle from Plato and Aristotle that analytic philosophy has taken up mainly from professional inertia. The temptation should be resisted. The question of whether properties are universals, tropes, natural classes, or nothing but predicates-applied-to-objects bears on some of the most central questions in philosophy, and the connection is not merely tangential.

The deepest connection is to laws of nature. On Armstrong's necessitation view, the law "all copper conducts electricity" is the higher-order fact N(COPPER, CONDUCTS-ELECTRICITY) — a relation between universals that directly explains why the regularity holds necessarily rather than accidentally. Without universals, this explanation is unavailable. Lewis's Humean alternative treats laws as patterns in the distribution of particular matters of fact, identified by their role in the best systematic description of the mosaic. The choice between these two accounts determines whether laws have any modal force of their own or whether necessity is a projection of our descriptions onto a mosaic of contingent facts. This choice, in turn, ramifies into counterfactual reasoning, scientific explanation, and the foundations of physics. When we ask whether it was *necessary* that the electron had this charge, or whether it merely happens to, we are asking a question that cannot be answered without taking a position on universals.

Natural kinds are similarly at stake. Scientific classification — into electrons, gold, water, tigers — is supposed to track objective joints in nature, groupings that are not artifacts of our interests or historical contingencies. If there are universals, natural kinds are straightforwardly real: the kind ELECTRON is the universal ELECTRON-NATURE, and all electrons share this universal by instantiating it. If there are no universals, natural kinds must be grounded in something else — resemblance classes, natural classes, or trope-type resemblance — and the question of what makes a class or resemblance group a *natural* one requires either a primitive naturalness (Lewis) or an appeal to science (what our best theories identify). Neither alternative is cost-free, and the stakes for scientific realism about kinds are real.

## The Ramifications Beyond Physics

The connections run into unexpected territory. Mathematical objects — numbers, sets, functions — are typically characterized as abstract entities. If properties and relations are universals, mathematical objects can be understood as abstract universals; mathematical truths are true of them in the same way that physical truths are true of physical universals. Without universals, the platonist account of mathematics must be grounded differently, and nominalist programs for reconstructing mathematics without abstract entities face well-known difficulties (Goodman and Quine's *Nominalism*; the Field program in *Science Without Numbers*).

Moral realism connects to the universals debate in an equally direct way. If there are moral properties — GOODNESS, JUSTICE, CRUELTY — and if properties are universals, then moral claims can be straightforwardly true in virtue of objects instantiating moral universals. Without universals, moral realism must find another ground for moral objectivity, and the options are narrower. Some moral realists accept non-natural moral properties; others try to naturalize them as natural-kind properties or functional properties. Each strategy has its own relationship to the universals debate.

Philosophy of mind depends on the universals debate through the theory of mental content. Mental states — beliefs, desires, experiences — are attributed using general terms: "she believes that it will rain." What grounds the generality of mental properties? Are they universals instantiated by many minds, natural classes of mental states, or resemblance classes of mental tropes? Token-identity theory and multiple realizability sit differently on these different ontologies, and the debate about functionalism in philosophy of mind is partly a debate about which kind of property mental properties are.

These connections do not settle the universals debate by themselves, but they show why it is not merely technical. Getting the ontology of properties right matters for the foundations of science, mathematics, ethics, and mind. The debate is worth having.
