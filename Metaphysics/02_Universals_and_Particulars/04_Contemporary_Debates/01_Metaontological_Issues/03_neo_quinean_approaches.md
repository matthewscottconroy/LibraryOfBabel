# Neo-Quinean Approaches

---

Quine bequeathed analytic metaphysics a criterion: we are ontologically committed to whatever entities are quantified over in our best theories. To determine what exists, find the best total theory of the world and read off its existential commitments from its logical form. "There are tigers" commits us to tigers; "there is a prime between 7 and 11" commits us to numbers (or at least to the truth of a sentence that canonically has numbers in its domain). If we can paraphrase apparent commitments away — rewrite the theory to avoid quantifying over the entities in question without losing explanatory power — those entities are dispensable. If we cannot, they are real.

Applied to the universals debate, the neo-Quinean question is: do our best theories quantify over universals? Van Inwagen argues that they do, through the ordinary sentences we accept as true. "Courage is a virtue," "Red is a color," "Charge is a fundamental property of electrons" — in each of these, the apparent subject term ("courage," "red," "charge") functions as a singular term referring to a property. If the sentence is true and the subject term genuinely refers, properties exist. Since van Inwagen takes these sentences to be true and their logical form to require singular reference to properties, he concludes that we are ontologically committed to properties as universals — not by philosophical theorizing but by our ordinary acceptance of everyday and scientific claims.

The nominalist responses to this argument are by now familiar: paraphrase, rejection of face-value interpretation, or deflationism. "Courage is a virtue" can be paraphrased as "all courageous persons are virtuous in respect of their courage." "Red is a color" can be paraphrased as "all red things are colored." The paraphrases eliminate the apparent singular reference to properties. Van Inwagen's rejoinder is that the paraphrases lose something — the original sentence says something about courage as such, not merely about courageous persons — and that systematically treating apparent singular terms as disguised general terms requires implausibly revisionary semantics of ordinary language. The dispute about paraphrase is partly semantic and partly about which theory is more theoretically honest.

## Sider on Structure

Ted Sider's *Writing the Book of the World* (2011) advances a neo-Quinean ontology with a distinctive twist. The question is not merely which entities our theories quantify over but which terms *carve reality at its joints* — which terms in our fundamental theory track genuine metaphysical structure. Some terms track structure (electron, charge, mass), while others are merely useful tools without deep metaphysical backing (grue, being a thing touched by Julius Caesar at noon on a Tuesday). The entities that are genuinely real are those that correspond to terms carving at the joints of the fundamental description.

This is Sider's version of Lewis's naturalness, but where Lewis took naturalness as a primitive feature of properties, Sider takes joint-carving as the fundamental notion for the entire ontological enterprise. Universals that feature in fundamental physics carve at the joints and are real in the most robust sense; the "properties" of folk psychology or literary criticism do not carve at the joints and have at most a derivative or conventional status.

Applied to the universals debate: the question becomes not "do universals exist?" in the flat Quinean sense — which would be answered affirmatively by pointing to our ordinary discourse — but "are there universals that figure in the fundamental description of the world?" This is a more demanding question, one that connects the metaphysics of properties directly to the philosophy of physics. Whether the terms of fundamental physics (mass, charge, spin, color charge in QCD) carve at the joints is not something that conceptual analysis alone can determine; it requires engaging with the physics and with questions about how scientific theories represent the world.

## Schaffer on Fundamentality

Jonathan Schaffer proposes a further reorientation of the debate. The important ontological question, he argues, is not what exists — nearly everything we ordinarily accept exists, from tables and chairs to numbers and properties — but what is *fundamental*, what is the ground floor of reality. The universals debate should be reframed: not "do universals exist?" but "are universals fundamental — do they ground everything else, or are they themselves grounded in something more basic?"

Lewis's natural classes exist if classes exist, and Armstrong's universals exist if universals are real, but one framework is more fundamental than the other. The grounding structure determines which entities are bedrock and which are derived. This shift does not dissolve the universals debate but changes its focus from the flat question of existence to the hierarchical question of fundamentality — which is, arguably, what the debate was really always about. Whether the world's fundamental structure consists of universals (Armstrong), tropes (Campbell, Maurin), or a mosaic of perfectly natural intrinsic properties (Lewis) is the substantive metaphysical question, and it is not answered by pointing out that all three theories accept the existence of objects that have properties.

The neo-Quinean approaches collectively insist that the universals debate is substantive — a genuine question about the structure of reality, not a verbal dispute or a confusion between internal and external questions. They disagree about exactly what the question is: whether it is a question about the existential commitments of our theories (van Inwagen), about which terms carve at the joints (Sider), or about what is fundamental (Schaffer). Each formulation highlights different aspects of what is at stake and makes different demands on the evidence that could settle it.
