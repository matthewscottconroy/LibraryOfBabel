# Properties and Science

The problem of universals was long treated as a purely a priori discipline — a matter of conceptual analysis and logical argument, with no particular connection to empirical science. This insulation from science is increasingly untenable. The question of whether properties are universals, tropes, or something else has direct implications for the interpretation of scientific theories, the analysis of natural laws, and the understanding of what scientific explanation achieves.

Physics provides the most direct challenge. Quantum mechanics describes elementary particles in terms of properties like spin, charge, and mass — but particles of the same type are entirely interchangeable, with no intrinsic difference between one electron and another of the same type. This "quantum indiscernibility" has been interpreted as evidence that electrons lack individual identities, as support for a bundle theory in which particles are just bundles of properties, and as evidence for an ontology of fields rather than particles. The choice between these interpretations is not a purely philosophical matter; it requires engaging with the physics.

The special sciences raise different questions. Biology identifies species, genera, and other taxa — but biological taxonomy is notoriously unstable, revised by new phylogenetic methods and contested by competing concepts of species. Are species natural kinds with genuine universals? Or are they historical lineages — individuals — that do not correspond to traditional universals at all? Psychology and the social sciences identify types — mental states, social roles, economic categories — whose relationship to physical universals is deeply unclear. Multiple realizability (the fact that the same mental type can be realized by different physical configurations) seems to argue against type-identity between psychological and physical properties.

This section examines how the debate about universals and properties connects to actual scientific practice, asking which metaphysical picture best fits the properties and kinds that science identifies, and what the ontological status of scientific kinds tells us about the general theory of properties.
