# Natural Properties in Science

---

The notion of a natural property is not merely a philosopher's technical posit — it is implicit in scientific practice at every level. When physicists measure the charge of an electron, they are not merely noting that something satisfies a predicate. They are tracking a property that is conserved, that figures in causal laws, that is the same in every electron regardless of where or when the electron exists. When biologists classify organisms into species, they are attempting to track objective groupings that reflect evolutionary history, reproductive compatibility, and shared causal mechanisms — not arbitrary collections. When chemists organize the elements by atomic number, they are revealing a structure in nature that was there before anyone discovered it. Natural properties are those that figure in these genuine scientific structures: they ground objective similarity, support inductive inference, and explain why regularities hold as laws rather than accidents.

The connection to the universals debate is immediate. Whatever natural properties are, they are not arbitrary. The class of all electrons is natural; the class of all things that are either electrons or tax attorneys is not. The property of having atomic number 79 is natural; the property of being gold or being green is not (or not perfectly natural). If natural properties are universals, their naturalness flows from their being one repeated entity instantiated by all their instances. If natural properties are classes, their naturalness is Lewis's primitive — some classes just are natural, and we discover which by doing science. If natural properties are resemblance classes of tropes, their naturalness is grounded in the exact resemblance of the relevant tropes.

None of these accounts reduces the concept of naturalness to something more basic and unproblematic. The universals account takes naturalness as following from the objectivity of universals themselves; the class account takes it as a primitive feature of certain classes; the trope account builds it into the exact resemblance relation among the relevant tropes. All three invoke something at the fundamental level that explains why certain properties are natural without eliminating the notion. The debate is about which account best explains the role naturalness plays in science and metaphysics, not about whether naturalness is real.

## Scientific Essentialism and Natural Kinds

Kripke's *Naming and Necessity* (1980) and Putnam's *Mind, Language and Reality* (1975) gave philosophical expression to something that chemistry and physics seemed to already know: natural kind terms are rigid designators, picking out the same kind in all possible worlds, and natural kinds have real essences discovered empirically, not stipulated by definition. The essence of water is H₂O — a fact about the molecular structure of water, discovered by science, not a fact about the meaning of the word "water." The essence of gold is having atomic number 79. In every possible world where water exists, it is H₂O; in every possible world where gold exists, it has atomic number 79.

This scientific essentialism connects natural kind membership to deep structural properties: an element is gold in virtue of its nuclear structure, not in virtue of superficial properties like color or malleability. The natural property that grounds gold-membership is the property of having atomic number 79, and this property explains why gold has all its other characteristic properties — its malleability, its conductivity, its resistance to oxidation. The underlying structural property explains the superficial ones.

Applied to the universals debate, Ellis's scientific essentialism (*Scientific Essentialism*, 2001) argues that natural kinds have intrinsic dispositional essences — their identity is constituted by their causal powers, not by qualitative character alone. The universal ELECTRON-CHARGE is not a bare quality but a power: it essentially involves the tendency to attract positive charges, repel negative charges, and deflect in electromagnetic fields. Laws of nature express the essential natures of natural kind properties, not contingent regularities that could in principle have been different. This is a strongly anti-Humean reading of natural properties, and it connects the metaphysics of universals to the debate about dispositionalism and the necessity of laws.

## Naturalness and Scientific Methodology

The distinction between natural and gerrymandered properties has practical significance for scientific methodology. Goodman's grue paradox makes the point theoretically: "grue" (green before a specified time or blue after) is a well-defined predicate, but regularities stated in terms of grue are not inductively projectible in the way that regularities stated in terms of green are. We project "all emeralds are green" from past observations; we do not project "all emeralds are grue" even though our observations are equally compatible with both. The asymmetry tracks the naturalness of green over grue.

In scientific practice, the same asymmetry is implicit everywhere: scientists test for regularities among natural kind members (all electrons have charge -e, all gold has atomic number 79) and dismiss regularities among gerrymandered collections as coincidental. The theory of natural properties explains why some experimental strategies are fruitful — look for regularities among members of the same natural kind — and why others are not. Without a distinction between natural and gerrymandered properties, the methodology of inductive science would be arbitrary, and there would be no basis for preferring one generalization over another among the infinitely many that fit any finite data set. This connection between naturalness and the foundations of scientific inference is one of the strongest arguments for taking the metaphysics of natural properties seriously.
