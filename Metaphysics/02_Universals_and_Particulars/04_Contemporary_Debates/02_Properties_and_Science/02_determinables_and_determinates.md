# Determinables and Determinates

---

Color provides the clearest example of a hierarchically structured family of properties. Being colored is a property; being red is a more specific property; being scarlet is more specific still. Every scarlet thing is red, and every red thing is colored, but a colored thing need not be any particular color and a red thing need not be any particular shade of red. The implication runs from specific to general — from determinate to determinable — and not the other way. This is the determinable/determinate distinction: a *determinable* property is one that admits of more specific specifications (*determinates*) within a dimension of variation; a *determinate* is one such specific specification.

The logical structure of the relation is precise. For a determinable D with determinates F₁, F₂, ..., Fₙ:

- F₁(x) → D(x): being scarlet entails being red; upward entailment from determinate to determinable.
- F₁(x) → ¬F₂(x): being scarlet excludes being crimson in the same respect; downward exclusion among co-ordinates.
- D(x) → (F₁(x) ∨ F₂(x) ∨ ... ∨ Fₙ(x)): being colored requires being some specific color; covering exhaustion.

The hierarchy can iterate: SCARLET is a determinate relative to RED but a determinable relative to even more specific shades. At the bottom are perfectly determinate properties — specific spectral reflectance values, specific charge values — that admit of no more specific determinate within their dimension. Armstrong holds that only perfectly determinate properties are genuine universals; determinables like COLOR or RED are at best ranges of determinates, not genuine entities in their own right. His reasoning is sparse-realist: genuine universals ground genuine objective similarities and figure in causal laws, and determinables are too coarse-grained to do this directly — there is no causal law of the form "everything colored behaves in such-and-such way."

The objection to Armstrong's restriction is that it threatens to eliminate most of the vocabulary of the special sciences and folk psychology. PAIN, BELIEF, RED, MAMMAL — all are determinable properties that are not perfectly determinate. If only perfectly determinate physical properties are genuine universals, then the generalizations of biology, psychology, and even chemistry fail to pick out genuine properties. This seems to undermine the explanatory autonomy of higher-level sciences and to leave mental causation without a proper ontological basis.

## Determinables in Causation

Yablo's "Mental Causation" (1992) provides an influential account of how determinable properties can be causally relevant without generating overdetermination with their determinates. The setup: pain (a determinable) and C-fiber firing (a specific physical determinate that realizes pain) both seem to cause avoidance behavior. Are they redundant? Does one pre-empt the other?

Yablo argues they do not overdetermine the effect because they stand in the determinable/determinate relation rather than being two independent causes. The determinate (C-fiber firing) and the determinable (pain) are hierarchically related — one implies the other — and causation by both is not two independent causal contributions but a single causal contribution described at two levels of specificity. The determinable cause specifies *what kind of thing* caused the effect; the determinate cause specifies *precisely which* instantiation of that kind was operative. Neither is redundant because each captures something the other does not: the determinable captures the general causal pattern (pain causes avoidance), while the determinate captures the specific physical realization operative in this case.

This account defends the causal relevance of determinable mental properties within a framework that accepts the causal closure of the physical. The defense depends on the determinable/determinate relation being a genuine ontological relation — not merely a linguistic hierarchy but a real structural feature of the property landscape. If determinables are genuine properties, and if the determinable/determinate relation is genuinely distinct from the species/genus relation and from mere set-theoretic containment, then Yablo's account goes through. If determinables are merely convenient ways of grouping determinates (Armstrong's view), the account may not have the right ontological basis.

The determinables/determinates hierarchy also appears in ethics: GOOD may be a determinable; JUST, BENEFICENT, and COURAGEOUS are its determinates. Debates about whether moral properties are genuine or merely nominal often track debates about whether determinables in general are genuine. If only perfectly determinate natural-scientific properties are real, moral properties — which are determinables without a perfectly determinate physical correlate — fail to be real properties at all. Moral realists who want to acknowledge the reality of moral properties without identifying them with physical properties must therefore resist Armstrong's restriction to perfectly determinate universals, or find an alternative ontological account of determinable properties.
