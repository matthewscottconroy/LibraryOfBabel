# Higher-Order Properties

---

Some properties are had by objects, but some properties are had by properties. Being a color is a property of redness. Being fundamental is a property of mass and charge. Standing in the necessitation relation N to CONDUCTS-ELECTRICITY is a property that COPPER has. These are higher-order properties: properties of first-order properties rather than of ordinary concrete things. Their existence and ontological status are presupposed throughout this module — naturalness, fundamentality, supervenience, necessitation are all higher-order — and it is worth examining them directly.

The most important instance in the present context is Armstrong's necessitation relation. The law "all copper conducts electricity" is, on Armstrong's account, the second-order state of affairs N(COPPER, CONDUCTS-ELECTRICITY): the first-order universals COPPER and CONDUCTS-ELECTRICITY stand in the second-order universal N. This higher-order entity — the necessitation relation — is what distinguishes the law from an accidental generalization. Without it, the regularity would be a brute coincidence; with it, the regularity is necessitated. N is thus a higher-order universal, a property not of particular objects but of pairs of first-order universals.

Van Fraassen's *inference problem* targets exactly this structure. Even if N(COPPER, CONDUCTS-ELECTRICITY) is a fact, why does it follow that each particular piece of copper conducts? The second-order fact is a fact about universals, not about copper pieces; the law is supposed to govern particular instances; and it is not obvious how a fact about universals entails anything about particulars. Armstrong's response is that this entailment is analytic of what "necessitation" means: N is defined as the relation such that N(F, G) entails ∀x(Fx → Gx). But this response threatens to make the necessitation relation too thin to do real explanatory work — if N's entailing the regularity is definitional, what does invoking N actually explain?

## Higher-Order Logic and Ontological Commitment

The formal setting for higher-order properties is second-order logic (SOL), which allows quantification over predicates and properties in addition to individuals. Where first-order logic (FOL) permits ∃x(Copper(x) ∧ Conducts(x)), second-order logic permits ∀F ∀G (N(F, G) → ∀x(F(x) → G(x))), quantifying over the properties F and G. Armstrong's necessitation principle written formally: N(F, G) → ∀x(F(x) → G(x)).

The ontological stakes of this quantification are real. SOL quantification over properties carries commitments to properties as entities, which is why Quine and Field resist second-order logic and insist on working in first-order logic for foundational purposes. The price of SOL's expressive power is a richer ontology. Many important mathematical truths — categorical characterizations of arithmetic, for instance — are expressible in SOL but not in FOL, which is one source of pressure toward accepting properties as genuine entities rather than mere linguistic conveniences.

Whether one takes higher-order quantification to commit to higher-order entities depends in part on one's broader view about whether properties are real. The abundant realist accepts the quantificational commitments at face value; the nominalist will seek first-order paraphrases; the sparse realist will accept higher-order commitment only for properties that carve at the joints.

## Functionalism and Second-Order Mental Properties

Higher-order functionalism in philosophy of mind provides perhaps the most practically important application of higher-order properties. Mental properties on this view are second-order: PAIN is the property of having some first-order physical property P such that P is caused by tissue damage and causes avoidance behavior. The mental property is defined by its causal role, and the physical property that plays that role is the realizer. This makes PAIN a higher-order property — a property of properties — and accounts for multiple realizability: different first-order physical properties can play the pain-role in different organisms, so PAIN can be instantiated by different physical substrates.

The advantages are significant: multiple realizability is accommodated, mental causation is preserved (mental properties are defined by causal roles, so they cause things), and supervenience on the physical is secured (the mental role is defined in terms of physical causes and effects). The difficulties are equally significant. The phenomenal character of pain — what it is like to be in pain — does not seem to be captured by the causal role definition. One can conceive of a system with all the right causal connections (responding to tissue damage, generating avoidance behavior) but no phenomenal experience. If so, the phenomenal character is not a higher-order functional property but something additional. The interaction between higher-order functionalism and the hard problem of consciousness is one of the deepest problems at the intersection of metaphysics and philosophy of mind, and it connects the abstract debate about higher-order properties to the most pressing questions about the nature of experience.
