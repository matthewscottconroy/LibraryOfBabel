# Exercises — Properties and Science

*Problems for engaging with naturalness, determinables, and scientific classification.*

---

**Exercise 1 — Naturalness in Biology**
Is BEING-A-TIGER a natural property? If the natural properties are those of fundamental physics (mass, charge, spin), then macro-biological properties seem not to be natural. But tigers form a genuinely coherent kind. How should we understand the naturalness of biological kinds? Does the answer depend on whether species are universals, kinds, or individuals?

**Exercise 2 — Determinables and Mental Causation**
Pain is a determinable property (realized by different physical properties in different creatures). Kim argues that the causal work is done entirely by the physical determinates, leaving the mental determinable causally redundant. Yablo responds that determinables can be causally relevant even if determinates do the "fine-grained" causal work. Explain both positions and assess which is more compelling.

**Exercise 3 — Higher-Order Properties**
Armstrong uses the second-order universal NECESSITATION to ground laws of nature: N(F, G) means F necessitates G. Van Fraassen objects: what is necessitation? If it is a contingent relation between universals, then laws are contingent — but that is what we wanted to avoid by positing universals. If it is a necessary relation, we need an explanation of why it holds necessarily. Assess this objection.

**Exercise 4 — Scientific Essentialism**
Ellis argues that fundamental physical properties (like charge) have essential dispositional natures — they are necessarily the powers they are, and laws of nature are therefore necessary rather than contingent. Lewis argues that the same categorical properties could have played different causal roles in different possible worlds. What would it take to settle this dispute empirically? Is it empirically settleable?
