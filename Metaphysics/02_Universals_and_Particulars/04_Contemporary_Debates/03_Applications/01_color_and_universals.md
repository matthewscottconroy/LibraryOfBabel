# Color and Universals

---

Red things look red. But what exactly is the red they share? Is redness a universal — a single entity wholly present in each red tomato, each red rose, each red bus? Is it a natural class — the set of all red things, natural in Lewis's primitive sense? Is it a resemblance class of red tropes — each red thing's individual redness, similar to every other such trope? Or is redness not really in the things at all but in the experiences they cause, a feature of how they appear to us rather than how they are?

The color case is one of the most instructive test cases for the universals debate because it presses all the familiar issues while connecting them to something we know well from perception and physics. Naive color realism holds that colors are intrinsic, mind-independent properties of physical surfaces — the tomato is red in the way it is spherical. This aligns naturally with universals realism: RED is a genuine universal shared by all red things. But the scientific account of color perception makes this naive view difficult to sustain. The same spectral reflectance profile can appear different colors under different illuminants; two surfaces with different reflectance profiles can appear the same color (*metamerism*). Colors as perceived are not simple intrinsic surface properties but are relational in a complex way, depending on the surface, the illuminant, and the visual system of the perceiver.

Dispositional color realism responds by holding that colors are real but dispositional: to be red is to have the disposition to appear red to normal perceivers in standard conditions. This is a more modest realism — colors are in the objects, but as dispositions rather than categorical intrinsic properties. It is compatible with a trope-theoretic account: each red thing has its own redness-trope, a specific disposition-trope that is the particular tendency of this surface to produce red-experiences. The universal REDNESS becomes the resemblance class of all such disposition-tropes. The dispositional account avoids the straightforward physicalist identification of redness with spectral reflectance while preserving color realism against anti-realist alternatives.

## Three Positions

Locke's distinction between primary and secondary qualities articulates the strongest anti-realist position. Primary qualities — shape, size, motion — are genuine features of bodies existing independently of any perception. Secondary qualities — color, taste, sound, smell — are "powers to produce various sensations in us by their primary qualities" (*Essay*, II.8). The tomato is not intrinsically red; it has the power to cause red-experiences in normal perceivers, a power that depends on its surface microstructure (a primary quality). On this view, "this tomato is red" is literally false if taken as attributing an intrinsic quality to the tomato; the correct claim is the dispositional one. Redness is in the experience, not in the object. This Lockean view implies that ordinary color attributions are systematically misleading, which is a significant cost.

Color physicalism tries to resist this implication by identifying colors with physical properties of surfaces — specifically with spectral reflectance profiles. The universal RED is identified with a specific reflectance universal. The difficulties are well-known. Metamerism shows that two surfaces with different reflectance profiles can appear the same color under certain conditions, which means the color-as-perceived is not simply the reflectance. Illumination-dependence shows that the same reflectance profile can produce different apparent colors under different illuminants, which means the color-as-perceived varies in a way the reflectance does not. And individual variation in color perception — due to age, genetics, or condition — shows that "normal perceiver" cannot be defined purely by reference to the physics of the surfaces.

The sparse realist has a natural position here: redness, if it exists as a universal, is not a perfectly natural property in Lewis and Armstrong's sense. It is not among the fundamental properties of physics. Its naturalness is context-relative and perceiver-relative — it carves the world at joints that are joints for beings with our kind of visual system, not joints that would be recognized from a view from nowhere. This suggests that redness is a higher-level, functionally defined property grounded in more fundamental physical properties, rather than a fundamental universal in its own right.

## What the Color Case Illustrates

The color case illustrates several general themes in the universals debate. First, it shows the pressure that scientific accounts of perception exert on naive realism about properties. Properties that seemed like paradigm cases of genuine features of the world — the redness we see directly — turn out, under scientific scrutiny, to be more complex, relational, and perceiver-dependent than they first appeared. The universals debate must engage with this scientific pressure rather than treating properties in abstraction from our best empirical understanding of them.

Second, it illustrates the divide between sparse and abundant realism. The abundant realist accepts redness as a genuine property because property terms function in genuine true predications. The sparse realist insists that only properties that carve nature at its deepest joints deserve to be called genuine universals, and redness — because it is perceiver-relative and illumination-dependent — fails this test. The debate about color is partly a debate about which standard of genuineness is appropriate.

Third, the dispositional account of color points toward the broader debate about categorical versus dispositional properties. If redness is a disposition rather than a categorical quality, then the color case supports a dispositionalist picture of properties generally — and thereby connects to the trope theory of powerful qualities and to the anti-Humean account of laws as grounded in dispositional essences.
