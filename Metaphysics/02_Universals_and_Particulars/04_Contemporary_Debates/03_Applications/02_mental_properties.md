# Mental Properties

---

Pain is had by many beings — humans, dogs, octopi — and we take this to mean that they share something: the property of being in pain. What they share is not a particular neural state, since their neural architectures are radically different. It is a functional state: a state caused by tissue damage and causing avoidance behavior, however that state is physically realized. But is this functional state a genuine property, a universal, something real? Or is "being in pain" a useful predicate that picks out a heterogeneous disjunction of physical states with no more unity than the class of things that are green or greater than seven?

The debate about mental properties is a special case of the debate about universals, but it is a special case with very high stakes. If mental properties are genuine — if PAIN and BELIEF are real universals or at least real functional kinds — then mental explanations have genuine explanatory force and mental causation is real. If mental properties are not genuine, psychology and folk psychology describe patterns that cannot be grounded in a real similarity among the instances, and mental causation faces the threat of being mere epiphenomenalism dressed up in functional vocabulary.

Putnam's multiple realizability argument (*Mind, Language and Reality*, 1975) drove the decisive wedge against type-identity theory. The argument: if pain is identical with the universal C-FIBER-FIRING, then any creature without C-fibers cannot be in pain. But octopi and Martians plausibly feel pain without having C-fibers. So pain cannot be identical with any specific physical type. Formalized:

1. If type identity theory is correct, mental type M is identical with physical type P.
2. If M = P, nothing can instantiate M without instantiating P.
3. Multiple realizability: different physical types P₁, P₂, P₃, ... can each realize M.
4. Therefore, M ≠ P for any specific P, and type identity theory is false.

Functionalism is the natural response: mental properties are second-order functional universals, defined by causal roles. PAIN is the property of having *some* first-order property that is caused by tissue damage and causes avoidance behavior. This is multiple-realizable by definition: any physical property that plays the pain-role counts as a realizer. The multiple realizability problem is dissolved.

## The Exclusion Problem

Kim's exclusion argument (*Mind in a Physical World*, 1998) poses the deepest challenge to non-reductive physicalism. Physical events have sufficient physical causes (the causal closure of physics). If mental events are distinct from physical events, and if mental events are not causally overdetermined, then mental events have no causal power — they are epiphenomenal. The argument:

1. Every physical event has a sufficient physical cause.
2. Mental events are distinct from physical events (non-reductive physicalism).
3. Mental events are not causally overdetermined by independent causes.
4. Therefore, mental events have no causal power of their own.

This threatens to make mental properties — however real as functional kinds — causally irrelevant. The properties do all their causal work at the physical level; the mental level supervenes without contributing.

Non-reductive physicalists have responded in several ways. Token identity theory maintains that each particular mental event is identical with a particular physical event, while denying that mental types are identical with physical types. If my pain-token is identical with my C-fiber-firing-token, then the causal work done by the mental event is done by (and is identical with) the causal work done by the physical event. Davidson's anomalous monism (*Mental Events*, 1970) develops this position: token mental events are identical with token physical events (physicalism), but mental types are not reducible to physical types (anomalism) because the constitutive rationality of mental descriptions does not reduce to physical law. The cost is that mental properties, as types, remain causally inert even if mental tokens are causally efficacious through their physical identities.

Trope theory offers a resource here. If mental tropes are identical with physical tropes — each particular mental property-instance is identical with a particular physical property-instance — then mental causation is real at the token level without requiring type identity. The mental-trope and the physical-trope are one entity, described under different aspects. Kim's exclusion argument targets the distinctness of mental events from physical events; if mental tropes are identical with physical tropes, the distinctness premise is denied at the token level.

## Davidson and Supervenience

Davidson's anomalous monism holds that mental properties supervene on physical properties — there can be no mental difference without a physical difference — while being irreducible to them. Supervenience gives mental properties a form of dependence on the physical without identity. This has become one of the central tools in non-reductive physicalism: mental facts supervene on physical facts, which explains their reliability (our mental states reliably track the world through their physical bases) and their dependence on the physical, without requiring that mental predicates are synonymous with physical predicates.

Kim pressed that supervenience is insufficient: even if the mental supervenes on the physical, the mental might be causally inert if the physical level is causally complete. Supervenience ensures the co-variation of mental and physical facts but does not explain how the mental level can itself be causal. Whether the mental can be both genuinely supervening and genuinely causal depends on whether supervenience implies grounding in a sense that preserves causal relevance — a question that connects the philosophy of mind directly to the metaphysics of grounding and the theory of properties.

The debate about mental properties is a test case for the entire apparatus developed in this module. Whether mental kinds are genuine universals, whether functional properties are real properties, whether token-level trope identities suffice for mental causation, and whether grounding preserves causal relevance — all of these questions must be answered before we can give a satisfying account of the mind-body relation, and none can be answered without engaging the ontology of properties.
