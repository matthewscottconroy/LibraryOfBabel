# Moral Properties

---

Is cruelty genuinely wrong, or is "wrong" merely an expression of our attitude toward cruelty — our disapproval, projected onto an act that is otherwise neutral? Is there a property WRONGNESS that cruel acts instantiate, independently of whether anyone recognizes it? These questions have occupied moral philosophy for centuries, but their connection to the universals debate is underappreciated. If properties are universals, and if moral properties are real, then GOODNESS and WRONGNESS are abstract entities that acts and states of affairs instantiate. If properties are not universals but natural kinds discovered by science, then moral properties must either be identified with natural-scientific properties or be denied existence. The ontology of properties does not settle metaethics, but it sharply constrains the available options.

Moore's non-naturalist realism (*Principia Ethica*, 1903) takes the strongest position: GOODNESS is a genuine property but is not identical with any natural property. It is a *sui generis* non-natural universal, necessarily instantiated by whatever is good, known through a faculty of moral intuition rather than empirical investigation. Moore's Open Question Argument supports this: for any natural property N, the question "Is N good?" remains genuinely open — it is not analytic that what promotes pleasure, or what maximizes flourishing, is good. If GOODNESS were identical with N, the question would be trivially closed, as "Are bachelors unmarried?" is trivially closed. The open-ness of the question shows that GOODNESS is not identical with any N.

If Moore is right, moral properties are universals in a Platonic sense: abstract, non-spatiotemporal, causally inert, necessarily existent. This gives moral realism genuine metaphysical substance — there is something in the world that makes acts right or wrong — but at a significant ontological cost. How do we come to know about causally inert non-natural properties? What is the mechanism of moral intuition that gives us access to the non-natural realm? These epistemological challenges parallel the Benacerraf problem for mathematical Platonism.

## Cornell Realism and the A Posteriori Alternative

The Cornell realists — Boyd, Sturgeon, Railton, Brink — sought to preserve moral realism while naturalizing it. Moral properties are natural properties, but the identification is not analytic or a priori. Just as water is a posteriori identical with H₂O — a chemical discovery, not a conceptual necessity — goodness is a posteriori identical with some natural property, perhaps the homeostatic cluster of properties that tend to go together and contribute to human flourishing. The Open Question Argument fails to show that goodness is non-natural because it assumes that if goodness were natural, the identification would be analytic. But a posteriori identities are genuine identities even if they are not analytically obvious.

Sturgeon's explanatory argument adds a causal dimension. If Lincoln's perceiving that slavery is wrong explains why he acted as he did, and if that belief was produced by slavery's actually being wrong, then the moral property of wrongness is causally efficacious — it explains behavior as the distal cause of a morally sensitive observer's belief. Causally efficacious properties are real. Therefore wrongness is real. This is the moral analogue of the scientific realist argument for theoretical entities. Mackie's error-theoretic response is to deny that Lincoln's belief was reliably produced by moral facts: we can explain his behavior by citing his belief without positing that any moral fact caused the belief.

## Error Theory and Quasi-Realism

Mackie (*Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong*, 1977) accepted the Moorean analysis of moral discourse as purporting to describe objective, non-natural properties but argued that no such properties exist. Moral claims are all false — in error — because they presuppose the existence of objective values that are both metaphysically extravagant (unlike anything in the natural world, necessarily motivating) and epistemologically inaccessible (no ordinary faculty can detect them). This is the error theory: moral properties function like universals in our moral discourse, but the universals do not exist.

Blackburn's quasi-realism offers an alternative exit from moral realism that does not require systematic error. Starting from expressivism — moral utterances express attitudes, not beliefs — Blackburn's project is to "earn the right" to realism-talk from within the expressivist framework. We can say there are moral truths, moral facts, and moral knowledge, because these are truths, facts, and knowledge within the practice of moral discourse. This is Carnapian in spirit: the internal claim that cruelty is wrong is true (within the moral framework); the external question of whether there are really moral properties is either meaningless or merely pragmatic.

The supervenience of the moral on the natural — the widely accepted constraint that there cannot be a moral difference without a natural difference — has been a testing ground for these positions. For the realist, this supervenience seems to demand explanation: why are moral properties necessarily correlated with natural ones? For the non-naturalist, the correlation seems arbitrary unless moral properties are somehow constituted by natural properties, which threatens to collapse non-naturalism into naturalism. For the anti-realist, the supervenience is explicable through the logic of rational attitude-formation: agents hold that situations with the same natural features merit the same moral assessment, and this is a constraint on practical rationality, not a metaphysical necessity about moral universals.

## Parfit and Normative Realism

Parfit's *On What Matters* (2011) offers a distinctive form of non-naturalist realism that attempts to disarm the queerness objection. Moral facts are normative facts about reasons: facts about what reasons there are for action and attitude. These normative facts do not require a special causal or spatiotemporal realm; they are abstract truths in a domain parallel to mathematics and logic. Just as there are facts about what follows logically from what — facts accessible through rational reflection rather than empirical investigation — there are facts about what reasons there are. These normative facts are non-natural but not mysterious: they are truths in a sui generis normative domain.

The convergence argument adds support: Kantian ethics, contractualism, and consequentialism, while starting from different premises, converge on substantially the same moral conclusions. This convergence is best explained by there being moral facts that all three frameworks imperfectly track, just as the convergence of different scientific methods on the same results is best explained by there being facts the methods are detecting. The universals theorist can ask where GOODNESS lives in this picture: on Parfit's account, it is a normative universal, a property that situations instantiate when they give everyone sufficient reasons to value them — an abstract object in the domain of reasons, no more mysterious than the natural numbers.

Whether this is a satisfying account of moral reality, or whether it replaces the queerness of non-natural properties with the equally puzzling character of normative reasons, is the central dispute in contemporary metaethics. What is clear from the universals perspective is that moral ontology is not insulated from general property ontology: the same choices — universals, natural kinds, tropes, or nominalism — must be made for the moral domain, with the same theoretical costs and benefits at stake.
