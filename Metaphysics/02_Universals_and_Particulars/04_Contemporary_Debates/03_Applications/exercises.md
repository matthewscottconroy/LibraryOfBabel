# Exercises — Applications

*Problems for engaging with property theory in color, mind, and morality.*

---

**Exercise 1 — Color Realism**
Is the redness of a tomato an intrinsic, mind-independent property? Evaluate the following positions: (a) colors are categorical intrinsic properties (naive realism); (b) colors are dispositional properties to look colored to normal perceivers (dispositional realism); (c) colors are features of experiences, not of external objects (anti-realism). For each, identify the strongest objection.

**Exercise 2 — Multiple Realizability and Universals**
The multiple realizability argument against type identity theory says: if pain can be realized by different physical properties in different creatures, then pain is not identical with any single physical type. Formulate this as a precise argument. Now construct a response from the perspective of: (a) functionalism (mental types are higher-order functional types); (b) trope theory (mental tokens are physical tokens, without mental type-identity).

**Exercise 3 — Moral Universals**
Moore argues that GOODNESS is a genuine property but not a natural property. What does it mean for a property to be "non-natural"? How would Moore respond to the objection that non-natural moral properties are causally inert and hence play no role in moral psychology? Is there a coherent non-naturalist moral realism that avoids this problem?

**Exercise 4 — Expressivism vs. Moral Realism**
The expressivist says "Torture is wrong" expresses disapproval of torture rather than attributing a property to torture. The moral realist says it attributes the property WRONGNESS to torture. What is the difference? Can the expressivist account for the following data: (a) moral claims can be used as premises in valid arguments; (b) we can be mistaken about moral claims; (c) moral progress seems to occur?

**Thought Experiment — The Color Spectrum Shift**
Suppose you discover that all your life, when you see something red, your internal experience is qualitatively identical to what other people experience when they see something green, and vice versa. This is perfectly consistent with your behavior and all your color reports (you call both experiences "red" and "green" in the same way others do). Does this scenario show that colors are dispositional (defined by their effects on behavior) rather than categorical (intrinsic features of objects)? What are the implications for color realism?
