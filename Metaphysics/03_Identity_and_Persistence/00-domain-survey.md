# Domain Survey: Identity and Persistence

## Overview

Identity and persistence are among the most practically urgent topics in metaphysics — they concern what makes you the same person you were twenty years ago, whether the heap of planks that has had every original plank replaced is still the Ship of Theseus, and whether the caterpillar and the butterfly are one organism or two. Yet these practical questions immediately generate deep philosophical puzzles about the nature of identity itself: what it means for one thing to be numerically the same as another thing at a later time, whether apparent cases of "the same thing in different states" should really be analyzed as distinct things that share their parts, and whether the boundaries of objects are determinate enough for identity statements to have objective truth values.

The domain divides into four interconnected problem areas. **Numerical identity** — the relation a thing bears to itself and nothing else, formalized by Leibniz's Law (*x = y → (Fx ↔ Fy)*) — must be distinguished from **qualitative identity** (exact similarity). Two identical twins are qualitatively identical but numerically distinct. The central metaphysical puzzle is what *grounds* numerical identity over time — what makes it the case that the adult writing this sentence is the same person as the child who first read a philosophy book. Without a principled account, identity over time seems like a brute, unintelligible fact.

**Personal identity** is philosophically central partly because it connects metaphysical questions to ethical ones. If what matters in survival is strict numerical identity, then the prospect of teletransportation, fission, or gradual psychological replacement presents genuine moral stakes. If, as Parfit argues, what matters is not strict identity but psychological continuity, then our prudential concern for our own future selves — and our sense of personal responsibility for past actions — may be either rationally reassessable or shown to be based on a metaphysical confusion. The psychological criterion (Locke, Sydney Shoemaker, Derek Parfit), the biological criterion (Eric Olson's animalism), and the narrative/social criteria (Marya Schechtman) represent fundamentally different views about what persons essentially are.

**Material constitution** asks whether a statue and the clay it is made of are numerically identical. The Leibniz's Law argument says they cannot be (the clay can survive being squashed; the statue cannot), so we have coincident objects — two distinct objects occupying the same spatial region simultaneously. But many philosophers resist this conclusion: having two things where there seems to be only one is metaphysically costly and generates puzzles about how they interact. The four-dimensionalist strategy proposes that "the statue" and "the clay" are not two numerically distinct three-dimensional objects but two distinct four-dimensional worms, sharing the same temporal stage right now but having different histories and futures.

**Temporal parts** — the four-dimensionalist framework — connects the identity debate to the ontology of time. Perdurantism holds that objects have temporal parts (stages) just as they have spatial parts, and that persistence through time consists in the existence of a series of temporal stages united by the right relations. Endurantism holds that objects are wholly present at each time they exist — there are no temporal parts. The choice between these frameworks has implications for nearly every other debate in this domain.

## Major Positions and Their Logical Relations

### Theories of Personal Identity

**Psychological continuity theories**: Personal identity consists in overlapping chains of psychological connections — memories of one's past experiences, continuity of beliefs, desires, intentions, and character. Developed by John Locke (memory criterion), Sydney Shoemaker, and most influentially by Derek Parfit.
- Locke: personal identity consists in the continuity of consciousness, especially memory of past experiences.
- Parfit (*Reasons and Persons*, 1984): reductionism about persons — a person is nothing over and above a series of physical and psychological events; personal identity consists in overlapping chains of psychological connectedness; personal identity is not what matters for survival.
- *Fission problem*: if your brain is split and each hemisphere transplanted into a different body, each resulting person has equally good psychological continuity with you. Who are you? Psychological theory forces a choice: either both are you (violating transitivity of identity) or neither is (counterintuitively, since each has equal claim).

**Biological/animalist theories** (Eric Olson, Peter van Inwagen): You are a biological organism, a human animal; personal identity is whatever makes a human animal persist — roughly, the continuation of a functioning biological life. What matters for survival is not psychological continuity but the continuation of a biological organism.
- *Advantage*: avoids fission problem; no two candidate persons in the normal case; biological identity is physically determinate.
- *Problem*: what happens to my personal identity if my brain is transplanted? The brain seems to carry "me" with it, but the organism stays behind. Animalism seems forced to say I stay with the body — but this conflicts with intuitions about what matters in survival.

**Narrative/social theories** (Marya Schechtman): What makes a life a single person's life is the narrative the person uses to give it meaning and coherence. Identity is constituted partly by self-understanding and partly by social recognition.
- *Problem*: this conflates practical identity (what makes my life mine in the sense that matters for self-concern) with metaphysical identity (what makes this person numerically the same as that past person).

**No-self theories**: Buddhist-influenced views (Parfit, Hume's bundle theory of persons) hold that there is no persistent self at all; the appearance of personal identity over time is a construction from momentary states.

### Material Constitution and Coincidence

**Constitution as identity**: The statue is identical to the clay that constitutes it. Modal differences (clay survives squashing; statue does not) are explained as *de dicto* rather than *de re* — the descriptions "the statue" and "the clay" pick out the same object, but the modal claims are sensitive to how the object is described, not to different modal profiles of distinct objects. (Gibbard's "Lumpl and Goliath.")

**The Constitution View** (Lynne Rudder Baker): The statue and the clay are numerically distinct objects that are related by **constitution** — a non-identity relation that holds when one object is "made of" another in the right circumstances. The clay constitutes the statue without being identical to it; they share the same spatial region but differ in their persistence conditions.
- *Problem*: two objects in exactly the same place at the same time, made of exactly the same matter, seems bizarre. How do they differ if all their matter, location, and causal powers are the same?

**Four-dimensionalism / temporal parts** (Sider, Lewis): "The statue" and "the clay" are distinct four-dimensional worms with different temporal extents, sharing a common temporal stage. No two three-dimensional objects coincide at any moment — the coincidence is between two cross-temporal series (worms) that overlap at a shared stage. This resolves the coincidence worry by denying that there are coincident three-dimensional objects.

**Eliminativism**: Only one of the candidate objects really exists; the other is a linguistic construction or a useful fiction. Van Inwagen's mereological nihilism for non-living artifacts: there is no such object as the statue or the clay lump; there are only simples arranged statue-wise.

### Persistence: Endurantism vs. Perdurantism

**Endurantism** (three-dimensionalism): Objects are wholly present at each time they exist. Persistence through time is not analogous to extension through space. There are no temporal parts. The object that exists at *t1* is numerically identical to the object that exists at *t2* — the very same thing.
- *Problem of temporary intrinsics* (Lewis): If I am straight now and bent later, I have the property *being-straight* and the property *being-bent*. But if I am the very same individual at both times, I have contradictory properties. Endurantism must explain this by indexing properties to times (Lewis objected this conflates instantiation with something else) or by holding that properties are relations to times.

**Perdurantism** (four-dimensionalism): Objects have temporal parts (stages) just as they have spatial parts. The object that exists at *t1* is a distinct temporal stage from the object that exists at *t2*; they are related by a trans-temporal relation (I-relation, or being part of the same temporal worm). The whole four-dimensional object is the sum of its temporal stages.
- *Advantage*: solves temporary intrinsics by saying different temporal parts have different intrinsic properties (my *t1*-stage is straight; my *t2*-stage is bent).
- *Problem*: there are infinitely many four-dimensional worms sharing any given temporal stage; which one is "me"? The worm view seems to multiply objects without bound.

**Stage theory** (Hawley, Sider): The entities that persist are not temporal worms (collections of stages) but individual stages themselves. You are a stage, not a worm. You persist by having counterpart stages at other times (your temporal counterparts). This combines four-dimensionalism's solution to temporary intrinsics with an ontology that takes stages as the fundamental particulars.

## Key Arguments and Counterarguments

### Leibniz's Law Argument for Non-Identity of Statue and Clay

**P1.** The statue has the property *being possibly destroyed by being squashed*.
**P2.** The clay does not have the property *being possibly destroyed by being squashed*.
**C** (by Leibniz's Law). The statue ≠ the clay.

**Counterargument** (Gibbard): Modal properties are description-relative (*de dicto*). The property *being possibly destroyed by being squashed* is not an intrinsic property but a property under a description. The same object is such that, as a statue, it would be destroyed; as a lump, it would survive. No contradiction.

**Four-dimensionalist response**: P1 and P2 are true because "the statue" picks out a different four-dimensional object from "the clay." These objects share a temporal stage but differ in their futures, which explains the modal difference without invoking non-identity of three-dimensional coincidents.

### The Fission Argument against Psychological Continuity

**P1.** If psychological continuity is sufficient for personal identity, and the left hemisphere is psychologically continuous with the pre-fission person, the pre-fission person is identical to the left-hemisphere person.
**P2.** If psychological continuity is sufficient for personal identity, and the right hemisphere is psychologically continuous with the pre-fission person, the pre-fission person is identical to the right-hemisphere person.
**P3.** The left-hemisphere person ≠ the right-hemisphere person (they are two distinct people after fission).
**C.** (by transitivity of identity). Contradiction.

**Parfit's response**: The right response to fission is to abandon the question "which one is really me?" as ill-posed. Personal identity is not what matters; what matters is psychological continuity, and in fission cases both successor-persons have what matters. The lesson is that we should not care about identity per se.

### The Puzzle of the Ship of Theseus

A ship has every plank replaced one by one. When the process is complete, is the resulting ship the same ship? What if the original planks were collected and reassembled into a ship? Which ship is the original?

**Sortal-relative identity** (Wiggins): identity is always identity *as a ϕ*, where ϕ is a sortal concept (ship, person, organism). Different sortals may yield different verdicts on persistence. Wiggins: there is one ship identity relation, and it requires spatio-temporal continuity; the reassembled ship is not the original.

**Perdurantist response**: Both "ships" are four-dimensional objects, and they share temporal stages from before the process began. There is no puzzle about which is "really" the original — the question presupposes a three-dimensional framework inappropriate for temporal persistence.

### The Argument from Animalism

**P1.** You came into existence when the organism (human animal) you are came into existence.
**P2.** You will cease to exist when that organism ceases to exist.
**C.** You are that organism.

**Psychological criterion response**: deny P1 — a person came into existence when the organism became psychologically sophisticated enough to sustain consciousness and psychological continuity. The organism exists before the person; hence the person is not identical to the organism.

## Key Papers and Books

1. **Parfit, Derek, *Reasons and Persons* (1984), Part III** — The most influential work on personal identity in the analytic tradition; argues for reductionism and that identity is not what matters for survival; contains the fission, teletransportation, and spectrum arguments.

2. **Wiggins, David, *Sameness and Substance* (1980; 2nd ed. 2001)** — Defends a neo-Aristotelian account of identity as sortal-relative; develops the notion of a substance-concept as determining persistence conditions; a sustained response to both Parfit and four-dimensionalism.

3. **Lewis, David, "Survival and Identity" (1976)** — Defends perdurantist framework for personal identity; introduces the I-relation and argues that fission cases are compatible with four-dimensionalism.

4. **Olson, Eric, *The Human Animal* (1997)** — Systematic defense of animalism; argues that we are organisms and that psychological continuity theories systematically misidentify what we are.

5. **Baker, Lynne Rudder, *Persons and Bodies* (2000)** — Defends the Constitution View; argues that persons and organisms are constitutionally related but numerically distinct.

6. **Sider, Theodore, *Four-Dimensionalism* (2001)** — The most comprehensive defense of temporal-parts theory; argues for perdurantism on grounds of theoretical unity and as a solution to the temporary intrinsics problem and coincidence puzzles.

7. **Burke, Michael, "Preserving the Principle of One Object to a Place" (1994)** — Defends a "dominant kinds" view to avoid coincident objects; influential in the constitution debate.

8. **Gibbard, Allan, "Contingent Identity" (1975)** — The classic defense of contingent identity for statue/clay cases; introduces Lumpl and Goliath and argues for description-relative modal properties.

## Live Debates and Open Questions

1. **Is there a non-circular account of psychological continuity?** Parfit's chains of psychological connections require specifying which connections (memories, intentions) count; specifying the relevant connections seems to presuppose a notion of personal identity, generating circularity.

2. **How does the fetus/person transition work for animalists?** If you are identical to the animal, and the animal existed before personhood-grounding psychological properties emerged, then you existed before you were a person. This generates puzzles about fetal moral status.

3. **Four-dimensionalism and vagueness**: If persistence consists in the existence of temporally overlapping stages, and it is indeterminate when a stage belongs to a given worm (when exactly does a ship cease to exist?), does this force vagueness in identity — generally held to be incoherent?

4. **Grounding persistence**: What grounds the fact that a given temporal stage is a stage *of this person* rather than some other worm? The perdurantist owes an account of the I-relation that does not appeal to numerical identity.

5. **Structural identity and composition**: Composition determines what object results from putting parts together; but the same parts can be assembled differently. Does the identity of a complex object depend on its structure as well as its parts? Hylomorphist accounts (Fine, Johnston) say yes; mereological approaches say no.

## Connections to Other Domains

**Identity/Persistence and Ontology (Domain 01)**: The coincidence problem connects directly to general questions about ontological economy and the proliferation of entities. The distinction between numerical and qualitative identity requires a framework of properties (Domain 02) and their instantiation.

**Identity/Persistence and Mereology (Domain 11)**: Four-dimensionalism is a mereological thesis — temporal parts are parts in the mereological sense. The Special Composition Question (when do parts compose a whole?) applies to temporal as well as spatial composition. The constitution problem is the mereological question of whether two objects can have exactly the same proper parts.

**Identity/Persistence and Time (Domain 06)**: Endurantism and perdurantism commit to different views of time. Perdurantism fits most naturally with a B-theoretic, block universe view of time; endurantism may be more naturally paired with presentism or the growing block view, though this is contested.

**Identity/Persistence and Personal Identity in Free Will (Domain 08)**: Parfit's reductionism about persons has immediate implications for moral responsibility and prudential rationality. If there is no deep fact about personal identity, the basis for holding someone responsible for distant past actions becomes uncertain.

**Identity/Persistence and Philosophy of Mind (Domain 07)**: The persistence conditions of persons connect directly to the mind-body problem — if you are essentially psychological (psychological criterion), then mental properties are at least partly constitutive of your persistence conditions; if you are essentially a biological organism, mental properties are secondary.
