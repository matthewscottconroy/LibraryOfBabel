# Identity and Persistence

You are not the same collection of particles you were ten years ago. Most of your cells have been replaced; your neural connections have rewired; your beliefs, desires, and memories have shifted in ways large and small. Yet you persist. The person reading these words is, in some sense that matters deeply, the same person who existed a decade ago — the same one who formed certain commitments, who is owed debts, who is responsible for past actions. The question of personal identity is not an abstract curiosity. It underlies every legal system, every moral practice of holding people responsible, every relationship that extends through time.

But the problem is broader than personal identity, however urgently that case presents itself. A ship is repaired plank by plank until no original part remains — is it still the same ship? A lump of clay is shaped into a statue — are there now two things where there was one, or one thing with two aspects? A river flows continuously, yet its water is always different — is the Mississippi the same river it was a century ago, or a different one with the same name? A nation changes its constitution, its territory, and its entire population over millennia — what makes it the same nation? Each of these cases is philosophically tractable only if we have a theory of what identity consists in and what makes a thing persist through change.

This unit investigates identity and persistence across four interconnected chapters, moving from the most basic logical and metaphysical questions to the most philosophically charged applications.

We begin with **identity** itself — both the distinction between numerical and qualitative identity, and the celebrated problem of personal identity. Numerical identity is the relation every thing bears to itself and to nothing else: the relation Hesperus bears to Phosphorus, as Frege's famous example established. Qualitative identity is something weaker: the relation two perfect duplicates bear to each other when they are alike in every property. Confusing these two relations generates philosophical errors with serious consequences. The chapter on personal identity traces the debate from Locke's memory criterion through Hume's bundle view, through Parfit's radical revisionism, and into contemporary discussions of psychological continuity, biological animalism, and the implications of brain transplant scenarios.

**Persistence** raises the question of how objects endure or are extended through time. The endurantist holds that a persisting object is wholly present at each moment of its existence — the same entity, in its entirety, at different times. The perdurantist holds that a persisting object is a four-dimensional whole with temporal parts, so that "the same object at different times" is a loose way of speaking about different temporal stages of a larger worm-like entity. This disagreement about persistence is not merely terminological. It has implications for how we understand change, motion, causation, and the very structure of time.

**Constitution and coincidence** addresses the deeply puzzling cases in which two distinct objects seem to occupy the same location at the same time. A clay statue and its clay appear to be co-located: they share all their parts, yet they seem to differ in their modal and historical properties (the clay could survive being squashed; the statue could not). Are they really two objects? If so, how can two things share all their parts? If not, how do we account for their apparent differences? The metaphysics of coincidence and vagueness extends this problem into questions about whether identity can come in degrees.

Finally, the **necessity of identity** takes up one of the most important developments in twentieth-century metaphysics: Kripke's argument that identity statements between rigid designators, when true, are necessarily true. If Hesperus is Phosphorus, it could not have been otherwise — there is no possible world in which Hesperus exists and Phosphorus exists and they are distinct. This result has profound consequences for the philosophy of mind, the philosophy of mathematics, and the entire framework of modal metaphysics.

These questions are among the most persistent in philosophy precisely because they resist easy resolution. The puzzles return in new forms no matter how carefully we think them through. That is not a reason for despair — it is a sign that we are dealing with something genuinely deep.
