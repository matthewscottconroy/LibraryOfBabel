# Identity

Identity is the most basic relation in logic — the relation everything bears to itself and to nothing else. Leibniz's Law states its core: if x and y are identical, then whatever is true of x is true of y and vice versa. This seems unassailable. Yet identity generates some of the deepest puzzles in metaphysics, because the things we want to identify seem to shift their properties over time, or have different properties in different possible worlds, or seem to be the same as things that are numerically distinct from them.

The first distinction needed is between *numerical* identity and *qualitative* identity. Two objects are qualitatively identical if they share all the same properties — two freshly minted coins from the same die, for example. But two qualitatively identical objects are numerically distinct: there are two of them, not one. Numerical identity is the strict identity relation; qualitative identity is similarity without limit. Confusing these generates philosophical errors that compound in dramatic ways when we think about identity over time and across possible worlds.

Personal identity is the domain where identity questions become most urgent and most contested. What makes you the same person you were ten years ago? Locke proposed a memory criterion: personal identity consists in continuity of memory — you are the same person as a past person if you can remember that person's experiences as your own. Hume radicalized this: there is no persisting self beyond the bundle of experiences, and personal identity is a useful fiction. Contemporary debates between psychological continuity theories (Parfit) and biological or animalist theories (Olson) engage with the intuitions these thought experiments generate: what happens if your brain is split and each half is transplanted into a different body? What happens if your memories are wiped and replaced? What would survival consist in, if not identity?

This chapter develops the conceptual foundations of identity theory and applies them to the case of persons. The goal is both to clarify what we mean by "same" and to understand why this apparently simple question resists every apparently simple answer.
