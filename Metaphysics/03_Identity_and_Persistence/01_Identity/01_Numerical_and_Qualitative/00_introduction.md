# Numerical and Qualitative Identity

"The twins are identical." "This is the same river I swam in as a child." "I am the same person I was ten years ago." These sentences use "same" and "identical" in importantly different ways. The twins may be qualitatively identical — exactly alike in every perceptible respect — while being numerically distinct: two individuals, not one. The river and I, on the other hand, are claimed to be numerically identical to things that existed in the past: there is one river, one person, that exists now and existed then.

The distinction between numerical and qualitative identity is foundational for all the debates that follow in this unit. Numerical identity — the relation that everything bears to itself and to nothing else — is what Leibniz's Law governs: if x and y are numerically identical, then every property of x is a property of y and vice versa. Any apparent failure of Leibniz's Law is either a puzzle to be solved or evidence of a hidden equivocation. Qualitative identity — the relation of being exactly similar, of sharing all qualitative properties — is a different and more complex matter.

The application of Leibniz's Law generates puzzles immediately. Suppose this lump of clay is identical to the statue it constitutes. The statue was created yesterday; the lump existed before the statue was created. By Leibniz's Law, if the statue is the lump, then the lump was not created yesterday (since the lump is the same as something created yesterday). But we stipulated that the lump existed before yesterday. Contradiction. The puzzle forces a choice: accept that the lump and statue are numerically distinct (the "two-thingsist" position), or explain away the appearance of contradiction by denying that "was created yesterday" is a genuine property in the relevant sense.

The formal theory of identity — including the logical properties of the identity relation (reflexivity, symmetry, transitivity) and the role of Leibniz's Law — provides the tools for prosecuting these debates. This section develops the formal theory and introduces the central puzzles that drive the investigation of identity through time and across possible worlds.
