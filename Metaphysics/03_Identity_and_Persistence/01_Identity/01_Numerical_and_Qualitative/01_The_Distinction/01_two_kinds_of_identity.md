# Two Kinds of Identity

*The distinction between numerical identity (being one and the same thing) and qualitative identity (being exactly alike).*

---

Consider two cars rolling off the same assembly line on the same day — identical make, model, color, trim, every measurable feature the same. We say, loosely, that they are "the same car," and we mean something true. Now consider a single car spotted at noon and again at three o'clock in the same parking lot. We say it is "the same car," and we mean something entirely different. The first "same" is about resemblance; the second is about numerical oneness. Conflating them produces endless philosophical confusion, and distinguishing them is the first order of business.

*Numerical identity* is the relation every thing bears to itself and nothing else. If A and B are numerically identical, there are not two things — there is one, described from two angles. *Qualitative identity* (or exact resemblance) holds between things that share all their properties: the two factory cars are qualitatively identical and numerically distinct. The crucial logical point is that qualitative identity does not entail numerical identity. The Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles (PII) asserts the entailment — ∀F(Fa ↔ Fb) → a = b — and is disputed. By contrast, numerical identity trivially entails qualitative identity: if a and b are one thing, they cannot differ in any property, since there is no "they."

## Formal Statement

We can render the two relations precisely. Numerical identity is captured by "=" in first-order logic with identity: ∀x(x = x) and ∀x∀y(x = y → (Fx ↔ Fy)) for all predicates F. Qualitative identity is the relation described by PII: ∀F(Fa ↔ Fb) → a = b, which claims that qualitative indistinguishability entails numerical identity. The Indiscernibility of Identicals — the left-to-right direction — is universally accepted as logically necessary. PII — the right-to-left direction — is a substantive metaphysical thesis.

The distinction has roots in Aristotle's *Metaphysics*, where he distinguishes being "one in number" from being "one in species." Bucephalus and Alexander's steed are one in number; any two horses are one in species. Leibniz and Hume both thematized the contrast, and it enters the modern discussion through Russell's logic and Frege's distinction between *Sinn* and *Bedeutung*: two co-referential terms may express different senses while designating the same object. The informativeness of "Hesperus is Phosphorus" — an empirical discovery, not a tautology — depends precisely on this: numerical sameness with qualitative distinctness-as-presented.

## Two Cases Worth Holding Steady

Consider Alice and Beth, monozygotic twins stipulated to be qualitatively identical — same intrinsic properties, same appearance, same histories as far as we know. They are numerically distinct: there are two people. No degree of qualitative similarity collapses numerical diversity. This is not a counterexample to PII as typically formulated, since PII is usually restricted to properties that exclude bare spatiotemporal location; Alice and Beth are clearly located differently. The twins case shows that our loosest sense of "same" — matching in all observable respects — simply does not do the work that numerical identity does.

Max Black's thought experiment (1952) presses harder. Imagine a universe containing only two perfectly symmetrical, qualitatively identical iron spheres separated by two miles. If PII is true — qualitative indistinguishability entails numerical identity — there is only one sphere. Yet the scenario stipulates two. Bundle theorists, who identify objects with their qualitative properties, must either deny that Black's scenario is metaphysically possible or find some distinguishing qualitative feature — perhaps the relational property "being two miles from another sphere," though each sphere has that property equally. The debate remains genuinely open.

## Why the Distinction Matters

Numerical identity is what is at stake in every substantive metaphysical question about persistence, constitution, and modality. When we ask whether the person who woke up this morning is the same as the one who fell asleep last night, whether a statue is identical with the clay, whether I could have been someone else — all these are questions about the *one-or-two* question, about numerical identity, not about resemblance or shared type.

The formal study of identity in logic begins with first-order logic with identity, where "=" is a primitive relation satisfying specific axioms. The philosophical questions begin where the logic ends: what makes it the case that something is identical with itself, and what grounds the relation of numerical identity? These deeper questions connect to debates about haecceity, bare particulars, and the metaphysics of individuation explored in subsequent sections.
