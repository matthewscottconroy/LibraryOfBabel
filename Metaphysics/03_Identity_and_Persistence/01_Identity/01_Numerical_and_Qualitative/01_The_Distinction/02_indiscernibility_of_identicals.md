# The Indiscernibility of Identicals

*Leibniz's Law and why numerical identity implies sharing all properties.*

---

If Hesperus is identical with Phosphorus — if they are the same planet, Venus — then every property of Hesperus is a property of Phosphorus: the same mass, the same orbital period, the same chemical composition, the same distance from the Earth on any given night. This seems obvious once stated. If a and b are *one* thing — not two — there is no logical room for them to differ. To differ is to be two things, one with a property the other lacks. Numerical identity closes that gap entirely.

This principle — the Indiscernibility of Identicals — is formally rendered as:

> **(II)** ∀x∀y(x = y → ∀F(Fx ↔ Fy))

It is provable as a theorem in standard first-order logic with identity, following from the reflexivity axiom ∀x(x = x) together with the Leibniz's Law substitution schema. Note that (II) is distinct from the Identity of Indiscernibles (PII), which runs in the opposite direction:

> **(PII)** ∀x∀y(∀F(Fx ↔ Fy) → x = y)

(II) is universally accepted as logically necessary. (PII) is a substantive metaphysical thesis that is disputed.

## (II) as a Test for Non-Identity

The principal philosophical use of (II) is as a *test for non-identity*: if a has a property that b lacks, then a ≠ b. This inference — by contraposition of (II) — is one of the most powerful tools in philosophy:

- P1. If a = b, then ∀F(Fa ↔ Fb). [Leibniz's Law]
- P2. There exists a property F such that Fa but ¬Fb.
- C. Therefore, a ≠ b.

We can put this more sharply with cases. The statue and the clay have different modal properties: the statue would be destroyed by squashing, the clay would not. By Leibniz's Law, the statue ≠ the clay. Hesperus and Phosphorus turn out not to differ in any genuine property, confirming their identity. Mental states and brain states, philosophers of mind have argued, may differ in phenomenal character — the *what-it's-like* — which, if genuine, constitutes evidence against type identity theory. In each domain, (II) does the work.

## Apparent Violations

Several systematic classes of case appear to violate (II). Understanding why they are only *apparent* violations illuminates the scope of the law.

Consider propositional attitudes. George believes Hesperus is a star but does not believe Phosphorus is a star, not knowing they are the same planet. Does the planet then have the property "being believed by George to be a star" under one name but not the other? The resolution lies in distinguishing *de re* from *de dicto* contexts. The relevant "property" here is not a property of the planet *as such* — it depends on how the planet is described or conceptualized. Properties of the planet *de re* (its mass, orbital path, chemical composition) do not differ. Frege's framework handles this cleanly: "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" have different senses (*Sinnen*) even though they share a reference (*Bedeutung*). In belief reports, what matters is the sense, and differences in sense are not differences in the object.

Modal properties work similarly. The Morning Star might have failed to be the Evening Star — so it seems to have the property "could have failed to be the Evening Star" that the Evening Star lacks. But this is a *de dicto* modal claim: it is about descriptions, not about the planet. Read *de re* — "Venus is such that it might not have been Venus" — the sentence is simply false, and no violation of (II) arises. Kripke's rigid designation dissolves the puzzle: since both "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" rigidly designate Venus, the identity is necessary, and the apparent modal difference traces to how we access the planet through descriptions that might have applied to different objects.

Temporal properties raise a further worry. Socrates was sitting at t₁ and standing at t₂. How can one object have both sitting and standing, which seem mutually exclusive? The endurantist response: properties are relativized to times. Socrates has "sitting-at-t₁" and "standing-at-t₂," which are not contradictory. This response raises its own issues — Lewis argues it makes all intrinsic properties relational — but those concerns belong to the discussion of temporary intrinsics.

## The Pattern and the Upshot

The pattern is general. Any intensional operator — necessity, possibility, belief, knowledge, desire — creates contexts in which substitution of co-referring terms may fail. The methodological lesson is constructive: when an apparent violation of (II) arises, ask whether the attributed property is *de re* (a genuine property of the object itself) or *de dicto* (dependent on a description or mode of presentation). If *de dicto*, (II) does not require the property to be shared. If *de re*, the property is the same for identical objects, and what seemed like a violation turns out to be an illusion generated by intensionality.

Leibniz's Law survives, but it requires that properties be understood in a precise, non-intensional way. Its role as a test for non-identity makes it indispensable in debates about coincident objects, personal identity, mental and physical identity, and transworld identity. Understanding where (II) applies in full force and where apparent violations are illusory is a prerequisite for serious work in any of these areas.
