# Identity and Diversity

*What grounds the fact that things are diverse — numerically many rather than one?*

---

We know what it means for two things to be identical: there is, in the end, only one of them. But what does it mean for two things to be *two*? The question may appear trivial — two things are two things — but the triviality disappears once we ask what *grounds* or *explains* diversity rather than merely asserting it. Something must make it the case that there are two iron spheres here rather than one. The metaphysics of diversity is, in this sense, the flip side of the individuation problem.

The most natural answer is that things are diverse because they differ in some property. This is the contrapositive of the Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles (PII):

> **(PII)** ∀x∀y(∀F(Fx ↔ Fy) → x = y)

Equivalently by contraposition: if x ≠ y, then ∃F(Fx ∧ ¬Fy). Diversity is always grounded in qualitative difference. Leibniz held PII to be a necessary truth grounded in the principle of sufficient reason: there could be no sufficient reason for God to produce two qualitatively indistinguishable individuals, since their diversity would be a distinction without a difference — a violation of rationality. The world, on this picture, contains no brute diversities.

## Black's Challenge

Max Black (1952) disputed this by constructing a coherent scenario in which PII fails. Imagine a universe containing exactly two iron spheres, a mile apart, with exactly the same chemical composition, size, mass, and every other intrinsic and relational property. By hypothesis, ∀F(F Sphere₁ ↔ F Sphere₂). If PII is true, Sphere₁ = Sphere₂ — there is only one sphere. But the scenario stipulates two. Contradiction. Therefore PII is not necessarily true.

Defenders of PII respond in several ways. The haecceitist move: Sphere₁ and Sphere₂ differ in their *haecceities* — their bare thisnesses. But haecceities are by definition non-qualitative, and PII as Leibniz intended it concerned only qualitative properties; appealing to haecceities concedes the point. The relational properties move: Sphere₁ has "is two miles from something" — but so does Sphere₂, since each is two miles from the other. No asymmetric relational property emerges from a perfectly symmetric scenario. The coherence-denial move: perhaps no universe with exactly two qualitatively identical spheres is metaphysically possible. This requires explanation, and it is not obvious what would provide one.

## Four Grounds for Diversity

For those unsatisfied with brute diversity, the most developed proposals locate the ground in four different metaphysical features.

*Spatiotemporal individuation* holds that two things are diverse iff they occupy different spatiotemporal locations or have different causal histories grounded in different spatiotemporal origins. This is the most naturalistically appealing answer. The two spheres are diverse because they occupy different regions of space. If we count spatiotemporal location as a "property," the spheres differ in their relational spatial properties. Critics note that this move works only if we already know that there are two spatial regions — and knowing that seems to presuppose the diversity we were trying to explain.

*Bare particulars*, or substrata, offer a different ground. Each object has a bare particular — a propertyless "it" that instantiates properties but is itself without qualitative character. Two spheres can share all qualitative properties because their bare particulars are numerically distinct. Diversity is grounded in the numerical distinctness of bare particulars rather than in qualitative differences. The objection is familiar: a thing with no properties seems to be nothing at all, and how the bare particular could have numerical identity without any qualitative character to distinguish it is left unexplained.

*Bundle theory* runs into the problem from the other direction. If objects are identical with their bundles of qualitative properties, two spheres with identical bundles are the same sphere — and PII becomes a definitional truth. The bundle theorist faces a dilemma: accept PII (only one sphere exists) or find a non-qualitative ground for diversity (spatiotemporal location, haecceity), which re-introduces something the theory was meant to avoid.

*Haecceities* — individual essences, properties like *being Socrates* that only one individual can have — ground diversity without appealing to qualitative difference. Two objects are diverse because they have numerically distinct haecceities. This explains how two qualitatively indistinguishable spheres can be two. But haecceities are controversial precisely because it is unclear what they are or how we could know which haecceity belongs to which object independently of already knowing which object we are tracking.

## The Option of Brute Diversity

There is a fifth option: that numerical diversity is simply basic, not grounded in anything further. On this view, there is no explanation of why there are two things rather than one — there is only the fact that there are. This is a form of anti-haecceitism about diversity, and it avoids the costs of bare particulars, haecceities, and bundle theory alike. The cost is explanatory: diversity becomes a primitive, without analysis or ground.

The diversity question is a facet of the broader problem of individuation — what makes an individual the *particular* individual it is, as opposed to some other individual of the same qualitative type. Matter, form, spatiotemporal location, and haecceity have all been proposed as individuators. Each proposal has costs. The question presses the same issue from the side of distinctness rather than identity, and the most honest conclusion is that no fully satisfactory answer yet exists.
