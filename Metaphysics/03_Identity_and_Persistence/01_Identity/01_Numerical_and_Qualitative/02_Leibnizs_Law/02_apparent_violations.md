# Apparent Violations of Leibniz's Law

*How to handle cases where identical things seem to have different properties.*

---

The test for non-identity is powerful, but it generates apparent puzzles when it seems to misfire — when things we know to be identical appear to differ in properties. These apparent violations arise systematically in three kinds of context: propositional attitudes, modal claims, and temporal properties. Understanding how to resolve them illuminates both the scope of Leibniz's Law and the semantics of property attribution.

## Class One: Propositional Attitudes

Consider the Fregean puzzle. George believes Hesperus is a star but does not believe Phosphorus is a star, not knowing they are the same planet. Since Hesperus = Phosphorus (both are Venus), Leibniz's Law seems to require that George's beliefs about both are the same. The argument making the violation appear:

- P1. Hesperus = Phosphorus.
- P2. George believes Hesperus is a star.
- P3. George does not believe Phosphorus is a star.
- C. Hesperus has a property (being believed by George to be a star) that Phosphorus lacks. So Hesperus ≠ Phosphorus — contradicting P1.

The resolution denies that "being believed by George to be a star" is a genuine *de re* property of the planet. It is a *de dicto* property — one that depends on which *description* is used to pick out the planet, not on the planet itself. The *de re* properties of Venus (its mass, orbit, composition) are the same under both names.

Frege's framework handles this by distinguishing sense (*Sinn*) from reference (*Bedeutung*). "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" have different senses even though they share a reference. In belief reports, what matters is the sense: the report "George believes Hesperus is a star" does not attribute the same content as "George believes Phosphorus is a star," even though both names refer to the same planet. Leibniz's Law concerns genuine properties of objects; properties that depend on modes of presentation are not genuine in the relevant sense.

## Class Two: Modal Properties

A related puzzle arises for modal properties. The Morning Star might (in some possible world) fail to be the Evening Star — so the Morning Star seems to have the property "could have failed to be the Evening Star," while the Evening Star cannot have failed to be itself. Does this establish non-identity?

No. The apparent violation arises from a *de dicto* reading of the modal claim: "The Morning Star might not have been the Evening Star" says something about the descriptions "Morning Star" and "Evening Star," not about the planet Venus. Read *de re* — "Venus is such that it might not have been Venus" — the sentence is simply false, and no violation of Leibniz's Law arises.

Kripke's rigid designation account dissolves the puzzle entirely. Since both "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" rigidly designate Venus — picking out the same individual in every possible world — "Hesperus = Phosphorus" is necessarily true. The apparent modal difference was generated by describing Venus under a description that might have applied to a different planet in other circumstances, not by a genuine modal difference in Venus itself.

## Class Three: Temporal Properties

Temporal properties seem to generate violations of a different kind. Socrates was sitting at t₁ and standing at t₂. Does he have both the property "sitting" and "standing" — which are mutually exclusive — in violation of classical logic?

The resolution: properties are relativized to times. Socrates has "sitting-at-t₁" and "standing-at-t₂," which are not contradictory. A further question arises: if "sitting" is really "sitting-at-t" for some time t, have we made all properties relational? Lewis argues this threatens the intrinsic/extrinsic distinction — see the discussion of temporary intrinsics. One endurantist move is to treat "instantiation at a time" adverbially: the manner of instantiation modifies *when* the property is had, not the property itself. Spelling this out without circularity is non-trivial, but the distinction is real and worth pursuing.

## The General Pattern and Its Lesson

The pattern illustrated by beliefs and modalities generalizes to any intensional operator — necessity, possibility, belief, knowledge, desire, causal description. Each creates contexts in which substitution of co-referring terms may fail. This is Leibniz's Law in *extensional* contexts that matters, not a general principle about properties under *intensional* descriptions.

The standard approach (drawing on Quine, Frege, and Kripke at different points) is:

1. Identify the intensional operator creating the apparent violation.
2. Determine whether the attributed property is *de re* (a property of the object itself) or *de dicto* (a property of how the object is described or conceptualized).
3. If *de dicto*, the property is not a genuine property of the object, and Leibniz's Law does not require it to be shared.
4. If *de re*, check whether the attributed property is in fact the same property for both objects — which it must be, if they are identical.

The methodological lesson is constructive: systematic apparent violations of Leibniz's Law in a given domain signal that the domain involves intensionality, and that the predicates in use are description-sensitive rather than purely object-tracking. Mapping out where and why Leibniz's Law holds in full force — and where its application requires care — is itself a significant philosophical achievement that illuminates the structure of our conceptual frameworks.
