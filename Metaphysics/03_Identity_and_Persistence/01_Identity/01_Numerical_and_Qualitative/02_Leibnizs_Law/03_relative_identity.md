# Relative Identity

*Geach's thesis that identity is always relative to a sortal and there is no absolute numerical identity.*

---

When Heraclitus stepped into the Thames twice — or so the legend runs — he stepped into the same river but not the same water. The river persists despite the flow of different molecules; the water is replaced and is not the same water. Here is the puzzle: if we ask simply "is this the same?" without specifying *what kind of thing* we are asking about, the question has no determinate answer. For the river, yes; for the water, no. This observation drives Peter Geach's doctrine of relative identity, one of the most challenging departures from standard logical orthodoxy in twentieth-century metaphysics.

In *Reference and Generality* (1962), Geach argues that identity claims are always made relative to a sortal concept: "a is the same F as b" — the same horse, the same river, the same person. There is no further, sortal-independent sense in which a is simply the same as b. Just as "a is taller" without a comparison class is incomplete, "a is the same" without a sortal is grammatically defective. Every meaningful sameness-claim has the form *a is the same F as b*, for some sortal F. Moreover, Geach asserts that "a is the same F as b" and "a is the same G as b" are logically independent — neither entails the other.

## Motivating Cases

The river case is the paradigm, but others sharpen the point. An amoeba divides to produce two daughter amoebas. Which, if either, is the same amoeba as the parent? Under a biological sortal, perhaps neither; under a material-continuity sortal, both have some claim. If there is only absolute identity, we must give a fact-of-the-matter answer. Geach suggests the question has no unique answer in the absence of a specified sortal. Similarly, a sacred stone worshipped by two tribes, each carving different aspects of it over time, is perhaps the same *stone* throughout but not the same *statue* on each tribe's reckoning. Same F, not same G.

Geach takes a further, more radical step: he claims that relative identity is incompatible with Leibniz's Law as standardly formulated. If a is the same river as b but not the same water, then — on a naive reading — a has the property "is not the same water as b" while b does not. This seems to violate Leibniz's Law. Geach accepts this consequence: relative identity is a non-Leibnizian identity. This makes his view not merely a refinement of standard logic but a revision of it.

## The Transitivity Objection

Critics press a decisive objection. Consider:

- P1. a is the same river as b (at different times, same river).
- P2. b is the same river as c (at yet another time, same river).
- C. a is the same river as c. [Transitivity of "same river"]

If transitivity fails for long chains — if a series of river-identity steps can take us arbitrarily far from where we started — then "same river" is not an identity relation in any logically recognizable sense. It becomes something more like "sufficiently continuous" or "causally connected," relations that are not transitive when extended indefinitely. Geach bites this bullet: relative identity relations need not be transitive across long chains or across shifts in what the sortal covers. The cost is significant — it makes relative identity a much weaker logical tool than absolute identity.

## Wiggins's Response

David Wiggins, in *Sameness and Substance* (1980), defends a modified position that accepts the *dependence* of identity on sortals without accepting Geach's full relativism. Wiggins holds that numerical identity is absolute and obeys Leibniz's Law — there is one identity relation, "=." But the *criteria* for tracking identity through change are always sortal-dependent. To determine whether a is the same individual as b, we need to know what *kind* of thing a and b are, because different sortals carry different identity criteria and persistence conditions.

The appearance of identity being relative, on Wiggins's account, arises from applying different sortals to the same situation — not from there being multiple irreducible identity relations. When we say the river is the same but the water is not, we are tracking the river (a different object from the water) using river-criteria, and noting that the water molecules constituting it have changed. We are comparing different objects — the river and the water — not applying two different senses of "same" to one object. There is one identity relation; it is *applied* through sortal-relative criteria.

## Implications

The debate about relative identity connects to debates about coincident objects (is the statue the same as the clay?), the persistence of organisms (is the post-fission animal the same animal as the pre-fission one?), and the philosophy of personal identity. If identity is relative, then the question "is this the same person as that person?" may have different answers relative to different sortal concepts of person — biological, psychological, social. The radical implication is that there is no fact of the matter about personal identity independent of the sortal we use to track persons.

Most philosophers resist this conclusion, preferring either Wiggins's moderate sortal-dependence-of-criteria (while retaining absolute identity) or an eliminativist account of the problematic cases. But Geach's challenge has permanently enriched the dialectic by making explicit the sortal-relativity of our ordinary identity-tracking practices — a relativity that even those who reject relative identity must acknowledge and explain.
