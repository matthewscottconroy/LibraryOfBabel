# Exercises — Numerical and Qualitative Identity

*Problems on the distinction between numerical and qualitative identity.*

---

**Exercise 1 — Leibniz's Law as a Test**
Use Leibniz's Law to evaluate whether the following pairs are numerically identical or numerically distinct:
(a) The lump of clay and the statue it constitutes (the lump survives squashing; the statue does not)
(b) Cicero and Tully
(c) Pain and C-fiber firing (pain is felt as unpleasant; C-fiber firing is not felt as unpleasant per se)

In each case, identify the property one item appears to have that the other lacks, and assess whether this property is a genuine *de re* property or a *de dicto* attribution.

**Exercise 2 — Relative Identity**
Geach claims "a is the same river as b" does not entail "a is numerically identical with b" in the absolute sense. Construct the strongest argument for this position, then construct the strongest objection. Can you devise a case where relative identity gives the intuitively right answer and absolute identity gives the wrong one?

**Exercise 3 — Frege's Puzzle**
"Hesperus = Phosphorus" is informative; "Hesperus = Hesperus" is not. Both seem to express the same numerical identity. Explain Frege's resolution in terms of sense and reference. Is there another way to explain the difference in informativeness that does not require the distinction between sense and reference?

**Exercise 4 — Counting and Identity**
How many animals are in a room containing a dog, the dog's body, and the dog's brain? Aristotelians say one animal; some anti-coincidentalists say three distinct coincident objects. What criteria determine the answer, and how does the identity question bear on them?
