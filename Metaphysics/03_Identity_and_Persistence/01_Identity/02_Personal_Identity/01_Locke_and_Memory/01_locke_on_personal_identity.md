# Locke on Personal Identity

*Locke's influential account of personal identity as constituted by memory and consciousness.*

---

Suppose a person commits a crime and then, through brain damage, loses all memory of it. Is it just to punish them? The naïve answer — the same body is present, the same brain — seems to say yes. But there is something uncomfortable about punishing a person for an act they cannot remember and, in some sense, cannot own. Locke's intuition is that this discomfort tracks something real: that what makes a person the *same person* across time is not bodily continuity but the reach of consciousness. This intuition is the foundation of the modern debate about personal identity.

John Locke's treatment in *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (Book II, Chapter 27, added in the 1694 second edition) is the founding text. Locke begins by distinguishing three different kinds of identity that are often conflated. *Material substance identity*: a mass of matter is the same substance as long as the same atoms are present; atomic replacement destroys this identity. *Organism identity*: a plant or animal is the same organism as long as the same life persists — the same organization of matter into a unified life-supporting structure, even as cells are replaced and matter exchanged. *Personal identity*: something distinct from both.

A person, for Locke, is "a thinking intelligent being that has reason and reflection, and can consider itself as itself, the same thinking thing, in different times and places." Personal identity consists in *consciousness* — specifically, in the ability to extend one's consciousness to past actions and experiences. The tripartite distinction is philosophically revolutionary: it shows that personal identity need not coincide with material or organic identity. A person can persist even if material substance changes, and can fail to persist even if the organism does.

## The Memory Theory

Locke's positive account: wherever consciousness can reach back, there personal identity reaches. If I can remember performing an action, that action is mine; if I cannot, it may be the action of the same human animal but not of me qua person. We can state this as:

> **(LMT)** Person P at time t₂ is the same person as person Q at time t₁ iff P at t₂ can remember Q's experiences at t₁ (or has a chain of memories linking back to them).

The chain condition is needed to handle cases where direct memory does not reach far enough back. Locke is ambiguous about whether strict direct memory is required or whether an overlapping chain suffices; neo-Lockeans (Grice, Shoemaker, Parfit) made the chain condition explicit.

The thought experiment that dramatizes this is the Prince and the Cobbler. Imagine that the soul (consciousness) of a prince, carrying with it the memories of the prince's past experiences, enters the body of a cobbler. Whom do we have? Locke says: the prince — not the cobbler. The person goes with the consciousness, not with the body. The cobbler's body may look like a cobbler, but it is the prince who is morally responsible for what this body does, because it is the prince who is *conscious* in it. This dramatically separates personal identity from bodily identity: the human animal (the cobbler's body) persists, but the *person* — the unit of moral and legal responsibility — has changed.

## Historical Context and Legacy

Locke is writing against two rival views. Cartesian soul-based identity holds that persons are souls, and personal identity is identity of soul. Locke objects that we have no way of knowing whether the same soul is present; moreover, even if the same soul is present but with no memory connection, it seems wrong to hold the later person responsible for the earlier soul's actions. Bodily identity theories hold that personal identity is bodily continuity. Locke objects that this makes the criminal who loses all memory of their crime the same person as the criminal — yet morally, they seem different, since punishment aims at reformation and deterrence of the *conscious* agent.

The memory theory threads between these: it ties personal identity to what is practically and morally significant (consciousness and memory) rather than to metaphysical substances about which we have limited epistemic access. This insight — that personal identity is constituted by psychological rather than purely biological or metaphysical facts — remains the dominant framework in contemporary philosophy of personal identity, even after the objections of Reid, the refinements of the neo-Lockeans, and Parfit's radical reductionism have transformed the debate almost beyond recognition.
