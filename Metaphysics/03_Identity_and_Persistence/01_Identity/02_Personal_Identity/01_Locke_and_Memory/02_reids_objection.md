# Reid's Objection

*Thomas Reid's circularity argument against Locke's memory theory.*

---

Thomas Reid's objections to Locke's memory theory, developed in *Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man* (1785, Essay III, Chapter 4), are devastating in their simplicity. Reid raises two distinct challenges — a transitivity objection and a circularity objection — and both cut to the logical heart of Locke's account.

## The Brave Officer Case

The transitivity objection arrives through a vivid story. A young boy is flogged for stealing an orchard. As a young officer, he captures an enemy standard and clearly remembers the flogging. As an old general, he clearly remembers capturing the standard but has completely forgotten the flogging. According to Locke's memory theory:

- The young officer is the same person as the flogged boy (he remembers the flogging). Officer = Boy.
- The old general is the same person as the young officer (he remembers capturing the standard). General = Officer.
- The old general is *not* the same person as the flogged boy (he has no memory of the flogging). General ≠ Boy.

But identity is transitive: if A = B and B = C, then A = C. We can formalize the contradiction:

- P1. ∀x∀y∀z((x = y ∧ y = z) → x = z). [Transitivity of identity]
- P2. General = Officer. [Memory theory]
- P3. Officer = Boy. [Memory theory]
- P4. General ≠ Boy. [Memory theory]
- C. Contradiction from P1, P2, P3, P4.

Locke's memory theory, applied naively, violates the transitivity of identity. But transitivity is not a contingent feature of our identity concept — it is a logical theorem, derivable from the axioms of identity. A relation that fails transitivity simply is not identity. The memory theory, then, does not define personal identity; it defines something weaker.

## The Circularity Objection

Reid's deeper objection is that Locke's account is circular in a way that renders it uninformative. Locke defines personal identity in terms of remembering: *I am the same person as the one who did F iff I can remember doing F*. But genuine memory is already a *personal* phenomenon: I remember *my own* past experiences. What distinguishes genuine memory from a mere seeming-memory — a vivid delusion, say — is precisely that genuine memory connects to one's own past. I genuinely remember the flogging iff the flogging happened to *me* — the very person who is now remembering it.

This means that in the definition "Person A at t₂ is the same as Person B at t₁ iff A remembers B's experiences," the word "remembers" already encodes personal identity. A remembers B's experiences only if A = B. To use genuine memory to define personal identity is to invoke personal identity within the definition itself:

- P1. Genuine memory of an action E entails that the person remembering E is the same person as the one who performed E.
- P2. Locke defines personal identity in terms of genuine memory.
- C. Locke's definition presupposes personal identity in the *definiens*, rendering it circular.

## Shoemaker's Response: Quasi-Memory

Sydney Shoemaker (1959) introduced the concept of *quasi-memory* (q-memory) precisely to escape this circularity. Q-memory is defined without presupposing personal identity:

> Person A at t₂ q-remembers an experience E iff A has an apparent memory of E that was caused, in the right way, by E's occurrence.

Q-memory does not require that A is the *same person* as the one who had E — only that A's current memory-state was causally produced by E. The circularity is broken. Neo-Lockean theories then define personal identity in terms of q-memory rather than genuine memory.

The neo-Lockean response to transitivity is parallel: replace direct memory with *overlapping chains* of psychological connections. The general is psychologically continuous with the boy even if he cannot directly remember the flogging, as long as there is a chain of overlapping psychological connections linking them through intermediate stages (the officer who remembers both). Psychological continuity is defined as the ancestral of direct psychological connectedness — and the ancestral of a relation is transitive.

## Residual Problems

Even with these refinements, problems remain. The fission problem is the most pressing: if a person's brain is divided and each half transplanted into a new body, both resulting persons are causally connected (by q-memory) to the original and both are equally psychologically continuous with it. But by transitivity of identity, they cannot both be identical with the original, since they are distinct from each other. So neither is the original — yet the original seems to have survived in two branches. Causal continuity theories cannot resolve this without abandoning the claim that psychological continuity constitutes identity. A further difficulty: what counts as the "right kind" of causal mechanism? If my brain is destroyed and a perfect replica is constructed via some bizarre process, is the replica me? Specifying which causal processes are identity-preserving turns out to be notoriously difficult.

Reid's objections thus generated a productive research program that transformed the simple memory theory into sophisticated neo-Lockean theories of psychological continuity, ultimately culminating in Parfit's Reductionism. The naïve theory could not survive Reid's critique; but the critique itself, by exposing precisely what was wrong, pointed toward the repairs.
