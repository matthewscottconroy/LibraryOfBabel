# Bodily Continuity

*The view that personal identity consists in continuity of the body.*

---

Psychological theories of personal identity face a persistent objection: amnesiac patients remain the same people. A person who suffers complete amnesia — who wakes up remembering nothing of their prior life — has not, by any ordinary standard, ceased to exist and been replaced. They are the same person, bewildered. Yet Lockean theories, at least in their naive forms, struggle to account for this. If personal identity is constituted by memory continuity, the amnesiac seems to be a new person — which is counterintuitive.

Bodily continuity theories offer a different anchor. What makes you the same person over time is not the psychological connections between stages but the continuity of your body — specifically, the persistence of your brain and central nervous system. The brain is the organ of thought, memory, and personality; wherever the brain goes, the person goes. This fits common intuitions about neurosurgical cases and explains why brain death is treated as person-death. The view can be stated formally:

> **(BC)** Person P at t₂ is the same person as person Q at t₁ iff the brain of P at t₂ is physically continuous with the brain of Q at t₁ — connected by a spatiotemporally continuous sequence of brain-matter in normal biological function.

This is a *physical* criterion, not a psychological one: what matters is the material continuity of the brain, not (directly) the psychological states it subserves. Physical continuity of the brain grounds personal identity even when psychological continuity is interrupted — during dreamless sleep, coma, or general anesthesia.

## Advantages Over Pure Psychological Theories

Bodily continuity theories have several advantages over pure psychological theories. They handle amnesia cases cleanly: the brain persists through the amnesia, grounding personal identity even when psychological connections are severed. They handle extreme character change after brain injury: the same organism — the same brain — persists, even if its outputs differ dramatically. They are skeptical about teletransportation scenarios precisely because they require physical continuity, not just the reconstruction of psychological states — which is arguably a feature, not a bug, matching our best scientific understanding of what persons are. And they vindicate legal and medical practice: brain-death criteria for death, organ donation practices, and surgical procedures all presuppose something like bodily (brain) continuity as the criterion of personal identity.

## The Brain Transplant Intuition

The most powerful challenge to bodily continuity comes from a thought experiment. Your brain is removed and transplanted into a new body (Body B). The Brain-Recipient wakes up with all your memories, beliefs, personality, and psychological states. The strong intuition: the Brain-Recipient is *you*. You went with your brain.

But if bodily continuity is what matters, which body is yours? The body from which your brain came is now braindead. Defenders of brain-based bodily continuity handle this by clarifying that "bodily continuity" means continuity of the *brain* specifically, not the whole body — and your brain is the most important part of you for personal identity. The transplant case confirms this: you go with your brain. This shows that the view is actually a *brain continuity* view, not a whole-body continuity view.

## The Bisection Problem

Brain-based bodily continuity still faces the fission puzzle. If the brain is divided and each half implanted into a new body, bodily continuity holds for both resulting persons (each has half the original brain). Yet both cannot be identical with the original:

- Both halves continue bodily (brain) continuity with the original.
- By Leibniz's Law and transitivity of identity, both cannot be the original (they differ from each other in many properties).
- So neither is the original.
- Yet both seem equally good candidates for being the original.

Physical continuity does not solve the fission problem; it only relocates it. The same logical structure that bedevils psychological theories — two successors with equal claims — recurs here.

## Bodily Continuity Without Psychology

A persistent worry is whether bodily continuity is *sufficient* as well as necessary for personal identity. A human animal in a persistent vegetative state (PVS) with no psychological activity whatsoever is biologically alive. If personal identity requires only bodily continuity, the PVS patient is still a person. Many find this uncomfortable — it seems to them that personhood, in the morally and metaphysically significant sense, may already have ended. This suggests that bodily continuity may be necessary but not sufficient. What is also required is the *capacity* for psychological activity grounded in the biological substrate — an irreversible loss of that capacity ending personhood even while the body persists. This caveat brings the bodily continuity view close to animalism, which holds that we are biological organisms and our identity conditions are those of organisms, organisms that include the capacity for psychological life as part of their normal biological functioning.
