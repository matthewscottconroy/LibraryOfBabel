# Animalism

*The view that we are fundamentally biological organisms and that personal identity is animal identity.*

---

Sit back for a moment and consider: there is a human animal in your chair right now. That animal is thinking these very thoughts. You are thinking these thoughts. If there is only one thing in the chair doing the thinking — which there surely is — then you are that human animal. This is the core of the *thinking animal argument*, due to Eric Olson (*The Human Animal*, 1997), and it is the most direct route to animalism: the view that human persons are essentially biological organisms, and that our identity conditions are the identity conditions of organisms.

Animalism is defended by Olson, Peter van Inwagen (*Material Beings*, 1990), and in part by P. F. Strawson (*Individuals*, 1959). It stands in systematic opposition to psychological theories of personal identity. The thinking animal argument targets those theories directly. If you are a *psychological subject* — defined by your psychological continuity — and the animal in your chair is a biological organism defined by its biological continuity, then you and the animal are, in principle, distinct entities. Since psychological and biological criteria can come apart, there are, on psychological theories, two distinct thinkers sharing the same thoughts in the same place. That seems absurd. The most parsimonious conclusion is that you *are* the animal.

On animalism, personal identity just is animal identity. The animalist criterion:

> **(AN)** Person P at t₂ is the same person as person Q at t₁ iff P at t₂ and Q at t₁ are the same biological organism — connected by spatiotemporally continuous life processes.

Psychological continuity may be a *sign* of animal persistence, but it is not constitutive of it. An animal can persist through radical psychological discontinuity — through amnesia, personality change, even through conditions where no psychological states obtain at all.

## Animalism on the Standard Thought Experiments

Animalism generates distinctive, often counterintuitive verdicts on standard cases.

*Brain transplant*: The original animal ceases to exist when its brain is removed. The transplanted brain, placed in a new body, is part of a *new* animal — not the original. Thus the recipient of your brain is *not* you; you died when your organism was destroyed by brain removal. This contradicts the strong intuition that you would go with your brain. Olson accepts this counterintuitive result.

*Fission*: The original animal ceases to exist when its brain is divided — the division ends the biological organism's integrity as a unified living system. Two new animals emerge, neither of whom is the original. Olson accepts this too.

*Teleportation*: The original animal is destroyed and a new animal created at the destination. The teleported "person" is not you; you die in the scanner.

*PVS patients*: An animal in a persistent vegetative state is still alive — the organism persists — so the PVS patient is still the same person. Animalism does not require psychological activity for personal persistence.

## Van Inwagen's Route

Peter van Inwagen's version of animalism arrives by a different road — via his answer to the "Special Composition Question" about when parts compose a whole. Van Inwagen's answer: composition occurs only in organisms (living biological creatures). Non-biological aggregates — statues, tables, chairs — do not really exist as composite objects; only the simples arranged table-wise exist. This radical ontology entails that the only genuine composite objects are organisms. Since persons are composite material things, they must be organisms. Van Inwagen's path to animalism is indirect (via ontological parsimony about composition) but converges with Olson's direct argument.

## Moral Status

Animalism has implications for moral status and moral responsibility. If persons are animals, biological continuity is what grounds moral responsibility — the same biological organism who committed the crime is the one punished. This is in tension with the Lockean intuition that psychological connections, specifically memory, are what morally matter. Olson argues that our intuitions about moral responsibility are not as tightly tied to psychological continuity as Lockeans assume. Even in everyday practice, we hold people responsible for actions they cannot remember — actions performed during blackouts or in early childhood. Biological continuity, not psychological connectedness, is the actual basis of our legal and moral practices. Whether Olson is right about this empirical claim about our practices is a further question.
