# Problems for Animalism

*The main challenges facing the view that persons are identical with biological organisms.*

---

Animalism offers a principled, parsimonious account of personal identity: we are animals, and our persistence conditions are animal persistence conditions. But the view's virtues are inseparable from its costs. The same systematic commitments that make animalism coherent generate verdicts that many find deeply difficult to accept. Three clusters of problems stand out.

## The Brain Transplant Intuition

When your brain is transplanted into a new body, the intuitive verdict is almost overwhelming: *you* go where your brain goes. The recipient of your brain wakes up with your memories, personality, beliefs, and sense of self. It seems natural — indeed obvious — to say that this person is you.

Animalism says otherwise. You die when your organism is destroyed by brain removal. The brain-recipient is a new person — not you. Olson accepts this, arguing that our intuitions in brain transplant cases reflect a confused picture of what persons are. We are animals; animals do not travel with their organs.

The challenge is to explain why, if the brain is the most psychologically important organ — the seat of memory, personality, and consciousness — brain continuity fails to constitute personal continuity. The animalist must say that organ continuity is insufficient for personal continuity even when the organ in question subserves all psychological life. This is a steep commitment, and it requires the animalist to diagnose our strong contrary intuitions as systematic error rather than tracking something real.

## The Too Many Thinkers Problem, Reconstructed

The thinking animal argument — animalism's primary positive case — turns against itself in a different form. Animalism says you are the animal. But consider: the animal existed before you developed psychological capacities (as a fetus or infant) and will persist after you permanently lose them (in PVS). During those periods, is there a *person*? If personhood requires psychological capacities, and animalism identifies persons with animals, then either:

(a) Persons exist without psychological capacities — which animalism accepts but many find it hard to swallow — or

(b) There are periods when the animal is not a person, which raises the question why the person and the animal are ever identified, if they can come apart temporally.

The animalist must either accept (a) with its implications for the moral status of fetuses and PVS patients, or explain why (b) does not reintroduce a distinction between person and animal that undermines the original identification.

## Persons with Non-Animal Bodies

If there were rational creatures made of different materials — silicon-based entities, uploaded digital minds, or the rational beings of Leibniz's monadology — they would presumably be persons. But they would not be animals. Animalism seems too anthropocentric: it identifies the kind *person* with the kind *human animal*, but personhood seems to be a more general notion that applies to any rational, self-conscious, self-governing entity.

The animalist can respond: the correct account of persistence for any kind of person tracks that kind's biological (or functional-biological) identity. A silicon entity's "animalism" would track the persistence of its silicon substrate in its functional organization. But this response makes animalism a special case of a more general functionalist or biological criterion, and loses the distinctive force of the claim that we are *animals* specifically.

## The Fetus/PVS Dilemma

On animalism, the human organism begins at fertilization (or at some early developmental stage). The animal persists through the embryonic and fetal stages. If persons are animals, persons begin at fertilization. Many find this too demanding: fertilized eggs and early-stage embryos lack any psychological states, self-consciousness, or rationality. The animalist must either accept that persons begin at fertilization (with significant implications for the abortion debate) or distinguish between the animal and the person, allowing the animal to pre-exist the person — which undermines the identification of person with animal.

A parallel problem arises at the end of life: an organism in PVS is still alive. If persons are animals, the PVS patient is still a person. Yet many hold that personhood in the morally and metaphysically significant sense has already ended. This suggests that the biological notion of a living organism extends both earlier (embryos, fetuses) and later (PVS patients) than the ordinary notion of a person.

Olson accepts most of these costs as the price of a principled, non-ad-hoc account. He argues that our intuitions in thought experiment cases are unreliable — shaped by self-interest, practical concerns, and ordinary-language habits that do not track the underlying metaphysics. The right account of personal identity is the one that coheres with our best scientific understanding of what we are, and that understanding says we are biological organisms. Moreover, Olson notes, the alternatives — psychological theories and constitution views — face at least as many problems (the fission problem, the too-many-thinkers problem in another guise, the arbitrariness of the causal condition). Animalism's costs are no worse than the costs of its rivals.
