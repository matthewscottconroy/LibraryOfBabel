# Reasons and Persons

*Parfit's reductionist account of persons and its ethical implications.*

---

Derek Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* (1984) is one of the most important philosophical books of the twentieth century. Its third part, on personal identity, advances a *Reductionist* view: personal identity consists in nothing over and above the holding of certain physical and psychological relations. There is no further fact about personal identity beyond those physical and psychological facts.

This is, at first glance, a negative thesis — a denial of something. What is being denied? Parfit calls the rival view *Non-Reductionism*: the view that persons are separately existing entities whose identity constitutes a further fact beyond physical and psychological facts. Non-Reductionism is motivated by deeply felt intuitions. When I wonder whether *I* will survive a surgical procedure, I seem to be asking a question with a determinate answer even if all the physical and psychological facts were fixed. My concern for my future self has a special character — not merely concern for whoever will be psychologically continuous with me but concern for *me* specifically. Personal identity seems to be all-or-nothing: one either survives or one does not.

On the Non-Reductionist view, associated historically with Descartes and phenomenologically with some common-sense intuitions, there is a separately existing *self* or *ego* whose persistence is what personal identity consists in. This self is not reducible to physical or psychological facts — it is a further entity, beyond the brain and body and psychological states.

## Parfit's Argument Against Non-Reductionism

Parfit's argument proceeds through fission cases. Suppose your brain is divided, and each hemisphere is transplanted into a new body. The resulting persons (Left and Right) are each psychologically continuous with you. By hypothesis, all the physical and psychological facts are fixed. The Non-Reductionist says there is a further fact about whether *you* survive — but what could that further fact be? Both Left and Right have equal claims to be you. Four possibilities:

(a) You are Left — but what grounds this, given Left's and Right's symmetrical claims?
(b) You are Right — same problem.
(c) You are both — impossible, since Left ≠ Right and identity is transitive.
(d) You are neither — then you have ceased to exist despite what appears to be twice the normal survival.

No answer is available from within the Non-Reductionist framework. The further fact supposedly grounding personal identity is not there. Therefore, Non-Reductionism is false.

## The Reductionist View and Relation R

Parfit argues that personal identity holds to degrees and can be indeterminate. In fission cases, the question "does the original person survive?" has no determinate answer — not because we lack information but because the facts of the case (psychological continuity into two branches) do not uniquely determine a "yes" or "no." The Reductionist holds:

> **(R)** The correct description of a fission case is: the original person does not survive as a single person, but *Relation R* holds between the original and each of the two successors. There is no further fact about identity.

Relation R is psychological continuity and connectedness holding in the right way — what the neo-Lockean tradition built up through the causal condition and chain requirement. Parfit now applies it in a surprising direction:

> **(WM)** What matters in survival is Relation R, not identity.

In ordinary survival, Relation R holds with identity. In fission, Relation R holds without identity. Since Relation R holds in both cases, what one should care about is preserved in both — and so there is as much reason to look forward to both successors as to look forward to one's ordinary future self.

## The Hume Connection and the Ethical Upshot

Parfit draws a connection to Hume's bundle theory of the self. Hume, in the *Treatise of Human Nature* (Book I, Part IV, Section VI), reported that when he introspected most carefully he could never catch *himself* — only a bundle of perceptions, ideas, and experiences. There was no separately existing *I* behind the experiences; there was only the stream. Parfit endorses a Humean conclusion: when we investigate persons, we find only physical and psychological events in causal relation — brains, bodies, experiences, memories, intentions. There is no separately existing self. The self is a construction or a pattern, not a substance.

This metaphysical conclusion is not academic. If there is no separately existing self, and identity is not what matters, then the self-interest principle — the principle that rational agents give special weight to their own future welfare — lacks its presumed metaphysical foundation. We return to this in the discussion of ethics and identity. The point here is structural: Parfit's metaphysical argument against Non-Reductionism is meant to have direct practical consequences. Getting the metaphysics right changes how we should live and what we should care about.
