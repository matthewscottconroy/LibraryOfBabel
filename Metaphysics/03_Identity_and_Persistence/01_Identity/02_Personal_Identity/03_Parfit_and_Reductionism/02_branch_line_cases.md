# Branch Line Cases

*Parfit's thought experiments about fission, teleportation, and branching that challenge ordinary identity thinking.*

---

Parfit introduces a series of thought experiments not as puzzles to be solved but as instruments for loosening the grip of our ordinary concept of personal identity. Each case is constructed to isolate the features of ordinary survival and reveal that what we care about in survival can come apart from numerical identity. The cases are compelling because they do not require exotic metaphysics — they require only that we think carefully about the logical structure of identity claims.

## The Fission Case

Your brain is divided, and each half is transplanted into a new body (each body receiving one hemisphere). Each resulting person wakes up with roughly half your memories, personality, and psychological continuity. Call them Left-Person and Right-Person. Both are psychologically continuous with you. They are numerically distinct from each other: they are in different bodies and will immediately diverge in experience. By the transitivity of identity:

- P1. If Left = You and Right = You, then Left = Right. [Transitivity]
- P2. Left ≠ Right. [They are distinct persons]
- C. Not both: Left = You and Right = You.

Since Left and Right have exactly symmetrical claims to be you, neither can be preferred. So neither is you. You have ceased to exist.

Yet from the perspective of what happens to you, this seems *better* than ordinary death. In ordinary death, psychological continuity is completely severed. In fission, it is preserved in two branches. How can it be worse than death to survive in two branches rather than one? Parfit's conclusion: identity is not what matters. What matters is psychological continuity and connectedness (Relation R), and this can obtain without unique identity. When I consider my relation to future persons, I should care not about whether they will be *me* but about whether they will have appropriate psychological connections to me.

## The Teleporter Case and the Branch Line

A teleporter records your complete physical structure, destroys your body, transmits the information, and reconstructs you at the destination. Standard assumption: the person who steps out is you. Now consider a variant — the branch-line case: the teleporter malfunctions. The original body is *not* destroyed; both the original and the replica are now alive. There are two qualitatively identical persons: you (on Earth) and the Martian replica.

By physical and psychological continuity, the original is *you* — the original body persists. The replica is psychologically continuous with the original just as much as the original is with itself. But they are two different people.

Here is Parfit's key observation: in the *successful* teleportation case (where the original is destroyed), the situation from the replica's perspective is identical to the branch-line case from the Martian's perspective. A person wakes on Mars with your memories and psychological states. If in the branch-line case we judge the Martian to be a replica (not you), why do we judge the Martian in the successful case to be you?

The only difference is whether the original was destroyed. But if that destruction is what makes the Martian "you" in the successful case, then my survival depends on something *extrinsic to what happens to me* — on whether someone else (a numerically distinct person) was destroyed. This seems wrong. My survival should be an intrinsic matter, determined by what happens in my causal stream.

## The Gradual Replacement Case

Suppose your neurons are replaced one by one with silicon chips that perform the same functions. After 10% replacement, you seem clearly to be you. After 100% replacement, you are entirely silicon — but functionally identical. At what point, if any, did you cease to exist?

If there is a sharp cut-off point (say, at 50% replacement), this seems arbitrary: why should replacement of the 500th chip end your existence when replacement of the 499th did not? If there is no sharp cut-off — if the case is genuinely vague — then identity itself is indeterminate. This challenges the view that physical continuity of the brain is what matters, since the gradual replacement involves no abrupt discontinuity, only a smooth transition from organic to silicon.

Parfit constructs a "combined spectrum" of cases from one extreme (clearly survival: brain carefully maintained and rehoused) to the other (clearly non-survival: complete destruction and replacement by an unconnected person). Along the spectrum, physical and psychological continuity are gradually reduced. If no sharp cut-off is non-arbitrary, identity is vague — a matter of degree. And if identity is vague, then what we care about in survival is the degree of Relation R, not the all-or-nothing of identity.

## Responses

*Lewis's four-dimensionalist response*: In fission, there are two distinct four-dimensional worms that share a common initial segment. Both are "the original" relative to their temporal stages — there were always two overlapping worms. No one ceases to exist in fission; what happens is that two worms are revealed to have been there all along. This preserves identity at the cost of accepting that you were never alone in your body.

*Johnston's "closest continuer" response*: In ordinary cases, identity tracks the closest psychological continuer. In fission, there is no unique closest continuer (both successors are tied), so identity fails to determine a successor — not because identity is indeterminate but because the criteria for identity are not met. This preserves identity as all-or-nothing but accepts that it sometimes fails.

*The animalist response* (Olson): Fission destroys the original animal. Neither successor is the original person. Organisms die when destroyed, and brain bisection destroys the organism. Parfit's conclusion — "identity is not what matters" — is too quickly drawn. The right conclusion is that organisms have ordinary, determinate identity conditions.

These responses show that psychological continuity alone does not constitute identity; uniqueness and causal mechanism must be specified — and even then, edge cases reveal that identity may not be the deep further fact it appears to be.
