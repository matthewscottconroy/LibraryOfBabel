# Ethics and Identity

*The ethical implications Parfit draws from the Reductionist view of personal identity.*

---

Parfit explicitly aims to draw ethical conclusions from his metaphysical views about personal identity. This ambition is itself philosophically significant: it shows that metaphysics is not merely an academic exercise but has practical stakes. The most basic assumption of rational agency — that it is rational to give special weight to one's own future welfare — is, Parfit argues, undermined by the Reductionist account of persons.

Standard decision theory takes self-interest as one of the fundamental reasons for action. A rational agent cares more about her own future welfare than about the welfare of strangers. This asymmetric concern for one's future self is typically taken for granted — it is built into expected utility theory, into prudential reasoning, and into most accounts of rational agency. The justification assumed: one's future self is *identical* to one's present self — it is literally *oneself*.

But Parfit argues that if Reductionism is correct — if identity is not a further fact beyond psychological and physical continuity — the sharp privileging of self-interest over the interests of others loses its presumed foundation:

- P1. The justification for special self-concern is that one's future self is *identical* to one's present self — it is literally *oneself*.
- P2. Identity is not what matters; what matters is Relation R (psychological continuity and connectedness).
- P3. Relation R can hold between oneself and strangers (if closely connected by empathy, shared values, etc.) to nearly the same degree as between oneself and one's future self.
- C. The sharp distinction between self-concern and other-concern lacks its presumed metaphysical grounding.

This does not show that self-concern is irrational, but it does show that the *degree* of concern should be proportional to the degree of Relation R — not rigidly determined by the all-or-nothing of identity. If the seventy-year-old one will become shares fewer psychological connections with one's present self than one's best friend currently does, the metaphysical basis for caring more about that future self than about the friend becomes difficult to state.

## The Non-Identity Problem

Parfit also identifies a profound problem for consequentialist ethics that arises from his metaphysical views. The non-identity problem concerns policies that harm future generations. Suppose that adopting Policy A (heavy industrial development) will cause a lower quality of life for future people, while adopting Policy B (environmental restraint) will cause a higher quality of life. It seems that Policy A wrongs future people. But here is the difficulty: the specific future people who will exist under Policy A are *different people* from those who would have existed under Policy B. Different energy policies mean different social arrangements, different meetings, different conceptions, different people. Under Policy B, different people would have been born. The people harmed by Policy A do not exist under Policy B — they cannot be harmed by a policy that would have prevented their existence. And their lives under Policy A (even if lower quality) are worth living.

This creates a genuine puzzle: Policy A seems clearly worse, but it cannot be said to harm the specific people who exist under it, since under the alternative policy, those very people would not have existed. Parfit's metaphysical views about what constitutes a person make this problem precise. Our ordinary concepts of harm and benefit — which are comparative and person-relative — cannot straightforwardly handle cases where the population itself depends on our choices.

## The Repugnant Conclusion

Parfit also discusses the "Repugnant Conclusion" in population ethics: on certain total utilitarian views, a world of many billions of people living lives barely worth living is morally better than a world of millions of people living excellent lives. This is not directly derived from Reductionism about personal identity, but both the non-identity problem and the Repugnant Conclusion arise from the same attempt to think rigorously about future persons — their numerical distinctness, the conditions of their existence, and our obligations to them. Personal identity and population ethics are deeply intertwined.

## Responses

Nozick argues that the self has a separateness and uniqueness that grounds self-concern regardless of Reductionism. Even if identity holds in degrees, the phenomenological unity of consciousness — the experience of being a single subject — provides reason for self-concern not reducible to degree of Relation R.

Johnston argues that Parfit conflates two questions: (a) what constitutes identity (a metaphysical question) and (b) what justifies self-concern (a practical/ethical question). Even if Reductionism is true about (a), it does not follow that (b) must be revised. Self-concern may be justified by practical norms rather than by metaphysical facts about identity.

Williams argues that the boundary between self and other, even if metaphysically somewhat arbitrary, is practically and psychologically fundamental. Personal projects, commitments, and integrity require a sense of oneself as a distinct agent with special commitments. Reductionism undermines this without adequate justification.

These ethical applications remain contested. But they establish Parfit's central conviction: that getting the metaphysics of persons right has direct implications for how we should live and what we should care about. Metaphysics and ethics are not separate disciplines but aspects of the same inquiry.
