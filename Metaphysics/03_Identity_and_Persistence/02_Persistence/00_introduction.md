# Persistence

How does a thing persist through time? This sounds like a question that answers itself — a thing persists through time by being there, at one time and then at another. But this answer conceals a genuine puzzle about the relationship between an object and its temporal stages.

The simplest view — endurantism — holds that an object is wholly present at each time it exists. When we say that the same leaf is green in summer and brown in autumn, we mean that one and the same object, not just a temporal stage of that object, is first green and then brown. But if Leibniz's Law holds, an object cannot have incompatible properties: being green and being brown (at the same time) are incompatible. The endurantist must locate the incompatibility in temporal qualifications: the leaf is green-at-summer and brown-at-autumn, where these are relational properties that apply to the whole object at different times.

The rival view — perdurantism — holds that objects are four-dimensional wholes extended through time just as they are extended through space. The leaf is not wholly present in summer; what is present in summer is the summer-stage of the leaf, a temporal part that is distinct from the autumn-stage. There is no contradiction in the summer-stage being green while the autumn-stage is brown, just as there is no contradiction in the leaf's left half being wet while its right half is dry.

These are not merely alternative notations for the same facts. Endurantism and perdurantism generate different predictions about persistence through radical change, about the metaphysics of personal identity, about the structure of time, and about the relationship between identity and composition. The chapter develops both views, examines the arguments for each, and introduces the further complication of Sider's stage theory, which holds that ordinary object-talk is most naturally interpreted as quantification over instantaneous stages rather than four-dimensional worms or three-dimensional endurants.
