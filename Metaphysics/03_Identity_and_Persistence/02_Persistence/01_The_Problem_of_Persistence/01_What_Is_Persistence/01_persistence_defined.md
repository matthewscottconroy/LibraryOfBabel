# Persistence Defined

*What it means for an object to persist through time — to exist at more than one time.*

---

Here is a table that was in this room yesterday and is in this room today. We say without hesitation: it is the same table. But what does this mean, exactly? That there is a table-yesterday and a table-today connected by some relation? Or that there is one complete table present on both occasions? Or that there is a table-shaped stretch of spacetime? These are not merely different ways of saying the same thing — they reflect genuinely different pictures of what persistence is.

Persistence is the phenomenon of existing at more than one moment of time. Ordinary concrete things persist: they came into existence at some time and continue to exist at later times. This seems obvious, but the metaphysical analysis of persistence is far from straightforward, and the choice between competing analyses has downstream consequences throughout the theory of identity and change.

## Two Broad Pictures

Two broad views dominate the debate. On *endurantism* (three-dimensionalism), a persisting object is *wholly present* at each moment of its existence. The very same complete object — all of it — exists at time t₁ and also at time t₂. The object is three-dimensional (extended in length, width, and height but not in time in the relevant sense), and it simply *is* the case that this three-dimensional thing exists now and also existed yesterday. There is one object, numerically the same, at different times.

On *perdurantism* (four-dimensionalism), a persisting object is *spread through time* the way a spatially extended object is spread through space. The object has temporal parts (stages) at each moment, and these temporal parts are as real as the spatial parts of a spatially extended object. What we call "the table" is a four-dimensional entity — a "temporal worm" — that has table-shaped stages at each time of its existence.

Terminological note: *persistence* is the generic phenomenon, neutral between these views. *Endurance* names the specific form of persistence on the endurantist view — being wholly present at each time. *Perdurance* names the perdurantist version — existing at multiple times by having distinct temporal parts at each. Kathrine Hawley (*How Things Persist*, 2001) and Ted Sider (*Four-Dimensionalism*, 2001) provide systematic treatments.

## A Third Option: Presentism

A third approach is *presentism*: only the present exists, and past and future objects do not exist at all. On presentism, "persistence" must be analyzed in terms of current facts — that the currently existing table has existed in the past — rather than in terms of an object's existing at multiple times, since past times do not exist. Persistence talk is true, but it is not made true by an object's literal existence at multiple times.

Presentism faces a serious challenge: providing *truthmakers* for past-tensed claims. What makes it true *now* that the table existed yesterday, if past times and objects no longer exist? The presentist must appeal to current dispositional properties, traces, or primitive tensed facts. This is a substantive commitment, and it is not obvious the resources are adequate. There is also tension with special relativity, which denies an absolute present — a point that cuts against both presentism and some versions of endurantism.

## The Ship of Theseus

The paradigm case for thinking about persistence is the Ship of Theseus. The ship's planks are replaced one by one; each replaced plank is set aside and later assembled into a new ship. After all planks have been replaced, two ships are afloat: the sailing ship (with continuous spatiotemporal and functional continuity with the original) and the assembled ship (made from the original planks). Which is the Ship of Theseus?

The puzzle shows that persistence is not trivial: two different criteria of identity — spatiotemporal/functional continuity and material continuity — apply to what we take to be a single persisting object, but they come apart. The endurantist must give a principled account of which criterion takes precedence. The perdurantist can say: the four-dimensional ship-worm tracks the continuous spatiotemporal entity; the reassembled ship is a different worm composed from scattered temporal parts. Neither answer is obviously correct. Persistence conditions are substantive and debatable — which is why the ship of Theseus has been a test case in philosophy for two thousand years and continues to be one.
