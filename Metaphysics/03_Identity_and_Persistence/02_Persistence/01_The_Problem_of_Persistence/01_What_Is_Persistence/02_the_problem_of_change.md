# The Problem of Change

*How an object can have contradictory properties at different times while remaining numerically one.*

---

Socrates was sitting at noon and standing at one o'clock. Socrates is, by hypothesis, one thing — numerically the same person at both times. But being-sitting and being-standing seem mutually exclusive: nothing can be both. How can one object have both? This is the problem of change — sometimes called the problem of temporary intrinsics — and it is one of the central challenges for the metaphysics of persistence.

The puzzle rests on two claims that jointly resist satisfying resolution:

1. *Intrinsicality*: Being-sitting and being-standing are *intrinsic* properties — features of Socrates *in himself*, not relative to anything external. They characterize the shape and posture of Socrates considered alone.
2. *Contradiction*: Socrates cannot simultaneously have contradictory intrinsic properties. Having both would violate the classical principle of non-contradiction.

Together, these claims entail that Socrates cannot be both sitting and standing — yet he is, at different times. The problem arises specifically for intrinsic properties that change. Extrinsic (relational) properties can vary over time without contradiction: Socrates is *taller than Theaetetus* at t₁ and *shorter than Theaetetus* at t₂ (if Theaetetus grows). This is unproblematic because "taller than" is relational. But sitting and standing seem to be properties of Socrates *considered in himself*, not relative to anything else. That is what makes the problem sharp.

## Three Responses

The natural first response is *relativization to times*. Being-sitting and being-standing are not plain properties but relations to times. Socrates is *sitting-at-noon* and *standing-at-one*. These are not contradictory because they are different relations. Leibniz's Law is satisfied: Socrates has sitting-at-noon, and anything identical with Socrates also has sitting-at-noon.

The objection (due to Lewis): this strategy makes all "intrinsic" properties into relational ones. If being-sitting is really being-sitting-at-t, then sitting is not intrinsic after all — it is a relational property involving Socrates and a time. The distinction between intrinsic and extrinsic properties collapses. If shape and posture are extrinsic (time-indexed), what is genuinely intrinsic? We have paid for logical coherence at the price of eliminating the very category — intrinsic properties — that was used to state the problem.

The *temporal parts* response (Lewis's preferred solution) avoids this cost. Socrates is a four-dimensional temporal worm. The noon-temporal-part of Socrates is sitting; the one-o'clock-temporal-part is standing. These are two different objects (temporal parts), each having its property without contradiction — just as the left half of a table being brown and the right half being white is no contradiction. Intrinsic properties remain intrinsic: the noon-stage of Socrates instantiates sitting *simpliciter* — without any time-index, without any relational structure. No single object has contradictory properties.

The cost: positing temporal stages for every persisting object at every moment is ontologically extravagant. And it is unclear that temporal stages are the right kind of thing to be bearers of properties — a momentary stage, with no temporal extension, might not be able to instantiate properties that presuppose duration. Velocity, for instance, is defined as change in position over time; an instantaneous stage seems to have no time over which to change.

*Adverbialism* offers a third path: properties are had "at times" in an adverbial sense, where the temporal modifier attaches to the *instantiation relation* rather than to the property itself. Socrates has sitting *temporally-at-noon*, where "temporally-at-noon" modifies the manner of instantiation rather than making sitting time-indexed. The advantage: properties remain genuinely intrinsic. The objection: adverbialism is a label rather than an explanation. What does it mean to instantiate a property "temporally" or "at-t"? If the instantiation relation itself has a time parameter, we have not escaped the problem — we have moved it from the property to the relation.

## Lewis's Argument for Temporal Parts

Lewis argued that the relativization response is the least satisfactory because it makes temporary intrinsics relational, undermining the distinction between intrinsic and extrinsic properties. His argument:

- P1. Properties like being-spherical and being-dented are genuine intrinsic properties.
- P2. An intrinsic property is one whose instantiation does not depend on relations to other things.
- P3. If being-spherical-at-t is a relation between an object and a time, then whether Socrates is spherical-at-t depends on his relation to t — which makes it extrinsic.
- P4. Therefore, time-relativization makes intrinsic properties extrinsic.
- P5. The temporal parts solution preserves intrinsicality: the spherical stage instantiates being-spherical *simpliciter*, without reference to any time.
- C. The temporal parts solution is superior to time-relativization.

If we accept that the intrinsic/extrinsic distinction is fundamental to metaphysics — as Lewis and many others argue — then this is a compelling reason to adopt temporal parts. The problem of temporary intrinsics thus converts a seemingly commonsense issue (how can one thing change?) into a problem that requires sophisticated metaphysical resources to solve. Lewis uses it to motivate four-dimensionalism as the best solution. The endurantist must respond with a principled account of time-indexed properties that does not make all properties relational — and that task has generated a substantial literature in philosophy of time, mereology, and the metaphysics of properties.
