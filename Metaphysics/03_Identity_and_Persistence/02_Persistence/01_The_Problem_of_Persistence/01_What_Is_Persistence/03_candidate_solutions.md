# Candidate Solutions

*Overview of the main approaches to the metaphysics of persistence.*

---

The persistence debate divides along a fault line: do objects persist by being wholly present at each time they exist, or by having distinct temporal parts spread through time? This question gives rise to four main positions, each with a distinctive account of what objects are, how properties are instantiated, and how change is possible. This file provides a comparative overview; subsequent sections treat each view in depth.

*Endurantism* (three-dimensionalism) holds that objects persist by being *wholly present* at each time of their existence. The same complete object — the whole table — exists now and existed yesterday. Properties are time-indexed relations: "being-sitting-at-t" rather than "being-sitting." Change is a matter of having different time-indexed properties without any inconsistency. Endurantism fits ordinary language and common sense closely: when we say "the same table has moved," we naturally think of one complete thing in two different locations over time. Its core advantage is this match with ordinary experience and phenomenology; its core challenge is Lewis's temporary intrinsics objection — time-indexing seems to make all properties relational. Key defenders include Haslanger (1989), van Inwagen (1990), and Lowe (1998).

*Perdurantism* (four-dimensionalism) holds that objects persist by having distinct temporal parts at each time. The "table" as ordinarily conceived is a four-dimensional object with temporal stages. When a temporal stage is sitting at t₁ and another is standing at t₂, there is no contradiction because these are different stages. Perdurantism fits naturally with special relativity's four-dimensional spacetime and provides clean solutions to fission, coincidence, and branching cases. Its core advantage is elegant handling of temporary intrinsics — intrinsic properties are instantiated *simpliciter* by stages; its core challenge is ontological extravagance (temporal stages for every moment) and the counterintuitive consequence that persisting objects are never wholly present. Key defenders include Lewis (1976, 1986) and Sider (2001).

*Stage theory*, developed by Sider (drawing on Hawley and influenced by Lewis's counterpart theory), is a hybrid view. The *referents* of singular terms like "this table" are instantaneous or brief temporal stages rather than enduring objects or temporal worms. Persistence claims ("the same table was here yesterday") are analyzed via a *temporal counterpart relation* among stages — a relation of qualitative and causal continuity holding between successive stages of what we ordinarily call the same persisting thing. Stage theory handles temporary intrinsics without time-indexing (stages have intrinsic properties *simpliciter*) while preserving the intuition that "this table" refers to a complete present thing. Its core challenge: cross-temporal identity claims become claims about counterpart relations, not strict identity — which seems to change the subject.

*Presentism* holds that only the present exists; past objects do not exist at all. "The table persisted" means that there are (now) past-tensed truths about the table's former existence. This avoids positing temporal parts but faces challenges for truthmakers of past-tensed claims and for special relativity (which denies an absolute present). Key defenders include Bigelow (1996), Zimmerman (1998), and Crisp (2003).

## Comparative Summary

| View | Object at t | Property instantiation | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Endurantism | Wholly present | Time-indexed relations | Different time-indexed properties |
| Perdurantism | Part present (stage) | Simpliciter, by stages | Different stages have different properties |
| Stage theory | Stage is the referent | Simpliciter, by stages | Counterpart stages differ |
| Presentism | Only now | Simpliciter | Past truths without past objects |

## Connections to Other Debates

These views are not independent commitments. Each connects to the broader network of positions in metaphysics. Perdurantism and stage theory presuppose temporal parts, which requires a mereology that extends to the temporal dimension — and universal composition (any collection of temporal stages composes a worm) is commonly assumed by four-dimensionalists but is controversial. Stage theory explicitly extends Lewis's counterpart theory from modal to temporal contexts: the same formal apparatus that handles "I could have been a plumber" handles "I will be tired tomorrow," with temporal counterparts playing the role that possible-world counterparts play modally.

Four-dimensionalism is often claimed to fit naturally with special relativity, which describes the world as a four-dimensional spacetime manifold with no privileged simultaneity. Endurantism's claim that objects are wholly present at a time requires a privileged "now," which special relativity denies. This empirical consideration gives four-dimensionalism a structural advantage, though endurantists have developed responses.

Finally, each view of persistence generates a distinctive account of personal identity: endurantists typically favor psychological or biological criteria for the wholly present person; perdurantists can accommodate branching (fission) by positing overlapping worms; stage theorists follow Parfit in treating "what matters" as a matter of counterpart relations rather than strict identity.
