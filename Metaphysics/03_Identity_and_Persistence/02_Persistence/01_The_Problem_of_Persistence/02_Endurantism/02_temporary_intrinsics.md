# Temporary Intrinsics

*Lewis's argument that endurantism cannot handle intrinsic properties that change over time.*

---

David Lewis's argument from temporary intrinsics is one of the sharpest challenges to endurantism. It begins from a definition: a property is *intrinsic* iff whether something has it depends only on what that thing is like in itself, not on what is outside it or what relations it stands in. Being spherical is intrinsic; being taller than Napoleon is extrinsic. This distinction is fundamental to metaphysics, and Lewis argues that endurantism cannot preserve it.

## Lewis's Argument

Consider a ball that starts spherical and ends dented. At t₁ the ball is spherical; at t₂ it is dented. If the ball is numerically one enduring object wholly present at both times, it seems to have contradictory intrinsic properties: it is both spherical and non-spherical. The endurantist must dissolve this apparent contradiction, and has three main options.

- P1. Being-spherical and being-dented are intrinsic properties of the ball.
- P2. Intrinsic properties do not depend on relations to other things (by definition).
- P3. If the ball endures (is wholly present at t₁ and t₂), the ball has both being-spherical and being-dented — seemingly contradictory properties.
- P4. The three main endurantist responses (time-indexing, presentism, adverbialism) each make the relevant properties relational.
- C. Endurantism cannot handle changing intrinsic properties without either accepting contradiction or making intrinsic properties relational.

## Three Responses, Three Problems

The first response: *time-indexing the property*. Being-spherical-at-t₁ and being-dented-at-t₂ are different properties, neither contradictory. But Lewis objects that if being-spherical is really the relational property being-spherical-at-t, then sphericity is not intrinsic after all — it is a relational property, and the distinction between intrinsic and extrinsic collapses. Haslanger and Johnston respond by distinguishing properties that are *temporally located* (instantiated at a time, where the time specifies when without making the property relational) from properties that are genuinely *relational* (whose instantiation depends on a relation to a distinct entity). Lewis's complaint is that this distinction is not adequately grounded: if a property's instantiation essentially involves a time parameter, the time is a relatum and the property is relational, full stop.

The second response: *presentism*. The ball is spherical *simpliciter* (no time-indexing), and was formerly also spherical and is now dented. Since only the present exists, there is no contradiction: the ball has only one (currently instantiated) intrinsic property. But Lewis objects that presentism handles temporary intrinsics at the cost of denying the existence of past and future, which creates its own problems: what makes it true *now* that the ball was spherical? If past times do not exist, there are no past facts to make past-tensed claims true. And temporal passage seems to require the co-existence of different times — without that, it is unclear what distinguishes the ball's being spherical-then from its being spherical-now.

The third response: *adverbialism*. The ball is spherically-at-t₁ and dentedly-at-t₂, where the temporal modifier attaches to the instantiation relation rather than the property. This preserves intrinsicality of the property but requires a complex account of what "instantiating a property temporally" means. Lewis's objection: adverbialism is a label rather than an explanation. If the instantiation relation itself has a time parameter, the relational character of the time-parameter reappears at the level of instantiation, not the property — a structural problem, not a solution.

## Lewis's Solution: Temporal Parts

Lewis opts for temporal parts: the spherical temporal-part and the dented temporal-part are distinct objects, each having its intrinsic property without contradiction. The spherical stage of the ball is spherical *simpliciter* — no time parameter, no relational property. The dented stage is dented *simpliciter*. No single object has contradictory properties. Intrinsicality is preserved.

The cost is the positing of temporal parts — a significant ontological commitment. But Lewis argues the cost is worth paying to preserve the intrinsic/extrinsic distinction, which is fundamental to many areas of metaphysics. The dialectical significance of the argument is strategic: Lewis uses it to motivate four-dimensionalism as the best solution. Endurantists must respond with a principled account of time-indexed properties that does not make all properties relational — a task that has generated a substantial literature in philosophy of time, mereology, and the metaphysics of properties.
