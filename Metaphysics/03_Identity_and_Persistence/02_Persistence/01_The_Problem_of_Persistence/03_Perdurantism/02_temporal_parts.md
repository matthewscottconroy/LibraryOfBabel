# Temporal Parts

*The nature and ontological status of temporal stages of persisting objects.*

---

What exactly is a temporal part? The question is not merely terminological. Temporal parts are the fundamental ontological commitment of four-dimensionalism, and the status of that commitment — whether temporal parts are genuine concrete entities, useful theoretical fictions, or incoherent postulations — determines whether the four-dimensionalist program succeeds.

A temporal part (or temporal stage) of an object is a thing that is part of the object, exists only for an instant or brief period, and is spatially coincident with the whole object during the time of its existence. Formally, Oₜ is a temporal part of object O at time t iff:

1. Oₜ is a part of O.
2. Oₜ exists at t (and perhaps only at t or during a brief interval including t).
3. Oₜ occupies the same spatial region as O at t.
4. Every part of Oₜ that exists at t is also a part of O at t.

Conditions (1)-(4) make temporal parts analogous to spatial parts: as a spatial part of an object occupies a proper subregion of the object's spatial extent, a temporal part occupies a proper sub-interval of the object's temporal extent. When we say "the apple changed from green to red," the temporal-parts theorist means: the green temporal stage at t₁ and the red temporal stage at t₂ are both parts of the apple-worm, and they differ in color.

## The Ontological Status of Temporal Parts

Lewis treats temporal parts as genuine concrete entities — as real as spatial parts. A temporal stage of the table exists during the time of its existence, occupies the same spatial region as the whole table during that time, has the same intrinsic properties as the whole table during that time, and is a proper part of the four-dimensional table-worm. They are not abstract entities, not mere descriptions, not conceptual constructions — they are concrete physical things that happen to exist for a brief time.

Critics raise two objections to this picture. First, an intuitive objection: we never encounter temporal stages directly — we encounter persisting objects. We say "hello to my friend," not "hello to today's-stage-of-my-friend." The perdurantist responds that we also never encounter spatial parts *directly* in this sense — we encounter complete objects, yet spatial parts are real. The parallel is disanalogous, however: spatial parts can be physically separated from their wholes (a leg detached from a table), but temporal parts cannot be separated. The concept of "removing" yesterday's stage from an object is incoherent. This asymmetry is precisely what the endurantist appeals to.

Second, some philosophers (e.g., Chisholm) accept the *convenience* of temporal-parts talk while denying the ontological commitment. On this view, "the Monday-stage of the table" is useful shorthand but does not refer to a genuine entity distinct from the table itself. This instrumentalist stance preserves the technical apparatus while declining its metaphysical implications.

## Instantaneous vs. Finite-Duration Parts

A key question is whether temporal parts are *instantaneous* (having no finite temporal extent) or have *finite duration*. Instantaneous parts have zero temporal extent — they exist "at an instant." But velocity is defined as change in position over time; any property involving temporal extension (duration, process, change) cannot be possessed by an instantaneous stage. Sider responds by adopting *closed* temporal parts — stages that exist at exactly one instant but are defined as the limit of a sequence of finite-duration stages. Velocity at an instant is then defined mathematically (as a derivative), not as an intrinsic property of the instantaneous stage.

Finite-duration parts have genuine temporal extent but may *overlap* with each other: a one-hour stage and an adjacent one-hour stage share a moment (if the intervals are closed). Standard mereological axioms must be modified to handle overlapping temporal parts. Sider (*Four-Dimensionalism*, Chapter 3) defends instantaneous temporal parts as the fundamental temporal parts, with finite-duration stages being fusions of instantaneous ones.

## Temporal Parts, Mereology, and Fission

The positing of temporal parts connects four-dimensionalism to mereology. Lewis holds that ordinary composition principles — including unrestricted composition — apply across the temporal dimension:

> **(UTC)** For any objects x₁, ..., xₙ existing at (possibly different) times t₁, ..., tₙ, there exists a four-dimensional object whose temporal parts include x₁ at t₁, ..., xₙ at tₙ.

This unrestricted temporal composition gives rise to "arbitrary" four-dimensional objects — worms composed of spatiotemporally scattered temporal parts with no natural unity. Lewis accepts this consequence: the world is full of arbitrary four-dimensional worms, most of which we have no interest in and no names for. The worms we care about (persons, tables, horses) are distinguished by qualitative and causal coherence, not by ontological privilege.

Temporal parts play a central role in Lewis's treatment of fission cases. When a person's brain is divided, there are (according to Lewis) two distinct four-dimensional person-worms that share all their pre-fission temporal parts. Before fission, we referred to "one person" — but we were actually in the presence of two overlapping worms, distinguishable only in virtue of their diverging post-fission stages. Neither worm ceases to exist at fission; both continue. This account dissolves Parfit's puzzle: there is no fact about "whether the original person survived fission" because there were always two persons (two worms) sharing earlier stages. After fission, those two persons are simply distinguishable by their later stages.
