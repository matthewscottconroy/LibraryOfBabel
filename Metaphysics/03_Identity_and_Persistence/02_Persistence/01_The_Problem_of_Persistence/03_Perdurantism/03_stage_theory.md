# Stage Theory

*Sider's temporal counterpart theory and its approach to persistence claims.*

---

Worm theory — Lewis-style four-dimensionalism — says that "this table" refers to a four-dimensional temporal worm, the entire extended object through time. Stage theory says something different: "this table" refers to the *current stage* of the table — the instantaneous or brief entity existing right now. The past table and the present table are not numerically the same object (as the endurantist says) and not numerically distinct parts of a larger whole (as the worm theorist says); they are related by a *temporal counterpart relation*, a relation of qualitative and causal continuity among stages.

Stage theory was developed by Theodore Sider (drawing on work by Katherine Hawley and motivated by Lewis's counterpart theory). Its key move is to import into the temporal dimension the same apparatus Lewis uses in the modal dimension. Just as "I could have been a plumber" is analyzed (on Lewis's view) as "some possible-world counterpart of mine is a plumber," "I was taller yesterday" is analyzed as "some yesterday-stage that is my temporal counterpart was taller." Formally: let TC(x, y, t) mean "x is a temporal counterpart of y at time t." Then:

> **(ST)** "The table now is the same table as the table that was here yesterday" is true iff the current table-stage TC-relates to a yesterday table-stage.

Cross-temporal persistence claims are about counterpart relations among stages, not numerical identity of a persisting object.

## Advantages of Stage Theory

Stage theory combines the theoretical advantages of four-dimensionalism with a reference scheme that is, in one respect, closer to the endurantist's. Against endurantism: stage theory handles temporary intrinsics without time-indexing. The current stage of the table has its properties intrinsically (being-spherical, being-red) without time-indices. A stage is spherical *simpliciter* — no temporal relativization needed. Against worm theory: stage theory can accommodate the intuition that "this table" refers to a *complete present thing*, not a temporal stage of a larger four-dimensional worm. The present table is a complete entity, wholly present; it is just that persistence claims are made via counterpart relations rather than strict identity.

Handling coincidence is one of stage theory's neatest results. At a time when a statue and a lump coincide, they are the *same stage* — there is one stage at that spatiotemporal location. But the statue-sortal and the lump-sortal carry different temporal counterpart relations: the statue-temporal-counterpart relation preserves shape; the lump-temporal-counterpart relation preserves matter. So "the statue exists before the sculpting" is false (no stage earlier than the sculpting is a statue-temporal-counterpart of the current stage), while "the lump exists before the sculpting" is true (a stage of the clay before the sculpting is a lump-temporal-counterpart of the current stage). Two "objects" with different temporal histories are revealed to be one stage associated with two different temporal counterpart relations — an elegant economy.

## The Main Objection: Cross-Temporal Identity

The stage-theorist cannot straightforwardly say that *I will be* the same person tomorrow. I can only say that a future stage will be my temporal counterpart. But this seems to change the subject rather than express a claim about *me* specifically:

- P1. When I make plans for tomorrow, I care about whether *I* will be alive tomorrow.
- P2. On stage theory, "I will be alive tomorrow" means "a temporal counterpart of my current stage will exist tomorrow."
- P3. What I care about is my literal numerical identity with the tomorrow-person, not merely a counterpart relation.
- C. Stage theory misrepresents the subject of future-directed self-concern.

The stage theorist's response: this objection assumes that numerical identity is what matters in self-concern. But following Parfit, numerical identity is *not* what matters — what matters is Relation R (psychological continuity and connectedness). Stage theory formalizes this Parfitian conclusion: the temporal counterpart relation just *is* Relation R. When I care about my tomorrow-stage, I am caring about the appropriate psychological and causal continuant of my current stage — which is exactly what I *should* care about, on Parfit's view.

A further objection: if counterpart relations are contextually variable (as Lewis holds for possible-world counterparts), then whether the same table has persisted may be context-dependent. Different contexts may generate different counterpart relations and thus different verdicts about whether identity-through-time holds. This makes identity seem arbitrary in an uncomfortable way. Stage theory remains a minority view but has attracted significant philosophical attention as a sophisticated hybrid that captures the advantages of both endurantism and perdurantism while raising its own distinctive puzzles about persistence and self-concern.
