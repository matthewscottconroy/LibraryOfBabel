# Review Questions — Persistence

1. Persistence is described as existing at more than one moment of time, yet presentism denies that past and future objects exist at all. What exactly does a presentist mean when they say a table "persisted" through the night, and why is the truthmaker problem particularly acute for them rather than for endurantists or perdurantists?

2. Lewis's temporary intrinsics argument is presented as a reason to prefer perdurantism over endurantism. Reconstruct the argument carefully, then evaluate Haslanger's distinction between "temporally located" and "genuinely relational" properties. Does her distinction successfully preserve the intrinsic/extrinsic contrast, or does Lewis's complaint still bite?

3. Stage theory is described as combining the theoretical advantages of four-dimensionalism with a reference scheme closer to endurantism. Yet its treatment of cross-temporal identity claims as counterpart relations rather than strict identity seems to change the subject when I claim that I will be the same person tomorrow. Does the stage theorist have a principled reply to this objection, and is the reply consistent with Parfit's conclusion that identity is not what matters?

4. The Ship of Theseus presents two rival criteria of persistence: spatiotemporal/functional continuity (the sailing ship) and material continuity (the reassembled ship). How would a perdurantist, an endurantist, and a stage theorist each adjudicate which ship is the original? Does any view give a clearly superior answer, or does each merely relocate the problem?

5. Endurantists are charged with being unable to handle coincident objects (statue and clay) without accepting that two distinct objects occupy the same place at the same time. Perdurantists handle coincidence by positing overlapping temporal worms. Evaluate the force of each response: does the perdurantist dissolve the coincidence problem or merely redescribe it at a different ontological level?

6. The four-dimensionalist framework is said to fit naturally with special relativity's four-dimensional spacetime, while endurantism requires a privileged "now" that special relativity denies. How decisive is this empirical consideration? Are there endurantist responses to the relativity objection, and what would they cost?

7. Lewis's stage theory applies his counterpart-theoretic apparatus from the modal domain (possible worlds) to the temporal domain. The temporal counterpart relation does for "I was taller yesterday" what the possible-world counterpart relation does for "I could have been taller." What are the structural benefits of this parallel, and what structural disanalogies between time and modality might undermine it?

8. Defending endurantism, the chapter invokes parsimony (one object rather than infinitely many stages) and agency (a deliberating stage must be numerically the same as the later acting stage for practical rationality). Assess the agency argument: does perdurantism genuinely fragment agency in a way that prevents a coherent account of deliberation and moral responsibility, or does it only require a revision of how we think about the unity required for those concepts?
