# Constitution and Coincidence

Phidias takes a lump of gold and fashions it into a statue of Athena. Before he begins, there is one thing — the lump. After he is done, there is still one lump. But is there now also a statue? And if so, is the statue identical to the lump, or are they two numerically distinct objects that coincide?

The case for their identity is simple: they are made of exactly the same gold, occupy exactly the same space, and share all their parts. Surely there is just one thing there, not two. The case for their distinctness is equally simple: the lump could survive being melted down and recast; the statue could not survive this. The lump existed before Phidias began sculpting; the statue did not. They appear to have different modal and historical properties, and if they have different properties, Leibniz's Law tells us they are distinct.

But if they are distinct, we have two objects made of exactly the same matter, occupying the same space at the same time. Is this coherent? It seems strange to say that two things can be in the same place at the same time, composed of exactly the same parts, yet numerically distinct. Some philosophers accept coincident objects as a genuine feature of the ontological landscape; others deny the phenomenon by revising either Leibniz's Law or our modal intuitions.

The constitution view — developed by Lynne Rudder Baker and others — holds that the statue is *constituted by* the lump without being identical to it. Constitution is a relation distinct from identity: the lump constitutes the statue without being the statue. This allows us to explain the modal difference (the statue and the lump have different persistence conditions) while accepting that they share their material.

The chapter also takes up the problem of vagueness in identity: are there cases where identity is genuinely indeterminate rather than determinately true or false? The metaphysics of coincidence and vagueness are closely connected, and working through them together illuminates both.
