# The Constitution Relation

A lump of clay and a statue occupy the same region of space at the same time. They share the same particles, the same shape, the same color. Yet they seem to be different things: the lump can survive being reshaped, but the statue cannot; the statue was created when the artist finished sculpting, but the lump existed before. If constitution were identity — if the lump just were the statue — these modal differences would be impossible: a single thing cannot both survive and fail to survive the same event.

Most contemporary philosophers who accept the reality of coincident objects explain them through a constitution relation that falls short of identity. The lump constitutes the statue without being identical to it: they share all their intrinsic properties at the present time (same matter, same shape, same mass) but differ in their essential properties (the lump is essentially lumpy matter; the statue is essentially a representation of a particular kind) and hence in their persistence conditions (the lump survives squashing; the statue does not). Constitution is a many-one relation — many things can constitute a single thing, though in the typical case it is one-to-one.

The constitution view invites the question: what grounds the modal difference between the lump and the statue? If they share all their intrinsic properties here and now, where does the difference in persistence conditions come from? The most common answer invokes sortal concepts: what kind of thing something is determines how it can change while remaining in existence. The lump is a piece of clay — its persistence is governed by the persistence conditions of lumps of clay. The statue is an artifact of a certain kind — its persistence is governed by the persistence conditions of statues of that kind. The same matter can simultaneously constitute objects of different kinds.

Critics of the constitution view worry about "too many things" — the crowd of coincident objects that populate ordinary reality seems metaphysically overcrowded. This section examines the constitution relation in detail, the theoretical motivations for it, and the main alternatives.
