# Constitution vs. Identity

*Why the relation between a statue and its clay is not numerical identity.*

---

Suppose a sculptor shapes a lump of clay into a statue. We now seem to have two things before us — the lump and the statue — though we can lift only one object, measure only one mass, and point to only one location. The puzzle is that both cannot be true: either we have two objects or we have one, and neither answer is immediately comfortable. The temptation to say they are one and the same thing is powerful. But Leibniz's Law establishes that they cannot be identical.

The argument for non-identity:

- P1. If a = b, then ∀F(Fa ↔ Fb). [Leibniz's Law]
- P2. The clay has the property "existed before 2005" (if the clay predates the statue's creation).
- P3. The statue does not have the property "existed before 2005" (it was created in 2005).
- P4. Therefore, the clay ≠ the statue. [By modus tollens on P1 with P2 and P3]

Additional property-differences reinforce the conclusion. Modally: the statue would be destroyed by being squashed into a ball; the lump of clay would not. The statue has the property "would be destroyed by squashing"; the lump does not. Historically: the lump was created by combining two smaller lumps; the statue was created by sculpting. These differences survive the de re/de dicto distinction — they are genuine modal and historical differences in the objects, not artifacts of how they are described.

## The Constitution Relation

The constitution view — defended by Lynne Rudder Baker (*Persons and Bodies*, 2000), Michael Rea, and Judith Jarvis Thomson — holds that constitution is a genuine but non-identity relation: the clay *constitutes* the statue without *being* the statue. The clay and the statue are numerically distinct objects that coincide — they are in the same place at the same time, made of the same matter, but have different persistence conditions and different modal profiles.

Baker's account: x constitutes y at t iff (1) x and y are spatiotemporally coincident at t; (2) x and y are of different kinds (e.g., x is a lump of clay, y is a statue); (3) the kind of y (statue) has a different persistence condition than the kind of x (lump); and (4) if something of x's kind is in the right circumstances, then something of y's kind exists. The constitution relation is not symmetric (the clay constitutes the statue, not vice versa), is grounded in sortal kinds with different persistence conditions, and involves an asymmetric dependency: during coincidence, GOLIATH "borrows" its physical properties from LUMP — is bronze, is heavy, is in the studio — because LUMP is all these things. But GOLIATH and LUMP differ in their intrinsic modal and sortal properties.

## The Main Alternatives

The natural first response is not Baker's but elimination or reduction. *Four-dimensionalism* (Lewis, Sider) holds that the clay and the statue are different temporal worms with different temporal extents. They share temporal stages during the coincidence period but are distinct worms. No mystery about spatial coincidence: two four-dimensional objects share a temporal segment. This avoids positing two coincident three-dimensional objects: instead, two four-dimensional objects share a temporal segment.

*Eliminativism* denies that one or both objects really exist. Van Inwagen-style eliminativism denies the existence of statues (only the clay-simples arranged statue-wise exist). Others deny the existence of unorganized lumps (only the statue — a structured entity with form — is a genuine object). The apparent coincidence disappears when one or both candidates are eliminated from the ontology.

*Relative identity* (Geach, Wiggins) holds that they are the same *bronze* but not the same *statue*. There is no absolute fact of identity or non-identity. This avoids the coincidence problem by denying that the question "are they the same?" has an absolute answer.

*Monism* holds that there is only one object — the bronze in a certain configuration — and our different descriptions ("lump," "statue") pick out different aspects or ways of describing the same thing. The apparent modal difference (the statue can't survive squashing; the lump can) is explained by pragmatic differences in how we describe and care about the object, not by genuine differences in the object itself.

## The Central Challenge for Constitution Theory

The main challenge for the constitution view: explaining how two distinct objects can coincide without violating the intuition that two objects cannot be in exactly the same place at the same time. If two objects are in the same place, made of the same matter, with all their subatomic parts in common, in what sense are they two objects rather than one?

Baker's response: they are distinguished by their *kinds* and the different *persistence conditions* those kinds carry. Being a statue involves having the form and function of a statue — conditions that can be satisfied or fail to be satisfied independently of whether the matter persists. Being a lump involves just having a certain mass of matter in coherent spatial proximity — conditions that differ from those for being a statue. Two objects of different kinds can coincide because different conditions govern their existence. This is an essentially Aristotelian move, connecting constitution theory to the hylomorphic framework — and it requires that kinds generate genuine metaphysical distinctions, not just different descriptions of the same thing.
