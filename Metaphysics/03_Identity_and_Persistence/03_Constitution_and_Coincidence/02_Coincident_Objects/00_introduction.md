# Coincident Objects

The lump and the statue are not the only candidates for coincident objects. If we accept the general picture on which things can be made of the same matter, in the same place, at the same time, while remaining numerically distinct, the consequences ramify. A person and her body seem to coincide: they share all the same matter and occupy the same region. A river and the quantity of water that constitutes it seem to coincide, though the quantity of water changes when any molecule enters or leaves. A forest and the trees that compose it seem to coincide, though the forest can survive the removal of a tree that the collection of trees cannot.

The puzzle of coincident objects is not merely that coincidence is intuitively strange — it is that the standard arguments for coincident objects seem to multiply them without principled limit. The same argument that establishes the lump-statue as a coincident pair will generate coincident pairs whenever matter of one kind constitutes an object of another kind. Since matter is constituted by particles, molecules, atoms, and compounds at various levels of description, the number of coincident objects at any location is potentially enormous.

Opponents of coincident objects have proposed various strategies to avoid them. Four-dimensionalists hold that the lump and the statue are not distinct objects at a time but distinct four-dimensional worm-segments: the lump is the entire spacetime worm, while the statue is a temporal part of that worm. Eliminativists about artifacts hold that there are no statues in addition to the lump — "statue" is a practical concept applied to lumps for artifact-involving purposes, without introducing new objects. Relativists about identity hold that the lump and the statue are the same object considered under different sortals, rejecting the view that identity is absolute.

This section examines these competing strategies and assesses whether any can avoid the counterintuitive consequences of both coincidentalism and its rivals.
