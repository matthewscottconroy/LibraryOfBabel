# Vagueness and Identity

When does a heap of sand cease to be a heap? When does a bald man become not-bald? These are the classic sorites puzzles — puzzles generated by predicates whose application conditions are sensitive to small incremental differences, even though no single grain of sand makes the difference between a heap and a non-heap. Vagueness is a pervasive feature of natural language, and it creates profound difficulties for the metaphysics of identity and persistence.

Consider a material object that gradually loses parts: a ship has planks replaced one by one, until eventually all the original planks have been replaced. At which point — if any — did the original ship cease to exist and a new ship come into existence? If identity over time is an all-or-nothing relation (as formal logic requires), then there must be some precise point at which the old ship ended and the new one began, even if no one can say which point that is. But this seems arbitrary: why should the replacement of one particular plank mark the crucial moment?

The alternative is to allow that identity can be vague — that it can be indeterminate whether the new ship is the same as the old ship. But this position is philosophically dangerous. Gareth Evans's famous argument purports to show that vague identity leads to contradiction: if it is indeterminate whether a = b, then there is a property that a has (the property of being such that it is indeterminate whether it is identical to b) that b lacks (since it is not indeterminate whether b is identical to b), and by Leibniz's Law, a ≠ b.

Most philosophers respond to Evans's argument by locating the vagueness in our descriptions rather than in the world: it is our concept of "ship" or "person" that fails to determine a precise boundary, not the world itself that is vague. But this semantic response has its own difficulties, and some philosophers have argued that vagueness in the world is unavoidable. This section examines these competing responses and their implications for the metaphysics of identity and persistence.
