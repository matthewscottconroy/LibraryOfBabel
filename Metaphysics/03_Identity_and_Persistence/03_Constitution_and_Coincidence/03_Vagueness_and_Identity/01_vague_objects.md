# Vague Objects

*Whether objects themselves can have vague or indeterminate identity.*

---

Where does Mount Everest end? Not at any precise line of rock: the mountain grades into surrounding terrain, and any precise boundary would be arbitrary. But is this vagueness a feature of the *word* "Everest" — a matter of our name having imprecise application conditions — or is it a feature of the *mountain itself*? The first view is semantic vagueness; the second is ontic vagueness. They are genuinely distinct positions, and the dispute between them bears on questions from personal identity (when does a person begin to exist?) to composition (when does adding parts create a new object?).

The standard view is that vagueness is always semantic. When we say "Tibbles the cat is on the mat" in a borderline case, the vagueness is a feature of "on," not of the world. The rival view holds that there genuinely are objects in the world with indeterminate properties or boundaries. A cloud is not merely described with vague terms — it *is* an object with fuzzy spatial boundaries. There is no precise fact (even in principle) about which water molecules are "in" the cloud and which are "out."

## Arguments for Ontic Vagueness

The *arbitrariness argument*: if all vagueness were semantic, every apparent case of vagueness could be resolved by choosing a more precise language. But for many objects, no precise boundary seems *correct* — it would be arbitrary to say the cloud ends at this precise water droplet rather than that one. The most natural explanation is that the cloud genuinely has an indeterminate boundary.

The *intrinsic character argument*: objects seem to have their properties independently of how we describe them. Clouds existed before any language existed to describe them; their boundaries are determined by the physical arrangement of water droplets, not by our descriptions. Since the physical arrangement is genuinely indeterminate (no sharp boundary in the pattern of droplet density), the boundary is ontically vague.

The *phenomenological argument*: vague objects seem to be phenomenologically basic. We encounter clouds, mountains, and persons as bounded entities, and the vagueness of their boundaries is part of their character as we experience them — not a deficiency in our descriptions.

## Arguments Against Ontic Vagueness

*Evans's argument* (discussed in the next file): Gareth Evans (1978) argued that identity statements cannot be indeterminately true or false. If ontic vagueness implies indeterminate identity (as it would if a cloud's boundary were indeterminate, making it indeterminate whether Cloud C = Cloud C'), Evans's argument yields a contradiction.

*The semantic ascent strategy*: any apparent case of ontic vagueness can be re-described as semantic vagueness by ascending to talk about predicates and their application conditions. "The cloud has a vague boundary" can be restated as: "It is vague where our predicate 'cloud' applies." Semantic vagueness is sufficient to explain all the appearances without ontic vagueness.

*The precisification strategy*: for any apparently vague object, all legitimate precisifications of the relevant predicates yield precise objects. Our term "the cloud" indeterminately refers to one of them. There is no single vague object — there are many precise objects, and vagueness is a feature of reference, not of the objects referred to.

## Candidate Examples and the Sorites Connection

Candidate examples of vague objects include clouds (no precise spatial boundary), mountains (where does Everest end?), organisms (at what moment does a fetus become a human organism?), and heaps (at what precise number of grains does a heap begin to exist?). In each case, the developmental or compositional process is continuous; any precise boundary would be arbitrary.

The Sorites paradox generalizes the vagueness problem: for any gradable predicate F, there seems to be no sharp threshold at which F applies. One grain is not a heap; adding one grain to a non-heap does not make it a heap; yet a million grains is a heap. If this pattern extends to existence predicates ("is a heap," "is a person," "is a cloud"), then existence itself may be vague — and therefore objects can have indeterminate existence, which is a form of ontic vagueness.

Barnes and Williams (2011) defend a sophisticated version of ontic vagueness that accepts indeterminate identity in some cases while resisting Evans's argument. Their view treats "determinately" as an operator on propositions rather than on properties of objects, and shows that Evans's argument does not go through in this framework. The debate about vague objects remains active, with implications for personal identity, the metaphysics of composition, and the general theory of categories and kinds.
