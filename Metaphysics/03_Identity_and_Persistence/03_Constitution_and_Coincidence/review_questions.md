# Review Questions — Constitution and Coincidence

1. The Leibniz's Law argument for the non-identity of GOLIATH and LUMP uses modal and temporal property differences. Some philosophers argue that modal properties (what would happen if the bronze were squashed) supervene on categorical properties, so physically indistinguishable objects cannot differ modally. If this supervenience claim is correct, what becomes of the argument that GOLIATH ≠ LUMP, and what must the constitution theorist say in reply?

2. Baker's constitution view holds that GOLIATH "borrows" its physical properties from LUMP — same mass, same location, same chemical composition — yet GOLIATH and LUMP are numerically distinct. Critics ask whether this means the region contains twice the mass. Reconstruct Baker's response, and evaluate whether the notion of "derivation" she invokes is explanatorily adequate or merely labels the puzzle.

3. The Problem of Many (Unger) shows that the statue-lump puzzle is not exotic but infects ordinary objects: any cloud, table, or person seems to admit of indefinitely many near-coincident candidate-objects. Of the responses canvassed — vagueness, eliminativism, epistemicism, supervaluationism — which is most consistent with the chapter's earlier treatment of what makes coincident objects numerically distinct? Are there tensions between accepting constitution dualism for the statue case and accepting any of the Problem of Many solutions?

4. Hylomorphism explains the constitution relation through form and matter: the statue is the bronze organized by the statue-form. How does this framework handle the modal difference between the statue and the lump? Does the hylomorphic account give a more principled grounding for the modal differences than a bare appeal to distinct sortals, or does it just push the question back to what "form" is?

5. Evans's argument concludes that identity statements cannot be indeterminately true or false. The standard response concedes that the property λx.∇(x = a) is description-sensitive and therefore falls outside the scope of genuine Leibniz's Law applications. But if description-sensitive properties are excluded from Leibniz's Law, does this exclusion threaten other uses of Leibniz's Law elsewhere in the module — for example, the use of modal properties to establish GOLIATH ≠ LUMP?

6. Supervaluationism preserves classical tautologies (like the law of excluded middle) at the level of supertruth while allowing individual disjuncts to lack a determinate truth value. One objection is that this makes the truth of a disjunction independent of the truth of its disjuncts. Evaluate whether this consequence is genuinely paradoxical or whether it reflects a principled distinction between the semantic level of evaluation and the logical level of entailment.

7. The constitutionalist (Baker, Wiggins) and the four-dimensionalist (Lewis) both aim to handle coincident objects, but by opposite strategies: the former multiplies distinct three-dimensional objects at the same location; the latter multiplies overlapping four-dimensional worms. What would each view say about a case where the constitutionalist and the four-dimensionalist agree on the conclusion (e.g., that the pre-sculpting lump and the post-sculpting statue are different objects) but disagree on why?

8. Vague objects, the Problem of Many, and coincident objects all challenge the assumption that the world carves cleanly into discrete, bounded individuals. How does the broader picture that emerges from this chapter — of objects with sortal-relative persistence conditions, indeterminate boundaries, and constitution relations — bear on the identity-logical framework developed in the first chapter? Specifically: can the chapter's conclusion that vagueness is best treated as semantic rather than ontic be reconciled with the chapter's acceptance that different sortals generate different, genuine persistence conditions?
