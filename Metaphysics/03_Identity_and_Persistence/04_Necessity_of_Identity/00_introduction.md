# Necessity of Identity

In 1970, Saul Kripke delivered the lectures that became *Naming and Necessity*, and the philosophy of language and metaphysics has not been the same since. Among his most consequential claims was this: if a and b are identical, then necessarily a = b. Identity, where it holds, holds necessarily. The evening star and the morning star are the same object — Venus. This is not a contingent fact. There is no possible world in which the evening star exists and the morning star exists and they are distinct. The identity of Hesperus and Phosphorus is a necessary truth, even if it is not a truth that can be known a priori.

This is striking because it overturns a previously standard assumption: that necessary truths are always a priori knowable, and that a posteriori truths are always contingent. Kripke showed that these epistemic and modal categories come apart. "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is knowable only a posteriori — we had to discover it by astronomical observation — but it is true of necessity: there is no possible world where Hesperus is not Phosphorus.

The argument has profound implications for the philosophy of mind. If mental state type M and brain state type B are identical — if pain is C-fiber firing — then their identity is necessary: there is no possible world in which pain exists without C-fiber firing or C-fiber firing without pain. But this modal claim seems wrong: surely there could be creatures who are in pain without having C-fibers. Kripke uses this intuition to argue against type identity theory in the philosophy of mind. The argument is among the most discussed in twentieth-century philosophy.

This chapter also examines identity across possible worlds — the question of what it is for an individual to exist in more than one possible world. Kripke's framework of rigid designation, Lewis's counterpart theory, and the debate between haecceitism and anti-haecceitism all bear on this question. Sortal dependence — the view that identity is always relative to a sortal concept — provides a different angle, suggesting that "is the same thing" always needs to be completed with "as what kind of thing."
