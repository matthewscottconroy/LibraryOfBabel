# Kripke on Identity

In a series of lectures delivered in 1970 and published as Naming and Necessity, Saul Kripke transformed the philosophy of language, metaphysics, and epistemology simultaneously. At the center of his revolution was a seemingly technical claim about the logic of identity: if two names are both rigid designators — if they refer to the same object in every possible world in which that object exists — then the identity statement containing them, if true, is necessarily true.

The argument is simple but its consequences are profound. "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" are both names for the planet Venus. They are rigid designators: "Hesperus" refers to Venus in every possible world, not merely to whichever object appears in the evening sky in a given world. Since both names pick out Venus, the statement "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is necessarily true — there is no possible world in which Hesperus is not Phosphorus. Yet this was an astronomical discovery; it was not known a priori. Here is a necessary truth that is known empirically, a posteriori.

This combination — necessary a posteriori truths — shattered the traditional assumption that necessity and apriority go together. Before Kripke, it was widely assumed that what is necessary can be known a priori, and what is known empirically is merely contingent. Kripke showed that these are independent dimensions: there are truths that are metaphysically necessary but knowable only through experience. "Water is H₂O" is necessary — there is no possible world in which water is not H₂O — but this is something we discovered through chemistry, not through conceptual analysis.

The implications for the metaphysics of identity are immediate: if any true identity statement between rigid designators is necessarily true, then contingent identity — the identity of x and y being merely a contingent fact — is impossible when the identity is between genuine names. This bears directly on the philosophy of mind: if mental state types are identical to physical state types, that identity is necessary — and that has consequences for the analysis of multiple realizability and functionalism.

This section examines Kripke's argument for the necessity of identity with care, distinguishing the logical, semantic, and metaphysical components, and assessing the implications for philosophy of mind and the broader theory of modality.
