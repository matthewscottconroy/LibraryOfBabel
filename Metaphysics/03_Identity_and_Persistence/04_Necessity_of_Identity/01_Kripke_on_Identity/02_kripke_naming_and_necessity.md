# Kripke's Naming and Necessity

*The core arguments of Kripke's lectures and their implications for identity.*

---

Before Kripke's 1970 Princeton lectures — published as *Naming and Necessity* in 1980 — the dominant view of how names refer was descriptivism: the referent of a name is whatever object uniquely satisfies the associated description (or cluster of descriptions). "Aristotle" refers to whoever taught Alexander and wrote the *Metaphysics* and founded the Lyceum. On this view, the meaning of "Aristotle" is given by such descriptions, and "Aristotle was the teacher of Alexander" is analytically true if "teacher of Alexander" is among the associated descriptions.

Kripke's critique of this picture is comprehensive and devastating. Consider the modal objection first. Even if Aristotle did in fact teach Alexander, he might not have — "Aristotle might not have taught Alexander" is plainly true. But if "Aristotle" just means "the teacher of Alexander," then "Aristotle might not have taught Alexander" means "the teacher of Alexander might not have taught Alexander" — which is either necessarily false or trivially true in a vacuous sense. Descriptivism generates the wrong modal verdicts.

The epistemic objection runs parallel: "Aristotle was the teacher of Alexander" is an empirical claim, not analytic. We could have been wrong about it; it might have turned out that someone else taught Alexander while the man we call Aristotle did something else entirely. But if "Aristotle" means "the teacher of Alexander," the sentence is analytically true. Descriptivism generates the wrong epistemic status. A third problem is the reference-fixing problem: most people associate very few correct descriptions with "Gödel." If descriptivism is right, most uses of "Gödel" fail to refer to Gödel — which seems absurd.

## Rigid Designation

Against descriptivism, Kripke proposes that proper names are *rigid designators*: they pick out the same individual in every possible world (in which that individual exists). "Aristotle" in every possible world refers to Aristotle — the actual man — regardless of what properties he has in that world. Definite descriptions are (typically) *non-rigid* or *flaccid*: "the teacher of Alexander" in a world where someone else taught Alexander refers to that other person, not to Aristotle.

Rigid designation explains why "Aristotle might not have taught Alexander" is not self-contradictory: "Aristotle" rigidly refers to the actual man; the sentence says truly that the actual man might not have taught Alexander. It explains why "Hesperus = Phosphorus" is necessary: both names rigidly designate Venus; in every world where Venus exists, both names refer to Venus, and Venus = Venus.

How does a rigid name get its reference? Not by description (which is flaccid). Kripke proposes that reference is fixed by a causal-historical chain: an initial "baptism" or reference-fixing event, followed by subsequent uses of the name inheriting their reference from earlier uses via a causal chain leading back to the baptism. A speaker using "Aristotle" refers to Aristotle iff their use is causally and historically connected to the original baptism in the right way. This is the *causal-historical theory of reference*.

## Natural Kind Terms

Kripke extends the analysis to natural kind terms: "gold," "water," "tiger," "heat." These terms are also rigid designators — they pick out the same natural kind in every possible world. "Water" rigidly designates H₂O; in every possible world, water is H₂O (even if in some world the watery-looking stuff is XYZ). This generates necessary a posteriori truths:

- "Water is H₂O" is necessary and a posteriori (discovered empirically, not known a priori).
- "Heat is the motion of molecules" is necessary and a posteriori.
- "Gold has atomic number 79" is necessary and a posteriori.

These are *scientific essences* — necessary truths about kinds discovered through scientific investigation. The essentiality is real, not analytic: the world turns out to be structured so that water just *is* H₂O, and this is not a definitional truth but a metaphysical one.

## Implications for Identity

The central implications of *Naming and Necessity* for the theory of identity:

1. *Necessity of identity*: if "a" and "b" are both rigid designators, and a = b, then □(a = b). Identity statements between rigid designators are necessarily true if true at all.

2. *Necessary a posteriori truths*: many identity statements are discovered empirically but hold necessarily. The a priori/necessary distinction must be kept separate.

3. *Essentiality of origin and natural kind*: individuals have essential properties — their origin (from certain parents, certain gametes), their natural kind (Aristotle is essentially human), their qualitative constitution (water is essentially H₂O).

4. *Against contingent identity theories in philosophy of mind*: if mental and physical state types are identical, the identity is necessary — undermining functionalist attempts to maintain contingent type-identity. These arguments permanently changed how philosophers think about names, natural kinds, and modality.
