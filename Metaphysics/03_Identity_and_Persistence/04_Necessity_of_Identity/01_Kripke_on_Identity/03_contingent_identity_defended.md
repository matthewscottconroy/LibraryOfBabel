# Contingent Identity Defended

*Philosophers who have argued that some identity statements are contingently true.*

---

Kripke's argument for the necessity of identity is compelling, but some philosophers have defended the possibility of contingent identity. Their strategies vary, and each reflects a different metaphysical commitment. We should consider these defenses not merely to assess Kripke's argument but because they illuminate the general structure of identity claims and their modal status.

## Strategy 1: Relative Identity

If identity is always relative to a sortal (Geach), then "a is the same F as b" might be contingently true even if names rigidly designate. The water in my glass and the water I drank yesterday are the same *water* but might not have been (the water could have been replaced). This is contingent identity relative to the sortal WATER, not absolute identity.

Kripke's response: relative identity of this form is not absolute numerical identity. The argument for necessity of identity concerns absolute identity — the relation "=" such that a = b implies ∀F(Fa ↔ Fb) and a = a. Relative identity ("same water," "same river") is a weaker, sortal-relative relation that does not satisfy full Leibniz's Law and does not straightforwardly generate necessity results.

## Strategy 2: Stage Theory

If the objects we refer to with names are temporal stages rather than enduring objects (stage theory), and if persistence relations between stages are contingent, then "a = b" — where a and b are stages connected by a contingent persistence relation — is contingently true. Sider's stage theory combined with a non-trivial temporal counterpart relation generates contingent identity-statements in this sense. "I am the same person as the child born in 1985" is analyzed as: my current stage and that child-stage are temporal counterparts. This counterpart relation holds contingently.

But note: this is not a case of two rigid designators both picking out the same individual and the identity being contingent. Rather, the "identity" claim is not a strict identity claim at all — it is analyzed as a counterpart claim. Kripke's argument concerns strict identity between rigidly designated individuals; stage theory reinterprets the claim as not being about strict identity.

## Strategy 3: Material Constitution and Contingent Coincidence

The clay and the statue coincide contingently: they happen to coincide at this time, though they might not have coincided if the clay had been sculpted differently. But this uses "identical" in the loose sense of "coincident," not in the strict sense of numerical identity. On the constitution theory, the clay and the statue are not strictly identical — they are numerically distinct objects that coincide. Their coincidence is contingent, but their non-identity is necessary (since they differ essentially in their persistence conditions). There is no contingent strict numerical identity here; there is only contingent coincidence between numerically distinct entities.

## Strategy 4: The Mind-Body Case

Some philosophers argue that mental states are *contingently* identical with physical states — pain is (contingently) C-fiber firing, in a world-relative sense. Token identity theory holds that each particular pain token is contingently identical with a particular neural event token. For *type* identity, Kripke argues this is impossible: if pain-type = C-fiber-firing-type, this is necessary. For *token* identity, the situation is more complex. If "this pain" and "this neural event" rigidly designate the same event, their identity is necessary. But if "this pain" is a description ("whatever pain is occurring now") rather than a rigid designator, the identity is contingent in the epistemic sense. The standard move: distinguish the *epistemic* contingency of identity statements (we could have been wrong; we discovered the identity empirically) from their *metaphysical* necessity (having been discovered, the identity holds in all possible worlds).

## Why Necessary Identity Seems Counterintuitive

The intuition that some identities are contingent often stems from three sources. First, *conflating epistemic and metaphysical modalities*: "this could have been a different planet" feels true, but it reports an epistemic possibility (about how the description might have applied), not a genuine metaphysical possibility. Second, *confusing descriptions with names*: definite descriptions are non-rigid; swapping descriptions for rigid names changes the modal status of claims. Third, *thinking of identity as a relation between independent entities*: if a and b are thought of as independently existing before their "identification," then their coming together seems contingent. But if a = b, there never were two independent entities — there was always just one.

The most common resolution accepts Kripke's framework and distinguishes: *epistemic contingency* (we might not have discovered that a = b) from *metaphysical necessity* (given that a = b, in every possible world where a/b exists, a = b). The epistemic appearance of contingency is explained by the *modes of presentation* through which we access the identical object. These modes of presentation are contingent (they depend on our observational situation), but the identity of the objects presented is necessary. This resolution preserves Kripke's framework while explaining why necessary identities seem contingent to us.
