# Important Terms and Ideas: Kripke on Identity

**Rigid designator**: A term that picks out the same individual in every possible world in which that individual exists. Proper names (like "Aristotle") and natural kind terms (like "water") are paradigm rigid designators.

**Necessity of identity**: Kripke's thesis: if a = b, then necessarily a = b. If the morning star and the evening star are identical (both are Venus), then they are necessarily identical — there is no possible world where the morning star exists but is not the evening star.

**Contingent identity statements**: Statements of identity that are true but appear to be merely contingent — "The morning star is the evening star" could have been false. Kripke argues this appearance is misleading: the statement is necessary; the contingency belongs to the names picking out different things in different epistemic contexts.

**Epistemic vs. metaphysical possibility**: Kripke's distinction: something is epistemically possible if it is consistent with what we know; metaphysically possible if it could genuinely be the case. The appearance of contingency in identity statements is epistemic, not metaphysical.

**A posteriori necessity**: Kripke's claim that some necessary truths are knowable only through empirical investigation — "Water is H₂O" is necessary (water could not fail to be H₂O) but discovered empirically, not a priori.

**The Barcan-Marcus argument**: The formal argument that in quantified modal logic, if identity holds (Fa and a=b), then it holds necessarily. This pre-Kripkean argument exploits the logic of identity in modal contexts.

**Transworld identity**: The question of whether one and the same individual can exist in multiple possible worlds. Kripke defends transworld identity via rigid designation; Lewis proposes counterpart theory as an alternative.
