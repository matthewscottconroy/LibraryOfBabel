# Transworld Identity

*The problem of what makes an individual the same individual across different possible worlds.*

---

When we say "Aristotle could have been a carpenter," we seem to be saying something about Aristotle himself — that the actual man, the one who wrote the *Metaphysics* and taught Alexander, might have spent his life making furniture instead. This presupposes that Aristotle himself, not merely someone very like him, exists in the possible world where the carpentry happens. But what grounds this? What makes the carpenter in that world *Aristotle* rather than some other individual who happens to resemble him? This is the problem of transworld identity.

The problem is acute because individuals in different possible worlds may differ dramatically in their qualitative properties. In some world, "Aristotle" is a man who spent his whole life farming, having died before meeting Plato. What makes this farmer the *same* individual as the actual Aristotle who wrote the *Metaphysics*? They share some qualitative properties (same origin, same early life), but differ in many others. If we say that qualitative similarity is what grounds transworld identity, we face the question of how much similarity is required and whether a series of small steps could take us from Aristotle to Noah — the Chisholm paradox.

## Haecceitism

For haecceitists, transworld identity is grounded in primitive thisness (haecceity). Aristotle has a haecceity — *being Aristotle* — that persists across all possible worlds where he exists. The individual doing carpentry in another world is Aristotle because he has Aristotle's haecceity, not because of any qualitative similarity to actual Aristotle.

A haecceity is a non-qualitative property: one that only one individual can have, is not definable in purely qualitative terms, and grounds that individual's identity across all contexts. The objection: haecceities are metaphysically extravagant. They are non-qualitative, non-relational properties with no causal or physical role. How could we know which haecceity belongs to which individual independently of knowing which object we are tracking? They explain transworld identity by positing a primitive that stands in need of explanation itself.

## Qualitativism and Origin-Based Criteria

For qualitativists, transworld identity must be grounded in qualitative similarity: the carpentry-world individual is Aristotle iff he is sufficiently similar to actual-Aristotle in the relevant respects. This is close to counterpart theory, but qualitativist transworld identity maintains that it is *Aristotle himself* who exists in the carpentry world, not a mere counterpart.

The relevant similarity can be specified in different ways. *Origin-based*: the individual in w is Aristotle iff he originates from the same parents (the same gametes) as actual-Aristotle. *Continuity-based*: the individual in w is Aristotle iff he is continuous (physically and biologically) with actual-Aristotle across the relevant trajectory of development. *Qualitative cluster*: the individual in w is Aristotle iff he shares enough of actual-Aristotle's qualitative properties to be the closest match.

## The Chisholm Paradox

Roderick Chisholm raised a paradox through a series of examples. Consider two individuals, Adam and Noah. Consider a world w₁ very close to the actual world, where Adam has one property slightly different. Consider w₂ where Adam has two properties different. Continue the series until, at wₙ, the individual has all of Noah's properties and none of Adam's. At what point does the individual cease to be Adam and become Noah?

If there is a precise cut-off point, it is arbitrary: why does property number k mark the boundary rather than k+1? If there is no cut-off — if Adam can survive any degree of property change across worlds — then any individual could be any other individual in some possible world. Kripke's response: transworld identity is constrained by essentialist properties. Adam has *essential* properties (his origin, his biological nature) that he cannot lack in any possible world where he exists. The Chisholm-series reaches a point where the individual lacks Adam's essential properties — at that point, the individual is not Adam. The essentiality of origin provides a principled constraint on transworld identity without arbitrary cut-offs.

## Kripke's Approach

Kripke's rigid designation accounts for transworld identity in a roundabout way: the name "Aristotle" rigidly designates its bearer, so "Aristotle could have been a carpenter" is true iff the individual Aristotle himself exists in some possible world as a carpenter. Which individual is Aristotle across worlds? Kripke is somewhat evasive: we pick out Aristotle in the actual world, and the relevant possible worlds are those where *that individual* — the one we pick out — has different properties. Transworld identity for individuals is in some sense primitive: we do not analyze what makes the individual the same person across worlds; we simply track the individual we start with and ask in what worlds *he* exists with different properties. Lewis's counterpart theory offers a systematic alternative to this picture — the subject of the next file.
