# Counterpart Theory

*Lewis's alternative to transworld identity: individuals exist in only one world, and cross-world claims are about qualitatively similar counterparts.*

---

When I say "I could have been a plumber," I seem to be saying something about *me* — that *I myself* could have had different properties. Lewis denies this. He holds that individuals are *world-bound* — they exist in only one possible world — and that when I say I could have been a plumber, I am saying something about someone else: a counterpart of mine in another possible world who is a plumber. Counterpart theory is Lewis's systematic alternative to transworld identity, and it dissolves the puzzles of transworld identity by denying that any individual exists in more than one world.

Counterpart theory rests on two claims. First, *world-boundedness*: every individual exists in exactly one possible world. There is no transworld identity. Aristotle exists only in the actual world. Second, *the counterpart relation*: cross-world claims about an individual x are analyzed in terms of the counterpart relation. A *counterpart* of x in world w is an individual in w that resembles x sufficiently (in the contextually relevant respects) and resembles x more than any other individual in w does.

"Aristotle could have been a carpenter" is true iff some counterpart of Aristotle in some possible world is a carpenter. Formally, let C(x, y) mean "x is a counterpart of y." Then:

> **(CT)** "x could have been F" is true iff ∃w∃y(y ∈ w ∧ C(y, x) ∧ Fy)

## Advantages of Counterpart Theory

Counterpart theory avoids the puzzles of transworld identity entirely. What grounds the identity of the carpentry-world individual with Aristotle? Nothing — because they are not identical. The carpentry-world individual is a distinct person who happens to be Aristotle's counterpart. The Chisholm paradox does not arise because there is no chain of "same individual" claims leading absurdly from Adam to Noah.

Contextual sensitivity of modal claims is a feature Lewis embraces. What counts as a counterpart is contextually determined. In one context, the relevant similarity for "Aristotle could have been a carpenter" concerns psychological and social characteristics. In another context, it concerns biographical origin. Counterpart theory allows modal claims to be context-sensitive in a principled way.

The theory coheres with Lewis's modal realism: possible worlds are concrete, spatiotemporally isolated universes — as real as the actual world. Individuals, as parts of their worlds, cannot belong to multiple worlds. Counterpart theory is the natural accompaniment to this picture.

## The Intuitive Objection

The main objection is intuitive: when I say "I could have been a plumber," I seem to be saying something about *me* — not about someone else who happens to resemble me. Lewis bites this bullet and argues that the intuitive resistance is based on a confused picture of cross-world identity. The intuition that I could have been a plumber is satisfied by counterpart theory because the counterpart relation is the right kind of relation to underwrite modal truths about individuals. When I care about whether *I* could have been a plumber, what I care about is whether some sufficiently similar individual in some world is a plumber. Counterpart theory captures this concern without the metaphysical mystery of an individual existing in multiple worlds.

## The Counterpart Relation's Vagueness

A structural feature: the counterpart relation is vague and contextually determined. This generates contextual variation in modal truth values. In context C₁ (focusing on psychological similarity), "I could have had different parents" may be true. In context C₂ (focusing on origin), "I could have had different parents" may be false. Critics argue this makes too many modal claims context-dependent — modal truths become facts about contexts and similarity relations rather than stable facts about the world. This seems to undermine the realist interpretation of possible worlds that Lewis wanted.

## Comparison with Kripkean Transworld Identity

For Kripke, transworld identity is primitive and grounded in the actual individual picked out by a rigid designator. The same individual exists in multiple possible worlds. For Lewis, no individual exists in multiple worlds; counterpart relations replace identity. In practice: for Kripke, "Aristotle could have been a carpenter" says something about Aristotle himself — the actual man. For Lewis, it says something about Aristotle's counterpart — a distinct individual in another world. These interpretations yield different verdicts about what is possible for an individual: Kripkean transworld identity constrains possibility by what the actual individual can be; Lewisian counterpart theory constrains possibility by what sufficiently similar individuals in other worlds are. The debate between them is partly a debate about whether we want a robust individual-centered notion of possibility (Kripke) or a relation-centered notion sensitive to context and similarity (Lewis).
