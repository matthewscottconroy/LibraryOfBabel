# Essentiality of Origin

*Kripke's argument that individuals necessarily originate from the particular matter or parents they actually came from.*

---

In *Naming and Necessity*, Kripke argues not only that identity is necessary but that the *origin* of an individual is essential to it. A person could not have had different parents than those they actually had; a table could not have been made from different wood than the wood it was actually made from. Origin is not a contingent feature of an individual's history — it is part of what makes the individual the very individual it is.

Kripke's thought experiment concerns Queen Elizabeth II. Could the Queen have originated from different parents — say, from Harry and Sally rather than from King George VI and Queen Elizabeth The Queen Mother? Kripke's answer: no. The Queen could not have originated from a different sperm and egg than the ones that actually produced her. Any individual who originated from Harry and Sally would be a numerically different individual — not the Queen, but someone else who might have happened to become Queen in that world.

The argument:

- P1. The Queen actually originated from gametes G₁ (egg) and G₂ (sperm) — specific gametes from her actual parents.
- P2. Origin is essential: no individual could have originated from numerically different gametes from those from which it actually originated.
- P3. Any individual originating from different gametes (from Harry and Sally) would be numerically distinct from the Queen.
- C. The Queen is essentially the daughter of her actual parents (in the sense that she could not have originated from different parents).

## The Table Example

The table example makes the same point for artifacts. This particular table (the one Kripke uses while lecturing) could not have been made from a completely different piece of wood. If a table had been constructed from different wood, it would have been a different table — not the same table with different matter, but a numerically distinct artifact. This is the *essentiality of original matter*: what piece of matter something is originally made from is essential to it. Small variations might be tolerable (replacing a few molecules does not destroy identity), but complete replacement of the original matter would produce a different object.

The underlying rationale: if we think of identity in terms of what makes an individual the very individual it is, origin is the most fundamental fact about it. The biological process that produces a person determines which individual comes into existence. A different biological process would have produced a different individual. More formally: there is no world w in which an individual with a completely different origin is numerically identical with the actual Queen. For any such individual x in w, x ≠ the Queen — because the Queen just *is* the individual who originated from those particular gametes in the actual world. This is a claim about the metaphysics of individuality: what makes an individual the particular individual it is, rather than some other possible individual, is its actual origin.

## Objections

*The four-percent problem* (Salmon, Noonan): Kripke's argument seems to allow that the Queen could have originated from slightly different gametes (a small variation in genetic material). But if she could have originated from gametes that are 4% different, and those gametes could have come from gametes that are 4% different again, then by a series of small steps, she could have originated from completely different gametes. The essentiality of origin generates a sorites paradox. Nathan Salmon argues that this shows essentiality of origin admits of degrees or involves vagueness — there is no sharp boundary at which "same gametes" shades into "different gametes."

*The reductio of haecceitism*: if origin grounds transworld identity without haecceities, then origin-essentialism is an alternative to haecceitism. But some philosophers argue that origin-essentialism is itself a disguised haecceitism: saying "the individual with origin O is essentially the individual with origin O" is just saying that the individual has a primitive identity (its origin serves as a haecceity). This reduces origin-essentialism to a form of the view it was meant to replace.

*The transitivity worry*: if A's origin from gametes G₁ and G₂ is essential, and if G₁ had certain causal antecedents, are those antecedents also essential? Can we run origin-essentialism back through generations without limit? Kripke does not specify how far origin-essentialism extends.

## Implications

Essentiality of origin partially grounds what makes an individual the same individual in different worlds. Whatever properties the Queen might have had, she must have originated from those particular gametes. This constrains transworld identity without requiring haecceities: the Queen in world w is the actual Queen iff the individual in w originated from those same gametes. This is a qualitative constraint (gametic origin is a qualitative fact about biological individuals) that serves a haecceity-like function (fixing individual identity across worlds) without positing a primitive non-qualitative thisness. The right account of how far origin-essentialism extends is a matter of ongoing debate, with implications for cloning (are clones numerically identical to their genetic sources?), artificial reproduction, and the beginning of personal identity.
