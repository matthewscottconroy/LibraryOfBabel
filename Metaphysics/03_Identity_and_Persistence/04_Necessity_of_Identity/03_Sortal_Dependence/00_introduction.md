# Sortal Dependence of Identity

Is there a brute fact about whether this river at t₂ is the same as that river at t₁, or does the fact depend on which sortal concept — which kind — we are using to track the object over time? Wiggins's theory of sortal-dependent identity holds that identity claims over time are always implicitly relativized to a kind: "x at t₁ is the same F as y at t₂," where F is a sortal predicate that provides the criteria of identity. There is no bare, kind-independent fact of numerical identity over time; there are only sortal-relative identity facts.

The motivation for sortal dependence comes from cases where it seems unclear how many individuals are present, or whether the individual at one time is the same as the individual at another time, until we fix which kind we are interested in. A quantity of water that flows through a river valley might be the same water (chemical constitution preserved) but not the same river (rivers require stable channels); the atoms in a human body are replaced over time but the person persists. What kind we use to track the entity determines what counts as persistence and what counts as cessation and replacement.

This view has consequences for the metaphysics of identity that challenge the standard logical treatment. Classical logic treats identity as a binary relation — x is identical to y, or x is not identical to y — without any relativization to a third term. If identity is sortal-dependent, then the same two things might be the same F and not the same G, which seems to violate the transitivity and the objectivity of identity. The relativist about identity accepts this consequence; the absolutist about identity holds that there is a single, unrelativized identity relation and that sortal concepts merely provide heuristics for tracking it.

This section examines sortal dependence, Wiggins's positive theory, the debate between relative and absolute identity, and the implications of the choice for the metaphysics of persons, artifacts, and organisms.
