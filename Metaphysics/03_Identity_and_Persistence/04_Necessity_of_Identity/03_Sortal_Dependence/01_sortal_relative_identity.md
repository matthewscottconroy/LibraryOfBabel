# Sortal Relative Identity

*The view that identity conditions depend on which kind (sortal) the object is classified under.*

---

"Is this the same river?" The answer seems to depend on what we are asking about. The Thames that Heraclitus stepped in is the same *river* as the Thames today — same channel, same watershed, same hydrological function — but the *water* flowing through it is different. Is the Thames the same water? No — different H₂O molecules. Is it the same river? Yes. The question "is this the same?" has no determinate answer until we specify the *kind* of thing we are asking about. This observation is the basis of sortal relative identity, developed by Peter Geach (*Reference and Generality*, 1962) and refined by David Wiggins (*Sameness and Substance*, 1980; *Sameness and Substance Renewed*, 2001).

Identity claims, on this view, are always relativized to a sortal concept: "a is the same F as b" — the same river, the same person, the same organism, the same statue. The relation "a is the same F as b" is distinct for each sortal F, and "a is the same river as b" and "a is the same water as b" are two different relations, neither of which entails the other. Formally:

> **a is the same F as b**

where F is a sortal concept (RIVER, PERSON, ORGANISM, STATUE). Two things can be the same F without being the same G.

## Geach's Radical Claim

Geach claims that there is no absolute identity underlying these sortal-relative identities. "A is the same as b" without a sortal is not a meaningful claim. Every meaningful sameness-claim has the form *a is the same F as b*, for some sortal F. This is a radical departure from standard logic. On the standard view, there is one identity relation "=" satisfying reflexivity, symmetry, transitivity, and Leibniz's Law. On Geach's view, "same F" and "same G" are different relations, neither of which is the absolute identity relation.

The logical implication: Geach's relative identity is not transitive in the way absolute identity is. It is possible that a is the same river as b and b is the same river as c while a is not the same river as c — if the river changed course over time in a way that makes step-by-step identity not transitively transmit over long chains. Geach also denies Leibniz's Law for sortal-relative identity. If a is the same river as b but not the same water, then a and b can differ in properties — for instance, a has the property "contains the same water as a" in a way b does not. Geach accepts this consequence: relative identity is a non-Leibnizian identity.

## Wiggins's Moderate View

Wiggins accepts the *dependence* of identity-tracking on sortals without accepting Geach's full relativism. Wiggins holds three things: numerical identity is absolute and obeys Leibniz's Law — there is one identity relation "="; our *epistemic access* to identity facts requires sortal concepts (without a sortal, we cannot determine whether the object encountered now is the same individual as the one encountered earlier); and different sortals carry different criteria of identity through time (RIVER carries continuity of channel and function; ORGANISM carries biological continuity).

For Wiggins, the sortal provides the *criteria* for applying the absolute identity relation, not a relativization of identity itself. When we say "same river," we are applying the absolute identity relation using river-criteria; when we say "same water," we are using water-criteria. These criteria pick out different objects (the river and the water), but the identity relation itself is absolute.

## Key Terminology

Wiggins distinguishes: *sortal concepts* like PERSON, RIVER, HORSE, STATUE that carry with them criteria of individuation (what makes something one person rather than two) and criteria of identity through time; *characterizing concepts* like SLEEPING, WISE, RED that characterize individuals without providing identity criteria ("is this the same sleeping entity?" is not a well-formed question); *phase sortals* like LARVA, INFANT, PRISONER that apply for only part of an individual's existence; and *substance sortals* like HORSE, PERSON, OAK TREE that apply for the whole life of an individual and provide fundamental identity criteria.

## Sortal Dependence and Coincident Objects

Sortal-dependence of identity connects directly to debates about coincident objects: the lump of clay and the statue are the same *physical substance* in one sense but numerically distinct objects in another, because they fall under different sortals with different persistence conditions. The lump is the kind of thing that can survive radical deformation; the statue is not. This connection shows that sortal-dependence is not merely a logical or semantic thesis but has substantive metaphysical implications: objects of different kinds can coincide, have different persistence conditions, and be tracked by different criteria — all without abandoning absolute numerical identity (on Wiggins's view) or by embracing relative identity (on Geach's).
