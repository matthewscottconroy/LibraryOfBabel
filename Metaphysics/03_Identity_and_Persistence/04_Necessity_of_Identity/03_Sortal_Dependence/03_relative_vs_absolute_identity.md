# Relative vs. Absolute Identity

*The debate over whether identity is always relative to a sortal or is an absolute relation.*

---

The central dispute in the theory of identity is between Geach's *relative identity* thesis and the standard *absolute identity* view. The stakes are not merely logical. How we answer the question bears on whether there is a fact of the matter — independent of which sortal we use — about personal identity, coincident objects, and persistence through time.

The standard view, endorsed by Frege, Russell, and virtually all logicians: there is exactly one identity relation — numerical identity — which is a logically primitive equivalence relation satisfying Leibniz's Law. When we ask "is a the same as b?", there is a fact about whether a and b are numerically one and the same thing. The apparent difference between "same river" and "same water" arises from the different *objects* being identified (the river vs. the water), not from different identity relations being applied.

Geach's thesis is that there is no absolute identity underlying the sortal-relative identities. "A is the same as b" without a sortal is not a meaningful claim. Two things can be the same F without being the same G. The logical difference is significant: absolute identity is transitive (if a = b and b = c, then a = c), but relative identity need not be. Geach further denies Leibniz's Law for sortal-relative identity: if a is the same river as b but not the same water, then a and b can differ in properties (specifically, a has the property "contains the same water as a" in a way b does not), and Geach accepts this consequence.

The debate can be framed as a dispute about the logical form of identity statements:

| View | Form of "a is the same as b" | Leibniz's Law? | Transitivity? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Absolute identity | a = b (primitive relation) | Yes | Yes |
| Relative identity (Geach) | Same-F(a, b) (different relation for each F) | No | Not guaranteed |
| Wiggins (moderate) | a = b, tracked by F-criteria | Yes | Yes |

## The Incoherence Objection to Relative Identity

Critics argue that relative identity is logically incoherent:

- P1. If a is the same river as b but not the same water, then a and b are numerically two things (since they differ in at least one property: "being the same water as a").
- P2. If a and b are numerically two things, saying they are "the same river" is misleading — "same river" is not an identity relation but a relation like "belongs to the same river system as."
- C. Relative identity either collapses into absolute identity (with different objects being tracked) or collapses into a non-identity relation (like "belonging to the same river").

Geach must deny P1: for him, "a and b differ in a property" does not entail they are numerically two things, because "numerically two things" presupposes absolute identity. Without absolute identity, the objection is question-begging. This is a coherent response, but it makes relative identity difficult to state without already invoking the absolute identity framework that Geach is rejecting.

## Wiggins's Resolution

Wiggins's moderate position resolves the tension by distinguishing two claims. First: *identity is absolute* — "=" is a single, logically determinate relation, not a family of sortal-relative relations. Second: *tracking is sortal-relative* — to determine whether a at t₂ is the same individual as b at t₁, we need a sortal concept F to fix what criteria apply. The sortal provides the criteria; the identity relation is absolute.

This allows Wiggins to accommodate the intuition that river-identity and water-identity yield different verdicts (they track different objects — the river and the water) while preserving the logical properties of absolute identity. The Heraclitus case is resolved as follows:

- The river Thames at t₁ = the river Thames at t₂. [Absolute identity, tracked by RIVER-criteria]
- The water-in-the-Thames at t₁ ≠ the water-in-the-Thames at t₂. [Absolute non-identity, tracked by WATER-criteria]

No conflict arises because we are comparing different objects (the river and the water), not applying two different senses of "same" to one object.

## Connection to Coincident Objects

The relative-vs.-absolute debate directly bears on coincident objects. If identity is absolute and Leibniz's Law holds, then two coincident objects with different modal properties are straightforwardly non-identical. If identity is relative, then "the lump and the statue are the same bronze" and "the lump and the statue are different statues" can both be true without contradiction — because they express different sortal-relative identity claims. Geach's relative identity thus provides a distinctive solution to the problem of coincident objects that avoids both the multiplication of coincident entities and their elimination.

The debate remains unresolved, partly because it involves both logical questions (about the formal properties of identity) and metaphysical questions (about what grounds individual identity) that are not easily separated. Wiggins's moderate position is widely regarded as the most defensible formulation — preserving both the logical clarity of absolute identity and the substantive insight that identity-tracking is always sortal-guided.
