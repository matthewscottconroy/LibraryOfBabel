# Review Questions — Necessity of Identity

1. Kripke distinguishes necessity (a metaphysical notion) from apriority (an epistemological notion), and uses this distinction to establish the existence of the necessary a posteriori. Reconstruct the argument that "Hesperus = Phosphorus," if true, is necessarily true. At which step does the argument require rigid designation as a premise rather than deriving it as a conclusion?

2. The argument against type identity theory in philosophy of mind runs as follows: if pain = C-fiber firing, the identity is necessary; but we can conceive of pain without C-fiber firing; therefore, the identity does not hold. Kripke explains away the apparent contingency of "Hesperus = Phosphorus" by noting that our modes of access to Venus (morning appearance vs. evening appearance) are contingent. Why, on Kripke's own account, can't the type-identity theorist offer an analogous explanation — that our modes of access to pain (phenomenal character) and C-fiber firing (physical description) are contingent even though the identity is necessary?

3. Lewis's counterpart theory and Kripke's transworld identity give structurally different accounts of what it means for a modal claim to be true about an individual. For Kripke, "Aristotle could have been a carpenter" concerns Aristotle himself in another world. For Lewis, it concerns Aristotle's counterpart — a distinct individual. What is at stake morally and practically in this difference? Does it matter, for any purpose beyond philosophical theory, whether the carpenter in another world is literally me or merely my counterpart?

4. Essentiality of origin claims that the Queen could not have originated from different gametes. Nathan Salmon's "four-percent problem" presses this with a sorites: if small variations in origin are tolerable, a series of small variations might allow any origin at all. Evaluate the strategies available to Kripke for resisting this. Does the sorites problem here parallel the sorites problem for vague objects in the previous chapter, and can the same solutions (supervaluationism, epistemicism) be deployed?

5. Sortal relative identity (Geach) and Wiggins's moderate sortal-dependence are presented as two responses to cases like the river/water distinction. The chapter argues that Wiggins's position preserves the logical properties of absolute identity while accommodating the intuition that different sortals yield different verdicts. Does Wiggins's position actually avoid the charges brought against Geach — particularly the charge that sortal-relative verdicts seem to generate violations of Leibniz's Law — or does it only relocate them?

6. Stage theory (from the Persistence chapter) analyzes "I was taller yesterday" via temporal counterparts, exactly as Lewis's counterpart theory analyzes "I could have been taller" via possible-world counterparts. Given Kripke's critique of the counterpart-theoretic account of transworld claims — that it changes the subject from me to someone else — does the same critique apply to stage theory's treatment of temporal claims? Is there a principled reason to accept temporal counterparts while rejecting modal counterparts, or vice versa?

7. The chapter examines contingent identity as defended through relative identity, stage theory, and constitution/coincidence. Kripke's response in each case is that what appears to be contingent strict identity turns out to be either non-strict identity or an epistemic phenomenon. Assess whether this response is available in the constitution case: when the chapter argues that GOLIATH and LUMP differ essentially in their persistence conditions, is the modal difference between them a case of genuine contingent numerical identity, or is it — as Kripke would insist — a case of necessary non-identity between two distinct objects?

8. Wiggins's substance sortal framework claims that every individual falls under exactly one substance sortal that governs its fundamental persistence conditions. The debate between animalism and psychological theories is, on this view, a debate about which substance sortal — HUMAN ORGANISM or PERSON — is the fundamental kind for beings like us. How does the introduction of substance sortals advance or reframe the personal identity debate from the first chapter? Does it resolve, dissolve, or merely rename the core disagreement between Olson and the neo-Lockeans?
