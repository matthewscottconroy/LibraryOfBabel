# Domain Survey: Causation and Laws

## Overview

Causation and natural laws are among the structural features of reality that make it intelligible. We explain events by citing their causes; we predict future events by appealing to laws that generalize past regularities. Science is constituted largely by the discovery of causal mechanisms and the formulation of laws. Yet despite this central role in our understanding of the world, both causation and lawhood remain deeply contested philosophical concepts. What is it for one event to cause another? Are causes and effects distinct entities, or does causation reduce to something else — patterns of constant conjunction, probability-raising, counterfactual dependence? And when we say it is a law of nature that all massive bodies attract, are we describing a contingent regularity that happens to hold throughout the universe, or a necessitation relation that could not be otherwise given the natures of the things involved?

The debate about causation has been dominated by two traditions that trace back to Hume. **Humean** accounts hold that causation is nothing over and above patterns of constant conjunction (or probabilistic regularities) in the world; there is no "necessary connection" in nature beyond the regularity itself. The mind projects necessity onto events it has observed in regular succession. **Non-Humean** accounts hold that the Humean analysis is inadequate — it fails to capture the directedness of causal relations (the asymmetry between cause and effect), the relevance of causal mechanisms (mere correlation is not causation), and the distinction between laws and accidental generalizations. Non-Humeans posit real causal powers, dispositions, or necessitation relations that constitute causation as something more than regularity.

Laws of nature present an analogous debate. **Regularity theories** (Humean), descending from Mill and most influentially developed by David Lewis as the Best Systems Account, hold that laws are the axioms and theorems of the simplest, strongest systematization of all particular facts. A generalization is a law if it belongs to the optimal deductive system that balances simplicity and strength in describing the Humean mosaic. **Universals theories** (Armstrong, Dretske, Tooley — the DTA account) hold that laws are not generalizations about particulars but relations between universals: it is a law that Fs are Gs because there is a necessitation relation *N(F, G)* that holds between the universals F and G. **Dispositionalist theories** (Bird, Mumford, Ellis) hold that laws flow from the intrinsic natures — the essentially causal dispositions — of natural kinds; laws are metaphysically necessary because things of a given kind must behave as they do, given what they essentially are.

The domain also encompasses **counterfactual theories of causation** (Lewis), which analyze causal claims in terms of counterfactual dependence: *c* causes *e* if had *c* not occurred, *e* would not have occurred. This approach connects causation intimately to the semantics of counterfactuals and thus to the theory of possible worlds. And it encompasses the debate about **dispositions and causal powers** as the basis for an alternative, powers-based ontology in which objects have intrinsic properties that are essentially directed toward their effects — a view that rivals both the Humean mosaic and the DTA account.

## Major Positions and Their Logical Relations

### Theories of Causation

**Regularity theory** (Hume, Mackie): An event *c* causes an event *e* if and only if (i) *c* occurs before *e*, (ii) *c* and *e* are contiguous, and (iii) all events of type C (the type of *c*) are followed by events of type E (the type of *e*). Mackie's INUS condition refines this: *c* is an Insufficient but Necessary part of an Unnecessary but Sufficient condition for *e*.
- *Problem*: spurious correlations — symptoms cause neither each other nor the disease, yet the regularities are real. The barometer's falling does not cause rain, though all barometer-fallings in the relevant circumstances are followed by rain.

**Counterfactual theory** (Lewis 1973, 2000): Causation is analyzed through chains of counterfactual dependence. *e* counterfactually depends on *c* if: had *c* not occurred, *e* would not have occurred. Causal chains connect the cause to the effect by intermediate steps of counterfactual dependence.
- *Problem 1*: **Preemption** — Billy throws a rock, smashing a window; Suzy also throws, but Billy's rock arrives first. Had Billy not thrown, Suzy's rock would have smashed the window. So the window does not counterfactually depend on Billy's throw — yet Billy's throw caused the window to break.
- *Problem 2*: **Overdetermination** — Two fires arrive at a house simultaneously, either sufficient to burn it. Neither is a counterfactual cause of the burning.
- Lewis's late solution (2000): "Influence" — *c* causes *e* if variations in the manner, timing, or whether of *c* would produce corresponding variations in *e*.

**Mechanist/interventionist theories** (Machamer, Darden, Craver; Woodward): Causation is analyzed in terms of interventions — manipulable relations between variables — or in terms of mechanisms, sequences of entities and activities productive of effects.
- Woodward's *Making Things Happen* (2003): *X* causes *Y* (in the interventionist sense) if an ideal intervention on *X* would change *Y*. This is explicitly pragmatic: causation is defined relative to a system of variables and the background context of interventions.
- *Problem*: circularity objections — the notion of an "ideal intervention" seems to presuppose causation.

**Powers/dispositionalist theories** (Shoemaker, Mumford, Martin, Bird): Objects have causal powers — intrinsic dispositions directed toward their manifestations. Fire has the power to burn; glass has the fragility to shatter. Causation is the exercise of a power by one entity on another; the effect is the manifestation of the power.
- *Advantage*: explains the directedness (asymmetry) of causation without appealing to time-direction or possible worlds.
- *Problem*: what grounds the claim that an unmanifested disposition (a glass that is never dropped) is still fragile? The dispositional predicates seem true, but what makes them true if there is no actual manifestation?

**Singularism** (Ducasse, Anscombe): Causation is a primitive, irreducible relation between individual events; no analysis in terms of regularities, counterfactuals, or mechanisms is possible. We perceive causation directly in paradigm cases.
- *Problem*: epistemic — if causation is a primitive unanalyzable relation, how do we detect its absence in spurious correlations? What distinguishes mere sequence from genuine causation?

### Theories of Laws of Nature

**Naive regularity theory** (simple Humeanism): A law is a universal generalization that is true, contingent, and expressed by a "purely qualitative" predicate. "All copper conducts electricity" is a law; "all objects in my pocket are coins" is an accidental generalization.
- *Problem*: how to distinguish laws from accidental generalizations — both are true universal generalizations. The difference seems to be that laws support counterfactuals ("if this were copper, it would conduct") and accidental generalizations do not — but this invokes modality that regularity theorists want to avoid.

**Best Systems Account** (Mill-Ramsey-Lewis, MRL): Laws are the axioms and theorems of the system that best balances simplicity and strength in summarizing all particular facts. A generalization is a law if it appears as an axiom or theorem in every Lewisian Best System.
- *Advantage*: distinguishes laws from accidental generalizations by theoretical role, not metaphysical necessity.
- *Problem*: the choice of the "best system" seems to be mind-relative (which system is simplest depends on our standards of simplicity); Lewis insisted the system must be stated in perfectly natural predicates, but this reintroduces natural properties as a non-Humean primitive.

**DTA account** (Dretske 1977, Tooley 1977, Armstrong 1983): A law is a necessitation relation *N* between universals *F* and *G*: *N(F, G)* makes it the case that all Fs are Gs. Laws are not general truths about particulars but second-order relations between universals that "enforce" regularities at the level of particular things.
- *Advantage*: explains the modal force of laws (counterfactual support) via genuine necessitation between universals.
- *Problem*: **inference problem** (Lewis): what explains the inference from *N(F, G)* to *all Fs are Gs*? The relation *N* seems to be a mysterious middle term. Armstrong's response: *N(F, G)* is a state of affairs that entails the corresponding regularity, though the entailment is a brute metaphysical necessity, not a logical one.

**Dispositional essentialism** (Bird 2007, Ellis 2001): Laws are metaphysically necessary because they flow from the essential natures of natural kinds. Being negatively charged essentially involves having the disposition to repel other negative charges; the law of electrostatic repulsion is not contingent but necessary given what it is to be negatively charged. Laws are not superimposed on passive properties but are constituted by the dispositional natures of the properties themselves.
- *Advantage*: explains why laws could not have been otherwise (given the same properties) without positing DTA-style universals or Best Systems relativization.
- *Problem*: **quid pro quo problem** (Armstrong): if properties are nothing but their causal roles — if charge is constituted entirely by its dispositions — then properties seem to be defined only in terms of other properties, generating a holistic web with nothing to anchor it to concrete reality.

## Key Arguments and Counterarguments

### Hume's Argument Against Necessary Connection

Hume: In any causal sequence, we observe only the temporal succession of events — the cause followed by the effect — but never the "necessary connection" that supposedly holds between them. Since all ideas derive from impressions, and we have no impression of necessary connection in the external world, we have no idea of it. What we call "necessity" is a projection of the mind's habit of expectation onto the world. Therefore, there is no real necessary connection in nature; causation reduces to constant conjunction plus the mind's association of ideas.

**Non-Humean response**: Even if Hume is right that we do not perceive necessary connections, it does not follow that they do not exist — there may be features of the world that systematically elude perception, such as dispositions, powers, and mechanisms that are causally efficacious without being visible. (Harré and Madden, *Causal Powers*, 1975)

### The Inference Problem for DTA Laws

**P1.** Armstrong holds that it is a law that Fs are Gs iff there is a necessitation relation N(F, G) between the universals F and G.
**P2.** From the existence of N(F, G), Armstrong infers that all Fs are Gs.
**P3.** But what licenses this inference? The relation N(F, G) is a second-order fact; the regularity "all Fs are Gs" is a first-order generalization. Nothing in the logical or metaphysical structure of a relation between universals entails anything about individual Fs and Gs, unless N itself is given a further analysis.
**C.** The DTA account relies on an unexplained inference from the existence of a relation between universals to a regularity among particulars.

**Armstrong's response**: The entailment is a brute metaphysical entailment, not a logical one. The nature of the N relation is such that its existence guarantees the corresponding regularity. *Objection*: this response renders N a mysterious, ad hoc posit rather than a genuinely explanatory entity.

### The Causal Exclusion Argument (Kim)

This argument is discussed more fully in Domain 07 (Philosophy of Mind), but it is also a general problem for any dualist ontology:

**P1.** If mental event *m* causes physical event *p*, then *m* is causally sufficient for *p*.
**P2.** Physical event *p* has a sufficient physical cause *p'*.
**P3.** Assuming no systematic overdetermination, *m* = *p'*.
**C.** Mental events that cause physical events are identical to physical events (Kim's eliminative conclusion), or mental causation is excluded by the physical (the exclusion conclusion).

### Counterfactual Preemption

**Billy** (B-rock) and **Suzy** (S-rock) both throw; B-rock arrives first and breaks the window; S-rock would have broken it otherwise.

On Lewis's original counterfactual analysis: *Had Billy not thrown, the window would still have broken* (Suzy's rock would have broken it). So the window does not counterfactually depend on Billy's throw. But Billy's throw caused the breaking.

**Lewis's late response** (2000): The window's breaking *would have been different* (in timing, manner) had Billy not thrown — the breaking counterfactually depends on the *manner* of Billy's throw if not on its occurrence. This is "influence" rather than simple counterfactual dependence.

**Objections**: Schaffer argues that influence analysis fails for "trumping preemption" — cases where the preempting cause completely mirrors the preempted cause in influence but is still the preemptor. Influence analysis generates wrong verdicts.

## Key Papers and Books

1. **Hume, David, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748), Sections IV–VII** — The founding text for regularity accounts; the argument against necessary connection in nature.

2. **Lewis, David, "Causation" (1973)** and **"Causation as Influence" (2000)** — The definitive versions of the counterfactual theory; the 2000 paper responds to preemption problems.

3. **Armstrong, D. M., *What is a Law of Nature?* (1983)** — Systematic defense of the DTA account; the most thorough treatment of universals-based theories of laws.

4. **Woodward, James, *Making Things Happen* (2003)** — Develops the interventionist account of causation; the most influential recent alternative to both Humean and dispositionalist accounts.

5. **Bird, Alexander, *Nature's Metaphysics* (2007)** — Systematic defense of dispositional essentialism; argues that all fundamental properties are purely dispositional and laws are metaphysically necessary.

6. **Mackie, J. L., *The Cement of the Universe* (1974)** — Classic treatment of causation; develops the INUS condition and the contrast between singular and general causation.

7. **Dretske, Fred, "Laws of Nature" (1977)** — One of the three founding papers of the DTA account; clear statement of the universals-based theory.

8. **Collins, John, Hall, Ned, and Paul, L. A., eds., *Causation and Counterfactuals* (2004)** — The essential anthology for the counterfactual theory and its rivals.

## Live Debates and Open Questions

1. **Causal relata**: What kinds of things are cause and effect? Lewis uses fine-grained possible events (Kim-style property-instances at spacetime regions); Davidsonian approaches use coarse-grained concrete events; the powers view suggests that tropes or property-instances are the fundamental causal relata. The choice affects the formulation of all causal theories.

2. **Causal asymmetry**: Why does causation run from past to future and not the reverse, given that the fundamental laws of physics are time-symmetric? Interventionists ground asymmetry in the asymmetry of intervention possibilities; Lewis grounded it in the asymmetry of overdetermination; powers theorists may ground it in an intrinsic temporal directionality of dispositions.

3. **Probabilistic causation**: Most real-world causal claims are probabilistic: smoking causes cancer but does not guarantee it. Probabilistic theories of causation (Suppes, Cartwright) analyze causation as probability-raising. Problems: probability-lowering causes, Simpson's paradox, and the challenge of distinguishing causal probability-raising from mere correlation.

4. **The Humean mosaic and quantum mechanics**: Quantum entanglement seems to involve non-local correlations that violate Humean independence assumptions. If the fundamental ontology of the world includes entangled states, the Humean "mosaic" of local particular facts may not be well-defined.

5. **Mechanisms in the special sciences**: Biology, neuroscience, and psychology explain by describing mechanisms — sequences of entities and activities. Does this constitute a distinctive ontological category (mechanisms), or can it be reduced to fundamental causal facts?

## Connections to Other Domains

**Causation/Laws and Ontology (Domain 01)**: The DTA account requires universals; the dispositionalist account requires causal powers as real properties; the Best Systems Account requires natural properties as the primitive input. The ontological commitments of one's theory of laws directly feed into one's general ontology.

**Causation/Laws and Modality (Domain 05)**: Counterfactual analyses of causation depend on the semantics of counterfactuals, which in turn depends on the metaphysics of possible worlds. Laws are connected to modality: laws support counterfactuals, while accidental generalizations do not. Dispositional essentialism holds that laws are metaphysically necessary, directly connecting the metaphysics of laws to modal metaphysics.

**Causation/Laws and Free Will (Domain 08)**: Causal determinism — the thesis that every event is causally necessitated by prior events together with the laws of nature — is the threat that generates the free will problem. The character of determinism depends on what laws are and what causation amounts to.

**Causation/Laws and Metaphysics of Science (Domain 10)**: The reduction/emergence debate is a debate about whether the laws of higher-level sciences can be derived from, or supervene on, the laws of physics. This requires analyzing what laws are and what inter-level causation amounts to.

**Causation/Laws and Philosophy of Mind (Domain 07)**: Mental causation — the question of how mental states cause physical effects — is one of the central puzzles in philosophy of mind, and its analysis depends on a prior account of causation (counterfactual, powers-based, or regularity) and of how mental properties relate to physical laws.
