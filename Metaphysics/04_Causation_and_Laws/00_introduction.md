# Causation and Laws

Strike a match and it lights. Release a stone and it falls. Heat a gas and it expands. These are causal sequences — events that produce, necessitate, or bring about other events. We rely on causal reasoning constantly: in medicine, when we identify what causes a disease; in engineering, when we design systems to produce desired effects; in law, when we determine who caused a harm. Causal reasoning is so pervasive in science and ordinary life that it seems unproblematic — until you try to say precisely what causation is.

David Hume posed the challenge with characteristic sharpness. We never perceive causation directly; we see one billiard ball strike another and the second begin to move, but we do not see the causing. All we observe is constant conjunction: events of one type are regularly followed by events of another. From this observation alone, Hume argued, we cannot derive any concept of *necessary* connection — of one event genuinely making another happen. The "cement of the universe," as he called it, may be more like wallpaper paste: it looks solid until you examine it closely. If Hume is right, the causal structure we take ourselves to be discovering in the world may be largely a projection of our habits of expectation.

Whether Hume is right about this is among the most consequential questions in metaphysics, because the answer shapes how we understand natural laws. Are laws of nature genuine necessities — constraints that the world *must* obey — or are they merely patterns, the world's actual regularities summed up in compact equations? The Humean tradition holds that laws are nothing over and above regularities; the necessitarian tradition insists that there must be something in nature — natural necessity, causal powers, nomic universals — that makes the regularities hold. This disagreement has ramifications across the philosophy of science, the metaphysics of mind, and fundamental physics.

This unit examines causation and natural laws across four chapters. The opening chapter surveys the major **theories of causation**: the regularity view inherited from Hume, the counterfactual analysis that became dominant through Lewis's work (roughly: C causes E if E would not have occurred without C), the mechanistic approach that locates causation in underlying physical processes, and the revived interest in causal powers — intrinsic dispositions of things that actively produce effects. No single analysis commands consensus, and understanding why illuminates what causation fundamentally is.

The second chapter turns to **laws of nature** and the debate about their status. The Humean "Best Systems Analysis" treats laws as the axioms of the most informationally efficient and simple systematic summary of all the world's particular matters of fact — laws are not necessities but optimal descriptions. Anti-Humean views, particularly Armstrong's necessitarianism and the powers ontology of Ellis and Bird, treat laws as expressing genuine modal connections between properties, or as consequences of the intrinsic natures of the things that instantiate them. The differences between these views are not merely verbal; they bear directly on whether the world could have had different laws, and what it would mean if it did.

**Dispositions and powers** receives dedicated attention as a chapter in its own right, because the revival of powers ontology is among the most significant recent developments in metaphysics. If objects have genuine causal powers — if glass is intrinsically fragile, if charge essentially repels — then laws of nature may be grounded in the natures of things rather than in Humean patterns. This raises questions about what dispositions are, how we distinguish genuine from merely nominal dispositions, and whether science can be fully understood through the lens of dispositional essences.

**Probability and chance** rounds out the unit by examining objective probability — the kind of chance that, if it exists, is not a matter of our ignorance but a genuine feature of the physical world. Quantum mechanics appears to be irreducibly probabilistic; whether that appearance corresponds to something fundamental about nature, or whether hidden variables restore determinism, is a live question. Interpreting objective probability requires distinguishing it from epistemic probability, developing adequate theories of its role in laws, and reckoning with the implications of chance for rational belief.

The thread connecting all these discussions is a single question: what kind of a thing is the world's causal-nomic structure? Is it built into the fabric of reality, or is it something we read in from outside?
