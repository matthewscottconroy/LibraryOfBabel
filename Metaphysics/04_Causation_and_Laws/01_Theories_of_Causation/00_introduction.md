# Theories of Causation

Causation is everywhere in our explanatory practices, yet it has proven extremely difficult to say what it is. We say that the fire caused the building to collapse, that the vitamin C caused the patient's recovery, that the assassination of Franz Ferdinand caused the First World War. In each case we imply not just that one event occurred before another, but that the first produced, necessitated, or brought about the second — that there is a genuine connection between cause and effect that goes beyond mere temporal succession.

Hume's skepticism about this apparent connection has structured every subsequent discussion. Hume argued that what we actually observe is constant conjunction: whenever an event of type C occurs, it is followed by an event of type E. We never observe the *necessity* of this succession; we infer it from repeated experience and project it onto the world as if it were a genuine feature of things. If Hume is right, causation is not a feature of the world but a habit of expectation — a psychological response to constant conjunction rather than a metaphysical relation.

The Humean tradition in contemporary metaphysics — exemplified by Lewis's counterfactual analysis — attempts to give a rigorous account of causation without invoking non-Humean necessitation. On Lewis's view, C causes E if and only if E is counterfactually dependent on C: if C had not occurred, E would not have occurred. This analysis is elegant and connects causation to our practices of counterfactual reasoning, but it faces a battery of problems: preemption cases (where two independent sufficient causes compete), overdetermination cases (where two causes are jointly or severally sufficient), and cases involving double prevention.

Anti-Humean alternatives posit genuine causal powers or mechanisms in nature. Causal power theories hold that objects have intrinsic dispositions to produce effects — glass has a power to break when struck, aspirin has a power to relieve headaches — and that causation consists in the exercise of these powers. Mechanistic theories hold that causation requires an underlying physical process connecting cause and effect. Process theories, associated with Salmon and Dowe, identify causation with the transmission of conserved quantities along continuous processes.

This chapter develops each major theory of causation, evaluates the objections to each, and asks what the persistent difficulty of finding an adequate analysis reveals about the nature of causation itself.
