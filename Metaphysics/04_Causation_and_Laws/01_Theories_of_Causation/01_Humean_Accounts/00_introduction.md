# Humean Accounts of Causation

Hume's analysis of causation is one of the most influential and most contested moves in the history of philosophy. The philosophical tradition before Hume assumed that causation is a real, intrinsic relation between events — that when fire causes pain, there is a genuine necessitating connection between the two. Hume denied that any such connection can be observed: we see the fire, we feel the pain, we observe that fire-types are regularly followed by pain-types, but we never observe any third thing — the necessary connection — linking the two events. The idea of necessary connection, Hume concluded, derives not from an impression of anything in the external world but from the mind's habit of expecting the second event after experiencing the first.

Hume's positive account of causation — the regularity theory — holds that causation consists in nothing more than constant conjunction: c causes e if and only if events of c's type are regularly followed by events of e's type, and c is spatiotemporally contiguous with e and precedes it in time. This eliminates mysterious necessary connections and grounds causal facts in the pattern of observed regularities. The price is that causation is deflated: a single-case claim that this particular event caused that particular event is true only in virtue of a general regularity, and where there is no regularity there is no causation.

The neo-Humean program — associated above all with David Lewis — seeks to preserve the spirit of Hume's analysis while using possible-worlds semantics to give a more sophisticated account. Lewis's counterfactual theory holds that c causes e if and only if had c not occurred, e would not have occurred. This captures the intuition that causation involves dependence, while remaining consistent with the Humean commitment to a world of distinct existences with no intrinsic necessary connections. The analysis generates a rich account of causal asymmetry, preemption, and overdetermination — though not without difficulties.

This section examines the Humean tradition of causal analysis, from Hume's original regularity theory through the neo-Humean counterfactual approach, with attention to both the theoretical motivations and the main counterexamples.
