# Hume's Two Definitions of Causation

Watch the billiard ball. One ball rolls toward another, makes contact, and the second rolls away. You witnessed a cause and its effect — or did you? Hume's great question is precisely what you actually saw. Not the ball's motion, surely; you saw that. But the causing — the force, the compulsion, the production of one event by another — where exactly did you observe that? Look as carefully as you like at the single collision, and you will find only succession and contiguity. The ball moves, contact occurs, the second ball moves. That is all.

Out of this careful observation Hume draws his first definition. A cause is "an object followed by another, where all objects similar to the first are followed by objects similar to the second" — causation as constant conjunction, a regularity of succession among event types. But Hume immediately offers a second definition, and the fact that he cannot rest with the first is what makes his account philosophically electric. On the second definition, a cause is "an object followed by another, whose appearance conveys the thought to the other." Here the focus has shifted from world to mind: the "necessary connection" we think we observe is actually the felt determination of the mind to pass from the thought of cause to the thought of effect, a habit produced by repeated observation.

Formalized, the two definitions run:

- **D1**: C causes E iff (i) C is spatiotemporally contiguous to E, (ii) C is temporally prior to E, and (iii) all events of the same type as C are followed by events of the same type as E.
- **D2**: C causes E iff (i) C is spatiotemporally contiguous to E, (ii) C is temporally prior to E, and (iii) the appearance of an event of the type of C produces a determination of the mind to form the idea of an event of the type of E.

D1 describes the world; D2 describes the mind's response to the world. If D1 were fully adequate, D2 would be redundant. That Hume offers both tells us something important: he found neither alone satisfying, and the gap between them opens onto everything interesting.

## The Historical Occasion

Hume's account was shaped by the tradition he was dismantling. Descartes and Leibniz had maintained that causal relations are grounded in necessary rational connections derivable by the intellect. Malebranche denied genuine creature causation altogether — all causal efficacy belonged to God alone (occasionalism). Locke retained a looser notion of powers, still speaking as if objects possessed real causal virtues. Against all of them, Hume pressed the empiricist demand: show me the impression. If no impression of necessary connection can be found in experience, the concept cannot be legitimately formed.

This is the Copy Principle at work: every genuine idea traces back to some sensory impression that is its source. Applied to causation, it generates a dilemma. Either we have a genuine impression of necessary connection in the world — and Hume challenges anyone to identify it — or the idea of such a connection is not a legitimate idea at all, but a fiction we project onto experience.

## What the Gap Between the Definitions Suggests

Three influential readings of Hume turn on how we interpret the gap.

The deflationary reading (Stroud, Beauchamp and Mappes) holds that the two definitions are complementary perspectives on a single phenomenon. D1 gives the external, objective pattern; D2 gives the psychological basis of our causal concept. Neither alone is the full account.

The skeptical realist reading (Galen Strawson) argues that Hume accepted that there must be some objective necessary connection in the world — he was skeptical only about our epistemic access to it. The two definitions then mark the gap between what there is (unknown power) and what we can know (constant conjunction plus psychological determination).

The projectivist reading (Blackburn) takes Hume as an early projectivist: the necessity we attribute to causal relations is projected onto the world from our psychological responses. D1 describes the stimulus; D2 describes the projected content.

## The Core Argument

Hume's argument against necessary connection as a real external feature can be stated with unusual precision:

- **P1**: Every genuine idea must be derived from a corresponding impression.
- **P2**: We have no impression of necessary connection between distinct events in any single observed instance.
- **P3**: The impression we have of "necessary connection" is internal — the felt determination of the mind after repeated observations.
- **C**: The idea of necessary connection, as applied to external events, is not a genuine impression-derived idea about the world; it is a projection of a psychological state.

This argument is devastatingly clean. It does not deny that causes produce effects. It denies that "production" picks out anything in the external world beyond constant conjunction.

## Objections Worth Marking

Three objections have shaped the subsequent debate. Thomas Reid pressed that we directly perceive the exercise of causal power in our own volitions: when we will to move a limb, we experience the power in a way that mere regularity cannot capture. Hume's response is that we experience the succession of willing and moving, but the "power" we feel is still just the determination of the mind — not an observable necessary connection between volition and movement.

A contemporary objection notes that the two definitions are not equivalent: there are cases of constant conjunction without causation (two synchronized clocks showing the same time) and cases where we would attribute causation in the absence of established regularity (unique, singular events). Hume's response would be to deny the second category — all causation requires law-backed regularity. That response is exactly what the singularist tradition, running from Anscombe onward, refuses to grant.

Anscombe's challenge goes to the heart of D1: we see the knife cut, the ball break the window. Causation is observable in individual instances. Regularity is neither necessary nor sufficient. This launches a direct assault on the Humean program — not by finding a subtle flaw in the argument, but by insisting that the phenomena do not support the initial framing.

What unites both of Hume's definitions is the absence of any reference to necessary connection as a mind-independent, external feature. The philosophical tradition after Hume has in large measure been an attempt either to vindicate this reductionism (Mackie's INUS conditions, Lewis's counterfactual theory, Pearl's do-calculus) or to reject it (Armstrong, Anscombe, Molnar, Bird). The two definitions mark the opening of a debate still far from settled.
