# Constant Conjunction and Its Role in Hume's Theory

Consider the billiard ball again. Ball A rolls, strikes Ball B, and Ball B moves. We have watched this happen many times — in this room, in other rooms, with different billiard balls of different weights and materials. What have all these observations taught us? According to Hume, exactly one thing: that such collisions are regularly followed by such movements. Not that they must be, or that there is some force linking the motion of one ball to the motion of another. Just: they always are. The pattern in our experience is all there is. This is the doctrine of constant conjunction, and it is the core of Hume's first definition of causation.

There is nothing more to what we observe — no hidden bond, no necessary connection, no productive force — beyond the pattern of succession. The "cement of the universe" (Mackie later borrowed the phrase admiringly) cannot be seen, touched, or measured. If we ask what grounds the causal claim that A caused B to move, the Humean answer is: only the regularity.

## The Pattern and the Psychological Turn

Hume's epistemological argument for this proceeds in two stages. In the first stage, he examines a single causal instance — the collision — and finds only three components: contiguity in space (cause and effect are adjacent, or connected by a continuous chain), succession in time (the cause precedes the effect), and constant conjunction (events of this type have always been followed by events of the effect type). Nowhere in any single observation does Hume find an impression of necessary connection in the external world.

In the second stage, Hume identifies where the sense of connection does originate: in the mind's own response to repeated observation. After watching many instances of fire followed by heat, the mind forms a habit of inferring from fire to heat. The felt "impression of necessary connection" is this internal feeling of expectation — the customary determination of the mind to pass from one idea to another. We then project this psychological compulsion onto the world and mistake it for an external feature.

We can state the constant conjunction account formally:

- **CC**: C causes E iff (∀x)(∀y)[(x is of the same type as C ∧ y immediately follows x) → y is of the same type as E]

A universal generalization over event types. Its virtues are parsimony and falsifiability. Its vulnerabilities are the standard problems for any regularity account — and they are serious enough that the doctrine has not survived without significant modification by any of its successors.

## Consequences of the Reductionist Picture

What Hume calls "power," "efficacy," "force," and "energy" — the terms of art in natural philosophy — turn out on this picture to have no referent in the external world beyond regularities. These are descriptions of our mental habits of inference, not features of external things. To say that aspirin has the power to relieve pain is to say that aspirin-taking is regularly followed by pain relief — nothing more.

The consequences are radical, and Hume accepted them. This reductionism has seemed simultaneously liberating and deeply unsatisfying. It strips causation of a mysterious metaphysical component. But it also seems to underdescribe what is happening when an explosion causes destruction. The intuition that the explosion did something to the building — that there was real causal action, not merely a succession — is hard to capture in mere constant conjunction.

## The Objections That Bite Hardest

Notice that if C is always followed by E, then trivially all E-type events are preceded by C-type events. The regularity runs symmetrically, but causation does not. Hume's response — add temporal priority as a separate condition — merely relocates the problem: why do causes precede effects? The answer cannot itself be that causes-preceding-effects is the regularity we observe, since that would be circular.

The epiphenomenon problem cuts deeper still. Two symptoms of the same disease may be constantly conjoined without either causing the other. A barometer falling is constantly conjoined with storms but does not cause them; both are caused by a common factor. Constant conjunction alone cannot distinguish genuine causes from epiphenomena or from effects of common causes. Reichenbach's screening-off principle attempts to handle this: if Z is a common cause of C and E, then P(E | C & Z) = P(E | Z) — conditioning on Z renders C and E independent. This is a probabilistic refinement of the constant conjunction framework, but it already imports more structure than bare regularities contain.

Finally, the single-case problem: some causal events are unique. If causation requires constant conjunction, we must find regularities at some level of generality for every singular causal claim. But the appropriate level of generality is always contestable and sometimes unavailable. The first use of a nuclear weapon caused widespread destruction — how do we specify the type of which this is an instance?

## Historical Influence

Hume's constant conjunction account was the starting point for the entire empiricist tradition in the philosophy of causation. Mill's methods refined and extended it. Mackie's INUS conditions gave it greater flexibility. Lewis replaced it with counterfactual dependence while remaining within the broadly Humean, non-modal spirit. Pearl's causal Bayesian networks are, in a certain sense, the most sophisticated development of the Humean idea that causal structure is a feature of patterns among events, discoverable from observational data. That all of these represent developments of, rather than departures from, Hume's core claim is a tribute to how much the constant conjunction account put in place — and to how much it left for the tradition to struggle with.
