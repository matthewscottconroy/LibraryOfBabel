# Mackie's INUS Conditions

Here is the situation we actually face when we investigate a house fire. The cause was a short circuit — or so the investigator says. But was a short circuit sufficient for the fire? No: there was also flammable material, oxygen, and no working sprinkler system. Remove any of these and the fire would not have occurred. Was the short circuit necessary for the fire? No: arson or a lightning strike could equally have started it. Yet we do not think the causal attribution is wrong. What, then, are we actually saying when we call the short circuit the cause?

This is J.L. Mackie's opening question in "Causes and Conditions" (1965) and *The Cement of the Universe* (1974), and it motivates his central contribution: the INUS condition. A cause, on Mackie's analysis, is an **I**nsufficient but **N**ecessary part of an **U**nnecessary but **S**ufficient condition. The formulation is deliberately technical, but the insight is natural: real causal factors are typically neither sufficient nor necessary on their own, but they occupy a determinate role in a larger complex that does suffice.

## The Framework in Detail

Let A, B, C... be factors that together form a minimally sufficient condition for effect E — a conjunction where no proper subset is sufficient on its own. Then A is an INUS condition for E iff A is an insufficient but necessary part of at least one unnecessary but sufficient condition for E. More precisely: there exists a conjunction ABC such that (1) ABC is sufficient for E — whenever A, B, and C all obtain, E follows; (2) ABC is unnecessary for E — E can occur through other sufficient conditions such as DEF; (3) A is necessary within ABC — removing A leaves BC insufficient; (4) A alone is insufficient for E.

Trace through the house fire example. The short circuit (A) combines with flammable material (B), oxygen (C), and absent sprinklers (D) to form a minimally sufficient condition: any house with all four features will catch fire if the circuit shorts. This sufficient condition is unnecessary — arson or lightning could also cause the blaze. A is necessary within the sufficient condition — without the short circuit, B, C, and D together would not have produced the fire. And A alone is insufficient. The short circuit is therefore an INUS condition, and this, Mackie says, is what we mean when we say it "caused" the fire.

The full schema:

- **(inus)**: A is a cause of E iff A is an INUS condition within at least one member of the set of minimally sufficient conditions for E, and at least one such sufficient condition was actually instantiated.

## Causal Selection and Pragmatics

Notice that oxygen (C) qualifies as an INUS condition by exactly the same analysis. It is equally an insufficient but necessary part of the very same sufficient condition. Yet we name the short circuit as "the" cause and treat oxygen as mere background. Why?

Mackie's answer draws on what is normal, salient, or controllable relative to a background field. Oxygen is normally present and expected; the short circuit was the abnormal departure. We implicitly ask: what caused this fire as opposed to the normal, non-fire state of affairs? The short circuit is what changed. We also tend to identify causal factors that agents can intervene on — fixing electrical systems is practically available; removing oxygen from buildings is not.

This pragmatic dimension does not undermine the objective content of the INUS analysis. The metaphysics specifies which factors count as INUS conditions; the pragmatics specifies which INUS condition we select as "the" cause in a given investigative context.

## Problems for the Account

The overdetermination problem is the most serious. If two fires simultaneously approach a barn from different sides, either alone sufficient to burn it, then neither fire is a necessary part of any actual sufficient condition — each makes the other redundant. Mackie acknowledged this as a genuine difficulty and suggested that overdetermination cases are genuine causes even if they violate the necessity requirement within the sufficient condition. But this looks like an ad hoc concession that abandons the central feature of the analysis.

The background field problem is subtler: the assessment of which factors are necessary within a sufficient condition depends on specifying the background conditions taken for granted. Different specifications of the field yield different INUS conditions. Mackie's concept of the field is underspecified, and this underspecification can make the analysis fragile in exactly those cases — complex interventions, unusual contexts — where it is most needed.

Lewis's critique is the most general: the INUS analysis is still fundamentally a regularity account — it appeals to which sufficient conditions are present in all actual cases — and it shares the deeper problems of regularity theories. Lewis's counterfactual approach was developed explicitly as an alternative that addresses these difficulties at their root.

## Significance

Despite these objections, Mackie's INUS framework was enormously influential. In legal philosophy, the distinction between INUS cause and background condition maps naturally onto the distinction between proximate and but-for causes. In epidemiology, the "sufficient component cause" model (Rothman) is directly derived from Mackie: every cause is a component of some sufficient cause package, and the collection of sufficient cause packages specifies the full causal epidemiology of a disease. The framework continues to structure debates in public health ethics about attributable fractions and causal responsibility. Mackie showed, as no one before him had shown with such precision, that ordinary causal attribution is sophisticated, context-sensitive, and irreducibly complex — and that any analysis adequate to it must be equally so.
