# Problems for the Regularity Theory

The regularity theory faces persistent objections that reveal deep tensions in the Humean program of reducing causation to patterns. These are not merely technical difficulties; they expose what the program costs, and why most contemporary philosophers of causation have moved beyond bare regularities to something more.

## Laws and Accidents: The Fundamental Problem

Consider two true universal generalizations:

- (L) All copper conducts electricity.
- (A) All gold spheres are smaller than one mile in diameter.

Both are — as far as we know — true. But (L) is a law of nature, while (A) is an accidental regularity. It holds because of contingent facts about where gold is found and how much can be assembled, not because of anything essential about gold. The pure regularity theory cannot distinguish (L) from (A): they are formally identical universal quantifications over all actual objects.

The philosophical significance of the difference is unmistakable. (L) supports counterfactuals: if this were copper, it would conduct electricity. (A) does not: if this were a gold sphere one mile across, we cannot infer from the accidental generalization that it would be small. (L) can be used to explain why copper conducts. (A) cannot explain why any gold sphere is small. A regularity theory that cannot draw this distinction cannot ground counterfactuals or explanation — two of the central functions laws are supposed to perform.

## The Direction Problem

If all C-type events are followed by E-type events, it is equally true that all E-type events are preceded by C-type events. Both are regularities. But only C causes E, not vice versa. Hume's own response is to add temporal priority: causes precede effects. But this merely relocates the problem. Why do causes precede effects? The answer cannot itself be derived from the regularities. Moreover, temporal priority seems too strong: if backward causation is coherent (see the module on causal asymmetry), it is too strong as a necessary condition; and cases of simultaneous causation — pressing a table into a wall simultaneously causes the wall to be pressed — show that temporal priority is not always available to distinguish cause from effect.

## Vacuous and Uninstantiated Laws

Vacuously true universal generalizations qualify as laws on the naive regularity theory: "all unicorns are white" is vacuously true (there are no unicorns) and would therefore count as a law. This is clearly wrong.

Conversely, many genuine laws govern situations that never actually occur. A law about what would happen if two perfectly isolated particles collided may hold even if the universe never contains perfectly isolated particles. The regularity theory requires instantiation — laws are patterns over actual events — but laws are supposed to have modal force, telling us what would happen under conditions that may never obtain. Armstrong's necessitation account allows for uninstantiated laws because the relation N(F, G) holds between universals, not between instances. The Best System Analysis has more difficulty here: a best system can only systematize actual patterns, not potential ones.

## The Ceteris Paribus Problem

Most laws in the special sciences — and arguably many in physics — hold "other things being equal." Galileo's free-fall law applies absent air resistance. Hooke's law applies within the elastic limit. Demand falls as price rises, unless the good becomes a status symbol. These laws are not strict regularities. A pure regularity theory must either deny that such hedged generalizations are genuine laws (which is implausibly restrictive) or explain what the ceteris paribus clause means without circularity.

Option (b) is difficult. "Other things being equal" cannot mean "everything else is as it actually is" (which makes the law trivially true and uninformative) and cannot mean "no interfering causes are present" (since "interfering cause" is itself a causal concept, generating circularity for a reductive regularity account).

## Spatial and Temporal Locality

The regularity theory faces a challenge from laws that are supposed to hold globally. What licenses the inference from "all observed copper conducts" to "all copper everywhere in the universe, including 12 billion light-years away, conducts"? We have observed a tiny fraction of all copper. The inference requires some principle of global uniformity that the regularity theory cannot provide from within its own resources. This is a variant of the problem of induction — the regularity theory lacks the modal resources to explain why local regularities should be spatiotemporally universal.

## Common Causes and Spurious Correlation

Reichenbach's common cause objection: two events that are constantly conjoined may be effects of a common cause rather than causally related to each other. A barometer falls and a storm arrives: constantly conjoined, but the falling barometer does not cause the storm. Both are caused by a change in atmospheric pressure. Reichenbach's principle of the common cause states that if A and B are correlated but neither causes the other, there is a common cause C that screens off the correlation: P(A | B & C) = P(A | C). This principle allows us to distinguish genuine causal regularities from spurious correlations. But the principle is itself not derivable from bare regularities — it presupposes richer causal structure.

## What the Problems Motivate

These problems are severe enough that most contemporary philosophers of science accept that laws have a modal character that cannot be captured by regularities alone. The debate has shifted from whether Humeanism can be maintained to how non-Humean the final account must be.

The natural first response — the Best System Analysis — attempts to distinguish laws from accidents by appeal to theoretical virtues: laws are the regularities that appear in the best systematization of all facts, the system with optimal balance of simplicity and informativeness. This gives a principled (though contested) way to handle the accidental regularity problem. But it requires appeal to natural properties and faces its own difficulties with the ceteris paribus and uninstantiated law problems.

Further along the anti-Humean spectrum, Armstrong's necessitation theory gives laws modal force beyond bare regularities by positing a primitive necessitation relation between universals. Dispositional essentialism (Bird) makes laws follow necessarily from the essential natures of properties. Powers theories (Molnar, Mumford) ground regularities in the intrinsic causal powers of entities, treating laws as derivative on powers rather than fundamental. Each pays a different philosophical price; none inherits the elegant parsimony of the naive regularity account. That parsimony, it turns out, was purchased at too high a theoretical cost.
