# Lewis on Causation: The Counterfactual Analysis

We explain and predict by asking what would have happened if things had been different. The causal claim "the spark caused the fire" is intimately connected to the counterfactual "if the spark had not occurred, the fire would not have occurred." David Lewis's proposal, developed in "Causation" (1973), was to make this connection constitutive: causation just is — or is grounded in — counterfactual dependence between events. The analysis draws on his broader possible worlds framework for evaluating counterfactuals, and it marked a decisive break from the regularity tradition while remaining within the broadly Humean, non-modal spirit.

## The Analysis Stated

A counterfactual "if p had been the case, q would have been the case" (p □→ q) is true at a possible world w iff in the nearest possible worlds where p is true, q is also true. "Nearest" is measured by overall similarity to the actual world: the closest worlds are those that depart from actuality as little as possible while making the antecedent true.

Lewis's analysis of causation proceeds in two stages. First, event E *counterfactually depends* on event C iff had C not occurred, E would not have occurred: ¬C □→ ¬E (for actual events, this is the non-trivial direction; the forward conditional is trivially true when both C and E occur). Second, C *causes* E iff there is a chain of stepwise counterfactual dependence leading from C to E. The chain clause handles preemption cases: if A causes B and B causes E, we can trace a causal path A → B → E even if E does not directly depend on A.

The similarity metric for possible worlds is carefully ordered. First, avoid big violations of actual laws (miracles). Second, maximize the spatiotemporal region of perfect match with the actual world. Third, minimize further differences, even at the cost of small local miracles. This ordering generates the temporal asymmetry Lewis needs: worlds that are identical to the actual world up to the time of the cause, then diverge through a small localized miracle, are closer than worlds that diverge throughout. The direction of causation comes from the direction of closest divergence.

## What the Analysis Captures

Lewis's analysis captures several intuitive features elegantly. Because of how world-similarity works, causes precede effects — the asymmetry is built into the closeness metric. The chain structure gives us transitivity: if A causes B and B causes C, the chain yields A causes C. And causal explanation becomes explanation by counterfactual relevance: citing C explains E because E was counterfactually dependent on C. We care about causes partly because they represent leverage points for intervention — knowing C causes E tells us that preventing C would prevent E.

## Difficulties

A significant difficulty concerns specifying the similarity metric precisely. Lewis held that worlds sharing the actual laws and initial conditions up to some time t, then diverging through a small localized miracle, are closest. Critics have objected that the similarity ordering can make wrong predictions in cases where small causes have large effects — and the question of which features of possible worlds are "similar" to actuality is not obviously determinate.

The backtracking problem is more troubling. Sometimes it seems natural to reason backward: if E had not occurred, C would not have occurred either. Lewis calls these "backtracking" counterfactuals and holds they are not the relevant kind for causal analysis. But distinguishing the right (forward-tracking) from the wrong (backtracking) counterfactuals requires appealing to causal considerations, which threatens circularity in the analysis.

## The Core Problem: Preemption

The core problem is preemption. Assassin A fires at the victim, killing them at t₂. Assassin B also fires, but B's bullet arrives at t₃ > t₂. The victim is already dead when B's bullet arrives. Did A's shot cause the death?

Intuition: yes. Counterfactual verdict: had A not shot, B would have killed the victim — so the death does not counterfactually depend on A's shot. The simple analysis gives the wrong answer.

Lewis responded with the chain structure: there is a chain of counterfactual dependence between A's shot and the victim's death at each intermediate step. The victim's wound depends on the bullet's trajectory at t₁; the trajectory at t₁ depends on the shot; the death depends on the wound. B's shot never intersects this chain, so B's shot — though it would have caused death in A's absence — is causally irrelevant in the actual sequence.

This handles early preemption but creates its own problems for late preemption cases, where the competing cause is close enough to the effect that no intermediate links can be found. Lewis continued refining the analysis throughout his career, eventually developing the notion of causal influence in "Causation as Influence" (2000) — a more permissive relation that allows degree-sensitive counterfactuals to capture what was missed by strict dependence.

## Lewis's Influence

Despite its problems, Lewis's analysis transformed the field. It shifted attention from regularity to counterfactual dependence, provided a technically sophisticated framework, and identified with precision the cases that any adequate theory must handle. The literature on preemption, overdetermination, and fragility of events that Lewis generated is among the richest in the metaphysics of causation. His possible worlds semantics remains the standard background for evaluating counterfactuals, even for philosophers who reject his specific analysis of causation. Whatever one thinks of the analysis, the debate it framed has been unavoidable.
