# Preemption and Overdetermination

The counterfactual theory holds that C causes E iff E would not have occurred had C not occurred (¬C □→ ¬E). Preemption and overdetermination are cases where this counterfactual fails while the causal intuition remains firm. Understanding them is not just an exercise in defeating a particular theory — these cases reveal something about the structure of causation that any adequate account must face.

## Early Preemption

Early preemption occurs when two potential causes operate sequentially, and the earlier one "gets there first," preventing the later one from producing the effect.

The firing squad case: Assassin A fires at the victim, killing them at t₂. Assassin B also fires, but B's bullet arrives at t₃ > t₂. At the moment of death, B's bullet is still in flight. Did A's shot cause the death? Intuitively, yes — A shot and killed the victim. But the counterfactual test fails: had A not shot, B would have killed the victim. The death does not depend on A's shot.

Lewis's chain response: there is a chain of counterfactual dependence between A's shot and the victim's death:

- The position of A's bullet at t₁ depends on the shot.
- The victim's wound at t₂ depends on the bullet's position at t₁.
- The victim's death depends on the wound.

At each intermediate step, dependence holds. B's shot never intersects this chain in the actual sequence, so B is causally inert here. This handles early preemption: the chain runs from A to the death, and B has no actual causal connection to it.

## Late Preemption

Late preemption is more challenging. Now the backup cause is neutralized only at the very last moment, leaving no room for intermediate links.

Consider: Person A drains all the water from a traveler's canteen. Person B, independently, had already punctured the canteen before A could refill it. The traveler dies of thirst. Both A and B did something causally relevant, but A acted first and it was A's action — emptying the canteen — that actually caused the death, not B's (which would have caused it in A's absence). But finding intermediate counterfactual steps between A's action and the death is difficult — the backup cause was close enough to the effect that no clean "intermediate difference" can be identified.

Lewis introduced the notion of **quasi-dependence** and later **causal influence** to handle cases like this: even if the effect does not depend on whether C occurred, it may depend on when, how, or in what manner C occurred — on intrinsic features of C that differ from what the backup would have produced. These features vary between C and the backup cause, generating a form of influence even without strict counterfactual dependence.

## Symmetric Overdetermination

Symmetric overdetermination is the hardest case. Two fires simultaneously approach a barn from different sides; each alone would have been sufficient to burn it. Together they do. Which fire caused the barn's destruction? Intuitively, both did. Yet neither fire passes the counterfactual test: had Fire A been absent, Fire B would have burned the barn; had Fire B been absent, Fire A would have done so. Neither fire is counterfactually necessary for the outcome.

Consider the available responses. One is to accept overdetermination: both fires were causes, and causation does not require counterfactual dependence — actual sufficiency grounds causation even without counterfactual necessity. This is a Mackie-style response, accepting that the counterfactual theory is simply false in overdetermination cases.

A second response denies that true simultaneous overdetermination ever occurs: in the closest possible world, one fire arrives slightly before the other and preempts. Real simultaneous overdetermination may be nomically impossible. This bites the bullet on the actual cases: if overdetermination is physically impossible, we need not account for it.

A third response invokes aspects of the effect: the barn burned in a particular way — with heat and flames from both sides. The precise nature of the destruction depended on both fires. Each fire was causally relevant to some aspect of the effect, even if not to whether the effect occurred at all.

## Trumping (Schaffer)

Jonathan Schaffer introduced trumping cases to challenge even more sophisticated analyses. Two sorcerers simultaneously cast spells of equal potency to turn a potion blue. One sorcerer is senior and, by a meta-magical law, the senior sorcerer's spell "trumps" the junior's. The potion turns blue: which spell caused it?

Intuition: the senior sorcerer's spell. But both spells are causally symmetrical in their counterfactual and probabilistic contributions — neither would have changed anything had the other been absent. The trumping case challenges analyses that rely on counterfactual or probabilistic differences, since neither spell makes any such difference.

Schaffer uses this to argue that any adequate theory must be sensitive to the **actual causal process** — not merely to counterfactual patterns. The senior spell is the cause because it is the one that actually produced the effect, and there is an objective fact about which influence was "responsible." Process theories and powers theories handle this naturally; purely counterfactual theories do not.

## Lessons

Taken together, these cases suggest three conclusions. First, counterfactual dependence is neither sufficient nor necessary for causation: overdetermination cases show it is not necessary, and preemption cases require elaborate reconstruction to preserve the analysis. Second, causal selection among competing sufficient conditions is not arbitrary — there are objective facts about which condition "actually produced" the effect. Third, any adequate theory must be sensitive to the actual causal process: the spatiotemporal path of causal influence from cause to effect, not merely the pattern of counterfactual dependences.

These conclusions have driven philosophers toward process theories (Salmon, Dowe), powers theories (Molnar, Mumford), and mechanistic theories as alternatives to the purely counterfactual approach. The hard cases turn out to be philosophically illuminating: they reveal that the causal relation has structure that the counterfactual approach, for all its elegance, cannot fully capture.
