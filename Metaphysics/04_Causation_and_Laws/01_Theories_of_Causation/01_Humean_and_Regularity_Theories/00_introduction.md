# Humean and Regularity Theories of Causation

The most austere form of the Humean analysis of causation is the regularity theory: causation is nothing over and above regular succession. Event c causes event e if events of the same type as c are regularly followed by events of the same type as e, and c precedes e and is spatiotemporally contiguous with it. This analysis requires no mysterious necessitating powers, no primitive causal connections, no metaphysical glue holding events together. Causation is a pattern in the world's history, observable in principle by any careful investigator.

The regularity theory faces a set of well-known counterexamples. Some regularities are not causal: the regular succession of day and night does not mean that day causes night; the barometer's drop regularly precedes the storm without causing it. Some causal relations do not involve regularities in the strict sense: a unique event (the Big Bang, a particular human decision) can be a cause even if there is no type-regularity of which it is an instance. And causation has a direction — cause precedes effect — that the regularity theory cannot explain without helping itself to temporal asymmetry.

John Stuart Mill's method of difference is perhaps the most sophisticated version of the regularity approach for scientific practice: C causes E if, across a range of cases where E is present, C is always present, and across cases where E is absent, C is always absent. This method of systematic elimination has proved enormously useful in epidemiology, pharmacology, and experimental science. Yet it is better understood as a method for identifying causes than as a metaphysical analysis of what causation is.

The INUS condition analysis — developed by Mackie — is a more sophisticated regularity theory: c is a cause of e if it is an Insufficient but Necessary part of an Unnecessary but Sufficient condition for e. This captures the complex multi-factor structure of causal explanation in science while remaining within the Humean framework. This section examines these developments of the regularity tradition and their philosophical costs.
