# Hume's Two Definitions of Causation

David Hume's treatment of causation in the *Treatise of Human Nature* (1739) and the *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748) is the starting point for virtually every subsequent debate in the metaphysics of causation. What makes Hume's account remarkable is not merely its content but its audacity: he claimed that when we look carefully at the world, we cannot find anything that corresponds to the popular notion of necessary connection between cause and effect. The bond we think we perceive is not out there in the world — it is, in a sense, projected onto the world by our minds.

Hume famously offered two definitions of causation, a fact that has puzzled interpreters ever since. On the first definition, a cause is "an object, followed by another, and where all the objects similar to the first are followed by objects similar to the second." This is the regularity or constant conjunction definition: causation just is the pattern of one type of event reliably following another. There is nothing more to it than that. The second definition adds a mental dimension: a cause is also "an object followed by another, and whose appearance always conveys the thought to that other." This epistemic or psychological definition focuses on the inference our minds make from observing one event to expecting another.

The relationship between these two definitions is controversial. Some scholars treat them as complementary, with the first giving the objective facts and the second describing our epistemic access to those facts. Others argue the definitions are in tension, with the second smuggling in a psychological notion of necessary connection through the back door. Simon Blackman and Peter Millican have written extensively on which interpretation does more justice to Hume's overall project.

Central to understanding Hume's view is his famous "missing shade" argument about impressions and ideas. Hume held that all genuine ideas must be traceable back to sensory impressions. When we look for an impression of necessary connection — the supposed "tie" or "power" that makes the cause produce the effect — we cannot find it in any single observation. What we find instead, after repeated observations of the same sequence, is a feeling in our minds: a customary transition of thought. The impression of necessary connection is this psychological response, not a feature of external events.

This move has profound consequences. It means that causation, as ordinarily understood to involve genuine necessity, does not exist in the external world. What does exist is constant conjunction. Hume was not skeptical about the practical importance of causal reasoning — he thought it was indispensable for everyday life and science. But philosophically, he argued, we must recognize that "necessary connection" is a projection of habit onto a world of mere regularities.

The subsequent history of the regularity theory is largely a story of attempts to refine this core insight while avoiding the serious objections it faces, particularly the problem of distinguishing genuine causal laws from accidental regularities.
