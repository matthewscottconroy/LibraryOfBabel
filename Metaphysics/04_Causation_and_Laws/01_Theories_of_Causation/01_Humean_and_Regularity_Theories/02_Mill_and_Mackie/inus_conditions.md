# INUS Conditions and Complex Causation

J.L. Mackie's analysis of causation, developed in his paper "Causes and Conditions" (1965) and the book *The Cement of the Universe* (1974), represents one of the most sophisticated attempts to work within the Humean regularity tradition while accounting for the complexity of real causal situations. Central to his account is the concept of an INUS condition: an Insufficient but Necessary part of an Unnecessary but Sufficient condition.

The idea captures a common feature of causal attributions. When we say that a short circuit caused a house fire, we do not mean that short circuits are always sufficient for fires — they are not, because many other conditions must also obtain (the presence of flammable material, absence of suppression systems, and so on). Nor do we mean that short circuits are necessary for fires — they are not, because fires can have many different causes. Rather, the short circuit was a necessary part of a sufficient condition: the full package of conditions that, in this case, were jointly sufficient for the fire. And the short circuit was needed to complete that package; without it, that particular sufficient condition would not have been realized, though other sufficient conditions might have been.

More precisely: C is an INUS condition of E if there is some set of conditions S such that C is a necessary member of S, S is sufficient for E, and S is not necessary for E (because E has other sufficient causes). The actual cause of E in a given case is an INUS condition of E, combined with the other factors that made S sufficient on that occasion.

Mackie's account handles several features of causal reasoning that simpler regularity accounts cannot. It accommodates the asymmetry of overdetermination: if two fires are set simultaneously and either would have destroyed the building, each fire was sufficient but neither was necessary. Each was still a member of a sufficient condition — just not a necessary member of that condition. The INUS analysis can represent this. It also handles preventive causation: if someone removes oxygen from a burning room, the oxygen removal was a necessary part of a sufficient condition for the fire going out.

Critics have noted that the analysis still faces difficulties with cases of symmetric overdetermination, where two events are exactly simultaneous causes and neither is strictly necessary for the effect. There are also questions about how to individuate the "unnecessary sufficient conditions" — different partitions of background conditions yield different verdicts about what counts as a cause.

Nevertheless, Mackie's INUS conditions marked a significant advance in regularity-based approaches and remain a reference point in discussions of causation, especially in legal and scientific contexts where identifying the actual cause among many contributing factors is practically important.
