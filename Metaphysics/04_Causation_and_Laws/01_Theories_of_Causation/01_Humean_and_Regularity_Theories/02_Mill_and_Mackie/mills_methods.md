# Mill's Methods of Experimental Inquiry

John Stuart Mill's *System of Logic* (1843) remains one of the most systematic attempts to formalize the procedures by which we identify causal relationships from observation and experiment. Mill was working within a broadly empiricist tradition that sought to ground all knowledge in experience, and his account of inductive logic was meant to show how causal knowledge is possible without appealing to any a priori principles of necessary connection.

Mill's methods are a set of procedures for identifying causal factors by examining patterns of presence and absence across cases. The Method of Agreement states that if two or more instances of a phenomenon share only one antecedent circumstance, that circumstance is probably the cause. If people in diverse locations all develop the same disease, and the only thing they have in common is exposure to a particular bacterium, we infer that the bacterium is causally relevant. The Method of Difference states that if two cases are identical except that one contains the phenomenon and one does not, and they differ in only one antecedent circumstance, that circumstance is the cause. This is the logic of controlled experiments: hold everything constant except one variable.

The Joint Method combines these two: if in cases where the effect occurs a certain factor is always present, and in cases where it is absent a certain factor is always absent, that factor is causally relevant. The Method of Concomitant Variation is particularly important for continuous variables: if variations in one phenomenon are accompanied by variations in another, a causal relationship is likely. The Method of Residues instructs us to subtract from a complex phenomenon those parts already known to be caused by antecedent factors; what remains was caused by other factors.

Mill was aware of limitations. The methods work only when we have already identified the relevant circumstances to examine — they do not tell us which factors to include in our experimental designs. They are also susceptible to failures when multiple causes can independently produce the same effect (plurality of causes) or when effects are produced only by joint operation of several factors (intermixture of effects).

Contemporary philosophers of science have debated whether Mill's methods can be given a fully rigorous foundation. The theory of design of experiments associated with Fisher, and more recently the program of causal Bayesian networks associated with Pearl, Spirtes, and Glymour, can be seen as successors to Mill's project: formalizing the logic by which we identify causal structure from patterns of statistical dependence and independence.

For the metaphysics of causation, Mill's methods are important primarily as evidence that causal reasoning follows systematic patterns — patterns that any adequate theory of causation must explain. Whether those patterns reveal something deep about the nature of causation or are merely reliable heuristics for tracking regularities remains contested.
