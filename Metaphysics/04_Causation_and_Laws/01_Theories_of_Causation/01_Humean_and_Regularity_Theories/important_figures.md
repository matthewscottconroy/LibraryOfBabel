# Important Figures: Humean and Regularity Theories

**David Hume (1711–1776)** is the founding figure of the regularity tradition. His analysis of causation in the *Treatise of Human Nature* and the *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* set the agenda for modern philosophy of causation. His two definitions — one objective (constant conjunction), one psychological (customary inference) — and his account of the missing impression of necessary connection remain central reference points.

**John Stuart Mill (1806–1873)** developed the most systematic account of causal inference in the empiricist tradition. His five methods in *A System of Logic* (1843) formalized the logic of experimental reasoning and influenced the development of scientific methodology as much as philosophy.

**J.L. Mackie (1917–1981)** refined the regularity tradition with his INUS condition analysis. *The Cement of the Universe* (1974) remains one of the most comprehensive treatments of causation in the analytic tradition, covering regularity theories, counterfactual theories, and causal realism.

**Bertrand Russell (1872–1970)** famously argued in "On the Notion of Cause" (1912) that mature science has no use for the concept of causation as philosophers understand it, preferring instead functional equations expressing relationships between quantities. This provocative thesis has been debated ever since.

**Ernest Nagel (1901–1985)** defended a refined regularity account in *The Structure of Science* (1961), attempting to distinguish causal laws from accidental generalizations through formal criteria.

**Frank Ramsey (1903–1930)** introduced the idea that laws are not regularities but something more like inference rules or practical commitments, influencing later deflationary accounts of laws.

**Simon Blackburn (b. 1944)** developed a quasi-realist interpretation of Hume's account, arguing that our causal talk can be vindicated without positing genuine causal necessities in the world.
