# Lewis's Counterfactual Theory of Causation

David Lewis's counterfactual analysis of causation, presented in "Causation" (1973) and refined in subsequent papers, offered the most influential alternative to regularity theories. Lewis's central idea is beautifully simple: C causes E if and only if, had C not occurred, E would not have occurred. Causation is a matter of counterfactual dependence.

This analysis is embedded in Lewis's broader modal framework. He took possible worlds seriously as genuine entities, and he analyzed counterfactuals using similarity relations among possible worlds. A counterfactual "If P were the case, Q would be the case" is true at the actual world if and only if Q is true at the nearest possible worlds where P is true — the worlds that differ minimally from actuality in order to make P true.

Applying this to causation: C caused E if the nearest possible world where C did not occur is a world where E did not occur either. The sugar dissolved because the water contained it — in the nearest world where we remove the sugar, the dissolution does not occur. The bullet caused the death — in the nearest world without the bullet, the victim lives.

Lewis's framework has several attractive features. It naturally explains the asymmetry of causation: causes precede effects because of the temporal asymmetry in how we evaluate similarity of possible worlds. It handles complex causal chains: if A causes B and B causes C, then there is counterfactual dependence all the way along the chain. It also connects to our ordinary understanding of causal reasoning, since we routinely reason about what would have happened had things been different.

The analysis was presented alongside Lewis's theory of laws of nature — his "best system" account — and his Humean supervenience thesis: the claim that everything supervenes on the distribution of local qualitative facts across spacetime. Causation, on this view, is not a primitive feature of the world but is constructed from the patterns of events and the similarity relations among worlds.

This gives Lewis's project a sweeping ambition: to show that an apparently mind-independent, necessary feature of reality (causation) can be analyzed using the resources of Humean empiricism augmented with possible worlds. Whether this project succeeds depends on the analysis handling the hard cases — preemption, overdetermination, fragility — and Lewis devoted much of his later career to this task.
