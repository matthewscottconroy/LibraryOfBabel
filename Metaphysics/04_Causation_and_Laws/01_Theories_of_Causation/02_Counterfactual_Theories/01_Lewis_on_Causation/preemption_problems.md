# Preemption and the Limits of Counterfactual Analysis

The counterfactual analysis of causation faces its most serious challenges from cases of preemption and overdetermination, where the simple counterfactual test gives wrong verdicts. Lewis devoted substantial effort to handling these cases, and his successive refinements reveal both the resources and the limits of the counterfactual approach.

Early preemption is the clearest challenge. Suppose two assassins, A and B, both shoot at the victim simultaneously, but A's bullet arrives first and kills the victim. B's bullet was preempted — it was in flight, but the victim was already dead when it arrived. Now consider: would the victim have died if A had not shot? Yes — because B's bullet would have done the job. So by the simple counterfactual test, A did not cause the death. But this is obviously wrong: A is the actual cause.

Lewis's initial response was to introduce the concept of a causal chain. Even if E does not counterfactually depend on C, C may cause E via a chain of intermediate events, each of which counterfactually depends on the previous one. A caused an intermediate event (the bullet traveling), which caused another (the bullet striking), which caused the death. At each step there is genuine counterfactual dependence. B's bullet never entered this chain.

But this solution faces late preemption cases, where the preempting cause and the preempted cause converge so that there is no clean cut-off point for the chain. Suppose A's and B's bullets both enter the victim simultaneously, but A's bullet causes death and B's does not because of a subtle difference in trajectory. Here both are in flight at the same time, and the intermediate events from each bullet's trajectory counterfactually depend on the respective shots. Lewis's later work introduced the notion of influence: C causes E if C's occurrence (or non-occurrence) makes a difference to some features of E, even if not to whether E occurs. But this requires a fine-grained individuation of events that many find problematic.

These difficulties have led some philosophers to abandon the counterfactual approach and pursue causal process theories, mechanistic theories, or interventionist theories. Others have offered different strategies for handling preemption within the counterfactual framework. The debate continues to be active, with no consensus on the best solution.
