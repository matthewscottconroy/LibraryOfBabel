# Invariance, Manipulation, and Scientific Causation

A distinctive feature of Woodward's interventionism is its emphasis on invariance as the mark of genuine causal relationships. A relationship is invariant if it continues to hold under a range of interventions and background conditions. This is not just the observation that the relationship holds across many cases — it is the stronger claim that the relationship holds when we actively manipulate one of its relata.

Invariance explains why scientists distinguish laws from accidental generalizations. The relationship between temperature and the pressure of a gas is invariant: if we intervene to change the temperature (by heating or cooling the container), the pressure changes accordingly, across a wide range of circumstances and materials. The relationship between shoe size and reading ability is not invariant under interventions on shoe size. This distinction, which the pure regularity theory struggles with, falls out naturally from the interventionist framework.

The emphasis on manipulation also explains the asymmetry of causation. If X causes Y, then intervening on X changes Y, but intervening on Y does not (in general) change X. This asymmetry reflects the direction of causal influence and is encoded in the directed edges of causal graphs. Reversing the direction would require that intervening on the effect changes the cause — which is generally not possible without also intervening on the cause via some other route.

Woodward draws on a contrast between two types of knowledge: correlational knowledge and causal knowledge. Knowing that two variables are correlated tells you what to expect when one changes in the natural course of events. Knowing that one causes the other tells you what to expect when you intervene to change one. These are different, and the difference matters practically — for medical treatment, policy intervention, and engineering design.

Critics have noted that the interventionist framework, while illuminating the structure of causal reasoning in science, may not capture all of what we mean by causation. Historical causation — the battle caused the collapse of the empire — does not obviously involve the possibility of intervention. Causation at the fundamental physical level may not involve manipulation either. Woodward has argued that the interventionist notion generalizes, but the extensions are sometimes contested.
