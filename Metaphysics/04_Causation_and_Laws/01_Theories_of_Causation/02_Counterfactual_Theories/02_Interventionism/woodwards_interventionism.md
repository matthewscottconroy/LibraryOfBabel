# Woodward's Interventionist Theory

James Woodward's interventionist theory of causation, developed in *Making Things Happen* (2003), represents a significant departure from both regularity theories and Lewis-style counterfactual analyses. Woodward's central claim is that causal relationships are relationships that are invariant under interventions. To say that X causes Y is to say that if one were to intervene to change X in the right way, Y would change accordingly.

The key concept is intervention: a process that changes the value of X in a way that is exogenous to the existing causal structure — it reaches X "from outside" the system, setting its value independently of its normal causes. This is importantly different from ordinary counterfactuals: the question is not just "what would happen if X were different?" but "what would happen if X were set to a different value by an appropriate causal process?"

Woodward's analysis is explicitly non-reductive in one sense: he does not try to analyze causation in non-causal terms, because intervention is itself a causal notion. What he aims for instead is an account that illuminates the structure of causal reasoning, particularly in science. The concept of invariance under intervention explains why causal claims support manipulations and control in ways that mere correlations do not. The correlation between shoe size and reading ability among children does not support the intervention "change shoe size to improve reading," because the correlation is not invariant under interventions on shoe size.

This gives Woodward's theory a pragmatic dimension: causal relationships are those that are relevant to prediction and control, and this relevance is cashed out in terms of what would happen under interventions. The theory has affinities with the directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) used in causal modeling by Judea Pearl and others. In fact, Woodward's interventionism can be seen as the philosophical articulation of the conceptual foundations of causal graphical models.

Objections to the interventionist theory include circularity worries (intervention is causal, so the analysis uses the notion it is analyzing), concerns about causation in closed systems where interventions are impossible, and worries about whether the theory handles fundamental physics, where there may be no meaningful notion of externally imposed interventions. Woodward has responded to these objections with varying degrees of success, and the debate continues.
