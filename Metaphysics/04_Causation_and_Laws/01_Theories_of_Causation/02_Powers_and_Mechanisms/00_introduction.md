# Powers and Mechanisms

The Humean tradition in the philosophy of causation is united by a commitment to analyzing causation in terms of regularities, counterfactuals, or other extensional features of the world — without appealing to anything "intrinsic" in the causal relation itself. The powers and mechanisms tradition reverses this order of explanation: causation is intrinsic to the world, built into the natures of things through their causal powers, and what Humeans take as the analysis of causation is actually a downstream symptom of the deeper causal structure.

A power (or disposition) is an intrinsic property of a thing that constitutes its capacity to bring about or be affected by certain effects. Salt's solubility is a power: it is an intrinsic feature of sodium chloride that when placed in water, it dissolves. This power is not identical to the regularity of salt-dissolving-when-placed-in-water; it is what explains and grounds that regularity. If powers are real intrinsic features of things, then causation is not a matter of observed patterns but of the manifestation of those powers in particular circumstances.

The mechanisms approach, influenced by Machamer, Darden, and Craver's work in philosophy of science, takes a related but distinct approach: causation is to be understood in terms of mechanisms — organized systems of interacting entities and activities that produce, underlie, or maintain phenomena. To explain something causally is not merely to cite regularities or counterfactuals but to describe the mechanism by which the cause brings about the effect. Mechanisms are not just lists of parts; they are organized wholes in which the activities of the parts collectively produce the behavior of the whole.

Both approaches face the challenge of giving an account of causal necessity that is not merely epistemic. The powers theorist claims that powers intrinsically necessitate their manifestations (in the right circumstances); but what does this intrinsic necessitation consist in? This section examines the powers and mechanisms approaches, their philosophical motivations, and the problems they must solve.
