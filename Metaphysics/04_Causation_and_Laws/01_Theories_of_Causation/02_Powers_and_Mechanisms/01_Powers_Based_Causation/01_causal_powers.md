# Causal Powers as Fundamental

Something is missing from the Humean picture, and it is easy to identify: the productive oomph. When a fire melts wax, we want to say that the fire did something to the wax — not merely that fire-events are regularly followed by wax-melting-events. When a drug relieves pain, we want to say that the drug acted on the relevant receptors in a way that produced the relief — not merely that drug-administrations are constantly conjoined with relief episodes. The regularity account describes the pattern; powers theorists insist that what we are really after is the capacity that generates the pattern. The causal powers approach holds that causation is grounded in genuine, irreducible powers or dispositions inherent in the objects or properties involved, and that these powers are what explain the regularities, not the other way round.

## The Historical Shape of the Debate

The view has deep roots. Aristotle distinguished active powers (capacities to act) from passive powers (capacities to be acted upon). The fire has the active power to melt wax; the wax has the passive power to be melted. When they interact, the powers are mutually manifested in the event of melting. Causation is not a mysterious relation between distinct events but the natural expression of the natures of the objects involved.

The Aristotelian tradition was displaced by the mechanistic philosophy of the seventeenth century: Descartes, Boyle, and Locke preferred explanations in terms of matter in motion, without appeal to Scholastic "occult qualities." Hume drove this further, denying that powers are real features of the world at all. The contemporary revival of powers ontology — from the 1970s onward — is a systematic response to the Humean program, driven by the conviction that its costs are too high.

## The Fundamental Claim

The central thesis, shared by Molnar, Mumford, Martin, Ellis, and Bird, is this:

- **Powers Thesis**: Some properties — perhaps all fundamental natural properties — are intrinsically dispositional. Their very nature consists in a directedness toward certain manifestations. Being charged is constitutively the power to attract opposite charges and repel like ones. Being fragile is constitutively the power to break when struck. These properties are not bare categorical qualities that happen to be connected to their effects by external laws; they are essentially directed toward their effects.

Notice the explanatory direction this inverts. Ask the Humean: why does copper reliably conduct electricity? Answer: it just does; the regularity is a brute fact. Ask the powers theorist: because copper has the intrinsic power of electrical conduction. The regularity holds because of this intrinsic feature. Powers provide explanatory grounding for regularities that regularity accounts cannot provide for themselves.

More precisely:

- **P1**: Copper reliably conducts electricity — a stable, universal regularity.
- **P2**: The Humean asks why this regularity holds. Within the regularity framework, there is no further explanation: it just does.
- **P3**: The powers theorist says: copper conducts electricity because it has the intrinsic power of electrical conduction. The regularity holds because of this intrinsic feature.
- **C**: Powers provide explanatory grounding for regularities that regularity accounts cannot provide for themselves.

## Ellis and Mumford

Brian Ellis, in *Scientific Essentialism* (2001), argues that natural kinds — gold, electron, water — have intrinsic essential natures that determine their causal powers, and that these natures are discovered empirically but are metaphysically necessary. Being an electron is necessarily being a particle with the charge and mass that electrons actually have, and these properties ground the laws governing electron behavior.

Stephen Mumford develops a "mutual manifestation" model in *Dispositions* (1998) and *Laws in Nature* (2004): causation is not a relation between discrete events but a process of mutual manifestation. The power of the striker and the liability of the struck object manifest together in the causal event. Neither power alone suffices; both must be co-present. This model handles masking naturally — if the passive power is masked (the fragile glass is protected), the manifestation does not occur because the mutual conditions are unmet. It also captures the reciprocal character of many causal interactions: both the virus and the vulnerable host contribute to the infection.

## Objections and Their Force

The Humean challenge: we observe no power in any single instance — only succession. Powers theorists respond that Hume was looking for the wrong thing. We do not observe a distinct entity "power" separate from the property itself; what we observe is the property in action. The power of charge to repel is not something over and above the charge — it is the charge in its essential nature.

The regress problem is more pressing. If powers are individuated by their manifestations, and manifestations are themselves powers (since all fundamental properties are powers), we get an infinite chain: every power points to further powers, with no categorical terminus. This is the "always packing, never arriving" objection (Martin). Powers theorists respond either by denying the pure powers thesis (allowing some categorical grounding) or by arguing that a self-sustaining web of mutually individuating powers is coherent — a closed circle of mutual determination rather than a vicious regress.

The identical manifestations problem: two distinct properties can have identical manifestations — can we distinguish them? Bird argues that properties are individuated by their full nomic role across all possible conditions, not just a single manifestation. This gives fine-grained individuation.

## Connections to Agency

The powers framework connects naturally to debates about free will. If persons have genuine causal powers — irreducible capacities to initiate action — this supports libertarian accounts that cannot be captured by regularity-based or counterfactual accounts. Agent causation (Roderick Chisholm, Timothy O'Connor) holds that persons exercise a distinctive kind of power: the power to originate events not fully determined by prior causes, closer to Aristotle's praxis than to natural causation. Whether the powers tradition can sustain this extension is contested, but the conceptual connection is genuine.
