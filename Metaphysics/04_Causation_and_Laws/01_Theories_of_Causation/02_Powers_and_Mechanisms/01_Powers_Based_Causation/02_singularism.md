# Singularism About Causation

We see the knife cut. We see the rock break the window. We see water flowing carry the boat downstream. The Humean tradition tells us that what we see in these cases is one event followed by another, and that the "causation" is something we infer from patterns — a projection of our habitual expectations onto individual occurrences. Elizabeth Anscombe, in "Causality and Determination" (1971), found this description completely at odds with the phenomenology. We do not see succession and infer causation; we see the cutting, the breaking, the carrying. The causal relation is there to be observed, not reconstructed from regularities.

This is the starting point for singularism: the view that token causal relations are not grounded in or dependent on regularities or laws. Each individual causal event is what it is — a cause — independently of whether it is an instance of a general causal pattern.

## The Core Claims

The singularist maintains three connected theses. First, **Singular Priority**: the fact that C caused E on this occasion is metaphysically more basic than the fact that C-type events are regularly followed by E-type events. Second, **Independence**: token causation does not require type causation. There can be token causal relations that are not instances of any general law or regularity. Third, **Observability**: causal relations can be directly perceived in individual instances, without inference from patterns.

These stand in sharp opposition to the Humean tradition, which holds that singular causal claims are always grounded in regularities.

## Anscombe's Argument

Anscombe criticized the Humean tradition for two connected mistakes. The invisibility thesis: Hume's argument purports to show that causal necessity is not observable. But what Hume showed is only that *strict necessity* — logical entailment — is not observable. There is no reason why ordinary causal relations require such strict necessity to be real and observable. The type requirement: Hume assumed that causation must be grounded in regularities connecting types. But the range of causal vocabulary — cutting, pushing, breaking, scraping, bending, burning — describes singular causal relations that are observable directly.

The argument:

- **P1**: We observe causation in individual instances (we see the rock break the window).
- **P2**: If all causation required instantiation of regularities, we could not observe causation in individual instances.
- **P3**: We can and do observe causation in individual instances.
- **C**: Not all causation is grounded in regularities.

## Singularism and Powers

Singularism connects naturally to the causal powers tradition. If objects have intrinsic causal powers, then each manifestation of a power is a singular causal event — the power is exercised in this particular case, regardless of how many times similar cases have occurred. The causal relation holds between these particular events because of the nature of the objects involved, not because of a general pattern.

Harre and Madden's related position (*Causal Powers*, 1975) develops the notion of "natural necessity": singular causal transactions involve a necessity that is neither logical nor mere regularity. Things have intrinsic natures that make them necessarily produce certain effects when conditions are right. This natural necessity is between particulars, not between types, and is observable in the same way that other properties of things are observable.

## The Epistemological Challenge

The main challenge for singularism is explaining how we can know about singular causal relations if not through experience of regularities. When we judge that "the virus caused the illness," we seem to rely on medical knowledge connecting viruses of this type to illnesses of this type. Remove all knowledge of regularities, and it becomes unclear how we could make any singular causal judgment.

Anscombe's response: we observe causation directly in clear cases. These observations are of singular causal events, not inferences from patterns. The Humean treats such observations as observations of succession from which we *infer* causation; Anscombe holds that causation itself is the object of perception, not a theoretical posit. This is a genuine disagreement about the phenomenology of causal observation, and it is not easily resolved by argument alone.

The unintelligibility objection cuts harder: if singular causal relations are primitive and not grounded in any general patterns, what determines which events are causally related? The singularist must say that causal relations are simply primitive — they obtain or they do not. But this makes causation an unintelligible brute fact, beyond any further explanation.

## Legacy

Singularism remains a minority position but a philosophically important one. It has influenced legal philosophy, where specific causation — this drug caused this injury — is distinct from statistical causation. It has influenced medical ethics, where token-level claims about individual patients matter morally in ways that population-level type-causal claims do not. And it connects directly to the problem of mental causation, where token mental events may cause token physical events without there being strict psychophysical laws — a structure that Davidson exploited in his anomalous monism. Anscombe's challenge to the Humean assumption that all causation requires law-backing remains alive and influential, even for philosophers who ultimately reject her conclusions.
