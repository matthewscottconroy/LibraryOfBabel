# Causal Process Theories

Between the billiard ball's motion and the other ball's movement, something happens. The first ball traverses space, comes into contact, and momentum passes from one to the other. The regularity theorist describes the before and after. The counterfactual theorist asks what would have happened otherwise. But neither quite describes what happens *in between* — the continuous physical transmission that constitutes the causal connection. Process theories locate causation precisely here: not in the discrete events and their relations, but in the spatiotemporally continuous paths along which energy, momentum, and other conserved quantities are transmitted.

This approach gives causation a naturalistic, physics-friendly character. Rather than positing mysterious relations between events, process theories describe causation as it appears in physics: the transfer of conserved quantities along world-lines.

## Salmon's Original Account: Mark Transmission

Wesley Salmon's original process theory (1984) distinguished causal processes from pseudo-processes using the **mark transmission criterion**. A causal process is a world-line of an object that can transmit a mark — a local modification — without further intervention. The mark "travels with" the process.

A moving car is a causal process: dent it at one point and the dent persists along the subsequent trajectory. The shadow of a moving car is a pseudo-process: alter the shadow at one point (by projecting a differently-shaped beam) and the alteration does not persist — the shadow is produced fresh at each moment by the car blocking the light. Similarly, a rotating spotlight creates a moving patch of light across a wall; introduce a colored filter at one moment and the subsequent patch returns to its original color, because the spot is produced by the beam, not by the prior position of the spot.

## Dowe's Conserved Quantity Theory

Salmon later replaced the mark criterion — which has its own difficulties — with the **conserved quantity criterion**, developed systematically by Phil Dowe in *Physical Causation* (2000). The CQ theory defines:

- A **causal process** as a world-line of an object that possesses a conserved quantity (energy, momentum, charge, baryon number, lepton number, etc.).
- A **causal interaction** as an intersection of world-lines where conserved quantities are exchanged.

Formally:

- **Process Criterion**: W is a causal process iff W is a world-line of object O and O possesses a conserved quantity Q throughout W.
- **Interaction Criterion**: Two world-lines W₁ and W₂ interact causally at point p iff they intersect at p and there is an exchange of conserved quantities at p.

Causation between events is then derivative: event C causes event E iff there is a causal process (or chain of causal processes) from C to E, where each link involves the transmission of conserved quantities. The cue ball rolling across a billiards table carries kinetic energy; when it strikes the object ball, momentum is exchanged; the object ball moves, carrying away the transferred momentum. This is the full causal story, told in physically rigorous terms.

## Advantages of the Process Account

The CQ theory is directly connected to physics — conserved quantities are well-defined, measurable quantities recognized by fundamental physics. This gives causation a non-mysterious, scientifically respectable grounding. The theory is also genuinely objective and mind-independent: whether energy is being transmitted along a world-line is not a matter of human interests, contrastive expectations, or pragmatic context. It is a physical fact.

The theory also explains spatiotemporal continuity naturally. The causal process must extend unbroken from cause to effect, which is why we do not accept "action at a distance" without a mediating process. The continuous transmission of energy or momentum is the causal connection — it is not just correlated with it.

## Limitations

Causation by omission is the most pressing problem. Not watering a plant causes it to die, but there is no conserved quantity transmitted from the absence of water to the dying plant. The theory models only "positive" causation — causation by transmission — and cannot account for negative causation involving omissions, preventions, or absences. Dowe distinguishes genuine causation (which must involve a process) from quasi-causation or "being responsible for" (which can involve omissions). But this seems to change the subject rather than explain what we ordinarily mean by "the dry spell caused the crop failure."

Mental causation is another difficulty. If we want to understand how mental events cause physical events, what conserved quantities are being transmitted? Neural activity involves physical processes with conserved quantities, but the relevant causal description is at the mental or functional level, not the physical level. The CQ theory applies at the level of physics; it has nothing to say about the level at which psychological explanations operate.

Higher-level causation — economic crises causing unemployment, social norms causing behavioral changes — involves no individual conserved-quantity transfers. The CQ theory gives no account of such causal claims, and it is unclear how to extend it without losing what makes it attractive.

Late in his career, Salmon acknowledged these limitations and moved toward a pluralist view: different accounts of causation may be appropriate for different levels and types of causal claim. The CQ theory captures physical causation; other accounts are needed for higher-level causal phenomena. This pluralist gesture reflects a general tension in process theories: their strength — physical grounding — is simultaneously their limitation. A fully adequate theory of causation must span from the quantum-level interaction to the social-level generalization, and no single physically-grounded framework has yet achieved this.
