# What Is a Mechanism?

When a neuron fires, we can cite its cause — prior depolarization reaching threshold — but this leaves something unexplained. The cell biologist wants to know *how* the depolarization propagated, what entities were involved, what they did, and how they were arranged in time and space to produce the result. The demand here is not for more instances of a covering law; it is for an account of the production — the organized process that brings about the phenomenon. This is the demand for a mechanistic explanation.

A mechanism, in the philosophical sense, is an organized system of entities and activities whose operation produces, underlies, or maintains a phenomenon. To explain a phenomenon by citing its mechanism is to show how the organized operation of parts gives rise to the whole. This is the framework that has dominated philosophy of biology and neuroscience since the 1990s.

## Machamer, Darden, and Craver's Canonical Account

Machamer, Darden, and Craver's influential 2000 paper "Thinking About Mechanisms" offered a canonical characterization: mechanisms are entities and activities organized to produce regular changes from start or set-up conditions to finish or termination conditions.

Three components are essential. **Entities** are the things that have activities — neurons, enzymes, ions, cells, organisms. **Activities** are what entities do — neurons fire, enzymes catalyze, ions flow, ribosomes translate. Activities are the causal producers of change; they are not merely patterns of behavior but genuine ontic features of the world, the things entities do. **Organization** is the spatial and temporal arrangement of entities and their activities — the sequential order of biochemical reactions, the hierarchical organization of neural circuits, the timing of gene expression.

What makes a mechanism more than a list of parts is the organization: how the parts fit together, interact, and sustain the phenomenon across time.

## Glennan's Invariant Generalizations

Stuart Glennan's approach (*The New Mechanical Philosophy*, 2017) defines a mechanism as a system of interacting parts whose interactions, in virtue of their properties and spatiotemporal organization, are responsible for the phenomenon to be explained. Crucially, the interactions among parts are governed not by fundamental laws of nature but by "invariant generalizations" — generalizations describing the stable behavior of component parts under specified conditions. This gives a non-Humean account of the regularities that constitute mechanistic explanations without requiring primitive necessitation.

## Craver's Etiological and Constitutive Mechanisms

Carl Craver's *Explaining the Brain* (2007) distinguishes two dimensions of mechanistic explanation that are easily conflated.

**Etiological mechanisms** describe the causal history of a phenomenon — the events and processes that causally produced it. The etiological mechanism for a particular neural firing includes the receptor activation, ion channel opening, membrane depolarization, and action potential propagation that preceded it.

**Constitutive mechanisms** describe the system of components whose organized activity constitutes the phenomenon at a finer level. The constitutive mechanism for long-term potentiation includes the synaptic receptors, signaling molecules, and cellular machinery that together implement it. Here the relationship is not causal in the ordinary temporal sense but constitutive: the mechanism is the phenomenon at a finer grain of description.

This distinction matters: the neuroscientist who explains memory by appeal to synaptic plasticity is giving a constitutive mechanistic explanation, not an etiological one. The synaptic changes do not cause memory; they constitute it at the cellular level.

## Mechanisms vs. Regularities

Mechanisms differ from simple regularity accounts in that they describe the productive process, not just the pattern. Two events may be correlated without sharing a mechanism — shoe size and vocabulary in children correlate because both develop with age, but no mechanism connects shoe size to vocabulary. And the same mechanism may produce variable outcomes depending on context — a neuron's mechanism for firing is the same across many conditions, but whether it fires in any particular case depends on the total input pattern.

Mechanistic explanation gives us the "how" of causation — the details of the process — not merely the "that" of correlation.

## The Action Potential as Worked Example

The mechanism of action potential propagation illustrates all of this concretely.

1. *Set-up conditions*: The neuron is at resting membrane potential (~−70 mV). Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels are closed.
2. *Triggering activity*: Sufficient depolarization at the axon hillock (summation of excitatory inputs).
3. *Propagation mechanism*: Na⁺ channels open; Na⁺ ions flow in; membrane depolarizes further; K⁺ channels open (delayed); K⁺ ions flow out; membrane repolarizes; Na⁺ channels inactivate (refractory period); the cycle propagates along the axon in one direction.
4. *Termination*: The action potential reaches the axon terminal; the refractory period prevents backward propagation.

This specifies entities (Na⁺ channels, K⁺ channels, ions), activities (opening, closing, ion flow, depolarization), and organization (the sequential timing and spatial propagation). It answers not just *that* action potentials propagate but *how* — giving genuine mechanistic explanation.

Mechanisms are explanatorily powerful precisely because they decompose the phenomenon into components that can be independently studied and manipulated. A mechanistic explanation supports counterfactuals (block the Na⁺ channels, and the action potential will not propagate), predicts interventions (tetrodotoxin blocks Na⁺ channels and causes paralysis), and provides the basis for multi-level explanation. This explanatory richness goes well beyond what either regularity theories or counterfactual theories alone provide.
