# Mechanisms in Biology and the Life Sciences

What explains gene expression? Not a covering law relating gene to protein — no such law exists at the right level of generality. What explains it is a process: RNA polymerase binding, transcription, processing, export, translation, folding, function. Each step is itself explicable by the interactions of molecular entities with known properties (base pairing, hydrogen bonding, enzyme catalysis). Biology proceeds mechanistically in a way that is not quite like derivation from laws, and the philosophical implications of this are worth attending to.

## Molecular Biology as the Paradigm

The explanation of gene expression involves RNA polymerase binding to a promoter sequence on DNA; transcription of the template strand into pre-mRNA; processing (5' capping, 3' polyadenylation, splicing of introns); export to the cytoplasm; ribosomal translation of codons into amino acids; and post-translational modification and protein folding. Each step is intelligible through the properties of the relevant molecular entities. This hierarchy of mechanisms — cellular, molecular, atomic — is part of what makes biological explanation feel genuinely explanatory rather than merely descriptive. We understand gene expression when we can trace the process from start to finish.

## Etiological and Constitutive Explanation in Biology

Craver's distinction between etiological and constitutive mechanisms is crucial for biology. An etiological explanation of cancer asks why this organism developed it: the causally relevant history includes exposure to carcinogens, somatic mutations in proto-oncogenes, and uncontrolled cell division. A constitutive explanation of cancer asks what cancer is: the cellular and molecular mechanisms that constitute the cancerous state — abnormal proliferation via dysregulated cell cycle checkpoints, evasion of apoptosis, angiogenesis, invasion. The constitutive mechanism is not a cause of cancer; it is what cancer is at a finer level of description.

Both kinds of explanation are legitimate and practically important. Clinicians need both: why this patient developed cancer (etiological) and what cancer involves at the molecular level (constitutive) — the latter guiding targeted therapy.

## Teleological Considerations

Biological mechanisms introduce teleological considerations that purely physical mechanisms do not. Mechanisms in biology can malfunction or function imperfectly, and understanding a mechanism includes understanding what it is *for* and what counts as its proper functioning. The heart's mechanism produces blood circulation. When the mechanism malfunctions (arrhythmia, valve defect), we describe this as malfunction — not merely as a different physical outcome. This normative dimension connects to debates about biological function.

On the etiological theory of function (Wright, Millikan), the heart pumps blood because it was selected by evolution for that function. On the dispositional theory (Cummins), blood-pumping is the heart's function because it is the capacity of the heart that contributes to the circulatory system's operation. Either way, the normative dimension — what the mechanism is supposed to do — distinguishes biological from purely physical mechanisms, and connects to our concept of disease as mechanism malfunction.

## The Reductionism Question

The explanatory practice of molecular biology raises the question of reduction: do higher-level biological explanations reduce to molecular mechanisms?

The reductionist view holds that all biological phenomena are in principle explicable in terms of molecular mechanisms, which reduce to chemistry, which reduces to physics. Higher-level explanations are useful summaries but are ontologically reducible. The non-reductionist view (Kitcher, Fodor) insists that higher-level biological explanations are not reducible because biological kinds are multiply realizable. The concept of a gene, a membrane, a predator-prey relationship abstracts over heterogeneous molecular details. Molecular explanation misses the biological pattern — the explanation of why natural selection favors certain traits cannot be captured in molecular terms, because selection operates on fitness differences defined relative to environmental context.

**Mechanistic pluralism** (Craver, Bechtel) takes the most nuanced position: mechanistic explanations are inherently multi-level. A complete explanation of associative learning involves mechanisms at the synaptic, circuit, regional, and behavioral levels simultaneously. No single level is fundamental; each level captures something genuine and contributes to the full explanation. Reduction is not elimination: understanding the molecular mechanism does not render the cellular or behavioral description redundant.

## Evidence, Intervention, and Discovery

The mechanistic framework drives experimental practice. If a phenomenon is constituted by a mechanism, then intervening on mechanism components should change the phenomenon (entity manipulation), disrupting the mechanism should disrupt the phenomenon (bottom-up), and altering the phenomenon should be reflected in changes to the mechanism (top-down). Craver calls these "interlevel experiments."

The evidence that LTP (long-term potentiation) underlies memory formation comes from exactly this bidirectional constraint: manipulating NMDA receptors at the molecular level changes memory performance at the behavioral level, and this bidirectional coupling supports the mechanistic hypothesis. Neither direction alone would establish the mechanistic relationship; both together do.

In medicine, mechanistic reasoning plays a distinctive and sometimes contested role. Epidemiological evidence establishes type-level causal associations (smoking causes lung cancer); mechanistic understanding explains why at the molecular level (carcinogens in tobacco smoke cause DNA damage leading to oncogene activation). Both are needed. Statistical evidence without mechanism leaves possible confounding unexplained. Mechanism without statistical evidence risks describing an in vitro artifact that does not operate in vivo. Bradford Hill's criteria for causal inference in epidemiology include "biological plausibility" and "coherence" — essentially, consistency with mechanistic knowledge. The integration of statistical and mechanistic evidence remains the gold standard for establishing causal claims in medicine.
