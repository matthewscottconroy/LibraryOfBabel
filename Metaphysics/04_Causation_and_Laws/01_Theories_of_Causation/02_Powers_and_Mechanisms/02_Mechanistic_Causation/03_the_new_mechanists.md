# The New Mechanists

For most of the twentieth century, the received philosophical account of scientific explanation was the Deductive-Nomological model: to explain an event is to derive its description from general laws plus initial conditions. The model had logical clarity and appealed to the image of physics as the paradigm of explanation. But it never quite fit biology. Working biologists do not explain phenomena by subsuming them under universal laws; they explain them by identifying mechanisms — by decomposing phenomena into the organized activities of component entities and showing how those activities produce the phenomenon in question. The "new mechanism," emerging in the 1990s, made this methodological fact into a philosophical program.

The key figures — Peter Machamer, Lindley Darden, Carl Craver, Stuart Glennan — developed mechanistic explanation as an alternative to the covering-law model (Hempel and Oppenheim 1948). The DN model classified irrelevant factors as explanatory (the flagpole-shadow problem), allowed deduction of causes from effects (the symmetry problem), and could not capture the explanatory structure of biology. The new mechanists argued that explanation in biology and neuroscience is not nomological but *mechanistic*: it works by decomposing phenomena into the organized activities of component entities.

## Glennan's Mechanism Account

Stuart Glennan defined mechanisms as systems of interacting parts causally responsible for a phenomenon. The laws governing part interactions are not fundamental laws of nature but "invariant generalizations" — stable descriptions of component behavior under specified conditions. Key features: mechanisms are **system-relative** (what counts as a mechanism depends on the system and phenomenon being analyzed); they exhibit **hierarchical organization** (the parts of a mechanism may themselves be mechanisms); and the laws governing components are **phenomenological** rather than fundamental (they describe component behavior without giving the underlying microphysical basis). This yields a non-Humean account of scientific regularities — the invariant generalizations are grounded in the physical structure of the components — without requiring primitive necessitation.

## Machamer, Darden, and Craver (MDC 2000)

The MDC framework became the standard reference point. Its distinctive contribution was the concept of **activities** as irreducible causal categories. Activities are "the producers of change" — not mere patterns or regularities but genuine ontic features of the world, the things entities do. Neurons *fire*; enzymes *catalyze*; ribosomes *translate*. These activities are not reducible to the properties of the entities involved, nor simply events described in terms of property instantiations. They are a distinct ontological category.

This has been controversial. Critics (Psillos) argue that activities are not a genuine ontological addition but simply events described at an appropriate level of abstraction. Defenders (Machamer) insist that activities capture something missed by entity-based and event-based ontologies alike: the *doing*, the causal productivity, that is the core of mechanistic explanation.

## Craver's Inter-Level Framework

Craver's *Explaining the Brain* (2007) refined the framework with several important distinctions. He contrasts **how-possibly models** — which describe mechanisms that could produce the phenomenon — with **how-actually models** — which describe the mechanism that does produce the phenomenon in the system under study. Only how-actually models provide genuine scientific explanations. A how-possibly model is a heuristic, a hypothesis to be tested. Evidence from manipulation, lesion, and recording studies is needed to upgrade how-possibly to how-actually.

The criterion of **mutual manipulation** specifies when a component is mechanistically relevant to a phenomenon Φ: X is relevant iff (a) manipulating X changes Φ, and (b) manipulating Φ changes the behavior of X. This bidirectional criterion distinguishes genuine mechanism components from mere correlates — a component that only changes when Φ changes (bottom-up) but cannot itself be manipulated to change Φ (top-down) is not a genuine part of the mechanism.

## Objections

The causation presupposition is the most direct: mechanisms are defined in terms of causal interactions among parts, so if the goal is to illuminate causation through mechanisms, the account is circular. The new mechanists acknowledge this but hold that the goal is not a reductive analysis of causation — it is illumination of the structure of scientific explanation, which can presuppose causation without circularity for that purpose.

Discrete parts and continuous processes raise a different difficulty. The mechanistic framework assumes that phenomena can be decomposed into discrete entities and their activities. But some phenomena in physics and chemistry — field dynamics, thermodynamic processes, quantum interference — do not involve discrete, independently characterizable parts. Statistical mechanics explains thermodynamic properties not by identifying discrete mechanisms but by applying probabilistic reasoning to many-particle systems.

Extensions of the framework to social mechanisms (Hedström and Ylikoski) and cognitive mechanisms (Piccinini on computation) face the challenge that the relevant entities (agents, representational states) are not as clearly individuated as molecular entities. Whether the MDC schema applies without modification to these domains is contested — but the attempt itself reflects a confidence in the framework's generative power.
