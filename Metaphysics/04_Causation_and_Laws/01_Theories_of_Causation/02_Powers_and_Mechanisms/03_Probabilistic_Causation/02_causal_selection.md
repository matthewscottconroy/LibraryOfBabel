# Causal Selection: Which Factor Is 'The' Cause?

Suppose we have identified every condition jointly sufficient for a house fire: the short circuit, the flammable materials, the oxygen, the absent sprinklers, the absence of the fire brigade at the moment of ignition. Mackie's analysis correctly identifies all of these as causally relevant — each is a necessary part of a sufficient condition. Yet we say "the short circuit caused the fire," not "the presence of oxygen caused the fire," even though both are equally INUS conditions. The metaphysical question — which conditions are causally relevant — is distinct from the selective question that now confronts us: which one is "the" cause?

## Pragmatic Theories of Causal Selection

The natural first response is that causal selection reflects practical interests. We select the factor that departs from normal or expected conditions. Oxygen is normally present and expected; a short circuit is abnormal. The abnormal factor is selected as "the" cause against the background of normal conditions (Hart and Honoré 1959; Hilton and Slugoski 1986).

We also tend to select factors that it was within someone's power to prevent or alter. A safer electrical installation would have prevented the fire; we cannot prevent the presence of oxygen in buildings. The controllable factor is causally relevant in the practically important sense.

Different investigators asking different questions will select different INUS conditions as "the" cause. The fire marshal wants to know what started the fire (the short circuit). The building code inspector wants to know why the fire spread quickly (flammable materials). The insurance assessor wants to know why the sprinkler failed. Each context selects a different factor because different questions are being asked.

## Contrastive Causation: Schaffer's Account

Jonathan Schaffer argues that causal claims are inherently contrastive: C (rather than C*) causes E (rather than E*). The cause and effect are both contrastive — selected against a background of alternatives. This makes causal claims four-place rather than two-place: CAUSES(c, c*, e, e*), where c* is the cause-contrast (what else might have happened instead of c) and e* is the effect-contrast (what alternative outcome we are interested in).

Ask whether the spark or the oxygen caused the explosion:

- CAUSES(spark, no spark, explosion, no explosion): True. Pressing the spark rather than not having it caused the explosion rather than no explosion.
- CAUSES(oxygen, no oxygen, explosion, no explosion): Also true. Having oxygen rather than not having it caused the explosion rather than no explosion.
- CAUSES(spark, match, explosion, no explosion): False. A match would also have caused the explosion; the spark rather than the match did not cause the explosion rather than no explosion.

Different contrasts yield different (and potentially all true) causal claims. Causal attribution is context-sensitive without being arbitrary: once the contrast is specified, there is a fact of the matter about which contrastive claim is true.

## Hart and Honoré: Legal Causation

H.L.A. Hart and Tony Honoré's *Causation in the Law* (1959) argued that legal and moral contexts have their own standards for causal selection. Their central distinction separates a **cause** (a factor responsible for the harm — typically an abnormal intervention by a human agent) from a **condition** (a merely background factor, typically a normal, expected state of affairs).

The legal notion of causation focuses on actions of agents against a background of natural processes. When a person starts a fire during a drought, the arsonist's action is the cause (abnormal agent intervention); the drought conditions are the background (natural). This reflects the practical orientation of legal attribution: we hold agents responsible, not circumstances.

The but-for test ("but for which the harm would not have occurred") is the law's fundamental causal test: an event C is a but-for cause of E iff E would not have occurred but for C. But-for causation is necessary for legal liability in most jurisdictions, though not sufficient — it is too inclusive, since many remote factors satisfy the but-for test.

## The Metaphysics vs. the Pragmatics

A deep question is whether causal selection is merely pragmatic or has a metaphysical component. On the pragmatic view, the metaphysical facts fully determine which conditions are causally relevant; causal selection only reflects our contextual interests. There is no objective fact about which INUS condition is "the" cause in a metaphysically deep sense — just facts about which we care about in context.

Schaffer's contrastive account is more nuanced: some contrastive causal claims are objectively true and others false, given a specified contrast. The context determines which contrast is relevant, but once specified, the answer is determined by the causal structure of the world. The objectivity lies in the conditional: given the contrast, the causal facts decide.

## Implications for Moral Responsibility

Causal selection has direct implications for moral responsibility. We hold agents responsible for harms they caused, not merely for harms with which their actions were correlated. Whether we attribute the harm to the agent's action rather than to background conditions depends on which contrast is morally salient — typically, the contrast between what the agent did and what they should have done instead.

This normative dimension modifies the purely metaphysical notion of causation: the correct contrast class for causal attribution in moral contexts is partly fixed by what the agent should have done, not merely by what would have happened under counterfactual alternatives. Legal and moral contexts thus embed causal attribution within a richer normative framework. Understanding causal selection is therefore not just a metaphysical project — it is also an inquiry into how causal concepts function within practices of praise, blame, and responsibility.
