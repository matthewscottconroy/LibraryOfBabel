# Dispositions and Causal Powers

The causal powers approach to causation represents a significant departure from both Humean regularity theories and counterfactual analyses. Rather than analyzing causation in terms of patterns of events or counterfactual dependencies, powers theorists hold that causation is grounded in the intrinsic dispositions or powers of objects. An object has causal powers — capacities to produce certain effects — and these powers are genuine, irreducible features of reality.

The basic idea has ancient roots in Aristotelian natural philosophy, which recognized that objects have active and passive powers: the fire has the active power to heat, and the wax has the passive power to be melted. These powers explain why certain events occur: the wax melts because the fire's heating power activates the wax's susceptibility. This explanatory structure is different from regularity explanations: we are not merely noting that fires are constantly conjoined with melting wax, but pointing to an intrinsic feature of the fire and the wax that makes the melting intelligible.

Contemporary powers theories, associated with philosophers like Sydney Shoemaker, C.B. Martin, George Molnar, Stephen Mumford, and Brian Ellis, have developed this Aristotelian insight in sophisticated ways. On these views, a power is a dispositional property: a property whose nature is essentially directed toward its manifestation. Fragility is a disposition to break under impact. Solubility is a disposition to dissolve in water. Charge is a disposition to exert forces on other charged objects.

The power is not merely a tendency for a regularity to hold. Rather, the power "points toward" its manifestation in a way that is intrinsic to the property itself. This directedness or intentionality of dispositions is emphasized by Martin and Heil. A power is for its manifestation — it is constitutively oriented toward producing a certain type of effect.

This view has important consequences for the metaphysics of causation. If powers are real, then causation is not merely a matter of events following each other. When a cause produces its effect, the power of the cause is genuinely activated and brings about the effect. There is a real productive relationship in the world, not just a pattern of succession. This is the "oomph" or "cement" that Humean analyses deny but many philosophers feel must be there.
