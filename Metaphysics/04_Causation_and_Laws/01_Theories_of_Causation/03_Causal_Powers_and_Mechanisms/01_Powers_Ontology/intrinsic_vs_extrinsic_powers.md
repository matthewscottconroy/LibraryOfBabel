# Intrinsic and Extrinsic Grounds of Dispositions

A central debate in the metaphysics of powers concerns whether dispositions are grounded in categorical (intrinsic, non-dispositional) properties or whether they are themselves fundamental and irreducible. This debate connects to broader questions about the nature of properties and the structure of physical reality.

The standard Humean view holds that dispositions are grounded in categorical bases. Fragility is grounded in the molecular structure of the glass — a categorical arrangement of molecules that, together with relevant laws of nature, gives rise to the disposition to break. The disposition is not a primitive feature of reality; it is a derived, relational feature that depends on categorical properties and laws. On this view, a "pure disposition" — a disposition with no categorical basis — is a philosopher's fiction.

The opposed view, developed by C.B. Martin, George Molnar, and others, holds that dispositions can be fundamental. On this "pure dispositionalism," properties are essentially dispositional: their very nature consists in their causal roles. The charge of an electron is constitutively a power to repel other electrons and attract protons — there is no underlying categorical property that "has" this disposition. The disposition goes all the way down.

Brian Ellis's "scientific essentialism" argues that natural properties, as identified by fundamental physics, are dispositional through and through. The charge of a particle is not a categorical property that happens to be dispositionsto exert electromagnetic forces; it is essentially a power. The laws of nature hold necessarily because they express the essential natures of the properties that figure in them.

Sydney Shoemaker took an intermediate view: properties are individuated by their causal roles (their potential to contribute to causal interactions), but this does not commit us to full-blown pure dispositionalism. The identity conditions for properties are causal, but there is still categorical structure in the world.

An objection to pure dispositionalism is the "always packing, never arriving" problem (Martin). If every property is a disposition to manifest another disposition, we get an infinite regress with no categorical grounding. Defenders respond that dispositions can be reciprocal: the manifestation of one power may be another power, without regress, because powers can be mutually grounding or can have manifestations that are events rather than further properties.
