# Manifestation, Interference, and Finkish Dispositions

One of the technical challenges for powers theories is giving a satisfactory account of when dispositions manifest and when they are blocked or masked. A fragile glass does not always break — it breaks only when struck under the right conditions and in the absence of protective interference. A soluble substance does not always dissolve — it dissolves only when immersed in the right solvent. How should we understand the relationship between the disposition and its manifestation?

The simple conditional analysis held that "x is disposed to M if C" means "if C were the case, x would M." But this analysis fails for finkish dispositions (discovered by C.B. Martin). A finkish disposition is one that would disappear if the triggering condition were to obtain. Imagine a sorcerer who ensures that whenever a fragile glass is about to be struck, he magically makes it strong. The glass satisfies the disposition conditional — if struck, it would not break, because the sorcerer would intervene — but the glass is still fragile. Its fragility is "finked": the triggering condition would remove the disposition.

Alexander Bird and Lewis proposed a "ceteris paribus" modifier: x is disposed to M if C, all else being equal. But this faces masking cases: the disposition is present, the triggering condition obtains, but some interfering factor prevents manifestation. The disposed-to conditional fails, yet the disposition exists. A buried land mine is disposed to explode when stepped on, but it has been defused without removing the disposition.

These cases have led many powers theorists to hold that dispositions are not analyzable in terms of conditionals at all. Rather, dispositions are primitive categorical features of objects that have an intrinsic "pointing toward" their manifestation. This is the view of Martin and Heil: properties are dual — they are both dispositional and qualitative, neither reducible to the other.

The debate about manifestation and interference matters for causation because it concerns when causal powers are activated and when they produce their effects. Understanding interference and prevention is essential for a complete theory of causation: preventive causation, causation by omission, and the difference between a cause and a mere enabling condition all turn on questions about how dispositions interact.
