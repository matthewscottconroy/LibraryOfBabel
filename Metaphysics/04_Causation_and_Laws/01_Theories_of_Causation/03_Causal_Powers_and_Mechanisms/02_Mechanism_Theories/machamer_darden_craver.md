# Mechanism Theories of Causation

Mechanism theories of causation hold that causal explanation consists in identifying mechanisms: organized systems of entities and activities that produce, underlie, or maintain a phenomenon. This approach, associated with Peter Machamer, Lindley Darden, and Carl Craver (MDC), has been particularly influential in philosophy of biology and neuroscience, where causal explanation routinely invokes mechanisms.

On the MDC account, mechanisms are entities and activities organized such that they are responsible for the phenomenon to be explained. Entities are the components of mechanisms — molecules, cells, organs, organisms. Activities are what entities do — enzymes bind to substrates, action potentials propagate along axons, muscles contract. The organization of entities and activities — their spatial and temporal arrangement, their relations of activation and inhibition — determines what the mechanism produces.

This framework gives a distinctive account of what causal explanation accomplishes. To explain why neurons fire is not merely to cite a prior event and note a regularity. It is to describe a mechanism: sodium channels open, sodium ions flow in, membrane potential changes, and eventually an action potential is generated. Each step in the mechanism involves entities engaging in activities, producing intermediate states, which in turn enable further activities. Causal explanation is mechanistic description.

Craver has applied this framework specifically to neuroscience, arguing that both etiological and constitutive explanations are mechanical. Etiological explanations cite earlier events in the mechanism's history; constitutive explanations describe the components and their activities that make up the phenomenon. His book *Explaining the Brain* (2007) is an extended defense of this mechanistic picture.

The mechanism approach has been criticized for presupposing causation at the level of entities and activities rather than analyzing it. If activities are defined as things entities do that produce effects, causation appears in the definition. Defenders respond that the goal is not to analyze causation in non-causal terms but to illuminate the structure of causal explanation, particularly in the special sciences.

The framework also faces questions about the spatial and temporal limits of mechanisms, about how to individuate mechanisms from their environments, and about how to handle emergent phenomena that are not obviously compositional.
