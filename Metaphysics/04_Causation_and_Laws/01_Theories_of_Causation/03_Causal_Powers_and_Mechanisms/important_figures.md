# Important Figures: Causal Powers and Mechanisms

**Aristotle (384–322 BCE)** introduced the distinction between active and passive powers and the concept of formal, material, efficient, and final causes. His hylomorphic framework grounded the causal powers tradition that runs through medieval philosophy to the present.

**Sydney Shoemaker (b. 1931)** argued that properties are individuated by their causal roles. His "Causality and Properties" (1980) is foundational for contemporary powers theories.

**C.B. Martin (1924–2008)** developed the notion of the "finkish" disposition and argued that properties are "double aspect" — both dispositional and qualitative. His *The Mind in Nature* (2008, with John Heil) is a central text.

**George Molnar (1934–1999)** defended pure dispositionalism and the view that powers have genuine intentionality (directedness toward manifestations), in *Powers: A Study in Metaphysics* (2003).

**Brian Ellis (b. 1929)** developed "scientific essentialism," holding that natural properties are essentially dispositional and that the laws of nature hold necessarily. See *Scientific Essentialism* (2001).

**Stephen Mumford (b. 1967)** has developed the powers ontology extensively, arguing in *Laws in Nature* (2004) that laws of nature can be eliminated in favor of irreducible powers.

**Wesley Salmon (1925–2001)** developed the causal process theory in *Scientific Explanation and the Causal Structure of the World* (1984).

**Phil Dowe** refined Salmon's account with the conserved quantity theory in *Physical Causation* (2000).

**Peter Machamer, Lindley Darden, and Carl Craver** developed the mechanism framework for causal explanation in the life sciences. Craver's *Explaining the Brain* (2007) is the most comprehensive philosophical treatment.
