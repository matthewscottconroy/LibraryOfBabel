# Causal Asymmetry

Causation is asymmetric: if C causes E, then E does not cause C. This seems almost definitional — yet explaining where the asymmetry comes from is surprisingly difficult, especially in light of the time-symmetry of the fundamental laws of physics. The laws of classical mechanics, electromagnetism, and (with minor qualifications) quantum mechanics are time-symmetric: they have the same form under time-reversal. A film of billiard balls colliding can be run backward and the backward film is physically possible. So why do causes always precede effects? Why does causal influence always run forward in time?

This is not a terminological question. The asymmetry of causation underlies the asymmetry of explanation, memory, knowledge, and agency. We explain the present by the past (not vice versa), we remember the past and predict the future (not vice versa), and we can influence future events but apparently not past ones. All of these asymmetries connect to causal asymmetry. Explaining causal asymmetry means explaining some of the deepest structural features of our temporal existence.

## The Temporal Account

The simplest account: causes precede effects because of the direction of time. The causal arrow just is the temporal arrow. But this relocates the problem: what explains the direction of time?

Physics offers one candidate — the **thermodynamic arrow**: the direction in which entropy increases. The second law of thermodynamics is not time-symmetric; it has a preferred temporal direction. And since the laws of fundamental physics are time-symmetric, the thermodynamic arrow must be explained by boundary conditions — specifically, the extraordinarily low entropy state of the early universe. On this account, the causal arrow derives from the thermodynamic arrow, which derives from cosmological initial conditions. The asymmetry of causation is an artifact of the special initial conditions of the Big Bang, not a fundamental feature of the laws themselves.

## Lewis's Account: Asymmetry of Overdetermination

Lewis grounded causal asymmetry in the **asymmetry of counterfactual dependence**, which he derived from an "asymmetry of overdetermination." Any past event leaves many traces and records scattered across the future: the state of the universe at any time is a rich compendium of information about what happened before. But the future is not similarly overdetermined by a rich set of anticipatory signals from the present.

This asymmetry means that if we subtract any particular cause from the past, the effect can be "rescued" by the remaining traces — the world can be patched up to make C's absence consistent with almost everything else. If we subtract any particular effect from the future, the cause is not similarly rescued — the world must diverge significantly to accommodate the absence of E.

Lewis argued this asymmetry explains why counterfactuals run forward: "if C had not occurred, E would not have occurred" is true; "if E had not occurred, C would not have occurred" is typically false, because C's occurrence left many independent traces. This grounds the temporal direction of causation in patterns of overdetermination rather than in a primitive temporal asymmetry.

## Price's Perspectivalist Challenge

Huw Price (*Time's Arrow and Archimedes' Point*, 1996) argues that Lewis's account, and most philosophical accounts of causal asymmetry, illicitly exploit our temporal perspective as agents embedded in time. When we say "the past is overdetermined by the future," we are already assuming we know less about the future than the past. But from a "view from nowhen" — from outside of time, describing the complete spacetime manifold — there is no preferred direction. The asymmetry is in our epistemic position, not in the world.

From the "Archimedean" standpoint, the world is a four-dimensional block of events with no intrinsic earlier-later relation. The causal arrow we perceive reflects how we are embedded in time as agents, not a feature of the block universe itself. Price's perspectivalism does not deny that causation has an asymmetric character in our experience; it denies that this asymmetry is a metaphysical feature of the external world rather than a product of our epistemic situation.

## Powers Theories and Temporal Directionality

Powers theorists face the asymmetry problem in a different form. If powers are intrinsic to objects and constituted by their manifestations, why do manifestations always come after the exercise of the power? Several responses present themselves.

One is to build temporal directionality into powers themselves: powers are essentially *forward*-directed, constitutively pointed at future manifestations. This avoids the problem but may be ad hoc unless grounded in a deeper account of temporal direction. A second response grounds the temporal direction of powers in the thermodynamic arrow, deriving the forward orientation of manifestation from the same cosmological source as the rest of temporal asymmetry. A third response (Mumford) holds that powers are non-temporally directed — they are directed at their manifestations without the manifestations being essentially in the future. Time's direction explains why we observe powers manifesting forward, but this is not part of the essential nature of powers.

## The Interventionist Account

Woodward's interventionism provides a different kind of account of causal asymmetry, based on **agency**. Causes are prior to effects in the following practical sense: we can intervene on a cause to change an effect, but we cannot (in standard cases) intervene on an effect to change the cause. This is not purely a metaphysical claim about time's direction but a claim about the structure of agential intervention.

Why can we change effects by changing causes but not vice versa? Partly because of temporal direction (we cannot change the past), but also because of the structure of causal mechanisms: a genuine cause is connected to its effect by a mechanism that runs from cause to effect, not the reverse. This gives an agent-relative account of causal asymmetry — from the standpoint of an agent embedded in time with a certain direction of practical access, causes are what we manipulate, effects are what we produce. The asymmetry is partly perspectival but not thereby merely subjective: it reflects the real structure of the agent's situation in time.
