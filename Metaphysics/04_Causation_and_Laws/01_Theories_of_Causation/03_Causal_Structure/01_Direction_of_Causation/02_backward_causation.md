# Backward Causation

We standardly assume that causes precede effects. This temporal priority is so deeply embedded in our thinking about causation that it can seem analytic — part of what we mean by a cause. Yet philosophers have argued that backward causation is at least conceptually coherent, and physicists have taken seriously whether it might be physically possible. The question is worth pressing precisely because it illuminates what the temporal asymmetry of causation actually rests on.

## Conceptual Coherence

The first question is whether backward causation is even coherently describable — whether there is a consistent scenario involving backward causal influence that does not generate contradiction. Michael Dummett's "Bringing About the Past" (1964) made the case. He imagined a tribe whose chief performs a rain dance after the young men have left on a dangerous expedition, believing that the dance will cause them to have been brave. This seems absurd — how can you cause something that has already happened? But Dummett argued that if the chief does not know the outcome and will not discover it until the men return, there is no logical incoherence in supposing that the action causes the past state. The impossibility is epistemic (we usually know the past and can verify it independently) rather than metaphysical.

The key condition for coherent backward causation is **causal isolation**: the backward causal influence should not be discoverable by the agent before acting, otherwise the agent could use the information to prevent the cause, generating a causal loop and contradiction.

## The Causal Loop Problem

The main objection to backward causation is the paradox of causal loops. If present C causes past E, and E causes some intermediate event F, which causes something that prevents C, we have a contradiction: C both occurs (it causes E) and does not occur (it is prevented by F). The logical structure:

- **P1**: C occurs and causes E (which is before C).
- **P2**: E causes F.
- **P3**: F prevents C.
- **C**: C both occurs (P1) and does not occur (P3). Contradiction.

Three responses to this paradox have been developed. The **consistency constraint** approach accepts that backward causation is possible only for self-consistent causal histories. The Novikov Self-Consistency Principle holds that only causal histories that do not generate contradictions can exist — you cannot travel back to kill your grandfather because any attempt to do so would fail as part of the consistent causal history. The **epistemic constraint** approach holds that backward causation is possible only in conditions where the agent cannot acquire information that would allow them to close the loop. The chief cannot know the outcome of the expedition before performing the dance; if he could, he could act to prevent the past state, generating contradiction. The **many-worlds resolution** handles backward causation by branching: the world where C occurs and causes E is a different branch from the world where C is prevented. No contradiction arises because the two branches are distinct.

## Physical Cases

In physics, several theories have been interpreted as involving backward causation, though the interpretation is contested. The Wheeler-Feynman absorber theory of electromagnetism uses both retarded (forward-in-time) and advanced (backward-in-time) electromagnetic waves. The advanced waves propagate backward from the absorber to the emitter. Whether this constitutes backward causation or is merely a formal representation of a time-symmetric process is debated.

Several approaches to the foundations of quantum mechanics have explored backward causation as a way of explaining quantum correlations without non-locality. The two-state formalism of Aharonov et al. uses both forward-evolving and backward-evolving quantum states. The "transactional interpretation" (Cramer) explicitly invokes forward and backward waves that "handshake" to produce quantum events. These interpretations remain minority views, but they show that backward causation is at least taken seriously at the foundations of physics.

## Philosophical Assessment

Three conclusions are worth drawing. First, the concept of causation does not analytically include temporal priority. Temporal priority is a contingent feature of actual causation in our world — derivable from its low-entropy initial conditions — not a necessary feature of causation as such. Second, backward causation is possible only for self-consistent causal histories — the possible worlds where backward causation occurs are those where no contradictions arise. Third, even in a world with backward causation, the epistemic asymmetry of memory and anticipation would likely persist, grounded in thermodynamics. We could have backward causal influence without knowing about it, just as we are sometimes influenced by causes we cannot observe.

The question of backward causation connects deeply to the metaphysics of time (the block universe versus dynamic time), the foundations of physics (time reversal symmetry), and the analysis of counterfactual conditionals (can we reason backward from effects to causes?). It is, in short, not an exotic puzzle but a pressure point on some of the most fundamental commitments in our picture of causal reality.
