# Causal Relata: Events, Facts, or Processes?

Before we can theorize about causation, we need to settle what kinds of things causation relates. The striking of a match causes the igniting of the match — but what exactly are these things, the striking and the igniting? Are they events? Facts? States of affairs? Processes? This is the question of causal relata, and the choice turns out to have real consequences for how the theory of causation must be framed.

## Events as Causal Relata

The most common view in analytic philosophy is that causation is a relation between events. But events can be individuated more or less coarsely, and the choice matters.

Lewis analyzed causation as a relation between events that are concrete, spatiotemporally local occurrences, individuated finely: the specific manner and timing of an event is part of its identity. The death that was quick and painless is a different event from the death that was slow and agonizing, even if the same individual died in both cases. This fine-grained individuation allows a richer treatment of causal relevance — we can ask not only whether C caused E to occur but how C caused E to occur in the specific manner it did.

Jaegwon Kim analyzed events as **property exemplifications**: an event is the instantiation of a property P by an object x at a time t — written [x, P, t]. This structured account connects events directly to properties, enabling analysis of different aspects of causal relevance. We can distinguish whether causation (did C cause E to occur at all?), how causation (did C cause E to occur in the manner it did?), and when causation (did C cause E to occur at the time it did?). Each question concerns different features of the events, selectable by varying the property specification.

## Facts as Causal Relata

Some philosophers prefer facts rather than events as causal relata. Facts are more fine-grained and include negative facts (the absence of rain caused the crop to fail), which events cannot easily represent.

Jonathan Bennett drew a careful distinction between events (concrete happenings, the particulars of Kim's account) and facts (true propositions about how things are). Many ordinary causal statements are naturally read as relating facts: "The fact that it rained caused the match to be cancelled." "What caused the fire was the electrical fault, not the presence of oxygen." These locutions resist translation into event-talk without distortion.

The fact view accommodates negative causation ("the absence of rain caused the drought"), generic causation ("fear causes sweating"), and aspectual causation ("it was *because* he was drunk that he crashed" — pointing to a specific aspect of the situation). The objection is that facts seem too fine-grained — they multiply causal relata implausibly, and the view needs a theory of which facts are causally relevant that turns out to be as difficult as specifying which features of events are causally relevant.

## Processes as Causal Relata

Wesley Salmon preferred **causal processes** as the fundamental causal relata: spatiotemporally continuous physical processes that transmit energy and momentum. On this view, causation is not a relation between discrete events but a feature of continuous physical processes.

This is more congenial to physics, where causation involves the transfer of physical quantities along world-lines rather than relations between discrete happenings. The causal process from explosion to building collapse is the spatiotemporally continuous transmission of energy through the structural elements, not a relation between two discrete events.

The advantages — naturalism, objectivity, spatiotemporal continuity — are genuine. The disadvantages are equally genuine: no process runs from the absence of watering to the dying plant, so causation by omission is problematic; no physical process connects social pressure to behavioral compliance, so higher-level causation is out of reach; and distinguishing causal processes from pseudo-processes requires a criterion (mark transmission, conserved quantity possession) that has its own difficulties.

## States of Affairs and Tropes

Some metaphysicians prefer **states of affairs** or **tropes** (property instances) as causal relata. A trope is the particular instance of a property: this particular redness of this particular rose, not the universal redness, not the rose itself. States of affairs composed of tropes can serve as fine-grained relata that are sensitive to the particular properties instantiated.

## Implications for Causation Theory

The choice of causal relata affects several downstream theoretical questions. If relata are events (concrete happenings), absences are not events and cannot be causes. If relata are facts, the fact that rain was absent can be a cause. If relata are property exemplifications (Kim), then different properties of the same situation can be differently causally relevant — enabling discussion of mental causation, where the mental property and the physical property of the same event may have different causal roles. If relata are processes, multi-level causation is difficult to analyze; if they are facts, facts at different levels can stand in causal relations independently.

No consensus has emerged on the correct ontology of causal relata, suggesting that the choice is partly a matter of which theoretical purpose is being served. Different projects — the analysis of mental causation, the theory of causal explanation, the practice of scientific inference — may benefit from different choices of relata.
