# Token and Type Causation

A surgeon removes a patient's appendix, and the patient recovers. Did the surgery cause the recovery? We need to be careful here: there are two quite different questions being asked. The first is about this particular surgery and this particular recovery — a specific causal transaction between specific events on a specific day. The second is about whether surgery of this type generally causes recoveries of this type — a general causal pattern. These are logically independent, and conflating them generates persistent confusions in science, law, and philosophy.

## The Distinction Defined

**Token causation** (singular causation) is a relation between particular events, states, or processes. "The surgery on March 5th caused this patient's recovery." The relata are particulars — dated, located, individual happenings.

**Type causation** (general causation) is a relation between types or kinds of events. "Surgery causes recovery." The relata are event types — repeatable patterns of occurrence.

The logical independence cuts both ways. Type causation without token causation: smoking causes cancer (in general), but perhaps no particular cancer was actually caused by this particular smoker's smoking — they might have developed cancer anyway from a genetic predisposition. Token causation without type causation: the singularist claims this is possible — a unique, unrepeatable causal interaction that is not an instance of any general causal pattern. Regularity theorists deny this: all token causation is grounded in type causation.

## Token Causation in Philosophical Theory

Most philosophical theories of causation focus on token causation. Partly because token causation seems conceptually primary — we begin with paradigmatic individual causal transactions and build our theory from there. Partly because token causation is what matters morally and legally: courts ask whether *this* drug caused *this* patient's harm, not whether the drug causes harm in general. And partly because token causation is metaphysically more fundamental on non-Humean views: the general patterns derive from individual causal facts, not vice versa.

Counterfactual theories (Lewis), process theories (Salmon), and powers theories (Molnar) are all primarily accounts of token causation.

## Type Causation in Scientific Practice

Type causation is what is straightforwardly established by scientific methods: randomized controlled trials compare outcomes in treated and control groups to establish that the treatment type causes the outcome type. Epidemiological cohort studies track populations to establish that exposure type causes disease type. Laboratory experiments establish general causal laws governing types of physical, chemical, or biological processes.

The statistical evidence for type causation is always limited: it establishes that cause-type and effect-type are systematically related across a population. It does not establish which individuals in the population were causally affected.

## The Gap Between Token and Type

The gap creates philosophical and practical puzzles that the courts have struggled with. Even if we know that Drug X causes cancer (type causation), can we establish that Drug X caused this particular plaintiff's cancer (token causation)? Epidemiological evidence establishes type causation; proof of specific causation requires additional steps — that the plaintiff's cancer was of the type that Drug X is known to cause, that the plaintiff was exposed to Drug X, that there are no other causes more likely to explain this specific cancer.

The probabilistic approach to specific causation offers a formula: probability of causation = (P(E|C) - P(E|¬C)) / P(E|C). If Drug X raises the probability of cancer from 0.01 to 0.05, then for a plaintiff who developed cancer, the probability that Drug X caused it is 0.8 (80%). Courts have debated whether probabilistic evidence of this kind is sufficient for token causal attribution.

## Davidson's Anomalous Monism and the Type/Token Gap

Donald Davidson's anomalous monism holds that mental events are token-identical to physical events. Mental events cause physical events (token causation) because they are identical to physical events that cause physical events. But Davidson also held that there are no strict psychophysical laws — no type causal generalizations connecting mental types to physical types.

This creates a puzzle: how can we have token mental-to-physical causation without type mental-to-physical causation? Davidson's answer is that it is the physical properties of the mental event — not the mental properties — that figure in the strict causal laws. But then the mental properties seem causally irrelevant, which threatens the autonomy of mental causation. This is the problem of **causal exclusion**, and it connects directly to the type/token distinction: token causation without type causation turns out to be philosophically unstable in the mental case, generating pressure toward either reducing mental causation to physical causation or reconceiving the relation between levels.
