# Causation at Higher Levels of Description

Hippocampal damage causes memory loss. Stress causes cortisol release. Inflation reduces unemployment. Each of these is a genuine causal claim — the kind we rely on in neuroscience, medicine, and economics. But each describes causation at a level of description higher than fundamental physics. And that fact generates a philosophical puzzle: if physical causes are always sufficient for physical effects, what room is left for causes specified at higher levels?

## The Causal Exclusion Problem

Jaegwon Kim's **causal exclusion argument** is the sharpest challenge to higher-level causation:

- **P1** (Causal completeness of physics): Every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause.
- **P2** (No overdetermination): Mental causes do not overdetermine physical effects — they do not cause in addition to physical causes.
- **P3** (Supervenience): Mental properties supervene on physical properties — no mental difference without physical difference.
- **C**: Mental properties do no independent causal work — the physical causes are always sufficient, and mental causes would be redundant.

The argument generalizes. If physical causes are sufficient and there is no overdetermination, then psychological, biological, and social properties are causally epiphenomenal — they "ride along" on physical causation without contributing anything. Psychology, biology, economics, and sociology would not be describing genuine causal relations but merely convenient summaries of underlying physical causation.

## The Multiple Realizability Defense

Jerry Fodor's response (in "Special Sciences," 1974, and subsequent work): mental properties — and special science properties generally — are **multiply realizable**. The same mental state can be instantiated by many different physical configurations. The psychological law "Desire D together with Belief B causes Action A" captures a real pattern that is invisible at the physical level because it abstracts over heterogeneous physical realizers.

The argument:

- **P1**: Scientific laws identify repeatable, projectible patterns across instances.
- **P2**: Higher-level kinds (mental, biological, economic) form such patterns even though they are multiply realizable by heterogeneous physical structures.
- **P3**: Identifying these patterns as causal generalizations adds genuine explanatory content not captured by physical descriptions.
- **C**: Higher-level causal laws are not reducible to physical laws and describe genuine causal regularities.

The physical-level law connecting neurochemical states to behavioral states would hold only for specific biochemical configurations — it would not generalize across the heterogeneous physical realizers of "desire" and "action." The higher-level psychological generalization does generalize. It captures real structure that the physical description misses.

## The Interventionist Answer

Woodward's interventionism provides one of the clearest defenses of higher-level causation. A property F is causally efficacious at level L iff there is an intervention I on F-type events (at level L) such that intervening to change F changes the outcome, and the relationship between F and the outcome is **invariant** under this intervention across a range of background conditions.

If intervening on mental states — through therapy, argument, medication, persuasion — systematically changes behavior in ways that pure physical intervention targeting the realizers would not predict from the physical description alone, then mental causation is real. We know mental causation is real because we know how to change behavior by changing minds. The invariance criterion captures why mental causal generalizations are scientifically valuable: they hold stably across the heterogeneous physical realizers of mental states, giving them predictive and explanatory scope that no physical generalization can match.

## Scientific Practice as Evidence

There is a strong argument from scientific practice. The special sciences — chemistry, biology, neuroscience, economics — explain phenomena at their own levels, and they do so successfully. Hippocampal damage causes memory loss: a genuine causal claim at the neural level. Natural selection causes adaptation: a genuine causal claim at the biological level. Neither of these can be replaced by a molecular or physical description without loss of explanatory content. Their predictive accuracy, their support for counterfactuals, and their guidance of successful interventions provide strong inductive evidence that higher-level causal claims are genuine.

## Kim's Revised Position and Downward Causation

Kim later acknowledged that multiple realizability creates genuine obstacles for simple reduction. He proposed **functional reduction** as a middle path: higher-level properties can be reduced to their physical realizers, but only locally — each specific realizer type reduces to a specific physical property. This gives local reduction without global type-type reduction, while preserving the causal efficacy of higher-level properties: to be in mental state M is to be in whichever physical state realizes M in this organism at this time.

Downward causation — the causal influence of higher-level systems on their lower-level components — is particularly contested. Stress (psychological) causes cortisol release (hormonal), which causes immune suppression (cellular), which changes gene expression (molecular). These are genuine causal chains running from higher to lower levels. Whether they constitute "downward causation" in a philosophically interesting sense (irreducible higher-level causation) or merely a causal chain at lower levels (the psychological event of experiencing stress is ultimately a physical event causing downstream physical events) is precisely the point of contention in the exclusion debate. The answer depends on whether higher-level causal generalizations are genuinely independent of the physical details — and this is a question that scientific practice, not just philosophical argument, will help to settle.
