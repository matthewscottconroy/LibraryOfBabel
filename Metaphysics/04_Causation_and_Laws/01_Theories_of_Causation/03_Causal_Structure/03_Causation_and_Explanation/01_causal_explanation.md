# Causal Explanation

The flagpole casts a shadow of a certain length. We can derive the shadow's length from the flagpole's height, the sun's angle, and the laws of optics. Equally, we can derive the flagpole's height from the shadow's length, the sun's angle, and the same laws. Both derivations are deductively valid from genuine scientific laws. Yet the flagpole explains the shadow; the shadow does not explain the flagpole. Explanation is asymmetric; derivation is not. This failure of the covering-law model — the Deductive-Nomological model of Hempel and Oppenheim (1948) — is what motivates the search for a genuinely causal account of explanation.

## The Covering-Law Model and Its Failure

The DN model held that to explain event E is to derive a description of E from general laws and initial conditions:

- L₁, L₂, ..., Lₙ (Laws)
- C₁, C₂, ..., Cₘ (Initial conditions)
- ————————————
- E (Explanandum)

For paradigmatic physical explanations — deriving the period of a pendulum from Newton's second law and the pendulum's geometry — this model seemed to work. The problems are the symmetry problems. The flagpole problem: since the laws of optics are symmetric, the shadow's length is derivable from the flagpole's height just as the height is derivable from the shadow. But the shadow does not explain the flagpole. The common cause problem: we can derive "this person has yellowed fingers" from "this person is a smoker" via a statistical law. But yellowed fingers do not explain the smoking; both are explained by the smoking as common cause.

Both problems show that derivation from laws is not sufficient for explanation. What is doing the real work is causal structure.

## Counterfactual Accounts of Explanation

The natural view connects causal explanation to interventionism: to explain E by citing C is to say that had C not occurred (or had we intervened to prevent C), E would not have occurred. Causal explanations thus support counterfactuals — they tell us what would have been different had the cause been absent.

This handles both symmetry problems. The flagpole counterfactually determines the shadow's length; the shadow's length does not counterfactually determine the flagpole's height (the flagpole would be the same height regardless of the shadow). So causation and explanation run from flagpole to shadow. Yellowed fingers and lung cancer are not causally related — neither counterfactually determines the other given the common cause. Only the common cause explains both.

## Salmon's Causal Mechanical Account

Wesley Salmon held that to explain an event is to specify the causal process(es) that produced it — tracing the spatiotemporal path of energy and causal influence from cause to effect. On this view, explanation is not a logical relation between statements but an ontic relation between events: explaining E by citing C describes the actual causal mechanism that produced E, giving a "causal history" rather than merely a "causal law."

For Salmon, explaining why a particular billiard ball moved involves tracing the mechanical process: the motion of the cue ball, its transmission of momentum through the collision, and the resulting motion of the object ball. The law of equal and opposite reactions is invoked not to derive the outcome but to describe the process that produced it.

## Mechanistic Explanation

Mechanistic explanations in biology and neuroscience have a different structure: they describe the organized system of entities and activities whose operation produces the phenomenon. Craver distinguishes two mechanistic modes. An **etiological mechanistic explanation** cites the causal history — the sequence of events that caused the phenomenon (why did this neuron fire? because of the excitatory inputs that depolarized it past threshold). A **constitutive mechanistic explanation** describes the components that constitute the phenomenon at a finer level (what is long-term potentiation? the mechanism of synaptic weight change involving AMPA receptor insertion, spine growth, and protein synthesis).

Both forms are genuinely explanatory. Etiological explanation answers "why did this happen?" and constitutive explanation answers "what is this at a deeper level?"

## Mathematical Explanation and the Limits of Causality

Not all scientific explanation is causal. Several cases resist analysis in causal terms. Why is there no Eulerian path through Königsberg's bridges? Because of the mathematical structure of the graph — the degrees of the nodes. This explanation invokes no causal process; it shows that the outcome was mathematically inevitable. Why does a system conserve angular momentum? Because it is spherically symmetric (Noether's theorem). This explanation by symmetry shows why something must be so, given the symmetry of the equations. Why do predator-prey populations oscillate with specific period relationships? Because of the mathematical structure of the Lotka-Volterra equations. No particular causal event explains the period; it is a mathematical consequence of the system's structure.

These cases support a pluralism about explanation: causation is one important but not exclusive explanatory mode. Mathematical, structural, and symmetry-based explanations are genuine and irreducible to causal explanation.

## Explanation and Understanding

Recent work has shifted attention from the logical structure of explanation to **understanding** — the cognitive state produced by a good explanation. Understanding comes in degrees and kinds: mechanistic understanding (knowing how it works), counterfactual understanding (knowing what would have been different), theoretical understanding (seeing how the case fits a general framework). Good causal explanations produce understanding by revealing why the effect was not merely accidental — why, given the causal structure of the world, it had to happen (or was probable) in the circumstances. This connection between causation, explanation, and understanding is what gives causal explanation its central place in scientific reasoning.
