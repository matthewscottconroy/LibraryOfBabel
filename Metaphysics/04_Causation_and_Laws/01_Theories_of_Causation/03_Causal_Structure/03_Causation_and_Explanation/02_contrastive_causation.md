# Contrastive Causation

Why did the plant die? The question seems clear enough. But consider three accurate answers: "because I didn't water it" (contrast: watering versus not watering); "because I watered it at all — the roots rotted" (contrast: some water versus none); "because this plant died rather than that one — it was in direct sunlight" (contrast between plants). Each answer is correct relative to a different contrast class. Each highlights a different factor because a different alternative is being held against the actual outcome. Causal attribution is, in this way, implicitly contrastive — we always select causes against a background of alternatives, even when we do not state this explicitly.

Jonathan Schaffer has developed this observation into a systematic theory: causal claims are inherently four-place rather than two-place.

## Schaffer's Contrastive Account

On the standard view, "C causes E" is a relation between two things. Schaffer proposes replacing it with:

- **Contrastive causation**: C rather than C* causes E rather than E*. (Formally: CAUSES(c, c*, e, e*))

The cause-contrast C* is what we are comparing the cause to — what else might have happened instead of C. The effect-contrast E* is the alternative outcome we are interested in distinguishing. The contrastive claim says: it is the fact that c rather than c* occurred that accounts for e rather than e*.

Consider: asked why Jones pressed the accelerator, different answers are correct under different contrasts. CAUSES(pressing accelerator, pressing brake, speeding up, slowing down): true — pressing the accelerator rather than the brake caused the car to speed up rather than slow down. CAUSES(pressing accelerator, pressing gradually, lurching forward, moving smoothly): also true — pressing hard rather than gently caused lurching rather than smooth acceleration. Both contrastive claims are true; they answer different questions about the same event.

## Resolving Apparent Disagreements

The contrastive framework resolves apparent disputes about causation that arise from implicit contrast variation.

Why is Socrates dead? "Because he drank the hemlock" (contrast: drinking versus not drinking). "Because he was condemned by the Athenians" (contrast: condemnation versus acquittal). "Because he refused to leave Athens" (contrast: staying versus fleeing). These look like competing answers, but each is correct relative to its contrast class. The "real" answer depends on what question is being asked — what alternative is being contrasted with the actual situation.

Schaffer argues this shows that causal questions are always implicitly contrastive, and apparent disagreements dissolve once contrasts are made explicit.

## The Semantics of Contrastive Causal Claims

**CAUSES(c, c*, e, e*)** is true iff: in the actual world c occurred and e occurred; and in the nearest world where c* occurred instead of c, e* occurred instead of e. This is a counterfactual analysis of contrastive causation: the cause-contrast explains the effect-contrast by showing what would have happened if the cause-contrast had obtained instead.

This connects naturally to van Fraassen's contrastivism about explanation: "why?" questions always have the form "why P rather than some alternative Q?", and a good explanation identifies the cause of P rather than Q. Explanatory relevance is therefore contrastive: a factor is explanatorily relevant only if it makes a difference to which contrast obtains.

## Objections and Replies

**The mind-dependence objection**: Contrastivism makes causation mind-dependent — what the cause is depends on what alternatives the inquirer has in mind. This undermines objectivity. Schaffer distinguishes what makes something a cause from which causal claim we are making. The objective causal structure of the world determines which contrastive causal claims are true; context determines which claim we are making. Causation is objective; causal attribution is context-sensitive. The context-sensitivity of attribution does not infect the objectivity of the underlying facts.

**The proliferation objection**: If all causes are contrastive, there are vastly many true causal claims — one for each choice of contrasts. This seems to implausibly multiply truths. Schaffer's reply: this is a feature, not a bug. The world has a rich causal structure; contrastive causation makes explicit the different ways in which different factors contribute to different aspects of the outcome. The proliferation of truths reflects the real complexity of causal structure.

**The transitivity objection**: Ordinary causation is transitive — if A causes B and B causes C, then A causes C. But contrastive causation is not obviously transitive: CAUSES(a, a*, b, b*) and CAUSES(b, b*, c, c*) do not guarantee CAUSES(a, a*, c, c*) because the effect-contrast b* in the first claim may not match the cause-contrast b of the second. Schaffer's reply: transitivity holds when contrasts are appropriately chained — when the effect-contrast of one link is the cause-contrast of the next. This is a refinement, not a refutation.

Contrastivism about causation connects naturally to broader contrastivism in epistemology and semantics, where it has been independently motivated. The cumulative case for treating causal claims as implicitly contrastive — from the semantics of "because"-clauses, from the sensitivity of causal attribution to context, and from the resolution of apparent causal disputes — is among the more compelling in the recent literature on the structure of causation.
