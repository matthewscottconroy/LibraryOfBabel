# Causation in the Special Sciences

The law of demand: if the price of a good rises, quantity demanded falls, other things equal. Organisms evolve to maximize fitness. Hippocampal damage causes memory loss. These are causal claims from economics, biology, and neuroscience — each stated at a level of description that cannot be reduced to physics without loss of what makes the claim informative. Whether such higher-level causal claims are genuine, what grounds them, and how they relate to physical causation is one of the most pressing questions at the intersection of philosophy of science, metaphysics, and philosophy of mind.

## Fodor's Irreducibility Argument

Jerry Fodor's "Special Sciences" (1974) launched the modern debate. His argument:

- **P1**: Special science laws connect higher-level kinds (economic, psychological, biological) that are not reducible to physical kinds.
- **P2**: The higher-level kinds are **multiply realizable**: "inflation" can be physically implemented in vastly different ways — metallic currencies, paper notes, digital transactions, barter systems.
- **P3**: Any physical law that captured the economic generalization would have to list all the heterogeneous physical realizers, yielding an unwieldy disjunctive "law" rather than the clean economic generalization.
- **C**: Special science laws are irreducible because they capture genuine patterns at higher levels that no physical law can capture without loss.

These higher-level patterns are **projectible**: they support inductive inference from known instances to new ones, even though their physical realizers are heterogeneous. This projectibility is evidence that the special science kinds are genuine natural kinds, appropriate for causal explanation.

## Woodward's Invariance-Based Defense

James Woodward's interventionist account gives a different reason for the autonomy of special science causation. A causal generalization at any level is genuine iff the relationship it describes is **invariant** under interventions. The claim "aspirin causes headache relief" is invariant across different physical implementations of the headache mechanism (in different people, different ages, different biochemical configurations), and invariant under a range of background conditions. It is not invariant under all interventions — it fails in some individual cases and in cases of aspirin resistance. But the invariance is sufficient for genuine causal knowledge: the relationship holds robustly enough to ground reliable predictions and interventions.

The physical-level description does not exhibit this invariance without massive qualification. The physical generalization connecting neurochemical states to outcome states holds only for specific biochemical configurations — it does not generalize across the heterogeneous physical realizers of "headache" and "relief." The higher-level generalization captures something the physical description misses.

## The Causal Exclusion Challenge Revisited

Kim's exclusion argument runs: if physical causes are sufficient for physical effects (causal completeness of physics), and mental causes are distinct from physical causes (non-identity), then mental causes are either epiphenomenal or create overdetermination. For special sciences generally: if physical causes always suffice, then biological, psychological, and economic causes are superfluous.

The overdetermination response accepts that every physical effect caused by a higher-level cause is overdetermined. Kim resists this because ordinary overdetermination (two fires, two bullets) is a contingent coincidence; if every mental event overdetermined its physical effects, this would be a systematic, non-accidental coincidence that requires explanation.

Non-reductive functionalism (Fodor) holds that higher-level properties are causally efficacious in their own right because they figure in projectible, invariant causal generalizations. The physical realizers are also causally efficacious, but at a different level of description. There is no exclusion because the two causal claims operate at different levels of the same causal structure.

Yablo's counterfactual response treats the higher-level cause and its physical realizer as standing in the determinable-determinate relation: the physical property is a highly specific realization of the more general psychological kind. The effect might have occurred even if the cause had been realized differently physically (because any physical realizer of the psychological kind would have caused the same effect). The psychological causal claim is robust in a way the physical causal claim is not.

## Davidson's Anomalous Monism

Davidson's position ("Mental Events," 1970) threads between reduction and dualism. Token identity: every mental event is identical to some physical event. Anomalism: there are no strict laws connecting mental descriptions to physical descriptions — the mental is "anomalous." Token causation: mental events cause physical events by virtue of being identical to physical events that cause physical events.

The problem: if mental properties play no role in strict laws, what causal work do they do beyond being instantiated by causally relevant physical events? The mental properties seem to be along for the ride — a consequence Davidson cannot comfortably accept given his commitment to the reality of mental causation. This is the anomalous monism problem: mental causation at the token level without genuine causal relevance of mental types.

## Economic Laws as a Case Study

Economic laws are instructive. The law of demand is quantitative, systematic, and clearly higher-level. It is multiply realizable: applicable to gold markets, fish markets, and digital goods markets. It is ceteris paribus: holds only in the absence of interfering factors (regime change, behavioral anomalies). It supports interventions: price controls, subsidies, and taxes work because economic causal laws hold.

The interventionist criterion confirms genuineness: intervening on price causes systematic changes in quantity demanded, in ways predictable from the economic generalization but not easily predictable from the physical description of the transactions. This is just Woodward's invariance criterion applied at the economic level.

Cartwright argues more radically that there are no universal laws even in physics — only "nomological machines" (highly organized, shielded experimental setups that produce reliable regularities). Laws are relative to these setups, not universal truths about nature. On this view, special science laws are no less "laws" than physical laws; both are context-relative regularities of limited domain. Whether this radical pluralism is ultimately defensible, or whether it concedes too much, is an ongoing debate — but it captures something important: the context-sensitivity of causal generalizations is a feature of all science, not a deficiency unique to the special sciences.
