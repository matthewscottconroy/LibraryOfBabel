# Review Questions — Theories of Causation

1. Hume offers two definitions of causation — one in terms of constant conjunction, the other in terms of the mind's inferential habit — and concedes they are not obviously the same definition. What is at stake in that gap? Can both definitions be true simultaneously, or does the second smuggle in exactly the psychological necessity that the first was meant to expunge?

2. Mackie's INUS analysis was designed to handle the complexity of real causal situations — multiple sufficient conditions, partial causes, overdetermination. To what extent does it genuinely improve on the simple regularity account, and to what extent does it still fail to distinguish causal laws from accidental regularities? What residual problem does it leave unresolved?

3. Lewis's counterfactual theory grounds causal asymmetry in the asymmetry of overdetermination: later states leave many independent traces of earlier ones, but not vice versa. Evaluate this maneuver. Is it a genuine explanation of why causes precede effects, or does it covertly presuppose a prior temporal direction in the way "traces" and "records" are characterized?

4. The preemption cases — early preemption, late preemption — represent a sustained stress test on Lewis's counterfactual analysis. Trace how each successive refinement (causal chains, influence) responds to a new type of counterexample, and assess whether the progressive complexity of the theory is a sign that the counterfactual approach is approaching the truth or approaching its limits.

5. Woodward's interventionism is explicitly non-reductive: intervention is itself a causal notion. Does this make his account circular, or is there a principled distinction between the kind of illumination he claims to offer — showing the structure of causal reasoning — and the kind of reduction Lewis and the regularity theorists sought? What would be lost if we settled for Woodward's weaker goal?

6. The mechanism theories of Machamer, Darden, and Craver analyze causal explanation as the identification of organized entities and activities. Compare this with the causal powers approach: both reject the regularity account, but they locate the explanatory work differently (in mechanisms versus in intrinsic dispositional properties). Are these competing accounts or potentially complementary?

7. The distinction between token and type causation is logically clean but generates pressure at the intersection of science and law. Token causation without type causation is conceptually possible on some views — but Davidson's anomalous monism illustrates how unstable this combination becomes. Why does the type/token gap create a specific crisis for non-reductive accounts of mental causation in particular?

8. Causal explanation faces cases — the flagpole and its shadow, Noether's theorem and conservation laws, mathematical graph theory and bridge topology — where the best explanations appear non-causal. Do these cases show that causal explanation is one mode among many, or can they be reinterpreted within a suitably broad causal framework?
