# Humean Views on Laws of Nature

A law of nature is not merely a generalization that happens to be true. "All ravens are black" might be true (if it is true at all) as a matter of contingent fact; but "All electrons have charge −e" seems to be true as a matter of law — it could not be otherwise, it supports counterfactuals ("if this particle were an electron, it would have charge −e"), and it connects to a systematic body of scientific theory in ways that accidental generalizations do not. The question is what makes the difference.

The Humean faces this question as a direct challenge. On the Humean picture, the world is a mosaic of particular facts — events and their properties, distributed across spacetime — with no intrinsic necessary connections between distinct events. Everything is what it is, and nothing is intrinsically connected to anything else. In this world, what could a law of nature be, other than an unusually general and systematic regularity?

Lewis's Best System Analysis (BSA) is the most developed Humean answer. The laws of nature are the axioms of the deductive system that best systematizes all the particular facts of the world — the system that achieves the best balance of simplicity (few axioms) and strength (many consequences). On this view, laws are not discovered by examining individual causal relations; they emerge from a global perspective on the entire history of the world. A regularity is a law if it appears as an axiom or theorem of the best system.

The BSA faces the objection that the balance between simplicity and strength is evaluated relative to our current scientific concepts and standards of elegance — making laws seem partly mind-dependent in a way that seems in tension with their objective, scientific character. Lewis addressed this concern by insisting that the relevant standards of simplicity and strength are perfectly natural and not subjective; but the worry persists about whether the BSA genuinely captures the necessity and counterfactual-supporting character of laws.

This section examines Humean views on laws, with particular attention to Lewis's BSA and the objections it faces.
