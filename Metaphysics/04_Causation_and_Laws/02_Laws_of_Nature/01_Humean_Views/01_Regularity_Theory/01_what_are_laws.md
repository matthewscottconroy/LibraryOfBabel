# What Are Laws of Nature?

Here is a natural piece of reasoning. All observed copper conducts electricity. We have tested this many times, in many contexts, with many samples. We infer that all copper conducts electricity — including copper we have not yet observed, copper on the other side of the universe, and copper that would exist if we were to create it. What licenses this inference from the observed to the unobserved? The answer we want to give is: a law of nature. Laws are supposed to support just this kind of projection, to explain why the regularity holds everywhere and always, and to ground the counterfactual that if this were copper, it would conduct.

But what are laws? Not merely true universal generalizations — since some of those are accidental ("all the coins in my pocket are silver") and are not supposed to support such inferences. Something more. What more, and why — this is the central question.

## Characteristic Features of Laws

Several features are characteristic of laws, though each is contested. **Generality**: laws apply to all instances of the relevant kind — not just observed instances but all actual and possible ones. **Projectibility**: laws support induction and counterfactuals. "If this were copper, it would conduct electricity" is licensed by the law; "if this coin were in my pocket, it would be silver" is not licensed by the accidental generalization. **Nomic necessity**: laws hold with a kind of necessity stronger than mere coincidence — not just that they happen to hold but that they must hold, in some sense. **Explanatory role**: citing a law explains why an event occurred, in a way that citing an accidental regularity does not. **Universality**: laws hold across all of spacetime, not just in particular localities.

The philosophical question is what grounds these features. What makes "all copper conducts electricity" a law while "all coins in my pocket are copper" is merely accidental?

The counterfactual test captures part of the difference. Laws support counterfactuals; accidents do not. The projectibility test: laws license induction to unobserved instances; accidents do not. The explanatory test: laws explain; accidents merely describe.

## Competing Accounts

The natural first response is the **naive regularity theory**: laws are simply true universal generalizations. This fails because it cannot distinguish laws from accidents — they are formally identical universal quantifications over all actual objects.

This drives us toward the **Best System Analysis** (Mill-Ramsey-Lewis): laws are regularities that appear in the best systematization of all facts — the deductive system with the optimal balance of simplicity and informational strength. By requiring that a generalization earn its place in the best system, the BSA distinguishes laws from accidents: the accidental generalization "all coins in my pocket are silver" does not appear in the best systematization of the world, while "all copper conducts" does.

But the BSA has its own difficulties — chiefly, that "best" appears to depend on human cognitive values, threatening to make laws mind-dependent. This drives further toward **necessitation accounts** (Armstrong-Dretske-Tooley): laws involve a second-order necessitation relation N between universals, N(F, G), giving them modal force beyond bare regularities. Or toward **dispositional essentialist accounts** (Bird, Ellis): laws follow from the essential natures of natural properties — what it is to be charged, to have mass, to be fragile. Or toward **primitivism** (Maudlin): laws are fundamental primitives, irreducibly governing rather than merely describing.

## Laws and Scientific Practice

From a philosophy of science perspective, laws serve several indispensable roles. They explain particular events (covering-law model, however inadequate). They license predictions: given the state of the system at t, the laws specify what state it will be at t+1. They unify diverse phenomena: Newton's laws unify terrestrial and celestial mechanics; Maxwell's equations unify electricity, magnetism, and optics. And they are idealized — scientific laws often apply exactly only under ideal conditions, which raises the question of whether the laws describe the real world or only idealized models.

Cartwright argues that no law of physics describes what actually happens in the world — only models do. Whether this is a feature of laws or a reason to revise our concept of a law is debated; but the observation that laws require idealization is a genuine challenge for any account that identifies laws with true universal generalizations about actual events.

## The Epistemology of Laws

How do we know what the laws are? Inductivism: we observe regularities and extrapolate. This faces Hume's problem — no finite set of observations logically entails a universal generalization. Hypothetico-deductivism: we conjecture laws, derive predictions, and test them against experience. Inference to the best explanation: the best explanation of observed regularities is that there is a law underlying them. And all scientific methodology gives weight to simpler hypotheses — which connects directly to the BSA, if laws are what appear in the simplest adequate systematization.

The epistemology and metaphysics are not independent. What we can know about laws depends in part on what laws are. Non-Humean accounts that give laws genuine modal force offer a basis for confident inductive inference that the Humean account cannot provide — but they require postulating entities (necessitation relations, essential natures, primitive governance) that go beyond what observation directly reveals.
