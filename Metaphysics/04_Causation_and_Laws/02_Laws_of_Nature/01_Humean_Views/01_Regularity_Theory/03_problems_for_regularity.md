# Problems for Regularity Theories of Laws

Here is a pair of true universal generalizations: all gold spheres are less than one mile in diameter; all uranium spheres are less than one mile in diameter. Both are universally true, spatiotemporally unrestricted, and concern natural kinds. If laws of nature are simply true universal generalizations, both should be laws. But only the second is. A sphere of uranium cannot exceed critical mass without spontaneously fissioning — the nuclear physics makes it impossible. No comparable physical principle rules out a gold sphere a mile across; there simply happens to be no such sphere. This asymmetry is the heart of the problem for regularity theories, and every other difficulty maps onto it: regularity theories capture what is the case, but laws are about what must be the case, and the must cannot be read off from the pattern of actual events.

## The Central Objections

The accidental regularity problem has a precise structure. Laws support counterfactuals; accidents do not. "All copper conducts electricity" licenses "if this rod were copper, it would conduct." "All coins in my pocket are silver" does not license "if this coin were in my pocket, it would be silver." For the naive regularity theory, both are true universal generalizations with no further modal character. Nothing in the theory explains the difference. Chisholm pressed this objection decisively: the very distinction we need — between generalizations that support counterfactuals and those that do not — is precisely what a bare regularity theory cannot draw. Any theory that succeeds must explain this, and that explanation will require modal resources beyond the regularities themselves.

The Best System Analysis attempts to handle the gold/uranium asymmetry by appealing to theoretical systematization. The uranium generalization earns its place in the best deductive system because nuclear physics explains why no uranium sphere can exceed critical mass; the gold sphere generalization does not earn its place because no comparable theoretical principle explains it. The law-like status is conferred by systematic role, not by the generalization itself. This is a real advance, but it postpones rather than dissolves the problem: what makes systematic role law-conferring? The BSA must invoke notions of simplicity and unification that themselves have modal or normative character.

Uninstantiated laws press the regularity account from a different direction. A law might hold in a world where the relevant entities never actually exist. The Lorentz force law governs any charged particle — including particles with charge-to-mass ratios that no actual particle possesses. The generalization "if a particle with ratio r existed, it would follow a circular path in a magnetic field with radius mv/qB" is law-like even with no actual instances. A regularity theory that identifies laws with regularities over actual events cannot accommodate this. The Humean faces a dilemma: either allow that laws can be uninstantiated (in which case they are not regularities over actual events) or deny that there are uninstantiated laws (in which case the account is scientifically false). Neither option is comfortable.

The non-Humean can handle this cleanly. Armstrong's necessitation relation N(F, G) holds between universals, not between instances; universals can stand in this relation whether or not they happen to be instantiated. Tooley extended the point by arguing for transcendent universals that exist without any instances whatever. Whether one accepts that ontology, the contrast with regularity theories is instructive: what the necessitation account gets for free, the regularity account cannot purchase without revising its central commitments.

## Ceteris Paribus Laws

Most laws in the special sciences — and arguably many in physics — hold other things being equal. Organisms maximize fitness, ceteris paribus. Prices fall when supply increases, ceteris paribus. Unsupported bodies fall with acceleration g, ceteris paribus, absent air resistance, electromagnetic forces, and other interfering factors. These are genuine laws, and any adequate account must accommodate them. But a pure regularity theory must either deny that ceteris paribus claims are genuine laws — a verdict that is implausibly restrictive given their central role in biological, social, and physical explanation — or interpret the ceteris paribus clause non-vacuously. The second option is the worse one for the regularity theorist: specifying what counts as an "interfering condition" requires exactly the causal and dispositional concepts the Humean wants to analyze away, not presuppose.

Woodward's interventionist response reframes the question. Ceteris paribus laws describe invariant relationships — relationships that hold under a range of interventions even if not universally. The law that prices fall with increased supply holds under a range of background conditions; its status as a law turns on this invariance, not on strict universality. This is a genuine move, but it is no longer a regularity account in the traditional sense: invariance under interventions is a modal notion, not a statement about actual regularities. Woodward's account belongs to a different theoretical tradition.

Cartwright's response is more radical: laws do not describe regularities at all. They describe what happens in idealized nomological machines — highly controlled experimental setups that are deliberately constructed to realize stable regularities. Outside these setups, there are no regularities; there are only capacities. "All unsupported bodies fall with acceleration g" describes what happens when all interfering factors are eliminated, not what actually happens in the world's messiness. Laws describe the capacities of things, not what they always do. This dissolves the problem of ceteris paribus by denying that universal regularities are what laws are about in the first place.

## Temporal Direction

Many laws describe processes with a preferred temporal direction. Heat flows from hot to cold; entropy increases; evolution proceeds by selection on heritable variation. Regularities, strictly speaking, are bidirectional: if all F's are G's, all G's are preceded by F's. The asymmetry of thermodynamic laws — the fact that heat flows from hot to cold and not vice versa — requires more than a bare regularity. The Humean can point to the thermodynamic arrow of time as the ground: the second law of thermodynamics is itself a regularity that encodes a temporal asymmetry, grounded in the initial conditions of the universe. But this relocates the problem rather than solving it: why does the universe have the initial conditions that generate the thermodynamic arrow? That question cannot be answered by appeal to any regularity, since it concerns the very conditions that generate the regularities we find.

## The Problem of Local Evidence and Global Inference

The regularity theory faces a structural challenge from induction. We have observed copper conducting electricity in a tiny fraction of all actual copper. What licenses the inference to all copper everywhere, including copper in distant galaxies and in the deep past? The necessitation theorist has a direct answer: the law N(Copper, Conductor) holds between universals; the universals are global in their reach. The Humean has only the mosaic of local particular facts, and projecting from that mosaic to a global generalization requires a principle of uniformity that the regularity theory cannot provide from within its own resources. This is not a new problem — it is Hume's own problem of induction — but the regularity theory of laws, rather than solving it, presupposes it solved. To say that laws are regularities is to say that we can infer from observed regularities to unobserved cases; but this inference itself requires that laws govern the unobserved cases, not merely that they describe the observed ones.

## Assessment

These problems are severe enough that most contemporary philosophers of science accept that laws have a modal character that cannot be fully captured by regularities alone. The debate has shifted from whether Humeanism can be maintained at all to how non-Humean the final account must be: whether the modality must be primitive (Maudlin), derived from universals (Armstrong), or expressed through essential dispositional properties (Bird). What is no longer seriously defended is the naive regularity theory in its original form. The theory's parsimony — its genuine philosophical virtue — becomes a liability when every task we assign to laws (grounding counterfactuals, explaining regularities, accommodating uninstantiated cases, handling ceteris paribus clauses, orienting temporal direction) requires modal resources that bare regularities simply cannot provide.
