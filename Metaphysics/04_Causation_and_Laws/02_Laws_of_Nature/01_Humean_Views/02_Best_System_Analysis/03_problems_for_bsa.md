# Problems for the Best System Analysis

The Best System Analysis is the most developed Humean account of laws of nature, and its virtues are real. But the objections to it are also real, and they reveal that the account's apparent parsimony is partly illusory. The problems cluster around two themes: the dependence of "best" on evaluative standards that look suspiciously mind-dependent, and the question of whether the vocabulary in which the best system is stated can be specified without circularity or non-Humean commitments.

## The Language Dependence Problem

The BSA selects the best system formulated in some vocabulary. But which vocabulary? Without restrictions, the account becomes trivial. We could introduce a gerrymandered predicate F defined to hold of exactly the things that participate in whatever actual regularities there are, and state the single axiom "everything is F." This system is maximally simple — one axiom — and maximally strong — it covers all actual facts. But it expresses no law, because F does not track any genuine feature of reality.

Lewis's solution is to require that the best system be formulated in terms of "perfectly natural" properties — those that carve nature at its joints. Natural predicates correspond to genuine joints in nature; gerrymandered predicates do not. Only systems stated in natural vocabulary are eligible for being the best. This rules out the F-axiom and the grue-based generalizations that would otherwise swamp the system.

The solution works, but it carries a significant cost. It requires a prior ontology of natural properties whose identity is determined independently of laws. If natural properties are defined by their causal or nomological roles, and causal roles are partly determined by the laws, then there is a circularity: laws presuppose natural properties, which presuppose causal roles, which presuppose laws. Lewis held that natural properties are primitive — they are those that figure in the fundamental ontology of the world, independently of any functional or nomological characterization. But this is a substantial non-Humean commitment. Armstrong pressed the point: the BSA is not a fully reductive account of laws; it smuggles in natural-property realism through the back door, and those natural properties are doing significant metaphysical work that the bare Humean mosaic cannot provide.

## The Mind-Dependence Worry

"Best" is defined in terms of human cognitive values — simplicity, strength, and fit. This seems to make laws mind-dependent: laws are those regularities that best suit human cognitive interests. But laws are supposed to be objective features of nature, independent of what we happen to find simple or elegant.

The argument runs:

- **P1**: What counts as the "best" system depends on what we regard as simple and strong.
- **P2**: What we regard as simple and strong depends on human cognitive architecture, language, and interests.
- **P3**: Laws are the regularities in the best system.
- **C**: Laws are mind-dependent — dependent on human cognitive standards.

Armstrong pressed this objection: the BSA conflates laws with our best theories. We want the laws to be objective features of the world that our theories aim to capture; the BSA makes laws constitutively dependent on what our theories look like. If different rational agents with complete information found different deductive systems equally compelling, which one contains the genuine laws?

Lewis's response was to idealize: the best system is determined by the perfectly rational cognitive agent with complete information, not by human beings with limited knowledge and biases. Laws are what would appear in the ideal system, not in any actual human theorization. This response is natural, but it postpones rather than resolves the problem. What counts as a "perfectly rational" standard of simplicity? This is itself contested, and different rational agents might weight the trade-off between simplicity and strength differently. The appeal to perfect rationality pushes the problem back rather than eliminating it.

Loewer's response is to accept a degree of conventionality. Different conventions yield different best systems and thus different laws, but these conventions are not arbitrary — they are grounded in the physical structure of the world and the practical needs of reasoning agents embedded in that world. The conventionality does not undermine the objectivity of the facts that ground the choice of convention. Whether this is ultimately satisfying depends on how much mind-dependence one is prepared to accept in the foundations of a theory of laws.

## Small Worlds and Ties

In worlds with very few events, there may be no non-trivial best system. If a world contains only a single electron, there are no interesting generalizations to systematize, and the account gives no clear verdict about what the laws are. Similarly, if multiple systems achieve the same balance of simplicity and strength — if there are ties — the BSA gives no unique verdict. Competing systems might include incompatible generalizations, both equally supported by the mosaic. Lewis's account needs a tie-breaking principle, and it is not obvious what that principle should be or whether it can be stated without appeal to further evaluative standards.

## Quantum Mechanics and Holism

Quantum mechanics presents specific challenges. Quantum states are not local: an entangled two-particle state cannot be decomposed into a product of individual particle states. The quantum state is holistic in a way that violates Lewis's foundational assumption that the Humean mosaic consists of local, intrinsic, qualitative properties distributed across spacetime. If the fundamental ontology of the actual world includes irreducibly non-local states, then the Humean supervenience on which the BSA rests is empirically false.

Lewis acknowledged this with characteristic candor: "I am not ready to take the draconian measure of abandoning Humean supervenience. Its a posteriori falsity, if that is what it is, is a strike against it but not a fatal one." He treated Humean supervenience as a research program to be vindicated if possible, not as an established fact. Recent Humeans — Esfeld, Ney, Albert — have explored whether quantum mechanics can be recast in locally supervenient form, for example by treating the wavefunction on configuration space as the fundamental ontology, with local facts supervening on it. Whether this strategy succeeds is an ongoing debate in the philosophy of physics.

The BSA's overall situation is this: it remains the most sophisticated and defensible Humean account of laws, but its defensibility is closely tied to the defensibility of Humean supervenience. If the actual world is non-Humean at the quantum level, the BSA cannot be a correct account of the actual world's laws. And if the account requires primitive natural properties to avoid trivialization, it is not as parsimonious as it initially appears. These are not knockdown objections, but they are genuine constraints on what the BSA can deliver.
