# Anti-Humeanism About Laws

The Humean picture is tidy and economical: there are particular events arranged in spacetime, and laws of nature are the best summaries of the regularities in those arrangements. But "summary" is the revealing word. A summary describes what happens; it does not explain why it happens. And there is a persistent intuition that laws of nature do not merely describe the world's regularities but govern them — that the gravitational law is not just a fact about how masses have been distributed across spacetime but what constrains masses to attract one another in the future, in the past, and in counterfactual scenarios. This is the governing conception of laws, and it drives every major anti-Humean account.

## The Governing Intuition and Its Bite

The explanatory challenge to Humeanism can be put simply. Saying "all copper conducts because all copper conducts" is not an explanation; it is a tautology. If laws merely describe regularities, they cannot explain them without circularity. But if laws govern — if there is a necessary connection between being copper and conducting electricity — then the regularity is explained: copper conducts because it is subject to a law that makes it conduct. The governing conception gives laws genuine explanatory force that the Humean description cannot match.

The natural first response is the **Armstrong-Dretske-Tooley (ADT) account**: laws are second-order states of affairs in which the universal F stands in a necessitation relation N to the universal G, making it the case that all F's are G's. Notation: N(F, G). The relation N is primitive and irreducible — it is not a regularity, not a Best System theorem, not a disposition. N(F, G) entails (∀x)(Fx → Gx), so the necessitation of universals entails the regularity of their instances. And crucially, N(F, G) holds contingently: there are possible worlds where F and G do not stand in this relation, so the law could have been otherwise. This preserves the scientific finding that laws are discovered empirically rather than known a priori.

Armstrong's motivation is that regularities cannot explain themselves. The pattern of copper samples conducting electricity, however extensive, does not explain why the pattern holds — it just is the pattern. The necessitation relation N is what explains why F-instances are always G-instances: they are necessarily connected. The regularity follows from the higher-order fact, and the higher-order fact is not itself just a regularity.

But the ADT account faces the inference problem: N(F, G) is supposed to entail (∀x)(Fx → Gx), but why? If N is a primitive relation among universals, what guarantees that its holding implies anything about the particular instances of those universals? The entailment cannot be brute — that would just relocate the explanatory gap — but it cannot be derived from the nature of N without circularity. This is not a decisive refutation, but it is a genuine difficulty that Armstrong struggled to resolve.

## Dispositional Essentialism

Brian Ellis's scientific essentialism takes a different route. Properties have essential natures that determine their causal roles, and laws express these essential natures. The law that like charges repel is not a contingent necessitation between universals but a consequence of what it is to be charged. In all worlds where charge exists with its actual essential nature, like charges repel. The necessity is grounded in essences, not in a primitive second-order relation.

The contrast with Armstrong is telling. For Armstrong, the necessitation relation N holds contingently — there are worlds with the same universals F and G but without the law N(F, G). For Ellis, if property P has its actual essential nature, the laws governing P hold necessarily. What we might imagine when we "imagine gravity going as the inverse cube" is not the same property of mass behaving differently — it is a different property, or a variant, with a different essential nature. The laws of nature, on this view, are metaphysically necessary, not contingent. This conflicts with the intuition that they are empirically discovered and could have been otherwise, but Ellis accepts this consequence and argues that what we take to be imaginable is not always genuinely metaphysically possible.

## Maudlin's Primitivism

Tim Maudlin's *The Metaphysics Within Physics* (2007) defends the most direct position: laws of nature are fundamental primitives that cannot be analyzed in any further terms. They are not patterns in the mosaic, not relations between universals, not expressions of dispositional essences — they just are laws, irreducibly. What makes the mosaic have the character it has is the laws; the direction of explanation runs from laws to mosaic, not from mosaic to laws.

Maudlin frames this through the DIME framework, distinguishing the Distributions of matter, Initial conditions, Metric structure, and Evolution laws — the dynamical laws of development. These dynamical laws are primitive features of the world that are not derived from or reducible to the distribution of matter. They govern how the distribution evolves.

The virtue of primitivism is that it avoids the difficulties that plague more ambitious accounts. There is no inference problem, no regress through essences, no dependence on human cognitive values. Laws are just there, as fundamental features of reality. The cost is ontological richness: we must accept irreducible laws as primitives over and above the pattern of events, which is generous by Ockham's lights. But Maudlin's response is that ontological generosity is exactly what science requires — the world contains governing laws, and any account that tries to analyze them away will leave out what is scientifically most important.

## Lange's Stability Account

Marc Lange, in *Laws and Lawmakers* (2009), develops a distinctive approach based on counterfactual stability rather than necessitation or essence. A set of truths Γ is a set of laws iff Γ is stable: each member of Γ would have remained true under any counterfactual supposition consistent with Γ itself. Laws are truths that would hold even under a very wide range of counterfactual suppositions; accidents would not. "All copper conducts" remains true under counterfactual suppositions about unusual conditions, rare samples, and temporal reversal — because it is a law. "All my coins are silver" would fail under the supposition that I added a gold coin — because it is accidental.

This account is anti-Humean in refusing to reduce laws to patterns in the mosaic. But it does not posit necessitation relations or essential natures. Instead, it characterizes laws by their role in the space of counterfactual possibilities — their stability across the range of nomically possible worlds. Laws form a self-sustaining set of truths whose members support one another's stability.

## Assessment

The debate between Humean and anti-Humean accounts of laws comes down to a question about explanation and ontological economy. Anti-Humean accounts explain why regularities hold — because of N(F, G), essential natures, governing laws, or stable preservation across counterfactuals. Humean accounts describe regularities without explaining them, at the price of accepting that the regularities are brute facts. If explanation is a theoretical virtue that carries ontological weight — if positing more is justified when it explains better — then anti-Humean accounts have an advantage that parsimony alone cannot defeat. Whether that advantage is real depends on one's views about what genuine explanation requires, and this is one of the most genuinely contested questions in contemporary metaphysics.
