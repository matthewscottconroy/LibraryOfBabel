# The Best System Analysis

The Best System Analysis (BSA) of laws of nature is the most sophisticated contemporary Humean account. First articulated by Frank Ramsey and then developed by David Lewis, the BSA holds that the laws of nature are the regularities that appear in the best axiomatic system for describing all the particular facts of the universe.

The "best" system is one that achieves the optimal trade-off between two virtues: simplicity and strength. Simplicity is a measure of how few axioms are required and how elegant those axioms are. Strength is a measure of how much the system tells us — how many particular facts it entails. These virtues pull in opposite directions: a maximally simple system (say, one with no axioms) has no strength, and a maximally strong system (one that lists every particular fact) has no simplicity. The best system strikes the optimal balance.

Lewis added a third virtue, fit, when extending the framework to probabilistic laws. A probabilistic law ascribes objective chances to events. The best system for a probabilistic world is one that is simplest, strongest, and whose chances fit the actual frequencies as closely as possible.

The BSA captures the intuition that laws are theoretical summaries of the world's patterns, selected for their epistemic virtues. It explains why scientists care about simplicity: simple laws are better summaries, not because nature is simple, but because the simplest system is the most informative one relative to its complexity. It also explains the connection between laws and counterfactuals: if a regularity is a law, it supports counterfactuals because it is part of the best system, and the best system characterizes how things would have been in nearby possible worlds (those most similar to actuality).

A significant objection concerns the reference class problem: the BSA identifies laws relative to a perfectly natural physical language, but what counts as "natural" needs to be specified. Lewis relied on natural properties — those that "carve nature at its joints" — to define the language. But this threatens to make natural properties primitive and unexplained, or to explain them in terms of laws, creating circularity.

Jonathan Cohen and Craig Callender have developed a "New Best System" approach that allows different sciences to have their own best systems, relativized to the goals and practices of each science. This pluralistic version of the BSA trades the ambition of a single, unified system for a more permissive account of the diversity of laws across sciences.
