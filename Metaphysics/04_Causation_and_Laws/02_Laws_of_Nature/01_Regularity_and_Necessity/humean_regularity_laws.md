# Humean Regularity Accounts of Laws

The Humean view of laws of nature holds that laws are regularities — they describe patterns in the world without adding anything ontologically extra. There are no necessities "out there" in nature, no governing principles that force events to follow certain patterns. The world is a mosaic of local, intrinsic facts, and laws are generalizations over this mosaic.

The most influential contemporary Humean account is the Best System Analysis (BSA), developed by Frank Ramsey, refined by David Lewis, and defended by Barry Loewer and others. On this view, the laws of a world are the theorems of the best deductive system for that world: the system that achieves the optimal balance of simplicity and strength (informational content). A regularity counts as a law if and only if it appears in this best system.

The BSA is designed to draw the distinction between genuine laws and accidental regularities that the simple regularity account cannot. "All copper conducts electricity" earns a place in the best system because including it adds significant information with minimal cost to simplicity. "All the coins in my pocket are copper" does not improve the best system — it is too specific and too local to be worth stating. Laws are distinguished by their theoretical virtues, not by any intrinsic necessitation.

The BSA has been defended as capturing what scientists actually mean by "laws": the best systematic summary of the universe's behavior. It explains why scientists care about laws — they are the organizing principles of the best theory. It also explains why we care about simplicity: simple laws are preferable not because nature is simple, but because simpler systems are better summaries.

Critics of the BSA press several objections. The analysis appeals to "simplicity" and "strength," which are relative to a choice of language and conceptual scheme — there is no language-independent fact about which system is simplest. This seems to make laws mind-dependent. Lewis responded that the best system is identified relative to the perfectly natural properties that carve nature at its joints, but specifying these requires realism about natural properties that many Humeans resist.

There is also the problem of universality: if a world had only one instance of a regularity, would the BSA count it as a law? Probably not, because a single-instance generalization adds too little to the system. But physicists sometimes posit laws that may have had very limited instantiation.
