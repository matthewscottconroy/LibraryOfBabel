# Armstrong on Universals and Laws

David Armstrong's theory of laws of nature is part of a broader ontological program that includes his account of universals, states of affairs, and truthmakers. For Armstrong, laws are second-order states of affairs: relations of necessitation holding between first-order universals. Understanding Armstrong's view requires setting it within this broader ontological context.

Armstrong is a realist about universals: he holds that there are genuine, mind-independent universals that are instantiated in particulars. Unlike Platonic universals, Armstrong's universals exist only insofar as they are instantiated — he is an "immanent" or Aristotelian realist. The universal of being copper exists because there are copper things; if all copper were destroyed, the universal would cease to exist.

Laws, on Armstrong's view, involve a second-order universal: the relation of nomological necessitation, symbolized N. When the law "All F's are G's" holds, this is because the universal F stands in the necessitation relation N to the universal G. This second-order state of affairs — N(F, G) — is what makes the first-order regularity hold. The law is not identical to the regularity; it is the higher-order fact that explains the regularity.

This means that Armstrong's laws are "governing" rather than "descriptive": they do not merely record patterns but are metaphysically responsible for those patterns. The universals F and G, being necessarily connected by N, ensure that every instance of F is an instance of G. This is stronger than the Humean claim that every F has in fact been G.

Armstrong uses this framework to address the problem of uninstantiated laws. Can there be a law about particles of a type that never actually exist? For Armstrong, the answer requires care: since universals are immanent, uninstantiated universals do not exist, and laws without instantiated universals are problematic. Armstrong allows for "potential" laws but is cautious about their status.

His treatment of probabilistic laws extends the framework: probabilistic laws are relations of probabilistic necessitation between universals. N(F, G, p) means that being F probabilistically necessitates being G to degree p. This is meant to explain why the relative frequency of G among F's is approximately p without reducing this to a mere pattern.
