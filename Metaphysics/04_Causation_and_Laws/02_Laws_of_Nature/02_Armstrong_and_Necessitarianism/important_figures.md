# Important Figures: Armstrong and Necessitarianism

**David Armstrong (1926–2014)** is the central figure. His three-volume study of universals, laws, and truthmakers — *Universals and Scientific Realism* (1978), *What is a Law of Nature?* (1983), and *A World of States of Affairs* (1997) — constitute a systematic metaphysical program. Armstrong's defense of immanent realism about universals and his necessitation account of laws shaped the agenda for three decades.

**Fred Dretske (1932–2013)** independently developed a necessitation account of laws in "Laws of Nature" (1977), simultaneously with Armstrong and Tooley. His work in epistemology (tracking theories of knowledge and perception) connected to his interest in objective relations between properties.

**Michael Tooley (b. 1941)** contributed the third strand of the DTA account, particularly emphasizing the possibility of uninstantiated laws — laws governing property-types that never actually occur. His *Causation: A Realist Approach* (1987) defends a realist view of both laws and causation.

**Brian Ellis (b. 1929)** developed scientific essentialism as an alternative to both Humean accounts and the DTA framework. His emphasis on the dispositional essences of natural properties has been highly influential.

**Kit Fine (b. 1946)** developed the notion of essence-based necessity, arguing that many necessities hold by virtue of the essences of the entities involved. His work has influenced both the metaphysics of laws and the metaphysics of modality.

**Alexander Bird (b. 1964)** defended a powerful version of the dispositionalist/essentialist view in *Nature's Metaphysics* (2007), arguing that all fundamental properties are dispositions and that laws of nature are metaphysically necessary.
