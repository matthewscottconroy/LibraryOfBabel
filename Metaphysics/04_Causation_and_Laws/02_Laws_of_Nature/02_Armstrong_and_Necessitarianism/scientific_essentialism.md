# Scientific Essentialism and Natural Necessity

Brian Ellis's "scientific essentialism," developed in *Scientific Essentialism* (2001) and *The Philosophy of Nature* (2002), offers a distinctive alternative to both Humean regularity accounts and Armstrong's necessitation theory. Ellis holds that the properties of natural kinds — the properties studied by fundamental physics and chemistry — are essentially dispositional, and the laws of nature hold with genuine necessity because they express the essential natures of these properties.

On Ellis's view, fundamental physical properties like electric charge, mass, and spin are not categorical properties that happen to exert forces and follow mathematical laws. Rather, these properties are constitutively dispositional: it is of the essence of electric charge to repel like charges and attract unlike charges. The law governing electromagnetic interaction is not a separate fact about the world, superadded to the distribution of charges; it is entailed by the very nature of charge. Laws hold necessarily because the properties they describe are essentially the way they are.

This view connects to the broader tradition of "essentialism" about natural kinds. Just as it is essential to water to be H2O (Kripke's insight), it is essential to electrons to have negative charge. These essential properties include the dispositions to behave in certain ways under certain conditions. The science of chemistry and physics is thus, in part, the investigation of essential natures.

Scientific essentialism has consequences for modality. If laws hold necessarily by virtue of the essences of properties, then they hold in all possible worlds where those properties exist. This is a form of natural necessity: the laws could not have been different without the properties themselves being different. There are no nomologically impossible worlds where, say, gravity goes as the inverse cube instead of the inverse square — because gravity would simply not be gravity in such a world.

Critics object that scientific essentialism makes modal claims that are difficult to verify. How do we know which of a property's features are essential and which are contingent? Ellis responds that this is an empirical question, answered by the practice of science — we discover essences the same way we discover other features of nature.
