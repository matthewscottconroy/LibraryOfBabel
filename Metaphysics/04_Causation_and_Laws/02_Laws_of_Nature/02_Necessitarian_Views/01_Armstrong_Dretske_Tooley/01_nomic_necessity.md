# Nomic Necessity

Philosophers have long recognized that there are different strengths of necessity, and that the necessity of laws of nature is not the strongest. "2+2=4" cannot fail to be true; "water is H₂O" cannot fail to be true once we have fixed which substance water is; but "masses attract according to the inverse-square law" is the sort of thing that is necessarily true given the laws of nature, while remaining conceivably false in worlds governed by different laws. This intermediate grade — necessity given the laws — is what we call nomic necessity. Understanding it matters because it is what distinguishes laws from accidents, grounds counterfactual support, and underwrites the reliability of scientific prediction.

## The Three Grades

The hierarchy runs from strongest to weakest. **Logical and mathematical necessity**: a proposition necessarily true by pure logic or mathematics, whose denial is logically contradictory. "All bachelors are unmarried" and "2+2=4" are in this category. Alternative laws of logic are not even coherently conceivable without contradiction. **Metaphysical necessity**: a proposition necessarily true given the essences of the entities involved. "Water is H₂O" is metaphysically necessary — no possible world contains water that is not H₂O, because water is constituted by its molecular structure. But this is not logically necessary: "H₂O is XYZ" is not logically contradictory; we discovered the identity empirically. **Nomic necessity (physical necessity)**: a proposition necessarily true given the laws of nature. It is nomically necessary that masses attract according to the inverse-square law, given that this is a law. But there are possible worlds where gravity goes as the inverse cube — worlds with different laws — so the necessity is not metaphysical.

Some accounts collapse nomic necessity into metaphysical necessity. Dispositional essentialists (Bird, Ellis) argue that if properties are essentially dispositional — if the property of mass just is, in its essential nature, the power to attract according to the inverse-square law — then in every world where mass exists, the law holds. On this view, "worlds where gravity goes as the inverse cube" are worlds without the property of mass but with a different property playing a somewhat similar role. The actual property of mass necessarily obeys the law. The conceivable inverse-cube world is not a world where our actual laws fail, but a world with different fundamental properties. Whether this is right depends on substantive questions about the nature of properties that we will need to work out separately.

## Why Nomic Necessity Matters

The question is not merely taxonomic. Nomic necessity does the following philosophical work.

It explains counterfactual support. "If this were copper, it would conduct electricity" is licensed by the law because copper's conductance is nomically necessary — it holds even in counterfactual scenarios. "If this coin were in my pocket, it would be silver" is not licensed by the accidental regularity because the accident does not hold with nomic necessity; the accidental is not preserved across nearby counterfactual possibilities.

It grounds prediction and control. We rely on laws for prediction and intervention not merely because they have held in the past but because they hold with a kind of necessity. If laws were mere regularities without necessity, inductive inference from past regularities to future behavior would be rationally ungrounded — a version of Hume's problem of induction without any resolution. Nomic necessity provides exactly what the inductive inference needs: a guarantee that the regularity will continue to hold.

It gives laws genuine explanatory force. "Why does this piece of copper conduct?" has a deep answer only if there is something it is to be copper that makes conductance necessary. If copper conductance were merely a regularity, the answer "because all copper conducts, and this is copper" would be explanatorily empty — it would restate the regularity rather than explain it. If copper conductance is nomically necessary, the answer gains force: copper must conduct, given what the laws of nature are.

## The ADT Grounding

The Armstrong-Dretske-Tooley account grounds nomic necessity in a necessitation relation N between universals: N(F, G) says that the universal F stands in a primitive, irreducible second-order relation to G, making it the case that all F's are G's. The necessity is a posteriori — we discover N(F, G) through empirical science, not a priori reasoning — and synthetic — it does not follow from the meanings of F and G alone. Crucially, it is also contingent: there are possible worlds where F and G exist as universals but do not stand in the necessitation relation, so the laws could have been otherwise. This preserves the intuition that scientific laws are discovered empirically and are not metaphysically necessary truths.

## Objections

The Humean challenge: we never observe nomic necessity; we only observe regularities. Positing nomic necessity as an additional feature of the world is an unjustified metaphysical addition. The reply: the regularities are all we observe, but they are not all there is. If laws held with mere regularity rather than necessity, their universality would be either a brute fact or a lucky coincidence extending across all of space and time. Nomic necessity explains why the regularity holds everywhere and always; bare regularity cannot explain itself.

The conceivability objection: we can coherently imagine a world where gravity goes as the inverse cube. If such a world is genuinely conceivable, it is possible; and if it is possible, the inverse-square law is not metaphysically necessary. Bird's reply: what we are imagining is not the same property of mass behaving differently, but a world with a different property — one that plays a gravity-like role but with a different essential nature. Once we fix the identity of the property, the law follows necessarily. The conceivability tracks the contingency of which properties exist, not the contingency of the laws given the properties. Whether this reply is fully convincing is one of the substantive disputes between necessitarian and Humean accounts.
