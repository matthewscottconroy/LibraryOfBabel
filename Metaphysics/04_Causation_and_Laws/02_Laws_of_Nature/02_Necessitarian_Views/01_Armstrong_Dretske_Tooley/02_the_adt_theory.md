# The Armstrong-Dretske-Tooley Theory

The regularity theory says that "all F's are G's" is a law just in case it is a true universal generalization. The Armstrong-Dretske-Tooley theory says this is wrong: the law "all F's are G's" involves an additional higher-order fact — that the universal F stands in a necessitation relation N to the universal G. Developed independently by Fred Dretske ("Laws of Nature," 1977), Michael Tooley ("The Nature of Laws," 1977), and David Armstrong (*What is a Law of Nature?*, 1983), this account represents the most sustained realist alternative to Humean approaches, and it puts the problem of laws squarely in the domain of the metaphysics of universals.

## The Core Claim

**N(F, G)** — the universals F and G stand in the necessitation relation — is the ontological core of the ADT account. The law "all electrons have negative charge" is not simply (∀x)(Electron(x) → NegativeCharge(x)). It is the higher-order fact that the universal Electron stands in N to the universal NegativeCharge. The first-order regularity follows from this higher-order fact, but the higher-order fact is ontologically additional to it.

This is the move the ADT account needs to distinguish laws from accidents. Two possible worlds might share the same first-order regularity — every F happens to be a G in both — while differing on whether N(F, G) holds. In the world with N(F, G), the regularity is law-backed; in the world without it, the regularity is accidental. "All gold spheres are smaller than a mile in diameter" lacks the corresponding necessitation relation, while "all electrons have negative charge" has it. The ADT account can draw the distinction; the regularity theory cannot.

## Universals and Their Varieties

The ADT account requires a robust realism about universals — genuinely existing entities that are multiply instantiated in particular things. But the three proponents differed on which variety of universals to accept, and this disagreement matters philosophically.

Armstrong was an immanent realist: universals exist only if instantiated. No actual instance, no actual universal. This is motivated by empiricism about ontology — postulate only what is required by the actual world — but it creates difficulty for laws about particle types that do not actually exist. Armstrong explored responses involving possible worlds and partial solutions, but none was fully satisfying.

Tooley was a transcendent realist: universals can exist without instances. This is required to accommodate uninstantiated laws — laws governing hypothetically defined particle types that have never been observed. If N(F, G) holds between universals F and G even when nothing instantiates F, then the law holds even without any actual instances. The cost is ontological: transcendent universals are abstract entities that exist entirely independently of the physical world, which is a significant commitment.

Dretske's contribution was primarily semantic: he argued that law statements have irreducible logical form — they are not first-order universal quantifications over particulars but second-order statements about universals. Whatever the metaphysics of universals, the form of a law statement is not captured by "(∀x)(Fx → Gx)."

## Properties of the Necessitation Relation

N has several important structural features. It is **second-order**: a relation between universals, not between particular things or events. It is **irreducible**: not analyzable in terms of first-order regularities or any other concepts — this is what distinguishes the ADT account from all regularity theories. It **entails** the regularity: N(F, G) entails (∀x)(Fx → Gx). It is **contingent**: N(F, G) holds in some possible worlds but not others, so the laws of nature could have been otherwise — fitting the scientific finding that laws are discovered empirically, not derived a priori. And it is **governing**: N(F, G) governs the behavior of F-instances; they must be G-instances because the universals are necessarily connected.

## The Explanatory Advantage

Armstrong emphasized the explanatory advantage over regularity theories. Consider the question "Why does this piece of copper conduct electricity?" On the Humean account: "Because all copper does, and this is copper." This restates the regularity rather than explaining it. On the ADT account: "Because the universal Copper stands in the necessitation relation N to the universal Conductor. Since N(Copper, Conductor), anything instantiating Copper must instantiate Conductor." This is explanatory in a different sense: the regularity is derived from the higher-order fact that grounds it.

Whether this is genuine explanation rather than explanation-sounding redescription is exactly what the inference problem challenges. But the structure of the answer is different from the Humean's, and the difference captures a real intuition: there is something about being copper that makes conductance necessary, and the ADT account gives this something a name and a place in the ontology.

## Contrast with Dispositional Essentialism

The ADT account and dispositional essentialism both posit non-Humean modal structure, but they locate it differently. For ADT, modal structure is in the second-order relation N between universals. The properties F and G are not essentially connected; N is an additional connection imposed on them. This allows that the same universals could have had different laws in worlds without N(F, G). For dispositional essentialism (Bird), modal structure is in the essential natures of the properties themselves: F's essential nature is to necessitate G, and N is not an additional relation but a consequence of what F essentially is. On this view, the same property cannot have different laws in different worlds — the laws are metaphysically necessary given which properties exist. The ADT account preserves the contingency of laws; dispositional essentialism does not. This is one of the sharpest metaphysical divides in the theory of laws.
