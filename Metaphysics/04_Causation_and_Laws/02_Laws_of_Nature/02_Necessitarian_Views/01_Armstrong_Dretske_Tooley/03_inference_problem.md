# The Inference Problem for the ADT Theory

The ADT account says that laws are second-order states of affairs: when N(F, G) holds, the universal F stands in a necessitation relation to the universal G, and this higher-order fact explains why every F-instance is a G-instance. But Lewis raised a pointed objection that has become the central challenge for the account. Even granting that N(F, G) holds — even accepting the necessitation relation as a primitive metaphysical fact — what guarantees that individual particulars instantiating F also instantiate G? The higher-order relation is between universals; particular objects are not universals. How does the relation among abstracta constrain the behavior of concreta?

## The Problem Stated

The worry has a precise structure. Consider an analogy: the fact that France is larger than Belgium does not entail that every Frenchman is larger than every Belgian. The relation holds between the countries, not between their individual members. Analogously, one might worry, the fact that the universal F is necessarily connected to the universal G does not entail that every particular instantiating F also instantiates G — unless we have an additional principle connecting facts about universals to facts about their instances.

Formally:

- **P1**: N(F, G) — the universals F and G stand in the necessitation relation.
- **Gap**: What connects this higher-order relation to first-order facts about particulars?
- **Target**: (∀x)(Fx → Gx) — every F-instance is a G-instance.

Lewis asked: how does the ADT theorist bridge P1 to the target? Two options present themselves, and both are problematic.

**Option 1** is that N(F, G) logically entails (∀x)(Fx → Gx). If the entailment is logical, then the content of N already encodes the universal regularity. The explanation of the regularity by the necessitation relation is then circular: we are saying the regularity holds because of a higher-order fact that has the regularity built into it. The necessitation relation is just another way of asserting the regularity, not an independent explanation of it. If N(F, G) simply means "all F's are G's, plus something extra," then the "something extra" does no independent explanatory work.

**Option 2** is that the entailment is not logical but substantive — a metaphysical principle that what holds between universals must hold between their instances. But this principle is itself mysterious and unexplained. Why should a relation between abstracta constrain the behavior of concreta? This looks like an additional metaphysical commitment over and above the necessitation relation itself. We now need not only N but a "principle of instantiation" connecting N-facts about universals to first-order facts about particulars. And this principle is a further primitive requiring explanation.

## Armstrong's Response

Armstrong's response is that this objection misunderstands the nature of necessitation. N is not a logical entailment but an irreducible, primitive metaphysical relation that by its very nature constrains instances. When F necessitates G, this just is the fact that F-instances are G-instances — the necessitation and the regularity are necessarily co-present, not because one derives from the other but because they are inseparable aspects of the same state of affairs.

Demanding a further explanation for why N(F, G) constrains instances is like demanding an explanation for why modus ponens is truth-preserving. At some point, primitive connections must be accepted. The regress of explanations must stop somewhere, and it stops at the primitive relation N. Being F just involves being necessarily connected to G: this is not a mysterious action at a distance from abstracts to particulars but the nature of universals to be active principles in their instances.

Whether this response is convincing depends on how comfortable one is with primitive metaphysical connections. Lewis remained skeptical: even accepting N as primitive, we are owed an account of why N(F, G) makes each individual F-instance a G-instance. The relation is between universals; it is a further and apparently underexplained step that it constrains instances. What we want is some understanding of how the higher-order and first-order levels are connected; asserting that N is primitive and by its nature constraining does not provide that understanding.

## Alternative Responses

Dispositional essentialism dissolves the inference problem rather than solving it. If laws are not imposed on properties by an external relation N but instead follow from what the properties essentially are, then there is no gap between the property and its causal role. Being charged constitutively involves the power to attract opposite charges. The law is internal to the property's nature; there is nothing for an inference problem to bite on.

Maudlin's primitivism takes a different route. Laws are primitive features of the world that govern particulars directly. There is no need for a second-order relation between universals because laws are not facts about universals at all — they are facts about the world as a whole, specifying what must happen. The inference problem arises only for those who, like Armstrong, try to ground laws in relations between universals; it does not arise for an account on which laws are just the fundamental governance structure of the world.

The Humean response treats the inference problem as a reductio: it reveals a deep tension in any attempt to ground the behavior of particulars in relations among universals. Particulars just behave as they do; the "laws" are summaries of that behavior. The ADT account tries to add an explanatory layer — universals plus N — that, on close inspection, does not actually explain anything beyond what the Humean mosaic contains. The inference problem is not incidental but reflects the essential difficulty of the project.

## Significance

The inference problem does not obviously refute the ADT account. Armstrong's response — that N is primitive and by its nature constraining — is not obviously wrong. What the problem reveals is the extent to which the ADT account depends on a primitive, unanalyzed connection between the higher-order and first-order levels. This is a feature for those who accept that some primitives must be tolerated; it is a liability for those seeking metaphysical transparency. The problem also illuminates what distinguishes ADT from dispositional essentialism: both are anti-Humean, but they locate the modal structure differently, and only ADT faces the inference problem in this form.
