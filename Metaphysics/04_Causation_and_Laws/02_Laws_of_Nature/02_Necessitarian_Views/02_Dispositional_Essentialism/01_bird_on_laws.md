# Bird on Dispositional Essentialism and Laws

What makes a property the property it is? The Humean answer — broadly speaking — is that properties have intrinsic qualitative natures, quiddities, that are independent of any causal role they happen to play. The laws connecting properties are then contingent additions to the ontology, imposed on the properties from outside. Alexander Bird's *Nature's Metaphysics* (2007) argues that this picture is fundamentally mistaken. Properties are not bare qualitative characters awaiting causal roles; they are constitutively the powers they are. And if that is right, the laws connecting properties are not external impositions but necessary consequences of what the properties essentially are.

## The Core Thesis

Bird's central claim is that natural properties are individuated and constituted entirely by their dispositional profiles — by what would happen if something instantiated the property under various conditions. The charge of an electron is not a quiddity with only contingent connections to causal behavior; it is constitutively the power to repel other electrons and attract protons. The property just is this causal role. Remove the causal role, and you no longer have the same property — you have a different one, or no property at all.

This is a strong claim. It is not merely that properties happen to be connected to causal roles, but that the causal role constitutes what the property is. This generates the central consequence for laws:

- **P1**: The property of charge is constitutively the power to repel like charges and attract opposite charges.
- **P2**: Anything with this power is, by definition, subject to the law governing how like charges interact.
- **C**: The law governing charge's interaction holds necessarily in any world where charge with its actual essential nature exists.

Laws are not additional contingent facts about the world, imposed on properties by some external necessity relation. They are entailed by what the properties essentially are. Laws are therefore **metaphysically necessary**, though they are discovered **a posteriori** through empirical investigation — exactly the structure Kripke identified for "water is H₂O."

## Coulomb's Law as a Case Study

Consider Coulomb's law: the force between two charged particles is F = k_e × q₁q₂/r². On Bird's view, this law is not a BSA summary of observed regularities, and it is not an Armstrong-style necessitation relation N(Charge, Coulomb-force) imposed contingently on the universals involved. The property of electric charge has an essential nature that includes a directed tendency: like charges repel with force proportional to the product of charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance. This is what charge is. In any world where something genuinely has the property of electric charge with its actual essential nature, Coulomb's law governs its interactions. There is no possible world where something has charge — in the actual sense — but fails to obey Coulomb's law. Such a world would contain a different property.

This has a striking epistemic consequence. We cannot coherently imagine a world where charge exists but like charges attract. If we seem to imagine this, we are actually imagining either a world with a different property that resembles charge in some respects, or we are using the word "charge" to pick out whatever property plays a charge-like role in the imagined world — and in a world where that role is played differently, the relevant property has a different nature. The apparent conceivability of "charge with different laws" does not establish genuine metaphysical possibility of charge with different laws. The conceivability intuition is tracking the contingency of which properties exist, not the contingency of what laws hold given those properties.

## Objections

The **always-packing, never-arriving objection** (Black, Martin) asks: if all properties are essentially dispositional, then the manifestations of any power are themselves powers, whose manifestations are further powers, and so on without end. There is no categorical ground for the structure of powers — the web of dispositions points without ever arriving at any actualized basis. Bird's response is that some manifestations are not powers but actual events — actual episodes of energy transfer, particle interaction, field disturbance. The regress terminates in actualities. Alternatively, powers can be mutually individuating in a self-supporting web without requiring a categorical terminus.

The **rotating properties objection** (Black) notes that if properties are individuated by their causal roles, then a world where property F plays the role of actual G and property G plays the role of actual F would be indistinguishable from the actual world. Does this show that there is no genuine difference — confirming dispositional essentialism — or that there must be some quidditative difference the dispositionalist cannot capture? Bird's response: the scenario is not a genuinely different possible world. If F and G are individuated by their causal roles, then in the imagined world F just is the property we call G and G just is the property we call F. The "difference" is verbal.

The **necessity problem** presses the intuition that nature could have been otherwise — that there could have been different fundamental laws. Bird's account denies this given the actual properties: the laws are necessary given which properties exist. What is contingent is which properties are actual. The contingency is real but it is at the level of which properties exist, not at the level of what laws govern those properties.

## Relation to Scientific Essentialism

Bird's account is continuous with Brian Ellis's broader scientific essentialism, which holds that natural kinds — not just properties — have essential natures determining their causal powers. Laws of nature are consequences of what natural kinds essentially are; science discovers essences, and through essences, the necessary structure of the world. Bird focuses primarily on fundamental physical properties, giving the account greater connection to contemporary physics. The shared structure is what matters: laws are not contingent connections between properties but necessary consequences of what those properties are, and discovering the laws is discovering what the properties essentially are.
