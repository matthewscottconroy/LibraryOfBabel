# Quidditism Versus Dispositional Essentialism

What makes a natural property the property it is — what makes mass mass, and charge charge, rather than some other properties? This question turns out to be one of the most contentious in the metaphysics of properties, with consequences that reach into the theory of laws, the semantics of natural kind terms, and the structure of modal space. The two positions that have crystallized around this question are quidditism and dispositional essentialism, and the choice between them reflects a deep disagreement about the relationship between the intrinsic nature of properties and their causal behavior.

## The Question and the Two Answers

Quidditists hold that fundamental properties have intrinsic qualitative characters — quiddities — that are metaphysically prior to and independent of any causal or nomic role the property plays. Mass is individuated by being mass: a specific qualitative nature. The gravitational behavior of mass is a contingent fact about how mass interacts with other properties via the laws of nature. The quiddity is the property; the causal role is imposed from outside.

Dispositional essentialists hold that properties are individuated entirely by their causal and dispositional profiles — by what they do. Mass is individuated by its gravitational behavior, its role in F = ma, its interaction with other properties. There is no "extra" quidditative nature over and above this causal profile; to know the causal role is to know what the property is.

Lewis implicitly endorsed quidditism as part of his Humean supervenience program. If properties have quiddities independent of their causal roles, then the laws connecting properties are contingent: it is possible for the same properties to be distributed differently and governed by different laws. This is exactly what the Humean requires — all connections between distinct existences are contingent. Quidditism allows for alien possibilities: worlds with the same fundamental properties but governed by entirely different laws.

Bird's dispositional essentialism denies this. Properties cannot have different causal roles and remain the same properties. Therefore, in any world where the actual properties exist, the actual laws hold. The modal space is more constrained than quidditism allows.

## The Modal Implications

The modal implications of the two views differ dramatically. On quidditism, the space of possible worlds is vast: for any set of properties, there are worlds where those properties are governed by entirely different laws. Mass could attract like charges and repel opposite charges in some world; the property of mass would remain the same quiddity, but the laws would be different.

On dispositional essentialism, the space of possible worlds is more constrained. Once the properties are fixed by their essential natures, the laws are fixed. Different possible worlds have different properties or the same properties arranged differently — but not the same properties with different laws. The modal space has a two-level structure: which properties exist is contingent; what laws govern those properties, given their existence, is necessary.

## The Epistemological Argument Against Quidditism

Bird's strongest argument is epistemological. If properties have quidditative natures independent of their causal roles, we cannot have epistemic access to those natures — we know properties only through their causal manifestations. Quiddities are therefore noumenal: beyond epistemic reach, like Kantian things-in-themselves. An epistemically inaccessible entity is a theoretical liability; Ockham's razor recommends eliminating it. This parallels arguments against Lockean real essences — the supposed unknowable intrinsic natures of substances. If we can know properties only through their causal roles, and if positing quiddities adds nothing epistemically accessible, quiddities are idle wheels.

The parallel with Kant is illuminating, because the standard Kantian response is also available here: the epistemological inaccessibility of quiddities might reflect a limitation of our epistemic situation rather than a reason to deny their existence. But Bird's point is that theoretical posits that make no explanatory difference and are in principle undetectable should be rejected — and quiddities appear to satisfy this condition exactly.

## The Epistemological Argument for Quidditism

Lewis and others can argue in the opposite direction. If properties are individuated solely by their causal roles, then any world with different properties playing the same causal roles would be identical to the actual world — since the causal structure would be the same. But we have the intuition that there could be worlds with the same laws (same causal roles) but with genuinely different properties underlying those roles.

This is the **permutation argument**: swap all the causal roles of all properties, letting mass play the role of charge and charge play the role of mass. On dispositional essentialism, this would create a world with the same laws (because the roles are the same) but different properties. But the world seems identical to actuality — all the structural facts are preserved. Does this show there is no genuine difference, confirming dispositional essentialism? Or does it show that there must be some quidditative difference the dispositionalist cannot capture, confirming quidditism? Bird's response is that the scenario is merely verbal: the imagined world just relabels the actual properties. But whether this response is adequate or whether it begs the question against the quidditist is exactly what the debate turns on.

## Connection to Physics and Mathematics

The debate connects to considerations from physics and mathematics that resist easy resolution. The standard model defines fundamental properties entirely by their quantum numbers — which are effectively their causal roles in interactions. This suggests dispositional essentialism: the properties are their causal roles, and there is no further quidditative character. Structural mathematics describes objects entirely by their structural and relational properties, and physical structuralists argue that this is how we should understand physical properties too.

Whether these considerations settle the debate depends on contested questions about scientific realism and the interpretation of quantum mechanics. What the debate reveals, at minimum, is that the choice between quidditism and dispositional essentialism is not merely a metaphysical curiosity but has genuine consequences for how we understand the relationship between properties, laws, and the space of metaphysical possibilities — consequences that physics and philosophy must work out together.
