# Necessary Laws and Their Philosophical Significance

The claim that laws of nature are metaphysically necessary — that they could not have been otherwise — is one of the most striking consequences of dispositional essentialism. It conflicts with the widespread intuition that natural laws are contingent, discovered empirically, and that alternative laws are at least conceivable. If Bird and Ellis are right, this intuition is partly mistaken: what seems to be the conceivability of alternative laws is actually the conceivability of worlds with different properties, and once we fix the properties, the laws are fixed with them. The philosophical consequences of this view are far-reaching.

## The Thesis

On dispositional essentialism, laws follow from the essential dispositional natures of properties. If those natures are necessarily what they are — in the sense that a property with a different nature would be a different property — then:

**Necessary Laws Thesis (NLT)**: For any actual law L expressing the causal profile of an actual property P, L is metaphysically necessary. There is no possible world where P exists with its actual essential nature and L fails.

This is metaphysical necessity, not logical necessity. L's denial is not logically contradictory; there is no formal inconsistency in "charge attracts like charges." It is simply that any world where L fails is a world that does not contain P with its actual essential nature — it contains a different property, or none. The necessity is grounded in essences, not in logic.

## Science as Modal Discovery

If laws are necessary, then what we discover through science is not merely how things happen to be but how they must be. The periodic table, the laws of thermodynamics, the inverse-square law of gravitation — these are not contingent features of the actual world that might have been different. They reflect the essential natures of natural properties and kinds. Scientific explanation becomes illumination of natural necessity: when we explain why copper conducts electricity by appeal to band structure and Fermi energy, we are explaining why it must behave this way, given what copper is.

This transforms the epistemological status of scientific laws. We discover them a posteriori through observation and experiment — there is no other way. But what we discover is a necessary truth. This is the same structure as Kripke's "water is H₂O": discovered a posteriori, necessarily true if true. Scientific knowledge on the necessary laws view is a posteriori knowledge of metaphysical necessity — a category Kripke opened up and that dispositional essentialism fills with scientific content.

## Implications for Modal Space

Necessary laws constrain the space of possible worlds in a distinctive way. On quidditism — the Humean picture — the modal space is vast: any combination of properties and laws is metaphysically possible, so there are worlds with the same fundamental particles as ours but with gravity going as the inverse cube. On dispositional essentialism, the space of possible worlds is two-dimensional. Which properties exist is contingent: different worlds may contain different fundamental properties, with different essential natures, governed by different laws. But what laws hold given those properties is necessary: once the existence of a property with a specific essential nature is fixed, the laws governing it are fixed.

The contingency in nature, on this view, is at the level of which properties are actual — which properties happen to be instantiated in the actual world. There is no further contingency at the level of what laws govern those properties. Some find this an improvement: the contingency is real but located at the right level, rather than making everything about nature contingently different.

## The Conceivability Argument and Its Response

The most persistent objection to necessary laws is the conceivability argument. We can vividly imagine a world where gravity follows an inverse-cube law. If such a world is genuinely conceivable, it is possible; and if it is possible, the inverse-square law is not metaphysically necessary.

The response distinguishes two things. When we seem to imagine "gravity going as the inverse cube," we are imagining either the word "gravity" applying to a different property, or a world with a different property that plays a role somewhat similar to mass. We are not imagining the actual property of mass — the property constitutively defined by its inverse-square gravitational behavior — exhibiting different behavior. Once we hold fixed the identity of the property as constituted by its causal role, the conceivability intuition dissolves: there is nothing coherent to imagine.

The response also invokes the general point that conceivability does not entail metaphysical possibility. The conceivability of "heat is not molecular motion" before the kinetic theory did not make it genuinely possible that heat is not molecular motion. We were conceiving of the phenomenal character of heat without conceiving of its underlying nature. Similarly, the conceivability of "gravity is inverse-cube" may involve conceiving of a gravitational phenomenon without conceiving of the property of mass and its essential nature.

## Residual Questions

If laws are necessary, is there a further explanation of why the actual laws are what they are — why gravity goes as the inverse square rather than the inverse cube? For the Humean, the laws are what they are contingently, and no further explanation is available. For the dispositional essentialist, the specific character of the laws is determined by the specific essential natures of the actual properties. But why do the actual properties have the essential natures they have? Bird's response: the essential natures of the actual properties are brute facts about which properties are actual. There is no further explanation; the explanation bottoms out in what properties exist. Critics find this unsatisfying — it merely pushes the contingency back one level, from laws to the existence of particular properties. But Bird's position is that explaining laws in terms of essential natures is genuine explanatory progress. To say "copper must conduct because of what copper essentially is" is more illuminating than saying "copper conducts, contingently, because the laws of this world happen to be what they are."

Whether this is a real gain depends on what we think explanations are for. If explanation bottoms out in essential natures rather than in brute contingent laws, the world has a more intelligible structure — but the intelligibility terminates in a different set of brute facts, not in no brute facts at all.
