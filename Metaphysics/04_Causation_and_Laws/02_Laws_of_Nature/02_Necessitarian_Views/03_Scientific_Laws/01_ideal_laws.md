# Ideal Laws and Their Approximations

Boyle's law holds exactly only for ideal gases. Newton's first law holds for bodies subject to no forces. The Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium holds for infinitely large, perfectly isolated, randomly mating populations. None of these conditions are ever perfectly realized. Real gases deviate from ideal behavior; no physical body is ever entirely force-free; every real population is finite and exposed to selection. And yet these are paradigmatic scientific laws — central to explanation, prediction, and theoretical understanding in their respective domains. Something must be wrong with any account that requires laws to be strictly true of actual objects.

## The Problem and Its Depth

The problem of idealized laws is not peripheral. It is not that a few minor laws have been messily stated and could be cleaned up with more care. It is that idealization is a pervasive feature of scientific practice, extending to the most fundamental laws of physics. If Boyle's law PV = nRT is not strictly true of any actual gas, then it is not a true universal generalization in the sense the naive regularity theory requires. Yet it seems to be a genuine law — one that explains the behavior of gases, that supports counterfactuals ("if this gas were heated at constant volume, its pressure would increase"), and that figures essentially in derivations throughout thermodynamics.

The choice, faced squarely, is between three options. We can require that laws be strictly true and then acknowledge that most scientific laws are not laws but approximations to laws. We can relax the truth requirement and say that laws can be approximately true or true only under ideal conditions. Or we can revise the account of what laws are so that idealization is not a deficiency but a feature.

## Cartwright's Diagnosis

Nancy Cartwright's *How the Laws of Physics Lie* (1983) drew the radical conclusion. She distinguished phenomenological laws — low-level empirical generalizations that describe what actually happens in specific experimental contexts — from fundamental laws — the deep theoretical laws of physics like Newton's second law, Maxwell's equations, and the Schrödinger equation. Phenomenological laws are relatively accurate descriptions of particular well-controlled situations. Fundamental laws are not literally true of actual objects; they describe what would happen in idealized single-cause scenarios.

The simulacrum account: the laws of physics are true of ideal models, and we use those models as approximations to real systems. Real objects are subject to multiple interfering forces; the fundamental laws describe what each force would do in isolation. "The laws of physics lie" — not in the sense of deliberate deception, but in the sense of not being literally true of the actual world. They are true of abstract model systems.

Cartwright later developed this into a capacities view in *Nature's Capacities and Their Measurement* (1989). The fundamental laws do not describe regularities at all — they describe the capacities of things. "Aspirin relieves headaches" describes aspirin's capacity, not a regularity. The capacity is real even if not every aspirin-taking relieves every headache; the capacity may be masked, counteracted, or overridden in particular cases. Idealized laws describe the operation of capacities under ideal conditions; real-world behavior is the outcome of multiple capacities interacting. This connects directly to the powers tradition: Cartwright's capacities are Molnar's powers and Bird's dispositional essences in a different register.

## Ceteris Paribus Laws

A common response is to interpret laws as ceteris paribus: "All F's are G's, other things being equal." The clause is meant to exclude cases where interfering factors prevent the manifestation. But giving non-trivial content to "other things being equal" is harder than it looks. If "interfering factors" is defined as whatever prevents G from following F, then the law "all F's are G's, ceteris paribus" is trivially true and empirically empty — it says nothing that could be false. The other horn: if the clause is specified so precisely that it applies only to a narrow class of ideal conditions, the law covers almost no real cases.

Fodor's defense of special science laws shows that the dilemma is not inescapable. "Desire D together with Belief B causes Action A, other things being equal" is non-vacuous because there is a real, specifiable class of interfering conditions — other desires, physical incapacitation, competing beliefs — that can in principle be enumerated and studied empirically. The ceteris paribus clause is not infinite in its elasticity; it has specific content that could make the generalization false.

Woodward's invariance response reframes the question more productively. What makes a law-like generalization genuine is not strict universality but invariance under a specified range of interventions. The law of demand holds invariantly across a wide range of realistic economic conditions; violations occur at the edges of this invariant range, and identifying those edges is part of understanding the law. The ceteris paribus clause specifies the range of invariance; within that range, the relationship is law-like.

## A Hierarchy of Laws

The idealization problem connects naturally to the hierarchical structure of science. Fundamental laws — the Schrödinger equation, the field equations of general relativity — are strict, precise, and universal in scope. Derived laws — the ideal gas law, the Arrhenius equation for reaction rates, the laws of thermodynamics — follow from fundamental laws together with boundary conditions and approximations. Phenomenological laws — Mendel's laws of inheritance, the Hardy-Weinberg principle, the law of supply and demand — describe patterns in complex systems subject to many interfering factors.

At each level, the laws are less strict but more applicable to the phenomena of interest at that level. The idealization increases as we move from fundamental to applied, but the explanatory and predictive value often increases too — because higher-level laws describe patterns that are relevant to the systems we actually care about. Whether the idealized higher-level laws are genuinely laws, or merely useful approximations to what the fundamental laws entail, is a question that different metaphysical accounts answer differently. Humean accounts are comfortable with a hierarchy of regularities; powers accounts can treat each level as describing real capacities operating at that level.
