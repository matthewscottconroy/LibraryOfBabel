# Special Science Laws

Laws in the special sciences — biology, psychology, economics, sociology — differ from the laws of fundamental physics in ways that are philosophically significant. They are almost always hedged, holding ceteris paribus rather than strictly. The kinds that feature in them are multiply realizable: the same mental state, the same economic phenomenon, the same biological function can be physically realized in heterogeneous ways. And they operate at levels of description — organism, mind, economy, society — that do not obviously reduce to physics without loss of the structure that makes the laws informative. Whether these features disqualify special science laws from being genuine laws, or whether they reveal something about what laws actually are, is one of the most productive questions at the intersection of philosophy of science and metaphysics of causation.

## Fodor's Defense

Fodor's argument for the irreducibility and autonomy of special science laws begins from multiple realizability. The same psychological state — pain, belief, desire — can be physically implemented in vastly different neural configurations, and not just in different organisms of the same species but across wildly different kinds of animals and potentially different kinds of physical systems. The same economic phenomenon — inflation, money, market equilibrium — can be physically implemented in coin-based economies, paper currency economies, digital economies, and barter systems. There is no single physical type that corresponds to the special science type.

The argument runs:

- **P1**: Laws connect natural kinds.
- **P2**: Special science kinds (pain, money, fitness) are not identical to physical kinds because they are multiply realizable.
- **P3**: There is no disjunctive physical kind corresponding to a special science kind that preserves the law's explanatory structure. The disjunction of all physical realizers of "pain" is not a natural kind — it appears in no physical law.
- **C**: Special science laws are irreducible. They connect genuine natural kinds at higher levels that cannot be identified with physical kinds.

The conclusion is that the special sciences have their own genuine natural kinds and their own genuine, if ceteris paribus, laws. Reduction to physics would require either identifying special science kinds with physical kinds (which multiple realizability prevents) or replacing the special science law with a massive physical disjunction (which is not a law at all). The special sciences are not reducible without loss.

## Davidson's Anomalous Monism and Its Tension

Davidson's "Mental Events" (1970) argued that there are no strict psychological laws — the mental is "anomalous" in the relevant sense. Mental concepts are normatively constrained: we attribute mental states in ways that make the agent rational, and this normative dimension prevents strict law-like regularities connecting mental state types to physical state types. Therefore, there are no strict psychophysical laws, even if there are token-level mental-physical identities.

The consequence: psychology does not have laws in the sense required for the covering-law model of explanation. Psychological explanations cite reasons — beliefs and desires — not covering laws. This gives psychological explanation a distinctive rationalizing structure.

But Davidson's position creates a tension. If mental properties play no role in strict laws, what causal work do they do beyond being instantiated by causally relevant physical events? The mental properties seem to be along for the ride. Davidson wanted genuine mental causation but got a picture where mental types appear causally inert — the anomalous monism problem. This is a version of the exclusion argument: if physical causes are always sufficient and mental types play no role in laws, mental causation is epiphenomenal.

## Woodward and Mitchell on Law-Like Invariance

Woodward's interventionism provides the most unified treatment. A generalization at any level is law-like iff it is invariant under interventions: the relationship holds stably across a range of background conditions and interventions within the relevant domain. This applies equally to fundamental physics and to the special sciences.

What makes special science generalizations law-like is not universality — they do not hold everywhere and always — but invariance within their domain. "Aspirin relieves headaches" is invariant across different biochemical implementations of the headache mechanism, in different people, at different times. No single physical generalization could capture this without listing all the heterogeneous physical implementations. The higher-level generalization describes a real invariant relationship that the physical description misses.

Mitchell's pragmatic pluralism generalizes this. Scientific laws come in a variety of types — necessary, contingent, idealized, approximate, ceteris paribus — and there is no single correct account of all of them. Special science laws are contingent, ceteris paribus, approximate regularities that hold in specific conditions and for specific systems. They are genuinely scientific — projectible, explanatory, predictive — without being universal or strict. The diversity of law types across sciences reflects the diversity of the world's causal structure, not any deficiency of the special sciences.

## The Ceteris Paribus Objection

Some philosophers (Schiffer, Kim) remain skeptical. The ceteris paribus qualifications on special science laws are so extensive that they say very little. "People maximize utility, other things being equal" seems true no matter what, because "other things being equal" can always be invoked when the generalization fails. If the clause is infinitely elastic, the law is vacuous.

The defense: ceteris paribus clauses are not infinitely elastic. There are specific, identifiable conditions under which the generalizations fail, and identifying those conditions is part of the scientific work. A genuine special science law has a specifiable domain of application. The law of demand fails in Giffen good cases, in highly irrational markets, and in conditions of extreme information asymmetry — and these failure conditions are scientifically discoverable, not definitionally excluded. The challenge is to specify the domain precisely, which is an empirical challenge, not a reason for philosophical skepticism about the existence of special science laws. The ceteris paribus qualifier is not a license for ad hoc protection but a shorthand for a real, investigable boundary condition on the law's domain of invariance.
