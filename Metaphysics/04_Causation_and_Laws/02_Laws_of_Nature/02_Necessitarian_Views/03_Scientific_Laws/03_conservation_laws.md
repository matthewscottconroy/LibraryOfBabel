# Conservation Laws and Their Metaphysical Status

Energy is conserved. So is linear momentum, angular momentum, and electric charge. These conservation laws hold across every known physical process, constrain what is physically possible, and are connected through Noether's theorem to the deep symmetry structure of spacetime. They are not merely the most fundamental laws of physics in an informal sense — they are, in a precise mathematical sense, consequences of the most fundamental features of how physics is structured. What we make of them philosophically depends on what we take laws to be.

## What Conservation Laws Are

A conservation law states that some physical quantity Q of a closed system remains constant through time: dQ/dt = 0 for a closed system. The major conservation laws are well-known: conservation of energy, linear momentum, angular momentum, electric charge, and (in known physics) baryon number and lepton number. Energy can be converted between forms — kinetic to potential, chemical to thermal — but is neither created nor destroyed. Momentum is redistributed among interacting bodies but not destroyed. Charge cannot be created or annihilated except in matter-antimatter pairs that net to zero.

Emmy Noether's theorem (1915) establishes the profound result: every continuous symmetry of a physical system's action corresponds to a conserved quantity. The key correspondences are:

- Time-translation symmetry (laws are the same at all times) ↔ conservation of energy
- Space-translation symmetry (laws are the same at all places) ↔ conservation of momentum
- Rotational symmetry (laws are the same in all directions) ↔ conservation of angular momentum
- U(1) gauge symmetry of the electromagnetic field ↔ conservation of electric charge

The implications are striking: energy is conserved because the laws of physics are the same today as yesterday. Momentum is conserved because the laws are the same here as there. These are not independent brute facts but consequences of the spacetime structure within which physical laws operate.

## Philosophical Implications of Noether's Theorem

Noether's theorem raises the question of hierarchy. If conservation laws follow from symmetries, and symmetries are structural features of the fundamental dynamical laws, then conservation laws are not independent laws but consequences of deeper ones. This suggests a genuine hierarchy: dynamical laws → symmetries → conservation laws. Whether this hierarchy represents real metaphysical dependence or is merely a mathematical derivation relation depends on what laws are.

It also raises the question of necessity. If the spacetime symmetries — time-translation, spatial translation, rotational invariance — are necessary features of physical law, then the conservation laws are necessary too. But are spacetime symmetries necessary? If spacetime could have had different structure, it could have lacked time-translation symmetry, and energy conservation would fail. Whether this is genuinely possible or only apparently so depends on contested questions in the metaphysics of spacetime.

## Conservation Laws on Different Metaphysical Accounts

The different theories of laws give different verdicts on what conservation laws are.

For the Humean, conservation laws are striking regularities that appear in the best deductive system for the actual world. Their connection to symmetries is mathematically remarkable but metaphysically unremarkable: symmetry principles are also regularities in the best system. Conservation laws are contingent — a world where energy occasionally disappeared would be metaphysically possible, though not actual. There is no further necessity to conservation laws beyond their presence in the best system.

For the ADT necessitation theorist, conservation laws are instances of the general framework: N(Closed-system, Conserved-energy) is a second-order fact about universals. The connection to Noether's theorem shows a mathematical relationship between necessitation facts, but the necessitation itself is contingent.

For the dispositional essentialist, conservation laws express essential features of the relevant quantities. Energy is constitutively that which is conserved across processes — this is part of what energy is. The conservation law is not an additional fact about the world but a consequence of the essential nature of energy as a physical quantity. Noether's theorem then shows that this conservation follows from temporal symmetry, and the temporal symmetry may itself be essential to the structure of time. On this view, violations of energy conservation are not metaphysically possible: a world where "energy" fails to be conserved would contain a different quantity, not energy.

This last position coheres with what physics tells us about conservation. In relativistic quantum field theory, energy conservation is tied to the unitarity of the evolution and the structure of the vacuum state. Violations would require fundamental changes to the structure of quantum mechanics, not just different laws operating on the same physical quantities.

## Conservation Laws and Causation

Dowe's conserved quantity theory of causation connects conservation laws to causation in the most intimate way: causation just is the transfer of conserved quantities. On this view, what makes something a cause is precisely that it transfers energy, momentum, or charge along a physical process. Conservation laws are therefore not merely background conditions for causal processes but partly constitutive of the causal relation itself. This gives an unusually direct answer to the question of what causation is: a causal process is one that exhibits the exchange of conserved quantities, and whether such exchange has occurred is in principle empirically determinable.

The philosophical consequence is that if we understand conservation laws well, we thereby understand the objective physical basis of causation — at least at the level of fundamental physical processes. Whether this account of causation extends adequately to higher-level causal claims in the special sciences is a further question, one that the discussion of causation at higher levels of description addresses.
