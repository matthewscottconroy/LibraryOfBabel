# Review Questions — Laws of Nature

1. The simple regularity theory cannot distinguish a law from an accidental generalization, since both are universally true. The Best System Analysis (BSA) tries to fix this by appealing to simplicity and strength. But critics charge that simplicity is relative to a choice of language, making laws mind-dependent. How serious is this objection, and how does Lewis's appeal to "perfectly natural properties" attempt to answer it — and what does that appeal itself cost?

2. Armstrong's DTA theory posits a second-order relation of necessitation, N(F, G), that holds between universals and explains why all F's are G's. Lewis's inference problem charges that nothing in this account explains why the second-order relation produces the first-order regularity. Lay out the problem as precisely as you can and assess the strongest response Armstrong can give. Does the comparison to dispositional properties help or hurt?

3. Dispositional essentialism (Bird, Ellis) holds that properties are individuated entirely by their causal profiles, so the laws governing a property are metaphysically necessary given its existence. The quidditist (Lewis) denies this. What are the modal implications of each view — specifically, what kinds of possible worlds each view permits — and which view's modal space better fits both our scientific and our commonsense intuitions?

4. There are three different locations for modal structure in the debate about laws: in a second-order necessitation relation between universals (DTA), in the essential natures of the properties themselves (dispositional essentialism), and nowhere — because laws are just regularities selected by theoretical virtues (BSA). What is the deepest disagreement between these positions? Is it a disagreement about ontology, about modality, or about both?

5. Special science laws are hedged, multiple-realizable, and lack the universality of fundamental physics. Kim argues that this makes the special sciences ontologically problematic — either higher-level causes reduce to or are excluded by physical causes. Woodward's interventionism offers a different criterion: a generalization is law-like iff it is invariant under interventions within its domain. Does Woodward's criterion give the special sciences what they need, or does it merely rename the problem?

6. Mumford argues that if objects have genuine intrinsic causal powers, laws of nature become ontologically redundant — they are just descriptions of what powers do and can be eliminated from our ontology. Carroll disagrees, arguing that something must explain the specific quantitative profiles of powers. Reconstruct this dispute carefully. Is it a genuine disagreement about what exists, or a disagreement about which direction explanation runs?

7. The BSA grounds laws in fit, simplicity, and strength — all theoretical virtues chosen by us. This raises the question of whether laws are genuinely "in the world" or features of our best systematization of the world. How does this tension connect to the debate about the realism of laws in the special sciences? Do the BSA and special science laws face the same objectivity problem?

8. Scientific essentialism entails that the laws of nature are metaphysically necessary — there is no possible world with the same properties governed by different laws. If this is right, what are the implications for how we should understand scientific counterfactuals and thought experiments in physics (e.g., "what if gravity were an inverse-cube law instead of inverse-square")? Are such thought experiments about genuinely possible worlds or merely about mathematically coherent models?
