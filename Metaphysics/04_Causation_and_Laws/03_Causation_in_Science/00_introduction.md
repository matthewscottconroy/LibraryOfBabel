# Causation in Science

Scientific explanation is pervasively causal. When a geologist explains a mountain range, she identifies the tectonic forces that produced it. When an epidemiologist explains an outbreak of disease, she identifies the causal pathway from pathogen to infection to transmission. When a physicist explains the trajectory of a projectile, she identifies the forces that caused the deviation from straight-line motion. Science seems to be, to a large extent, the project of identifying causes.

But science is also probabilistic — and causation in probabilistic contexts raises distinctive challenges. Epidemiological studies identify risk factors for disease: smoking increases the probability of lung cancer. But an increased probability of an outcome is not the same as causing it. Many smokers never develop lung cancer; some non-smokers do. Probabilistic causation — the analysis of causation in terms of probability-raising rather than in terms of necessitation or sufficient conditions — attempts to capture the epidemiological case. But it faces the challenge of distinguishing genuine causation from mere correlation, and of handling cases where two factors raise each other's probability without any causal connection between them.

Causal explanation in science also raises the question of what makes an explanation causal: is it the subsumption of events under laws, as the covering-law model proposes? Or is it the identification of mechanisms — the underlying processes through which causes produce effects? The mechanistic account of explanation has become increasingly influential in the biological and social sciences, where appeal to higher-level mechanisms seems to do genuine explanatory work even when reduction to physics is unavailable.

This chapter examines probabilistic causation and causal explanation in science, developing the major accounts and evaluating them against the range of cases they must handle. The connection to the broader theories of causation examined in the previous chapter is maintained throughout: the difficulties of probabilistic causation reveal what is most fundamental about the causal relation.
