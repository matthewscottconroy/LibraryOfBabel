# Probabilistic Causation

Smoking causes cancer. This claim is well established and of immense practical importance. Yet it is not a deterministic claim: not all smokers develop cancer, and some non-smokers do. The causal relationship between smoking and cancer is probabilistic — smoking raises the probability of cancer. This is the central case for probabilistic theories of causation, which analyze the causal relation in terms of probability-raising rather than counterfactual dependence or nomic regularity.

The basic idea of probabilistic causation, associated with Suppes and Cartwright, is that C causes E if C raises the probability of E — if P(E|C) > P(E|¬C). This captures the epidemiological picture: a factor is a cause of a disease if its presence increases the rate of the disease in a population. The analysis handles the cases where deterministic causation fails: single events, probabilistic processes, and causal relations where the cause is neither necessary nor sufficient for the effect.

The main difficulties are cases of spurious correlation and Simpson's paradox. Spurious correlations arise when C and E are both caused by a common cause D: the barometer's drop and the storm are correlated, but neither causes the other — they share a common cause in the atmospheric conditions. Probabilistic theories must distinguish genuine causal relations from spurious correlations, typically by appeal to "screening off": D screens off the C-E correlation if, conditional on D, C and E are probabilistically independent.

Simpson's paradox is the more challenging case: an overall positive correlation between C and E can be reversed within each subgroup of a partition. This shows that probability-raising in the whole population does not guarantee probability-raising in every subgroup — the identification of the right reference class for probability calculation is itself a metaphysically laden question.

This section examines probabilistic theories of causation, their application to causal inference in science, and the conceptual difficulties they generate.
