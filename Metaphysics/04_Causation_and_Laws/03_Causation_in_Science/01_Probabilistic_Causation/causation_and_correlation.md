# Causation and Correlation in Scientific Practice

One of the most practically important distinctions in science is between causation and correlation. Two variables may be correlated — statistically associated — without one causing the other. The correlation may reflect a common cause, a coincidental pattern, or a complex confounded relationship. The dictum "correlation does not imply causation" is methodologically central to the design of scientific studies and the interpretation of their results.

The epidemiological literature has developed criteria for moving from correlation to causal inference. Bradford Hill's criteria (1965) are the most influential: a causal relationship is supported by (among other things) the strength of the association, its consistency across studies, its specificity, a dose-response relationship, temporal precedence of the cause, biological plausibility, and experimental evidence of reversibility. These are not individually necessary or sufficient but serve as a checklist for cumulative evidence.

More formally, the framework of causal graphs (Pearl, Spirtes-Glymour-Scheines) provides criteria for identifying causal relationships from observational data, given assumptions about the absence of hidden common causes. The key insight is that causal structure imposes constraints on patterns of conditional independence: variables that are causally unrelated become independent when you condition on the right set of variables (those that block all paths between them in the causal graph). This allows algorithms to search for the causal graph that best explains the observed pattern of independence relationships.

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) break the confounding problem by randomly assigning units to treatment and control conditions. By randomizing, we ensure that any observed difference between treatment and control groups is due to the treatment, not to any common cause. RCTs are the "gold standard" for causal inference precisely because randomization eliminates confounding by design.

But philosophical questions remain. Even in an ideal RCT, we establish a population-level causal effect. Whether any particular individual was caused to recover (or not) by the treatment is a question of token causation that the RCT evidence cannot settle. This gap between population-level effects and individual token causation is a persistent issue in the philosophy of medicine and epidemiology.
