# The Deductive-Nomological Model of Explanation

The Deductive-Nomological (DN) model of scientific explanation, developed by Carl Hempel and Paul Oppenheim in their landmark 1948 paper "Studies in the Logic of Explanation," provided the first rigorous formal account of scientific explanation. The model holds that to explain an event is to subsume it under a general law: a scientific explanation is a deductive argument in which the event to be explained (the explanandum) is derived from general laws and initial conditions (the explanans).

In the DN model, an explanation has two components: the explanans (the explaining statements) and the explanandum (what is to be explained). The explanans must contain at least one general law, the laws must be lawlike (not accidental generalizations), the explanans must be true (or at least well-confirmed), and the explanandum must be derivable from the explanans. When all these conditions are satisfied, the explanation is said to achieve scientific understanding by showing that the explanandum was to be expected given the laws and initial conditions.

The model captures a genuine feature of scientific explanation: many explanations in physics and chemistry really do involve deriving particular phenomena from laws and boundary conditions. The explanation of the path of a projectile proceeds by stating Newton's laws, the initial velocity and angle, and deriving the trajectory. The explanation of the boiling point of water at a given altitude derives from thermodynamic laws and the atmospheric pressure.

Despite its initial appeal, the DN model faces serious objections. The most famous is the irrelevance problem: it is possible to construct DN arguments that are clearly not explanatory. "The flagpole is 30 feet tall" can be explained by deriving its height from the length of its shadow and the angle of the sun — but intuitively, the shadow does not explain the height; the height explains the shadow. DN arguments can run in either direction, yet explanation is asymmetric. The model does not capture the asymmetry of causal direction.

Hempel also developed a probabilistic version — the Inductive-Statistical (IS) model — for cases where the explanans includes probabilistic laws. Here the explanandum is not deductively derived but made highly probable by the explanans. This faces additional problems, including the problem of epistemic relativity (the explanation's adequacy depends on which reference class we use for probability calculations).
