# Causation in the Special Sciences

A recurring question in the philosophy of science is whether the causal explanations of the special sciences — biology, psychology, economics, sociology — are genuine or whether they must ultimately be reduced to physics. This question connects to broader issues about the autonomy of the special sciences, the reality of higher-level causes, and the appropriate standards for causal explanation.

Jerry Fodor's celebrated paper "Special Sciences" (1974) argued that the special sciences are irreducible to physics not because physics is false but because physical realizers of higher-level kinds are heterogeneous. Mental states, for example, can be realized by diverse physical structures across different species and possibly across different substrates altogether. A law of psychology connecting belief and desire to action cannot be expressed at the physical level because the physical realizers of beliefs and desires are wildly various. This is the multiple realizability argument.

Fodor concluded that the special sciences are autonomous: their laws are genuine, their causal explanations are real, and they do not need to be reduced to physics to be scientifically respectable. The causal efficacy of higher-level properties — mental states, biological traits, economic incentives — is not undermined by the fact that these properties are realized by physical properties.

But this creates a philosophical puzzle: if higher-level causes are real, how do they interact with lower-level physics? If mental causes (beliefs and desires) produce behavior, and physical causes (brain states) also produce behavior, we seem to have two causes for the same event. This is the problem of causal overdetermination, or what Jaegwon Kim calls the exclusion problem: the physical cause seems to exclude the mental cause from being the real cause.

Kim argued that the autonomy of the special sciences is illusory: either higher-level properties are identical to or realized by physical properties, in which case the higher-level cause is really just the physical cause redescribed, or they are genuinely distinct, in which case they are causally inert epiphenomena. This dilemma has been called the "causal exclusion problem" and has dominated the philosophy of mind since the 1990s.

Responses include non-reductive physicalism (accepting realization without reduction), property dualism (accepting genuinely distinct mental properties), and various forms of emergentism (arguing that higher-level properties have genuine downward causal powers).
