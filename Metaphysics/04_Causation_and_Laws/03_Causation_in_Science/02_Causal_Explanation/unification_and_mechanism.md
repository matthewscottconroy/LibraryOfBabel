# Unification and Mechanistic Approaches to Explanation

The failure of the DN model to capture causal direction and explanatory asymmetry led to alternative accounts. Two important alternatives are the unification account (Friedman, Kitcher) and the mechanistic account (Salmon, MDC).

Michael Friedman proposed in 1974 that explanation works by unification: a good explanation reduces the number of independent phenomena we must accept by deriving many phenomena from fewer independent principles. Explanation is not about deduction per se but about increasing the unity of our total picture of nature. Newtonian mechanics explains by showing that terrestrial and celestial mechanics, tidal phenomena, and the precession of equinoxes are all consequences of a small number of principles.

Philip Kitcher elaborated this unification account in terms of argument patterns. A scientific theory provides an argument pattern — a schema for deriving explananda — and the value of the theory consists in the number and diversity of phenomena it can explain using a single pattern with minimal modifications. The ideal is a unified "worldview" that derives the most facts from the fewest independent commitments.

The unification account has the virtue of explaining why we find reductive explanations satisfying: explaining mental phenomena in terms of neural mechanisms, or biological phenomena in terms of chemistry, or chemical phenomena in terms of physics achieves unification by reducing the number of independent explanatory levels. But critics argue that not all good explanations are unifying: some local, detailed mechanistic explanations are scientifically valuable precisely because they are highly specific rather than general.

The mechanistic account, by contrast, holds that explanation consists in identifying mechanisms: organized systems of entities and activities whose operation produces the phenomenon to be explained. This is detailed, local, and causal. Salmon's causal process theory and the MDC account of mechanisms both fall in this tradition. On these views, understanding comes not from subsuming phenomena under general laws but from tracing the causal processes and component activities responsible for the phenomenon.

The debate between unificationists and mechanists reflects a genuine tension in scientific practice: biology and neuroscience tend toward mechanistic explanations, while fundamental physics tends toward more abstract, unifying laws. A pluralist might hold that both are legitimate forms of explanation that serve different cognitive interests.
