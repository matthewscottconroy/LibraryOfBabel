# Review Questions — Causation in Science

1. Probabilistic theories of causation define causation as probability-raising: C causes E iff P(E|C) > P(E|¬C). But causes can lower the probability of their effects (a stiff crosswind sends a ball through a gate it would otherwise have missed), and spurious correlations satisfy the probability-raising condition. How does Suppes's screening-off condition attempt to handle the latter problem, and what residual difficulty does the probability-lowering case reveal about whether probability-raising can be the whole story?

2. The deductive-nomological model fails to capture the asymmetry of causal explanation: the shadow length and the sun's angle entail the flagpole's height just as well as the flagpole's height and the sun's angle entail the shadow length, yet explanation runs only one way. What does this failure reveal about the relationship between explanation and causation? Does a shift to counterfactual or interventionist accounts of explanation solve the asymmetry problem, or merely relocate it?

3. Ned Hall argues that we conflate two distinct causal concepts: "production" (a causal process generating an effect) and "dependence" (counterfactual or probabilistic dependence of the effect on the cause). In which contexts — scientific, legal, everyday — does the production/dependence distinction matter most? Can a single unified theory of causation accommodate both, or is pluralism about causation the right conclusion?

4. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are the gold standard for establishing type causation at the population level, but they cannot settle questions of token causation for individuals. The gap between population-level effects and individual token causation is practically significant — in medicine, law, and policy. What philosophical resources are available to bridge this gap, and why is a purely probabilistic approach insufficient?

5. Fodor's multiple realizability argument holds that special science kinds (pain, money, fitness) cannot be identified with physical kinds, so special science laws are irreducible. But Kim's exclusion argument replies that if mental or biological properties are causally efficacious, they either are physical properties or they are causally inert. Reconstruct both arguments and evaluate whether Yablo's determinable/determinate strategy offers a genuine escape from the dilemma.

6. The unification account of explanation (Friedman, Kitcher) and the mechanistic account (Salmon, Machamer-Darden-Craver) represent two quite different visions of what scientific understanding is. Which account fits better with explanatory practice in fundamental physics? In molecular biology and neuroscience? Does the diversity of what counts as a good explanation across science support pluralism about explanation, or does it suggest one account is correct and the other is tracking something else?

7. The causal graphical models framework (Pearl's do-calculus) provides formal criteria for distinguishing causal claims from mere associations and for identifying when causal effects can be estimated from observational data. How does this framework relate to Woodward's interventionism? Is the do-calculus a formalization of interventionism, or does it embody independent commitments about the nature of causation?

8. If token causation is what primarily matters morally and legally — courts ask whether this drug caused this patient's harm — but science primarily establishes type causation, there is a persistent mismatch between what science can deliver and what justice requires. Does this suggest that our legal practices rest on a philosophical mistake, or that token causation is a genuine and scientifically approachable concept that requires methods beyond population-level statistics?
