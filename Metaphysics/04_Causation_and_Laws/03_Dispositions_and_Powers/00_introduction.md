# Dispositions and Powers

Glass is fragile: it will break if struck with sufficient force. Sugar is soluble: it will dissolve if placed in water. A match is flammable: it will ignite if struck in the presence of oxygen. These are dispositional properties — properties that characterize what a thing is disposed to do under certain conditions, even if those conditions never obtain. The glass may never be struck; it is still fragile. The sugar may never be placed in water; it is still soluble.

Dispositions have played a central role in philosophy of science and metaphysics for decades, but their status has become especially contentious in the debate between Humean and anti-Humean ontologies. For the Humean, the world consists entirely of categorical, non-dispositional properties at the fundamental level; dispositions are real but grounded in categorical properties together with the laws that govern them. A glass's fragility is not a further feature of the glass beyond its categorical structure; it is that structure in combination with the laws that determine what happens to glasses when struck. For the anti-Humean powers theorist, dispositions are fundamental and irreducible: to have a power just is to have a genuine causal potentiality, and the laws of nature follow from these powers rather than the other way around.

The debate has consequences throughout metaphysics. If powers are fundamental, then causation is grounded in the natures of things: causes produce effects because they have the relevant intrinsic causal powers, not because of some external law that connects them. If powers are fundamental, then laws of nature are not contingent regularities but necessary consequences of what things are. If powers are fundamental, then the world has an internal causal structure that makes it more than the "intrinsic, local, non-modal, categorical" mosaic that Humeans describe.

This chapter develops the metaphysics of dispositions and powers, examining what it means to have a disposition, how dispositions are individuated, and what their relationship to categorical properties, laws, and causation is. The reader will find that the questions raised here connect to virtually every other topic in this unit.
