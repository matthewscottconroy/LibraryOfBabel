# Defining Dispositions

This glass is fragile. We say this without having dropped it, without having struck it, without having done anything to test it. We are not describing what has happened to the glass but what would happen if the right conditions obtained. A fragile thing is one that would break under the right kind of stress — and it has this property now, even if it is never stressed, even if it survives its entire existence wrapped in cotton and stored in a cupboard. This is the peculiar character of dispositions: they are properties that are constitutively connected to what would happen under conditions that may never obtain. Understanding what this amounts to — whether it is a genuine feature of reality or a projection of our conditional thinking onto an unconditional world — is one of the central questions in the metaphysics of properties.

## Dispositions and Their Categorical Counterparts

The contrast is between dispositional and categorical properties. A categorical property is one that is "manifested" simply by being instantiated — it does not point toward any hypothetical behavior. The shape of an object is the standard example: being square is just having a certain geometric form, independently of any conditional behavior. Dispositional properties are different: they are constitutively connected to what would happen if certain conditions obtained. Fragility is not a geometric or mass property; it is the property of being such that striking would cause breaking. The "would cause" is essential to what the property is.

The philosophical question is whether this distinction is a distinction between two fundamental types of property or merely a distinction in how we describe properties that are all, at bottom, the same kind of thing. The three main positions are worth distinguishing. **Pure categoricalism** (Hume, Lewis, Armstrong in some moods) holds that all fundamental properties are categorical — they have no intrinsic dispositional character. Dispositions are derivative: a thing has the disposition to behave B in conditions C iff it has some categorical property (the categorical basis of the disposition) and there is a law connecting that property to behavior B in conditions C. On this view, a glass is fragile because it has a certain molecular structure plus there is a law that objects with this structure break when struck. Dispositions are redescriptions of categorical facts plus laws.

**Pure dispositionalism** (Molnar, Mumford, Bird in some respects) holds the opposite: all fundamental properties are dispositional — they constitutively point toward their manifestations. What seems categorical, including shape and mass, is actually dispositional at the deepest level. **The dual-aspect view** (Martin, Heil) takes a middle path: properties have both a categorical and a dispositional aspect — two sides of the same coin. The categorical aspect is the property qua intrinsic qualitative feature; the dispositional aspect is the property qua tendency toward certain manifestations. These are not two properties but two ways of regarding the same property.

## The Connection to Conditionals

The connection between dispositions and conditionals is central to the analysis. To have a disposition D to manifest M under stimulus S is — approximately — for it to be the case that: if S were to obtain, M would result. In the SCA notation, using the counterfactual conditional □→: (x were in S) □→ (x would M). This conditional structure is what makes dispositions modal: they are not simply about what is the case but about what would be the case. The possibility of possessing a disposition without ever manifesting it — a fragile glass never struck — shows that dispositions go beyond actual categorical facts.

But the conditional structure creates a puzzle immediately. We say something has a disposition even when the triggering condition does not obtain. What grounds the truth of the conditional about the unstruck glass? If there is nothing in the world beyond actual categorical facts, the conditional is grounded in categorical properties plus laws — which is the pure categoricalist's answer. If there is something more — a genuine dispositional property that makes the conditional true — then dispositions are irreducible features of reality.

## Finks and Masks

The simple conditional analysis faces two kinds of counterexample that push against any straightforward reduction. **Finkish dispositions** (C.B. Martin, 1994): suppose a sorcerer enchants a glass such that the moment anyone tries to break it, it becomes magically strong. The glass has the intrinsic property of fragility — its molecular structure is that of fragile glass — but the conditional "if struck, would break" is false, because the sorcerer would intervene. The disposition is real; the conditional is false. **Masked dispositions** are different: a sugar cube in a saturated solution is soluble but fails to dissolve. The disposition is present, the trigger condition obtains, but the manifestation is blocked by an external interfering factor.

These cases reveal a gap between having a disposition and the corresponding conditional being true. Any adequate analysis of dispositions must thread between two dangers: if it is too strict and equates dispositions with the truth of their corresponding conditionals, finks and masks show that objects can have dispositions while the conditionals are false. If it is too loose and merely identifies dispositions with some bare intrinsic property without any connection to conditionals, the essential connection between dispositions and what would happen is lost. The refined analyses — Lewis's intrinsic basis account, Bird's stimulus-inclusive analysis — attempt to navigate this gap, and their partial success is itself philosophically instructive: it suggests that dispositions cannot be fully analyzed in categorical terms plus conditionals, which supports the view that dispositions are ontologically genuine and not fully reducible.
