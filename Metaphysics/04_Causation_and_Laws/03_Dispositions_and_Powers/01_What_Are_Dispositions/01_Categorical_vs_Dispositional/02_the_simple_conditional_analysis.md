# The Simple Conditional Analysis of Dispositions

The simplest account of what it is to have a disposition is also the most natural: to be fragile is to be the kind of thing that would break when struck; to be soluble is to be the kind of thing that would dissolve when immersed in water; to be magnetic is to be the kind of thing that would attract iron. In each case, having the disposition is equivalent to the truth of a corresponding conditional. This is the Simple Conditional Analysis, and its attractions are real. Its failures, once identified, are equally illuminating.

## The Analysis and Its Virtues

**SCA**: x has disposition D (to manifest M in response to stimulus S) iff: (x were in S) □→ (x would M).

The □→ is the subjunctive conditional: the "would" counterfactual. Fragility is the property x has iff if x were struck with sufficient force, x would break. Solubility is the property x has iff if x were immersed in water, x would dissolve. The analysis is bidirectional: having the disposition implies the conditional, and satisfying the conditional is sufficient for having the disposition.

The virtues are genuine. The analysis is reductive: it analyzes dispositional claims — which seem to involve modal or counterfactual content — in terms of counterfactuals, which can themselves be given a possible-worlds analysis. Dispositions are not mysterious unmanifested properties but are fully captured by their potential manifestations. The analysis connects dispositions to testability: to say something is fragile is to make a prediction about what would happen if it were struck. And Gilbert Ryle used conditional analyses in *The Concept of Mind* (1949) to deflate the dualist's picture of mental states: to say someone is intelligent is not to attribute a ghostly inner state but to say they would solve problems, learn quickly, understand explanations — a cluster of dispositions analyzable as conditionals. This was a powerful deflationary move.

## Martin's Finkish Dispositions

C.B. Martin's 1994 paper introduced finkish dispositions, which refuted the simple analysis in both directions.

**Against the sufficiency of disposition for the conditional**: A glass has the disposition of fragility — its molecular structure is that of fragile glass. But a sorcerer has enchanted it: the moment anyone tries to break it, the sorcerer makes it strong. The disposition is present; the conditional is false. The SCA says fragility just is the truth of the conditional, so if the conditional is false, fragility is absent. But fragility is present. The SCA is wrong.

**Against the sufficiency of the conditional for the disposition**: A non-fragile glass would break if struck — because a sorcerer would make it fragile the moment anyone struck it. The conditional is true; the disposition is absent. The SCA says the truth of the conditional suffices for the disposition. But the disposition is absent. The SCA is wrong again.

Formally: in the first case, x has fragility but ¬[(x were struck) □→ (x would break)]. In the second, ¬(x has fragility) but (x were struck) □→ (x would break). Both directions of the biconditional fail.

## Masked Dispositions

Masked dispositions form a different class of counterexample. A sugar cube is immersed in water that is already saturated with sugar. The cube has the disposition of solubility — it would dissolve in unsaturated water — the trigger condition obtains, it is in water, but the manifestation is absent because the water is already saturated. The conditional "if immersed in water, would dissolve" is false in this context, but the disposition is genuinely present.

In finkish cases, the disposition disappears when the trigger is applied; in masked cases, the disposition is present and active but its manifestation is blocked by an external interfering factor. The distinction matters for understanding what dispositions are: finks work by changing the object's dispositional profile; masks work by counteracting an unchanged dispositional profile.

## Responses and Their Limits

Various modifications have been proposed. Lewis's intrinsic basis account (discussed separately) invokes the intrinsic categorical property that grounds the conditional: x has D iff there is an intrinsic property I of x such that, if x were in S and had I, x would M. This handles finks — when the sorcerer intervenes, the intrinsic structure of the glass is unchanged, so the glass remains fragile by Lewis's analysis — but not masks, where the intrinsic structure is present and unchanged while external factors block manifestation.

The ceteris paribus conditional adds: x has D iff if x were in S, and other things were equal, x would M. But "no interfering factors" is defined as whatever does not prevent M, making the clause trivially true whenever M fails due to interference — and therefore vacuous.

Precise specification of conditions — listing all specific types of interfering factors — avoids vacuousness but requires an enumeration that seems impossibly long and perhaps infinite. The lesson is that any analysis that genuinely handles finks and masks without circularity has not yet been produced. This is itself philosophically significant: it suggests that the connection between dispositions and conditionals is not one of identity but of something more complex — a grounding relation mediated by intrinsic properties, laws, or causal structure. The failure of the SCA has pushed the field toward either a categorical basis account (Lewis), an intrinsic powers ontology (Martin, Molnar), or a refined modal analysis that acknowledges the irreducibility of dispositional or causal concepts.
