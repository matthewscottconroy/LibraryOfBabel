# Refined Analyses of Dispositions

The simple conditional analysis fails because finks and masks drive a wedge between having a disposition and satisfying the corresponding counterfactual conditional. A glass can be fragile while "if struck, it would break" is false (when a sorcerer intervenes); a sugar cube can be soluble while "if immersed in water, it would dissolve" is false (when the solution is saturated). This does not mean dispositions cannot be analyzed — it means the simple analysis was the wrong one. The refined analyses that followed tried to preserve the conditional structure while handling the counterexamples, and their progressive failure illuminates something important about the nature of dispositions.

## Lewis's Intrinsic Basis Account

David Lewis proposed replacing the simple conditional with a conditional that quantifies over the object's intrinsic properties:

**Lewis's Modified SCA**: x has disposition D to M in response to S iff there is an intrinsic property I of x such that: if x were in S and retained I, x would M.

The key move is invoking an intrinsic property I — the categorical basis of the disposition — rather than letting the conditional range over all possible circumstances. Fragility is not the conditional itself but the intrinsic property (molecular structure, crystal lattice arrangement) that grounds the conditional. When the sorcerer intervenes, the sorcerer changes something external to the glass. The glass's intrinsic structure is unchanged. So Lewis's analysis correctly judges the glass fragile: it has an intrinsic property I such that if struck while retaining I, it would break. The fink case is handled.

Masking is not handled. In masking cases, the intrinsic property I is present and unchanged; the trigger condition obtains; but an external factor blocks manifestation. Lewis's analysis asks: is there an intrinsic I such that, if struck with I, the glass would break? In a masked case, the answer is yes — the intrinsic structure is that of a fragile glass. But the conditional "(struck & retains I) □→ would break" may still be false if the mask is severe enough. The analysis does not capture the difference between a situation where the glass is fragile but masked and one where the glass is genuinely not fragile.

## Bird's Stimulus-Inclusive Analysis

Alexander Bird proposed a different refinement:

**Bird's Analysis**: x has disposition D to M in response to S iff x's intrinsic state grounds a causal process that, in the absence of interfering conditions, would yield M when S obtains.

The "absence of interfering conditions" clause is designed to handle masking: the sugar cube would dissolve if immersed in water absent the interference of saturation. The disposition is present even when masked, because in the absence of the mask, the manifestation would occur. This gets the right result in the cases: the fragile glass has the disposition because its intrinsic causal structure would yield breaking in the absence of sorcerous intervention or protective packaging.

But the analysis is not fully reductive. "Interfering conditions" means conditions that prevent the manifestation — and this is a causal concept. The analysis uses causal and dispositional concepts to characterize dispositions, which is circular in the relevant sense: we are presupposing causal and dispositional notions in the analysis of what it is to be a disposition. Bird accepts this limitation explicitly: the analysis clarifies the structure of dispositional claims but does not eliminate dispositional or causal concepts.

## Choi and Manley-Wasserman

Sungho Choi proposed that the conditional should hold "in normal circumstances": x has D iff in normal circumstances, if x were in S, x would M. But "normal" is not neutrally specifiable — what counts as normal for a glass differs in an ordinary kitchen and in a sorcerer's laboratory. Normal circumstances are those in which there are no finks and no masks; but this makes the account circular when offered as an analysis of dispositions.

Manley and Wasserman took a different approach. Rather than seeking necessary and sufficient conditions for having a disposition, they proposed that dispositions come in degrees proportional to the proportion of selected circumstances in which the manifestation occurs. A salt crystal is highly soluble (dissolves in nearly all aqueous circumstances); a noble metal is barely corrosive. The analysis handles finks and masks differently: a sorcerer's intervention reduces the proportion of striking circumstances in which the glass breaks, so the glass's fragility is diminished or absent in a sorcerer-populated world. This yields the counterintuitive result that the glass is not fragile if there is a reliable sorcerer about — which seems wrong, since fragility should be an intrinsic property of the glass, not a function of what sorcerers are doing in its environment. But the degree-based approach has independent virtues for capturing the gradience of dispositional ascriptions.

## The Dispositionalist Conclusion

Martin, Mumford, and other dispositionalists draw a principled conclusion from the progressive failure of these analyses. Every refined analysis eventually invokes dispositional or causal concepts — grounding, causal process, interfering conditions, normal circumstances — and none succeeds in giving a fully non-circular account.

The argument:

- **P1**: All refined analyses of dispositions invoke dispositional or causal concepts at some point.
- **P2**: If every analysis of X requires concepts of the same kind as X, then X is not analyzable in terms of other kinds of concepts.
- **C**: Dispositions are primitive — real, irreducible features of the world that cannot be fully analyzed in categorical terms plus laws.

This is the view of Martin (*The Mind in Nature*, 2008), Molnar (*Powers*, 2003), and in important respects Mumford (*Dispositions*, 1998). Dispositions are real, intrinsic features of things — as real as categorical properties — constitutively directed toward their manifestations. The systematic failure to analyze them away is not a philosophical scandal but a metaphysical discovery: the world contains irreducible dispositionality, and our attempts to paraphrase it away will always leave a residue.
