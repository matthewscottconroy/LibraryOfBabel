# Real Powers and the Ontology of Dispositions

The Humean picture of properties is of bare categorical natures — shapes, masses, charges — which have no intrinsic connection to any behavior. Laws of nature are the additional facts that connect these neutral properties to their effects. Charge would not repel like charges if the laws were different; it just happens to repel them, given the laws we have. Against this picture stands the powers tradition, which holds that properties like charge, mass, fragility, and solubility are not neutrally categorical but constitutively connected to their effects. The power to repel is not an external law imposed on charge; it is what charge is. Understanding the case for real powers, and the main objections to it, is essential for understanding the contemporary debate about properties and laws.

## The Aristotelian Background and Its Revival

The Aristotelian roots are transparent. Aristotle distinguished what a thing is in act from what it is capable of becoming or doing. Powers are potentialities — actual features of things that point toward possible states. The fire has the actual power to heat; the wax has the actual power to be melted. These are not regularities of behavior but intrinsic features of the objects. The mechanistic philosophy of the seventeenth century displaced this picture. Descartes, Boyle, and Gassendi preferred explanations in terms of the shapes, sizes, and motions of particles — purely categorical properties. Locke retained talk of "powers" but grounded them in the primary qualities of matter. Hume completed the displacement by denying that any impression of power is observable in external events: all we observe are regularities.

The contemporary revival of powers ontology is driven by the recognition that the Humean picture generates a different mystery. If fundamental properties are categorical, then laws of nature must be additional facts that connect properties to their effects. But this makes laws mysterious — contingent extra features of the world not grounded in the natures of things. Why should a categorical property P, with no intrinsic connection to any behavior, be connected by a law to behavior B? The law is an unexplained brute fact. Powers ontology avoids this by building causal roles into the properties themselves.

## Shoemaker's Identity Conditions Argument

Sydney Shoemaker's argument from property identity (1980) provides one of the most powerful cases for real powers. The argument begins with a principle about what it is for two properties to be identical: two properties are the same property iff they have the same causal role — the same potential to contribute to causal interactions. If this is right, then properties are individuated by their causal roles, and causal roles are therefore essential to properties. But a property whose causal role is essential is just a power: having the property constitutively involves having the corresponding causal potentials. The argument from identity conditions to dispositionalism is elegant because it does not appeal to intuitions about what "power" means but to a general principle about property individuation.

The consequence for laws is direct: if properties are individuated by their causal roles, then the laws connecting properties to their effects follow necessarily from what the properties are. The laws are not contingent additional facts imposed on neutral properties; they are expressions of the essential natures of the properties. This is the dispositional essentialist view of laws.

## Molnar on Physical Intentionality

George Molnar's *Powers* (2003) defends pure powers ontology and introduces the notion of **physical intentionality** to characterize the distinctive feature of powers. Intentionality in the mental sphere is the directedness of beliefs toward their contents: a belief that it is raining is about the state of the world's being rainy, and this directedness persists even if the belief is false. Molnar argues that powers have an analogous directedness toward their manifestations. The power of charge to repel like charges is directed toward repulsion even when no repulsion occurs — no like-charged particle is nearby. The power is real and genuinely directed; its directedness is just unrealized.

The analogy is imperfect but illuminating. Powers share with mental intentionality four features: directedness toward an object or state, independence from the existence of the manifestation (a power can point toward a manifestation that never occurs), independence from actuality (the manifestation need not occur for the power to exist), and intrinsicality (the directedness is a feature of the power itself, not derived from its relations to other things). The differences are equally important: mental intentionality has semantic content, can be satisfied or frustrated, and is normatively constrained. Physical intentionality lacks these features — powers are not true or false; they simply exist and either manifest or do not.

## The Main Objection

The main objection to real powers is that properties that "point toward" possible manifestations seem metaphysically mysterious — primitive intentionality built into the physical world. The objection formulates: if charge has the intrinsic power to repel like charges, this power "points toward" repulsion even when no repulsion occurs. What is this "pointing toward"? It is not a spatial or temporal relation; it is not an extensional physical relation. It seems to require something like an intentional relation — which looks suspiciously mentalistic.

Molnar's reply: the "spookiness" reflects genuine metaphysical complexity, not an explanatory failure. The alternative — categorical properties with no intrinsic connection to their causal roles — is equally mysterious. How do categorical properties become causally active? Positing external laws to connect them just relocates the mystery: why do those laws hold? Powers ground causal action from within the property itself, which is at least no more mysterious and arguably more illuminating. Physical intentionality need not be assimilated to mental intentionality; it is a simpler, more basic form of directedness that is primitive in the ontology of physical reality.
