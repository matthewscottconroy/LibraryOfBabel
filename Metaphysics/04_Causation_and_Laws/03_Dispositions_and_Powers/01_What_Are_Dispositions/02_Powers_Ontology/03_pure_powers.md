# Pure Powers and the Regress Problem

The pure powers thesis is the most radical form of dispositionalism. Where mixed views allow that properties have both categorical and dispositional aspects, pure powers ontology holds that all fundamental properties are purely dispositional — they have no categorical nature whatsoever, no quidditative character independent of their causal roles. They are individuated entirely by what they do. The thesis is suggested by fundamental physics, where properties like charge, mass, and spin appear to be defined entirely by their roles in physical law, with no additional intrinsic character. But pure powers face a distinctive and serious objection — the "always packing, never arriving" regress — and the responses to it reveal the deepest issues in the ontology of powers.

## The Pure Powers Thesis

**PPT**: All fundamental natural properties are pure powers — individuated entirely by their dispositional profiles, with no additional categorical or quidditative nature.

The fundamental properties of physics are the natural test case. Charge is defined by its role in electromagnetic interactions; mass by its role in F = ma and gravitational attraction; spin by its role in determining how a particle transforms under rotation. There seems to be no "categorical extra" to charge beyond its electromagnetic role. The property just is its causal-dispositional profile. This is what some call structural realism applied to properties: the world's fundamental structure is constituted by powers and their mutual manifestations, not by bare quiddities that contingently happen to have causal roles.

The contrast with mixed views matters. Martin and Heil hold that properties have both a categorical and a dispositional aspect — two sides of the same coin. The pure powers theorist denies the categorical side: there is no non-dispositional intrinsic nature, only the dispositional profile all the way down.

## The Regress Objection

The regress objection, stated clearly by C.B. Martin, runs as follows:

- **P1**: Pure powers are individuated by their manifestations — by what they point toward.
- **P2**: If all fundamental properties are pure powers, then the manifestations of any given power are themselves powers.
- **P3**: Therefore, the manifestations of any given power are in turn individuated by their manifestations, which are further powers, individuated by further manifestations, and so on without end.
- **C**: The chain of powers never arrives at any categorical reality that could ground the whole structure.

The metaphor: packing a suitcase for a journey you never take, where the contents are other suitcases, each containing further suitcases. Always packing, never arriving at any actual content. If there is no categorical ground, the powers have no real nature — they are place-holders pointing to other place-holders in an endless chain. The world would be all potentiality and no actuality, a web of powers with nothing categorical for them to be powers of.

## Responses

The first response denies P2. Not all manifestations of powers are themselves powers. When the power to break manifests, the manifestation is the breaking — an actual event of structural integrity being lost. This event is not a further power; it is an actuality. The chain terminates in actual events, not in further powers. This response is plausible if we allow that events are ontologically distinct from properties. Powers manifest in events; events are not powers. The regress runs through properties; it terminates at the level of actual occurrences.

Bird's mutual individuation response takes a different route. Powers can individuate each other in a self-supporting web, without requiring a categorical terminus and without generating an infinite regress. Consider a minimal two-power system: P₁ is individuated by its manifestation M₁ (which is P₂ being exercised), and P₂ is individuated by its manifestation M₂ (which is P₁ being exercised). The two powers individuate each other without either being grounded in a categorical property. The web is self-supporting: given P₁ and P₂, each is what it is in virtue of its relation to the other.

Whether this is viciously circular depends on whether mutual individuation is coherent. Critics argue that if P₁'s nature is constituted by its relation to P₂, and P₂'s nature is constituted by its relation to P₁, then neither has a determinate nature independently of the other — and there is nothing to fix which powers the system contains. Defenders argue that mutual individuation is no more problematic than the mutual individuation found in structural mathematics, where objects are defined by their roles in a structure rather than by intrinsic features.

The third response, associated with Mumford, rejects the ground-seeking demand itself. The assumption behind the regress objection is that powers are real only in virtue of being grounded in something non-dispositional. But this is precisely the Humean assumption the powers ontologist is rejecting. Why should powers require categorical grounding? The assumption is that categorical properties are ontologically primary and dispositions derivative. But this assumption is question-begging in the context of a debate about whether powers are ontologically primitive. Powers are real as powers — their reality does not require categorical grounding any more than categorical properties require dispositional grounding.

## The Mixed View

An influential alternative to both pure categoricalism and pure dispositionalism is the dual-aspect view (Martin, Heil). Every property has both a categorical and a dispositional aspect — not two properties but two ways of regarding the same property. The shape of a glass is categorically the arrangement of its material parts and dispositionally the power to fit into containers of that shape, cast shadows of certain dimensions, and break along certain lines when struck. The regress is avoided: there is always a categorical aspect to serve as the terminus. The challenge for this view is explaining how the two aspects relate — whether they are genuinely distinct or merely two descriptions of one thing. Martin's answer is that they are "reciprocal partners": neither prior to the other, together constituting the full nature of the property.

The choice between pure powers, the mixed view, and pure categoricalism is not merely a metaphysical choice but connects to the interpretation of fundamental physics. If quantum numbers constitute what fundamental particles are — if charge is its electromagnetic role and nothing more — then the pure powers view captures the ontology of physics. If there is some additional quidditative nature beyond the roles, the mixed view or categoricalism becomes attractive. Physics itself has not settled this question, and perhaps cannot: the question of whether quantum numbers are pure roles or are grounded in additional intrinsic natures may be one that physical theory underdetermines.
