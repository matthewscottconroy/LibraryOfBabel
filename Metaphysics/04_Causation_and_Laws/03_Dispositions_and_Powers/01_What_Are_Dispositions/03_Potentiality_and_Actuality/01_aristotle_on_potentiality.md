# Aristotle on Potentiality and Actuality

Contemporary debates about dispositions and powers did not arise in a vacuum. The fundamental distinction they depend on — between a thing's actual properties and its capacities for change and action — was worked out in detail by Aristotle, and the framework he developed remains the conceptual backdrop for the contemporary discussion. Understanding Aristotle's potentiality/actuality distinction illuminates both what is genuinely at stake in modern debates and why the Humean elimination of powers has always had a philosophically dissatisfied opposition.

## The Basic Distinction

For Aristotle, a dunamis — a potentiality or power — is a principle of change or of being changed. The distinction operates in two related domains. **Active potentialities** are capacities to bring about change in other things: the fire has the active potential to heat; the teacher has the active potential to teach. They are directed outward, toward effects they can produce in appropriate objects. **Passive potentialities** are capacities to be changed by something else: the wax has the passive potential to be shaped or melted; the student has the passive potential to learn. Passive potentialities are capacities for receiving the action of active potentialities.

The actualization of a potentiality is its exercise — the fire's actually heating the wax, the teacher's actually teaching the student. Aristotle calls this energeia: the realized state toward which the potential was directed. What is philosophically important is that the potential is real before its actualization. The wax's potential to be melted is a genuine feature of the wax now, not a description of what the wax does when heat is applied. The fire has the power to heat even when nothing is being heated. This is the claim that the contemporary powers tradition inherits and defends.

## Why Potentiality Matters

Aristotle developed the potentiality/actuality framework to solve several classical problems. The Parmenidean challenge to change: how can something come to be what it was not? Parmenides had argued that change is impossible — what is cannot become what it is not, and what is not cannot come to be. Aristotle's answer: the thing that changes has the *potential* to be what it becomes. Change is the actualization of a potential, not creation from nothing. The clay that becomes a pot had the potential to be a pot; the transition is from potentiality to actuality, not from nothing to something.

The framework also dissolves the problem of non-being: the future statue does not exist yet, but we can meaningfully discuss it because it exists as a potentiality — a genuine feature of the world (the marble with its potential to be shaped) even before it is actual. And Aristotle defined motion itself as "the actuality of the potential as potential" — the process of a potential's being actualized while it is still in process. The moving arrow is in the actuality of its potential to be at the target, but this actuality is incomplete; the arrow is potentially-at-target but not yet actually there.

The four causes connect to the framework directly. The **material cause** (what a thing is made of) is its potential: the bronze is the material cause of the statue because it is the potentiality for the statue's form. The **formal cause** (what a thing is) is the actuality of the matter. The **efficient cause** (the sculptor) brings about the actualization. The **final cause** (the completed statue) is the telos toward which the actualization is directed. Causation, for Aristotle, is the process of actualization: efficient causes bring about the actualization of passive potentials in material things.

## Contemporary Relevance

Several aspects of Aristotle's framework are directly relevant to contemporary debates. Aristotle's insight that potentials are constitutively directed toward their actualizations — a potential is always a potential for something — anticipates Molnar's physical intentionality. Powers are not bare undirected features of things; they are pointed toward their manifestations. The fragility of the glass points toward its breaking; this directedness is part of what the power is.

Aristotle's distinction between active and passive powers connects to Mumford's mutual manifestation account of causation: both the active power of the cause and the passive receptivity of the effect must be co-present for causation to occur. The striking blow (active power) and the fragility of the glass (passive power) must combine to produce the breaking. Neither alone suffices.

Aristotle also held that actuality is in some sense prior to potentiality — the actual is what the potential is a potential for; the potential is defined in terms of the actual. This connects to the contemporary debate about the regress of powers: if pure powers are individuated by their manifestations, and the manifestations are themselves powers, the chain of definition must terminate somewhere in actuality. Aristotle's priority of actuality suggests that the chain cannot be entirely dispositional; there must be actual states that ground the dispositional structure. Whether this supports the mixed view (categorical and dispositional aspects together) or only shows that events terminate the regress is a question the contemporary debate has not fully resolved.
