# Powers and Modality: What Is Possible?

If objects have genuine powers — intrinsic capacities to behave in certain ways — this has consequences not just for how we explain regularities but for what we take to be possible. The Lewisian account of modality grounds possibility in the existence of concrete possible worlds: any logically consistent description describes a genuinely possible world, and these worlds are as real as the actual world. The powers-based approach to modality is more constrained and more internal to the actual world: what is possible is determined by the actual powers of actual entities and their possible combinations. The contrast is sharp, and it reveals what is philosophically at stake in accepting a powers ontology.

## The Powers-Based Account

On a powers-based account, **possibility is grounded in powers**: P is possible if there is some possible configuration of powers that would produce P. The impossible is what no combination of powers could produce. The contrast with Lewis matters: for Lewis, the space of possible worlds is vast, including worlds where the laws of nature are entirely different. For the powers theorist, the space of genuine possibilities is more constrained — it is determined by the actual powers of actual entities and the ways those powers can combine. There are no possible worlds where charge — real charge, with its actual essential nature — attracts like charges, because charge's repulsive disposition is part of what charge is. A world where "charge attracts" either contains a different property with a different name, or it is genuinely impossible.

The argument:

- **P1**: What is possible is determined by what could happen given the causal structure of the world.
- **P2**: The causal structure of the world is constituted by the powers of the entities in it.
- **P3**: Therefore, what is possible is determined by the powers that exist and their possible combinations.

This gives possibility a grounding in the actual world rather than in an abstract space of separately existing worlds.

## Mumford and Anjum on Powers and Causation

Ruth Mumford (Anjum) and Stephan Mumford develop a distinctive powers-based account of possibility in *Getting Causes from Powers* (2011). Their key thesis is that causes tend toward their effects without necessitating them. A power is like a vector: it has a magnitude (how strong the tendency) and a direction (what it tends toward). Multiple powers acting on a system combine like vectors: they may reinforce each other, partially cancel, or completely cancel. The manifestation is the resultant of all the powers at work.

This gives a picture of causation that avoids both strict determinism — every cause necessitates its effect — and mere regularity — effects follow causes without any productive connection. Causes tend toward their effects; they make the effects more likely and contribute to producing them, but other powers may partially cancel the tendency. Possibility on this view: P is possible if there is some combination of powers whose resultant tends toward P — if P is in the "attractor space" of some combination of powers. Impossibility is not failure of logical consistency but absence of any power combination that tends toward P.

## Bird's Constraint: Given Powers, Laws Are Necessary

For Bird's dispositional essentialism, powers constrain not just possibility but necessity. Given the existence of a property with its essential dispositional nature, the laws governing that property hold necessarily. What is nomically possible is determined by the actual properties and their essential natures. There are no nomically possible worlds where the actual properties violate their actual laws — such worlds are either worlds with different properties or genuinely impossible.

The space of nomically possible worlds is the space of worlds with the actual properties arranged differently — same laws, different distribution. This is a significant restriction of modal space compared to Lewis's version, and it has consequences for how we think about scientific necessity. If the laws are necessary given the properties, then scientific discovery is a form of modal discovery: learning what the laws are is learning what is nomically impossible.

## Actualism and the Problem of Alien Possibilities

The powers-based approach to modality is naturally aligned with actualism: the possibilities grounded in powers are powers that actually exist. Possible worlds are not separately existing concreta but are accessible from the actual world via the exercise of actual powers. This gives an actualist modal ontology grounded in the real dispositional character of actual things.

The difficulty is with alien possibilities — properties that exist in other possible worlds but not in the actual world, with no intrinsic connection to actual properties. Lewis's modal system requires alien properties: without them, the space of possible worlds would be restricted to rearrangements of actual properties. Powers-based modality struggles with alien properties: if possibility is grounded in actual powers, then properties with no actual powers in their essential natures are not genuinely possible. The space of possibilities is limited to rearrangements of actual properties and combinations of their powers.

Whether this is a feature or a bug depends on one's modal commitments. The powers theorist takes it as a feature: the actual world's powers determine what is genuinely possible, and there is nothing more to possibility than that. The Lewisian takes it as a cost: denying alien possibilities seems to impoverish the modal space unnecessarily and may threaten the Kripkean intuition that different fundamental properties are genuinely conceivable — and therefore genuinely possible. The debate here connects to the deeper question of whether conceivability is a reliable guide to metaphysical possibility, which neither side has fully resolved.
