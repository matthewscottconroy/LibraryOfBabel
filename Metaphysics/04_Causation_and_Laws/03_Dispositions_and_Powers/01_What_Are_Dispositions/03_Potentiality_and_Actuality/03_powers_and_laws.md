# Powers and Laws: Eliminating Laws?

If objects have genuine intrinsic causal powers, and if these powers explain the regularities we call "laws of nature," then a radical possibility presents itself: perhaps laws of nature are not additional features of reality at all. Perhaps they are merely descriptions of what powers do — convenient summaries of causal behavior that add no new ontological commitment over and above the powers themselves. This is the eliminativist position developed by Stephen Mumford in *Laws in Nature* (2004), and it is one of the most provocative conclusions in contemporary metaphysics of science.

## Mumford's Eliminativist Argument

The argument is structurally simple:

- **P1**: The regularities we observe — that copper conducts, that like charges repel, that masses attract — are explained by the powers of things. Copper's power to conduct explains the regularity; charge's power to repel like charges explains the pattern of repulsion.
- **P2**: If the regularities are explained by powers, then positing laws as additional features of reality that explain the same regularities adds nothing explanatory.
- **P3**: By Ockham's razor, we should not multiply entities beyond necessity.
- **C**: Laws of nature are ontologically redundant — they can be eliminated from our ontology without loss of explanatory power.

Each function we normally assign to laws, Mumford argues, can be discharged by powers. Laws support counterfactuals — but so do powers: "if this were copper, it would conduct" is grounded in copper's intrinsic power to conduct, not in a law imposed on copper from outside. Laws explain behavior — but powers explain more directly: this copper conducts because copper has the power to conduct, which is part of what copper is. Laws license prediction — but again, powers do the work: we predict conductance because we know the power is present. Laws unify diverse phenomena — but the uniformity of copper conductance across instances is explained by the uniformity of the power, the same intrinsic property present in all copper.

## Objections and Replies

The quantitative specification problem is the strongest objection. The power of charge to repel like charges includes a specific mathematical profile: Coulomb's law, F = k_e × q₁q₂/r². A bare power "to repel" without this specific quantitative form seems underspecified. Where does the exact inverse-square dependence come from, if not from a law? Mumford's reply: the quantitative profile is encoded in the power itself. The power of charge is constitutively the power to repel with force proportional to the product of charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance. The mathematical structure is part of the essential nature of the power; it is not an external law imposed on a bare tendency to repel.

The uniformity problem asks: even if each instance of charge has the same power, what guarantees that all instances produce the same behavior? Why should the power of charge in this electron be the same as the power of charge in that one? The reply invokes the universality of the property type: all tokens of the charge property share the same essential nature — they are tokens of the same universal — and the universal nature ensures uniformity across instances without any additional law.

The third objection — that powers presuppose laws — claims that specifying what a power does already invokes a law, so we have not really eliminated laws. Mumford's response: describing what the power does and stating a law that governs the property are two descriptions of the same fact, not two separate facts. "The power operates according to Coulomb's law" is just a way of saying what the power is. "Coulomb's law governs charge" is just a way of saying what charge's power is. There is no ontological gap between the two descriptions.

## Carroll's Defense of Irreducible Laws

John Carroll, in *Laws of Nature* (1994), defends laws as irreducible features of the world. His argument against the eliminativist: even granting that powers exist, something must ground the specific quantitative profile of each power. The power of charge to repel "with force proportional to the product of charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance" has this specific form. If we ask why the profile is inverse-square rather than inverse-cube, the answer cannot simply be "because that is what the power is" — we want to know what makes the power the way it is. Carroll's suggestion, aligned with Maudlin, is that laws are primitive: they constrain what powers do by governing the quantitative profile of their operation.

This dispute is ultimately about explanatory structure. Mumford holds that powers are explanatorily prior; laws are descriptions of powers. Carroll and Maudlin hold that laws are explanatorily prior; powers are manifestations of laws. Both sides agree that something fundamental is at stake; they disagree about which direction the explanation runs.

## The Intermediate Position

Between Mumford's eliminativism and Carroll's primitivism there is a middle ground: powers are ontologically primary, and laws are real but derivative on powers. Laws are true descriptions of how powers behave; their truth is grounded in the powers, not in primitive law-entities or necessitation relations between universals. This preserves the explanatory role of laws in scientific explanation — scientists are not wrong to invoke laws — while locating their ontological ground in the powers of things.

This intermediate position is arguably the most defensible. It respects both the phenomenology of scientific explanation, which regularly invokes laws, and the metaphysical insight of the powers tradition, which holds that laws cannot simply float free of the intrinsic natures of the entities they govern. Laws are real but not primitive; they are the way the world's powers express themselves when we describe those powers in the most general and systematic way. Whether this is genuinely different from Mumford's eliminativism — whether derivative laws are real laws or merely useful descriptions — is a residual question, but it may be one where the difference is more verbal than substantive.
