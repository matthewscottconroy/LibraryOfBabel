# Review Questions — Dispositions and Powers

1. The simple conditional analysis (SCA) holds that having a disposition just is satisfying the corresponding subjunctive conditional. Martin's finkish dispositions refute the SCA in both directions: a fragile glass can fail the conditional (if the sorcerer intervenes), and a non-fragile glass can satisfy it (if the sorcerer would make it fragile when struck). Why does the SCA fail symmetrically in both directions, and what does this tell us about the relationship between dispositions and counterfactuals?

2. Lewis's modified analysis handles finks by invoking an intrinsic categorical basis: x is disposed to M iff x has an intrinsic property I such that, if in the stimulus and retaining I, x would M. But masked dispositions — where the trigger obtains, the intrinsic basis is present, yet manifestation is blocked externally — defeat Lewis's analysis. Does the persistence of masking cases, even after finks are handled, suggest that the project of analyzing dispositions in categorical-plus-conditional terms is inherently limited?

3. The pure categoricalist holds that all fundamental properties are categorical, with dispositions derivative on categorical bases plus laws. The pure dispositionalist holds the opposite. The dual-aspect view (Martin, Heil) treats these as two ways of regarding the same property. What is at stake in this three-way dispute? Is it a dispute about what the world contains, or a dispute about which conceptual framework best captures something all parties agree exists?

4. Shoemaker's argument from property individuation holds that properties are identical iff they have the same causal role, and therefore that causal roles are essential to properties, making every property a power. Assess this argument. Does it establish dispositionalism, or does it merely show that properties are individuated by their actual causal roles in the actual world — without entailing that those roles are essential rather than contingent?

5. The pure powers thesis faces the regress objection: if every power is individuated by its manifestation, and manifestations are further powers, we get a regress with no categorical terminus. Bird's response is mutual individuation — a self-supporting web of powers. Martin's response is that manifestations are actual events, not further powers. Evaluate both responses. Which, if either, successfully blocks the regress without conceding anything to the categoricalist?

6. Mumford argues that if causal powers are real, laws of nature are eliminable — they are merely descriptions of what powers do. This has a direct implication for the chapter on laws of nature: if powers are ontologically primary, the entire debate between regularity theories and necessitarianism may be misdirected. Is Mumford's eliminativism about laws the natural completion of the powers ontology, or does it overreach? What does Carroll's defense of irreducible laws add that powers cannot supply?

7. Molnar introduces the concept of "physical intentionality" to characterize what powers do: like mental states, powers are directed toward their manifestations, even when no manifestation occurs. How illuminating is this analogy between mental intentionality and physical directedness? Does it explain the distinctive modal character of dispositions, or does it introduce a new mystery — primitive intentionality in the physical world — that is at least as puzzling as what it was meant to clarify?

8. The debate between categorical and dispositional properties has direct implications for the metaphysics of essence and modality. If properties are essentially dispositional (dispositionalism), then the laws governing a property are necessary given its existence — the kind of necessity associated with scientific essentialism. If properties are categorical (Humeanism), different laws connecting the same properties are possible. How should the findings of this chapter reshape how you read the earlier discussions of possible worlds, nomic necessity, and counterfactual analysis?
