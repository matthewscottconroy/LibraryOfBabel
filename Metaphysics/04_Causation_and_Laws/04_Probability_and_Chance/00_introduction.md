# Probability and Chance

When a physicist says that a radioactive atom has a fifty percent probability of decaying in the next hour, she seems to be saying something objective — not just that we don't know whether it will decay, but that there is a genuine, world-level indeterminacy about what will happen. This is objective probability, or chance — probability that is a feature of the world itself rather than a measure of our ignorance. Whether objective probability exists, and if so what its metaphysical nature is, are among the deepest questions in the philosophy of science.

The challenge is sharpest when we contrast objective probability with subjective probability, or credence — the probability that a rational agent assigns to a proposition given her evidence. Subjective probability is well-understood: it is a measure of degree of belief, constrained by coherence requirements (Bayesian probability). But objective probability seems to require more: not just that a rational agent should assign credence one-half to the decay, but that there is some feature of the atom — its objective propensity or chance — that makes credence one-half the correct credence to have. What is that feature?

Frequency interpretations hold that objective probability just is relative frequency — the probability of an outcome is the limit of the relative frequency of that outcome in an infinite sequence of trials. This view is extensional and empirically tractable, but it generates the reference class problem: the same event-type can have different frequencies in different reference classes, and there is no single fact about which class is the right one. Propensity interpretations hold that probability is a disposition of an experimental setup to produce outcomes at certain rates — a genuine feature of the physical situation, not just the observed frequency. Lewis's Principal Principle connects objective chance to rational credence: a rational agent with no inadmissible information should set her credence in an outcome equal to its objective chance.

Quantum mechanics adds a further dimension: if the Born rule gives objective probabilities for measurement outcomes, and the Schrödinger equation is deterministic, where do the probabilities come from? Different interpretations of quantum mechanics — Copenhagen, many-worlds, GRW, pilot wave — give different answers. This chapter develops the major positions on objective probability and examines their interaction with determinism, quantum mechanics, and the epistemology of rational belief.
