# Objective Probability

When a fair coin is tossed, there is a 50% chance that it will land heads. This is not merely a fact about our ignorance — about what we know or don't know. It is a fact about the physical situation itself, prior to the toss. At least, this is what we ordinarily say. But what does it mean for there to be an objective, physical probability — a chance — in the world, as opposed to merely a subjective credence in our minds?

Frequentism is the most straightforward empiricist account: the probability of heads is the long-run relative frequency of heads in a series of tosses of this coin under similar conditions. Probabilities are defined as limits of relative frequencies in infinite sequences. This makes objective probability empirically respectable — we can in principle measure it — but it faces the reference class problem (which sequences count as "similar conditions"?), the problem of single-case probabilities (what is the probability of a unique, unrepeatable event?), and the idealization involved in infinite sequences of actual events.

Propensity theories hold that probabilities are dispositional properties of physical situations — tendencies to produce outcomes with specific frequencies. The coin has a propensity of 0.5 to land heads; this propensity is an intrinsic feature of the coin-toss setup, not defined by any actual sequence of outcomes. Propensity theories handle single-case probabilities naturally: the propensity of this particular toss to land heads is 0.5, whether or not there are many similar tosses.

Lewis's "Principal Principle" articulates the connection between objective chances and rational credences: if one knows that the chance of E is p, then one's rational credence in E should be p (in the absence of other information). This principle constrains the relationship between metaphysics (what objective chances are) and epistemology (what we should believe), but does not settle the question of what chances fundamentally are.
