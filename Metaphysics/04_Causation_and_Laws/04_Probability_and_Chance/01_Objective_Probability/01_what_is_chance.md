# What Is Objective Chance?

A radioactive atom has a fifty percent chance of decaying in the next ten minutes. A fair coin has a fifty percent chance of landing heads. An electron passing through a double slit has a certain probability of being detected at any given location on the screen. These probability attributions are not expressions of ignorance. An omniscient physicist who knew every fact about the current state of the universe would not say "I can now eliminate the uncertainty" — in an indeterministic world, there is no uncertainty to eliminate. The chance is a feature of the world, not a gap in our knowledge. This is the idea of objective chance, and understanding what it amounts to — whether such a thing is genuinely coherent, what underwrites it, and how it connects to rational belief — is one of the central questions in the metaphysics of science.

## The Contrast with Epistemic Probability

The distinction between objective chance and epistemic probability is the organizing distinction for the whole topic. Epistemic probability — or credence — is a rational agent's degree of belief in a proposition, reflecting their epistemic state: what they know and do not know about the world. Objective chance is a feature of the world itself, independent of any observer's knowledge. In a deterministic world, every event is necessitated by prior conditions and laws, and epistemic probability would be all there is: uncertainty about outcomes reflects ignorance of initial conditions, not any objective indeterminacy. In an indeterministic world, objective chance is an additional metaphysical category — a real feature of the world that even perfect knowledge cannot eliminate.

Whether the actual world is deterministic or indeterministic is contested. Standard interpretations of quantum mechanics assign irreducible, non-epistemic probabilities to measurement outcomes, suggesting that if quantum mechanics is correct, there are genuine objective chances at the fundamental physical level. The question of whether there are objective chances in deterministic worlds — for example, whether the probabilities of statistical mechanics are objective — is a further complication discussed below.

## The Philosophical Accounts

Several accounts of what objective chance is have been proposed, and they divide sharply on whether chance is reducible to the pattern of actual events or is something genuinely additional to that pattern.

The **frequentist account** identifies chance with actual or hypothetical limiting frequencies: the chance of a coin landing heads is the proportion of heads in a long sequence of tosses. The **propensity account** treats chance as a primitive dispositional property of physical setups — a tendency to produce certain outcomes with certain frequencies. The coin toss has a propensity of 0.5 to land heads; this propensity is a real feature of the world, not a summary of frequencies. The **Humean account** (Lewis's BSA extended to probability) treats chances as part of the best system: the probability function that, together with categorical facts, forms the simplest and most informative systematization of all actual frequencies. Chances supervene on the total pattern of events. The **primitivist account** treats chances as fundamental features of reality, not reducible to frequencies, propensities, or best systems.

## Lewis's Principal Principle

Whatever account of objective chance one favors, there is a nearly universally accepted principle connecting objective chance to rational belief: Lewis's **Principal Principle (PP)**. A rational agent who knows that the objective chance of event E at time t is p should have credence p in E's occurrence, in the absence of inadmissible information.

Formally: **Cr(E | Ch_t(E) = p & K) = p**, where K is any admissible background information — information that does not concern the future beyond what is captured by the chance function.

This principle captures the essential role of objective chance in epistemology: it is what connects the metaphysical fact that the chance is p to the epistemic fact that a rational agent should believe with credence p. Without this connection, objective chance would be metaphysically real but epistemologically idle. With it, the objective chance constrains rational credence in exactly the way the structure of the world should constrain rational belief.

The PP raises a deep problem for Humean accounts of chance. If chances supervene on the total pattern of events — including future events — then knowing the complete future gives "admissible" information that violates the PP. If I knew that this radium atom would decay in the next hour, the PP should make my credence equal to one, not to the objective chance. But if the objective chance itself is partly constituted by future events (because it supervenes on the total mosaic, which includes those events), then we have a potential incoherence. This is the "undermining futures" problem, and it is one of the deepest challenges for Humean accounts of objective chance.

## Why Objective Chance Matters

The scientific and practical importance of objective chance is difficult to overstate. Probability is pervasive in fundamental physics, biology, medicine, and social science. If all probability were merely epistemic, these scientific applications would be systematically misdescribed: they would be reporting facts about our ignorance when they appear to be reporting facts about the world. Probabilistic causation — where causes raise the probability of their effects without determining them — requires objective chance to be a genuine form of causation rather than an epistemic placeholder. The connection between rational belief and the world encoded in the PP grounds a principled basis for rational credence convergence across agents. And some accounts of free will appeal to objective chance at the quantum level as the source of causal indeterminism. Whether quantum chance can do this work is contested, but the existence of objective chance is the precondition for such accounts even being coherent.
