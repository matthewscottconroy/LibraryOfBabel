# Frequentism About Probability

The simplest thought about what a probability is: a number that measures how often something happens. The probability of heads is one half because, in a long series of coin tosses, heads comes up about half the time. This is the frequentist approach to probability, and it has the considerable virtue of grounding probability in observable features of the world — frequencies — rather than in mysterious metaphysical entities like propensities or primitive chances. But it faces deep difficulties, and understanding those difficulties illuminates why alternative accounts of probability were developed.

## Von Mises and the Collective

Richard von Mises, in *Probability, Statistics and Truth* (1928), provided the most rigorous formulation of frequentism. Motivated partly by the desire to make probability a scientific, objective concept grounded in observable data rather than in metaphysical talk of tendencies or beliefs, he required that probability be defined relative to a **collective** — a sequence satisfying two conditions. First, the **limit condition**: the limiting frequency of any outcome exists and is stable. Second, the **randomness condition**: the limiting frequency is the same in any subsequence selected by a place-selection rule based only on positions in the sequence, not on outcomes. The randomness condition encodes the intuition that a genuinely random sequence has no pattern that can be exploited to select a biased subsequence.

Three varieties of frequentism are worth distinguishing. **Actual finite frequentism** identifies probability with the actual frequency in all actual trials. If a coin is tossed once and lands heads, the "probability" of heads is 1.0 — clearly wrong, and too sensitive to small samples generally. **Hypothetical frequentism** (Von Mises, Venn) identifies probability with the limiting frequency in an idealized infinite sequence of trials under the same conditions, avoiding the small-sample problem at the cost of introducing a theoretical idealization — infinite sequences never actually exist. **Actual limiting frequentism** (Reichenbach) identifies probability with the limiting frequency in the actual sequence of events of that type, assuming the world has infinitely many repetitions — less idealized than Von Mises but problematic for event types with only finitely many instances.

## The Core Objections

The single-case problem is the most fundamental. Frequentism defines probability only for event types, not individual events. But we routinely make probability attributions to single events: "the probability that this particular radioactive atom decays in the next hour is 0.5," "the probability that this patient survives the surgery is 0.7," "the probability that this coin, tossed once, lands heads is 0.5." Frequentism defines probability as a feature of the collective, not of the individual event. What is the reference class for "this particular atom's decay"? All atoms of this isotope? All atoms in this laboratory? Von Mises acknowledged this: probability is always relative to a collective; there is no probability of a single event considered in isolation. This is an uncomfortable consequence when we want to apply probability to unrepeatable individual events.

The reference class problem is endemic to frequentism and arguably its deepest difficulty. The probability assigned to an event depends on which reference class it is assigned to. What is the probability that this 55-year-old male patient survives heart surgery? Reference class: all patients who had heart surgery — 0.95. Reference class: all 55-year-old male patients — 0.93. Reference class: all 55-year-old male patients with diabetes — 0.88. Reference class: all patients with this specific surgical team, age, condition, and history — varies. Different reference classes give different probabilities, and frequentism provides no principled way to select the "right" one. Probabilities become relativistic — relative to a choice of reference class — rather than objective features of the individual event. Reichenbach's practical guideline is to use the narrowest possible reference class for which reliable frequency data are available, but this is a practical heuristic, not a principled metaphysical criterion.

Technical difficulties compound these problems. Infinite sequences never actually exist, making the limiting frequency a mathematical idealization we can never verify. And Von Mises's randomness condition was shown by Wald to be unformulable without excluding some place-selection rules arbitrarily; Church's modification using recursive place-selection rules imposes a computability constraint that may be too restrictive.

## The Frequentist Legacy

Despite these difficulties, frequentism has been enormously influential in scientific practice. Classical statistics — Neyman-Pearson hypothesis testing, confidence intervals, p-values — is grounded in frequentist reasoning. The p-value is the probability of obtaining a result as extreme as observed, assuming the null hypothesis, which is a frequency concept. Epidemiology, quality control, and much applied statistics operate within a frequentist framework.

The philosophical problems with frequentism motivated the development of alternative accounts. Propensity theory restores single-case probability by treating probabilities as intrinsic dispositional features of physical setups rather than summaries of frequencies. Bayesian approaches replace objective frequency with subjective credence, treating probability as a rational agent's degree of belief subject to the axioms of probability theory. Neither of these alternatives is problem-free, but each addresses a specific weakness of frequentism: propensity theory handles the single-case problem; Bayesian approaches handle the reference class problem by making the choice of prior explicit and subject to updating by evidence. Frequentism remains the dominant framework in applied statistics, but its philosophical foundations are widely regarded as in need of revision.
