# The Propensity Theory of Probability

The fundamental difficulty with frequentism is that it makes probability a feature of reference classes rather than of individual events. But when quantum mechanics assigns a probability of 0.5 to a specific particle being detected at a specific location on a screen, this seems to be a fact about that particular particle in that particular experimental situation — not about any long-run frequency. Karl Popper developed the propensity theory in response to this problem: probability is a primitive dispositional property of physical systems or experimental setups, a tendency to produce certain outcomes with certain frequencies. Propensities are real features of the world, as real as mass or charge, and they ground single-case probability attributions directly.

## The Theory and Its Varieties

Popper introduced the propensity interpretation in "The Propensity Interpretation of the Calculus of Probability and the Quantum Theory" (1957). His motivation was exactly the quantum mechanical case: the probability of a quantum measurement outcome is a single-case propensity — a tendency of the physical situation to produce outcomes — not a limit of frequencies in hypothetical sequences.

Two versions of propensity theory are importantly different. On the **long-run propensity theory**, a propensity is a tendency to produce a certain relative frequency in repeated trials. The propensity of the coin toss to produce heads is 0.5, meaning: if repeated many times under the same conditions, the frequency of heads would converge to 0.5. But this version effectively collapses back into frequentism: propensities are individuated by the long-run frequencies they tend to produce, with no independent characterization of what the propensity is beyond the frequency.

On the **single-case propensity theory**, a propensity is a property of an individual trial that determines a probability independent of any reference to frequencies. The coin on this particular toss has a propensity of 0.5 to land heads — and this is a fact about this toss, not about any sequence. This gives probabilities to genuinely unrepeatable individual events: the probability that this atom decays in the next second is 0.3; the probability that this surgery succeeds for this patient is 0.85. These are single-case facts about specific situations, not disguised claims about frequencies.

Popper held that propensities belong to the **experimental setup** rather than to the object alone. The propensity of a die to land six belongs to the entire setup — die, tossing mechanism, surface — not to the die in isolation. The probability of a radioactive atom decaying depends on the measurement interval specified; the same atom has different propensities for different time windows. And the probability of a quantum particle going left depends on the measuring apparatus, not just the particle. Setup-relative propensities capture the context-dependence of quantum probabilities more naturally than single-case propensities attributed to the object alone.

## Humphreys's Paradox

Paul Humphreys (1985) raised a fundamental objection. Propensities must be asymmetric: they run from setup to outcome, from cause to effect. But probability is symmetric by Bayes' theorem. If A has a propensity to cause B, then P(B|A) is well-defined. But Bayes' theorem also gives P(A|B) = P(B|A) × P(A) / P(B). So P(A|B) is also well-defined. Can A have a propensity to be caused by B — a backward-running propensity? This seems physically incoherent: propensities run forward, not backward. But probabilities run both ways.

The consequence: if propensities cannot capture inverse conditional probabilities, they cannot be identical to conditional probabilities. Propensity theory cannot be a general account of all probability; at best it captures forward-directed, causal probabilities but not inverse, diagnostic probabilities. The natural response is pluralism: propensities capture objective, causal probabilities; epistemic or diagnostic probabilities are a different kind. This is defensible but concedes that propensity theory is not a complete theory of probability.

## Further Objections

The **explanatory emptiness objection** claims that "the coin lands heads with probability 0.5 because it has a propensity of 0.5 to land heads" explains nothing — it restates the probability in different vocabulary. The response is that this misrepresents what propensity theory achieves. The propensity of a symmetric coin to land heads is grounded in the symmetry of its geometry and the physics of the toss — not just in the observed frequencies. The propensity account connects probability to the physical structure of the world in a way that frequentism does not. Accepting propensity as a primitive is accepting a genuine explanatory terminus, not an empty redescription.

**Quantum contextuality** presents a specific challenge: the probability of a quantum measurement outcome depends on which other measurements are being made simultaneously. This contextuality is difficult to accommodate in a propensity theory that treats propensities as intrinsic properties of the quantum system alone. But setup-relative propensities handle contextuality naturally — the relevant setup includes the measurement context, and the propensity is a property of the full experimental setup (system plus apparatus), not of the quantum system alone.

## Connection to the Powers Tradition

The propensity theory is naturally aligned with the broader powers tradition in metaphysics. If physical properties are constitutively dispositional — directed toward their manifestations — then probabilistic dispositions are simply cases where the directedness is not guaranteed but merely tends. A radioactive nucleus's tendency to decay is a probabilistic power: a power that tends toward decay with a specific probability. Smoking's tendency to cause cancer is a probabilistic power of the smoking-organism interaction. The coin's propensity to land heads is a probabilistic power of the coin-toss setup.

Cartwright's capacities framework integrates this: capacities manifest probabilistically in real conditions, where multiple capacities interact and interfere. The probability of an outcome reflects the strength and balance of relevant capacities, not just the frequency of the outcome type. This gives propensity theory a natural home within a broader dispositionalist ontology, where the fundamental features of the world are irreducibly modal — powers, dispositions, and tendencies — rather than categorical regularities that chance connections describe.
