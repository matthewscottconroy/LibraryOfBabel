# Lewis on Chance: The Principal Principle

The connection between objective chance and rational belief is obvious at the intuitive level: if a fair coin has a fifty percent chance of landing heads, a rational agent who knows this should have a credence of one half that it will land heads. But making this connection precise — and grounding it in a general account of what objective chance is — turns out to be philosophically demanding. David Lewis's contributions to this problem are two: the Principal Principle, which states the connection between chance and rational belief with precision, and the Best System account of what chances are, which tries to ground objective chance in the Humean mosaic without positing irreducible modal primitives.

## The Principal Principle

Lewis introduced the Principal Principle in "A Subjectivist's Guide to Objective Chance" (1980):

**PP**: Cr(E | Ch_t(E) = p & K) = p

Where Cr is a rational agent's credence function, Ch_t(E) = p is the proposition that the objective chance of E at time t is p, and K is any admissible background information — information not concerning the future beyond what is captured in the chance statement. Informal statement: if a rational agent knows that the objective chance of event E is p, and has no inadmissible information about whether E will occur, then the agent's degree of belief in E should be p.

The PP is widely accepted as capturing the essential role of objective chance in epistemology. It is what connects the metaphysical fact (the objective chance is p) to the epistemic fact (the rational agent should believe with credence p). Without this connection, objective chance would be real but epistemologically idle. With it, objective chance directly constrains rational credence in the way that facts about the world should constrain rational belief.

The **admissibility condition** is crucial but hard to specify precisely. Admissible information includes the history of the world up to time t, the laws of nature, the chance setup itself, and pure mathematics and logic. Inadmissible information is information about the future — in particular, whether E will occur. If an agent already knows whether E occurred, the credence should be set to 1 or 0, not to the chance p. Historical records of the future, direct knowledge of E's occurrence — these defeat the PP by providing direct evidence that bypasses the chance.

## Chances in the Best System

Lewis's metaphysical account of what chances are integrates with his broader BSA program. The Best System identifies laws as the theorems of the deductive system that best balances simplicity, strength, and — for probabilistic laws — fit. **Fit** measures how well the system's probability assignments match the actual sequence of outcomes: how probable the actual course of events is given the system's probability function. A system with perfect fit would assign probability 1 to everything that actually happens, but such a system would be maximally complex. The best system trades fit against simplicity: including a simple probabilistic generalization (like the Born Rule in quantum mechanics) sacrifices some fit in exchange for great gains in simplicity and strength.

Lewisian chances are the probability values that appear in the best system's probabilistic laws. They are objective features of the world — not mere epistemic probabilities — but they supervene on the total pattern of events: Humean supervenience again. The chances are the world's way of summarizing, in the simplest possible way, the statistical structure of its history.

## The Undermining Futures Problem

The most serious challenge to Lewis's account: if chances supervene on the total pattern of events, then future events partly determine current chances. But the PP connects current chances to current rational credence. The problem arises when the future actual frequency differs significantly from the current chance assignment.

Suppose the current objective chance of outcome E is p = 0.5. This chance supervenes on the total mosaic, including future events. Suppose the actual future frequency of E is 0.9. The best system for a world where E occurs with frequency 0.9 would assign E a chance closer to 0.9 than to 0.5. So the "actual" current chance, if we knew the full future, would be closer to 0.9. But the PP instructs the agent to set credence to the chance p = 0.5. The actual future "undermines" the current chance — the future is one that makes the current chance assignment incorrect.

Lewis proposed a modification: the **New Principle**: Cr(E | T_t & K) = Cr(E | Ch_t(E) = p & K), where T_t is the complete Humean theory of the actual world including all actual history. This modification accounts for the possibility of undermining futures by conditioning on the full theory. Critics have questioned whether the New Principle fully preserves the intuitive content of the original PP, or whether it has become too complex to serve its original role.

## Hall's Critique

Ned Hall argued that Lewis's account conflates two concepts that should be kept separate: **chance** (objective single-case probability, what the PP governs) and **frequency** (actual long-run frequency of the outcome). If chance is just a feature of the best system grounded in frequencies, then the PP seems to become circular: we should set credence to the chance, but the chance is just a refined summary of frequencies, and we are told to set credence to a summary of what actually happens. Hall suggests a two-concept view: there are two notions of probability — the BSA-grounded notion governing Humean systems, and a separate primitive notion of objective chance that governs single cases. These may coincide in practice but are conceptually distinct. This is essentially a concession that the Humean account cannot fully capture all that we need from objective chance.

Lewis's account remains the most developed Humean approach to chance, and defending it is closely tied to defending Humean supervenience as a whole. Whether the undermining futures problem is fatal, or whether modifications can save the account, is the central question that divides Humean from non-Humean approaches to objective probability.
