# Chance in Deterministic Worlds

If the laws of nature are deterministic — if the state of the world at any time uniquely determines its state at all later times — does it make sense to speak of objective probability? In a deterministic world, every event either must occur (given the laws and the prior state) or cannot occur. What room is left for chances? The standard answer, associated with Laplace's demon thought experiment, is none: in a deterministic world, all probability is epistemic, reflecting the limitations of our knowledge rather than any objective indeterminacy. But this standard answer faces a serious difficulty: many sciences use probability in ways that seem to be more than epistemic, even in contexts that are classically deterministic or that abstract away from any underlying indeterminism.

## The Laplacean View and Its Challenge

Laplace's "demon" (1814) imagined an intelligence that knows the current state of all particles and all the forces acting on them — such a being could predict all future events with certainty. For this intelligence, nothing would be uncertain; all probability would dissolve into knowledge. This implies that probability arises only from the limitations of human knowledge in deterministic worlds, not from any objective feature of the world.

But this view faces the problem that many sciences use probability in deterministic contexts in ways that do not look merely epistemic. Statistical mechanics models the behavior of gases, liquids, and solids using probability distributions over microstates, even though the underlying molecular dynamics are deterministic (classically). The probability that a gas sample occupies a particular microstate is a well-defined, scientifically useful quantity. Evolutionary biology uses probability distributions over gene frequencies, mutation rates, and selection coefficients — the underlying molecular processes may be deterministic, yet the population-level probabilities are scientifically real and explanatorily essential. Classical probability assigns uniform probabilities to game outcomes based on symmetry, without any assumption of indeterminism.

If all these probabilities are merely epistemic, they are reporting facts about human ignorance rather than facts about the world. But this seems to misrepresent the scientific function of these probabilities — they are used to make objective, world-directed predictions, not to describe cognitive limitations.

## Albert and Loewer's Mentaculus

David Albert and Barry Loewer developed the most philosophically sophisticated account of how deterministic worlds can contain genuine objective probability — the **mentaculus**. The key idea: a probability distribution is defined over the initial conditions of the universe, specifically over the set of possible initial microstates compatible with the **Past Hypothesis** — the constraint that the early universe had an extremely low entropy state. The probability measure is uniform (Liouville measure) over microstates compatible with the Past Hypothesis.

The resulting probability distribution is defined over the full history of the universe from the initial state. It generates probability values for all macro-events — thermodynamic events, biological events, human actions — by integrating over the microstate possibilities. The distribution is objective: it is grounded in the actual physical structure of the universe and the constraint from the Past Hypothesis. And it is non-epistemic: it does not reflect ignorance of the actual microstate but rather the genuine statistical structure of the world given only macro-level constraints.

The Past Hypothesis is essential. Without it, a uniform distribution over all possible microstates would predict entropy decrease as often as entropy increase — giving the wrong thermodynamic picture. Conditioning on the Past Hypothesis gives a distribution that correctly accounts for the thermodynamic arrow of time: entropy-decreasing microstates are simply too few in the constrained space to occur with significant probability. The mentaculus thereby grounds the Second Law of thermodynamics as a consequence of the probability distribution, not as a separate law.

## Loewer's Humean Account

Loewer defends the view that there are genuine Humean chances even in deterministic worlds. The Best System for the actual world includes probabilistic laws not because the world is indeterministic but because including those laws dramatically improves the simplicity/strength balance of the system. The chances in statistical mechanics are genuine features of the world in the Humean sense — features of the Best System — even without propensities or indeterministic dispositions. These chances ground a form of the Principal Principle: a rational agent who knows that the chance of E in the BSA sense is p should have credence p in E.

Critics question whether BSA-grounded probabilities have the right character to serve as objective chances — whether they are genuinely "in the world" rather than features of our systematization of the world. The reply: the BSA is not merely a human systematization but the best systematization of all facts, evaluated by an ideal observer. The probabilities that appear in this ideal system are objective features of the pattern of facts, not subjective impositions.

## Typicality

An alternative to probability in deterministic worlds uses the concept of **typicality** rather than probability. A microstate is "typical" if it shares the statistical properties of most initial conditions in the relevant class. The Second Law of thermodynamics is then reinterpreted: typical initial conditions (given the Past Hypothesis) lead to entropy increase. This is not a probabilistic claim but a claim about the measure of initial conditions. Most initial conditions consistent with the Past Hypothesis lead to entropy increase; the few that do not are atypical.

This approach is used in the foundations of Bohmian mechanics — a deterministic quantum theory that recovers the Born Rule from a typicality argument. The "probabilities" in this account are not genuine objective chances but measures of how common a certain type of initial condition is, not what the chance of any particular outcome is. Whether typicality is a respectable substitute for probability, or whether it merely relocates the question (what grounds the typicality measure?), is an ongoing debate in the foundations of statistical mechanics and quantum mechanics.
