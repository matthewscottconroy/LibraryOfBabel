# The Problem of Induction

The sun has risen every day in recorded history. We infer it will rise tomorrow. Copper has conducted electricity in every sample we have tested. We infer it will conduct in untested samples. The inference from the observed to the unobserved is so natural that we barely notice ourselves making it. But Hume noticed, and what he found was that the inference has no rational foundation — or at least, no foundation that does not itself invoke inductive reasoning. This is the problem of induction, and it connects directly to the metaphysics of laws, the structure of scientific confirmation, and the nature of probability.

## Hume's Argument

Hume presented the problem in both the *Treatise* (1739) and the first *Enquiry* (1748). The argument has two parts.

Against rational justification: inductive inferences cannot be validated by reason alone. "The sun will rise tomorrow" is not a logical consequence of "the sun has risen every day so far." The sun's not rising tomorrow is logically consistent with all past observations. Reason can identify logical contradictions and tautologies; it cannot validate extrapolation from observed to unobserved cases.

Against empirical justification: nor can induction be justified by appealing to its past success. The argument "induction has worked in the past, therefore it will work in the future" is itself an inductive inference. To justify induction by induction is circular. There is no non-circular way to demonstrate that past success licenses future success.

Hume's conclusion: inductive inference is a habit of mind, not a rational principle. We are psychologically constituted to expect the regular to continue, but we cannot justify this expectation. The "necessity" we attribute to inductive connections is projected by the mind, not derived from reason or experience.

## The Scope of the Problem

The problem extends far beyond simple "more of the same" inference. It applies to scientific laws: the inference from "copper has conducted in all observed instances" to "all copper conducts everywhere and always" is inductive. It applies to causal claims: the inference from "fire has always preceded smoke in our experience" to "fire causes smoke" is inductive. It applies to theoretical entities: the inference from observable phenomena to unobservable electrons or dark matter involves induction. And probabilistic reasoning is no escape: even "this drug has worked in 70% of trials, so it will work in approximately 70% of future cases" is an inductive inference.

If induction cannot be justified, all of these inferences are unjustified. The consequences for scientific knowledge are enormous — as Hume fully recognized, and as none of his successors have fully resolved.

## Responses and Their Limits

Popper's falsificationism attempted to eliminate induction from science by showing that scientific method is deductive, not inductive. Science proceeds by attempting to falsify hypotheses through negative tests, not by confirming them through positive instances. The logic is modus tollens: H predicts O; observation reveals ¬O; therefore ¬H. Refutation is deductively valid; confirmation is not.

But Popper's response faces the Duhem-Quine problem: a hypothesis H is never tested alone but always with auxiliary hypotheses A (about instruments, background conditions). When ¬O is observed, we can infer ¬(H & A), but not ¬H alone — we could equally infer ¬A. And Popper's notion of "corroboration" — a hypothesis that has survived rigorous tests is corroborated — cannot guide rational belief without some inductive principle connecting past survival to future reliability. The falsificationist cannot escape the need for inductive inference in the evaluation of theories.

Strawson argued that the problem is a conceptual confusion: to ask "is induction rational?" is to ask whether rational empirical inference is rational, since what "rational" means for empirical inference just is inductive inference. This dissolution of the problem has seemed to most philosophers to be question-begging: it does not explain what is special about inductive procedures that makes them the standard of rationality.

## The Connection to Laws

The problem of induction and the metaphysics of laws are deeply intertwined. If laws of nature have genuine modal force — if "all copper conducts" expresses a necessity rather than a coincidence — then inductive inference is supported by something more than past pattern: the law guarantees that future copper will conduct for the same reason past copper did. On the Humean view, there are no laws in this strong sense, only regularities. And then the problem of induction bites hard: what licenses the inference from past regularity to future regularity? Nothing, on a purely Humean picture, except the psychological habit Hume identified.

This is why non-Humean accounts of laws are partly motivated by epistemological concerns: they provide what the Humean cannot — a rational basis for confident inductive inference. The law N(Copper, Conductor) holds in virtue of a necessitation relation between universals; this relation holds in all instances, past and future; and knowing the law is therefore knowing something that licenses the extrapolation. Whether this really solves the problem or merely pushes it back (what licenses the inference that the necessitation relation persists?) is a further question. But the connection between the metaphysics of laws and the epistemology of induction is as direct as any connection in philosophy.
