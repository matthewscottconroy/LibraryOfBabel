# Goodman's New Riddle of Induction

Suppose we have solved Hume's problem — suppose we have shown, to our satisfaction, that inductive inference from observed to unobserved cases is rationally justified. A further problem remains: which regularities should we project? Not every observed regularity supports inductive projection to unobserved cases. The challenge of identifying which generalizations are "projectible" is Goodman's new riddle, and it shows that even a solution to Hume's problem leaves the core epistemological difficulty untouched.

## The Grue Predicate

Nelson Goodman introduced the predicate "grue" in *Fact, Fiction, and Forecast* (1955). An object x is **grue** iff either (i) x is examined before time T and found to be green, or (ii) x is not examined before T and x is blue. Choose T to be some future time — say, January 1, 2050.

Now observe that all emeralds examined so far are green. Since they are examined before T and found to be green, they are also grue. Our observations confirm "all emeralds are green." By the same logic of confirmation, they equally confirm "all emeralds are grue." "All emeralds are green" predicts that unexamined emeralds will be green. "All emeralds are grue" predicts that unexamined emeralds (not examined before T) will be blue. These predictions are contradictory. Yet both hypotheses receive identical inductive support from the same evidence. The problem: which should we accept?

The "old" Humean problem asks why we should project any regularity at all. Goodman's new riddle takes inductive inference for granted and asks which predicates we should use. Even granting that regularities can be projected, the standard logic of confirmation gives no basis for preferring "all emeralds are green" over "all emeralds are grue." Something more is needed.

## Goodman's Solution and Its Problems

Goodman's own solution invokes **entrenchment**: a predicate is projectible iff it has a long history of successful use in inductive inferences. "Green" has been used throughout human history with great success; "grue" has not. Entrenchment is asymmetric, and we should project well-entrenched predicates.

The solution is pragmatic and historically sensitive — entrenchment is a fact about our linguistic practices, not about the world. This makes it vulnerable to the charge of anthropocentrism: if humans had always used "grue" instead of "green," grue would be well-entrenched and we should project it. Whether emeralds are green or grue in the future would then depend on historical linguistic accident, which seems wrong. The success of past inductions using "green" does not guarantee the success of future inductions — this was Hume's point, and it applies equally to the meta-induction that entrenchment is a reliable guide to projectibility.

## Lewis's Response: Natural Properties

Lewis's response to the riddle connects it to a substantive ontological claim. "Green" is more projectible than "grue" because it corresponds to a genuinely natural property — one that carves nature at its joints. Natural properties are sparse, fundamental features of reality. "Grue" does not correspond to any natural property; it combines a temporal/epistemic clause (examined before T) with a color predicate, yielding a gerrymandered disjunction.

Lewis's criterion: project predicates that express natural properties; do not project unnatural ones. This gives a principled answer grounded in the structure of the world, not in our linguistic history. The answer also connects the riddle of induction to the BSA account of laws (only natural predicates appear in laws) and to causal explanation (only natural properties are causally relevant). The entire Lewisian metaphysical framework hangs together: naturalness appears simultaneously in the ontology of properties, the metaphysics of laws, and the epistemology of induction.

The difficulty is that we need some account of which properties are natural and how we come to know this. If natural properties are theoretical posits inferred from the success of our inductive practices, the account risks circularity. If they are primitive, metaphysically basic features of reality, the account is illuminating but explanatorily incomplete — it tells us that the world has natural joints without telling us what grounds those joints.

## Bayesian Responses

Bayesians address Goodman's riddle through prior probability assignment. A rational Bayesian agent should assign much higher prior probability to "all emeralds are green" than to "all emeralds are grue." This differential prior reflects background knowledge: emeralds' color is determined by their mineral chemistry (chromium content), not by examination time. After updating on observed green (equivalently, grue before T) emeralds, the posterior on "all emeralds are green" will be enormously higher than on "all emeralds are grue," because the differential prior is reinforced rather than overcome by evidence that is equally consistent with both hypotheses.

This response addresses the riddle without solving it at the level of logic: prior probabilities, not the logic of confirmation, do the work of selecting among competing hypotheses. The choice of priors encodes the entrenchment (Goodman) or naturalness (Lewis) of the predicates. Objective Bayesians can argue that the correct prior for "all emeralds are grue" should be much lower than for "all emeralds are green" because the latter is simpler and involves a natural predicate.

What the Bayesian response shows is that the problem of induction is ultimately a problem about the rationality of prior probability assignments. Hume's problem is about whether any projection is rational; Goodman's riddle is about which projections are rational. Both ultimately require that rational inquiry be sensitive to the structure of the world — to which regularities reflect genuine natural structure and which reflect accidental or gerrymandered groupings. The answer to both problems, in the end, requires metaphysical commitments about the structure of nature that the formal logic of confirmation and the mathematics of probability cannot supply by themselves.
