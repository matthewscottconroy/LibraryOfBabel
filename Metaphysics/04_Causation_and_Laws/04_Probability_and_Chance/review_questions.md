# Review Questions — Probability and Chance

1. The distinction between objective chance and epistemic probability is philosophically central, but establishing that the distinction is real requires showing that some probabilities are not expressions of ignorance. What is the strongest scientific case for genuine objective chance — is it quantum mechanical indeterminism, thermodynamic irreversibility, or something else? And does the case depend on settling contested questions in the interpretation of physics?

2. Frequentism faces the single-case problem: probability is always relative to a reference class, never to an individual event. Yet single-case probability attributions are ubiquitous in science and medicine ("this atom has a 50% chance of decaying"). Does the reference class problem show that frequentism is the wrong account of probability, or merely that it needs supplementation? What is the best frequentist response?

3. The long-run propensity theory identifies propensities with tendencies to produce certain long-run frequencies, while the single-case propensity theory treats them as irreducible features of individual setups. Humphreys's paradox shows that propensities cannot be straightforwardly identified with conditional probabilities because propensities are asymmetric (cause to effect) while Bayes' theorem makes conditional probability symmetric. Does Humphreys's paradox refute propensity theory, or merely show that propensity theory can only be a partial account of probability?

4. Lewis's Principal Principle states that a rational agent who knows that the objective chance of E is p should have credence p in E. This elegant constraint connects metaphysics to epistemology. But the "undermining futures" problem threatens it: if Humean chances supervene on the total mosaic including future events, then certain future frequencies would undermine the current chance assignment and make the PP incoherent. How serious is this problem for the Humean account, and is Lewis's New Principle an adequate repair?

5. Albert and Loewer's mentaculus proposes genuine objective probabilities in a deterministic world by positing a probability distribution over initial conditions constrained by the Past Hypothesis. If this account succeeds, it shows that indeterminism is not necessary for objective chance. But is the resulting probability genuinely "in the world," or is it a feature of our choice of the uniform (Liouville) probability measure over initial conditions — a choice that itself needs justification?

6. Goodman's new riddle shows that even granting that inductive inference is rational, we need a principled criterion for which predicates are projectible. Lewis's response invokes natural properties that "carve nature at its joints." But natural properties are themselves a contested theoretical posit. Does Lewis's solution to Goodman's riddle presuppose the very ontological realism that the riddle was meant to challenge — making the solution circular — or does it rather show that realism about natural structure is a precondition for any solution to the riddle?

7. The connection between propensity theory and the powers tradition is close: probabilistic propensities are powers that point toward their manifestations with a specific degree rather than with certainty. If you accept the powers ontology from the previous chapter, does propensity theory follow naturally as its extension to stochastic causation? Or do propensities introduce new problems — especially Humphreys's paradox — that powers theory was not designed to handle?

8. Objective chance matters for several debates downstream: for free will (if determinism is true there is no room for chance; if genuine quantum chance exists, does it help or hurt the free will debate?), for moral responsibility (if an agent's action was only probabilistically caused, is it still their action?), and for the interpretation of scientific laws (probabilistic laws require objective chance). Sketch how the concept of objective chance connects to at least two of these downstream debates, and identify which account of chance — frequentism, propensity, or Lewisian BSA — would best serve those applications.
