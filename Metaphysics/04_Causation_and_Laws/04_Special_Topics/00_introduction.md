# Special Topics in Causation

Causation is not a uniform phenomenon that presents the same structure in every domain. Once the general theories of causation have been developed, certain domains raise distinctive puzzles that test those theories and require specialized treatment. This chapter examines two such domains: mental causation and the relationship between causation and the direction of time.

Mental causation poses a challenge at the intersection of philosophy of mind and metaphysics of causation. If mental events are identical to physical events (type or token identity theory), then mental causes are physical causes and there is no special problem. But if mental properties are distinct from physical properties — if the mental is not reducible to the physical — then a deep worry arises. Physical events are causally closed: every physical effect has a sufficient physical cause. If mental properties are not identical to physical properties, then any physical effect that a mental event appears to cause already has a sufficient physical cause — leaving no causal role for the mental properties as such. This is the exclusion problem, and it threatens the causal relevance of the mental across every non-reductive framework. The chapter develops the problem and examines the responses available to the non-reductive physicalist.

The relationship between causation and time raises equally deep questions. We ordinarily think of causation as directed in time: causes precede effects, and effects do not precede causes. But the fundamental physical laws are time-reversal invariant: they work equally well forward and backward in time. There is nothing in the equations of physics that privileges the earlier time as the cause and the later time as the effect. So the temporal direction of causation — the fact that causes always precede effects — seems to require explanation in terms that go beyond the fundamental laws. Various accounts — appeal to counterfactual asymmetry, to the thermodynamic arrow, to causal powers — are examined and evaluated. The question of whether backward causation is possible, and what it would mean if it were, connects to the philosophy of time and the possibility of time travel.

These special topics do not merely apply the general theory; they test it in conditions that reveal its deepest commitments.
