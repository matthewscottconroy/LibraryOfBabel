# The Causal Exclusion Problem

Mental causation — the capacity of mental states to cause behavior — is one of the most contested problems in the philosophy of mind and the metaphysics of causation. The exclusion problem, developed systematically by Jaegwon Kim, poses a dilemma for anyone who holds both that mental states are causally efficacious and that mental states are realized by or supervene on physical states.

The problem arises from two attractive but apparently conflicting commitments. First, causal closure of the physical: every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause. The physical world is causally self-contained — there is no need to invoke non-physical causes to explain physical events. Second, mental-physical supervenience: mental events supervene on physical events in the sense that there could be no mental difference without a physical difference. Third, mental causation: mental events cause physical events (behavior). If I form an intention to raise my arm, this mental event causes the arm to rise.

The problem is: if the physical cause of the arm rising is sufficient (by causal closure), and the mental event is distinct from the physical event (as non-reductive physicalism holds), then the mental event is at best causally redundant — a "dangler" that makes no independent causal contribution. The physical event does all the work. Mental events are epiphenomenal: they are caused by physical events but have no causal power themselves.

Kim argued that non-reductive physicalism — which accepts supervenience but rejects reduction — is untenable precisely because it cannot secure genuine mental causation. The only ways to secure mental causation are either to identify mental properties with physical properties (reductionism) or to embrace substance dualism (which violates physical causal closure). The non-reductive physicalist is caught between epiphenomenalism and one of these alternatives.

Responses to the exclusion problem include: (1) embracing reductionism, (2) arguing that causal closure is false or restricted, (3) accepting a form of overdetermination (the mental cause and the physical cause are both genuine), (4) arguing that mental causation is a matter of causal relevance rather than causal sufficiency, and (5) arguing that the problem presupposes an overly individualistic conception of mental causation that should be replaced with a more holistic or contextual view.
