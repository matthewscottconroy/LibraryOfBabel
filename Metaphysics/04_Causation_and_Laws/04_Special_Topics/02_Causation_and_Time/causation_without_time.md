# Causation Without Time: Simultaneous and Structural Causation

The standard picture of causation assumes temporal succession: causes come before effects. But there are cases where causation appears to be simultaneous or even atemporal. Kant famously used the example of a lead ball resting on a cushion: the weight of the ball causes the depression in the cushion, but the cause and effect appear to be simultaneous rather than successive. How should we think about such cases?

Kant used simultaneous causation to argue that temporal succession is not constitutive of causation. The objective criterion for causation, on Kant's transcendental idealist view, is that if we take away the cause, the effect is removed — a counterfactual criterion rather than a temporal one. The asymmetry of causation is grounded in the asymmetry of counterfactual dependence, not in temporal order.

Contemporary debates about simultaneous causation arise particularly in structural equation models. In a system of simultaneous equations representing static equilibrium — for example, supply and demand equations in economics — the "causes" (supply shifters, demand shifters) and "effects" (price, quantity) are determined simultaneously. The causal interpretation of these structural equations does not obviously require temporal priority.

Causal structuralism, associated with philosophers like James Ladyman and Don Ross, holds that structure is more fundamental than objects and their properties. If the world is fundamentally a web of structural relations without an underlying substrate of objects, the temporal dimension of causation becomes less central. What matters is the network of dependence relations, which may or may not be temporal.

Eternal mathematical truths present a limiting case. Does the axiom of Peano arithmetic cause the truth of 2+2=4? This seems wrong — mathematical truths do not have causes in the usual sense — but there is a sense in which the axioms explain the theorems, and this explanation is a form of grounding rather than causation. The distinction between causation and grounding (metaphysical dependence) is itself an important topic in contemporary metaphysics.
