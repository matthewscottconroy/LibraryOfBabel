# The Temporal Direction of Causation

One of the most fundamental features of causation is its temporal direction: causes precede their effects. We do not ordinarily think that effects can precede their causes, and this asymmetry is central to our understanding of causal order. But explaining why causation has this direction — and whether it necessarily does — turns out to be surprisingly difficult.

The Humean wants to derive the direction of causation from the direction of time. Causes come before effects because of the temporal asymmetry of the world: entropy increases toward the future, thermodynamic processes are irreversible, and records of the past are accessible while records of the future are not. On this view, the asymmetry of causation is just the asymmetry of time.

But this faces a problem: the fundamental laws of physics are (with minor exceptions) time-symmetric. The equations describing physical processes work equally well in both temporal directions. If the laws are time-symmetric, why do the processes they describe have a preferred direction? This is the problem of the arrow of time, and it connects directly to the asymmetry of causation.

David Lewis argued that the asymmetry of causation is derived from the asymmetry of counterfactual dependence, which is in turn explained by a de facto temporal asymmetry: the world happens to be such that later states are much more constrained by earlier states than earlier states are by later states. This explains both why causes come before effects and why we can "see" the past in records but cannot see the future.

Michael Dummett and others have argued for the possibility of backward causation — causation running from later to earlier events. Dummett's case involves a chief who performs a rain dance today, believing that it causes his tribe to have been brave yesterday. This seems incoherent, but Dummett argued that with the right understanding of counterfactuals and causal connection, backward causation is conceptually coherent. Whether it is physically possible is a separate question: some interpretations of quantum mechanics (retrocausation) appear to involve backward causation.
