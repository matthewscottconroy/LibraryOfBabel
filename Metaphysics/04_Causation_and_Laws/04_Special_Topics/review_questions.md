# Review Questions — Special Topics in Causation

1. Kim's exclusion problem rests on three premises: causal closure of the physical, mental-physical supervenience, and the distinctness of mental from physical properties. Non-reductive physicalists typically reject the distinctness claim or reinterpret what it means. Identify exactly which premise Kim's strongest critics deny, and assess whether the denial is ad hoc or independently motivated.

2. Yablo's response to the exclusion problem appeals to the determinable/determinate relation: the mental is to the physical as being colored is to being red — not identical, not distinct, but related as determinable to determinate. Does this analogy actually dissolve the exclusion problem, or does it merely redescribe it? In particular, does a determinable property do genuine causal work over and above the determinate that realizes it?

3. Downward causation — higher-level properties causally influencing lower-level components — appears to conflict with causal closure. Strong emergentists claim higher-level properties have irreducible causal powers; weak emergentists accept supervenience but allow that higher-level descriptions are essential for prediction. Is either form of emergentism compatible with causal closure, or must one choose between causal closure and the reality of higher-level causation?

4. The asymmetry of causation — causes precede effects — is a datum that any theory must explain. Lewis grounds it in the asymmetry of overdetermination (past events leave many traces; future events do not similarly anticipate the present). Price challenges this by arguing the asymmetry is perspectival, reflecting how we as agents are embedded in time, not a feature of the spacetime block itself. Evaluate this exchange. If Price is right that causal asymmetry is perspectival, what follows for the metaphysical status of causal claims?

5. Retrocausation — causation running backward in time — faces the causal loop objection: if a present event causes a past event that in turn prevents the present event, we get a contradiction. Is this objection fatal to all forms of backward causation, or only to specific forms? Does quantum mechanics (the two-state vector formalism, the Wheeler-Feynman absorber theory) provide physically coherent models of retrocausation, or are these better interpreted in a way that avoids genuine backward causal influence?

6. Causation and grounding (metaphysical dependence) are both asymmetric explanatory relations, yet they differ: causation is typically between events in time, while grounding holds between facts or entities where priority is a matter of ontological dependence rather than temporal order. The case of simultaneous causation (Kant's lead ball and cushion) and structural equation models blurs the temporal criterion. Does this suggest that temporal priority is merely a contingent feature of most causation rather than part of its essence?

7. The causal exclusion problem for mental causation is structurally parallel to the exclusion problem for special science causation generally: if physical causes are always sufficient, what causal work do biological, psychological, or economic properties do? Trace this parallel and assess whether the same solution that works for special science causation (Woodward's invariance criterion, Fodor's multiple realizability) also works for mental causation, or whether mental causation poses a distinctive additional problem.

8. The topics of this chapter — mental causation, temporal direction, and retrocausation — each connect to debates about free will and moral responsibility. If mental causes are epiphenomenal (Kim's worry), if causal asymmetry is perspectival (Price), and if backward causation is coherent, what are the implications for the standard picture of agents as forward-directed causes of their own actions? Identify which of these three results, if established, would most radically challenge our ordinary conception of agency.
