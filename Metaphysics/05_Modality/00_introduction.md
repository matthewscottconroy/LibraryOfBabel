# Modality

Something happened today: you read this sentence. Something else did not happen today: you traveled to the Andromeda galaxy. Both claims are straightforwardly true. But now consider a different kind of claim: it is *possible* that you could have traveled to Andromeda, even if you didn't; it is *necessary* that two plus two equals four, regardless of what anyone does or believes. Possibility and necessity are modal concepts — they concern not just what is, but what could be, what must be, what couldn't have been otherwise. And modal claims seem to be among the most common and important things we say.

We make modal claims in science: the laws of nature tell us not just what happens but what *must* happen, what *couldn't* fail to happen in the right circumstances. We make modal claims in ethics: moral obligations bind us to actions we *must* perform, or prohibit actions we *cannot* permissibly take. We make modal claims about persons: Aristotle *could* have become a physician instead of a philosopher; the number seven *could not* have been even. These distinctions — between what is necessary and what merely happens to be, between what is possible and what is impossible — seem to carve reality at its joints. But what exactly are they tracking?

The philosophy of modality tries to answer this question by investigating the metaphysical foundations of possibility and necessity. The central challenge is that modal facts seem to be about non-actual ways the world could be — about possibilities that don't exist in the way that actual events exist. How can talk about what *could have been* be genuinely true or false if there is nothing — no mere possibility — for it to be *about*? This is the metaphysical puzzle that generates the theory of possible worlds.

This unit develops the main positions and problems in the metaphysics of modality across four chapters. We begin with **possibility and necessity** at the basic level — the modal concepts themselves, their logical structure, and the systems of modal logic that formalize our reasoning about them. Understanding the formal structure of modality is not merely a technical prerequisite; it reveals substantive constraints on any adequate metaphysical account.

The chapter on **possible worlds** examines the dominant contemporary framework for understanding modal claims: the idea that a proposition is possible if it is true in some possible world, and necessary if it is true in every possible world. But what are possible worlds, exactly? David Lewis's modal realism holds that possible worlds are concrete realities, as real as the actual world, differing only in being spatiotemporally disconnected from us. This is a striking and systematic view that resolves many puzzles — but at the cost of an ontology that many philosophers find extravagant. The alternatives (modal ersatzism, combinatorialism, primitivism about modality) offer different trade-offs between ontological economy and theoretical power.

**Essence and essentialism** returns modal metaphysics to Aristotle's ancient claim that things have natures — properties they could not lack without ceasing to be what they are. Kripke's *Naming and Necessity* reinvigorated essentialism in the analytic tradition, arguing that proper names are rigid designators and that statements like "water is H₂O" are necessary, not contingent. Kit Fine's subsequent distinction between essence and necessity has pushed the debate further: some necessary truths about a thing are not grounded in its essence, and essence cannot simply be read off the pattern of modal facts. Anti-essentialists challenge the coherence or usefulness of the essential/accidental distinction altogether.

Finally, **modal epistemology** asks how we come to know modal truths. We cannot travel to possible worlds and observe what holds in them; we cannot run experiments on what couldn't be otherwise. Yet we seem to have robust modal knowledge — we know that it is impossible for there to be a round square, possible for there to be water without oxygen, necessary that if Socrates exists, he is self-identical. What cognitive faculties give us access to such facts? What is the relationship between conceivability and possibility? These questions close the gap between the metaphysics of modality and its epistemology, showing that the two inquiries must advance together.
