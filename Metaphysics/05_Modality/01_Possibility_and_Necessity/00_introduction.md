# Possibility and Necessity

Some things could not be otherwise: that two plus two equals four, that nothing is both entirely red and entirely blue at the same time, that if Socrates exists then Socrates is identical to Socrates. These are necessary truths — truths that hold not just in the actual world but in every possible world, in every way things could have been. Other things happen to be the case but could have been otherwise: that the Earth has exactly one moon, that water has the molecular structure it has, that you chose to read this sentence today. These are contingent truths — true, but not necessarily so.

The distinction between necessity and contingency is deeply embedded in both ordinary reasoning and scientific practice. We distinguish between laws of nature (which hold necessarily) and initial conditions (which are contingent). We distinguish between what something essentially is and what it merely happens to be. We distinguish between what could not have been otherwise (logical truths, mathematical truths, arguably metaphysical truths) and what could have been different (historical events, physical constants, personal choices). Philosophy's task is to give a principled account of this distinction.

The formal tools of modal logic provide a framework: necessity is truth in all possible worlds; possibility is truth in some possible world. The accessibility relation between possible worlds determines which modal claims hold: something is necessarily true if it is true in all worlds accessible from the actual world; possibly true if it is true in some accessible world. Different modal logics (K, T, S4, S5) result from different constraints on accessibility, and choosing among them is partly a matter of semantics and partly a matter of metaphysics.

But the formal framework raises rather than settles the metaphysical question: what are possible worlds? This chapter develops the basic modal concepts and the modal logic needed to reason about them. It examines the different systems of modal logic and what they commit us to, introduces the key distinctions (de re versus de dicto modality, logical versus metaphysical necessity) and prepares the ground for the detailed examination of possible worlds that follows.
