# Basic Modal Concepts

Possibility and necessity are among the most fundamental concepts in our intellectual repertoire. We use them constantly: we say that water is necessarily composed of hydrogen and oxygen; that it is possible that we might have lost the war; that it is impossible for there to be a round square. These claims are not about what is actually the case but about what must be the case, could be the case, or cannot be the case. They are modal claims — claims that involve the logical categories of possibility, necessity, and impossibility.

The philosophical analysis of these concepts is more difficult than it might appear. What does it mean to say that something is possible? One tempting answer is epistemic: something is possible if we cannot rule it out, given our current knowledge. But this conflates two different kinds of possibility: epistemic possibility (compatible with what we know) and metaphysical possibility (compatible with the nature of reality). Many things are epistemically possible — compatible with my current knowledge — that are not metaphysically possible: it might be epistemically possible, given my limited knowledge, that water has a different chemical composition; but it is not metaphysically possible, since water is necessarily H₂O.

Logical possibility — consistency with the laws of logic — is the broadest kind of possibility. Metaphysical possibility — consistency with the nature of reality — is typically narrower: not every logically consistent scenario is metaphysically possible. And natural or physical possibility — consistency with the laws of nature — is narrower still: some things are metaphysically possible that are physically impossible. Understanding the relationships among these different kinds of modality, and determining which is most fundamental, is a central task of modal metaphysics.

The formal tools of modal logic — systems like S4, S5, and their semantics in terms of accessibility relations among possible worlds — provide a precise language for modal claims. The philosophical question is what the formal machinery represents: what are possible worlds, what is accessibility, and how do the formal systems connect to the underlying metaphysical reality?

This section introduces the basic modal concepts and their formal representation, preparing the reader for the more substantive metaphysical questions that follow.
