# Logical Versus Metaphysical Modality

Here is a claim that seems obviously necessary: "All bachelors are unmarried." And here is another that seems just as necessary: "Water is H₂O." But if we ask how we know each of them, the answer is quite different — and that difference turns out to mark a philosophically fundamental distinction.

We know that all bachelors are unmarried because we know what "bachelor" means. We know that water is H₂O because chemists investigated water's molecular structure. One piece of knowledge is a priori; the other is a posteriori. Yet both, Kripke argued, are necessary. This is the puzzle of the necessary a posteriori — and recognizing it forces us to separate two notions of necessity that philosophers had long treated as one.

## What Logical Necessity Is

A proposition is logically necessary if its negation is a logical contradiction — if it is provable from purely logical axioms, or if it holds in virtue of logical form alone. The negation of "All bachelors are unmarried" is incoherent given what "bachelor" means. "P or not-P" is a theorem of classical logic. In formal terms, P is logically necessary iff ⊨ P: it is a tautology, or provable from logical axioms without empirical content. In first-order logic, a logical necessity is a sentence true in every interpretation of the non-logical vocabulary — true regardless of how the world happens to be.

Logical possibility is accordingly the widest modality: anything that can be stated without self-contradiction is logically possible. A golden mountain involves no formal contradiction. A round square does — roundness entails no sharp corners, squareness entails sharp corners. Formal inconsistency is the only barrier.

## What Metaphysical Necessity Is, and Why It Differs

Metaphysical necessity is different in kind. A proposition is metaphysically necessary if it holds in every possible world — every genuine way reality might have been. Formally, P is metaphysically necessary iff □P is true, where □ is interpreted over the space of metaphysically possible worlds.

Logical necessity entails metaphysical necessity: any logical truth holds in every possible world, simply because there is no world in which it fails. But the converse fails. Consider Kripke's central examples. "Water is H₂O" is metaphysically necessary — in every possible world, water has this molecular structure, because water just *is* H₂O. There is no possible world where water exists but is something other than H₂O. Yet we could not have known this a priori. The discovery required Lavoisier and a great deal of subsequent chemistry. The necessity is metaphysical; the mode of discovery is empirical.

The formal argument is clean:

- P1: Names and natural kind terms are rigid designators — they pick out the same thing in every possible world where that thing exists.
- P2: "Water" and "H₂O" are rigid designators of the same substance.
- P3: If two rigid designators co-refer, then their identity statement is necessarily true whenever true.
- C: "Water is H₂O" is necessarily true.

Yet premise P2 is known only through empirical investigation. The conclusion is thus both necessary and a posteriori.

## The Two-by-Two Matrix

Before Kripke, the widespread assumption was that necessity and a priori knowability march together. Logical positivists identified the necessary with the analytic and the a priori as a single category. Kripke showed this was wrong. We now have four cells:

|                    | A priori       | A posteriori     |
|--------------------|----------------|------------------|
| **Necessary**      | "2+2=4"        | "Water is H₂O"   |
| **Contingent**     | "The meter bar is 1m" | "Caesar crossed the Rubicon" |

The upper-right cell — necessary a posteriori — was invisible before Kripke. So was the lower-left — contingent a priori, illustrated by the original definition of the meter, where "one meter" was fixed as the length of a particular bar in Paris, making it a priori that the bar was one meter long while the bar's length remained contingent. The a priori, the necessary, and the analytic are three genuinely distinct concepts.

## Two Objections Worth Considering

A conventionalist will say that "water is H₂O" is necessary only because we have decided that "water" means H₂O. The necessity is merely linguistic. But this understates the situation. The claim entails that any sample of water — past, future, anywhere in the universe — is composed of H₂O molecules. That goes far beyond any linguistic decision. Moreover, the necessity was discovered rather than stipulated: we did not define "water" as H₂O and then notice a tautology. We discovered that the thing our term picks out has this molecular structure. Discovery is not stipulation.

A Quinean will push harder: there is no principled distinction between logical and metaphysical necessity; both dissolve into a web of belief. But Kripke's necessary a posteriori is precisely not analytic — it resists classification as a truth of meaning. The distinctions Kripke draws are between modes of knowing (a priori versus a posteriori) and modes of being (necessary versus contingent). Quine's attack on the analytic/synthetic boundary does not obviously touch these independent distinctions.

## The Scope of Metaphysical Necessity

We should also notice a live dispute about how permissive the space of metaphysically possible worlds is. Kripke and Fine hold that the space of metaphysical possibilities is constrained by the essences of things: what is metaphysically possible is determined by what natures allow. On this view, some things logically possible may be metaphysically impossible. "Socrates is not human" is logically coherent — no formal contradiction — but may be metaphysically impossible, given that humanity is essential to Socrates. Metaphysical possibility is then a proper subset of logical possibility.

Timothy Williamson has argued instead that metaphysical modality is precisely logical modality: what is logically possible is what is metaphysically possible, and the essences of things add no further restrictions beyond logical consistency. On this view the two spaces coincide, making the modal space maximally permissive.

The consequences of this debate are wide. For philosophy of language, it touches the semantics of natural kind terms. For philosophy of mind, it shapes whether the mind-body identity theory entails a metaphysical necessity analogous to "water is H₂O." The dissociation between necessity and a priority — Kripke's central contribution — was the opening move that made all these questions tractable.
