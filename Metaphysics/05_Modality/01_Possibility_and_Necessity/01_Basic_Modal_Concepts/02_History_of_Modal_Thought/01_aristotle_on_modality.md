# Aristotle on Modality

Long before there was a formal modal logic, Aristotle noticed that things do not merely happen to be some way — they must be, or happen to be, or could have been otherwise. This triple distinction of necessary, contingent, and possible runs through his logic, his physics, and his metaphysics in ways that still repay careful attention. Aristotle was the first to give systematic treatment to modal inference; he was also the first to ask what, in the nature of things, grounds modal truth.

## The Modal Syllogistic

In the *Prior Analytics*, Aristotle extended his syllogistic to modal propositions, recognizing four forms: the necessary ("A necessarily belongs to all B," formalized as □∀x(Bx → Ax)), the contingent ("A possibly belongs to all B"), the merely possible, and the impossible. He analyzed what conclusions follow when premises of different modal types are combined — so-called "apodeictic" syllogisms where both premises are necessary, "problematic" syllogisms where they are possible, and mixed cases.

The mixed cases generated controversy that has lasted two millennia. Aristotle claims that from a necessary and an assertoric (plain, non-modal) premise, a necessary conclusion follows. From "Necessarily all animals are mortal" and "All humans are animals," he concludes "Necessarily all humans are mortal." Standard modal logic would resist this: the conclusion should be merely assertoric, since only the major premise carries necessity. The tension arises because Aristotle's system operates with de re necessity grounded in the natures of things — relative necessity, one might say — rather than the de dicto operator □ of modern systems. His logic and our logic are not straightforwardly translatable.

## Potentiality, Actuality, and De Re Modality

Aristotle's deepest modal ideas appear in his metaphysics, in the distinction between potentiality (dunamis) and actuality (energeia). Things are possibly F if they have the potentiality to be F; they are necessarily F if they cannot be otherwise given their nature. This is de re modality in its original form: modal properties attach to substances through their intrinsic natures, not through logical relations among propositions.

We should notice something important about this conception of potentiality: it is narrower than modern logical possibility. The lump of clay is potentially a statue, because becoming a statue is an actualization of dispositions the clay actually has. But the lump is not potentially a prime number — prime-numberhood is simply not the kind of thing a lump of clay could become, not because of any logical contradiction, but because no disposition of the clay is oriented toward mathematical properties. Possibility for Aristotle is grounded in the natures and capacities of actual things; it is not simply logical coherence.

The modal vocabulary of essence flows directly from this. An essential property of x is one x necessarily has given its nature — one x cannot lack while remaining the kind of thing it is. An accidental property is one x happens to have but could lack:

- □[x is human → x is rational]: Necessarily, any human is rational — because rationality is the defining nature of the human.
- ¬□[x is human → x is musical]: Not necessarily, any human is musical — being musical is accidental.

Aristotle's essentialism is naturalistic rather than stipulative: essences are discovered through biological and philosophical investigation of what things fundamentally are. The natural scientist who investigates the nature of a species is, on Aristotle's picture, in the business of discovering de re necessities.

## Absolute and Hypothetical Necessity, and Future Contingents

Aristotle also distinguished absolute necessity from conditional or hypothetical necessity (*Physics* II.9). Some things hold necessarily without qualification: mathematical truths, the incommensurability of the diagonal with the side of a square. Other things are necessary only given certain conditions: given that a house is to be built, materials are (conditionally) necessary. Much of what we call physical necessity — the necessity with which causes produce their effects — is for Aristotle a form of conditional necessity given the natures of things.

*De Interpretatione*, chapter 9, raises a different kind of modal problem: future contingents. Is "There will be a sea battle tomorrow" now true or false? If it is now determinately true, does determinism follow? Aristotle's solution is subtle and still disputed: he may be denying bivalence for future contingent statements — neither the prediction nor its negation is now true. Or he may be accepting bivalence while denying that determinate truth entails determinism. Either way, the modal content is explicit: a future contingent event is one for which ◇E ∧ ◇¬E — both possibilities are genuinely open. The question is whether this openness is compatible with there being a fact now about what will happen. This problem connects directly to Arthur Prior's tense logic and to contemporary debates about the open future, and Aristotle's tentative answer remains a live option in those debates.

## Aristotle's Legacy

What Aristotle established — the de re / de dicto distinction, the essential / accidental distinction, the absolute / conditional distinction, the connection between essence and necessity, the logical structure of modal inference — provided the framework for all subsequent work in modal metaphysics. Medieval logicians systematized and extended it; early modern philosophers either inherited or reacted against it. The neo-Aristotelian turn in contemporary metaphysics, associated with Fine, Loux, Oderberg, and others, has explicitly revived Aristotelian categories as the foundation for understanding essence, necessity, and the real definition of things. That revival is not antiquarianism — it represents a genuine alternative to the possible-worlds orthodoxy, one whose roots go back to Aristotle's conviction that modal truth is grounded in the natures of things themselves.
