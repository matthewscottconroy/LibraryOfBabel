# Medieval Modal Logic

Medieval philosophers approached modal logic under considerable pressure: they needed to give an account of God's power and knowledge that was both theologically defensible and philosophically coherent. What can an omnipotent being do? What does God know about contingent future events? Does foreknowledge eliminate contingency? These theological imperatives drove medieval thinkers to develop modal distinctions of genuine philosophical power — distinctions that, stripped of their theological framing, proved indispensable to twentieth-century analytic philosophy.

## Abelard and the De Dicto / De Re Distinction

Peter Abelard (1079–1142) gave the de dicto / de re distinction its canonical medieval formulation. A de dicto modal claim predicates necessity or possibility of a proposition: "Necessarily, all bachelors are unmarried." A de re modal claim predicates necessity or possibility of a thing with respect to a property: "Socrates is necessarily rational."

In formal terms:

- De dicto: □(∀x)(Bx → Ux) — the necessity governs the whole proposition.
- De re: (∀x)(Bx → □Ux) — the necessity governs the predication relative to each individual.

Abelard saw clearly that these two forms can come apart. "The man who is sitting is necessarily a man" is de dicto necessary — necessarily, a man is a man. But the man who happens to be sitting is only contingently sitting: the de re claim "the man who is sitting is necessarily sitting" is false. Scope matters enormously, and conflating the two forms generates errors that continue to appear in philosophical argument.

This distinction is the formal ancestor of Kripke's treatment of scope in quantified modal logic, and of Aquinas's celebrated analysis of divine foreknowledge: God necessarily knows the future (de dicto), without the future's events being necessary (de re). The logical tools are medieval; the applications extend to the present day.

## Duns Scotus: Absolute Possibility and Synchronic Contingency

John Duns Scotus (1266–1308) took a decisive step toward the modern notion of possible worlds. For Scotus, something is absolutely possible if it involves no contradiction — and this absolute possibility is independent of what actually exists. God's omnipotence extends to all absolute possibilities; which of these possibilities becomes actual is a matter of God's free will. The actual world is one member of a space of absolutely possible states; God freely actualizes one.

This picture foreshadows Lewis's plurality of worlds in a striking way: the space of absolutely possible states is fully real as a space, even if only one member of it is actualized. Modal truths hold across this space. The theological context drives Scotus to recognize a very wide range of possibilities — possibilities unconstrained by the actual except by the limit of consistency — in a way that anticipates the plenitude principles of contemporary modal realism.

Scotus also developed the important concept of synchronic contingency: a thing is synchronically contingent if, at the very instant it is actual, it *could have been otherwise*. This is not mere temporal contingency — the fact that things change over time — but genuine metaphysical contingency. The actuality of the thing excludes its not being actual, but the possibility of its not being actual was real at the very same instant. This subtle idea anticipates contemporary debates about the relationship between actuality and possibility. Scotus's modal ontological argument also prefigures Plantinga's: if an essentially necessary being is possible — if the concept is consistent — then it must be actual, because a necessarily existing being that was merely possible but non-actual would be a contradiction in terms.

## William of Ockham: Nominalism and Modal Discourse

William of Ockham (1287–1347) brought his nominalism to bear on modal discourse. Ockham argued that possibility and necessity are not features of abstract objects or universals but of individual things and propositions. His famous razor — do not multiply entities beyond necessity — applied directly to modal ontology: we should explain modal truth in terms of how individual things are and could be, not in terms of abstract possible worlds or Platonic forms.

When we say "It is possible for this stone to be warm," we say something about the stone and its intrinsic capacities — not about abstract possibilities. This places Ockham in an interesting relation to contemporary combinatorialists like Armstrong, who similarly try to ground modal truth in the actual capacities of actual things without invoking separate abstract or concrete possible worlds.

Ockham's treatment of divine foreknowledge of future contingents extended and sharpened Aristotle's. God knows contingent future events directly — by a kind of intuitive cognition of the future event itself — rather than by knowing necessities. This preserves the contingency of future events while maintaining omniscience, by distinguishing the mode of knowledge (direct, non-inferential) from its content (contingent events). It is a philosophically sophisticated move, and the modal point — that knowing something is compatible with its being contingent — is important independently of its theological application.

## The Theological Dimension

Medieval modal logic was embedded in theology in ways that forced precision. The central question was: what can God do? Scotus's answer was clear: God can do anything absolutely possible — anything that involves no contradiction. God cannot make a round square, not because this exceeds divine power, but because there is nothing to make: the description is incoherent, there is no possible object to create. This is the clearest medieval statement of the logical criterion for impossibility: impossibility is logical contradiction, not physical or natural impossibility.

Ockham drew a further distinction between God's absolute power (potentia absoluta) — extending to all logically possible states of affairs — and God's ordained power (potentia ordinata) — the power constrained by the choices God has actually made in creating the world and establishing its order. This distinction anticipates the modern distinction between metaphysical possibility (what is possible absolutely) and nomological possibility (what is possible given the actual laws). The boundary that Ockham drew for theological reasons is structurally the same boundary that contemporary philosophers of science draw when asking whether the laws of nature could have been different.

## Legacy

The medieval tradition preserved and transmitted modal logic through the scholastic curriculum into early modern philosophy. Leibniz, whose possible-worlds framework is the direct ancestor of modern modal semantics, was thoroughly schooled in scholastic logic. The Scotist concept of absolute possibility — logical consistency as the criterion of possibility, independent of actuality — was the core of Leibniz's modal framework. The de dicto/de re distinction survived into early modern philosophy through scholastic transmission, and was rediscovered — often without acknowledgment — by twentieth-century logicians developing quantified modal logic. The theological context that drove medieval thinkers to make these distinctions did not limit their application: it sharpened them.
