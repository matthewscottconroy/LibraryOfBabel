# From Leibniz to Lewis: The History of Possible Worlds

When a contemporary philosopher says "there is a possible world in which there are talking donkeys," what kind of claim is that? A metaphor? A formal device? A literal truth about a concrete reality as substantial as the one we inhabit? The answer you give to that question divides the major positions in modal metaphysics. But the concept of possible worlds did not begin as a technical device — it began as theology.

## Leibniz: The Theological Origin

For Leibniz, the question of possible worlds was inseparable from the question of creation. God conceives of infinitely many possible worlds — each a complete, internally consistent description of a way things could be — and creates the best one. A proposition is necessarily true if it is true in all possible worlds God conceives; contingently true if true in the actual world but not all possible ones; impossible if true in none. This gave the first systematic possible-worlds semantics, though embedded in a framework of divine intellect and volition.

Two technical contributions stand out. First, Leibniz analyzed necessary truth as truth in all possible worlds and contingent truth as truth in the actual but not all possible worlds — the analysis that, stripped of theology, became the foundation of Kripke semantics three centuries later. Second, his theodicy (*Theodicy*, 1710) argued that God's perfection ensures the actual world is the best among all possibilities. Voltaire savaged this in *Candide*, but the philosophical framework — a space of fully specified alternatives, one of which is actualized — endured.

Hume's contribution was simpler but influential. His conceivability criterion — nothing we can form a coherent idea of is absolutely impossible (*Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, II) — identified possibility with imaginability. This fed directly into twentieth-century discussions of the conceivability-possibility inference. Kripke's necessary a posteriori would provide the decisive counterexample: "water is not H₂O" is conceivable but metaphysically impossible.

## Kant: Necessity as Cognitive Structure

Kant transformed the modal landscape by making necessity a feature of our cognitive framework rather than of the world in itself. The categories of pure understanding — including necessity — are forms imposed by the mind on experience. Necessity is a feature of synthetic a priori judgments, not of things as they are in themselves.

The consequence is that necessity becomes transcendental rather than absolute: it holds for any possible experience governed by our cognitive form, not for all possible states of affairs simpliciter. The laws of Newtonian mechanics are necessary not because the physical world must be as Newton described, but because they express the form through which we experience the physical world. This is deeply revisionary. It was Kripke who showed, in the next philosophical era, that Kant's equation of necessity, a priority, and analyticity was mistaken — that these are three distinct concepts that come apart in important cases.

## C.I. Lewis: Formal Modal Logic

In the early twentieth century, C.I. Lewis developed the first formal modal logics, motivated by dissatisfaction with the material conditional (P → Q is true whenever P is false or Q is true, which validates the theorem "If it is raining, then grass is made of glass"). He sought a strict conditional that captures genuine necessary connection.

His systems S1 through S5, developed in *A Survey of Symbolic Logic* (1918) and *Symbolic Logic* (1932, with Langford), represent increasingly strong modal logics. S5 — with the axioms K, T, 4, and 5 — became the standard system for metaphysical necessity:

- K: □(P → Q) → (□P → □Q)
- T: □P → P
- 4: □P → □□P
- 5: ◇P → □◇P

S5 corresponds semantically to an equivalence relation on worlds: reflexive, symmetric, and transitive. In S5, what is necessary is necessarily necessary, and what is possible is necessarily possible. Modal truths are stable across worlds: no world can "see" additional possibilities that another cannot see.

## Kripke: Possible-Worlds Semantics

The semantics that made all this precise was developed by Saul Kripke, Jaakko Hintikka, Stig Kanger, and others in the late 1950s and early 1960s. Kripke published his first completeness results at the age of nineteen. A Kripke frame is a pair ⟨W, R⟩ — a set of possible worlds and an accessibility relation between them. A model adds a valuation function assigning truth values to atomic propositions at each world. The key semantic clauses:

- □P is true at world w iff P is true at every w' such that wRw'.
- ◇P is true at world w iff P is true at some w' such that wRw'.

Kripke's completeness theorems showed that each standard modal system is sound and complete with respect to its corresponding class of frames — that syntactic provability matches semantic validity. This was a landmark in mathematical logic. Then, in *Naming and Necessity* (1970/1980), Kripke applied the formal apparatus to substantive metaphysics: arguing for the necessary a posteriori and the essentiality of origin. The formal tools became the medium for claims about essence, identity, and natural kinds. This crossing from formal logic into metaphysics is what made the work transformative.

## David Lewis: Concrete Possible Worlds

David Lewis took the final step — and the most radical one. In *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986), Lewis argued that possible worlds are not abstract representations but concrete entities as real as the actual world. They are genuine alternative universes: maximal spatiotemporally connected systems of objects, causally and spatiotemporally isolated from one another.

"The actual world" is, on Lewis's view, an indexical: it picks out whichever world the speaker inhabits, just as "here" picks out the speaker's location. All worlds are ontologically on a par. Modal truths are truths about the distribution of properties and relations across the space of concrete worlds. Properties are sets of possible individuals; propositions are sets of possible worlds; causation, counterfactuals, laws, and content all receive possible-worlds analyses. The theoretical payoff is enormous. The ontological cost — infinitely many concrete universes, causally inaccessible to us — is equally enormous.

The history from Leibniz to Lewis is one of progressive secularization, formalization, and eventually radicalization. Leibniz's theological framework is stripped away; formal logic provides rigorous semantics; Lewis takes the semantic apparatus at face value and insists that the entities it posits — possible worlds — are real. Whether to accept that conclusion, or instead treat possible worlds as useful fictions or abstract ersatzes, is the central debate in the metaphysics of modality. All the subsequent discussion in these notes revolves around that question.
