# The Necessary A Posteriori

The Babylonians who named the morning star "Hesperus" and the evening star "Phosphorus" did not know they were naming the same planet. Centuries later, astronomers discovered that both names pick out Venus. This was an empirical discovery — it required observation, not logical inference. Yet once made, it seems to reveal something that could not have been otherwise: Hesperus could not have failed to be Phosphorus, because they are the same planet, and Venus could not have failed to be self-identical. The discovery was a posteriori; the discovered truth is necessary. This is Kripke's necessary a posteriori.

Before *Naming and Necessity* (1980), the combination seemed paradoxical. The tradition assumed that necessary truths are precisely those knowable a priori — by pure reason, independently of experience. Kripke showed that assumption was wrong. The necessary and the a priori are distinct categories. The consequences extend from the philosophy of language into the metaphysics of natural kinds and the philosophy of mind.

## The Core Examples

The paradigm cases are of several kinds. Natural kind identities: "Water is H₂O" is necessarily true, since water just *is* H₂O — in any possible world where water exists, it has this molecular structure. Yet we could not have known this without chemistry. "Gold is the element with atomic number 79" is the same sort of case. Identity statements between proper names: "Hesperus is Phosphorus" required astronomical observation, but the identity, once established, is necessary — Venus could not have been two things. Essentiality of origin: "The Queen is the daughter of King George VI" is necessarily true (if Kripke's essentiality of origin is correct), yet it is known through the historical record, not through pure reason.

## The Argument

Kripke's argument is grounded in the theory of rigid designation:

- P1: Names like "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" are rigid designators — they pick out the same object in all possible worlds where that object exists.
- P2: "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" both rigidly designate Venus.
- P3: If a = b, and "a" and "b" are rigid designators, then "a = b" is necessarily true — since in every world, a = a = b.
- P4: Hesperus = Phosphorus (empirically discovered).
- C: Necessarily, Hesperus = Phosphorus.

Yet P4 is a posteriori — it required empirical investigation. So the conclusion is both necessary and a posteriori. The key is seeing why philosophers failed to notice this earlier: they confused the epistemic question of how we know "Hesperus is Phosphorus" (a posteriori) with the metaphysical question of whether it could have been otherwise (it could not). The appearance of contingency is epistemic, not metaphysical.

## Explaining the Appearance of Contingency

A natural objection: we can surely imagine a possible world where Hesperus and Phosphorus are different planets — a world where the terms refer to different things. Doesn't this show contingency?

Kripke's reply distinguishes two scenarios. What we can imagine is a world where the descriptions that actually fix reference to Venus — "the morning star," "the evening star" — are satisfied by different heavenly bodies. In such a world, if we use "Hesperus" to mean "the morning star," it would refer to a different object. But the rigid designator "Hesperus," as we actually use it, picks out Venus — not whatever plays the morning-star role. There is no possible world where Hesperus (Venus) fails to be Phosphorus (Venus), because Venus is necessarily self-identical.

The appearance of contingency arises from conflating the rigid names with the non-rigid descriptions that happen to have fixed their reference. The descriptions vary across possible worlds; the names do not. The epistemic situation — we did not know a priori that they co-referred — gives rise to the illusion of metaphysical contingency.

## Natural Kind Terms and the Philosophy of Mind

The extension to natural kind terms is the most philosophically consequential application. "Pain is C-fiber firing" — if true, this is necessarily true (identity is necessary between rigid designators of kinds). But we know it only through neuroscience.

Kripke, however, argued that this identity, even if necessary, generates a distinctive problem for physicalism. With "water is H₂O," we can explain the appearance of contingency: we can imagine being in epistemic circumstances where what we call "water" turns out to have a different structure, so the conceivability of water not being H₂O reflects a genuine epistemic possibility (a different underlying chemistry) rather than a metaphysical one. But with pain, the phenomenal character seems to be the very essence of what we are referring to with "pain" — there is no reference-fixing description that could be imagined satisfied by something other than the phenomenal property. So if pain = C-fiber firing, the appearance that this could have been otherwise ought to be explainable in the same way as the water case — but it does not seem to be. Kripke regards this as evidence against the identity theory, or at least as showing that it faces a burden of explanation that the natural kind identities do not.

## Two-Dimensional Semantics

Two-dimensional semantics (Chalmers, Jackson) provides a framework for systematically analyzing the necessary a posteriori. The primary intension of "water" picks out the actual watery stuff — whatever the watery stuff of our world is. The secondary intension rigidly picks out H₂O in all counterfactual scenarios. "Water is H₂O" is contingent with respect to its primary intension (at a world where the watery stuff is XYZ, the primary intension of "water" picks out XYZ, not H₂O) but necessary with respect to its secondary intension (water, i.e., H₂O, is necessarily H₂O). The a posteriori character reflects the primary dimension; the necessity reflects the secondary.

This framework systematizes the Kripkean insight: the necessary a posteriori is the result of a term whose primary and secondary intensions diverge. We discover empirically which substance the primary intension picks out; once discovered, that substance's identity is necessary. If some necessities are known a posteriori, the epistemology of modality cannot be simply the epistemology of pure reason — empirical investigation can itself be a route to modal knowledge, and that is a remarkable result.
