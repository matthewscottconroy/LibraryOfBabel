# The Contingent A Priori

Alongside the necessary a posteriori, Kripke identified another surprising category: the contingent a priori. These are truths that are contingent — they could have been otherwise — yet knowable a priori, without empirical investigation. Like the necessary a posteriori, this category was invisible to philosophers who assumed the a priori/a posteriori and necessary/contingent distinctions perfectly aligned.

## The Standard Meter

The clearest example involves the original definition of the meter. Suppose "one meter" is introduced with the reference-fixing intention: *let "one meter" denote the length of the standard meter bar (Bar S) in Paris*. Then:

"The standard meter bar is one meter long"

can be known a priori by anyone who grasps this stipulation. No measurement is needed — the definition guarantees it. Yet this proposition is contingent: Bar S could have been manufactured to a different length. If it had been, "one meter" would have referred to that different length, and Bar S would not have been one meter long by that standard. The contingency concerns the length of the physical object; the a priori character concerns the definitional relationship between the term and that object.

The structure is reference-fixing by description. "One meter" is introduced by fixing its reference to the actual length of Bar S, not by describing what that length is. This makes the resulting claim a priori (knowable from the stipulation alone) while leaving it contingent (Bar S could have been otherwise).

## The General Pattern

This case generalizes. Consider Kripke's "Julius" example: "Julius" is introduced as a name for whoever actually invented the zip. Then "Julius invented the zip" is a priori (it follows from the introduction of the name) but contingent (someone else might have invented it; Julius, whoever he actually is, might not have been the inventor).

The structure:
- "Julius" is defined as whoever actually invented the zip.
- A priori: If the zip was invented, Julius invented it — by stipulation.
- Contingent: Julius (the actual inventor) might not have invented the zip — someone else could have done so.

"Julius invented the zip" is a priori because it follows from the stipulation and the bare existence of the invention. It is contingent because the actual inventor's inventing the zip is a contingent fact about that person.

## Two-Dimensional Analysis

Two-dimensional semantics (Davies and Humberstone, 1980; Chalmers, 2004) provides the systematic framework. Expressions have two intensions:

- **Primary intension**: what the expression picks out in world w, considered as actual. For "one meter," the primary intension assigns to each world the length that Bar S has in that world.
- **Secondary intension**: what the expression picks out in world w, considered as counterfactual, with the actual world fixed. For "one meter," the secondary intension rigidly picks out the actual length of Bar S — approximately 39.37 inches.

"Bar S is one meter long" is a priori because its primary intension is true at every world considered as actual — in any world you consider, Bar S has the length that "one meter" (by its primary intension) denotes there. It is contingent because its secondary intension can be false at counterfactual worlds where Bar S has a different length than it has actually.

The a priori character is explained by the primary intension; the contingency by the secondary intension. The two dimensions come apart, which is exactly the point.

## Philosophical Significance

The contingent a priori matters because it shows that a priori knowledge does not guarantee necessity. We can know something a priori that is nevertheless contingent — that depends on how things happen to be. This undermines the traditional Kantian equation of a priori with necessary.

An objection due to Evans deserves mention. One might argue that "Bar S is one meter long" is not really contingent: what the sentence expresses is not a fact about a physical object but something about the meaning of "one meter," and that meaning could not have been otherwise. So the proposition is necessary after all.

Kripke's reply: this conflates use and mention. The name "one meter" was introduced via a contingent reference-fixing description. Even if the linguistic meaning is fixed necessarily by the stipulation, what the stipulation refers to — the length of Bar S — is contingent. The proposition expresses a contingent fact about a physical object (that it has a certain length), even if the terms are introduced by reference-fixing. The physical fact is contingent; that is what matters.

A second objection: these cases are all highly artificial, involving definitional stipulations. Does the contingent a priori occur outside technical philosophical examples?

The reply is about the principle, not the examples. Whatever one thinks about the scope of the category, the conceptual point is established: a priori knowability and metaphysical necessity are genuinely independent. Even if the examples are somewhat artificial, the principle they illustrate — that how we come to know something (a priori vs. a posteriori) and whether it could have been otherwise (necessary vs. contingent) are different questions — is real and consequential for both the epistemology of modality and the foundations of semantics.
