# References and Primary Sources: Basic Modal Concepts

## Foundational Texts

- Aristotle, *De Interpretatione* (c. 350 BCE). Chapters 9 and 12–13 present the earliest systematic treatment of modal terms and their logical behavior, including the famous sea-battle argument about future contingents.

- Leibniz, G.W., *Theodicy* (1710). Introduces the concept of possible worlds as a theological and metaphysical tool, grounding necessity in what holds across all possibilities and contingency in what holds only in some.

- Kripke, Saul, *Naming and Necessity* (1980). The published version of 1970 lectures that transformed modal metaphysics; distinguishes epistemic from metaphysical modality and revives essentialism using possible-worlds language.

- Lewis, David, *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986). Defends modal realism, arguing that possible worlds are as real as the actual world and that modal truths reduce to truths about these worlds.

## Contemporary Works

- Williamson, Timothy, *Modal Logic as Metaphysics* (2013). Argues that modal logic has direct metaphysical import and defends necessitism about objects—the view that every object necessarily exists.

- Sider, Theodore, "Reductive Theories of Modality," in *The Oxford Handbook of Metaphysics* (2003). A clear survey of attempts to reduce modal facts to non-modal ones, including Lewisian and actualist strategies.
