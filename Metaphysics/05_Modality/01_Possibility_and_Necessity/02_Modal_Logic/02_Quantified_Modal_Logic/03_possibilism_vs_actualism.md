# Possibilism Versus Actualism in Quantified Modal Logic

When we say "there might have been a golden mountain," do we thereby incur an ontological commitment to a golden mountain of some kind — a golden mountain that exists in some possible world, even if not in this one? Possibilists say yes. Actualists say no: there are no objects other than actual ones, and our modal discourse about golden mountains can be cashed out without quantifying over non-actual entities. This debate connects directly to quantified modal logic, the Barcan Formula, and some of the deepest questions about what there is.

## Possibilism

Possibilists hold that the domain of objects includes not only actual things but also merely possible things — things that could have existed but do not. When we say "there might have been a golden mountain," the possibilist takes this to be about a specific possible object — the golden mountain — that exists in the space of possibilities even though it is not actual.

Formally: there exist objects x such that x does not belong to the domain of the actual world but does exist in some accessible world. Possibilism supports unrestricted quantification over possible individuals and validates the Barcan Formula: if possibly something is F, then there is something — a possible object — that is possibly F.

Meinong's original possibilism was extreme: not only possible but impossible objects (like round squares) have some form of being. Contemporary possibilism is more restrained, restricting the non-actual domain to genuinely possible objects. Lewis's modal realism is the most developed possibilist view: the inhabitants of other worlds are concrete realities, equally real as actual individuals, merely spatiotemporally isolated from us. "Merely possible" is a perspective-relative indexical label, not a mark of inferior ontological status.

## Actualism

Actualism holds that the only objects are actual ones. Everything that exists is among the actual things: ∀x∃w_actual(x ∈ D(w_actual)). When we say "there might have been a golden mountain," we are not quantifying over a non-actual golden mountain; we are saying something about how the world could have been, without presupposing the existence of a non-actual entity.

The advantage of actualism is parsimony: it avoids the exotic ontological category of non-actual objects. The challenge is explaining our apparently true modal discourse about things that don't exist — how can "there might have been a golden mountain" be true if there is no golden mountain, even a non-actual one?

Plantinga's actualism addresses this through haecceities and abstract states of affairs. There are no non-actual individuals, but there are haecceities — abstract individual essences — that exist necessarily as abstract objects. "Possibly there exists a Sherlock Holmes" means there is a possible world (a maximal state of affairs that possibly obtains) in which the Holmesian haecceity is instantiated. Holmes doesn't exist, but his essence exists necessarily as an abstract object. Possible worlds can represent possibilities about individuals through their individual essences without requiring those individuals to exist non-actually.

## Lewis's Possibilism: Modal Realism in Detail

Lewis's possibilism provides a reductive analysis of modal claims. "Possibly a golden mountain exists" is true because at some concrete world, there is a golden mountain — a real concrete entity. Modal claims reduce to claims about the distribution of properties across concrete worlds. "Actual" is indexical — it picks out the world the speaker inhabits — so every individual is actual relative to its own world. From our perspective, the inhabitants of other worlds are merely possible; from their perspective, we are.

The advantage: possible worlds provide explicit, concrete truthmakers for modal claims. The disadvantage: the ontological cost is enormous — Lewis posits infinitely many concrete universes.

## The Logic of the Debate

The formal connection between this debate and QML runs through domain conditions:

- Possibilism: variable or expanding domains, with possible objects existing in some worlds but not others. The Barcan Formula may fail: ◇∃xFx ↛ ∃x◇Fx, because the witness for the antecedent may be a merely possible object not in the actual domain.
- Actualism (Plantinga-style): inner/outer domain semantics, with haecceities playing the role of actual representatives of possible individuals. The Barcan Formula is handled through the existence of haecceities.
- Necessitism (Williamson): constant domains, with everything existing necessarily in a thin sense. The Barcan Formula holds, but its bite is defused by thinning the concept of existence.

## The Deeper Disagreement

The debate may have a verbal dimension. "There exists a possible golden mountain" might just mean "A golden mountain is possible" — which both possibilists and actualists agree with. The dispute about whether there are "objects" in modal space may partly concern how to interpret quantifiers in modal discourse rather than an underlying modal fact.

But the dispute is not purely verbal. Lewis and Plantinga genuinely disagree about the ontological inventory: Lewis thinks concrete possible individuals are as real as actual ones; Plantinga thinks all there is are actual things. This difference generates different predictions about which formal principles are valid in QML — specifically about the Barcan Formula — and that formal divergence is a substantive disagreement, not merely a terminological one. The possibilism/actualism debate is one of the central issues at the intersection of formal modal logic and the metaphysics of what there is.
