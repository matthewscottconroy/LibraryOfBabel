# Review Questions — Possibility and Necessity

1. Logical necessity entails metaphysical necessity, but not vice versa. What does the existence of this gap — illustrated by "water is H₂O" — reveal about the relationship between meaning and the structure of reality? What would have to be true for the gap to close, and who in this chapter argues that it does?

2. Kripke's discovery of the necessary a posteriori requires separating three concepts that Kant and the logical positivists ran together: the necessary, the a priori, and the analytic. Which of these distinctions is doing the most philosophical work in establishing that "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is necessary yet knowable only empirically? Could one accept the necessary a posteriori while rejecting rigid designation, or is rigid designation the indispensable premise?

3. The contingent a priori — illustrated by the original meter bar — shows that a priori knowability does not guarantee necessity. Evans objected that the proposition is not genuinely contingent; Kripke replied that this conflates use and mention. Reconstruct both sides of this exchange and assess which is more persuasive.

4. Epistemic modality and metaphysical modality can diverge: what is epistemically possible (for all one knows) need not be metaphysically possible (genuinely realizable). Yet Chalmers argues that *ideal* positive conceivability entails *primary* metaphysical possibility. What exactly is the two-dimensional framework supposed to accomplish here, and why do physicalists think it does not deliver the kind of possibility that matters for debates about the mind?

5. Aristotle's de re modality is grounded in the natures and potentialities of actual things — "possible" means capable of actualization given what something is, not logically coherent. How does this differ from the space of logical possibility, and does the difference matter when modern modal logic treats all logically consistent descriptions as representing genuine possibilities?

6. Duns Scotus's concept of synchronic contingency — that something is contingent if, at the very instant it is actual, it could have been otherwise — anticipates contemporary debates about whether actuality is compatible with genuine metaphysical possibility. How does this medieval distinction bear on Lewis's later claim that "actual" is an indexical rather than a mark of metaphysical privilege?

7. The Barcan Formula — if possibly something is F, then there is something that is possibly F — is valid in constant-domain quantified modal logic but fails in variable-domain models. What ontological commitment does validity of the Barcan Formula entail, and how do Lewis, Plantinga, and Williamson each arrive at different verdicts about it from their respective frameworks?

8. The modal ontological argument in S5 is formally valid: from ◇□(God exists), □(God exists) follows. The chapter notes that all philosophical work falls on the premise. How does the choice of modal system (S4 versus S5) affect the argument's validity, and what does this reveal about the relationship between substantive metaphysical commitments and the selection of a modal logic?
