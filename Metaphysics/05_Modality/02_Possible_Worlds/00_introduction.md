# Possible Worlds

"There is a possible world in which you became a musician instead of reading this book." This talk of possible worlds is ubiquitous in contemporary philosophy, serving as a framework for analyzing modality, counterfactuals, propositions, properties, and a vast range of other concepts. But what are possible worlds, exactly? Are they genuinely existing concrete realities like our own, or are they merely abstract representations, or useful fictions, or primitive posits?

David Lewis's modal realism is the most audacious answer: possible worlds are genuine concrete realities, just as real as the actual world, differing from it only in not being spatiotemporally related to us. When we say something is possible, we are saying it is true in some concrete world of the same kind as ours. The actual world is the one we inhabit; it is not privileged except by being ours. This view is philosophically systematic: it provides reductive analyses of possibility, necessity, counterfactuals, properties, propositions, and causation. Its cost is an enormous ontology of concrete worlds, inhabited by counterparts of actual things.

The alternatives — grouped under the heading of "ersatzism" — hold that possible worlds are abstract objects that *represent* ways things could be, rather than concrete realities that *are* ways things are. On linguistic ersatzism, a world is a maximal consistent set of sentences; on propositional ersatzism, it is a maximal consistent set of propositions; on structural ersatzism (associated with Sider and others), it is a maximal instantiable structure. These views avoid Lewis's extravagant ontology at the cost of raising questions about the nature of the abstract objects posited and the primitive modality that governs them.

Actualism — the view that only the actual world exists — must give a different account: modal claims are true or false but do not quantify over genuinely existing non-actual worlds. The actualist must explain what makes a non-actual possibility real without appealing to non-actual concrete realizers. This chapter develops these positions and the arguments for each, setting up the investigation of essence and essentialism that follows.
