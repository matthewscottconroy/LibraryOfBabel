# Lewis's Modal Realism

David Lewis's modal realism is one of the most audacious and most carefully argued positions in twentieth-century metaphysics. Lewis held that possible worlds are not abstract constructions, linguistic descriptions, or fictional entities: they are concrete universes, as real as the actual world, existing in their own spacetime systems, populated by physical objects, living beings, and everything else we can conceive to be possible. The actual world is not ontologically privileged; it is simply the world we happen to inhabit, marked out by the indexical "actual" just as "here" marks the place we happen to be.

The theoretical benefits of modal realism are remarkable in their breadth. With possible worlds as concrete entities, Lewis could analyze modality without using modal primitives: "it is possible that P" means "there exists a possible world in which P"; "it is necessary that P" means "P is true in every possible world." Counterfactual conditionals — "if P had been the case, Q would have been" — are analyzed as claims about the truth of Q in the possible worlds most similar to the actual world where P is true. Properties are sets of possible individuals across all worlds; propositions are sets of possible worlds. The entire modal vocabulary is analyzed in non-modal terms, grounded in the concrete plurality of worlds.

The cost of modal realism is ontological extravagance of a spectacular kind. An infinite plurality of concrete universes, each as real as our own, is a large theoretical commitment. Lewis argued that this commitment is justified by the theoretical benefits — just as our commitment to spacetime points and electrons is justified by the theoretical benefits of physical theory. But many philosophers find the ontological price too high, suspecting that possible worlds can be understood more economically as abstract constructions without the full metaphysical weight of genuinely existing concrete universes.

This section examines Lewis's positive case for modal realism, the theoretical unity it provides, and the most serious objections — the incredulous stare, the problem of isolation, the problem of actuality — and Lewis's responses.
