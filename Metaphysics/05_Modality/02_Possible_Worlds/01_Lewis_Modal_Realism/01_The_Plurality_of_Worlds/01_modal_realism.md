# Lewis's Modal Realism

When we say "there might have been talking donkeys," what are we saying something true about? The natural answer is: about possibility, about how things could have been. But possibility seems to demand something to make it true. Lewis's answer was as simple as it was radical: talking donkeys really exist. Not here, not in our universe, but in one of the infinitely many concrete universes that are just as real as ours. Modal realism — the thesis at the heart of *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986) — is the view that possible worlds are not abstract representations or useful fictions but genuine concrete realities.

## The Core Thesis

The thesis has a stark simplicity. There are many worlds. Each world is a maximal spatiotemporally connected system of objects. No world is more real than any other — the actual world is actual only in the indexical sense that "here" picks out the speaker's location. What we call "the actual world" is simply the world we inhabit; its inhabitants call it actual, just as we call ours actual. All worlds are ontologically on a par.

Modal operators, on this picture, are quantifiers over concrete worlds:

- ◇P is true iff there is a possible world w at which P is true.
- □P is true iff P is true at all possible worlds.
- @P (actually P) is true at w iff P is true at the world of the context.

"Possibly there are talking donkeys" means there is a concrete world — a genuine universe — containing talking donkeys. The quantifier is unrestricted: it ranges over real concrete entities.

Lewis supports a **principle of recombination** to guarantee plenitude: "Anything can coexist with anything else, at least provided they occupy distinct spatiotemporal positions. Anything can fail to coexist with anything else" (*Plurality*, p. 87–88). This grounds the modal realist's claim that the space of worlds is maximally rich: every way things could be is a way some world is, including worlds containing alien properties never instantiated in ours.

## The Analytical Payoff

Lewis's primary motivation was not metaphysical enthusiasm for concrete universes — it was theoretical. Modal realism "earns its keep" by providing a unified analysis of a strikingly wide range of concepts:

**Propositions**: The proposition that P is the set of possible worlds at which P is true. Propositions are sets of worlds — a clean, precise account requiring no sui generis propositional entities.

**Properties**: A property is a set of possible individuals (from all worlds). Properties are abundant — every set of possible individuals constitutes a property. This allows properties to be as fine-grained as needed.

**Counterfactuals**: "If A were the case, C would follow" (A □→ C) is true iff the closest possible world where A obtains is a world where C also obtains. Closeness is similarity to the actual world.

**Causation**: Causal facts are analyzed in terms of counterfactual dependence, which is analyzed in terms of possible worlds. C causes E iff, in the closest world where C doesn't occur, E doesn't occur either.

**Dispositions**: X is fragile iff, in the closest world where X is dropped, X shatters. Dispositions are counterfactual regularities.

**Laws of nature**: Laws are regularities identified by the "best system" — the systemization of the actual world's regularities that best balances simplicity and strength (the Ramsey-Lewis account).

The theoretical payoff is supposed to offset the cost of positing infinitely many concrete worlds. Lewis was explicit that modal realism is not empirically testable — it is assessed by theoretical virtues: systematicity, fecundity of analysis, and explanatory power.

## Individuation of Worlds

Worlds are individuated by spatiotemporal connectivity. Two individuals are in the same world iff they are spatiotemporally connected (directly or through a chain of connected pairs); in different worlds iff they are spatiotemporally isolated from each other. This criterion is non-circular: it does not define worlds in terms of possible worlds or modal concepts. It is naturalistic — spatiotemporal relations are the most fundamental concrete relations we know of.

The criterion has challenges: quantum entanglement, multiverse interpretations of quantum mechanics, and abstract mathematical objects all raise questions. But Lewis held that spatiotemporal connectivity is the best available criterion for world-individuation, and that these challenges, though genuine, do not undermine the general framework.

## The Plenitude and Its Implications

Lewis's plenitude principle generates an extraordinarily rich modal space. Combined with recombination, plenitude implies that every logically consistent property distribution is realized at some world, that there are worlds with alien properties (properties not instantiated in our world), and that the number of possible worlds is not merely infinite but uncountably infinite — for any finite description of a world, there are uncountably many worlds satisfying that description with different fine-grained distributions of properties.

This plenitude is both the greatest strength and the most controversial feature of the view. It guarantees that modal quantifiers range over a maximally rich domain. But it also generates the ontological commitment that strikes most philosophers as extravagant — the commitment that Lewis himself acknowledged generates the incredulous stare.

Whether the analyses modal realism enables are so systematic and powerful as to justify the cost is a debate about the proper methodology of metaphysics: how to weigh ontological parsimony against theoretical unification and explanatory power. Lewis argued the case explicitly and in detail; his critics have not lacked for responses. The debate continues.
