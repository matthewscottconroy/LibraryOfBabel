# Arguments for Modal Realism

Lewis was careful to describe his case for modal realism as a cumulative one rather than a knockdown proof. No single argument compels acceptance, but each one shifts the theoretical balance, and taken together they constitute a formidable case. Understanding these arguments is essential for assessing whether the theoretical benefits justify the ontological costs — and for understanding what the alternatives must offer if they are to compete.

## The Analysis Argument

The primary motivation is analytical: modal realism provides the best available analyses of a wide range of concepts we are already committed to. The argument runs:

- P1: The following analyses are correct, or best available: propositions are sets of possible worlds; properties are sets of possible individuals; causation is analyzed in terms of counterfactual dependence; counterfactuals are analyzed in terms of closeness of possible worlds; dispositions are analyzed in terms of what would happen in nearby worlds; laws of nature are identified via the Ramsey-Lewis best-system analysis.
- P2: These analyses presuppose that possible worlds and possible individuals are genuine entities.
- C: Possible worlds and possible individuals are genuine entities.

This is inference to the best analysis: the theoretical cost of modal realism is paid by the systematic work it does. If the analyses are correct, we are already committed to the entities that make them true. Lewis acknowledges that each analysis faces objections — the counterfactual analysis of causation has been challenged by preemption and overdetermination cases; the best-systems analysis of laws has been challenged by the arbitrariness of the simplicity metric. But Lewis's claim is that his analyses are the best available, and that the alternatives face equally serious or worse problems.

## The Argument from Uniformity and Indexicality

Lewis argues that "actual" is an indexical like "here" or "now." Just as "here" refers to the speaker's location and "now" to the time of utterance, "actual" refers to the world of the speaker. "My world is actual" is trivially true for any world-inhabitant — just as "I am here" is trivially true at any context.

This uniform treatment of actuality avoids making an arbitrary metaphysical distinction between the actual and the merely possible. All worlds are ontologically on a par; the only difference is indexical, not metaphysical. This is analogous to eternalism about time: all times are ontologically on a par, with "present" being a contextual label rather than a mark of metaphysical privilege.

Critics argue the temporal analogy is imperfect: "here" picks out a region of a single physical space we all share, while "actual" on the modal realist view picks out one of infinitely many disconnected universes. The indexicality of spatial terms does not obviously generalize across causally isolated universes. Lewis holds that this is simply what the indexicality amounts to in the modal case — the intuition that our world is uniquely real is an artifact of occupying it, analogous to the intuition that our present moment is uniquely real.

## The Argument from Plenitude and Truthmakers

Our modal reasoning presupposes a wide range of genuine possibilities. We treat modal claims as genuinely true or false. If these claims are genuine truths, they require truthmakers: something in virtue of which they are true. The natural truthmakers for modal claims are possible worlds.

- P1: All truths have truthmakers.
- P2: "Possibly there are dragons" is a genuine truth.
- P3: The truthmaker for this truth must be a dragon or dragon-containing reality of some kind.
- C: There is a genuine possible world containing dragons.

Critics of the truthmaker principle (nominalists) reject P1. Others accept P1 but argue that abstract states of affairs or ersatz worlds can serve as truthmakers. Lewis argues that abstract truthmakers face a "representation problem": abstract entities represent possibilities only in virtue of a further account of their representational capacity, which threatens regress. Concrete worlds, by contrast, do not represent — they just *are*. No further representational account is needed. When we say "the world where dragons exist is a concrete world containing dragons," the dragons are there, not merely represented.

## The Argument from Parsimony of Kinds

The most common objection to modal realism is that it is ontologically extravagant. Lewis responds by distinguishing parsimony of kinds from parsimony of individuals. Ockham's razor, properly understood, recommends against multiplying *kinds* of entity without necessity — not against multiplying individual tokens of the same kind.

Modal realism does not introduce new kinds of entity: possible worlds and their inhabitants are made of the same stuff as actual things — concrete physical objects and their properties. What modal realism multiplies is the number of concrete individuals, not the number of kinds. Actualist alternatives (Plantinga's states of affairs, linguistic ersatzism's abstract sentences) introduce genuinely new kinds of entity: abstract objects of various types.

If parsimony of kinds is what matters, modal realism may actually be more parsimonious than its rivals. This argument is less persuasive to those who hold that sheer number of individuals is also a theoretical cost — that ontological extravagance in quantity, not just in kind, is a genuine defect. The dialectic turns on a substantive question about what Ockham's razor is really asking us to minimize.

## Assessment

Lewis's case is a paradigm of systematic philosophical argument. The cumulative case is formidable — each of the analyses he offers is genuinely illuminating, and the theoretical unity achieved across propositions, properties, causation, counterfactuals, laws, and content is impressive. Whether it is ultimately compelling depends on one's views about the relative weights of theoretical virtues, the acceptability of concrete worlds causally isolated from us, and the availability of competing analyses that provide similar benefits at lower cost. These are questions the rest of the section on possible worlds takes up directly.
