# The Actuality Operator and Indexicality

One of the most philosophically charged features of Lewis's modal realism is his treatment of actuality: the claim that "the actual world" does not pick out a metaphysically special world but picks out whichever world the speaker inhabits — just as "here" picks out the speaker's location and "now" picks out the time of utterance. Actuality is indexical, not absolute.

## The Formal Actuality Operator

Formally, we can enrich modal logic with an actuality operator @:

- @P is true at world w (in a model M) iff P is true at the designated actual world w* of the model.

The actual world w* is fixed as a parameter of the model — in two-dimensional semantics, it is the world of the context of utterance. The actuality operator is a **rigidifier**: @P evaluated at any world gives the truth value of P at the actual world, regardless of which world is currently being evaluated.

This allows distinctions the bare modal operators cannot capture. ◇P says P is true at some accessible world. ◇@P says: at some accessible world w, @P is true — which means P is true at the actual world w*. So ◇@P says something possible such that, when the evaluation shifts to that possible world, P remains true at the actual world w*. This is equivalent simply to: P is true at w* (since @P is true everywhere iff P is true at w*). The distinction between ◇P and ◇@P matters in two-dimensional contexts, where the primary and secondary intensions of terms can diverge.

## Indexical Versus Absolute Actuality

Lewis's indexical treatment is in direct contrast with "absolute" accounts of actuality. On the absolute view, "actual" applies uniquely to the world we inhabit — the world that is real in the full, concrete sense, while other possible worlds merely represent possibilities. Plantinga and most actualists take this view.

On the absolute account, the actual world has a special metaphysical status that other worlds lack. Possible worlds, if posited at all, must be something other than concrete realities — they must be abstract representations of a philosophically inferior sort. The actual world is special not merely from our perspective but absolutely.

Lewis argues that this view is incoherent if possible worlds are concrete realities. If all worlds are equally real, none can be metaphysically special — the actual world can only be special relative to us. The indexical treatment is compelled by the conjunction of modal realism and the ontological parity of worlds. The "absolute actuality" view smuggles in a metaphysical distinction that the realist cannot make sense of.

## Implications

For de re actuality claims: "Actually, water is H₂O" is true iff at the world of the context, water is H₂O. The actuality operator rigidifies the truth conditions to the world of utterance — a useful device for distinguishing what is actually the case from what is merely possible.

For modal embeddings: the sentence "It might have been the case that what is actually water is not water" can be represented as ◇(@water ≠ water). Using the actuality operator: @water rigidly picks out H₂O (since that's what water actually is). The sentence then says: at some possible world, H₂O is not water. But if "water" is also rigid, H₂O = water at every world where water exists — so the sentence is false. The rigidification of @-operator combined with rigid designators gives tools for precisely representing the semantic content of modal claims.

## The Metaphysics of Actuality

Peter van Inwagen and Plantinga have argued that absolute actuality is non-negotiable: the intuition that our world is uniquely real — not merely contextually "ours" — is a datum of modal thought. Lewis responds that this intuition is an artifact of being embedded in a world: we see our world from the inside and naturally regard it as uniquely real, just as we naturally regard our present time as uniquely real. The intuition does not track a metaphysical distinction; it merely reflects our perspective.

Whether Lewis or the absolute-actuality theorist is right here depends on one's prior commitments about the ontological status of possible worlds. If one is already a modal realist, the indexical account is the only coherent option. If one is not, the absolute account seems more natural. The debate is largely driven by prior metaphysical commitments rather than settled by independent arguments. What makes it philosophically significant is that the choice between indexical and absolute actuality is not an isolated terminological matter — it is a fundamental commitment about the ontological inventory of modal reality.
