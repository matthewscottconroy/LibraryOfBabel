# The Incredulous Stare

Lewis named it himself: the incredulous stare. It is the expression on the face of a philosopher who has just been told that there are infinitely many concrete universes, every one of them as real as the one we inhabit — universes containing every combination of objects and properties consistent with logic. The starer does not offer a counter-argument. They simply find the thesis preposterous, and they believe their reaction to be evidence.

Lewis took this seriously. "The incredulous stare is a confrontation between rival philosophical intuitions. They speak for themselves, so I shall say no more about them. I would rather discuss objections that take the form of arguments" (*Plurality*, p. 133–134). He acknowledged that sheer implausibility is a genuine theoretical cost, not merely a psychological embarrassment.

## The Nature of the Objection

The incredulous stare is not a formal argument but an expression of a powerful pre-theoretical conviction: there is only one concrete universe — ours. The claim that there are infinitely many equally real concrete universes, causally and spatiotemporally isolated from ours, containing all manner of beings and events, strikes most people not merely as surprising but as metaphysical fantasy. It is not clear how to weigh this conviction as evidence, but Lewis never denied that it carries some weight.

The deeper issue is the formal claim behind the stare. Lewis's realism depends on a **plenitude principle**: for every consistent combinatorial description of a world, there exists a concrete world satisfying it. This is an existential claim of extraordinary strength — it posits concrete objects for every logical possibility. The incredulous starer denies this existential claim. The denial is coherent: "the fact that a description is logically consistent does not entail that there is a concrete world satisfying it." This is a perfectly defensible position, and what the starer finds unconvincing is Lewis's answer that the plenitude principle earns its keep through theoretical analysis.

## Lewis's Responses

Lewis made several responses to the incredulous stare, none individually decisive.

First, the theoretical benefits argument: modal realism provides unified analyses of propositions, properties, causation, counterfactuals, and other important concepts. If no other view provides analyses of equal quality, the theoretical gains may justify the ontological extravagance. This is an invitation to reflective equilibrium — to weigh intuitive plausibility against theoretical benefit. Lewis is explicit that the cost is high but insists the benefit is higher.

Second, the parsimony of kinds argument: accepting infinitely many worlds does not increase the number of *kinds* of entity posited. Everything in every world consists of the same basic types — physical objects and their spatiotemporal relations. The multiplication is not of kinds but of tokens. If ontological parsimony concerns kinds, not tokens, modal realism may be more parsimonious than its abstract alternatives.

Third, modal realism is not uniquely counterintuitive. Any theory that takes possible worlds seriously will face some version of the incredulous stare. Ersatzism posits abstract propositions or states of affairs, which are not obviously more intuitive than concrete worlds. Fictionalism involves claiming that our best modal reasoning is conducted within a fiction. The incredulous stare is not specifically targeted at Lewis; it applies to any serious theoretical commitment in modal metaphysics.

## The Question of Evidential Weight

How much evidential weight should common-sense intuitions carry against theoretical arguments? Lewis's philosophical pragmatism about this is clear: intuitions are data, but they are defeasible. Philosophical progress sometimes requires revising deeply held intuitions in light of theoretical argument. The history of science is full of counterintuitive theories — heliocentrism, special relativity, quantum mechanics — that turned out to be correct.

Against this, there are principled reasons to weight the intuition that there is one concrete universe very heavily. It is not a parochial cultural artifact but a near-universal, apparently non-revisable commitment. Lewis might respond: the intuition that the Earth is stationary seemed equally non-revisable. Theoretical arguments can override even the most deeply held intuitions when the theoretical case is strong enough.

The key disanalogy: scientific theories that overturned common-sense intuitions did so with the support of vast bodies of empirical evidence — they explained observable phenomena that their rivals could not. Lewis's modal realism cannot be empirically tested in any direct sense. The analogy from scientific revisionism to metaphysical revisionism requires that theoretical virtues in philosophy (systematization, unification, analytical power) carry similar weight to empirical confirmation in science. Whether they do is a contested methodological claim.

## The Debate Continues

Philosophers who take the incredulous stare seriously have pursued alternatives: ersatzism (abstract possible worlds preserving the analytical benefits), primitivism (treating modal facts as primitive and not analyzable in terms of worlds at all), and fictionalism (using possible worlds talk instrumentally without commitment to their existence). Each alternative is subject to philosophical objections, but they illustrate that the incredulous stare need not be met with acceptance — it can motivate the search for alternatives that preserve Lewis's theoretical benefits at lower ontological cost. The incredulous stare remains a significant constraint on modal realism: a view that so radically violates common sense needs exceptionally strong theoretical justification.
