# The Isolation Objection

Lewis's definition of a world as a maximal spatiotemporally connected system of objects entails that possible worlds are causally and spatiotemporally isolated from one another. No signal, no particle, no causal influence crosses from one world to another. This isolation is built into the very criterion that individuates worlds. It generates two related problems: an epistemological problem about how we can know anything about other worlds, and a metaphysical problem about what makes the isolation criterion the right one.

## The Epistemological Problem

The standard account of knowledge requires some appropriate connection between the knower and the facts known. For empirical knowledge, this connection is causal: the world causes our perceptions, and the causal history grounds the reliability of our perceptual beliefs. Mathematical Platonism faces an analogous challenge, famously pressed by Benacerraf: if mathematical objects are causally inert, how do we reliably form true beliefs about them?

Lewis's worlds are supposed to be concrete entities — as physically real as our world — yet causally inaccessible. The challenge in argument form:

- P1: For genuine knowledge of a domain D, the knower must have some appropriate epistemic access to D.
- P2: Lewis's possible worlds are concrete entities causally and spatiotemporally isolated from us — we have no causal access to them.
- P3: We have no constitutive or logical connection to the specific individuals in other worlds.
- C: We cannot have genuine knowledge of other possible worlds on Lewis's account.

Lewis's response: our knowledge of possible worlds is not perceptual but rational. We know what is possible through rational reflection, imagination, and conceptual analysis. The knowledge is a priori or semi-a priori — not based on causal interaction with the worlds but on our understanding of what is coherent and consistent.

This response faces a pointed challenge: the epistemological parity objection. If our knowledge of possible worlds is through rational reflection rather than causal interaction, why does Lewis need to posit *concrete* worlds rather than abstract representations? If the epistemology is conceptual rather than perceptual, abstract ersatz worlds would do equally well epistemologically. The move to concrete worlds appears unmotivated from an epistemological perspective — we pay the ontological cost of concreteness without gaining any epistemological benefit.

A more severe form: on Lewis's view, there is no mechanism by which the actual distribution of properties across concrete worlds could cause our modal intuitions to be accurate. Our modal intuitions — that round squares are impossible, that talking donkeys are possible — are reliable, but their reliability cannot be grounded in causal contact with the concrete worlds that make them true. This leaves an explanatory gap analogous to Benacerraf's problem for Platonism.

## The Metaphysical Problem

The isolation objection also raises a problem about the criterion for world-individuation. Lewis defines worlds as maximal spatiotemporally connected systems. But this criterion seems to invite a charge of circularity. Why is spatiotemporal connectivity the principle that individuates worlds? The natural answer is: because it tracks causal isolation — things that could interact are in the same world, things that could not are in different worlds. But causal isolation is itself a modal notion. The criterion for world-individuation seems to presuppose the concept of modality that worlds are supposed to illuminate.

Lewis's response: spatiotemporal connectivity is not arbitrary. It tracks the most fundamental form of concrete connection we know of. Possible worlds are individuated by the limits of concrete structure, and spatiotemporal connection is the best available criterion for such limits. He acknowledged challenges from quantum entanglement and the multiverse interpretation of quantum mechanics but held that these edge cases do not undermine the general framework.

## The Representation Problem for Ersatzism

Lewis turned the epistemological challenge into an argument against his actualist competitors. If possible worlds are abstract representations rather than concrete realities, we need an account of how abstract objects represent possibilities. Lewis argued this representation relation is either circular (using modality to explain what it means to represent a possibility) or regressive (requiring a further account of how the abstract representor links to what is represented).

The isolation objection targets Lewis's concrete worlds; the representation problem targets abstract ersatz worlds. Both point to the same underlying difficulty: our epistemic access to modal space is puzzling regardless of how we characterize that space. Lewis chose to bite the bullet on the epistemological isolation and insist that rational reflection provides access. Ersatzists bite a different bullet: postulating an unexplained representational relation between abstract objects and modal facts.

This is a genuine dilemma, and Lewis was right to press it. Either we accept concrete worlds (with causal isolation) or we accept abstract worlds (with unexplained representation). Neither option is epistemologically clean. The choice turns on which form of mystery is less intolerable — and that is ultimately a judgment call about the respective theoretical costs.
