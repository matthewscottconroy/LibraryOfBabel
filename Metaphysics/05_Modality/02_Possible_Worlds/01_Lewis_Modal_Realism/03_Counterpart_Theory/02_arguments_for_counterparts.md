# Arguments for Counterpart Theory

Lewis offered several arguments for counterpart theory over transworld identity. Each illuminates something different about what is at stake in the choice between them.

## The Mereological Argument

If the same individual could be part of different possible worlds, this would complicate the mereology of worlds. Worlds are maximal connected systems — each world is the sum of everything spatiotemporally connected to any given individual in it. If individual a is a part of both world w₁ and world w₂, then w₁ and w₂ share a part. But then the "sum of everything connected to a" would include all of w₁ and all of w₂ — making them both parts of a single larger world, contradicting the assumption that they are distinct worlds.

The conclusion is that transworld identity is incompatible with the mereological definition of worlds. If worlds are individuated by spatiotemporal connectivity, no individual can be in more than one world. Counterpart theory, which places each individual in exactly one world, is the natural consequence of this criterion.

## The Temporal Analogy Revisited

Lewis's most powerful argument builds on his account of temporal persistence. Consider how we handle temporal variation: Socrates is standing at t₁ and sitting at t₂. Two solutions:

- **Endurantism**: Socrates is wholly present at both times, with world-indexed (or time-indexed) properties: standing-at-t₁, sitting-at-t₂.
- **Perdurantism**: There are distinct temporal parts: the t₁-Socrates-part is standing, the t₂-Socrates-part is sitting. No single part has contradictory properties.

Lewis defends perdurantism: temporal variation is handled by temporal parts. He then extends this reasoning to modal variation:

- **Transworld identity solution**: Humphrey exists in both w₁ and w₂, with world-indexed properties. (Analogous to endurantism.)
- **Counterpart solution**: The w₁-Humphrey-counterpart wins; the w₂-Humphrey-counterpart loses. Different individuals, no contradiction. (Analogous to perdurantism.)

If we accept perdurantism for temporal variation, we have strong reason to accept the counterpart-theoretic treatment for modal variation. The theoretical unity is elegant — both temporal and modal variation are handled by the same kind of principle, differing only in the index (time vs. world).

## Addressing "Humphrey Doesn't Care"

Kripke pressed an objection: Humphrey cares about himself winning, not about some counterpart winning. Counterpart theory gives the wrong truth conditions for "Humphrey might have won" — the truth conditions are about the counterpart, not about Humphrey.

Lewis's response distinguishes truth conditions from subject matter. When we say "Humphrey might have won," the subject matter is Humphrey — the claim is about him. But the truth conditions involve counterparts: the claim is made true by facts about a counterpart who wins. These come apart: I can make a claim about you whose truth conditions involve hypothetical scenarios you do not occupy, and the claim is still genuinely about you.

Humphrey's concern for himself under the guise "I myself" is a concern for his actual situation. Counterpart theory does not require that Humphrey's concern extends to his counterparts. His modal profile — what alternatives were open to him — is determined by counterparts, but the concern is entirely actual. The worry that counterpart theory mislocates the subject of modal claims arises from conflating truth conditions with subject matter.

## The Argument from Anti-Haecceitism

Consider two possible worlds that are intrinsically qualitatively identical — same distribution of properties and relations — but are supposed to involve different individuals. Haecceitism allows for this: primitive thisnesses (haecceities) can distinguish individuals that are qualitatively identical. Lewis is anti-haecceitist: there is nothing more to individuals than their qualitative properties. Two qualitatively identical worlds are not distinct.

If Lewis is right, then transworld identity claims that go beyond qualitative similarity are unmotivated. "The very same Humphrey" in another world means the most similar individual to Humphrey — which is just the counterpart. Counterpart theory is what remains when you take anti-haecceitism seriously: modal identity is qualitative similarity, and the counterpart relation is exactly the relevant similarity relation.

## The Argument from Modal Logic

Counterpart theory generates a different modal logic than standard S5 for de re claims involving specific individuals. Standard S5 validates formulas that seem to generate necessarily existing concrete individuals. Counterpart theory naturally generates contingent existence: an individual in our world has counterparts in other worlds only if those worlds contain sufficiently similar individuals, which need not always be the case.

This is arguably an advantage. Concrete individuals should exist contingently; necessarily existing concrete individuals would be philosophically bizarre. The formal behavior of counterpart theory is better calibrated to the intuitive contingency of concrete existence than transworld identity theories, which tend to validate the existence of an individual across all worlds once they are postulated in any one.

Kripke accepted transworld identity and rejected counterpart theory, insisting that Humphrey himself — not a counterpart — might have won the election. But Kripke also accepted world-indexed properties for the same individual across worlds, which commits him to a view that faces puzzles about what grounds the identity. Counterpart theory avoids these puzzles by not positing transworld identity at all: de re modal properties are explicitly world-comparative, and the clarity of this treatment is a genuine theoretical advantage.
