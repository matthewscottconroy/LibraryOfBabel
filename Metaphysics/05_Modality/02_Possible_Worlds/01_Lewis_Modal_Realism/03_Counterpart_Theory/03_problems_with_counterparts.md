# Problems with Counterpart Theory

Counterpart theory faces significant objections. The most philosophically important concern whether it gives the right truth conditions for de re modal claims, whether its context-sensitivity generates unacceptable indeterminacy about essence, and whether the resulting modal logic validates what it should. These are not technical quibbles — they go to the heart of whether counterpart theory captures what de re modality is.

## Kripke's Humphrey Objection

Kripke's objection is the most famous. When we say "Humphrey might have won the election," we express a claim about Humphrey — about him, the man. Counterpart theory says the claim is made true by a counterpart of Humphrey winning. But Humphrey doesn't care about his counterpart; he cares about himself. The counterpart winning is not the same as Humphrey winning. So counterpart theory seems to give the wrong truth conditions: it makes the claim about someone else.

Lewis's response distinguished the formal truth conditions from the subject matter and pragmatics of modal claims. Formally, "Humphrey might have won" is made true by a counterpart. But this does not mean we are claiming something about the counterpart rather than Humphrey — the counterpart *represents* a possibility for Humphrey. The truth conditions and the subject matter come apart.

Critics find this response unsatisfying. The usual connection between truth conditions and subject matter is precisely that the truth conditions identify what the claim is about. If the truth conditions involve counterparts, the claim seems to be about counterparts, not about Humphrey himself. The distinction between truth conditions and subject matter looks ad hoc — introduced to handle the objection rather than motivated independently.

## The Context-Sensitivity Problem

The context-sensitivity of counterpart relations creates a different problem. If there is no single, determinate counterpart relation, many de re modal claims may lack determinate truth values. "Humphrey might have been taller" is true or false only relative to a choice of counterpart relation — and there may be no privileged choice.

This indeterminacy contrasts with our pre-theoretic confidence that de re modal claims often have determinate truth values. "Socrates could not have been a number" seems determinately true, not merely true relative to some similarity weightings. If counterpart theory gives indeterminate truth values for such claims, it fails to capture the structure of de re modality.

Lewis's reply: context-sensitivity is not indeterminacy. In a given context, the relevant similarity weighting is determined. The sentence "Humphrey might have been taller," in the context of discussing his physical possibilities, has a determinate truth value relative to that context. Context-sensitivity no more undermines semantic content here than the context-sensitivity of "tall" undermines "Humphrey is tall."

The rejoinder: for "tall," there is typically a contextually determined standard, and the sentence has a determinate truth value relative to it. For counterpart theory, the choice of similarity metric is much more open and less constrained by linguistic and practical context. The context may leave many counterpart relations equally eligible, generating genuine indeterminacy that mere context-sensitivity cannot resolve.

## Essential Properties and Counterpart Sensitivity

A related problem concerns de re necessity. If Socrates is essentially human, counterpart theory says every Socrates-counterpart is human. But the context-sensitivity of counterparthood means that whether there is a non-human Socrates-counterpart depends on the context.

In a context where the similarity metric weights biological properties heavily, there is no non-human Socrates-counterpart. In a context where it weights psychological properties heavily, there might be one. This makes essential properties context-sensitive in a way that many essentialists find deeply unsatisfying. Whether Socrates is essentially human is not a conversational matter — it is a fact about Socrates. Counterpart theory seems to make essence conversationally variable, which conflicts with the essentialist picture.

Lewis's response: "essentially human" can be captured by specifying that Socrates is human in every world where he has a counterpart, under the *most natural* counterpart relation. Humanity is essential because, under the naturalness-weighted counterpart relation, every counterpart is human. This is a defensible reply, but it trades on a conception of naturalness that Lewis takes as primitive — and it is unclear whether the notion of natural counterpart relations does enough work to fully vindicate the essentialist's intuitions.

## Other Difficulties

The theory can generate counterintuitive results about coincident counterparts. If two actual persons are very similar, a single world-individual might be a counterpart of both. This generates the apparent consequence: "It is possible that John and Mary are one person." Lewis's reply is that the relevant counterpart relation for personal identity is sufficiently restrictive to prevent this in ordinary contexts — but the worry is that the flexibility of the theory makes it too easy to generate odd consequences by selecting unusual counterpart relations.

Finally, standard modal logic validates the principle that □P → P for de re claims: if something is necessarily F, then it is F. But counterpart theory potentially validates some strange inferences in cases involving multiple simultaneous counterparts of the same individual. Lewis restricted his theory to prevent the worst cases, but the issue reveals that counterpart theory's modal logic is more complex than standard S5, and the complexities do not always favor the theory.

The debate over counterpart theory is ultimately a debate about whether de re modality can be grounded in inter-world similarity, or whether it requires a more robust notion of trans-world identity and intrinsic essence. Those who follow Kripke and Fine in thinking that essence is not merely a matter of similarity across worlds will find counterpart theory's analysis ultimately inadequate.
